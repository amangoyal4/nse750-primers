00

The one-page view: a gas shock that handed the brands a market

In 2026 the cheapest tile makers in India became the most expensive to run, and the export trade that usually soaks up their surplus closed at the same time.

India makes roughly one in every six ceramic tiles produced in the world, second only to China, and about three-quarters of those tiles come out of a single district in Saurashtra: Morbi. For two decades Morbi's hundreds of family-owned kilns set the floor price of a square metre of tile in India, and the branded companies (Kajaria, Somany, H&R Johnson and a handful of others) earned their living by charging a premium on top of it for design, consistency, a dealer they trusted and a guarantee that the box would match the sample. The industry's revenue was about ₹51,000–53,000 crore in FY26 on CRISIL's count, roughly two-thirds sold at home and one-third exported.

The defining fact of the industry is that a tile is fired clay, and firing clay at 1,100–1,220 °C takes a great deal of fuel. Morbi's kilns run on piped natural gas from a single supplier, Gujarat Gas, and on propane landed from the Gulf. When the West Asia war began on 28-Feb-2026, both fuels spiked. Morbi's piped-gas price rose from about ₹48 per standard cubic metre (SCM) before the war to ₹90 (plus 6% VAT) from 2-Sep-2026. Most of the cluster shut from about 5-Mar to 15-Apr-2026. Morbi's makers raised their prices by 35–40%. Kajaria, whose plants in Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh buy contracted gas at ₹63–71 per SCM, raised its prices by only 12–13%.

The result inverted the usual logic of a cost shock. Gas is a risk for every tile maker, but in 2026 it was a much bigger risk for the cheapest producer than for the brand. Kajaria's chairman estimates that the gap between a Kajaria tile and a comparable Morbi tile narrowed from about 40% before the war to 20–25% by October. With the premium halved, homeowners and contractors traded up. Kajaria sold 31.81 MSM of tiles in Q2 FY27 (quarter to Sep-2026), 10% more than a year earlier, against an industry it estimates grew 5–6%; revenue rose 24% to ₹1,468 crore, EBITDA margin was 19.5% and profit after tax rose 43% to ₹189.5 crore.

Exports amplify every swing. India exported about US$2.43 billion of tiles in FY24 (roughly ₹20,100 crore), US$2.13 billion in FY25 (₹18,000 crore) and a similar amount in FY26. In FY27 the trade is heading for ₹11,000–12,500 crore, a fall of 30–40%, because freight has doubled, the Gulf states renewed anti-dumping duties on Indian tiles from 28-May-2026, the United States charges a countervailing duty plus a 10% Section 301 tariff, and Mexico imposed provisional anti-dumping duties of up to US$10.98 per square metre from 28-Sep-2026. When exports fail, Morbi normally dumps its surplus at home and the brands suffer, as they did in FY25. In 2026 Morbi's costs rose so far that much of that surplus was never made: Kajaria's chairman said in late October that 40–50% of Morbi's plants were idle.

Three caveats keep this from being a simple victory lap. First, the branded group barely gained volume as a whole (about +1.5% for the four that disclose it, below the market's 5–6%): in Q1 FY27 Kajaria grew 6% and Orient Bell 23%, but H&R Johnson's tile volume fell 18% and Varmora's fell 15% in the first half. The winners were the brands with their own plants outside Morbi on contracted gas; the losers were those that depended on Morbi joint ventures, outsourcing or propane. Second, the largest unlisted Morbi brand, Simpolo, earned an 18.5% operating margin on ₹2,157 crore of revenue in FY26, so a Morbi address does not doom a well-run brand. Third, the windfall depends on gas staying expensive in Gujarat; if it eases, Morbi will cut prices and the gap will widen again, and Kajaria has said its own prices would follow.

For investors the industry has one listed lens in the NSE 750, and it is an unusually dominant one. Among the four listed tile pure-plays (Kajaria, Somany, Asian Granito and Orient Bell), Kajaria earned 47.5% of FY26 revenue but 68.9% of operating profit, 82.3% of net profit and 81.0% of market value. The other three together earned ₹105 crore of profit on ₹5,339 crore of revenue, a 2.0% net margin; Kajaria alone earned ₹487 crore on ₹4,830 crore. The market has priced that difference: Kajaria trades at 27.5 times trailing earnings and 5.8 times book, Somany at 20.3 times and 2.6 times, Asian Granito below book.

In plain terms

Indian tiles are a commodity made mostly by small factories in one Gujarat town, wrapped in a thin layer of brands. In normal times the small factories win on price. In 2026 their fuel bill nearly doubled and their overseas customers vanished, so the brands, whose factories sit elsewhere on cheaper contracted gas, could sell more tiles at a smaller premium and still earn record margins. The question for the next two years is how much of that gain stays once Gujarat's gas price falls.

The report is built around five forces. The first is the gas cost curve: who pays what for fuel, and how the 2026 shock rewired it (section 04, a deep dive). The second is the export valve: India's tile surplus either leaves the country or lands on the domestic market, and in 2026 the valve shut (section 05, a deep dive). The third is the brand-versus-Morbi price gap, the single number that governs organised share (section 06). The fourth is where capacity is being built and whether brands own or rent their kilns: Kajaria is adding 23.5 MSM of its own glazed vitrified capacity outside Gujarat, and the Tirupati belt of Andhra Pradesh is becoming a second cluster (section 11, a deep dive). The fifth is demand: real-estate completions, renovation and the shift to large-format glazed vitrified tiles (section 07). Section 13 is the KPI masterclass: how to read a tile company's numbers, including the definitional traps that make headline volumes and realisations easy to misread.

01

Clay, gas, kiln, dealer: how a square metre of tile earns money

A tile is cheap to design, expensive to fire, heavy to move and sold through a dealer the buyer trusts. Profit sits at the two ends: the gas meter and the brand.

A ceramic tile starts as a body of mixed minerals: ball clay, china clay (kaolin), feldspar and quartz sand, mined mostly in Rajasthan and Gujarat. The mix is ground wet in ball mills, sprayed into a hot-air drier to make a free-flowing powder, pressed into shape by hydraulic presses of 3,000–7,000 tonnes, dried, glazed, decorated by digital inkjet printers and fired in a roller-hearth kiln at 1,100–1,220 °C for 40–70 minutes. Vitrified tiles are then polished and squared. Two of these steps, the spray drier and the kiln, consume most of the fuel; that is why fuel is a quarter or more of production cost and why the location of a plant relative to cheap gas matters more in tiles than in most building materials.

The products differ mainly by how much water the fired body absorbs, which tracks density, strength and price. Ceramic wall tiles absorb more than 10% and are the cheapest; vitrified tiles absorb less than 0.5% and are hard enough for floors that take heavy traffic. Within vitrified, polished vitrified tiles (PVT) carry their colour through a polished layer, while glazed vitrified tiles (GVT, and polished GVT) have a digitally printed glaze that can mimic marble, wood or stone. GVT is the growth product: the 600×1200 mm format is now, in Kajaria's words, "the most prevalent size which is sold in the market", and slabs up to 1,600×3,200 mm compete with granite and quartz countertops.

From the factory a tile travels a long, layered route. Branded makers sell to dealers (Kajaria has about 1,800, roughly 450 of them exclusive, and says "we don't have distributors, we have dealers"); dealers sell to sub-dealers and retail counters; the final buying decision is often shaped by a contractor, mason or architect rather than the homeowner. Project sales to builders, hospitals, airports and government buildings go direct. Tiles weigh about 20 kg per square metre, so freight over 1,000 km can be 8–12% of the landed price; that is why a plant in Rajasthan or Andhra Pradesh has a structural edge over Morbi when selling in Delhi or Chennai.

Profit concentrates in Step 2 (whoever has the cheapest fuel) and Step 3 (whoever can charge a premium for the name). Morbi units compete in Step 2; the brands win in Step 3 and use Morbi as a flexible factory.

Where is the value captured? The brand-and-distribution layer earns most of the industry's profit. Kajaria earned an 18% operating margin and a 23% return on capital in FY26 while buying about a quarter of the tiles it sold from independent Morbi units; a typical unbranded Morbi factory earns a low-single-digit to roughly 10% EBITDA margin in a normal year and loses money in a gas spike. On FY26 figures (dossier arithmetic from Kajaria's disclosures), Kajaria sold tiles from its own plants at about ₹370 per square metre, from its Morbi and Telangana subsidiaries at ₹344 and outsourced tiles at ₹359, so bought-in tiles carried almost the full brand price. The brand's margin on an outsourced tile is the difference between that realisation and the Morbi ex-factory price, which was 25–35% lower before the war.

Further up the chain, Italian and Chinese machinery makers (presses, kilns, continuous-press slab lines) and Spanish and Italian glaze and ink chemists capture technology rent, and gas suppliers capture the energy rent whenever fuel spikes. Further down, the brands increasingly sell the system rather than the tile: large, dense tiles need polymer adhesives instead of cement mortar, which is why Kajaria's adhesive revenue grew from ₹76.8 crore in FY25 to ₹136.4 crore in FY26, rose 69% in Q2 FY27 and is guided to about ₹240 crore in FY27 and ₹400 crore in FY28. The same logic links tiles to bathroom fittings, which Kajaria sells under the Kerovit name and Cera and H&R Johnson sell alongside their tiles.

In plain terms

A tile company makes money in two places: by firing clay more cheaply than its rivals, and by persuading buyers to pay extra for its name. Morbi is very good at the first and mostly ignores the second. The big brands are good at the second and, in 2026, found themselves better at the first too, because their gas was cheaper than Morbi's for the first time in years.

Two adjacent industries help explain the economics. Tiles compete for the same renovation budget as paints and wood panels (see the paints and plywood and laminates reports), and the brand-plus-dealer model in tiles resembles the paints model with one crucial difference: a paint company owns a recipe and a tinting machine, while a tile company owns a kiln that anyone in Morbi can replicate for a few crore rupees. Tiles also share an energy problem with glass, the other fuel-hungry silicate furnace industry, and a construction cycle with cement, though cement goes in at the start of a building and tiles at the end.

02

A ₹52,000 crore industry, two-thirds at home and one-third at sea

The size depends on who is counting. CRISIL's documented sample is the cleanest anchor; management's estimates and the Varmora prospectus sit on different perimeters.

Four incompatible numbers for the size of the Indian tile industry circulate, and a reader must know which is which before comparing anything. The cleanest is CRISIL Ratings' estimate, based on a sample of 40 companies covering about a quarter of revenue: industry revenue of ₹51,000–53,000 crore in FY26, with domestic demand about two-thirds (roughly ₹35,000 crore) and exports about one-third. The export part is consistent with government trade data, which put tile exports at about US$2.1 billion (₹18,000–18,500 crore) in FY26. Varmora Granito's IPO prospectus, reproduced by PL Capital, gives a series from ₹36,000 crore in FY19 to ₹51,700 crore in FY24, ₹53,100 crore in FY25 and a projected ₹76,900 crore in FY29; it is the same order of magnitude as CRISIL's total and is best read as total industry revenue rather than the domestic market.

Kajaria's chairman offered a third view on the Q2 FY27 call: about ₹48,000 crore of domestic sales plus ₹12,000 crore of exports, roughly ₹60,000 crore in FY27, "due to the price hike also". That implies domestic value up by more than a third in a year, which is possible only with about 20% price inflation and a different basis, probably consumer prices rather than factory prices. It is management's estimate and should be quoted as such. A fourth figure, "₹75,000 crore" for India's ceramics industry (Business Today, March 2026), includes sanitaryware. Our own Kajaria primer's "US$10–11 billion domestic market" is too high against every other source and is not used here.

Growth has been modest in volume and faster in value. In a normal year Indian tile volume grows 5–6%; Kajaria uses that estimate for FY27, and MECS, the Italian ceramics research house, forecasts Indian demand rising about 778 MSM over 2023–28, a 6–7% annual rate and the fastest of any major producer. On the prospectus segment series, glazed vitrified tiles were 29% of industry value in FY19, 35% in FY24 and are projected at 45% in FY29, while polished vitrified fell from 31% to a projected 21%. Kajaria's own volume history shows the industry's rhythm: 75 MSM in FY21 (a COVID year, down 3.4%), 92 MSM in FY22 (+21.5%), 102 in FY23, 108 in FY24, 114.69 in FY25 and 118.52 in FY26 (+3.3%, with the first nine months almost flat). In plain terms

The industry sells roughly ₹52,000 crore of tiles a year, of which about ₹35,000 crore stays in India. Kajaria's tile revenue of ₹4,280 crore is therefore about 12% of the domestic market by value, and its 118.5 MSM about 6% by volume. The difference between those two shares is the brand premium.

03

Morbi and the cost of a tile: one district, one gas pipe, a quarter of the bill

Morbi makes 70–75% of India's tiles from about 800 fuel-buying units. Its fuel bill, not Kajaria's, sets the price floor for the whole country.

Morbi, a district in Saurashtra between Rajkot and the Gulf of Kutch, grew from wall-tile workshops in the 1980s into one of the largest ceramic clusters in the world. PL Capital estimates it makes 70–75% of India's tiles; older figures of "~90%" and "1,800-plus kilns" (used in our Kajaria primer) count kilns rather than units and overstate the share. The most precise unit count comes from the gas supplier itself: in February 2026, before the war, Gujarat Gas had 377 piped-gas customers in Morbi and about 415 propane users were supplied by the state oil companies, so roughly 790–800 units bought fuel. Gujarat government figures of ~1,200 include sanitaryware, frit and ancillary units. The cluster employs about four lakh people directly.

Morbi's competitive edge has always been cost: cheap land, clustered suppliers of frit, glaze, packaging and machinery service, a deep pool of migrant labour, and fuel bought at industrial rates. Its disadvantage is that it is concentrated in one place, on one gas network. In 2019 the National Green Tribunal ordered Morbi's units off coal gasifiers, which pushed the cluster onto piped natural gas and propane. Before the 2026 war about 70% of Morbi's fuel was propane, landed at Kandla from the Gulf, and the rest piped gas from Gujarat Gas, whose Morbi price is linked to marine gas oil and therefore to crude. That combination means Morbi's cost curve is set by Gulf energy prices and shipping, not by anything that happens in India.

In plain terms

Kajaria does not have the cheapest gas in the industry; Cera and Orient Bell pay less. What Kajaria has is gas that is much cheaper than Morbi's, on contracts that did not double when the Gulf caught fire, at plants close to the big markets of the North and South. Capacity tells the same story from another angle. India's tile capacity is roughly 3,000–3,300 MSM (INDICATIVE), and CRISIL puts industry asset utilisation at only 64–66%: the country can make far more tiles than it sells. Morbi's capacity is probably 2,200–2,500 MSM (INDICATIVE; no primary source publishes it). The branded listed makers and Simpolo together own about 336 MSM of capacity, only 12–13% of the national total, yet they earn roughly 40% of domestic value. Organised players are concentrated in value, not in kilns, because they outsource 20–30% of what they sell (ICRA) and charge a premium on all of it. That chronic overcapacity is why Morbi prices fall quickly whenever exports falter, and why a supply shock that idles Morbi's marginal kilns is the brands' best friend.

The section that follows takes the gas shock in detail. Two points from the cost structure matter for everything after it. First, the input that moved in 2026 was the one where the brands and Morbi pay most differently, so the shock changed relative costs, not just absolute ones. Second, the brands are also Morbi's customers: a quarter of Kajaria's volume and a larger share of Somany's and H&R Johnson's is bought from Morbi units, so when Morbi's costs rise the brands' purchase costs rise too. The winners in 2026 were those with the largest share of their own non-Gujarat output and the pricing power to pass the rest through.

04

Ninety rupees a cubic metre: how the 2026 gas shock rewired Morbi's cost curve Deep dive

Morbi's piped gas went from about ₹48 to ₹90 per SCM in six months, its propane supply broke, and for the first time in its history the whole cluster shut. The brands' gas barely moved by comparison.

Every Indian tile maker had lived through a gas shock before. In 2022, when Russia invaded Ukraine and spot LNG went above US$40 per mmbtu, Morbi's piped gas rose to about ₹65–70 per SCM, several hundred units shut for a month, and Kajaria's net profit fell 10% in FY23 even as revenue rose 18%. The 2026 shock was different in three ways: it was larger, it hit propane as well as piped gas, and it arrived when the export market was already closing. Together those differences turned a cost problem for the whole industry into a competitive advantage for the brands with plants outside Gujarat.

The sequence, February to October 2026

The West Asia war began with US and Israeli strikes on Iran on 28-Feb-2026. Within days the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted and Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG complex was struck. India receives 50–60% of its LNG through Hormuz (ICRA), and Morbi's propane comes from the Gulf through Kandla. Propane supply to Morbi fell by about 70% in March. The government issued an emergency allocation order capping industrial and commercial city-gas customers at 80% of their previous six-month average use, and Gujarat Gas invoked force majeure, cut Morbi's supply by about half and terminated some of its MGO-linked contracts. Gujarat Gas advised a price of ₹85–88 per SCM against about ₹41 before. With neither fuel available at a workable price, Morbi shut from about 5-Mar-2026. The price path matters less than its link. Gujarat Gas prices Morbi's tile category off marine gas oil, a crude-oil product, so the cluster's fuel cost tracks Brent and the rupee rather than domestic gas policy; its September formula equated US$28.294 per mmbtu to ₹90 per SCM at ₹96 to the dollar. With Indian spot LNG reaching US$28.4 per mmbtu in September, Morbi's two fuels became interchangeable and both expensive, which is why units switched back and forth between them week by week (the gas distribution report covers the supplier side, including how Morbi's swing demand moved Gujarat Gas's volumes from 0.4 to 8 and back to about 3 mmscmd during 2026).

What the shock did to the cost of a square metre

No company discloses how much fuel it burns per square metre, so the arithmetic must be built from the supplier's volumes. Before the war Morbi used about 8.0 mmscmd of fuel on a natural-gas-equivalent basis (Gujarat Gas's ~2.4 mmscmd of piped gas plus ~5.6 mmscmd of propane). Over a year that is about 2.9 billion SCM, which divided by Morbi's output of roughly 1,750–1,900 MSM gives about 1.5–1.7 SCM per square metre (INDICATIVE). At 1.6 SCM, moving from ₹48 to ₹90 plus 6% VAT (₹95.4) adds about ₹76 per square metre to a Morbi tile (INDICATIVE). On a pre-war ex-factory price of about ₹230 for a mid-range Morbi GVT tile, that is a third of the selling price, which is consistent with the 35–40% price increases Morbi actually pushed through.

The state of Morbi: date-stamp every statement

How much of Morbi is working depends on when the question was asked, and the listed companies' answers conflict for a real reason. In August, Somany's managing director said Morbi had "resumed 100% production" from the end of May on expensive gas, and that 15–17% of Morbi's capacity would probably never restart; Orient Bell said in the same month that Morbi plants were "running at capacity" after mid-June. Those statements describe the June–August window, when propane was back and cheaper than piped gas. On 26-Oct, after the 2-Sep increase to ₹90 and an October propane spike, Kajaria's chairman said 40–50% of Morbi's 400–500 plants were not working. Varmora, the only listed pure-Morbi maker, said in October that the cluster had added three or four new GVT lines in the first half of FY27 but shut 10–11 PVT and ceramic plants, so there was "net-net no capacity addition". The fair summary: Morbi largely restarted by June, and a large share idled again from September.

Who else lost, and who did not gain

The shock did not reward every brand. H&R Johnson, the tile division of Prism Johnson, sources heavily from Morbi joint ventures and outsourcing; its Q1 FY27 tile volume fell 18% to 10.8 MSM because of "temporarily low inventory levels and unhealthy SKU mix following earlier Morbi disruptions", even as its realisation rose 17% to ₹394 per square metre. Its management is the most cautious in the industry, warning that "the pricing benefit from temporary supply tightness is likely to moderate as industry supply normalises" (see the Prism Johnson primer). Varmora, whose plants are almost all in Morbi and run partly on propane that cost ₹80–92 per SCM, lost 15% of its sales volume in the first half of FY27. Cera, which buys most of its tiles from Morbi, faced higher purchase costs without a manufacturing offset, though tiles are only 11% of its revenue (see the Cera Sanitaryware primer).

The policy response was limited. The Gujarat government has offered ₹115 crore of assistance to about 2,200 Morbi beneficiaries over two years, and the Morbi associations lobbied for lower VAT and gas allocation, but no fuel subsidy of the kind that would restore the pre-war cost curve was announced. The merger of GSPC, GSPL and GSPC Energy into Gujarat Gas, effective 1-May-2026, consolidated the state's gas businesses but left Morbi with a single piped-gas supplier; Kajaria's management put the contrast bluntly on the Q1 call: in the North it buys from GAIL, "a much, much bigger player", while "in Morbi, there is only one gas supplier", which was partly importing on spot.

The sensitivity investors should carry

For Kajaria the rule of thumb is that each ₹10 per SCM on its roughly 85 MSM of own and subsidiary production costs about ₹110 crore a year (₹10 × ~1.3 SCM × 85 million sq m, INDICATIVE), equal to about 2.3% of FY26 revenue or 13% of FY26 EBITDA, if none of it is passed on. The more important sensitivity, though, is relative: what matters for Kajaria's volume and pricing is the gap between its own gas cost and Morbi's. At pre-war prices that gap was close to zero in the West and modestly favourable in the North; at ₹90 Morbi gas it is about ₹20–25 per SCM in Kajaria's favour, worth some ₹40–60 per square metre of competitive room on a Morbi-made tile (INDICATIVE). That room is what Kajaria is using to win volume rather than to widen margins.

In plain terms

Investors usually treat gas as a cost to fear. For Indian tiles in 2026 the useful question was not "how high is gas?" but "how much higher is Morbi's gas than the brand's?". When that difference is large, the brands win share; when it closes, Morbi wins it back. Watch the gap, not the level.

When the export valve shuts: India's tile trade from boom to walls Deep dive

Exports doubled in four years to US$2.43 billion, then hit freight, duties in the Gulf, the US and Mexico, and a war. FY27 is on course for the steepest fall on record, and India's own wall against China lapses in February 2027.

Morbi has always made more tiles than India buys. In good years the surplus leaves the country, and Indian tile exports doubled from about ₹10,000 crore in FY20 to a peak of about US$2.43 billion (roughly ₹20,100 crore) in FY24, helped by post-COVID demand, anti-dumping walls against Chinese tiles in the US, EU and Gulf, and India's cost advantage. In bad years the surplus stays at home and pushes domestic prices down. The export trade is therefore the valve that regulates the Indian price: when it shuts, the brands normally suffer.

The numbers, reconciled

Export figures in circulation disagree by up to a fifth for the same year, so the source matters. Government data compiled by DGCI&S and published by CAPEXIL, the export promotion council, show ceramic tile exports of US$2,430.7 million in FY24 and US$2,127.5 million in FY25, down 12.5% in dollars; at the year-average rupee that is about ₹20,100 crore and ₹18,000 crore, which confirms PL Capital's series and refutes a "₹16,000 crore, down 20%" figure attributed to a Kajaria presentation. For FY26 CAPEXIL reported US$2,115 million, which it called a record but which is in fact 0.6% below FY25; at about ₹87.5 to the dollar that is ₹18,000–18,500 crore, so Kajaria's management's "₹16,000 crore" for FY26 is too low. FY26 was flat; the collapse is a FY27 event.

Why the 2026 export collapse did not flood India

In FY25 the export valve shut partly (exports fell 12.5% in dollars), and the result was textbook: Morbi stock was diverted home, PL Capital described "inventory accumulation and dumping in the domestic market", and Kajaria's margin fell to about 13–14% while its profit dropped 31%. In FY27 the valve is shutting much harder, yet the domestic market has tightened rather than flooded. The difference is that the gas shock raised Morbi's cost of making a tile above the price at which it could dump one. A unit that cannot sell abroad and cannot cover its fuel bill at home switches off. Varmora's numbers show it: its export volume fell 41% in the first half of FY27 and its domestic volume 8%, so it lost share at home too even after raising prices 17%. Exports and gas are best read together: a shut valve with cheap gas is bad for the brands; a shut valve with expensive gas is good for them.

In plain terms

When Morbi cannot export, it normally sells the surplus in India at a discount and drags everyone's prices down. In 2026 Morbi's fuel became so expensive that it could not afford to make the surplus in the first place. If gas gets cheaper while exports stay blocked, the old pattern returns: cheap tiles flood India and the brands' margins fall.

February 2027: India's own wall may come down

The policy event with the largest potential effect on domestic prices is not abroad. India's anti-dumping duty on Chinese porcelain and vitrified tiles, US$1.87 per square metre under Notification 09/2022-Customs (ADD) of 24-Feb-2022, runs for five years and lapses around 23-Feb-2027 unless a sunset review is initiated before then. As of 1-Nov-2026 no initiation for tiles had been found; the only ceramics sunset review initiated by the trade remedies authority in September 2026 concerned tableware, and a news report says the four tile associations "had requested" a review. At ₹95 to the dollar the duty is worth about ₹178 per square metre, more than twice the INDICATIVE ₹76 per square metre the gas shock added to Morbi's cost, and 55–75% of a Morbi ex-factory price. Chinese tile makers, burning cheaper gas and facing a shrinking home market (China's output fell by about 820 MSM in 2024), would be competitive in India without it. Imports are negligible today (roughly US$10 million in 2024 on one low-reliability estimate); the duty is the reason.

A lapse would not hurt every Indian tile maker equally. Chinese competition would bite first in vitrified and large-format tiles sold on price, which is Morbi's export and domestic core, and in slabs and thin panels (though thin panels under 5 mm are already excluded from the duty). Branded makers would face pressure on the outsourced part of their range and on project sales where buyers compare landed prices. The BIS quality-control order on pressed ceramic tiles (IS 15622, amendment deadline 28-Jun-2026) is a second, non-tariff barrier, but it screens quality rather than price. This is a binary event for FY28, and it belongs at the top of the watch list.

In plain terms

India charges Chinese tile makers about ₹178 a square metre to sell vitrified tiles here. That charge expires in February 2027 unless the government opens a review, and nobody has publicly opened one yet. If it goes, the cheapest tiles in India might again come from China rather than Morbi.

06

The price gap: how a 40% premium became 20–25%, and who actually gained

Organised share rises when the gap between a branded tile and a Morbi tile narrows. In 2026 it narrowed sharply, but the volume went to the brands with their own non-Morbi kilns, not to the branded group as a whole.

If one number governs organised share in Indian tiles, it is the price gap: how much more a buyer pays for a branded tile than for a comparable one from a Morbi dealer. Kajaria's chairman estimates it at about 40% before 28-Feb-2026 for a tile from Kajaria's North or South plants, and 20–25% after the shock. Orient Bell's management put the same idea differently in August: "if the gap was ₹100, the gap has come down to ₹50–55". Two independent companies describing a halving of the premium is about as good as the evidence gets; no one measures it systematically. The number is management's estimate, not data, and should be quoted that way.

The arithmetic behind it is simple. Start with a Morbi tile at an index of 100 and a Kajaria tile at 140. Morbi raises its price by 35–40% (call it 37.5%) to 137.5; Kajaria raises by 12.5% to 157.5. The gap is now 14.5%, narrower than the 20–25% management quotes. The difference suggests Morbi gave some of its increases back: an analyst on Kajaria's Q2 call reported Morbi "discounts and price reductions" in September when propane was 20–25% cheaper than piped gas. Kajaria's own Morbi plants raised prices 18–19%, Somany 16–17% on average, Varmora 17% and Orient Bell 18–19%, so the brands that make or buy tiles in Gujarat raised by more than Kajaria's northern and southern plants.

Kajaria's strategy since the shock is explicit: hold prices and take volume. It took no increase in Q2 FY27 and intends none in Q3; its managing director said "we need to go more for volumes", and its chairman that "even 18%, 19%, that's a good margin. The company's own FY26 annual report states the counter-argument in its risk section: that "a significant portion of the volume uplift is attributable to supply disruptions caused by the ongoing conflict in the Middle East rather than to underlying demand strength". In plain terms

The shock helped brands that make their own tiles outside Gujarat and hurt brands that buy them from Gujarat. Kajaria and Orient Bell sold more; H&R Johnson and Varmora sold less; the branded group as a whole sold only slightly more square metres at much higher prices. "Brands win" is too broad. "Brands with their own non-Morbi kilns win" is what the numbers show.

Simpolo complicates the picture in a useful way. It is a Morbi brand, unlisted, rated AA− by ICRA, with revenue of ₹2,157 crore in FY26 (up 16%), an operating margin of 18.5% and capacity doubled to 39.6 MSM after new plants at Tirupati in Andhra Pradesh (8.2 MSM, November 2025) and Malia in Gujarat (11.1 MSM, May 2026). Its domestic sales are 94% of revenue. A Morbi address did not stop Simpolo earning a Kajaria-like margin, because it built a brand (Neotra at the premium end, Nexion below it), focused on large formats and took "timely price hikes" in Q1 FY27. Its Tirupati plant also places it in the same new cluster as Kajaria and Somany. The lesson is that the gap rewards brand plus location, and that some Morbi players are building both.

07

Who lays the floors: completions, renovation and the big-format upgrade

Tiles go in at the end of a building's life cycle, so demand follows completions, not launches. Volume grows 5–6% a year; value grows faster as buyers move to large glazed vitrified tiles and the adhesive that fixes them.

A tile is one of the last things to go into a building. Floors and walls are finished after the structure, plumbing and wiring, so tile demand follows the completion and handover of homes rather than their launch or sale. CRISIL puts the lag between a real-estate launch and tile demand at three to four years. That lag is why the tile cycle often looks out of step with the property headlines: a weak year for housing sales can coexist with a strong year for tiles if the projects launched three years earlier are now being finished.

Nobody publishes a primary split of tile demand by end-use, so the shares below are INDICATIVE composites from industry commentary: new residential construction about 45–55% of volume, replacement and renovation about 25–35%, and commercial, institutional and government projects about 15–25%. Within residential, urban apartments in the large cities drive vitrified and large-format demand, while rural and small-town homes, including the PMAY housing schemes, buy cheaper ceramic floor tiles, mostly from Morbi. The replacement market is steadier than new construction, driven by an ageing post-2000 urban housing stock and by households moving from mosaic, marble and old ceramic floors to vitrified tiles.

Tier-2 and tier-3 towns, and the dealer as gatekeeper

The second growth vector is geographic. Kajaria says it is "going deeper down to the Tier 2, Tier 3 cities" with more exclusive showrooms; it plans to add about 100 dealers, including 50 exclusive ones, while letting about 110 weaker dealers go, keeping the total near 1,800. In smaller towns the buying decision is usually made at the dealer's counter on the contractor's advice, so a brand needs presence in the shop and a reason for the dealer to push it: margin, credit terms, reliable supply and a sample that matches the box. In 2026 reliable supply was the decisive factor, because dealers who bought from Morbi could not get stock during the March–April shutdown, while Kajaria's North and South plants kept running.

The format upgrade and the adhesive pull-through

The third driver is product. The 600×1200 mm glazed vitrified tile is now the best-selling size, and large formats need different installation: a dense, low-absorption tile does not bond reliably with cement mortar, so it needs polymer-modified adhesive and often epoxy grout. That turns each square metre of large tile into a second sale. Kajaria's adhesive revenue was ₹54 crore in Q2 FY27, up 69%, and it is building capacity in steps of about 9,000 tonnes a month (Gailpur, Erode from May 2026, and Durgapur for ₹34 crore by December 2027), each worth ₹60–65 crore of revenue on a single shift. About 65% of its adhesive volume is still bought in. The same pull-through is why Pidilite, Ardex Endura and MYK Laticrete compete in tile adhesives, and why adhesive margins, which Kajaria describes as "very good", matter to the tile makers.

Two further features of demand matter for reading quarterly numbers. Seasonality is pronounced: the April–June quarter is the weakest (heat, labour migration, the monsoon's arrival), and January–March the strongest, as dealers stock up before new price lists in April. Q1 FY27 was additionally distorted because dealers pre-bought in March ahead of 1-April price increases, Morbi was shut, and labour was short because of state elections and an LPG shortage that sent workers home. In plain terms

The homes being finished in 2026 were mostly sold in 2022–23, and there are a lot of them, especially at the upper end. That supports tile demand now, even though affordable-housing sales are falling. The fastest-growing part of the market is large glazed tiles, which also need special adhesive, so a brand that sells both earns twice from the same floor.

About 40% of domestic value is earned by large organised players who own only 12–13% of national kiln capacity. The rest is Morbi's long tail, which is shrinking at the margin but still sets the price.

ICRA's June 2026 estimate is that large organised players hold about 40% of the domestic tile market and small or unorganised units about 60%. Owners: families, one private-equity name, one multinational

Ownership in Indian tiles is overwhelmingly by founding families. Kajaria's promoters hold 46.74% (after tendering in the 2026 buyback), Somany's 55.2%, Orient Bell's 65.7%, Asian Granito's 36.9%; the Raheja group controls Prism Johnson and therefore H&R Johnson. Private equity is rare: Carlyle invested in Varmora around 2018 and remains a shareholder after the IPO; a Motilal Oswal-led group put about ₹415 crore of preference capital into Simpolo in FY23; WestBridge held 15% of Kajaria's bathware subsidiary until Kajaria bought it out for ₹50 crore in Q2 FY27. The only multinational of size is RAK Ceramics of the UAE, whose Indian business shrank 9.2% in 2025 and 15.8% in Q2 2026 in its reporting currency, citing gas shortages in Morbi. Kajaria is the only Indian tile maker with meaningful foreign institutional ownership (12.05%) and domestic institutional ownership (26.09%) as of September 2026.

China is almost absent from the Indian market. Chinese tile imports were roughly US$10 million in 2024 on one low-reliability estimate, because India's anti-dumping duty adds about ₹178 per square metre and Indian costs were lower than Chinese landed costs before 2026. Chinese manufacturers have instead set up plants abroad, in Saudi Arabia, Jordan and Kenya, which compete with Indian exports rather than with Indian domestic sales. Section 05 explained why that could change after February 2027.

In plain terms

A handful of brands earn about 40% of the money while owning about one-eighth of the kilns. They do it by buying tiles from smaller factories and selling them under their own names. That works brilliantly when the smaller factories are desperate for orders, and less well when, as in 2026, the smaller factories cannot run.

In plain terms

Owning a tile brand is not enough, and owning kilns is not enough. The model that has worked through every Indian tile cycle is a strong brand that sells tiles from its own plants in places with cheaper, steadier gas, topped up with tiles bought from Morbi when they are cheap. Kajaria is the purest example; Simpolo is the newest.

Rules of the kiln: tax, trade walls, pollution orders and gas allocation

Tiles are lightly regulated as a product but heavily shaped by policy at the edges: GST that pushed buyers to formal invoices, a pollution order that moved Morbi onto gas, and trade remedies at home and abroad.

No regulator sets tile prices and no licence is needed to build a kiln. Policy matters to the industry through five channels: indirect tax, trade remedies, product standards, environmental orders and gas allocation. Each has shifted the balance between Morbi and the brands at some point, and two of them (the China anti-dumping duty and gas allocation) are live in 2026–27.

GST: 18%, unchanged by GST 2.0

Ceramic, vitrified and porcelain tiles (HSN 6907) are taxed at 18%. They were placed at 28% when GST began on 1-Jul-2017 and cut to 18% from 15-Nov-2017. GST 2.0, effective 22-Sep-2025, left tiles at 18% (Notification 9/2025-Central Tax (Rate), Schedule II entry 238); blog claims that tiles moved "from 28% to 18%" in 2025 confuse the 2017 cut with the 2025 reform. What GST 2.0 did change was cement (28% to 18%), which slightly lowers construction cost, and raw marble and granite blocks (12% to 5%), a small relative advantage for natural stone. Sanitaryware stays at 18%. GST and e-invoicing (now mandatory above ₹5 crore of turnover) are the main formalisation forces behind organised share gains: before 2017 small units could under-invoice and undercut brands by the tax they did not pay.

Trade remedies and the BIS order

India's anti-dumping duty on Chinese porcelain and vitrified tiles (US$1.87 per square metre; lapses around 23-Feb-2027) and the duties India's exporters face in the Gulf, the US and Mexico are covered in section 05. The second barrier to low-quality imports is the BIS quality-control order for ceramic tiles, sanitaryware and laboratory ware (2023), which mandates the IS 15622 standard for pressed ceramic tiles; a BIS circular of 2-Apr-2026 set 28-Jun-2026 as the last date for implementing Amendment No. 2. A quality order screens out sub-standard imports and raises compliance costs for the smallest domestic units, but it does not protect against well-made, cheaper imports.

Environment and energy

The 2019 National Green Tribunal order that took Morbi off coal gasifiers is the single most consequential environmental decision for the industry. It cleaned Morbi's air and moved the cluster onto piped gas and propane, which are cleaner but far more expensive and import-dependent; that move is the root of Morbi's 2026 vulnerability. Plants elsewhere still use other fuels where permitted: Kajaria's West plants use coal in spray driers, its Nepal joint venture runs on coal gas, its North plants burn biofuel for about 30% of their needs, and Varmora's new Tezpur plant in Assam uses a coal gasifier. The March 2026 gas allocation order, which capped industrial city-gas users at 80% of their recent average, showed that in a supply emergency tile makers rank below households and transport in the queue for gas.

Labour and safety

Morbi employs about four lakh people directly and supports about nine lakh livelihoods, by Gujarat government figures, most of them migrants from Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Odisha. Own, subsidiary, outsourced: why the leader is building kilns outside Gujarat again Deep dive

Kajaria sells about 40% more tiles than its own and subsidiary plants make. In 2026 renting Morbi's kilns became more expensive and less reliable, so the company is adding 23.5 MSM of its own GVT capacity in Rajasthan and Andhra Pradesh, and the Tirupati belt is turning into a second cluster.

The branded tile model rests on a quiet piece of arbitrage. A brand does not need to make every tile it sells; it needs to control the design, the grading and the box. ICRA estimates that organised players rely on third-party manufacturing for 20–30% of what they sell. Kajaria is a good example: in FY26 it sold 118.52 MSM of tiles, of which 62.85 MSM came from its own plants, 26.26 MSM from majority-owned subsidiaries in Morbi and Telangana, and 29.41 MSM from independent Morbi units it does not own. That outsourced quarter is the industry's shock absorber: in a glut the brand buys cheaply and earns a fat trading margin; in a shortage it can cut purchases and protect its own plants' utilisation.

Three different "volumes": always say which

Before using any Kajaria volume figure, a reader must know which of three it is. Sales volume (118.52 MSM in FY26) is what investors usually quote and includes outsourced tiles. Production volume (84.59 MSM in FY26) counts only own and subsidiary plants. Capacity is 82.70 MSM across eight consolidated plants, excluding the 5.10 MSM Nepal joint venture, which is 50% owned and equity-accounted, so its output appears in neither sales nor production volume (its profit shows as a share of JV result, ₹2.61 crore in Q2 FY27). Including Nepal, capacity is 87.80 MSM, which is the "about 88 million square metres" management cites on calls. When management says it will sell "about 130 million" in FY27 and "the rest, 42 million, outsourced", that 42 MSM bundles subsidiary and third-party volume against the 88 MSM base including Nepal, and is not comparable with the 29.41 MSM "outsource" line in the investor update. Our Kajaria primer's "~88 MSM in nine plants" double-counts Nepal and the Morbi subsidiaries; the correct description is 82.70 MSM consolidated across eight plants plus 5.10 MSM in the Nepal JV.

Four decades of cycles: gas times exports, not gas alone

The industry's history is a sequence of shocks in fuel, freight and trade. The brands do worst when Morbi has cheap fuel and nowhere to send it, and best when Morbi's fuel is dear and its exports are blocked.

Indian tiles began with H&R Johnson, which started making ceramic tiles in 1958 and for decades defined the category. Somany followed in 1968; Kajaria built its first plant at Sikandrabad in Uttar Pradesh in 1988 with Spanish collaboration; Nitco and Orient Bell (formed from Orient Ceramics and Bell Ceramics) grew in the same era. Through the 1990s tiles were a small, mostly ceramic-wall business; floors were mosaic, marble or cement.

The 2000s brought vitrified tiles, which displaced marble and mosaic on urban floors, and Morbi's industrialisation, as Italian and Chinese machinery and cheap coal gas turned wall-tile workshops into a cluster of hundreds of factories. Between 2010 and 2016 digital inkjet printing made marble and wood looks cheap to reproduce, glazed vitrified tiles became the growth product, and Kajaria added Morbi joint ventures and South Indian plants and entered bathware in 2014. Rising Chinese imports led India to impose anti-dumping duty on Chinese vitrified tiles in 2017. GST, first at 28% and then 18%, began to narrow small units' tax advantage, and the 2019 coal-gasifier ban moved Morbi onto gas.

Reading a tile maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen numbers explain almost everything about a tile company's results. Most of them are easy to misread, because companies define volume, realisation, margin and return differently, and some do not disclose them at all.

A tile company's revenue is the number of square metres it sells multiplied by the average price it gets for each. Its operating profit is what is left after paying for fuel, materials, bought-in tiles, people, freight and advertising. Everything else is detail on those two lines. This section takes each driver in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, what moves it, what good looks like, how Kajaria and its peers compare, and where the definitions trip people up. Kajaria is used as the worked example throughout because it discloses more than any other Indian tile maker.

The equation: what management controls and what it does not

The revenue identity is simple: tile revenue equals sales volume (in MSM) times realisation (₹ per square metre). Sales volume itself is the sum of own production, subsidiary production and outsourced purchases. Realisation depends on product mix (ceramic, PVT, GVT, slabs), format, region, channel (retail or project) and, above all, the gap between the brand's price and Morbi's. The profit identity follows: EBITDA equals volume times the difference between realisation and variable cost per square metre, minus fixed costs. Variable cost includes body materials, glaze, packaging, fuel (SCM per square metre times ₹ per SCM), power, freight and, for outsourced tiles, the purchase price; fixed cost covers employees, plant overheads, advertising and head office.

EBITDA was ₹286.50 crore, a 19.51% margin; after depreciation of ₹43.10 crore, finance cost of ₹5.28 crore, other income of ₹15.76 crore, the Nepal JV's ₹2.61 crore, a ₹1.55 crore exceptional charge, tax of ₹64.22 crore and minority interest of ₹1.20 crore, net profit was ₹189.52 crore.

Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE, round numbers). A brand sells 100 MSM at ₹400 per square metre: revenue ₹4,000 crore. Fuel costs ₹85 per square metre and other variable costs (materials, bought-in tiles, freight, packaging) ₹170, so each square metre contributes ₹145, or ₹1,450 crore in total. Fixed costs are ₹700 crore, leaving EBITDA of ₹750 crore, an 18.75% margin. Now flex it. Five per cent more volume adds ₹72.5 crore of EBITDA (+9.7%), because fixed costs do not rise. Five per cent more price, with no change in cost, adds ₹200 crore (+26.7%). A ₹10 per SCM rise in gas at 1.3 SCM per square metre, applied to 75 MSM of own production and not passed on, costs ₹97.5 crore (−13%). Price is the most powerful lever, which is why the brand-versus-Morbi gap matters so much; volume is the safest, which is why Kajaria chose it in 2026.

In plain terms

Square metres sold times the price per square metre gives revenue. Take away fuel, materials and bought-in tiles per square metre, multiply by the square metres again, take away fixed costs, and you have operating profit. A small change in price moves profit far more than a small change in volume, and the gas price is the one big number management cannot control.

What it is. KPI 6 · Capacity utilisation: how hard the kilns are working

What it is. Production as a share of nameplate capacity. Formula. Own plus subsidiary production ÷ (annual capacity × fraction of year). Worked example. Demand, product mix (smaller formats and simpler designs run faster), maintenance shutdowns, fuel availability (Morbi's plants were idle in March–April) and debottlenecking. Good above 90%, average 75–90%, weak below 70%. Peers. Run Kajaria's Q2 FY27 (quarter to Sep-2026) through the framework and the pieces fit. Volume rose 10.2% against a market Kajaria puts at 5–6%, so it gained share. The share of outsourced tiles is steady at about a quarter, so a sudden Morbi price cut would show up first in outsourced realisation. The bathware and adhesive businesses add revenue but, apart from adhesives, little margin yet: the "others" segment earned 8.1% in FY26 against 15.2% for tiles. Definition traps, collected

  • Three volumes. Kajaria's sales (118.52 MSM), production (84.59 MSM) and capacity (82.70 MSM, or 87.80 with Nepal) are different numbers; management's "42 MSM outsourced" mixes subsidiaries and third parties.
  • Realisation is derived. Nobody reports ₹ per square metre; it is tile revenue ÷ volume and blends price, mix and source.
  • Revenue bases. Kajaria's company revenue includes a small plywood line (₹4,832.50 crore FY26; ₹4,683.24 crore FY25); screener restates without it (₹4,830 crore; ₹4,635 crore).
  • Margin bases. Company EBITDA vs screener OP; segment result (after depreciation) vs EBITDA; company decks vs broker notes (Somany 11.6% vs 10.86%).
  • Gas units. ₹ per SCM vs US$ per mmbtu (about 30 SCM per mmbtu); Morbi's ₹90 excludes 6% VAT; propane is heat-equivalent.
  • The price gap. 40% → 20–25% is management's estimate, not measured data, and compares a North/South Kajaria tile with a Morbi tile sold by a Morbi dealer.
  • Net cash. Company (₹635 crore) vs screener (₹422 crore) at Sep-26; NBFC loans and the Nepal standby letter of credit sit outside both.
  • ROCE and ROE. Company ROCE excludes cash and is annualised quarterly; screener includes cash. FY26 company ROE 16.71%, not the Q2 FY27 annualised 23.68%.
  • Market size. CRISIL ₹51,000–53,000 crore (total; ~⅔ domestic) vs management's ₹48,000 crore domestic (FY27E) vs the Varmora prospectus series; never chain them.
  • Exports. CAPEXIL/DGCI&S US$ data vs management's rupee round numbers (Kajaria's "₹16,000 crore" for FY26 is about 12% low).
  • Morbi unit counts. Units (~800 fuel buyers) are not kilns (1,800+) and not "plants" (400–500 per Kajaria); date-stamp every "idle" percentage.
  • Exceptional items. Screener folds exceptionals into other income (Kajaria's Dec-25 quarter shows −₹28 crore other income, which includes the ₹19.81 crore Kerovit fraud loss).
In plain terms

Before comparing any two tile numbers, ask three questions: is this sales or production, company or screener, and which quarter? Half the apparent disagreements between sources in this industry disappear once those three are answered.

How to read a tile company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Tile volume and its growth (minute 1). Find sales volume in MSM and the YoY change. Compare with the market (5–6% in a normal year) and with Kajaria, the bellwether. Red flag: volume below 3% while peers grow.
  2. Volume by source (minute 2). Own, subsidiary/JV and outsourced. Rising outsourced share in a Morbi shock is a risk; rising own share is a sign of control.
  3. Realisation (minute 3). Divide tile revenue by volume. Ask whether a rise came from price, mix or source; check management's comments on price increases and discounts.
  4. Gas price by region (minute 4). ₹ per SCM for each plant region and the guidance for next quarter; compare with Morbi's piped gas and propane. Red flag: "spot" exposure rising.
  5. EBITDA margin and EBITDA per sq m (minute 5). Did margin rise because price beat cost, or because volume spread fixed costs? Check material cost as a % of sales.
  6. Utilisation and capex (minute 6). Production ÷ capacity; any new lines commissioning; capex per MSM. Red flag: new capacity at low utilisation (sanitaryware at 42–54% is Kajaria's example).
  7. Fixed costs (minute 7). Employee cost and other expenses in rupees; A&P commentary. A rising ad budget is fine if volume follows.
  8. Working capital and cash (minute 8). Working-capital days, debtors and inventory; net cash or debt; any new loans or treasury placements.
  9. Below the line (minute 9). Exceptional items, JV share, minority interest, other income. Red flags: recurring "one-offs", subsidiary losses, related-party flows.
  10. Guidance and the Morbi read (minute 10). Volume and margin guidance, the price gap estimate, the share of Morbi running, exports, and any comment on the China anti-dumping duty. Write down what would make you change your mind next quarter.
In plain terms

In ten minutes you want four answers: did it sell more square metres than the market, at a better price, at a fuel cost below Morbi's, and without tying up more cash? If all four are yes, the quarter was good whatever the headline profit says.

The global lens: higher margins than the world leader, a tenth of its size

Kajaria earns roughly three times the operating margin of Mohawk's global ceramic business and matches RAK Ceramics, with far less capital. Globally, China is shrinking, India is growing fastest, and Spain's 2022 gas shock was the template for Morbi's 2026.

The world's largest flooring company, Mohawk Industries of the US, runs a Global Ceramic segment (Dal-Tile, Marazzi, Eliane, Vitromex) with sales of US$3,435.7 million in the first nine months of 2026, up 6.7%, or about US$4.5 billion annualised. Its Q3 2026 sales were US$1,128.6 million (+2.2%) and its operating margin was 5.8% (6.5% adjusted), down from 6.5% a year earlier. Mohawk says it has offset "underlying cost inflation… fully… through pricing, mix and productivity". Kajaria's FY27 revenue will be roughly US$0.6 billion at ₹94–96 to the dollar, about 13% of Mohawk Ceramic's, but its EBIT margin of about 16% and EBITDA margin of 19.5% are far higher than Mohawk's single-digit operating margin (INDICATIVE comparison: different accounting, scale and markets).

RAK Ceramics of the UAE is the closest global analogue. It earned revenue of AED 3.28 billion in 2025 (+1.6%), EBITDA of AED 623.6 million (19.0%) and net profit of AED 248.5 million, with a 40% gross margin. In Q2 2026 its revenue was AED 822.8 million (−0.5%), EBITDA margin 19.1% and net profit AED 68.3 million (+2.9%). Its geographic split shows the mirror image of India's export collapse: UAE sales rose 22.7% to AED 298.2 million and Saudi Arabia 11.6%, behind the GCC anti-dumping wall that keeps most Indian tiles out, while its India business fell 15.8% in its reporting currency, which RAK attributed to "temporary production interruptions caused by gas shortages in Morbi". Three global lessons apply to India. First, gas shocks restructure tile industries. What the market pays for: a brand valued at eight to thirteen times the cost of its kilns

Kajaria's share price fell while its earnings rose about a quarter, so its multiple has compressed from about 38 times to 27.5 times. Investors are paying for the brand, the distribution and the through-cycle returns, not for the kilns.

At the 30-Oct-2026 close Kajaria's market value was ₹17,430 crore at ₹1,109 a share: 27.5 times trailing earnings, 5.8 times book value, about 19 times FY26 EBITDA and about 16.6 times trailing EBITDA on company net cash, with a dividend yield of 1.26%. In July 2026, when our primer was last refreshed, the stock traded at about 38 times earnings. The de-rating happened because the price fell (from about ₹1,240 to ₹1,109) while trailing profit rose from ₹487 crore (FY26) to ₹604 crore (four quarters to September 2026). The stock is down 8% over a year and has compounded at −2% a year over five years, even though profit grew at about 11% a year over the same period; its 52-week range is ₹870 to ₹1,276. The market is not paying more for Kajaria's 2026 windfall; if anything it is discounting it.

One way to see what investors pay for is to compare enterprise value with the replacement cost of capacity. Kajaria's enterprise value (market value minus ₹635 crore of net cash) is about ₹16,800 crore, or about ₹203 crore per MSM of consolidated capacity. Its new GVT lines cost ₹16–27 crore per MSM. The market therefore values Kajaria at roughly eight to thirteen times what it would cost to rebuild its kilns (INDICATIVE). The difference is the value of the brand, the 1,800-dealer network, the outsourcing relationships that let it sell 40% more than it makes, and its record of earning 17%-plus on capital through every cycle. Somany's enterprise value is about ₹43 crore per MSM of its own and JV plant capacity, Varmora's about ₹62 crore (on gross debt), Orient Bell's about ₹11 crore; the market prices them much closer to replacement cost.

Technology and disruption: slabs, kilns, vinyl and the design copier

The threats to Indian tiles are mostly within the industry (slab overcapacity, design copying, gas dependence) rather than from substitutes. The opportunities are in adhesives, large formats and energy efficiency.

Tiles are an old product made with modern machinery, and most of the technology risk is about who adopts that machinery fastest. Five shifts matter for Indian makers over the next five years; none of them threatens the product itself in the near term, but each changes who earns the margin.

Large-format slabs and thin panels. Continuous presses (SACMI Continua+, System LAMGEA) make porcelain slabs up to 1,600×3,200 mm and panels under 6 mm thick that compete with granite and quartz countertops and with natural marble on walls. Morbi has invested heavily: Varmora counts five to six continuous-press lines in the cluster. Kajaria has two capable lines and makes slabs "as and when we need to", calling the segment growing "but not at a very high pace". The risk is that Morbi's slab capacity outruns demand and triggers a price war at the premium end, where the brands earn most.

Energy in the kiln. Spain and Italy are trialling hydrogen blends and electric kilns under European carbon pricing; India is moving more slowly. Indian makers are shifting spray driers to biomass (about 30% of fuel at Kajaria's North plants) and shortening firing cycles, which Kajaria expects to cut its gas cost "significantly" from January 2027. Captive renewables are spreading: Kajaria owns 19.77% of a solar park company, and Prism Johnson is investing in a captive wind project. The EU's carbon border adjustment does not currently cover ceramics, but any extension would matter for exports. The 2026 shock has shown that energy security, not just energy price, is a competitive variable: India imports 50–60% of its LNG through Hormuz, Morbi has one piped-gas supplier, and its propane comes through Kandla.

Substitutes. Vinyl planks (SPC and LVT), laminates and engineered wood are growing quickly in offices and in Western homes, but remain niche in Indian homes, where tiles suit the climate, the cleaning habits and the price points (the plywood and laminates report covers the wood-panel side). Natural stone gained a small advantage when GST on marble and granite blocks fell to 5% in September 2025. Polished concrete and micro-toppings are design niches. The near-term substitution risk for Indian residential floors is low.

Design is copied in weeks. Digital inkjet printing means any Morbi unit can reproduce a Kajaria design quickly and cheaply. The moat therefore lies less in design than in consistency (shade and size matching across batches), distribution, after-sales trust and the ability to supply large projects reliably. That is why the brands invest in exclusive showrooms, large-format experience centres (H&R Johnson has 22) and project desks rather than in design alone.

Channel and system selling. Online and direct-to-consumer tile sales remain tiny because tiles are heavy, breakable and must be seen. The real channel shifts are to exclusive branded showrooms in smaller towns and to direct project sales to large developers. And the tile sale is becoming a system sale: adhesive, grout, spacers and sometimes bathware sold with the tile, which favours brands with a full range (Kajaria, Somany, H&R Johnson) over single-product Morbi units. Pidilite, Ardex Endura and MYK Laticrete compete for the adhesive part.

Bull vs bear: a permanent narrowing, or a one-year windfall?

The bull case says 2026 accelerated a shift that was already under way. The bear case says the shift lasts only as long as Gujarat's gas stays expensive and India's China duty stays in place.

The argument about Indian tiles in late 2026 comes down to one question: is the brand-versus-Morbi gap that narrowed to 20–25% going to stay narrow? The bull says yes, because buyers who switched will stay, because Morbi's least efficient capacity has closed for good, and because the brands are now building their own cheaper capacity outside Gujarat. The bear says no, because Morbi's gas will fall eventually, its idle plants will restart, the export market will stay shut, and the surplus will come home exactly as it did in FY25, possibly with Chinese tiles alongside it after February 2027.

The bull case

  • Structural share gain: Kajaria's volume growth began in Q4 FY26 (+11%), before the war, after its reorganisation; Q2 FY27 +10% vs a market of 5–6%.
  • Morbi's tail is shrinking: 10–11 PVT and ceramic plants shut in H1 FY27 against 3–4 new GVT lines; Somany expects 15–17% of Morbi capacity never to restart.
  • Cheaper own capacity: 23.5 MSM of new GVT at ₹16–27 Cr per MSM on contracted gas, cutting reliance on Morbi outsourcing; Tirupati becomes a second, non-Gujarat cluster.
  • Demand support: a decade-high 5.40 lakh home completions scheduled for 2026 and premium housing growing; GVT heading for 45% of industry value.
  • Formalisation and systems: GST, e-invoicing and adhesive pull-through favour brands; Kajaria adhesives to ~₹400 Cr by FY28.
  • Balance sheet and returns: net cash ₹635 Cr, ROCE ≥17% every year since FY15, promoters on nil salary.

The bear case

  • Gas mean-reverts: if Gulf energy prices ease, Morbi's gas falls, it cuts prices and the gap re-widens; Kajaria has said its prices would follow.
  • Exports stay shut: GCC, US and Mexico duties outlast the war, so Morbi's restarted capacity floods India as in FY25.
  • China duty lapses: no sunset review found for the US$1.87/m² duty expiring ~23-Feb-2027; Chinese GVT could land below Morbi's post-war cost.
  • The group barely grew: branded Q1 FY27 volume rose only ~1.5%, well below the market; gains came from H&R Johnson and Varmora, not from Morbi's unbranded tail alone.
  • No price tailwind in FY28: management guides value growth ≈ volume growth; FY27's ₹1,000 Cr-plus EBITDA guidance implies a softer H2.
  • Company-specific flags: bathware at 42–54% utilisation and a ₹19.8 Cr fraud loss; ₹155 Cr NBFC loans; ₹130 Cr Nepal standby LC.

Both cases have evidence. The bull's best point is timing: Kajaria's volume growth predates the war, which suggests the reorganisation, not just the gas shock, is doing some of the work; its own annual report names the opposite view as a risk and rejects it. The bear's best point is history: in FY25, with exports falling and gas cheap, Kajaria's margin fell to about 13–14% and its profit by 31%, and nothing in the industry's structure has changed enough to make that impossible again. The most useful way to hold both is to watch the variables in section 21: Morbi's gas price relative to the brands', Morbi's running rate, the export run-rate, and DGTR's decision on the China duty. If Morbi's gas falls below about ₹60 per SCM while exports remain near ₹1,000 crore a month, the bear case gains weight; if Morbi's gas stays above ₹80 and the China duty is reviewed, the bull case does.

In plain terms

The optimist thinks 2026 permanently moved Indian homeowners toward branded tiles and moved branded factories out of Gujarat. The pessimist thinks it was a one-off that ends when Gulf gas gets cheaper. Both agree on what to watch: the gas price in Morbi, how many Morbi kilns are running, and whether India keeps its duty on Chinese tiles.
