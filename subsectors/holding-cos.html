00

The one-page view: two wrappers, three jewels and a widening discount

Each of India's two listed financial holdcos owns one very valuable thing. The market prices each wrapper far below what it holds, and in 2026 the gap got wider.

A financial holding company makes nothing. Cholamandalam Financial Holdings (CFHL) owns 43.74% of Cholamandalam Investment and Finance (CIFC), one of India's best-run vehicle and small-business lenders, plus 60% of Chola MS General Insurance and 49.5% of a small risk-services firm. Religare Enterprises (REL) owns 63.20% of Care Health Insurance, the country's second-largest standalone health insurer, plus a cash-rich lending shell, a small stockbroker and a small housing-finance company. Both are registered with the Reserve Bank of India as Core Investment Companies (CICs), which means at least 90% of what they own must sit in group companies. Each holdco is, in effect, a listed ticket to one jewel.

On 22 October 2026 the two tickets were worth ₹31,892 crore together: ₹24,714 crore for CFHL at ₹1,316 a share and ₹7,178 crore for Religare at ₹208. What they own, valued on market prices where they exist and on stated assumptions where they do not, is worth about ₹72,700 crore (indicative). That is a combined discount of roughly 56%: a buyer of the two wrappers pays about 44 paise for each rupee of underlying value. CFHL's discount alone is about 60% to its sum-of-the-parts and about 57% to its CIFC stake by itself, ignoring everything else it owns. Religare's is about 37% in our base case, but anywhere from 5% to 50% depending on what Care Health is worth, because Care has no market price.

Five forces shape everything in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them.

  • The holding-company discount is structural, and in 2026 it widened. CFHL's discount to its CIFC stake moved from about 53–54% at the end of June to about 57% on 22 October (indicative). CFHL fell about 20% over that period while CIFC fell about 15%: the wrapper behaves like a geared bet on the jewel.
  • Reported earnings are not the holdco's earnings. CFHL's consolidated FY26 profit of ₹5,485 crore includes the share belonging to CIFC's and Chola MS's other owners; only about ₹2,441 crore is attributable to CFHL shareholders. Religare's reported profit understates its economics because it still consolidates Care on a statutory accounting basis that defers multi-year premium.
  • Regulators hold the unlock keys. The RBI caps CIC dividends at 60% of standalone profit, limits groups to two layers of CICs and must approve any scheme. On 6–7 August 2026 it declined Religare's demerger without giving reasons. IRDAI's 30 July 2026 rule on merging a holdco into its insurer only works once that holdco has no other business.
  • The jewels' cycles are out of phase. CIFC is growing assets 23% with a 21% return on equity. Chola MS is in a soft general-insurance market with combined ratios of 115–120%. Care Health is growing more than 40% after GST was removed from retail health premiums, on a thin 1.58× solvency ratio.
  • Capital flows down the chain, not up. CIFC has called a board meeting on 28 October to consider a QIP, Care needs rights issues and sub-debt, and Religare is waiting for ₹881 crore of warrant money from holders whose ₹235 strike is above the ₹208 share price. Meanwhile the two holdcos paid ₹24 crore of dividends in FY26 on ₹2,527 crore of attributable profit.

01

What a holding company makes: a liquid claim on a locked block

The product is a listed share that stands for an illiquid or controlled stake. The price of that product is the discount.

A financial holdco earns in two ways that look nothing alike. The first is cash: dividends and fees that its subsidiaries actually pay it, which show up in its standalone accounts. The second is accounting: its share of the subsidiaries' profits, which shows up in its consolidated accounts whether or not a rupee moves. For CFHL in FY26 the first number was ₹89.79 crore of standalone revenue. The second was ₹2,441 crore of attributable consolidated profit. The holdco's shareholders own a claim on the second number but are paid out of the first, and the distance between them is the starting point for understanding why the discount exists.

Between the jewel's profit and the holdco shareholder's bank account, value leaks out at five points. Each one is a reason for the discount, and each is measurable:

  • The other owners. 56.26% of CIFC's profit, 40% of Chola MS's and 36.8% of Care's belong to someone else. Consolidated profit shows all of it; the holdco owns only its slice.
  • Retention at the jewel. CIFC paid ₹2.00 a share on FY26 earnings of ₹61.41, a payout of about 3%. Chola MS has paid nothing for five years and has not raised capital for 13. Care pays nothing and needs money.
  • Retention at the holdco. CFHL passed on ₹24.41 crore of the ₹74.88 crore of dividends it received, 33%. Even if it wanted to pay everything, the RBI caps a CIC's dividend at 60% of its standalone profit. Religare passes on nothing because it is not allowed to.
  • Tax. Dividends are taxed in shareholders' hands; any stake sale would attract 12.5% long-term capital-gains tax; and since October 2024 buybacks are taxed as dividends, which removes the cleanest discount-closing tool.
  • Head-office cost. Trivial at CFHL (₹3.89 crore a year). Material at Religare (about ₹50 crore of expenses and a ₹34 crore standalone loss in FY26), which capitalised at eight times is worth about ₹270 crore of NAV (indicative).

In plain terms

CFHL "earns" ₹2,441 crore a year on paper, but its own bank account receives about ₹90 crore and pays out about ₹24 crore. A shareholder who buys CFHL for income is buying a 0.1% yield. A shareholder who buys it for value is betting that the paper earnings will one day turn into something they can collect, through a higher share price, a merger or a distribution. Until that happens, the market charges a large discount for the wait.

The holdco's "price" is therefore the discount, and that price moves. It narrows when investors expect an unlock (a listing, a merger, a large special dividend) and when markets are buoyant, and widens in drawdowns, when the jewel needs capital, or when a regulator closes a door. In 2026 all three widening forces arrived together for these two companies: a repo-rate hike on 7 October that hit lender valuations, a CIFC capital raise on the horizon, and the RBI's refusal of Religare's scheme in August.

02

Size and growth: a ₹47,618 crore revenue line that is really a lender and two insurers

Consolidated, the two holdcos look like large, fast-growing financial companies. That is because they consolidate their jewels in full.

On a consolidated basis CFHL and Religare reported combined FY26 revenue of ₹47,618 crore, up 17.3% on FY25, and combined profit including minorities of ₹5,558 crore, up 12.9%. Those numbers are real but misleading as a measure of "the sub-sector". Almost all of CFHL's revenue is CIFC's interest income and Chola MS's premium income, consolidated line by line because CFHL treats both as subsidiaries under Ind AS (it consolidates CIFC on de facto control even though the Murugappa group's combined stake in CIFC has been below 50% since December 2024). Between 85% and 94% of Religare's revenue is Care Health's net premium. Strip out the jewels and the holdco layer is tiny: combined standalone income of about ₹106 crore and a combined standalone profit of about ₹36 crore in FY26.

Quarterly figures sharpen the picture. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) CFHL's consolidated revenue rose 19.6% to ₹11,114 crore and group profit 42% to ₹1,789 crore; attributable profit was about ₹806 crore against ₹579 crore a year earlier, up 39%. In plain terms

The sub-sector is essentially CFHL: it accounts for 82% of combined revenue, 97% of attributable profit, 77.5% of market value and 84% of base-case NAV. Religare matters for a different reason. It is the swing factor in valuation, because the price of Care Health inside it is the only market signal of what a fast-growing private health insurer is worth before listing.

03

The businesses underneath: trucks, motor policies and retail health

A holdco's demand drivers are its jewels' demand drivers. Here that means the vehicle cycle, a soft general-insurance market and a health-insurance boom triggered by a tax change.

Three industries sit under these two wrappers. The largest by value is retail non-bank credit, through CIFC. India's NBFC retail loan book is expected by ICRA to cross ₹30 lakh crore by FY27, growing 16–18% a year; at ₹2.54 lakh crore CIFC holds roughly 8–9% of it (indicative). The second is general insurance, through Chola MS: an industry that wrote ₹3,36,123 crore of gross direct premium (GDPI) in FY26, up 9.25%. The third is standalone health insurance, through Care: a ₹45,866 crore segment in FY26 that grew 30.7% in the first half of FY27.

The vehicle cycle drives CIFC's largest book and Chola MS's largest line. In Q1 FY27 industry volumes were strong: heavy commercial vehicles up 13% (their best-ever first quarter), light CVs up 18%, small CVs up 31%, cars and utility vehicles up 26% (a record first quarter), two-wheelers up 20% and tractors up 19%. GST 2.0, which from 22 September 2025 cut the rate on small cars and two-wheelers from 28% to 18%, is a large part of that. CIFC's vehicle-finance disbursements rose 21% to ₹16,503 crore and the segment's pre-tax profit rose 50% to ₹945 crore. Chola MS should have been a beneficiary too, through motor insurance on new vehicles; as Part 11 shows, it largely was not.

Health insurance after GST 2.0 is the fastest-moving demand driver in the sub-sector. Claims inflation is the counter-force. Medical inflation runs at 12–14% a year, and claims frequency rises as more people are covered. Chola MS's loss ratio on health, travel and personal accident rose from 73.0% in FY25 to 88.0% in FY26 and 90.4% in Q1 FY27; it responded by cutting its health premium 27% in H1 FY27 to re-price group accounts. Care's Ind AS claims ratio was 68.2% in Q1 FY27. On motor, third-party premiums have not been raised for three to four years while court awards keep rising, and Chola MS has not yet provided for a pending Supreme Court ruling on compensation for homemakers in third-party claims.

Rates are the last driver and the newest. The RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, reversing part of the 2025 easing cycle. CIFC's CFO had budgeted for 25–50 basis points of hikes and expected cost of funds to rise about 10 basis points in the second half, with bank loans making up about half of borrowings. For the insurers, higher yields lift income on new investments but cause mark-to-market losses on existing bonds. For the holdcos themselves, higher discount rates usually widen holdco discounts, because a distant and uncertain unlock is worth less when money is dearer.

In plain terms

Under one roof, CFHL owns a lender enjoying a vehicle boom and an insurer struggling in a price war. Under the other, Religare owns a health insurer riding a tax cut. The holdco price is a weighted bet on these three cycles, minus a discount, and the three cycles are not moving together.

04

Where ₹100 goes: three money machines and one rationed input

A lender keeps about ₹17 of each ₹100 of revenue, a soft-market general insurer under ₹4 of each ₹100 of premium. At the holdco layer, the scarce input is capital.

The supply side of a holding company is simple: its only input is capital already sunk into the stakes. CFHL's three stakes cost ₹1,276 crore and are worth about ₹61,300 crore at today's prices (indicative); Religare's investments cost ₹2,485 crore. What matters is the cost structure of the jewels, because that decides how much profit there is to look through to, and the capital each jewel needs to keep growing, because that decides whether money flows up or down.

A vehicle and small-business lender such as CIFC borrows wholesale from banks, bond markets, commercial paper, external commercial borrowings and securitisation at about 8–8.5%, and lends at 14–20%. Of each ₹100 of FY26 revenue, about ₹46 went to its lenders as interest, ₹19 to staff, branches, technology and collections, ₹12–13 to loan losses and provisions, ₹1 to depreciation and ₹6 to tax, leaving about ₹17 as profit (indicative composite from CIFC's FY26 consolidated accounts: revenue ₹31,120 crore, interest ₹14,384 crore, PAT ₹5,233 crore). The two variables that management controls most directly are operating cost and credit cost. Interest cost is largely set by the market and the RBI.

Which inputs drive the cost of that capital? Who owns the jewels: families, a Japanese partner and a private-equity continuation fund

The listed holdcos are only one of several owners in each operating company. The others decide much of what the holdco can do.

India has a handful of listed financial holding companies, but only two in the NSE 750 universe are classified as such. Others sit elsewhere in our library: Bajaj Holdings & Investment and Tata Investment Corporation are covered in the investment companies report, and Bajaj Finserv, Aditya Birla Capital and Max Financial are operating-company holdcos rather than CICs in the narrow sense. The useful comparison is the discount, because all of them face the same question: why pay full price for a wrapper when the jewel inside can be valued, and sometimes bought, separately?

On indicative pre-tax bases, CFHL's ~60% discount to its sum of the parts sits beside Bajaj Nivesh's ~61% at the wide end of Indian holdcos; Bajaj Holdings is at about 46%, Religare at about 37% in our base case and TSF Investments at about 32% (the last three from this report and our investment companies report, each NAV built differently). Part 19 sets these against holdcos abroad.

Indian financial holdcos whose jewel is listed (CFHL with CIFC, BHIL with Bajaj Finserv and Bajaj Auto) carry the widest discounts, because the alternative of buying the jewel directly is one click away and far more liquid. CFHL has about 43,800 shareholders; CIFC has about 1.7 lakh. Where the jewel is unlisted, as with Religare and Care Health, the holdco is the only listed door to the asset, which supports a narrower discount and turns the holdco into the jewel's de facto public listing.

Regulators hold the keys: RBI, IRDAI and the tax code

Every route by which a holdco's discount could close, whether a payout, a merger, a demerger or a listing, needs at least one regulator's consent. Several are capped by rule.

Both members are registered with the RBI as Core Investment Companies. Their subsidiaries include an Upper Layer NBFC (CIFC), a middle-layer investment-and-credit NBFC (Religare Finvest), a housing finance company (Religare Housing), a stockbroker and two insurers regulated by IRDAI. That gives three regulators a say over almost any change, and in 2026 two of them acted in ways that shaped this sub-sector.

RBI: the Core Investment Company rulebook

The RBI's Master Direction on Core Investment Companies (2016, updated 1 August 2025) sets the frame. A CIC is a non-bank whose assets are at least ₹100 crore (alone or with group CICs) and which raises or holds public funds. At least 90% of its net assets must be in group companies, with at least 60% in their equity; it may not trade those investments except by block sale. Its adjusted net worth (ANW), which is owned funds plus 50% of the unrealised appreciation on quoted investments, must be at least 30% of risk-weighted assets, and outside liabilities may not exceed 2.5 times ANW. Since August 2020 a group may have no more than two layers of CICs (paragraph 7). Any change of control, any shareholding change of 26% or more and any change of more than 30% of the board needs prior RBI approval (paragraph 27).

Two rules bear directly on the discount. Paragraph 21A caps a CIC's dividend at 60% of its net profit (10% if it is not fully compliant). For CFHL, whose standalone profit was ₹70.41 crore in FY26, that means a maximum payout of about ₹42 crore, or ₹2.25 a share: a 0.17% yield at ₹1,316, against ₹1.30 actually paid. And the two-layer rule means the Murugappa structure, with Ambadi Investments above CFHL, is already at the limit: no further CIC can be inserted, and any simplification has to remove a layer rather than add one.

Religare faces an extra, company-specific restriction. In a letter of 5 April 2019, reiterated on 19 December 2019, the RBI advised Religare to stop paying dividends "till further orders", in the context of the 2016–18 diversion of funds through Religare Finvest's corporate loan book under the previous promoters. The FY26 annual report says that instruction is still in force. Religare Finvest's own Corrective Action Plan, imposed in January 2018, was lifted only on 23 July 2025.

In plain terms

A CIC cannot pay out more than 60% of its own small profit, and Religare cannot pay anything at all. So even a holdco board that wanted to close the discount by handing cash to shareholders could not do it at scale. The rulebook was written to stop CICs becoming leveraged shadow banks, and it does that well; a side effect is that it also stops them being efficient conduits of cash.

RBI: the jewel and the schemes

CIFC is on the RBI's Upper Layer list under scale-based regulation, which brings bank-like rules: a minimum common equity tier-1 ratio of 9%, large-exposure limits and a mandatory listing (already met). Its asset-quality reporting is shaped by the RBI's November 2021 clarification that a loan cannot be upgraded from non-performing until all arrears are cleared, which is why CIFC shows a higher RBI-basis gross NPA (4.50%) than its Ind AS Stage-3 ratio (3.29%). The RBI's November 2023 increase in risk weights on bank lending to NBFCs and on unsecured consumer credit, partly reversed from April 2025, touched CIFC's ₹15,884 crore consumer and small-enterprise book.

The decisive RBI power in 2026 was its approval of schemes of arrangement involving NBFCs and CICs. Religare's board approved a demerger on 14 February 2026; the stock exchanges issued their observation letters on 7 July; and in letters of 6 August (to Religare) and 7 August (to Religare Finvest) the RBI said the request "could not be acceded to", giving no reasons. Religare's shares fell 8.2% to ₹235.40 on 7 August. Part 10 examines what this blocks.

IRDAI: owning an insurer, accounting for it, and merging into it

IRDAI's rules govern who may own an insurer and how its profit is counted. Anyone holding 10% or more, or exercising control, is a "promoter" and must be approved; transfers above set thresholds need approval too. IRDAI's amendment regulations of 30 July 2026 (the Registration, Capital Structure, Transfer of Shares and Amalgamation of Insurers Amendment) reset those thresholds to 5, 10, 25, 50 and 75%, and treat passive dilution in a rights issue as a transfer. More importantly for this report, they allow a non-insurance company to amalgamate into an insurer only if it holds more than 50% of that insurer and has no other non-insurance business on the date of application, with consideration paid only in equity and a fee of ₹10 lakh per entity. Religare meets the first test (63.2% of Care) and fails the second (it still owns a lender, a broker and a housing financier).

On accounting, three IRDAI decisions overlap. From 1 October 2024 multi-year premiums must be recognised 1/n per year, depressing reported premium and profit for fast-growing health insurers through FY25–FY27. From 1 April 2026 insurers moved to Ind AS 117, but both Chola MS and Care took a one-year forbearance and will report under Ind AS only from 1 April 2027. And IRDAI's 2023 expenses-of-management regulations replaced line-by-line commission caps with an overall cap (30% of gross premium for general insurers, 35% for standalone health insurers), which is why Chola MS's reported commissions jump between FY23 and FY24 in its five-year table.

On 23 September 2026 IRDAI published a consultation paper titled "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution", with comments due by 25 October 2026. As reported, it proposes to phase general insurers' EoM limit down to 20% of GDPI over five years, cut health new-business commissions from about 40% to 15–20% and motor own-damage commissions from 16% to about 5%, and ban "dark patterns" in online selling. That would help Chola MS's and Care's expense ratios, and cut fee income at distributors, including CIFC, which received ₹256.6 crore of commission from Chola MS in FY26.

SEBI and tax

SEBI's takeover code made the Burman family make an open offer at ₹235 a share in September 2023 when they crossed 25% of Religare; it was resisted by the board of the time and completed on 13 February 2025, with change of control recorded on 18 February 2025 at 25.16%. Schemes such as Religare's demerger need stock-exchange observation letters before court and regulatory approval. On tax: demergers that meet section 2(19AA) of the Income Tax Act are tax-neutral, and Religare's scheme was drafted to comply; dividends are taxed in shareholders' hands, with section 80M letting a holdco deduct dividends it passes on so that tax does not cascade; long-term capital gains on any stake sale are taxed at 12.5% (since the July 2024 Budget); and since 1 October 2024 buybacks are taxed as deemed dividends in shareholders' hands, which removes the buyback as a cheap way to close a discount.

"₹1 of Chola for 43 paise": anatomy of the CFHL discount Deep dive

CFHL is worth less than half of a single listed shareholding it owns. Measured three ways, the discount is 54–60%, it widened through 2026, and nothing in the structure forces it to close.

On 22 October 2026 CFHL held 37,28,85,889 shares of CIFC. At CIFC's closing price of ₹1,531, that block was worth ₹57,089 crore. CFHL's own market value was ₹24,714 crore. A buyer of CFHL was therefore paying 43 paise for each rupee of CIFC it owned, and getting the 60% stake in Chola MS General Insurance, the 49.5% of Chola MS Risk Services and ₹110 crore of bank deposits thrown in for nothing. This section takes that gap apart: how to measure it, how it has moved, what causes it and what could close it.

Measuring it three ways

The discount depends on the denominator, so it should always be quoted with its basis and its date. The narrowest measure compares CFHL's market value with its listed CIFC stake alone, which needs no assumptions: 1 − 24,714 ÷ 57,089 = 56.7%. The standard measure compares it with a full sum of the parts, which needs an assumption for Chola MS: with Chola MS at 2.0× its June 2026 net worth of ₹3,415 crore, CFHL's NAV is ₹61,348 crore, ₹3,267 a share, and the discount is 59.7% (59.0–60.4% for Chola MS at 1.5–2.5× book). The tax-adjusted measure deducts the 12.5% long-term capital-gains tax that would fall due if the stakes were ever sold above their ₹1,276 crore cost; post-tax NAV is about ₹53,840 crore and the discount 54.1%. All three are indicative and all three are wide.

In plain terms

However you measure it, the market values CFHL at a little over 40% of what it owns. Most of the measurement debate is about the last few points. The big number, the CIFC stake, is not an estimate: it is a share count multiplied by a price on the screen.

How it moved in 2026

At the end of June 2026 CFHL traded at about ₹1,653 and CIFC at ₹1,791.70. On the corrected share count that put CFHL's market value at about ₹31,040 crore against a CIFC stake of about ₹66,810 crore, a stake-only discount of about 53.5% (indicative). By 22 October CFHL had fallen 20.4% to ₹1,316 while CIFC had fallen 14.6% to ₹1,531, and the discount had widened to 56.7%. Over the four months CFHL moved about 1.4 times as much as CIFC. Over a full year CFHL fell 29%; it was trading just above its 52-week low of ₹1,280 against a high of ₹2,064, while CIFC (high ₹1,952, low ₹1,299) was about 22% off its peak.

Why does the wrapper amplify the jewel? Arithmetically, if the discount were constant, CFHL would move about 93% as much as CIFC, because CIFC is 93% of its NAV. It moved 140% as much because the discount widened at the same time. That is the normal pattern for holdcos: discounts widen in falling markets, when liquidity is scarce and the unlock looks further away, and narrow in rising ones. The 7 October repo hike, an expected QIP at CIFC and broad selling of financials all pushed in the same direction this autumn.

There is a small regulatory footnote to the same story. RBI's CIC rules measure adjusted net worth by adding 50% of unrealised gains on quoted stakes, as at the last audited balance sheet. CFHL's audited capital ratio jumped from 1,999% in March 2025 to 2,466% in March 2026, which back-solves to a CIFC price of roughly ₹1,607 at the March 2026 audit (indicative). At ₹1,531, CIFC is about 5% below that mark, so the next audited ANW will fall unless CIFC recovers. It does not affect any constraint (the ratio is far above 30%), but it is a reminder that even the regulator's view of CFHL's net worth is a mark-to-market on CIFC.

What causes it

In rough order of weight for CFHL, six forces keep the discount wide:

  • The jewel is listed and liquid. Anyone who wants CIFC can buy it directly, in a stock with four times as many shareholders and much deeper trading. The wrapper has to offer a reason to hold it, and the reason is price.
  • Almost no cash comes through. CFHL paid ₹24 crore of dividends on ₹2,441 crore of attributable earnings in FY26, a 1% payout and a 0.10% yield. CIFC retains 97% of its own profit, Chola MS pays nothing, and the CIC rules cap CFHL's own payout at 60% of a small standalone profit.
  • Tax leakage. Any exit from the stakes would attract 12.5% capital-gains tax, and since October 2024 a buyback is taxed as a dividend. The discount cannot be arbitraged cheaply.
  • No control premium. The Murugappa family owns 46.4% of CFHL and is not a seller. No bidder will pay up for a block it cannot buy, so the "takeover value" that supports some discounted stocks does not exist here.
  • The rules lock the structure. The CIC framework requires 90% of assets in group companies and allows only two CIC layers; CFHL cannot diversify, trade its stakes or add a layer.
  • Dilution without participation. CIFC raises equity (₹1,370 crore of CCD conversions in FY26, ₹200 crore in July and ₹430 crore in October 2026, and now a QIP) and CFHL, with ₹110 crore of cash, does not take part. Its stake fell from 44.34% in March 2025 to 43.74% in June 2026 with the share count unchanged.

What would close it, and why none is likely

Five levers could narrow the discount. Each is either small, slow, tax-inefficient or not in the company's hands.

  • Pay out more. At the 60% CIC cap CFHL could pay ₹2.25 a share instead of ₹1.30. That is a 0.17% yield: welcome but irrelevant to a 57% discount. A real change would need CIFC to pay out more, which a lender growing 23% a year and raising equity will not do.
  • Buy back shares. CFHL has about ₹110 crore of cash, enough for 0.4% of its equity, and the buyback would be taxed as a dividend in shareholders' hands. It could in theory borrow against its CIC leverage headroom, but borrowing to buy back a holdco's own shares is precisely what the CIC rules were written to discourage.
  • Merge CFHL into CIFC. A reverse merger would hand CFHL's shareholders CIFC shares and remove the wrapper. It would need RBI and IRDAI approval (CFHL also owns 60% of an insurer, which a lender cannot easily hold), would cost the family a layer of control and is not on the table.
  • Crystallise Chola MS. With 100% foreign ownership now permitted, MSI could buy CFHL's 60%. At the lowest listed-peer price-to-book of 3.5×, CFHL's stake would be worth ₹7,170 crore against our ₹4,098 crore base: about ₹3,070 crore more NAV, roughly 5%, and real cash at the holdco. Neither party has said it wants this.
  • Wait for a bull market. Historically the most reliable narrower. Discounts on Indian holdcos narrowed in 2021–24 and widened in 2018–20 and 2026.

Whose profit is it? Reported, attributable and look-through earnings

CFHL's headline profit is 2.2 times what its shareholders own. Religare's is a quarter of its economic earnings. Getting the profit number right is the first step in valuing either.

Data sites show a single "net profit" line for each company. For a holdco that line can be badly misleading, in opposite directions for the two members. For CFHL it overstates what shareholders own because it includes minorities; for Religare it understates economic earnings because of insurance accounting. Every valuation multiple in this report uses one of three bases, labelled each time: reported PAT including minorities, PAT attributable to the holdco's shareholders, and look-through economic PAT, which uses each subsidiary's most economically meaningful profit figure.

CFHL: 55% of the profit belongs to someone else

CFHL's FY26 consolidated profit was ₹5,485 crore. That is 100% of CIFC's consolidated profit (₹5,233 crore), 100% of Chola MS's Ind AS contribution (₹253 crore) and about ₹12 crore from other entities, less holdco items. CFHL owns 43.74% of the first and 60% of the second. Its annual report (Note 46) shows ₹2,942.61 crore and ₹101.28 crore attributed to non-controlling interests, leaving ₹2,441.3 crore attributable to CFHL shareholders: ₹2,290 crore from CIFC and ₹151.93 crore from Chola MS. Screener's P/E of 9.26 correctly uses attributable EPS (₹142.12 trailing). Dividing CFHL's market cap by ₹5,485 crore would give a meaningless 4.5 times.

A health insurer in a CIC's clothing: Religare after the RBI said no Deep dive

Religare tried to become a pure Care Health holding company. The RBI declined. Under IRDAI's new rule that refusal blocks both of its routes to unlock, and the warrant money it needs is under water.

Strip away the history and Religare Enterprises is a listed wrapper around 63.2% of India's second-largest standalone health insurer, plus a lender with almost no loans, a small broker and a smaller housing financier. Care Health accounts for about 89% of its base-case NAV. For a company in that position the obvious move is to make the wrapper disappear: separate the non-insurance businesses and let shareholders own Care more directly. Religare's new management tried. This section explains what it tried, what the RBI's refusal blocks, and why the refusal matters more than it first appears.

The scheme the RBI declined

On 14 February 2026 the boards of Religare and Religare Finvest approved a scheme of arrangement. Religare's "financial services undertaking", its lending, broking, investment and ancillary businesses, with FY25 turnover of ₹457.29 crore (6.2% of consolidated), would be demerged into RFL. Every Religare shareholder would receive one RFL share for each Religare share; RFL's existing capital would be cancelled; and RFL would list on BSE and NSE with a shareholding that mirrored Religare's. Religare would keep only its Care stake. The stated aims were to separate the investor bases, sharpen regulatory focus and match each business with its own risk framework. The demerger was drafted to be tax-neutral under section 2(19AA).

Any explanation is therefore speculation, and should be read as such. Three hypotheses have been aired. First, the RBI may not want a new listed NBFC carrying the legacy of the 2016–18 corporate-loan-book diversion, with a shareholding mirroring a holdco whose promoter changed only in 2025. Management also conceded that its earlier expectation of completion in Q1 FY28 would slip.

Why the refusal blocks both doors

On the same call the MD mentioned "a new IRDAI guideline… [on] the collapse of a non-insurer into an insurer" that Religare was evaluating. That is IRDAI's amendment of 30 July 2026, and its conditions turn the RBI's refusal from a setback into a roadblock. A non-insurance company may amalgamate into an insurer only if it owns more than 50% of that insurer, which Religare does, and has no other non-insurance business on the date of application, which Religare does not while it owns RFL, Religare Broking and Religare Housing. The demerger the RBI blocked is exactly the step that would have made Religare eligible.

Warrants under water

Religare's capital plan depends on a ₹1,500 crore warrant issue approved by shareholders on 8 August 2025 (83.79% in favour). It allotted 6,38,29,782 warrants at ₹235, the original open-offer price, to Burman entities (₹750 crore) and others including The Hindustan Times Ltd, Ashish Dhawan and JM Financial Credit Solutions. Holders paid 25% (₹375 crore) upfront in September 2025 and must pay the remaining 75% and convert within 18 months, by about March 2027. By 13 August 2026 Religare had received ₹618 crore; ₹881 crore, on roughly 5.0 crore warrants, was still due. With the stock at ₹208, a holder who converts pays another ₹176.25 for a share worth ₹208, on top of ₹58.75 already paid. A purely financial holder would rather forfeit; a strategic holder such as the promoter family may convert to raise its stake and fund Care. Fully converted, Religare would have about 39.5 crore shares.

The lending stubs: capital waiting for a plan

Religare Finvest has about ₹914 crore of net worth, ₹609 crore of cash and liquid assets, a capital ratio of 238% and a net loan book of ₹53.1 crore spread across about 240 customers, four branches and three states. Its SME book fell from ₹286 crore to ₹138 crore during FY26. A new CEO, Srinivasan Karthik (ex-HDB Financial), joined in 2026; lending is to "restart in due time". Religare Housing, 87.5% owned through RFL, has a ₹247 crore book, a gross NPA of 4.4%, BBB- ratings and a ₹18.45 crore FY26 loss; Religare has committed ₹250 crore more to it. Management's stated ambition is a ₹10,000–15,000 crore loan book on ₹1,500–2,000 crore of capital, with no timeline and business plans written by executives who had been in their jobs one to three months. Until that plan exists, the stubs are about ₹1,000 crore of capital earning a deposit rate inside a group whose insurer is short of capital.

In plain terms

Religare is three things at once: a ₹10,000-crore-plus stake in a fast-growing health insurer, about ₹1,300 crore of under-used lending and broking capital, and a regulatory puzzle. The market currently pays ₹7,178 crore for the bundle. If the RBI lets the stubs go, the bundle could become listed Care shares. If it does not, Religare stays a holdco that cannot pay dividends, needs its warrant-holders to pay ₹176 more than the share is worth, and keeps sending money down to Care.

Chola MS in a soft market: a decent franchise losing ground Deep dive

CFHL's insurer compounded premium at 16% a year for four years. Then the general-insurance cycle turned, and its combined ratio reached 120%. What it is worth inside CFHL depends on which Chola MS you believe in.

Chola MS General Insurance is the part of CFHL most investors never look at, because at about 7% of NAV it barely moves the sum of the parts. It deserves a closer look for three reasons. It is the best window into how CFHL would handle a capital call from a subsidiary. It is the asset whose value could most easily be crystallised in cash, now that its Japanese partner may legally own 100%. And its current troubles show how quickly a general insurer's profits can disappear when the market softens.

Five good years, then a turn

From FY22 to FY25 Chola MS grew fast. Gross direct premium rose from ₹4,824 crore to ₹8,124 crore; profit rose from ₹77 crore to ₹507 crore; return on net worth climbed from about 4% to about 18.5%; and the solvency ratio ended FY25 at a comfortable 2.18×. It did this without raising a rupee of new equity: total capital ever infused is ₹442 crore, the last of it 13 years ago, and accumulated profits make up 87% of its ₹3,415 crore net worth. The engine was motor insurance sold through the Murugappa network, especially commercial vehicles and tractors financed by CIFC, plus crop insurance tenders and group health.

The group synergy has a price

Chola MS's distribution edge is the Murugappa network: it is the preferred insurer for about 30% of CIFC-financed commercial vehicles (according to CRISIL) and sells through CIFC's 1,820 branches, a group corporate agent and its own 595 points of presence. That edge is paid for. In FY26 Chola MS paid CIFC ₹256.6 crore of commission, paid the group corporate agent ₹225.6 crore and paid a group services company ₹181 crore of management expenses; it also holds ₹736 crore of CIFC securities and pays CFHL a ₹10 crore brand fee. If IRDAI's distribution consultation lowers commission and EoM limits, Chola MS's combined ratio improves and CIFC's fee income falls. For CFHL the two effects roughly cancel.

What it is worth inside CFHL

Listed general insurers trade at 4.3–5.4 times book and listed health insurers at 3.5–4.2 times (22 October 2026). Those multiples are for listed, liquid, controlling stakes in companies with 12–17% returns (ICICI Lombard, Go Digit) or high growth (Star, Niva). Chola MS is a 60% joint-venture stake that pays no dividend, earns about 10% on equity in a good year (2.6% non-annualised in Q1 FY27), runs a 115–120% combined ratio and has a foreign partner with its own plans. We therefore use 1.5–2.5 times net worth, ₹5,100–8,500 crore for 100% and ₹3,070–5,120 crore for CFHL's 60% (illustrative). Our own primer had used ₹10,000–13,000 crore; on current numbers that looks generous. The case for the higher range is the listed-peer floor: at Niva Bupa's 3.5 times, Chola MS would be worth about ₹11,950 crore, CFHL's share ₹7,170 crore. Even that adds only about 5% to CFHL's NAV.

Care Health and GST 2.0: the land-grab year Deep dive

The removal of GST from retail health premiums set off the fastest growth in Indian health insurance in years. Care is winning share. It is also running its solvency close to the floor to do it.

Care Health Insurance began life in 2012 as Religare Health Insurance and was renamed in 2020. It is now India's second-largest standalone health insurer, with 24.2% of SAHI premium in H1 FY27, and the asset behind about 89% of Religare's NAV. In the twelve months since individual health premiums became GST-free, it has grown faster than any of its large rivals.

The growth

Care's gross written premium was ₹7,022 crore in FY24, ₹9,223 crore in FY25 and ₹11,417 crore in FY26 on the full-premium (n) basis, up 24%; on the statutory 1/n basis the last two figures are ₹8,562 crore and ₹10,416 crore (up 21.7%). In Q1 FY27 premium grew 37% on the n basis to ₹3,247 crore and 41.8% on the 1/n basis to ₹3,028 crore, with retail up 45%. GI Council data show the momentum continuing: Care's H1 FY27 direct premium was ₹6,192.7 crore, up 42.2%, with retail health up 50.7%, and September 2026, the month the exemption's first anniversary fell, was still up 44%. Care now writes 12.8% of all retail health insurance sold in India.

How much capital does growth consume? A rough rule (illustrative) is that each ₹1,000 crore of extra annual premium needs ₹150–200 crore of additional capital to hold solvency at 1.7×, of which Religare's 63.2% share is about ₹100–125 crore. Capital flows down the chain, not up

Holdco shareholders want cash to rise from the jewels. In 2026 the money moved the other way: QIPs, rights issues, sub-debt and warrants, against a trickle of dividends.

The textbook case for owning a holdco at a discount is that it buys a stream of dividends at a markdown: if the holdco yields more than the jewel, the discount pays you to wait. That case fails for both members, because in FY26 almost no cash rose up either chain while large amounts of capital were needed lower down.

CIFC's QIP and CFHL's shrinking stake

CIFC is growing assets at about 23% a year and earning a 21% return on equity, so on its own it would generate capital roughly as fast as it uses it. The size and price are not known. Its capital ratio of 19.81% (Tier I 14.81%) is about five points above the minimum, and a QIP buys headroom for the next two to three years of growth.

CFHL cannot follow. A QIP is offered only to qualified institutional buyers, and in any case CFHL has about ₹110 crore of cash. Each issue therefore shrinks its percentage. CFHL's stake fell from 44.34% in March 2025 to 43.76% in March 2026 and 43.74% in June 2026, with its share count unchanged at 37,28,85,889; after the October CCD tranche it is about 43.5%. The Murugappa group's combined stake, already 48.93%, would fall to about 47.1% after a ₹5,000 crore issue.

Dilution in percentage terms is not the same as a loss of value. If CIFC sells new shares at a fair price, the cash it receives is worth exactly what the new shareholders paid, and CFHL's smaller slice of a bigger company is worth what its bigger slice of a smaller company was. The damage is subtler. Each percentage point of dilution transfers about ₹1,310 crore of today's CIFC value away from CFHL's look-through (before counting the new capital's value to CIFC), shrinks CFHL's share of all future growth, and pushes the family's control percentage further below 50%. For a holdco whose discount is partly a "we cannot follow our own jewel" discount, every QIP is a reminder.

The insurers' calls

Both insurers need capital or will soon. Chola MS's solvency has slipped to 1.93× and both parents are, in CRISIL's words, expected to contribute if needed; CFHL's share would be 60%. Care needs regular top-ups to grow at 40%, and Religare must fund about 63% of each round or accept dilution, which under IRDAI's July 2026 rules now counts as a share transfer needing approval. Religare's lending stubs add a third claim: ₹250 crore promised to Religare Housing. Against all of this the two holdcos generate about ₹85 crore a year of cash between them, all of it at CFHL.

In plain terms

A holdco shareholder's ideal is a jewel that throws off cash, a holdco that passes it on, and no capital calls. Here the jewels are growing too fast to pay out, the holdcos are not allowed to pay out much, and the next capital call is always on the calendar. Until that changes, the discount is not just a sentiment number: it is a fair charge for owning a structure where money only flows one way.

A century, two families, five shocks

One holdco is the residue of a 1949 manufacturing company; the other is what was left after a founder-led fraud. Their histories explain why each discount looks the way it does.

Murugappa. The Murugappa group was founded in Chennai in 1900. The company now called CFHL was incorporated in 1949 as part of the Tube Investments (TI) business. Cholamandalam Investment and Finance followed in 1978, went through a joint venture with Singapore's DBS (as Cholamandalam DBS, unwound around 2010) and grew into a vehicle-finance specialist. Chola MS General Insurance was formed with Mitsui Sumitomo in 2001; MSI raised its stake from 26% to 40% around 2016. In 2017 TI's manufacturing businesses were demerged into a new Tube Investments of India, and the old company, renamed TI Financial Holdings and then Cholamandalam Financial Holdings, became a pure holding company (its standalone revenue fell from ₹4,769 crore in FY16 to ₹40 crore in FY17). A dispute within the family in 2020–22 was settled. Since then CIFC's assets have grown from about ₹55,000 crore in FY19 to ₹2.54 lakh crore, turning CFHL into a ₹25,000–40,000 crore company on about ₹1,400 crore of standalone book.

Religare. Religare Enterprises was incorporated in 1984 and built by Malvinder and Shivinder Singh, formerly of Ranbaxy, into a broking, lending, insurance and asset-management group; it listed in December 2007. Religare Health Insurance launched in 2012. Between 2016 and 2018 roughly ₹2,300 crore was allegedly diverted through Religare Finvest's corporate loan book to entities linked to the founders; the promoters' holding collapsed from 50.93% in March 2017 to 3.08% a year later, the RBI put RFL under a corrective plan in January 2018 and barred Religare from paying dividends in 2019, and the brothers were arrested in 2019. Under Rashmi Saluja, Religare cleaned up, culminating in RFL's ₹2,178 crore settlement with 16 lenders in March 2023. The Burman family then took control through a contested open offer completed in February 2025, and the 2025–26 period brought new directors, the lifting of RFL's corrective plan, the quashing of its fraud classification, the warrant issue, the demerger plan and the RBI's refusal.

Three cycles recur through this history and are worth recognising when they return. The first is the NBFC funding cycle: liquidity shocks (IL&FS in 2018, DHFL in 2019, COVID in 2020) widen spreads and test lenders' access to money, and CIFC, with AA+ ratings and group backing, has passed through each one, but its share price and therefore CFHL's fell sharply each time. The second is the NBFC credit cycle, visible in CIFC's credit cost: low in FY23–24 (0.9–1.0% of assets), rising as unsecured lending was squeezed (1.4% in FY25, 1.7% in FY26) and now easing (1.5% in Q1 FY27). The third is the general-insurance underwriting cycle: hard markets after big losses or tariff changes, soft markets when capacity floods in. The fourth cycle is the holdco-discount cycle itself. Reading a financial holdco: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Two layers of numbers: the holdco's own (NAV, discount, look-through earnings, pass-through, CIC ratios) and its jewels' (a lender's margins and credit, an insurer's combined ratio and solvency). Here is what each one means, how to compute it and where it misleads.

Analysing a financial holding company means reading two sets of accounts at once. The top layer tells you what the wrapper owns, what it costs and how much cash reaches you. The bottom layer tells you whether the things it owns are getting more or less valuable. Most mistakes come from using a bottom-layer number as if it were a top-layer one (consolidated profit as the holdco's profit) or from mixing bases (a 1/n combined ratio against an Ind AS one). This section works through both layers, one KPI at a time.

The holdco equation, in rupees

A holdco's market value can be written as one line: market cap = (1 − discount) × [Σ (stake × value of each subsidiary) + holdco cash − holdco debt − capitalised holdco cost]. The bracket is the net asset value (NAV), or sum of the parts. The value of a listed subsidiary is its market cap; of an unlisted one, a peer multiple of book, premium or profit. The capitalised cost is the holdco's annual running cost times a multiple (we use eight). The discount is whatever the market charges for the wrapper.

In plain terms

A holdco is worth what it owns, minus what it costs to run, minus a discount. What it earns is its share of what its companies earn. What it pays you is a separate and much smaller number. Every KPI below is a way of measuring one of those three things.

1. NAV per share (sum of the parts)

What it is: the holdco's net asset value divided by its share count, the per-share value of everything it owns. Formula: [Σ (shares held × price) for listed stakes + Σ (stake × estimated value) for unlisted ones + cash − debt − capitalised costs] ÷ holdco shares. Worked example: CFHL's ₹57,089 crore CIFC stake plus ₹4,098 crore for Chola MS, ₹50 crore for risk services and ₹111 crore of cash gives ₹61,348 crore, or ₹3,267 a share on 18.776 crore shares (indicative). What moves it: the jewel's share price above all (a 10% move in CIFC moves CFHL's NAV 9.3%), then the assumed multiple for unlisted stakes and any dilution. Benchmark: NAV per share has no good or bad level; what matters is the price as a share of it, and whether it is compounding (CFHL's has grown roughly with CIFC). Comparison: CFHL trades at 40% of NAV per share; Religare at 63% undiluted and 67% fully diluted.

The definition trap: how you count a listed stake. Multiplying CFHL's 43.74% by the CIFC market cap shown on screener (₹1,31,315 crore) gives ₹57,437 crore; multiplying the 37,28,85,889 shares CFHL actually holds by ₹1,531 gives ₹57,089 crore. The difference (₹348 crore) arises because screener's share count includes convertible debentures that had not yet converted. Always use shares held × price. And for unlisted stakes, always state the multiple: CFHL's NAV ranges ₹60,303–62,382 crore across Chola MS at 1.5–2.5× book, and Religare's ₹7,526–14,478 crore across Care at ₹10,000–21,000 crore.

2. Holdco discount, measured three ways

What it is: how far the market value sits below NAV. Formula: 1 − market cap ÷ NAV. Three versions matter. Stake-only compares market cap with the listed stake alone (no assumptions; CFHL 56.7%). SOTP uses the full NAV (CFHL 59.7%; Religare 36.6%). Post-tax deducts capital-gains tax on the embedded gain, here 12.5% of NAV less cost (CFHL 54.1%; Religare roughly 30–35%). Worked example: 1 − 24,714 ÷ 61,348 = 59.7%. What moves it: market mood (discounts widen in drawdowns), payout policy, unlock news, regulatory decisions, dilution risk at the jewel and liquidity of the holdco's shares. Benchmark (Indian holdcos, indicative): under 30% good, 30–50% average, over 50% weak. Comparison: CFHL is weak on every basis; Religare average on the base case but anywhere from 5% to 50%.

The definition trap: quoting a discount without its basis and date. CFHL's "discount" in our own June primer was about 54% stake-only; on 22 October the same measure was 56.7% and the SOTP figure 59.7%. Comparing one company's stake-only discount with another's post-tax discount can make a 60% discount look like 45%. A second trap: for an unlisted jewel the discount is only as good as the jewel's valuation, so a "37% discount" on Religare really means "37% if Care is worth ₹16,000 crore".

3. Look-through PAT

Worked example: Religare FY26 = 63.2% × ₹387 crore (Care, Ind AS) + ₹138.8 crore (RFL) + ₹23.1 crore (broking) − 87.5% × ₹18.45 crore (housing) − ₹33.8 crore (holdco) ≈ ₹357 crore (illustrative). Benchmark: judged as a multiple: market cap ÷ look-through PAT, compared with the jewel's own P/E. A holdco at a 50–60% discount on a jewel at 20–25× should trade at roughly 10×. Comparison: CFHL 10.1×, Religare 20.1× (about 27× excluding RFL's one-off recoveries), combined 11.4×, against CIFC at 24.9× and listed health insurers at 57–101×.

The definition trap: choosing the subsidiary's profit basis. A second trap is quality: one-off write-backs (RFL's ₹93.7 crore) inflate look-through earnings that will not recur.

4. Attributable versus reported PAT (the minority share)

What it is: the split of consolidated profit between the holdco's shareholders and the subsidiaries' other shareholders (non-controlling interests, NCI). Formula: attributable PAT = consolidated PAT − NCI; NCI share = NCI ÷ consolidated PAT. Worked example: CFHL FY26: ₹5,485 crore − ₹2,942.61 crore − ₹101.28 crore = ₹2,441.3 crore; NCI share 55.5%. What moves it: the holdco's stakes (falling with each CIFC dilution, so the NCI share creeps up) and the mix of profit between subsidiaries owned at different percentages. Benchmark: none as such; the point is to use the right one. Comparison: CFHL's attributable profit is 44.5% of its reported figure. Religare's is the other way round: ₹86 crore attributable against ₹73 crore reported, because Care's other shareholders (36.8%) absorb part of the losses Care contributes on the consolidation basis.

The definition trap: data sites label the consolidated figure "net profit" without saying it includes minorities. CFHL's own primer did the same. Dividing CFHL's market cap by ₹5,485 crore gives 4.5×, a number that describes nothing. Always check that EPS (which is attributable) times shares matches the profit figure you are using.

5. Standalone cash and debt

What it is: the holdco's own balance sheet, excluding subsidiaries: cash and liquid investments, borrowings, guarantees given. Formula: standalone cash − standalone borrowings; separately, contingent liabilities (guarantees, disputed tax). Worked example: CFHL: ₹109.65 crore of deposits, no borrowings since FY24, contingent liabilities under ₹2 crore. Religare: about ₹81 crore of other assets, ₹6 crore of borrowings (March 2026), but ₹545 crore of standalone contingent liabilities including a ₹380 crore guarantee of Religare Broking's bank lines. What moves it: dividends received, capital sent down, dividends paid, warrant or equity money raised. Benchmark: good = net cash covering several years of holdco costs and any foreseeable capital call; weak = borrowing at the holdco to fund subsidiaries. Comparison: CFHL comfortably net cash but small relative to its subsidiaries' possible needs; Religare net cash at period-ends but dependent on warrant inflows and, before they arrived, on short-term promoter and NBFC loans (₹10 crore and ₹50 crore at 12% in 2025).

The definition trap: "debt-free" at the holdco can coexist with large consolidated debt (CFHL's group borrowings are ₹2.1 lakh crore, almost all CIFC's) and with guarantees that are debt in all but name. Read the standalone balance sheet and the contingent-liabilities note.

6. Dividend pass-through ratio

7. CIC ratios: capital ratio and leverage

What they are: the RBI's two prudential tests for a Core Investment Company. Formulas: capital ratio = adjusted net worth (owned funds + 50% of unrealised gains on quoted investments) ÷ risk-weighted assets, minimum 30%; leverage = outside liabilities ÷ ANW, maximum 2.5×. A third test, investments in group companies ÷ net assets, must be at least 90%. Worked example: CFHL June 2026: ANW ₹30,812.80 crore ÷ RWA ₹1,306.00 crore = 2,359%; outside liabilities ₹4.89 crore ÷ ANW = 0.0002×; group investments 99.82%. Benchmark: capital ratio over 100% good, 50–100% average, under 50% weak; leverage under 0.5× good. Comparison: CFHL is far above every threshold; Religare reports only that it complies.

The definition trap: the huge headroom does not mean the holdco can fund its subsidiaries. CFHL could in theory borrow about ₹77,000 crore under the leverage test, but any CIC borrowing has to be serviced from dividend income of ₹75–90 crore a year. 8. The jewel as a lender: AUM growth and net interest margin

What they are: the size and growth of the loan book, and the spread it earns. Formulas: AUM growth = AUM this period ÷ AUM a year earlier − 1; NIM (CIFC's "net income margin") = (interest and fee income − cost of funds) ÷ average assets. Worked example: CIFC's Total AUM grew from ₹2,07,663 crore (June 2025) to ₹2,54,392 crore (June 2026), up 22.5%; Q1 FY27 income was 14.9% of assets and cost of funds 6.7%, a NIM of 8.2%. What drives them: vehicle sales, product mix (higher-yield used vehicles, consumer and small-business loans lift NIM), borrowing costs and the pass-through of repo moves. CIFC's CFO expects cost of funds to rise about 10 basis points in H2 FY27 after the 7 October repo hike but hopes to hold NIM at 8.2%. Benchmark (vehicle/SME NBFC, indicative): AUM growth over 20% strong, 12–20% average; NIM over 7.5% good, 6–7.5% average. Comparison: CIFC is strong on both; Religare's lenders are too small to measure (RFL ₹53 crore; housing ₹247 crore).

9. Credit cost and asset quality: Stage-3 versus GNPA

What they are: how much the lender loses on bad loans, and how many loans are bad. Formulas: credit cost = loan losses and provisions ÷ average assets (or AUM); gross Stage-3 = loans more than 90 days overdue under Ind AS ÷ gross loans; RBI-basis GNPA = non-performing loans under the RBI's income-recognition rules ÷ gross loans; provision coverage = Stage-3 provisions ÷ Stage-3 loans. Worked example: CIFC June 2026: gross Stage-3 3.29%, net Stage-3 1.78%, coverage 45.7%; RBI-basis GNPA 4.50%, NNPA 2.95%; Q1 FY27 credit cost 1.5% (₹922 crore of net credit losses). What drives them: the economy (rural income, monsoon, freight rates), underwriting, collections (CIFC has ~30,000 collection staff) and seasonality (Q1 is usually the weakest quarter). Benchmark (indicative): credit cost under 1.2% good, 1.2–2.0% average, over 2% weak; RBI GNPA under 3% good, 3–5% average. Comparison: CIFC average on both, which is acceptable for a vehicle-heavy book; management's FY27 credit-cost guidance of ~1.5% was reached in Q1.

The definition trap: the gap between Stage-3 and GNPA. Since November 2021 the RBI has required that a non-performing loan stay classified as such until all arrears are cleared, while Ind AS Stage-3 can cure earlier. Both of CIFC's numbers are correct; quote the basis. Another trap: CIFC changed when it recognises disbursements from Q1 FY27 (at cheque clearance rather than handover), which slightly flatters yields.

10. RoA and RoE of the jewel

What they are: profit relative to assets and to shareholders' equity. Formulas: ROA-PBT = NIM − opex ratio − credit cost (all on average assets); ROA-PAT = ROA-PBT × (1 − tax); RoE = PAT ÷ average equity ≈ ROA-PAT × leverage. Worked example: CIFC Q1 FY27: 8.2% − 3.0% − 1.5% = 3.7% ROA-PBT; × 0.75 ≈ 2.8% ROA-PAT; × about 7.5 times leverage ≈ 21.2% RoE. FY26: 8.0 − 3.1 − 1.7 ≈ 3.3% (rounding), ROA-PAT 2.5%, RoE 19.7%. What drives them: the three ratios plus leverage, which equity raises reduce. Benchmark (NBFC, indicative): RoA over 3% good, 2–3% average; RoE over 18% good, 14–18% average. Comparison: CIFC's RoA-PAT of 2.5–2.8% is average-to-good and its RoE good; management calls a 3.5% PBT-ROA the "minimum". Chola MS's RoNW of 10.4% (FY26) and Care's Ind AS RoE of 14.5% are the insurer equivalents.

The definition trap: a QIP lowers RoE mechanically by cutting leverage, even if the business is unchanged. When CIFC's RoE dips after the 28 October raise, separate the leverage effect from any change in margins or credit.

The definition trap: industry market shares are on GDPI, so quoting Chola MS's GWP growth hides a shrinking direct book. And comparing Care's n-basis growth with a peer's 1/n growth overstates the gap.

13. Solvency ratio

What it is: the insurer's available capital relative to the capital IRDAI requires. Formula: available solvency margin ÷ required solvency margin; minimum 1.50×. Worked example: Chola MS 1.93× (June 2026); Care 1.58×. A ratio of 1.58× means Care holds 58% more capital than the minimum, or 0.08× above it. What drives it: retained profit (on the statutory basis), premium growth (which raises required capital), investment mark-to-market, and capital raised (equity counts fully; sub-debt within limits). Benchmark (indicative): over 2.0× good, 1.7–2.0× average, under 1.7× weak. Comparison: Chola MS average and falling; Care weak, though ₹300 crore of sub-debt raised in August–September 2026 should restore it to about 1.7× (illustrative).

The definition trap: solvency is computed on statutory IGAAP figures, so Care's Ind AS profit (₹387 crore in FY26) does not help it; only its 1/n profit (₹12 crore) does. Sub-debt raises solvency without adding equity, but adds a 10% coupon.

14. Float and investment yield

What they are: the money an insurer holds between collecting premium and paying claims, and what it earns on it. Formulas: investment leverage = investment book ÷ net worth; investment yield = investment income ÷ average investments. Worked example: Chola MS: ₹19,172 crore of investments on ₹3,415 crore of net worth = 5.6×; FY26 yield 7.70% (₹1,448 crore of income). Care: ₹11,751 crore on ₹2,718 crore = 4.3×; yield 7.1%. A 5.6× leverage means each 1% of yield adds about 5.6% to return on equity before underwriting. What drives them: premium growth and claims tail (motor third-party claims are paid years later, so the float is large), interest rates and asset mix (Chola MS: G-secs ~34%, equities under 7%). Benchmark: 4–6× leverage typical for Indian non-life insurers; yield tracks government-bond rates. Comparison: Chola MS's larger float is why it stays profitable at a 115% combined ratio; Care plans to bring leverage down to ~3.7× with fresh equity.

The definition trap: investment income can hide underwriting losses for years. A rising yield (Chola MS: 6.55% in FY23 to 7.70% in FY26) flatters profit while the combined ratio worsens; when rates fall, the cover disappears.

15. QIP dilution of the holdco's stake

What it is: the fall in the holdco's percentage when the jewel issues shares the holdco does not buy. Formula: new stake = shares held ÷ (old share count + new shares); new shares = issue size ÷ issue price. Worked example: CIFC ₹5,000 crore QIP at ₹1,531 = 3.27 crore new shares; 37.29 ÷ (85.25 + 3.27) = 42.1%, a 1.6-point fall (illustrative). What drives it: the jewel's growth versus its internal capital generation, its payout and its capital ratio relative to the floor. Benchmark: good = the jewel funds growth internally; weak = repeated issues without the holdco. Comparison: CFHL lost 0.6 points in FY26 (CCD conversions) and faces more after 28 October; Religare runs the other way, gaining Care share by taking up more than its rights entitlement.

The definition trap: dilution at a fair price does not destroy the holdco's value on the day, because the new cash adds to the jewel. What it costs is a smaller share of future earnings and, for a promoter group already below 50%, control. Distinguish "percentage diluted" from "value lost".

In plain terms

When CIFC sells new shares, CFHL's slice gets thinner but the cake gets bigger by exactly the money raised. CFHL does not lose value on the day. It loses its claim on part of every future year's growth, and the family's grip on CIFC loosens a little more.

How to read a holding company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the jewel, not the holdco (2 minutes). CIFC reports first (Q2 FY27 on 28 October). Pull AUM growth, disbursement growth, NIM, credit cost, Stage-3 and RBI GNPA, and capital ratio. Red flags: credit cost above 1.8%, Stage-3 rising more than 30 bp quarter on quarter outside Q1, NIM falling more than 20 bp.
  2. Check the insurer pages (2 minutes). For Chola MS (inside CFHL's results, 6 November): GDPI growth versus the industry, combined ratio, loss ratio by line, solvency. For Care (inside Religare's): GWP growth on n and 1/n, Ind AS PBT and CISR, solvency. Red flags: combined ratio above 120%, solvency below 1.8× (Chola MS) or 1.6× (Care) without a capital plan.
  3. Find attributable profit (1 minute). Take EPS × shares, not "net profit". For CFHL, expect it to be about 45% of consolidated PAT. For Religare, compare the reported figure with Care's Ind AS profit × 63.2%.
  4. Read the standalone results (1 minute). Dividend income arrives in the September and March quarters at CFHL. Look for any change in dividends received, any capital sent down, any borrowing.
  5. Update the NAV (1 minute). Shares held × the jewel's price; your multiple for unlisted stakes; standalone cash. Recompute the discount on all three bases.
  6. Check the stake (30 seconds). Did the jewel issue shares (CCDs, QIP, ESOPs)? Recalculate the holdco's percentage.
  7. Scan for corporate actions (1 minute). Board-meeting notices, RBI or IRDAI letters, scheme updates, warrant conversions, rights issues. For Religare, any RBI response is the single most important item.
  8. Read the regulatory notes (30 seconds). CIC ratios, any change in RBI restrictions, accounting-basis changes (Ind AS 117 from April 2027).
  9. Compare with management's last guidance (30 seconds). CIFC: 22–23% disbursement growth, ~1.5% credit cost, PBT-ROA of at least 3.5%. Care: CISR toward 100% within two years; solvency 1.7×. Chola MS: motor OD loss ratio below 80% "over a period".
  10. Write down one sentence (30 seconds). "The jewel did X, the discount moved from Y to Z, and the next catalyst is W." If you cannot write it, you have not finished reading.
In plain terms

A holdco quarter is really three or four quarters stacked on top of each other. Read the jewels first, because they decide the value; then the holdco's own accounts, because they decide the cash; then the stake and the regulatory notes, because they decide whether anything changes. The holdco's own headline profit is the least useful number in the release.

The global lens: India's holdcos sit at the Korean end

Holdco discounts are a worldwide phenomenon. India's are among the widest, and its tools for closing them are among the weakest.

Holding-company discounts are not an Indian peculiarity. Indicative mid-points for 2023–26 put Korean chaebol holdcos at about 60%, Exor at about 50%, GBL at about 45%, Prosus at about 40% after its buyback programme, Sofina at about 35%, and Investor AB at roughly 0–15%, sometimes trading at a premium (these figures come from our investment companies report and are themselves indicative). On that scale, CFHL (~60%) and Bajaj Nivesh (~61%) sit at the Korean end, BHIL (~46%) in the European middle, and Religare's base case (~37%) closer to Sofina.

The foreign holdcos that have narrowed their discounts used tools that are either unavailable or expensive in India. Prosus/Naspers and Exor run systematic buybacks funded by dividends from their holdings; in India, buybacks are taxed as deemed dividends in shareholders' hands since October 2024, and a CIC cannot borrow to fund one without straining the spirit of its rules. Investor AB publishes NAV every quarter, distributes generously and is seen as a capital allocator rather than a control vehicle; Indian CICs are capped at a 60% payout of standalone profit and exist mainly to hold control. Korea's "Value-Up" programme, launched in 2024, has pushed chaebol holdcos to simplify and return capital; India's closest equivalents are the RBI's two-layer cap for CICs and SEBI's special call auctions for illiquid holdcos (from June 2024), which push gently towards simpler structures but do not force distributions.

The jewels' valuations tell the other half of the global story. India's listed general and health insurers trade at 3.5–5.4 times book; Japan's MS&AD, Chola MS's partner, trades at about 1.0–1.5 times, and Asian insurers broadly at 1–2 times. CIFC trades at 4.3 times book and 22.8 times trailing earnings; US and European consumer-finance lenders typically trade at 1–2 times book. The difference is growth: CIFC's assets are compounding at over 20% and Indian insurance premiums at 9–30%, against low single digits in mature markets. That growth premium is what makes the jewels valuable even at mediocre returns, and it is why the holdco discounts, large as they are, still leave CFHL at about 1.6 times book.

In plain terms

Abroad, holdcos that want to close their discount buy back their own shares, pay out most of what they receive and publish their NAV every quarter. Indian financial holdcos face a tax on buybacks, a cap on payouts and a regulator that must approve any restructuring. It is not surprising that their discounts look Korean rather than Swedish.

Technology and disruption: threats to the jewels, and to the wrapper itself

The disruptive forces here are less about apps than about rules: who may distribute insurance and at what commission, how insurers count profit, and whether regulators still tolerate multi-layer groups.

Lending competition. CIFC's vehicle and small-business franchise rests on 1,820 branches, 87% of them in Tier III–VI towns, about 30,000 collection staff and decades of underwriting data on truck operators and small businesses. Banks are pushing into the same customers through co-lending with NBFCs and their own rural branches, and fintech lenders compete in consumer credit. CIFC's own digital bets sit in its consumer and small-enterprise book and two subsidiaries, Paytail and Payswiff. The risk is concentrated in unsecured lending, where CIFC's net credit loss fell from 6.7% to 4.7% but remains several times its secured books. Its newest push is the opposite of digital: 360 more gold-loan branches in FY27, aiming to lift that book from ₹2,143 crore to about ₹5,000 crore by March 2027.

Insurance distribution. Online aggregators and bank-led bancassurance (after IRDAI relaxed tie-up limits in 2024) have changed how general and health insurance is sold. Religare's own web aggregator, MIC Insurance Web Aggregator, surrendered its licence with IRDAI approval on 5 August 2026. The larger change may come from the regulator. IRDAI's 23 September 2026 consultation, "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution", proposes (as reported) to lower general insurers' expense-of-management limit from 30% towards 20% of GDPI over five years, cut health new-business commissions from about 40% to 15–20%, cut motor own-damage commissions from 16% to about 5%, and ban manipulative "dark patterns" online. For Chola MS and Care that would lower costs and help combined ratios. For distributors it would cut revenue: CIFC earned ₹256.6 crore of commission from Chola MS alone in FY26, and its CFO declined to size the impact ("let the circular come"). Comments close on 25 October 2026.

Health-claims technology. Cashless settlement "everywhere", the National Health Claims Exchange (NHCX) and automated claims processing are making claims faster and cheaper to handle; Care settles about 67% of retail claims cashless. This is a "disruption" to reported numbers rather than to business, but it will move both holdcos' reported earnings: Religare's should converge towards its look-through figure once Care is consolidated on Ind AS.

Broking. Discount brokers (Zerodha, Groww, Angel One) and SEBI's tightening of futures and options trading from November 2024 compress the economics of small full-service brokers such as Religare Broking, with about 2.4 lakh active clients and ₹47,946 crore of assets under custody.

Holdco obsolescence. Regulators increasingly prefer flatter groups. The RBI's two-layer cap for CICs (2020), the mandatory listing of Upper Layer NBFCs, SEBI's special call auctions for illiquid holdcos (June 2024) and now IRDAI's holdco-into-insurer amalgamation rule all push gently towards simplification. None forces it, but together they make it more likely over a decade that at least one of these wrappers is dissolved by merger rather than by market forces.

In plain terms

The most important "technology" risk for this sub-sector is a rulebook. If IRDAI cuts commissions, the insurers gain and CIFC's fee line loses. If Ind AS arrives, Religare's reported profit jumps. If the regulators keep nudging groups to simplify, one day a holdco may simply merge away. None of these is about apps.

Bull versus bear

Both cases are strong, and both turn on things the holdcos do not control: CIFC's share price, the discount and two regulators.

The two sides of the argument are summarised below. Most of the disagreement is about time: the bull case requires the discount to narrow or a structural event to occur; the bear case only requires the status quo to continue while capital keeps flowing down.

The bull case

  • Cheap access to a top-tier lender. CFHL prices CIFC, with 23% AUM growth and a 21% RoE, at about 43 paise on the rupee and 10 times attributable earnings.
  • The discount is cyclical as well as structural. It was about 53–54% in June; a return there with CIFC flat would lift CFHL about 7%. Holdco discounts have narrowed in every bull market.
  • Care is a scarce growth asset. Number two in SAHI, 42% growth, 24% share and rising; listed peers would value it at ₹15,500–17,000 crore, against about ₹9,450 crore implied through Religare.
  • The Religare end-game exists. IRDAI's July 2026 rule creates a route to list Care by merging the holdco into it. One RBI approval away.
  • Reported profit will catch up. Care on Ind AS from April 2027 should lift Religare's reported earnings towards ~₹300–400 crore.
  • Crystallisation options. 100% insurance FDI lets MSI buy Chola MS; a cash sale would add about 5% to CFHL's NAV and real cash at the holdco.

The bear case

  • No mechanism closes the discount. Payout capped by the RBI, buybacks taxed, no control premium, no merger on the table. CFHL's discount has widened over ten years.
  • Geared to CIFC's downside. CFHL fell 1.4 times as much as CIFC from June to October; a repo-hike cycle and NBFC credit stress would hit both layers.
  • Dilution. CIFC's QIP and CCDs shrink CFHL's stake and the family's control without CFHL participating.
  • Chola MS in a soft market. 120% combined ratio, losing motor share, solvency falling, Ind AS and risk-based capital ahead; a capital call would hit a holdco with ₹110 crore of cash.
  • Religare's key is with the RBI. No reasons given for the refusal; no timeline; dividend ban since 2019; governance history the regulator remembers.
  • Care needs capital. 1.58× solvency, ₹300 crore of 10% sub-debt, rights issues ahead, and ₹881 crore of warrant money owed at a strike above the share price.
