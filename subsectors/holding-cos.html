Financial holding companies: a rupee of value for 44 paise

A complete guide to India's two listed financial holding companies, Cholamandalam Financial Holdings and Religare Enterprises: what a holding company actually produces, why the market prices each of them at well under half the value of what they own, how the RBI and IRDAI hold the keys to every unlock, and how a vehicle lender, a general insurer and a health insurer underneath them are running on three different cycles at once.

00

The one-page view: two wrappers, three jewels and a widening discount

Each of India's two listed financial holdcos owns one very valuable thing. The market prices each wrapper far below what it holds, and in 2026 the gap got wider.

A financial holding company makes nothing. Cholamandalam Financial Holdings (CFHL) owns 43.74% of Cholamandalam Investment and Finance (CIFC), one of India's best-run vehicle and small-business lenders, plus 60% of Chola MS General Insurance and 49.5% of a small risk-services firm. Religare Enterprises (REL) owns 63.20% of Care Health Insurance, the country's second-largest standalone health insurer, plus a cash-rich lending shell, a small stockbroker and a small housing-finance company. Both are registered with the Reserve Bank of India as Core Investment Companies (CICs), which means at least 90% of what they own must sit in group companies. Each holdco is, in effect, a listed ticket to one jewel.

On 22 October 2026 the two tickets were worth ₹31,892 crore together: ₹24,714 crore for CFHL at ₹1,316 a share and ₹7,178 crore for Religare at ₹208. What they own, valued on market prices where they exist and on stated assumptions where they do not, is worth about ₹72,700 crore (indicative). That is a combined discount of roughly 56%: a buyer of the two wrappers pays about 44 paise for each rupee of underlying value. CFHL's discount alone is about 60% to its sum-of-the-parts and about 57% to its CIFC stake by itself, ignoring everything else it owns. Religare's is about 37% in our base case, but anywhere from 5% to 50% depending on what Care Health is worth, because Care has no market price.

Five forces shape everything in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them.

  • The holding-company discount is structural, and in 2026 it widened. CFHL's discount to its CIFC stake moved from about 53–54% at the end of June to about 57% on 22 October (indicative). CFHL fell about 20% over that period while CIFC fell about 15%: the wrapper behaves like a geared bet on the jewel.
  • Reported earnings are not the holdco's earnings. CFHL's consolidated FY26 profit of ₹5,485 crore includes the share belonging to CIFC's and Chola MS's other owners; only about ₹2,441 crore is attributable to CFHL shareholders. Religare's reported profit understates its economics because it still consolidates Care on a statutory accounting basis that defers multi-year premium.
  • Regulators hold the unlock keys. The RBI caps CIC dividends at 60% of standalone profit, limits groups to two layers of CICs and must approve any scheme. On 6–7 August 2026 it declined Religare's demerger without giving reasons. IRDAI's 30 July 2026 rule on merging a holdco into its insurer only works once that holdco has no other business.
  • The jewels' cycles are out of phase. CIFC is growing assets 23% with a 21% return on equity. Chola MS is in a soft general-insurance market with combined ratios of 115–120%. Care Health is growing more than 40% after GST was removed from retail health premiums, on a thin 1.58× solvency ratio.
  • Capital flows down the chain, not up. CIFC has called a board meeting on 28 October to consider a QIP, Care needs rights issues and sub-debt, and Religare is waiting for ₹881 crore of warrant money from holders whose ₹235 strike is above the ₹208 share price. Meanwhile the two holdcos paid ₹24 crore of dividends in FY26 on ₹2,527 crore of attributable profit.

01

What a holding company makes: a liquid claim on a locked block

The product is a listed share that stands for an illiquid or controlled stake. The price of that product is the discount.

A financial holdco earns in two ways that look nothing alike. The first is cash: dividends and fees that its subsidiaries actually pay it, which show up in its standalone accounts. The second is accounting: its share of the subsidiaries' profits, which shows up in its consolidated accounts whether or not a rupee moves. For CFHL in FY26 the first number was ₹89.79 crore of standalone revenue. The second was ₹2,441 crore of attributable consolidated profit. The holdco's shareholders own a claim on the second number but are paid out of the first, and the distance between them is the starting point for understanding why the discount exists.

Between the jewel's profit and the holdco shareholder's bank account, value leaks out at five points. Each one is a reason for the discount, and each is measurable:

  • The other owners. 56.26% of CIFC's profit, 40% of Chola MS's and 36.8% of Care's belong to someone else. Consolidated profit shows all of it; the holdco owns only its slice.
  • Retention at the jewel. CIFC paid ₹2.00 a share on FY26 earnings of ₹61.41, a payout of about 3%. Chola MS has paid nothing for five years and has not raised capital for 13. Care pays nothing and needs money.
  • Retention at the holdco. CFHL passed on ₹24.41 crore of the ₹74.88 crore of dividends it received, 33%. Even if it wanted to pay everything, the RBI caps a CIC's dividend at 60% of its standalone profit. Religare passes on nothing because it is not allowed to.
  • Tax. Dividends are taxed in shareholders' hands; any stake sale would attract 12.5% long-term capital-gains tax; and since October 2024 buybacks are taxed as dividends, which removes the cleanest discount-closing tool.
  • Head-office cost. Trivial at CFHL (₹3.89 crore a year). Material at Religare (about ₹50 crore of expenses and a ₹34 crore standalone loss in FY26), which capitalised at eight times is worth about ₹270 crore of NAV (indicative).

In plain terms

CFHL "earns" ₹2,441 crore a year on paper, but its own bank account receives about ₹90 crore and pays out about ₹24 crore. A shareholder who buys CFHL for income is buying a 0.1% yield. A shareholder who buys it for value is betting that the paper earnings will one day turn into something they can collect, through a higher share price, a merger or a distribution. Until that happens, the market charges a large discount for the wait.

The holdco's "price" is therefore the discount, and that price moves. It narrows when investors expect an unlock (a listing, a merger, a large special dividend) and when markets are buoyant, and widens in drawdowns, when the jewel needs capital, or when a regulator closes a door. In 2026 all three widening forces arrived together for these two companies: a repo-rate hike on 7 October that hit lender valuations, a CIFC capital raise on the horizon, and the RBI's refusal of Religare's scheme in August.

02

Size and growth: a ₹47,618 crore revenue line that is really a lender and two insurers

Consolidated, the two holdcos look like large, fast-growing financial companies. That is because they consolidate their jewels in full.

On a consolidated basis CFHL and Religare reported combined FY26 revenue of ₹47,618 crore, up 17.3% on FY25, and combined profit including minorities of ₹5,558 crore, up 12.9%. Those numbers are real but misleading as a measure of "the sub-sector". Almost all of CFHL's revenue is CIFC's interest income and Chola MS's premium income, consolidated line by line because CFHL treats both as subsidiaries under Ind AS (it consolidates CIFC on de facto control even though the Murugappa group's combined stake in CIFC has been below 50% since December 2024). Between 85% and 94% of Religare's revenue is Care Health's net premium. Strip out the jewels and the holdco layer is tiny: combined standalone income of about ₹106 crore and a combined standalone profit of about ₹36 crore in FY26.

Quarterly figures sharpen the picture. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) CFHL's consolidated revenue rose 19.6% to ₹11,114 crore and group profit 42% to ₹1,789 crore; attributable profit was about ₹806 crore against ₹579 crore a year earlier, up 39%. Religare's revenue rose 26.4% to ₹2,353 crore, but it reported a consolidated loss of ₹47 crore against a ₹8 crore profit, while Care's own Ind AS profit rose to ₹122 crore from ₹76 crore. Added together, the two holdcos' Q1 FY27 revenue was ₹13,467 crore (up 20.7%) and their profit including minorities ₹1,742 crore (up 37.4%).

In plain terms

The sub-sector is essentially CFHL: it accounts for 82% of combined revenue, 97% of attributable profit, 77.5% of market value and 84% of base-case NAV. Religare matters for a different reason. It is the swing factor in valuation, because the price of Care Health inside it is the only market signal of what a fast-growing private health insurer is worth before listing.

03

The businesses underneath: trucks, motor policies and retail health

A holdco's demand drivers are its jewels' demand drivers. Here that means the vehicle cycle, a soft general-insurance market and a health-insurance boom triggered by a tax change.

Three industries sit under these two wrappers. The largest by value is retail non-bank credit, through CIFC. India's NBFC retail loan book is expected by ICRA to cross ₹30 lakh crore by FY27, growing 16–18% a year; at ₹2.54 lakh crore CIFC holds roughly 8–9% of it (indicative). The second is general insurance, through Chola MS: an industry that wrote ₹3,36,123 crore of gross direct premium (GDPI) in FY26, up 9.25%. The third is standalone health insurance, through Care: a ₹45,866 crore segment in FY26 that grew 30.7% in the first half of FY27.

The vehicle cycle drives CIFC's largest book and Chola MS's largest line. In Q1 FY27 industry volumes were strong: heavy commercial vehicles up 13% (their best-ever first quarter), light CVs up 18%, small CVs up 31%, cars and utility vehicles up 26% (a record first quarter), two-wheelers up 20% and tractors up 19%. GST 2.0, which from 22 September 2025 cut the rate on small cars and two-wheelers from 28% to 18%, is a large part of that. CIFC's vehicle-finance disbursements rose 21% to ₹16,503 crore and the segment's pre-tax profit rose 50% to ₹945 crore. Chola MS should have been a beneficiary too, through motor insurance on new vehicles; as Part 11 shows, it largely was not.

General insurance is the most mature of the three markets and, in 2025–26, the weakest. Industry GDPI grew 9.25% in FY26 and 8.9% in the first half of FY27, but the mix underneath is volatile. Health, at 40.8% of FY26 premium, is the largest and fastest-growing line. Motor (32.2%) has grown with vehicle sales. Fire fell 26.6% in H1 FY27 because insurers cut prices to win renewals after IRDAI removed tariff floors on fire and engineering covers in 2024, and crop insurance (through the state-owned AIC) more than halved. Industry combined ratios, the sum of claims and expenses as a share of premium, deteriorated from about 112.6% in FY25 to about 117.8% in FY26 according to Chola MS's management, with motor at about 128%. That is a soft market: insurers are competing on price and losing money on underwriting, relying on investment income to make a profit.

Health insurance after GST 2.0 is the fastest-moving demand driver in the sub-sector. From 22 September 2025, premiums on individual health and life policies were exempted from the 18% GST they had carried, an effective price cut of about 15% for buyers (indicative: 1 ÷ 1.18). Insurers lost input-tax credit on their own costs, which raised expenses by an estimated 3–5 percentage points, partly passed on. The demand response has been large and has not faded. Retail health premium across all insurers grew 32.0% in H1 FY27; standalone health insurers grew 30.7%; Care Health grew 42.2%, and its September 2026 premium, written in the month the exemption's first anniversary fell, was still up 44%. Care's managing director called it "a land-grab moment".

Claims inflation is the counter-force. Medical inflation runs at 12–14% a year, and claims frequency rises as more people are covered. Chola MS's loss ratio on health, travel and personal accident rose from 73.0% in FY25 to 88.0% in FY26 and 90.4% in Q1 FY27; it responded by cutting its health premium 27% in H1 FY27 to re-price group accounts. Care's Ind AS claims ratio was 68.2% in Q1 FY27. On motor, third-party premiums have not been raised for three to four years while court awards keep rising, and Chola MS has not yet provided for a pending Supreme Court ruling on compensation for homemakers in third-party claims.

Rates are the last driver and the newest. The RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, reversing part of the 2025 easing cycle. CIFC's CFO had budgeted for 25–50 basis points of hikes and expected cost of funds to rise about 10 basis points in the second half, with bank loans making up about half of borrowings. For the insurers, higher yields lift income on new investments but cause mark-to-market losses on existing bonds. For the holdcos themselves, higher discount rates usually widen holdco discounts, because a distant and uncertain unlock is worth less when money is dearer.

In plain terms

Under one roof, CFHL owns a lender enjoying a vehicle boom and an insurer struggling in a price war. Under the other, Religare owns a health insurer riding a tax cut. The holdco price is a weighted bet on these three cycles, minus a discount, and the three cycles are not moving together.

04

Where ₹100 goes: three money machines and one rationed input

A lender keeps about ₹17 of each ₹100 of revenue, a soft-market general insurer under ₹4 of each ₹100 of premium. At the holdco layer, the scarce input is capital.

The supply side of a holding company is simple: its only input is capital already sunk into the stakes. CFHL's three stakes cost ₹1,276 crore and are worth about ₹61,300 crore at today's prices (indicative); Religare's investments cost ₹2,485 crore. What matters is the cost structure of the jewels, because that decides how much profit there is to look through to, and the capital each jewel needs to keep growing, because that decides whether money flows up or down.

A vehicle and small-business lender such as CIFC borrows wholesale from banks, bond markets, commercial paper, external commercial borrowings and securitisation at about 8–8.5%, and lends at 14–20%. Of each ₹100 of FY26 revenue, about ₹46 went to its lenders as interest, ₹19 to staff, branches, technology and collections, ₹12–13 to loan losses and provisions, ₹1 to depreciation and ₹6 to tax, leaving about ₹17 as profit (indicative composite from CIFC's FY26 consolidated accounts: revenue ₹31,120 crore, interest ₹14,384 crore, PAT ₹5,233 crore). The two variables that management controls most directly are operating cost and credit cost. Interest cost is largely set by the market and the RBI.

The lender keeps a sixth of what it earns. The general insurer keeps almost nothing from insuring and lives off the interest on money it holds for policyholders. Capital is the holdco's "capacity"

Manufacturers have plants and utilisation rates. A financial group's equivalent is regulatory capital: each operating company must hold a minimum amount of equity against the risk it takes, and growth uses that headroom up. The capital sits where it is not needed: Religare Finvest has a capital ratio of 238% on a ₹53 crore loan book, and CFHL's CIC ratio is twenty-three times the minimum. Which inputs drive the cost of that capital? For CIFC, the main one is the cost of borrowing: its ratings are AA+ across India Ratings, CARE and ICRA, and it held ₹22,765 crore of cash and liquid assets plus undrawn lines, ₹23,984 crore of total liquidity, in June 2026. For the insurers, it is claims inflation (medical costs rising 12–14% a year; motor repair costs; third-party court awards), reinsurance pricing (capacity "robust" at the start of FY27, according to Chola MS, partly because new reinsurers are "burning capital" to buy market share), and distribution costs, which IRDAI caps through an overall expenses-of-management (EoM) limit of 30% of gross premium for general insurers and 35% for standalone health insurers. IRDAI's 23 September 2026 consultation proposes to lower those limits, which Part 07 discusses.

The holdco layer has one more cost: the cost of holding capital that does nothing. CFHL keeps about ₹110 crore in bank deposits; Religare Finvest keeps about ₹609 crore of cash against almost no lending. Idle cash at a holdco earns a deposit rate while shareholders value the holdco on the jewel's much higher return on equity. It is a small drag at CFHL and a larger one at Religare, where about ₹900 crore of the group's capital sits in a lender with no loan book.

The listed holdcos are only one of several owners in each operating company. The others decide much of what the holdco can do.

India has a handful of listed financial holding companies, but only two in the NSE 750 universe are classified as such. Others sit elsewhere in our library: Bajaj Holdings & Investment and Tata Investment Corporation are covered in the investment companies report, and Bajaj Finserv, Aditya Birla Capital and Max Financial are operating-company holdcos rather than CICs in the narrow sense. Chola MS: a 60:40 joint venture with MS&AD

Chola MS General Insurance was formed in 2001 as a joint venture with Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance, part of Japan's MS&AD group, which describes itself as the world's eighth-largest insurance group and number one in ASEAN. MSI holds 11,95,22,280 of Chola MS's 29,88,05,141 shares (40%) and CFHL holds the other 60%. Two things about this ownership matter for CFHL. First, IRDAI's rules require an Indian promoter to remain "fit and proper", and CRISIL lists "reduction in ownership by the group below a majority holding" as a rating sensitivity for Chola MS. Second, the Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025, which received assent on 20 December 2025 and mostly took effect on 5 February 2026, raised the foreign-investment cap in insurance from 74% to 100%. MSI could now legally buy control. If it ever did, it would put a transaction price on 60% of an asset that CFHL's shareholders currently have to value by assumption.

In its market, Chola MS is mid-table. It wrote 2.31% of industry GDPI in FY26 and 2.06% in H1 FY27, and 5.25% of motor premium in FY26 (fifth in motor) falling to 4.86% in H1 FY27. The private multi-line leaders are ICICI Lombard, Bajaj General, Tata AIG, HDFC Ergo, Go Digit, SBI General, IFFCO-Tokio and Reliance General, with the four state-owned insurers holding about 30% of the market. Our general insurance report covers the competitive landscape in full.

Four ways to hold a jewel, and what each one is worth

A listed jewel, an unlisted jewel, a joint-venture insurer and a set of operating stubs each call for a different valuation method and attract a different discount.

Financial holdcos come in recognisable types. The type decides how to value the holdco, which number to watch, and how wide a discount is "normal". CFHL is mostly the first type with a little of the third; Religare is mostly the second with a meaningful amount of the fourth.

07

Regulators hold the keys: RBI, IRDAI and the tax code

Every route by which a holdco's discount could close, whether a payout, a merger, a demerger or a listing, needs at least one regulator's consent. Several are capped by rule.

Both members are registered with the RBI as Core Investment Companies. Their subsidiaries include an Upper Layer NBFC (CIFC), a middle-layer investment-and-credit NBFC (Religare Finvest), a housing finance company (Religare Housing), a stockbroker and two insurers regulated by IRDAI. That gives three regulators a say over almost any change, and in 2026 two of them acted in ways that shaped this sub-sector.

RBI: the Core Investment Company rulebook

The RBI's Master Direction on Core Investment Companies (2016, updated 1 August 2025) sets the frame. A CIC is a non-bank whose assets are at least ₹100 crore (alone or with group CICs) and which raises or holds public funds. At least 90% of its net assets must be in group companies, with at least 60% in their equity; it may not trade those investments except by block sale. Its adjusted net worth (ANW), which is owned funds plus 50% of the unrealised appreciation on quoted investments, must be at least 30% of risk-weighted assets, and outside liabilities may not exceed 2.5 times ANW. Since August 2020 a group may have no more than two layers of CICs (paragraph 7). Any change of control, any shareholding change of 26% or more and any change of more than 30% of the board needs prior RBI approval (paragraph 27).

Two rules bear directly on the discount. Paragraph 21A caps a CIC's dividend at 60% of its net profit (10% if it is not fully compliant). For CFHL, whose standalone profit was ₹70.41 crore in FY26, that means a maximum payout of about ₹42 crore, or ₹2.25 a share: a 0.17% yield at ₹1,316, against ₹1.30 actually paid. And the two-layer rule means the Murugappa structure, with Ambadi Investments above CFHL, is already at the limit: no further CIC can be inserted, and any simplification has to remove a layer rather than add one.

Religare faces an extra, company-specific restriction. In a letter of 5 April 2019, reiterated on 19 December 2019, the RBI advised Religare to stop paying dividends "till further orders", in the context of the 2016–18 diversion of funds through Religare Finvest's corporate loan book under the previous promoters. The FY26 annual report says that instruction is still in force. Religare Finvest's own Corrective Action Plan, imposed in January 2018, was lifted only on 23 July 2025.

In plain terms

A CIC cannot pay out more than 60% of its own small profit, and Religare cannot pay anything at all. So even a holdco board that wanted to close the discount by handing cash to shareholders could not do it at scale. The rulebook was written to stop CICs becoming leveraged shadow banks, and it does that well; a side effect is that it also stops them being efficient conduits of cash.

RBI: the jewel and the schemes

CIFC is on the RBI's Upper Layer list under scale-based regulation, which brings bank-like rules: a minimum common equity tier-1 ratio of 9%, large-exposure limits and a mandatory listing (already met). Its asset-quality reporting is shaped by the RBI's November 2021 clarification that a loan cannot be upgraded from non-performing until all arrears are cleared, which is why CIFC shows a higher RBI-basis gross NPA (4.50%) than its Ind AS Stage-3 ratio (3.29%). The RBI's November 2023 increase in risk weights on bank lending to NBFCs and on unsecured consumer credit, partly reversed from April 2025, touched CIFC's ₹15,884 crore consumer and small-enterprise book.

The decisive RBI power in 2026 was its approval of schemes of arrangement involving NBFCs and CICs. Religare's board approved a demerger on 14 February 2026; the stock exchanges issued their observation letters on 7 July; and in letters of 6 August (to Religare) and 7 August (to Religare Finvest) the RBI said the request "could not be acceded to", giving no reasons. Religare's shares fell 8.2% to ₹235.40 on 7 August. Part 10 examines what this blocks.

IRDAI: owning an insurer, accounting for it, and merging into it

IRDAI's rules govern who may own an insurer and how its profit is counted. Anyone holding 10% or more, or exercising control, is a "promoter" and must be approved; transfers above set thresholds need approval too. IRDAI's amendment regulations of 30 July 2026 (the Registration, Capital Structure, Transfer of Shares and Amalgamation of Insurers Amendment) reset those thresholds to 5, 10, 25, 50 and 75%, and treat passive dilution in a rights issue as a transfer. More importantly for this report, they allow a non-insurance company to amalgamate into an insurer only if it holds more than 50% of that insurer and has no other non-insurance business on the date of application, with consideration paid only in equity and a fee of ₹10 lakh per entity. Religare meets the first test (63.2% of Care) and fails the second (it still owns a lender, a broker and a housing financier).

On accounting, three IRDAI decisions overlap. From 1 October 2024 multi-year premiums must be recognised 1/n per year, depressing reported premium and profit for fast-growing health insurers through FY25–FY27. From 1 April 2026 insurers moved to Ind AS 117, but both Chola MS and Care took a one-year forbearance and will report under Ind AS only from 1 April 2027. And IRDAI's 2023 expenses-of-management regulations replaced line-by-line commission caps with an overall cap (30% of gross premium for general insurers, 35% for standalone health insurers), which is why Chola MS's reported commissions jump between FY23 and FY24 in its five-year table.

On 23 September 2026 IRDAI published a consultation paper titled "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution", with comments due by 25 October 2026. As reported, it proposes to phase general insurers' EoM limit down to 20% of GDPI over five years, cut health new-business commissions from about 40% to 15–20% and motor own-damage commissions from 16% to about 5%, and ban "dark patterns" in online selling. That would help Chola MS's and Care's expense ratios, and cut fee income at distributors, including CIFC, which received ₹256.6 crore of commission from Chola MS in FY26.

SEBI and tax

SEBI's takeover code made the Burman family make an open offer at ₹235 a share in September 2023 when they crossed 25% of Religare; it was resisted by the board of the time and completed on 13 February 2025, with change of control recorded on 18 February 2025 at 25.16%. Schemes such as Religare's demerger need stock-exchange observation letters before court and regulatory approval. On tax: demergers that meet section 2(19AA) of the Income Tax Act are tax-neutral, and Religare's scheme was drafted to comply; dividends are taxed in shareholders' hands, with section 80M letting a holdco deduct dividends it passes on so that tax does not cascade; long-term capital gains on any stake sale are taxed at 12.5% (since the July 2024 Budget); and since 1 October 2024 buybacks are taxed as deemed dividends in shareholders' hands, which removes the buyback as a cheap way to close a discount.

In plain terms

For CFHL the rules make the structure stable: it cannot pay much out, cannot add layers and has no reason to change. For Religare the rules make the structure stuck: it wants to change, has a route in principle, and needs the RBI to say yes to the first step. In both cases the most important decision-maker for the discount sits outside the company.
