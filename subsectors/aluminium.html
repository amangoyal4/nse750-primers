00

The one-page view: three businesses priced off one exchange

Two listed companies, a world-class cost position, and earnings set by forces that sit in London, Tokyo, Beijing, the Gulf and Washington.

Aluminium in the NSE 750 is only two stocks, but they hold three very different businesses. NALCO (National Aluminium Company) is a government-controlled, debt-free producer that digs bauxite in Odisha, refines it into alumina, smelts part of that alumina into metal with its own coal-fired power and exports the rest. Hindalco does the same upstream at roughly three times NALCO's metal scale, and then adds two things NALCO does not have: Novelis, the world's largest maker of rolled aluminium sheet and its largest recycler, which sells mostly to American and European can and car makers, and Birla Copper, India's largest custom copper smelter. Together the two companies earned ₹2,92,787 crore of revenue in FY26 and ₹19,188 crore of reported profit after tax, and they were worth ₹2,56,583 crore at the 30 October 2026 close.

The single most important fact about this sub-sector is that neither company sets the price of what it sells. Primary aluminium is priced off the London Metal Exchange (LME) cash price plus a regional delivery premium, and converted into rupees at whatever the exchange rate happens to be. An Indian smelter with captive coal and power produces a tonne of hot metal for about $1,700–1,950. When the LME averaged $2,202 a tonne in FY24, that left Hindalco's Indian upstream business $822 of EBITDA per tonne. When it averaged $3,577 in Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), the same business earned a record $2,331 a tonne, a 55% margin. The plants did not change; the price did. That is why NALCO's operating margin has ranged from 6% (FY20) to 51% (Q1 FY27) within six years.

The report is built around five forces that explain almost every number in it:

  • The price stack. LME plus a regional premium plus the rupee. In 2026 the US Midwest premium hit a record of about $1.19 a pound (roughly $2,620 a tonne on top of LME, per press reports) because of a 50% US tariff, and the Japanese premium went from $195 to $395 and back to $255 a tonne as Gulf supply vanished and returned. None of this is controlled in India (Sections 03 and 07).
  • The cost curve. Alumina, power and coal decide who survives a low price. Captive bauxite, alumina, coal and power are the moat; NALCO sits near the bottom of the world alumina cost curve, and the 2024–25 alumina spike turned that into a windfall (Section 04).
  • Novelis. A conversion-spread and recycling business, not a bet on the LME. It carried Hindalco through two mill fires, a $5 billion greenfield plant in Alabama and US tariffs, and it is the reason Hindalco's balance sheet is levered while its Indian business is minting cash (Section 11).
  • Supply has stalled while India's demand gap widens. China's 45 million-tonne cap has been reached, the West Asia war knocked out a slice of Gulf smelting, and India used 6.12 million tonnes of aluminium in FY26 against 4.26 million tonnes of primary output, filling the gap with 2.03 million tonnes of imported scrap and with metal imports (Sections 02, 06 and 07).
  • Carbon. India's smelters run on coal and emit about 19 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent for every tonne of metal at Hindalco. Europe's carbon border tax entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026, and India's own carbon market has started setting targets for aluminium units (Section 13).

In plain terms

India makes aluminium about as cheaply as anyone in the world, because its smelters sit on their own coal and run their own power stations. But the metal is sold at a world price. When that price is high, as it was in the first half of 2026, the profits are enormous; when it falls, they shrink fast. Hindalco softens the swing with a large business that rolls metal into sheet for a fee (Novelis) and a copper smelter, but that fee business has had a bad two years and has borrowed heavily. NALCO has no such cushion and no debt: it is the purest Indian play on the metal and alumina prices.

01

From red earth to rolled sheet: how the metal is made and where money is made

Six tonnes of ore, two tonnes of alumina and a small town's electricity make one tonne of aluminium. The money sits where the scarce input is owned.

Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the earth's crust, but it never occurs as metal. It is locked in bauxite, a reddish clay-like ore that is roughly 45–50% alumina (aluminium oxide) in the East Coast deposits of Odisha and Andhra Pradesh. Getting the metal out takes two industrial steps, each with its own chemistry, capital and cost structure, and this two-step chain explains most of the industry's economics.

The first step is the Bayer refinery. Bauxite is crushed and digested in hot caustic soda, which dissolves the alumina and leaves behind a red, alkaline waste called red mud or bauxite residue (about 1–1.5 tonnes for every tonne of alumina). The dissolved alumina is precipitated as hydrate and then calcined (roasted) into a white powder, calcined alumina. In India it takes roughly 2.6–3.4 tonnes of bauxite to make a tonne of alumina; NALCO dug 77.0 lakh tonnes of bauxite in FY26 and produced 22.75 lakh tonnes of calcined alumina, a ratio of about 3.4, partly because of stock build and moisture. A refinery's main costs are bauxite (mined captive, with a royalty linked to the LME), caustic soda, and the coal or fuel oil that raises steam and fires the calciners.

The second step is the Hall–Héroult smelter. Alumina is dissolved in a molten fluoride bath inside long rows of electrolytic cells called pots, and a huge direct current splits it into aluminium and oxygen. The oxygen burns away the carbon anodes (made of calcined petroleum coke and coal-tar pitch), which is why smelters consume about 0.4–0.45 tonnes of anode carbon per tonne of metal. Each tonne of aluminium needs about 1.93 tonnes of alumina and 13,000–15,000 kilowatt-hours of electricity; NALCO's 6,953 million units of net generation for 4.72 lakh tonnes of cast metal works out to about 14,700 kWh a tonne. That is why smelters are, at heart, power plants with a metal attached. In India they are coal power plants: about 10–11 tonnes of thermal coal are burned for every tonne of aluminium.

The ratios matter because they tell you where the margin sits. The rule of thumb in the industry is roughly 4–6 : 2 : 1 from bauxite to alumina to metal by weight, or simply "2 to 1" from alumina to aluminium. A company that owns the bauxite, the refinery, the coal and the power station pays cost at every step. A smelter that buys alumina on the open market and power from the grid pays somebody else's margin at every step, and is the first to close when prices fall. In India all three primary producers (VAML, Hindalco and NALCO) are integrated to a high degree, which is the foundation of their cost advantage.

After the smelter, value is added by casting and conversion. Liquid metal is cast into ingots, sows and T-ingots for sale, into wire rod (the single largest Indian product, used for overhead conductors and cables), into billets for extrusion presses (windows, façades, rail coaches, solar frames, battery enclosures) and into slabs for rolling mills. Rolling mills turn slabs, and increasingly scrap, into flat-rolled products: beverage-can sheet, car body sheet, aerospace plate, building sheet and foil. This is Novelis's business, and Hindalco's Indian downstream business at Hirakud, Belur, Taloja, Mouda and the new Aditya FRP mill.

Bars are not additive: they are per tonne of different products. A tonne of rolled sheet at Novelis earns about a quarter of what a tonne of smelted metal earned at Hindalco in Q1 FY27, but it earns it in every year, not only when LME is high.

Four patterns stand out. First, the integrated smelter with captive power captures what the industry calls the metal rent, the gap between the realised price and the cash cost; in Q1 FY27 that was 45–55% of revenue. Second, the merchant alumina refiner earns a spread that is normally thin (around $50–150 a tonne) but can explode, as it did in late 2024 when alumina briefly exceeded $700–780 a tonne. Third, converters such as Novelis earn a steadier $450–600 a tonne regardless of the metal price, because the metal is passed through to the customer. Fourth, the custom copper smelter has almost lost its fee: the 2026 treatment and refining charge benchmark is zero, so Hindalco's copper business now lives on by-products such as sulphuric acid and on premiums for copper rod.

In plain terms

Making aluminium is two factories in a row: one turns red ore into white powder, the next turns the powder into metal using a staggering amount of electricity. Whoever owns the ore, the power station and the coal keeps the profit from every step. Hindalco and NALCO own nearly all of it, which is why they make money even when prices are low. The businesses that roll the metal into sheet earn a fee instead, smaller but steadier.

One more structural point matters for reading the accounts: Hindalco's segments trade with each other. The upstream business sells metal to the downstream business, and Novelis is consolidated in rupees from US dollar accounts prepared under US accounting rules. Adding up Hindalco's FY26 segment revenues (Novelis about ₹1.62 lakh crore at ~₹88 to the dollar, copper ₹69,838 crore, upstream ₹41,447 crore, downstream ₹15,938 crore) gives about ₹2.89 lakh crore, against consolidated revenue of ₹2,74,944 crore. The ~₹14,000 crore difference is inter-segment elimination. Segment revenues are never additive.

02

A 6-million-tonne appetite: India's demand and the gap it imports

Consumption has grown about 12% a year since FY22; primary output about 1.5%. Scrap and imports fill the difference.

India is the world's second-largest producer of primary aluminium after China, with about 5.8% of world output, yet it does not make enough metal for itself. In FY26 the country produced 42.57 lakh tonnes (4.26 million tonnes) of primary aluminium, up only 1.4% from 42.00 lakh tonnes, according to the Ministry of Mines. Apparent consumption, counting primary metal, imports and scrap, was 6.12 million tonnes, according to the Ministry of Mines series quoted in NALCO's Q1 FY27 investor deck. Consumption was 3.90 million tonnes in FY22, so it has grown at about 12% a year for four years while primary output grew at about 1.5% a year. Hindalco's own estimate of Indian demand, on a slightly different basis, is about 1.49 million tonnes for Q1 FY27 alone, up 3% year on year, or roughly 6 million tonnes annualised. The two sources broadly agree, and both are well above the 4.5–5 million tonne figure that industry bodies used until recently.

Scrap is the biggest single import. India imported 2.03 million tonnes of aluminium scrap in FY26, up 12%, worth about $4.55 billion, according to DGCI&S data. That is equivalent to roughly 45–50% of India's primary production by weight. The secondary industry, hundreds of remelters clustered around Jamnagar, Chennai, Delhi NCR and Pune, is essentially an importer of American, Gulf and British scrap, and it competes with primary metal in castings, alloys and extrusions. The 2026 closure of the Strait of Hormuz disrupted part of this flow, because the UAE and Saudi Arabia together supplied about 18% of the tonnage.

What drives the demand? NALCO's Q1 FY27 deck gives the end-use split shown below. Nearly half the metal goes into electrical uses: overhead conductors, power cables, transformer windings and busbars. That is the direct link to the transmission build-out covered in the power transmission report and the conductor makers in the cables report. The second driver is copper substitution. With copper near $14,000 a tonne and aluminium near $3,000, a ratio of about 4.5 times and a decade high, cable and conductor makers switch to aluminium wherever codes allow; Indian copper demand fell 9% year on year in Q1 FY27, as the copper report describes. Aluminium carries about half the conductivity of copper per unit of cross-section but at less than a third of the weight, so for overhead lines it has always been the metal of choice; the price gap is now pushing it further into building wire and low-voltage cables.

The rest of the demand story is about intensity. India's per-capita use is still only about 3 kg a year against roughly 12 kg for the world, according to the Aluminium Association of India (an industry-body figure, indicative). An Indian car carries about 40–50 kg of aluminium against 160–200 kg in global averages (industry estimate, indicative). Electric vehicles use roughly twice as much aluminium as combustion cars because battery packs and lightweight bodies need it. Solar module frames, beverage cans (Indian can-sheet supply is short and imports are rising) and Vande Bharat aluminium rail coaches all add new tonnes. NITI Aayog projects Indian aluminium demand to grow at about 6–8% a year to 2030, a more conservative path than the Aluminium Association of India's ambition of 10 million tonnes by 2030.

There is a near-term wrinkle. Hindalco's Q1 FY27 call noted that electrical demand was weak in the quarter because contractors deferred projects while metal prices were volatile, even as demand for flat-rolled products grew more than 10%. Domestic producers are leaning into the home market anyway: NALCO sold 97% of its metal in India in FY26 (4.61 of 4.74 lakh tonnes) and 99% in Q1 FY27, against a historical 30–40% export share, and it lifted its domestic premium from about $60 to about $110 a tonne in its latest tender.

In plain terms

India needs roughly 6 million tonnes of aluminium a year and its three smelters make about 4.3 million. The gap is filled mostly with imported scrap and imported metal, and the gap is growing because demand is rising about eight times faster than smelting capacity. That gives domestic producers a large home market to sell into, and it explains why they are fighting over import duties (Section 08) and why all three are building new capacity (Section 06).

03

The price nobody in India sets: LME, premia and the rupee

A tonne sold in Odisha is priced off a London screen, adjusted by a premium set in Tokyo or Chicago, and converted at the day's exchange rate.

Every tonne of primary aluminium sold by Hindalco, NALCO or VAML is priced as the sum of three parts. The first is the LME cash price, the benchmark for standard P1020 ingot traded on the London Metal Exchange. The second is a regional premium, the extra a buyer pays to have duty-paid metal delivered in a particular market: the US Midwest premium, the Rotterdam premium in Europe, the quarterly Main Japanese Port (MJP) premium in Asia, and in India a domestic premium that producers set from their own tenders. The third is a product or value-added premium for wire rod, billets, slabs and alloys rather than plain ingot. The total is then converted into rupees at the exchange rate. Indian producers control none of the first two, some of the third, and none of the fourth.

Why did the price rise so far? The supply side stopped growing (Section 07): China's output reached its government cap, the West Asia war knocked out Gulf smelters from March 2026, and exchange inventories drained. LME warehouse stocks averaged about 237,000 tonnes in October 2026, against 499,000 tonnes in January 2026 and 1.65 million tonnes in July 2020. Low visible stocks make the price more sensitive to any disruption. Both managements now expect it to settle lower: NALCO's view is about $3,000–3,100 for FY27 once the war premium fades (raised to $3,200–3,300 for calendar 2026 in its Q1 deck), and Hindalco regards about $3,200 as a fair FY28 price once Gulf smelters return.

Premia became almost as important as the LME in 2026, and they moved for reasons that had nothing to do with Indian demand. The US Midwest premium went above $1 a pound for the first time on 23 January 2026 and reached a record of about $1.19 a pound (roughly $2,620 a tonne) in June 2026, before easing to $1.09 a pound ($2,403 a tonne) on 11 September. The cause was the US Section 232 tariff on aluminium, raised to 50% from 4 June 2025: the premium has to rise until imported metal, duty paid, competes with domestic supply. The Japanese MJP premium, settled quarterly, went from $195 a tonne in Q1 2026 to $350–353 in Q2 and $395 in Q3 as Gulf metal could not reach Japan and Korea, then fell 35% to $255 for Q4 (settled 13 October 2026), the first sign that the Gulf disruption is unwinding. The Rotterdam duty-paid premium was about $518–560 a tonne in June.

In plain terms

An Indian aluminium company's selling price is decided by a London exchange, a premium negotiated in Japan or the US, and the rupee. In plain terms

The US tariff is a strange gift. It does not touch Indian smelters directly, because India exports little primary metal to America, but it lifts the price Americans pay for every tonne, which keeps world prices firmer and lets Novelis pass higher premiums through. The cost curve is the moat: alumina, power and coal Deep dive

Price is set abroad; cost is set at home. Captive bauxite, alumina, coal and power put India's three producers in the cheapest third of the world, and NALCO's refinery near the very bottom.

If the price is out of the producer's hands, the only lasting competitive advantage is cost. The world's smelters can be lined up from cheapest to dearest by their cash cost per tonne, a ranking the industry calls the cost curve. When demand falls and the LME drops, the most expensive smelters lose money first and eventually close; the cheapest keep earning. The LME, over a full cycle, tends to be pulled toward the cost of the marginal producer near the top of the curve. A producer's position on the curve therefore decides two things at once: how much it earns at a given price, and whether it survives a bust.

Three inputs dominate a smelter's cash cost: alumina (about 1.93 tonnes per tonne of metal), power (about 14,000–15,000 kWh) and carbon (anodes made from petroleum coke and pitch). In Indian conditions power is about 31–40% of smelting cost (NALCO's Director (Finance) put it at about 31% of its ₹1.56 lakh per tonne FY26 metal cost), alumina about 25–30% if valued at cost, and carbon about 15–18%. Alumina: the second commodity

Alumina is unusual because it is both an input and a product. For a smelter that buys it, it is the largest raw-material cost. For a refinery that sells it, it is revenue. NALCO is both: its Damanjodi refinery produced 22.75 lakh tonnes of calcined alumina in FY26, of which about 9.2 lakh tonnes went to its own smelter and 14.46 lakh tonnes were sold, 13.08 lakh tonnes of it exported through Visakhapatnam. Hindalco is "net long" about 800,000 tonnes a year after feeding its own pots, selling about 138,000 tonnes in Q1 FY27 and guiding to about 190,000 in Q2. VAML has moved from buying alumina to being self-sufficient as its Lanjigarh refinery has expanded.

The alumina price, quoted free on board (FOB) in Western Australia, normally trades at roughly 15–18% of the LME price, though contract linkage has recently fallen to 11–11.5%. In 2024 it broke away. Bauxite export disruptions in Guinea, the closure of Alcoa's Kwinana refinery in Australia and outages at Indian and Chinese refineries tightened the market at the same time. Platts averaged $507 in Q3 2024; CRU's index hit $702.50 on 31 October 2024, the highest in more than 35 years; Platts quoted $765 on 14 November; and press reports put spot above $780 in mid-December. At the peak, alumina was about 30% of the LME price, roughly double its normal share. Then supply responded. Global alumina output reached a record 36.3 million tonnes in Q3 2025, and by Q1 2026 Platts averaged only $307.

Power and coal: the cost you can control

Indian smelters are almost entirely powered by captive coal-fired plants. NALCO runs a 1,200 MW captive power plant at Angul; Hindalco has Renusagar (about 827 MW) and roughly 900 MW each at Aditya and Mahan, among others. That is the core of the cost advantage: Indian grid power for industry costs far more per unit than the ₹3.15 per kWh NALCO reports, and a smelter on grid power would be uncompetitive. It is also the core of the carbon problem (Section 13).

Captive power is only as cheap as the coal that feeds it. NALCO needs about 7.2 million tonnes of coal a year. Its captive Utkal D and E blocks supplied 4.0 million tonnes in FY26, up 42%, and are targeted at 4.8 million tonnes in FY27 once an expanded environmental clearance arrives. The rest, about 2.5–2.7 million tonnes, comes on linkage from Mahanadi Coalfields, with 10–15% bought at e-auction. The landed cost difference is material: about ₹1,500 a tonne for captive coal, ₹1,800 for linkage and ₹2,100 for e-auction. At roughly 10–11 tonnes of coal per tonne of aluminium, moving one million tonnes of NALCO's coal from linkage to captive saves about ₹30 crore a year; moving it from e-auction to captive saves about ₹60 crore.

In plain terms

In plain terms

When NALCO's new refinery stream reaches full output in FY28, the alumina side of the business becomes as important as the metal side. A small move in the alumina price will then matter more to NALCO's profit than a large move in the aluminium price.

The fourth archetype, the non-integrated smelter, does not exist among the Indian members, but it matters because it sets the price floor. A Gulf or Chinese smelter that buys alumina at market and power at a contract price is the marginal producer; when the LME drops toward its cost, it curtails, and supply falls until the price recovers. India's producers earn the difference between that marginal cost and their own. When alumina spiked in 2024, the marginal cost rose sharply, which helped lift the LME, while NALCO, Hindalco and VAML paid their own lower cost. That combination, a higher world price set by squeezed buyers of alumina and an unchanged domestic cost, is the clearest illustration of why integration is the moat.

That is why NALCO, with a fraction of Hindalco's revenue, earns almost a third of the two companies' combined profit, and why judging the businesses by margin alone misleads.

Three smelters and a crowd: industry structure

Primary aluminium is a three-company oligopoly; downstream and recycling are crowded, fragmented and exposed to imports.

India's primary aluminium industry is one of the most concentrated in the economy. Three companies make every tonne. In FY26, according to the Ministry of Mines monthly summary, the country produced 42.57 lakh tonnes: Vedanta's Jharsuguda smelters about 18.57 lakh tonnes and BALCO 5.92 lakh tonnes, together about 24.49 lakh tonnes (57.5%) for what is now Vedanta Aluminium Metal (VAML); Hindalco's Renukoot, Hirakud, Mahan and Aditya smelters 13.36 lakh tonnes (31.4%); and NALCO's Angul smelter 4.72 lakh tonnes (11.1%). Installed capacity was 43.03 lakh tonnes a year, so the industry is running flat out; NALCO's smelter produced 103% of its 4.60 lakh tonne nameplate.

Downstream is a different world. In flat-rolled products Hindalco is the largest Indian producer, with roughly 40% of the market (an industry estimate) and a new 170,000-tonne Aditya FRP mill ramping up. Extrusions are crowded with private players, the largest being the unlisted Jindal Aluminium. Foil makers include Hindalco, SRF's Altech business and smaller firms, and they compete with Chinese imports that are restrained by anti-dumping duties (Section 08). Secondary aluminium, made from scrap, is fragmented into hundreds of remelters. No multinational owns an Indian smelter; global players reach India only through trade.

China is the shadow over all of it. It produces about 60% of the world's primary aluminium and is the largest exporter of semi-finished products such as foil, sheet and extrusions. Its removal of the 13% VAT export rebate on aluminium semis from 1 December 2024 reduced its semis exports, but Indian downstream makers still compete with Chinese and ASEAN product at home. Indian producers' defence has shifted from tariffs, which the 1 February 2026 Budget left unchanged, to quality rules: the BIS Quality Control Order for aluminium products, whose large-firm compliance date is 1 December 2026.

In plain terms

Only three companies smelt aluminium in India: VAML (the Vedanta group's aluminium arm), Hindalco and NALCO, in roughly a 57 : 31 : 11 split. Because the plants are already running above capacity and demand is growing fast, all three are building more. The real competition is not between them but with imported scrap, imported metal and Chinese semi-finished products, which is why the industry's lobbying focuses on duties and quality rules.

Concentration has consequences for pricing behaviour. Indian producers set their domestic premiums from their own export tenders rather than competing them away, and NALCO's lift from about $60 to about $110 a tonne happened in a market where imports set the ceiling: a domestic buyer can always import primary metal at LME plus the Asian premium plus 7.5% duty and freight. That ceiling is the real link between trade policy and producer margins, and the reason a change in the import duty is worth tens of dollars a tonne to every Indian smelter.

07

Supply has stalled: China's ceiling and the Gulf shock Deep dive

World output grew only about 1% in 2025. China hit the cap it set itself, and a war took out part of the Gulf, the one region that had been adding cheap tonnes.

For two decades the aluminium market had one dominant supply story: China. Chinese output went from a few million tonnes in the early 2000s to more than 40 million tonnes, close to 60% of the world, built mostly on coal power and cheap capital. Every rally in the LME was eventually capped by new Chinese pots. That story has ended, and the end of it is the single biggest reason prices were so strong in 2025–26.

In 2017, as part of its supply-side reform, Beijing set a ceiling of about 45 million tonnes on the country's primary aluminium capacity. For years the cap was not binding because output was below it. It is now. China's National Bureau of Statistics put 2025 output at 45.02 million tonnes, up 2.4% and slightly above the cap; the International Aluminium Institute's estimate, on a different basis, is 44.2 million tonnes. Output for January–September 2026 was 35.03 million tonnes, up 3.8%, an annualised pace of about 46.7 million tonnes, with a record month of 3.98 million tonnes in August 2026. These figures come from press summaries of the official data and should be treated as INDICATIVE, but the direction is clear: China is now running at or slightly above its ceiling, with growth coming from restarts, efficiency and relocation of capacity to hydro-rich Yunnan rather than from new permits. The government's Action Plan for 2025–27 keeps the cap in place.

The Gulf: a cheap-power hub caught in a war

The Gulf Cooperation Council countries were the industry's other growth engine. Abundant cheap gas let the UAE, Bahrain, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and Oman build some of the world's largest and lowest-cost smelters; the region makes about 6 million tonnes, roughly 9% of world capacity and about 23% of capacity outside China. Almost all of it is exported, and much of that leaves through the Strait of Hormuz.

The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 hit this hub directly. Hormuz was effectively closed in March and April, until a ceasefire on 7 April, and talks on transit fees were still running in June and July. Bahrain's Alba, one of the largest single-site smelters in the world, shut potlines 1–3 (about 19% of its capacity) from 15 March. EGA's Al Taweelah smelter in the UAE, the country's largest, was hit in an Iranian attack on 28 March 2026, went into full emergency shutdown and declared force majeure on 12 April. Qatar's Qatalum, a Hydro joint venture, moved from full shutdown to about 60% of capacity, keeping pots warm because a frozen potline can take six to twelve months to restart. By late August about 25% of Al Taweelah had been restarted (later reports suggest about 50%), with full output targeted for Q1 2027; in September Alba was running lines 4–6 (about 1.3 million tonnes a year) and exporting through Jeddah and Sohar, with lines 1–3 still idle. The shock rippled into the two Indian members in four ways. First, it lifted the LME and, more sharply, the Asian premium: Gulf metal normally supplies Japan and Korea, so the MJP doubled to $395 for Q3 2026. Hindalco's export mix (23–27% of upstream volume) and its downstream pass-through both benefited. The supply picture is now turning. China's cap is leaking rather than holding firm, with output annualising above 46 million tonnes; Indonesian smelters are ramping; and Gulf capacity should be largely back by early 2027. NALCO's deck puts global visible inventory at only about 0.26 million tonnes but expects the market to balance in 2027. Both managements guide LME at roughly $3,000–3,300, below the Q1 FY27 average of $3,577. The right reading of the record Q1 FY27 margins is therefore a peak created by a temporary supply shock on top of a structural slowdown, not a new run-rate. The structural part (China's cap) supports prices above the old $2,200–2,500 range; the temporary part (the Gulf) is fading.

In plain terms

For twenty years China kept adding smelters and kept a lid on prices. It has now stopped, by its own rule, at about 45 million tonnes. Then a war knocked out a big chunk of the Gulf's smelting just as stocks were low, and prices jumped. The Gulf plants are coming back and China is quietly producing a little above its cap, so the jump is fading. But the end of China's expansion is permanent, which is the main reason prices are unlikely to return to the lows of 2020 or even 2023.

In plain terms

The war helped NALCO's metal and hurt its alumina at the same time: Gulf smelters that could no longer ship metal also stopped buying NALCO's alumina. Hindalco, which sells mostly metal and rolled products and only a little alumina, gained more cleanly.

08

The rulebook: duties, quality orders, US tariffs and PSU rules

India protects its smelters less than it protects most industries; the fight is over scrap. The US protects its own market with a 50% tariff, which Novelis feels.

Policy touches aluminium at five points: import duties, quality and trade-defence rules, mining and royalty law, US trade policy (through Novelis), and the government's role as NALCO's controlling shareholder. Carbon regulation is large enough to have its own section (Section 13).

Import duties: the scrap fight

India levies a basic customs duty of 7.5% on primary aluminium, 7.5–10% on downstream products and only 2.5% on aluminium scrap. Copper scrap has carried no duty since 1 February 2025, so aluminium scrap is the last major non-ferrous scrap still dutiable. Primary producers argue that cheap scrap imports undercut them. The Aluminium Association of India and the Federation of Indian Mineral Industries have both asked for a uniform 15% duty on primary and downstream products, and higher duty on scrap. The recyclers, represented by the Material Recycling Association of India, asked the Prime Minister's Office in July 2026 to remove the 2.5% scrap duty altogether, because their raw material is overwhelmingly imported. The Union Budget of 1 February 2026 left aluminium duties unchanged. The next decision point is the Budget of 1 February 2027.

Why does a duty matter so much? Because the import parity price sets the ceiling on the domestic premium. If a buyer in Pune can import ingot at LME plus the Asian premium, plus 7.5% duty and freight, an Indian producer can charge roughly up to that level. At an LME of $3,000, each percentage point of duty is worth about $30 a tonne of potential domestic premium. Raising the duty from 7.5% to 15% could, in principle, allow domestic premia to rise by up to about $225 a tonne; in practice competition among the three producers and scrap substitution would capture only part of it (an illustrative calculation). For NALCO, with about 4.6 lakh tonnes of domestic metal sales, every $10 a tonne of domestic premium is worth roughly ₹45 crore a year.

Quality orders and anti-dumping

With tariffs unchanged, the operative barrier is now quality regulation. The Aluminium and Aluminium Alloy Products (Quality Control) Order, 2026 (S.O. 1319(E)), dated 11 March and published 13 March 2026, requires BIS certification for a list of aluminium products, with staggered compliance dates: 1 December 2026 for large enterprises, 1 March 2027 for small and 1 June 2027 for micro enterprises, varying by product (dates from secondary sources). Non-certified imports, often Chinese semis, are blocked. Separately, anti-dumping duties on aluminium foil from China, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand have been extended to 15 December 2026 pending a sunset review requested by Hindalco and SRF Altech, and a fresh probe into Chinese foil was reported in June 2026.

The US: Section 232 at 50%

The US Section 232 national-security tariff on aluminium was introduced at 10% in 2018, raised to 25% under Proclamation 10895 (10 February 2025, effective 12 March 2025) and to 50% from 12:01 a.m. on 4 June 2025 under the proclamation of 3 June 2025, covering primary metal, semis and a long list of derivative products, with the UK treated separately. On top of that, a 10% Section 301 tariff on Indian goods has applied since 24 July 2026 and is under legal challenge. India's direct exports of aluminium to the US are small, so the main exposure is through Novelis, which imports hot band and coil into its US plants from its Canadian and Brazilian mills. Tariffs cost Novelis $143 million of adjusted EBITDA in FY26 and $70 million in Q1 FY27 alone (against $24 million in Q4 FY26). Management says "some level" will persist for a couple of quarters while supply chains are rearranged, falling to nearly nil as Oswego and Bay Minette reduce import needs. Separately, US Customs has required Novelis to deposit about $114 million (₹1,066 crore) against a claim that certain Brazilian shipments attract anti-dumping duty and additional tariffs; Novelis is appealing and treats the loss as remote.

NALCO is a Navratna central public-sector enterprise under the Ministry of Mines, with the Government of India holding 51.28% since March 2021. 09

Six decades of cycles: booms, busts and defining bets

Every big decision in Indian aluminium was made at a turning point in the price, and the industry's history is a record of who was early and who was late.

Indian aluminium began as a state-led industry built for self-reliance. Hindalco was founded in 1958 by the Birla group, with its Renukoot smelter fed by the captive Renusagar power station, a model that the whole industry later copied. BALCO was set up as a public-sector company in 1965. NALCO was incorporated on 7 January 1981 and commissioned its Panchpatmali mine, Damanjodi refinery and Angul smelter between 1985 and 1987 with French Pechiney technology; it listed in 1992 and became a Navratna in 2008. In 2001 Sterlite, the Vedanta group's Indian company, bought 51% of BALCO in one of the first big privatisations.

The defining bet came in 2007, when Hindalco bought Novelis for about $6 billion, then among the largest overseas acquisitions by an Indian company, close to the top of the cycle. The 2008–09 crash followed almost immediately: the LME fell from about $3,300 in July 2008 to about $1,300 by February 2009, Novelis lost heavily and Hindalco raised equity. The next wave, from 2010 to 2016, was India's own smelter build-out (Hindalco's Mahan and Aditya, Vedanta's Jharsuguda expansions and the Utkal alumina refinery), and it collided with Chinese overcapacity that pushed the LME to roughly $1,450–1,500 in 2015. Vedanta booked an operating loss of ₹18,479 crore in FY16; NALCO's profit fell to ₹787 crore.

Three lessons from the history are worth carrying into the rest of the report. First, capacity decisions are made at the top and commissioned at the bottom: India's 2010–16 smelters were planned in the 2007–08 boom and arrived into the 2015 glut. The current wave (Aditya, BALCO, NALCO's Angul expansion, Bay Minette) is being planned at record margins; the test is what LME they meet in FY28–FY31. Second, balance sheets decide who survives a bust: Hindalco had to raise equity in 2009 because of the Novelis debt, and its leverage is rising again now. Third, the profit pool moves between links of the chain: the metal in 2021–22, alumina in 2024, premia in 2025–26. A producer that owns more links is exposed to more of these windfalls and fewer of the squeezes.

In plain terms

Aluminium booms and busts roughly every three to five years. The Indian companies have learned the hard way that the time they feel richest is when they commit to new plants, which then open into a glut. Today they are again at record profits and again building. The difference this time is that China has stopped adding capacity, which may make the next downturn shallower than 2015 or 2020.

Reading an aluminium company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about an aluminium producer. Most are per tonne, most are in dollars, and almost every one has a definition trap.

An aluminium producer's profit can be written as one equation. For an integrated primary producer such as NALCO or Hindalco's Indian upstream business, EBITDA equals the tonnes of metal sold multiplied by the gap between the realised price (LME plus regional premium plus product premium) and the cash cost of making a tonne, plus the tonnes of alumina sold multiplied by the gap between the alumina price and the alumina cash cost, plus smaller items such as surplus power. Everything is then converted into rupees. A converter such as Novelis has a different equation: shipments multiplied by a conversion margin per tonne, with the metal passed through. The KPIs in this section are simply the terms of these two equations, measured so they can be compared over time and across companies.

1. LME realisation and premia

What it is. The price actually received per tonne of metal, compared with the LME average for the same period. The difference is the premium captured: regional premium, product premium for wire rod, billets and alloys, and any value-added primary products. Formula. Realisation per tonne = segment revenue ÷ tonnes shipped; premium captured = realisation − LME average. Worked example. Hindalco's India upstream revenue in Q1 FY27 was ₹13,403 crore on 335 kt shipped, about ₹4.0 lakh a tonne or roughly $4,234 at ₹94.5, against an LME average of $3,577: about $657 a tonne above LME. In Q4 FY26 the same calculation gives about ₹3.37 lakh ($3,682 at ₹91.5) against LME $3,195, about $487 above. What drives it. Regional premia (the MJP for exports), the domestic premium set in tenders, the share of value-added products and, at Hindalco, external alumina sales, which are included in upstream revenue. Benchmark. A good integrated producer captures more than $400 a tonne over LME in a tight market; $150–400 is average; below $150 is weak. How the members compare. Hindalco captured about $657 in Q1 FY27; NALCO's aluminium segment earned about ₹2.73 lakh a tonne in FY26 (₹12,945 crore on 4.74 lakh t), about $3,100 at ₹88, roughly $330 above the FY26 LME of $2,771; Alcoa, a US reference, realised $4,527 in Q3 2026 against an LME of $3,227, because it sells into the Midwest premium. The trap. Upstream revenue per tonne is not a metal price. Hindalco's includes alumina sales and value-added products, and NALCO's segment revenue includes rolled products and wire rod. Compare like with like over time, not across companies.

2. Alumina cost and the alumina-to-LME ratio

What it is. For a smelter, the cost of the 1.93 tonnes of alumina in each tonne of metal; for a refiner, the cash cost of making a tonne of alumina. The alumina-to-LME ratio expresses the alumina price as a percentage of the metal price. Formula. Alumina cost per tonne of metal = 1.93 × alumina price (or cost); ratio = alumina price ÷ LME. Worked example. At $350 alumina, a smelter buying at market spends about $676 per tonne of metal on alumina. At the late-2024 spike of about $780, it spent about $1,505, an extra $830 a tonne. NALCO, at a cash cost of about ₹21,000 (about $222 at ₹94.5), spends about $429. What drives it. Bauxite supply (Guinea, Australia), refinery outages, caustic soda and energy prices, and, on the price side, the balance between new Indonesian and Indian refineries and smelter demand. Benchmark. Alumina cash cost below $250 a tonne is good, $250–330 average, above $330 weak; for the ratio, the long-run norm is about 15–18% of LME, contract linkage has fallen to 11–11.5%, and anything near 30% signals a squeeze on non-integrated smelters. How the members compare. NALCO's ₹20,000–22,000 a tonne is among the lowest in the world; Hindalco does not disclose its alumina cost but is net long about 800 kt; VAML is now roughly self-sufficient. The trap. Alumina is a product for NALCO and an input for everyone else. A high alumina price is good news for NALCO and mildly good for Hindalco (net long), but bad for non-integrated smelters. And NALCO transfers alumina to its own smelter at roughly cost, so its segment report shifts profit from alumina to metal.

4. Power and coal per tonne

What it is. The electricity consumed per tonne of metal (kWh/t), and the cost of that power per unit and per tonne. Formula. kWh/t = net power used by the smelter ÷ tonnes of metal; power cost per tonne = kWh/t × cost per kWh; coal per tonne = kWh/t × coal burned per kWh (about 0.7–0.8 kg for Indian grades). Worked example. NALCO's 6,953 million units of net generation for 4.72 lakh tonnes of metal is about 14,700 kWh a tonne. At ₹3.15 a unit, that is about ₹46,300 a tonne, close to the ~31% share of its ₹1.56 lakh cost that management cites. At the same consumption, power at ₹4.50 a unit would cost ₹66,150 a tonne, and at an industrial grid tariff of about ₹7 (indicative) ₹1.03 lakh, which would wipe out much of the margin. What drives it. Pot technology (modern AP36/AP40 or EGA DX cells use less), pot-line stability, power-plant heat rate and plant load factor, coal quality and the coal mix (captive about ₹1,500 a tonne landed, linkage ₹1,800, e-auction ₹2,100). Benchmark. Below 14,000 kWh a tonne (AC, all-in) is good, 14,000–15,000 average, above 15,000 weak; captive coal share above 75% is good, 40–75% average. How the members compare. NALCO about 14,700 kWh a tonne and about 57% captive coal; Hindalco does not disclose kWh/t but runs newer pot lines at Aditya and Mahan and is adding captive mines. The trap. Smelter (DC) consumption is lower than all-in (AC) consumption including rectifier losses and auxiliaries; and "power cost" quoted per unit may exclude the capital cost of the captive plant, which sits in depreciation.

12. Dividend payout and return on capital employed

What it is. ROCE measures the operating return on all the capital in the business; payout measures how much of the profit is returned to shareholders. Formula. ROCE = EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), screener basis; payout = dividends declared for the year ÷ PAT. Worked example. NALCO declared ₹11.50 a share for FY26 on 183.66 crore shares: ₹2,112 crore, or 36% of consolidated PAT of ₹5,797 crore; its ROCE was 39.6%. Hindalco paid ₹5 a share (about ₹1,123 crore, 8% payout) with a ROCE of 13.2%. Together the two paid about ₹3,235 crore, 16.9% of combined FY26 PAT. What drives it. For ROCE, the metal price (NALCO's ROCE ranged from 14% in FY23 to 44% in FY25) and the size of unproductive CWIP; for payout, policy (the DIPAM rule for CPSEs of at least 30% of PAT or 4% of net worth) and capex needs. Benchmark. Through-cycle ROCE above 18% is good, 10–18% average, below 10% weak; for a PSU, payout above 40% is good, 30–40% average. How the members compare. NALCO's ROCE has been 34%, 14%, 17%, 44% and 40% from FY22 to FY26; Hindalco's 17%, 11%, 11%, 15% and 13%. NALCO's FY26 segment note shows ROCE of 59% in chemicals and 158% in aluminium. The trap. NALCO's segment ROCE is inflated because alumina moves to the smelter at cost and ₹9,053 crore of unallocated capital, mostly cash, is excluded. Hindalco's ROCE is depressed by ₹49,526 crore of CWIP and a fire year at Novelis.

13. Carbon intensity

What it is. Greenhouse-gas emissions per tonne of aluminium, usually scope 1 (direct, from anodes and process gases) plus scope 2 (from electricity). Formula. tCO₂e emitted ÷ tonnes of metal. Worked example. Hindalco reports 19.0 tCO₂e a tonne for Q1 FY27 (19.4, 19.5, 19.1 and 18.8 across the four quarters of FY26). At an EU carbon price of about €80 a tonne, that footprint would be worth about €1,500 a tonne of metal if all of it were priced, against a direct-only footprint of roughly 2 tonnes, worth about €160 (ILLUSTRATIVE). What drives it. Above all, the power source: coal-fired power produces most of the footprint; hydro-based smelters emit about 4 tonnes. Then anode efficiency and PFC control. Benchmark. Below 4 is "low-carbon" and good, 4–12 average, above 12 weak; Indian coal-based metal is 16–20. How the members compare. Hindalco 19.0; NALCO does not publish a comparable quarterly figure but is coal-based like Hindalco. The trap. The EU's carbon border tax currently prices only direct emissions for aluminium, so today's bill is small; the 19 tonnes matters through customers' procurement standards and any future widening of the tax. 16. Definition traps, collected

  • Three Hindalco EBITDAs. Company consolidated EBITDA was ₹38,097 crore in FY26 (includes other income, before fire exceptionals); screener's operating profit was ₹34,880 crore (excludes other income, which was −₹4,074 crore because screener books fire losses there); Novelis adjusted EBITDA was $1,645 million under US accounting. Quote the basis every time.
  • Reported vs pre-exceptional PAT. Hindalco's FY26 PAT was ₹13,391 crore reported and ₹18,733 crore before exceptional items. Q1 FY27's ₹7,013 crore is after a −₹2,299 crore exceptional.
  • Novelis $462 or $500? Both are right: $462 reported, $500 excluding Oswego, tariffs and the Sierre flood.
  • Oswego is three numbers. $925 million of FY26 losses net of property insurance; $100–150 million inside adjusted EBITDA; and a cash hit of $1.4 billion net of $300 million recovered so far, with an ultimate net cash cost of about $600 million expected (indicative, depends on insurance).
  • Upstream EBITDA per tonne is not LME minus cost. It includes alumina sales, value-added products and captive power.
  • Cost of production bases differ. Hot-metal cost at Hindalco and VAML in dollars, NALCO's metal cost in rupees; inclusion of alumina, royalty and overheads varies.
  • NALCO's EBITDA includes other income. Its ₹8,613 crore FY26 EBITDA = revenue ₹17,843 crore + ₹666 crore non-operating income − ₹9,896 crore opex. Screener's operating profit is ₹7,928 crore (44.4%).
  • NALCO standalone vs consolidated. FY26 PAT ₹5,816 crore standalone, ₹5,797 crore consolidated (JV losses).
  • Alumina is a product and an input. High alumina prices help NALCO and hurt non-integrated smelters; NALCO's segment report transfers alumina at cost.
  • Capacity, production, cast metal and sales differ. NALCO's refinery nameplate is 21 lakh tonnes, but it produced 22.75 lakh tonnes of calcined alumina in FY26.
  • Segment revenues are not additive. Hindalco's segment sum exceeds consolidated revenue by about ₹14,000 crore of eliminations.
  • Screener's P/E for Hindalco strips exceptionals. It shows 9.55×; m-cap ÷ trailing reported PAT is 12.1×.
  • Net debt bases. Use company net debt (Hindalco ₹64,841 crore at Mar-26; NALCO net cash ₹8,350 crore), not screener's borrowings-minus-investments.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in aluminium has two or three versions. Hindalco has three EBITDAs and two profits; Novelis has two dollars-per-tonne figures; NALCO's EBITDA includes interest on its cash. The safest habit is to ask, every time, "on which basis?" and to compare a company only with itself unless the bases match.

17. How to read an aluminium company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the LME and premia for the quarter. Write down the LME average (Q1 FY27 $3,577; Q2 FY27 $3,227), the MJP, the Midwest premium and the rupee. These explain most of the change before you open the results.
  2. Pull metal and alumina volumes. Tonnes shipped, cast and sold; alumina produced and sold. Volumes at both members are near nameplate, so a big swing usually means an outage or inventory change. Red flag: falling volumes without an explanation.
  3. Read the cost of production. Hindalco gives it in dollars on the call; NALCO in rupees. Compare with guidance (Hindalco guided +5–6% QoQ for Q2 FY27). Red flag: cost rising faster than guidance two quarters running.
  4. Compute or read EBITDA per tonne. Hindalco upstream, downstream, copper and Novelis each report it. Ask how much of the change is price and how much is cost. Red flag: EBITDA per tonne falling while the LME is flat.
  5. Check hedges and one-offs. Hedge losses or gains (Hindalco 29% of FY27 at $3,004), inventory revaluation and inter-segment eliminations (−₹778 crore in Q1 FY27), and "unallocable and GAAP adjustments" (+₹2,286 crore in Q1 FY27). Red flag: a quarter that beats only because of these lines.
  6. Separate reported from pre-exceptional PAT. Note the exceptional item and its cash effect. Red flag: recurring "exceptional" charges.
  7. At Hindalco, read Novelis separately. Shipments, adjusted EBITDA per tonne, tariff costs, fire recoveries, free cash flow and leverage (4.5× at June). Red flag: EBITDA per tonne below $500 in H2 FY27, or leverage not falling by Q4 FY27.
  8. Check net debt and capex. Hindalco's net debt (₹77,495 crore at June) and ratio (1.95×); NALCO's cash pile and project spend. Red flag: Hindalco above the 2× ceiling.
  9. Listen for project dates. Bay Minette hot mill; Aditya Phase 1 first metal; NALCO 5th stream first alumina, Pottangi road, smelter board approval. Red flag: a date slipping by more than a quarter.
  10. Finish with guidance. LME view (both managements guide about $3,000–3,300), cost guidance, alumina price view and dividend (NALCO's interim dividend decision on 2 November). Then compare the quarter's annualised EBITDA with the market value: at the 30 October close the combined EV/EBITDA was 6.7× on FY26.
In plain terms

To read an aluminium result quickly: note the metal price for the quarter, check how many tonnes were made and what each tonne cost, look at the profit per tonne, strip out hedges and one-offs, and at Hindalco look separately at Novelis's fee per tonne and its debt. If profit per tonne moved roughly in line with the metal price minus cost inflation, the quarter was ordinary; if not, find the line that explains it.

Novelis: a converter, not a bet on the metal Deep dive

Two fires, a $5 billion greenfield mill and a 50% US tariff turned the world's largest aluminium roller and recycler from Hindalco's stabiliser into its balance-sheet problem. The recovery is now under way.

Novelis is the largest single business inside the two members by revenue. It shipped 3,557 thousand tonnes of flat-rolled aluminium in FY26 and earned net sales of $18.4 billion, roughly ₹1.62 lakh crore at ₹88 to the dollar, more than half of Hindalco's consolidated revenue. It makes beverage-can sheet (the largest end market, around 55–60% of shipments on the company's historical disclosures), automotive body sheet (about 20%), aerospace plate and specialty products, from mills in North America, South America, Europe and Asia. It also describes itself as the world's largest aluminium recycler: recycled content was 61% of its input in FY26 (63% in FY24), with about 2.16 million tonnes of scrap bought or tolled, up from 33% in FY11. Hindalco bought it in 2007 for about $6 billion and added the US company Aleris for about $2.8 billion in 2020.

The first thing to understand is that Novelis is not a bet on the LME. It buys primary metal and scrap at LME plus a regional premium, rolls it, and sells sheet at the metal cost plus a conversion premium, with contracts that pass the metal price through. Its profit is the conversion margin per tonne, measured as adjusted EBITDA per tonne, which has ranged between about $460 and $560 for five years. High metal prices inflate its revenue and shrink its percentage margin without changing the dollars it earns per tonne. What does move its earnings is utilisation of its mills, the mix of products, the gap between scrap and primary prices (the cheaper scrap is, the more it earns on its recycled content), tariffs on the metal it imports into the US, and timing effects when metal prices move faster than its pass-through.

Copper: Hindalco's third leg, where the fee went to zero

Birla Copper serves more than half of India's copper needs, but the treatment charges that used to pay a custom smelter have collapsed. By-products now carry the business.

Hindalco's third business is Birla Copper at Dahej in Gujarat, a custom smelter with about 500,000 tonnes a year of cathode capacity, a continuous-cast rod mill, and plants for sulphuric acid, phosphoric acid and DAP fertiliser and precious-metal recovery. In FY26 it sold 487,000 tonnes of copper metal and 373,000 tonnes of continuous-cast rod, earning revenue of ₹69,838 crore. Hindalco says it serves more than half the country's copper requirement. The copper report covers the Indian copper chain in full; this section explains what the business contributes to Hindalco.

A custom smelter does not own mines. It buys copper concentrate from miners in Chile, Peru, Australia and elsewhere, and is paid a treatment and refining charge (TC/RC), a fee per tonne of concentrate and per pound of payable copper, for turning it into cathode. It also earns "free metal" (copper recovered above the payable percentage), by-product revenue from sulphuric acid, fertiliser, gold and silver, and a premium on rod over cathode. For years the TC/RC was the backbone: around $80 a tonne and 8 cents a pound in normal years, worth roughly $300 per tonne of copper (indicative). In 2025–26 global smelting capacity, especially in China, grew much faster than mine supply, and smelters bid fees down to win concentrate. Carbon and CBAM: the coming tax on coal-based metal Deep dive

Indian aluminium is among the cheapest in the world and among the dirtiest. Europe's border tax prices only a sliver of that footprint today; the risk is that the sliver grows.

Aluminium is one of the most carbon-intensive materials in common use, and where it is made matters more than how. A smelter running on hydropower in Norway, Canada or Iceland emits about 4 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per tonne of metal, mostly from the carbon anodes and the alumina refinery. A smelter running on coal emits 16–20 tonnes, because roughly 14,000–15,000 kWh of coal-fired electricity goes into every tonne. Hindalco reports 19.0 tCO₂e a tonne for its aluminium in Q1 FY27; NALCO and VAML are coal-based in the same way. The cost advantage of captive coal power and the carbon disadvantage are the same fact seen from two sides.

How CBAM works for aluminium

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026 for aluminium, steel and other products. Importers into the EU must buy certificates covering the embedded emissions of the goods they import, at a price linked to the EU carbon market; the charge phases in as EU producers' free allowances phase out, from about 2.5% of the full charge in 2026 to 100% by 2034 (an indicative schedule). Crucially for India, aluminium is in the part of CBAM's annex that counts direct emissions only. The indirect emissions from electricity, which are 80% or more of an Indian smelter's footprint, are not priced today. Whether captive power counts as indirect has not been tested in an EU ruling that the dossier could fetch, so treat that point as the prevailing reading rather than settled law.

Where a producer cannot document its actual emissions, CBAM applies default values. Under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621, India's default for unwrought aluminium (CN 7601) is 1.87 tCO₂e a tonne, or 2.06 with the 2026 mark-up; bars and rods (7604) 3.41; plates and sheets (7606) 4.13. The mark-ups rise from 10% in 2026 to 20% in 2027 and 30% from 2028, to push exporters to report real data. Certificate prices have been reported at about €75.36 for Q1 2026, €75.28 for Q2 and €82.32 for Q3 (press summaries, INDICATIVE); first certificate purchases are due in February 2027.

The practical effect today is small for the members' own exports. Novelis, with 61% recycled content, sells some of the lowest-carbon sheet in the industry, because remelting scrap uses about 5% of the energy of primary smelting.

India's own carbon market

India is building a domestic system that may eventually matter more than CBAM. The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS), under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022, covers nine energy-intensive sectors, including aluminium. Greenhouse-gas emission-intensity target rules notified on 8 October 2025 set unit-level targets for 13 aluminium smelters and refineries, on an FY24 baseline, for FY26 and FY27; an amendment effective 13 January 2026 added sectors. Units that beat their targets earn tradable credits; units that miss must buy them. The unit-level targets for Hindalco and NALCO were not available for this report; a draft example for one Vedanta smelter showed a path from 13.49 to 13.23 to 12.83 tCO₂e a tonne (a different boundary from Hindalco's 19.0), implying cuts of about 2–3% a year (INDICATIVE). The EU's draft rules of May 2026 on deducting a carbon price already paid abroad list the UK, Chinese and Californian schemes but not India's CCTS, so Indian exporters cannot yet offset domestic carbon costs against CBAM.

The responses so far

Indian producers have three levers, none cheap. The first is round-the-clock renewable power: solar and wind with storage or hydro firming, supplying a smelter's flat 24-hour load. Hindalco runs a 65 MW RTC renewable project at Aditya, which it describes as the only operational captive RTC renewable plant in India; it holds 26% of an Ayana SPV (188 MW solar plus 146 MW wind) that supplies 100 MW of RTC power to one smelter, and a 100 MW RTC hybrid at Mahan. Its renewable capacity was 470 MW at the end of Q1 FY27, targeted at 884 MW plus 125 MW of RTC power by the end of FY27. That is still a small fraction of the roughly 2,200 MW of continuous load its Indian smelters draw (an estimate from 13.4 lakh tonnes at ~14,700 kWh a tonne). The second lever is recycling, which Hindalco is expanding in both aluminium and copper. The third is process efficiency: lower anode consumption, PFC control and better pot technology.

NALCO's choices point the other way for now. Its 0.5 million tonne Angul smelter expansion will be powered by a new 1,080 MW coal-fired captive plant built with NLC India, commissioning around 2030–31. That locks in coal-based emissions for decades, at the moment CBAM's mark-ups and India's CCTS targets are tightening. NALCO does have 198 MW of wind capacity (307 million units in FY26) and is piloting gallium recovery from red mud, but its metal will remain coal-based. Longer term, inert-anode technology (the ELYSIS joint venture of Alcoa and Rio Tinto, and Rusal's work) would remove direct CO₂ from electrolysis, but commercial scale is not expected before about 2030, and it does nothing about the power.

In plain terms

Indian aluminium is cheap because it is made with coal power, and dirty for the same reason. Europe's new border tax counts only the small direct emissions from the smelting pots, not the coal power, so it costs Indian exporters very little in 2026. The danger is a future change that counts the power too, which would add something like €1,500 a tonne at today's carbon price, and customers who simply refuse coal-based metal.

In plain terms

Hindalco is buying renewable power and recycling more scrap to bring its footprint down. NALCO's next smelter will run on a new coal plant. Both choices make sense on today's costs; they will look very different if carbon prices rise.

Hindalco's group dot sits in the middle only because it averages two very different businesses: an Indian upstream earning 55% margins and Novelis, with a 9% margin and 4.5× leverage, in the opposite corner. The second map shows where the combined profit pool really is. Hindalco's Indian upstream alone earned ₹18,884 crore of EBITDA in FY26 at a 46% margin, more than NALCO's two segments combined. Novelis is the other large pool, earning about ₹14,500 crore at a 9% margin. NALCO's aluminium segment (₹5,564 crore of EBIT at 43%) and alumina segment (₹2,034 crore at 30%) are high-margin but smaller. Hindalco's copper (₹2,809 crore at 4.0%) and Indian downstream (₹978 crore at 6.1%) are small and thin, which is why management's "downstream 4× by FY30" ambition matters: it is an attempt to grow these small, thin-margin dots into a larger and steadier pool of conversion profit alongside the volatile upstream.

The third map is the cost-curve argument in one picture. The cheapest producers either own their alumina and coal power (NALCO, VAML, Hindalco) or have very cheap gas (the Gulf). The expensive ones buy alumina and run on dear power (many Chinese merchant smelters), and they set the price floor in a downturn. NALCO's position, top right, explains why it earned money even in FY20, when the LME was at $1,457: its operating profit that year was ₹487 crore, thin but positive. Hindalco sits slightly lower on cost, partly because its reported figure is in dollars at a quarter-end exchange rate and partly because its newer smelters carry higher fixed costs, but it is firmly in the low-cost half.

Who is winning, then? On the metal, NALCO and Hindalco's Indian upstream are both winning at current prices, with NALCO the more efficient converter of LME into profit and Hindalco the larger. On growth, Hindalco is ahead: Aditya adds 374,000 tonnes of metal and 850,000 tonnes of alumina by FY29, against NALCO's 1 million tonnes of alumina in FY28 and 500,000 tonnes of metal around 2031. On resilience, NALCO wins clearly, with net cash and no exposure to US tariffs or rolling-mill fires. On carbon, neither is winning.

In plain terms

NALCO is the most efficient and safest of the businesses here, but also the smallest and most exposed to the metal and alumina prices. Hindalco's Indian smelters are just as profitable at today's prices and are growing faster, but Hindalco also owns Novelis, which earns a thin fee on huge volumes and carries most of the group's debt.

In plain terms

NALCO has been earning 40–50% operating margins for two years straight, a level it last saw only briefly in 2022. Hindalco's reported profit has been bumpy because of the fire charges at Novelis, but its operating profit has climbed steadily and hit a record in April–June 2026.

The global lens: low-cost producers at commodity multiples

India is the world's second-largest primary producer and among its cheapest, yet its companies trade at the same six to seven times EBITDA as Western smelters.

Seen from outside, India's aluminium industry is a low-cost, coal-based, fast-growing producer in a market dominated by China. India made about 4.26 million tonnes of primary aluminium in FY26, about 5.8% of world output, second only to China's 45 million tonnes. Its three integrated producers sit in the first or second quartile of the cost curve alongside the Gulf smelters, below most Chinese and Western capacity. Yet the valuation gap one might expect is not there: global peers trade at roughly 4–7 times EBITDA (indicative ranges), and Hindalco (6.9×) and NALCO (5.7×) sit squarely inside that band. Both stocks have fallen 25–29% from their highs while earnings hit records. The market is discounting the fall in LME before it shows up in the results.

Technology and disruption: what could change the economics

The smelting process is 140 years old. What is changing is the power behind it, the share of scrap in the mix, and the products aluminium goes into.

Aluminium is not an industry where a new technology arrives every few years. The Hall–Héroult cell and the Bayer refinery both date from the 1880s. The changes that matter are slower and come from five directions.

Decarbonised power. For Indian smelters, the only realistic route to lower carbon is replacing captive coal power with round-the-clock renewable power, which means solar and wind plus storage or firming, contracted to match a smelter's flat 24-hour load. Hindalco is furthest along, with a 65 MW RTC plant at Aditya, 100 MW of RTC power from its Ayana stake and a 100 MW RTC hybrid at Mahan, and a target of 884 MW of renewables plus 125 MW RTC by the end of FY27. RTC renewable power currently costs more than captive coal, so every step trades margin for carbon. NALCO's new 1,080 MW plant is coal-based, a lock-in risk for the 2031 smelter.

Inert anodes. The ELYSIS joint venture of Alcoa and Rio Tinto, and Rusal separately, are developing inert anodes that emit oxygen instead of CO₂ from the pots. That would remove the direct emissions that CBAM currently prices. Commercial scale is not expected before about 2030, and it does nothing about coal power.

Recycling. Remelting scrap uses about 5% of the energy of primary smelting, and recycled metal is already about a third of global supply (an industry estimate). Novelis's 61% recycled content is the industry benchmark for flat-rolled products. In India, scrap imports have grown about 12% a year, and recycled metal competes with primary in castings, alloys and extrusions. Hindalco is building copper and e-waste recycling (50 kt, commissioning FY27) and aluminium recycling capacity; EPR rules from April 2026 and recycled-content mandates from FY28–FY29 should formalise the secondary industry. Over a decade, recycling will take a growing share of Indian demand growth that would otherwise have gone to primary producers.

New products. The fastest-growing uses are in energy transition: battery foil (Hindalco's Aditya battery-foil plant is commissioned and supplying global cell makers), battery enclosures (Hindalco's Chakan plant supplied about 10,000 enclosures for Mahindra's BE 6 and XEV 9e electric vehicles by Q4 FY25), lightweight car bodies, solar frames and conductors for the transmission build-out. Hindalco also commissioned coated air-conditioner fins at Taloja and inner-grooved copper tubes. These products earn conversion margins that are steadier than smelting margins.

Smelter and refinery technology. Newer pot technologies (AP36/AP40 at Hindalco's Aditya and Mahan, EGA's DX and DX+ for NALCO's expansion) use less power per tonne and run at higher amperage. NALCO is piloting gallium recovery from red mud, and Hindalco runs India's first bauxite-residue backfilling at Dalla in Uttar Pradesh. Operational risk is the dark side: Qatalum kept pots warm in 2026 because a frozen potline takes six to twelve months to restart, and Novelis's Oswego fires showed how much a single-site rolling-mill accident can cost.

Substitution. Aluminium is gaining from copper in cables, conductors and air-conditioner tubes while the copper-to-aluminium price ratio stays near 4.5 times. It is losing only marginally to plastics and glass in packaging, and competes with advanced high-strength steel and carbon fibre in cars; see the iron and steel report for the steel side of that contest. Smelters also depend on carbon anodes made from calcined petroleum coke and pitch, an input covered in the electrodes and refractories report.

In plain terms

The way aluminium is made will not change soon. What will change is the electricity behind it (more renewables, slowly and at a cost), the share of recycled metal (rising every year) and the products it goes into (batteries, electric cars, power lines). Indian producers that can sell low-carbon or value-added metal will earn steadier margins than those selling plain coal-based ingot.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian aluminium

The structural case is strong; the cyclical timing is not. Both can be true at once.

The bull and bear cases below apply to the sub-sector as a whole, with notes on where each member is more or less exposed. Neither is a forecast; each sets out what has to go right or wrong.

The bull case

  • Supply has stopped growing. China is at its 45 Mt cap, Gulf restarts only bring back lost tonnes, and LME stocks are about 237 kt. Prices are unlikely to revisit the $2,200 of FY24.
  • World-class costs. Captive bauxite, alumina, coal and power put both members in the first or second quartile; NALCO's alumina earns money where Western refiners break even.
  • A domestic market growing ~12% a year. India used 6.12 Mt in FY26 against 4.26 Mt of output, with copper substitution and the grid build-out adding tonnes.
  • Growth that is funded. NALCO's 5th stream is paid from cash; Hindalco's Aditya expansion adds 374 kt of metal and 850 kt of alumina by FY29.
  • Novelis turning. $563 a tonne in Q1 FY27, Oswego restarted, tariffs fading, positive FCF targeted in Q4 FY27 and Bay Minette shipping from Q1 FY28.
  • Modest valuations. 5.7–6.9× FY26 EBITDA, NALCO yielding 3.7% with net cash.

The bear case

  • Peak earnings. Q1 FY27 margins rested on a temporary Gulf shock; LME is already 17% below May's average and both managements guide $3,000–3,300.
  • Costs rising as prices fall. Hindalco guided +5–6% QoQ for Q2; NALCO's metal cost rose to ~₹1.70 lakh a tonne against a ₹1.60 lakh guidance.
  • Hindalco's leverage at the top of the cycle. 1.95× group, 4.5× Novelis, ₹49,526 Cr of CWIP and a Bay Minette whose cost doubled to ~$5 billion.
  • Alumina placement. NALCO adds 1 Mt of alumina in FY28 just as its Gulf customers (40–52% of exports) are impaired and Indonesia adds supply.
  • Carbon. 19 tCO₂e a tonne; CBAM mark-ups rising to 30% by 2028; NALCO's new smelter locked into coal.
  • Governance and policy. NALCO's board without independent directors for much of 2026; a possible government stake sale; Hindalco's new brand royalty; duty decisions in the 1 Feb 2027 Budget.

Which side has the better of it depends on the horizon. Over one to two years the bear case has more force, because the earnings of Q1 FY27 were a peak and Q2 and Q3 FY27 should show margin compression, with NALCO more exposed to the LME and Hindalco more exposed to Novelis's execution. Over five years the bull case looks stronger: the end of Chinese capacity growth, India's demand gap and the members' cost positions are structural. NALCO is the cleaner way to own the structural case but carries more cyclical earnings risk; Hindalco offers growth and a recovering Novelis but carries balance-sheet risk.

In plain terms

The long-term story for Indian aluminium is good: cheap production, a fast-growing home market and a world that has stopped adding smelters. The short-term story is less good: the metal price has already come off its peak and costs are rising, so the next few quarters' profits should be lower than the record April–June quarter.
