Indian diversified commercial services: six tolls on flows they don't own

A complete guide to a sub-sector that is really five businesses sharing one trick: a diamond grader, a visa-centre operator, a cash-logistics network, a card and statement bureau, a staffing company and the government's auctioneer. Each is paid a fee on a flow that belongs to someone else, and who sets that fee explains why operating margins run from 60% down to 2%, and why the market pays 25 times earnings for one toll and 11 times for another.

00

The one-page view: six companies, five businesses, one trick

Nothing ties these six together by product or customer. What ties them is the shape of the money: each takes a fee on a flow it does not own.

"Diversified commercial services" is a classification label, not an industry. Under it sit a diamond-grading laboratory (International Gemological Institute, IGI), a visa and consular outsourcer that works for about 46 governments (BLS International), a payment-card and secure-document bureau (Seshaasai Technologies), India's largest cash-logistics and ATM-services network (CMS Info Systems), the country's largest private employer of contract staff (Quess Corp) and a government-owned e-auction house (MSTC). They share no raw material, no customer and no cost structure. Read them as one "sector" and every average misleads you.

They do share one thing, and it is the organising idea of this report. Every one of them is paid per unit of somebody else's flow. IGI owns none of the diamonds it grades; it charges about ₹891 a report. BLS owns none of the visas; it charges a service fee per application that the client government caps. CMS owns none of the cash it moves; it charges per ATM, per pick-up point and per van run. Seshaasai owns none of the bank accounts behind the cards and statements it produces. Quess owns none of the wages it pays; it bills the wage plus a thin markup. MSTC owns none of the coal blocks, scrap or land it auctions; it takes a fraction of a per cent of the value sold. A "toll on a flow you don't own" is the business model of all six.

What separates them is who sets the size of the toll. Where the toll is set by trust that the buyer demands (IGI's grading report) or by a government mandate (MSTC is the designated disposal platform), operating margins are about 60%. Where it is set by a contract or tender with a powerful customer (foreign governments for BLS, banks for CMS and Seshaasai), margins sit at 24–28%. Where it is set by open competition for a commodity service (general staffing), the margin is about 2%. That ladder, not the industry label, is how to sort these companies.

The chart above holds the report's central puzzle. IGI earns 5% of the revenue and commands 39% of the market value. BLS earns 32% of the net profit (helped by a sub-10% tax rate on profits made abroad) and is valued at only 20%. Quess is the reverse of IGI. CMS, with 13% of the profit, has 8% of the value. The market is not paying for size. It is paying for how secure and how expandable each toll looks, and on 23 October 2026 it valued the six at prices ranging from 24.8 times trailing earnings (IGI) to 10.8 times (CMS).

In plain terms

These six companies are toll-booth operators on six different roads. IGI's road is the flow of diamonds from Surat's polishing units to the world's jewellery counters; BLS's is the flow of travellers needing visas; CMS's is the flow of banknotes to ATMs and from shops back to banks; Seshaasai's is the flow of bank cards and statements to customers; Quess's is the flow of wages to contract workers; MSTC's is the flow of government-owned assets to buyers. None of them owns the traffic. Their profits depend on how many vehicles pass, how much each one pays, and above all on who is allowed to set the price at the booth.

The four forces that define the group

Four forces shape the economics of these six companies, and each gets its own deep dive below.

  • The lab-grown grading boom (IGI). Lab-grown diamond (LGD) prices have collapsed, from about $1,502 per carat wholesale in 2019 to about $100 in 2025–26 for a common one-carat stone, and cheap stones mean more stones and more reports. IGI graded 12.8 million reports in calendar 2025 and 4.35 million in July–September 2026 alone (+26%). The open question is how far a ₹891 fee can fall as a share of a falling stone price.
  • The government as customer. BLS's customers are foreign governments and India's Ministry of External Affairs (MEA). In October 2025 the MEA debarred BLS from new tenders for two years; the Delhi High Court set the order aside in December. MSTC's owner is the government and its flows are mandated by law. One policy decision moves these two companies more than any competitor.
  • Cash is not dying, ATMs are (CMS, Seshaasai). Banknotes in circulation rose 11.9% to ₹41.24 lakh crore in FY26 while India's ATM count fell from 2.19 lakh to 2.09 lakh in two years. The physical layer of Indian payments is shrinking in specific places, not everywhere.
  • Staffing under the labour codes (Quess). Four labour codes took effect on 21 November 2025. They redefine wages, extend gratuity and change the statutory cost of every associate. For a business earning about ₹362 a month of operating profit per general-staffing associate, an illustrative ₹336 a month of extra statutory cost is the whole margin unless it is billed on.

Two further features run through every section. First, the balance sheets are clean. None of the six carries meaningful operating debt. Screener shows "borrowings" at several of them, but at IGI those are lease liabilities, at MSTC a 2008-09 legal dispute with Standard Chartered Bank, and at BLS and Quess they are dwarfed by company-reported cash (₹1,617 crore and ₹271 crore at June 2026). The risk in this group lies in contracts, regulation and customer concentration, not in leverage. Second, the ownership is unusual: a private-equity house (Blackstone) controls IGI and, separately, BLS's largest rival VFS Global; two founding families control BLS and Seshaasai; Fairfax-linked entities and the founder anchor Quess; the President of India owns 64.75% of MSTC; and CMS has no promoter at all after its private-equity owner sold out.

In plain terms

Do not average these companies. A 60%-margin grader and a 2%-margin payroll company cannot be blended into a meaningful "sector margin" of 13%. The useful questions are company-specific: how many units of flow pass the booth, what each unit pays, who can change that price, and how long the booth's licence lasts.

01

Five tolls, one model: what each archetype sells and to whom

The six companies fall into five archetypes. Each sits at a different point in a value chain it does not control, and each charges a different unit of fee.

The quickest way into this group is to follow each flow from its source to its end user and mark where the member stands. In every case the member is a middleman with a licence, a reputation or a network that the flow's owner cannot easily replicate. The licence differs: trade acceptance for IGI, a government contract for BLS, bank empanelment and card-network certification for Seshaasai, RBI-standard vans and vaults for CMS, compliance capacity for Quess, and a government designation for MSTC. The economics follow from how hard that licence is to obtain and how often it must be renewed.

Certification: the grader between the polisher and the counter

A diamond's value depends on four characteristics (carat, colour, clarity, cut) and, since lab-grown stones became chemically identical to mined ones, on its origin. Neither buyer nor seller can see these reliably by eye, so a third party grades the stone and issues a report. IGI sits between the cutting and polishing units (India cuts and polishes about 95% of the world's diamonds, by IGI management's count) and the wholesale and retail markets. It owns no stones and takes no price risk. It is paid when the stone is graded, whatever happens to it afterwards.

Government-outsourced citizen services: renting a sovereign's front office

Consulates do not want to run high-street offices that take fingerprints and check documents. They outsource the front end to a visa-application-centre operator and keep the decision. The applicant pays two separate amounts: the visa fee, which goes to the embassy and is not BLS's revenue, and a service fee that BLS keeps. The client government sets or caps that service fee; under the European Union's visa code it may not exceed half the visa fee (the Schengen visa fee has been €90 since June 2024). BLS earns at submission whether or not the visa is granted. Its newer Indian business, run through BLS E-Services and acquired companies, operates assisted-digital and banking-correspondent points and now Aadhaar enrolment centres under a UIDAI contract.

Cash and card infrastructure: the physical layer of payments

Two members serve the plumbing of Indian payments. CMS moves cash. Banknotes leave RBI's presses for about 2,599 currency chests run by banks; from there CMS's vans carry them to branches and ATMs, load and reconcile the machines, collect cash from shops and quick-commerce hubs and process it in vaults. CMS manages cash for about 70,000 ATMs a day and 65,000-plus retail points, and claims 60% of ATM cash management and 38% of retail cash solutions. Seshaasai makes and personalises the cards that sit in wallets and prints or digitally sends the statements, cheque books and policy documents that banks and insurers owe their customers. Its licence is card-network certification (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay) and PCI audits of its secure facilities.

Staffing: a payroll with a balance sheet attached

A staffing company becomes the legal employer of workers who do the client's work at the client's site. Quess recruits, onboards and pays about 4.82 lakh associates, deducts and deposits provident fund (PF) and state insurance (ESI), provides for bonus and gratuity, and invoices the client each month for the wage, the statutory costs and a markup. The markup is the only part that is really Quess's; the rest passes through. That is why ₹15,305 crore of FY26 revenue produced ₹325 crore of operating profit. Since April 2025 the listed company has been a staffing pure-play: its business-process and facility-management arms were demerged into two separately listed companies, Digitide Solutions and Bluspring Enterprises (covered in our BPO & KPO report).

Government e-auction: the Republic's auctioneer

MSTC (formerly Metal Scrap Trade Corporation) runs the online auctions through which ministries, states and public-sector companies sell what they own: coal and mineral blocks under the mining laws, scrap from steel plants, railways and defence, land parcels, liquor licences, red sanders, condemned government vehicles. The seller pays MSTC a commission, which on bulk commodities is a fraction of a per cent and on vehicle scrapping about 2.5–3%. In FY26 ₹79,737 crore of value passed through its platforms and its revenue was ₹370 crore. MSTC is almost entirely a disposal platform: on the buying side, the government's procurement marketplace GeM handles about ₹5 lakh crore a year.

In plain terms

The quality of each toll depends on how hard it is to get the licence and how often it has to be won again. IGI's licence (trust in its reports) is renewed by every buyer every day but cannot be bought quickly by a rival. MSTC's is a government designation. BLS's must be re-won contract by contract. CMS's and Seshaasai's rest on bank tenders and certifications. Quess's licence, the ability to run a compliant payroll, is the easiest to copy, which is why its margin is the thinnest.

02

Sizing the flows: big traffic, small fee pools

The flows these companies tax are huge. The fees they collect from them are small, and in several archetypes the listed member already holds a large share of the pool.

Because each member takes a slice of a flow, two sizes matter: the size of the flow (value of diamonds graded, visa applications, cash in circulation, wages paid, value auctioned) and the size of the fee pool that the flow generates. The second is what matters for revenue, and it is always far smaller. ₹79,737 crore of auctions on MSTC's platforms produced ₹370 crore of revenue. ₹41.24 lakh crore of banknotes support a cash-logistics and ATM-management market that CMS's management sizes at about ₹20,000 crore. The global diamond-grading fee pool, built from IGI's commissioned industry study, is about $0.26–0.32 billion, roughly ₹2,500–3,000 crore at ₹94.9 to the dollar.

Visa outsourcing: a $1.7 billion market with room to grow

BLS's own May 2026 investor deck, citing unnamed industry reports, sizes the outsourced visa market at $1.7 billion and 46.0 million applications in 2024, 31% of a $5.4 billion total visa market by value and 38% of 121 million applications by volume. In plain terms

The flows are measured in lakh crore; the fees in hundreds or thousands of crore. That gap is the business model: a thin slice of something large. It also means the listed members are already big fish in small ponds. CMS claims 60% of ATM cash management, IGI claims most of lab-grown certification, and Quess employs a quarter of India's formal flexi workers. Growth from here comes from the flow growing, from the fee per unit rising, or from moving into a neighbouring pond.

One more sizing point matters for valuation. The fastest-growing pools are not the largest. Outsourced visa services are projected to grow about 14% a year by value to 2029; lab-grown jewellery certification is growing at 30–50%; remote monitoring and technology services at CMS are guided to grow 35–40% in FY27. The largest pool, staffing, grows with formal employment at single digits to low teens. Market value in this group has followed the growth and margin of the pool, not its size.

Cash usage, bank outsourcing and card issuance

CMS depends on two things that are moving in opposite directions. Cash itself keeps growing; India's ATMs do not. ATM transaction value in a month fell from ₹2.77 lakh crore (March 2024) to ₹2.20 lakh crore (July 2026) as UPI took over small payments, while banknotes in circulation grew 11.9%. Cash is increasingly held, used in informal trade and collected from shops rather than withdrawn from machines. CMS's second driver is outsourcing: about 60–65% of India's ATMs now have outsourced cash logistics, against about 50% a decade ago, and banks are handing out larger integrated contracts (HDFC Bank's 6,000-ATM, five-year, ₹400 crore mandate in 2026). Seshaasai rides card growth (credit cards +10.4%), renewal cycles, exports to Europe and Africa, and the growth of bank and insurer communications, now increasingly digital.

Formal hiring and the labour codes

Quess's demand comes from employers who want flexible, compliant labour. Formalisation helps: social-security coverage in India rose from 19% in 2015 to 64.3% in 2025 according to ILO figures cited by TeamLease. The labour codes, effective 21 November 2025, push more employers to hand compliance to specialists but also raise the cost per head. The Indian Staffing Federation's data show the pause and the restart: flexi employment grew only 0.5% quarter-on-quarter in October–December 2025 as clients waited for clarity, 0.9% in January–March 2026, and 2.6% in April–June 2026 (+7.5% year-on-year). Professional staffing for global capability centres, which make up 71% of Quess's professional headcount, is the faster, higher-margin engine.

The government's sale calendar

MSTC's demand is the government's decision to sell. Coal blocks must be auctioned under the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act and minerals under the amended mining law; 147 commercial coal blocks have been auctioned since 2020. The 15th commercial round contained 17 mines, of which 6 were sold in August 2026, and the 16th round, with 25 blocks, was launched on 17 September 2026. Public-sector scrap, condemned government vehicles (which must be scrapped after 15 years), land parcels, liquor licences and coal-linkage auctions fill the rest of the calendar. In April–June 2026 MSTC auctioned ₹2,259.52 crore of Telangana land parcels for the state's industrial corporation in a single programme. The volume of this flow is lumpy and political; the mix within it is what drives MSTC's revenue, as section 07 shows. See our coal report for the commercial-mining context.

In plain terms

Two of the flows are growing fast for reasons that have nothing to do with the economy as a whole: cheap lab-grown diamonds and the outsourcing of visa processing. Two are growing at the pace of the economy but face a technology shift in one part: cash (fine) versus ATMs (shrinking), and cards (fine) versus paper statements (shrinking). Staffing grows with formal jobs. MSTC's flow is whatever the government decides to put up for sale, which can fall in value in a year when the company's revenue rises.

The practical lesson for an investor is to track the flow indicator that matters for each company and not a common macro proxy. GDP growth tells you little about IGI (lab-grown jewellery fashion and US holiday orders matter more) or MSTC (the coal round calendar matters more). Monthly RBI data on ATMs, cash and cards, GJEPC export data, ISF's quarterly flexi count and the Ministry of Coal's round announcements are the indicators section 20 turns into thresholds.

Operating leverage: where the fixed costs sit

In plain terms

A cheap lab-grown diamond still needs a report if it is going to be sold as a graded stone. When the price of the stone falls from $1,500 to $100, the jeweller can afford to set many more of them, and each one is a fee for IGI. That is the boom. The limit is reached when the report costs too large a share of the stone's value to be worth buying; IGI's own deck puts the fee at about 2–3% of the retail price of a one-carat lab-grown stone today, far above the fraction of a per cent it is on a natural stone.

The competitive opening: GIA's change of heart

In 2025 GIA, the other grading heavyweight, announced it would stop grading lab-grown diamonds on the four-Cs scale. Trade press quoting GIA's own statement reported the change as announced in June 2025, with criteria published on 26 August 2025 and effect from 1 October 2025; lab-grown stones would instead be labelled "premium" or "standard", or not graded, for $15 a carat. GIA's position, implicitly, is that detailed grading of a manufactured stone adds little value. That is good news for IGI in the near term: the market for four-Cs-graded lab-grown reports, where IGI claims 65–75% of loose-stone certification, has lost its most credible alternative supplier. It is also a warning. If the most respected lab thinks grading lab-grown stones finely is not worth much, buyers may come to agree.

The bargaining-power problem: a concentrated customer base

The single most important disclosure of the past year came from IGI's own chief financial officer on the Q1 FY27 call: 12–14 growers make about 80% of India's lab-grown diamonds, and the top 8–10 of them contribute close to 40–50% of IGI's revenue. Management reads those growers' own margins at about 10–12%. Thin-margin, concentrated customers who account for half your revenue are a negotiating threat; they have already won a 25% fee cut once, in 2024. A grower consolidation, a shift by large growers to a rival or to GIA's cheaper descriptive reports, or a grower-led push for another price cut is a more realistic threat to the ₹891 fee than any technology.

The balance sheet and the owner

IGI has no borrowings. The ₹143–150 crore that screener shows as "borrowings" are lease liabilities on its labs (₹149.8 crore at September 2026). First-half FY27 operating cash flow was ₹415.7 crore. The constraint is not money but ownership. The December 2024 IPO raised ₹4,225 crore, of which ₹1,475 crore was fresh money used to buy IGI's Belgian and Dutch groups from the promoter and ₹2,750 crore went to Blackstone through an offer for sale. In plain terms

IGI's strengths are real: rising volumes, a rising average fee, falling cost per report, no debt and a rival that has stepped back. Its weaknesses are just as specific: about half its revenue comes from a handful of thin-margin growers who have already forced one price cut, its India margin is being diluted to support its overseas labs, and its owner must sell shares. The share price will be driven by which of those lists the next few quarters lengthen.

Two scenarios bracket IGI's next three years. In the first, lab-grown jewellery keeps spreading, natural stones recover, the coloured-stone and Middle East expansion adds volume, and report growth of 15–20% with a stable ARP keeps EBITDA compounding at the guided 20%. In the second, growers consolidate and demand a second fee cut of the 2024 size; at constant volume a 10% fee cut removes about 16% of EBITDA (an ILLUSTRATIVE sensitivity on Q2 FY27 figures: ₹38.8 crore of a ₹244.5 crore quarter), more than a 10% fall in volume would, because most of IGI's costs are fixed. Fee is worth more to IGI than volume, and fee is where its customers have leverage.

BLS sells to governments; MSTC sells for one. Both earn high margins on mandated flows, and both can be switched off, or on, by a single official decision.

Two members have governments on the other side of every transaction. BLS: the debarment and what the court actually said

MSTC: what the balance sheet really says

Two lines in MSTC's balance sheet mislead anyone reading it quickly. Why Q1 FY27's 15% was really about 10%

Quess reported April–June 2026 revenue of ₹4,182 crore, up 14.5%, with EBITDA of ₹85 crore (+21%) and net profit of ₹82 crore (+61%). In plain terms

What the labour codes change for a staffing company

The four codes (Wages, Industrial Relations, Social Security, and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions) replaced 29 laws from 21 November 2025, with central and state rules still being notified through 2026. Three mechanics matter most for staffing. First, "wages" must be at least 50% of total pay, and allowances above 50% are added back when calculating PF, gratuity and leave encashment, which raises statutory costs for anyone whose basic pay was set low. Second, fixed-term employees earn gratuity after one year instead of five. Third, contractors need a single pan-India licence and face limits on contract labour in "core activities". The codes also create a social-security fund for gig and platform workers financed by aggregators at 1–2% of turnover, which narrows the cost advantage of gig platforms over formal staffing.

The tax qualification nobody talks about

Quess's auditor, Deloitte Haskins & Sells, qualified its review of the Q1 FY27 results, as it qualified the FY26 audit. The issue is section 80JJAA of the Income-tax Act, a deduction for companies that add new employees. The tax department has disallowed Quess's 80JJAA claims and its goodwill depreciation for FY17 to FY23, arguing among other things that there is no employer-employee relationship between Quess and its associates. That argument strikes at the staffing model itself. Appeals are pending at the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (FY18–22), the Commissioner (Appeals) (FY17) and the Dispute Resolution Panel (FY23); a January 2024 order of the National Financial Reporting Authority on the chartered accountant's certification of FY19–21 claims is stayed by the Delhi High Court. Separately, an EPFO demand of ₹71.7 crore for PF on certain salary components in FY19 has been stayed by the tribunal.

The rule-setters: who regulates each toll

Every member's fee is shaped by a rulebook it does not write: visa codes, procurement rules, RBI standards, card-network certification, the labour codes, mining law and the listing rules.

Regulation does not just constrain these businesses; it often creates them. The European Union's visa code allows external service providers and caps their fees. RBI's cash-logistics standards pushed small operators out and handed the market to a few. Card networks' certification makes card personalisation a licensed activity. The labour codes push employers toward compliant staffing firms. The mining laws require coal and mineral blocks to be auctioned. Only IGI's licence is mainly a matter of trade acceptance, and even there disclosure rules on lab-grown stones shape demand.

Government procurement and the visa code

Two sets of rules shape BLS. The client governments' own procurement and consular rules decide who runs their centres and what they may charge; the EU visa code, which governs Schengen consulates, allows external providers and caps their fee at half the visa fee, which since June 2024 means €45 on a €90 visa (about ₹4,950 at €1 ≈ ₹110). BLS's FY26 gross visa revenue of about ₹4,173 per application, across all its corridors, sits below that ceiling. In India, the General Financial Rules give ministries the power to debar bidders; the MEA used it against BLS in October 2025 and the High Court's reasoning (section 07) now limits how such powers can be used. The EU's Entry/Exit System went live progressively from 12 October 2025 and its travel-authorisation scheme for visa-exempt travellers is expected around late 2026; neither removes the need for visa centres for visa-required nationals such as Indians.

RBI, cash and cards

RBI shapes both CMS and Seshaasai indirectly, through the banks that buy their services. Its 2018 standards for banks' cash-logistics vendors (minimum net worth, fleet size, GPS and CCTV, armed guards, vault and cassette-swap rules) consolidated the industry. Its decision to raise ATM interchange fees from 1 May 2025 (to ₹19 per financial and ₹7 per non-financial transaction) improved the economics of brown-label ATMs like those CMS runs. Its currency management decides how many notes reach the chests, which in April–May 2026 became a revenue constraint. For cards, the regulators are the networks and the PCI Council, which certify personalisation bureaux; RBI's 2022 tokenisation rules reduced re-issuance after fraud, and its continuous cheque-clearing system, phased in from October 2025, speeds cheques without changing demand for cheque books.

The labour codes and tax

The four labour codes are covered in section 09. Two other rules matter for this group's taxes. BLS's sub-10% tax rate rests on profits earned in UAE and other overseas subsidiaries; the UAE has levied a 9% corporate tax since 2023, and the OECD's 15% global minimum applies only to groups with revenue above €750 million, which BLS is below. Quess's tax rate depends on section 80JJAA, now in dispute. GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, left the 18% rate on these services broadly unchanged.

Mining law, scrappage, EPR and the dividend rule

MSTC exists because the law says certain government assets must be sold by auction. The Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act 2015 and the amended Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act require coal and mineral blocks to be auctioned. The 2021 vehicle-scrapping policy requires government vehicles older than 15 years to be scrapped from April 2023, and MSTC runs auctions of the resulting scrap. Extended-producer-responsibility rules for plastic, e-waste and batteries create a market in certificates, for which MSTC has built an exchange that awaits the pollution board's approval. As a central public-sector enterprise, MSTC follows DIPAM's dividend guideline (at least 30% of profit or 4% of net worth, whichever is higher); its FY26 payout was 51%.

The listing rule that creates two overhangs

India's minimum public shareholding rule (Rule 19A of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Rules) requires at least 25% of a listed company's shares to be held by the public, with three years from listing to comply. IGI (listed December 2024; promoter 76.55%) and Seshaasai (listed September 2025; promoter 81.80%) both exceed the 75% promoter ceiling. Five decades of shocks: how six tolls were built

Most of these businesses began as functions the state or the banks performed themselves. Outsourcing, private equity, demonetisation, COVID and a 2025 of corporate surgery shaped what they are today.

The six companies have very different ages. MSTC was incorporated in 1964 as the Metal Scrap Trade Corporation; IGI was founded in Antwerp in 1975; Seshaasai began as Seshaasai Business Forms in Mumbai in 1993. The rest are products of the outsourcing wave of the 2000s: BLS won its first visa contract (Portugal, in New Delhi) in 2005; Quess began as IKYA in 2007; CMS's cash business was built from 2008–09 with backing from Blackstone. What unites their histories is a recurring pattern: a state or bank function is outsourced, a private operator builds scale, private equity consolidates it, and the stock market eventually buys it.

From state function to listed service (pre-2016)

MSTC launched e-auctions in 2002, turning a scrap trader into a platform. Embassies outsourced visa front offices from the early 2000s. Banks outsourced ATM cash. Private equity arrived in force in the 2010s: Fairfax took control of IKYA in 2013 and renamed it Quess; Baring Private Equity Asia bought CMS in 2015. Quess listed in July 2016 and spent the following years buying businesses, building the conglomerate it would dismantle in 2025.

Demonetisation, rules and a failed listing (2016–2019)

Demonetisation in November 2016 briefly crushed and then rebuilt cash volumes. RBI's 2018 standards for cash-logistics vendors forced consolidation. MSTC listed in March 2019 just as its trading book, which financed commodity purchases for clients, imploded, producing an FY19 loss of about ₹307 crore and a decade-long discount.

COVID and the rebound (2020–2024)

COVID stopped international travel and with it visa applications; it also made Indians hoard cash (cash reached 14.4% of GDP in FY21). Quess booked a loss of about ₹432 crore in FY20 on impairments. CMS listed in December 2021. The rebound was strong for the travel and diamond tolls: BLS's revenue rose from ₹850 crore in FY22 to ₹2,193 crore in FY25, helped by the ~₹720 crore acquisition of Turkey's iDATA (closed July 2024); IGI's revenue rose from ₹491 crore in calendar 2022 to ₹1,053 crore in 2024 as lab-grown stones took over. Blackstone bought IGI in 2023 and floated it in December 2024 at ₹417 a share; it listed at ₹510.

2025: a year of corporate surgery

Almost every member changed shape in 2025. Quess split into three. MSTC completed the sale of Ferro Scrap Nigam. AGS Transact, CMS's listed rival, entered insolvency. Seshaasai listed. The MEA debarred BLS and the High Court reversed it. GIA stopped four-Cs grading of lab-grown diamonds. The labour codes took effect. Few members ended the year in the shape they started it.

The cycle that matters for each

Each toll has its own cycle, and none of them is the Indian economic cycle. IGI's is lab-grown price deflation, which has been a tailwind and becomes a headwind only when the fee becomes too large a share of a stone's value. MSTC's is the auction calendar and commodity prices.

In plain terms

History in this group is mostly about owners and rules, not demand. Private equity built CMS, IGI and VFS; a demerger rebuilt Quess; a loss-making trading book scarred MSTC; a ministry and a court shook BLS. When you read any of these companies' five-year numbers, ask first what changed in the company's shape, because in most cases something did.

Reading a commercial-services company: the KPIs that matter Deep dive

Six archetypes, one master equation: units of someone else's flow, times the fee per unit, minus the cost to serve. Each company discloses a different piece of it, and each piece has a trap.

Every company in this group can be written as the same equation. Revenue is the number of units of flow that pass the booth multiplied by the fee per unit. Operating profit is revenue less the cost of serving each unit and the fixed cost of the booth. Below that, some members earn income on money they hold (MSTC's float, IGI's and BLS's cash) and all pay tax at very different rates. The art is knowing which term each company's management actually controls, which one it merely reports, and which one the reader has to derive.

4. Customer concentration (Seshaasai)

What it is. The share of revenue that comes from a company's largest customers, usually the top one and the top ten. Formula. Top-10 share = revenue from the ten largest customers ÷ total revenue. Worked example. Seshaasai's top ten customers provided 56% of its Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹376.5 crore, about ₹211 crore, or an average of about ₹21 crore each in the quarter. What moves it. Winning new mid-sized customers lowers it; losing small ones (as Seshaasai lost insurance clients that moved to digital delivery) raises it; large public-sector tenders can raise it sharply in a single quarter. 10. Return on equity

What it is. Net profit as a share of shareholders' equity: how much each rupee of owners' capital earns. Formula. ROE = net profit ÷ average equity; it can be split into net margin × asset turnover (revenue ÷ assets) × leverage (assets ÷ equity). Worked example. Quess earns a net margin of about 1.45%, but its revenue is about five times its assets (₹15,305 crore on ₹3,039 crore) and its assets about 2.6 times its equity, so ROE comes to about 19% on period-end equity (screener's 21.3% uses average equity). IGI reaches 55.8% the other way, with a 44% net margin on a modest asset base.

What moves it. Margin for the platforms; turnover for the pass-through; idle cash for everyone (cash earns 6–7% before tax and drags ROE down). CMS's 12.7% is depressed by a heavy depreciation charge on owned ATMs and by its cash; BLS's 32.1% includes a low tax rate. Benchmarks. Above 25% is strong, 15–25% average, below 15% weak.

What it is. The share of profit paid out as dividends (and, in effect, through buybacks). Formula. Payout = dividends ÷ net profit; dividend yield = dividend per share ÷ share price. Worked example. What moves it. Owner preferences (DIPAM's guideline for central PSUs such as MSTC; private equity's preference for buybacks or exits), reinvestment needs (Seshaasai's capex, BLS's acquisitions) and listing-rule events (a promoter preparing to sell may prefer dividends). 15. Definition traps, collected

Every number in this group comes with a definitional catch. The list below collects the ones that have already misled published analysis, including earlier versions of our own primers.

  • IGI's year. IGI moved from a December to a March year-end. Its audited period is 15 months to March 2026 (revenue ₹1,597.6 crore, profit ₹711.2 crore, 16.5 million reports). This report's FY26 (₹1,294 crore revenue, ₹572 crore profit) is constructed from four quarters to match the other five.
  • "Revenue" means six things. Gross billing including wages (Quess), a service fee excluding the government's visa fee (BLS), commission on value sold (MSTC), fees plus product sales (CMS), part product and part service (Seshaasai), certification fees plus education (IGI).
  • Quess is most of every sum. 64% of combined revenue, 10% of profit, 11% of market value. Lead with ex-Quess figures.
  • BLS's net revenue per application is net of cost of services; gross per application fell 5% in FY26 while net rose 13.7%. Do not infer volumes from it.
  • BLS's profit has minorities. Total Q1 FY27 profit ₹201.6 crore, attributable ₹190 crore.
  • CMS's EBITDA is ₹596 crore (24.0%) for FY26, not the ~₹804 crore (~32%) once printed, which was operating profit plus depreciation.
  • CMS's machine counts: ATMs managed daily (~70,000), managed ATMs (~31,000 rising to ~39,000 plus 6,000), touchpoints (~1.55 lakh) and monitored sites (50,000+) are different things.
  • Quess's Q1 FY27 revenue includes a ₹176 crore profit-neutral labour-code pass-through; underlying growth was about 9.7%, not 14.5%. Its ₹261 crore of tax refunds is cash, not profit.
  • Quess's history is a broken series because of the 2025 demerger; FY23 includes businesses now owned by Digitide and Bluspring.
  • MSTC's GMV is not its sales, its "borrowings" are a 2008-09 legal dispute, and its cash partly belongs to bidders and sellers.
  • IGI's "borrowings" are leases. IGI has no debt and about ₹560 crore of cash and bank balances.
  • Cash in circulation: ₹41.24 lakh crore is banknotes (RBI); ₹41.68 lakh crore includes coins.
  • Market shares are self-reported: IGI's 65–75% of lab-grown loose certification, Seshaasai's 31.9% of card issuance, CMS's 60% and 38%, and BLS's 17% by value all come from company-commissioned studies or management statements.
  • IGI's India margin falls when it pays commissions to its overseas subsidiaries; use consolidated margins.
In plain terms

In this group the most dangerous number is the one that looks most familiar. "Revenue", "EBITDA", "debt", "cash" and "year" each mean something different at two or more of the six companies. Before comparing any two figures, check that they are the same thing measured the same way over the same months.

16. How to read a commercial-services company's quarterly results in ten minutes

The checklist below works for any of the six. It is ordered so that the first five minutes tell you whether the toll is growing and the second five whether the numbers can be trusted.

  1. Find the unit count (minute 1). Reports (IGI), applications (BLS), ATMs and points (CMS), associates (Quess), GMV (MSTC); for Seshaasai, the mix percentages. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter, because every one of these flows is seasonal. Red flag: the unit count is missing from a presentation that usually carries it.
  2. Divide to get the fee per unit (minute 2). Revenue per report, gross and net revenue per application, revenue per point, revenue per associate-month, take rate. Ask whether the change came from price or mix. Red flag: a "per unit" metric defined net of a cost that is falling.
  3. Strip out pass-throughs and one-offs (minute 3). Quess's labour-code revenue, product sales at CMS, acquisitions at BLS (iDATA, Aadifidelis, Atyati), AGL at IGI. Compute organic growth. Red flag: headline growth well above organic growth with no bridge provided.
  4. Check the operating margin on the right base (minute 4). Use segment margins where they exist (BLS's visa ~40% and digital ~8%; Quess's general staffing ~1.4% and professional ~11%) and compare with guidance (CMS ~27%, BLS ~40%/~8%). Red flag: consolidated margin up while the core segment's margin is down.
  5. Look below operating profit (minute 5). Other income (MSTC's float, IGI's and BLS's cash), depreciation (CMS's owned ATMs), tax rate (BLS rising to ~12%; Quess's 80JJAA), minorities (BLS). Red flag: profit growth driven by other income or a lower tax rate.
  6. Read the cash flow (minute 6). Operating cash flow against operating profit; receivable days at Quess (28 in Q1 FY27) and CMS; inventory at Seshaasai; deposits at MSTC. Red flag: three years of falling cash conversion, as at Quess.
  7. Check the balance sheet with the traps in mind (minute 7). Leases are not debt (IGI); legal liabilities are not debt (MSTC); bank balances are cash even if screens miss them (BLS). Note buybacks (CMS's ₹168 crore) and acquisitions that consumed cash.
  8. Read the contingent liabilities and the auditor's report (minute 8). Quess's qualified review over section 80JJAA (contingent liability ₹388 crore) and its EPFO demand; subsidiary auditor changes at BLS. Red flag: any new qualification, emphasis of matter or auditor resignation.
  9. Scan the contract and regulatory news (minute 9). Mandates won and lost (BLS), bank mandates and repricing (CMS), tenders (Seshaasai), coal and mineral rounds (MSTC), state labour-code rules (Quess), lab-grown pricing and grower concentration (IGI). Red flag: a material contract mentioned only in a stock-exchange filing, not in the presentation.
  10. Close with the register (minute 10). Promoter holding against the 75% cap (IGI, Seshaasai), FII trends (BLS, CMS), pledges, and any offer for sale announced. Red flag: a promoter sell-down timed just after an unusually strong quarter.
In plain terms

The ten-minute routine boils down to three questions. Is more traffic passing the booth? Is each vehicle paying more, and why? And is the money that the results say was earned actually arriving in the bank, free of claims by tax authorities, minorities or counterparties? A company that passes all three is collecting a real toll.

25× for one toll, 11× for another: the valuation spread explained

The market is not pricing these companies on size or even on margin. It is pricing how secure each toll looks, and who could take it away.

On 23 October 2026 the six traded at trailing P/E multiples from 24.8 (IGI) to 10.8 (CMS), at EV/EBITDA multiples from about 20 times (IGI) to about 5 times (CMS), and at market values from 12.8 times revenue (IGI) to 0.30 times (Quess). Some of that spread is accounting: Quess's revenue is mostly wages, so its price-to-sales ratio is meaningless, and MSTC's cash includes other people's money. Most of it is a judgement about the durability of each toll.

What the market is paying for

Read the map with the deep dives in mind and four pricing rules emerge. Trust networks get the highest multiple. IGI's toll is renewed by every buyer who asks for its report; it is growing, and its fee is defended by acceptance rather than a contract. That earns 24.8 times earnings despite a mandatory sell-down and a concentrated customer base. Mandated flows get a solid but capped multiple. MSTC's toll is protected by law but its flow and its priorities are set by its owner; 17.2 times earnings and a 2.74% yield reflect a monopoly with no say over its own growth. Technology and disruption: which tolls software can bypass

Technology threatens the physical layer of three tolls and the price of a fourth. It also lowers each operator's own cost to serve.

The disruption question differs for each archetype, and in most cases technology both threatens and helps. It can remove the need for a physical visit, a physical card or a physical ATM; it can make a rival's grading lab as accurate as an incumbent's; it can match workers to jobs without an employer of record. It can also cut the incumbent's cost per unit and open new tolls next to the old ones.

  • Lab-grown commoditisation and the fee floor (IGI). As a one-carat lab-grown stone falls to about $100 wholesale, a report fee of 2–3% of the retail price starts to look expensive, and below some value threshold stones will go uncertified or receive cheaper screening reports. GIA's move to descriptive "premium/standard" reports signals one lab's view that detailed lab-grown grading is worth little. This is the most important long-run risk to IGI's volume-times-fee equation.
  • Automated grading (IGI). Machine vision for colour and clarity lowers the cost of entry for rival labs and could compress fees. It also lowers IGI's own cost per report, which fell about 4% in Q2 FY27, and management names AI and automation as a differentiator. Reputation, not technology, remains the moat.
  • Digital visas and biometric reuse (BLS). E-visas, the EU's planned digital Schengen application platform (expected around 2028 or later) and reuse of stored biometrics under the EU's Entry/Exit and Visa Information Systems could reduce in-person centre visits. For now, Schengen, UK and US regimes require biometric capture in person, and the EU's ETIAS affects only visa-exempt travellers.
  • UPI, the digital rupee and the ATM (CMS). India's ATM count fell 4.3% in two years and monthly ATM withdrawals by value 21% in 28 months as UPI took over small payments (UPI value of about ₹314 lakh crore in FY26, +21%). CMS's response is managed services, technology and payments (guided to grow 35–40% in FY27), HAWKAI remote monitoring (a ₹3,000 crore BFSI plus ₹5,000 crore non-BFSI market by its estimate) and ALGO MVS software (63,000-plus ATMs).
  • Tokenisation, virtual cards and e-statements (Seshaasai). Tokenised and virtual cards reduce re-issuance after fraud, and paperless statements cut print volumes; insurers are moving policies to digital delivery. Seshaasai's answer is omni-channel digital communication for the same clients, premium and metal cards, and RFID tags (18% of revenue).
  • Gig platforms and AI matching (Quess). Gig marketplaces can match low-skill workers to short jobs without a staffing firm. The social-security code's levy on aggregators narrows their cost advantage, and Quess is using AI-assisted sourcing to raise recruiter productivity.
  • GeM and state portals (MSTC). MSTC's platform is not technologically unique; any ministry could route disposals through GeM or a state portal. Its defence is the trust of sellers and bidders, its auction experience and its designation.
  • Cyber and data security (all). BLS holds passport and biometric data, Seshaasai card data and PINs, CMS cash and ATM telemetry, Quess payroll records. A serious breach would be a contract-loss event for any of them.
In plain terms

Software threatens tolls that exist only because something physical has to happen: a fingerprint taken in a room, a note put in a machine, a card printed and posted. It threatens the price, not the existence, of a toll based on trust, such as a grading report. The members are all moving their revenue toward the digital or trust-based parts of their business; the question is whether they move faster than the physical parts shrink.

The pace matters more than the direction. ATM decline has been gradual (about 2% a year), not a collapse, and cash itself is growing; card issuance is still rising; visa outsourcing is still taking share from consulates. The near-term technology risk is concentrated in specific revenue lines, ATM replenishment at CMS, physical renewal cards at Seshaasai and possibly the fee per lab-grown report at IGI, rather than in any business as a whole.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the tolls

The bull case rests on growing flows, defended fees and clean balance sheets. The bear case rests on concentrated customers, government discretion and the physical layer's decline.

Because the six businesses differ so much, the bull and bear cases are really six cases each, linked by the toll logic. The summary below gives the strongest version of each side across the group; the company profiles and deep dives give the company-specific detail.

The bull case

  • Growing flows: lab-grown jewellery certification +47%, outsourced visas heading from 38% to 50% of applications by 2029, banknotes +11.9%, credit cards +10.4%, formal flexi staffing +7.5% a year.
  • Defended tolls: IGI's lab-grown dominance after GIA's retreat; CMS's 60% share after AGS's collapse; BLS's five-to-ten-year exclusive contracts; MSTC's legal mandate.
  • Operating leverage: IGI's margin rose from 58.0% to 61.4% as volumes rose 26%; CMS raised margin guidance to ~27% as capex falls.
  • Clean balance sheets: every member is effectively debt-free; BLS (₹1,617 Cr), IGI (~₹900–1,000 Cr) and Quess (₹271 Cr) hold net cash, and buybacks have begun (CMS ₹168 Cr).
  • Cheap where it is risky: CMS at ~5× EV/EBITDA and BLS at ~8× price in a great deal of bad news.

The bear case

  • Concentrated customers: IGI's top 8–10 growers are 40–50% of revenue and have won a 25% fee cut before; Seshaasai's top ten are 56%; CMS depends on a few large banks.
  • Government discretion: the MEA can re-notice BLS on service quality; MSTC's flows and priorities are set by its owner; PSU-bank repricing for CMS runs through an industry committee.
  • Physical decline: fewer ATMs, fewer paper documents, more virtual cards, and eventually digital visa applications.
  • Overhangs: Blackstone must sell IGI shares, Seshaasai's founders must sell, Quess faces a qualified audit and a ₹388 Cr contingent tax liability.
  • Margin drift: BLS's growth is coming from 8–15%-margin businesses; Quess's margin can vanish if labour-code costs are not re-billed.

Weighing the two, the bull case is strongest where the flow is growing and the fee is set by acceptance rather than contract: IGI, and to a lesser extent MSTC and BLS's visa franchise. The bear case is strongest where the flow itself is shrinking (ATM cash) or the fee is a thin markup set by competition (general staffing). The valuations already reflect much of this ranking, which is why the more interesting questions are at the edges: whether IGI's growers can force a second fee cut, whether BLS's contracts and governance prove sturdier than its price, and whether CMS's margin and cash flow recover enough to make its low multiple look wrong.

In plain terms

The optimist sees six businesses collecting fees on growing flows with no debt. The pessimist sees customers with too much bargaining power, governments that can change the rules and a physical world that is slowly going digital. Both are describing the same companies; they differ on which of those facts will matter more over the next three years.
