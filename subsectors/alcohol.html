Breweries & distilleries: the state takes more than the brand

A complete guide to India's listed spirits and beer makers: how a bottle of whisky is priced, taxed and split between the state, the trade and the brand owner; why policy in one state can move a national company's quarter; how premiumisation, Indian single malts, the India–UK trade deal, the grain and glass squeeze and a wave of brand deals are reshaping the six companies in the NSE 750; and what every important number in their results actually means.

00

The one-page view: a tax collector that happens to own brands

India's six listed alcohol makers earn ₹35,000 crore of net revenue, collect even more than that in excise for the states, and are valued at more than 70 times their combined profit.

An Indian alcohol company is three businesses at once. It is a consumer brand house that has to persuade a drinker to trade up from a ₹640 bottle of whisky to a ₹1,200 one. It is a manufacturer that buys grain spirit, glass and cartons in markets it does not control. And it is, in practice, an unpaid tax collector for 28 state governments, each of which sets its own duty, its own route to market and often the price on the shelf. Anyone who analyses these companies as if they were ordinary consumer staples misses the third role, and the third role explains most of what happens to their volumes and margins in any given year.

Six companies in the NSE 750 make up this sub-sector. United Spirits (Diageo's Indian arm, McDowell's, Royal Challenge, Signature, Antiquity, Black Dog and locally bottled Johnnie Walker) is the largest by value. Radico Khaitan (Magic Moments vodka, 8PM, Rampur single malt) is the fastest premiumiser with its own grain distilleries. United Breweries (Kingfisher, controlled by Heineken) is the only beer company. Allied Blenders and Distillers (Officer's Choice, ICONiQ White, Sterling Reserve) is the large Indian-owned whisky volume house, listed in July 2024. Tilaknagar Industries (Mansion House brandy) bought Imperial Blue, India's third-largest whisky, from Pernod Ricard in December 2025. Piccadily Agro built Indri, now India's best-selling single malt, on top of a Haryana sugar mill and distillery.

Five forces explain almost everything in this report, and the sections are built around them.

  • The state owns the price. Alcohol is a state subject under the Constitution and sits outside GST. Each state sets excise, decides who may sell wholesale and retail, and in many states approves the shelf price. United Spirits collected about ₹15,350 crore of excise in FY26, more than its own ₹12,467 crore of net revenue. In 2025–26, Maharashtra's duty increase cut the industry's sales there by about 20% in a single quarter. Sections 04 and 05 cover this.
  • Premiumisation is the engine. Industry volume grows 4–6% a year; industry value grows 10–12%. The difference is drinkers trading up. United Spirits now earns 91.7% of its net sales from "Prestige & Above" brands; Radico's P&A volume grew 35.8% in Q1 FY27 while its total volume grew 2.8%. Section 08.
  • Trade policy and the single-malt moment. Indian single malts outsold Scotch single malts in India in 2024. On 15 July 2026 the India–UK trade agreement halved the customs duty on Scotch from 150% to 75%, falling to 40% over ten years. Section 09.
  • The input squeeze. Extra-neutral alcohol (ENA), the base spirit, rose from about ₹53 to ₹80 a litre between FY20 and FY27 (Crisil projection) as fuel ethanol competed for the same grain, and the 2026 West Asia crisis pushed up glass and packaging. Because shelf prices need state approval, cost spikes hit margins for several quarters. Section 10.
  • Brand M&A and portfolio reshaping. The multinationals are leaving mass brands and non-core assets (United Spirits sold its Popular brands in 2022 and agreed to sell the Royal Challengers Bengaluru cricket franchise for ₹16,660 crore in 2026; Pernod sold Imperial Blue), and Indian owners are buying scale. Section 12.
In plain terms

When you buy a ₹640 bottle of everyday whisky, roughly ₹440 goes to the state as excise and VAT, about ₹108 to the shop and the distributor, and about ₹92 to the company whose name is on the label. Of that ₹92, the company spends about ₹54 on alcohol, glass and packaging and keeps about ₹13 as operating profit. Everything about this industry — why premium bottles matter so much, why one state's budget can wreck a quarter, why margins move with grain prices — follows from that split. These per-bottle figures are illustrative, built in Section 01.

Beer is the exception that proves the rule. Kingfisher's maker sells a cheap, bulky product taxed per litre, ships it in returnable glass, earns most of its year in the April–June summer quarter and needs price approvals in about 30 states. Its EBITDA margin has sat near 9% for six years. The market still pays about 92 times trailing earnings for it, on a return on capital of about 11%. Section 11 explains why both facts can be true at once.

The market pays well for all of this. On 19 October 2026 the trailing P/E ranged from 40.5× (Piccadily) to 93× (Allied Blenders), and EV/EBITDA on FY26 from about 26× to 58×, three to six times the multiples of Diageo, Pernod Ricard or Heineken (indicative global comparison, Section 18). The reasons are a long volume runway (Indians drink about 3.2 litres of pure alcohol a year against a world average of about 5.1), premiumisation that is still early, and very few listed names with thin free floats. The risks are just as specific: state policy shocks every two to three years, input-cost spikes that cannot be passed on quickly, receivables owed by state corporations, and, for two of the six, acquisition debt.

Timing. As of 19 October 2026 none of the six has reported Q2 FY27 (July–September). Radico Khaitan, United Breweries and Allied Blenders report on 27 October; United Spirits on 28 October; Tilaknagar and Piccadily have not announced dates. Q1 FY27 is therefore the latest quarter common to all six, and the Q2 prints will be the first to show a full quarter of the India–UK FTA and Karnataka's new strength-based excise.

For readers coming from other consumer reports: the brand-building and distribution logic will feel familiar from packaged foods and personal care, but the price-setting does not. A biscuit maker can raise prices nationally overnight; a whisky maker must file cost sheets state by state and wait. The on-trade (bars, hotels, restaurants) links this sub-sector to hotels, where liquor licences and bar revenue matter for food-and-beverage income.

01

How a bottle makes money: from grain to the state's till

The maker keeps between a seventh and two-fifths of the shelf price, depending on how premium the bottle is. The state keeps half to two-thirds.

What the industry sells

Most of what the six companies sell is IMFL ("Indian-made foreign liquor"): Western-style spirits made in India from extra-neutral alcohol, malt spirit, flavours and water. By volume, Crisil puts the FY25 mix at whisky 65%, brandy 20%, rum 12% and white spirits (vodka and gin) 3%. IMFL is about 80% of the industry's value. Around it sit four smaller pools. Bottled-in-origin (BIO) imports such as Scotch, Irish whiskey and tequila are about 2% of the spirits market by volume. Bottled-in-India (BII) brands are imported bulk Scotch blended and bottled locally; Diageo's Black & White, VAT 69 and, in some states, Johnnie Walker Red Label are made this way, and bulk Scotch malt is also a blending ingredient in premium Indian whiskies such as Royal Challenge, Signature, Blenders Pride and Imperial Blue. Indian single malts (Amrut, Paul John, Rampur, Indri, Godawan and newer craft names) are small in volume but the fastest-growing slice of value. Beer is mostly "strong" lager of 5–8% alcohol, with Kingfisher Strong the single largest pool.

Two other products appear in the accounts and confuse comparisons. Country liquor is cheap, molasses-based, state-specific and often sold under state tenders; it is not IMFL. And bulk ENA and fuel ethanol are sold by companies that own distilleries (Radico, Piccadily) to other bottlers or to the oil marketing companies under the ethanol-blending programme. Radico's "non-IMFL" line and most of Piccadily's revenue are this kind of business, which carries much lower margins than branded spirits.

The chain has one feature found almost nowhere else in Indian consumer goods: a state-level gate in the middle. Because each state taxes "imports" from other states more heavily, a national brand needs bottling inside most of the states where it sells, either in its own plants or through contract "tie-up" bottlers. United Spirits runs a largely asset-light network of this kind; Radico and Piccadily own their distilleries; Allied Blenders and Tilaknagar mix owned and contract units. Route to market then splits four ways: state corporation monopolies that buy all wholesale stock (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala and others), open private wholesale and retail (Maharashtra, Goa, Uttar Pradesh, Haryana), government retail (Delhi's roughly 792 outlets, Kerala's BEVCO, Tamil Nadu's TASMAC), and prohibition (Gujarat since 1960, Bihar since 2016, Nagaland, Mizoram and Lakshadweep).

Three things stand out. First, the state is the biggest beneficiary of every bottle, taking 45–69% of the shelf price in these examples. In some high-tax states the share on a cheap bottle is higher still. Second, the maker's share of the shelf price rises steeply with price point: from about 14% of MRP on the regular whisky to about 24% on the premium one and about 42% on the single malt. Many states use slabs that cap the rupee duty at higher price points, and liquid and glass cost far less than the price difference. Third, profit per bottle rises much faster than price. The drinker pays 1.9 times as much for the premium whisky as for the regular one, but the maker's net revenue is 3.1 times and its EBITDA 4.5 times as large. One luxury case earns roughly the EBITDA of 38–40 regular cases. That arithmetic is why every company in this report is trying to move its drinkers up the price ladder.

In plain terms

A company selling cheap whisky is doing a lot of work for very little money: it pays for the alcohol, the glass and the trucks, collects tax for the state, waits months to be paid, and keeps about ₹13 a bottle. A company selling premium whisky does similar work for ₹58 a bottle. That is why investors pay far more for a rupee of premium sales than for a rupee of mass sales.

Why the mass bottle is so fragile

The thin profit on a regular bottle makes it very sensitive to small changes. In the illustrative regular-whisky example, the ₹13 of EBITDA sits on top of ₹54 of costs. An increase of ₹5 a litre in the price of ENA adds about ₹1.7 to the cost of each bottle (each 750 ml bottle at 42.8% alcohol needs about 0.34 litres of ENA), which removes about ₹20 of EBITDA per case, or 13%. A 10% rise in glass prices removes about ₹17 a case, or 11%. And if a state refuses a 5% price increase for a year while costs rise, the maker loses roughly ₹4.6 of net revenue per bottle, about a third of its EBITDA per case. These sensitivities, which are illustrative, explain the margin troughs that hit mass-heavy portfolios in FY23 and that hit Tilaknagar again in Q1 FY27, when its EBITDA margin fell 751 basis points on glass inflation and integration costs.

In plain terms

"Revenue" means two different things in this industry. The statutory "revenue from operations" includes the excise the company collects for the state; "net revenue" or "net sales value" (NSV) strips it out. United Spirits' FY26 gross revenue was about ₹27,816 crore, but about ₹15,350 crore of that was excise. Its real revenue was ₹12,467 crore. Every margin in this report is on the net figure unless it says "gross".

02

Big, slow and getting dearer: the size of India's drinking

India is the world's third-largest alcohol market by volume, but its spirits volume barely grew in FY25. Value grows two to three times faster than volume.

Crisil Ratings, using CIABC and industry data, puts India's legal alcohol consumption at 6,308 million litres in FY25: 3,615 million litres of IMFL, 2,661 million litres of beer and only 32 million litres of wine. In the industry's own unit, the 9-litre case of twelve 750 ml bottles, IMFL was about 402 million cases. CIABC's figure is 40.17 crore cases, up 1.4%; Crisil's litres show a 0.6% decline. The small conflict probably reflects rounding and base revisions, and the safe reading is that spirits volume was roughly flat in FY25. Crisil projects IMFL at 3,948 million litres and beer at 3,027 million litres by FY27, growth of 4–6% a year for spirits and 6–7% for beer. India ranks third globally by total alcohol volume.

The five-year record is modest. IMFL volume compounded at 3.1% a year between FY20 and FY25 and beer at 5.4%. Both collapsed in the Covid year (IMFL −12%, beer −29%), recovered by FY23, and have since been held back by state policy rather than by demand. Industry revenue, by contrast, grew at about 11% a year over the three years to 2025, and Crisil expects 10–12% in FY27. The gap between volume growth of 4–6% and value growth of 10–12% is premiumisation and price, and it is the central economic fact of the sub-sector.

How large is the industry in rupees? No authoritative total exists. Crisil's sample of 31 companies, which it says represents about 30% of the industry, had net revenue of ₹47,700 crore in FY25 (₹35,700 crore distillers, ₹12,000 crore brewers), heading to about ₹58,800 crore in FY27. Grossing that up crudely gives industry net revenue of roughly ₹1.5–1.6 lakh crore (INDICATIVE), and a retail value, once state levies and trade margins are added back, of perhaps ₹4–5 lakh crore (INDICATIVE; unsourced). States' combined liquor taxes, excise plus VAT, are commonly put at ~₹3 lakh crore or more a year (INDICATIVE; not verified against the RBI's state-finances data). Karnataka alone collected about ₹41,000 crore of excise in FY26.

Whisky country, with a southern centre of gravity

India is a whisky market. Whisky's share of IMFL volume rose from 62% in FY20 to 65% in FY25, growing at about 4.1% a year. White spirits (vodka and gin) grew fastest at about 5.5% a year but from a tiny base. Brandy, concentrated in the South, grew about 2% a year and rum, strong in the East and in the defence canteen channel, only about 0.6%. Applying those shares to ~402 million cases gives roughly 261 million cases of whisky, 80 million of brandy, 48 million of rum and 12 million of white spirits in FY25 (INDICATIVE arithmetic; Allied Blenders' primer cites a higher ~280 million cases for whisky).

Per-head consumption is the long-term argument for the industry. Indians consume about 3.2 litres of pure alcohol a year against a world average of about 5.1, and the gap is wider still for beer. Exports are small (India-made spirits exports were roughly ₹2,100–3,100 crore a year in CY20–CY24, indicative), although single malts are export-led: Rampur ships to more than 100 countries and Indri to about 28. Globally, the picture is the reverse of India's. IWSR reports that global alcohol volume shrank for a third straight year, Scotch exports fell 4.3% in volume in 2025, and India was the main growth engine, with spirits up about 4% in volume and 6% in value in 2025.

In plain terms

India is not drinking a lot more each year; it is drinking better. Spirits volume grows by mid-single digits in a good year and not at all in a bad one, but the average bottle gets more expensive every year. A company that only sells more cases of cheap whisky is running to stand still; a company that sells a rising share of dearer bottles grows its profit much faster than the market's volume.

03

Who drinks, and why more each year

Ten to twelve million Indians reach legal drinking age every year. Income, urbanisation and occasion drinking push them up the price ladder, while weather, elections and state policy decide any one quarter.

The demand base is demographic. Crisil, citing MOSPI, estimates that 63% of Indians are above the legal drinking age and that 10–12 million new consumers cross it every year. The age itself varies by state: 18 in Goa, Karnataka and Rajasthan among others, 21 in most states, 25 in Punjab and historically in Delhi and for spirits in Maharashtra. Per-capita income has grown at about 6% a year against about 5% for the world, and the urban share of the population, around 36%, is where branded IMFL and beer are bought. Social and occasion drinking (weddings, parties, cocktail culture) and drinking by women are both rising, which helps vodka, gin, flavoured spirits and premium beer more than mass whisky.

Five demand drivers, and how strong each one is

  • New drinkers. The legal-age population adds roughly 2–3% to the drinking base each year. This is the slow, reliable floor under volume.
  • Trading up. Crisil's price-band data show the Popular segment's share of IMFL volume falling from 38.0% in FY20 to 32.9% in FY25 and a projected 31% in FY27, while Luxury rises from 2.0% to 3.4% and 4.1%. Luxury volume compounded at 14.6% a year from 2020 to 2025; Popular at about 0.2%. Section 08 covers this in depth.
  • Category shifts. White spirits and agave are the fastest movers. Radico's Magic Moments vodka sold 3.25 million cases in Q1 FY27, up 43% in volume and 51% in value. IWSR recorded Indian vodka up 14% in volume and 12% in value in 2025, and tequila up 34%. Brandy and rum are flat to slow.
  • Beer substitution. Beer gains when states tax it lightly relative to spirits. Karnataka's switch to strength-based excise in May 2026 made beer and premium spirits relatively cheaper and mass spirits dearer.
  • Distribution reach. When a state reopens private retail (Andhra Pradesh in October 2024) or lists new brands, volumes can jump. When it restricts outlets or raises duty, they fall. This driver is entirely in the state's hands.

Seasonality: beer in summer, spirits in the festive season

The two halves of the industry peak in different quarters. Beer is a summer business: April–June is the peak, and a heat wave can add several percentage points of growth. United Breweries' Q1 FY27 sell-out volume rose 13% in a hot summer, with the beer category up about 13%. The July–September monsoon quarter is the weakest, and United Breweries' operating margin in that quarter fell to 6% in FY26. Spirits peak in October–December, the festive and wedding season. Radico says its Q2 volumes typically run 20–30% above Q1 as stocking for the festive season begins. Election periods also matter: dry days and tighter enforcement depressed sales in Q1 FY25 during the general election.

Channels: canteens, bars and duty-free

Three channels behave differently from ordinary retail. The Canteen Stores Department (CSD), which supplies the armed forces, is a large and price-controlled channel for rum and brandy; Piccadily has moved its Camikara rum into it. The on-trade (bars, pubs, hotels and restaurants) is growing with urban pub culture and is where new brands are tried and premium brands are seen; its economics overlap with the food-and-beverage line of hotels. Travel retail and exports matter mostly for luxury single malts, which use international awards and duty-free visibility to build credibility before they price up at home.

In plain terms

Demand for alcohol in India rises steadily because more people reach drinking age and earn more each year. But in any single quarter the numbers are set by things the companies cannot control: how hot the summer was, whether there was an election, and above all whether a big state changed its tax or its shops. That is why a quarter's volume growth tells you little on its own; you need to know which states drove it.

The demand picture explains why the listed companies talk about "ex-Maharashtra" or "ex-policy-state" growth so often. United Spirits' rest-of-India business, excluding Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh, grew about 10.9% in FY26 against a 7.7% headline. In Q1 FY27 its P&A volume fell 1.3% as reported but rose 6.4% excluding Maharashtra. Underlying demand is healthy; policy subtracts or adds several percentage points in any year. The next two sections explain how.

04

Twenty-eight landlords: the state as owner of the price Deep dive

Excise collected for the states is larger than the companies' own revenue. In 2025–26 single-state decisions in Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Delhi moved national results more than demand did.

Under the Constitution, "intoxicating liquors" are a state subject (State List, Entry 8), and so is the excise duty on them (Entry 51). Article 47, a Directive Principle, urges the state to work towards prohibition. Alcohol for human consumption was kept out of GST in 2017, so each state levies its own excise and VAT and keeps all of it. GST still applies to the industry's inputs, such as glass and cartons, but because the output is outside GST the input tax cannot be credited and becomes a cost. The Centre's levers are narrow: customs duty on imports, FSSAI labelling standards, competition law and advertising rules. Everything that decides the price, the route to market and the shop count belongs to the states.

Excise is bigger than the business

The clearest way to see the state's weight is to compare the excise a company collects with its own revenue. United Spirits reported gross revenue from operations of about ₹27,816 crore in FY26. About ₹15,350 crore of that was excise passed through to state governments, leaving net sales of ₹12,467 crore. The company collected ₹1.23 of tax for every rupee it kept. United Breweries' Q1 FY27 gross revenue of ₹5,919 crore became about ₹3,067 crore net, implying excise of about 48% of gross; Allied Blenders' ₹1,809 crore gross became ₹984 crore net, about 46%.

Four route-to-market models

States differ not only in how much they tax but in how they sell. In corporation states (Tamil Nadu's TASMAC, Karnataka's KSBCL, Telangana's TGBCL, Andhra Pradesh's APSBCL, Kerala's BEVCO, Rajasthan's RSBCL) a state-owned company buys all wholesale stock, decides which brands to list, often sets or approves the price and pays the maker, sometimes slowly. In open states (Maharashtra, Goa, Uttar Pradesh, Haryana and, partly, West Bengal) private wholesalers and licensed retailers compete, and the maker's own sales force matters more. A few states run government retail: Delhi's roughly 792 outlets through four agencies, Kerala's BEVCO and Tamil Nadu's TASMAC shops. And four states and a Union Territory have prohibition: Gujarat (since 1960, with a permit system relaxed for GIFT City in 2023), Bihar (since April 2016), Nagaland, Mizoram and Lakshadweep.

Maharashtra: how one state took a fifth off an industry

Maharashtra is India's largest open IMFL market, and in June 2025 it changed the formula. MML carries a minimum price of ₹148 per 180 ml against ₹205 for IMFL. Crisil notes that IMFL excise in the state rose by more than 50% after 14 stable years; CIABC warned that shelf prices could rise by up to 85%; ISWAI, the body representing the multinational spirits makers, said affected brands fell 35–40% and went to the Bombay High Court over MML's exclusion of their products.

Karnataka: the first strength-based tax

Karnataka, which collected about ₹41,000 crore of excise in FY26, switched on 11 May 2026 to an "alcohol-in-beverage" (AIB) system that taxes the quantity of pure alcohol rather than the declared price band. It also cut the number of IML price slabs from 16 to 8 and will phase the change in over three to four years. The early reported effect, from a single outlet's survey and therefore INDICATIVE, was premium Scotch and mild beer 20–25% cheaper and mass brands 20–30% dearer. Under the old slab system a cheap, strong whisky paid relatively little duty per litre of alcohol; under AIB it pays the same per litre of alcohol as a premium one, so the cheap bottle's price rises most.

The listed companies' first data points fit that logic. Radico's P&A volume in Karnataka rose 83% in Q1 FY27 against a P&A category up about 9%. Tilaknagar reported record Karnataka sales of 3 lakh cases in July 2026, up 30%. United Spirits cited Karnataka slab changes, alongside Maharashtra, for the fall in its Popular realisations. Beer, at 5–8% alcohol, is a structural winner of any strength-based tax. If other states copy Karnataka, the tax system itself would start to push drinkers towards beer and premium spirits.

In plain terms

Most states tax a bottle by its price. Karnataka now taxes it by how much alcohol it contains. That makes a cheap but strong whisky more expensive and a pricier, smoother one or a beer relatively cheaper. It is a tax change that favours exactly the products the big companies are trying to sell more of, and it is the first state to try it.

Andhra Pradesh, Delhi, Telangana and Tamil Nadu

Andhra Pradesh ran liquor retail through the state corporation from 2019 to 2024, with persistent complaints that national brands were kept off shelves. In October 2024 the new government restored private retail, awarding 3,736 outlet licences by lottery and capping the cheapest 180 ml at ₹99 (whether that cap materialised at scale is disputed). United Spirits re-entered the state, a visible FY26 tailwind, and Radico says it holds about a quarter of the market where it distributes directly. Delhi sells through about 792 government outlets with retail margins fixed at ₹50 a bottle for IMFL and ₹100 for foreign liquor. A fixed rupee margin gives a shopkeeper the same reward for a ₹3,000 bottle as for a ₹700 one, so premium brands are under-stocked. Delhi's 2021–22 liberalised policy was withdrawn in 2022 amid a CBI and ED investigation; a new policy is expected in October 2026, reportedly with higher excise, higher fixed margins and more premium stores.

Telangana shows the third role of the state: customer and debtor. TGBCL buys all wholesale stock and pays the maker the gross price including excise. When the state's finances are tight, it pays late. United Breweries suspended beer supplies to the corporation in January 2025, citing unpaid dues (reported at about ₹900 crore, INDICATIVE) and no price revision since 2019, and resumed in February 2025 after a price increase of about 15%. Tamil Nadu's TASMAC is the largest single liquor buyer in the country; the new government's 2026 white paper put FY26 excise duty at ₹11,836 crore, the lowest among peer states, which suggests pressure to raise it. Southern brandy, Tilaknagar's core market, is sold mainly through these corporations.

Why analysts model state by state

Put together, the 2025–26 record shows that policy, not demand, set the national numbers. United Spirits' rest-of-India business grew about 10.9% in FY26 against a 7.7% headline; Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh, in opposite directions, explained the gap. In Q1 FY27 Maharashtra subtracted about 7.7 percentage points from United Spirits' P&A volume growth (+6.4% ex-Maharashtra vs −1.3% reported). Radico's P&A grew 35.8% nationally, with Karnataka at +83%. None of the companies discloses state-level revenue, so investors reconstruct exposures from management commentary: Maharashtra matters most for United Spirits and Allied Blenders, the southern corporation states for Tilaknagar, Uttar Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh for Radico, and Haryana and export markets for Piccadily's Indri.

The pattern repeats on a two-to-three-year cycle: a state raises duty or changes its retail model, volumes fall for three or four quarters, the companies adjust prices and mix, and then another state moves. The companies best placed to absorb this are those with the widest state spread, the strongest premium brands (premium drinkers are less price-sensitive and slabs cap their duty) and balance sheets that can tolerate a delayed payment from a state corporation.

In plain terms

An Indian alcohol company does not have one market; it has about 28, each with its own landlord who can change the rent without notice. A good year for the company is often just a year in which no big state changed the rules. When reading results, always ask which states helped and which hurt, because that usually matters more than anything the company did.

05

The rulebook beyond excise: price approvals, ad bans and the state as debtor

Even where duty is stable, the state controls when prices rise, how brands may advertise and how quickly the makers are paid.

Price approvals: why margins lag costs

Many states require the maker to file a cost sheet and get the MRP or ex-distillery price approved before it can change: Tamil Nadu through TASMAC, Telangana, Karnataka, Rajasthan and Delhi among them. Approvals are typically annual, sometimes rarer. Telangana went from 2019 to 2025 without a beer price revision. The result is a built-in lag of one to three years between an input-cost increase and the price increase that offsets it. In the meantime the maker absorbs the cost. That lag is the mechanism behind the margin troughs of FY23, when ENA and glass inflation pushed United Breweries' operating margin to 8%, Radico's to 11% and Allied Blenders' to 6%. It is also why United Breweries, facing the 2026 West Asia packaging shock, had to negotiate increases in 22 states during Q1 FY27 to bring its estimated cost hit down from ₹400–500 crore to ₹350–400 crore.

In plain terms

A soap company that faces higher costs can raise prices across India next month. A beer or whisky company has to ask each state for permission, one by one, and some states say no for years. So when costs jump, these companies' profits fall first and recover later, often much later.

The state corporation as debtor

In corporation states the government company is the maker's only customer, and it pays the gross price including excise. That inflates receivables when measured against net revenue: a 45-day credit period on gross sales looks like 90-plus days on net sales. Corporations also stretch payments when their state's budget is under pressure. The listed companies' FY26 debtor days on net sales show the effect: about 72 days at Radico, whose sales lean towards open markets and direct distribution, but 106 at United Spirits, 116 at United Breweries, 168 at Allied Blenders and 198 at Tilaknagar (inflated by the Imperial Blue acquisition, since the balance sheet carries a full receivables book against only four months of sales).

The advertising ban and surrogate marketing

Direct advertising of alcohol is banned on television under the Cable Television Networks Rules, and the Consumer Protection Authority's June 2022 guidelines, together with the ASCI code, restrict "surrogate" advertising that promotes a banned product indirectly. Brands therefore advertise same-name extensions: music CDs, club soda, packaged water, glassware and events. Royal Stag's "Make It Large" music and cricket properties, Kingfisher soda and Imperial Blue's music CDs are the familiar examples. Sports sponsorship has been the largest surrogate platform, and United Spirits' ownership of Royal Challengers Bengaluru gave it a year-round one until it agreed to sell the franchise in March 2026. The ban raises the cost of building a new brand and favours companies with existing brand equity and distribution, which partly explains why the industry's leaders have held their positions for decades.

Competition law and the courts

In 2021 the Competition Commission fined the beer industry about ₹873 crore for cartelising prices, with United Breweries' share about ₹752 crore. Carlsberg was also fined; AB InBev, which had acquired SABMiller's Indian business, turned approver and escaped a penalty. United Breweries' appeal is pending before the NCLAT, so the ₹752 crore remains a contingent liability, about 2.4% of its market value. The CCI also clears mergers in the sector: Imperial Blue's sale to Tilaknagar in October 2025 and the RCB sale on 30 June 2026. The courts have intervened before too: the Supreme Court's 2016 order barring liquor sales within 500 metres of highways, effective April 2017 and later diluted, shut thousands of outlets for months.

In plain terms

Beyond tax, the state decides when prices can rise, how brands can advertise and how fast the makers get paid. Each of those rules quietly favours big, established companies with strong brands and deep pockets: they can wait for price approvals, carry slow-paying receivables and keep their brands visible without TV ads. That is why this industry has so few successful new entrants.

China, unlike in most Indian manufacturing sub-sectors, is irrelevant here except for some packaging machinery.

In plain terms

Two global companies, Diageo and Pernod, have long owned the expensive end of Indian whisky. Indian family firms own most of the cheap end and are now buying their way up. Beer is mostly Kingfisher. Because almost every listed company has a dominant owner and very few shares trade freely, the stocks are scarce, and that scarcity is part of why they are expensive.

A Diageo-style brand house, an integrated premiumiser, two mass-to-premium volume houses, a sugar mill turned single-malt distiller and a brewer: the same industry, five different sets of economics.

Why the economics differ

Realisation per case is the first reason. Moving from a mass to a premium bottle can raise net revenue per case two to five times while the cost of the liquid, glass and packaging per case rises much less, so gross margin and EBITDA per case climb with mix. United Spirits' estimated NSV of about ₹2,000 a case (INDICATIVE) is nearly double Allied Blenders' ₹1,093.

Ownership of the base spirit is the second. Radico and Piccadily distil their own ENA and malt. That protects them when grain and ENA prices spike (Section 10), but it adds fixed assets and links part of their revenue to low-margin bulk alcohol and fuel ethanol. United Spirits buys most of its base spirit and keeps capital light, which is one reason its return on capital is the highest in the group.

The brewer's ceiling is the third. Beer is cheap per litre, heavy to ship and taxed per litre, its glass circulates and breaks, and its prices need approval in about 30 states. United Breweries has the gross margin of a consumer company and the EBITDA margin of a logistics business.

Leverage is the fourth. Tilaknagar borrowed to buy Imperial Blue and Allied Blenders rebuilt debt on working capital after its IPO. Their FY26 interest bills (₹113 crore and ₹135 crore) take a large bite out of EBITDA before it reaches profit, so their price-to-earnings ratios look far higher than their EV/EBITDA multiples.

The premiumisation ladder, and the Indian single malt Deep dive

The only lever the companies fully control. Every leader's margin gain since FY23 has come from selling a dearer mix, and India's own single malts now outsell Scotch single malts at home.

Industry volume grows 4–6% a year; industry value grows 10–12%. The difference is premiumisation: drinkers moving from a ₹600 bottle to a ₹1,000 one, from a ₹1,000 bottle to a ₹2,000 one, and at the top from blended whisky to single malt. Because the maker's rupee profit per bottle rises much faster than the shelf price (Section 01), each step up the ladder multiplies the company's earnings. This is the one growth engine the companies control themselves, and they all describe their strategy in its terms.

Single malt is a different business from blended whisky in one crucial way: time. Malt spirit has to mature in casks for three to five years before it can be sold, and in India's heat it loses about 10–12% of its volume a year to evaporation (the "angel's share"), against about 2% in Scotland. Scotch after the FTA: half the duty, the same excise

The India–UK agreement in force from 15 July 2026 halves customs duty on Scotch at once and takes it to 40% over ten years. State excise does not change, so the shelf-price effect is much smaller than the headline.

India and the UK signed their Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA) on 24 July 2025, and it entered into force on 15 July 2026. For Scotch whisky, customs duty fell from 150% to 75% immediately and steps down to 40% by the tenth year, around 2036. For gin and other UK spirits the path is slower on the published tariff schedule: 110% in year one, then roughly 4 points a year to 75% from year ten (some press reports say gin follows the Scotch path; the schedule shows the slower one). The agreement also sets minimum import prices of US$5 per litre CIF for bulk spirits and US$6 per 750 ml bottle, indexed every 15 years to the spirits wholesale price index; the reading of the schedule in the source used here applies these to gin and other UK spirits rather than to Scotch, which instead faces rules of origin.

Why a 50% duty cut becomes a 7–8% price cut

Customs duty is levied on the import value of the liquid, which for a bottle of Scotch is a modest part of the Indian shelf price. On top of the duty-paid cost sit state excise, VAT and trade margins, none of which the agreement touches. The obvious winners are the importers of bottled-in-origin Scotch, Diageo and Pernod. Less obvious, and probably more important for the listed companies, is bulk Scotch as an ingredient. The grain-to-glass squeeze: how ethanol blending rewired the cost curve Deep dive

ENA rose about 50% in seven years as fuel ethanol bid for the same grain, and the 2026 West Asia crisis hit glass, packaging and freight. Costs move monthly; prices move when states allow.

More than half of an IMFL maker's cost sits in three inputs: the base spirit, the glass bottle and the packaging. All three have been inflationary since FY20, and two of them are now linked to energy policy. The base spirit competes with India's ethanol-blending programme for grain and molasses; glass is made in gas-fired furnaces whose costs rose with the 2026 West Asia crisis. Because shelf prices need state approval, these shocks hit margins for two to six quarters before price increases catch up. United Breweries' FY26 profit fell 6.6% despite higher volumes, and Tilaknagar's Q1 FY27 EBITDA margin fell 751 basis points, for exactly this reason.

Why: fuel ethanol bids for the same grain

India reached 20% ethanol blending in petrol (E20) in 2025. To get there, the government set administered prices at which the oil marketing companies buy ethanol from distillers, giving them an assured, guaranteed buyer. For the 2024–25 ethanol supply year the procurement prices, as reported and not re-verified here, were about ₹71.86 a litre for ethanol from maize or damaged grain, ₹58.50 from FCI rice, ₹57.97 from C-heavy molasses, ₹60.73 from B-heavy molasses and ₹65.61 from cane juice. Grain distilleries diverted maize and rice towards fuel; maize prices rose and India became a net maize importer in 2024. Potable ENA now has to price at or above what a distiller could earn selling ethanol to an oil company, so the fuel price has become a floor under the drinks industry's main raw material. ICRA warned that grain diversion "could keep prices firm and make ENA costlier". The pressure eased somewhat in late 2025: FCI cut the price of rice sold to distilleries from ₹2,800 to about ₹2,250 a quintal in November 2024, and Emkay reported in January 2026 that grain prices around ₹22–23 (assumed per kg) had softened.

In plain terms

The alcohol in your whisky and the ethanol mixed into your petrol come from the same grain and the same distilleries. Since the government guarantees a price for fuel ethanol, distillers will only sell drinking alcohol if it pays at least as well. So petrol policy now sets a minimum price for the main ingredient of Indian whisky.

A company that distils its own ENA from grain pays the cost of grain and processing rather than the market price of ENA, and the gap widens when ENA is scarce. Radico's primer puts its captive cost advantage at ₹6–9 a litre; Emkay estimated the industry's make-versus-buy gap at ₹10–11 a litre in January 2026. Radico has about 33 crore litres a year of captive ENA capacity including a joint venture, against a requirement of 28–30 crore litres, so it is self-sufficient with a surplus that it sells as bulk spirit or ethanol. At ₹6–9 a litre on 28–30 crore litres, the captive edge is worth roughly ₹170–270 crore a year (INDICATIVE), equal to about 16–26% of Radico's FY26 EBITDA of ₹1,022 crore. Glass, packaging and the 2026 West Asia shock

Glass is the second-largest cost and the least discussed. Bottle glass is made from sand, soda ash and limestone melted in furnaces fired by natural gas or fuel oil, so its price moves with energy. The domestic supply base is small: AGI Greenpac, Piramal Glass and Hindusthan National Glass (which went through insolvency and was acquired by AGI). Tilaknagar's Q1 FY27 EBITDA margin fell to 16.1% from 23.6%, which it attributed to glass inflation and Imperial Blue transition costs. Radico quantified packaging volatility at about ₹30 crore in Q1 FY27 and still expanded margins, because its mix improved faster.

Who is most exposed

Exposure depends on three things: the share of mass products (where ENA and glass are a larger share of the price), whether the company owns its spirit, and how quickly its key states grant price increases. Allied Blenders and Tilaknagar, with large mass and semi-premium volumes and partial integration, are the most exposed spirits makers; a ₹5 rise in ENA per litre costs a regular whisky about 13% of its EBITDA per case (Section 01, illustrative). United Breweries is the most exposed overall, because beer's liquid is cheap and its packaging is heavy. Radico and Piccadily are protected on spirit but not on glass. United Spirits is the least exposed in percentage terms because ENA and glass are a small share of a premium bottle's price, and the FTA cut in Scotch duty partly offsets its other input inflation.

In plain terms

When grain or glass gets more expensive, the alcohol companies cannot simply raise prices; they have to wait for each state to approve. Companies selling cheap bottles suffer most because raw materials are a bigger share of a cheap bottle. Companies that make their own alcohol suffer less. The listed companies' margins over the next year depend heavily on whether ENA really reaches ₹80 and how quickly the glass shock fades.

Beer's regulatory ceiling: a great market at a 9% margin Deep dive

Beer volume grows faster than spirits and India drinks very little of it per head, yet Kingfisher's maker has earned a 9% EBITDA margin for six years. The market still pays about 92 times its earnings.

On paper beer is the better market. Crisil projects beer volume growing 6–7% a year, from 2,661 million litres in FY25 to 3,027 million in FY27, faster than spirits. Why beer earns less

Four features cap beer's margin in India. Excise per litre on a cheap liquid: many states tax beer by volume, so the state's share of a ₹180 bottle is large relative to the brewer's ₹62 of net revenue. Two centuries, six shocks: how the industry got its shape

A colonial-era trade rolled up by one tycoon, taken over by multinationals after his empire collapsed, repeatedly hit by state policy, and now consolidating again around premium brands.

The industry's roots go back to McDowell & Co. (1826) and the colonial distillers such as Shaw Wallace and Mohan Meakin. Its modern shape was set by Vijay Mallya's UB Group, which rolled up spirits into United Spirits and beer into United Breweries, the home of Kingfisher. Other families built rival franchises: the Khaitans bought the loss-making Rampur Distillery for ₹16 lakh in 1972 and turned it into Radico; Kishore Chhabria created Officer's Choice in 1988; the Dahanukars built Tilaknagar on brandy. The 2000s were the volume boom: Radico launched 8PM (1999) and Magic Moments (2006), Royal Stag and Officer's Choice became multi-million-case brands, UB bought Shaw Wallace (2005) and Scotland's Whyte & Mackay (2007) with debt, and Heineken entered United Breweries through the break-up of Scottish & Newcastle in 2008.

Six shocks then reshaped it. The Kingfisher collapse (2012–14): when Kingfisher Airlines failed, Mallya's pledged shares passed to lenders and Diageo took control of United Spirits in 2013–14; Heineken took control of United Breweries in June 2021. The regulatory wave (2016–17): Bihar's prohibition from April 2016, the Supreme Court's highway ban effective April 2017, Kerala's bar closures and demonetisation, all within about two years. Covid (2020): lockdown closed shops from March to May 2020; reopening brought punitive surcharges such as Delhi's 70% "corona cess" and Andhra's 75% increases; IMFL volume fell 12% and beer 29% in FY21. Input inflation (2021–23): ENA rose from about ₹58 to ₹63 a litre and glass inflated, and margins troughed in FY23 at 13% for United Spirits, 11% for Radico, 8% for United Breweries and 6% for Allied Blenders. Policy whiplash (2022–26): Delhi's liberalised policy scrapped amid a CBI and ED investigation (2022), Andhra Pradesh's return to private retail (2024), Maharashtra's 4.5× duty and MML (2025), Karnataka's strength-based tax (2026). And consolidation and trade reform (2022–26): the Inbrew disposal, Allied Blenders' IPO, Imperial Blue, the RCB sale and the India–UK agreement.

The equation: what management controls and what it does not

Every spirits company's profit can be written as one chain. Net revenue equals cases sold times the net realisation per case (after excise), summed across states and price bands. EBITDA equals net revenue times gross margin, minus advertising and promotion, minus overheads. The chain looks simple; what makes it useful is knowing which links management controls. It controls mix (which brands it pushes), A&P, overheads and, partly, volume. It does not control excise, the timing of price increases, the price of ENA and glass, or the policy shocks that swing volume in a state.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). A brand house sells 10 million cases a year at an average net realisation of ₹1,500 a case, so net revenue is ₹1,500 crore. If excise is about 52% of gross, its statutory "revenue from operations" is about ₹3,125 crore, of which about ₹1,625 crore is excise passed to the states. With a 44% gross margin it earns ₹660 crore of gross profit. It spends 9% of net revenue (₹135 crore) on A&P and ₹300 crore on overheads, leaving EBITDA of ₹225 crore: a 15% margin, or ₹225 a case. If it shifts one million cases from a ₹1,100 Popular brand to a ₹2,100 P&A brand, net revenue rises by ₹100 crore with no extra volume, and because the extra realisation comes with only modestly higher costs, most of that flows to gross profit. That is premiumisation in one sentence.

The same arithmetic explains why the market values volume and mix so differently. Ten per cent more cases at the same mix lifts EBITDA by roughly 10% plus a little operating leverage, but it also needs 10% more working capital, glass and trucks. Ten per cent more realisation from mix lifts gross profit by more than 10%, because the extra rupees carry almost no extra material cost, and needs little extra capital. A company that grows EBITDA per case is compounding on a base the states and input markets cannot easily take away; a company that grows only cases is exposed to every duty change and every ENA spike on a larger base. The KPIs below are ordered to follow the equation: volume, mix and realisation first, then the cost lines, then the cash and capital measures that decide how much of the profit an investor actually captures.

In plain terms

An alcohol company's profit is: how many cases it sells, times what it keeps per case after tax, times the share left after paying for alcohol, glass and boxes, minus advertising and running costs. The company can choose which brands to push and how much to advertise. The state decides the tax and when prices can rise; grain and energy markets decide what the alcohol and glass cost.

1. Volume (million cases)

What it is: the number of 9-litre cases (twelve 750 ml bottles) sold in a period. Beer companies use 7.8-litre cases of twelve 650 ml bottles. Formula: cases sold, usually own-brand IMFL; state whether royalty or franchise brands are included. Worked example: Allied Blenders sold 35.9 million cases in FY26, up 8.5%, and 9.0 million in Q1 FY27, up 6.2%. Drivers: new drinkers, distribution, competitive pricing and, above all, state policy. Benchmark: industry volume grows 4–6% a year in a normal year (Crisil); above 8% is good, below 3% weak, but always check for acquisitions and royalty brands. Comparison: Radico 36.6 million (FY26), Allied Blenders 35.9 million, Tilaknagar about 20 million in FY26 and 8.7 million in Q1 FY27 after Imperial Blue. United Spirits does not disclose cases; about 60–65 million is an estimate (INDICATIVE), not the stale "80 million" quoted in its primer. United Breweries publishes growth rates only (Q1 FY27 sell-out +13%, sell-in +9%). Piccadily does not disclose branded cases. The trap: volume has three bases: own brands only, own plus royalty brands, and sell-in (shipped to trade) versus sell-out (bought by consumers). Radico's FY26 growth is +29.8% on one base and +16.8% on another.

2. P&A share

What it is: the share of sales coming from Prestige & Above brands. Formula: P&A net sales ÷ total net sales (value basis) or P&A cases ÷ total cases (volume basis). Worked example: United Spirits' Q1 FY27 P&A net sales of ₹2,478 crore on standalone net sales of ₹2,703 crore gives 91.7%. Drivers: brand investment, new launches (ICONiQ White), disposals of mass brands (Inbrew), state taxes that make premium relatively cheaper (Karnataka). Benchmark: above 80% of value is good, 50–80% average, below 50% weak; on volume, above 60% good. Comparison: United Spirits 91.7% of value; Radico 70.3% of IMFL value and 53.1% of IMFL volume; Allied Blenders 57.3% of value and 47.2% of volume, up from about 30% of value in FY23; Tilaknagar claims more than 90% of volume. The trap: every company draws the P&A line at a different price, so levels are not comparable across companies. Track each company's own share over time, and prefer value to volume.

3. Net realisation per case

What it is: net revenue, after excise, earned per case. Formula: net revenue ÷ cases. Worked example: Tilaknagar's Q1 FY27 net revenue of ₹1,046 crore on 8.7 million cases is ₹1,202 a case. Drivers: mix (the biggest), list-price increases granted by states, trade discounts, and the share of low-realisation bulk or royalty sales. Benchmark: above ₹2,000 good, ₹1,200–2,000 average, below ₹1,200 weak for IMFL. Comparison: United Spirits about ₹1,950–2,050 (INDICATIVE, since its cases are estimated), up from about ₹1,605 in FY23; Radico ₹1,684 on total Q1 FY27 revenue (including bulk ENA) and an estimated ₹1,290–1,360 for IMFL alone; Tilaknagar ₹1,202; Allied Blenders ₹1,093 (FY26). A strong beer case earns about ₹744 per 7.8-litre case in the illustrative example. The trap: dividing total revenue (including bulk spirit, ethanol or sugar) by branded cases overstates branded realisation; dividing gross revenue by cases mixes in excise.

4. Excise as a share of gross revenue

What it is: the share of statutory revenue that is tax collected for the state. Formula: excise duty ÷ gross revenue from operations. Worked example: United Spirits' ₹15,350 crore of excise ÷ ₹27,816 crore of gross revenue = 55%. Drivers: state mix (high-tax states push it up), price-point mix (slab caps make premium bottles' share lower), and policy changes. Benchmark: 45–60% is typical; it is a structural exposure measure, not a performance score. Comparison: United Spirits about 55% (FY26), United Breweries about 48% and Allied Blenders about 46% (Q1 FY27, derived). Radico, Tilaknagar and Piccadily: n/a (gross figures not sourced). The trap: headline margins quoted "on revenue from operations" are on the gross figure. United Spirits' margin looks like about 8–9% on gross and about 18% on net; same company, same year.

In plain terms

Before comparing any margin, check whether it is calculated on revenue including the tax collected for the state (gross) or excluding it (net). The same company can show a 9% margin on one and an 18% margin on the other. This report always uses net, and so should you.

5. Gross margin

What it is: the share of net revenue left after the cost of materials (spirit, malt, glass, packaging). Formula: (net revenue − material costs) ÷ net revenue. Worked example: in the illustrative P&A bottle, net revenue of ₹288 minus COGS of ₹150 leaves ₹138, a 48% gross margin. Drivers: mix (up), ENA and glass prices (down when they rise), Scotch duty (the FTA helps), and price increases granted by states. Benchmark: for spirits above 47% good, 40–47% average, below 40% weak; for beer above 45% good. Comparison: Radico 49.1% in Q1 FY27 (up 610 basis points year on year); United Spirits 46.4% in FY26 and 46.1% in Q1 FY27; United Breweries about 41% in Q1 FY27 (45.4% in Q4 FY26); Crisil's composite about 41%. Allied Blenders, Tilaknagar and Piccadily gross margins were not sourced on a comparable basis. The trap: some companies include bottling fees and inward freight in COGS, others in overheads, so a 2–3 point gap may be classification rather than economics.

6. A&P as a share of net revenue

What it is: spending on advertising (mostly surrogate), sponsorships and trade promotion. Formula: A&P ÷ net revenue. Worked example: United Spirits spent ₹1,295 crore on A&P in FY26 on net sales of ₹12,467 crore, about 10.4%. Drivers: launches, competitive intensity, sponsorship cycles (IPL), and management's choice to reinvest gross-margin gains. Benchmark: 9–12% for premium brand houses investing in brands; 6–9% average; below 5% signals under-investment, above 13% may mean buying share. Comparison: United Spirits 11.5% in Q1 FY27 with FY27 guidance of 10.5–11%; Radico 7–8%; Allied Blenders and Tilaknagar roughly 5–8% (INDICATIVE); United Breweries and Piccadily not disclosed on a comparable basis. The trap: A&P is the easiest line to cut to protect a quarter's EBITDA. 7. EBITDA margin (net basis)

What it is: operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, as a share of net revenue. Formula: EBITDA ÷ net revenue. Worked example: Radico's Q1 FY27 EBITDA of ₹349 crore on ₹1,684 crore of net revenue is 20.7%. Drivers: gross margin, A&P choices, operating leverage on overheads, and segment mix (bulk ENA and sugar dilute). Benchmark: spirits above 18% good, 13–18% average, below 13% weak; beer above 12% good, 8–12% average. Comparison (Q1 FY27): Radico 20.7%, Piccadily 18.5% (company), Tilaknagar 16.1% (down 751 basis points), United Spirits 16.0% (standalone, down 30 basis points), Allied Blenders 12.2% (company), United Breweries about 9.2%. The trap: quarters are seasonal (spirits peak in Q3, beer in Q1), so compare a quarter with the same quarter a year earlier; and check whether "EBITDA" is screener's operating profit or the company's figure: Tilaknagar's FY26 EBITDA is ₹419 crore on screener and about ₹352 crore on the company's basis.

8. EBITDA per case

What it is: rupees of operating profit earned on each case. Formula: EBITDA ÷ cases. Worked example: Allied Blenders' FY26 EBITDA of ₹542 crore (screener) on 35.9 million cases is ₹151 a case, about ₹12.6 a bottle; on the company's ₹568 crore it is ₹158. Drivers: realisation per case and gross margin, less A&P and overheads per case. It rises fastest with mix. Benchmark: above ₹300 good, ₹150–300 average, below ₹150 weak. Comparison: United Spirits about ₹350–380 (INDICATIVE); Radico about ₹348 in Q1 FY27 and ₹279 for FY26 (both including non-IMFL earnings); Tilaknagar about ₹194 (Q1 FY27); Allied Blenders ₹151–158. The trap: this is the most informative single number for valuation (EV ≈ cases × EBITDA per case × multiple), but only if cases and EBITDA are on the same basis; adding bulk-spirit EBITDA to branded cases flatters it.

9. State concentration

What it is: how much of a company's sales come from its largest state or group of states. Formula: top-state sales ÷ total sales (rarely disclosed). Worked example: in Q1 FY27, Maharashtra alone turned United Spirits' P&A volume from +6.4% to −1.3%, a swing of 7.7 percentage points. Drivers: history (Tilaknagar's southern brandy), home-state advantage (Radico in Uttar Pradesh), and corporation listings. Benchmark: no state above 15% is good; 15–25% average; above 25% weak. Comparison: none of the six discloses state-level revenue; exposures come from commentary. United Spirits and Allied Blenders are most exposed to Maharashtra; Tilaknagar to the southern corporation states; Radico to Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka; Piccadily to Haryana and exports. The trap: a company reporting strong national growth may simply have been lucky with policy that year; ask for ex-policy-state growth, as United Spirits now reports.

10. ENA cost and captive share

What it is: the price of the base spirit, and the share of a company's needs it makes itself. Formula: ₹ per litre of ENA; captive share = own ENA production ÷ requirement. Worked example: a regular bottle needs about 0.34 litres of ENA; at ₹73 a litre that is about ₹25, or 27% of the maker's ₹92 net revenue (illustrative). Drivers: grain and molasses prices, fuel-ethanol procurement prices, FCI rice sales, monsoon. Benchmark: captive share above 100% good, 50–100% average, below 50% weak (for mass-heavy portfolios). Comparison: Radico about 110–118% (33 crore litres against 28–30 crore litres); Piccadily self-sufficient with surplus; Allied Blenders and Tilaknagar partial; United Spirits largely buys. Industry ENA about ₹73 a litre in FY25, a projected ₹80 in FY27 (Crisil; units assumed). The trap: captive capacity protects margins in a spike but drags them when ENA is cheap and adds capital; a company that sells its surplus as fuel ethanol reports that as low-margin revenue.

11. Capacity and maturing inventory

What it is: distillation capacity, quoted in kilolitres per day (KLPD), and the stock of maturing spirit. Formula: KLPD × operating days ≈ annual litres; inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods × 365. Worked example: 250 KLPD running 330 days produces about 8.25 crore litres a year. Drivers: capex (Piccadily's Chhattisgarh plant, Radico's Sitapur), maturation policy (single malts need 3–5 years), and working-capital discipline. Benchmark: 120–220 inventory days is normal for blenders; above 250 is a warning for a blender but by design for a malt maker. Comparison: Piccadily 369 days (maturing malt; capacity heading to about 460 KLPD); Tilaknagar 319 (Imperial Blue stock build); Radico 221; Allied Blenders 221; United Spirits 216. United Breweries' screener figure of 432 days is a calculation quirk (excise-inclusive balances on net sales) and should be ignored. The trap: rising inventory at a blender usually means unsold stock or a weak quarter; at a malt maker it can mean future revenue. Ask which.

12. Receivable days and cash conversion

What it is: how long customers (often state corporations) take to pay, and how much EBITDA turns into operating cash. Formula: receivables ÷ net revenue × 365; cash conversion = operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: United Spirits' FY26 operating cash flow of ₹1,459 crore on EBITDA of ₹2,279 crore is a 64% conversion. Drivers: state mix (corporation states pay slowly and pay gross), working-capital builds after acquisitions or IPOs, and inventory policy. Benchmark: debtor days below 75 good, 75–120 average, above 120 weak; cash conversion above 70% good. Comparison: debtor days Radico 72, Piccadily 89, United Spirits 106, United Breweries 116, Allied Blenders 168, Tilaknagar 198 (Section 05). Cash conversion in FY26: Allied Blenders 67%, United Spirits 64%, United Breweries 54%, Piccadily 51%, Tilaknagar −106%. The trap: because corporations owe the gross price, debtor days measured on net revenue look twice as long as the true credit period. 13. Net debt to EBITDA

What it is: how many years of EBITDA it would take to repay net borrowings. Formula: (borrowings − cash and liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: Tilaknagar's net debt of about ₹2,100 crore at June 2026 ÷ annualised Q1 FY27 EBITDA of about ₹676 crore is about 3.1×. Drivers: acquisitions, working capital, capex, dividends. Benchmark: below 0.5× good, 0.5–2× average, above 2× weak. Comparison: United Spirits net cash (proxy −0.3×, and much more if the RCB sale closes); Radico about 0.25× and targeting debt-free by Q2 FY27; United Breweries about 1.6× on borrowings of ₹1,317 crore including leases; Allied Blenders 1.7× (company, June 2026; about 2.1× on gross borrowings); Piccadily about 2.0×; Tilaknagar about 3.1× annualised (about 4.3× on trailing EBITDA). The trap: screener has no cash line, so "borrowings minus investments" overstates net debt for cash-rich companies; and stitched acquisition years make trailing EBITDA too low, so use annualised post-deal EBITDA but say so.

14. Return on capital employed

What it is: operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + net debt), or ÷ (fixed assets + net working capital). Worked example: in the illustrative brand house below, EBIT of ₹195 crore on capital employed of about ₹1,008 crore is about 19%. Drivers: margin, asset intensity (owned distilleries, breweries and maturing stock raise capital; tie-up bottling lowers it), working capital and acquisitions (goodwill and brands inflate capital). Benchmark: above 20% good, 12–20% average, below 12% weak. Comparison (FY26): United Spirits 26.4%, Radico 24.2%, Allied Blenders 18.4%, Piccadily 18.0%, Tilaknagar 11.4% and United Breweries 10.7%. The trap: Tilaknagar's capital now includes about ₹3,400 crore of acquired brand value, so its ROCE will look low until Imperial Blue's earnings catch up; Piccadily's includes years of maturing malt.

15. Valuation per case: what the market pays for each case of volume

What it is: enterprise value divided by annual cases, a way to compare what investors pay for a company's volume with what acquirers pay for brands. Formula: (market cap + net debt) ÷ annual cases. Worked example: Allied Blenders' EV of about ₹21,887 crore (market cap ₹20,736 crore plus proxy net debt ₹1,151 crore) on 35.9 million cases is about ₹6,100 per annual case. Drivers: EBITDA per case and the multiple the market applies to it. Benchmark: there is no fixed range; what matters is the gap between listed values and transaction prices. Comparison (INDICATIVE, because United Spirits' cases are estimated and Tilaknagar's are annualised): Radico about ₹16,300 per case, United Spirits about ₹15,600, Allied Blenders about ₹6,100, Tilaknagar about ₹4,500. Tilaknagar paid Pernod about ₹1,540 per case for Imperial Blue (₹3,442 crore for 22.4 million cases). The trap: a high value per case is justified only by high EBITDA per case; Radico and United Spirits command similar values per case, but United Spirits earns it with a richer mix and Radico with faster growth.

The comparison makes one point vivid. The stock market values a case of Tilaknagar's volume, most of it Imperial Blue, at roughly three times what Tilaknagar paid Pernod for the same brand's cases in 2025. Either Pernod sold cheaply because a mass brand did not fit its premium strategy, or the market is pricing in a margin improvement that Tilaknagar has yet to deliver: its Q1 FY27 EBITDA per case was about ₹194 against management's aim of 16–18% margins. It is the clearest example in the sector of how much of the valuation rests on future mix rather than current earnings.

The anatomy shows three things. First, more than half of net revenue goes on materials, so a 10% rise in ENA and glass together (₹55 crore) would cut EBITDA by about a quarter unless prices rise. Second, the business ties up about ₹1,000 crore of capital to produce ₹225 crore of EBITDA, mostly in receivables and inventory rather than plants; working capital, not capex, is the main use of cash. Third, interest of ₹45 crore takes a fifth of EBITDA, which is why the leveraged makers' earnings multiples look so much higher than their EV/EBITDA multiples. If the same company moved one million cases from Popular to P&A, net revenue would rise by ₹100 crore with no extra volume and little extra capital.

The anatomy shifts predictably across the archetypes. For United Spirits, the same template would show a higher realisation (about ₹2,000 a case), a gross margin near 46%, A&P above 10% and EBITDA near 18%, with little debt and lighter fixed assets because much bottling is done by tie-up partners, which is why its ROCE is above 26%. For Radico, ENA would cost less than the market price because it is distilled in-house, but fixed assets would be heavier. For Piccadily, inventory would be far larger because malt must age for years. For a brewer, materials would be a smaller share but freight, glass handling and energy larger, and EBITDA would be nearer 9%. Using one template for all six makes the differences visible: they come from mix, ownership of the base spirit and working capital, not from any mystery in the accounts.

In plain terms

A typical mid-size whisky company keeps about ₹15 of every ₹100 it earns after tax-collection as operating profit, and about ₹7.50 as final profit. Most of its money is tied up waiting to be paid by state corporations and sitting in warehouses as stock, not in factories. Its best lever is to sell a dearer mix; its biggest risks are raw-material prices and the states.

Definition traps, collected

Almost every disagreement about these companies' numbers comes from one of the traps below. They matter more in this sub-sector than in most because the excise wedge doubles the size of the revenue line, because three of the six went through a deal or a reclassification in FY26, and because no two companies define their premium segment, their volumes or their EBITDA the same way. Before comparing any two figures, check each against this list.

  • Gross vs net revenue. Statutory revenue includes excise; net revenue does not. Never add gross and net figures across companies or compare a gross-basis margin with a net-basis one. Screener's "Sales" for these six is net.
  • Three kinds of volume. Own brands vs own plus royalty brands (Radico's "Regular" includes royalty brands); sell-in vs sell-out (United Breweries' Q1 FY27: +9% vs +13%); 9-litre spirits cases vs 7.8-litre beer cases.
  • P&A has no common definition. Value or volume, and each company's own price line. Crisil's industry 67.1% uses broader bands.
  • United Spirits' sports revenue. Consolidated figures appear to include RCB's IPL revenue in earlier periods but not after it was classified as held for sale, so screener shows FY26 growth of +3.3% and Q1 FY27 of −10.4%, against company like-for-like NSV growth of +7.7% and +6.0%. Use the company figures.
  • Tilaknagar's stitched year. FY26 includes about four months of Imperial Blue. Reported PAT ₹21 Cr vs "adjusted" ₹232 Cr; EBITDA ₹419 Cr (screener) vs ₹352 Cr (company). Trailing P/E is not meaningful; use EV/EBITDA on annualised post-deal EBITDA and say so.
  • Piccadily is three businesses. Its blended 23% margin is not a single-malt margin; branded alcobev is ~30% of revenue. Company revenue (₹270.5 Cr in Q1 FY27) and screener revenue (₹251 Cr) also differ.
  • Beer is not spirits. Never compute a single "sub-sector margin" without splitting the brewer out (17.4% vs 8.7% in FY26).
  • Working-capital ratios. Debtor days on net sales are inflated by gross-of-excise receivables; United Breweries' 432 inventory days and 361 payable days on screener are a calculation quirk.
  • Net debt proxies. Screener-based "borrowings minus investments" ignores cash and overstates net debt; borrowings include lease liabilities.
  • Company vs screener EBITDA. Allied Blenders ₹568 Cr vs ₹542 Cr; Tilaknagar ₹352 Cr vs ₹419 Cr. State which one you use.

How to read an alcohol company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Net revenue first, and its basis. Find net revenue (NSV, net sales, "income from operations net of excise"). If only gross is given, subtract excise. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter: spirits peak in Q3 and beer in Q1.
  2. Volume, with its basis. Cases, split into P&A and Popular (or Regular). Note whether royalty brands, acquisitions or sell-in vs sell-out distort the comparison.
  3. Price/mix. NSV growth minus volume growth. Positive and rising means premiumisation is working; negative means discounting or a mass-heavy shift.
  4. The state commentary. Which states helped and hurt? Is there an "ex-Maharashtra" or "ex-policy states" number? Any new duty, retail change or price approval?
  5. Gross margin and its explanation. Look for ENA, glass and packaging commentary and for price increases granted. A gross-margin rise with flat mix usually means input relief, which can reverse.
  6. A&P. As a % of NSV. A falling A&P ratio that props up EBITDA is a warning sign.
  7. EBITDA margin and EBITDA per case. Check whether the figure is company-defined or screener operating profit, and whether bulk ENA or sugar is mixed in.
  8. Below the line. Other income (United Spirits' Q1 FY27 PAT included about ₹150 Cr of one-off dividend income), exceptional items (Tilaknagar's transition costs), interest and tax.
  9. Cash and working capital. Receivables, inventory and operating cash flow, at least at half-year. Rising receivables in corporation states are an early warning.
  10. Debt and guidance. Net debt vs targets (Tilaknagar's ₹1,700 Cr by March 2027; Radico debt-free), and the guidance on P&A growth, margins and A&P.

Red flags: volume growth driven by sell-in while sell-out lags; gross margin rising only because A&P was cut; receivables growing faster than revenue; net debt rising after an acquisition when management guided it down; "adjusted" profit that stays far above reported profit for more than a few quarters; and growth that disappears once a single state is excluded.

Applied to the latest common quarter, Q1 FY27, the checklist sorts the six quickly. Radico passes almost every step: volume barely grew, but price/mix was strongly positive, P&A rose 35.8%, gross margin rose 610 basis points with A&P held at 7–8%, and net debt is heading to zero. United Spirits shows healthy price/mix (P&A net sales +10.1%) and a better gross margin, but volume fell in Maharashtra, it chose to spend more on A&P, and its PAT growth of 51.6% was flattered by about ₹150 crore of one-off dividend income. Allied Blenders grew volume and P&A but its margin slipped to 12.2% and interest still takes about a quarter of EBITDA. Tilaknagar doubled revenue through the acquisition, but its margin fell 751 basis points, adjusted profit remained far above reported profit and net debt rose to ₹2,100 crore. United Breweries sold more beer (sell-out +13%) and cleaned up trade inventory, but its gross margin fell to about 41% and PAT fell 9.6%. Piccadily grew branded alcobev 47% but its blended margin fell to 18.5% as new capacity added depreciation and the bulk businesses diluted the mix. The Q2 FY27 reports on 27–28 October will be the first to show a full quarter of the FTA and Karnataka's new tax, and they cover the spirits makers' pre-festive stocking quarter, so read price/mix and the state commentary first.

In plain terms

To read an alcohol company's results quickly: use net revenue, split growth into volume and price/mix, ask which states moved the numbers, check whether margins rose because of better mix or just cheaper inputs or lower advertising, and make sure the profit turned into cash. Most surprises in this sector are hiding in the state commentary and the working capital.

How to read the table

Start with the split between spirits and beer. The five spirits makers earned a 17.4% EBITDA margin on ₹25,773 crore of net revenue; United Breweries earned 8.7% on ₹9,240 crore. Blending them into a single 15.1% hides both. The market values the two groups at almost identical EV/EBITDA multiples (about 45× and 41×), which means investors pay nearly as much for each rupee of the brewer's thin EBITDA as for the spirits makers' richer one, a bet on beer margins improving.

Then look at United Spirits' weight. It is 36% of revenue, 43% of EBITDA and 57% of profit, but only 43% of market value, because its P/E of 53.5× is the lowest of the large names. Excluding it, the other five trade at about 94 times combined FY26 profit and 45 times EBITDA, with a combined ROE of only about 10%. Much of the sector's valuation therefore rests on smaller companies' future growth rather than on today's earnings.

Growth needs adjusting. Tilaknagar's +69.9% is acquisition-driven; Radico's +24.7% and Piccadily's +25.5% are mostly organic; United Spirits' true like-for-like growth is +7.7%, not screener's +3.3%. United Breweries grew 3.6%, the slowest. Excluding United Spirits and Tilaknagar, the remaining four grew Q1 FY27 net revenue 8.6% year on year, a fair reading of the organic pace.

Tilaknagar's FY26 PAT of ₹21 crore is depressed by acquisition costs; Allied Blenders' ₹220 crore is after ₹135 crore of interest; United Spirits' profit includes other income of ₹516 crore. Who is winning: mix, balance sheets and the price of both

The winners combine rising premium share with a clean balance sheet. The market has noticed, and has priced the winners accordingly.

Three maps sort the six. The first plots growth against margin, the simplest test of who is compounding. The second plots return on capital against the earnings multiple, to see whether the market pays for quality. The third plots four-year revenue growth against EV/EBITDA, to see whether it pays for growth. A heat-map scorecard then rates each company on nine dimensions.

On growth and margin, Radico is the clearest winner among the larger companies: 24.7% revenue growth at a 16.9% margin in FY26, rising to 20.7% in Q1 FY27. Piccadily has the highest margin and similar growth but is small and mixed. United Spirits is the margin leader among the large companies but grew only 7.7% like-for-like, held back by Maharashtra and by having already sold its mass brands. United Breweries is the laggard on both axes.

On quality and price, the map's most striking feature is that the market does not pay most for the best returns. United Spirits earns 26.4% on capital at 53.5× earnings; United Breweries earns 10.7% at 91.8×; Allied Blenders earns 18.4% at 93×. Part of the explanation is scarcity (thin free floats at United Breweries and Allied Blenders), part is expectation (beer margins recovering; Allied Blenders' interest bill falling), and part is that United Spirits' growth has slowed. Piccadily is cheapest on earnings because the market discounts its sugar and bulk-spirit businesses.

On growth and valuation, Radico stands alone at about 58× EBITDA with a 20.5% four-year revenue CAGR. The cluster of United Spirits, United Breweries and Allied Blenders at roughly 40–43× despite very different growth (6.4%, 12.2% and 9.9% a year) suggests the market applies a sector multiple to them more than a company-specific one. Tilaknagar looks cheap on FY26 EBITDA, and cheaper still (about 23×) on annualised post-deal EBITDA, but carries the most debt and integration risk.

Global lens: why India pays 40–90 times what the world pays 15

Global drinks groups trade at mid-teens earnings multiples while their Indian subsidiaries and rivals trade at three to six times that. Scarcity, runway and early premiumisation explain most of the gap.

The global drinks industry is in a slow patch. IWSR reported in June 2026 that global alcohol volume fell for a third consecutive year; Scotch exports fell 4.3% in volume and 1.8% in value in 2025; US tariffs and weak demand in China have hurt the big spirits groups; and moderation trends among younger drinkers, along with GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, have become a de-rating argument for the whole category in developed markets. India is the exception. IWSR described India's growth as keeping the global whisky market afloat in 2026, with Indian spirits up about 4% in volume and 6% in value in 2025 while the rest of the world shrank. India is Scotch's largest export market by volume, and a priority market for Diageo, Pernod and Heineken alike.

Why the gap exists

Four arguments explain the premium. Scarcity: there are only six listed alcohol makers in the NSE 750, and the free floats are thin (about 5% public at United Breweries, about 11% at Allied Blenders), so domestic funds and index buyers compete for a small supply of shares. Runway: per-head consumption of about 3.2 litres of pure alcohol against a world average of 5.1, and beer at about 2 litres a head against 15 in Asia-Pacific (indicative), imply decades of volume growth where developed markets are shrinking. Early premiumisation: luxury is only 3–4% of Indian IMFL volume, against double-digit shares in mature markets (indicative), so the mix engine that drives margins has a long way to run. Earnings growth: the spirits makers grew revenue 14% in FY26 and their margins are still rising, against low-single-digit growth for the global groups.

Against these sit arguments for a discount that the market largely ignores. State policy risk has no equivalent in the parents' main markets. Indian margins are well below global ones (United Spirits' 18% EBITDA against Diageo's roughly 30% operating margin; United Breweries' 9% against Heineken's roughly 15%), partly because excise-heavy Indian pricing leaves less room for the maker and partly because of royalty and related-party flows to parents, which minority shareholders have long debated. And global de-rating forces, from moderation to GLP-1 drugs, could reach Indian valuations even if Indian demand holds up.

Compared with other Indian consumer sub-sectors, alcohol's multiples sit at the high end of the range investors pay for packaged foods and personal care, despite lower returns on capital at several of the companies and far more regulatory risk. The difference is the growth runway and the scarcity of listed alternatives.

In plain terms

Diageo, Pernod and Heineken trade at about 14–17 times earnings because their home markets are mature and shrinking. Their Indian businesses and Indian rivals trade at 40–90 times because India is one of the few places where people are still drinking more and trading up, and because there are very few such stocks to buy. That premium is a bet on many years of growth, and it leaves the shares exposed if growth slows.

Technology and disruption: tax design, moderation and the limits of the disruptor

The biggest disruptions in Indian alcohol are regulatory and fiscal, not technological. The industry's gatekeepers have so far defeated the venture-funded challengers.

Most consumer sub-sectors worry about digital disruption: direct-to-consumer brands, quick commerce, private labels. In Indian alcohol these forces are muted, because the state controls distribution and advertising. The disruptions that matter are of a different kind.

  • Strength-based taxation. Karnataka's AIB system is the most important structural change in a decade. If other states copy it, the tax system would systematically favour beer and lower-strength products over spirits, and premium over mass within spirits, since mass brands lose their per-litre tax advantage. It is a tailwind for United Breweries and the premiumisers and a headwind for mass-heavy portfolios.
  • Moderation and GLP-1 drugs. Globally, younger consumers drinking less and weight-loss drugs that reduce cravings are a de-rating force for alcohol stocks. India's exposure so far looks small, given rising legal-age populations and incomes, but this is an estimate rather than a measured effect.
  • Low- and no-alcohol and ready-to-drink. Crisil expects demand for low-alcohol beverages to expand. Flavoured beers, RTD cocktails and light spirits are growing from small bases; they play to the brewers and to the premium white-spirits brands.
  • Category churn. White spirits and agave are the fastest-growing categories. Brandy and rum are flat. Portfolios tilted to the growing categories (Radico's vodka, United Spirits' gin and Smirnoff) gain share without needing more whisky volume.
  • State retail digitisation. Track-and-trace excise labels (holograms and QR codes), e-auctions of retail licences and debates about online or home delivery (allowed in some states during Covid) could change route-to-market economics and cut leakage, but remain state by state.
  • Ethanol policy. Talk of moving beyond E20 towards E27 or E30 blending would raise fuel demand for the same grain and molasses, keeping ENA firm and widening the advantage of captive distillers.
  • The fragile disruptor. B9 Beverages' reported distress (single source) shows how hard the state maze is for new brands without existing distribution, bottling in every state and the balance sheet to wait for corporation payments.

Inside the companies, the technology that matters is mundane but valuable: revenue-growth management (pricing and pack-price architecture by state), demand planning for maturing stock, and distribution analytics in open-market states. In most industries new technology or start-ups threaten the leaders. In Indian alcohol the rules are so tight that start-ups struggle to survive and established brands are protected. The changes that could really reshape the business are new tax designs, like Karnataka taxing by alcohol strength, and shifts in what people drink, like the move towards vodka, gin and beer.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian alcohol stocks

The structural case is strong and widely understood. The risks are specific, recurring and mostly outside management's control.

The bull case

  • Runway: 10–12 million new legal-age drinkers a year, per-head consumption well below world levels, and beer at a fraction of Asian norms.
  • Premiumisation compounding: luxury volume growing about 15% a year, P&A shares rising at every company, and margins up 5–9 points at the spirits leaders since FY23.
  • Favourable tax design: Karnataka's strength-based excise rewards beer and premium spirits; if copied, it tilts the whole system towards the listed companies' growth segments.
  • Cheaper Scotch inputs: the FTA lowers the cost of bulk Scotch in premium blends, helping United Spirits and Tilaknagar.
  • Capital returns and deleveraging: RCB proceeds at United Spirits (if the deal closes), Radico debt-free, Tilaknagar and Allied Blenders paying down debt as working capital normalises.
  • Consolidation: fewer, stronger players as multinationals exit mass and challengers fail.

The bear case

  • State shocks: Maharashtra cut the industry ~20% in a quarter; Delhi, Tamil Nadu and others are reviewing policy; the next shock is a matter of when.
  • Input costs vs lagged prices: ENA heading to ₹80 a litre, glass exposed to energy shocks, and price approvals that take a year or more.
  • Valuation: ~71× combined earnings and 40–58× EV/EBITDA leave the shares vulnerable to any slowdown in premium growth.
  • FTA competition: cheaper Scotch narrows the gap for Indian single malts and luxury IMFL.
  • Leverage and working capital: Tilaknagar's net debt rising, Allied Blenders' interest bill, slow-paying state corporations.
  • Beer's stuck margin: six years at ~9% despite revenue doubling.
  • Overhangs: Allied Blenders' promoter sell-down by ~July 2027, United Breweries' ₹752 Cr CCI penalty, Inbrew franchise decisions, RCB closing uncertainty.

The balance between the two cases differs by company. For United Spirits, the bull case rests on capital return and steady premium growth, and the bear case on Maharashtra and slower growth. For Radico, the bull case is the strongest in the group operationally, but the valuation already assumes it continues; any miss on its 25%-plus P&A guidance would hurt. For United Breweries, everything depends on margin recovery. For Allied Blenders and Tilaknagar, deleveraging and premium mix are the swing factors. For Piccadily, branded growth and the demerger decide whether the market starts valuing it as a single-malt company.

Two things would change the overall verdict. On the upside, a second large state adopting strength-based excise, or a benign ENA year combined with price increases, would lift margins sector-wide. On the downside, a Maharashtra-style shock in a southern corporation state, or evidence that the FTA is pulling luxury drinkers back to Scotch, would hit the companies the market currently values most highly.

In plain terms

The long-term case is simple: more Indians are drinking, and they are buying better bottles every year. The short-term risks are just as simple: a state can change the tax overnight, grain and glass can jump in price, and the shares are already priced for strong growth. Owning these companies means accepting the regular shocks for the long-run trend.
