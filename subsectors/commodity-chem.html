00

The one-page view: six companies, three chemistries, one price-setter in Inner Mongolia

The label "commodity chemicals" covers three businesses with almost nothing in common except that each sells a molecule whose price is set outside India.

This sub-sector holds six listed companies, and they make three unrelated families of chemicals. Tata Chemicals, GHCL and Archean Chemical are inorganic and marine-chemistry businesses: they turn salt, limestone, trona ore and underground brine into soda ash, sodium bicarbonate, industrial salt and bromine. Supreme Petrochem and Styrenix Performance Materials are styrenics converters: they import styrene monomer by ship and polymerise it into polystyrene, expandable polystyrene and ABS for fridges, air-conditioners, packaging and cars. India Glycols is a bio-route chemicals maker that turns molasses and grain into ethanol and then into ethylene oxide derivatives; on 1 September 2026 it split itself into three companies and kept only the chemicals.

Together the six reported ₹31,784 crore of revenue in FY26, ₹4,252 crore of operating profit (a 13.4% margin) and a combined reported loss of ₹332 crore. The loss is almost entirely one item: Tata Chemicals' ₹1,956 crore exceptional charge for closing its last UK soda ash plant and writing down US goodwill. Excluding that charge, the six earned ₹1,624 crore. Their combined market value on 21 October 2026 was ₹44,053 crore, and Tata Chemicals alone is 35% of it.

Five forces define the sub-sector today, and the rest of this report is built around them:

  • China broke the soda ash price. Inner Mongolia added more than 10 million tonnes of mostly natural soda ash capacity in about two years. Chinese light soda ash fell to about RMB 1,156 a tonne by end-June 2026, a decade low, and Chinese inventories reached a record 1.73 million tonnes. India's import share doubled to 25–26%. Tata Chemicals' US plant, one of the lowest-cost in the world, earned an EBITDA of one US dollar a tonne in Q1 FY27, against US$41 a year earlier.
  • Trade remedy is the swing variable. The Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) recommended anti-dumping duties of US$79–113 a tonne on soda ash from Turkey, Russia, the USA and Iran in September 2025, but no customs notification was found and the duty appears never to have been imposed. A minimum import price of ₹20,108 a tonne proved porous. A safeguard investigation started in March 2026 is due for a final determination around 16 November 2026, the single biggest catalyst for GHCL and Tata Chemicals' Indian business.
  • Three chemistries, one label. In Q1 FY27 the sub-sector split: the styrenics pair's operating profit rose 160% on roughly 24% lower volume, while the inorganic trio's fell 9% on higher volume. Any "sector" average blends the two and describes neither. This report shows the subgroups every time.
  • The delta, not the price. Styrenics makers earn the gap between the polymer price and the styrene they buy. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 blew the general-purpose polystyrene delta from about US$200 a tonne to more than US$300, and Supreme Petrochem's management itself called the resulting margin "an aberration".
  • Capex at the trough. Indian soda ash capacity is set to rise from about 4.65 million tonnes a year to about 6.8 million by FY30, led by GHCL's ₹6,500 crore Kutch greenfield. Archean is building a ₹2,067 crore silicon-carbide chip fab; Supreme Petrochem and Styrenix are adding polystyrene and ABS lines. Roughly ₹10,000 crore of announced projects are going in at the bottom of the cycle.

The share chart tells the market's story in one glance. Supreme Petrochem earns 12% of the group's operating profit but carries 31% of its value: investors are paying for a domestic polystyrene franchise after a blow-out quarter. In plain terms

These six companies sell things nobody brands: the alkali that goes into window glass, the salt that goes into Chinese chlor-alkali plants, the plastic inside a refrigerator door. None of them sets its own price. Soda ash is priced off the cheapest tonne that can reach India, and today that tonne is dug out of the ground in Wyoming, Türkiye or Inner Mongolia. Polystyrene is priced off styrene made in Korea or the Gulf. The question for each company is not "how much can it charge?" but "how low is its cost, and how long can it wait for the cycle to turn?"

Two corrections to keep in mind throughout. First, India Glycols' FY26 numbers describe a company that no longer exists: the listed INDIAGLYCO now holds only the bio-based chemicals and industrial gases businesses, so its market value of ₹2,042 crore cannot be set against its pre-demerger profit. This report excludes it from every valuation ratio. Second, Tata Chemicals' "net cash" on data screens is an artefact of treating strategic Tata-group shareholdings as cash; on the company's own definition it carries net debt of ₹5,961 crore (March 2026), falling to ₹5,692 crore in June 2026. Read with those two adjustments, the sub-sector is a mix of a fortress (GHCL), a leveraged global major (Tata), a resource company with an expensive option (Archean), two spread traders riding a lucky quarter (the styrenics pair) and a newly slimmed bio-chemicals stub (India Glycols).

01

From brine pans to styrene tankers: how these six make money

Four value chains, each with a different raw material, a different cost driver and a different place where the profit sits.

Every commodity chemical business has the same skeleton: buy or extract a raw material, spend energy to transform it, ship a tonne of product, and keep the gap between the selling price and the cash cost. What differs is which of those steps the company controls. A soda ash maker that owns its salt pans and limestone quarries controls most of its cost; a polystyrene maker that imports every tonne of styrene controls almost none of it. That single distinction explains most of the differences in margins, returns and valuations across the six.

Natural soda ash skips Steps 1–3: trona ore is mined or solution-mined, dissolved, calcined and crystallised, using about half the energy (5–7 GJ a tonne) and producing no calcium chloride. That is the whole of China's and the US's cost advantage.

Soda ash (sodium carbonate) is the sub-sector's biggest molecule. Tata Chemicals makes it at Mithapur in Gujarat by the synthetic Solvay route, at Green River in Wyoming from natural trona ore, and at Lake Magadi in Kenya from lake trona. GHCL makes it synthetically at Sutrapada in Gujarat. Light grade goes into detergents and chemicals; dense grade goes into glass, which takes 50–55% of world demand. Sodium bicarbonate, made by reacting soda ash with carbon dioxide, is a smaller, steadier derivative used in food, pharmaceuticals (including haemodialysis) and flue-gas cleaning.

Marine chemicals are Archean's business. It pumps sub-soil brine from the Rann of Kutch at Hajipir, strips bromine out of it with chlorine and steam, crystallises the remaining liquor into industrial salt in solar pans, and recovers sulphate of potash from what is left. The brine costs nothing; the costs are energy, chlorine, acid, pan maintenance and, for the salt, ocean freight to buyers in China, Japan and Korea. Bromine goes into flame retardants, clear-brine fluids for oil wells, pharmaceutical and agrochemical intermediates and zinc-bromide batteries.

Styrenics are the second family. Supreme Petrochem, India's largest polystyrene maker with about half the domestic market, runs a roughly 4.22 lakh tonne a year complex at Amdoshi in Maharashtra and Manali near Chennai, making general-purpose polystyrene (GPPS), high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), expandable polystyrene (EPS), India's only extruded polystyrene (XPS) insulation board, and a new modified ABS (mABS) line. Styrenix, the former INEOS Styrolution India, makes ABS under the Absolac brand, along with polystyrene and SAN, in Gujarat, and since January 2025 also owns an 85,000 tonne a year ABS plant in Thailand. Neither makes its own styrene; both buy it in dollars at Asian prices.

Bio-glycols are India Glycols' route. It ferments molasses and grain into ethanol, dehydrates the ethanol into bio-ethylene, oxidises that to ethylene oxide (EO), and turns EO into glycols, ethoxylates, glycol ethers and polyethylene glycols for personal-care, agrochemical and textile formulators. The same ethanol stream used to feed two other "doors" as well, fuel ethanol for petrol blending and potable spirits; both left with the 1 September 2026 demerger into IGL Spirits.

Where is the value captured? In soda ash, it sits with the lowest-cost tonne, and since 2023 that has been a natural tonne. Synthetic producers inside India survive because ocean freight of roughly US$40–70 a tonne, the minimum import price and any duty fence the domestic market. In styrenics, value usually sits with the styrene producer, and only reaches the polymeriser when deltas blow out, as they did in Q1 FY27. In bromine, value is extraction rent on free brine, and the price cycle decides how much of it shows up. In bio-glycols, value migrated away from monoethylene glycol, a petrochemical giant's game dominated in India by Reliance, towards ethoxylates and specialty derivatives, and to the alcohol itself, which has now been separated.

In plain terms

Ask of each company: what does it own that its competitor has to buy? GHCL owns its salt, limestone and lignite. Tata owns a hundred years of Wyoming trona. Archean owns access to brine that costs nothing. Supreme Petrochem and Styrenix own nothing upstream: their edge is being the domestic supplier who can deliver a specific grade next week. That answer predicts who survives a downturn far better than revenue size does.

02

A ₹31,784 crore group that split in two in one quarter

Size, growth and the subgroup arithmetic: flat revenue for three years, then a quarter in which styrenics boomed while soda ash stayed bust.

The Indian markets these companies serve are modest by chemical-industry standards. Indian soda ash demand was about 5.3–5.5 million tonnes in FY26, worth roughly ₹13,000–15,000 crore at ₹24,000–28,000 a tonne (an INDICATIVE estimate: about 4.5–4.6 million tonnes of domestic output plus about 0.7 million tonnes of net imports). Installed capacity was about 4.65 million tonnes a year. India's combined polystyrene, EPS, ABS and SAN market is about 1 million tonnes, worth an INDICATIVE ₹12,000–15,000 crore. India is about 6% of world soda ash demand and growing faster than the world, at roughly 4.5–6% a year against 3–3.5% historically.

The six companies' combined revenue has gone nowhere for three years: ₹31,958 crore in FY25 and ₹31,784 crore in FY26, a 0.5% decline. That figure carries a caveat: Supreme Petrochem's FY25 base is standalone while its FY26 is consolidated, so the growth rate is approximate. Underneath the flat total, Tata Chemicals and GHCL shrank as soda ash prices fell, Styrenix grew 15% because it added the Thailand plant, and India Glycols grew 12% on fuel ethanol before the demerger.

Exports and overseas production matter more here than in most Indian sub-sectors. Archean exports about 78% of its revenue. About 55% of Tata Chemicals' group revenue comes from its overseas subsidiaries in the US, the UK and Kenya (an approximate primer figure). Supreme Petrochem normally exports 15–20% of its volume, but in Q1 FY27 its exports ran at only 10–12% of normal because war-time freight and styrene prices made them unprofitable. Styrenix's Thailand plant contributed ₹320.9 crore of revenue in Q1 FY27, close to a third of the consolidated total.

The bigger structural number is capacity. India's soda ash capacity is set to grow from about 4.65 million tonnes to about 6.8 million tonnes by FY30, an increase of roughly 46%, while demand growing at 4.5–5% a year would reach an INDICATIVE 6.4–6.7 million tonnes by then. If every announced plant arrives on time, India could swing from importing a quarter of its soda ash to being roughly self-sufficient, which would cut imports' pricing influence but intensify domestic competition. That is the arithmetic behind the "capex at the trough" debate in Section 13.

03

Glass, detergents, fridges and flame retardants: who buys the molecules

Demand is broad, slow-moving and mostly industrial. Solar glass is the one genuinely new driver; appliances are the swing factor for styrenics.

Commodity chemicals sit two or three steps away from the consumer, so their demand follows industrial activity rather than any one product cycle. Soda ash demand tracks glass furnaces and detergent plants, which run continuously; polystyrene and ABS demand tracks appliance, packaging and car production, which is seasonal; bromine demand tracks electronics, construction codes and oil-well completions around the world.

Glass is the anchor. A tonne of glass needs roughly 0.2 tonnes of dense soda ash. Flat glass for buildings and cars, container glass for beverages, pharmaceuticals and food, and now solar glass for photovoltaic modules all draw on the same molecule. Glass furnaces run for 15 years without stopping, so demand is steady, but it is also slow: the industry's historic growth has been 3–3.5% a year.

Solar glass is the new driver. India had about 119 GW of installed solar capacity in October 2025 against a 300 GW target, and GHCL's managing director estimates that solar glass alone could add about 1 million tonnes of soda ash demand over five years, roughly a fifth of today's market. Policy is pushing in the same direction: the Approved List of Models and Manufacturers for solar cells (from June 2026) and anti-dumping duties on Chinese solar glass favour domestic solar-glass lines, which buy domestic soda ash. That is the core reason the industry forecasts 4.5–5% annual demand growth for FY27–30, and the main justification for the capacity additions.

Detergents take 15–18% of soda ash, and here the market structure matters as much as demand. India's two largest detergent makers, Nirma and RSPL (Ghari), also make their own soda ash, so a large slice of light-ash demand never reaches the open market. Tata and GHCL sell to the remaining detergent makers and to the chemicals industry.

Appliances drive styrenics. HIPS lines refrigerator interiors; ABS makes the housings of air-conditioners, washing machines and small appliances; GPPS and EPS go into packaging, disposables and cold chains; XPS boards insulate buildings and cold stores. Appliance demand was hit in FY26 by a cool, wet 2025 summer, and in Q1 FY27 Supreme Petrochem reported that almost half of its non-OEM demand "evaporated" as converters waited out the war-time spike in styrene prices. Cars and two-wheelers (interiors, helmets) are Styrenix's second leg.

Flame retardants for electronics and construction are the largest use; clear-brine fluids used to complete oil and gas wells are the most cyclical; pharmaceutical and agrochemical intermediates and the PTA catalyst are steady; zinc-bromide and flow batteries are a small option on energy storage, in which Archean holds an 18.14% stake through Offgrid Energy Labs. Industrial salt goes to chlor-alkali and soda ash plants in China, Japan and Korea.

Seasonality differs by chemistry. Soda ash is flat through the year because furnaces run continuously, with a mild festive lift from detergents. Styrenics peak in Q4 and Q1 of the fiscal year as appliance makers build stock before summer. Archean's salt business is hit every Q2 by the monsoon, which disrupts pan harvesting and logistics: its September 2025 quarter was its weakest, at ₹233 crore of revenue. India Glycols' former spirits business peaked in the festive Q3.

In plain terms

Demand is not the problem for most of these companies. Indian glass, solar and appliance demand is growing faster than the world's. The problem is that demand growth of 5% a year is easily swamped by a single new Chinese plant, and India is the market where the world's surplus tonnes look for a home. Volume is rarely what moves these stocks; price is.

In plain terms

Look for the biggest cost line, because that is where the next quarter's surprise will come from. For GHCL it is the coal bill; for Supreme Petrochem and Styrenix it is the price of styrene on the day they bought it compared with the day they sold the polymer; for Archean it is the cost of shipping a tonne of salt to Japan. Two further points about costs. First, operating leverage differs sharply. Soda ash plants run at 95–98% utilisation regardless of price, because the fixed costs of a continuous process plant are high and shutting a furnace customer down is not an option; price therefore absorbs the whole cycle. Nirma, imports and two ABS makers: who really sets supply

The most important supplier in Indian soda ash is unlisted, and the second most important is "imports". In ABS, two domestic makers share the market with Korean and Chinese cargoes.

Industry structure in this sub-sector is lopsided: a few domestic producers per molecule, at least one large unlisted player, and imports that act as a price-setting competitor. Understanding who supplies the marginal tonne explains why the listed companies cannot simply raise prices when their costs rise.

Soda ash: three producers and an importer

India's roughly 4.65 million tonnes of soda ash capacity is concentrated in three companies. Nirma, delisted in 2012 and controlled by the Karsanbhai Patel family, is the largest at about 1.3–1.5 million tonnes across Bhavnagar and Porbandar (where its group company Saurashtra Chemicals operates); it is also its own biggest customer, because it makes detergents, and it owns Searles Valley Minerals, a natural soda ash producer in California bought in 2007. GHCL is second at about 1.2 million tonnes and claims a 26% market share; Tata Chemicals' Mithapur plant is third at about 1.09 million tonnes. The top three hold roughly 78–79% of capacity. Behind them come RSPL, the maker of Ghari detergents, at about 0.5–0.6 million tonnes, and two small listed players, DCW and Tuticorin Alkali Chemicals and Fertilisers, neither of them in the NSE 750.

The trona tsunami: how Inner Mongolia broke the soda ash price Deep dive

Ten million tonnes of cheap natural capacity in two years turned China from importer to exporter, pushed American and Turkish tonnes towards India, and left synthetic producers everywhere fighting for the bottom of the cost curve.

The soda ash industry spent a century in two camps. Synthetic producers in Europe, India and coastal China made it by the Solvay process from salt and limestone. Natural producers in Wyoming and Türkiye mined trona ore and needed half the energy. The two coexisted because natural deposits were rare and far from Asian customers. Between 2023 and 2025, China broke that equilibrium.

Inner Mongolia's ten million tonnes

Inner Mongolia Boyuan's Alxa project, built on one of the largest trona deposits outside the US, started a first phase of about 5 million tonnes a year in 2023 and is heading for about 7.8 million tonnes. Other Inner Mongolia additions took the region's new capacity to more than 10 million tonnes in about two years, according to GHCL's managing director. China's output grew 10–18% a year in 2023–24, against 3–4% for the world. For a market of 65–70 million tonnes, that is like adding a seventh of global demand in two years, at the lowest cash cost in the world.

The price did what commodity prices do. Chinese light soda ash, which traded at RMB 3,000–3,500 a tonne at the 2021–22 peak, fell to about RMB 1,212 by late March 2026 and RMB 1,156 by the end of June 2026, its lowest in nearly a decade. At about ₹13.1 to the yuan, that is roughly ₹15,100 a tonne, half of what an Indian buyer paid in Mumbai. Dense soda ash for export was quoted at US$185 a tonne FOB Qingdao in June 2026, and Tata Chemicals' Q1 FY27 commentary put Chinese export prices at US$160–180 a tonne. Chinese inventories reached a record 1.73 million tonnes.

The displacement route to India

A puzzle follows. India's safeguard petition names the US, Turkey and Russia as the sources of its import surge, not China. Why? Because Chinese tonnes mostly go to Southeast Asia, the natural market for both Chinese and American exports. When Chinese soda ash undercuts American and Turkish soda ash in Indonesia, Thailand and Vietnam, those producers look for another market large enough to absorb their tonnes, and India, growing faster than the world and protected only by freight and a porous minimum price, is the obvious one.

Three conclusions follow. First, natural soda ash from Türkiye, China or the US can be delivered to India for less than an Indian synthetic plant spends to make it. Indian producers survive because imports are sold at a price, not at cost, and that price includes the importer's margin, customs duty and port charges; but the gap gives importers room to cut when they need volume. Second, Chinese synthetic and dual-process plants, the old marginal producers, are now underwater on exports unless their ammonium chloride by-product earns enough to subsidise the soda ash; that is the capacity that has to close for the cycle to turn. Third, Indian synthetic producers earn roughly ₹6,000–9,000 a tonne at today's domestic price, while importers from Türkiye and the US earn more on the same price, which is why the safeguard petition names them rather than China alone.

In plain terms

A Turkish or American mine can dig soda ash out of the ground and ship it to Gujarat for less than it costs GHCL or Tata to make it at Sutrapada or Mithapur. That has always been true. What changed is that China built enough natural capacity to push those Turkish and American tonnes out of Southeast Asia, so they now come to India in volume. The Indian makers are still profitable, but only because of the freight, duty and minimum price that stand between the foreign mine and the Indian customer.

Who has already lost

The casualties so far are the highest-cost synthetic plants in Europe and the weakest parts of the global majors. Tata Chemicals closed its Winnington soda ash plant in the UK in 2014 and has now ended production at Lostock, its last UK soda ash site, taking a ₹1,956 crore exceptional charge in FY26 that also covered a write-down of US goodwill. Its UK business now makes bicarbonate, salt (British Salt, Middlewich) and pharmaceutical-grade salt. Even low-cost natural producers are bleeding. Tata Chemicals' Green River operation in Wyoming ran at 97% utilisation in Q1 FY27 and sold 614,000 tonnes, 13% more than a year earlier, yet earned EBITDA of about US$1 a tonne, against US$41 a year earlier. Who survives, and when it ends

Survival in a commodity glut depends on three things: position on the cost curve, protection from the marginal tonne, and balance-sheet stamina. On those tests, GHCL is a synthetic producer with captive inputs, a protected domestic market and net cash; it can wait. Tata's Indian business is in a similar position, but its group carries net debt of about 3.3 times EBITDA and an overseas business that is earning almost nothing. The weakest players globally are standalone synthetic plants in Europe and older Chinese synthetic plants without ammonium chloride credits.

The cycle ends when enough high-cost capacity closes, Chinese inventories are drawn down, or demand catches up, most likely a combination. Industry analysts quoted by the US trade administration see no recovery before late 2027. Solar glass is the main demand hope; Chinese capacity discipline is the main supply hope. For India specifically, the decision that matters sooner is a policy one: whether the safeguard investigation ends in a quantitative restriction or a duty in November 2026.

In plain terms

The soda ash downturn is not a demand problem; it is a cost-curve problem. A new, cheaper way of making the product arrived at scale in one country, and every older, more expensive producer has to either close, cut costs or get protection. Indian investors should watch three numbers: the Chinese light soda ash price (recovery needs it well above RMB 1,250), Chinese inventories (falling from 1.73 million tonnes), and Tata's US EBITDA per tonne (back above US$25 would signal the worst is over).

Trade remedy is the swing variable: duties that lapsed, a floor that leaked, a safeguard due in November

For India's soda ash makers, a Commerce Ministry decision can move profits more than any plant. Policy also matters, less dramatically, for polymers, ethanol and land.

When the cheapest tonne in the world can be delivered to India below a domestic producer's cash cost, the profit of that producer depends on what stands in the way: freight, customs duty, and trade remedies. India has tried every tool in the trade-remedy kit on soda ash over the last 17 years, and the record is mixed. The current chapter, a safeguard investigation started in March 2026, is the most important near-term event for GHCL and for Tata Chemicals' Indian business.

The record so far

India imposed a safeguard duty on Chinese soda ash in 2009, then anti-dumping duties (ADD) on soda ash from China, the EU, Kenya, Iran, Pakistan, Ukraine and the US in 2012 and on Turkey and Russia in 2013. Those duties expired or were withdrawn by about 2018. In January 2021 the DGTR issued final findings on soda ash from Turkey and the US on an application by DCW, RSPL and GHCL, but the Finance Ministry did not impose a duty. In 2023 the government set a minimum import price (MIP) of ₹20,108 a tonne, later extended to 31 December 2025; GHCL's managing director described it as giving "some small benefit" because sub-MIP imports continued. Whether the MIP was extended into 2026 could not be confirmed.

Then came the case that should have mattered. On 29 September 2025 the DGTR issued final findings in case AD(OI)-29/2024, recommending anti-dumping duties of US$79–113 a tonne on soda ash from Turkey, Russia, the USA and Iran, with a lower US$17–27 a tonne for Sisecam, and issued a corrigendum on 4 November 2025. No customs notification imposing the duty was found. The recommendation therefore appears to have lapsed, and this report treats it as not imposed.

Why the industry switched from anti-dumping to a safeguard

The shift tells you how cheap the competing imports really are. An anti-dumping duty is country-specific and requires proof that the exporter sells in India below its home-market price or cost, the "dumping margin". Natural soda ash from Wyoming and Türkiye is not dumped in that sense; it is simply cheaper to make. A safeguard measure, by contrast, covers imports from all origins and requires only an "unforeseen surge" in imports causing "serious injury" to the domestic industry. It can take the form of an extra ad valorem duty or of a quantitative restriction (QR) that caps import volumes. The Alkali Manufacturers Association of India, representing DCW, GHCL, Tata Chemicals, RSPL and Nirma, sought a safeguard, and the DGTR initiated the investigation on 16 March 2026. The final determination is estimated around 16 November 2026. Neither the form nor the size of any measure has been decided.

Policy beyond soda ash

Polymers. The government rescinded BIS Quality Control Orders on 14 polymers, including ABS, polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, EVA, polyurethane and polycarbonate, around 12 November 2025 (some sources say 20 November). The QCOs had acted as a non-tariff barrier by requiring foreign plants to be certified; their removal reopened the door to Chinese and other imports, a negative for Styrenix's and Supreme Petrochem's pricing power and a positive for downstream converters (the plastics report covers that side). Whether a polystyrene or EPS QCO was in the same batch was not confirmed. Basic customs duty on polymers is about 7.5% plus a cess. The 2022 single-use plastics ban targets thin disposables, including thermocol decorations and some cutlery, a structural overhang on GPPS and EPS disposables.

Ethanol. India reached about 20% ethanol blending in petrol in ethanol supply year (ESY) 2024-25. The oil marketing companies' procurement prices for ESY 2025-26 are administered by feedstock, from ₹57.97 a litre for C-heavy molasses to ₹71.86 for maize. The ESY 2025-26 first-cycle tender sought 1,050 crore litres and attracted offers of 1,776 crore litres, a sign of industry overcapacity. These matter to IGL Spirits, the demerged entity, more than to the listed chemicals stub. Molasses and alcohol are state subjects, and Uttar Pradesh reserves molasses for country liquor (see the Alcohol report).

Environment and land. Soda ash plants produce calcium chloride effluent and sit in coastal regulation zones. GHCL's Kutch greenfield environmental and forest clearances were challenged; the National Green Tribunal's western bench dismissed the appeals in August 2026, though a Supreme Court appeal remains possible. In October 2026 the Supreme Court restrained the Gujarat government from coercive action against Tata Chemicals in a Mithapur land dispute. Archean and Tata both operate near the Rann of Kutch's wild-ass sanctuary and eco-sensitive zones.

Semiconductors and tariffs. Under the India Semiconductor Mission, the cabinet approved SiCSem's silicon-carbide fab at Bhubaneswar, a ₹2,067 crore project in which Archean is the sponsor; central fiscal support under the scheme is typically about half of project cost (Section 10). US tariffs on Indian goods, a 10% Section 301 duty since 24 July 2026, bite little here: Tata's US business sells from inside the US, and Archean's exports go mainly to North Asia and global bromine buyers.

In plain terms

Policy helps and hurts different members. A soda ash safeguard helps GHCL and Tata; the polymer QCO rescission hurt Styrenix and Supreme Petrochem by letting more imports in; ethanol pricing matters mainly to the spirits company India Glycols has just spun off; the semiconductor scheme is what makes Archean's chip fab affordable at all. When reading any of these stocks, ask which government decision is pending and which direction it cuts.

The entry is a sign that the economics of extracting bromine from Gujarat brine at today's prices are attractive enough to draw in a cash-rich neighbour.

The delta, not the price: styrenics after the West Asia shock Deep dive

Supreme Petrochem and Styrenix sell polymers but earn a spread over imported styrene. In Q1 FY27 a war in the Gulf widened that spread and handed both companies an inventory windfall; the question is how much survives.

A polystyrene maker's revenue tells you almost nothing about its profit. When styrene prices rise, polymer prices follow, revenue jumps and margins may shrink or swell depending on timing. The number that matters is the "delta": the polymer price minus the price of the styrene (and, for ABS, the acrylonitrile and butadiene) needed to make it. Supreme Petrochem's management quotes it in US dollars a tonne, because both prices are set in Asian dollar markets.

Two prices, one spread

Making a tonne of general-purpose polystyrene takes almost exactly a tonne of styrene monomer. Supreme Petrochem's normal GPPS delta is about US$200 a tonne, or about ₹19,000 at ₹94.9 to the dollar; for HIPS, which adds polybutadiene rubber, the historic delta is US$275–300. Conversion costs, meaning power, people, additives, packing and overheads, are about ₹5,000–10,000 a tonne, so a normal delta leaves an EBITDA of roughly ₹10,000–14,000 a tonne (an ILLUSTRATIVE range calibrated to Supreme Petrochem's FY26 operating profit of ₹525 crore on an estimated 0.36–0.38 million tonnes). That is a thin margin on a product selling for ₹1.4–1.5 lakh a tonne, which is why the converter's job is to sell specialty grades, run full and manage inventory.

What the West Asia shock did

The war in West Asia from 28 February 2026, with the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and Brent crude peaking around US$118 a barrel, hit both legs of the spread. Styrene monomer, quoted CFR China, rose from about US$911 a tonne in mid-January 2026 to US$1,315–1,325 by mid-April; Indian domestic styrene went from ₹80 a kilo in December 2025 to ₹92 in January 2026 and higher thereafter. Polymer prices rose faster: Indian polymer prices were up 43% by 19 March, according to the plastics industry. After the 7 April ceasefire, styrene eased to US$1,250–1,260 by mid-May and US$1,075–1,085 by early August, before rebounding to US$1,200–1,210 on 26 August.

Inventory gains: the hidden half of the blow-out

Supreme Petrochem holds roughly 45–60 days of styrene and polymer stock, about 45,000–60,000 tonnes. A US$200 a tonne move in styrene, about the January-to-April change, is worth roughly ₹85–115 crore on that stock (an ILLUSTRATIVE calculation: 45,000–60,000 tonnes × US$200 × ₹94.9). That is a third or more of the quarter's ₹332 crore operating profit. The same mechanism runs in reverse: styrene fell about US$240 a tonne from mid-April to early August, so the September quarter could carry an inventory loss, partly offset by the late-August rebound. Styrenix faces the same effect on a smaller stock.

In plain terms

Imagine buying a tonne of styrene for ₹85,000, holding it for six weeks while a war pushes the market price to ₹1.25 lakh, then selling the polystyrene made from it at a price based on ₹1.25 lakh. Part of your profit came from making polystyrene, and part from owning styrene during a price spike. In Q1 FY27 the second part was large. When styrene falls, the same stock produces a loss. A converter's "normal" profit is what is left after these gains and losses wash out over a year or two.

What normal looks like

Mid-cycle thinking is the discipline this sub-segment demands. If deltas return to US$200–250 and inventory effects net to zero, Supreme Petrochem's EBITDA per tonne would revert to roughly ₹12,000–16,000 and Styrenix's to somewhere near ₹14,000–18,000 given its richer ABS mix (both ILLUSTRATIVE). On those numbers, Supreme Petrochem's trailing P/E of 28.1, which includes the blow-out quarter, overstates normal earnings power, while Styrenix's 12.6 is closer to a mid-cycle multiple. Each US$10 a tonne on the delta is worth about ₹34–36 crore of annual EBITDA to Supreme Petrochem (on 0.36–0.38 million tonnes) and about ₹24 crore to Styrenix (on about 0.25 million tonnes), an ILLUSTRATIVE sensitivity.

The ABS import gap and the expansions

The structural story is better than the cyclical one. India imports roughly 35–45% of its ABS, and the domestic makers are adding capacity to replace imports rather than betting on new demand. Styrenix plans to add 50,000 tonnes of ABS in H2 FY27 and another 50,000 in FY28 (our primer's "about 70,000 tonnes" is superseded), on top of an Indian base of about 100,000 tonnes after debottlenecking; sources on its exact plant-by-plant capacity conflict. Its Thai plant, at 85,000 tonnes a year, runs at "relatively low utilisation" because new customers take 12–24 months to qualify grades. Bhansali Engineering Polymers, the other Indian ABS maker, has scaled back from a plan for about 200,000 tonnes to a 75,000-to-100,000 tonne debottleneck. Supreme Petrochem is adding an 80,000 tonne a year polystyrene line at Amdoshi for ₹325 crore by March 2029, within a roughly ₹900 crore programme, commissioned EPS phase II (to 115,000 tonnes) on 14 April 2026, and is ramping mABS, which ran at only about 65% after a critical equipment failure in December 2025.

The risks to the structural story are real. China more than doubled its ABS capacity between 2020 and 2025, and the November 2025 rescission of the polymer quality control orders reopened India to those imports. Polypropylene compounds are replacing HIPS and ABS in some appliance parts; paper and PLA are replacing polystyrene in disposables. The Plastics report covers the downstream converters that buy these polymers, including The Supreme Industries, which owns about 30.8% of Supreme Petrochem and equity-accounts its share of profits; investors aggregating both reports should not count Supreme Petrochem's profits twice.

In plain terms

Supreme Petrochem and Styrenix are good businesses with a domestic edge, but Q1 FY27 was not their normal. A war widened the gap between what they sell and what they buy, and the styrene in their warehouses rose in value at the same time. Both effects fade. The test comes with Q2 FY27 results, starting with Supreme Petrochem's board meeting on 23 October 2026: watch whether EBITDA per tonne falls back towards ₹15,000–20,000, and whether volumes and exports recover.

Eighty-seven years, four cycles: the history that explains today's balance sheets

From a salt works at Mithapur in 1939 to a three-way demerger in 2026: overseas acquisitions, a super-cycle, a Chinese cost shock and a restructuring wave.

Each company's balance sheet today carries the imprint of a specific era. Tata Chemicals' debt and impairments trace to overseas acquisitions made in 2005–2008. GHCL's net cash traces to a decade of deleveraging and the demerger of its textiles business. Archean's equity base traces to an IPO at the top of the 2022 bromine boom. Styrenix exists because a global major decided its Indian business was worth more to a local owner. Reading the history makes the present easier to interpret.

The seawater-chemistry era (1939 to the 1990s)

Tata Chemicals was founded at Mithapur in 1939 to make salt and soda ash on the Saurashtra coast, where solar salt pans, limestone and a port sit together. Gujarat Heavy Chemicals (GHCL) built its synthetic soda ash plant at Sutrapada in the 1980s, Saurashtra Chemicals and DCW built theirs, and Nirma entered soda ash in the 1990s to integrate its detergents. In styrenics, India's first ABS plant was built in the 1970s by the company that became Styrenix, and Supreme Petrochem was set up as a Raheja group and Supreme Industries joint venture around 1989–90. India Glycols made monoethylene glycol from molasses in 1989.

Globalisation and leverage (2005–2010)

Tata Chemicals bought Brunner Mond, with its UK plants and Kenya's Magadi, in 2005–06, and General Chemical Industrial Products, with the Green River trona mine in Wyoming, in 2008, briefly becoming the world's second-largest soda ash producer. Nirma bought Searles Valley Minerals in California in 2007. GHCL bought a Romanian soda ash plant, later exited. India imposed a safeguard duty on Chinese soda ash in 2009. The debt Tata raised in this period, and the goodwill it recorded, is still being worked through.

Restructuring (2010s)

Anti-dumping duties on soda ash from seven origins followed in 2012 and on Turkey and Russia in 2013. Tata closed the Winnington plant in the UK in 2014, exited fertilisers in 2018, and demerged its consumer business, including the Tata Salt brand, into Tata Consumer Products in February 2020. GHCL deleveraged and later demerged textiles (appointed date 1 April 2022, GHCL Textiles listed in 2023), becoming a pure soda ash company.

The super-cycle and the Chinese shock (2021–2026)

The Covid recovery and the 2022 energy shock produced a rare simultaneous peak. Chinese soda ash reached RMB 3,000–3,500 a tonne and the Mumbai price about ₹53 a kilo in May 2022; Archean's operating margin hit 44% in FY23, and its November 2022 IPO, raising ₹1,462 crore, came at the top. Then Inner Mongolia's natural capacity arrived from 2023, prices halved, India's import share doubled, Tata closed Lostock and took its FY26 exceptional charge, WE Soda bought Genesis, and Solvay cut Spain. In styrenics, Shiva Performance Materials bought INEOS's 61% of INEOS Styrolution India (renamed Styrenix in January 2023, completed March 2023) and then the Thai plant (January 2025). The polymer QCOs were rescinded in November 2025, and the West Asia war from February 2026 produced the widest styrenics deltas and the sharpest bromine spike in years. India Glycols' demerger completed the period.

Reading a commodity chemicals company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twelve numbers that explain almost every quarter these six companies report, how to calculate each, what good looks like, and where each one misleads.

A commodity chemicals company is a machine that converts tonnes of capacity into tonnes sold, and tonnes sold into a margin per tonne. Almost every number in its results is a piece of one equation. Learn the equation, and the quarterly results stop being a list of figures and become a short story: did the company sell more tonnes, at a better spread, at a lower cost, than last year?

Management controls the two ends of the equation, capacity (through capex decisions taken years earlier) and fixed costs, and partly controls utilisation. The middle term, the spread between realisation and variable cash cost, is mostly set by markets: the import parity price for soda ash, the Asian polymer and styrene prices for styrenics, the Chinese bromine price, the coal and freight markets. That is the defining feature of a commodity business, and it is why the companies with the best results are usually those with the lowest costs rather than the highest prices.

A worked example in round numbers shows how the pieces fit (ILLUSTRATIVE). Take a soda ash plant with 1,000,000 tonnes of capacity running at 95%, so it sells 950,000 tonnes. If it realises ₹25,000 a tonne, revenue is ₹2,375 crore. If variable cash costs (salt, limestone, coke, energy, ammonia, freight) are ₹16,000 a tonne, the contribution is ₹9,000 a tonne, or ₹855 crore. Subtract ₹300 crore of fixed costs and EBITDA is ₹555 crore, a 23.4% margin. Now let the price fall by ₹1,000 a tonne, about 4%: revenue falls ₹95 crore and, because no cost has moved, EBITDA falls the same ₹95 crore, or 17%. That ratio of a 4% price move to a 17% profit move is operating leverage, and it is the single most important idea in this report.

The equation takes a slightly different shape in each archetype, and knowing which term dominates tells you where to look first. For GHCL and Tata's Indian plant, capacity and utilisation barely move from year to year, so almost all of the change sits in realisation, with energy cost a distant second. For Tata's US plant, realisation is set in two markets, a domestic US contract market and an export market in Southeast Asia where Chinese tonnes compete, so the export share of volume is itself a profit driver. For Archean, the equation has to be run three times, for bromine, salt and derivatives, and bromine's tiny tonnage at a high price dominates salt's huge tonnage at a thin margin. For the styrenics pair, the realisation and the feedstock cost move together, so the spread and the inventory effect are the only terms that matter in the short run. For the retained India Glycols, the equation is a chain: ethanol cost per litre, conversion to ethylene oxide, and the price of each derivative grade.

In plain terms

In these businesses a small change in price becomes a big change in profit, because almost all the costs stay where they were. A 4% lower soda ash price can mean 17% lower operating profit; a US$100 a tonne wider polystyrene delta can triple it. When you read a result, first find out what happened to the spread per tonne. Everything else is secondary.

1. Capacity and utilisation

What it is. Capacity is the nameplate number of tonnes a plant can make in a year; utilisation is output (or sales) divided by capacity. Formula: utilisation = tonnes produced ÷ nameplate capacity. Worked example: GHCL runs about 1.2 million tonnes of soda ash capacity at 98%, so it produces roughly 1.18 million tonnes; Archean's bromine plant, at about 42,500 tonnes of capacity and an estimated 14–15 thousand tonnes sold in FY26, ran at roughly 35% (ILLUSTRATIVE).

What drives it. In continuous-process plants such as soda ash, utilisation stays high whatever the price, because the cost of stopping and restarting a plant, and of losing a glass-furnace customer, exceeds the loss on marginal tonnes. In styrenics, converters cut runs when deltas are negative or demand dries up, as in Q1 FY27. In bromine, utilisation is set by demand and by how much bromine the company chooses to keep for its derivatives subsidiary. Benchmarks: soda ash above 95% is good, 85–95% average, below 85% weak; for bromine above 70% is good and below 40% weak.

2. Realisation per tonne

What it is. The average net price received per tonne (or kilo) sold, after discounts and before taxes. Formula: realisation = segment revenue ÷ tonnes sold. Worked examples: Archean's Q1 FY27 bromine revenue of ₹133.3 crore over 4,175 tonnes is about ₹3.19 lakh a tonne, or ₹319 a kilo. Supreme Petrochem's ₹1,715 crore over 70,842 tonnes is about ₹2.42 lakh a tonne, up from about ₹1.49 lakh a year earlier. Styrenix's ₹1,011 crore over 50,800 tonnes is about ₹1.99 lakh, up from about ₹1.41 lakh (all ILLUSTRATIVE calculations).

What drives it. For soda ash, the import parity price, the light-versus-dense mix and the share of bicarbonate. For bromine, the Chinese and global price and the share of derivatives. For polymers, the styrene price, which passes straight through, plus grade mix. Benchmarks: Indian soda ash above ₹35 a kilo is good, ₹27–35 average, below ₹27 weak (Mumbai was about ₹30–31 in Q1 FY27, an INDICATIVE figure); bromine above ₹350 a kilo is good, ₹220–350 average, below ₹220 weak.

5. Spread or delta

9. Inventory gains and losses on feedstock

What it is. The profit or loss that arises because a company buys raw material at one price and sells the finished product when the raw-material price has moved. Formula: inventory effect ≈ tonnes of feedstock and product in stock × change in feedstock price over the holding period. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): Supreme Petrochem holds roughly 45,000–60,000 tonnes of styrene and polymer; a US$200 a tonne rise in styrene, about the January-to-April 2026 move, adds about ₹85–115 crore (45,000–60,000 × US$200 × ₹94.9) to one quarter's operating profit, and a fall of the same size removes it.

What drives it. The size and direction of feedstock price moves and the days of stock held. It matters most for styrenics, where feedstock is 75–85% of cost; it is small for soda ash, where raw materials are captive and cheap. Benchmark: none; the right treatment is to strip it out and estimate the underlying spread. How members compare: neither styrenics company discloses inventory gains separately; infer them from the gap between management's quoted market deltas and the delta implied by the accounts. The trap: a quarter with a large inventory gain flatters the TTM P/E for a year. Supreme Petrochem's 28.1 times trailing earnings includes Q1 FY27 in full.

In plain terms

Part of a styrenics company's profit in a volatile quarter comes not from making plastic but from holding styrene while its price changes. That part is luck, and it reverses. When a converter reports a record margin in a quarter when styrene spiked, assume some of it will be handed back when styrene falls.

10. Capex per tonne and asset turnover

What it is. Capex per tonne is the investment needed for each tonne of new annual capacity; asset turnover is revenue divided by net fixed assets. Formulas: capex intensity = project cost ÷ tonnes of annual capacity added; asset turnover = revenue ÷ net block. Worked examples: GHCL's Kutch project costs about ₹59,000 per tonne of capacity (₹6,500 crore ÷ 1.1 million tonnes); Supreme Petrochem's new polystyrene line about ₹40,600 per tonne (₹325 crore ÷ 80,000 tonnes), but a tonne of polystyrene sells for six to eight times a tonne of soda ash. What it is. Return on capital employed, operating profit after depreciation (EBIT) divided by debt plus equity, measured across a full cycle rather than in one year. 15. Definition traps, collected

  • Consolidated vs standalone vs attributable. Tata Chemicals' Q1 FY27 profit was ₹60 crore consolidated, about −₹17 crore attributable to its shareholders, and ₹343 crore standalone (India). Quote the basis every time.
  • Reported vs pre-exceptional. Tata's FY26 reported loss of ₹1,715 crore includes a ₹1,956 crore exceptional charge; pre-exceptional profit was about ₹241 crore. GHCL's Q1 FY27 PAT of ₹191 crore includes a ~₹54 crore exceptional gain.
  • Company EBITDA vs screener operating profit. Archean's FY26 company EBITDA of about ₹308 crore vs screener's ₹239 crore; GHCL's Q1 FY27 EBITDA of ₹233 crore on the company basis. Use one basis in any comparison.
  • Net debt. Tata's company net debt (₹5,961 crore) vs screener's "net cash" (₹1,885 crore) created by counting strategic investments as cash.
  • Mixed-base growth. Supreme Petrochem's "−11%" FY26 revenue decline compares consolidated FY26 with standalone FY25.
  • Gross vs net revenue. India Glycols' FY26 gross revenue of about ₹9,827 crore includes state excise on liquor; net revenue is ₹4,211 crore.
  • Pre- vs post-demerger. India Glycols' P&L is pre-demerger; its market value is post-demerger. Its P/E and P/B are not meaningful.
  • Capacity. Owned vs operated, nameplate vs demonstrated, and closed plants still counted (Tata's UK).
  • "Spread". A US$ delta for styrenics, realisation minus cash cost for soda ash, price minus energy and chlorine for bromine. EBITDA per tonne compares only within one archetype.
  • Standalone ratios presented as company ratios. Styrenix's ~33% ROCE and ~25% ROE are standalone India; consolidated ROCE is 13.8%.
  • Import share basis. Net imports (~700,000 tonnes in FY26) and "25–26% import share" may be on different bases.
In plain terms

Most of the mistakes made in reading these six companies are definitional, not analytical. Before comparing any two numbers, check that they are on the same basis: same entity (standalone or consolidated), same profit line (reported or pre-exceptional), same debt definition, same period. In this sub-sector, at least one company in every comparison will be on a different basis unless you check.

16. How to read a commodity chemicals company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volumes first (minute 1). Find tonnes sold by product: soda ash, bromine, salt, polystyrene, ABS. Compare with the same quarter last year. If volume is not disclosed, note it as a red flag.
  2. Realisation (minute 2). Divide segment revenue by tonnes. For a converter, compare it with the change in the styrene price over the quarter; for soda ash, with the import parity price.
  3. EBITDA per tonne (minute 3). Operating profit ÷ tonnes. Compare with last year and with the benchmark band for that archetype. This is the single most informative number.
  4. The spread commentary (minute 4). Read management's quoted deltas (Supreme Petrochem), price changes per tonne (Tata's "+₹2,000/t") and bromine realisation (Archean). Check them against the implied figures from steps 2–3.
  5. Inventory and one-offs (minute 5). Look for inventory gains, exceptional items, provision reversals (Tata's ₹43 crore in Q1 FY27) and forex. Strip them out.
  6. Costs (minute 6). Power and fuel as a share of revenue; freight; any warning about energy pass-through, as GHCL gave for later FY27.
  7. Segments and geography (minute 7). For Tata, the three Essentials segments and the US EBITDA per tonne; for Archean, bromine vs salt vs Acume; for India Glycols, chemicals vs gases after the demerger.
  8. Profit basis (minute 8). Consolidated vs attributable vs standalone. Note minority interests (Rallis at Tata).
  9. Balance sheet and capex (minute 9). Net debt on the company's definition, capex spent, capital work in progress and project timelines (Kutch, ABS phase 1, PS line, SiCSem).
  10. Policy and outlook (minute 10). Trade remedies, Chinese prices, styrene and freight. Red flags: falling volume with rising margin explained only by "pricing"; a sharp gap between company EBITDA and reported operating profit; capex rising while EBITDA per tonne falls; repeated "exceptional" items.

The global lens and the technologies that could reset the curve

India's six are mid-sized players in global industries run by Turkish, American, Israeli and Korean majors. The technology risks run from natural soda ash to sodium-ion batteries and paper cups.

Each of the sub-sector's molecules is priced globally, so the right comparison set is global. On that comparison, Tata Chemicals is a genuine global major in soda ash, Archean is a top-five bromine extractor by capacity, GHCL is a low-cost but sub-scale synthetic producer, and the styrenics pair are regional converters without the feedstock integration that the global leaders enjoy.

Two global facts matter most for Indian investors. In soda ash, the global industry is in surplus by roughly 15 million tonnes (INDICATIVE), and the lowest-cost new tonnes are Chinese and Turkish natural capacity; Indian synthetic plants will never win on cost against them, only on proximity, protection and service. In bromine, supply is concentrated in a handful of brine deposits in Israel, Jordan, Arkansas and Shandong, and in a politically fragile region; that concentration is why prices can double in a quarter, and why a reliable Indian producer such as Archean can command a place in global buyers' supply chains.

Technology and disruption

  • Natural vs synthetic soda ash. The disruption already under way. Each new natural tonne pushes a synthetic tonne up the cost curve; synthetic soda ash also emits roughly 0.4–0.5 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne (INDICATIVE), a liability if carbon pricing reaches it. EU CBAM does not yet cover soda ash. Tata's Magadi plant runs the world's first electric soda ash calciner, and its UK business captures CO₂ to make bicarbonate.
  • Glass demand. China's 2024–25 solar-glass overbuild showed that solar demand can disappoint, and thinner glass and bifacial modules reduce soda ash use per gigawatt.
  • Batteries. Sodium-ion cathodes use soda ash, and lithium carbonate refining needs about 2 tonnes of soda ash per tonne of lithium carbonate equivalent: small upsides today. Zinc-bromide and flow batteries are an option for bromine.
  • Flame retardants. Phosphorus-based alternatives and EU regulatory pressure on some brominated flame retardants are a slow-moving threat to bromine's largest market.
  • Styrenics substitution. Polypropylene compounds replace HIPS and ABS in some appliance parts; paper and PLA replace polystyrene disposables; chemical recycling of polystyrene is a niche positive.
  • Bio-route chemicals. Bio-MEG competes with petro-MEG at US$450–550 a tonne; the green premium depends on brand owners' Scope-3 commitments.
  • Silicon carbide. Archean's SiCSem fab enters a fast-growing market for EV and power electronics chips dominated by global specialists; the technology risk sits entirely outside its core business.
In plain terms

The biggest technology threat to Indian soda ash makers is not a new invention but an old one: digging soda ash out of the ground instead of making it from salt and limestone. That method now has enough capacity in China, Türkiye and the US to set the world price for years. The threats to styrenics and bromine are slower substitution stories; the opportunities are in solar glass, batteries and, if it works, Archean's chip fab.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The bull case rests on a policy decision, a Chinese supply response and India's demand growth; the bear case on a glut that outlasts balance sheets and a styrenics quarter that will not repeat.

Because the six companies are exposed to different cycles, the bull and bear cases are really three pairs of arguments, one for soda ash and salt, one for bromine, and one for styrenics, plus a general argument about capital allocation at the bottom of the cycle. The lists below gather the strongest points on each side; investors should weigh them company by company rather than for the "sector" as a whole.

The bull case

  • A trough is visible. Chinese soda ash at a decade low (RMB 1,156/t), Tata US at US$1/t and record Chinese inventories are the conditions in which high-cost capacity closes and cycles turn.
  • Policy optionality. A safeguard duty or quantitative restriction around 16 November 2026 could add hundreds of crores of annual EBITDA to GHCL and Tata India (each ₹1,000/t ≈ ₹100–115 crore, ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Indian demand. Soda ash demand growing 4.5–5% a year, led by solar glass (+~1 mt over five years); ABS imports of 35–45% to substitute.
  • Cost moats. GHCL earns a 17.4% ROCE at the bottom; Archean a 22% margin; Tata owns ~100 years of Wyoming trona.
  • Cheap assets. Tata at 0.73× book with investments worth ~64% of its market value at book; GHCL at ~3.8× EV/EBITDA with net cash.
  • Bromine tightness. Concentrated, politically exposed supply and a net-importing China support prices; Archean has ~60% spare capacity to exploit any recovery.

The bear case

  • The glut can last. ~15 mt of global surplus (INDICATIVE) and the lowest-cost new capacity in China; analysts see no recovery before late 2027.
  • Policy has disappointed before. The 2021 and 2025 ADD recommendations were never imposed and the MIP leaked.
  • India may build its own glut. Capacity 4.65 → ~6.8 mtpa by FY30 against demand of ~6.4–6.7 mt (INDICATIVE).
  • Styrenics peak earnings. Q1 FY27 margins rested on a war-time delta and inventory gains; management itself called them an aberration; SPL trades at 28× trailing earnings and 5.8× book.
  • Capital allocation risk. GHCL's ₹6,500 crore Kutch plant is 1.8× its market value; Archean's ₹2,067 crore chip fab is far outside its core.
  • Balance-sheet and basis risk. Tata's ~3.3× net debt/EBITDA and further impairment risk; India Glycols' debt allocation unknown after the demerger.

On balance, the sub-sector's near-term direction is set by two dated events, the Q2 FY27 results season (from 23 October) and the safeguard determination (around 16 November), and one undated one, the timing of a Chinese soda ash recovery. The soda ash pair have the most to gain from the first two; the styrenics pair the most to lose from Q2's normalisation; Archean sits between, depending on how much of the bromine spike survived into the September quarter.

In plain terms

The optimist sees soda ash at the bottom, a government about to protect domestic makers and Indian solar factories about to need a million more tonnes. The pessimist sees a world with too much soda ash for years, a government that has said no before, and two plastics companies whose best quarter in years is being priced as their new normal. Both are describing the same facts; the difference is timing.
