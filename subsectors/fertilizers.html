The one-page view: a business whose margin is a government number minus a world-market number

Nine listed companies that sell the same few products at prices set in Delhi, buy their raw materials at prices set in the Gulf, Morocco and Qatar, and wait for the state to pay the difference.

India's listed fertiliser makers do not set the price of what they sell. The farmer's price of urea, the country's most-used fertiliser, has been fixed at ₹242 for a 45-kilogram bag for more than a decade. The farmer's price of the phosphate and potash fertilisers (DAP and the NPK "complexes") is formally free but informally capped. The government pays everything above those prices, which in FY26 came to about ₹1.86 lakh crore on the revised budget. A fertiliser company's margin is therefore the gap between two numbers it does not control: what the state reimburses, and what gas, ammonia, phosphoric acid, sulphur and potash cost on the world market, converted at whatever the rupee is worth that week.

This report covers the nine NSE 750 members in the sub-sector. Four are private: Coromandel International, Chambal Fertilisers, Paradeep Phosphates and Deepak Fertilisers & Petrochemicals. Three are central public-sector companies: FACT, RCF and National Fertilizers (NFL). Two are controlled by the Gujarat government: GNFC and GSFC. Together they reported ₹1,50,048 crore of revenue and ₹7,666 crore of profit after tax in FY26, and were worth ₹1,70,213 crore in the market on 16 October 2026. The largest producer in the country, the cooperative IFFCO, is not listed, and neither are KRIBHCO, the three HURL plants or Ramagundam; they appear in this report as competitors and benchmarks.

Three of the nine are not really fertiliser businesses any more, and the reader should know that from the start. Deepak earns most of its profit from technical ammonium nitrate (TAN), the explosive used in mines. GNFC's chemicals division (TDI, acetic acid, nitric acid) earned ₹425 crore of segment profit in Q1 FY27 while its fertiliser division lost ₹85 crore. GSFC is a third chemicals and a large portfolio of shares in other Gujarat companies. They sit in the sub-sector because they share the ammonia and acid chain with fertiliser, and this report shows them whole while saying which slice is fertiliser.

The five forces that shape the sub-sector

  • The state is price-setter, paymaster and, for five of the nine, owner. Urea's farm price is frozen; the subsidy on phosphate and potash (the Nutrient-Based Subsidy, NBS) is reset each season; and the subsidy is paid only after a sale is recorded on a point-of-sale machine. Profit is what policy leaves behind. Read the subsidy design before the profit and loss account.
  • The country imports its molecules, and the subsidy lags. About 61% of the DAP and 26% of the urea India used in FY26 was imported, along with all of its potash and most of its phosphoric acid, sulphur and ammonia. Costs move daily in dollars; the subsidy moves twice a year in rupees. The 2026 shock (phosphoric acid at about $1,700 a tonne, sulphur near $1,000, the rupee near ₹95) showed how lopsided that is.
  • The budget, not demand, decides the cash. The nine companies made ₹7,666 crore of profit in FY26 but generated −₹1,129 crore of operating cash and added ₹10,452 crore of borrowings (+62%). The FY27 subsidy budget of ₹1.71 lakh crore was 58% spent by 19 August, against an official estimate of need near ₹2.41 lakh crore.
  • In urea, efficiency against a government norm is the only margin a company controls, and 2026 reset the norms. RCF's Thal plant was tightened (a ₹171.5 crore hit, due in Q2 FY27); GNFC's Bharuch plant was relaxed (+₹61 crore). A new investment policy (NIPU-2026) offers 12–16% assured return on equity for about 10 million tonnes of new capacity.
  • Escaping the subsidy decides the rating. TAN, TDI, acetyls, caprolactam, crop protection and captive acid carried 2026's profits and earn the higher multiples. The one anomaly is FACT: 28% of the group's market value on a loss, because almost none of its shares trade.

Revenue and profit barely correlate. NFL has more revenue than Chambal and earned about a ninth of its profit. GNFC has the second-smallest revenue and the fourth-largest profit. The reasons are the subject of most of this report: urea makers book the subsidy as revenue, so a rise in gas prices swells revenue without adding profit; efficient plants keep the gap between the norm and actual gas use; and chemicals earn market prices while fertiliser earns a regulated return.

In plain terms

A fertiliser company in India is a processor working on a government contract. The government fixes the selling price, reimburses most of the cost by a formula, and pays when its budget allows. The company makes money by running its plant more efficiently than the formula assumes, by buying its raw materials well, and by selling things the government does not price, such as chemicals and pesticides. In 2026 the formula fell behind world prices and the budget ran short, so the processors with the most non-fertiliser business did best.

The year of this report matters. In March 2026, strikes on Qatar's LNG export terminals cut gas to India's urea plants; urea output fell to 17.5 lakh tonnes that month against 24.7 lakh tonnes a year earlier. Ammonia, sulphur and phosphoric acid, most of which India buys from West Asia, surged. The rupee hit a record ₹95.80 per dollar in May. The government raised the Kharif 2026 subsidy rates in April, before the worst of the cost rise, and ICRA has since called those rates inadequate and kept a negative outlook on the sector (30 September 2026). The latest results available are for April–June 2026 (Q1 FY27); they still look healthy for most phosphate makers because of inventory bought earlier. This report explains why the second and third quarters are likely to look worse unless the government raises the Rabi 2026-27 rates, which had not been announced on 17 October.

01

How a tonne earns: from a tanker of gas or acid to a bag on a farmer's cart

Three products, three molecules, three regulatory regimes, and one government that sits at the end of every chain.

Plants need three main nutrients from fertiliser: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P, counted in the trade as P2O5) and potassium (K, counted as K2O). Sulphur (S) is increasingly added as a fourth. Every fertiliser on an Indian shelf is a way of packing some combination of those into a 45- or 50-kilogram bag, and the chemistry of making each one decides who makes money from it.

Nitrogen comes from ammonia, and ammonia comes from natural gas (or, at a few new plants, from coal). A urea plant combines ammonia with carbon dioxide from the same gas to produce urea, which is 46% nitrogen and is by far India's largest fertiliser at about 39–40 million tonnes a year. Ammonia can also be turned into nitric acid and then into ammonium nitrate, which is used as a fertiliser in some countries but in India mostly goes into mining explosives (TAN). Phosphorus comes from rock phosphate, which is mined in Morocco, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, China and a few other places. The rock is dissolved in sulphuric acid (made by burning sulphur, a refinery by-product mostly from the Gulf) to make phosphoric acid. Phosphoric acid plus ammonia makes DAP (18% N, 46% P2O5) and the various NPK grades. Potassium comes from potash (MOP, muriate of potash), mined in Canada, Belarus and Russia. India has no commercially mined potash, so all of it is imported and traded, not made.

The nutrient content matters commercially because the NBS subsidy is paid per kilogram of nutrient. A tonne of DAP carries 460 kilograms of P2O5 and 180 of N, so it attracts a large subsidy; a tonne of SSP carries only 160 kilograms of P2O5 and attracts much less. A maker's product mix is therefore a choice about how much of its revenue to take from the state and how much from the farmer.

Three regulatory buckets

The single most important classification in this sub-sector is not by company size or ownership but by which regulatory regime each product sits in:

  • Urea, under a fixed farm price and a cost-plus concession. The farmer pays ₹242 a bag (about ₹5,360 a tonne before the neem-coating charge and taxes). The government pays each plant its "recognised cost" minus that price. Recognised cost is calculated from a plant-specific energy norm (gigacalories of gas per tonne) times the pooled gas price, plus fixed costs. Makers: Chambal, NFL, RCF, GNFC and GSFC (small), and Paradeep (Goa and Mangalore units).
  • Phosphate and potash (P&K) fertilisers, under the Nutrient-Based Subsidy. Since April 2010 the government pays a fixed number of rupees per kilogram of N, P, K and S, reset each Kharif (April–September) and Rabi (October–March) season. The farm price is "decontrolled" but companies are expected to hold it steady. Makers: Coromandel, Paradeep, FACT, GSFC, RCF (its Suphala brand) and Deepak (Mahadhan), plus the trading books of Chambal and NFL.
  • Industrial chemicals from the same chain, at market prices. Deepak (TAN, nitric acid, IPA, ammonia), GNFC (TDI, acetic acid, methanol, nitric acid, ammonium nitrate), GSFC (caprolactam, nylon-6, melamine), RCF (methanol, ammonium nitrate, nitric acid, DMF), NFL (nitric acid, ammonium nitrate), Chambal (TAN from August 2026) and FACT (caprolactam, now shut).

Where the value is captured

Four things separate a good fertiliser business from a poor one, and they recur through every later section. First, owning a cheap molecule upstream: Deepak's 500,000-tonne ammonia plant at Taloja, Coromandel's new phosphoric and sulphuric acid plants at Kakinada and its stake in a Senegal rock mine (BMCC), Paradeep's sulphuric acid and its Moroccan co-owner OCP, and GSFC's newly approved acid plants at Sikka. Second, running a urea plant more efficiently than its government norm, which Chambal's 2019-built Gadepan-III plant does. Third, brand and distribution for products the government does not price: crop protection chemicals, specialty nutrients, retail stores (Coromandel has about 1,100–1,200 "Mana Gromor" outlets). Fourth, merchant chemicals sold at import-parity prices, such as TAN, TDI and acetic acid. A company that does none of these four, and simply converts imported acid and ammonia into DAP, captures the least value and carries the most risk when the spread between subsidy and input cost goes wrong.

In plain terms

A urea maker is paid a government-set price that covers its costs by formula, so its money comes from keeping actual costs below what the formula allows. A maker of DAP from imported acid buys Moroccan or Jordanian acid at world prices and sells to Indian farmers at a capped price, with the government adding a fixed amount per kilogram of nutrient; when acid prices jump and that amount does not, the maker loses. Making TAN or TDI is a normal chemical business with normal market prices. Each of the nine companies is some blend of these three.

The reader who wants the adjacent chemistry should see our reports on chemical intermediates, where nitric acid and ammonia derivatives also appear, and on fluorochemicals, which matters here because Paradeep's planned aluminium fluoride plant will use fluosilicic acid, a by-product of phosphoric acid making.

02

Size, subsidy and growth: a ₹3 lakh crore economy that the budget measures best

India is the world's second-largest user of fertiliser nutrients and its largest importer of urea and DAP; the cleanest measure of the industry's size is the government's subsidy bill.

Measuring the size of India's fertiliser industry is awkward because the price of the product is not a market price. The nine listed companies' FY26 revenue of ₹1,50,048 crore is roughly half the industry by value; IFFCO alone reports a turnover of around ₹40,000–45,000 crore. A broader indicative estimate takes the volumes consumed in FY26 (about 39–40 million tonnes of urea, 10 million of DAP, 14–15 million of NPK complexes, 6 million of SSP and 2.4 million of potash) and multiplies them by a blended "true" cost of ₹40,000–90,000 a tonne. That gives a delivered fertiliser economy of roughly ₹3.0–3.5 lakh crore (INDICATIVE), of which farmers pay perhaps ₹0.5–0.6 lakh crore through retail prices and the state pays most of the rest.

The government subsidy is the cleanest size number, because it is published. It was ₹1,86,460 crore in FY26 on the revised estimate: ₹1,26,460 crore for urea and ₹60,000 crore for P&K under NBS. One secondary source puts FY26 actual spending higher, at about ₹2.11 lakh crore, but that figure is unconfirmed; sources also disagree on the original FY26 budget (₹1.57 lakh crore and ₹1.68 lakh crore are both quoted). For FY27 the budget estimate is ₹1,70,799 crore (urea ₹1,16,799 crore, NBS ₹54,000 crore), and an official quoted in August put the year's real need at about ₹2.41 lakh crore.

The pattern is a ratchet with spikes. The subsidy roughly doubled between FY20 and FY22, peaked at about ₹2.51 lakh crore in FY23 when the Russia–Ukraine war drove gas, ammonia and phosphate prices to records, then settled near ₹1.8–1.9 lakh crore. The FY23 profit peak of ₹9,347 crore was not a fertiliser peak. It was built on chemical spreads at GNFC (PAT ₹1,472 crore) and GSFC (₹1,266 crore), and on inventory gains at the phosphate makers when the subsidy rose faster than the cost of stock already bought. When those reversed in FY24, combined profit halved to ₹5,049 crore, even though the volume of fertiliser sold was broadly steady. FY26's ₹7,666 crore came from a different mix: a strong year for phosphate converters (Paradeep's profit rose 50%), steady urea earnings (Chambal +18%), and a recovery at GNFC. Coromandel's profit fell 8% even as its revenue rose 31%, for reasons covered in section 17.

In plain terms

Do not read a fertiliser company's revenue growth as a sign of a growing business. A 25% rise in revenue can simply mean that gas or phosphoric acid got more expensive and the subsidy passed it through. Profit, profit per tonne and cash collected are the numbers that tell you whether the business did better.

For a sense of scale abroad, the nine Indian companies' combined revenue (about $17 billion at ₹88/$) is smaller than the revenue of a single global major such as Nutrien. Indian makers are converters and regulated utilities rather than owners of cheap gas or mines, which is why their margins are lower and why section 19 compares them with global peers carefully.

03

Rain, rice and a farmer who sees no price

Demand is set by the monsoon, the crop mix and a retail price so low that urea use keeps rising whatever the government says about using less.

Fertiliser demand in India is unusually insensitive to price, because for the largest product the farmer barely pays one. Urea at ₹242 a bag is a fraction of its cost, so farmers use it generously and the country's nutrient balance is far from the agronomic ideal. The usual target ratio of nitrogen to phosphate to potash is about 4:2:1; India's actual use is much more nitrogen-heavy because urea is so cheap relative to everything else. That imbalance is a policy problem the government has tried to address with soil health cards, nano urea and the PM-PRANAM scheme (2023), which rewards states that cut chemical fertiliser use. None of these has yet bent the urea curve.

The monsoon and the crop mix

Volumes in any one year depend on rain. About half of India's farmland is rain-fed, and a weak monsoon means less sowing, less fertiliser applied and, for the following Rabi season, lower reservoir and soil-moisture levels. 2026 is an El Niño year. The India Meteorological Department cut its forecast to 90% of the long-period average in late May. June rainfall came in at only 60% of normal, July recovered to 103%, and the two months together were at 87%. The kharif sowing shortfall narrowed from about 23% at the end of June to about 3% by the end of July; a later report put the final kharif area 4.7% lower. Chambal's management said in its Q1 FY27 commentary that the monsoon "caught up sharply in July" and that urea and DAP offtake were healthy. End-of-season rainfall figures were not available for this report.

The crop mix shapes what is used. Minimum support prices for paddy, wheat and sugarcane, which are heavy users of urea, keep those crops attractive. Horticulture, pulses and oilseeds use more NPK, SSP and specialty nutrients (water-soluble fertilisers, micronutrients, bio-stimulants), which is where Coromandel and Deepak push their non-subsidised lines. Seasonality: only the full year is a fair comparison

Fertiliser is sold ahead of sowing. The peak quarters are July–September (Q2, the Kharif top-dressing and the run-up to Rabi) and October–December (Q3, Rabi sowing). January–March and April–June are weaker. Coromandel's FY26 quarterly revenue shows the shape, and NFL's profit shows how a thin-margin utility can lose money in the weak quarters and still make a profit for the year: it lost ₹39 crore and ₹36 crore in the first two quarters of FY26 and then earned ₹135 crore and ₹152 crore.

For FY27 the demand outlook is cautious. ICRA expects fertiliser volumes to be flat to 5% lower, because of the uneven monsoon and because availability of imported DAP and NPK may be tight in the Rabi season. The Department of Fertilizers reported Kharif DAP availability of 40.29 lakh tonnes against a requirement of 32.35 lakh tonnes in July, a comfortable position, but press reports in August flagged falling domestic DAP and NPK output and slower imports as a Rabi risk. In plain terms

Indian farmers do not buy less urea when it gets more expensive to make, because they never see that cost. They buy less when it does not rain, or when the bags are not in the shop. So for this sub-sector, demand is mostly about rain and supply, and the price signal that would normally ration a scarce product is absorbed by the government budget instead.

The structural risk to demand is policy, not technology. If the government moved from paying the subsidy to companies to paying it directly to farmers as cash, or capped the number of subsidised bags per farmer using soil-health data, the amount of urea used could fall sharply. Such proposals have been discussed for a decade and have not been implemented; section 20 treats them as the biggest long-term regime risk.

04

Imported molecules: a country that buys its nitrogen, phosphate and potash in dollars

The supply side of Indian fertiliser is a shopping list written in West Asia, Morocco, Jordan, Canada and China, and the costs on it are the costs the subsidy is supposed to cover.

India makes most of its urea, about two-fifths of its DAP and none of its potash. Behind even the domestic production sits a second layer of imports: the ammonia, phosphoric acid, rock phosphate and sulphur that domestic plants convert. Measured by molecules rather than by finished bags, the Indian fertiliser industry is a large converter of imported intermediates, and that is the fact from which most of its risk flows.

In FY26 India produced 293.26 lakh tonnes of urea (down from 306.67) and imported 103.50 lakh tonnes (up 83% from 56.47), so imports supplied about 26% of the urea the country used, against 16% a year earlier. DAP was more import-heavy still: production of 39.14 lakh tonnes (up from 37.72) and imports of 61.94 lakh tonnes (up 36% from 45.69), so about 61% of supply came from abroad. Total fertiliser production across all products was 517.49 lakh tonnes. All potash (MOP) is imported, as is about 90% of the rock phosphate used to make phosphoric acid, a majority of the phosphoric acid itself, and most of the sulphur and of the ammonia used by phosphate makers.

What hurts whom

The same input shock lands differently on each business model. For urea makers, higher gas prices are passed through in the concession, so the hit lands on working capital (the subsidy receivable grows with the gas bill) and on plants that use more energy than their norm (each extra gigacalorie costs more). For phosphate converters, the hit is to margin, because the NBS rate is fixed for the season while acid, ammonia and sulphur prices rise, and to availability, because in 2026 sulphur and acid were rationed. Coromandel cut its plants to 72% utilisation in Q1 FY27 "amid volatile sulphur prices", choosing not to make product it would sell at a loss. For chemical makers the shock was a windfall: ammonia, nitric acid, acetic acid, TDI and solvent prices all rose, and Deepak, GNFC and RCF's chemicals division reported strong Q1 FY27 results. Section 09 tells that story in sequence.

Supply is also constrained by plant capacity. India's urea capacity sits at about 30 million tonnes a year, and ICRA expects urea plants to run at below 80% of capacity in FY27 because of gas constraints. On the phosphate side, Coromandel's granulation capacity is about 38 lakh tonnes a year (calculated from its Q1 production of 6.9 lakh tonnes at 72% utilisation), Paradeep sold 42.1 lakh tonnes in FY26 from its Paradeep, Goa and Mangalore plants, FACT has 6.33 lakh tonnes of FACTAMFOS capacity, and RCF's Suphala line makes about 6.6 lakh tonnes of NPK. The new urea capacity promised under NIPU-2026 (about 10 million tonnes over 8–9 plants) would cut urea imports sharply if built, but none of it can arrive before the late 2020s.

05

Who makes India's fertiliser: a cooperative giant, a PSU bloc and a handful of private converters

The largest producer is not listed, the government owns or controls much of the rest, and the private listed companies compete on cost, reach and availability rather than on price.

The structure of the Indian fertiliser industry is a product of six decades of state building. In the 1960s and 1970s the government built plants directly (the Fertilizer Corporation of India, later split into NFL, RCF and others), sponsored cooperatives (IFFCO in 1967, KRIBHCO), and encouraged states to set up their own (GSFC in 1962, GNFC in 1976). Private capital came later and mostly into phosphates and into a few efficient urea plants. The result is an industry in which the listed private sector is a minority of urea production, the largest single producer is a cooperative, and five of the nine listed companies are controlled by a government.

Urea: three blocs and no price competition

India's urea production is split roughly three ways (approximate shares): cooperatives (IFFCO and KRIBHCO, about 30% or a little more), central PSUs and PSU joint ventures (NFL, RCF, HURL's three plants at Gorakhpur, Barauni and Sindri, Ramagundam Fertilizers in which NFL holds 26%, Brahmaputra Valley, and in future Talcher, together about 35%), and private and state companies (Chambal, Matix, Indorama India, Yara's former Babrala plant now with Indorama, GNFC and GSFC, the remainder). Imports are canalised: only designated agencies (Indian Potash Ltd, NFL and the state trading companies) may import urea, through government-directed tenders.

Five ways to earn on a subsidy

The nine companies fall into five business models with sharply different economics: a cost-plus utility, an import converter, a nitrogen-chemicals integrator, a state petrochemical hybrid and an agri-solutions platform.

Sorting the companies by business model rather than by ownership explains most of the differences in their returns. The same urea plant earns a low but steady return whoever owns it; the same DAP granulator gains or loses with the spread between subsidy and acid prices; a TAN plant earns whatever the mining market pays. Each company is a mix, but each has a dominant model.

A. The cost-plus urea utility

A urea plant is paid its recognised cost plus a regulated return, so the business looks like a utility: low margins (NFL's FY26 EBITDA margin was 3.9%, RCF's 5.2%), low but positive returns, and little sensitivity to volume. The levers are operational. A plant that uses less gas per tonne than its norm keeps the difference; a plant that runs above its reassessed capacity earns an incentive linked to import parity; a plant that breaks down loses both. Chambal shows what the model can earn at its best: a fully depreciated older base plus a 2019-built plant that is among the most energy-efficient in India gives it a 25% return on capital employed and a 13% EBITDA margin (including its trading and crop protection businesses). NFL, with older northern plants built for naphtha and converted to gas, shows the model at its weakest.

B. The imported-intermediate converter

A phosphate converter buys phosphoric acid and ammonia (or makes the acid from imported rock and sulphur), granulates DAP and NPK, and sells at a capped retail price plus the NBS. Its margin is the spread between subsidy-plus-retail and input cost, and that spread can swing by thousands of rupees a tonne within a season. Paradeep, the purest example, earned about ₹5,366 of EBITDA per tonne in FY26, up from about ₹4,430 in FY25, and its profit rose 50%. FACT is the same model done badly: a loss-making PSU with ₹3,985 crore of borrowings whose interest bill of about ₹250 crore a year exceeds its operating profit.

C. The nitrogen-chemicals integrator

Deepak owns a 500,000-tonne ammonia plant at Taloja (commissioned August 2023), fed by contracted LNG from Equinor, and turns most of its ammonia into nitric acid and TAN, of which it has about 44% of the Indian market. When merchant ammonia spiked to $850–900 a tonne in May 2026, Deepak kept selling TAN at import-parity prices while its own ammonia cost was set by its gas contract, so its Q1 FY27 EBITDA margin reached 26%. Chambal joined this model in August 2026 when its 2.4-lakh-tonne TAN plant began commercial production.

D. The state fertiliser and petrochemical hybrid

GNFC and GSFC were built by the Gujarat government as fertiliser companies and grew chemical arms that now dominate their profits. GNFC makes toluene di-isocyanate (TDI, used in polyurethane foam), acetic acid, nitric acid, aniline and formic acid alongside about 6.4 lakh tonnes of urea; GSFC makes caprolactam (for nylon-6), nylon chips and melamine alongside DAP, ammonium phosphate sulphate and ammonium sulphate. Both sit on large cash or investment books and almost no debt, and both earn returns well below their potential because the cash earns little and the state controls capital allocation.

E. The agri-solutions platform

Coromandel started as a phosphate maker and has added crop protection chemicals (about 15% of revenue and about 34% of EBITDA in FY26, including NACL), specialty nutrients, retail stores, nano fertilisers and agricultural drones (Dhaksha). The fertiliser business still provides most of the revenue, but the non-subsidised businesses provide the growth and the stability that justify a multiple about twice the sub-sector's.

The rulebook: a frozen price, a per-kilo subsidy, a single brand and a sale that must be scanned

Eleven policy instruments decide almost everything about a fertiliser company's revenue, and most of them change on a government timetable, not a market one.

Indian fertiliser is one of the most regulated industries in the country. The urea price is set under the Essential Commodities Act and the Fertiliser Control Order; urea plants are paid under a pricing scheme that dates back to 2003 and was layered with energy norms in 2015; phosphate and potash are paid under a separate scheme introduced in 2010; and since 2018 every subsidised sale has to be recorded on a point-of-sale device against the buyer's identity. Urea: cost-plus, with a plant-specific yardstick

The urea pricing system is a modified cost-plus scheme. Each plant has a "recognised cost" per tonne made up of energy (the plant's energy norm in gigacalories per tonne, multiplied by the pooled gas price), fixed costs (salaries, repairs, overheads and a return on net worth, set at levels last comprehensively revised many years ago), bagging, neem coating and freight. The government pays the plant its recognised cost minus the ₹242 retail price, as a "concession". The energy norm is the key: if a plant uses less energy than its norm, it keeps the saving; if it uses more, it absorbs the loss. Norms have been tightened in steps since 2015 to push old plants toward modern efficiency, and the 2025–28 norms, notified in July 2026 with effect from April 2025, are section 08's subject.

Plants also earn an incentive for producing above their "reassessed capacity" (a capacity figure the government fixed for each plant), paid at a rate linked to the import price of urea. When imported urea is expensive, as in 2026, that incentive is worth more, which rewards plants that run hard. When gas is short, as in March 2026, plants cannot run at all.

Phosphate and potash: a per-kilo subsidy, reset each season

For P&K fertiliser the government does not reimburse cost. It pays a fixed rupee amount per kilogram of each nutrient, announced before each Kharif and Rabi season, and leaves companies to set retail prices. In theory that makes the retail price free; in practice the government expects companies to hold prices steady (DAP at about ₹1,350 a bag has been held for years) and uses "special packages" when costs rise, such as the ₹3,500-a-tonne top-up on DAP. The result is a system in which the company bears the risk of input costs moving within a season, and the government decides twice a year how much of a cost rise to absorb.

DBT, the Bharat bag and canalisation

Since 2018, a company is paid its subsidy only after the fertiliser is sold to a farmer and the sale is scanned on a point-of-sale machine at the retailer. That rule had a good purpose (it cut leakage of subsidised fertiliser to industry and across borders) and a side effect: it ties the company's cash to two things it does not control, the timing of the farmer's purchase and the availability of budget. In a normal year the subsidy arrives within a few weeks of the sale; in a year when the budget runs out, claims queue. The single "Bharat" brand, introduced in October 2022, standardised packaging so that the government could move stock between companies and regions more easily; for companies it removed most of the value of a fertiliser brand. Canalisation of urea imports means that, in urea, the government itself is the marginal supplier; that keeps domestic makers from facing import competition but also from earning import-parity prices.

In plain terms

The government decides three things that matter more than anything a fertiliser company's management does: how much each tonne is worth (through the concession and NBS rates), how much the farmer pays (through the retail price), and when the money arrives (through DBT and the budget). Companies decide how efficiently they make the product and what else they sell.

Two smaller instruments matter for particular companies. TAN is classified as an explosive precursor and needs a licence under the Explosives Act, administered by PESO; that is a barrier to entry that protects Deepak's and now Chambal's TAN margins. Anti-dumping duties have at times protected TDI (GNFC), melamine and caprolactam (GSFC) from Chinese imports, and their renewal or expiry moves those companies' chemical spreads. Finally, the rule that listed companies must have at least 25% public shareholding hangs over FACT, where the government and another government entity hold about 99%, and over RCF and NFL, which sit right at the 75% limit. RCF's shareholders approved a follow-on public offer of up to ₹1,500 crore on 25 September 2026; section 18 discusses what it means.

Urea: a frozen price, an assured return and norms that just moved Deep dive

The urea business is a regulated utility in which the only margin a company controls is how much gas it burns against a government yardstick; in July 2026 the government reset the yardsticks and launched a new policy to build ten million tonnes of plants.

Urea is where the state's grip is tightest. The farmer has paid ₹242 for a 45-kilogram bag for more than a decade; the government pays each plant the rest of its recognised cost; and the plant's profit is decided by a handful of parameters set in Delhi. To understand Chambal, NFL, RCF, and the urea slices of GNFC, GSFC and Paradeep, you need to understand four of those parameters: the energy norm, the gas price, the fixed-cost allowance and the investment policy for new plants. All four were in motion in 2026.

Norm revisions cut both ways, plant by plant, which makes them hard to forecast from outside. Thal is a relatively modern plant whose actual consumption the government judged it could tighten towards; Bharuch is older and was given relief. NFL's three northern plants, built for naphtha in the 1970s and converted to gas, are the obvious candidates for pain if the government applied Thal's logic to them, and NFL has not yet disclosed its new norms. Investors should treat the missing disclosures as the biggest single unknown for NFL's FY27 earnings.

The fixed-cost question

The other half of recognised cost, the fixed-cost allowance for salaries, repairs and a return on net worth, has not been comprehensively revised for older plants in many years, and old PSU plants (NFL, RCF) complain that it no longer covers their real costs. The Fertiliser Secretary was reported as saying the government would raise fixed costs for urea makers by year-end; the date of that statement is unclear and nothing has been notified. A fixed-cost revision would help NFL and RCF more than the norm tightening hurts them, so it is a genuine upside risk for the PSU urea makers, but it is not in anyone's numbers yet.

NIPU-2026: twelve to sixteen per cent, assured

On 15 July 2026 the Cabinet approved the National Investment Policy for Urea 2026 (NIPU-2026), designed to add about 10 million tonnes of gas-based urea capacity across 8–9 plants and cut imports. Its main features: an assured return on equity of at least 12% and at most 16%; fixed and variable costs treated separately for subsidy; fixed costs converted to rupees after four years at the prevailing exchange rate (protecting investors against a falling rupee on dollar-denominated capital costs); and a claimed saving of more than ₹250 crore per plant compared with plants built under NIP-2012. The earlier policy produced six units, four through PSU joint ventures (HURL's three plants and RFCL) plus Chambal's Gadepan-III and Matix. Candidates under the new policy include Chambal's fourth train (about ₹10,000 crore; financial bids were due in mid-October 2026), NFL's planned greenfield, a new complex at Namrup in Assam approved earlier in 2026, and a possible RCF–GAIL plant in Vidarbha.

For a listed company, a 12–16% assured return on equity is a modest but very safe return, roughly the same as a regulated power utility's. For Chambal, which earns 20% on equity today, a fourth train would add scale and stability rather than raise returns, and would require it to borrow heavily for the first time in years. The more important signal is that the government wants domestic urea capacity badly enough to guarantee returns for it: the import line of the subsidy budget, which ran out first in 2026, is the reason.

Nano urea: much publicity, little displacement

IFFCO launched nano urea, a liquid sprayed on leaves, in June 2021, claiming that one 500-ml bottle could replace a 45-kilogram bag. Uptake has been slow and its efficacy is contested. To 30 November 2025, 14.11 crore bottles had been produced and 11.62 crore sold in total; sales were about 2.07 crore bottles in FY25 and about 1.45 crore nano urea bottles (plus 65 lakh nano DAP) in FY26, against a target of 8 crore. IFFCO has not submitted an evaluation report; studies by the National Productivity Council and the Indian Council of Agricultural Research are under way, and a Punjab Agricultural University trial reported yield drops. Even taking IFFCO's one-bottle-one-bag claim at face value, 2 crore bottles a year would displace about 0.9 million tonnes of urea, at most 2.3% of annual use. In practice the displacement is far smaller: urea sales still grew about 4% in FY26.

The West Asia shock of 2026: gas cut, molecules scarce, rupee weak Deep dive

Strikes on Qatar's LNG plants in March set off a chain that ran through every input India's fertiliser makers buy, blew the subsidy budget and inverted the usual ranking of who earns most.

For most of the past decade the main external risks to Indian fertiliser makers were Chinese export policy and the post-2022 normalisation of prices. In March 2026 a different risk arrived. The escalation of the conflict between the United States, Israel and Iran led to Iranian strikes on Qatar's Ras Laffan and Mesaieed complexes, which halted LNG exports. Qatar is India's largest LNG supplier, and LNG is the marginal fuel for its urea plants. Within weeks the shock spread to ammonia, sulphur and phosphoric acid, most of which India buys from the same region, and to the rupee.

The war cut India's gas, made its imported ingredients much dearer and weakened its currency. The P&K squeeze: a subsidy set in April, raw materials priced in July Deep dive

At Q3 2026 input prices a tonne of DAP made from imported acid or imported finished loses about ₹28,000–30,000 against the Kharif subsidy; Q1 results were still profitable, and the reasons why tell you what Q2 and Q3 will look like.

Phosphate and potash makers live on a spread they do not set. The government fixes the subsidy per kilogram of nutrient for a six-month season; the company sets a retail price that the government expects to stay put; and the company buys phosphoric acid, ammonia, sulphur, rock and potash at whatever the world charges that quarter, in dollars. When the subsidy is reset after costs rise, the spread is healthy. When costs rise after the subsidy is reset, the spread collapses. 2026 is the second case.

Working the numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE)

Start with what a tonne of DAP earns at Kharif 2026 rates. It carries 180 kilograms of nitrogen at ₹47.32 (₹8,518) and 460 kilograms of P2O5 at ₹52.76 (₹24,270), a subsidy of ₹32,788. Add the special DAP package of ₹3,500 a tonne, and the state pays ₹36,288. If the farmer pays ₹1,350 for a 50-kilogram bag (an assumption: actual 2026 retail prices could not be verified), that is ₹27,000 a tonne, and total realisation is about ₹63,288. The state pays 57%.

Now the cost. Imported DAP at the September 2026 price of $925 a tonne delivered, at ₹95 to the dollar, costs ₹87,875; adding about ₹4,000 of port handling, bagging, freight and dealer costs (estimate) gives ₹91,875. The importer loses about ₹28,600 on every tonne. Before the crisis, DAP at $650 and ₹88 cost ₹57,200, plus ₹4,000, against a Kharif 2025 realisation of about ₹58,300; the loss was then about ₹2,900 a tonne. Both are consistent with ICRA's statement that DAP imports were loss-making despite the special package. DAP made from imported phosphoric acid needs about 0.468 tonnes of P2O5 and about 0.22 tonnes of ammonia per tonne. At Q3 2026 prices (acid $1,700, ammonia about $600) that is about $928 of raw material, or ₹88,160 at ₹95, plus about ₹5,000 of conversion cost: ₹93,160, a loss of about ₹29,900 a tonne. At Q2 2026 prices (acid about $1,360, ammonia about $850, rupee about ₹93), the raw material was about $824, or ₹76,600, and the loss about ₹18,300.

Why Q1 was still profitable

The spot arithmetic says phosphate makers should have been losing money heavily by the middle of 2026. The reported numbers say otherwise: Paradeep earned about ₹7,310 of EBITDA per tonne in Q1 FY27, a record, and Coromandel still made ₹382 crore of profit. The gap between spot arithmetic and reported results has at least five causes, and each one tells you something about the next two quarters.

  • Inventory. Companies sold product made from acid and ammonia bought months earlier at lower prices. Q1 sales drew on stock bought in Q4 FY26 and early Q1; the $1,700 acid contracted for July–September is consumed in Q2 and Q3.
  • The April subsidy increase. NBS rose 10–21% per nutrient from 1 April, ahead of the Q3 acid price, so Q1 realisation rose before most of the cost did.
  • Captive and term-priced inputs. Coromandel produced 1.2 lakh tonnes of phosphoric acid in Q1 at Kakinada; Paradeep raised sulphuric acid output 32%; OCP and JPMC supply on quarterly contracts rather than spot.
  • Mix. Companies shifted toward SSP (Coromandel's SSP volumes rose 19%), lower-phosphate grades and traded MOP, and cut volumes of the worst-margin products.
  • Retail prices. If companies have quietly raised DAP or NPK retail prices above the ₹1,350 and ₹1,725 assumed here, the spot losses are overstated. Current retail prices could not be verified.

The first two causes run out over Q2 and Q3 FY27. The third and fourth are durable advantages for the integrated companies. The fifth is unknown. The most likely outcome, absent a large Rabi subsidy increase, is that EBITDA per tonne at the non-integrated converters falls sharply in Q2 and Q3 FY27 from Q1's levels, and that companies cut volumes of loss-making grades, which in turn tightens availability for the Rabi season. ICRA's negative outlook makes the same call. This is a judgement on direction and sensitivity, not a forecast of reported numbers.

In plain terms

A phosphate maker in mid-2026 was selling bags made from cheaper raw materials bought earlier, at a subsidy rate that had just gone up. That made the April–June quarter look good. The raw materials it is using now cost far more, and the subsidy has not moved since April. Unless the government raises the subsidy for the winter season, the July–December quarters should look much worse.

Who is most exposed

FACT buys ammonia, phosphoric acid and sulphur and makes 20:20:0:13 and ammonium sulphate; it has no captive acid and ₹3,985 crore of borrowings, and it already lost money in Q1 FY27. It also had a phosphoric acid shortage in FY25, so availability is a repeat risk. Paradeep is the purest converter but has sulphuric acid capacity of its own (raised by 0.6 million tonnes a year in FY26) and a structural acid channel through OCP; its EBITDA per tonne is the number to watch. GSFC's DAP and APS margins compressed in Q1 (revenue +64%, EBITDA +21%), and its answer, phosphoric and sulphuric acid plants at Sikka approved on 14 October 2026, will take years. Coromandel has its own acid at Kakinada and a stake in a rock mine, but still buys sulphur and much of its acid, and its Q1 profit fell 24% while it gained market share. RCF's Suphala NPK line and Deepak's nitrophosphate (which uses nitric acid rather than sulphuric acid, and Deepak's own ammonia) are smaller exposures within larger businesses.

In plain terms

The companies that make their own acid, own a share of a mine or have a long-term supplier behind them will bend in the P&K squeeze. The companies that buy everything on the spot market will break, or cut production. In 2026, owning the molecule is the difference between a bad quarter and a loss.

Profit without cash: the subsidy budget runs out before the year does Deep dive

The nine companies booked ₹7,666 crore of profit in FY26 and generated negative operating cash; the difference is money the government owes them, financed by ₹10,452 crore of new borrowing.

In most industries, a year of record revenue and rising profit produces cash. In Indian fertiliser it can produce debt. Revenue is mostly subsidy, the subsidy is a receivable until the government pays it, and the government pays out of an annual budget that is set before the year's prices are known. When prices rise or the budget is set too low, claims queue, receivable days stretch, and companies borrow to fund the gap. FY26 was such a year, and FY27 is shaping up to be worse.

Why FY26 went this way

Four things combined. The bill overran the budget: the FY26 revised estimate of ₹1.86 lakh crore was well above the original budget (₹1.68 lakh crore in one source, ₹1.57 lakh crore in another), and one unconfirmed source puts actual spending at ₹2.11 lakh crore, which if right means claims spilled into FY27. The West Asia cost spike arrived in Q4 FY26, at the end of the fiscal year, when the budget was already stretched. Imports surged (urea +83%, DAP +36%), and imports draw on the same budget lines. In plain terms

When the government runs short of subsidy money, it does not cut the subsidy rate. It pays later. The company still records the subsidy as revenue and profit, but the cash does not arrive, so it borrows from banks to pay its suppliers. The profit is real in the sense that the government will eventually pay; the cost is the interest in the meantime and the risk that the queue gets longer.

The FY27 setup is worse

By 19 August 2026, 58% of the FY27 budget of ₹1,70,799 crore had been spent, about ₹99,000 crore in under five months, leaving about ₹71,700 crore for the remaining seven and a half months, the busiest of the year. The imported-urea line was 92.4% spent. Against an official estimate of need near ₹2.41 lakh crore, the shortfall is about ₹70,000 crore. Unless Parliament approves supplementary grants (the winter session is the usual moment), receivable days at the urea makers and phosphate converters are likely to stretch further. In past shortfalls (notably FY23) the government arranged "special banking arrangements" under which banks lent to companies against the government's unpaid subsidy, with the government bearing part of the interest; a repeat would ease the cash pressure but not the timing.

What the float costs

The cost of carrying unpaid subsidy shows up as interest. Paradeep's FY26 interest bill of ₹528 crore was 2.4% of revenue, much of it on working-capital borrowings; FACT's ₹250 crore of interest exceeded its EBITDA; RCF's rose to ₹295 crore and NFL's to ₹253 crore. If the ₹5,500 crore of FY26 working-capital borrowing (ESTIMATE) carries an average rate of about 8%, it costs about ₹440 crore a year in interest, about 6% of the nine companies' combined profit. A further ₹70,000 crore shortfall spread across the whole industry, of which the listed nine might carry a third or more, would multiply that. The subsidy-receivable line is the hidden profit-and-loss item in this sub-sector.

Disclosure is poor. Most companies report subsidy receivables only at the half-year or in credit rating rationales. The latest figures available for this report were Coromandel's ₹3,785 crore at end-December 2025 (against ₹2,095 crore a year earlier), NFL's ₹2,954 crore at end-December 2025 (₹2,575 crore at FY25) and an undated ₹2,346 crore for Chambal. None of the nine had published a March 2026 or June 2026 figure in the sources available. Section 14 shows how to approximate the number from debtor days and how to read it.

In plain terms

Watch the government's budget for fertiliser as closely as you watch any company's results. If Parliament tops it up in the winter session, receivables will start to come down and the debt taken on in 2026 will be repaid. If it does not, the companies will keep reporting profits and keep borrowing, and the weakest balance sheets (FACT, NFL, RCF, Paradeep) will feel it first.

The chemicals windfall: who escapes the subsidy, and for how long

In 2026 the best-earning businesses in this sub-sector were not fertiliser at all; the market has noticed, and the companies are investing to escape further.

The most profitable assets in the Indian fertiliser sub-sector in 2026 make explosives, foam chemicals, solvents, pesticides and nylon intermediates. They share plants, ammonia and acid with the fertiliser lines, which is why they sit in fertiliser companies, but they are priced by markets rather than by the government. When the West Asia shock made ammonia and its derivatives scarce, these businesses captured import-parity prices on products whose inputs were partly contracted or produced in-house. The result was a quarter in which GNFC's fertiliser business lost money while its chemicals business earned more than the whole company had a year earlier.

Six decades of cycles: from the Green Revolution to the Gulf war

The industry was built by the state, priced by the state and periodically rescued by the state; its cycles are policy cycles laid on top of world commodity cycles.

Indian fertiliser history divides into three eras. From the 1960s to 1991 the state built capacity to feed the Green Revolution and paid each plant its cost plus a return under the Retention Price Scheme (1977). From 1992 to 2014 the government tried, unevenly, to step back: it decontrolled phosphate and potash in August 1992 (DAP prices jumped and use fell), moved urea to group-wise pricing in 2003 and introduced the per-nutrient subsidy for P&K in 2010. Since 2015 the direction has been toward efficiency and control at the same time: energy norms, gas pooling and neem coating for urea (2015), DBT (2018), a single national brand (2022), and now NIPU-2026's assured returns for new plants.

    Subsidy peaks at about ₹2.51 lakh crore; NBS P rate ₹66.93 in Rabi 2022-23; nine-company PAT peaks at ₹9,347 crore on chemical spreads and inventory gains.
  • FY24
    Normalisation: P rate cut to ₹20.82 (Rabi 2023-24); nine-company PAT halves to ₹5,049 crore. Deepak's Taloja ammonia plant commissioned (Aug 2023).
  • FY25 – FY26
    Phosphate margin recovery (Paradeep), record urea sales, imports surge. Coromandel buys 53% of NACL (Aug 2025); Kakinada acid plants commissioned; Paradeep–MCFL merger completed (appointed date 1 Apr 2024).
  • 2026
    The West Asia shock (section 09), the 2025–28 energy norms and NIPU-2026 (section 08), and the blown subsidy budget (section 11).

Each cycle has followed the same pattern. A world price shock raises input costs; the subsidy follows with a lag; companies with inventory bought before the shock book gains, then companies without integration book losses; the government raises subsidy rates or adds special packages; the budget overruns; and payments slow. When world prices fall, the subsidy is cut, often faster than costs fall, and margins compress again from the other side. The FY23-to-FY24 swing shows the downside of the cycle as clearly as 2026 shows the upside of costs: the P rate fell by two-thirds in a year and combined profit halved.

In plain terms

Fertiliser cycles in India are not driven by farmers buying more or less. They are driven by world prices moving faster than the government's subsidy rates, in either direction. Good years come when the subsidy has just been raised and costs start to fall; bad years come when costs rise and the subsidy has not caught up. Knowing which side of that lag the industry is on is most of the job.

The M&A and listing history is short. Paradeep's IPO (May 2022), Goa purchase (2022) and Mangalore merger turned a single-plant Odisha company into a three-plant national player. Coromandel's purchases of a stake in BMCC (2022–24) and of NACL (2025) took it upstream into rock and sideways into crop protection. Deepak restructured into crop nutrition and mining chemicals subsidiaries in 2024–25 (the current status of that restructuring was not re-verified for this report). GNFC bought back shares in 2023. Otherwise the industry's shape has been set by policy-driven plant building rather than by consolidation.

Reading a fertiliser maker: the KPIs that matter Deep dive

Revenue is a poor guide in this sub-sector; tonnes, rupees per tonne, gigacalories, receivable days and cash conversion tell you whether a fertiliser company did well. Here is how to compute, benchmark and read each one.

A fertiliser company's results look like a normal company's results, but most of the familiar numbers mislead. Revenue moves with world prices and subsidy rates rather than with business performance. EBITDA margins are low by design in urea and mean little across business models. Profit can rise while cash falls. The useful numbers are per tonne and per day: how much the company earns on each tonne it sells, how much gas it burns per tonne of urea against its norm, how many days the government takes to pay, and how much of the profit arrives as cash. This section teaches those numbers one at a time.

In plain terms

To understand a fertiliser company, split its profit into three piles (phosphate fertilisers, urea and everything else), work out what each pile earns per tonne or per rupee of sales, and then subtract the interest it pays while waiting for the government. Revenue and margin percentages only confuse the picture.

Realisation per tonne, and the state's share of it

What it is. The total revenue a company receives per tonne sold: the retail price the farmer pays plus the subsidy the government pays. Formula. Realisation = MRP per tonne + subsidy per tonne (+ any special package). The state's share = subsidy ÷ realisation. Worked example. At Kharif 2026 rates, a tonne of DAP realises about ₹27,000 (assumed retail) + ₹32,788 (NBS) + ₹3,500 (package) = ₹63,288, of which the state pays 57%. A tonne of urea at Q1 FY27 gas realises about ₹42,170, of which the state pays about 87%. A tonne of NPK 10:26:26 realises about ₹53,569, of which the state pays about 36%.

What drives it. Subsidy rates (reset each season for P&K; driven by gas prices and norms for urea), the retail price (fixed for urea, informally capped for P&K) and product mix. A company shifting from DAP to NPK or SSP lowers its realisation per tonne and its dependence on the state. Benchmark. There is no good or bad realisation in itself; what matters is the state's share, because that share is a receivable. A state share above 70% (urea) means most revenue arrives on the government's timetable; below 40% (NPK) means most arrives from dealers within normal credit terms. How the nine compare. Companies do not disclose realisation per tonne consistently; it can be approximated as segment revenue ÷ tonnes sold. Urea-heavy companies (NFL, Chambal, RCF) have the highest state share; NPK-heavy companies (Coromandel, Paradeep's NPK) the lowest. Definition trap. Realisation that includes traded goods (imported DAP or MOP bought and resold) mixes a thin trading margin with manufacturing margin; always ask whether the tonnes are made or traded.

EBITDA per tonne (phosphates and NPK)

What it is. The operating profit a P&K maker earns on each tonne of fertiliser sold, the single best measure of its spread. Formula. EBITDA per tonne = EBITDA ÷ tonnes sold. State whether EBITDA includes other income (Paradeep's company figure does; screener's operating profit does not) and whether tonnes include traded product. Worked example. Paradeep's FY26 company EBITDA of ₹2,259 crore over 42.1 lakh tonnes sold is about ₹5,366 a tonne. Its Q1 FY27 screener operating profit of ₹720 crore over 9.85 lakh tonnes is about ₹7,310 a tonne (about ₹7,760 on the company's own ₹764 crore figure).

What drives it. The gap between NBS plus retail price and input cost (section 10), inventory timing (stock bought cheap and sold after a subsidy increase flatters it), product mix (NPK and SSP margins differ from DAP), the share of captive acid and ammonia, plant utilisation (fixed costs over more tonnes), and the share of traded tonnes, which earn a few hundred rupees at best and often lose money. Benchmark (house view, INDICATIVE). Above ₹5,000 a tonne is good; ₹3,000–5,000 average; below ₹2,500 weak. How the nine compare. Paradeep ₹4,430 (FY25) → ₹5,366 (FY26) → ~₹7,310 (Q1 FY27). Coromandel's nutrient business earned about ₹4,960 a tonne in FY26 on an estimate (66% of group EBITDA attributed to nutrients ÷ about 43 lakh tonnes of phosphatic sales). FACT, GSFC and RCF do not disclose a comparable figure. Definition trap. A single quarter's EBITDA per tonne can be distorted by inventory gains or losses and by subsidy rate changes that apply to stock already held. Use four-quarter rolling figures and compare like quarters.

EBITDA per tonne (urea), and why you rarely see it

What it is. The operating profit on each tonne of urea a company makes. Formula. Urea segment EBITDA ÷ urea tonnes produced or sold. Worked example. Crisil estimates the legacy industry average at about ₹1,700 a tonne before the latest norm cut and about ₹1,250 after. A 1.8-million-tonne plant earning ₹1,250 a tonne makes about ₹225 crore of EBITDA. What drives it. Energy efficiency against the norm (worth ₹2,500–3,800 a tonne for an efficient modern plant at 2025–26 gas prices), the recognised fixed-cost allowance against actual fixed costs, output above reassessed capacity, and downtime. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Above ₹2,000 a tonne is good for a legacy plant; ₹1,000–2,000 average; below ₹1,000 weak.

How the nine compare. None of Chambal, NFL or RCF discloses a urea-only EBITDA per tonne. Chambal's total FY26 EBITDA divided by about 3.4 million tonnes of own urea gives about ₹7,900 a tonne, but that number includes its large trading book and its crop protection and specialty nutrients business; it is a ceiling, not a urea margin. NFL's total EBITDA of ₹837 crore over about 3.5 million tonnes of urea capacity is about ₹2,350 a tonne, again including traded and industrial products. Definition trap. Dividing total company EBITDA by urea tonnes always overstates the urea margin when the company also trades fertiliser, makes chemicals or sells crop protection. Use segment results where a company reports them, and Crisil's industry figures otherwise.

In plain terms

A good phosphate maker earns ₹5,000 or more on a tonne in a normal year; a typical old urea plant earns about ₹1,250. The phosphate margin swings with world prices; the urea margin swings with government formulas. Neither number is printed in most results, so you usually have to estimate it from tonnes and profit.

Energy consumption against the norm (Gcal per tonne)

What it is. The amount of gas energy a urea plant uses to make a tonne of urea, compared with the government's plant-specific norm. Formula. Energy efficiency gap = norm Gcal/t − actual Gcal/t; value per tonne = gap × gas price per Gcal. Worked example. A plant with a norm of 5.984 using 5.40 Gcal saves 0.584 Gcal a tonne; at ₹6,503 per Gcal (Q1 FY27 gas at ₹95) that is about ₹3,800 a tonne, or ₹380 crore a year on a million tonnes. What drives it. Plant vintage and technology (modern single-train plants such as Gadepan-III, HURL and Matix use 5.0–5.5 Gcal; 1970s plants converted from naphtha use more), maintenance, revamps (ammonia converter and CO2-recovery upgrades), and steady running (start-ups and shutdowns waste energy).

Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Below 5.5 Gcal a tonne is good; 5.5–6.2 average; above 6.2 weak. How the nine compare. Actual consumption is not disclosed quarterly. The disclosed norms are RCF Thal 5.984 (new), GNFC Bharuch 6.370 (new), NFL Vijaipur 5.5 and NFL's three northern plants 6.5 (2024-25 targets), and a legacy composite of about 5.67 (Crisil). Chambal's Gadepan-III is among the most efficient plants in India, but its actual figure is not public. Definition trap. Norms are retrospective: the 2025–28 norms notified in July 2026 apply from April 2025, so a company can book a catch-up gain or loss for past quarters in a single quarter, as RCF (−₹171.5 crore) and GNFC (+₹61 crore) will in Q2 FY27. Strip these out to see the run-rate.

Gas price and the value of a gigacalorie

What it is. The pooled delivered gas price for urea plants, converted into rupees per gigacalorie, which sets the value of every unit of efficiency or inefficiency. Formula. ₹ per Gcal = $ per MMBtu × 3.968 × ₹ per $. Worked example. At $12.5 and ₹87 (roughly FY26), a gigacalorie is worth about ₹4,300; at $17.25 and ₹95 (Q1 FY27), about ₹6,500. What drives it. LNG prices, the share of cheap domestic gas allocated to fertiliser, the rupee, and pipeline tariffs. Benchmark. Not a good-or-bad number; a higher gas price raises both the subsidy bill and the stakes of efficiency. How the nine compare. All urea makers pay the same pool price; Chambal disclosed its Q1 FY27 average of about $17.25. Deepak is the exception: its Taloja ammonia runs on its own LNG contract with Equinor. Definition trap. A rising gas price raises urea revenue (through the concession) without raising profit; do not read the resulting revenue growth as business growth.

Capacity utilisation

What it is. Production as a share of capacity. For urea, the reference is the plant's "reassessed capacity" fixed by the government; for P&K, nameplate granulation capacity. Formula. Utilisation = production ÷ capacity. Worked example. Coromandel produced 6.9 lakh tonnes of phosphatic fertiliser in Q1 FY27 at 72% utilisation, implying annual granulation capacity of about 38 lakh tonnes (6.9 ÷ 0.72 × 4). What drives it. Gas availability (urea, March 2026), raw material availability and price (P&K, 2026), planned and unplanned shutdowns, and in P&K a deliberate choice not to make loss-making product. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Urea: above 105% of reassessed capacity good (it earns the incentive), 95–105% average, below 90% weak. P&K: above 90% good, 75–90% average, below 75% weak.

How the nine compare. FACT ran its FACTAMFOS plant at about 102% and its ammonium sulphate plant at about 111% in FY25. GNFC's TDI ran at about 85% of its 67,000-tonne nameplate in FY26 (the primer says about 80%). Coromandel ran at 72% in Q1 FY27 by choice. Chambal's Q1 FY27 urea sales of 0.62 million tonnes (Gadepan-I 0.10, II 0.19, III 0.33) were hit by planned and extended shutdowns. ICRA expects urea plants to run below 80% in FY27. Definition trap. Low utilisation in P&K can be good management (refusing to make product at a loss), while high utilisation can be bad (building expensive stock ahead of a subsidy cut). In urea, reassessed capacity is often below nameplate, so utilisation above 100% is normal and is what earns the incentive.

What it is. Tonnes of fertiliser sold in the period, split into manufactured and traded, and the company's share of national sales of that product. Formula. Subsidy receivable days

What it is. How long, on average, the government takes to pay the subsidy a company has earned. Formula. Subsidy receivable days = subsidy receivable ÷ (annual subsidy income ÷ 365). Where the receivable is not disclosed, total debtor days (trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365) are a usable proxy for a company whose revenue is mostly subsidy. Worked example. A company that earns ₹10,950 crore of subsidy a year earns about ₹30 crore a day; if it is owed ₹1,800 crore, its receivable days are 60. If the budget runs short and the receivable rises to ₹3,000 crore, the days rise to 100, and at 8% the extra ₹1,200 crore costs about ₹96 crore a year in interest (ILLUSTRATIVE). What drives it. The adequacy of the annual budget, the timing of supplementary grants, the share of revenue that is subsidy, and the speed of DBT processing. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Below 45 days is good (DBT pays roughly weekly when funds exist); 45–90 average; above 90 weak.

How the nine compare. On the debtor-day proxy for FY26: RCF 93, NFL 92, Paradeep 80, Deepak 74, Chambal 36, GNFC 30, Coromandel 24, GSFC 23 (FACT not available). The few disclosed subsidy receivables are Coromandel ₹3,785 crore at end-December 2025 (₹2,095 crore a year earlier), NFL ₹2,954 crore (₹2,575 crore at FY25) and Chambal ₹2,346 crore (undated). Definition trap. Year-end receivables can look healthy if the government clears claims in March to use up the budget; the average through the year can be much higher. Half-year and rating-agency figures are often more revealing than March balances. Debtor days also mix dealer receivables (normal commercial credit) with subsidy receivables; for companies with large chemical or crop protection businesses (Deepak, GNFC, Coromandel) the proxy is weaker.

Working-capital days

What it is. The number of days of sales tied up in receivables and inventory, net of what the company owes suppliers. Formula. Working-capital days = (receivables + inventory − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. A company with ₹20,000 crore of revenue, ₹4,000 crore of receivables, ₹3,000 crore of inventory and ₹5,000 crore of payables has ₹2,000 crore of net working capital, about 37 days. What drives it. Subsidy receivables, the price of inventory (2026's expensive acid and ammonia inflate it), supplier credit (import letters of credit and usance), and seasonal stocking before Kharif and Rabi. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Below 30 days good; 30–60 average; above 60 weak. How the nine compare. Definition trap. A low working-capital figure can hide a large receivable if payables (often import credit) are also large: RCF's 27 days sit alongside 93 debtor days and a doubling of "other liabilities" to ₹7,461 crore. What it is. The share of reported profit that arrived as operating cash. Formula. Cash conversion = cash flow from operations ÷ profit after tax. Worked example. Net debt to EBITDA, and interest cover

What it is. How many years of operating profit it would take to repay net debt, and how many times operating profit covers interest. Formula. Net debt ÷ EBITDA = (borrowings − cash and liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA; interest cover = EBITDA ÷ interest. Worked example. Paradeep's ₹6,906 crore of borrowings less ₹25 crore of investments over ₹2,208 crore of EBITDA is about 3.1 times; its interest cover is about 4.2 times (2,208 ÷ 528). FACT's ₹3,800 crore of net debt over ₹69 crore of EBITDA is about 55 times, and its interest cover is below one (₹69 crore of EBITDA against ₹250 crore of interest). What drives it. Subsidy receivables (which are effectively sovereign credit), capital spending, and the earnings cycle. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Below 1× good; 1–2.5× average; above 3× weak.

How the nine compare. FY26: FACT about 55×; NFL 4.7× and RCF 4.3× (gross borrowings; RCF 2.9× if its ₹1,380 crore of investments, mostly the Talcher JV, are netted, which this report does not do because they are not cash); Paradeep 3.1×; Deepak 1.4× on the company's own net debt of ₹4,719 crore at June 2026 against trailing EBITDA (about 2.8× on FY26 EBITDA); Chambal about zero; Coromandel, GNFC and GSFC net cash. Definition trap. Leverage funded by government receivables is different from leverage funded by capex: the first unwinds when the budget is topped up, the second does not. Also, screener's "investments" for GSFC (₹4,610 crore) are strategic stakes in listed Gujarat companies, not cash; netting them makes GSFC look far more cash-rich than its operations are.

What it is. Operating profit as a share of the capital (debt plus equity) used to earn it, and net profit as a share of shareholders' equity. Formula. Non-subsidy profit share

What it is. The share of a company's operating profit that comes from businesses the government does not price: chemicals, crop protection, specialty nutrients, explosives. Formula. Non-subsidy share = EBIT (or EBITDA) of non-subsidy segments ÷ total segment EBIT (or EBITDA). Worked example. In Q1 FY27 GNFC's chemicals segment earned ₹425 crore and its fertiliser segment lost ₹85 crore, so total segment profit was ₹340 crore and the chemicals share was about 125%. What drives it. Strategy and capital allocation over years, and chemical price cycles in any one quarter. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). In plain terms

The more of a company's profit comes from things the government does not price, the more the market will pay for it, and the less a late subsidy cheque or a stingy NBS rate can hurt it. But check whether that share is a structure the company built or a spike in chemical prices that will fade.

Definition traps, collected

  • Revenue growth is not business growth. Subsidy pass-through makes revenue track gas and acid prices. FY24 revenue fell 24% with steady volumes.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. Paradeep's company EBITDA (₹2,259 crore) includes ₹51 crore of other income; screener's operating profit (₹2,208 crore) does not. Per-tonne figures differ by about ₹120.
  • Made versus traded tonnes. Traded MOP, DAP and urea add tonnes and revenue at thin or negative margins; total tonnes overstate the manufacturing business.
  • Urea margins from total EBITDA. Dividing a company's total EBITDA by urea tonnes includes trading, chemicals and crop protection; it is a ceiling, not a urea margin.
  • Retrospective norm changes. The 2025–28 energy norms apply from April 2025; catch-up amounts for past periods land in a single quarter (RCF −₹171.5 crore, GNFC +₹61 crore in Q2 FY27).
  • Inventory gains and losses. A subsidy increase on stock already held, or a fall in input prices after expensive buying, can swing one quarter's EBITDA per tonne by thousands of rupees.
  • Debtor days are a proxy. They mix dealer credit with subsidy receivables, and year-end balances can be flattered by March clearances.
  • Investments are not always cash. GSFC's ₹4,610 crore and RCF's ₹1,380 crore are strategic stakes; GNFC's inter-corporate deposits (about ₹2,400 crore) sit in current assets rather than in investments.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. FACT's figures here are standalone; NFL's consolidated figures include its 26% of Ramagundam; GSFC's standalone FY26 profit (₹652 crore) differs from consolidated (₹673 crore).
  • P/E on a tiny float. FACT's market value reflects about 1% of its shares; its "multiple" is not a valuation signal (section 18).

How to read a fertiliser company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find the tonnes first (1 minute). Sales volume by product, split into manufactured and traded, and production. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter: seasonality is large. Red flag: total volume up only because of traded MOP or DAP.
  2. Compute EBITDA per tonne (1 minute). EBITDA ÷ tonnes, stating whether other income is included. For P&K makers, compare with ₹5,000; for urea, look for the segment result. Red flag: a big jump in a quarter where the NBS rate rose, which usually means an inventory gain that will reverse.
  3. Check the input cost commentary (1 minute). What did management say about phosphoric acid, ammonia, sulphur and gas, and how many months of inventory are held? Red flag: "elevated raw material costs" alongside rising volumes of DAP.
  4. Read the subsidy line (1 minute). Any mention of NBS rates, special packages or concession adjustments, including retrospective items. Red flag: a large "prior-period subsidy" credit or debit inside EBITDA.
  5. Look for energy-norm and fixed-cost changes (1 minute). Any notification, catch-up amount or provision. Red flag: none disclosed for a plant whose peers have been revised.
  6. Split the segments (1 minute). Fertiliser, chemicals, crop protection. Which one moved profit? Red flag: chemicals carrying a loss-making fertiliser business through a price spike.
  7. Check receivables and borrowings (1 minute). At half-year and year-end, read the balance sheet; otherwise, look for comments on subsidy outstanding. Red flag: borrowings rising faster than capital spending.
  8. Check interest and other income (1 minute). Rising interest without new capex means the float is growing. Other income can hide a weak operating quarter (FACT relies on it).
  9. Note the capex and project milestones (1 minute). TAN, acid, ammonia and urea projects: commissioning dates and cost. Red flag: slippage while borrowings rise.
  10. Put it against the policy calendar (1 minute). When is the next NBS reset, is a supplementary budget due, and what did the government spend this month? Red flag: a strong quarter just before a season whose subsidy rates have not been set.
In plain terms

Ignore the revenue headline. Find the tonnes, divide the profit by them, ask what raw materials and subsidy rates went into that profit, and then check whether the cash came in. If profit per tonne jumped in the same quarter the subsidy rose, assume part of it is a one-off.

FACT: why 28% of the group's value rests on 1% of one company's shares

FACT is valued at ₹47,443 crore, almost exactly the market value of Chambal, Paradeep and Deepak combined (₹47,800 crore), on a FY26 loss of ₹40 crore, a Q1 FY27 loss of ₹62 crore, net worth of about ₹1,350 crore and net debt of about ₹3,800 crore. Its price-to-book ratio is 35 times; its EV/EBITDA is about 740 times. No fundamental model produces those numbers. The explanation is supply. The government holds 90.00% and another government entity 8.96%; foreign and domestic institutions together hold 0.22%; the public holds 0.81%. Only about 1% of the shares, worth roughly ₹490 crore at the current price, can trade. A small number of buyers chasing a PSU narrative (land monetisation, a new NP plant, a revived caprolactam unit) can set a price for the whole company without any large seller to meet them. Our FACT primer says the free float is about 10%; on September 2026 shareholding it is about 1%.

The distortion has consequences beyond FACT. It inflates every aggregate that includes it: the group P/E of 22.2 times falls to 15.9 times without it, and the central-PSU bloc's P/E of 94.2 times falls to 13.9 times. It also creates a specific risk for FACT's holders. Listed companies must eventually have at least 25% public shareholding. To get there, the government would need to sell about 24% of FACT, about ₹11,400 crore of stock at the current price, roughly 23 times the value of today's float. Government companies have historically been given extended timelines to comply, and no sale has been announced, but any offer for sale or follow-on offer of that size would be priced far below the current market.

In plain terms

FACT's share price is set by the few people who trade the 1% of shares the government does not own. It says almost nothing about what the business is worth. Leave it out of any sub-sector average, and treat any government plan to sell its stake as a likely shock to that price.

The global lens: converters and utilities in a world of gas and mine owners

Global fertiliser majors own cheap gas or mines and sell at world prices; India's listed makers convert imported molecules under a capped return, which explains lower margins and, sometimes, similar valuations.

The world's large fertiliser companies are built on a resource. In nitrogen, CF Industries (US), Yara (Norway), Nutrien (Canada), QAFCO (Qatar), SABIC Agri-Nutrients and Ma'aden (Saudi Arabia), OCI and Fertiglobe, and Chinese producers buy gas cheaply and sell ammonia and urea at world prices. In phosphates, OCP (Morocco, with about 70% of the world's rock reserves and a co-owner of Paradeep's promoter), Mosaic (US), Ma'aden, PhosAgro (Russia) and Chinese producers such as Yuntianhua and Guizhou Phosphate own the rock. In potash, Nutrien, Belaruskali, Uralkali, Mosaic and K+S own the mines, with BHP's Jansen project coming. India's listed makers own almost none of these resources; they are converters and regulated utilities.

Technology and disruption: nano, green ammonia, coal and the bigger threat of a policy change

The technologies that might change Indian fertiliser are either slow (green ammonia, coal gasification) or overstated (nano urea); the disruption to fear is a change in how the subsidy is paid.

Nano fertilisers. Section 08 covered nano urea's slow uptake: about 1.45 crore bottles sold in FY26 against a target of 8 crore, contested efficacy, no completed evaluation, and at most 2.3% of urea displaced even on IFFCO's own claim. Nano DAP (about 65 lakh bottles in FY26) is earlier still. Coromandel sells nano DAP and nano urea (our primer's claim of about 50% market share is implausible against IFFCO's dominance and is unverified; it may refer to a sub-segment), and NFL plans nano capacity at Nangal. For investors this is a low near-term risk to volumes and a small product opportunity for the companies involved.

Green ammonia and green urea. Ammonia made from hydrogen produced by electrolysis using renewable power would cut India's dependence on imported LNG and ammonia. SECI ran green-ammonia procurement auctions in 2025, and NFL has announced plans for green ammonia. Costs are still well above grey ammonia at normal gas prices, but the 2026 shock narrowed the gap for a while, and a domestic green-ammonia supply would eventually change the cost base for phosphate makers who buy ammonia today. The horizon is five to ten years.

Coal gasification. Talcher Fertilizers (RCF, GAIL and Coal India, about one-third each) is building a 12.7-lakh-tonne urea plant fed by gasified coal, and GNFC signed a coal-to-chemicals MoU with GMDC in July 2026. Coal gasification offers independence from LNG but carries technology risk, high capital cost and a heavier carbon footprint. If Talcher works at its projected cost, it is a template for reducing gas dependence; if it runs late or over budget, as large first-of-a-kind plants often do, RCF's equity in it becomes a drag.

Policy disruption. The largest long-term risk to the business model is not a technology but a change in how the subsidy is paid. Proposals to transfer the subsidy directly to farmers' bank accounts as cash, or to cap the number of subsidised bags per farmer using soil-health data and land records, have been discussed for years. Either would expose urea to a real price signal for the first time in decades, likely reducing use, and would change working capital (companies would collect from farmers and dealers rather than wait for Delhi). No such change has been announced. Investors should treat it as a low-probability, high-impact risk that would hurt the urea utilities most.

Phosphate security. India's dependence on Morocco, Jordan and Saudi Arabia for rock and acid, and on the Gulf for sulphur, was tested in 2026. Indian rock deposits (mainly in Rajasthan) are small and low-grade. The strategic responses are captive overseas rock (Coromandel's BMCC stake in Senegal, targeting about a third of its needs), long-term supply contracts (Paradeep's OCP link) and domestic acid capacity (Coromandel Kakinada, GSFC Sikka). Expect more of all three, and expect companies with them to keep a valuation premium.

In plain terms

Nothing in the laboratory threatens these companies soon. Nano urea has not dented demand, and green ammonia and coal gasification are years from changing costs. What could change the industry quickly is a decision in Delhi to give farmers cash instead of cheap fertiliser. Until then, the practical technology race is about owning acid, ammonia and rock.

Bull vs bear: a protected market against a squeezed spread and an empty budget

The case for Indian fertiliser makers rests on guaranteed demand, sovereign counterparties and new routes out of the subsidy; the case against rests on everything that happened in 2026.

The bull case

  • Demand is guaranteed. Urea use grows 2–3% a year whatever the policy rhetoric; India imports a quarter of its urea and three-fifths of its DAP, so domestic makers never lack customers.
  • The counterparty is the sovereign. Subsidy receivables are delayed, not lost; when budgets are topped up, receivables fall and the debt taken on in FY26 is repaid.
  • New, assured growth. NIPU-2026 offers 12–16% assured return on equity for about 10 million tonnes of new urea; Chambal is bidding.
  • Escape routes are working. TAN (Deepak's Gopalpur, Chambal's new plant), crop protection (Coromandel), captive acid (Coromandel, Paradeep, GSFC) earn market returns and are growing.
  • Valuations are low outside FACT. 15.9× earnings ex-FACT, Chambal at 8× with a 20% ROE, GSFC below its portfolio, GNFC below book with 3.7% yield.
  • Input prices are already easing. Ammonia is down a third from its peak; Middle East urea halved from spring; the Rabi subsidy could be raised.

The bear case

  • The P&K spread is deeply negative at spot. About −₹28,000 to −₹30,000 a tonne on DAP at Q3 2026 inputs; Q1 profits were flattered by inventory and April's subsidy rise.
  • The budget is short. 58% spent by August against an official need near ₹2.41 lakh crore; receivable days and interest costs rise unless supplementary grants arrive.
  • Profit is not cash. FY26 operating cash flow −₹1,129 Cr against ₹7,666 Cr of profit; borrowings +62%.
  • Norms cut both ways. RCF's ₹171.5 Cr hit lands in Q2; NFL's norms for old plants are unknown.
  • The chemicals windfall is partly war premium. As ammonia and Gulf chemicals normalise, Deepak's and GNFC's Q1 margins will not repeat.
  • Ownership overhangs. FACT's ~1% float and a possible sell-down; RCF's FPO; state capital allocation at GNFC and GSFC; Deepak's SEBI warning.

The two cases disagree less on facts than on timing. Both agree that the subsidy will eventually be paid and that the government will eventually raise P&K rates if input prices stay high, because it cannot allow fertiliser shortages in a Rabi season. The bull case assumes the government moves quickly, input prices keep falling and the integrated companies' structural advantages show through. The bear case assumes the next two quarters show the full squeeze, that the budget gap is closed slowly, and that the chemical windfall fades before the new TAN and acid capacity starts earning.

In plain terms

Three things decide the next six months: how much the government raises the winter subsidy for phosphate fertilisers, how much extra money Parliament gives the fertiliser budget, and what the July–September results show about margins at Paradeep and Coromandel. Watch those before anything else.

Two quieter indicators are worth tracking too. The monthly Department of Fertilizers data on domestic DAP and NPK production will show whether companies are cutting output of loss-making grades, which would tighten Rabi availability and increase pressure on the government to act. And the rating agencies' commentary (ICRA's negative outlook of 30 September is the current baseline) tends to move before company guidance does; an upgrade of the outlook to stable would be the clearest external sign that the squeeze has passed.
