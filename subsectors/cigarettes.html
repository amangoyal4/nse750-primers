00

The one-page view: a tollgate whose toll just doubled

For six years the Indian cigarette was one of the most stable profit streams in the market. On 1 February 2026 the state rewrote its share of every stick, and the industry is now a contest between price, volume and the smuggler.

An Indian cigarette company runs a tollgate. It buys leaf from licensed farmers, turns it into sticks in a handful of factories, and sells those sticks through a million small shops to smokers who rarely switch brand. Advertising is banned, foreign companies cannot build factories, and new entrants have not appeared in decades. The state has always been the largest participant in that tollgate, because tobacco is taxed to discourage its use and to fund the budget. What changed in 2026 is the size of the state's share and the way it is charged.

Until 31 January 2026 a cigarette paid 28% GST plus a GST compensation cess (an ad valorem part and a per-stick part), a small basic excise and the National Calamity Contingent Duty (NCCD). From 1 February 2026 the cess ended, GST moved to 40% charged on the retail sale price, and a new central excise of ₹2,050 to ₹8,500 per 1,000 sticks, set by stick length, took the cess's place. The legal instrument is Notification 03/2025-Central Excise (G.S.R. 955(E), dated 31 December 2025). The Union Budget for FY27 shows what the government expects: basic excise duties of ₹90,810 crore in FY27 BE against ₹45,780 crore in FY26 RE, while the compensation cess, which raised ₹1,50,570 crore in FY25 across tobacco, pan masala, cars and coal, drops to nil. Industry and press estimates put tax at roughly 50–53% of a cigarette's retail price before the reset and roughly 60–70% after it (indicative; it depends on the stick).

The first full quarter under the new regime, Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), shows the damage. ITC's standalone excise bill went from ₹1,309 crore to ₹10,036 crore, and its cigarette profit before interest and tax (PBIT) fell 35.1% to ₹3,341 crore. Godfrey Phillips India (GPI), the only cigarette company in our NSE 750 universe, saw excise rise from ₹327 crore to ₹2,614 crore, which is ₹0.57 to ₹4.67 per stick; its EBITDA fell 46% to ₹182 crore and its profit after tax 44% to ₹198 crore, even though its domestic volume fell only 2%. VST Industries' operating profit fell 35%. The three listed makers' combined quarterly profit pool, on a mixed basis (ITC segment PBIT plus GPI and VST company operating profit), dropped from ₹5,560 crore to ₹3,572 crore, down 35.8% (indicative).

Volume held up far better than profit. GPI says its domestic volume fell "only 2%"; brokers put ITC's fall at around 4–5% (unverified broker estimate) and Emkay expects legal volumes to fall by "high single digits" in FY27. The reason is the structure of the new tax. Because the excise is a fixed rupee sum per stick, a maker's profit per stick collapses unless it raises the retail price by more than the tax. Every rupee of price that survives GST and the retailer's cut drops almost entirely into the maker's net revenue, because the biggest cost line, the excise, does not move with price. So the industry's way back runs through price, not volume. ITC's Classic Connect 20-stick pack went from ₹300 to ₹428 in three rounds and its Gold Flake Super Star 10s from ₹59 to ₹89 (dealer checks reported by Business Today, September 2026). GPI calls its own approach "balanced" and "phased and not in one go".

That price contest has a limit, and the limit has a name: illicit trade. The industry's own fact sheet, quoted in both GPI's and ITC's FY26 annual reports, puts smuggled and duty-evaded cigarettes at about 26% of India's cigarette market and the revenue loss at about ₹23,000 crore a year (industry estimate, indicative). Smuggled brands retail at roughly ₹10–15 a stick against ₹19 for a legal king-size slim (Nomura). A specific excise widens the gap at exactly the price points where smokers trade down.

Five forces define this sub-sector, and the report is built around them:

  • The February 2026 tax reset (deep dive, section 04): 40% GST on retail price plus a specific excise by length; profit per stick fell by a third (ITC) to a half (GPI) in a quarter.
  • Pricing power against volume (section 05): how much price the market can take, what FY14–FY17 taught, and why management teams are moving in phases.
  • Illicit trade (deep dive, section 06): the ceiling on legal pricing, measured only by the industry itself.
  • Philip Morris and the Modi family (deep dive, section 09, plus section 10): what GPI really is, who earns the Marlboro profit, and how the family's pay and disputes affect minority shareholders.
  • The leaf economy (section 11): a glut in Andhra Pradesh, auction prices down from ₹248.86 to ₹209.83 a kilo, a 2026-27 crop cut to 81 million kg, and exports of ₹17,192 crore.

Two further forces sit in the background: regulation beyond tax (an advertising ban, 85% pack warnings, a ban on e-cigarettes and heated tobacco, and no foreign direct investment in manufacture), and the global contrast. PMI now earns about 42% of its revenue (nine months of 2026) from smoke-free products; Indian makers earn none, because those products are illegal here. Indian cigarette companies therefore carry 100% combustible risk in a market where the state has just doubled its take.

ITC is the price-setter of this industry, with roughly three-quarters of legal volume and more than four-fifths of domestic value (indicative). Its full story, including its hotels demerger, packaged foods and paperboards, sits in our Diversified FMCG report; this report uses ITC's standalone cigarette segment as a reference and keeps its figures consistent with that report.

In plain terms

Until January 2026, every ₹100 a smoker spent on a legal premium cigarette sent roughly ₹50 to the government and about ₹30 to the maker as profit. Since February, the government takes closer to ₹69 and the maker about ₹14 unless it raises prices further (indicative composite for an ITC king-size stick). The makers are raising prices in steps, hoping smokers keep buying rather than switching to bidis, chewing tobacco or smuggled brands. The next twelve months, and the Budget on 1 February 2027, will show how much of the lost profit comes back.

01

How a cigarette earns: from a licensed farm to a loose stick

The money in Indian tobacco is made at one point in the chain, the branded stick, and taxed at another, the factory gate. Everyone else, from farmer to retailer, earns a thin slice.

A cigarette's journey begins on a farm licensed by the Tobacco Board, most often a flue-cured Virginia (FCV) farm in Andhra Pradesh or Karnataka. The Board, created by the Tobacco Board Act of 1975, registers growers and their curing barns, fixes an authorised crop size each season and runs electronic auction platforms where leaf merchants and manufacturers bid for bales. India grows about 1,037 million kg of tobacco of all types a year (FCV, burley, bidi, chewing and hookah tobaccos, according to the industry fact sheet quoted by GPI), but only a fraction of that is cigarette-grade FCV.

The leaf merchant or processor buys at auction, threshes the green leaf (separating lamina from stem), re-dries it, packs it and ages it for one to two years. That ageing is why cigarette companies carry large inventories: GPI held 233 days of inventory in FY26 and VST 359 days. Exporters at this stage include ITC's leaf business, GPI, VST and international leaf dealers such as Alliance One (Pyxus) and Universal, who operate in India.

Manufacture comes next, in two steps. Primary manufacture cuts and blends the leaf; secondary manufacture makes and packs the sticks. ITC has five cigarette factories (Bengaluru, Kolkata, Munger, Pune and Saharanpur). GPI has three plants: Rabale in Navi Mumbai (cigarettes, plus an R&D unit), Guldhar in Ghaziabad (cigarettes, owned by its subsidiary International Tobacco Company, which charged it ₹73 crore of manufacturing fees in FY26) and Ongole in Andhra Pradesh (reconstituted tobacco). VST runs a single integrated plant at Toopran near Hyderabad. Cigarette making is capital-light: GPI produced ₹6,391 crore of FY26 net revenue from ₹812 crore of fixed assets.

The tax gate is where the economics are set. Excise and NCCD are paid when sticks leave the factory, so a company finances the tax before it collects it, and a change in tax rates hits whatever stock sits in the warehouse. GST, now 40%, is charged on the transaction and, from 1 February 2026, valued on the retail sale price under CGST Rule 31D. Because GST is charged on a price that already includes the excise, every rupee of excise costs the smoker about ₹1.40 plus the retailer's margin. That multiplier is the reason a modest-sounding per-stick levy turns into a large retail price increase.

Distribution is a long, thin chain: company, carrying-and-forwarding agent, distributor, wholesaler, and finally a paan shop or kirana store. Advertising has been banned since COTPA in 2003, so the distribution network and the shelf behind the shopkeeper are the main marketing tools. Most Indian smokers buy single sticks, not packs, and loose sales are legal in most states (Karnataka banned them in 2025 and Himachal Pradesh in 2015). That habit makes the per-stick price (₹10, ₹15, ₹20) the real anchor of the price ladder, and it explains why makers re-engineer stick lengths and pack sizes rather than simply raising prices.

In plain terms

A cigarette company does not make much money from growing leaf or from distributing packs. It makes money from owning the brand a smoker asks for at the counter and from the state's rules that keep competitors out. The state, in turn, takes the biggest single slice of every stick at the factory gate, before the company has been paid. That is why the tax rate, not the leaf price or the factory cost, is the number that moves this industry's profits.

02

The missing statistic: how big the legal market really is

India publishes its tax collections to the rupee but not the number of cigarettes its citizens smoke. Every volume figure in this industry is an estimate, and the best new one says the market is bigger than most analysts assumed.

There is no official series for Indian cigarette volumes. ITC, which sells the large majority of legal sticks, does not disclose its volume. GPI discloses a monthly run-rate (2,001 million sticks a month in FY26, which is 24.0 billion a year). VST discloses annual volume in its annual report (8,356 million sticks in FY26). Everything else, including the industry total, comes from brokers, industry bodies and rating agencies, and the estimates do not always agree.

The figure most analysts carried into 2026 put ITC at about 70 billion sticks and the legal market at roughly 100–105 billion. Our Diversified FMCG report used ITC ~70 billion, labelled indicative. A newer and independent source points higher. In its rating rationales, CRISIL describes GPI as the second-largest maker with about 19% of volume (31 July 2026) and VST as the third with about 7% (14 May 2026). Applied to the companies' own disclosed volumes, those shares imply a legal market of roughly 120–126 billion sticks and ITC at about 85–90 billion, around 73% of volume. A second check supports this: in Q1 FY27 the three listed makers paid ₹13,255 crore of excise, of which ITC paid 75.7%, GPI 19.7% and VST 4.6%. Because excise is charged per stick by length, the excise split should sit between the volume split and the value split, and it does.

This report therefore uses volume shares of ITC ~73%, GPI ~20% (CRISIL ~19%) and VST ~7% on a legal market of about 120 billion sticks in FY26, and labels them indicative. The older ~70 billion ITC figure would put GPI nearer 23% and raise ITC's per-stick numbers by about a quarter. Readers should treat every per-stick figure for ITC in this report as a range.

Value tells a different story from volume. Stripping out GPI's international business (₹2,014 crore) and VST's leaf business (₹301 crore), the domestic net cigarette revenue of the three listed makers in FY26 was about ₹32,871 crore: ITC ~₹27,443 crore, GPI ~₹4,264 crore and VST ~₹1,164 crore. The gap tells you that GPI earns far less per stick than ITC, for two reasons explored later: a mix weighted to cheaper sticks, and the fact that it sells Marlboro at a transfer price to PMI's distributor rather than to the trade.

History adds a further caution. Industry estimates put legal volume at a peak of about 110 billion sticks in FY12, before five years of steep excise increases and the 2017 GST cess pushed it down to roughly 80–85 billion by FY18. Volumes recovered during the tax holiday of FY19–FY26, with VST's annual report putting industry growth at about 7% in FY26 alone. Those historical figures were built on older bases, so they cannot be laid directly against CRISIL's ~120 billion. The direction is reliable; the level is not.

The value of the market is easier to bound. VST's FY26 annual report, citing IMARC, puts the Indian cigarette market at about ₹1,20,000 crore in 2025 at consumer prices, with value-priced cigarettes more than 51% of it (indicative third-party estimate). Our Diversified FMCG report's composite gives ₹1.2–1.5 lakh crore at retail. In plain terms

Nobody officially counts India's cigarettes. The best recent estimate, from a rating agency, suggests about 120 billion legal sticks a year, of which ITC sells roughly three in four and Godfrey Phillips about one in five. But GPI earns only about one rupee in eight of the industry's domestic revenue, because its cigarettes are cheaper on average and its Marlboro is sold to a distributor at a wholesale transfer price. Always ask whether a "market share" is by sticks or by rupees.

03

Who smokes what: cigarettes are a minority product carrying the tax

India has one of the world's largest tobacco-using populations and one of its lowest cigarette consumptions per head. That paradox drives every tax and pricing decision in this report.

The Global Adult Tobacco Survey of 2016-17 (GATS-2, run by the Health Ministry with WHO and TISS, 74,037 respondents) found that 28.6% of Indian adults, about 267 million people, used tobacco in some form. But only 10.7% smoked, and only 4.0% smoked cigarettes. Bidis, hand-rolled in tendu leaf, were smoked by 7.7%. Smokeless tobacco (khaini, zarda, gutkha and chewing tobacco) was used by 21.4%, about 199 million adults. The earlier survey, GATS-1 in 2009-10, found 34.6% using tobacco and 14.0% smoking, so total use is falling. No results from a third survey were found for this report; the 2016-17 figures remain the latest official baseline.

Legal cigarettes, by ITC's estimate in its FY26 annual report, are about 10% of India's tobacco consumption, down from 21% in 1981-82, yet they contribute more than four-fifths of the tax collected on tobacco. That asymmetry is the industry's main argument in every pre-budget submission: the state taxes the organised, traceable 10% heavily and the unorganised 90% lightly, so each increase on cigarettes pushes some consumption toward forms that are cheaper and less taxed per unit of nicotine. Bidis attract 18% GST and minimal excise; chewing tobacco pays high ad valorem rates on paper but is sold through fragmented, hard-to-police channels.

The other striking number is consumption per head. ITC's annual report, citing the Tobacco Atlas (2022), puts Indian cigarette consumption at about 90 sticks per person per year, against 1,971 in China, 1,133 in Japan, 898 in Bangladesh, 897 in the US, 468 in the UK and 394 in Pakistan. Even adding the illicit market, India smokes only a fraction of the cigarettes its neighbours do. The industry reads that as headroom; public-health advocates read it as success that should not be reversed.

Six forces move demand for legal cigarettes, in roughly this order of importance:

  • Price and affordability. Studies and broker work put the short-run price elasticity of Indian cigarettes at about −0.3 to −0.5: a 10% price rise cuts volume by 3–5%. Q1 FY27 implied far less (about −0.1 for GPI and −0.2 for ITC), flattered by trade pre-loading and phased pricing (estimates).
  • Relative prices. What matters is not the cigarette's price alone but its gap to a bidi, a chewing-tobacco sachet and a smuggled stick. The reset widened all three gaps.
  • Incomes and premiumisation. VST's annual report described the mid-premium segment as "vibrant" in FY26 while the low-price segment lagged the industry. GPI's Stellar and Four Square Crush, and VST's EDITIONS, rode that wave.
  • Distribution reach. GPI's growth came with wider direct retail coverage; the same network now also carries Ferrero confectionery.
  • Regulation of access. Karnataka's 2024 amendment (gazetted 30 May 2025) raised the smoking age to 21, banned loose sticks and widened school zones; national amendments drafted in 2015, 2019 and 2020 were never enacted.
  • Enforcement against illicit trade. After the reset, this is the swing factor; HDFC Securities called illicit trade "the key risk to volume assumptions".

One structural feature amplifies all of these. Because most smokers buy single sticks, they experience price in steps: ₹10 to ₹12 per stick feels larger than ₹100 to ₹120 per pack, even though it is the same percentage. That is why makers launch new lengths and pack formats at old price points (section 04) and why price rises are spread across several small rounds rather than taken at once.

In plain terms

Most Indians who use tobacco chew it or smoke bidis; only about one adult in twenty-five smokes cigarettes. Yet cigarettes pay most of the tobacco tax, because they are made by a few large companies the taxman can see. Each time the tax on cigarettes rises sharply, some smokers move to cheaper forms that pay little tax, or to smuggled sticks that pay none. That substitution, not health campaigns alone, is what limits how far legal cigarette prices can rise.

04

Anatomy of the tax reset: what changed on 1 February 2026 Deep dive

A cess that the states received was swapped for an excise that the Centre collects, GST moved from 28% of the invoice to 40% of the shelf price, and the per-stick charge was re-set by length. Here is the arithmetic, stick by stick.

The 2026 reset was not announced as a tax increase. It was presented as the end of a temporary arrangement. When GST arrived in July 2017, tobacco, pan masala, cars, coal and aerated drinks were charged a GST compensation cess on top of GST to fund payments to states for revenue lost in the switch. Those payments ended in 2022, but the cess continued to repay loans the Centre had taken to compensate states during the pandemic. GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, created a 40% "demerit" slab; tobacco was kept on 28% plus cess until the loans were repaid. On 1 February 2026 the cess ended and tobacco moved into the new structure. The stated aim of the Central Excise (Amendment) Bill, introduced in the Lok Sabha on 1 December 2025, was "to keep taxes on these products at the existing level". The Budget figures and company results show it did considerably more than that.

Two features of the legal design matter for investors. First, the gap between the statutory schedule (₹2,700–11,000) and the effective rates (₹2,050–8,500) sits in an exemption notification. Exemptions under section 5A are granted, narrowed or withdrawn by notification, so on our reading the government can raise effective rates up to the statutory ceiling without going back to Parliament. Second, the old compensation cess was itself part specific (₹2,076–4,170 per 1,000 sticks by length) and part ad valorem (5%), so the new excise replaced it roughly at the bottom of the ladder (₹2,076 against ₹2,100 for a short filter) but doubled it at the top (₹4,170 against ₹8,500). The big change for the cheapest sticks came from GST rising to 40% and being charged on the retail price; for long sticks it came from both.

The arithmetic of one stick

The cleanest way to see the new structure is to walk one stick from the shelf back to the factory. Take an ILLUSTRATIVE king-size stick at ₹14, the composite used in our Diversified FMCG report for an ITC stick after the reset. The retailer and wholesaler keep about 8%, ₹1.12. The remaining ₹12.88 includes GST at 40%, so the ex-GST value is ₹12.88 ÷ 1.40 = ₹9.20 and the GST is ₹3.68. From the ₹9.20 the maker pays about ₹6.00 of excise and NCCD (₹5.40 basic excise for a 70–75 mm stick plus about ₹0.60 of NCCD and other duties, estimate). The maker is left with ₹3.20 of net revenue. If its leaf, paper, filter, conversion and distribution cost is about ₹1.20, its EBIT is about ₹2.00 a stick. The state takes ₹9.68 of the ₹14, or 69%.

Two multipliers make small tax changes large at the shelf. Because GST is charged on a price that already includes excise, ₹1 of excise becomes ₹1.40 after GST, and about ₹1.52 at retail once the trade adds its 8%. And because the tax on a king-size stick (₹9.68) is now larger than the maker's whole net revenue (₹3.20), a 10% retail price rise lifts the maker's net revenue by roughly 29%: ₹1.40 more at retail becomes ₹0.92 more net to the maker (₹1.40 × 0.92 ÷ 1.40), and every paisa of it falls to EBIT because the excise does not move with price.

In plain terms

The new excise is a fixed amount per cigarette, from about ₹2 for a short stick to ₹8.50 for a long one, and GST of 40% is then charged on top of the whole retail price. For a typical king-size cigarette the government now takes about ₹9.70 of every ₹14, and the maker about ₹3.20 before its own costs. Because the tax does not rise when the maker raises prices, every extra rupee a smoker pays goes mostly to the maker. That is why the industry's response has been to raise prices in steps.

Who loses most

The answer depends on what "lose" means. In rupees per stick, long and premium cigarettes pay the most: ₹8.50 of basic excise against ₹2.10 for a short filter. But premium smokers are the least price-sensitive and premium brands have the most room to raise prices, so their volume is the safest. In affordability terms the value end loses most. A value smoker faces a 40% GST on a higher retail price, a specific levy that is a larger share of a cheaper stick's price, and close substitutes in bidis and chewing tobacco. The maker's balance sheet shows the same thing. In Q1 FY27 excise was 1.9 times ITC's cigarette net revenue, 2.2 times GPI's and 2.4 times VST's. A year earlier the ratios were 0.18, 0.22 and 0.39. The smaller and more value-heavy the portfolio, the more of every rupee the state now takes relative to what the maker keeps.

The length-engineering response

A tax set by length invites makers to re-engineer length. ITC's most visible move was a 64 mm Gold Flake Deluxe at ₹94 for ten, launched alongside the 69 mm Gold Flake Regular, whose ten-stick pack went from ₹95 to ₹125 (Nomura, February 2026). The arithmetic is simple. The 69 mm stick sits in the 65–70 mm slab at ₹4.00 a stick; the 64 mm stick drops into the ≤65 mm slab at ₹2.10. That saves ₹1.90 of basic excise per stick, ₹19 per pack of ten before GST and about ₹29 at retail once GST and the trade margin are added (₹19 × 1.40 × 1.08, ILLUSTRATIVE). The Deluxe can therefore sit near the old Regular price while still earning a reasonable margin. ITC's Q1 FY27 presentation lists more than 30 "portfolio interventions", including "Sleek" and "Deluxe" formats, Rothmans GT and XT, and Longs variants. GPI's response leaned on price instead: Nomura reported Stellar Shift (a king-size slim) up 90% to ₹19 a stick, close to ITC's Classic Connect, signalling a shift "from volumes to margins" in slims.

Length engineering has limits. The filter-counting rule (11 mm minimum) already blocks the crudest tricks, and a government that sees volume migrating to the bottom slab can narrow the exemption on that slab by notification. Expect the next Budget to look at where volume has moved since February 2026.

In plain terms

Because the tax depends on how long a cigarette is, cutting a stick from 69 mm to 64 mm nearly halves the excise on it. ITC launched exactly such a shorter Gold Flake at roughly the old price of the regular one. Smokers get a familiar brand at an affordable price, the company keeps a margin, and the government collects less per stick than it planned. The government can respond by changing the slabs, so this is a move in a continuing game, not a permanent fix.

05

Price versus volume: how much the smoker will pay

The reset can be undone, for the makers, only at the till. The question for FY27 and FY28 is how many price rounds the legal market absorbs before smokers trade down, switch, or buy smuggled sticks.

Indian cigarette makers have always had pricing power. Addiction, brand loyalty, an advertising ban that protects incumbents and a market with three meaningful sellers mean that most years they raise prices a little ahead of tax and grow profits faster than volume. The reset tests that power at a scale not seen since the excise ratchet of FY13–FY17. Nomura estimated in February 2026 that the reset raised tax by 40–45% and that makers needed to raise prices by 35–40% to protect profit, against about 30% taken in the first round. By the end of Q1 FY27, our Diversified FMCG report estimates ITC had taken a cumulative 22–23% across its portfolio, with another 12–13% planned over two quarters. The September 2026 round was the next instalment.

What volume has done so far

Volume has fallen less than many expected. GPI's domestic volume was 1,866 million sticks a month in Q1 FY27 against 1,903 million a year earlier, down 2%, after a peak of 2,190 million in Q3 FY26. Brokers put ITC's Q1 decline at around 4–5% (reported, not verified for this report). VST does not disclose quarterly volume. Two effects flatter these numbers. Trade loading before the February price increases pulled some demand into Q4 FY26, and phased pricing means the full increase had not yet reached the shelf. Emkay (July 2026) expects legal cigarette volumes to fall "by high single digits" in FY27; CRISIL expects VST's cigarette revenue to be flat in FY27 despite higher taxes, supported by price increases.

The gap between those two numbers is the main uncertainty in every cigarette forecast.

The FY14–FY17 precedent

The last time taxes rose this fast, legal volumes fell for years. Between FY13 and FY17 excise on cigarettes was raised sharply almost every Budget, and the July 2017 GST cess added another step. Industry estimates put legal volume at a peak of about 110 billion sticks in FY12 and roughly 80–85 billion by FY18. Nomura has observed that industry revenues were flat over FY13–FY18, meaning price rises only offset lost volume. ITC's stock went nowhere for about five years and earned a "value trap" label. ITC's FY26 annual report says the stable taxes that followed "enabled the legal cigarette industry to recover volumes lost to illicit trade during the years of high incidence of taxation". That sentence is the industry's own summary of the precedent: high taxes moved volume to illicit trade, and it came back only when taxes stopped rising.

The difference this time is the size of the step. FY13–FY17 was a series of annual increases of 10–20%; the 2026 reset raised excise per stick about eightfold in one quarter. The precedent suggests the industry can absorb the increase over two to three years if rates then stay stable. It also suggests that another large increase in the February 2027 Budget would reset the clock.

In plain terms

Cigarette makers can usually raise prices without losing many smokers. After the 2026 tax rise they need to raise prices by roughly 35–40% to earn what they did before. They have taken about two-thirds of that so far, in steps, and volumes have fallen by a few per cent. History says the full increase will cost more volume, perhaps 6–10%, and that some of it goes to smuggled cigarettes. If taxes then stay flat for a few years, past experience says volume and profit recover.

The smuggler's margin: illicit trade as the ceiling on price Deep dive

The only competitor that can undercut a legal cigarette by ₹5–10 a stick is one that pays no tax. Measuring it is hard and the best data come from the industry itself, but the arithmetic of the reset favours it.

Every legal cigarette in India now carries more tax than its maker's whole net revenue. A smuggled or duty-evaded stick carries none. That gap is the illicit trader's margin, and the February 2026 reset made it wider than at any point in the past decade. The industry's own estimate, from the "Tobacco Fact Sheet India 2026" quoted in GPI's FY26 annual report, is that illicit cigarettes make up about 26.1% of India's cigarette market, roughly a third of legal volume, and cost the exchequer about ₹23,000 crore a year. ITC's FY26 report, citing Euromonitor, calls India the world's fourth-largest illicit cigarette market. These are industry-funded or industry-cited estimates; there is no independent post-reset measurement, so every number in this section is indicative.

Why a specific excise favours the smuggler

Nomura's channel checks after the reset found smuggled and imported brands retailing at about ₹10–15 a stick, against about ₹19 for a legal king-size slim such as Stellar Shift or Classic Connect. Walk the legal ₹19 stick through the tax (ILLUSTRATIVE, king-size slab): trade ₹1.52; GST on the remaining ₹17.48 is ₹4.99; basic excise ₹5.40 plus about ₹0.50 of NCCD (estimate). The state takes about ₹10.90, or 57% of the price; the maker keeps about ₹6.59 before its costs. A smuggler with landed costs of a few rupees can sell at ₹12.50, more than ₹6 below the legal price, and still keep more per stick than the legal maker does. Before the reset the state's take on the same stick was about half of a lower price, so the undercut available to the smuggler was much smaller.

The design of the new GST makes the gap self-reinforcing. Because GST is now 40% of the retail sale price, each legal price increase raises the tax as well; the smuggler's price rises only as much as he chooses. And because the excise is fixed per stick, the percentage gap is largest at the bottom of each slab's price range, where the marginal smoker sits. At the very bottom of the market, the competition is not the smuggler but the bidi and the chewing-tobacco sachet, which are taxed far more lightly per unit of nicotine.

The industry's message on this is unanimous and long-standing, and investors should read it as advocacy as well as analysis. GPI's chair wrote that the new tax structure "inadvertently led to growth of illicit tobacco trade". GPI's CEO called illicit trade "a significant concern for the legal industry". VST's chairman wrote that the challenge is "negating the impact of illegal cigarettes where the tax arbitrage has grown substantially". ITC argued that legal cigarettes are only 10% of tobacco consumption in India against about 90% globally.

What the evidence shows

The best external evidence comes from other countries. Australia's repeated tobacco tax increases took its illicit share from under 2% to about 14% between 2012 and 2020 (broker-cited figures). India's own FY13–FY17 episode, described in section 05, moved volume from legal to illicit, and ITC's annual report credits the FY19–FY26 tax stability with bringing it back. What is missing is a measurement of what has happened since February 2026. The industry's 26.1% predates the reset. Seizure data from the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence and Customs would be the earliest independent signal, and monthly excise collections against the Budget estimate the most direct one.

The state's counter-measures

The government did not raise the tax without also tightening enforcement. A written reply in the Lok Sabha on 28 July 2026 listed measures in force from 1 February 2026. The most important for investors is the move to value GST on the retail sale price, which removes the incentive to under-invoice factory sales. GPI's own contingent liabilities include an ₹82.90 crore demand alleging undervaluation of supplies under GST, a dispute that matters more now that the base has changed. India has not, to our knowledge, ratified the WHO FCTC Protocol on illicit tobacco trade, and no national digital tax-stamp system is in place.

The government has added new checks on factories and pricing, but the smuggler's margin is the main limit on how high legal prices, and legal taxes, can go.

A frozen structure: three makers, two foreign shareholders, no entrants

Law, not just brand loyalty, keeps the Indian cigarette market in the hands of the same companies it has belonged to for decades. The 2026 reset then concentrated what profit remains in the largest of them.

Most consumer industries in India have seen waves of new entrants: foreign brands, private labels, direct-to-consumer start-ups. Cigarettes have not, for three legal reasons. First, foreign direct investment in the manufacture of cigars, cheroots, cigarillos and cigarettes is prohibited under India's FDI policy (since 2010). A foreign tobacco company cannot build or buy a cigarette factory here; it can only license brands, hold minority stakes acquired long ago, or distribute. Second, cigarette manufacture is among the few activities that still need an industrial licence under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act. Third, advertising and sponsorship have been banned since COTPA in 2003, and since April 2016 85% of both main faces of every pack must carry pictorial health warnings. An entrant cannot buy awareness, and an incumbent's brands and distribution cannot be out-marketed.

The new tax adds a fourth, economic barrier. A specific excise charges every maker the same rupees per stick, so the advantages go to whoever can spread fixed costs, product reformulation and trade incentives across the most sticks, and whoever has the deepest portfolio across length slabs. That is ITC, which made more than 30 portfolio changes in Q1 FY27 alone.

In plain terms

No new company can start making cigarettes in India: foreign investment in cigarette factories is banned, a licence is needed, and advertising is illegal. Four business models, one tax: who earns what on a stick

The listed makers look alike from a distance. Up close they are different businesses: an integrated national leader, a licensed-MNC hybrid, a regional value player and the leaf merchants behind them all.

The simplest way to understand why ITC earns 77% EBIT margins on its cigarette net revenue while GPI earns 24% is to see that they are not running the same business. ITC sells its own brands, across every length slab, through its own national distribution, using leaf from its own procurement operation and paperboard and packaging from its own paperboards division. GPI runs a portfolio of its own brands in the north and west (where CRISIL says about 90% of its cigarette sales are made), manufactures a licensed international brand for a sister distributor, and exports leaf to that brand owner's affiliates. VST sells value and mid-priced brands in the south and east and exports some leaf. The leaf merchants buy, process and export, without brands.

Why is ITC's margin so far above even PMI's? Indian net realisation is struck after a very heavy, mostly specific tax, so the denominator is small; Indian cigarette makers spend almost nothing on advertising, which is banned; and ITC's scale, integration and price leadership give it costs per stick that smaller makers cannot match. GPI's margin is lower for three reasons that have nothing to do with how well it runs factories. Its segment includes leaf exports, a merchant business with low margins. Its Marlboro volume is sold to PMITPL at a transfer price, so part of the Marlboro profit is earned by the distributor, of which GPI owns only a quarter. And its own brands are weighted to cheaper sticks.

Under the new tax the archetypes diverge further. A specific excise is regressive on margin: it takes the same ₹4–6 per stick whether the stick sells at ₹10 or ₹20. The integrated leader with long, premium sticks can price through it; the value players face larger percentage price increases and more trade-down risk. Leaf-export earnings are untouched by domestic tax, and a weak rupee (about ₹94.9 to the dollar in October 2026, near a record low of ~₹96.8) helps them, which is why the leaf businesses of GPI and VST became relatively more important in FY27 even as global leaf prices softened.

In plain terms

ITC keeps about three-quarters of every rupee it receives after excise as profit, because it owns its brands, sells the most cigarettes and spends nothing on advertising. Godfrey Phillips keeps about a quarter, because a large part of its business is low-margin leaf trading, and part of the profit on Marlboro is earned by a separate distribution company it only part-owns. The two numbers measure different businesses, not different levels of skill.

GPI is usually described as "India's number two cigarette maker". The filings show four businesses stitched together by one relationship: domestic brands, a Marlboro factory, a leaf exporter and a quarter-share in Marlboro's Indian distributor.

That relationship touches almost every line of GPI's accounts. Following it through the FY26 annual report gives a clearer picture of the company than any market-share figure.

Engine 1: the domestic brands

GPI's own portfolio, named in its FY26 annual report, is Four Square (including the Four Square Crush range), Stellar ("higher growth segments and modern retail formats"), and the legacy Cavanders and North Pole; Red & White is a long-standing mass brand. This engine drove the doubling of domestic volume to 24.0 billion sticks in FY26, through wider direct outlet coverage (1,100+ distributors and a 9,000+ field force) and growth in mid-premium. It is also the engine most exposed to the reset, because its mix leans to regular and short sticks priced for value smokers. Its domestic net revenue was about ₹4,264 crore in FY26, roughly ₹1.78 per stick (estimate).

Engine 2: Marlboro, made for someone else to sell

GPI manufactures Marlboro in India under what it calls "exclusive procurement and supply agreements with Philip Morris International", and sells the sticks to Philip Morris India Trading Pvt Ltd (PMITPL), which distributes them. In FY26 GPI's sales to PMITPL were ₹2,746.70 crore, almost double FY25's ₹1,421.31 crore, and equal to 30.1% of GPI's standalone revenue from operations (₹9,119.02 crore on the gross, excise-inclusive basis). PMITPL is GPI's only customer above 10% of revenue. The related-party note adds that GPI's purchases from PMITPL "have been reduced by ₹4,859.39 crore… owing to sale of Marlboro cigarettes manufactured by the Company", a netting arrangement whose mechanics the annual report does not explain. The annual report's AOC-2 statement says there were no "material contracts or arrangements" with related parties requiring shareholder approval, and the licence and supply agreements' term and expiry are not disclosed anywhere in it.

The economics of Marlboro therefore sit partly outside GPI's operating profit. GPI earns a manufacturing and transfer-price margin on each stick it sells to PMITPL. PMITPL, which buys at that price and sells to the trade, made a profit of ₹1,141.56 crore in the year to 31 December 2025, about three-quarters of GPI's own FY26 PAT of ₹1,526 crore. GPI books its 24.80% share, ₹283.11 crore, in consolidated other income; in the standalone accounts it records the ₹258.41 crore dividend PMITPL paid it, which implies PMITPL distributed about ₹1,042 crore, roughly 91% of its profit (estimate). GPI's 49.6 lakh PMITPL shares cost ₹4.96 crore and are carried at ₹113.61 crore.

What this means for the investor

Put together, GPI is a challenger whose story is value under-capture, not hidden volume: it sells about a fifth of India's legal cigarettes and earns about 13% of the domestic value. The PMI relationship is both its moat and its dependency. It is the only Indian home of Marlboro, the world's largest cigarette brand, and PMI is a loyal buyer of its leaf. But the terms of the Marlboro agreements are undisclosed, about three-quarters of Marlboro's distribution profit stays with PMI's side of PMITPL, PMI decides where its leaf comes from, and PMI kept its smoke-free products, such as IQOS, outside the GPI relationship (per our primer). If heated tobacco were ever legalised in India, PMI, not GPI, would own that franchise. GPI's management says it will "strengthen partnership with Philip Morris International in the manufacture and distribution of Marlboro brand cigarettes in India". Investors should watch for any change in the supply terms and for PMITPL's dividend timing, which moves GPI's quarterly other income.

In plain terms

Godfrey Phillips is four businesses. It sells its own cheaper cigarette brands; it makes Marlboro and sells it at a set price to a Philip Morris-controlled distributor, of which it owns a quarter; it exports leaf, mostly to Philip Morris factories abroad, including one in Russia; and it collects a share of that distributor's profit. Philip Morris owns a quarter of GPI itself. So much of GPI's fortune depends on agreements with one partner whose terms shareholders have never seen.

In plain terms

When you read that GPI's "other income" was ₹489 crore, most of it is not interest on cash. About ₹283 crore is GPI's share of the Marlboro distributor's profit. That income fell by more than half in the first quarter after the tax rise, so it moves with the cigarette cycle like the rest of the business.

From farm to Marlboro: India's leaf economy in a glut year

Behind every Indian cigarette, and many foreign ones, is a regulated farm economy in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka. In 2025-26 it produced far too much leaf, prices fell below growers' costs, and the state cut next year's crop by nearly half.

India is the world's second-largest exporter of tobacco by quantity, after Brazil. Its flue-cured Virginia (FCV) crop, grown mainly in Andhra Pradesh's light and black soil zones and in Karnataka's Mysore light soils, is regulated more tightly than almost any other Indian crop. Under the Tobacco Board Act of 1975, the Board registers growers and curing barns, fixes an authorised crop size for each season, and runs electronic auction platforms where manufacturers, exporters and leaf dealers bid for bales. Growing more than the authorised crop attracts penalties, at least on paper.

The 2025-26 Andhra Pradesh season broke that system. Against an authorised crop of 142 million kg, production was estimated at about 236–240 million kg, two-thirds more. Global leaf demand was soft at the same time: VST's annual report describes "higher crop production across major origins, including Brazil, Zimbabwe, Malawi and India", "downward pressure on international tobacco prices" and "cautious procurement… staggered buying". The result was a buyers' market. By 4 September 2026, after 132 auction days, 86.59 million kg had been sold at an average of ₹209.83 a kilo, against ₹248.86 at the same stage a year earlier (Andhra Pradesh's agriculture minister K. Atchannaidu, via Akashvani). By 24 September 111.64 million kg had been sold at ₹213.27. No-bid lots, bales for which no buyer offered a price, peaked near 40% before falling by 11–12 percentage points. The Tobacco Board's own letter in February 2026 put the cost of cultivation at about ₹200 a kilo, so the average grower was barely covering costs.

Rules beyond tax: warnings, bans and the closed door

Tax sets the industry's profits; the rest of the rulebook sets its shape. India bans tobacco advertising, e-cigarettes and heated tobacco, and foreign investment in cigarette factories, and it is slowly tightening access.

The Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act of 2003 (COTPA) is the backbone. Section 4 bans smoking in public places. Section 5 bans advertising and sponsorship, which is why Indian cigarette makers spend almost nothing on marketing beyond point-of-sale presence and trade incentives. Section 6 bans sales to anyone under 18 and within 100 yards of an educational institution. Section 7 mandates health warnings, and since 1 April 2016 the Packaging and Labelling Rules have required pictorial warnings covering 85% of both principal faces of every pack, refreshed periodically (the latest set, from December 2022, includes a quitline). The industry's challenge to the 85% rule is still pending in the Supreme Court: the Karnataka High Court struck the rule down in December 2017 and the Supreme Court stayed that ruling.

Attempts to tighten COTPA nationally have stalled. Drafts in 2015 (withdrawn in 2017), 2019 and 2020 proposed banning loose-stick sales, raising the minimum age to 21, curbing in-shop promotion and raising fines; none had been enacted by October 2026. States have moved instead. Karnataka's 2024 amendment, gazetted on 30 May 2025, raised the age to 21, banned single-stick sales, widened school zones to 100 metres and banned hookah bars. Himachal Pradesh banned loose cigarettes in 2015. A national loose-stick ban would be the most damaging single rule for volume, because it would force smokers to buy packs and raise the cash outlay per purchase several-fold.

A century of tollgates: booms, ratchets and the 2026 reset

The Indian cigarette industry has been through the same cycle several times: a period of stable taxes in which volume and profits compound, then a tax ratchet that moves volume to illicit trade and stalls the stocks for years.

The industry's three listed houses are colonial-era institutions. ITC began as the Imperial Tobacco Company of India in 1910; Godfrey Phillips India was incorporated in 1936; VST began as Vazir Sultan Tobacco in Hyderabad. For most of the following decades the business was a protected oligopoly with specific excise duties by stick length, the system India still uses. The modern cycle starts in the early 2010s.

FY13–FY18: the ratchet and the lost years. Excise on cigarettes rose sharply almost every Budget from FY13 to FY17, and the GST compensation cess added another step in July 2017. Legal volume, which had peaked around 110 billion sticks in FY12 (industry estimate), fell to roughly 80–85 billion by FY18. Industry revenue was flat over the period, according to Nomura. Illicit trade grew. ITC's stock went nowhere for about five years. GPI's profits in that period were small and volatile: ₹183 crore in FY15, ₹137 crore in FY17.

FY19–FY26: the tax holiday. Apart from a ~16% increase in NCCD in the February 2023 Budget, rates were broadly stable for seven years. Volumes recovered: ITC grew cigarette volumes at 4.5–6.5% a quarter through FY26 (our Diversified FMCG report), the industry grew about 7% in FY26 (VST annual report), and GPI more than doubled its volume over FY22–FY26. ITC re-rated from about ₹200 to about ₹500 a share and peaked at ₹490 on 27 September 2024; VST peaked at ₹481 on 6 September 2024; GPI peaked at ₹3,691 (bonus-adjusted) on 5 September 2025. GPI's PAT rose from ₹260 crore in FY19 to ₹1,526 crore in FY26.

The lesson of the cycle is that the industry's profits are set more by the tax path than by any company's execution. In stable-tax years, volume grows modestly, prices rise a little faster than costs, and profits compound at high single digits to low double digits (ITC's cigarette PBIT grew at a 9.1% compound rate over FY22–FY26). Reading a cigarette maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

A cigarette company is a per-stick business wrapped in a tax system. Fifteen numbers, most of them measured per stick, tell you almost everything; the rest of the accounts mostly tell you how the tax was booked.

Most consumer-company analysis starts with revenue growth and margins. For a cigarette maker that is the wrong place to start, because since February 2026 the reported revenue line includes a tax that is now larger than the maker's own takings. The right place to start is the stick: how many were sold, what the maker kept from each one after the state's share, and what each one cost to make. Everything else follows. This section sets out the equation, walks through each native KPI with a worked example and the listed companies' numbers, builds an ILLUSTRATIVE anatomy of one stick, and ends with a ten-minute guide to a quarterly result.

The equation and a worked example

For any cigarette maker in any period, net revenue is the number of sticks sold multiplied by what the maker keeps per stick, and EBIT is that less the cost of making and distributing each stick. What the maker keeps per stick is the retail price, less the trade margin, less GST (now 40% of the retail sale price), less the specific excise and NCCD for that stick's length. Management controls the retail price, the mix of lengths and brands, and its own cost per stick. It does not control the excise, NCCD or GST rates, and it controls volume only indirectly, through price and distribution, against the pull of bidis, chewing tobacco and illicit sticks.

An ILLUSTRATIVE worked example in round numbers. A maker sells 20 billion sticks a year at an average retail price of ₹10. The trade keeps 8%, leaving ₹9.20; stripping 40% GST leaves ₹6.57; average excise and NCCD of ₹4.50 leaves net realisation of ₹2.07 a stick. Net revenue is 20 billion × ₹2.07 ≈ ₹4,140 crore. At a cost of ₹1.20 a stick (₹2,400 crore), EBIT is about ₹1,740 crore, a 42% margin on net revenue. Now raise the retail price 10% to ₹11 and assume volume falls 3% to 19.4 billion. Net realisation becomes ₹11 × 0.92 ÷ 1.40 − ₹4.50 = ₹2.73; net revenue ≈ ₹5,293 crore; cost ≈ ₹2,328 crore; EBIT ≈ ₹2,965 crore. A 10% price rise with a 3% volume loss lifts EBIT by about 70%. That is the operating leverage of a specific excise, and it works just as hard in reverse when the excise rises and price does not.

In plain terms

Because most of the tax is a fixed amount per cigarette, a small price increase lands almost entirely in the maker's pocket, and a small tax increase comes almost entirely out of it. That is why cigarette profits can swing by half in a quarter while the number of cigarettes sold moves by a few per cent.

What it is: the change in domestic sticks sold, year on year. Formula: sticks this period ÷ sticks in the same period last year − 1. KPI 6 · Tax incidence

What it is: all taxes on a stick as a share of its retail price. Formula: (GST + excise + NCCD) ÷ retail price. Worked example: the ILLUSTRATIVE ₹14 king-size stick carries ₹3.68 of GST and ₹6.00 of excise and NCCD, ₹9.68 in all, or 69%. Drivers: the rates, the stick's length (a fixed excise is a larger share of a cheaper stick in the same slab) and the retail price (GST rises with it). Benchmarks: from the industry's point of view, under 55% good, 55–65% average, over 65% weak; WHO recommends at least 75%. How they compare: no company reports it; GPI's taxes were 78.8% of gross sales value in Q1 FY27 (65.3% in FY26), but GSV excludes the retail margin and includes tax-free exports. Pre-reset incidence was about 53% (World Bank via TII); post-reset estimates run 60–70% (indicative; chart in section 12). Trap: a share of GSV is not a share of retail price; and officials' claim that incidence "remains broadly unchanged" was true only in the sense that the cess was replaced, not per stick.

Incidence also differs across the price ladder in a way that matters for trade-down. Within one length slab, the excise is the same rupee amount whatever the price, so a cheaper brand in that slab carries a higher tax share than a premium brand of the same length. Across slabs, the 40% GST on retail price is the same share for everyone, while the excise rises with length. A smoker trading down from a ₹14 king-size stick to a ₹10 short stick therefore saves ₹4, yet the state's take falls by about ₹4.55 (excise from ₹5.40 to ₹2.10, and GST falling with the price) while the maker's net revenue per stick can actually rise, from ₹3.20 to ₹4.07 in the ILLUSTRATIVE examples of section 04. That is the arithmetic behind length engineering, and the reason the government watches the mix of slabs in monthly collections.

KPI 7 · EBIT per stick

What it is: profit before interest and tax per stick, the single best measure of a cigarette maker's earning power. Formula: cigarette segment result (or company operating profit for a single-segment maker) ÷ sticks. Worked example: ITC FY26: ₹21,051 crore ÷ ~88 billion sticks ≈ ₹2.4 (₹3.0 on 70 billion). Drivers: net revenue per stick less cost per stick; after the reset, the gap between price taken and tax imposed. Benchmarks: above ₹2.5 good, ₹1–2.5 average, under ₹1 weak. How they compare: FY26: ITC ~₹2.4, GPI ₹0.61 (segment), VST ₹0.42. Q1 FY27: ITC ~₹1.6 (₹2.0 on the old base), GPI ₹0.25, VST about ₹0.24 (operating profit ÷ an assumed 2.1 billion sticks). KPI 10 · Auction price per kilo

What it is: the average price paid at Tobacco Board auctions, the main input cost signal for makers and the main income signal for growers. Formula: auction value ÷ kilos sold, by season and region. Worked example: AP 2025-26 to 4 September 2026: 86.59 million kg at ₹209.83 a kilo, about ₹1,817 crore; a year earlier the same stage averaged ₹248.86. Drivers: crop size against authorisation, global demand, manufacturers' inventory, and grade. Benchmarks: above ₹250 a kilo healthy for growers, ₹200–250 average, below ₹200 (about the cost of cultivation) weak. How they compare: the auction price is common to all buyers; what differs is how much leaf each maker carries (KPI 13) and how much it exports. A ₹40 a kilo fall cuts leaf costs, but leaf is a small share of a maker's cost per stick, so the effect on EBIT per stick is minor. Trap: early-season averages differ from final averages because better grades sell first; compare like-for-like dates, as the Andhra Pradesh minister did.

For the listed makers the auction price matters in two directions. As buyers, a soft season lets them restock aged leaf more cheaply and gives them some negotiating room with growers, which partly offsets the tax shock in cost per stick a year or two later, once that leaf is used. As exporters, a domestic glut coinciding with large crops in Brazil and Africa usually means weaker dollar prices abroad, so the margin between auction cost and export realisation does not necessarily widen. The authorised crop for 2026-27, cut to 81 million kg, is therefore a leading indicator for both: a smaller crop should firm auction prices from late 2026 and reduce the inventory leaf merchants need to carry.

KPI 15 · Illicit share

What it is: smuggled and duty-evaded cigarettes as a share of total consumption. Formula: illicit sticks ÷ (legal + illicit sticks). Worked example: ~40 billion illicit sticks on ~120 billion legal is 25% of the total, close to the industry's 26.1%. How they compare: an industry-wide figure; no company-level measure exists. Trap: the only Indian estimate is from an industry-funded fact sheet and predates the reset; treat it as indicative and as advocacy as well as data.

In plain terms

Three numbers decide whether the tax shock is being absorbed: excise per stick (what the state takes), net revenue per stick (what the maker keeps) and volume (how many sticks are still sold legally). If net revenue per stick climbs back toward its old level while volume falls only a few per cent, the industry is winning. If volume falls sharply and excise collections miss the Budget, the smugglers are.

Putting the KPIs together: an ILLUSTRATIVE sensitivity for GPI

The per-stick KPIs combine into a simple sensitivity that explains why the market watches price more than volume. Start from GPI's Q1 FY27 run-rate: about 22.4 billion domestic sticks a year (5.60 billion a quarter × 4) and a segment result of about ₹0.25 a stick, roughly ₹550 crore a year against ₹1,475 crore in FY26 (ILLUSTRATIVE; the segment includes exports, so this is a blend). Now vary each input on its own.

  • Price. Each ₹0.50 of average retail price that sticks, with volume unchanged, adds ₹0.50 × 0.92 ÷ 1.40 ≈ ₹0.33 of net revenue per stick, all of it profit because excise is fixed. On 22.4 billion sticks that is about ₹735 crore a year, more than the whole current run-rate.
  • Volume. Each 1% of volume lost at today's margin costs about 0.22 billion sticks × ₹0.25 ≈ ₹56 crore a year. A 5% volume loss costs about ₹280 crore.
  • Tax. Each ₹0.25 a stick of extra excise or NCCD not passed on removes about ₹560 crore a year, almost the whole current run-rate; passed on in full, it needs a retail increase of about ₹0.38 a stick (₹0.25 × 1.40 ÷ 0.92).

The asymmetry is the point. A ₹0.50 price rise is worth about thirteen times a 1% volume swing at current margins, which is why GPI and ITC are willing to give up a few per cent of volume to restore price, and why a tax change in the February 2027 Budget matters more than any plausible volume outcome. It also shows why the per-stick figures must be watched quarter by quarter: GPI's segment profit could plausibly double or halve within a year on movements of less than ₹1 a stick in price or tax. The same arithmetic applies to ITC and VST in proportion to their volumes, although ITC's higher starting margin per stick makes each 1% of volume worth more to it in rupees.

Definition traps, collected

  • Four revenue lines for one cigarette. Gross sales value (incl. GST, excise, NCCD) > gross revenue or "revenue from operations" (ex-GST, incl. excise) > net revenue (ex-GST, ex-excise). GPI Q1 FY27: ₹5,676 / ₹3,820 / ₹1,206 crore. Use net revenue for margins and growth.
  • The 1 February 2026 line. Gross revenue is not comparable across it, because excise replaced GST cess as the main tax and excise sits inside revenue.
  • Export share bases. GPI's "exports are 21% of net sales" uses an excise-inclusive denominator (₹9,082 crore); on net revenue (₹6,391 crore) unmanufactured exports are 30.4%.
  • Market share by sticks or by rupees. GPI is ~19–20% of legal volume (CRISIL-based) but ~13% of domestic value. ITC volume is undisclosed: use a range (70–90 billion) for any ITC per-stick figure.
  • Statutory versus effective excise. ₹2,700–11,000 per 1,000 sticks in the Act; ₹2,050–8,500 after the section 5A exemption. "Excise" in the accounts also includes NCCD. The Health Security se National Security Cess is a pan-masala levy, not a cigarette tax.
  • Margins on GSV. GPI's slides show EBITDA at 8.6% of GSV in FY26; on net revenue it is 24.8%. Only the latter is comparable.
  • Standalone versus consolidated ITC. FY26 cigarette revenue ₹37,100 crore standalone, ₹40,601 crore consolidated; Q1 FY27 excise ₹10,036 crore standalone, ₹10,409 crore consolidated (the gap is mainly Surya Nepal). This report and our Diversified FMCG report use standalone.
  • Transition quarters. Q4 FY26 contains one old-regime month, trade loading ahead of price rises and stock that had paid old duties; ITC's Q4 FY26 net revenue cannot be derived as gross minus excise. VST's FY26 margin of 30.7% includes a one-off tax-transition gain (CRISIL); ~24–25% is normal.
  • Other income is not all treasury. ₹283 crore of GPI's ₹489 crore consolidated "other income" is its share of PMITPL's profit.
  • Net cash includes a promoter stake. Screener's ₹3,299 crore of GPI investments includes ₹759 crore of shares in its own promoter company; CRISIL's liquid surplus is ~₹2,383 crore.
  • Per-stick blends. GPI's and VST's net revenue and profit include exports; dividing by domestic sticks overstates domestic per-stick values. Use them for direction, not level.
  • One-off cash events. GPI's ₹100 crore interim insurance receipt (Q1 FY27, against a ₹284.36 crore claim already booked as a receivable) is a balance-sheet item, not profit.
In plain terms

Most mistakes about cigarette companies come from comparing numbers on different bases: revenue with and without excise, market share in sticks or rupees, margins on gross sales or on net revenue, ITC's standalone or consolidated figures. Before comparing any two numbers, check that they are measured the same way.

How to read a cigarette maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find net revenue, not revenue. Subtract the excise line from revenue from operations (or use screener's "Sales"). Compare with the same quarter last year. Red flag: a headline that quotes gross revenue growth.
  2. Read the excise line and divide by sticks. For GPI use the monthly run-rate × 3. A rising excise per stick at stable rates means a premium mix shift; a falling one means slab engineering toward shorter sticks.
  3. Check volume. GPI's run-rate (Q1 FY27 1,866 million a month; Q2 FY26 base 1,985 million). For ITC, read the qualitative statement and broker estimates. Red flag: volume falling faster than 5% while price increases continue.
  4. Compute net revenue per stick. It tells you whether price is catching up with tax. GPI was ₹2.15 (blended) in Q1 FY27 against ₹2.60 a year earlier.
  5. Take the segment result, not company EBITDA. For ITC, the cigarette segment PBIT; for GPI, the "cigarette, tobacco and related products" segment result. Divide by segment net revenue for the margin (GPI 11.8%, ITC 62.5% in Q1 FY27).
  6. Compute EBIT per stick and compare with the pre-reset level. GPI ₹0.52 in Q1 FY26 and ₹0.25 in Q1 FY27; recovery toward ₹0.5 would signal pricing has caught up.
  7. Split other income. For GPI, separate the share of associates (PMITPL) from treasury income, and note PMITPL dividend timing in the standalone accounts.
  8. Read the export line and its commentary. GPI's unmanufactured exports (₹248 crore in Q1 FY27) and any mention of "geopolitical factors", Russia or PMI demand.
  9. At half-year and year-end, check the balance sheet. Receivables from PMITPL and PMI entities, inventory days, cash surplus, and any movement in the insurance receivable or the ₹759 crore promoter-company holding.
  10. Read management's pricing language and look for one-offs. "Phased", "staggered", "demand moderation" and any new price round. Red flags: trade loading before a price rise, transition credits (VST's Q4 FY26), insurance or tax items, and an interim dividend cut.
In plain terms

When a cigarette company reports, ignore the headline revenue. Subtract the excise, divide by the number of sticks, and compare the profit per stick with last year. If profit per stick is climbing back while volume holds, the price increases are working. If not, look at whether the company is losing volume or simply has not raised prices enough yet.

GPI's Q1 FY27 PAT fell 44.3%, the steepest of the three, against ITC's consolidated −15.6% (cushioned by its non-cigarette businesses) and ITC's cigarette segment −35.1%, and VST's −24.4%. The global rows are context, not comparables. Who is winning: price-setter, hybrid and value specialist

The reset sorted the industry by pricing power. Three maps show where Godfrey Phillips sits against ITC, VST and the global leader on realisation, valuation and ownership.

The first map plots what each maker keeps per stick against how much of it turns into profit. ITC sits in the top half because it combines a mid-level realisation with an exceptional margin: scale, integration and price leadership in an advertising-free market. PMI sits further right, with a higher realisation per unit but a lower margin, because it spends on marketing and reduced-risk products globally. GPI and VST sit together at the bottom left: similar margins in the low twenties, similar realisations once leaf is included. GPI's FY26 per-stick realisation including exports (₹2.61) was above VST's (₹1.75), but its segment margin was no higher, because its export business and Marlboro transfer pricing dilute the margin that its own brands earn.

The second map turns to valuation. It plots the latest change in profit against the trailing P/E. In a normal market, faster-growing companies trade at higher multiples. Here the order is inverted for GPI: it had the steepest Q1 FY27 profit fall of the Indian makers and the highest P/E. Part of that is arithmetic (a trailing P/E still includes three strong quarters of FY26), and part is the market paying for the PMI connection and GPI's growth record. PMI sits alone in the top right: growing earnings, a higher multiple. VST is in the bottom left: falling profits and the lowest multiple. ITC is in the middle, its group profits cushioned by FMCG, agri and paperboards.

The third map looks at ownership and payout, the governance lens. Each of the three Indian makers has a large foreign tobacco shareholder, and two have a large domestic one as well. GPI's dot is drawn larger because the Modi family's 47.5% is the largest domestic controlling block in the industry. It also has the lowest payout ratio. VST, with BAT and the Damani group, pays out 70%; ITC, with no promoter and BAT as the largest shareholder, pays out 88%. Concentrated family control at GPI coincides with retained cash, a cross-holding in the promoter company and executive pay at 10% of profit.

Putting the maps together: ITC is winning the reset. It has the highest margin, the most pricing power, the largest and still-rising share of the profit pool, and a portfolio deep enough to engineer lengths. GPI is a strong second by volume with real assets, Marlboro and PMI's loyalty as a buyer, but it captures little value per stick and its governance and payout choices cost it valuation. Margins tell a different story. ITC's cigarette PBIT margin on net revenue (about 72% pre-reset) is far above PMI's adjusted operating margin (~45%), because Indian net realisations are struck after a very heavy specific tax and Indian makers spend almost nothing on marketing. GPI's ~24% company margin is low by global standards for the reasons given in section 08. Tax burdens also differ sharply: ITC's annual report shows India's tax on 2,000 cigarettes at 4.90% of per-capita GDP before the reset, the highest in its sample, against 0.36% in the US and 0.73% in Japan.

Valuation has reversed. Indian staples have historically traded at a premium to global peers; today GPI (19.0× trailing), ITC (16.2×) and VST (12.1×) all trade below PMI's ~24× forward earnings (estimate). Investors are paying more for a global company with a smoke-free hedge and growing earnings than for Indian companies with a protected market, high margins and a tax shock. Illicit trade is another global comparison: India's ~26% (industry estimate) compares with Australia's rise from under 2% to about 14% after a decade of steep tax increases, and India is described by Euromonitor (via ITC) as the fourth-largest illicit market in the world.

For GPI specifically, the global lens is also a lens on PMI's priorities. PMI is a ~42% smoke-free company whose Indian presence is a minority stake, a licensed brand and a distributor. If India ever relaxed PECA, PMI would bring IQOS through its own channel, not through GPI's licence for Marlboro cigarettes. GPI's relationship with PMI is therefore valuable as long as India remains a cigarette market, and less certain if it ever stops being one.

In plain terms

The world's biggest tobacco companies now earn a fifth to two-fifths of their revenue from products that do not burn tobacco, and their profits are still growing. Indian companies are not allowed to sell those products, so they depend entirely on traditional cigarettes just as the tax on them has jumped. That is why investors today pay more for each rupee of Philip Morris's earnings than for ITC's or Godfrey Phillips'.

Technology and disruption: what could change the product

In most of the world the disruption to cigarettes is a new product. In India it is a ban on that product, a set of format tricks, and a tax administration that is learning to track sticks.

Reduced-risk products are banned. The Prohibition of Electronic Cigarettes Act, 2019 covers e-cigarettes and heat-not-burn devices, so IQOS, Ploom and glo cannot be sold in India. That removes the main global growth engine from Indian makers, and it makes disruption risk asymmetric for GPI. If the ban were ever relaxed, PMI would own the device franchise through its own channel; GPI's agreements cover Marlboro cigarettes, and PMI kept its smoke-free products outside the GPI relationship (per our primer). ITC, which owns its brands and has built R&D capability, would be better placed than GPI to launch a domestic alternative, but none is permitted today.

Nicotine pouches are a grey zone. PMI's ZYN shipped 3.2 billion pouches in the US in Q3-2026. In India pouches are not clearly covered by COTPA or PECA, may be treated as nicotine products by drug regulators, and imports are restricted. ITC's Agri business has scaled up exports of nicotine and nicotine derivatives and says that line has turned PBIT-positive, which positions it as a supplier to the global pouch market even if domestic sales remain closed.

Format engineering is the real near-term innovation. The length-based excise has produced a wave of "sleek", "micro", "Deluxe" and "Longs" formats, led by ITC's 64 mm Gold Flake Deluxe (section 04). This is disruption within the cigarette: it changes which slab volume sits in, and therefore the state's take and the maker's margin. The government's likely response is to redefine slabs or narrow the exemption where volume migrates.

Track-and-trace and capacity-based taxation. From February 2026 the government introduced a capacity-based excise levy for specified tobacco products, monthly capacity and production declarations, and track-and-trace for evasion-prone goods (Lok Sabha reply, 28 July 2026). A national digital tax-stamp system on cigarette packs, of the kind the WHO's illicit-trade protocol envisages, is not yet in place. When it arrives, it will help legal makers against duty-evading local factories more than against cross-border smuggling.

Quick commerce. GPI's annual report cites IBEF estimates of 70–80% annual growth in quick commerce. How age verification and COTPA's restrictions apply to app-based delivery of cigarettes is unclear, and no central rule was found for this report. Quick commerce also helped kill the economics of GPI's 24Seven convenience stores. For cigarette makers it is both a new channel and a regulatory risk.

Leaf technology. On the farm, GPI's Integrated Production System, its burley push and new varieties aim to improve yields and grade consistency; for exporters, traceable, residue-compliant leaf is increasingly a condition of selling to global buyers such as PMI and BAT.

Bull versus bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The bull case rests on pricing power and history; the bear case on the size of the tax step, the smuggler and the absence of a hedge. For Godfrey Phillips, governance is a third axis.

The bull case

  • Pricing power is intact. Volumes fell only 2–5% in Q1 FY27 against price rises of 20%+; ITC's Gold Flake Super Star is up 51% and Classic Connect 43% since February 2026.
  • Operating leverage works in reverse too. With a fixed excise, every price increase drops almost entirely to EBIT: in our GPI sensitivity, ₹0.50 a stick is worth about ₹735 crore a year.
  • History says stable taxes restore volume. FY19–FY26 recovered the volume lost in FY13–FY17; if FY28 brings no new increase, the pattern can repeat.
  • The structure is protected. FDI ban, licensing and the advertising ban keep out entrants; three makers with ~99% of legal volume.
  • Cash and yield. ITC and VST yield 5.6–6.1%; GPI has ~₹2,400 crore of liquid surplus and 30% ROCE; all three are close to debt-free.
  • Cheaper than global peers. Indian makers trade at 12–19× trailing earnings, below PMI's ~24× forward.

The bear case

  • The step is unprecedented. Excise per stick rose about eightfold in a quarter; prices must rise ~35–40% to restore profit, and about a third of that is still to come.
  • Illicit trade sets a ceiling. Smuggled sticks at ₹10–15 against ₹19 legal; the industry says illicit was ~26% before the reset; no one has measured it since.
  • The next Budget can reset the clock. 1 February 2027 is the first chance to raise NCCD or narrow the excise exemption, which needs no new law.
  • No smoke-free hedge. 100% combustible exposure while PMI is ~42% smoke-free; a relaxation would mainly benefit the MNCs.
  • Profit is concentrating in ITC. 93.5% of the Q1 FY27 pool; smaller makers' per-stick profits have collapsed to ~₹0.25.
  • GPI-specific: undisclosed PMI terms, Russia in the export book, ₹159 crore of family executive pay, 27% institutional dissent, a 51% payout and weak cash conversion.

Weighing the two, the sub-sector's outcome is largely binary on one input: whether the February 2027 Budget leaves cigarette taxes broadly unchanged. If it does, the arithmetic of a fixed excise favours the makers, and FY28 should show profit per stick recovering toward pre-reset levels on modestly lower volumes, led by ITC. If it adds another meaningful increase, the FY13–FY17 pattern of flat revenue, rising illicit share and stalled stocks becomes the base case. Godfrey Phillips adds a second variable: whether its phased pricing and PMI partnership can lift a segment margin that fell to 11.8%, and whether its board narrows the governance discount through payout and pay. Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • Basic excise duty — the central excise on cigarettes set by Notification 03/2025-CE: ₹2,050–8,500 per 1,000 sticks by length and filter, from 1 Feb 2026.
  • Bidi — tobacco hand-rolled in a tendu leaf; mostly unorganised and lightly taxed.
  • Compensation cess — the GST cess charged on tobacco and other demerit goods from July 2017 to 31 Jan 2026; ended when the cess-backed loans were repaid.
  • COTPA — the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act, 2003: public-smoking ban, advertising ban, sale restrictions and pack warnings.
  • Demerit slab — the 40% GST rate created by GST 2.0 (22 Sep 2025); applied to tobacco from 1 Feb 2026, valued on retail sale price.
  • Effective vs statutory rate — the Act's schedule sets ₹2,700–11,000 per 1,000 sticks; a section 5A exemption notification brings the payable rate down to ₹2,050–8,500.
  • FCV — flue-cured Virginia tobacco, the main cigarette leaf, grown in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka under Tobacco Board control.
  • Filter counting rule — for slab purposes a filter counts as 11 mm or its actual length, whichever is more.
  • Gross revenue — revenue from operations under Ind AS: excludes GST, includes excise.
  • GSV — gross sales value: what the trade pays the maker, including GST, excise and NCCD.
  • HSNS cess — Health Security se National Security Cess: a levy on pan masala (₹14,000 Cr FY27 BE), not on cigarettes.
  • HTU / heat-not-burn — heated-tobacco units used in devices such as IQOS; banned in India under PECA.
  • Illicit trade — smuggled cigarettes and locally made duty-evaded brands; ~26% of the market by industry estimate.
  • Length engineering — redesigning a stick's length to fall into a lower excise slab, e.g. 64 mm instead of 69 mm.
  • Loose sticks — single cigarettes sold individually; the norm in most states, banned in Karnataka and Himachal Pradesh.
  • NCCD — National Calamity Contingent Duty, an additional excise on tobacco since 2001; raised ~16% in Feb 2023 and unchanged by the reset.
  • Net revenue — revenue excluding GST and excise; the basis for margins and per-stick figures in this report.
  • No-bid lots — tobacco bales offered at auction that receive no bid.
  • PECA — Prohibition of Electronic Cigarettes Act, 2019, banning e-cigarettes and heat-not-burn devices.
  • PMITPL — Philip Morris India Trading Pvt Ltd (formerly IPM India Wholesale Trading), the Marlboro distributor; GPI holds 24.80%.
  • Price/mix — growth in revenue per stick from price increases and a richer product mix.
  • RSP and Rule 31D — retail sale price; CGST Rule 31D values GST on tobacco at the RSP from 1 Feb 2026.
  • Segment result — a business segment's profit before interest, unallocable items and tax (PBIT).
  • Slab — a length band in the excise schedule (≤65 mm, 65–70 mm, 70–75 mm, longer).
  • Smoke-free products — heated tobacco, e-vapour and oral nicotine (pouches); ~42% of PMI's revenue in 2026.
  • Specific vs ad valorem tax — a fixed rupee amount per unit versus a percentage of value.
  • Tobacco Board — statutory body under the Tobacco Board Act, 1975 that licenses FCV growers, fixes crop size and runs auctions.
  • Volume share vs value share — share of sticks sold versus share of rupees earned; GPI ~19–20% vs ~13%.
