00

The one-page view: a plywood franchise bolted to a board glut

Four panels share one dealer counter and one carpenter, but they earn wildly different returns. That gap is the whole story of this industry in 2026.

Every Indian wardrobe, kitchen cabinet, bed box and office desk is built from a wood panel. There are four of them. Plywood is thin sheets of peeled wood glued crosswise; it is what a carpenter builds with on site. Decorative laminates are the 1 mm skin of printed, resin-soaked paper that gives the panel its colour and its scratch resistance. MDF (medium-density fibreboard) is wood ground to fibre and pressed into a smooth, uniform board that factories can cut by machine. Particle board is the cheapest: wood chips and glue, flat-packed into the furniture you assemble at home. Together they form a market that this report estimates, bottom-up and as an illustrative figure, at roughly ₹55,000 Cr of manufacturer sales in FY26.

The four panels are sold through the same dealer and chosen by the same carpenter, yet they behave like four different industries. Plywood is labour-heavy and needs little capital, and in its branded form it earns rich returns. MDF and particle board are made on continuous presses that cost ₹700 Cr a line and earn money only when they run full. Laminates sit in between and can be exported. One listed company, Century Plyboards, makes all four plus a small logistics business, and its own segment accounts show the gap starkly: in FY26 its plywood segment earned a pre-tax return of about 41% on ₹1,055 Cr of capital, while MDF earned about 7% on ₹1,526 Cr and particle board lost money on ₹700 Cr.

Six forces define the industry today, and the report is built around them:

  • Formalisation and the brand moat. About 70% of plywood (an indicative industry figure) is still made by small, unbranded mills. Two BIS quality-control orders (QCOs), in force since 11 and 28 February 2025, shut most imports and force small mills to certify or exit. Branded plywood is growing three to five times as fast as the market: Century's plywood volume rose 16.5% in FY26 and 28.8% in Q1 FY27, against industry growth of 5–7%.
  • The MDF capacity glut. India can make 4.0–4.5 mn cubic metres (CBM) of MDF a year but uses about 3.0 mn. Another 0.85–0.9 mn CBM of lines arrives between late FY27 and mid-2027. MDF margins have fallen from 28–32% at the FY22–23 peak to 7–17% now.
  • Input costs that cannot be hedged. Timber is local, seasonal and bought from farmers; resins come from methanol, urea, melamine and phenol priced off gas and crude. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 sent Indian methanol up about 180% year on year by May. Branded plywood passed on a ~7% price rise; MDF tried 15% and rolled most of it back.
  • Capex and return dilution. A rupee of MDF or particle-board sales needs three to ten times the capital of a rupee of plywood. Century's return on average capital employed fell from 30.9% (FY23) to 12.3% (FY25) as it built boards, and recovered only to 13.4% (FY26).
  • Distribution and brand. The only durable moat is the dealer counter and the carpenter's trust. It explains why the same product, plywood, earns 16.9% at Century and about 3% at Greenpanel.
  • Laminate exports: freight beats tariffs. India's laminate makers export a third to two-thirds of their sheets. In 2026 the shock was container shortages and Gulf freight, not the 10% US Section 301 tariff.

In plain terms

India's best panel business is the least glamorous one: branded plywood, made partly in the company's own small factories and partly by contract mills, sold through thousands of dealers to carpenters who trust the name. It needs little money and earns a lot. The glamorous, factory-scale boards, MDF and particle board, need huge machines and are in oversupply. Century owns both, so its results each quarter are a tug-of-war between a franchise that is winning and a board business that is waiting for the glut to clear.

The investment case for the industry therefore turns on three questions that the rest of this report answers in detail. Will formalisation keep pushing branded plywood volumes up at 12–15% a year, as Century assumes when it targets 15% of the total plywood market within five years (from 9.5–10% today)? Will MDF capacity be absorbed by around FY28, so that board margins can return towards the mid-teens that managements describe as normal? And can companies fund the next round of capacity, Century's ₹12,000 Cr revenue aspiration for 2031 needs about ₹2,500 Cr more capex, without diluting returns again? The market's answer, on 23 October 2026, was to pay 47× trailing earnings for Century, about 31× for Stylam and Greenply, and barely book value for the MDF-heavy Greenpanel and Rushil.

01

How a panel earns: from a poplar farm to a carpenter's wardrobe

The raw material grows on farms, the glue comes from the Gulf, and the sale is made by a carpenter the manufacturer never meets.

A sheet of branded plywood starts on a farm in Punjab, Haryana or western Uttar Pradesh, where farmers grow poplar alongside wheat, or in Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Tamil Nadu, where eucalyptus is the plantation tree. The logs are sold through mandis such as Yamunanagar, peeled into veneers a millimetre or two thick, dried, glued crosswise with resin and hot-pressed. The best face veneers still come from abroad: India imported $485 m of veneer sheets in CY2025, mostly from Indonesia, Gabon, Vietnam and Tanzania. Century peels its own in Gabon, through Century Gabon SUARL, which handles about 200 CBM of logs a day.

The glue is the second input, and it links a farm product to the global energy market. Urea and methanol make formaldehyde, which makes urea-formaldehyde (UF) resin for moisture-resistant plywood, MDF and particle board. Phenol and formaldehyde make phenol-formaldehyde (PF) resin for boiling-water-proof and marine plywood and for the core of laminates. Melamine makes the hard top layer of a laminate and of pre-laminated board. India imports about 98% of its methanol, and until March 2026 about 88% of that came from the Middle East. Most large panel makers run captive resin plants, but they still buy the chemicals at world prices.

Value concentrates at Step 3 for boards (scale, utilisation) and at Steps 4–5 for plywood and laminates (brand, channel). That split is why plywood brands earn more on far less capital.

The third step, the factory, looks completely different by panel. A plywood unit is a shed of multi-daylight hot presses, peeling lathes and dryers, with hundreds of workers. It costs little: Century's 60,000 CBM Hoshiarpur plywood greenfield, due in Q3 FY27, is budgeted at ₹180 Cr, about ₹3,000 per CBM of annual capacity. Small mills cost far less, which is why thousands of them exist. An MDF plant is a continuous chemical-mechanical line: chippers, a pressurised refiner that turns chips into fibre, a resin blender, a 40-metre continuous press from Siempelkamp or Dieffenbacher, and sanders. Century's Badvel (Andhra Pradesh) MDF greenfield cost ₹730 Cr and its Chennai particle-board line ₹694 Cr. A laminate plant is a set of impregnation lines and presses; Century's third laminate press, added in an existing plant in July 2026, cost about ₹25 Cr, while the first two presses with the plant around them cost about ₹200 Cr.

The fourth and fifth steps are where branded plywood makes its money. Plywood moves from manufacturer to distributor to the neighbourhood "plywood and hardware" dealer, and the buying decision is usually made by the carpenter or contractor the homeowner hires. A homeowner fitting out a flat may spend ₹3–6 lakh on interiors and has no way to judge a sheet of plywood by looking at it; the carpenter does, and he also cares about whether the board warps, splits or attracts borers on his job. That is why brands invest in carpenter loyalty schemes, QR-code authentication and warranties: Century spends 4–4.5% of revenue on brand and schemes, and its "Total Cover" guarantee replaces the furniture, not just the board, if its Club Prime or Architect plywood fails, at a claim ratio of about 0.06%. MDF and particle board, by contrast, increasingly go to factories (OEMs) that buy on specification and price. Greenpanel sells 20–25% of its MDF to OEMs.

Two features of this chain matter for everything that follows. First, plywood volume does not have to be made in-house. Century sold 485,403 CBM of plywood in FY26 against its own capacity of 396,300 CBM, so at least 18–20% was made by contract mills to its specification (an indicative floor; the share made outside was at least about 28% in Q1 FY27). Greenply's own-manufactured share has risen from 58% to 68% of volume in a year. Outsourcing lets a brand grow without capital, at a lower margin per sheet; in-sourcing raises capital intensity but secures quality and margin. Second, boards cannot be outsourced in the same way: the value is in running your own continuous line full. That single difference drives the return gap.

In plain terms

A plywood brand sells trust, and a lot of that trust is earned at the dealer's counter and on the carpenter's job, not in the factory. A board maker sells a uniform industrial product, and its profit depends on keeping a very expensive machine busy. Same shop, same customer, two different businesses.

A final link in Century's chain is unrelated to panels. Its Logistics Services segment runs a container freight station and, since 2025, part of a port terminal ("Century Port") at Khidderpore Docks in Kolkata. It earned ₹159 Cr of revenue at a 16.3% EBITDA margin in FY26 and handled more than 9,000 containers in July 2026 with only half of its first phase operating. Management calls it non-core and is evaluating a strategic partner "at a valuation beneficial to Century". It is a small, real and separately valued asset that the panel story tends to hide.

02

Four panels, one ₹55,000 crore market: size and growth

No official census exists. Built up from company shares, the market is about ₹52,000–58,000 Cr, more than half of it plywood.

India publishes no reliable official figure for the size of its wood-panel market, because most plywood is made by small mills whose output is not counted and often not fully invoiced. This report builds the number bottom-up from company disclosures, and every figure in this section is an illustrative estimate. The anchor is Century's own statement on its Q1 FY27 call that it holds 9.5–10% of the total plywood market: its plywood segment sold ₹2,915 Cr in FY26, so the market is about ₹29,000–31,000 Cr. Laminates are sized from Greenlam's claimed 17.8% of the organised market, MDF from volume (about 3.0 mn CBM at ₹28,000–30,000 per CBM), and particle board from a rough volume estimate.

Three cross-checks support the order of magnitude. FIPPI, the plywood industry federation, put wood-panel imports (plywood, MDF and particle board) at ₹3,431 Cr in FY25, the pre-QCO peak; at about 6–7% of the market that implies a total near ₹50,000–55,000 Cr. MDF imports were about 10% of FY25 MDF demand, consistent with an ₹8,000–9,000 Cr domestic MDF market. And thin MDF (under 6 mm) is said to be about a quarter of MDF demand, 750,000–800,000 CBM. None of these is precise, but together they say the industry is a ₹50,000–60,000 Cr business at factory-gate prices, perhaps ₹70,000–80,000 Cr at retail after dealer margins and GST.

Growth differs sharply by panel. Plywood, the mature product, grows with housing and renovation at about 5–7% a year in volume. MDF grew about 15% in FY26, and managements expect "early double-digit to mid-teens" growth in FY27, because factory-made furniture is replacing site carpentry. Laminates grow with both and with exports. The long-run headroom argument rests on India's panel mix. Industry estimates put plywood at about 54% of India's wood-panel consumption, against roughly 20% globally, where MDF, particle board and OSB dominate because furniture is made in factories. As India's kitchens and wardrobes move from carpenter-built to factory-built, the mix should drift towards boards. That is the bet behind every MDF and particle-board line built in the last five years. It is a sound bet over a decade. The problem in 2026 is that the industry built a decade of capacity in about three years.

Exports are small relative to the domestic market, with one exception. India exported only $47.5 m of plywood and $58.9 m of MDF in CY2025, mostly to Bhutan, the Gulf and the United States, and it remains a net importer of panels by value. Decorative laminates are different: Merino exports about ₹735 Cr a year (29% of its revenue), Stylam exports about two-thirds of its sheets, and Greenlam claims more than 29% of India's laminate exports. Laminates are India's only globally competitive panel, a theme taken up in section 17.

In plain terms

Nobody counts this market properly, so treat any single figure with care. What is clear is the shape: plywood is more than half of it and grows slowly; boards are smaller and grow fast; laminates are the one panel India sells abroad. A company that wins share in plywood can grow at three times the market without the market growing at all.

03

Who buys a panel: flats, renovations and furniture factories

Panel demand follows housing handovers with a lag, renovation steadies it, and factory furniture is changing which panel gets bought.

The single biggest end-market for panels is the fit-out of new homes. A flat sold in a launch year is typically handed over three to four years later, and the owner then spends six to eighteen months, sometimes more, putting in kitchens, wardrobes and beds. Panel demand in FY27 is therefore driven by flats launched and sold in 2022–24, not by this quarter's bookings. That lag is good news for the next year or so, because the 2022–24 launch boom is now being delivered, and a warning for FY28–29, because sales in the big cities have plateaued.

Renovation and replacement are the second leg, and probably a third to a half of plywood and laminate demand (an indicative estimate; there is no primary source). A kitchen redone after fifteen years, a wardrobe added when a child is born, a shop refit: these are less sensitive to interest rates and are exactly where the carpenter's choice of brand counts most, because the job is small and the homeowner relies entirely on him. Commercial interiors, offices, hotels, hospitals and retail, add demand for laminates, compacts and cladding, though no one publishes a reliable split.

The third leg is changing the mix. Modular-kitchen and wardrobe factories, flat-pack retailers, office-furniture makers and contract manufacturers for export all prefer boards to plywood, because boards are uniform, machine-cuttable and cheaper per square metre. Managements at Century and Greenpanel put MDF industry growth at 15–20% in FY26 and "mid-teens" in Q1 FY27; Greenply's young Vadodara MDF line grew 35–40% in its latest quarter. Particle board is the next frontier, pulled by flat-pack furniture. The pull is real; the problem, covered in section 07, is that supply has grown faster still.

The fourth driver has nothing to do with the housing cycle: share shift from unorganised to organised. When a small mill in Yamunanagar loses a customer to Century because it cannot get a BIS licence, or because the builder now insists on invoiced, certified board, the market does not grow but the brand does. Century's plywood volume grew 16.5% in FY26 and 28.8% in Q1 FY27 against industry growth of 5–7%. Part of Q1 was dealers stocking ahead of an April price rise; most of it was share gain. Management's target, 15% of the total plywood market within five years from 9.5–10% now, implies 12–15% volume growth a year in a market growing 5–7%.

Exports are the fifth and smallest driver for boards and the largest for laminates. Before the West Asia war, Greenpanel sent 80–85% of its MDF exports to the Middle East; after it, Gulf container freight rose from $400–500 to $5,500–6,000 and its exports went to zero in Q1 FY27. The US takes a minority of Indian laminate exports (perhaps 10–15%, an indicative estimate) and very little MDF ($6.5 m in CY2025). The 10% Section 301 tariff on Indian goods since 24 July 2026 matters for laminate exporters with US distribution, but freight has done more damage this year.

In plain terms

The flats being handed over now were sold two or three years ago, so FY27 demand for panels looks fine. The flats being sold now are flat in number, and borrowing costs are rising, so FY28–29 is less certain. Meanwhile the big brands are growing regardless, by taking customers from small mills, and factories are switching from plywood to boards. Volume is not the problem in this industry. Price and capacity are.

04

Where ₹100 goes: timber, glue, a dealer's scheme and a lot of steel

Raw materials take about half of every rupee in all four panels. What differs is the capital needed to earn the rest.

Strip a panel company's income statement down and the first line is always raw materials: timber and veneer, resins and papers. They take roughly 50–55% of revenue in plywood and MDF and a little less in export laminates. After that the panels diverge. Plywood is labour-heavy, so wages take 12–14% of revenue; MDF is energy- and machine-heavy, so power and depreciation take more. All of them spend heavily on getting the product to the customer: freight, dealer discounts, carpenter schemes and advertising take 12–16% of revenue. Two cost lines deserve a closer look because they explain most quarter-to-quarter surprises. The first is employees. Century's employee cost was 15.2% of revenue in Q1 FY27, high for a manufacturer, because its plywood plants are labour-intensive and because it carries a sales force that calls on dealers and carpenters. Wage inflation and India's new labour codes (which cost Century about ₹65 Cr of one-off gratuity provision in FY26) therefore matter more here than in a cement plant. The second is selling cost, which includes dealer schemes. When demand is soft, companies rarely cut list prices; they raise schemes and cash discounts, which show up as lower realisation or higher selling cost. Greenpanel's Q1 FY27 commentary that its 15% MDF list-price increase netted only 7–8% after discounts, and was then rolled back, is the scheme mechanism at work.

Gross margin, often the first number analysts quote, is unreliable across companies here. Some report "cost of materials" alone (Greenpanel 52.7% gross margin in Q1 FY27, Greenlam 52.9%), some include power, labour and direct manufacturing costs (Century's deck shows gross profit of 32.5% of income in FY26 after such costs), and brokers re-compute on their own bases (one broker shows Century at 48.6% for FY26). Rushil's 39.0% in Q1 FY27 reflects its heavier MDF mix and an April maintenance shutdown. The KPI section returns to this trap; the rule is to compare a company's gross margin only with its own history.

In plain terms

Before depreciation, a plywood rupee and an MDF rupee look about equally profitable. After you count the machine, they are not close. Plywood ties up about ₹36 of capital per ₹100 of sales at Century; MDF ₹119; a new particle-board line, until it fills, ₹349. That one ratio explains why investors pay so much for branded plywood and so little for board capacity.

05

The carpenter's choice: brands, dealers and the 70% still unorganised

Plywood is a fragmented market where a few brands are taking share fast. Distribution, not manufacturing, is the moat.

Plywood is one of the last large building materials in India where most of the product is still made by small, unbranded firms. Roughly 70% of plywood (an indicative figure used by industry participants) comes from thousands of mills clustered in Yamunanagar in Haryana, Gandhidham and Kandla in Gujarat, Kerala, Punjab, western Uttar Pradesh and along the Nepal border. Their edge was historically tax: under-invoiced sales, cash payments and lower-grade core veneers. GST in 2017, e-way bills from 2018, e-invoicing thresholds that fell through 2023 and the 2025 QCOs have steadily removed that edge. Each step has handed share to the brands.

Why does a brand win? Not because branded plywood is a different product; a well-made BWP sheet from a Yamunanagar mill can be as good as a branded one. It wins because the buyer cannot tell the difference at the counter and the carpenter, who can, carries the risk. A carpenter who builds a ₹2 lakh kitchen with board that delaminates in two monsoons loses the next job. Brands sell him certainty: consistent thickness and glue, termite and borer treatment, and a guarantee he can show the homeowner. Century's "Total Cover" goes further than most, covering the cost of the furniture, not just the board, for ten years on its Club Prime and Architect ranges. Brands also sell him loyalty points, training, site support and authenticated sheets that are hard to counterfeit. Century spends 4–4.5% of revenue on all of this, including campaigns with Aamir Khan for Club Prime and Rahul Dravid for its HDF.

The second leg of the moat is the dealer. A plywood dealer stocks a few brands and many SKUs, extends credit to carpenters and contractors, and pushes whichever brand offers him reliable supply, good schemes and quick replacement of defective stock. Century's network of more than 4,100 dealers and about 19,000 retailers (ICRA) is the widest in the industry; Greenpanel runs about 2,300 distributors and 12,500 retailers; Rushil about 600 distributors and 4,400 retailers. A new entrant can buy a press in a year. It cannot buy 19,000 counters, or the decades of credit and trust behind them.

Outside plywood, structure is different. MDF was born formal: no small mill can afford a continuous line, so the industry is an oligopoly of about five named players plus a long tail of smaller multi-daylight and thin-board lines. Laminates sit in between, with a handful of large organised brands and many regional pressers. The large unlisted names matter. Action Tesa, a Delhi-based private group, was the first to install a Siempelkamp continuous MDF line in 2018 at Sitarganj and is commissioning a second plant at Visakhapatnam. Merino Industries, family-owned and effectively unlisted, is one of the two or three largest laminate makers, with FY26 revenue of about ₹2,521 Cr (a single secondary source citing its annual report). No multinational panel maker manufactures at scale in India; EGGER and Kronospan are present only as brands and importers, and IKEA buys board locally to its own specifications.

Ownership is concentrated too. Promoters hold 71.83% of Century, 50–57% of each of the listed reference peers, and foreign institutions own very little: Century's FII holding fell from 6.28% in March 2024 to 3.61% in September 2026, while domestic institutions rose from 14.51% to 19.48%. A free float of about 28% and low foreign ownership mean the stocks can move sharply on small flows.

In plain terms

The homeowner pays for plywood but the carpenter chooses it, and the dealer decides what is on the shelf. A brand that owns both relationships can raise prices when costs rise and take customers from small mills when rules tighten. That is a moat no machine can copy, and it is why Century's plywood earns six times Greenpanel's margin on the same product.

06

The wall that worked: what the BIS orders did to imports Deep dive

Two quality-control orders in February 2025 cut panel imports by 36–50% in a year. Veneer imports rose instead, and some product leaked through.

Until 2025, anyone could ship plywood, MDF or particle board into India subject to ordinary customs duty, and duties on ASEAN goods were low under the India–ASEAN trade agreement. Imports grew fast: plywood imports (HS 4412) rose from $102 m in CY2021 to $257 m in CY2024, mostly from Vietnam, Nepal, China and Indonesia; MDF and fibreboard (HS 4411) peaked at $124 m in CY2023. FIPPI put wood-panel imports at ₹3,431 Cr in FY25, growing about 15% a year for five years. Imports set the price ceiling for MDF and squeezed the bottom end of plywood.

The government's response was not a tariff but a standard. Under the BIS Act, a Quality Control Order makes a product's Indian Standard mandatory: it can be made, sold or imported only by a manufacturer who holds a BIS licence for that standard, with factory audits and testing. Two orders covered wood panels:

  • Wood Based Boards (Quality Control) Order, 2024: MDF, particle board and prelam board, block board. In force from 11 February 2025 for large and medium units, 11 May 2025 for small and 11 August 2025 for micro units.
  • Plywood and Wooden Flush Door Shutters (Quality Control) Order, 2024: general plywood, marine plywood, shuttering plywood and flush doors. In force from 28 February 2025, with small units from 28 May 2025 and micro units from 28 August 2025.

Both orders had first been notified in 2023 and were deferred by a year in March 2024 after industry requests; DPIIT then signalled no further extension. Managements still described them as in force in their August 2026 calls. (Greenpanel's FY26 annual report says the QCO was "implemented from October 2025"; that conflicts with the notified dates and appears to be a drafting error.)

The effect was immediate and large. FIPPI's monthly data show plywood imports collapsing from the February 2025 stockpiling peak to roughly an eighth of it two months later. On a calendar-year basis, UN Comtrade data show plywood imports down 43% in CY2025, MDF and fibreboard down 36% and particle board down 50%. Century's managing director said on the Q2 FY26 call that the QCO had "stopped all the imports… by 80%". Only two foreign MDF makers, one Thai and one Malaysian, held BIS licences at the time of FIPPI's study.

The wall is not watertight. FIPPI's study found three leaks. First, misclassification: 96–100% of the Vietnamese and Chinese plywood that still arrived after the deadline was declared under the residual "Others" sub-heading of HS 4412, which made BIS checks harder at the port. FIPPI named ports (JNPT, Chennai, Mundra and inland container depots at Ludhiana and Tughlakabad) and dozens of unlicensed exporters. Second, Nepal: nine Nepalese plywood makers hold BIS licences, and imports from Nepal surged in May 2025 to between 2 and 43 times their January–April average, which FIPPI suspects reflects licence misuse by re-routing third-country plywood. Third, CY2025 data include the pre-deadline rush, so the real post-QCO run-rate is lower than the annual total and the leak is a larger share of it than it looks.

A naming trap. One of the BIS-licensed Nepalese exporters named in FIPPI's study is "Century Ply Industries Pvt. Ltd." It is not a subsidiary or a disclosed related party of Century Plyboards (India) Ltd: the FY26 annual report lists 17 Indian subsidiaries, Century Gabon SUARL and Century Panels B.V. (Netherlands), and no Nepal entity. FIPPI's allegation concerns Nepalese exporters, not Century, and nothing on the filed record links the two.

Who gained? The answer is visible in Century's plywood segment. Volume growth accelerated from 6.2% in FY24 and 14.0% in FY25 to 16.5% in FY26 and 28.8% in Q1 FY27, and the EBITDA margin rose from 12.5% in FY24 to a record 16.9%. Greenply delivered double-digit plywood volume growth in Q1 FY27 and lifted its own-manufactured share. The QCO did two things at once: it removed the cheapest imported competition, and it forced small domestic mills to spend money on certification, which some cannot. Both push customers towards brands. Management credits the QCO and formalisation, not the housing cycle, for most of its plywood share gain.

What could weaken the wall? Three things. A government that rescinds or dilutes QCOs (it rescinded several chemical and polymer QCOs in November 2025, though not the wood-panel ones). Lax enforcement at ports and on the Nepal border. And, more subtly, BIS licences granted to large Vietnamese or Chinese mills, which would reopen the door to compliant imports. Each would show up first in monthly DGCI&S import data for HS 4412 and 4411, which is why those belong on any watch-list. For MDF in particular, imports were about 10% of demand in FY25 and probably 1–3% after the QCO (an indicative estimate); a return towards 5% would add to the glut covered in the next section.

In plain terms

India did not raise tariffs on plywood; it made a quality certificate compulsory, and almost no foreign mill had one. Imports of finished panels fell by a third to a half in a year, and Indian factories started importing the raw veneer instead and pressing it at home. The beneficiaries are the brands with licences, dealers and factories ready to fill the gap. The weak points are paperwork tricks at the port and the Nepal border.

In plain terms

The QCO helps plywood brands twice: it keeps cheap imports out and it makes life harder for small domestic mills that cannot afford certification. That is why Century's plywood volume grew almost 29% in a quarter in which the plywood market grew perhaps 6%.

Everyone built an MDF plant: anatomy of a capacity glut Deep dive

India can make about 4.2 mn CBM of MDF and uses about 3.0 mn. Another 0.85–0.9 mn CBM is on the way. Price discipline has already broken once.

MDF is the panel of the future in India, and every serious player has acted on that belief at the same time. In FY22–23, after COVID, MDF prices rose with timber and resin costs while imports were constrained by freight, and margins hit records: Century's MDF EBITDA margin was 31.7% in FY22 and Greenpanel's company margin 26%, with a 29% ROCE. Within three years Greenpanel added a 231,000 CBM line in Andhra Pradesh, Century built a Badvel greenfield and extended it, Greenply built Vadodara, Rushil expanded, Action Tesa ordered a Vizag plant, and a new entrant, Elixrr, started building in Madhya Pradesh. Demand grew strongly, about 15% in FY26, but supply grew faster, and in a business with high fixed costs that is enough to break prices.

The competitive dynamics are shifting. Greenpanel, historically priced at a premium to Century as the MDF specialist, told its Q1 FY27 call that it now prices at par with all peers to defend volume and utilisation. An analyst on the same call noted, and management did not dispute, that Century had out-sold Greenpanel in domestic MDF in each of the last two quarters. The capacity leader has become a price-follower. Greenpanel also carries a hidden obligation: ₹154 Cr of export obligations under the EPCG scheme (which let it import machinery duty-free against future exports), at a time when its exports are zero. Failing to meet them means repaying the duty saved, with interest.

Why does Century cope better on volume than Greenpanel, if not on margin? It has two plants, one in the North and one in the South, which cuts freight; it sells MDF through the same 4,100 dealers that sell its plywood; about 20,000 of its roughly 100,000 CBM in Q1 FY27 was pre-laminated, which realises about 50% more per CBM; and it can bundle. Its weakness is the subsidiary: the Badvel MDF and laminate plants sit in Century Panels Ltd, which lost ₹23.5 Cr at the PAT level in Q1 FY27 on ₹176 Cr of revenue. That loss is what a young line at modest utilisation, with full depreciation and interest, looks like.

In plain terms

Everyone saw that India would switch to factory-made furniture, and everyone built MDF plants at once. Demand is growing well, but the plants arrived faster, so about a third of the capacity is idle and the companies cannot raise prices even when their glue costs jump. The industry needs roughly two years of demand growth with no new plants to get back to health. It is getting new plants instead.

In plain terms

Bigger is not automatically better in MDF. The most profitable line in Q1 FY27 was Greenply's single, full plant in Gujarat, not the industry's largest network. In a glut, the winners are the ones whose machines are busy, close to customers and making higher-value boards.

08

Glue you cannot hedge: timber, resins and the West Asia shock

Half of every panel's cost is a farm product and a petrochemical. Neither can be locked in, and only brands can pass the swings on.

Panel makers face two raw-material cycles that have nothing to do with each other. Timber is a farm crop: poplar and eucalyptus planted by farmers five or six years earlier, sold through mandis, and priced by local supply, the monsoon and competing demand from paper mills. Resin chemicals are global petrochemicals priced off gas and crude and shipped through the Strait of Hormuz. There is no futures market for Indian plantation poplar, and the resin chemicals are bought by most panel makers in volumes too small to hedge efficiently. Both costs move faster than list prices can.

Timber: a local crop with long memories

Plantation timber prices run in multi-year cycles. Poplar sold for about ₹15,000 a tonne in 2012 and crashed below ₹5,000 after 2014 when a wave of planting matured, which helped plywood margins for years and then pushed farmers towards other crops. Trade press in March 2026 put peelable plantation timber at ₹9–11 per kg (₹9,000–11,000 a tonne) in both North and South India and board-grade eucalyptus at ₹4.5–6 per kg, and expected both to stay flat through 2026; these levels come from a single trade-press source and are illustrative. Company commentary is consistent: Century described timber as broadly stable in FY27 after a North India spike from the Punjab floods in Q2 FY26; Greenply assumes stable timber and treats any fall as a windfall; Greenpanel saved 6–7% on timber and efficiency in Q1 FY27 by using more mixed wood and less eucalyptus, which carries a 20–25% premium in South India. Monsoons raise MDF timber costs seasonally because wet wood weighs more and harvests slow.

Resins: the Gulf in every sheet

The resin story in 2026 is methanol. Formaldehyde, the building block of UF, MUF and PF resins, takes 46% of India's methanol demand, and India imports about 98% of its methanol: roughly 3.57 mn tonnes in FY26, about 88% of it from the Middle East until February 2026. When the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026, that supply line broke. CRISIL Intelligence reports that the Middle East's share of India's methanol imports fell to about 51% in March–May 2026, that the CFR India price was up about 130% year on year in April and 141% in May, and that the domestic price reached ₹73,950 a tonne in May, about 182% above a year earlier. The government waived import duty on methanol and 40 petrochemicals from early April to 15 July 2026, after which a 7.5% duty was reported reinstated. Prices eased in June after a US–Iran memorandum, and CRISIL expects an FY27 average of US$380–420 a tonne, still above the roughly US$300 of the pre-war period.

The pattern is the brand moat again. Branded plywood passed on about 7% and kept it, because a carpenter will not switch brands over a few hundred rupees a sheet. Laminates, sold on design and brand, passed on 7–10%. MDF, a specification product sold into a market with a third of capacity idle, could not. Imported inputs add a currency layer. Face veneer, decor paper, methanol and phenol are priced in dollars, and the rupee has weakened to about ₹94.9 per dollar (19 October 2026), close to its record low near ₹96.8. That is why companies report "EBITDA excluding forex" and why the KPI section insists on naming the basis.

In plain terms

A war in the Gulf more than doubled the price of the chemical that makes panel glue within three months. Plywood brands raised prices about 7% and customers paid. MDF makers tried 15% and customers refused, because there is too much MDF. Same cost shock, opposite outcomes, and the difference was pricing power, not efficiency.

From the asset-light plywood brand to the single-product MDF line, each model has its own margin, capital need and failure mode.

The listed and large unlisted panel makers fall into five business models. They differ less in what they make than in where their profit comes from: brand and channel, utilisation, design and export reach, or cross-selling. Rules of the game: quality orders, GST, duties and forests

The QCO is the policy that matters most. GST 2.0 left panels untouched, and trade remedies help suppliers more than panel makers.

Four sets of rules shape the economics of Indian wood panels: product standards (the BIS quality-control orders), indirect tax (GST), trade policy (customs duty, free-trade agreements and anti-dumping duties) and forestry law, plus export-market rules on formaldehyde emissions. The QCOs are covered in depth in section 06; this section summarises the rest and explains what each does to the brands, the unorganised mills and the importers.

GST. Plywood, MDF and particle board entered GST in July 2017 at 28%, the top slab, and were cut to 18% on 10 November 2017. The 2017 transition, followed by e-way bills (2018) and progressively lower e-invoicing thresholds (to 2023), did more for formalisation than any rate: an unorganised mill that sells without invoices now faces a paper trail at every check-post. GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, cut rates on many building materials, including cement from 28% to 18%, but left plywood, MDF, particle board, veneers and laminated wood (HSN 4410–4412) at 18%. Panels got no rate tailwind. Two secondary sources say raw timber (HS 4403) is taxed at 18% after the change; the notification text could not be verified, so this report states it only as "18% per trade guides". If correct, it raises input costs most for small mills that cannot claim input-tax credit.

Customs and trade agreements. Basic customs duty on plywood, MDF and particle board is reported at 10% plus a 10% social welfare surcharge and 18% IGST, per aggregator tariff pages rather than the official schedule, so treat the rate as indicative. The India–ASEAN agreement (AITIGA) lowers duty on ASEAN-origin goods, which is why Vietnam, Thailand and Malaysia dominated imports, and why the government used a standard (the QCO) rather than a tariff to cut them.

Anti-dumping duties. None applies to plywood, laminates or particle board. DGTR recommended anti-dumping duty on thin plain MDF (under 6 mm) from Vietnam, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia in 2021, but the Finance Ministry did not impose it. The one live measure works against panel makers: decorative paper from China carries duty of $110, $297 or $542 a tonne depending on the exporter (revised in June 2025), under a sunset review initiated in June 2026 at the request of ITC, and reported extended to 26 March 2027. It protects the domestic paper maker at the laminate maker's expense.

Forests and timber. The Supreme Court's 1996 orders in the Godavarman case shut forest-based plywood mills in Assam and the North-East and pushed the industry onto plantation timber in the North and South. Abroad, Gabon banned log exports in 2010, so veneer processing moved into Gabon's special economic zones (Century's Gabon unit is one example), and Myanmar's 2014 log-export ban eventually led Century to write off ₹49 Cr on its Myanmar subsidiary in FY23. Greenply reports more than 90 million trees planted under its farmer programme.

Emissions and export standards. Indian standards and branded marketing use the E0/E1/E2 formaldehyde emission classes. Exports of composite wood and laminated products to the United States must meet CARB Phase 2 and TSCA Title VI limits, which raises the bar for Indian exporters and favours those with certified, low-emission resin systems. Formaldehyde-free binders such as pMDI are an emerging requirement in Europe and North America.

Incentives. State capital subsidies and power concessions support plants in Andhra Pradesh and Punjab; Century has booked Punjab power-subsidy arrears for Hoshiarpur. EPCG ties duty-free machinery imports to future exports, a scheme that looked cheap until the Gulf lanes closed.

Four decades, five cycles: from forest mills to the board glut

The industry's history is a sequence of supply shocks: forest bans, tax changes, capacity waves, and now a war in the Gulf.

Indian wood panels have moved through five distinct cycles since the 1980s. Each began with a change in supply, not demand, and each ended with a different set of winners. Understanding them makes the present glut look less like an accident and more like the industry's habit.

The plywood era (1980s–2008). Century Plyboards was incorporated on 5 January 1982 in Kolkata by Sajjan Bhajanka and Sanjay Agarwal; Greenply was founded around 1984. Plywood was made largely from natural forest timber in Assam and the North-East until the Supreme Court's 1996 orders closed forest-based mills, forcing a move to plantation poplar in the North and eucalyptus in the South. Brands grew alongside a vast unorganised base, and the first decorative laminates plants appeared.

The first MDF wave (2009–2015). Greenply's MDF plant at Rudrapur (around 2010) and Rushil's Chikmagalur line introduced Indian-made MDF, but imports from Thailand, Vietnam and Malaysia under the ASEAN agreement capped prices. Greenlam was demerged from Greenply's decorative business and listed in 2015. Gabon's 2010 log-export ban and Myanmar's 2014 ban changed where face veneer came from.

GST and the second MDF wave (2016–2019). Century's Hoshiarpur MDF plant (₹335.6 Cr, commissioned 2016–17), Greenply's Andhra Pradesh MDF line (2018) and Action Tesa's Sitarganj continuous line (first board on 28 February 2018, about 511,000 CBM) arrived together, and MDF utilisation fell to about 40% in some months of 2018. GST arrived at 28% and fell to 18%. On 1 July 2019 Greenply demerged its MDF business and part of its plywood into Greenpanel Industries, which listed that year.

The post-COVID super-cycle (FY22–FY23). Home renovation surged, freight constrained imports, and timber and resin inflation was passed on easily. Century's MDF margin reached 31.7% in FY22 and its company ROACE 30.9% in FY23; Greenpanel's operating margin was 26% and ROCE 29%. Century's consolidated PAT was ₹313 Cr in FY22 and ₹384 Cr in FY23 (screener).

The glut and the wall (FY24–FY27). Everyone announced capacity at once. Century built Badvel MDF and laminates and Chennai particle board; Greenpanel added 231,000 CBM in Andhra Pradesh (March 2025); Greenply built Vadodara; Merino built Halol; Greenlam built Naidupeta; Action Tesa and Elixrr started new lines. MDF realisations fell and margins collapsed. At the same time the QCOs took effect in February 2025, shutting most imports and accelerating branded plywood. Then the West Asia war from 28 February 2026 doubled methanol prices and closed Gulf export lanes, and the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026. Greenpanel's share price fell from a 52-week high of ₹309 to ₹131.

The lesson of the five cycles is consistent. Plywood brands came through each with share gains, because every supply shock, whether a forest ban, a tax or a standard, hurt the small mills more than the brands. MDF makers have had two booms and two busts in fifteen years, and each bust came from capacity built in the preceding boom. FY22's 30% margins were not a new normal; they were a peak in a cycle that the industry then extended by building. The question for FY28 is whether this cycle's recovery can arrive before the next wave of lines does.

In plain terms

Every few years something cuts the supply of cheap panels, prices rise, and everyone builds new factories, which then cut prices again. Plywood brands have come out ahead every time because the shocks hit small mills hardest. MDF makers have ridden the roller-coaster twice and are near the bottom of the second dip.

Reading a panel maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every panel company's profit is volume times price minus timber, glue and machines. These are the numbers that tell you which of those moved, and the traps hidden in each.

Panel companies report a confusing mix of units: cubic metres for plywood at Century but square metres at Greenply and Greenpanel, cubic metres for MDF and particle board everywhere, and sheets, of varying thickness and size, for laminates. They report margins with and without foreign-exchange losses, before and after depreciation, at the segment and the company level. This section sets out the industry's profit equation, then works through each native KPI: what it is, how it is calculated, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, what good looks like, how the listed companies compare, and the trap in its definition. It ends with an illustrative anatomy of an MDF line, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected in one place, and a ten-minute routine for reading a quarterly result.

A panel company's operating profit is the sum, panel by panel, of volume times realisation less variable cost, minus fixed costs and the cost of the brand and channel. Some of these terms management controls; others it does not.

Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE, round numbers). A branded plywood business sells 100,000 CBM at ₹56,000 per CBM: revenue of ₹560 Cr. Variable costs of ₹40,000 per CBM (timber, veneer, resin, power, freight and contract-mill charges) total ₹400 Cr, leaving a contribution of ₹160 Cr. Fixed costs of ₹45 Cr and brand and channel spend of ₹24 Cr (4.3% of revenue) leave EBITDA of ₹91 Cr, a 16.3% margin. Now raise the price 5% with costs unchanged: revenue rises ₹28 Cr and all of it falls to EBITDA, which rises 31% to ₹119 Cr. Raise volume 5% instead, at the same price: contribution rises ₹8 Cr and EBITDA 9%. That asymmetry, price is worth three to four times volume, is why pricing power is the most valuable thing a panel brand has, and why a failed MDF price rise hurts so much.

In plain terms

A panel company makes money four ways: selling more, charging more, paying less for wood and glue, or spreading its fixed costs over more output. Charging more is by far the most powerful, because nearly all of a price rise is profit. Brands can do it; board makers in a glut cannot.

Volume: cubic metres, square metres and sheets

What it is. The quantity sold in the period. Century reports plywood, MDF and particle board in cubic metres (CBM) and laminates in sheets; Greenply and Greenpanel report plywood in million square metres (MSM); Stylam reports million sheets; Rushil reports laminate sheets in 1 mm equivalents. Formula: units sold, compared year on year. Worked example: Century sold 136,995 CBM of plywood in Q1 FY27 against 106,398 a year earlier, growth of 28.8%. What moves it: housing fit-out, renovation, share gain from unorganised mills, dealer stocking ahead of price rises (a factor in Century's Q1), capacity and outsourcing availability, and for MDF the shift to factory furniture. Benchmark: for branded plywood, good is at least twice industry growth (12% or more), average 6–10%, weak below 5%. For MDF, good is above industry "mid-teens" growth without price cuts.

Gross margin and the chemical cost share

What it is. Revenue less the cost of materials (and, in some definitions, power, labour and direct manufacturing costs), as a share of revenue. For panels the most useful sub-measure is the share of chemicals in raw materials, because it tells you how exposed a business is to the next resin shock. Formula: (revenue − material cost) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Century's MDF gross margin fell from 29.2% in Q1 FY26 to 21.3% in Q1 FY27, a drop of about 8 points, almost exactly the illustrative chemical-shock arithmetic in section 08 (a 40% resin rise on resin worth 15–20% of cost). What moves it: timber and chemical prices, product mix, and price increases. Benchmark: compare only within a company; definitions vary too much across companies. Greenlam says chemicals are about a third of its raw materials and up to about 35% of laminate input content; for MDF and particle board, resin is 20–35% of the cost of goods (indicative).

How the companies compare. Reported Q1 FY27 gross margins were Greenlam 52.9%, Greenpanel 52.7% (helped by cheap timber inventory carried from Q4), Century about 47% (on a broker's definition) and Rushil 39.0%. These are not comparable: Greenpanel and Greenlam subtract materials only, Century's deck subtracts manufacturing and direct expenses too (32.5% gross profit for FY26 on that basis), and brokers re-compute. The definition trap: inventory accounting. A company that bought timber or chemicals before a price rise reports a higher gross margin for a quarter or two and then gives it back. Greenpanel's Q1 FY27 margin was explicitly helped by low-cost Q4 inventory.

Timber cost

What it is. The price of plantation logs and veneer per tonne or per CBM, and the share of timber in raw materials. Formula: timber purchases ÷ tonnes or CBM bought; or timber cost per CBM of panel produced. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): at about ₹10,000 a tonne for peelable timber and roughly 1.5–2.0 tonnes of green log per CBM of plywood core after peeling losses, core timber costs about ₹15,000–20,000 per CBM of plywood, a quarter to a third of Century's ₹56,000 realisation; face veneer, resin and pressing come on top. Working-capital days

What it is. The cash conversion cycle: days of debtors plus days of raw-material and finished-goods inventory less days of payables. Formula (Century's method): debtors ÷ revenue × 365 + RM inventory ÷ revenue × 365 + FG inventory ÷ revenue × 365 − payables ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: Century's FY26 cycle was 41.8 debtor days + 43.4 RM days + 30.2 FG days − 34.1 payable days = 81.2 days; in Q1 FY27 it fell to 68.9. What moves it: timber stocking (mills buy ahead of the monsoon), imported veneer and chemical stocks, new-plant inventory build, dealer credit and price rises. Benchmark: good below 60 days, average 60–90, weak above 90 (company method).

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital used to earn it. What it is. Borrowings, with or without cash, leases and buyers' credit. Formula: gross debt = bank and term borrowings; total debt = gross + lease liabilities + buyers' credit; net debt = debt − cash and liquid investments. By June 2026 the deck figures were ₹1,644 Cr gross and ₹1,562 Cr net. Benchmark: net debt to EBITDA good below 1×, average 1–2.5×, weak above 3×.

Brand and channel spend

What it is. Advertising, carpenter and dealer schemes, influencer and loyalty programmes, as a share of revenue. Formula: brand-building and scheme costs ÷ revenue; rarely disclosed in full, because schemes are often netted off revenue. Worked example: Century said on its Q1 FY27 call that it spends 4–4.5% of revenue on "advertising, schemes, everything": about ₹215–245 Cr a year on FY26 revenue. What moves it: campaign launches (Club Prime with Aamir Khan, HDF with Rahul Dravid), new market entry, competitive intensity and the need to defend price rises. Benchmark: no peer discloses a comparable figure; the test is outcome: volume growth above industry with stable or rising realisation. The definition trap: schemes booked as a deduction from revenue lower realisation, while advertising sits in other expenses. A company can "cut costs" by moving spend from one to the other.

Export share and export realisation

What it is. The share of volume or revenue sold abroad, and the price received there relative to the domestic price. Formula: export volume ÷ total volume; export realisation ÷ domestic realisation. Worked example: Stylam sold 3.17 mn sheets in Q1 FY27, 2.07 mn of them abroad, an export share of 65% by volume, down from 68% in Q4 FY26; management says the domestic business is only about a quarter of revenue. Century's exports were ₹217.6 Cr in FY26 across 34 countries, about 4% of revenue. Greenpanel's MDF exports were 13,060 CBM in Q1 FY26 and zero in Q1 FY27. What moves it: freight rates and container availability, shipping-lane disruptions, the rupee, destination tariffs and product approvals with foreign distributors. Benchmark: for laminate makers, a high export share is good when lanes are open, because export prices and design approvals support margins; for MDF, exports are usually a way to fill idle capacity at a lower price. The definition trap: "exports" can mean FOB sales from India or sales by overseas subsidiaries; Century reports both (standalone FOB exports ₹129.2 Cr; consolidated overseas sales ₹153.6 Cr), and its laminate share of exports is not disclosed. Also, an EPCG-linked export obligation turns lost exports into a balance-sheet liability.

Capex per CBM of capacity

What it is. The cost of building one CBM of annual capacity, the number that decides what return a new plant can earn. Formula: project cost ÷ annual nameplate capacity. Worked example: Century's 60,000 CBM Hoshiarpur plywood greenfield costs ₹180 Cr, about ₹3,000 per CBM; its planned 330,000 CBM UP MDF line ₹800 Cr, about ₹2,400 per CBM. Plywood looks dearer per CBM, but a CBM of plywood sells for about ₹56,000 against ₹29,500 for MDF and needs far less working capital per rupee of margin, so plywood capex earns back much faster. What moves it: machinery origin (European versus cheaper Chinese lines), brownfield versus greenfield (Greenply says its second MDF line costs "much lower" per CBM than its first), land, and currency. The definition trap: compare capex with the revenue and margin it will support, not with tonnes or cubic metres alone, and include working capital and the start-up losses of the first two years.

In plain terms

An MDF line is a machine whose profit depends almost entirely on how full it is and what the market will pay. At 60% and a weak price it loses money; at 90% and a firm price it earns nearly ₹200 Cr of EBITDA. The machine is the same in both cases. That is why board makers' profits swing so violently and why investors pay so little for them in a glut.

Definition traps, collected

  • Plywood units. CBM (Century) and square metres (Greenply, Greenpanel) cannot be compared without a thickness assumption; 1 CBM of 18 mm ply ≈ 55.6 sq m.
  • Laminate sheets. Counts fall when sheets get bigger or thicker; Rushil reports 1 mm equivalents; Stylam's revenue per sheet includes non-laminate products.
  • Three segment margins. Century reports EBITDA, EBITDA ex-forex and segment EBIT ex-forex. Our primer printed EBITDA under an EBIT label.
  • Forex. Century's FY26 EBITDA was ₹660.6 Cr reported, ₹701.7 Cr ex-forex and ₹651 Cr on screener; Greenpanel's ₹80 Cr screener OP compares with ₹132.7 Cr company operating EBITDA ex-forex.
  • Consolidated vs standalone. Century's Badvel plants sit in subsidiary Century Panels, so standalone PAT (₹94.5 Cr in Q1 FY27) exceeds consolidated (₹83.3 Cr). Greenpanel's consolidated screener page is stale; use standalone.
  • Particle-board growth. Century's "+38%" was revenue; volume grew 53.4% while price fell 10.4%; ₹23 Cr of Q2 FY26 trial sales were capitalised.
  • Market share denominators. Century is 9.5–10% of total plywood and 30–32% of organised; Greenpanel's "~21%" is of MDF capacity, its volume share is ~16.5%.
  • Debt. Deck gross ₹1,531 Cr, ICRA total ₹1,764.9 Cr, deck net ₹1,472 Cr (Mar-26). Never call gross "net".
  • Working capital. Screener's COGS-based cycle (131 days) is not the company's revenue-based cycle (81 days).
  • Utilisation denominators. Century MDF was ~76% on ~570,000 CBM in FY26, not the ~82% computed on an older capacity figure; after the Q1 FY27 extension the base is ~627,000 CBM.
  • Trade data. UN Comtrade is calendar-year and in dollars; FIPPI is fiscal-year and in rupees; post-QCO imports hide under HS 4412 "Others".
  • Gross margin. Material-only (Greenpanel, Greenlam) and manufacturing-cost (Century deck) definitions differ by 15–20 points.

How to read a panel company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Segment revenue and volume first. Pull volume and realisation for each panel and split growth into the two. Volume-led in plywood is share gain; realisation-led in laminates is mix; volume-led with falling price in boards is a price war.
  2. Segment EBITDA margins, ex-forex. Plywood above 15% means pricing power is intact; MDF below 10% means the glut is still biting. Note the basis.
  3. Segment EBIT, if given. For MDF and particle board, check how much depreciation eats; a positive EBITDA with negative EBIT is a new line that has not filled.
  4. Utilisation. Compute it yourself from volume and stated capacity. Watch for line extensions and shutdowns that change the base.
  5. Price commentary. Did announced price rises stick? MDF rollbacks are the earliest sign of oversupply; plywood increases that hold are the clearest sign of brand strength.
  6. Raw-material commentary. Timber (North vs South, monsoon), chemicals versus pre-war, and any inventory gain or loss in gross margin.
  7. Forex line. Separate the euro-loan mark-to-market from operations; check where it sits (EBITDA or finance cost).
  8. Subsidiary losses. For Century, compare standalone with consolidated PAT; the gap is mostly Century Panels (Badvel).
  9. Debt and working capital. Net debt versus last quarter, cash-conversion days, and capex spent versus budget. Red flag: net debt up while volumes are up and capex is "small", which points to working capital.
  10. Guidance and capex. Note any new plant, land acquisition or partner (Century's logistics), and any change to the FY31 aspiration or the debt target of ≤1× EBITDA. Red flag: new board capacity announced while existing lines run below 75%.
In plain terms

The quickest read of a panel result is three numbers: plywood margin (is the brand still pricing?), MDF margin and utilisation (is the glut easing?), and net debt (is the build being paid for?). Everything else explains why those three moved.

In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading this industry come from definitions, not from the numbers themselves: square metres versus cubic metres, EBITDA versus EBIT, gross debt versus net, a company's working-capital days versus a database's. Fix the basis first and the story usually becomes obvious.

The global lens: Austrian families, Chilean forests and Brazilian eucalyptus

The world's panel leaders are many times Century's size, own their fibre or their decor chain, and earn steadier margins. None manufactures at scale in India.

Wood panels are a global industry dominated by a handful of family-owned or conglomerate-owned European and Latin American companies, plus China's vast and fragmented plywood and MDF sector. Comparing Indian makers with them shows what scale, integration and fibre ownership are worth, and why India's industry looks the way it does. The figures below come from company releases where noted; Kronospan and Sonae Arauco publish no fetchable group accounts, so they are described without figures.

EGGER (Austria, family-owned) is the closest model for what Century is trying to become: an integrated maker of particle board, MDF, OSB, decorative surfaces and laminate flooring, selling boards and decor through the same channel. In its year to 30 April 2026 it reported revenue of €4.26 bn (+3.4%), EBITDA of €541.8 m, a 12.7% margin (13.1% a year earlier), an equity ratio of 41.6%, investment of €450.1 m and about 12,000 employees. Its margin, in a mature European market with large, full plants, is roughly where Century's company margin is today; the difference is that EGGER's is steady through the cycle.

Arauco (Chile, part of Empresas Copec) makes pulp, sawn timber, MDF, particle board and plywood across Chile, Brazil, the United States, Canada and Mexico. Its 2025 revenue was US$6,084 m (−7.1%) and adjusted EBITDA US$1,331 m (−33.9%), a 21.9% margin in a weak pulp year. Its wood-products division had Q4 2025 revenue of US$739 m, about US$3.0 bn a year, and adjusted EBITDA of US$122 m, a 16.4% margin. Arauco's edge is its own forests: low-cost fibre that Indian makers, buying plantation timber from farmers, do not have.

Dexco (Brazil, formerly Duratex) is the closest listed emerging-market analogue to a diversified Indian panel company. On a single search-summary source, which makes these figures indicative, its 2025 recurring net revenue was R$8,249 m (+0.2%), pro-forma adjusted recurring EBITDA about R$2.47 bn, and its wood division posted record EBITDA of about R$1.55 bn with a Q4 margin of 28.8%. Brazilian MDF and particle board, made from the company's own eucalyptus, earn margins Indian MDF makers saw only at the FY22 peak.

Kronospan (Austrian-owned, private) is one of the world's largest makers of particle board, MDF, OSB and laminate flooring, with dozens of plants across Europe and beyond, and individual sites far larger than any Indian line. Sonae Arauco is the European and South African joint venture of Portugal's Sonae Indústria and Arauco, making particle board, MDF, OSB and decorative products. Both are relevant as benchmarks of scale and integration; neither manufactures in India. EGGER and Kronospan sell in India through importers and brand representatives, and the QCOs now require BIS licences for their boards too.

Technology and disruption: boards, surfaces and cheaper machines

The slow shift from plywood to boards is the biggest change; new surfaces, plastics and Chinese presses are the others.

The panel industry changes slowly, but it changes in ways that move value between business models. Six shifts matter for Indian makers over the next five years. Some favour the brands, some favour the board makers, and one, cheaper machinery, favours new entrants and makes the glut worse.

1. Boards replacing plywood in factory furniture. India's mix is about 54% plywood against roughly 20% globally (an indicative industry estimate). Every point of share that moves from plywood to MDF or particle board shifts value from an asset-light, brand-led business to a capital-heavy, utilisation-led one. Century hedges by owning both; Greenply has added an MDF line; Greenpanel is betting entirely on boards. The shift is good for long-run MDF demand and for companies with both channels, and bad for companies whose only moat is plywood brand.

2. Value-added boards. Pre-laminated MDF and particle board, thin HDF, moisture-resistant and fire-retardant grades and CNC-ready boards sell for 30–55% more per CBM than plain board. Greenply's pre-laminated MDF revenue rose 61% year on year in Q1 FY27; Rushil aims for value-added MDF to reach 60% of MDF revenue and half of volume in FY27; Century sold about 19% of its MDF as pre-lam in Q1 FY27. In a glut, value-added mix is the main defence of realisation.

Cheaper Chinese machinery is the disruption to fear, because it makes the next MDF plant easier to build.

Bull vs bear: formalisation dividend or board-capex trap?

The bull case rests on plywood share gains and an MDF recovery by FY28. The bear case rests on the glut lasting and the next round of capex arriving before it ends.

The investment debate on Indian panels, and on Century in particular, comes down to two timelines. One is the pace at which branded plywood takes share from small mills, which the QCOs have accelerated. The other is how long the MDF and particle-board glut lasts, which depends on demand growth, new lines and whether anyone stops building. A bull needs the first to stay fast and the second to end around FY28. A bear needs only one of them to disappoint.

The bull case

  • Formalisation has a long runway. About 70% of plywood is still unorganised. The QCOs cut finished-panel imports by 36–50% and force small mills to certify, and Century targets 15% of the total market in five years, from 9.5–10%, implying 12–15% volume growth a year in a market growing 5–7%.
  • Plywood pricing power is proven. In the 2026 resin shock, branded plywood passed on about 7% and kept it, and Century's plywood margin hit a record 16.9% in Q1 FY27. That segment earns about 41% pre-tax on its capital.
  • MDF demand is strong. Industry volumes grew about 15% in FY26 and "mid-teens" in Q1 FY27. If new lines slip and Greenpanel's pause holds, utilisation can pass 80% by FY28, the level at which MDF historically earned 20%+ margins.
  • Century's new assets have spare capacity. Management says the current asset base supports ₹7,500–8,000 Cr of revenue against ₹5,397 Cr in FY26; filling it needs little capex and lifts returns. ICRA projects leverage falling to 1.6–1.7× by March 2028.
  • Hidden value. The Kolkata logistics business (₹159 Cr revenue, 16.3% EBITDA, with a partner under evaluation) and a particle-board line heading from 3.7% towards a targeted ~15% margin.

The bear case

  • The glut gets worse before it gets better. About 0.85–0.9 mn CBM of new MDF lines (Action Tesa Vizag, Elixrr, Greenply line 2) arrive by mid-2027 on a base of about 4.2 mn CBM, while demand is about 3.0 mn. Price discipline already failed once in 2026.
  • Returns stay diluted. MDF and particle board hold 54% of Century's segment capital and earn 13% of its segment EBIT. The FY31 ₹12,000 Cr aspiration needs ~₹2,500 Cr more capex, and the UP and Odisha plans include ₹1,300 Cr of new board lines.
  • Inputs cannot be hedged. Methanol rose about 180% year on year in May 2026; the rupee is near record lows, raising veneer and chemical costs; a second Gulf flare-up would hit MDF margins again.
  • Housing demand may soften. Top-city sales were flat to down in Q3 CY2026, unsold stock rose 12%, and the repo rate rose to 5.50% in October 2026. Fit-out demand lags, so the hit would show in FY28–29.
  • Valuation leaves little cushion. Century trades at 47.4× trailing earnings and about 22× EBITDA on a 13% ROACE, and foreign investors hold only 3.6%, so liquidity is thin and moves can be sharp. A leak in the QCO wall (Nepal, misclassification, new foreign licences) would undercut the formalisation story.

Weighing them, the plywood half of the argument is stronger than the board half. The evidence for formalisation, record margins, share gains, price increases that hold, is in the reported numbers. The evidence for an MDF recovery is mostly in managements' expectations, while the capacity calendar points the other way for at least another year. Century is exposed to both, which is why its results swing between record plywood quarters and disappointing MDF ones, and why the market's verdict on it will turn on the first quarter in which MDF margins and utilisation rise together.

In plain terms

The good news is already visible: branded plywood is winning and can raise prices. The bad news is also visible: too many MDF plants and more on the way. What is not yet known is which arrives first, the end of the glut or the next round of factory building. That is the bet an investor in this industry is making.

Beyond the dates, three slower signals deserve attention. First, the US Section 301 litigation: a ruling against the 10% tariff would help laminate exporters at the margin. Second, the policy stance on QCOs: any move to dilute or exempt the wood-panel orders, or a surge in BIS licences to large Vietnamese or Chinese mills, would weaken the import wall. Third, interest rates: the RBI's October hike to 5.50% raises home-loan costs, and panel demand feels that with a lag of one to two years.

For Century specifically, the most informative single number in the 4 November results will be the MDF segment: margin, volume and utilisation together. A margin back above 10% with volume growth would say the Badvel extension and the price reset are working. A margin still in single digits would say the glut is winning. The second-most informative will be plywood: a margin at or above 16% after a quarter without pre-price-rise stocking would confirm that the brand, not the timing of dealer orders, drove Q1.

Over the next two weeks five of the six panel companies report. Watch whether MDF prices and margins have stopped falling, whether plywood margins held without the April stocking boost, and whether new MDF plants are opening on time. Those three answers will say more about the next year than any forecast.
