00

The one-page view: two businesses, one family's ambition

The NSE 750's two education stocks share a customer, not a model. One sells exam coaching to Indian teenagers; the other delivers Indian, Nepali and Nigerian students to British universities.

Listed Indian education is two companies that happen to share a label. PhysicsWallah (PW) is a test-prep company: it sells online courses for India's entrance and government exams at about ₹4,100 a year, and runs 366 physical and hybrid classrooms where a full-year JEE or NEET seat costs roughly ₹36,000–40,000. Crizac is a business-to-business recruitment platform: about 4,000 active student-counselling agents lodge applications through it, it routes them to around 200 paying universities, and when a student enrols the university pays Crizac a commission, two-thirds of which Crizac passes on to the agent. One is paid by parents in rupees; the other is paid by British universities in pounds.

What ties them together is the customer behind the money: the aspiring Indian family that spends heavily on a child's exam or degree because a seat at a medical college, an IIT or a British master's programme is a ticket to a different life. Both companies sell access to a gate. Neither controls the gate. The National Testing Agency (NTA) decides when NEET and JEE happen and whether a paper has to be re-set; the UK Home Office decides how many students may come, whether they can bring a spouse, and how long they may stay to work afterwards. In 2026 both gatekeepers moved against the two companies at once. NEET-UG was cancelled for a paper leak and re-held seven weeks late, and PW's paid users grew just 2.4% in Q1 FY27 (Jun-26). UK study-visa applications fell 19% in the year to September 2026, and Crizac's Q1 FY27 revenue fell 4%, with management guiding FY27 flat.

Put the two together and the "sector" numbers look odd. Combined FY26 revenue was ₹4,942 crore (+32.3%), operating profit (EBITDA excluding other income) ₹656 crore (13.3%), and reported profit after tax ₹195 crore. Crizac is 21% of the revenue but 112% of the profit, because PW lost ₹24 crore. PW is 94% of the ₹38,719 crore combined market value. The combined price-to-earnings ratio, 199 times, is meaningless: it averages a loss-maker priced as a long-runway platform with a profitable intermediary priced as if its best year is behind it. This report therefore shows the two companies side by side throughout, and treats aggregates as descriptive only.

The six forces that define the sub-sector

  • Test-prep demand is a demographic and exam-policy machine. About 22.8 lakh students registered for NEET-UG 2026, 15.4 lakh sat JEE Main and 14.9 lakh registered for CUET-UG, chasing roughly 1.1–1.2 lakh MBBS seats and 17,000–18,000 IIT seats. The pool is flat; what moves revenue is who wins the student and when the exam is held (Sections 03 and 04).
  • Online-to-offline is PW's defining bet. Offline is 45% of PW's revenue from 9% of its paying students, but the network lost ₹106 crore before rent accounting (pre-Ind AS) in Q1 FY27 and the roll-out has been throttled to "opportunistic" (Sections 08 and 09).
  • The edtech bust reset the competitive field. Byju's is insolvent, Unacademy sold for about 6% of its peak value, Allen's profit fell 70% and Aakash makes under 1% EBITDA. The survivors are the cheapest credible brands (Section 06).
  • Regulation of coaching is tightening on conduct, not banning the trade. The Ministry of Education's 2024 guidelines, Rajasthan's Act 16 of 2025 and the dummy-school crackdown raise the cost of running a centre (Section 11).
  • Visa politics is Crizac's demand curve. The UK takes 98.7% of its revenue; dependants are banned, the Graduate Route shrinks to 18 months from 1 January 2027, sponsor-compliance thresholds tightened in June 2026, and a £925-a-student levy arrives in 2028-29. Crizac is gaining share of a shrinking market (Sections 12 and 13).
  • Cash, treasury and price. Both companies collect cash before they deliver, both hold net cash, and the market prices them at opposite ends of the range: PW at about 9× sales against global peers at 0.3–1.8×, Crizac at about 11× earnings with a 5.6% dividend yield (Section 19).
In plain terms

Both companies make money from the same thing: Indian families paying to get a child through a gate that someone else controls. PhysicsWallah sells the coaching for India's entrance exams; Crizac sells the route into a British university. In 2026 an exam leak and a tougher British visa regime hit both in the same quarter. The market has decided that PW's growth can outrun such shocks and that Crizac's cannot, which is why one trades at nine times its sales and the other at eleven times its profit.

Three numbers frame the rest of this report. First, PW's offline network: if mature centres reach the 12–15% pre-Ind AS margin management describes, PW's earnings can rise several-fold on today's revenue; if the network stays loss-making, the online business's profits are spent subsidising classrooms. Second, Crizac's revenue per enrolment: it rose from about ₹3.6 lakh in FY24 to ₹4.2 lakh in FY26 and then fell about 17% year-on-year in Q1 FY27 as students shifted to cheaper universities, which is why 15% more enrolments produced 4% less revenue. Third, other income: the two companies earned ₹208 crore of treasury and other income in FY26 (screener basis), more than their combined reported profit. Until the operating businesses produce more, interest on cash is doing much of the work.

01

Two value chains: a ₹4,000 app and a £3,700 commission

PW earns from the student and spends on teachers and rent; Crizac earns from the university and spends on agents. Who pays decides almost everything else about the two businesses.

The test-prep value chain begins on YouTube. Alakh Pandey started the "Physics Wallah" channel in 2016, and free lectures remain the top of PW's funnel: a student watches free videos, downloads the app (78 million+ downloads by September 2025), and pays for a "batch", a structured year-long course with live and recorded lectures, notes, tests and doubt-solving, for a specific exam. The average online paying user paid ₹4,104 in FY26 (company "ACPU", average collection per user). From there PW sells upgrades: premium "Infinity" and "Infinity Pro" batches that bundle books and mentoring (attach rates of 18% and 9% in Q1 FY27), test series, printed material, and finally a classroom seat at one of its offline centres, where the average offline student paid ₹36,625 in FY26.

Value is captured at Step 2 (online, ~27% segment margin by management estimate for FY26) and Step 4 (offline, where rent and local faculty make margins depend on filling seats).

The economics of each step differ sharply. Content is made once and streamed to "mega classrooms capable of hosting over 200,000 students live in a single session", so the marginal cost of one more online student is close to zero; the costs that matter are faculty salaries, studios, cloud and marketing. Offline is a different business: a centre needs a leased building, local teachers who solve doubts face to face, counsellors and administrators, and it only makes money once enough seats are filled. That is why PW's online segment earned an estimated 27% margin in FY26 while the offline segment still lost money in Q1 FY27.

The recruitment value chain runs in the opposite direction. A student in Hyderabad, Kathmandu or Lagos walks into a local agent's office (Crizac calls them "counselling partners"; it had 17,400+ registered and about 4,000 active in Q1 FY27). The agent lodges the application on Crizac's platform. Crizac screens documents, checks for fraud and visa risk, routes the file to one of its 450+ partner universities, chases the offer and the Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies (CAS), and supports the visa. When the student enrols and passes the university's census date, the university pays Crizac a commission, "generally a percentage of the tuition fee of the first year" according to its prospectus. Crizac then pays the agent its share: 67.5% of revenue in FY26.

Who pays changes everything else. PW's customer pays upfront for a year of teaching, so PW holds the family's money before it delivers the service; at Mar-26 it carried about ₹1,209 crore of "other current liabilities", mostly fees received in advance. Crizac's customer, the university, pays after enrolment on 90–180-day terms, and Crizac pays its agents after it is paid, so receivables (₹239 crore at Mar-26) and agent payables (₹234 crore) roughly cancel. Both businesses need very little working capital and neither borrows. But only PW enjoys the classic "float" of a subscription business, which is one reason its free cash flow (₹494 crore in FY26) far exceeded its reported loss.

02

Size of the prize: a ₹1.4 lakh crore coaching habit and an 18-lakh diaspora of students

Both markets are large and fragmented. The listed companies are small slices of them, which is why share shifts matter more than market growth.

Indian test preparation is one of the largest private education markets in the world, but it is measured only in dollars. Redseer sized it at about $14.8 billion in FY26 (all entrance, government-job and professional exams), growing 12–15% a year to $23–26 billion by FY30. At ₹94.9 to the dollar that is an indicative ₹1.40 lakh crore today and ₹2.2–2.5 lakh crore by FY30. The conversion flatters the rupee value, because the rupee was weaker in 2026 than in most of the years the estimate covers. Undergraduate entrance preparation (JEE, NEET, CUET and similar) is about $3.8 billion of the total, an indicative ₹36,000 crore, and JEE plus NEET make up about 70% of it. Other research houses use different scopes, and a narrow "online test-prep" estimate of a few hundred million dollars is not comparable. No rupee series exists; every rupee figure for the market in this report is a conversion and is labelled indicative.

The organised players are small against these totals. PW (₹3,900 crore in FY26), Allen Career Institute (₹3,067 crore of operating revenue in FY25) and Aakash Educational Services (about ₹2,040 crore in FY26) add up to roughly ₹9,000 crore, about 6–7% of the indicative market. The rest is local tutors, small institutes, school-linked coaching and exam-specific specialists. That fragmentation is the bull case for scale players: share can be taken from thousands of small operators rather than from one another. It is also a warning, because the long tail is cheap, local and often outside the 18% GST that organised players charge.

The study-abroad market is measured in heads rather than rupees. India's Ministry of External Affairs told Parliament in 2025 that about 18 lakh Indian students were abroad: 12.54 lakh in higher education and 6.28 lakh at school level. The higher-education figure fell for the first time in four years, from 13.35 lakh in 2024. Canada still hosts the most Indian students, followed by the United States, the United Kingdom and Australia, but the flows into all four are shrinking because each tightened student visas between 2024 and 2026. Globally, Frost & Sullivan (cited in Crizac's investor presentation) puts internationally mobile students at about 6.4 million in 2020 rising to about 8 million by 2030, with spending on overseas education growing about 7% a year. Those global numbers are slow-moving; what matters for Crizac is the UK corridor, which Section 12 measures directly.

03

The exam machine: a flat pool of aspirants, a scarce supply of seats

Test-prep demand is set by how many students sit each exam and how few seats sit behind it. The pool has stopped growing; the money is in which coaching they buy, where and at what price.

India's entrance exams turn seat scarcity into coaching demand. In 2026, 22.79 lakh students registered for NEET-UG, the single gate to India's roughly 1.1–1.2 lakh MBBS seats (approximate), about one seat for every twenty aspirants. JEE Main, the engineering gate, drew 13.55 lakh registrations for the first session of Paper 1 (BE/BTech) and 15.38 lakh unique candidates sat across the two sessions; the top scorers then compete in JEE Advanced for roughly 17,000–18,000 IIT seats (approximate). CUET-UG, the gate to Delhi University and the central universities, registered 14.93 lakh, up 10% from 13.55 lakh. UPSC's civil-services preliminary exam had about 8.19 lakh registrations, 5.49 lakh sitting and 13,343 qualifying for the next stage. These ratios, not the size of India's youth cohort, are what make families pay for coaching.

The striking fact is that the pool has stopped growing. NEET registrations were about 24.1 lakh in 2024 (approximate), 22.76 lakh in 2025 and 22.79 lakh in 2026. JEE is roughly flat. Only CUET is clearly rising, because more central and state universities now use it for admission. India's fertility rate is around replacement (about 2.0 in the latest national family health survey), so the school-age cohort will shrink slowly from the 2030s. For a test-prep company this means growth has to come from three places other than more aspirants: taking share from offline incumbents and the unorganised tail; moving students up the price ladder (from free YouTube to a ₹4,000 batch to a ₹36,000 classroom); and opening new categories where coaching is under-penetrated.

PW's own enrolment data show the third lever at work. JEE and NEET made up 54% of PW's online enrolments in FY23, 31% in FY25 and 29% in FY26. Government-job exams went from nothing in FY23 to 9.3 lakh enrolments in FY25; Foundation courses for Classes 6–10 nearly tripled from 1.9 lakh to 5.3 lakh; board-exam and CUET preparation, civil services, commerce and CA all scaled from small bases. In FY26 state-board and vernacular courses reached 3.93 lakh enrolments and were EBITDA-positive in their first year, and premium batches crossed 9.6 lakh enrolments. Management groups its categories into three profit tiers: high profitability (NEET, JEE, CA, commerce, MBA, boards and CUET); a path to higher profitability (GATE, Foundation, UPSC, government exams, state boards); and growth investments that still lose money (NEET-PG, skills, Curious Jr. for young children, vernacular).

Where is the headroom? PW's own framing of its markets is the best available guide, though it is a company estimate and should be read as indicative. It claims about 20% of the 3 million-plus JEE and 5 million-plus NEET aspirants, but only about 2% of 9 million civil-services aspirants, 0.7% of 55 million government-exam aspirants, 0.4% of 100 million Foundation-age students and 0.3% of 58 million state-board students. The map below plots that framing. The top-left cluster (JEE, NEET) is where PW is already deep; the bottom-right (government exams, Foundation, state boards) is where the pools are vast but each student pays less, competition from free content is fiercest, and PW's profit tier is lower.

Affordability is the other half of demand. A PW online batch costs a few thousand rupees a year; a PW Vidyapeeth classroom year for JEE or NEET costs about ₹35,000–40,000; a premium Kota or Delhi programme can cost ₹1.5–2.5 lakh before hostel fees (approximate). The 2026 environment pushes families down that ladder: the rupee at about ₹95 to the dollar, the RBI's repo-rate rise to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and job anxiety all squeeze household budgets. That is good for the cheapest credible brand and bad for premium offline chains. It is also why PW's mix shift towards lower-priced categories has not hurt its online ACPU, which rose 11.4% to ₹4,104 in FY26: premium upsells offset cheaper entry products.

In plain terms

The number of students sitting India's big exams is no longer growing, but the number of seats behind them is still tiny, so families keep paying for coaching. A coaching company grows by winning students from rivals, by selling them a more expensive course, and by moving into exams where few students are coached today. PhysicsWallah has done all three, which is why JEE and NEET are now less than a third of its online enrolments.

04

Seven weeks in May: how one cancelled exam moved a listed company's quarter

The NTA's calendar is PW's demand curve. In 2026 a paper leak pushed NEET-UG back by seven weeks, and PhysicsWallah's busiest enrolment window moved with it.

Indian test prep runs on an academic clock, not a fiscal one. JEE Main is held in two sessions, in January and April; JEE Advanced follows in May or June; NEET-UG is normally held on the first Sunday of May; CUET-UG runs from May into June; and results arrive from late spring into July. Students who have just finished one cycle decide what to buy for the next one between March and September. PW says about 79% of its FY26 collections came in that March–September decision window. Because course fees are recognised as revenue over the life of the course, collections lead revenue: Q1 (April–June) is cash-heavy and revenue-light, Q2 and Q3 carry the profit and loss account, and Q4 is soft. Management's own advice is that "full year is a better basis to look into PW, not any single quarter".

Two broader lessons follow. First, the NTA's credibility is itself a demand driver. Every leak raises the value families place on "result-oriented" coaching and on mock tests that mirror the real paper, and a switch to computer-based NEET from 2027, which the government calls likely but has not finalised, would raise demand for online test series that simulate the format. In plain terms

When the government cancelled the May medical-entrance exam and held it again in late June, families put off buying the next year's coaching until the results were out. In plain terms

The edtech bust and the incumbents: how the cheapest brand outlasted the best-funded

Byju's, Unacademy and Vedantu raised billions and lost; Allen and Aakash are stalling; PW, the low-price survivor, is the only one that listed.

Indian education technology had a boom and a bust within five years. Venture funding peaked at about $4.0–4.1 billion in 2021 (PwC and Tracxn), when COVID pushed classrooms online and Byju's was valued at about $22 billion. It fell to $1.8 billion in 2022 and about $321 million in 2023; H1 2024 saw $164 million and H1 2025 $120 million. The model that failed was premium, sales-led and debt-funded: expensive tablet-and-content bundles sold by call centres and home visits, financed by investor money and, in Byju's case, a $1.2 billion term loan. When funding dried up and students returned to classrooms, customer-acquisition costs could no longer be covered.

Why did PW survive when better-funded rivals did not? Price, audience and cost discipline. It charged a fraction of Byju's and Allen's prices; its YouTube audience kept marketing below 10% of revenue; and it was profitable enough at the time of its 2022 $100 million round (WestBridge and GSV, at a $1.1 billion valuation) that it did not need to buy growth. The companies that tried to buy growth with investors' money, selling expensive courses through sales teams, ran out of money when funding stopped. Five business models, five margin structures

Online mass coaching, offline classrooms, school-plus-coaching, the B2B recruitment platform and the B2C counsellor make money in very different ways. The archetype explains the margin.

Education services split into five archetypes, and the two listed companies sit in three of them. The first is online mass test prep (PW online, Adda247, Testbook, Unacademy): content made once, streamed to millions, priced at ₹3,000–5,000 a year. Marginal costs are tiny, fees arrive upfront, and the swing factor is customer-acquisition cost. PW's online segment earned an estimated 27% margin in FY26 (a management figure; the first auditor-reviewed segment report, for Q1 FY27, shows online pre-Ind AS EBITDA of ₹67 crore, or 12%, in a seasonally light quarter).

The second is offline and hybrid coaching (Allen, Aakash, PW Vidyapeeth and Pathshala, FIITJEE): ₹35,000 to ₹2.5 lakh per student a year, with rent and faculty largely fixed per centre. Each new centre follows a J-curve: losses while seats fill, then a steady-state margin. PW says mature centres reach 12–15% pre-Ind AS margins; Aakash's whole network made 0.75% EBITDA in FY26; PW's offline segment lost 22% of revenue in Q1 FY27. The third is the integrated school-plus-coaching model of Narayana, Sri Chaitanya and the "dummy schools" that partner Kota institutes: students are enrolled in a school for the board certificate but spend their days in coaching. It earns two fees from one student, but it is the model most exposed to the CBSE's attendance rules.

The fourth is the B2B recruitment platform (Crizac, ApplyBoard in Canada, Adventus): it does not own the student relationship; it aggregates agents who do, and sells their flow to universities. Revenue per enrolled student is high (about ₹4.2 lakh at Crizac in FY26), but two-thirds passes to the agent. Capital needs are minimal and working capital roughly neutral. The fifth is B2C counselling and testing (IDP Education, Leverage Edu, Crizac's Global Tree acquisition): branch networks that own the student, earn the full commission and, in IDP's case, co-own the IELTS English test. It keeps more per student but carries branch costs and suffers most when volumes fall, as IDP's 27% drop in placements in FY26 showed.

The archetype also explains PW's blended margin. Its 9.5% operating margin in FY26 (screener basis) is a roughly 27%-margin online business carrying a still-loss-making offline network and several investment categories. Offline is 45% of PW's revenue from 9% of its paying students. It is where the money is per student and, so far, where the losses are.

PW opened its first physical centre in 2022. Four years later it runs 366 of them in 178 Indian cities and six in the UAE, and offline produced ₹1,774 crore of FY26 revenue, up 31%. The logic is simple: an online student pays about ₹4,100 a year; an offline student pays about ₹36,600. Many families, especially for JEE and NEET in Classes 11 and 12, want a classroom, a timetable, a teacher who checks attendance and a peer group. Online gave PW the brand and the audience; offline lets it charge nine times as much to the families who want more than a screen. The risk is equally simple. A centre has fixed rent and fixed local faculty, and it loses money until enough seats are filled.

Who regulates coaching: conduct rules, not a ban

India regulates how coaching is sold and run, not whether it may exist. The rules raise the cost of operating a centre and penalise the integrated school-plus-coaching model most.

Coaching sits in a regulatory gap. It is not "education" under the law in the sense that schools and colleges are, so it is not covered by the bodies that regulate them, and it is taxed at 18% GST like any other service (several Authorities for Advance Ruling have confirmed that a coaching centre is not an "educational institution" for the GST exemption). Education is on the Concurrent List of the Constitution, so both the Centre and the states can legislate, and enforcement falls to the states. For years the only rules were old and lightly enforced state acts (Goa and Karnataka in 2001, Uttar Pradesh in 2002, Bihar in 2010, Manipur in 2017). Student suicides in Kota, fire deaths in a Delhi basement coaching centre and complaints about fees and false advertising changed that between 2024 and 2025.

The Ministry of Education's Guidelines for Registration and Regulation of Coaching Centres, 2024, issued in January 2024, set the template. A "coaching centre" is any operation with more than 50 students, and each branch must register separately. Centres may not enrol students below 16 years of age or before the secondary-school examination. Tutors must be at least graduates. Advertisements may not promise ranks or marks or make misleading claims. Fees, course details and refund policies must be published, and a student who leaves mid-course is entitled to a pro-rata refund within 10 days, including hostel and mess fees. Centres need about one square metre per student, may not hold classes for more than five hours a day or at very early or late hours, and must provide mental-health counselling. Penalties are ₹25,000 for a first offence, ₹1 lakh for a second and cancellation of registration for a third. The guidelines are a model; states must adopt them in their own law.

Rajasthan, home of Kota and Sikar, went further. The Rajasthan Coaching Centres (Control and Regulation) Act, 2025 (Act No. 16 of 2025) was introduced in March 2025, sent to a Select Committee after criticism, reported back on 1 September 2025 and received the Governor's assent on 23 September 2025. It sets up a state authority and district committees, requires registration of centres with 50 or more students, imposes penalties of ₹2 lakh for a first offence and ₹5 lakh for a second, with cancellation for a third, requires pro-rata refunds within 10 days, allows fees to be paid in at least four instalments and requires receipts. The under-16 age bar in the central guidelines was dropped from the Rajasthan law. Notified rules had not been found as of October 2026. Assam approved its own Coaching Institutes Act in March 2025, and Kota's district administration added "Kota Cares" measures for 2025-26 (no hostel security deposits, fixed maintenance fees, anti-suicide devices on fans, CCTV and biometric attendance).

Term two: why Crizac is gaining share

Crizac's enrolments rose from 17,842 in FY24 to 21,710 in FY25 and 24,697 in FY26, and its share of all UK study visas rose from 3.5% to 6.0%. The regulatory engine behind that share gain is the Home Office's Basic Compliance Assessment (BCA), the annual test that every licensed student sponsor must pass. From 1 June 2026 the thresholds tightened: a university's visa refusal rate must be below 5% (previously 10%), at least 95% of students issued a CAS must enrol (previously 90%), and at least 85% must complete their course, rising to 90% from 1 June 2027. A public red-amber-green rating is set by the worst of the three. A university with a 4–5% refusal rate is already "amber", one bad cohort from action plans and recruitment caps. Universities have responded by concentrating recruitment on agents and platforms with low refusal and high enrolment rates, and by enforcing the Agent Quality Framework. A scaled, auditable aggregator like Crizac benefits; a small agent sending files directly does not.

Beyond the UK: four destinations closing at once, and the IDP mirror

Canada, the United States and Australia have all restricted student visas since 2024. The world's largest listed recruiter shows what that does to an intermediary's numbers.

Crizac's stated plan is to cut the UK to below 60% of revenue within three years by growing in Canada, Ireland, Germany, the Netherlands, New Zealand and elsewhere. The difficulty is that every large English-speaking destination tightened at the same time. Canada, which hosted about 4.27 lakh Indian students in 2025, introduced a study-permit cap in January 2024. From January 2026, 49,000 master's and doctoral permits at public institutions are exempt from the provincial attestation requirement. The Student Direct Stream, which fast-tracked Indian applicants, ended in November 2024, and the diplomatic rupture between India and Canada in 2023–24 compounded the fall. Study-permit holders fell from more than a million in January 2024 to about 725,000 by September 2025.

Booms, busts and shocks: forty years in one timeline

From Kota's classrooms to the edtech bubble, the listing window of 2025 and the double policy shock of 2026, the sub-sector has moved in waves.

The sub-sector's history falls into five phases. The Kota era (1985–2015) built the offline model: Allen (founded 1988), Bansal Classes, Resonance and Vibrant turned a Rajasthan industrial town into a city of about two lakh coaching students, while Aakash (Delhi, 1988) and FIITJEE (1992) built national chains. Coaching was cash, local and founder-run, and its marketing currency was toppers' photographs. The edtech boom (2015–2022) put coaching on phones. Byju's launched its app in 2015, Unacademy and Vedantu followed, and COVID moved all classes online in 2020–21. Funding peaked at about $4 billion in 2021; Byju's bought Aakash for about $950 million. Alakh Pandey's YouTube channel became PhysicsWallah the company in 2020 and a unicorn in June 2022 with a $100 million round from WestBridge and GSV, notable at the time for being profitable without heavy marketing. Meanwhile, Crizac, incorporated in Kolkata in 2011, built its agent network and its UK university relationships, adding Ireland, Canada, New Zealand and Australia between 2014 and 2018.

The bust (2022–2024) followed the funding drought. Byju's defaulted on its $1.2 billion term loan, lost its auditor and board members and entered insolvency in 2024; Unacademy and Vedantu cut thousands of jobs. PW opened its first offline centre in 2022 and went on an acquisition spree, and in FY24 it reported a ₹1,131 crore loss. In the same year Crizac acquired Crizac UK from the promoter group (consolidated from 20 November 2023) and paid a ₹74.9 crore break fee to a promoter-owned UK company. The listing window (2025) then opened. Crizac's July 2025 IPO was a 100% offer for sale by the promoters, subscribed 59.8 times; PW's November 2025 IPO raised ₹3,100 crore of new money and was subscribed 1.92 times. The policy shocks (2024–2026) arrived in parallel: the coaching guidelines, the UK dependants ban, the Canada caps, the 2024 NEET leak, US visa vetting, the UK White Paper and levy, the NEET-UG 2026 cancellation and, on the competitive side, upGrad's purchase of Unacademy.

Where does the cycle sit in October 2026? In Indian test prep it is a consolidation phase: the affordable online leader is near break-even and slowing its offline build, the offline incumbents are stalling, and the distressed edtech assets are being bought cheaply by the survivors (upGrad, Manipal). In plain terms

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). A coaching company with 50 lakh online payers at ₹4,000 each collects ₹2,000 crore online. If it also has 5 lakh classroom students at ₹36,000 each, offline brings ₹1,800 crore, and books and other lines ₹200 crore, for ₹4,000 crore in all. If teachers and staff cost 45% (₹1,800 crore), direct costs 20% (₹800 crore), marketing 9% (₹360 crore), other costs 13% (₹520 crore) and rent 6% (₹240 crore), pre-Ind AS EBITDA is ₹280 crore, 7%. Raise online ACPU by 10% and, because content costs do not rise with price, about ₹200 crore drops to EBITDA, which would take the margin to about 11%. Fill 10% more classroom seats without new centres and the effect is similar. That leverage on price and utilisation is why the KPIs below matter more than the headline revenue growth. For a recruiter, 25,000 enrolments at ₹4.2 lakh produce ₹1,050 crore; pay agents two-thirds and spend ₹60 crore on the platform, and EBITDA is about ₹290 crore.

In plain terms

PhysicsWallah's sales are the number of students who pay multiplied by what each pays, online and in classrooms. Crizac's sales are the number of students it places multiplied by what each one earns it. Everything in this section is a more precise way of measuring one of those few numbers, and of spotting when a company is describing them in a flattering way.

1. Paid users (online payers plus offline students)

What it is. PW's headline volume measure: unique online payers (counted by unique mobile number) plus offline student enrolments in the period. Formula: total paid users = online unique transacting users + offline enrolments. FY26: 4.87 M + 0.47 M = 5.34 M (+20%). Worked example: a student who buys a JEE batch and a test series counts once as a user but twice as an enrolment; a student who buys an online batch and later joins a Vidyapeeth may count in both the online and offline numbers. What drives it: the exam pools, PW's free YouTube reach (4.03 M daily active app users in Q1 FY27), price, new categories and the exam calendar. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): above 20% a year is strong for a scaled online coaching business; 10–20% average; below 10% weak. How the members compare: PW grew 20% in FY26 and 2.4% in Q1 FY27 (NEET shock); Crizac has no equivalent. The trap: the figure is cumulative within the period, so a quarter's users (2.49 M in Q1 FY27) can never be compared with a year's (5.34 M), and half-year figures (3.62 M in H1 FY26) are a third basis. Enrolments (4.80 M in FY25) exceed users (4.13 M) because one user buys several courses.

2. Online ACPU (average collection per user)

What it is. The average amount collected from each online paying user in the period. Formula: online collections ÷ online unique paying users. FY26: ₹4,104 (+11.4% from ₹3,683). Worked example: 1,000 students each buying a ₹3,500 batch, of whom 200 also buy a ₹2,000 Infinity upgrade and 100 a ₹1,000 test series, give collections of ₹40 lakh and an ACPU of ₹4,000. Drivers: list prices (PW has kept entry prices low), premium attach rates (Infinity 18%, Infinity Pro 9% in Q1 FY27), multi-course buying, category mix (JEE and NEET batches cost more than government-exam batches) and discounts. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): rising more than 5% a year is good; flat is average; falling is weak. Comparison: PW ₹4,104 FY26 and ₹4,311 in Q1 FY27 (+9.7%); Crizac n/a. The trap: ACPU is collections, not revenue. Revenue per online user, on reported revenue, was about ₹4,012 in FY26 (₹1,954 crore ÷ 4.87 M). Management also says users and ACPU will "trade off": cheaper categories add users and lower the average.

3. Offline students and revenue per offline student (ARPU)

What it is. The number of students enrolled in PW's centres and the average revenue each generates. Formula: offline ARPU = offline revenue ÷ offline enrolments in the period. FY26: 4.67 lakh students; ARPU ₹36,625 (−9%). Worked example: a centre with 1,000 full-year JEE students at ₹40,000 and 500 three-month crash-course students at ₹10,000 has revenue of ₹4.5 crore and an ARPU of ₹30,000, even though no fee was cut. Drivers: course length (full-year versus short courses), format (Vidyapeeth fees exceed Pathshala fees), category (CA, UPSC and government exams are cheaper per student), hostels and test series sold alongside. 4. Centres and revenue per centre

What it is. The size of the physical network and how much each centre earns on average. Formula: revenue per centre = offline revenue ÷ average centres open in the period. FY26: ₹1,774 crore ÷ ~276 average centres ≈ ₹6.4 crore (derived). FY25: ₹1,352 crore ÷ ~162 ≈ ₹8.3 crore. Worked example: a network that doubles its centres in a year will see revenue per centre fall even if every existing centre grows, because new centres open part-way through the year with few students. Drivers: centre age, format mix (Pathshalas average about 500 students, Vidyapeeths about 1,850), location and fee level. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for a full JEE/NEET classroom centre, ₹8 crore or more at maturity is good, ₹4–8 crore average, below ₹4 crore weak. Comparison: PW ₹6.4 crore FY26 (all formats); 366 centres at June 2026; Crizac n/a (its "capacity" is agents and university relationships, not seats). The trap: "centres" mixes four formats with very different economics, and the mix shifts: Pathshalas fell from 84 to 70 between March and June 2026 because the best were converted into Vidyapeeths, not because they closed.

5. Centre ramp, seat utilisation and cohort payback

What it is. How fast a new centre fills its seats and turns profitable. Formula: seat utilisation = students ÷ seats on an annual basis, counting multiple batches per seat; cohort margin = EBITDA of all centres opened in a given year ÷ their revenue. Worked example: a 1,000-seat centre running two batches a day can hold 2,000 students; at 1,800 students its utilisation is 1.8. In Section 09's ILLUSTRATIVE model a centre breaks even at about 1,450 students. Drivers: local brand, faculty quality, results in the first year, competition and the size of the town. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): utilisation above 2.0 is good, 1.6–2.0 average, below 1.5 weak; break-even within 24 months good, 24–36 average, longer weak. Comparison: PW utilisation rose from 1.77 to 1.99 and improves 5–7% a year; centres opened in 2022–24 were EBITDA-positive in FY26; the 2025 cohort made single-digit losses; hyperlocal centres "typically reach profitability within 24 months". Crizac n/a. The trap: cohort statements are management's, not audited, and do not include allocated corporate costs; a network can have every old cohort profitable and still lose money if it keeps opening new ones.

In plain terms

The number of centres tells you how big the network is; students per centre and fee per student tell you whether it makes money. A new centre loses money for its first year or two. A company opening many centres at once will look unprofitable even if every older centre is doing well, so the useful question is always how the centres opened two or three years ago are performing.

6. EBITDA definitions: pre-Ind AS, reported, adjusted

What it is. The operating-profit measure the company chooses to emphasise. Formulae (PW, FY26): pre-Ind AS EBITDA (₹300 crore, 7.7%) + lease payments (₹252 crore) + other income (₹232 crore) = adjusted EBITDA (₹784 crore, 20.1%); − ESOP (₹171 crore) − exceptional and one-time items (₹64 crore) = EBITDA (₹549 crore, 14.1%). Screener operating profit: ₹370 crore (9.5%). Worked example: a centre paying ₹80 lakh a year in rent shows that ₹80 lakh as a cost under pre-Ind AS; under Ind AS 116 it disappears from EBITDA and reappears as depreciation and interest, so reported EBITDA is ₹80 lakh higher with no change in cash. Drivers: lease intensity (offline networks), treasury size (other income) and stock-option grants. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for a hybrid test-prep company, pre-Ind AS EBITDA above 15% is good, 5–15% average, below 5% weak. Comparison: PW pre-Ind AS 7.7% FY26; Crizac 27.1% (company EBITDA; leases negligible, so its definitions barely differ). The trap: PW's headline EBITDA includes treasury income and its adjusted EBITDA adds back ESOP. Use pre-Ind AS for cash economics, screener operating profit for peer comparison, and quote the ₹549 crore only with "including ₹232 crore of other income".

7. Deferred revenue and upfront cash collection

What it is. Fees collected but not yet earned, held as a liability until the course is delivered. Formula: collections in the period − revenue recognised = change in deferred revenue. Worked example: a student pays ₹36,000 in April for a course from April to March. At June-end PW has collected ₹36,000 but recognised only ₹9,000; ₹27,000 sits as deferred revenue, and PW can earn interest on it. Drivers: growth in collections, course length, the timing of the enrolment season (about 79% of FY26 collections came between March and September) and refund rules. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): operating cash flow consistently above EBITDA is good for a prepaid model; roughly equal is average; below suggests refunds, instalments or receivables. Comparison: PW's other current liabilities, mostly fees received in advance, were about ₹1,209 crore at March 2026; operating cash flow was ₹833 crore in FY26 against a ₹24 crore loss; the Q4-to-Q1 cash build is about ₹600 crore. Crizac has no deferred revenue: it recognises commissions at a point in time on enrolment. The trap: deferred revenue makes cash look better than profit in a growing year and worse in a shrinking one, and state refund rules (pro-rata within 10 days) can erode it.

8. Marketing as a share of revenue (the CAC proxy)

What it is. Spending to win students, the best public proxy for customer-acquisition cost. Formula: marketing expense ÷ revenue; or marketing ÷ new paying users for a rough CAC. Worked example: ₹354 crore of FY26 marketing over about 5.34 M paid users is about ₹660 per user, roughly 16% of an online payer's ₹4,104 annual collection (ILLUSTRATIVE, since not all users are new). Drivers: the free YouTube funnel, results advertising (newspaper and hoardings in results season), entry into new categories and new geographies. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): below 8% of revenue is good; 8–12% average; above 15% marks a sales-led, Byju's-style model. Comparison: PW 9.6% FY25, 9.1% FY26 (guide 8–9%), 12.1% in the seasonal Q1 FY27; Crizac's marketing is small (partner programmes), within its ~5% opex. The trap: quarterly marketing ratios are distorted by seasonality, and some acquisition cost hides in other lines (free content production, discounts netted off revenue, counsellor salaries in employee cost).

9. Faculty cost and faculty attrition

What it is. The cost and stability of the teaching workforce. Formulae: employee cost ÷ revenue; faculty attrition = faculty leavers in the year ÷ average faculty. Worked example: a centre with 40 faculty losing 10 in a year has 25% attrition; if the leavers are its four best-known JEE teachers, enrolments may fall far more than 25% the next year. Drivers: pay increments (every April), poaching by rivals, teachers leaving to build their own YouTube following, AI substitution for content and doubt-solving roles. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): employee cost below 40% of revenue is good for test prep, 40–50% average, above 55% weak; faculty attrition below 15% good, 15–25% average, above 30% weak. Comparison: PW employee cost 48.9% FY26 (screener, incl. ESOP), 46.4% ex-ESOP in Q1 FY27; attrition 25–27% overall and 15–17% for lecture faculty; 7,268 faculty at June 2026. Crizac employee cost about 3% of revenue. The trap: "faculty" includes consultants (1,144 of 6,837 at March 2026), and screener's employee cost includes ESOP while the company's excludes it; the CFO's "lower by 2.6%" in Q1 FY27 referred to the ratio (percentage points), not the rupee cost, which rose 18%.

In plain terms

Teachers are both PhysicsWallah's product and its biggest cost. When pay rises faster than fees, margins fall; when a famous teacher leaves, students may follow. Marketing is the other cost to watch: PW spends about 9% of sales on it because YouTube brings students in free, and a rise towards the 15% that sales-driven rivals spent would be a warning sign.

10. Enrolments placed (Crizac)

What it is. The number of students who enrolled at a partner institution and generated a commission. Formula: enrolments = unique applicants × conversion rate. Q1 FY27: 47,488 unique applicants × ~10% = 4,751 enrolments. Worked example: 3.95 lakh applications in FY26 from perhaps 2.5 lakh unique students (each applies to several universities) converting at about 10% would give about 25,000 enrolments, close to the 24,697 reported (ILLUSTRATIVE; FY26 unique applicants are not disclosed). Drivers: UK visa volumes, Crizac's share, active agents (5,389 in FY26; 4,032 in Q1 FY27), deferrals between intakes and geopolitical shocks such as the 2026 West Asia flight disruption. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): growth above 15% good, 5–15% average, below 5% weak; conversion above 12% good, 8–12% average. Comparison: Crizac 17,842 (FY24) → 21,710 (FY25) → 24,697 (FY26, +13.8%); Q1 FY27 +15%. PW n/a. The trap: applications (3.95 lakh, +43% in FY26) grow much faster than enrolments and are not revenue; unique applicants are a third measure; and the deck counts 23,576 UK enrolments for the market-share calculation against 24,697 in total.

11. Revenue per enrolment

What it is. Gross commission earned per enrolled student, the "price" term in Crizac's equation. Formula: revenue ÷ enrolments. FY26: ₹1,042 crore ÷ 24,697 ≈ ₹4.22 lakh (derived). Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a student on a £20,000 first-year programme earning a 17% commission generates £3,400, about ₹4.25 lakh at ₹125 to the pound; if the same student chooses a £15,000 programme, the commission falls to £2,550 (about ₹3.2 lakh) with no change in Crizac's terms. Drivers: university mix (fee levels and commission rates differ), course level (master's versus undergraduate), the pound–rupee rate and any change in contract terms. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): rising is good, flat average, falling more than 10% a year weak. Comparison: Crizac ₹3.56 lakh (FY24) → ₹3.91 lakh (FY25) → ₹4.22 lakh (FY26); Q1 FY27 ₹4.23 lakh against about ₹5.07 lakh in Q1 FY26 (−17%). PW n/a. The trap: it is derived, not disclosed; quarterly figures swing with which universities have intakes in that quarter; and the commission rate itself (an estimated 15–20% of first-year tuition) is not disclosed, so a fall can be mix, FX or a rate cut and only management's word distinguishes them.

15. Treasury and other income

What it is. Cash, deposits and liquid investments, and the income they earn. Formula: net cash = treasury − borrowings (excluding lease liabilities); other income ÷ pre-Ind AS EBITDA measures how much of profit is interest. Worked example: ₹5,600 crore at 6.5% earns about ₹364 crore a year, more than PW's FY26 pre-Ind AS EBITDA of ₹300 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers: IPO proceeds, free cash flow, dividends, acquisitions and deposit rates (the RBI raised its repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which should lift deposit yields at the margin). Benchmark (INDICATIVE): other income below 20% of operating EBITDA is a clean earnings mix; 20–50% average; above 50% means treasury is carrying the profit line. Comparison: PW treasury ₹5,027 crore (March 2026) and ₹5,601 crore (June 2026, including ₹2,041 crore of unspent IPO money); other income ₹232 crore FY26 = 77% of pre-Ind AS EBITDA. 16. Anatomy of one typical unit: a single Crizac enrolment

Section 09 built an ILLUSTRATIVE classroom centre for PW. The equivalent unit for Crizac is one enrolled student, and FY26's reported totals allow a consistent breakdown. Each enrolment generated about ₹4.22 lakh of gross commission (about £3,380 at ₹125 to the pound, an INDICATIVE conversion). Crizac paid about ₹2.85 lakh of that to the agent and kept about ₹1.37 lakh. The costs of running the platform, offices in 11 countries and technology came to about ₹23,000 per student, leaving EBITDA of about ₹1.14 lakh per student, and after other income, depreciation and tax about ₹89,000 of profit. Multiply back by 24,697 enrolments and the numbers reproduce FY26 EBITDA (₹282 crore) and PAT (₹219 crore). The unit shows why volume matters so much: once the network exists, each additional student brings in about ₹1.1 lakh of EBITDA at a cost of very little extra effort.

Set the two units side by side and the contrast in risk becomes clear. PW's illustrative classroom centre needs about ₹2.5 crore of fit-out and roughly 1,450 students before it earns anything, and its biggest costs (teachers, rent) are committed before the first student walks in; its reward is a high-margin, cash-generative asset once full, with negative working capital because fees arrive upfront. Crizac's unit needs almost no capital: its biggest cost, the agent's share, is paid only when a commission is earned, so a bad year shrinks revenue and costs together and margins hold up, as FY26's 27.1% shows. The price of that flexibility is control. PW sets its own fees and decides where to open; Crizac's volume is set by a foreign government, its price by a university's fee level and contract, and its cash timing by the university's payment cycle. In valuation terms, PW's unit carries operating leverage, so profit can rise much faster than revenue once utilisation improves, while Crizac's unit carries policy leverage, so revenue can fall sharply without warning but profit falls only in proportion. A reader comparing the two KPI sets should therefore look for different warning signs: for PW, anything that keeps classrooms half-empty or forces fees down; for Crizac, anything that cuts the number of visas or the commission per student.

Definition traps, collected

  • Five EBITDAs at PW. Pre-Ind AS (₹300 crore), screener operating profit (₹370 crore), headline EBITDA (₹549 crore, including ₹232 crore of other income), adjusted EBITDA (₹784 crore, before ESOP) and, from Q1 FY27, segment EBITDA. Always name the basis.
  • Paid users are cumulative within a period. Quarterly, half-yearly and annual figures cannot be compared; enrolments exceed users because one user buys several courses.
  • ACPU is collections; ARPU is revenue. PW's online measure counts cash collected, its offline measure counts revenue recognised, and quarterly ARPU (₹12,633) is not an annual price.
  • PW's channel split was restated in Q1 FY27. FY26 online, offline and others (₹1,954, ₹1,774 and ₹172 crore) are on the old basis; for Q1 YoY use the restated Q1 FY26 (₹412, ₹428 and ₹7 crore), because books and hostel sales attached to a course moved into online and offline.
  • Centres mix four formats. Pathshala counts fell because centres were upgraded to Vidyapeeth, not closed.
  • Crizac's revenue is gross. About two-thirds is passed to agents; compare its margins with platforms on net revenue, not with IDP's gross.
  • Crizac's FY24 has two bases. ₹530 crore restated (UK subsidiary consolidated from 20 November 2023) versus ₹635 crore proforma; FY24 also contains a ₹74.9 crore break fee to a promoter-group company and ₹128.6 crore of treasury gains.
  • Applications, unique applicants and enrolments are three funnels. Only enrolments earn revenue.
  • UK market windows differ. Home Office "year ending" statistics and Crizac's April–March fiscal years cover different months; do not mix them in one comparison.
  • Screener's PW balance sheet is misleading. Its ₹1,876 crore of "borrowings" is ₹1,042 crore of leases plus ₹824 crore of converted preference capital plus ₹10 crore of real debt; true owners' equity is ₹4,520 crore (annual report), not ₹3,697 crore, so P/B is about 8.0×, not 9.7×.
  • Other income differs by source. PW's own FY26 other income is ₹232 crore; screener shows ₹179 crore on a different classification.
  • Reported PAT versus owners' PAT. Screener's PAT includes minorities (PW Q4 FY26: −₹69 crore) while the company reports the owners' share (−₹74.9 crore).
In plain terms

Many of the numbers these companies publish are correct but easy to misread: a quarter's users compared with a year's, cash collected compared with revenue earned, profit that includes interest on bank deposits, or a balance-sheet line that looks like debt but is not. Before comparing any figure across periods or companies, check what exactly it counts.

How to read an education company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the calendar. Note which quarter it is and what happened in it: exam dates and results for PW, intake timing (September, January, May) for Crizac. Q1 is PW's loss-making, cash-heavy quarter and Crizac's trough; Q4 is Crizac's peak. Compare with the same quarter last year, never the previous quarter.
  2. Revenue and its split. PW: online, offline and others, on the restated basis. Crizac: revenue and constant-currency growth. Red flag: revenue growth far above collections or enrolment growth.
  3. Volume. PW paid users (online and offline separately) and centres; Crizac enrolments and unique applicants. Red flag: volume falling while the company says demand is "deferred" for more than one quarter.
  4. Price. PW online ACPU and offline ARPU (quarter on quarter-last-year only); Crizac revenue per enrolment (derive it). Red flag: a sustained fall in Crizac's revenue per enrolment, which signals mix shift towards cheaper universities or a commission squeeze.
  5. The right profit line. PW: pre-Ind AS EBITDA total and by segment, especially offline. Crizac: company EBITDA margin against the 25–27% guide. Red flag: headline EBITDA rising while pre-Ind AS EBITDA does not.
  6. Other income. Compare it with operating EBITDA. Red flag for PW: other income larger than pre-Ind AS EBITDA for a second year.
  7. Costs that move. PW employee cost ex-ESOP and marketing as % of revenue against the same quarter last year; ESOP charge. Crizac agent payout ratio. Red flag: Crizac payouts above 70% or PW marketing above 12% for a full year.
  8. Cash. PW treasury (and IPO money unspent, from the monitoring agency's report); Crizac free cash, receivables and cash conversion. Red flag: Crizac receivable days above 100 or a rise in overdue university balances.
  9. Exceptional items and acquisitions. PW's Sarrthi, Xylem and Utkarsh buy-outs and fair-value charges; Crizac's bolt-on acquisitions and related-party payments. Red flag: repeated "one-time" items.
  10. Guidance and the next gate. PW's +30% revenue, +100% EBITDA and offline "near profitability"; Crizac's flat revenue and 25–27% margin; then the next NTA calendar and UK visa release. Red flag: guidance reaffirmed without the quarter's run-rate supporting it.
In plain terms

Read the calendar first, then volume and price separately, then the profit line that strips out rent accounting and interest income, then cash. For PhysicsWallah the question each quarter is whether classroom losses are shrinking; for Crizac it is whether more students placed is still turning into more money earned.

AI and disruption: cheaper teaching, or no need for teachers?

Generative AI cuts the cost of content, counselling and doubt-solving, and it destroyed one US education company's model. PW is betting it can be the one selling the AI tutor.

Artificial intelligence cuts both ways for test prep. On the cost side it is already working for PW. The company has built its own models (Aryabhata, a 20-billion-parameter model with about 3 billion active parameters, plus ConceptGuru and Awaaz), uses AI voice bots that handle more than 6,000 counselling calls a day at about ₹2.7 a minute, roughly 75% cheaper than human counsellors, and says 91% of its new software code is written with AI assistance. AI now drafts content, answers routine doubts and grades practice tests, which is changing the roles of content and doubt-solving faculty. The result shows up in the ratios: employee cost excluding ESOP fell from 49.0% to 46.4% of revenue in Q1 FY27, even though it rose 18% in rupees.

On the revenue side the threat is real. Chegg, the US homework-help subscription company, lost 39% of its revenue in 2025 as students substituted free chatbots for paid answers, and its trailing revenue is now down about 48% year-on-year; its market value is about $75 million. The lesson is that a business selling information that an AI model can produce for free has no moat. PW's defence is that coaching sells discipline, accountability, a timetable, a peer group and results, not just answers: a live batch with a teacher, tests that mimic the exam, a doubt-solving session and, for many families, a classroom. Its offline centres are partly an AI hedge for that reason. YouTube plays a similar double role: free lectures are PW's cheapest acquisition channel, but they also cap what students will pay for online content, which is one reason online ACPU is about ₹4,100.

PW is positioning AI as a product line, not only a cost saver. For student recruitment the AI question is disintermediation. Universities could recruit directly through digital marketing and AI application screening, and business-to-consumer platforms could bypass agents. Bull vs bear: the case for and against listed Indian education

The strongest arguments on each side, for the sub-sector as a whole and for each member.

The bull case

  • Seat scarcity is permanent. About 22.8 lakh NEET aspirants chase 1.1–1.2 lakh MBBS seats, and 15 lakh JEE candidates chase 17,000–18,000 IIT seats; families will keep paying for coaching whatever the size of the pool.
  • The affordable leader wins share. PW grew 35% in FY26 while Allen and Aakash stalled and the funded edtechs failed; its free audience keeps acquisition costs near 9% of revenue.
  • Offline has proven cohorts. Centres opened in 2022–24 were EBITDA-positive in FY26 and new hyperlocal centres break even within about 24 months; slowing openings lets the network mature.
  • Huge headroom outside JEE and NEET. Government exams, Foundation and state boards are pools of 55–100 million students where PW's share is under 1%.
  • Crizac is a share taker with a regulatory tailwind. Tougher UK compliance rules push universities to reliable recruiters; its share of UK study visas doubled to 6% in two years.
  • Cash everywhere. About ₹6,160 crore of combined net cash; PW's free cash flow far exceeds its profit; Crizac pays a 5.6% dividend yield.

The bear case

  • The gatekeepers decide volume. A cancelled NEET cut PW's paid-user growth to 2.4% in a quarter; UK study-visa applications fell 19% in a year and Crizac guides flat.
  • Offline may never earn its keep. The network lost 22% of revenue before rent accounting in Q1 FY27, needs a ~₹200 crore improvement to reach "near profitability", and ARPU fell 9% in FY26.
  • Treasury flatters PW. Other income was 77% of pre-Ind AS EBITDA in FY26; a doubling of headline EBITDA can happen largely through interest on IPO money.
  • Valuation leaves PW little support. About 9× sales and 83× operating profit against global peers at 0.3–1.8× sales.
  • Crizac's concentration is extreme and rising. 98.7% UK, 68.7% from ten universities, unprovided receivables, and counterparties of which 43% forecast deficits.
  • Governance and supply. Crizac's pre-IPO related-party transfers and 100% offer for sale; PW's disputed ₹192 crore tax demand and coming lock-in expiries; thin free floats at both.

The two members' bull and bear cases are not symmetrical. PW's outcome depends mostly on execution: whether it can fill and price its classrooms, keep teachers, and turn AI into a product. Those are decisions inside the company, and the guidance, cohort data and monitoring reports give investors a quarterly way to check. Crizac's outcome depends mostly on policy: how many students Britain admits, whether universities can afford to keep paying commissions, and whether diversification ever moves the UK below 90% of revenue. Those are decisions outside the company, and the best quarterly checks are government statistics rather than company reports.

Three developments would strengthen the bull case. For PW: an offline segment that reports positive pre-Ind AS EBITDA for a full quarter outside Q1, and pre-Ind AS EBITDA (not headline EBITDA) doubling in FY27. For Crizac: UK applications stabilising in the monthly data while revenue per enrolment recovers. Three would strengthen the bear case: PW reopening a fast centre roll-out before the existing network is profitable; another NTA disruption in 2027; and a UK university among Crizac's top ten delaying payments or cutting its agent commission. Neither company's price leaves much room to ignore the bear case: PW's because so much growth is already assumed, Crizac's because the market has already decided its best year is behind it, and any further policy shock would confirm that view.

The sub-sector view, then, is a split one. The structural demand is not in doubt: Indian families will keep spending heavily to get children through exams and into foreign universities. What is in doubt is how much of that spending reaches listed shareholders. In test prep, more of it now flows through an affordable, cash-rich, AI-equipped leader than ever before, but the leader has yet to show that its classrooms make money. In study abroad, a highly profitable intermediary is taking share of a corridor that governments are narrowing, and its customers, British universities, are themselves under strain.

In plain terms

The optimistic view is that Indian families will always pay to get their children through exams and abroad, and that both listed companies are the strongest in their fields. The pessimistic view is that governments control how many students get through each gate, that PhysicsWallah's classrooms still lose money and that Crizac depends almost entirely on Britain. Which view wins depends on PW's execution and on British politics.
