Indian housing finance companies: one licence, three businesses, banks set the price

A complete guide to the ten housing lenders in the NSE 750: why a salaried-prime mortgage lender, a small-town affordable lender and a state-owned urban-infrastructure financier share one label but almost nothing else; how banks' repo-linked loans set the price every one of them must live with; what the 2025 rate cuts did to their margins and what the October 2026 hike undoes; where small-ticket credit is starting to crack; and why the market values them anywhere from 0.69 to 2.90 times book.

00

The one-page view: three businesses wearing one licence

"Housing finance company" is a regulatory label. Behind it sit prime mortgage lenders, affordable lenders to the self-employed, and one government infrastructure financier, each earning similar returns in opposite ways.

India's home-loan market is ₹37.2 lakh crore of individual housing loans (IHL) outstanding at September 2025, growing about 9.5% a year, and it is small for the size of the economy: 11.2% of GDP in FY25, against roughly 25–30% in China and 45–60% in the United States and Britain. Banks own most of it. Public-sector banks hold 45% of home loans outstanding, private banks 35%, and housing finance companies (HFCs) only about 18%. HFCs are the specialist second channel. They exist where banks are slow, cautious or uninterested, and they survive only if they can fund themselves cheaply or underwrite borrowers that banks will not touch.

The ten listed members of this sub-sector look alike on a stock screen. They are not. Four are prime lenders to salaried borrowers who could just as easily go to SBI or HDFC Bank: LIC Housing Finance, Bajaj Housing Finance, PNB Housing Finance and Can Fin Homes. They lend at 9–10%, borrow at 7.3–7.7%, keep a spread of about 2–3 percentage points and stay alive on cheap money from a powerful parent. Five are affordable-housing lenders: Aadhar, Aavas, Aptus, Home First and India Shelter. They lend ₹8–12 lakh to shopkeepers, transporters, farmers and small-factory workers in tier-2, -3 and -4 towns, often to build a house on their own plot, at yields of 12.5–17%. The tenth, HUDCO, is a government-owned lender to state governments and urban bodies that the National Housing Bank (NHB) itself says has been a non-banking infrastructure finance company (NBFC-IFC) since August 2024. It sits in this report because the sub-sector map files it here, and because it shows what housing finance looks like when the borrower is a state rather than a family. We treat it throughout as a fourth, distinct archetype and show every total with and without it.

The single most useful idea in this report is that the two big archetypes reach the same return on equity by opposite routes. A prime lender earns a thin return on assets, about 2%, and multiplies it by six or seven turns of borrowed money. An affordable lender earns two to four times that return on assets but runs only 1.6–3 times leverage, because rating agencies, funding markets and its own post-IPO capital pile keep it conservative. Both land at 14–20% ROE. The risks they carry are therefore completely different: the prime lender's enemy is a bank cutting its mortgage rate by 25 basis points; the affordable lender's enemy is a bad year for the informal economy.

Five forces shape everything that follows. First, banks set the price. Since October 2019 banks must link floating retail loans to an external benchmark, almost always the RBI repo rate, so when the repo moves their home-loan rates move within a quarter. HFC borrowing costs follow two to four quarters later. Second, three models, three return engines, as above. Third, the rate cycle hits the archetypes in opposite directions. The 125 basis points of repo cuts between February and December 2025 squeezed prime HFC margins (LIC Housing's net interest margin fell from about 3.1% two years earlier to 2.58% in Q1 FY27) while widening affordable lenders' spreads; the 25 basis point hike to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, the RBI's first since February 2023, reverses the sign. Fourth, underwriting informal income is the affordable lenders' only durable edge, and it is starting to be tested: Aptus's gross NPA has risen from 1.19% to 1.70% in five quarters and CRISIL reports rising 90-day delinquencies in loans below ₹15 lakh. Fifth, ownership decides valuation: Blackstone controls Aadhar, CVC controls Aavas (and lost its CEO, business head, CFO and risk chief in 2026), Carlyle has left PNB Housing and Warburg has left Home First.

The market has already taken a view. Since the July–August primer snapshots, eight of the ten stocks have fallen, several by a fifth or more, and the combined market value is ₹2.29 lakh crore. Valuations now span a fourfold range: LIC Housing trades at 0.69 times book and 5 times earnings even though it earns a 14% ROE; Bajaj Housing trades at 2.90 times book and 24.7 times earnings on a 12% ROE. The market is not paying for this year's return. It is paying for how long a lender can grow without its spread or its credit costs breaking, and LIC's 4% loan growth against Bajaj's 25% explains most of that gap.

In plain terms

A housing finance company borrows money and lends it out as home loans. A prime one does this for salaried people at a thin margin, using lots of borrowed money and a famous parent's credit rating to keep its own borrowing cheap. An affordable one lends to people without payslips at a much fatter margin, using far less borrowed money. Both end up earning similar returns for shareholders. The danger for the first is a bank undercutting its rate; the danger for the second is its borrowers' small businesses having a bad year.

01

How a housing lender earns: borrow cheap, lend long, collect patiently

The product is a 15–30 year loan secured on a home. The profit comes from three places: the price of money, the ability to judge a borrower nobody else will judge, and the discipline to collect every month for two decades.

A housing finance company is a balance-sheet business. It raises money from banks, bond investors, the National Housing Bank and, in a few cases, the public, and lends that money out for 15 to 30 years against a house. Its income is the interest it charges; its biggest cost is the interest it pays. Everything else (staff, branches, technology, bad loans and tax) is deducted from the difference. There is no inventory, no factory and no brand premium in the usual sense. A home loan from LIC Housing and one from HDFC Bank are almost the same product. What differs is the borrower each lender is willing to serve and the price at which it can fund itself.

The products

Home loans finance the purchase of a built or under-construction flat, the construction of a house on the borrower's own plot (the dominant affordable-lender product), extensions and improvements, and balance transfers from another lender. Prime lenders mostly lend at floating rates; many affordable lenders lend at fixed or hybrid rates. Loans against property (LAP), which HFCs report as "non-housing loans", lend to self-employed borrowers against a house or shop for business use. They carry higher yields (15–20% at affordable lenders) and shorter tenors, and they are growing faster: HFC non-housing loans grew 28.1% in FY25 against 14.0% for housing loans, pushing housing's share of HFC books down from 75.8% to 73.6%. CRISIL expects affordable lenders' LAP to grow about 23% a year in FY27–28. Developer and construction finance, plus lease-rental discounting against commercial property, is chunkier, higher-yielding and historically where HFCs have lost money; Bajaj Housing keeps about a third of its book here, PNB Housing ran its own down to almost nothing after FY22. HUDCO alone lends wholesale to state governments, housing boards and urban local bodies, often against a state guarantee or budget support.

Where the value is captured

For a prime lender, value lives almost entirely in the cost of funds and the cost of operations. The product is priced off the bank repo-linked rate, so a prime HFC cannot charge much more than a bank; a 100 basis point funding advantage is roughly half of its entire spread. That is why parentage is the business: LIC, Bajaj Finance, Canara Bank and Punjab National Bank lend their names, their ratings and their distribution. Can Fin Homes runs a cost-to-income ratio of about 19%; Bajaj Housing spends about 20% of its net income on operations. For an affordable lender, value lives in underwriting informal income, which earns a yield premium of 3–7 percentage points over prime, and in collections discipline, which keeps credit costs at 0.1–0.5% of loans. The price is high operating cost, 2.5–4% of assets, because every loan needs a home visit and many need a collector. For HUDCO, value lives in near-sovereign funding at about 6.3% and in a policy pipeline of state projects; it runs a thin spread on a concentrated book with almost no operating cost.

How the distribution networks differ

The second difference shows up in branches. Prime lenders do not need many: their borrowers walk into a builder's site office or are introduced by a direct selling agent (DSA) or the parent's network, and much of the paperwork is standardised. Affordable lenders need density, because their borrowers live in small towns, their income must be verified in person, and the collection team must be able to reach a house within a day. HFCs as a whole had 10,237 branches at March 2025, 37.5% in the North and 37.5% in the South, and only 2.9% in the East, a gap that tells you where the informal-income mortgage model has and has not been built.

Fee income is a small but useful extra: processing fees, insurance commissions, and the gain or excess spread an HFC earns when it sells a pool of loans to a bank (direct assignment) or co-lends with one. For affordable lenders it adds 0.3–1% of AUM a year (indicative). Affordable housing loans count as priority-sector lending for banks, so banks buy them to meet their targets, which gives A-HFCs a recycling channel prime lenders lack.

In plain terms

Every HFC does the same five things: borrow, find a borrower, check that person can pay, collect the instalments for twenty years, and resell or refinance loans to free up money. The prime lenders are good at the first step because a famous parent lowers their borrowing cost. The affordable lenders are good at the third and fourth steps because they have learned how to judge and chase borrowers with no payslip. HUDCO is good at the first step because the government stands behind it.

02

The 11% mortgage market: large in rupees, small for the economy

Home loans outstanding have grown from ₹28 lakh crore to ₹37 lakh crore in two and a half years, yet mortgages are still only about a ninth of GDP. Banks have taken most of the growth.

Three different numbers get called "the housing loan market", and mixing them is the commonest mistake in writing about this sector. The first is individual housing loans (IHL) outstanding at banks, HFCs and regional rural banks: ₹37.18 lakh crore at September 2025, according to the NHB. This is the mortgage market. The second is HFC sector AUM, which includes LAP and developer loans as well as home loans: ₹10.6 lakh crore at March 2026, up about 15%, according to ICRA (NHB's audited figure for HFC loans was ₹9.61 lakh crore at March 2025, excluding HUDCO and Sammaan). The third is a broader "retail mortgage" universe of home loans plus LAP at all non-banks, which ICRA expects to reach ₹20 lakh crore by FY28. This report quotes the first for market size and the second for the HFC sector, and says which every time.

Penetration: the long runway, stated carefully

Home loans were 11.2% of GDP in FY25, up from 8.0% in FY15 (NHB). That is low against China (about 25–30%), Thailand (about 20–25%), Malaysia (about 35% or more), the US (about 45–50%) and the UK (about 60% or more); the peer figures are approximate and drawn from central-bank and BIS data. The gap is the bull case for every lender in this report, but it closes slowly. Ten years of rapid credit growth added about three percentage points of GDP, and Indian household debt overall is only about 41–42% of GDP (RBI Financial Stability Report). Penetration rises through incomes, urbanisation and formal title, not through lenders lending harder.

The HFC sector inside that market

HFC loans have grown faster than the overall mortgage market because HFCs also lend against property and to developers. 03

Who borrows, and why: a shortage at the bottom, growth at the top

India's housing shortage is almost entirely among lower-income households, yet HFC lending is drifting up-market. The gap between where the need is and where the safe loans are explains the whole affordable-lending model.

Demand for home loans comes from five sources, and they pull in different directions. The first is the housing shortage. The last official urban estimate, by the Technical Group on Urban Housing Shortage in 2012, put it at 18.78 million units, about 95% of them for economically weaker and lower-income households; later academic estimates put the urban shortage near 29 million in 2018 (approximate). Public programmes have chipped away at it: by November 2025 the urban housing scheme (PMAY-U and PMAY-U 2.0) had sanctioned 122.06 lakh houses and completed 96.06 lakh, and the rural scheme (PMAY-G) had sanctioned 3.86 crore and completed 2.90 crore. Most of those houses were built with grants, not mortgages.

The second is rising incomes and urbanisation. Urban India was about 36% of the population around 2021 and should reach about 40% by 2030 (UN estimates, approximate), and household formation runs ahead of population growth as families nuclearise. Each year more households cross the income level at which an EMI is affordable. The third is self-construction and the formalisation of informal income: about 45% of affordable-HFC lending is for self-construction or resale (CRISIL), and GST filings, UPI payment trails, Udyam registration and the Account Aggregator framework make a shopkeeper's income easier to verify than it was a decade ago. The fourth is tax incentives, and here the direction has turned: the Section 24(b) deduction on home-loan interest (up to ₹2 lakh) and the Section 80C principal deduction are available only under the old tax regime, and the new regime, the default since FY24 and made more attractive in the 2025 Budget, removes the deduction on a self-occupied home. That is a quiet structural negative for prime salaried demand. The fifth is premiumisation: premium and luxury homes rose from 7% of sales in Q1 2019 to 21% in Q1 2024, and the trend continued in 2025 (NHB).

Why would lenders retreat from the segment with the greatest need? Because that is where the stress is. The subsidy that mattered, and the one that does not yet

Government schemes used to be a major demand driver. Under the Credit-Linked Subsidy Scheme (CLSS) of 2015–2022, the NHB credited ₹39,742 crore of interest subsidy to 16.45 lakh lower-income households and ₹9,717 crore to 4.62 lakh middle-income households by September 2025. The subsidy was credited upfront as a present value, which cut the borrower's loan and EMI at once; it was a powerful tailwind for the affordable lenders in 2017–22. Its successor, the Interest Subsidy Scheme under PMAY-U 2.0 (from 1 September 2024), pays 4% on the first ₹8 lakh of a loan over 12 years, capped at ₹1.80 lakh and credited in five yearly instalments. By September 2025 it had sanctioned only ₹45.56 crore and released ₹40 crore to 10,439 beneficiaries through 51 lenders.

In plain terms

The families who most need a house are the riskiest to lend to, and lenders have quietly been backing away from the smallest loans while chasing bigger ones. The government subsidy that once made small loans attractive has been replaced by a scheme that almost nobody is using. So the affordable lenders' growth is coming from slightly better-off borrowers and from loans against property, not from the bottom of the pyramid.

Near-term risks to demand are mostly about the cost of money and the health of small businesses: the 7 October 2026 repo hike and the possibility of a second in December; consumer inflation of 5.5% and wholesale inflation of 11.4% in September 2026; tighter credit filters as West Asia-driven inflation feeds through (CRISIL); and microfinance stress spilling into the same households that take small home loans.

Where the money comes from: a lender is only as cheap as its funding

HFCs cannot take current or savings deposits. They borrow wholesale, from banks, bond markets and the National Housing Bank, and the rating on their bonds decides how much of every rupee of revenue they keep.

A bank funds its mortgages with current and savings deposits that cost 3–4%. An HFC cannot. It borrows from the same banks it competes with, sells bonds (non-convertible debentures, NCDs) to mutual funds, insurers and provident funds, raises a little commercial paper, takes refinance from the NHB and, if licensed, collects fixed deposits from the public. At March 2025 HFC borrowings totalled ₹8,19,581 crore: 42.2% from banks, 34.1% from debentures, 9.8% NHB refinance, 4.6% commercial paper, 3.1% public deposits, 0.3% foreign borrowing and 5.9% other sources. Off the balance sheet, HFCs also sell loans to banks (direct assignment), securitise pools, co-lend with banks, and raise equity.

The NHB: the cheapest long money for small lenders

The National Housing Bank is the HFC sector's development financier. Cumulative NHB refinance reached ₹4.24 lakh crore by September 2025, about two-thirds of it to HFCs, and outstanding refinance was ₹1,07,913 crore in June 2025. Its schemes include the Affordable Housing Fund refinance (₹5,791 crore in FY25 to 36,300 households), a fixed-rate refinance window (₹2,055 crore in FY25), and a special refinance scheme for small-ticket loans, with or without rooftop solar, that offers a 25 basis point concession. For an affordable lender, NHB money is long-tenor, rupee-denominated and cheaper than a bank loan, and it matches the long life of a mortgage better than three-year bank lines do.

In plain terms

An HFC is a middleman for money. The prime ones pass two-thirds of what they earn straight back to the banks and bondholders who lent to them, and survive by keeping everything else very cheap. Market yields rose by 20–90 basis points from June 2026, ahead of the October hike, so FY27 borrowing costs are heading up; ICRA expects HFC cost of funds to rise in FY27, cushioned by a growing share of higher-yield non-housing loans.

Banks set the price; HFCs choose where not to fight

A repo-linked bank mortgage at 7.5–8% caps what any prime HFC can charge, and a rule against prepayment penalties lets banks take the HFCs' best borrowers whenever they like.

Since 1 October 2019, Indian banks have had to link new floating-rate retail loans to an external benchmark, and nearly all chose the RBI repo rate. That decision, known as EBLR (external benchmark lending rate) pricing, changed the economics of housing finance. A bank home loan is now priced at repo plus a spread of roughly 2.0–2.5 percentage points, and when the repo moves the loan rate moves at the next reset, usually within a quarter. After the 2025 cuts bank home-loan rates started around 7.5% at SBI and around 7.9% at HDFC Bank (early-2026 comparison, secondary source). Prime HFCs price off their own prime lending rate, which they set, but their yields of about 9.1–9.6% cannot drift far above a bank's without losing the borrower.

The HDFC merger changed the competitor

Same ROE, opposite routes Deep dive

A prime lender earns 2% on its assets and borrows six times its equity. An affordable lender earns 4–8% and borrows two to three times. Both land near 15% ROE. Understanding why is the key to everything else in this sector.

Every lender's return on equity can be broken into two numbers: what it earns on its assets (return on assets, ROA) and how many rupees of assets it holds for each rupee of equity (leverage). For a lender whose assets are almost all loans, ROE is roughly ROA multiplied by one plus the debt-to-equity ratio. Prime and affordable HFCs choose opposite ends of that product.

Five lessons from the arithmetic

1. Same ROE, opposite routes. The affordable lender earns about 2.1 times the ROA on about 0.4 times the leverage.

2. The shock absorber differs. Pre-provision profit is what is available to absorb bad loans before the lender loses money. The prime lender's 2.69% covers its normal 0.2% credit cost about 13 times; the affordable lender's 5.51% covers 0.3% about 18 times. Under stress the picture narrows: if salaried prime borrowers pushed credit cost to 0.5%, the prime lender's cover would fall to about 5 times; if an informal-economy downturn pushed affordable credit cost to 1.5–2%, cover would fall to about 3 times (calculated on the illustrative numbers). Affordable lenders carry more equity precisely because their bad years are worse.

3. Prime lenders are more exposed to a bank price war. A 25 basis point cut in yield on ₹10,000 crore costs ₹25 crore of net interest income for both. After tax, that is a 10.0% fall in the prime lender's profit and a 4.8% fall in the affordable lender's (ILLUSTRATIVE).

4. Prime lenders are also more exposed to funding costs. A 25 basis point rise in cost of funds that cannot be passed on costs the prime lender ₹21.7 crore on ₹8,667 crore of borrowings (profit −8.7%) and the affordable lender ₹18 crore on ₹7,200 crore (profit −3.5%).

5. The affordable lender's hidden lever is gearing. Re-gear the affordable example to D/E 4× (equity ₹2,000 crore, borrowings ₹8,000 crore): interest expense rises to ₹632 crore, NII falls to ₹718 crore, PAT to about ₹355 crore, but ROE rises to about 17.8%. Apply Aptus's spread of about 8.9% at the same 4× gearing and ROE would be roughly 30%. Nobody lets Aptus do that today (rating agencies, lenders and its own caution keep its capital ratio near 75%), but this is why investors call affordable lenders' excess capital a valuation argument.

HUDCO: the fourth archetype, an infrastructure lender with a housing name

HUDCO lends to states, not families, and the regulator stopped counting it as an HFC in August 2024. It stays in this report because the sub-sector map puts it here, and because its numbers look like an HFC's until you read them closely.

The Housing and Urban Development Corporation was incorporated on 25 April 1970 to finance public housing and urban infrastructure, eighteen years before the National Housing Bank existed. For most of its life it was registered as a housing finance company. That ended quietly: the NHB's Report on Trend and Progress of Housing in India 2024-25 records that HUDCO was "accorded NBFC-IFC status in August 2024" and excludes it from HFC sector statistics. NBFC-IFC means a non-banking finance company specialising in infrastructure finance. The reason is the principal-business test (Section 09): an HFC must keep at least 60% of its assets in housing finance and 50% in housing loans to individuals, and HUDCO's book is overwhelmingly loans to state governments, housing boards, urban local bodies and their agencies, much of it for roads, water, sewerage and urban transport rather than homes.

So why keep it here? Because the NSE 750 sub-sector map files it under "Housing Finance Company", because investors still screen it alongside LIC Housing and PNB Housing, and because it is genuinely useful as a contrast. HUDCO shows what a housing-adjacent lender looks like when the borrower is a state: tiny operating cost, near-sovereign funding, a thin spread, very large single exposures, and credit risk that depends on state budgets rather than household incomes.

The rulebook: who regulates HFCs, and the rules that shape the business

Since 2019 the RBI writes the rules and the NHB inspects. The rules that matter most fix what an HFC may lend against, how much it must hold in capital, and how far it may stray from housing.

Until 2019 the National Housing Bank, set up under the National Housing Bank Act of 1987 and established on 9 July 1988, both regulated and supervised HFCs. The IL&FS default in September 2018 and the DHFL collapse that followed exposed how lightly a large part of the shadow-banking system was overseen. The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2019 moved HFC regulation to the RBI with effect from 9 August 2019. The NHB kept supervision (off-site returns and inspections), refinance, and its role as nodal agency for the PMAY interest subsidies. SEBI governs listed equity and debt; the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs runs PMAY; the Income-tax Act sets the borrower's tax incentives.

The principal-business test: why every HFC is mostly a home lender

The single rule that most shapes HFC balance sheets is the principal business criteria (PBC), introduced in the RBI's revised framework of 22 October 2020. To keep an HFC licence, at least 60% of total assets must be housing finance and at least 50% must be housing loans to individuals. The thresholds were phased in: 50% and 40% by March 2022, 55% and 45% by March 2023, 60% and 50% by March 2024. An HFC that fails is treated as an NBFC investment and credit company. This is why every HFC talks about LAP growth in the same breath as "within the regulatory limit", and why non-housing loans, though growing faster than housing loans, cannot take over the book.

The repo squeeze and the October hike Deep dive

Bank mortgages reprice within a quarter of an RBI move; HFC borrowing costs take two to four quarters. In 2025 that lag squeezed prime HFC margins while widening affordable lenders' spreads. The 7 October 2026 hike runs the film backwards.

The asymmetry at the centre of this section is mechanical. A bank's floating-rate home loan is linked to the repo rate and resets at the next reset date, usually within three months. A bank's deposits reprice more slowly, but its home-loan book follows the repo almost immediately. A prime HFC must keep its own lending rate close to the bank's or lose borrowers through balance transfer, so when banks cut, the HFC has to cut too. But the HFC's liabilities are bank loans that reset with MCLR over 3–12 months, bonds fixed until maturity, and NHB refinance, so its cost of funds falls only two to four quarters later. For as long as asset yields fall faster than funding costs, the margin shrinks.

In plain terms

When the RBI cuts rates, banks must lower their home-loan rates almost immediately. A prime HFC has to follow or lose customers, but the money it borrowed stays expensive for months. So for a year or so it earns less on its loans while still paying the old price for its money. Affordable lenders mostly escape, because banks are not competing for their borrowers, so they keep charging the same while their own borrowing gets cheaper.

The reversal: 7 October 2026

At its meeting of 5–7 October 2026 the RBI's Monetary Policy Committee raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50%, its first increase since February 2023, and changed its stance to "calibrated tightening". Consumer inflation was 5.5% and wholesale inflation 11.4% in September 2026, and SBI Research has forecast a possible second hike at the December meeting (2–4 December). The mechanics now run in reverse. Banks' EBLR home loans reprice upward within a quarter, which eases the price pressure on prime HFCs; if they raise their own lending rates promptly, their yields rise before their funding costs do. But funding costs are already rising: bond yields climbed 20–90 basis points from June 2026, and ICRA expects HFC cost of funds to rise in FY27. Affordable lenders with fixed-rate books face the opposite of last year: costs rise while yields are sticky, so spreads narrow. Aavas had cut its prime lending rate by about 10 basis points in June 2026, just before the turn, and brokers now expect its FY27 spread to fall below 5%.

Whether a prime HFC actually benefits depends on whether it has the nerve to raise its own rates.

Five decades, four shocks: how the industry got its shape

The state built the plumbing, HDFC built the industry, a 2014–18 boom over-reached, IL&FS and DHFL broke it, and the HDFC merger and an IPO wave remade it.

Indian housing finance is younger than most of its borrowers' houses. HUDCO came first, in 1970, as a state financier of public housing and urban infrastructure. HDFC Ltd, founded in 1977, created the private mortgage industry. The National Housing Bank Act of 1987 and the NHB's establishment in 1988 gave the sector a regulator and a refinancer, and three PSU-sponsored lenders followed to channel savings into housing: Can Fin Homes (1987, Canara Bank), PNB Housing (1988) and LIC Housing (1989).

The 1990s and 2000s were a golden age built on tax incentives (the Section 24 interest deduction and the Section 80C principal deduction) and falling interest rates after 2001. Banks entered mortgages aggressively from about 2001, ICICI and SBI first, and the HFC share of the market began a long decline that the HDFC merger completed. The affordable lenders were seeded in a short burst between 2009 and 2011: Aptus (2009), Home First (2010), India Shelter (2010), Aadhar (2010, as DHFL Vysya Housing Finance) and Aavas (2011, as AU Housing Finance), each founded to serve the informal-income buyer that banks and big HFCs ignored.

The 2014–18 boom and the 2018–19 bust

The defining episode is the boom that ended in September 2018. While public-sector banks were frozen by the 2015 asset-quality review, HFCs grew at 18–20% a year or more, funded heavily by short-term commercial paper bought by mutual funds and lent out for 15–20 years. Several large HFCs, including DHFL, Indiabulls Housing and PNB Housing, also lent heavily to property developers. When IL&FS defaulted in September 2018, mutual funds stopped rolling over HFC paper. Lenders with asset-liability mismatches and developer books were caught. DHFL, then the second- or third-largest HFC, defaulted in June 2019. The policy consequence was the transfer of HFC regulation to the RBI in August 2019 and the October 2020 framework. The commercial consequence is visible in every HFC's balance sheet today: longer-dated borrowings, large liquidity buffers (Home First held ₹2,272 crore in Q1 FY27 against ₹10,818 crore of borrowings), and, at most lenders, very small developer books.

The 2023 merger and the 2024 IPO wave

The second structural break came in 2023. HDFC Ltd's merger into HDFC Bank on 1 July moved roughly a third of the HFC sector into a bank and left LIC Housing as the largest HFC. The year after brought the IPO and control-change wave: Aadhar in May 2024 (₹3,000 crore at ₹315), Bajaj Housing in September 2024 (₹6,560 crore at ₹70, listing at ₹150), CVC's purchase of control of Aavas, and, in 2025–26, Blackstone's transfer of Aadhar to a new fund. By 2026 the sector's problems had become the mirror image of 2018's. In plain terms

The industry's worst moment came in 2018–19, when lenders that had borrowed short and lent long to builders ran out of money and DHFL collapsed. Everything about today's HFCs, from their long-term borrowing to their tiny builder loans, is a reaction to that. Then HDFC, the biggest HFC, became part of a bank, and a wave of listings and private-equity deals remade the rest.

One rupee, followed through: a worked example

Take ₹100 of loans at an affordable lender funded by ₹28 of equity and ₹72 of borrowings (ILLUSTRATIVE round numbers, consistent with Section 07). The loans yield 13.5%, so they earn ₹13.50 a year. The ₹72 of borrowings cost 7.9%, or ₹5.69. Net interest income is ₹7.81. Add ₹0.70 of fees and subtract ₹3.00 of operating costs and pre-provision operating profit is ₹5.51. Bad loans cost ₹0.30, leaving ₹5.21 before tax; tax at 22.5% takes ₹1.17. Profit is ₹4.04 on ₹100 of assets, an ROA of about 4%, and ₹4.04 on ₹28 of equity, an ROE of about 14.4%. Run the same ₹100 through a prime lender (9.5% yield, ₹86.7 borrowed at 7.4%, ₹0.15 of fees, ₹0.55 of opex, ₹0.20 of credit cost, 25% tax) and profit is about ₹1.87, an ROA of 1.9%, on ₹13.3 of equity, an ROE of 14.0%. Every KPI below is a piece of these two calculations.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 an affordable HFC lends, it keeps about ₹4 a year as profit, and because only ₹28 of that ₹100 is its own money, that ₹4 is a 14% return for shareholders. A prime HFC keeps under ₹2 per ₹100, but only ₹13 is its own money, so it also earns about 14%. The rest of this section explains each step from ₹13.50 of interest down to that ₹4.

1. AUM and AUM growth

What it is. Assets under management (AUM) is the total of loans the lender services: loans on its balance sheet plus loans it has sold to banks through direct assignment, securitised, or co-lent, where it still collects the EMIs. "Loan assets" or "loan book" counts only on-balance-sheet loans. Formula. AUM growth = AUM at period end ÷ AUM a year earlier − 1. Worked example. Bajaj Housing reported AUM of ₹1,58,190 crore at September 2026 against ₹1,26,749 crore a year earlier: growth of 24.8%. Its on-book loan assets were ₹1,39,850 crore, so about ₹18,000 crore (11–12%) of what it manages sits on someone else's balance sheet. Drivers. Growth equals disbursements minus runoff. Mortgage runoff, from scheduled repayments, prepayments and balance transfers out, is roughly 12–18% of the opening book a year (indicative). Benchmark. For prime lenders, 15% or more is good, 8–15% average, under 8% weak; for affordable lenders, 20–25% with stable asset quality is good, 15–20% average, and below 12% or above 35% is a warning, because very fast growth in small-ticket lending usually means loosened underwriting.

2. Disbursements

What it is. New loans paid out in the period, the input that builds AUM. Formula. Branch additions, sales productivity, builder tie-ups, the property cycle, and credit appetite. Benchmark. Good is disbursement growth above AUM growth (the book is accelerating); weak is falling disbursements.

3. Yield

What it is. The average interest rate the lender earns on its loans. Formula. Interest income on loans ÷ average loans (annualised for a quarter). Worked example. A lender earning ₹270 crore of interest in a quarter on average loans of ₹8,000 crore has an annualised yield of 270 × 4 ÷ 8,000 = 13.5%. Drivers. Borrower type (informal income pays more), product mix (LAP and developer loans pay more than home loans), ticket size, fixed versus floating rates, competition from banks, and the rate cycle. Benchmark. Prime 9.5–10%; affordable 13–15%, up to 17% if heavily weighted to LAP. A yield that rises sharply without a product-mix explanation is a sign of risk creep, not skill.

How they compare. Section 05 shows the ladder: LIC Housing 9.12%, PNB 9.47%, Can Fin about 10%, Aavas about 12.7%, Home First about 13.1%, Aadhar 13.5%, India Shelter about 14.8%, Aptus about 17%. Most of the gap between Aadhar and Aptus is product mix and geography: the more of the book that sits in loans against property and small-business use, the higher the yield and the higher the risk.

14. Branch productivity

What it is. How much book each branch carries. Formula. AUM ÷ branches. Worked example. Home First: ₹16,938 crore ÷ 175 branches ≈ ₹97 crore per branch; India Shelter: ₹11,284 crore ÷ ~307 ≈ ₹37 crore. Drivers. For affordable lenders, above ₹70 crore good, ₹40–70 crore average, below ₹35 crore weak. Section 20 charts the five. Definition trap. Branch counts are not standardised: Home First's 175 branches sit alongside 373 touchpoints, and a lender opening many branches at once will look unproductive for years.

15. Price-to-book and price-to-earnings

What it is. What the market pays for each rupee of net worth and of profit. Formulas. P/B = share price ÷ book value per share; P/E = price ÷ earnings per share; justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (cost of equity − g), where g is long-run growth. Worked example. A lender earning a sustainable 16% ROE, growing 11% a year with a 14% cost of equity, is worth (16 − 11) ÷ (14 − 11) ≈ 1.7× book (ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers. The durability of ROE and growth, more than this year's level. Benchmark. No fixed good or bad; P/B must be read against ROE and growth. P/E for prime lenders of 8–15× and affordable lenders of 15–25× has been typical. Section 18 applies this to all ten. Definition trap. Book value right after an equity raise is inflated relative to earning power, and P/E on a year with a tax write-back (HUDCO's 7.6×) or recoveries (PNB) understates the true multiple.

16. Pre-provision operating profit: the shock absorber

What it is. Profit before bad-loan charges and tax: the earnings power available to absorb credit losses before the lender loses money. Formula. PPOP = NII + fees − opex; usually expressed as a share of average AUM. Worked example. In the Section 07 example the prime lender's PPOP is ₹269 crore (2.69% of AUM) and the affordable lender's ₹551 crore (5.51%). If the affordable lender's credit cost jumped from 0.3% to 2%, it would still earn a pre-tax profit of ₹351 crore; the prime lender could absorb a credit cost of 2.69% before making a loss, but its salaried borrowers rarely default at even a fifth of that rate. Drivers. Everything above the credit-cost line. Benchmark. Prime above 2.5% good, 2–2.5% average; affordable above 5% good, 4–5% average. How they compare. Only a few lenders publish PPOP on a common basis, but the net-total-income-to-AUM proxy from FY26 accounts (revenue less interest, over March 2026 AUM) gives a rough ranking before opex: Aptus about 11.8%, India Shelter 9.8%, Aadhar 7.6%, Home First 7.1%, Aavas 6.8%, Can Fin 3.9%, PNB 3.8%, Bajaj 3.1%, LIC 2.8%, HUDCO 2.6% (calculated, INDICATIVE because it includes fees and mixes balance-sheet bases). Definition trap. PPOP is inflated by one-off gains on assignment and by treasury income, and some lenders report "operating profit" after a part of provisions; always rebuild it from NII, fees and opex. The ratio that matters is PPOP divided by a plausible stress credit cost, not by today's.

In plain terms

Before comparing two HFCs on any number, check three things: what the number is divided by, whether one-off items are inside it, and whether it is from the same quarter of the year. Most apparent differences between lenders shrink or flip once those three are fixed.

How to read an HFC's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. AUM and growth (minute 1). Find AUM and on-book loans, year-on-year and quarter-on-quarter. Red flag: growth well above guidance in small tickets, or a jump explained by assignment rather than lending.
  2. Disbursements (minute 2). Compare growth with AUM growth and check the recognition basis. Red flag: falling disbursements with rising AUM (runoff slowing because fewer borrowers can refinance away, which can mean weaker borrowers).
  3. Yield, cost of funds and spread (minute 3). Write the three numbers and their quarter-on-quarter change. Red flag for prime lenders: yield falling faster than cost of funds; for affordable lenders after October 2026: cost of funds rising with yield flat.
  4. NIM and its basis (minute 4). Check the denominator and whether fees are in. Red flag: a NIM rise explained only by a fee or assignment gain.
  5. Opex and cost-to-income (minute 5). Red flag: opex growing much faster than AUM without new-branch commentary.
  6. Credit cost (minute 6). Separate provisions from recoveries. Red flag: a low credit cost created by write-backs, or a rising one for three straight quarters.
  7. Stage 3, PCR and early buckets (minute 7). Compare with the same quarter last year. Red flag: 30+ dpd or 1+ dpd up year-on-year, or Stage 3 rising while PCR falls.
  8. ROA, ROE and one-offs (minute 8). Recompute ROA excluding tax items and recoveries. Red flag: headline ROE far above the recomputed figure.
  9. Capital and liquidity (minute 9). CRAR, D/E, liquidity buffer, rating actions. Red flag: a rating watch, or CRAR falling toward 20% at a fast grower.
  10. Guidance and governance (minute 10). Compare actuals with guidance on growth, spread or NIM and credit cost; note any senior departures, auditor or regulator comments. Red flag: missed margin guidance (PNB Housing's 3.50% against 3.55–3.65% in Q1 FY27) or management churn (Aavas in 2026).
In plain terms

On results day, read the numbers in the same order as the profit equation: how much was lent, at what price, with money that cost how much, at what running cost, with how many bad loans. Then check what was one-off and whether management did what it said it would. That order catches almost every problem worth catching.

Cracks in the small ticket Deep dive

Underwriting informal income is the affordable lenders' only durable edge. After five benign years, the first signs of strain are showing in the smallest loans, in loans against property, and at the two highest-yielding lenders.

The affordable-housing model rests on one claim: that a lender with enough local knowledge, field visits and collection discipline can lend to a borrower with no payslip and lose less than half a percent a year. For most of 2021–2025 the claim held comfortably. Sector gross NPA fell from 3.16% in March 2023 to 1.89% in March 2025, and the affordable lenders reported credit costs of 0.1–0.5%. But a model built on a benign cycle has to be judged on the turn, and three separate sources of evidence now point the same way.

Evidence one: the rating agencies

CRISIL found that more than 70% of affordable HFCs saw a notable rise in 90-plus-day delinquencies on loans below ₹15 lakh between FY24 and FY25, and that lenders were cutting sub-₹10 lakh disbursements because of borrower over-leverage and spill-over from microfinance stress: the same households often carry microfinance and consumer loans as well as a home loan. ICRA expects affordable-HFC gross NPA of 1.8–2.0% in FY27, above the 1.5–1.7% it expects for the sector as a whole. CRISIL also flagged that West Asia-driven inflation is tightening lenders' credit filters.

Evidence two: what lenders are actually doing

NHB data show HFC disbursements of loans up to ₹10 lakh fell 7.7% in FY25, while loans above ₹25 lakh rose to 60.9% of disbursements. Affordable lenders are leaning harder on loans against property, which yield more but are used for business purposes and secured on commercial or mixed-use collateral: HFC non-housing loans grew 28.1% in FY25 against 14.0% for housing loans, and CRISIL expects affordable lenders' LAP to grow about 23% a year in FY27–28, against 17–18% for home loans. India Shelter's book is about 43% LAP. More LAP keeps yields up, but it moves the book toward the borrowers and collateral that behave worst in a downturn.

In plain terms

The affordable lenders' whole advantage is that they lose very little money lending to people without payslips. For five years that was easy. How bad could it get? An illustrative stress

The affordable model's defence is its cushion. In the Section 07 example, pre-provision profit is 5.51% of loans. A stress year with credit costs of 1.5–2%, five to seven times today's level, would cut profit by roughly 40–55% but not produce a loss, and the equity cushion of 28% of assets would be untouched (ILLUSTRATIVE). Aptus, with pre-provision returns above 10% of assets and a 75% capital ratio, could absorb far more. The real risk is less a solvency event than a valuation one: affordable lenders trade at 1.9–2.6 times book on the assumption of 0.3–0.5% credit costs for many years, and a two-year episode at 1.5% would cut ROE to single digits while it lasted. The 2018–19 lesson is also relevant: the lenders that failed then were killed by funding, not credit. Today's affordable lenders hold large liquidity buffers and long-dated NHB and bank lines, so a credit wobble is unlikely to become a funding crisis unless it is accompanied by a governance shock.

In plain terms

Even if bad loans at an affordable lender went up five-fold, it would still make money, because its margins and capital are so large. What it would lose is its high share price, which assumes bad loans stay low for years. So the risk to an investor is bigger than the risk to the lender's depositors or bondholders.

PE in, PE out: the affordable-housing capital cycle Deep dive

Almost every affordable lender was built, rescued or sold by private equity. The cycle of rescue, listing and exit has created the franchises, and it is now their main governance risk.

Affordable housing finance in India is a private-equity creation. The model needs patient equity for five to eight years before it earns a respectable return: branches must be built, underwriting learned, a credit record established, and a rating earned that lets the lender borrow cheaply. Banks and big HFCs would not fund that; PE funds would, in exchange for control and an eventual exit. The result is that four of the five listed affordable lenders have had at least one PE owner change in the last five years, and the fifth, Aptus, saw its venture backer exit in FY26.

Blackstone and Aadhar: rescue, list, recycle

Aadhar began as DHFL Vysya Housing Finance, part of the Wadhawan group that ran DHFL. Blackstone bought it in 2019 as DHFL collapsed, kept the management and the branch network, and grew it into the largest listed affordable lender, with ₹31,364 crore of AUM at June 2026 across more than 600 branches. It listed in May 2024 at ₹315, with Blackstone selling ₹2,000 crore. Rather than sell the rest in the market, Blackstone transferred its stake from one fund to a new one, which triggered a mandatory open offer at ₹469.97; control passed to BCP Asia II Holdco VII on 26 February 2026 at 65.07%. Shareholders who tendered did well; the stock is ₹447 today. For investors the question is the eventual sale of a 64.5% stake, which will either come as a strategic sale (possibly at a premium) or as a long series of block trades (a supply overhang). Foreign portfolio holdings have been falling (4.11% at September 2026) while domestic institutions have been buying (11.51%).

CVC and Aavas: the hand-off that went wrong, so far

Aavas is the cautionary tale. Kedaara and Partners Group carved it out of AU Small Finance in 2016 and listed it in 2018; it built a reputation for hand-underwritten rural, self-employed borrowers in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh and the lowest credit cost among affordable lenders (about 13 basis points). In August 2024 CVC agreed to buy 26.47% from the two sponsors for ₹3,425 crore, plus an open offer at ₹1,767, and became promoter with 48.88%. In 2026 the senior team left: the chief executive in April, the chief business officer in May, and the chief financial officer and chief risk officer, who resigned in June effective 21 September, with interim replacements named. ICRA placed its AA rating on "Rating Watch with Developing Implications" and CARE put its AA rating on watch, covering ₹13,522 crore of instruments. On 22 June 2026 the company clarified that an ongoing NHB audit was routine and denied allegations of loan-classification discrepancies. None of this has yet shown up in the numbers: Q1 FY27 AUM grew 15.4%, disbursements 41%, gross Stage 3 was 1.11% and profit rose 23%. But the stock is down 37% since the July primer, to ₹1,195 against a 52-week high of ₹1,775 and below CVC's open-offer price. Our Aavas primer still describes Kedaara and Partners Group as the sponsors and omits these events; this report's figures supersede it.

The parents have their own questions

Parent ownership raises a different kind of question. Bajaj Finance owns 86.70% of Bajaj Housing (down from 88.70% after selling about 2% in the December 2025 quarter) and must eventually reach the 25% minimum public float; SEBI has relaxed timelines for very large issuers, and whether Bajaj Housing's deadline falls in September 2027 or later is unverified. Either way, roughly 12 percentage points, about ₹8,000 crore at today's price, must come to market (INDICATIVE). LIC owns 45.24% of LIC Housing; in the September 2026 quarter foreign holdings fell from 21.47% to 18.93%. Canara Bank keeps its Can Fin stake at 29.99%, just below the 30% at which it would have to consolidate. And the HDFC precedent hangs over every parent-owned HFC: when the regulatory and capital logic changed, the parent absorbed its HFC. No parent has signalled such a move, but investors price the possibility.

In plain terms

Private-equity funds built most of the affordable home lenders, listed them, and are now selling out or handing them to new funds. That is normal for PE, but each hand-off is a moment of risk: new owners, new managers, and a period when nobody quite knows who is in charge. Aavas is going through exactly that in 2026, and its share price has fallen by more than a third.

The lesson from this cycle is not that PE ownership is bad. Blackstone's rescue of Aadhar from the DHFL wreckage, Kedaara's build-out of Aavas and Warburg's backing of Home First created three of the best lenders in the sector. The lesson is that the transition from one owner to the next is when governance risk is highest, and that the 2018–19 crisis showed governance failure in an HFC can be existential. For each affordable lender, investors should know who the next seller is, how much stock they hold, and who would step in if the management team left.

In plain terms

When you buy shares in an affordable HFC you are also betting on its owner. Ask three questions: who owns the biggest stake, when will they want to sell, and who would run the company if the top team walked out tomorrow.

Who is winning: growth, quality and the governance discount

The best positions combine fast growth with a clean book. Only one large lender has both; the affordable lenders split between those that are growing cleanly and those whose growth is starting to cost them.

The simplest way to judge a lender is to ask two questions at once: how fast is it growing, and what is happening to its bad loans? Fast growth with rising bad loans is the classic warning sign in lending, because new loans look clean until they season. Slow growth with clean books is safe but unrewarding. The map plots the latest loan growth against the latest gross NPA, with the vertical axis inverted so that "better" is up.

The map sorts the field cleanly. Bajaj Housing is the only lender combining scale, 25% growth and a near-spotless book (gross NPA 0.29% at September 2026, Q2 FY27 profit ₹757 crore, up 17.7%). Its weakness is not on this chart: a 12% ROE, a thin spread exposed to bank pricing, and about a third of AUM in developer and lease-rental loans, the segment that hurt HFCs in 2018–22. Among affordable lenders, Aadhar and Aavas sit near the middle (15–18% growth, 1.1–1.3% GNPA), while Home First, India Shelter and Aptus grow 21–26% with gross NPA of 1.5–1.8%.

Why 0.69× to 2.90× book: what the market pays for

A lender is worth more than its book only if it can earn more than its cost of equity for a long time. The market is pricing durability, not this year's ROE, and it has cut every multiple since July.

For a lender, price-to-book is the natural valuation measure, because the balance sheet is the business. A lender that earns exactly its cost of equity, forever, is worth its book value. One that earns more is worth a premium that grows with the size of the excess and with how long it can grow while earning it. The standard shorthand is the justified price-to-book ratio: (ROE − g) ÷ (cost of equity − g), where g is long-run growth. It is a blunt tool, but it explains most of the range in this sector.

The global lens: why Indian mortgage spreads are so wide

Indian housing lenders earn spreads two to three times those of developed-market mortgage banks, and the affordable lenders earn returns on assets with no developed-market counterpart.

Mortgage lending in most developed markets is a low-margin, high-volume business run by deposit-funded banks or by government-sponsored securitisation machines. Comparing India with them shows why the Indian sector looks so profitable and why that profitability is not obviously permanent. First, Indian mortgage spreads are two to three times developed-market levels because nominal interest rates are higher (a 5.50% repo against roughly 3.5–4.5% policy rates in many developed markets) and because the market is under-penetrated, so lenders are not yet competing for the same marginal borrower. Second, the affordable lenders' 4–8% ROA has no developed-market analogue. Their nearest parallels are microfinance and small-business lenders, but their loans are secured by the borrower's first and only home, which changes both the willingness to repay and the loss when they do not. Third, global mortgage banks trade near book value. The Indian affordable lenders' 1.9–2.6 times is a bet on 18–25% growth for a decade; Bajaj Housing's 2.90 times is a bet on growth plus a rising ROE. Indonesia's state mortgage bank, at about half of book, is a reminder that a large housing gap does not by itself make mortgage lenders valuable; the economics and governance of the lender have to work too.

There is also a structural difference in who bears the rate risk. A US borrower typically takes a 30-year fixed mortgage, and the rate risk is passed through agency securitisation to bond investors. A UK borrower refixes every two to five years. Indian prime borrowers mostly pay floating rates, so the borrower bears the rate risk and the lender's margin depends on how fast it can pass changes through, which is exactly the mechanism behind Section 10. Indian affordable lenders, many of which lend at fixed or sticky rates, look more like UK lenders in that respect, but without a deposit base.

Finally, scale. India's roughly ₹37 lakh crore of individual home loans is under 2% of the US residential-mortgage stock (approximate). Mortgage-to-GDP of 11.2% against 45–60% in the US and UK is often quoted as proof of a long runway, and it is, but the path from 11% to 25% in other countries took decades and depended on formal land titles, stable inflation and deep long-term bond markets. India's progress on title digitisation (Section 20) and on long-dated bond markets will matter more to the sector's growth than any single lender's strategy.

In plain terms

Indian home lenders earn much fatter margins than lenders in rich countries, partly because Indian interest rates are higher and partly because relatively few Indians have a mortgage yet. Those margins should shrink over time as more lenders compete. The affordable lenders are a uniquely Indian business: nothing in developed markets earns their returns while lending against people's homes.

Technology and disruption: can banks learn to read informal income?

The affordable lenders' edge is judgement that used to require a field visit. Digital data, co-lending and land-record digitisation are making that judgement cheaper for them, and potentially available to banks.

The biggest long-term threat to the affordable model is not a recession; it is a bank that can underwrite a shopkeeper as cheaply and accurately as Aavas or Aptus. Banks already dominate salaried lending because a payslip and a bureau score are easy to process at scale. Informal income has been hard to verify, so it has been left to specialists willing to send a credit officer to see the shop, count the stock and talk to the neighbours. Three developments are eroding that difficulty. GST returns and UPI payment trails give a digital record of a small business's turnover; Udyam registration identifies the enterprise; and the Account Aggregator framework lets a borrower share bank-statement data with a lender in minutes, with consent. Aadhar's own management flags the question of "whether banks and prime HFCs push down into the ₹10–15 lakh ticket". Small finance banks and PSBs under priority-sector targets are already there.

Digital origination cuts cost, and Home First shows how much

The same tools help the affordable lenders themselves. Home First, which relies on app-led origination and data-driven underwriting, runs operating costs of 2.8% of assets with only 175 branches and carries about ₹97 crore of AUM per branch, nearly twice Aavas's and Aadhar's and two and a half times India Shelter's. If the most digital lender can run a branch at twice the productivity of the most traditional, technology could roughly halve the per-loan cost of the model (an inference from these figures, INDICATIVE). The Unified Lending Interface, e-stamping, e-signatures and state land-record digitisation under DILRMP all cut turnaround times further.

Co-lending: originator, not owner

Co-lending 2.0, effective 1 January 2026, lets any regulated lender co-lend any loan type with any other, each retaining at least 10%. For an affordable HFC this opens a different business: originate and service a loan, keep a slice on its own balance sheet, and pass the rest to a bank with cheaper deposits, earning fees and a share of the spread while using far less capital. Home First already reports spreads "ex co-lending". The trade-off is that part of the yield goes to the partner, and the lender's growth becomes partly dependent on banks' appetite. Over time it could turn the best affordable lenders into origination-and-servicing platforms whose value lies in their underwriting and collection engines rather than their balance sheets.

Title digitisation: a moat that may shrink

A large part of an affordable lender's diligence is legal: confirming that a self-built house on inherited land has a clean title, that there are no competing claims, and that the lender can enforce its security. As states digitise land records and registration, this diligence becomes cheaper and more standardised, which lowers legal risk for everyone but also commoditises one of the affordable lenders' edges (an inference, not yet visible in the numbers).

Other risks

Models trained on a benign cycle. Data-driven underwriting has been calibrated on 2021–26, five years of falling bad loans. Its first real test will be a downturn. Climate and physical risk. Self-built houses in flood-prone tier-3 towns are collateral that can be damaged or devalued, and few lenders disclose their exposure. Governance and key-person risk. The Aavas episode in 2026 (senior exits, a rating watch, a clarification about an NHB audit) is a reminder that in a lender, governance failure is not a slow-burning problem; the 2018–19 crisis showed it can be existential.

In plain terms

The affordable lenders' special skill is judging whether someone without a payslip can repay. New digital records of small-business income are making that easier, which helps the affordable lenders cut costs today but could let banks compete with them tomorrow. Which effect wins will decide whether their high margins last.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian housing lenders

The bull case rests on a long runway and proven underwriting; the bear case on banks, rates and a credit cycle that has not yet been tested.

Both cases are about the same five forces described at the start of this report. The bull case says the mortgage market is under-penetrated, the affordable lenders have a genuine underwriting skill, the rate hike helps prime margins, and valuations have already fallen. The bear case says banks will keep taking the best borrowers, small-ticket credit is turning, funding costs are rising, and ownership transitions are creating governance risk at the worst time.

The bull case

  • A long runway. Home loans are 11.2% of GDP against 25–60% in peer and developed markets; incomes, urbanisation and formal title should keep mortgage growth near 10–15% a year for a decade, faster for affordable lenders (ICRA: 19–21% in FY27).
  • Asset quality near its best. Sector gross NPA fell from 3.16% (Mar-23) to 1.89% (Mar-25); ICRA expects 1.5–1.7% in FY27. Bajaj Housing, Can Fin and PNB Housing run below 1%.
  • The rate turn helps prime lenders. The 7 October hike makes banks' repo-linked loans dearer within a quarter, easing the price pressure that cut LIC Housing's margin from ~3.1% to 2.58%.
  • Huge capital cushions. Affordable lenders hold 42–75% capital ratios; re-gearing alone could lift ROE by 3–10 points (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Valuations have reset. The group trades at 1.68× book and 11.9× earnings after falls of 7–37% since July; Aavas trades below its new owner's offer price and LIC Housing at 0.69× book.
  • Co-lending and digital tools let the best affordable lenders grow with less capital and lower cost.

The bear case

  • Banks set the price. EBLR pricing and free balance transfers cap prime yields and drain seasoned borrowers; HDFC Bank's return to mortgage pricing after its post-merger clean-up is a direct threat to prime HFCs.
  • Small-ticket credit is turning. CRISIL reports rising 90+ dpd below ₹15 lakh; Aptus's GNPA rose from 1.19% to 1.70% in five quarters; India Shelter's Stage 3 rose to 1.5% with 30+ dpd at 5.2%.
  • Funding costs are rising. Bond yields are up 20–90 bp since June 2026, and a second hike is possible in December; fixed-rate affordable books (Aavas, spread already 5.06%) will be squeezed.
  • Ownership transitions. Aavas's leadership exodus and rating watch, Blackstone's eventual sale of 64.5% of Aadhar, Bajaj Finance's required sell-down, and a 7% promoter at Home First.
  • Mission drift. HFCs are moving up-market and into LAP, where risk is higher and bank competition stronger; sub-₹10 lakh disbursements fell 7.7% in FY25.
  • Policy support has faded. PMAY-U 2.0's interest subsidy released ₹40 crore in its first year against ₹49,458 crore under CLSS; the new tax regime removes the home-loan deduction for most salaried buyers.

Weighing them, the bear points are mostly about the next two years and the bull points mostly about the next ten. The near-term evidence (prime margins at multi-year lows, affordable credit drifting, funding costs rising, two ownership transitions in progress) argues for selectivity rather than a sector view. The long-term evidence (penetration, underwriting skill, capital) argues that the best-run lenders in each archetype will compound.

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

Housing-finance terms
TermMeaning
HFCHousing finance company: a non-bank lender licensed by the RBI (supervised by the NHB) whose principal business is housing finance.
A-HFCAffordable housing finance company; by CRISIL's definition, a lender with most of its book in loans below ₹25 lakh.
NBFC-IFCNon-banking finance company – infrastructure finance company; HUDCO's status since August 2024.
IHLIndividual housing loan: a home loan to a person, as opposed to a developer or institution.
AUMAssets under management: loans on the balance sheet plus loans sold, securitised or co-lent but still serviced.
LAP / NHLLoan against property / non-housing loan: lending against a house or shop for business or personal use.
LRDLease-rental discounting: a loan against the future rent from a commercial property.
EBLRExternal benchmark lending rate: banks' floating retail loans linked to an external rate, usually the repo, since October 2019.
PLRPrime lending rate: the HFC's own reference rate for floating loans, which it sets.
Repo rateThe RBI's policy rate; 5.50% since 7 October 2026.
MCLRMarginal cost of funds-based lending rate: banks' internal benchmark, used for many bank loans to HFCs.
Yield / CoF / spreadInterest earned on loans; interest paid on borrowings; the difference between them.
NII / NIMNet interest income (interest earned minus paid) and the same as a % of assets; denominators vary by company.
NTINet total income: NII plus fees and other income.
PPOPPre-provision operating profit: NTI minus operating expenses.
Credit costProvisions and write-offs, net of recoveries, as a % of loans.
Stage 1 / 2 / 3Ind AS 109 categories: performing, significantly deteriorated (usually 30–90 days overdue), and credit-impaired (90+ days, close to NPA).
GNPA / NNPAGross and net non-performing assets as a % of loans.
PCRProvision coverage ratio: provisions held against Stage 3 loans ÷ Stage 3 loans.
dpdDays past due; 1+ dpd and 30+ dpd are early-delinquency buckets.
Bounce rateShare of monthly auto-debit (NACH) EMI instructions that fail on first presentation.
LTVLoan-to-value: the loan as a % of the property's value; capped at 90/80/75% by loan size.
CRARCapital to risk-weighted assets ratio; minimum 15% for HFCs.
PBCPrincipal business criteria: ≥60% of assets in housing finance and ≥50% in individual home loans.
SBR / Upper LayerThe RBI's scale-based regulation; the Upper Layer (assets ≥₹1 lakh Cr since June 2026) carries the strictest rules and a listing requirement.
NHBNational Housing Bank: HFC supervisor, refinancer and PMAY nodal agency.
Direct assignment / co-lendingSelling a pool of loans to a bank; jointly lending with a bank, each keeping at least 10% (Co-lending 2.0, from Jan-2026).
CLSS / ISSCredit-Linked Subsidy Scheme (PMAY, 2015–22, upfront subsidy) and Interest Subsidy Scheme (PMAY-U 2.0, from Sep-2024, paid in five instalments).
EWS / LIG / MIG / HIGEconomically weaker section (income ≤₹3 lakh), lower (₹3–6 lakh), middle (₹6–9 lakh) and higher income groups.
DSADirect selling agent: a third party that sources loan applications for a commission.
Balance transferMoving an existing loan to another lender at a lower rate; no penalty on floating-rate individual loans.
SARFAESIThe 2002 Act that lets secured lenders enforce security without going to court.
Justified P/B(ROE − growth) ÷ (cost of equity − growth): the book multiple a lender's returns can support.
