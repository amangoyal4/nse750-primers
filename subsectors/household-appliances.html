00

The one-page view: a long runway, a short season and a thin margin

Indian appliance makers sell into one of the least-penetrated big markets in the world, and still struggle to keep eight rupees of every hundred as operating profit.

The nine companies in this report sell the machines that make an Indian home comfortable: room air-conditioners, refrigerators, washing machines, televisions, ceiling fans, water heaters, mixer-grinders, stabilisers and water purifiers. Between them they booked ₹86,136 crore of revenue in FY26 and are worth ₹2.35 lakh crore on the stock market. The business has two faces. On one side, fewer than one Indian household in ten owns an air-conditioner and perhaps one in five owns a washing machine, so the unit runway runs for decades. On the other side, the group's operating margin fell from 9.1% to 7.5% in FY26 on flat sales, and its combined EBITDA dropped 17% to ₹6,470 crore.

Four forces explain that gap between promise and profit, and the report is built around them. The first is the weather trade. Room ACs are sold in a four-month window from February to June, and brands and dealers stock up before they know how hot the summer will be. The cool, rainy summer of 2025 cut industry AC volumes by 15–20% in April–July, left about 2.5 million units sitting in the channel and pushed Voltas's AC segment into a loss in the following quarter. The hot summer of 2026 reversed the volume (industry AC sales up 20–22% in April–June) but not the margin.

The second force is a stack of costs that brands cannot fully pass on. A new energy-label table from 1 January 2026 raised AC build costs by about 7–8% on a weighted basis; copper hit roughly US$14,500 a tonne in September 2026, according to a Haier India executive; the rupee weakened to around ₹95–96 to the dollar; freight rose 4–5%. Blue Star announced list-price increases of about 13% and realised only 3–5%. With room-AC capacity at 24–26 million units against 11–13 million of annual demand, nobody can hold price for long.

The third force is ownership. Half the group's market value sits in one stock, LG Electronics India, which listed in October 2025 through an IPO in which every share was sold by its Korean parent. The parent kept 85%, collects a royalty of 2.30–2.40% of net sales and has received no dividend since listing. Whirlpool Corporation has cut its stake in Whirlpool of India from 75% to 39.76% to pay down debt in the US, and Haier sold 49% of its Indian arm to Bharti and Warburg Pincus. Global parents are treating their Indian subsidiaries as assets to monetise, and investors in the listed arms need to understand what that means for cash and control.

The fourth force is a supply-side rebuild driven by policy. India banned imports of finished ACs with refrigerants in 2020, introduced a ₹6,238 crore production-linked incentive for AC and LED components in 2021, and from 8 May 2026 capped imports of small rotary compressors at 30% of each company's FY25 import volume until March 2027. The compressor, the most expensive part of an AC or a refrigerator, has become a strategic asset: Voltas has signed a 50:50 joint venture with Atomberg for 2.8 million compressors a year.

The valuation picture is where this sub-sector becomes an investment argument rather than an industry description. LG Electronics India trades at 64 times trailing earnings, Voltas at 73 times trough earnings and Blue Star at 61 times. At the other end, IFB and Crompton trade at about 15–16 times FY26 EBITDA, and Bajaj Electricals, which lost money in FY26, at 2.2 times book. The spread reflects three things the market is pricing: how much of FY26 was weather rather than structure, how much value a parent or promoter will leave in the listed company, and whether a business has a moat beyond its brand, such as a service annuity, a projects order book or captive components.

In plain terms

Indian appliance companies sell products almost everybody will eventually buy, so the long-term volume story is not in doubt. What is in doubt is how much each company keeps. Too much factory capacity, an AC season that lives or dies on the weather, rising copper and a weak rupee mean that the typical company keeps only five to ten rupees of operating profit per hundred of sales. The share prices are mostly a bet that FY26 was the bottom of a weather cycle and that margins will climb back towards FY25's levels.

The rest of the report works through the business in order: how the money is made and where it goes (Parts 1–6), the four defining forces as deep dives (Parts 7–10), the policy rulebook, the two adjacent businesses of small appliances and cooling projects, history, and then the analytical core: a masterclass on the measures that tell you whether an appliance company is doing well (Part 15), the peer table, positioning maps, company profiles, valuation, technology, the bull and bear cases and the indicators to watch.

01

How an appliance rupee is made: assembly is cheap, the brand and the service van are not

Copper, steel and a compressor go in at one end; a branded, installed and serviced machine comes out at the other. Most of the value sits at the two ends of that chain.

An air-conditioner or a refrigerator is a box of commodities wrapped around one expensive component. Copper tubes and coils carry the refrigerant; aluminium fins shed heat; steel and plastic panels form the body; a compressor pumps the refrigerant round the loop; a motor, a circuit board and a sensor tell it when to run. For LG Electronics India, raw-material purchases were 75.25% of revenue in FY25, according to its IPO prospectus. That single number explains most of the sub-sector's economics: when three-quarters of the price goes on materials, a few points of commodity inflation can wipe out most of the profit.

Value concentrates at Steps 4 and 5. Contract manufacturing lets any brand rent Step 3, which is why assembly earns little; component ownership at Step 2 is becoming a moat again because of compressor import quotas.

Step 2 is where India's dependence on imports shows. Compressors have historically been bought from China; so are many circuit boards, the magnets in efficient fan motors and some copper tube. ICRA estimates that 50–60% of a room AC's value is now made in India, with a target of 70–75% by FY28. LG India bought 54% of its raw materials domestically in FY25, up from 50.5% in FY23; the rest came from Korea (about 22%), China (about 6%), Singapore and Thailand. Blue Star says about 40% of its AC bill of materials is imported, mostly compressors.

Step 3 has become a rented service. In room ACs, ICRA says brands own 65–70% of capacity and contract manufacturers own 30–35%, with the seven largest brands holding about 80% of brand-owned capacity. The contract makers (Amber Enterprises, PG Electroplast, Dixon Technologies and Epack Durable, none of them in this report) will build a split AC for any brand that sends a specification. Voltas makes about three-quarters of its split ACs in-house and buys the rest. LG's two plants at Noida and Pune made more than 85% of its sales; it buys entry-level fridges, semi-automatic washers, window ACs and small TVs from third parties and imports premium products such as dishwashers and washer-dryers from LG group factories. The availability of contract capacity is the single biggest reason margins are thin: a new brand, an online label or a Chinese entrant can launch a full range without building a factory.

Step 4 is where pricing power should live, and for the MNC subsidiaries it is also where the parent takes its cut. LG India pays its Korean parent 2.30% of net sales on most products and 2.40% on LCD TVs and monitors under a licence effective from January 2023. Whirlpool of India signed new agreements in October 2025 under which its brand royalty steps up from 1.0% to 1.5% over seven years, with a technology fee of 0.6% rising to 0.65%. Indian-owned brands such as Voltas, Blue Star, Crompton and V-Guard pay no such toll, but they spend more of their own money on research and marketing to compete with global technology.

Step 5 is underrated. Large appliances still need delivery, installation and service, which is why offline retail held about 78% of the market in Redseer's estimate. A split AC needs a technician to mount it, a refrigerator needs a home visit when the compressor fails, and a water purifier needs its filter changed every year. LG's network of 1,006 service centres and about 13,368 engineers is as much a barrier to entry as its plants. For Eureka Forbes the service van is the business: service and annual maintenance contracts were about 31% of FY26 revenue.

In plain terms

Making an AC is mostly buying parts and screwing them together, and anyone can hire a contract factory to do that. What is hard to copy is a brand people trust, a dealer in every small town and a technician who turns up the day after the AC breaks. That is why the profitable end of this industry is the brand and the service network, and why the few companies that also make their own compressors are suddenly in a stronger position.

Two features of Step 5 shape working capital, which matters later when we look at returns. First, appliance makers are largely financed by their suppliers: they pay for components after 60–170 days and collect from dealers sooner, so several run negative cash conversion cycles (Bajaj −79 days, Crompton −24, IFB −10). Second, the channel carries inventory on the brand's behalf before the season. When the weather disappoints, that inventory comes back to haunt the brand as discounts and schemes the next quarter, a mechanism Part 7 follows in detail.

02

The runway: a ₹6.4 lakh crore market where most homes still lack the basics

The market is growing at about 11% a year in value, and penetration of ACs and washing machines is a fraction of China's. The difficulty is that nobody agrees on the exact numbers.

The best single estimate of the market's size comes from Redseer, whose report was filed with LG India's IPO prospectus. It puts India's appliances and consumer-electronics market, excluding mobile phones, at ₹6,37,000 crore in calendar 2024, growing to about ₹10,96,500 crore by 2029, an annual growth rate of about 11%. Over 2019–24 the market grew about 7% a year. In units, Redseer counts about 495 million appliances and electronic devices sold to households in 2024, rising to about 660 million by 2029, roughly 6% a year. India is about 6% of the global appliance and electronics market today and is expected to be about 8% by 2029.

Penetration: the runway, and the argument about how long it is

Penetration, the share of households that own a product, is the core of the long-term case, and it is also where sources disagree most. For room ACs, the commonly cited range is 8–10% of households, rising sharply in cities; the India Cooling Action Plan of 2019 used about 8% for 2017-18. Some trackers give 7–12%. For refrigerators, our own primers quote anywhere from 33% (LG) to 35–40% (Whirlpool) to 38% (IFB), while Data for India, using household survey data, says "nearly half" of households owned one by 2024. Washing machines range from 13% (LG primer) to 15–20% (Whirlpool primer) to about one in five households, or roughly 60 million homes (Data for India). We use 40–50% for fridges and 15–20% for washers, and flag both as indicative.

The reason for the disagreement is mostly method. Voltas's primer cites an industry projection that AC penetration could reach about 35% by 2030. That would require roughly quadrupling the installed base in five years and should be treated as a bull case, not a forecast. 03

Why people buy: seven demand drivers, one of them the sky

Incomes, prices and housing set the trend. Heat sets the year. Tax cuts and energy labels move individual quarters.

Demand for appliances responds to seven forces, and they work on very different time-scales. Some move slowly and are reliable; others arrive suddenly and dominate a single quarter. The investment mistake is to read a quarterly swing caused by a fast driver as a change in a slow one.

1. First-time ownership

The slowest and most powerful driver is the first purchase. As a household's income crosses certain thresholds, it buys a fridge, then a washing machine, then an AC, in roughly that order. Penetration of 8–10% for ACs and 15–20% for washers means most demand for decades will come from first-time buyers rather than replacement. Data for India's survey work shows how far this has to go: only a quarter of urban homes and 5% of rural homes own the full set of TV, fridge, AC or cooler, and washing machine.

2. Heat and the weather of a single summer

For ACs and, to a smaller degree, for fans, coolers, stabilisers and refrigerators, the weather of a given summer decides the year. ICRA reported that April–July 2024 room-AC volumes rose 40–50% in a heatwave, and that April–July 2025 volumes fell 15–20% after unseasonal rain and fewer heatwave days. In April–June 2026, a hot summer lifted industry volumes 20–22%. A warming climate raises the baseline over time, but it also makes the year-to-year swing larger. Part 7 is devoted to this driver.

3. Prices, tax and energy labels

Appliance prices move with tax and regulation as well as costs. The GST overhaul effective 22 September 2025 cut the rate on room ACs under two tonnes, dishwashers and large TVs from 28% to 18%. ICRA estimated a price effect of 6–8%, about ₹2,000–3,000 per AC. The cut was announced at the start of September, so buyers delayed purchases in August and September, freezing the channel for weeks before it boosted demand. Refrigerators and washing machines were already at 18% after the July 2018 rationalisation. In the other direction, the new energy-label table for ACs from 1 January 2026 added ₹500–2,500 per unit, and three rounds of cost-led price increases followed in 2026.

4. Incomes and credit

Most large appliances in India are bought on easy monthly instalments, often at "no-cost EMI" funded by the brand or retailer, through Bajaj Finance, banks and card issuers. Availability of this credit, and its cost to the brand, matters to demand at the margin. The income-tax changes in the February 2025 Budget, which raised the zero-tax threshold under the new regime to ₹12 lakh, put more disposable income in the hands of exactly the salaried urban households that buy ACs and front-load washers.

5. Housing and electrification

Fans, pumps, water heaters, wires and lighting follow new homes and rural electrification more than the weather. A new apartment needs four to six fans, a geyser, a pump in many cases, and wiring; a newly electrified rural home starts with a fan and a light. This is why Crompton, V-Guard and Bajaj are tied to real-estate and rural cycles, and why their businesses overlap with the wires and switchgear makers covered in our electrical equipment report and the pump makers in our pumps and engines report.

6. Premiumisation and replacement

Within each category, buyers keep moving up. About 80% of room ACs sold in the 12 months to June 2025 were inverter models, which save power by running the compressor at variable speed. Efficient BLDC fans (brushless DC motors) rose from 10–15% of fan sales in 2023 to 30–35% by the end of 2025. Whirlpool's front-load washer volumes roughly doubled in FY26. Large and OLED televisions take a growing share of LG's TV mix. Premium products carry higher prices and usually higher margins, so mix shift is one of the few levers that lets a brand grow profit faster than volume.

Whatever the driver, about 78% of sales still go through physical stores: multi-brand dealers, regional chains such as Vijay Sales, and national chains such as Croma and Reliance Digital. In plain terms

People buy their first fridge, washer and AC as they get richer, and that trend is steady. But in any single year, how hot April and May turn out to be matters more for AC sales than anything else. Tax changes and new energy labels shift prices by a few thousand rupees and can freeze or rush purchases for a month or two. Good analysis keeps the slow trend and the fast swings separate.

The cost structure explains why commodity prices and the rupee matter so much, and why the three business types in this bucket earn such different margins.

The inputs, and why they all moved the wrong way in 2026

Copper is the most important single input: it goes into AC and fridge tubes and coils, motor windings and every wire V-Guard sells. A Haier India executive put the price at about US$14,500 a tonne in September 2026, against US$8,000–9,000 a year earlier; that is a quoted figure, not an exchange print, but the direction is not in dispute. Aluminium, steel and polymers also rose, with the West Asia crisis cited for freight and plastics costs. Freight rose 4–5% in Q1 FY27, according to Voltas.

The rupee multiplies all of this. At about ₹95–96 to the dollar in late 2026 (our estimate), against roughly ₹85–88 a year earlier, every imported compressor, circuit board and kilogram of copper costs about 10% more in rupees before any change in its dollar price. Since roughly 40% of an AC's bill of materials is imported, the currency alone adds about four points to material cost on a typical AC. Voltas said it absorbed 10–12% year-on-year cost inflation in Q1 FY27 and still held gross margin flat at 21.9%, through price increases, mix and sourcing. That is the best case; Blue Star, Whirlpool and LG all saw quarterly margins fall at some point in the same period.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 a customer pays for an AC, about ₹75 goes on the parts. If copper rises 50% and the rupee falls 10% in the same year, the parts bill can go up by ₹5–8, which is close to the whole operating profit. That is why appliance companies raise prices several times a year when commodities move, and why their profits fall when they cannot.

One more line belongs in the ₹100 picture, though it does not appear in the P&L: working capital. Appliance brands pay suppliers late and are paid by dealers quickly, so suppliers fund much of the business. Voltas's payable days were 172 in FY26 against 113 inventory days and 78 receivable days, giving a cash conversion cycle of 19 days. That supplier funding is valuable, but it reverses in a bad year: when sales slow, payables shrink faster than inventory and cash flow suffers. Voltas generated only ₹71 crore of operating cash flow in FY26 on ₹516 crore of EBITDA.

Who sells what, and who isn't listed: shares that depend on who is counting

Room ACs are a five-way scramble led by Voltas; fridges and washers are LG's; fans are Crompton's. And several of the biggest rivals cannot be bought on any Indian exchange.

Market share in Indian appliances comes with a trap: there are at least four ways to measure it, and companies quote whichever flatters them. Secondary (sell-out) share counts what consumers bought from stores; primary (billing) share counts what the brand shipped to its distributors. Offline value share, the basis of LG's prospectus claims, ignores online sales, which are about a fifth of the market. And category definitions vary: "inverter ACs" is not "all ACs", and "front-load washers" is not "all washers". Every share figure in this report names its basis.

Room ACs: Voltas leads, and the claims do not add up

Voltas reported a secondary market share of 17.3% for the year to date to June 2026, with a lead of four percentage points over the nearest competitor. That puts the number two at about 13%. Imports and China

India has spent six years trying to replace Chinese imports. A. The MNC full-liners

LG and Whirlpool sell the full range of large appliances under a global brand, with technology and premium products supplied by the parent. They own their plants, run national service networks and hold large cash balances. Their economics are better than the Indian AC brands' because refrigerators and washing machines are less seasonal than ACs, and because the parent's research spreads across a global volume base. But they pay for that through royalties and, as Part 9 shows, through the parent's control of capital allocation. LG's 9.8% FY26 margin, down from 12.8%, is the benchmark for this model; Whirlpool's 6.0% shows what happens when an MNC subsidiary loses share in its strongest categories and is weak in ACs.

B. The Indian AC-and-projects houses

Voltas and Blue Star make most of their consumer money from room ACs and commercial refrigeration, and a large share of their total revenue from engineering projects: Blue Star's Segment I (projects and commercial AC) was ₹6,763 crore of its ₹12,402 crore in FY26, and Voltas's projects segment about ₹4,050 crore. C. The consumer-electricals companies

Crompton, V-Guard and Bajaj sell smaller, cheaper, faster-moving products through electrical retailers, where the electrician or plumber recommends the brand. Gross margins are higher, around 30–37%, and the business is less seasonal (fans peak in summer, geysers in winter). The model's risks are different: copper prices for wires, LED price erosion in lighting, the BLDC fan disruption, and channel resets when a company changes how it reaches dealers. Bajaj's FY26 margin of 3.1% shows what a botched channel transition costs; Crompton's 10.2% is closer to the model's normal.

D. The premium niche specialist

IFB built India's front-load washing machine market with technology from Bosch-Siemens and now leads in front-loaders (about 35%), dishwashers (about 50%) and dryers (about 80%), on management's estimates. Its materials are import-heavy, so margins have stayed at about 5–6% and swing between 4.6% and 7% from quarter to quarter. A fine-blanking business making precision auto parts (about ₹930 crore of revenue) gives it ballast.

E. The annuity

Eureka Forbes sells water purifiers and vacuum cleaners, but its value lies in the 10 million-plus homes it already serves. Service, filters and annual maintenance contracts were 31.4% of FY26 revenue. Under Advent International, which bought it in 2022, the margin rose from 6–7% to about 12% in three years. Its reported return on capital (5.9%) looks poor only because the acquisition put about ₹5,500 crore of goodwill and brand value on its balance sheet.

In plain terms

Comparing Voltas's 3.6% margin with Crompton's 10.2% tells you little on its own: they sell different things in different ways. Before judging any appliance company, work out which of the five models it follows, then compare it with others in the same model. The interesting question is why companies in the same model earn such different margins.

The archetypes also explain why the market values them differently. The annuity and the MNC full-liner are valued on durability. The AC-and-projects houses are valued on what their margins will be when the weather and costs normalise. The consumer-electricals names are valued on whether they can defend their categories against disruption. The niche specialist is valued like a small manufacturer. Those different questions produce the valuation dispersion examined in Part 19.

The summer that wasn't, and the one that was Deep dive

How a cool, rainy 2025 cut a sixth off the group's operating profit, why a scorching 2026 brought the volumes back, and why it did not bring the margins with them.

The room-AC business has a calendar unlike almost any other consumer product. The selling season runs from February to June: at Voltas, the January–June quarters produced about two-thirds of FY26 AC segment revenue. Brands build and ship stock to distributors and dealers from December onwards, on a guess about how hot April and May will be. If the summer is hot, dealers sell out, re-order at full price and the brand runs its factories flat out. If the summer is cool or wet, the stock sits in warehouses, dealers stop ordering, and the brand spends the next two quarters paying for discounts and schemes to clear it. This is the weather trade, and it explains more of the sub-sector's profit swings than any other single factor.

The fiscal-year illusion

Indian companies report on an April–March year, but the AC season runs from February to June. It straddles the fourth quarter of one fiscal year (January–March, when the channel is loaded) and the first quarter of the next (April–June, when consumers buy). The cool summer of 2025 therefore hit Q1 FY26, not FY25; the hot summer of 2026 showed up partly in Q4 FY26 (pre-loading) and mostly in Q1 FY27. Comparing fiscal years blurs the cycle. The right comparison is like-for-like quarters, and ideally the April–July window ICRA uses.

2024: the heatwave that set the bar

The summer of 2024 was one of the hottest on record across north and central India. ICRA reported that room-AC volumes rose 40–50% in April–July 2024, and FY25 closed at a record 12.5–13.0 million units. Every brand ran short of stock at some point; dealers paid full price; factories that had added capacity in 2022–23 filled up. Voltas's AC segment earned an 8.6% EBIT margin in Q1 FY25 and 10.0% in Q4 FY25, as the channel loaded up at full price for what everyone assumed would be another hot summer. LG's FY25 EBITDA margin reached 12.8%, the highest in at least five years.

The good year carried the seed of the bad one. Brands and contract makers responded to the shortage by adding capacity: ICRA estimated industry capacity at 24–26 million units by September 2025, up 40–50% in two years, with another ₹4,500–5,000 crore of capex planned over two to three years. Dealers, burned by stock-outs in 2024, ordered heavily for 2025.

2025: the summer that wasn't

Then it rained. Unseasonal rain and fewer heatwave days in April–June 2025 cut industry volumes by 15–20% in April–July, according to ICRA. Channel inventory roughly doubled to about 2.5 million units by July 2025, more than two months of normal-year sales sitting in dealers' and distributors' warehouses. Brands faced a choice between cutting prices to clear stock and losing share to those that did. Most did both. Voltas's AC margin fell from 8.6% to 3.6% in Q1 FY26, and to −3.8% in Q2 FY26, a loss, as it funded schemes to move the stock.

Two policy events compounded the damage. On 3 September 2025 the GST Council announced that the rate on room ACs would fall from 28% to 18% from 22 September. Consumers who had not yet bought simply waited, so August and most of September were dead months. Then, as the new energy-label table approached on 1 January 2026, the industry had to clear stock built to the old standard. The GST cut helped in the October–December quarter, and there was some buying ahead of the label change, but FY26 still ended with ICRA's expected 11.0–11.5 million units, 10–15% below FY25.

Three things drive the swing. Volume moves revenue directly. Price moves with volume: in a hot year brands withdraw schemes and sell more expensive models; in a cool year they discount to clear stock. And gross margin moves with both, because factory overheads are spread over more or fewer units and because discounting comes straight out of margin. Fixed costs barely change. The result is that a 20% rise in volume lifts EBIT by 71%, and a 15% fall cuts it by 68%. The royalty an MNC pays is charged on revenue, not profit, so in a cool year it can turn a small profit into a loss.

The 2026 twist is that volume ran "hot" but gross margin behaved more like a "normal" or even "cool" year, because the energy-label change added 7–8% to costs and copper, the rupee and freight added more, while competition prevented brands from passing it all on. Voltas's 5.3% AC margin in Q1 FY27 sits almost exactly between the example's normal and hot cases, despite hot-year volumes.

The channel-inventory multiplier

The channel turns weather into a two-season problem. In a cool year, the 2.5 million units left in the channel mean that the next season starts with dealers holding stock. Brands' primary billing then runs below consumer sell-out until that stock clears, which is why Blue Star lost 65 basis points of primary share but only 30 basis points of secondary share in Q1 FY27. It also means a brand that reports strong primary sales in a cool year may simply be pushing stock into dealers, storing up trouble. The best single number to watch is the channel inventory each brand reports in weeks of sales: Voltas's four weeks after Q1 FY27 is healthy; anything above eight weeks heading into a summer is a warning.

A hot year with capped margins: the cost stack nobody could pass on Deep dive

Energy labels, copper, the rupee, freight and a compressor squeeze all landed in the same twelve months, in an industry with twice the capacity it needs.

Industry room-AC operating margins have fallen from about 12% historically to 7.5–8%, according to Blue Star's management. In a year with record heat, the group's first-quarter EBITDA margin rose by only about 30 basis points. The reason is a stack of five cost pressures that arrived together, and an industry structure that makes it impossible to pass them on in full.

Layer 1: the new energy label

The Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) periodically raises the efficiency a room AC must achieve to earn each star rating. The table effective 1 January 2026 meant that a model rated three-star under the old table needed better compressors, larger heat exchangers and more copper to stay three-star. Voltas estimated the cost increase at about 5% for three-star models and about 15% for five-star, a weighted 7–8% across its range. ICRA put the consumer price effect at ₹500–2,500 per unit. Brands also had to clear old-label stock before the deadline, at a discount.

Layer 2: copper

Copper makes up a large share of an AC's material cost through tubes, coils and motor windings. A Haier India executive quoted the price at about US$14,500 a tonne in September 2026, against US$8,000–9,000 a year earlier. We have not verified that against an exchange print, but every brand cited copper as the main reason for price increases in 2026. Copper also drives V-Guard's wire prices, which is why its Q1 FY27 revenue growth of 23.5% was about 14 points of price and 9 of volume.

Layer 3: the rupee

At about ₹95–96 to the dollar (our estimate; the rate was not fetched), the rupee was roughly 10% weaker than a year earlier. With about 40% of an AC's materials imported, that adds about four points to material cost before any change in dollar prices.

Layer 4: freight and plastics

Voltas said freight costs rose 4–5% in Q1 FY27, and several companies cited the West Asia crisis for higher shipping and polymer costs.

Layer 5: the compressor squeeze

From 8 May 2026, imports of small rotary compressors were capped at 30% of each company's FY25 import volume, with the balance required to meet full Indian standards certification. Domestic capacity for these compressors is, in the industry body ICEA's words, "well below market demand". The quota raised the cost and reduced the availability of compressors for brands without captive supply, just as the hot summer lifted demand. Part 10 covers this in full.

The GST cushion that cannot be used twice

One thing helped in FY26: the GST cut from 28% to 18% lowered the consumer price of an AC by 6–8% without costing the brand anything. That gave brands room to raise their own prices later without the consumer seeing an increase versus a year earlier. As the worked example in Part 3 shows, by late 2026 the label and cost increases had used up that headroom. In plain terms

Brands put up their list prices by about 13% but could make only 3–5% stick, because factories are half-empty, dealers still had cheaper old stock and the market leader was chasing share. So sales rose strongly while profit margins stayed close to their lows.

What normal looks like now

Managements still guide to recovery. Voltas targets a 7–8% AC segment margin over the medium term; Blue Star guides its unitary segment to 7–7.5% and its projects segment to at least 6.5% in FY27. Those targets are well below the 10–12% the industry earned in good years before 2024, which is itself an admission that the structure has changed. The path back depends on three things: capacity growth slowing so that utilisation rises, the compressor quota easing or domestic supply catching up, and copper and the rupee stabilising. None is in the companies' control.

The non-AC businesses show the same pattern in milder form. Crompton's fans-and-appliances segment lost about 430 basis points of margin in Q2 FY26 on copper and weak demand before recovering. Eureka's margin dipped in Q1 FY27 on gross margin and advertising, and it guided FY27 margins flat. The only businesses that grew margins comfortably through the cost spike were those with recurring revenue (Eureka over FY26 as a whole) or a strong projects order book (Blue Star in FY26).

Foreign parents cash in: LG's sale, Whirlpool's retreat, Haier's partner Deep dive

Three global appliance groups have used their Indian subsidiaries to raise money for the parent in two years. Listed minorities need to know what the parent keeps, what it charges and what it may sell next.

Between February 2024 and December 2025, three global appliance groups sold large slices of their Indian businesses. LG Electronics of Korea sold 15% of LG Electronics India in a ₹11,607 crore IPO in which not a single new share was issued. Whirlpool Corporation sold down from 75% to 39.76% of Whirlpool of India to cut its US debt. Haier sold 49% of its unlisted Indian arm to Bharti Enterprises and Warburg Pincus. The pattern is clear: global parents see their Indian subsidiaries as among their most valuable assets, and listing or selling them is the way to turn that value into cash for the parent, not a way to fund growth in India.

LG: a 100% secondary IPO

LG Electronics India listed on 14 October 2025. The offer consisted entirely of 10.18 crore existing shares sold by LG Electronics Inc., priced at the top of the ₹1,080–1,140 band, raising ₹11,607 crore. At issue, the company was valued at ₹77,380 crore, about 35 times FY25 earnings; on 16 October 2026 it was worth ₹1,16,749 crore, 51% above the issue value, at ₹1,720 a share. On listing day, LG India's market value overtook that of its Korean parent, a sign of how differently the market values Indian consumer growth and a global electronics group.

Three features of the arrangement matter for minority shareholders. First, cash extraction before listing: LG India paid dividends of 87–193% of profit in FY21–FY24, returning more than it earned in some years, then paid nothing for FY25 or FY26. The parent took its cash out before the IPO and has received none through dividends since. Second, the royalty: under a licence effective 1 January 2023, perpetual and terminable on six months' notice, LG India pays its parent 2.30% of net sales on most products and 2.40% on LCD TVs and monitors. On FY26 sales of ₹24,605 crore, that is about ₹570 crore a year (our estimate), equal to about a third of LG India's ₹1,685 crore profit after tax. (Our LG primer quotes about 1.9% of sales; that may be the effective expensed ratio for a period, but the contractual rate in the prospectus is 2.30–2.40%.)

Six years of import substitution have narrowed to one part. Whoever controls compressor supply in 2026–28 controls how many ACs it can sell in a hot summer.

The compressor is the pump at the centre of an air-conditioner or a refrigerator, pushing refrigerant around the loop. It is the single most expensive component, and in India it has historically come from China. For room ACs the relevant type is the rotary hermetic compressor; inverter models use variable-speed versions driven by a brushless DC motor and an electronic controller. India has steadily localised the rest of the AC (ICRA estimates 50–60% of value is now domestic), but compressors, along with circuit boards and semiconductors, remain the largest gap.

The quota

The quality-control order for ACs and their parts, including hermetic compressors and temperature-sensing controls, dates from 2019. It requires components to carry Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) certification. Because domestic supply could not meet demand, the government granted exemptions. The amendment order of 8 May 2026 narrowed that exemption: until 31 March 2027, each company may import rotary hermetic compressors below two tonnes without full BIS compliance only up to 30% of the quantity it imported in FY25. Anything beyond that must come from certified sources, domestic or foreign. Blue Star's primer refers to a 40% limit for refrigerator compressors; the amendment sources we reviewed mention only the 30% limit for AC compressors, so the refrigerator figure is unverified.

The industry body ICEA welcomed the extension of the exemption but warned that domestic capacity for fixed-speed compressors is "well below market demand". Blue Star's management has argued that domestic capacity covers only about half of demand and that basing the quota on FY25 imports favours companies that imported heavily in that year, rewarding past dependence. For a brand that bought most of its compressors from China in FY25, the quota is generous; for one that was already sourcing domestically and wants to grow, it offers little room.

In plain terms

India can now assemble almost all the ACs it needs, but not make all the compressors that go inside them. From May 2026 the government limited how many uncertified compressors each company may import. That makes compressors scarce and dearer, and gives an edge to companies that make their own, such as LG and Daikin, or have arranged a partner, such as Voltas with Atomberg.

Three dates matter. The quota expires on 31 March 2027, just as the 2027 AC season peaks; whether it is extended, relaxed or tightened will shape that summer's supply. The Voltas–Atomberg JV is expected to start production around FY28, and ICRA's target of 70–75% indigenisation also falls in FY28. Until then, a hot summer in 2027 could see the industry short of compressors rather than assembly capacity. That would be the first time in years that supply, rather than demand, constrained AC sales, and it would favour the companies with secured supply on both volume and price.

In plain terms

Ten years ago the scarce thing in this industry was factory space; today there is twice as much as needed. The scarce thing now is a certified compressor. Investors should ask every AC company the same question: where will your compressors come from in the summer of 2027?

Tax: a one-time gift

The September 2025 GST reform was the largest price event for ACs in years. Cutting the rate from 28% to 18% lowered the consumer price by 6–8% at a stroke, roughly ₹2,000–3,000 a unit on ICRA's estimate. It also brought ACs in line with refrigerators and washing machines, which had been at 18% since July 2018, removing a tax penalty on cooling. In the short run it hurt: the cut was announced on 3 September and effective on 22 September, so buyers waited and the channel froze for most of the second quarter of FY26. Over twelve months it gave brands room to raise their own prices without the consumer seeing an increase. As Part 8 showed, that room has now been used.

Energy labels: a recurring cost with a purpose

The Bureau of Energy Efficiency raises the bar for each star rating every few years. That is good for consumers' electricity bills and for the grid, and it supports premiumisation, since efficient models carry higher prices. But each revision forces re-engineering, adds material (more copper, larger heat exchangers, better compressors) and leaves old-label stock that must be cleared at a discount. The January 2026 table cost about 7–8% on a weighted basis at Voltas. The mandatory fan label of January 2023 had a different effect: it made the efficient BLDC fan the default premium product, opening the door for Atomberg and forcing Crompton, Havells and others to respond.

Import rules and incentives: building an industry

The 2020 ban on importing finished ACs, the 2021 PLI and the quality-control orders together moved AC assembly almost entirely to India and created a contract-manufacturing industry worth tens of thousands of crore. The 2026 quota is the latest step and the most disruptive, because it bites on a component India cannot yet make in sufficient quantity. Policy has been consistent in direction (more local value) but abrupt in timing, and brands have had to absorb the cost of each step.

E-waste and refrigerants: slow-burning costs

Under the 2022 e-waste rules, producers must collect and recycle a target share of the products they sell, with floor prices for recycling certificates. The cost is recurring and rising; Whirlpool cited incremental e-waste provisions in Q4 FY26. The global phase-down of HFC refrigerants under the Kigali amendment requires India to freeze HFC consumption from 2028, pushing brands from R-410A to R-32 and eventually R-290 (propane), each requiring redesign and, for R-290, new safety standards in factories and installation.

Rules that bear on ownership

Two securities rules matter for this sub-sector more than most. The minimum public shareholding rule requires at least 25% public float, which means LG's parent must sell down from 85% over time. The Takeover Code's 25% trigger, which requires an acquirer crossing that level to offer for another 26%, shaped the failed Advent bid for Whirlpool of India: buying the parent's stake plus the open offer would have taken Advent to about 57%. Finally, the customs notice to LG India shows that the authorities are examining whether royalties paid to parents should be included in the value of imported parts. If that view spreads, every MNC subsidiary that both imports components from its group and pays a royalty is exposed.

In plain terms

The government has cut tax on ACs, made energy rules stricter, banned imported ACs, paid companies to make parts locally and limited compressor imports. Each step has a sensible purpose, but each one moved prices or costs suddenly, and companies had to absorb the shock. Anyone analysing this industry has to keep a calendar of policy dates alongside the weather forecast.

Fans, kitchens and filters: the small-appliance trade and its three resets

Crompton, V-Guard and Bajaj sell through electrical shops, not appliance showrooms; Eureka Forbes sells a subscription disguised as a purifier. Each is in the middle of a reset.

Small appliances and consumer electricals look calmer than ACs: less seasonal, higher gross margins and lower prices per item. But the last three years have brought three disruptions to this corner of the sub-sector: the efficient BLDC fan, which let a start-up take the premium end of the market; a wave of kitchen-appliance acquisitions that have not paid off; and copper's rise, which swells revenue in wires while squeezing margins elsewhere. Eureka Forbes sits beside them as the contrast: a business whose value is its installed base.

Crompton Greaves Consumer Electricals, spun out of Crompton Greaves in 2015 and controlled by Advent and Temasek until they exited, has no promoter today; domestic institutions own 66%. How projects make, and lose, money

Projects work differently from products. The contractor bids a price for a system, usually fixed or with limited escalation, and recognises revenue as the work progresses. Margins of 5–9% are normal. Cash is tied up in retention money (a share of each bill held back until completion), in work done but not yet billed, and in bank guarantees given to clients. The risks are cost overruns on fixed-price contracts, delays by the client or other contractors, disputes over variations, and, abroad, the creditworthiness and legal environment of the client. A single bad contract can wipe out a year's segment profit, as Voltas's Gulf guarantee did.

In plain terms

Blue Star and Voltas are two businesses in one: AC makers and building-services contractors. The contracting side does not care about the weather, which helped Blue Star a lot in the cool year, and it is now growing fast because of data centres. But contracting carries its own risks: fixed prices, slow payments and the occasional disastrous contract, as Voltas found in the Gulf.

The two engines interact in ways investors sometimes miss. In a cool year, projects keep factories and installation teams busy and support group margins. In a hot year, the AC side dominates and projects look dull. Blue Star's strategy treats data centres as a structural growth market to reduce its dependence on the weather; Voltas, after years of losses on international projects, has been more selective and has let its project business shrink as a share of revenue. For valuation, a rupee of projects profit is usually valued below a rupee of branded consumer profit because it is lumpier and more capital-intensive. Blue Star's 61 times earnings suggests the market is now treating its data-centre business as something closer to a growth franchise.

Nine decades, five shocks: how the sub-sector got its shape

Indian engineering houses, then MNCs, then contract makers, then private equity, and now the parents selling out: each wave left its mark on today's nine companies.

Every company in this report carries its history in its structure. The Indian-owned names were founded as engineering and trading houses between the 1930s and 1980s; the MNCs arrived after 1991; contract manufacturers and private equity reshaped the 2010s; and the 2020s have been defined by policy-driven localisation, the weather cycle and the parents' exit.

1930s–1980s: the engineering houses

Bajaj Electricals was founded in 1938, Blue Star in 1943 by Mohan T. Advani, and Voltas in 1954 as a joint venture of Tata Sons and the Swiss trading firm Volkart Brothers. IFB began as Indian Fine Blanks in 1974, making precision parts, and moved into washing machines with Bosch-Siemens technology from about 1989–91. V-Guard was founded in Kochi in 1977 to make voltage stabilisers for a power grid that could not hold steady voltage. Eureka Forbes was set up in 1982 by Forbes & Co and Electrolux to sell vacuum cleaners door to door, and later built Aquaguard. These companies grew in a protected economy, selling to a small middle class through dealer networks and, in Eureka's case, a direct sales force.

1990s–2000s: the MNCs arrive

Liberalisation brought the global brands. Whirlpool entered India in the late 1980s and absorbed Kelvinator's Indian business, becoming Whirlpool of India. LG Electronics India was incorporated in 1997 and launched its first refrigerators and washing machines the same year; Samsung followed a similar path. Both Korean companies built factories, service networks and distribution faster than domestic rivals, and by the 2010s LG led refrigerators, washing machines and panel TVs.

2010s: inverters, labels and contract makers

Energy labels moved from voluntary to mandatory, inverter ACs went mainstream (LG's AC range was fully inverter by 2016–17), and contract manufacturers such as Amber, PG Electroplast and Dixon grew to supply many brands. Havells bought Lloyd in 2017, adding a price-aggressive AC challenger. Private equity arrived: Advent and Temasek took control of Crompton after its 2015 demerger; Voltas and Turkey's Arçelik formed Voltbek in 2017–18 to make refrigerators and washing machines.

2020–2023: import substitution and acquisitions

The 2020 AC import ban and the 2021 PLI pushed manufacturing into India. Advent bought Eureka Forbes in 2022 for about ₹4,400 crore, Crompton bought Butterfly for about ₹2,076 crore, V-Guard bought Sunflame for ₹660 crore in January 2023, and Bajaj Electricals demerged its EPC business into Bajel Projects in 2023. LG India's current royalty licence took effect in January 2023.

2024–2026: weather, costs and exits

The 2024 heatwave produced record AC sales; the cool 2025 summer produced a bust; the hot 2026 summer brought volumes back. In the same period the parents moved: Whirlpool Corporation sold down twice, LG listed its Indian arm, Haier sold half of its Indian business. And policy turned to components, with the compressor quota of May 2026.

That is why appliance results are volatile, and why the best companies are those that manage the controllable terms tightly and use mix and sourcing to soften the rest.

A worked example in round numbers. An Indian AC brand sells one million units at a net realisation of ₹30,000, giving revenue of ₹3,000 crore. At a 22% gross margin it keeps ₹660 crore after materials. Variable selling costs of 10% of revenue take ₹300 crore and fixed costs ₹200 crore, leaving segment EBIT of ₹160 crore, a 5.3% margin. If the brand were an MNC subsidiary paying a 2.3% royalty, a further ₹69 crore would go to the parent, leaving ₹91 crore (3.0%). Add ₹40 crore of treasury income, deduct 25% tax, and the Indian brand earns ₹150 crore after tax. Now raise copper enough to cut gross margin by two points: ₹60 crore disappears from EBIT, 38% of the total. All figures in this example are illustrative.

In plain terms

An appliance company's profit is what is left after it pays for parts, selling, its own overheads and, if it is foreign-owned, a fee to its parent. Because parts take three-quarters of the price, a small change in parts costs makes a big change in profit. Most of the measures below are ways of watching one term of this sum.

KPI 1. Volume growth versus the industry

What it is. The brand's unit growth in a category minus the industry's unit growth over the same period. It is the cleanest test of whether a brand is winning or losing customers, because it strips out the weather that affects everyone. Formula: brand unit growth (%) − industry unit growth (%). Worked example: in Q1 FY27, Voltas's AC volumes rose about 45% against industry growth of 20–22%, an outperformance of roughly 23–25 percentage points; Blue Star's rose 18% against 21%, an underperformance of about 3 points.

What drives it: distribution reach, having stock when the heat arrives, price points, new models and how much channel inventory the brand carried into the season. Benchmarks: good is ten points or more above the industry; average is within five points either side; weak is three points or more below. Comparison: only Voltas and Blue Star disclose AC volume growth; LG, Whirlpool and IFB do not, and the consumer-electricals companies rarely give unit data. The trap: companies often quote primary volume growth (billing to distributors) against an industry figure based on secondary sales (consumer purchases). A brand that clears last year's channel stock will show weak primary growth even while consumers buy plenty of its product.

KPI 2. Market share, and which share

What it is. The brand's sales as a percentage of the category's. Formula: brand units (or value) ÷ category units (or value), measured either at sell-out (secondary) or at billing (primary), offline or total. Drivers: distribution, price ladder, service reputation, availability in peak weeks and launch cadence. KPI 3. Net realisation per unit

What it is. The average amount the brand actually receives for each unit, after GST, dealer and distributor margins, and promotional schemes. It differs sharply from the price on the shop tag. Formula: segment revenue ÷ units shipped; or, built up, consumer price ÷ (1 + GST) × (1 − channel margin) − schemes. Worked example: a ₹40,000 split AC including 18% GST is ₹33,898 before tax; after a 10% channel margin the brand receives about ₹30,500, and after a ₹500 consumer scheme about ₹30,000.

Drivers: mix (inverter, five-star, larger tonnage), scheme intensity (high in cool years), price increases and their realisation. Benchmarks: the right test is the trend, not the level: realisation growth of 3% or more a year from mix alone is good, flat is average, falling is weak. Comparison: none of the nine discloses per-unit realisation; Voltas and Blue Star give enough on volume and segment revenue to approximate it, the rest do not (n/a). The trap: revenue growth can come from price (V-Guard's Q1 FY27: about 14 points price, 9 volume) or from mix rather than more customers. In a commodity upcycle, revenue growth overstates real growth.

KPI 4. Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after materials and components. It is the first place commodity, currency and pricing pressure shows up. Formula: (revenue − materials consumed − purchases of traded goods ± change in inventories) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Voltas's Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹4,674 crore at a 21.9% gross margin left about ₹1,024 crore after materials, the same 21.9% as a year earlier despite 10–12% cost inflation.

Drivers: copper, steel and aluminium prices; the rupee; how much of the bill of materials is localised; the lag before price increases take effect; mix. Benchmarks: large appliances, good 28% or more, average 22–28%, weak below 22%; consumer electricals, good 35% or more, average 30–35%, weak below 30%. Comparison: Voltas 21.9% (weak end of average), V-Guard about 36–37% (good); Eureka's is about 50% or more on an unverified estimate; LG, Blue Star, Whirlpool, Crompton, IFB and Bajaj do not report a consistent figure in the material we reviewed (n/a). The trap: companies define gross margin differently. Some include traded goods bought from contract makers; some exclude freight-in; screener's "operating profit" skips gross margin altogether. Compare a company with itself over time before comparing it with others.

In plain terms

Gross margin tells you how much of each sale is left once the parts are paid for. If it is falling while sales rise, the company is either discounting or not keeping up with rising costs. For an AC maker, 22% is thin and 28% is healthy; for a fan or wire maker, the healthy level is above 35%.

KPI 5. Segment EBIT margin

What it is. The operating profit of a reported business segment as a share of its revenue. For the multi-segment companies it is far more informative than the group margin. Formula: segment result (before unallocated costs, interest and tax) ÷ segment revenue. Worked example: Voltas's unitary cooling segment earned ₹202 crore on ₹3,794 crore in Q1 FY27, a 5.3% margin; Blue Star's unitary products segment earned ₹49.7 crore on ₹1,689 crore, 2.9%.

Drivers: everything in the equation, at segment level. Benchmarks for AC segments: good 8% or more, average 5–8%, weak below 5%. What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest, tax and other income, as a share of revenue. It is the standard cross-company comparison. KPI 7. Channel inventory

What it is. The stock of a brand's products held by distributors and dealers, expressed in weeks or days of consumer sales. It is the leading indicator of next quarter's primary sales and pricing. Formula: units in trade ÷ average weekly sell-out. Worked example: if a brand's dealers hold 200,000 ACs and sell 50,000 a week, channel inventory is four weeks. If the summer turns cool and weekly sales halve to 25,000, the same stock becomes eight weeks, and the brand's next orders will fall until it clears.

Drivers: the weather relative to the pre-season guess, pre-loading ahead of price increases or label changes, and the brand's discipline in not stuffing the channel. Benchmarks heading into a season: four weeks or less is good, four to eight average, more than eight weeks weak. Comparison: Voltas reported about four weeks after Q1 FY27 against about 45 days during FY26; Blue Star about 45 days at trade level and about 60 days including its own warehouses (August 2026); industry stock in July 2025 was about 2.5 million units, roughly eleven to twelve weeks of a normal year's average sales. Others do not disclose (n/a). The trap: companies quote channel inventory on different bases (trade only, or including their own depots) and at different points in the season. Four weeks in July means something very different from four weeks in January.

KPI 8. Cash conversion cycle

What it is. The number of days between paying for materials and collecting cash from customers. A negative figure means suppliers are financing the business. Formula: receivable days + inventory days − payable days. Worked example: Voltas in FY26 had 78 receivable days and 113 inventory days but 172 payable days: 78 + 113 − 172 = 19 days. On ₹14,244 crore of revenue, each day of cycle ties up about ₹39 crore of cash.

Drivers: supplier credit terms, dealer credit and channel financing, inventory build before the season, and the share of projects (which tie up cash in retention money and unbilled work). Benchmarks: good below zero, average 0–30 days, weak above 45 days. Comparison: Bajaj (−79 days), Crompton (−24) and IFB (−10) are supplier-funded; Eureka (80) and V-Guard (60) carry the most inventory. The trap: the cycle is measured at the year-end balance sheet date, 31 March, which for AC makers falls in the middle of the pre-season build. It can also flatter a company in a weak year: Bajaj's −79 days partly reflects a working-capital release during its reset rather than a structural advantage.

KPI 10. Return on capital employed

What it is. Operating profit as a percentage of the capital (equity plus debt) used to earn it. It is the best single measure of business quality. Formula: EBIT ÷ (net worth + borrowings), usually averaged over the year. Worked example: LG India's ROCE of 32.3% means every ₹100 of capital earned about ₹32 of pre-tax operating profit; at Voltas's 9.0%, ₹100 earned ₹9.

Drivers: margin × asset turnover. Appliance makers turn capital over quickly, so even modest margins can give good returns; cash piles and acquisition goodwill depress them. Benchmarks: good above 20%, average 12–20%, weak below 12%. Comparison: LG stands out at 32%; Blue Star, IFB, V-Guard and Crompton cluster at 18–21%; Whirlpool, Voltas, Eureka and Bajaj are below 12%. The trap: ROCE is distorted by accounting as much as by operations. Eureka's 5.9% reflects about ₹5,500 crore of goodwill and brand value from Advent's 2022 purchase; Whirlpool's 10.7% is diluted by about ₹2,600 crore of cash that earns treasury rates; Voltas's includes its investment in the loss-making Voltbek JV. Strip out goodwill and surplus cash before judging.

KPI 12. Localisation and capacity utilisation

What they are. Two supply-side measures. Localisation is the share of materials bought domestically; utilisation is production as a share of installed capacity. Worked example: LG India bought 54% of its raw materials in India in FY25 and produced 1.114 crore units against capacity of 1.451 crore, 77% utilisation (84% in Q1 FY26).

Drivers: the PLI scheme, quality-control orders and compressor supply for localisation; the season, contract manufacturing and capacity additions for utilisation. The anatomy shows the industry's central problem in one table. A well-run AC plant at 70% utilisation earns about 21% pre-tax on its capital, a good return. At 90% it earns about 34%. Until demand catches up with capacity or capacity is retired, the average AC line in India will earn closer to the left-hand column than the middle one, whatever the weather in a given year. The companies that do better are those running their own plants fuller (LG at 77–84%) or buying from contract makers rather than building (Voltas's quarter of split ACs from outside suppliers).

In plain terms

A factory has costs that do not change much whether it is busy or idle. Run it at nine-tenths of capacity and it makes an excellent return; run it at half and it barely earns its keep. India's AC industry built so much capacity after the 2024 heatwave that the average factory is now about half-used, which caps everyone's returns.

How to use the scorecard. The benchmarks are ranges, not targets, and three cautions apply. First, compare within an archetype: a 5.7% EBITDA margin is weak for a fan maker but close to normal for a premium washer specialist with imported components. Second, compare the same point in the season: an AC maker's channel inventory, working capital and quarterly margin all look different in March than in September, so set each quarter against the same quarter a year earlier. Third, look for agreement between measures. A company whose volume beats the industry, whose gross margin holds and whose channel inventory falls is winning on substance; one whose primary sales beat the industry while channel inventory rises is borrowing from next quarter. The single most informative pairing in this sub-sector is AC segment margin against volume growth relative to the industry: together they show whether a brand is buying share with price or earning it with product and reach. Definition traps, collected

  • "Household appliances" is four businesses. Large appliances, cooling plus projects, consumer electricals and a service annuity. Never quote a sector margin without naming the archetype.
  • Market share has at least four bases. Secondary or primary, offline or total, volume or value, category or sub-category. LG's "leader" claims are offline value; Voltas's 17.3% is secondary volume.
  • The fiscal year splits the AC season. The cool 2025 summer hit Q1 FY26; the hot 2026 summer hit Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27. Compare like-for-like quarters, not fiscal years.
  • Primary versus secondary sales. Billing to distributors can run well ahead of or behind consumer purchases; channel inventory is the gap.
  • Price versus volume growth. In a copper upcycle, revenue growth in wires and appliances is partly price. V-Guard's 23.5% in Q1 FY27 was about 14 points price.
  • One-offs in EBITDA. Labour-code charges (Blue Star ₹38.8 Cr, Eureka ~₹40 Cr), gratuity reversals (Eureka ₹19.5 Cr gain), project guarantee losses (Voltas ₹433 Cr) and classification differences (Voltas Q1 FY27 ₹266 Cr company vs ₹228 Cr screener).
  • Exceptional items below the line. Crompton's ₹716 Cr Butterfly impairment turned an adjusted ₹502 Cr profit into a ₹231 Cr reported loss; screener files it in "other income".
  • Joint-venture losses. Voltbek's losses appear as a share of JV loss below EBITDA at Voltas, so Voltas's EBITDA overstates the economics of the whole group.
  • Revenue basis. IFB's sales are ₹5,619 Cr; its "total income" of ₹5,653 Cr includes other income. Our IFB primer used the latter.
  • Net cash basis. Screener shows investments minus borrowings but no cash line. Blue Star is "net debt ₹353 Cr" on that basis and "net cash ₹900 Cr" on the company's June 2026 statement; Whirlpool's ₹2,607 Cr cash does not appear at all. Name the basis.
  • ROCE distortions. Goodwill (Eureka) and surplus cash (Whirlpool, LG) depress reported returns; JV investments (Voltas) muddy them.
  • Royalty rate versus royalty expense. The contractual rate (LG 2.30–2.40%) and the expensed ratio in a given year can differ.

How to read an appliance company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, split into volume and price (minute 1). Pull headline revenue growth, then look for unit volume or management's split. In an AC company, compare volume growth with the industry figure from the same call. Red flag: revenue up mainly on price in a hot quarter.
  2. Segment revenue and margin (minutes 2–3). For Voltas and Blue Star, read the unitary cooling and projects segments separately; for V-Guard all four segments; for Crompton the ECD, lighting and Butterfly lines. Red flag: AC segment margin below 5% in the first quarter of a hot year.
  3. Gross margin (minute 3). Compare with the same quarter last year. Ask whether price increases kept up with copper, the rupee and freight. Red flag: gross margin down more than one point while revenue grows.
  4. EBITDA, cleaned (minute 4). Remove labour-code charges, gratuity reversals, guarantee write-offs and impairments. Check the company-basis figure against screener's. Red flag: an "adjusted" figure that excludes the same type of item every quarter.
  5. Channel inventory (minute 5). Find the weeks-of-stock comment in the call or presentation. Red flag: more than eight weeks heading into a summer, or management refusing to give a number.
  6. Market share (minute 6). Note the basis (secondary or primary). Red flag: primary share falling faster than secondary share for two quarters running (the channel is cutting orders).
  7. Below the line (minute 7). Joint-venture share (Voltbek), other income on cash, exceptional items, tax rate. Red flag: profit growth driven by treasury income rather than operations.
  8. Cash and working capital (minute 8). In half-year and annual results, check operating cash flow against EBITDA and the movement in inventory and payables. Red flag: payables shrinking while inventory rises.
  9. Order book and inflow (minute 9, projects only). Order book, quarterly inflow and the share from data centres or international work. Red flag: international provisions or guarantee invocations.
  10. Guidance and price actions (minute 10). Note management's outlook for the next quarter, segment margin targets and any price increase announced. Red flag: guidance cut while list prices are raised, which usually means the increases are not sticking.
In plain terms

Read appliance results in this order: how much more did they sell (and how much was just higher prices); how much did each business earn; did they keep up with rising costs; how much stock is sitting with dealers; and did the profit turn into cash. Those five answers tell you most of what matters in any quarter.

Who is winning: share at Voltas, margin at LG, momentum at V-Guard

Winning means different things in a cycle like this one: taking share, protecting margin, or turning a weak business around. Two maps and a scorecard sort the nine.

There is no single winner in household appliances in 2026. Voltas is winning the AC volume battle, and paying for it in margin. LG is winning on profitability, recovering faster than anyone from the FY26 trough. V-Guard had the strongest quarter in the group. Blue Star is winning in projects and losing ground in ACs. Whirlpool is losing on almost every measure while its parent decides what to do with it. The maps below show where each company sits.

Voltas: winning share, at a price

Voltas sold one million ACs in 81 days, grew AC volumes about 45% against 20–22% for the industry, and took its secondary share to 17.3%, four points ahead of the next brand. By the volume test of Part 15 it was the clear winner of the 2026 summer. But its AC margin of 5.3% remained well below the 8.6% of two years earlier, its projects business shrank and booked a ₹433 crore Gulf loss, and Voltbek still loses money. It chose share over margin, which is a reasonable choice for a market leader in a business where scale lowers unit costs, but the profit benefit lies in the future.

LG: winning on margin

LG India recovered faster than any of the nine: from a 4.8% EBITDA margin in Q3 FY26 to 12.5% in Q1 FY27, with profit up 27%. Its in-house compressors, full factories (77–84% utilisation) and a strong TV quarter (home entertainment revenue up 22.3%) gave it insulation the AC specialists lacked. It remains the clear leader in refrigerators and washing machines.

V-Guard and IFB: momentum

V-Guard grew revenue 23.5% and profit 76% in Q1 FY27, with margin up two points to 10.5%. Copper-led price increases in wires and hot-weather demand for stabilisers and inverters did the work. IFB grew 18.5% with margin up a point. Both had weak bases, but both are moving the right way.

Blue Star, Whirlpool and Bajaj: mixed or losing

Blue Star is winning in data centres and projects, where its order book grew 13.5%, but lost AC share in the hot quarter and saw its unitary margin halve to 2.9%. Whirlpool grew revenue 12% but saw margin fall 3.6 points and profit fall 29%, the worst profit performance among the nine in the quarter. Bajaj turned a profit after a loss-making FY26, but revenue barely grew. Crompton and Eureka were steady: double-digit growth, stable margins.

Technology and disruption: motors, refrigerants and the rented factory

The biggest disruptions in this sub-sector came not from smart features but from efficient motors, contract manufacturing and new refrigerants.

Appliance technology changes slowly, but when it changes it reshuffles market share. The inverter compressor transformed ACs over the 2010s; the BLDC motor is doing the same to fans; contract manufacturing has lowered the barrier to entry across categories; and the refrigerant phase-down will force another round of redesign. Connected and "AI" features get the marketing attention, but so far they are table stakes in premium ranges rather than sources of advantage.

Efficient motors: from inverter ACs to BLDC fans

Inverter ACs run the compressor at variable speed instead of switching it on and off, cutting power use substantially. They are now about 80% of AC units sold. The same idea applied to fans, the brushless DC (BLDC) motor, cut a ceiling fan's power draw from 70–75 watts to 28–35 watts. The mandatory fan energy label from January 2023 made the BLDC fan the obvious premium choice. Atomberg, a start-up with electronics and motor-control know-how, took about 60% of the BLDC segment before the established fan makers responded; Crompton has since built its own "Nucleus" BLDC platform and grew BLDC sales 45% in Q1 FY27. The same motor and controller expertise now enters compressors through the Voltas–Atomberg JV. The lesson is that in this industry, a shift in the core motor technology can let a newcomer leapfrog decades of distribution advantage.

Contract manufacturing: the rented factory

The second disruption is organisational. Contract manufacturers (Amber, PG Electroplast, Dixon, Epack) hold 30–35% of AC capacity and make for many brands. That lets e-commerce labels, retailer private labels and Chinese brands launch full ranges without building plants, and lets established brands add capacity without capex. Our Whirlpool primer's thesis is that this compresses entry barriers for everyone. It also shifts value: as contract makers integrate into components (heat exchangers, motors, PCBs and potentially compressors), they capture margin that used to sit with the brands. The listed contract makers are not in this bucket, but their growth is a direct cost to the brands' pricing power.

Refrigerants: the next redesign

Under the Kigali amendment to the Montreal Protocol, India will freeze consumption of hydrofluorocarbon (HFC) refrigerants from 2028 and then reduce it. The industry has already moved from R-22 to R-410A and R-32; the next step is towards R-290 (propane), which has very low global-warming potential but is flammable, requiring changes to design, factory safety and installation practice. Each transition adds cost and favours companies with research depth: the MNCs (through their parents) and the larger Indian brands.

Connected appliances and "AI"

Wi-Fi-connected ACs and washing machines, app control and "AI" modes (LG's ThinQ, Voltas's "AI Vertis" range) are now standard in premium ranges. They help a brand hold a price point but have not shifted market share, and they add electronics cost and import dependence (semiconductors). We see them as necessary rather than differentiating.

Climate, commercial cooling and water

A hotter climate raises baseline demand for cooling but also year-to-year volatility, which is the weather trade of Part 7. In commercial cooling, heat pumps, district cooling and data-centre liquid cooling may shift value away from conventional chillers; Blue Star's 10–12% share of data-centre chillers puts it at the centre of that change. For water purifiers, better municipal piped water under the Jal Jeevan Mission could, over many years, reduce the need for point-of-use purifiers in some areas, while grey-market filters attack Eureka's service annuity today.

Bull vs bear: a long runway against a crowded, costly road

The optimistic and pessimistic cases rest on the same facts: low penetration, overcapacity, cost pressure and foreign ownership. They differ on which wins out over the next three years.

The bull case for Indian household appliances is about demand and time; the bear case is about supply and margins. Both are grounded in evidence, which is why the stocks swing so much on each quarter's data.

The bull case

  • Penetration runway: ACs in 8–10% of homes, washers in 15–20%, against 60%-plus AC ownership in China. Incomes, credit and a hotter climate all push the same way.
  • FY26 was a weather trough: Q1 FY27 revenue +15% and profit +26% for the nine; channel inventory back to about four weeks at Voltas.
  • Premiumisation: inverter ACs, BLDC fans, front-load washers and large TVs carry higher prices and margins.
  • Localisation turns into a moat: the compressor quota and PLI reward companies with captive supply (LG, Voltas from FY28) and should eventually discipline new capacity.
  • Second engines: Blue Star's data-centre business and Eureka's service annuity grow regardless of the weather.
  • Balance sheets: the nine hold about ₹10,000 crore of net cash between them on our mixed basis; none needs to raise money.

The bear case

  • Overcapacity: AC capacity of 24–26 million units against 11–13 million of demand, plus contract makers selling to anyone, caps pricing for years.
  • Margins capped even in a hot year: industry AC margins down from about 12% to 7.5–8%; list-price increases of 13% realised at 3–5%.
  • The cost stack: copper (about US$14,500/t, quoted), a rupee near ₹95–96 to the dollar, energy-label revisions and compressor scarcity.
  • Parents' interests: rising royalties, no LG dividend, an LG sell-down still to come, a Whirlpool parent that may sell more, an Advent exit at Eureka.
  • Valuation: about 60 times adjusted earnings for the group, with Voltas at 73 times on a 9% ROCE.
  • Disruption: Atomberg in fans, Haier with Indian capital, Samsung and Daikin unlisted and free to price aggressively.

Where do we come out? The demand side of the bull case is very likely right over a decade; the question is the next three years. The evidence from the hot 2026 summer is the most informative data point available: volume recovered strongly, margins only partly. That suggests the bear's supply-side argument is the binding constraint for now. For margins to return towards FY25 levels, at least one of three things needs to happen: capacity growth slows, compressor supply eases, or copper and the rupee reverse. None is in the companies' hands.

The two cases also apply differently across the archetypes. The bull case is strongest for businesses with a moat beyond the AC cycle (LG's scale and compressors, Blue Star's data centres, Eureka's annuity, Crompton's fan leadership if it holds BLDC). The bear case bites hardest on AC-heavy companies without captive components and on the MNC subsidiary whose parent is a seller. Investors can be bullish on Indian appliance demand and still selective about which stocks capture it.

In plain terms

The optimists say India will buy many more appliances for many years, FY26 was a one-off bad summer and margins will recover. The pessimists say there are too many factories, costs keep rising and foreign parents take too much. The hot 2026 summer showed both are partly right: sales came back quickly, profits only slowly.
