00

The one-page view: the state sets the cost, the weather sets the price

Four listed companies, four different businesses, one administered squeeze — and, for once, a genuine shortage working in the mills' favour.

An Indian sugar mill buys its only raw material at a price fixed by the government, sells most of its output into a market the government polices with floors, quotas, stock limits and export bans, and sells its second product, ethanol, to state-owned oil companies at prices the government also sets. Its profit is what is left in the gap between those administered numbers, multiplied by however much cane the monsoon delivers. Every listed sugar company is, at bottom, a bet on how wide that gap will be.

In October 2026 the gap is being pulled in two directions at once. On the cost side, the Centre's Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) for cane rose again, to ₹365 per quintal for SS 2026-27, and Uttar Pradesh is expected to raise its own State Advised Price (SAP) before the state election due in early 2027. On the floor side, the minimum selling price (MSP) of sugar has been ₹31/kg since February 2019, and the price the oil companies pay for ethanol made from cane juice and B-heavy molasses has not moved since ESY 2022-23. That combination — a ratcheting input price and frozen output floors — is the "price sandwich" that runs through this report.

What rescues the arithmetic in 2026 is scarcity. India ended SS 2025-26 (30 September 2026) with roughly 4 million tonnes of sugar in stock, the lowest in nearly a decade, after a season in which production disappointed. The 2026 monsoon then finished at about 87% of its long-period average, the weakest since 2015. Ex-mill sugar, which traded at ₹39–41.5/kg through FY26, jumped to ₹45–49/kg by August. The government's response has been "food first": it prohibited exports from 13 May 2026, imposed dealer stock limits from 1 August, and on 16 October 2026 issued an ethanol tender for ESY 2026-27 that accepts no ethanol made from cane juice, syrup or B-heavy molasses at all.

That last decision reverses the policy that defined the industry from 2018 to 2025. The ethanol programme was sold as a way to turn surplus sugar into fuel; it reached 20% blending (E20) in 2025-26, but by then grain supplied about 72.5% of the 1,048.4 crore litres allocated. Mills built distilleries for a cane-based future and now find the oil companies prefer maize and rice. Some, like Triveni, had already switched; others, like Balrampur, sent more than half their cane down the B-heavy route in FY26 and must now re-plan.

The four listed members of this sub-sector make the same product in very different wrappers:

  • E.I.D.-Parry is a Murugappa-group holding company whose consolidated accounts are about 82% Coromandel International by revenue and 94% by EBITDA — the fertiliser maker it owns 55.58% of. Its own sugar, distillery, consumer and nutraceutical businesses lost money in FY26 (standalone PAT −₹708 Cr), and it closed its Kakinada refinery subsidiary on 31 March 2026.
  • Balrampur Chini Mills is the largest listed UP integrated miller (80,000 TCD, 1,050 KLPD of distilleries) and is commissioning an 80,000-tonne-a-year polylactic acid (PLA) bioplastics plant — the only product in the group without an administered price.
  • Triveni Engineering is a UP sugar, alcohol (including country liquor) and water company that demerged its high-margin gears business into Triveni Power Transmission (listed 12 October 2026), keeping 29.88% of it.
  • Shree Renuka Sugars is a Wilmar-controlled port refiner and miller with negative net worth, a US-dollar loan, and an interest bill (₹736 Cr in FY26) that its operations cannot cover.

Five forces shape everything that follows, and the report is built around them. First, the policy price sandwich: cane up every year, sugar and cane-route ethanol floors frozen. Second, the ethanol pivot reversed: from "divert sugar into fuel" (2018–2025) to "keep sugar for food, make fuel from grain" (2026). Third, weather and the sugar balance sheet: a ten-year-low stock, a weak monsoon, an export ban and world white-sugar prices that now sit above Indian prices. Fourth, integration and by-products: distilleries, bagasse power at a regulated tariff, Triveni's liquor brands and Balrampur's PLA plant. Fifth, capital structure and corporate form: a holdco discount at EID, a demerger at Triveni, a parent guarantee at Renuka.

In plain terms

Strip out EID Parry's fertiliser subsidiary and the listed Indian sugar business lost about ₹663 Cr in FY26, even though it sold more sugar at decent prices. Cane got dearer, ethanol prices did not move, one refinery was shut and another drowned in interest. FY27 should look better because sugar prices jumped after June on stock that was produced at about ₹37–38/kg, but that is a one-season windfall, and the government is already leaning against it.

Three dates matter in the next four weeks. Shree Renuka's board meets on 30 October 2026 to approve Q2 FY27 (July–September) results; Balrampur, EID Parry and Triveni have not announced dates but reported Q2 in November last year, so expect late October to mid-November. Uttar Pradesh's SAP for SS 2026-27 is due around the same time (last year's came on 29 October). And the first ESY 2026-27 ethanol allocations, from 1 November, will show how much cane-based ethanol the system really takes when only C-heavy molasses is allowed.

01

One tonne of cane: five products, three buyers, two prices that matter

How a mill earns, from the farmer's field to the oil company's depot — and where in the chain each rupee is decided.

A sugar mill is a seasonal chemical plant attached to a very large warehouse. For roughly 140–170 days a year (November to April in Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra, earlier in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu) it crushes cane around the clock; for the rest of the year it sells the sugar it stored, runs its distillery on molasses and grain, and repairs its boilers. Money is made in three places: the share of each tonne of cane that becomes sugar (recovery), the value of what is made from the rest (molasses, bagasse, press mud), and the timing of sales against a price that moves through the year.

The farmer's price is set before the crop is cut; the sugar price is set by the market months later. The mill carries that timing risk on a balance sheet full of inventory.

Step 1 is where the industry differs most from any other agri-processing business. Under the UP Sugarcane (Regulation of Supply and Purchase) Act and its equivalents, each mill gets a reserved cane area: farmers in it must supply that mill, and the mill must buy all the cane they register, at the state price. The mill cannot shop around for cheaper cane, and it cannot refuse a bumper crop. In return it gets a captive supply that no new entrant can poach. This is why mills invest in farmer development — seed varieties, drip irrigation, pest control — because recovery gains on their own command area are the one lever on raw-material cost they actually control.

Step 3 — the fork — is the decision that policy has turned on and off. A mill that crystallises as much sugar as possible leaves about 45 kg of "C-heavy" molasses per tonne, enough for 10–11 litres of ethanol. A mill that stops crystallising early leaves sugar-rich "B-heavy" molasses: it gives up roughly 20–25 kg of sugar to make about 20–25 litres of ethanol. A mill that ferments the cane juice or syrup directly makes no sugar at all and gets 70–80 litres. The rule of thumb is that every tonne of sugar forgone yields about 600 litres of ethanol. Section 05 works through which route pays at which prices; the short answer for 2026 is that sugar pays better than either diversion route at today's sugar price and today's frozen ethanol prices.

Three buyers dominate. Sugar goes mostly to traders and institutional buyers — and institutional demand (soft drinks, biscuits, confectionery, dairy, pharma) is now 60–65% of consumption, which is why the CACP has floated "dual pricing" with a higher price for industrial users. In plain terms

A mill pays roughly ₹3,950 for a tonne of cane in Uttar Pradesh and sells what comes out of it for roughly ₹5,600–5,700. About four-fifths of that is sugar. Whatever is left after paying for chemicals, power, people and repairs — typically ₹600–750 a tonne at the best UP mills — is the operating profit. Small changes in any of the big numbers move that thin layer a lot.

Where is value captured? The farmer captures the cane price, which is administered and has only ever gone up. The government captures the sugar price through the floor, release quotas, stock limits and export permissions, and it captures the ethanol price outright. The oil companies capture the foreign-exchange saving on imported crude (they have spent more than ₹1.7 lakh crore on ethanol over three supply years). What is left for the mill is recovery above the state average, by-product integration, product mix (refined and pharma-grade sugar, institutional premiums), and inventory timing. The best companies in this report are the ones that do all four well; the weakest have bet on a single lever that policy then removed.

02

Size and the three calendars: a ₹2 lakh crore pool counted three ways

India is the world's largest sugar consumer and second-largest producer; the numbers only make sense once you know which year they belong to.

India produced about 28 million tonnes (MT) of sugar, net of diversion to ethanol, in SS 2025-26, and consumed about the same. At an ex-mill price of about ₹40/kg that is a ₹1.1 lakh crore market before tax, with roughly the same amount paid to farmers for cane. Fuel ethanol adds about ₹70,000 crore a year at ESY 2025-26 prices and volumes (1,048 crore litres × ₹66.61 weighted average), though most of it is now made from grain. Add potable alcohol and bagasse power and the whole pool is roughly ₹1.9–2.1 lakh crore, an INDICATIVE estimate. The four listed members' sugar-related revenue (₹28,775 Cr without Coromandel) is about 14–15% of it.

Exports are the safety valve for a surplus and are always discretionary. India shipped a record 11.1 MT in 2021-22, when world prices rose and no subsidy was needed, then almost nothing once domestic stocks tightened. In SS 2025-26 the problem was reversed: quota was available (1.5 MT in November 2025 plus 0.5 MT in February 2026 per the trade press; Balrampur's annual report cites 1.58 MT) but world prices were below Indian parity for most of the season and only about 0.7–0.8 MT left the country before DGFT Notification 16/2026-27 prohibited exports on 13 May 2026. Balrampur itself physically exported only 9,030 tonnes of a 52,057-tonne quota and swapped the rest for domestic sales.

The growth story has therefore flipped. From 2018 to 2024 the industry's volume growth came from diverting more sugar into ethanol — diversion rose from about 0.3 MT to about 4 MT. From 2025 the government wants the sugar for food and has given the fuel business to grain. Sugar consumption grows at 1–2%; cane-route ethanol has a ceiling set in a Delhi tender; exports are shut. The listed mills' volume growth over the next two seasons will come from recovery gains, crushing-day gains and new products (PLA, CBG, aviation fuel feedstock), not from more cane.

In plain terms

Sugar demand barely grows, sugar supply jumps around with the rain, and the government decides how much of the swing is soaked up by exports or ethanol. A mill's results depend less on "the market" than on which of those valves Delhi opens in a given year — and in 2026 it has closed both.

03

The price sandwich: cane up every year, floors frozen since 2019

Force 1. The Centre and the states set the input price on a ratchet; the output floors have not moved. Margins depend on the market price staying far above the floor.

Every Indian sugar mill operates between two administered prices. Underneath is the cane price: the Centre's FRP, set each year by the Cabinet on the advice of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP), and, in Uttar Pradesh and a few northern states, a higher State Advised Price set by the state cabinet. On top is a set of floors: the sugar MSP (₹31/kg) and the ethanol price for each feedstock. The cane price has risen in almost every season; the floors have not. When the market price of sugar sits well above the MSP, the sandwich is tolerable. When it does not — as in 2018-19, when ex-mill sugar fell to ₹25–26/kg — the industry runs up cane arrears and Delhi intervenes.

The MSP is the most striking frozen number. It was introduced on 7 June 2018 at ₹29/kg, at the bottom of the 2017–2019 glut, to stop mills dumping sugar below cost, and raised to ₹31 on 14 February 2019. It has not moved in seven and a half years while cane has risen a third. ISMA has asked for at least ₹40.2/kg, its estimate of the cost of production at the current FRP; estimates for SS 2025-26 put the all-in cost at about ₹41.7/kg. No revision was found as of 24 October 2026. Today the MSP is irrelevant to prices — the market is at ₹45–49 — but it is the number that would bind in the next glut, and a glut becomes more likely if the zero-diversion ethanol policy persists into a good-monsoon year.

Policy has also learned to manage price from the quantity side. The effect, since 2019, has been to keep ex-mill sugar in a band of roughly ₹36–42/kg — a band wide enough to keep good mills profitable and narrow enough to keep the CPI quiet. The 2026 spike to ₹45–49 is outside that band, which is why the stock-limit order arrived within weeks and why investors should expect more intervention, not less, if prices hold.

Ethanol: the pivot that made the industry, now reversed

Force 2. From 2018 to 2025 Delhi paid mills to turn sugar into fuel. In 2026 it wants the sugar back and buys its fuel from grain.

The ethanol blending programme is the single biggest reason Indian sugar companies went from chronic losses in the early 2010s to steady mid-single-digit margins after 2018. It gave mills a second customer with an administered price, three-week payment terms and growing volumes, and it gave the government a way to drain surplus sugar without paying export subsidies. Blending rose from about 1.5% in ESY 2013-14 to 10% in June 2022 (five months ahead of target) and to 20% — E20 — in ESY 2025-26. The programme succeeded so well that it outgrew cane.

In plain terms

The government built the ethanol programme to soak up surplus sugar, then priced grain ethanol higher than cane ethanol to make sure it never depended on the sugar crop. It worked: India hit 20% blending with mostly maize and rice. Now that sugar is short, Delhi has simply told mills to stop making fuel from cane juice. Their distilleries still run, but on molasses left over after making sugar, or on grain bought in the market.

What comes next for demand? E20 is achieved and EID's chief executive expects it to stay at 20% "for the foreseeable future". Break-even sugar prices

Setting the contributions equal gives the sugar price below which each diversion route pays. Turned around, the arithmetic says what the ethanol price would have to be. At ₹41/kg sugar, B-heavy ethanol would need about ₹65/L and juice ethanol about ₹74/L to be worth making; at ₹45/kg sugar, about ₹69/L and ₹80/L. In plain terms

A mill makes about ₹1,100 more per tonne of cane by producing sugar than by turning the juice straight into fuel at today's prices. The government's cane-route ethanol prices were set for a world where sugar sold for ₹33–36 a kilo. With sugar at ₹45, nobody wants to divert, and the ban mostly confirms what the price signal was already saying.

Why mills diverted anyway in FY26

If sugar beat B-heavy at ₹40.7, why did Balrampur send 55.8% of its cane down that route in FY26? Three reasons the simple model leaves out. First, timing: the decision is made at the start of the crush (October–November) on the sugar price then, which was lower and was expected to stay lower given ISMA's early forecast of a 7.45 MT closing stock — a forecast that later collapsed to about 3.5–4.3 MT. Second, working capital: ethanol is paid in about three weeks, while sugar sits in the godown for six to nine months under the monthly release quota. At an 8–9% cost of funds, carrying sugar for six months costs roughly ₹1.6–1.8/kg, which lifts the B-heavy break-even from about ₹35.7 to about ₹37–38/kg. Third, contracts: allocations are bid a year ahead, and a mill with a B-heavy allocation and a dedicated distillery has fixed costs to cover. In a world of forecast surplus, diversion was a sensible hedge. In the world that actually arrived, it left money on the table.

The grain side of the feedstock war

Grain distilleries play a different game. Their feedstock is bought in the open market (maize) or from the Food Corporation of India at a reserve price (rice at ₹23.20/kg for ESY 2025-26, with 72 lakh tonnes allotted). Their margin per litre is the ethanol price minus grain cost, plus the credit from selling distillers' dried grains with solubles (DDGS) as cattle and poultry feed, minus conversion. Because the government priced maize ethanol highest, a well-run maize plant earned a healthy spread in 2023-26; the cut to ₹68.86 for 2026-27 narrows it.

What would bring diversion back

Three things would reopen the fork. A good 2027 monsoon and a big SS 2027-28 crop would push sugar back toward ₹36–38, below the break-evens, and Delhi would want the surplus drained. A price revision indexing cane-route ethanol to the FRP — recommended by the CACP, requested by the industry — would raise the break-evens. Or a step up in blending beyond E20 (E27/E30, flex-fuel E85 or ethanol-to-jet) would create demand the grain plants cannot cover alone. Balrampur's managing director noted that without diversion SS 2026-27 could end with stocks rebuilt to 4.5–5.0 MT by October 2027 — the kind of number that historically brings diversion back. Diversion is the state's surplus valve; it is closed, not removed.

In plain terms

Grain distilleries won the ethanol programme because the government paid more for maize ethanol than for cane ethanol and because grain is available every year regardless of the cane crop. Sugar mills' distilleries are not stranded — they still run on leftover molasses and, where built for it, on grain — but they no longer get the premium juice and B-heavy business that justified building them.

The cost of a kilo: where ₹100 of revenue goes

Cane is two-thirds of everything. The rest of the cost base is small, seasonal and mostly fixed — which is why recovery and crushing days matter so much.

A sugar mill's cost structure is unusually lopsided. Cane, bought at an administered price, takes two-thirds or more of revenue in an integrated Uttar Pradesh mill. Everything a management team can actually manage — chemicals, stores, packing, salaries, repairs, freight — fits into another fifth. Operating profit is the thin remainder, and it moves far more than revenue does when any of the big numbers shift.

The non-cane costs are largely fixed for the year. A mill employs a large seasonal workforce during the crush and a core staff year-round; its boilers, mills and centrifugals need the same off-season maintenance whether it crushes 120 days or 170. Every extra crushing day spreads those costs further, which is why crushing days and capacity utilisation are among the KPIs in section 12. The flip side is that a short season — from drought, disease or cane leaking to jaggery makers — raises the cost of every kilo. Triveni's SS 2025-26 crush fell 8.8% to 825.60 lakh quintals, yet its management said production cost stayed "almost similar" because gross recovery rose 26 basis points to 11.06%: recovery bought back what volume lost.

In plain terms

Roughly ₹68 of every ₹100 a UP mill earns goes straight to farmers for cane. The mill's own costs take about ₹21, and it keeps about ₹11 as operating profit. A mill that squeezes one more kilo of sugar out of each tonne of cane, or runs one more week, gets that extra output almost free — which is where the best operators earn their premium.

Working capital is the hidden cost. Sugar is made in five or six months and sold over twelve under the government's monthly release quota, so a mill ends the crush holding most of the season's output. Balrampur held 66.56 lakh quintals at ₹36.70/kg on 31 March 2026 — about ₹2,440 Cr at cost — and Triveni 4.45 lakh tonnes a year earlier. Inventory days run about 240–250 for the UP integrated mills, funded by bank borrowing against the stock. At a 7–9% cost of funds, carrying a kilo of sugar for six months adds ₹1.5–1.8 to its cost, and the RBI's 7 October 2026 repo increase to 5.50% nudges that up. Triveni's cost of funds fell to 6.8% in Q1 FY27 (from 7.5%), helped by subvented term loans from the ethanol-capacity scheme; Renuka's working-capital lines depend on Wilmar guarantees.

Industry structure: 530 mills, half of them co-operatives, four listed here

A fragmented, state-shaped industry in which the largest listed company makes about 3–4% of India's sugar.

India has roughly 530 sugar mills working in a typical season, about half of them co-operatives and half private. No company comes close to dominating: Balrampur, the largest listed group, produced about 0.97 MT of net sugar in SS 2025-26, roughly 3.5% of national output, and the top ten private groups together make perhaps a quarter to a third (an estimate). The four members of this report crushed about 26.5 MT of cane between them in their latest seasons and produced about 2.7 MT of sugar — around a tenth of the country's net output (estimate).

Concentration matters less here than in most industries because pricing power does not come from market share. No mill sets the sugar price; the national stock does. What scale does buy is cheaper finance (Balrampur is rated AA+), bargaining power with institutional buyers who want consistent refined and pharma-grade sugar, and the ability to spread distillery, cogeneration and R&D investment across more cane. Scale within a state also buys political weight in cane-price negotiations, though that cuts both ways when the state government wants to be seen being generous to farmers.

In plain terms

Sugar is made by hundreds of mills, half of them farmer co-operatives, and the biggest listed company makes less than a twentieth of the country's output. No one can push up the price on their own. What separates winners from losers is how much sugar each mill gets out of each tonne of cane, what it does with the leftovers, and how well it times its sales.

Two industry bodies speak for the mills: ISMA for private mills and the National Federation of Cooperative Sugar Factories (NFCSF) for co-operatives. ISMA's production forecasts are the market's reference point despite a run of large misses (its SS 2025-26 closing-stock estimate fell from 7.45 MT to about 3.5 MT). The government's own estimates come from the food ministry, which also issues the monthly release quotas that decide how much each mill may sell.

Imports are normally negligible — India is a net exporter and the basic customs duty on sugar is 100% — except for raw sugar brought in under Advance Authorisation to be refined and re-exported, which is the business model of Renuka's port refineries at Kandla and Haldia. The 2026 squeeze has prompted the first serious talk in a decade of India becoming a net importer. China does not compete with Indian sugar directly; it is a large importer of raws. Where China matters is in Balrampur's new product: Chinese producers have built large polylactic acid capacity since 2022, and they set much of the world PLA price that Balrampur's plant will sell against (see Plastics and section 17).

Ownership links run outside the industry too. Renuka's parent Wilmar is also the co-founder of AWL Agri Business (formerly Adani Wilmar), which from 1 July 2026 distributes Renuka's Madhur refined sugar across India; see Edible oil for AWL. EID Parry's main asset is a fertiliser and crop-protection company (Fertilisers, Agrochemicals). Triveni's demerged gears business is now a separate listed company. Sugar is a sub-sector in which corporate structure explains as much of the valuation as the mills do.

In plain terms

Every mill can sell sugar. The catch is that ethanol and electricity prices are also set by government, so "integration" diversifies the products more than it diversifies the policy risk.

Integration pays when the by-product line earns more than the sugar it competes with for cane, and when the plant runs full. In FY26 Balrampur's distillery earned an 11.81% segment margin against 9.35% for sugar; in Q1 FY27 the distillery earned 14.90% against 3.16% (flattered, management cautioned, by keeping Maizapur's off-season costs in the sugar segment). Cycles and the timeline: from a price cycle to a policy cycle

Two decades of gluts, droughts and rescues — and how the 2018 policy toolkit changed the shape of the cycle.

Until about 2018, Indian sugar ran on a three-to-four-year price cycle. High prices encouraged farmers to plant cane; a bumper crop crashed prices below the cost of production; mills fell behind on cane payments; farmers switched to other crops; supply fell and prices recovered. Listed sugar stocks were trading vehicles for that cycle. Since 2018 the state has used ethanol diversion, export permissions, buffer stocks and the MSP to keep ex-mill sugar in a band of roughly ₹36–42/kg. The cycle has not disappeared — 2026 is a spike outside the band — but its main driver is now policy, and the variable that moves listed earnings is the gap between the cane price and a policed sugar price.

Reading a sugar mill: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers that explain a sugar company's results — what each means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, and where the definitions trip people up.

A sugar company's annual report is full of agricultural and chemical measures that most investors skip: lakh quintals crushed, recovery percentages, kilolitres per day, units exported. They should not. Almost every movement in a mill's profit can be traced to five or six of them, and the companies disclose them more consistently than they disclose most financial detail. This section takes them one at a time and ends with a scorecard, a list of definitional traps and a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

The profit equation

The whole business fits on one line. Operating profit per tonne of cane equals what the mill earns from that tonne (sugar plus ethanol plus power plus other by-products) minus what it pays for the cane, minus the cost of converting it. Group EBITDA is that figure multiplied by the tonnes crushed, plus the margin on anything not made from the mill's own cane (grain ethanol, refined imported raws, liquor, PLA, water contracts), plus or minus the effect of valuing inventory.

Different members need different KPIs. For Balrampur and Triveni, the full cane-to-cash set applies: crush, recovery, realisation, inventory, distillery feedstock, EBITDA per tonne. For EID Parry, the operating KPIs apply to a business that is small relative to the group — the numbers that drive its share price are Coromandel's market value, the standalone balance sheet and the losses at the Tamil Nadu plants. For Renuka, the refinery's melt volume and margin, the rupee, and interest cost matter more than recovery. The sections below note where a KPI is not meaningful for a member, rather than forcing a comparison.

The same arithmetic gives the sensitivities that matter. On that mill, a ₹10/qtl rise in the cane price costs ₹15 Cr (15% of EBITDA). A ₹1/kg rise in the sugar price adds ₹16.5 Cr. A 0.1-point gain in recovery adds about ₹6 Cr of sugar with no extra cane. Scaled to Balrampur (about 10.3–10.4 MT of cane), ₹10/qtl on the SAP is about ₹104 Cr a year and ₹1/kg on sugar about ₹100 Cr; scaled to Triveni (8.3–8.8 MT), ₹10/qtl is ₹83–88 Cr.

In plain terms

Four things decide a mill's profit: how much cane it crushes, how much sugar it gets out of each tonne, what it sells everything for, and what it pays for the cane. The government sets the last one and heavily influences the third. The mill's real skill shows in the first two.

1. Cane crushed

What it is. The tonnes of cane put through the mills in a season, reported in lakh quintals (1 lakh quintals = 10,000 tonnes) or lakh tonnes. Formula. Sum of daily crush across mills over the season. Worked example. Balrampur crushed 1,043.04 lakh quintals in SS 2025-26 — 10.43 million tonnes — and 1,031.55 lakh quintals in FY26 (April–March), because the financial year cuts across two seasons. Drivers. Cane area in the command zone, yield per hectare (weather, variety, disease), how much cane leaks to jaggery and khandsari makers who pay cash, and how many days the mill runs. Benchmarks. Growth matters more than size: steady 2–5% a year growth in crush is healthy; a 10% fall is a red flag unless explained by weather. Comparison. SS 2025-26 / FY26: Balrampur 10.43 MT (+5.2%), Triveni 8.26 MT (−8.8%, poorer yields and jaggery diversion in western UP), Renuka 4.01 MT (FY26, about +9%), EID 3.84 MT (FY26, +2.6%, with Karnataka up and Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh down). Definition trap. Season and financial-year crush are different numbers; use the season figure for agronomy and the FY figure for per-tonne P&L maths.

2. Capacity utilisation and crushing days

What it is. How fully the installed crushing capacity (tonnes of cane per day, TCD) is used over the season. Formula. Cane crushed ÷ (TCD × crushing days). Worked example. A 10,000 TCD mill running 150 days could crush 15 lakh tonnes; if it crushes 12 lakh it is at 80%. Drivers. Cane availability above all; then breakdowns, the mode of operation (Triveni's annual report notes that C-heavy mode raises steam use and lowers crush rates) and the length of the season. Benchmarks. Good ≥90%, average 75–90%, weak below 70%; crushing days good ≥150, average 120–150, weak below 110. Definition trap. Companies rarely disclose crushing days, so outsiders must assume a season length; the chart below assumes 150 days and is only indicative.

Recovery is the sugar yield of the cane. A UP mill that gets 11.5 kg of sugar from every 100 kg of cane has a cost advantage of about ₹1.6 a kilo over one that gets 11 kg, with no extra spending. 4. Sugar realisation

What it is. The average price received per kilo (or quintal, or tonne) of sugar sold. Formula. Sugar sales revenue ÷ tonnes despatched, excluding GST. Worked example. Triveni despatched 2,77,403 tonnes in Q1 FY27 at ₹41,525/t — ₹41.53/kg — for sugar revenue of about ₹1,152 Cr before other segment income. Drivers. The national stock and season outlook; the mix of domestic, export and institutional sales; the share of refined and pharma-grade sugar (Triveni credits its premium to this); the timing of sales within the monthly quota. Benchmarks. Compare with the cost of production rather than an absolute: realisation at least ₹3/kg above cost is good, ₹0–3 average, below cost weak. In FY26 that meant ₹41–42 was healthy; at the August-2026 spot price of ₹45–49 the spread is unusually wide. Trap. Reported realisation lags spot by one to three months because sales are made from earlier contracts and quotas; Q1 FY27 realisations (₹40.0–41.6) did not yet reflect the August spike.

5. Inventory: volume, cost and the valuation effect

What it is. The sugar held at period end, in tonnes or quintals, and the cost per kilo at which it is carried. Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Formula. Closing stock (t) × carrying cost (₹/kg) = book value; (market price − carrying cost) × stock = the unrealised gain (or, if negative, a write-down). Worked example. Triveni held 3.59 lakh tonnes at ₹38.41/kg on 30 June 2026, about ₹1,379 Cr at cost. At ₹45/kg the same stock is worth about ₹1,616 Cr, an unrealised gain of roughly ₹237 Cr that flows through Q2–Q3 FY27 as the sugar is sold (an ILLUSTRATIVE estimate). Drivers. Season size, the monthly release quota, export permissions and the company's own decision to hold or sell. Benchmarks. There is no "good" level; the question is cost versus market. Trap. Because gains are not booked until sale, a rising market makes the next two quarters look better than the business really is, and a falling market forces a write-down to NRV that can make a quarter look worse. Neither is a structural margin.

Comparison. Balrampur carries the most stock at the lowest cost — 45.67 lakh quintals at ₹37.19/kg, or roughly ₹1,700 Cr at cost — so it has the most to gain from the 2026 price spike (about ₹357 Cr at ₹45, an estimate). Triveni's stock is smaller and slightly dearer. EID holds the least, at the highest cost (about ₹41.50/kg, reflecting Tamil Nadu's low recovery), so its gain is small. Renuka does not disclose comparable figures; much of its inventory is imported raw sugar and refined sugar for export, priced off world markets rather than Indian ex-mill.

8. Feedstock mix and route exposure

What it is. The share of ethanol made from each feedstock — C-heavy, B-heavy, juice/syrup, maize, rice. Formula. Litres by route ÷ total litres. Relative prices (section 05), allocation rules, and plant design. Benchmarks. Good: multi-feed plants with at least half the capacity able to switch to grain; average: partial flexibility; weak: cane-only. Trap. A high grain share looks like a hedge but brings commodity risk (maize prices) and a thinner margin at the reduced 2026-27 maize price.

Comparison. 9. Cogeneration units and tariff

What it is. Units of electricity exported to the grid or open-access buyers, and the tariff received. Formula. Power revenue = units exported × tariff. Worked example. Balrampur exported 2,132 lakh units to UPPCL at ₹4.37 and 1,541 lakh units to open-access buyers at ₹4.79 in FY26. That is 3,673 lakh units (36.7 crore kWh) at a blended ₹4.54, or about ₹167 Cr of revenue — watch the units, because "lakh units" and "crore kWh" are easily confused by a factor of 100. Drivers. Bagasse availability (crush), boiler pressure and efficiency, captive steam needs (C-heavy mode uses more), and the state regulator's tariff. Benchmarks. High-pressure (87–110 bar) plants are designed to export 100–130 kWh per tonne of cane in sugar-only operation; older low-pressure boilers export little. Reported exports are usually far lower: Balrampur's FY26 exports work out to about 36 kWh per tonne crushed, because its large distilleries take much of the steam and power. The illustrative mill in this report uses the design norm, so its power line is a best case; at Balrampur-like export levels it would be about a third as large. Trap. Retrospective tariff revisions (UPERC's from 1 April 2024) can put prior-year arrears into a single quarter's revenue, flattering it.

What it is. Profit before interest and tax (PBIT) of each reported segment as a share of that segment's revenue. Formula. Segment PBIT ÷ segment revenue. Worked example. What it is. Leverage and debt-service capacity. Formula. (Borrowings − cash and liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA; EBITDA ÷ interest. Worked example. 14. Cane arrears

What it is. Cane price owed to farmers beyond the statutory 14-day payment period. Formula. Cane dues payable − cane paid, usually expressed as a share of the season's dues. Worked example. Maharashtra's arrears were ₹4,898 Cr at 28 February 2026, against ₹2,949 Cr a year earlier; Uttar Pradesh had paid about 90% of 2025-26 dues; Triveni reported no arrears. Drivers. Sugar prices relative to cane cost, and access to working capital. Benchmarks. Good below 5% of dues, average 5–15%, weak above 15%. Trap. Arrears are a sector stress gauge more than a company metric; listed companies rarely disclose them, but rising state-level arrears are the earliest warning of a glut (they peaked near ₹21,000 Cr in April 2015).

Comparison. None of the four members is known for arrears today: Triveni reported none, Balrampur pays within the statutory window, and EID and Renuka operate mainly in Karnataka, where private mills pay FRP. The sector pattern is the warning sign: Maharashtra's arrears rose two-thirds in a year to February 2026, during a season with rising sugar prices, which says more about the co-operatives' finances than about the market. If the SAP rises sharply before the 2027 UP election while sugar prices fall back from their August peak, UP arrears are the number to watch.

15. Revenue per tonne of cane

What it is. Total revenue divided by cane crushed — a crude but useful measure of how much value a company extracts from each tonne, including by-products and non-cane lines. Formula. Revenue ÷ tonnes of cane crushed (same period). Worked example. Balrampur: ₹6,271 Cr ÷ 10.32 MT (FY26 crush) ≈ ₹6,080 a tonne, against a cane cost of roughly ₹3,950–4,000; the illustrative mill in this section earns ₹5,624 because it has no grain distillery and makes no B-heavy or syrup ethanol. Drivers. Sugar price, by-product depth, and non-cane revenue such as grain ethanol, liquor or refining. Benchmarks. For a UP integrated mill, ₹5,800–6,300 a tonne is typical at FY26 prices; the figure rises with every rupee on sugar. Trap. It flatters companies with large non-cane businesses: Triveni's ex-gears revenue per tonne (~₹6,700, an estimate) includes country liquor and water contracts, and Renuka's would be meaningless because two-thirds of its revenue is refined imported raw sugar.

Definition traps, collected

  • Three calendars. Sugar season (Oct–Sep), ethanol supply year (Nov–Oct) and financial year (Apr–Mar) never line up. FY26 profit mixes two seasons' cane costs.
  • Gross versus net production and recovery. Post-2022 national "production" is often gross (before diversion); company recovery may be gross or net. Balrampur's 11.24% is gross; its net was 9.28%.
  • Season versus FY crush. Balrampur's 1,043.04 lakh qtl is SS 2025-26; its FY26 crush was 1,031.55 lakh qtl.
  • Crore versus lakh litres. Balrampur's FY26 alcohol sales were 26.56 crore bulk litres; the national ESY 2025-26 allocation was 1,048.4 crore litres. A primer quoted "1,048 lakh BL" for Balrampur — a units-and-attribution error.
  • Gross versus net of excise. Triveni reports alcohol revenue including excise on liquor (Q1 FY27 ₹1,950 Cr gross vs ₹1,581 Cr net). Never add its gross segment revenue to another company's net revenue.
  • Consolidated is not EID. EID's consolidated revenue (₹38,534 Cr) is about 82% Coromandel; its standalone revenue is ₹3,120 Cr. The ~₹6,700 Cr sometimes quoted as EID's "own" business is the consolidated segment total including the closed refinery and overseas nutraceutical subsidiaries.
  • PAT including minorities. EID's consolidated PAT of ₹1,380 Cr includes the ~44% of Coromandel's profit owned by others; attributable PAT was about ₹569 Cr.
  • Operating profit after forex. Renuka's screener operating profit (₹22 Cr) is after a ₹211 Cr forex loss; its standalone EBITDA before forex was ₹358 Cr.
  • Pre- and post-demerger. Triveni's FY26 P&L includes the gears business that left on 1 April 2026; its market cap does not.
  • Inventory at cost. Stock is carried at the lower of cost and NRV; price rises show up as margin only when the sugar is sold.
  • Internal transfer prices. Molasses "sold" from sugar to distillery segments at internal prices shifts profit between segments.
  • Ethanol prices are ex-mill. Administered prices exclude 5% GST and transport; a company's realised price blends routes and ENA.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading sugar companies come from comparing numbers that look alike but are not: a season with a financial year, gross with net, crore with lakh, consolidated with standalone. Before comparing any two figures, check that they cover the same months, the same entity and the same definition.

How to read a sugar company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Identify the quarter's place in the season (1 minute). Q1 (Apr–Jun) and Q2 (Jul–Sep) are off-season sell-down quarters; Q3 (Oct–Dec) starts the crush; Q4 (Jan–Mar) carries peak crushing and inventory build. Compare with the same quarter last year, never the previous quarter.
  2. Sugar despatches and realisation (1 minute). Tonnes sold and ₹/kg. Is realisation moving with the ex-mill market or lagging it? A rising market plus low-cost stock means margin expansion for the next one to two quarters.
  3. Closing inventory: tonnes and cost per kg (1 minute). Multiply (market price − cost) by tonnes to estimate gains still to come. A write-down to NRV is a red flag for a falling market.
  4. Crush and recovery (in Q3/Q4, 1 minute). Cane crushed versus last season, gross and net recovery, and the diversion mode. A recovery fall with no change in mode is a genuine operating problem (disease, weather).
  5. Distillery volumes, realisation and feedstock (1 minute). Litres sold, ₹/L, grain share. Volume falls with stable PBIT (as at Triveni in Q1 FY27) can mean better feedstock economics; volume falls with falling PBIT mean lost allocations.
  6. Segment PBIT (1 minute). Sugar, distillery, cogen, others. Check whether internal molasses transfer prices changed, and whether any one-off (retrospective power tariff, impairment) sits inside a segment.
  7. EBITDA and margin versus the same quarter last year (1 minute). Strip out other income and exceptional items. For EID, separate Coromandel from the rest; for Renuka, separate forex.
  8. Debt and interest (1 minute). Gross debt against last year's same quarter (not last March), cost of funds, and any parent guarantees or support letters.
  9. Policy and price commentary (1 minute). Management's view on SAP, ethanol allocations, export/stock-limit rules and next season's crop. These drive the next two quarters more than the reported one.
  10. Red flags (1 minute). Inventory write-downs; rising cane arrears; falling distillery utilisation; exceptional impairments; auditor emphasis on going concern (Renuka); a widening gap between reported and "adjusted" profit.
In plain terms

For a sugar company's quarter, look at four things in order: how much sugar it sold and at what price, how much it still holds and at what cost, what its distillery did, and what management says about next season's cane price and ethanol rules. The headline profit matters less than these, because the industry's seasons make single quarters misleading.

Who is winning: margins, cane quality and where the market puts its money

With only four companies, a positioning map is less about clusters than about distances. Each map below plots the members on two measures that the earlier sections showed to matter, and the gaps between the dots explain most of the valuation gaps in the peer table. The first map is the simplest summary of financial health. Balrampur's 11.9% margin and nearly 10× interest cover put it alone in the top-right; Triveni, after losing its 37%-margin gears business, sits in the middle with about 7% and 5.5×. The second map shows why "integration" is not a single ranking. Renuka owns the most distillery capacity and its Karnataka mills report good recovery, but it lacks the cane to fill its stills and depended on the B-heavy route now barred. The third map is the market's verdict. Balrampur commands a value share four times its revenue share; EID's is half. Neither is irrational. Balrampur has the highest margins, the best interest cover, the largest inventory windfall in FY27 and the PLA option; EID's consolidated revenue belongs mostly to a company it owns 55.58% of and whose minority shareholders are not EID's. On a look-through basis EID's own business is small and loss-making, which is why the discount is wide (section 16).

In plain terms

Balrampur is winning on almost every measure: margins, safety, cane quality and investor support. Triveni is a steady second with the most flexible distilleries. EID Parry is two companies — a strong fertiliser group and a weak sugar arm. Renuka has good mills and big distilleries but carries debt it cannot service on its own.

What could change the ranking? A large SAP rise would hit Balrampur and Triveni hardest, since they crush only in UP. A prolonged diversion ban hurts Balrampur's distillery more than Triveni's. A successful PLA start-up would widen Balrampur's lead; a delayed or loss-making one would narrow it. For EID, any move to unlock the Coromandel stake — a distribution, a buyback funded by stake sales, or a restructuring of the Tamil Nadu plants — would move it toward the diagonal. For Renuka, the only thing that changes its position is capital: a Wilmar recapitalisation, conversion of debt to equity, or a buyout of minorities.

An 80,000-tonne bioplastics plant, ₹3,080 crore of capital and a state subsidy that depends on running it hard.

PLA is a biodegradable, compostable plastic made by fermenting sugar into lactic acid, converting it to lactide and polymerising it. They include a 50% capital subsidy on eligible investment (paid over seven years after a ten-year lag, according to the annual report, and only if utilisation reaches at least 40% in year one and 75% thereafter), full reimbursement of net SGST for ten years, a 5% interest subvention for seven years, and stamp-duty and electricity-duty exemptions, capped at 200% of eligible investment. If the capital subsidy is fully earned, the effective capital cost roughly halves and the pre-tax return on the remaining capital approaches 20% at full utilisation (ILLUSTRATIVE). The PLA term loan is repayable from Q3 FY29 in 20 quarterly instalments.

In plain terms

Balrampur is spending about ₹3,080 Cr to turn some of its sugar into biodegradable plastic. If the plant runs full and sells at the price the company expects, it could add half as much again to the company's operating profit. Until then it adds interest and depreciation, and the generous state subsidy is paid only if the plant runs hard. It is the only part of the business whose price no government sets — which is both the attraction and the risk.

The market risk is real. PLA is a global commodity dominated by NatureWorks (Cargill and PTT Global Chemical; about 150,000 tonnes a year at Blair, Nebraska, with a Thai plant announced), TotalEnergies Corbion (Rayong, 75,000 tonnes, lactide expanded to 100,000) and a fast-growing group of Chinese producers. The ₹2,000 Cr revenue guide implies about ₹2.5 lakh a tonne, roughly $2,630 at ₹94.9 — above the $1,800–2,500 range at which PLA has traded in recent years, and no public 2026 price was found to check it. Balrampur's answers are a domestic cost advantage (its own sugar, no import freight or duty), India's single-use plastics ban and extended producer responsibility rules, and a specific demand pool: management said packaging for pan masala and gutka, where food-safety rules are pushing makers away from conventional plastics, could "largely" absorb the full 80,000 tonnes. If that proves right, the executive director said, a second plant — "a parallel Balrampur" — is possible. For the wider plastics market see Plastics.

The bet also changes how Balrampur's balance sheet should be read. Borrowings rose from ₹2,627 Cr to ₹3,170 Cr in FY26 and capital work in progress from ₹106 Cr to ₹1,747 Cr; free cash flow was −₹346 Cr. Net debt of 3.6× EBITDA looks high for an AA+ company but includes about ₹1,086 Cr of PLA term debt against an asset not yet earning. Capital commitments fell to ₹984 Cr (from ₹1,471 Cr), almost all PLA. The sugar business itself remains conservatively financed. The market has already priced much of the option: Balrampur trades at 36.5× earnings and 3.1× book, the highest multiples in the group.

The global lens: Brazil sets the world price, India sets its own

How Indian mills compare with the world's low-cost producers, and why world prices matter less to them than to anyone else.

India is the world's largest sugar consumer and its second-largest producer, after Brazil, but it is only intermittently a large exporter, and its domestic price is managed by policy rather than set by the world market. That combination means world prices matter to Indian mills mainly at the margin: they decide whether exports are profitable in a surplus year, whether imports are possible in a deficit, and whether Renuka's refineries have a spread to work with.

The world price is set by Brazil's Centre-South region, which produces about 40–42 MT of sugar a year and decides, mill by mill and day by day, how much of its cane juice to make into sugar and how much into ethanol for Brazil's flex-fuel car fleet. Its sugar mix was about 50.7–51% in 2025-26, high by historical standards, which helped push ICE #11 raw sugar to five-year lows (about 13–14 ¢/lb) in February 2026. The West Asia war that began on 28 February lifted oil prices, raised the value of ethanol in Brazil and pulled the mix back toward ethanol; by 23 October 2026 ICE #11 for March 2027 delivery had risen to 20.24 ¢/lb and London #5 whites for December to $534.80 a tonne, a white premium of roughly $100 a tonne. Thailand (about 10 MT, the second-largest exporter), the EU's beet producers (Südzucker, Tereos) and a protected US market make up most of the rest of world trade.

Brazilian integrated millers such as São Martinho earn EBITDA margins three to four times those of Indian mills because they own or lease much of their cane land, pay farmers a formula price linked to what the sugar and ethanol actually sell for, and sell both products at market prices. The global balance for 2026-27 is turning tighter. Triveni's investor brief cited forecasts of world production of about 185 MT against consumption of about 180 MT — a thin surplus — with lower output expected from Brazil, the EU, the US and Thailand. The International Sugar Organization saw a 2025-26 surplus of 2.24 MT after a 3.2 MT deficit; Balrampur's CFO raised the possibility of a global deficit of 1–2 MT in 2026-27; and an Indian deficit, if the monsoon damage shows up, could tip the world into one. For Indian mills, a tighter world matters in two ways. It caps the risk of imports undercutting domestic prices, and it means that whenever export permission returns, Indian sugar will be competitive — the opposite of SS 2025-26, when most of a 1.5–2 MT quota went unused because India was too expensive.

In plain terms

Brazilian mills earn far fatter margins than Indian ones because they buy cane at prices tied to what they sell and sell at world prices. Indian mills live inside a managed system with a rising cane price and a policed sugar price. Today world sugar is dearer than Indian sugar, which protects Indian prices from imports — but because exports are banned, Indian mills cannot sell into the higher world price either.

World markets matter most for two members. Renuka's refineries live on the white premium and on export permissions; in a year when world whites are high but exports are prohibited, they lose both. Balrampur's PLA plant will sell into a global market shaped by NatureWorks, TotalEnergies Corbion and Chinese producers, so it will be the first part of an Indian sugar company whose margin depends on international competition rather than on Delhi. The rupee links both: at ₹94.9 to the dollar, a weaker rupee raises the rupee value of world sugar (helpful for exports, harmful for Renuka's dollar debt) and of imported PLA (helpful for Balrampur's domestic pricing).

Technology and disruption: grain, electric cars, biogas and jet fuel

The threats and options that could reshape the industry over the next decade, ranked by how live they are.

Sugar milling is an old technology and changes slowly; the disruptions that matter come from what happens to the products. Some are already here (grain ethanol), some are a decade away (electric vehicles eroding petrol demand), and some are options the mills are building (biogas, aviation fuel feedstock, bioplastics).

  • Grain-ethanol displacement (live). Grain supplied 72.5% of the ESY 2025-26 allocation and the 2026-27 tender bars juice and B-heavy ethanol entirely. Cane-route distillery capacity is stranded unless policy restores diversion. The hedge is multi-feed conversion — Triveni's two 200 KLPD plants and Balrampur's Maizapur unit can switch between molasses and grain — but grain brings commodity risk and a thinner margin at the lower 2026-27 maize price.
  • Electric vehicles against flex-fuel vehicles (slow). Rising EV adoption caps long-run petrol demand, and therefore the volume of ethanol E20 requires. The counter is flex-fuel vehicles that can run on up to 85% ethanol; the first car (Maruti WagonR FFV) and motorcycle (Hero) launched on 4 June 2026, and E85 outlets are being set up. Without a mandate beyond E20, FFVs are an option, not demand.
  • Sustainable aviation fuel (emerging). A SAF mandate of 1% (2027), 2% (2028) and 5% (2030) for international flights opens an ethanol-to-jet pathway. Volumes are small at first, but ethanol-to-jet could absorb surplus distillery capacity later in the decade.
  • Compressed biogas and second-generation ethanol (building). Press mud and bagasse can feed compressed biogas plants under the SATAT scheme, with a CBG blending obligation in city gas distribution from FY26 rising over time; second-generation ethanol from bagasse (IOC's Panipat plant; Praj's technology) could turn bagasse into fuel rather than boiler feed. Both change the value of by-products that today earn little.
  • Cogeneration value erosion (slow). Solar power bid at about ₹2.5 a unit undercuts bagasse cogeneration at ₹4–5. Discoms will resist tariff increases, and bagasse may be worth more as CBG or 2G feedstock than as fuel.
  • Sweetener substitution and health policy (long-dated). Sugar taxes, front-of-pack labelling by FSSAI, low-sugar reformulation by beverage makers and GLP-1 weight-loss drugs are long-run risks to industrial demand, which is 60–65% of consumption. None is visible in Indian volumes yet.
  • Cane varieties and climate (live). Red rot in Co-0238, UP's dominant variety, showed the risk of monoculture; varietal replacement is under way and helped Triveni lift recovery 26 basis points in SS 2025-26. El Niño and a weaker monsoon are a recurring threat to Maharashtra and Karnataka.
  • Mechanised harvesting (gradual). Labour shortages in Maharashtra are spreading cane harvesters; mechanisation can shorten harvest-to-crush time and improve recovery, but it needs consolidated plots that Indian smallholdings rarely offer.
  • New products from sugar (option). Balrampur's PLA plant is the first large test of whether Indian sugar can feed a chemicals chain — lactic acid, PLA, and potentially other bio-based chemicals — at prices the state does not set.

The common thread is that the cane mill is becoming a biorefinery whose main product mix is chosen by policy and relative prices rather than fixed. That favours companies with feedstock flexibility, high-pressure boilers, spare land and balance sheets strong enough to invest in the next product: Balrampur and Triveni more than EID's Tamil Nadu units or Renuka. It also raises the stakes of policy error. The ethanol build-out of 2019–2023 showed how quickly a government-sponsored capital cycle can produce overcapacity; installed distillery capacity of more than 1,990 crore litres against demand of about 1,100 crore litres is the result.

In plain terms

The biggest disruption to Indian sugar mills has already happened: the fuel business they were promised went to grain. The next wave — biogas, jet fuel, bioplastics, flex-fuel cars — offers new uses for the same cane, but each depends on new rules or new markets that do not yet exist at scale.

Bull vs bear: a windfall year inside a squeezed industry

The arguments on both sides for the listed sugar sub-sector, and a scorecard of the four members.

The bull and bear cases for Indian sugar in late 2026 are not about the same time horizon. The bull case is mostly about the next two to three quarters: scarce sugar, high prices and low-cost inventory. The bear case is mostly about the next two to three years: a rising cane price, frozen floors, a lost ethanol premium and the risk that a normal monsoon brings the surplus back. Both can be right at once.

The bull case

  • Genuine scarcity: closing stock around 4 MT (a near-decade low), a monsoon at 87% of normal, El Niño into 2027 and USDA's 14% cut to India's 2026-27 output.
  • An inventory windfall: stock made at ₹37–38/kg selling at ₹45–49; roughly ₹635 Cr of gains across Balrampur, Triveni and EID in Q2–Q3 FY27 (estimate).
  • Sugar beats diversion: at today's prices, making sugar earns about ₹1,100 more per tonne of cane than juice ethanol; the ban pushes volume into the better product.
  • World prices above India's: London #5 whites (~₹50.8/kg) sit above Indian ex-mill, capping import risk and making exports competitive once allowed.
  • Optionality: Balrampur's PLA (₹400–500 Cr of EBITDA at full, illustrative), Triveni's grain flexibility and liquor brands, EID's discount to its Coromandel stake, CBG and SAF.
  • Policy has learned: since 2018 the state has kept sugar in a band that keeps good mills profitable; MSP revision and ethanol-price indexation are both on the table.

The bear case

  • The sandwich tightens: FRP up 25.9% since 2021-22, an election-year UP SAP rise pending, MSP frozen at ₹31 since 2019 and cane-route ethanol prices frozen since 2022-23.
  • The ethanol premium is gone: grain has 72.5% of allocations; juice and B-heavy are barred; more than 1,990 crore litres of capacity chase ~1,100 crore litres of demand.
  • Government leans against prices: stock limits, export prohibition and the end of diversion are designed to bring prices down; ending diversion adds 2–3 MT of food sugar.
  • The surplus can return fast: forecasts have missed by 4 MT in a season; a normal 2027 monsoon could rebuild stocks to 4.5–5 MT and push prices back toward ₹40.
  • Weak links: the sugar universe without Coromandel lost about ₹663 Cr in FY26; EID's own business and Renuka are loss-making; Renuka's equity is negative.
  • Valuation: Balrampur at 36.5× earnings and 3.1× book already prices in PLA success and the 2026 windfall.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • FRP (Fair and Remunerative Price): the national minimum price mills must pay for cane, set each season by the Cabinet on CACP advice; ₹365/qtl at 10.25% recovery for SS 2026-27.
  • SAP (State Advised Price): a higher cane price set by some states, notably Uttar Pradesh (₹390 common / ₹400 early variety for 2025-26).
  • MSP (minimum selling price) of sugar: the floor below which mills may not sell sugar; ₹31/kg since 14-Feb-2019.
  • Sugar season (SS / SY): 1 October to 30 September. Ethanol supply year (ESY): 1 November to 31 October.
  • Quintal (qtl): 100 kg. 1 lakh quintals = 10,000 tonnes; 100 lakh quintals = 1 million tonnes. ₹/qtl ÷ 100 = ₹/kg.
  • TCD: tonnes of cane crushed per day — a mill's crushing capacity.
  • KLPD: kilolitres per day — distillery capacity. 100 KLPD × 330 days ≈ 3.3 crore litres a year.
  • Bulk litres (BL): litres as measured. 1 crore litres = 10 million litres = 10,000 kilolitres.
  • Recovery: sugar produced as a percentage of cane crushed. Gross recovery adds back sugar sacrificed to ethanol diversion; net recovery does not.
  • C-heavy molasses: the final molasses left after crystallising as much sugar as possible; about 45 kg per tonne of cane.
  • B-heavy molasses: molasses taken off at an earlier stage, richer in sugar; making ethanol from it sacrifices some sugar.
  • Juice / syrup route: fermenting cane juice or concentrated syrup directly into ethanol, making no sugar.
  • Diversion: sugar not made because cane went to B-heavy or juice ethanol; about 3 MT in SS 2025-26.
  • ENA (extra-neutral alcohol): high-purity alcohol for potable liquor, sold at commercial prices.
  • IMIL / IMFL: Indian-made Indian liquor (country liquor) and Indian-made foreign liquor (branded spirits).
  • DDGS: distillers' dried grains with solubles — animal feed left after grain fermentation.
  • Bagasse: the fibre left after crushing, burnt in boilers for steam and power. Press mud: filter cake from juice clarification, used for compost or biogas.
  • Cogeneration: producing steam and electricity together from bagasse; surplus power is sold to the grid.
  • ICE #11 / London #5: world benchmark futures for raw sugar (US cents a pound) and white sugar (US$ a tonne). The gap between them is the white premium.
  • ICUMSA: a colour measure for sugar; refined sugar is ICUMSA below 45.
  • E20: petrol blended with 20% ethanol, achieved in ESY 2025-26. FFV: flex-fuel vehicle able to run on high ethanol blends (up to E85).
  • OMC: oil marketing company (IOC, BPCL, HPCL) — the buyers of fuel ethanol.
  • LTOA: long-term offtake agreement between an OMC and a dedicated ethanol plant.
  • DFPD: the food ministry's Department of Food and Public Distribution, which sets release quotas, stock limits and the MSP.
  • ISMA / NFCSF: the Indian Sugar & Bio-Energy Manufacturers Association (private mills) and the National Federation of Cooperative Sugar Factories.
  • CACP: Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, which recommends the FRP.
  • NRV: net realisable value; inventory is carried at the lower of cost and NRV.
  • PLA (polylactic acid): a biodegradable plastic made from lactic acid produced by fermenting sugar.
  • SAF: sustainable aviation fuel; ethanol-to-jet is one production route.
  • CBG / 2G ethanol: compressed biogas, and second-generation ethanol made from crop residues such as bagasse.
  • Holdco discount: the gap between a holding company's market value and the value of the listed stakes it owns.
  • ECB: external commercial borrowing — a foreign-currency loan from overseas lenders.
  • Advance Authorisation: a scheme allowing duty-free import of inputs (such as raw sugar) for processing and re-export.
