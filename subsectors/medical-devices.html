00

The one-page view: India exports the tubes and imports the machines

Two listed companies, two different products, one shared trait: both turn cheap polymer or latex into high-margin goods because a regulator has certified the factory.

India's listed medical-devices sub-sector is small and lopsided. It has two members in the NSE 750. Poly Medicure (Polymed) makes hospital disposables: IV cannulae, infusion sets, dialysis consumables, catheters, and lately stents and trauma implants. Cupid makes male and female condoms, rapid diagnostic kits and, since 2024, perfumes, deodorants and face wash. Together they earned ₹2,233 crore of revenue in FY26 (the year to March 2026), up 20.6%, and ₹429 crore of reported profit after tax. At the 30 October 2026 close the stock market valued them at ₹58,333 crore.

That last number is where the sub-sector stops behaving like one. Polymed earns 84% of the combined revenue and 75% of the combined profit, yet it is only 25% of the combined market value. Cupid, with one-fifth of Polymed's revenue, is worth three times as much: ₹43,641 crore against ₹14,692 crore. Cupid trades at 318 times its trailing earnings and 97 times its book value; Polymed at 45.9 times earnings and 4.7 times book. Any "sector multiple" is therefore a statement about one small company's share price, and this report shows each aggregate with and without it.

Underneath the valuation gap the two companies share a business logic. Both make products that cost little in raw material. A cannula is a few grams of plastic and a steel needle; a condom is a few grams of latex. Both earn gross margins far above what the material suggests: 68.2% for Polymed and 58.6% for Cupid in FY26. The margin is paid for by certification. Hospitals in 125 countries buy Polymed because its plants carry European CE marks, US FDA registrations and Indian CDSCO licences, and because a cannula that fails inside a patient's vein is a liability no purchasing manager wants. Cupid sells to the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), the Global Fund's procurement agent and South Africa's health department because it is prequalified by the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNFPA for both male and female condoms, which the company says no other manufacturer had achieved first. Without those certificates, both would be commodity moulders competing with thousands of Chinese and Malaysian factories.

Five forces shape the sub-sector, and the rest of this report is organised around them.

  • Import dependence and the policy push. India imports 70–80% of the medical devices it uses, mostly electronic equipment. On the four core customs headings, imports were US$5.84 billion in FY26 against exports of US$2.41 billion, and China supplied about 17% of those imports (about 20% with Hong Kong). The state's answer is a ₹3,420 crore production-linked incentive (PLI) scheme, device parks, the National Medical Devices Policy 2023 and trade remedies such as the anti-dumping case on Chinese and Malaysian dialyzers that Polymed filed. Neither member receives PLI money.
  • Price controls and licensing. The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) caps stent prices (₹39,186 for a drug-eluting stent from 1 April 2026) and knee implants, and limits every other device to a 10% price rise a year. The Medical Device Rules 2017 have required a licence for every class of device since October 2023. Abroad, the EU's Medical Device Regulation (MDR) forces re-certification by 2027–28 and the US requires 510(k) clearance.
  • Exports, China+1 and tariffs. Polymed earns 69% of revenue outside India. The US put 100% tariffs on Chinese syringes and needles in September 2024 and on Chinese medical gloves in January 2026. India's own exporters have paid a 10% US Section 301 duty on top of normal duties since 24 July 2026, and the rupee is near ₹94.9 to the dollar. The trade data show the China+1 boom in Indian syringes, not in the cannulae Polymed makes.
  • The donor-funded condom market. The 2025 dismantling of USAID cut global public-sector condom buying. India's condom exports fell 27% in FY25 and rebounded 33% in FY26 as national tenders replaced donors. Karex of Malaysia, the world's largest condom maker, made almost no profit; Cupid doubled its revenue on a South African tender worth about ₹115 crore a year.
  • Capital allocation and valuation. Polymed's return on capital employed (ROCE) fell from 24% in FY24 to 12% in FY26 as it raised ₹1,400 crore of equity, built plants and bought European companies that earn little so far. Cupid has committed about ₹440 crore outside its condom business, roughly equal to its ₹451 crore net worth, including ₹331.5 crore of warrants in the listed retailer Baazar Style. The market rewards one and doubts the other.

In plain terms

These are two factories selling certified single-use products, mostly abroad. One is a steady, well-run plastics exporter that has spent heavily on plants and acquisitions and is waiting for the spending to earn a return. The other is a small condom maker that won a large government tender, doubled its sales, and is now spending its cash on becoming a consumer-products company. The stock market values the second at three times the first.

Both companies have a March year-end and both reported Q1 FY27 on 7 August 2026. Polymed's board meets on 5 November 2026 to approve Q2 FY27 (July–September), with an earnings call at 17:00 IST the same day. Cupid has not announced its board date; last year it reported Q2 on 12 November, and the regulatory deadline is 14 November. On 30 September it told the exchanges that Q2 total revenue would exceed ₹200 crore. Cupid has held no earnings call since January 2024, so its guidance arrives through press releases rather than question-and-answer sessions. The figures in this report are FY26 unless marked Q1 FY27 (Jun-26) or later.

One classification point matters before going further. This report covers the NSE 750's frozen July-2026 classification, "Healthcare · Medical Equipment & Supplies". Screener.in now files Cupid under consumer goods (personal care), and Cupid calls itself "Bharat's rapidly growing personal care company with a strong global healthcare franchise". In FY26, 24% of its revenue came from new consumer products that are not medical devices. Cupid is here on its legacy classification, and its valuation now rests on a consumer-goods story. That tension runs through the whole report.

01

Plastic, latex and the approval stack: how the money is made

The raw material is cheap. What customers pay for is sterility, a regulatory file and the certainty that the thing will not fail inside a patient.

Start with what a hospital nurse touches in a single shift. An IV cannula is a short plastic tube that slides off a needle into a vein; once it is in, the needle comes out and a drip set connects a fluid bag to it. A three-way stopcock lets a second drug enter the same line. A dialysis patient, three times a week, is connected by two fistula needles and a set of blood lines to a dialyzer, a cartridge of hollow fibres that filters the blood. Every one of these items is used once and thrown away. Polymed makes all of them, roughly 1.9 billion units a year across 15 plants in five countries.

The economics follow from that "use once" property. A disposable has no replacement cycle and no warranty claims. Demand is tied to procedures, not to hospital budgets for capital equipment, so it is steadier than demand for scanners or ventilators. The price per unit is small, so hospitals rarely haggle over a single cannula, but tender buyers (government medical-supply corporations, group purchasing organisations in the US, national health systems in Europe) negotiate hard on contracts for millions of units. And because the product enters the bloodstream, a buyer who switches to a cheaper supplier risks infections, leaks and needle-stick injuries to staff. Approved incumbents therefore hold their customers for years.

The same structure applies, with different inputs, to Cupid. A condom is made by dipping glass formers into compounded natural-rubber latex, curing it, stripping it off and testing every piece electronically for pinholes. Batches are then tested for burst volume and pressure against the ISO 4074 standard and the WHO/UNFPA specification. A female condom is a larger, more complex product made in far smaller volumes. Cupid's customers are mainly institutions: UNFPA under long-term agreements, the Partnership for Supply Chain Management (PFSCM, the Global Fund's procurement agent), national programmes such as South Africa's RT75 tender, and non-governmental organisations such as MSI and PSI. Prequalification is product-specific and site-specific, and independent labs test lots before they ship. A failed lot can cost a supplier its prequalification, and with it the tender market.

Where is the value captured? Not in the plastic. Polymed's FY26 cost of goods was ₹31.8 of every ₹100 of revenue; the other ₹68.2 paid for people, sterilisation, freight, regulatory work, selling and profit. Within disposables, value rises with complexity. Basic infusion sets, urine bags and Indian-tender syringes sell close to Chinese prices. Safety cannulae, needle-free connectors and closed systems earn a premium. PICC lines, implanted ports, dialyzers, stents and trauma implants earn the most and need the longest regulatory files and clinical evidence. Polymed says Citieffe's trauma implants sell in the US at four to five times European prices, which explains why a cannula company is buying an Italian orthopaedics firm and filing US approvals.

In condoms the value sits in prequalification and allocation. Karex, which makes more than five billion condoms a year, earned a net profit of RM0.29 million (about ₹0.7 crore) on RM462 million of revenue in its year to June 2026. Cupid, making roughly a tenth of that volume, earned ₹108 crore. The difference is not the latex. It is female condoms (a scarce prequalified product worth about 14 times a male condom per piece on the South African contract), a concentrated national tender, a weak rupee and an asset-light balance sheet. Section 16 takes this apart.

In plain terms

A cannula and a condom are both cheap to make. What makes them valuable is the paperwork and the testing that prove they are safe: European and US approvals for one, United Nations prequalification for the other. Those approvals take years to win and can be lost after a single bad batch. The two companies are, at heart, owners of approved factories.

The last layer of value is distribution, and both members are trying to climb into it. Global majors such as Becton Dickinson (BD), B. Braun and Terumo own the hospital contracts in the US and Europe. Indian makers often enter as private-label suppliers, earning the manufacturing margin but not the brand margin; PendraCare, the Dutch catheter maker Polymed bought in 2025, supplies two large OEMs, and P1H in Italy is the sole approved supplier of a Class III implant for one of the largest. Polymed is now going direct: a US subsidiary with group-purchasing contracts, a Brazilian licence platform (Medyneo), and Citieffe's own sales teams in Italy and Mexico. Cupid is going direct to Indian shoppers, through 1.5 lakh retail outlets and the stores of Baazar Style, the retailer in which it is investing ₹331.5 crore. In both cases the bet is that owning the customer is worth more than owning the factory. In both cases it costs money before it earns any.

02

Size and growth: a US$16 billion market with US$3 per head

India's device market is about 2% of the world's and growing at low-to-mid teens. Disposables, the segment the members serve, are a quarter of it.

The Indian medical-technology market was worth about US$14 billion in 2024 and an estimated US$16 billion in 2025, according to the BCG/EY figures Polymed reproduces in its presentations. At today's rupee (₹94.9 to the dollar) that is roughly ₹1.5 lakh crore. A separate consultancy, Rubix, puts 2025 at US$15.2 billion. The two agree on the size; they disagree sharply on the future. Rubix projects US$50.1 billion by 2030, a 26.9% compound annual growth rate. That figure matches the government's own National Medical Devices Policy target of US$50 billion and reads more like an aspiration than a forecast. BCG's US$30 billion "ambition" for 2030 implies about 13% a year from 2025, closer to the industry's actual record. The prudent reading is US$15–16 billion today, growing 12–15% a year.

The scale gap with the rest of the world is large. Global medtech sales were about US$682 billion in 2025 (BCG), so India is roughly 2% of the world market while holding 18% of its population. Per-head spending on medical technology is about US$3 in India against a global average of US$47. That gap is the long-run demand argument: as insurance (including the Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY scheme) pays for more hospital procedures and listed hospital chains add beds, each procedure pulls through cannulae, sets, catheters and dialysis kits. The hospitals report in this library (Indian Hospitals) describes the bed-building wave that feeds that demand.

What does the market consist of? On the BCG/EY split for 2025, electronic equipment (imaging, monitors, ventilators, other powered devices) is 56% of the Indian market. Disposables and consumables are 27%, in-vitro diagnostics (IVD) 8%, implants 7% and surgical instruments 2%. Disposables are therefore a market of about US$4.3 billion, or roughly ₹38,000–41,000 crore. Polymed's FY26 India revenue of ₹582 crore is about 1.5% of it. That share sounds small for a company that calls itself India's second-largest medical devices player, but the disposables market includes syringes, gloves, sutures, wound care and dozens of categories Polymed does not make, and much of it is supplied by unlisted companies and multinational subsidiaries.

In plain terms

India spends about US$3 a head a year on medical devices, the world about US$47. The market is growing in the low-to-mid teens, but more than half of it is electronic machines that India mostly imports. The listed companies sit in the smaller, cheaper end: disposables worth about ₹40,000 crore a year, and condoms worth a few thousand crore.

03

Imports, China and the policy push: the trade gap widened in FY26

India runs a deficit in equipment and something close to balance in consumables. The state's money goes mostly to the equipment side, not to the two listed members.

"India imports 70–80% of its medical devices" is the sentence every industry speech begins with. It comes from Rubix and similar studies; AiMeD, the domestic manufacturers' association, puts the figure at 80–85%. The customs data allow a more precise picture. On the four core chapter-90 headings (9018 medical and surgical instruments, 9019 respiratory and therapy apparatus, 9021 orthopaedic appliances and implants, 9022 X-ray and radiation apparatus), India exported US$2.41 billion and imported US$5.84 billion in FY26. Imports grew 13.7% and exports 4.7%, so the ratio of imports to exports widened from 2.2 times to 2.4 times in a single year. Converted at today's ₹94.9 to the dollar, that import bill is about ₹55,500 crore; at the FY26 average rate of roughly ₹88 it was about ₹51,400 crore.

Two cautions about these numbers. First, the core-four basket is narrower than the ministry's broader device basket, which includes parts of the plastics (39), rubber (40), machinery (84) and furniture (94) chapters. On that broader basis, FY25 device exports were US$4.01 billion, up 88% in six years, and imports about US$8.6 billion. Condoms (HS 4014.10) sit in chapter 40 and are tracked separately below. Second, a widely circulated figure of "₹28,106 crore exports and ₹94,664 crore imports in FY26" comes from an article dated 20 March 2026, before the year ended, on an undisclosed basis. This report does not use it.

China's role is subtler than the speeches suggest. On the core basket it supplied US$1,003 million in FY26, 17.2% of imports, up from 16.6% in FY25. Its share is highest in respiratory equipment (32%), imaging (21%) and dialysis apparatus (21%), and low in syringes and cannulae, where Singapore and the US dominate. AiMeD's complaint is about routing: imports from Hong Kong, Malaysia and Singapore rose sharply in 2024–25, and AiMeD argues that some are Chinese goods entering through free-trade partners. The dialysis data support that. Malaysian dialyzer imports rose 37.5% in FY26 and Hong Kong's rose sevenfold, from a small base.

For Polymed, China is a competitor in two places. At home, Chinese and Malaysian dialyzers enter duty-free under the ASEAN free-trade agreement and have forced price cuts; Polymed's renal revenue fell 3.8% in Q1 FY27 because, as management put it, it chose to hold price rather than defend volume. In third markets, Chinese factories compete on basic infusion sets and syringes. Polymed itself has run a plant in Laiyang near Qingdao since 2009, though its turnover there is small and shrinking (₹11.2 crore in FY26).

What the state is doing about it

Policy has four levers: subsidies for production, shared infrastructure, a regulatory and strategic framework, and trade remedies. The flagship is the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for medical devices, notified in 2020 with an outlay of ₹3,420 crore. It pays a percentage of incremental sales to approved makers in four target segments: cancer care and radiotherapy, radiology and imaging, anaesthesia/cardio-respiratory/renal care, and implants. By the government's 24 July 2026 reply to the Lok Sabha, 27 applications had been approved (14 of them MSMEs), 23 projects commissioned, ₹1,153 crore actually invested and ₹266.64 crore of incentives released. An earlier reply put cumulative releases at ₹133.95 crore to June 2025, so roughly ₹133 crore was paid in FY26; that is an INDICATIVE estimate from the difference. Cumulative eligible sales reached ₹10,413 crore by March 2025, of which ₹5,002 crore was exported. Named beneficiaries include Wipro GE, Siemens Healthineers, Philips, Nipro India (renal) and Meril. Neither Polymed nor Cupid is a named beneficiary.

The most direct policy lever on a member's profit is a trade remedy. In June 2026 the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) initiated an anti-dumping investigation into dialyzers from China and Malaysia (customs heading 9018.90.31, period of investigation calendar 2025), on Polymed's application. The only other Indian producer is Nipro Medical India, an export-oriented unit. Polymed claims an injury margin of 20%, and its managing director raised the case directly with the health minister. Section 18 explains why this matters less than it sounds: imports from the two countries were only about US$37 million in FY26, and DGTR's own notice records that the domestic industry was making losses on dialyzers.

In plain terms

India buys about two and a half dollars of medical devices abroad for every dollar it sells, and the gap is mostly scanners and machines. The government's subsidy money goes mainly to companies making those machines. The two listed members make the products India is already good at exporting, so they gain from policy indirectly: through tender preferences, a pending anti-dumping duty on Chinese dialyzers and small grants, not through large cheques.

The policy picture holds one more irony. The industry's argument for protection is that cheap Chinese imports undercut Indian makers, but the hardest-hit segments (imaging, respiratory) are ones where India has almost no domestic capacity to protect, while the segment where India is competitive (disposables) is already close to trade balance. Higher duties on equipment raise costs for hospitals and diagnostic chains (see the Diagnostics report), while doing little for the listed members. What helps Polymed most is not a tariff wall but faster CDSCO approvals for the products it moves from its European subsidiaries to Indian plants, which management says will not yield cross-selling before FY28.

Price caps and licences: who decides what a device may cost and where it may be sold

India caps the price of a few high-profile devices and limits every other price rise to 10% a year. Europe and the US do not cap prices; they gate market entry. Both regimes cost money and both create moats.

India's device makers learned in 2017 that the state will set prices where it sees profiteering. In February 2017 the NPPA brought coronary stents under price control by adding them to the national essential-medicines list and fixing ceiling prices; the price of a drug-eluting stent (DES) fell by roughly 85% overnight. Multinationals threatened to withdraw premium stents. In August 2017 the NPPA capped knee-implant prices. Both caps are still in place. The stent ceilings are revised every April by the wholesale price index: from 1 April 2026 (gazette S.O. 1587(E) of 25 March 2026) they are ₹10,762.15 for a bare-metal stent and ₹39,186.03 for a DES, including bioresorbable scaffolds, excluding GST. A year earlier they were ₹10,692.69 and ₹38,933.14; the 2026 rise was 0.65%. Hospitals must itemise the stent's brand, batch and cost on the patient's bill. The knee-implant cap was last extended to 15 November 2026 "or until further orders", and no decision on its renewal had been published by 1 November.

A common error needs correcting here. The NPPA's famous February 2019 "trade-margin rationalisation" order covered 42 non-scheduled anti-cancer drugs, not 42 devices. Trade-margin caps on devices came later, in 2021 during COVID, when the NPPA capped the margins distributors and retailers could add on pulse oximeters, blood-pressure monitors, nebulisers, digital thermometers, glucometers and oxygen concentrators. None of those are made by the two listed members.

For everything else, the binding rule is quieter. Under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order 2013, a non-scheduled product may not raise its maximum retail price by more than 10% in any 12 months. Every medical device is legally a "drug" for this purpose. That is the constraint on Polymed's Indian price increases: its 4–5% average rise in Q1 FY27, taken to pass on higher polymer costs after the West Asia war, sits within it. In April 2026 the government told the Rajya Sabha that it is not considering a "one nation, one price" rule for devices, because the existing caps already apply.

Licences: the rules that decide who may sell

India regulated most devices lightly until recently. The Medical Devices Rules 2017 came into force on 1 January 2018 and sorted devices into four risk classes, A (low, such as thermometers) to D (high, such as heart valves). From 1 April 2020 every medical device was notified as a "drug" and had to be registered. Licensing then became mandatory in two steps: Class A and B from 1 October 2022, Class C and D from 1 October 2023. Since then, every device sold in India needs a CDSCO manufacturing licence (forms MD-5 or MD-9) or an import licence (MD-15). That raised compliance costs for small makers and cut the long tail of unlicensed importers. It also gives CDSCO a gate on Polymed's own integration plan: products made by PendraCare in the Netherlands or Citieffe in Italy need Indian approval before they can be made or sold here, which is why management does not expect cross-selling before FY28.

In 2024 the government added a Uniform Code for Marketing Practices in Medical Devices, which governs gifts, sponsorships and samples for doctors. Condoms are older regulated products under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act (Schedule R; BIS IS 6200 and ISO 4074). IVD kits are licensed under the Medical Device Rules as in-vitro diagnostic devices.

Exports: Europe gates by certificate, the US by clearance

Europe does not cap device prices; it decides who may enter. The EU's Medical Device Regulation (Regulation 2017/745) replaced the older directives and became fully applicable on 26 May 2021. It demands more clinical evidence, closer post-market surveillance and fresh certification by a "notified body" for every product. Notified bodies were too few to re-certify Europe's installed base in time, so Regulation 2023/607 extended the transition: certificates issued under the old directives stay valid until 31 December 2027 for Class III and implantable Class IIb devices and 31 December 2028 for other classes, provided the maker filed an MDR application by May 2024. The cost is per product: a technical file, clinical evaluation and audit for each item in a catalogue of hundreds. Polymed has "30+ new products under registration in Europe" and about 25 more in the CE-marking pipeline. P1H is MDR-compliant and Citieffe has MDR clearance, which is part of why Polymed bought them. The EU's IVD Regulation runs on a similar extended timetable (to 2027–29 by class); Cupid's HIV and hepatitis-B kits already hold CE marks under it.

The US gates by FDA clearance. Most Class II devices (cannulae, sets, catheters) need a 510(k) premarket notification showing they are "substantially equivalent" to a device already on the market, and plants are registered and inspected. Polymed's group reports 15 US approvals obtained and 11–13 more in the pipeline, plus group-purchasing contracts signed. Its company breakdown reads as Polymed 4, Citieffe 10 and P1H 1 (15 in all), with PendraCare holding 8 clearances for a guiding catheter made for an OEM customer; that reconciliation is this report's reading of the slide. For condoms, the gate is WHO/UNFPA prequalification, which is specific to product and site. Cupid holds it for male and female latex condoms. Its new nitrile female condom must be prequalified separately, and USAID's own report records that only one female-condom maker (the FC2 plant in Malaysia) held US FDA 510(k) clearance.

Regulation therefore works in both directions. It is the moat: a Chinese moulder cannot sell a cannula into a German hospital without the same file Polymed spent years building, and a new condom factory cannot bid in a UNFPA tender without prequalification. It is also a cost and a single point of failure. Re-certifying a legacy CE portfolio by 2027–28 absorbs regulatory staff (Polymed has more than 200 people in quality and regulatory roles) and money that never shows up as a separate line. And because approvals are site-specific, a failed audit or a product recall can shut a plant out of a market at once. Investors should read the size of a company's regulatory team, and any warning letter or audit finding, as closely as its order book.

In plain terms

India caps prices; Europe and America cap entry. The listed members mostly sell into the second kind of market, where the hurdle is a stack of approvals rather than a price ceiling. That stack is expensive to build and quick to lose, which is exactly why the companies that hold it earn good margins.

Where ₹100 goes: people-heavy plastic, material-heavy latex

Polymed spends a third of its revenue on material and over 40% on people and overheads. Cupid spends over 40% on material and very little on anything else. Both earn a quarter to a third as operating profit.

The two members have the same broad margin but different cost shapes. Polymed's ₹1,875 crore of FY26 revenue went roughly as follows: ₹31.8 of every ₹100 on materials and consumables, ₹21.5 on employees, ₹1.6 on expensed research, and ₹21.0 on other expenses (power, freight, sterilisation, selling, regulatory fees and job work). Its share of an Egyptian associate added ₹0.3, leaving a company-defined operating EBITDA of ₹24.4. Below that line, ₹6.4 of other income (mainly treasury income on unspent equity raised in 2024) came in, while depreciation took ₹6.2, interest ₹1.0, the one-off labour-code charge and acquisition costs ₹0.9, and tax ₹5.7. Profit after tax was ₹17.1 of every ₹100.

Cupid's ₹357.7 crore was split very differently: ₹41.4 on raw materials, ₹8.9 on employees and ₹17.1 on other expenses, leaving ₹32.6 of EBITDA. Other income added ₹9.4, depreciation (₹1.4) and interest (₹0.8) took little, tax took ₹9.6, and profit after tax was ₹30.3. Cupid's fixed-asset base was only about ₹93 crore at March 2026, so depreciation is trivial; its people bill is small because latex dipping is automated and it runs essentially one plant.

The inputs, and what moved them in 2026

Polymed's main inputs are medical-grade polymers: plasticised PVC for tubing and bags, polypropylene, polycarbonate and ABS for hubs and chambers, polyurethane and fluoropolymers for catheter tubing, and silicone. Their prices follow crude oil and naphtha. When the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026 and the Strait of Hormuz closed, Indian polymer prices rose about 43% by mid-March; a ceasefire on 7 April brought Brent back to about US$95 from a peak near US$118. Polymed took average price increases of 4–5% in Q1 FY27 and said the impact had "peaked off as long as crude stays where it is". Stainless-steel needle tubing and EtO sterilisation are the other important inputs; prices for neither are disclosed.

Cupid's main input is centrifuged natural-rubber latex, plus nitrile for the new female condom, silicone oil and aluminium-foil wrappers. Natural rubber hit a record ₹284.50 a kilogram (RSS-4 grade, Kottayam) on 13 October 2026, driven by tyre demand and short supply (see Tyres, where the same commodity drives margins). Cupid said in May that it had secured more than six months of raw material, and in Q1 FY27 it pushed through "a minimum 10% price increase across the export portfolio". Karex raised its own prices 20–30% in April 2026, citing synthetic rubber, nitrile, foil and silicone oil.

Labour and freight were the other 2026 shocks. Haryana raised its minimum wage 35% from 1 April 2026, which pushed Polymed's standalone employee cost up about 28.5% year on year in Q1 FY27; most of its Indian plants are in Faridabad. The new national labour codes, notified on 21 November 2025, cost it a one-time ₹6.8 crore provision. Freight to the Middle East rose "two to three times in the last six months", in management's words, and orders sat at ports. For an exporter, a longer voyage means more working capital as well as more cost.

Who else makes it: unlisted giants, multinationals and a state condom maker

The listed sub-sector is two companies. The real competitive field is dozens of unlisted Indian firms, multinational subsidiaries and Chinese factories.

Most of India's device industry is not on the stock exchange. Rubix counts about 800 manufacturers, concentrated in four states (Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Haryana and Karnataka) and in clusters such as Faridabad, Ahmedabad and Baddi. In hospital disposables, the largest domestic name is Hindustan Syringes & Medical Devices (HMD) of Faridabad, the maker of Dispovan syringes and, by its own account, the world's largest single-site syringe maker. Claims of 8–10 billion units a year are not supported by any document found; the best evidence is that HMD made more than a billion 0.5 ml auto-disable syringes a year for COVID vaccination in 2021 and planned to lift total syringe capacity from 2.5 billion to over 3 billion. Its revenue is reported at "over ₹500 crore" on an undated basis. HMD's managing director coordinates AiMeD, the lobby that campaigns for higher tariffs on imported devices.

The multinationals are the incumbents in Polymed's main categories. BD India (with a plant at Bawal, Haryana), B. Braun Medical India, Terumo India and Nipro India are unlisted subsidiaries of global leaders in IV catheters, infusion, vascular access and renal care. Nipro is a PLI beneficiary for dialyzers and the only other domestic dialyzer producer named in the anti-dumping case. Smaller Indian makers include Romsons (Agra), Iscon Surgicals, Mediva and Sterimed. In cardiology, the field is led by Meril (Vapi; Bilakhia group; a PLI beneficiary), Sahajanand Medical Technologies (Surat; an IPO was attempted), Translumina (Dehradun) and the global majors Abbott, Boston Scientific and Medtronic. Polymed's new India and Asia-Pacific chief executive, Indranil Mukherjee, previously ran B. Braun India and Translumina. Healthium Medtech (sutures and surgical consumables) was bought by KKR from Apax in 2024, according to public reports not re-verified here.

In condoms the domestic field is led by the state. HLL Lifecare, owned by the Ministry of Health, makes the Moods brand and the free government-supply Nirodh, and is the largest government supplier; its disinvestment has stalled. Retail brands belong to pharma and consumer companies: Mankind Pharma's Manforce (more than 17% of the retail market), Reckitt's Durex, TTK's Skore, plus Okamoto and Playgard. Cupid's own retail brand is small; in Indian retail it has mainly been an OEM supplier. Globally, Karex of Malaysia is the volume leader (more than five billion a year), with Reckitt, Okamoto, Thai Nippon Rubber, several Chinese makers, HLL and Cupid behind it. In female condoms the incumbent is FC2, a nitrile product made in Malaysia. Its former owner Veru sold the business in December 2024 for US$18 million to funds managed by Riva Ridge Capital.

Five business models, and why the members are moving apart

The same factory skills can earn 1% or 30% depending on what is sold, to whom and with which certificate. Each member is migrating to a different model.

Five archetypes cover almost every company in this space, and the margin differences between them are large.

1. The export-led disposables manufacturer (Polymed standalone) sells millions of units of hundreds of products through distributors and OEMs abroad and through tenders and hospitals at home. Gross margins run 66–71% and EBITDA margins 25–28%. Capital intensity is moderate: asset turns (revenue divided by gross fixed assets) of 1.2–1.4 times and capex of 9–16% of sales in a build phase. The risks are price tenders, Chinese competition, currency and a failed regulatory audit.

2. The acquired Western specialist (Citieffe, PendraCare, P1H) sells implants, catheters or ports through surgeons and opinion leaders, often direct. Gross margins are high (Citieffe and PendraCare both about 68% on calendar 2025 revenue) but EBITDA margins are low because European sales and engineering staff are expensive: Citieffe about 18%, P1H 13.4%, PendraCare about 1% (€84,000 of EBITDA on €8.2 million of revenue). The plan is to move production to India and cut costs; the risk is integration.

3. The prequalified public-health supplier (Cupid's core, Karex's tender book, HLL) sells against tender allocations and long-term agreements. Gross margin is around 55–60% for Cupid and much lower for Karex; EBITDA ranged from 17% to 39% by quarter for Cupid between FY25 and Q1 FY27, while Karex was near breakeven. Assets are light (Cupid's fixed assets were ₹93 crore). The risks are donor budgets, re-bids and quality failures.

4. The branded consumer-wellness company (Manforce, Durex, Cupid's new FMCG arm) sells through distributors and retailers on brand and advertising. Gross margins of 60–70% fund heavy marketing, leaving steady-state EBITDA margins of 15–25% (INDICATIVE range). The risks are advertising burn, retail execution and working capital tied up in trade.

5. The capital equipment plus consumables model (Polymed's dialysis machines, about 1,100 installed, and its intravascular lithotripsy system) places a machine and earns on the single-use items each procedure consumes. Section 18 works through the numbers.

China+1, tariffs and the rupee: the boom went to syringes, not cannulae

America's 100% tariffs on Chinese syringes opened a door for Indian exporters. The customs data show who walked through it, and it was not the listed member.

In its four-year review of China tariffs, completed in 2024, the US Trade Representative raised the Section 301 duty on Chinese syringes and needles from 0% to 100% from 27 September 2024 (enteral syringes were exempt until 1 January 2026). Duties on Chinese rubber medical and surgical gloves went from 7.5% to 50% in 2025 and to 100% from 1 January 2026, and duties on facemasks and medical gowns also rose. A set of 178 product exclusions was extended to 10 November 2026. The intent was explicit: move American sourcing of basic medical consumables away from China. That is the "China+1" opportunity for Indian disposables makers.

India's own access to the US has been volatile. In 2025 the US imposed "reciprocal" tariffs on India under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), 25% plus a further 25%. A February 2026 India–US arrangement was reported to cut the effective rate on Indian devices from 50% to 18%, and on 20 February 2026 the IEEPA tariffs were struck down altogether. A temporary 10% surcharge under Section 122 applied until 24 July 2026. Since then, Indian goods have paid a 10% Section 301 duty on top of the normal most-favoured-nation (MFN) rate (China and Vietnam pay 12.5%), and that duty is under legal challenge. MFN duties on most disposables are low, 0–7%. Polymed's managing director put it simply on the Q1 FY27 call: "currently, the duty is only 10%", and "if we have 100% tariffs tomorrow or today night, I don't know".

The rupee: the tailwind nobody has to earn

The most immediate trade tailwind is not a tariff. It is the exchange rate. The rupee was about ₹94.9 to the dollar on 19 October 2026, close to a record low near ₹96.8, compared with an FY26 average of roughly ₹88. The Reserve Bank raised its repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, partly to defend the currency. For an exporter with mostly rupee costs, a weaker rupee raises revenue in rupees without raising most costs. Cupid says so plainly: "elevated USD/INR levels supporting export realizations". Polymed's exposure is mixed: much of its European revenue is in euros, and its acquired subsidiaries have euro costs, but its Indian plants export priced in dollars and euros with costs in rupees.

Condoms and the donor shock: a market cut in half, then rebuilt by national tenders

When USAID stopped buying in 2025, India's condom exports fell 27%. A year later they were back above the old level, but the buyers had changed.

Most of the world's public-health condoms have been bought by donors. The US Agency for International Development (USAID), through its Global Health Supply Chain Procurement and Supply Management (GHSC-PSM) project, approved procurement of 354.8 million male condoms, 2.6 million female condoms and 33.8 million lubricant sachets for 30 countries in its fiscal 2023 alone. Over fiscal 2016–23 it bought US$182.1 million of condoms and lubricants for 61 countries, 85.5% of the value going to Africa. In many countries USAID was almost the entire supply: it provided 98% of Zimbabwe's condoms and more than 90% of Liberia's male condoms. The Global Fund (through its procurement agent PFSCM) and UNFPA were the other large buyers.

In January 2025 the US froze foreign aid, and during 2025 USAID was dismantled and the US withdrew funding from UNFPA. The GHSC-PSM supply chain stopped. Kenya, which had received about 180 million condoms a year from the Global Fund and UNFPA, reported supply interruptions; UNFPA bought 6.6 million male condoms for Liberia in July 2025 as a stop-gap. The Global Fund's eighth replenishment in 2025 also fell short of its target. The effect on India's exports was immediate.

Three decades, six shocks: how the sub-sector got here

Both members were founded in the 1990s as small export manufacturers. Price caps, licensing, COVID, two equity raises, a change of control and a donor collapse made them what they are.

Cupid was incorporated in Nashik in 1993, listed on the BSE in 1995 and began production in 1996. Poly Medicure was founded in Faridabad in 1995 and raised ₹55 lakh in an IPO in 1996 (the company's presentation says 1996 and ₹55 lakh; the managing director said on a call that operations began in 1997 with a ₹2.65 crore IPO, and the written disclosure is used here). Through the 2000s both grew as certified exporters: Polymed built CE-marked cannula exports and set up a joint venture in Assiut, Egypt (2003) and a plant in Laiyang, China (2009); Cupid expanded capacity from 160 million to 400 million condoms in 2006, added female condoms in 2010 and later won large Brazilian orders.

The first shock was India's price-control turn in 2017, when stent and knee caps showed the state would intervene in device pricing. The second was regulatory: the Medical Device Rules (2018), the notification of all devices as drugs (2020) and full licensing (2023). The third was COVID. Demand for disposables, gloves, PPE and diagnostics spiked; the government capped trade margins on oximeters and similar devices and launched the PLI scheme. Polymed raised ₹400 crore in a qualified institutional placement (QIP) in 2021. Cupid's standalone revenue peaked at ₹160 crore in FY20 and fell to ₹133 crore by FY22 as COVID orders faded.

The fourth shock was a change of ownership and capital. In 2023 Cupid's founding Garg family sold about 41.8% to the Halwasiya group of Kolkata at ₹285 a share, about ₹159 crore; Aditya Kumar Halwasiya became chairman and managing director, and Columbia Petro Chem, a Halwasiya company, became a 12.95% holder. In February 2024 Cupid's shareholders approved a preferential warrant issue with ₹385 crore of stated objects. Polymed raised ₹1,000 crore in a QIP in August 2024. Weeks earlier its promoter holding had jumped from 53.15% to 66.03% with no new shares issued: Ezekiel Global Business Solutions LLP, a 12.88% holder classed as "public", joined the promoter group after an NCLT-sanctioned amalgamation made a promoter company its partner. The QIP then diluted the promoters to 62.56%.

The fifth shock was the 2025 collapse of donor condom funding, described in Section 9, which coincided with Cupid's South African win and Polymed's European acquisition spree (PendraCare and Citieffe in September–November 2025, Medyneo in Brazil in Q4 FY26) and its purchase, through India's insolvency process, of Himalayan Mineral Waters, a dormant company owning a factory next to Polymed's Haridwar plant and land in Bharuch, for ₹33.15 crore. The sixth was 2026: the West Asia war, the end of IEEPA tariffs and the start of India's 10% Section 301 duty, record rubber prices and a weak rupee. In the stock market, 2026 split the members: Cupid rose from about ₹46 to a high of ₹370 and joined the BSE Group A, the Nifty Smallcap 250 and the FTSE Emerging Markets All Cap index; Polymed fell from ₹2,094 to about ₹1,450.

Reading a device maker: the KPIs that matter Deep dive

A masterclass in the numbers: the profit equation, one native KPI at a time, an illustrative plant, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps and a ten-minute results checklist.

Every device maker's results can be read through one equation. Revenue is the number of units sold times the average price per unit. Gross profit is what is left after materials. EBITDA is gross profit minus people and running costs. Profit after tax adds treasury income and subtracts depreciation, interest and tax. The art is knowing which of those terms management controls and which it does not, and which of them each company defines in its own way.

For a disposables maker, management controls the product mix (more safety cannulae and PICC lines, fewer basic sets), the list price within the 10% Indian cap and within tender contracts abroad, the number of people it employs, and how much it spends on plants and acquisitions. It does not control polymer and latex prices, freight rates, the exchange rate, the timing of regulatory approvals or the budgets of donors and governments. For a condom maker, the biggest term (tender allocations) is set by a buyer, and the second-biggest (the dollar price) by a competitive bid.

A worked example: one Polymed unit, from price to profit

Take Polymed's FY26 consolidated accounts and spread them across the units it sold. It has capacity for about 1.9 billion units. Suppose it sold about 1.5 billion, roughly 80% utilisation (an assumption; the company does not disclose volumes). Then ₹1,875 crore of revenue means an average price of about ₹12.5 per unit. That average hides a wide range: a basic IV set sells for perhaps ₹10–20, a safety cannula for ₹25–60, a PICC line or a stent for thousands of rupees (INDICATIVE ranges). With a 68.2% gross margin, gross profit is about ₹8.5 per unit and materials about ₹4.0. People (₹2.7), other costs (₹2.6) and research (₹0.2) take the unit EBITDA down to about ₹3.05, which is 24.4%. Check: 1.5 billion units × ₹2.14 = ₹321 crore, the reported PAT. The whole example is ILLUSTRATIVE, built on an assumed volume.

In plain terms

Polymed sells each product for about ₹12.50 on average, spends about ₹4 on the material, about ₹5.50 on people and running costs, and keeps about ₹2 as profit after tax. Most of the profit comes from selling a very large number of approved items, not from a large margin on any single one.

1. Export share and revenue by region

What it is. The share of revenue earned outside India, and how fast each region grows. Formula: export revenue ÷ revenue from operations. Worked example: Polymed FY26: Europe ₹597 crore + rest of world ₹696 crore = ₹1,293 crore, divided by ₹1,875 crore = 69%. Cupid FY26: ₹208.13 crore of revenue from outside India ÷ ₹357.71 crore = 58.2% (the company states 59.3%, on its segment total). Drivers: new customer wins abroad, tender awards, freight and shipping disruption (the Middle East fell 32% in Q1 FY27), and the exchange rate, which inflates rupee export revenue when the rupee weakens. Benchmark: there is no "good" export share; a high share means more currency tailwind at ₹94.9 and more exposure to tariffs and freight. What matters is the split by region and its stability. How the members compare: Polymed's export share rose to 72% in Q1 FY27 including the acquired European units (68% organic). Cupid's exports are concentrated in a few institutional buyers.

The trap. "Exports" is not "Europe and the US". Polymed's 69% is 32% Europe and 37% rest of world, and its US sales are only about US$3.5–4 million. The rest-of-world bucket hides the Middle East, Africa, Latin America and Southeast Asia, which move very differently. Always ask for the regional split, and separate currency from volume.

2. Organic versus acquired growth

What it is. Revenue growth split into what the existing business grew by itself and what was added by buying companies. Formula: organic growth = (revenue − revenue of businesses acquired in the last 12 months − prior-year revenue) ÷ prior-year revenue. Worked example: Polymed Q1 FY27 revenue was ₹525.4 crore against ₹403.2 crore a year earlier, +30.3%. PendraCare and Citieffe contributed ₹72.3 crore, so organic revenue was ₹453.1 crore, +12.4%. Drivers: organic growth comes from volumes, prices and mix; acquired growth from deal timing. Benchmark: organic growth above 15% is good for an Indian disposables maker, 10–15% average, below 10% weak (INDICATIVE). Polymed's Q1 FY27 organic 12.4% is average; its FY27 standalone guidance (₹1,900–2,000 crore, +14–20%) requires faster growth in Q2–Q4. Management's long-term mix is about 80% organic and 20% inorganic, which an analyst on the call framed as 14–15% organic plus 3–4 points from acquisitions.

The trap. Acquired revenue arrives with its own costs. Citieffe and PendraCare run high gross margins but very high people costs, so acquired growth can lift revenue 30% while profit falls (as it did in Q1 FY27). Also watch for the year-on-year comparison changing when a deal anniversaries: from Q3 FY27 (Citieffe) Polymed's reported growth will fall back towards organic growth.

3. Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after the cost of materials and goods. Formula: (revenue − materials consumed − change in inventory − purchased goods) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Polymed FY26: ₹1,875.3 crore − ₹596.8 crore = ₹1,278.4 crore, 68.2%. Cupid FY26: ₹357.7 crore − ₹148.1 crore = ₹209.6 crore, 58.6%. Drivers: product mix (specialty products carry higher margins), polymer and latex prices, price rises passed through, backward integration, and inventory gains when raw-material prices rise after stock was bought cheaply. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): disposables above 68% good, 60–68% average, below 60% weak; condoms above 55% good, 35–55% average, below 35% weak. How the members compare: Polymed's consolidated margin jumped to 73.4% in Q1 FY27 as the higher-margin European units were consolidated; its standalone 71.5% was flattered by price rises and inventory gains and is guided to normalise at 67–69%, with consolidated at 71–72%. Cupid's 58.6% is high for a latex product because of female condoms and FMCG.

The trap. A rising gross margin is not always good news. Polymed's acquired units have higher gross margins but much higher people costs below the gross line, so the consolidated gross margin rose while EBITDA margin fell. Job work (outsourced manufacturing; ₹104 crore at Polymed standalone in FY26) may sit in "other expenses" rather than materials, which moves cost below the gross line. Compare gross margins only within the same company over time, and always read them next to EBITDA margin.

4. EBITDA margin, and its three bases

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, as a share of revenue. Formula: (gross profit − employees − other operating expenses) ÷ revenue, excluding other income. Worked example: Polymed FY26 company "Operating EBITDA" ₹457.7 crore ÷ ₹1,875.3 crore = 24.4%. Cupid FY26 ₹116.7 crore ÷ ₹357.7 crore = 32.6%. Drivers: gross margin, wage inflation (Haryana +35% from April 2026), operating leverage as plants fill, acquisitions with thin margins, and one-offs. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): disposables above 25% good, 18–25% average, below 18% weak; condoms and wellness above 25% good, 12–25% average, below 12% weak. How the members compare: Polymed's consolidated margin fell from 27.5% (FY25) to 24.4% (FY26) and 24.1% (Q1 FY27); its parent company held 26.8% and 28.0%. FY27 guidance is 23–25% consolidated and 25–27% standalone. Cupid's margin rose from 22.7% (FY25) to 32.6% (FY26) and 38.8% (Q1 FY27), on price, mix and the rupee. CRISIL's downgrade trigger for Polymed is a sustained margin below 20%.

The trap. Polymed's "EBITDA" exists on three bases: screener's operating profit (₹446 crore, 23.8%), the company's consolidated "Operating EBITDA" (₹457.7 crore, 24.4%, which excludes other income and ₹9.7 crore of acquisition costs and includes ₹5.7 crore of associate share) and the standalone figure (₹446.1 crore, 26.8%). The standalone ₹446.1 crore is coincidentally almost identical to screener's consolidated ₹446 crore. Do not mix them. This report uses screener operating profit in tables and the company figure in prose.

5. Other income as a share of profit before tax

What it is. How much of pre-tax profit comes from treasury income, interest, export incentives and gains rather than from operations. Formula: other income ÷ profit before tax. Worked example: Polymed FY26: ₹120 crore ÷ ₹427.5 crore = 28%. Cupid FY26: ₹33.7 crore ÷ ₹142.5 crore = 24%. Drivers: cash raised and not yet spent, interest rates, and mark-to-market on investments. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): below 10% good, 10–20% average, above 20% a quality flag. Both members were above 20% in FY26. How the members compare: Polymed's other income rose from 21% of PBT in FY22 to 28% in FY26, because about ₹484 crore of its 2024 QIP was still parked in funds and deposits at March 2026. Cupid's fell from ₹11–12 crore a quarter in H2 FY26 to ₹2 crore in Q1 FY27 as its cash moved into Baazar Style and the GII Healthcare fund; its Q1 FY27 profit growth was therefore entirely operating.

The trap. Treasury income disappears when the cash is spent. A company that deploys its cash into plants or acquisitions loses the interest immediately and gains the operating profit only later, so reported profit can fall even when the deployment is sensible. Conversely, a company sitting on cash looks more profitable than its operations are. Strip other income out before comparing operating performance.

7. Patents and regulatory approvals

What it is. The count of granted patents and, more importantly, of market approvals: US FDA 510(k) clearances, EU CE certificates under the MDR, Indian CDSCO licences, WHO/UNFPA prequalifications, and the pipeline of each. Formula: a count; the useful ratio is approvals in the pipeline ÷ approvals held, which shows the pace of market entry. Worked example: Polymed's group holds 15 US approvals with 11–13 in the pipeline, a pipeline ratio of roughly 0.8; it has 30+ products under EU registration and about 25 more in the CE pipeline; it launched 35 products in FY26 and 6 in Q1 FY27 and targets 25–30 a year. It holds 399 patents. Cupid holds WHO/UNFPA prequalification for male and female latex condoms and CE IVDR certificates for HIV 1&2 and hepatitis-B kits (plus CE for syphilis and pregnancy tests). Drivers: regulatory team size (Polymed has 200+ quality and regulatory staff), notified-body capacity in Europe, FDA review times. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): more than 20 FDA clearances good, 5–20 average, fewer than 5 weak. Polymed's group is average; its Indian parent alone (4) is weak.

The trap. Patents count innovation, not access. A device with 50 patents and no FDA clearance cannot be sold in the US; a device with no patents and a 510(k) can. And approvals are site-specific: two-thirds of Polymed's US approvals came with Citieffe and belong to its Italian plant, so moving production to India (management's cost plan) means fresh filings.

8. Capacity and utilisation

What it is. How many units a company can make a year and what share of that it uses. Formula: utilisation = units produced ÷ installed capacity. Worked example: Cupid's existing plant can make about 513 million male and 50 million female condoms a year (derived from the company's statement that Palava will add 770 million and 75 million, "1.5 times the existing output"), and management says it runs at "full capacity utilisation". Polymed's capacity rose from about 1.0 billion units in eight plants (FY20) to about 1.9 billion in 15 plants (FY26); it does not disclose utilisation. Drivers: demand, plant start-ups and ramp-ups, product changeovers. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): 80–90% is healthy; above 95% means growth needs new capacity; below 65% means fixed costs are under-absorbed. How the members compare: Cupid is at the limit, with Palava commissioning slipping from "Q2 FY27" to "imminent"; Polymed has two plants under construction (Faridabad–Palwal by March 2027; Noida, cardiology, commercial in Q1 FY28).

Cupid's "1.25 billion" is planned capacity, not today's; Polymed's "1.9 billion" counts very different products together. 9. Capex to sales, CWIP and asset turns

What it is. How much a company spends on fixed assets relative to revenue, how much of that is not yet producing (capital work in progress, CWIP, and land banks), and how much revenue each rupee of gross fixed assets generates. Cupid spent ₹24.7 crore in FY26 (6.9% of revenue), had ₹25.4 crore of CWIP and ₹64 crore of commitments: the Palava plant, which this report estimates at a total of about ₹90–130 crore (INDICATIVE). Drivers: build phases, acquisitions of land and plants. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): steady-state capex 6–10% of sales; 10–15% expansion; above 15% a heavy build phase. Asset turns above 1.5× good, 1.0–1.5× average. Polymed guides 1.2–1.4×.

Polymed's ₹386 crore of FY26 investment in subsidiaries is separate from its ₹308 crore capex, and its NCLT purchase of Himalayan Mineral Waters (₹33.15 crore, really a factory and land) appears as an "advance against purchase of company". 10. Receivable days and the cash conversion cycle

What it is. How long customers take to pay, and how long cash is tied up between buying materials and collecting from customers. 11. Cash conversion: from profit to free cash flow

What it is. How much of reported profit turns into operating cash, and how much is left after capex. Formulas: CFO ÷ EBITDA; free cash flow (FCF) = cash from operations − capex. What it is. Formulas: ROCE = EBIT ÷ (equity + debt); ROIC (company definition) = operating profit ÷ (capital employed − cash − CWIP and land bank − subsidiary investments). Worked example: Polymed's March 2026 capital employed on screener's basis was about ₹3,460 crore (equity ₹3,106 crore plus borrowings ₹354 crore). How to read a device maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue, reported and organic. Pull revenue and the year-on-year change, then subtract acquisitions (Polymed discloses them on the call). If organic growth is below 10% for a disposables maker, ask why before reading anything else.
  2. Revenue by region. Europe, rest of world, India for Polymed; the share of exports for Cupid. A sudden fall in one region (the Middle East in 2026) usually means logistics or currency, not lost customers.
  3. Segment mix. Infusion, renal, cardiology, orthopaedics and others for Polymed; female condoms and FMCG for Cupid where disclosed. Mix drives gross margin.
  4. Gross margin. Compare with the same quarter last year and the guidance band (Polymed standalone 67–69%, consolidated 71–72%). A jump above guidance often includes inventory gains that will reverse.
  5. Employee cost as % of sales. The fastest-moving cost in 2026 (Haryana wages, acquired European staff). A rise of more than one point needs an explanation.
  6. EBITDA margin on the company's basis. Check it against guidance (Polymed 23–25% consolidated, 25–27% standalone) and note one-offs.
  7. Other income and depreciation. Rising depreciation (new plants) and falling other income (cash spent) can cut profit while operations improve. Strip both to see operating trends.
  8. Profit after tax, and the tax rate. Compare PAT growth with EBITDA growth; a gap points to items 7 and 8.
  9. Balance sheet at the half-year. Net cash (on the company's definition), receivables and inventory days, capex and CWIP. For Cupid, check how much cash has gone into Baazar Style and GII.
  10. Guidance and the red flags. Did guidance change, and on which basis? Red flags: receivable days rising for two quarters, other income above a quarter of PBT, a change in revenue definition, a slipping plant date, a regulatory warning, or a new related-party flow.
In plain terms

Start with how much of the growth was bought and how much was earned, then check whether margins held after removing interest income and one-offs, and finish by checking whether the cash came in. For these two companies, the cash is where the surprises have been.

Same latex, opposite economics: why Cupid earns what Karex cannot Deep dive

The world's largest condom maker earned a 0.06% net margin in its latest year. A Nashik maker with a tenth of its volume earned 30%. The gap is explained by six things, and only some of them last.

Karex made more than five billion condoms in its year to June 2026 and earned RM291,000 of net profit on RM462.4 million of revenue. Cupid made roughly half a billion (INDICATIVE; volumes are not disclosed) and earned ₹108 crore on ₹358 crore. Same raw material, same dipping process, same ISO standard, overlapping customers. Why such different economics? Six explanations stack up, in roughly descending order of importance.

(a) Female-condom scarcity. On the South African contract a female condom sells for about US$0.38 and a male condom for about US$0.027, so each female condom brings in about 14 times the revenue. Female condoms were 17% of Cupid's FY26 revenue. Only a handful of female condoms are prequalified, and the incumbent, FC2, is made at one Malaysian plant now owned by private-equity-backed funds after Veru sold it for US$18 million in December 2024. Karex's volume is overwhelmingly male condoms, where Thai, Chinese, Malaysian and Indian plants compete on price. (b) A concentrated national tender. South Africa's RT75 allocation is worth about ₹115 crore a year, about a third of Cupid's FY26 revenue, for five years from December 2025. Karex's tender book was hit by the USAID collapse; Cupid, never a USAID supplier, picked up national buyers that replaced USAID. (c) The rupee. Cupid's export prices are in dollars and its costs in rupees; the rupee averaged about ₹88 in FY26 and is near ₹94.9 now, and Cupid itself credits "elevated USD/INR levels" for its export realisations. (d) Other income. ₹33.7 crore, 24% of Cupid's FY26 pre-tax profit, was treasury income on cash raised in 2024; that cushion fell to ₹2 crore in Q1 FY27. (e) The FMCG mix. 24% of FY26 revenue came from perfumes, deodorants and face wash, at an undisclosed margin. (f) An asset-light book. Fixed assets were only ₹93 crore and depreciation 1.4% of sales; Karex carries capacity for five billion pieces that its own management says the industry is "rationalising".

Which of the six last? Female-condom scarcity is the most durable, but it is not permanent. Four things would. Dialysis: the razor, the blade and the Chinese dialyzer Deep dive

Polymed's renal business pairs placed machines with the single-use kit every session consumes. The anti-dumping case on Chinese dialyzers matters less to group profit than it sounds.

India's dialysis demand is large and state-funded. The Pradhan Mantri National Dialysis Programme, launched in 2016, ran 1,856 haemodialysis centres with 13,535 machines across 751 districts at 30 June 2026, up from 1,704 centres a year earlier, according to parliamentary replies reported in the press; earlier replies cite about 28.5 lakh patients served and about 355 lakh sessions cumulatively. A haemodialysis patient typically needs three sessions a week, and each session consumes a pair of fistula needles, a set of blood lines and, unless reused, a dialyzer. Polymed makes all three, plus haemodialysis catheters, peritoneal-dialysis sets, transducer protectors and the machines themselves. It calls itself the first Indian company to manufacture dialysis products indigenously. It sold 450 machines in FY26 and had about 1,100 installed by July 2026. Renal revenue rose from ₹26 crore in FY20 to ₹187 crore in FY26.

Both members are net-cash companies whose recent results depend on how they spend capital. The market prices one as a high-quality compounder and the other as a future consumer franchise.

Technology and disruption: safety needles, DEHP, sterilisation and synthetic latex

Most disruption in this sub-sector is regulatory or material, not digital. The members' risk is falling behind a rule change.

Safety-engineered devices. The US Needlestick Safety and Prevention Act (2000) and the EU's Directive 2010/32/EU push hospitals from plain cannulae towards safety cannulae that shield the needle tip after use. India has no legal mandate, but hospital chains and insurers are moving that way; Polymed's MD expects Ayushman Bharat hospitals to become more "quality-oriented" as insurance deepens. Polymed holds patents in safety and needle-free designs, so the shift is an opportunity for it and a threat to commodity makers. DEHP-free PVC and PVC-free materials. The EU MDR requires justification for substances such as the plasticiser DEHP above 0.1% by weight in devices that contact the body, which forces reformulation of tubing and bags. Early movers gain; laggards face costly redesigns. Sterilisation. The US Environmental Protection Agency's 2024 rule on ethylene-oxide emissions could push sterilisation towards gamma or electron beam, adding capex for exporters that rely on EtO, as most disposables makers do.

Dialysis. Home haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis and changing reuse rules all alter consumables per patient; Polymed makes peritoneal sets as well as haemodialysis kits, which hedges part of the shift. Cardiology. Drug-coated balloons and intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) are growing in interventional cardiology; Polymed has a drug-eluting balloon with pre-clinical data and an IVL system "run on machine", but it is a late entrant against Meril and the multinationals, and its PACIFIER stent registry is still enrolling. Condoms. Synthetic condoms (polyisoprene, nitrile, polyurethane) are gaining over latex in regulated markets; Karex is scaling synthetics and Cupid's Palava lines are dual-polymer. HIV prevention is shifting towards oral PrEP and, since 2025, long-acting injectable lenacapavir, which may lower condom priority in HIV budgets. IVD. Rapid lateral-flow tests face a slow shift towards molecular point-of-care and self-testing, which matters for Cupid's small IVD line. Connected and AI-enabled devices are irrelevant for most disposables; they matter only for dialysis machines and the data they generate.

Bull versus bear

The case for and against the sub-sector, stated as plainly as the numbers allow.

The bull case

  • Certified moats: CE/MDR, FDA and WHO/UNFPA approvals turn cheap plastic and latex into 58–73% gross margins, and they take years to replicate.
  • Structural demand: India spends US$3 a head on devices against US$47 globally, and insurance plus hospital building raise procedure volumes; national condom tenders are replacing donors.
  • Polymed's operating core is intact: 28% standalone EBITDA in Q1 FY27, 17.6% European organic growth, about 17–21% returns on operating capital, ₹855 crore of cash and plants that should fill from FY27–FY28.
  • Trade tailwinds: a rupee near ₹95, US tariffs of 100% on Chinese syringes and gloves against 10% on India, and a dialyzer anti-dumping case.
  • Cupid's gated niche: dual prequalification, female-condom scarcity, a five-year South African contract, 39% EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27, and a competitor (Karex) that is shrinking capacity and raising prices.

The bear case

  • Returns on new capital: Polymed's ROCE fell from 24% to 12%; PendraCare earns about 1%; free cash flow has been negative for five years; receivables rose to 103 days.
  • Earnings quality: treasury income was 28% (Polymed) and 24% (Cupid) of FY26 pre-tax profit, and the rupee flatters FY27.
  • Concentration: about a third of Cupid's FY26 revenue, and perhaps two-thirds of its EBITDA (INDICATIVE), rests on one South African contract.
  • Capital allocation and disclosure at Cupid: ~₹440 crore committed outside the core, near-zero net cash by September (INDICATIVE), a full plant, a late new plant, board churn and no earnings calls.
  • Valuation: a combined 136× P/E; Polymed at ~32× EV/EBITDA against global peers near 12–14×; Cupid at 318× earnings and 97× book, owned largely by retail shareholders who arrived in 2026.

The bull and bear cases are not symmetrical across the two members. For Polymed, the debate is about timing: the business is good, and the question is whether the capital raised in 2021–24 earns its keep by FY28. For Cupid, the debate is about scale: the niche is real, and the question is whether the share price, which already discounts a consumer business many times larger, can be met by a company that is full at its only plant, spending its cash on other companies and growing its guidance faster than its capacity. A reader who is bullish on one and bearish on the other is not being inconsistent.

What would change each view? For Polymed, a quarter in which consolidated EBITDA margin rises above 25% with organic growth above 15% would strengthen the bull case; another quarter of falling profit, or an acquisition before PendraCare is fixed, the bear case. For Cupid, a Q2 balance sheet showing healthy cash, a commissioned Palava and a disclosed FMCG margin would strengthen the bull case; a Q2 that meets the ₹200 crore revenue mark mainly through the Baazar channel, with cash drained, would strengthen the bear.
