00

The one-page view: a record year, a dry monsoon and a high base

India sold more than a million tractors at home for the first time in FY26. FY27 has to live with that number.

India buys more tractors than any other country. In FY26 (April 2025 to March 2026) tractor makers dispatched 11,60,231 tractors to Indian dealers, up 23.5%, the first year above ten lakh, and shipped another 1.06 lakh abroad, for a total of about 12.66 lakh. A year earlier the domestic figure was 9.40 lakh, and as late as April 2025 CRISIL had forecast 9.75 lakh. The outturn beat that forecast by roughly a fifth.

The record was not one force but four stacked together. The 2025 south-west monsoon came in at about 108% of its long-period average (LPA), which filled reservoirs and set up a good rabi wheat crop. On 22 September 2025 the GST on tractors fell from 12% to 5%, cutting the shop price of a typical tractor by ₹40,000–60,000. Several states (Gujarat, Maharashtra and Bihar among them) paid out generous subsidies. And dealers and farmers bought ahead of a tougher engine-emission standard, TREM V, that was then due to raise the price of most tractors. The RBI had also cut its policy rate by 125 basis points over 2025, which made the loan that funds almost every tractor cheaper.

FY27 began strongly (Q1 FY27 domestic wholesale 3.39 lakh, +18.6%, the best first quarter on record) and then turned. July was up 20%, August up 6.5%, and September 2026 fell 18.1% to 1,19,667, against a base inflated by the GST cut and with the festive season shifted into October. The 2026 monsoon ended at about 87% of LPA (a deficit of 12.6%), which IMD-cited reports call the fourth weakest since 2001. The government then raised the wheat support price for the 2027-28 marketing season by only ₹25, to ₹2,610 a quintal, the smallest increase in about seventeen years. ICRA expects FY27 domestic volume to grow 1–4% and CRISIL 0–2%; Escorts Kubota, in August, said "mid-single-digit".

The economics are those of a profitable, cash-rich oligopoly. M&M's standalone farm-equipment segment earned a profit before interest and tax (PBIT) of ₹7,206 Cr on ₹36,409 Cr of revenue in FY26, a 19.8% margin. Sonalika earned an 18.8% operating margin in FY25; Escorts Kubota 13.2% EBITDA in FY26; TAFE 9.6% in FY25. Each of the three big independents sits on ₹7,600–9,840 Cr of cash and liquid investments. Funded challengers fight for share, but none has had to sell tractors at a loss to survive, and that is one reason industry margins have stayed inside a 13–20% band through good years and bad.

Escorts Kubota is the lens. It is the only listed company whose business is mainly tractors: M&M's farm business is about a fifth of a much larger group and V.S.T. Tillers is mainly a power-tiller maker. Kubota of Japan owns 54.07% of Escorts after buying in from 2020 and merging its Indian joint ventures into it in 2024. FY26 consolidated revenue was ₹11,540 Cr and reported profit ₹2,394 Cr, but that includes a ₹1,004 Cr gain on selling the railway-equipment division; the clean continuing profit was about ₹1,380 Cr. At ₹2,627 a share on 23 October 2026 the market value was ₹29,390 Cr, about a third of which is covered by ₹9,840 Cr of cash, deposits and treasury investments.

In plain terms

A tractor in India is a farmer's largest purchase after land, and he almost always borrows to buy it. He buys when his last two harvests were good, when crop prices are firm and when a lender says yes. FY26 had all of that plus a tax cut and a rush to beat a price-raising emission rule. FY27 has a weak monsoon, a tiny wheat price increase and last year's rush in the comparison base. The industry is not collapsing, but the easy growth is over for now.

The forces that define this industry, and the order in which this report takes them, are: (1) the monsoon, which drives farm cash flow and tractor demand with a lag of one or two seasons; (2) terms of trade, meaning crop prices (above all the minimum support price, MSP) against diesel, fertiliser and tractor prices; (3) policy price shocks, the GST cut that pulled demand forward and the TREM emission rules whose real cliff is now in 2032; (4) tractor finance, because roughly 95% of buyers borrow; (5) concentration and cash-rich challengers, which keep margins in a stable band; (6) Kubota, as a technology and export option for Escorts; and (7–8) the shift of demand towards the South and West and towards 41–50 HP tractors.

The rest of the report follows the money. Sections 1–4 explain how a tractor earns, how large the market is and what drives demand and credit. Section 5 is a deep dive on the FY26 record and why FY27 turns. Sections 6–11 cover costs, structure, business models, regulation, regional and HP mix, and the history of the cycle. Section 12 is the KPI masterclass, the core reference for reading any tractor company's results. Sections 13–15 are the peer table, the positioning maps and the Escorts profile. Sections 16–18 are deep dives on Japanese capital, exports and Escorts' cash. The report closes with technology, the bull and bear cases, what to watch and a glossary.

01

How a tractor earns: from steel and castings to a farmer's loan

A tractor maker is an engine and transmission assembler with a dealer network and a banker's view of rural India.

An Indian tractor is a simple, rugged machine sold to a customer whose income depends on the weather. That fact shapes everything about how the industry makes money: who it sells through, how it prices, how it finances its customers and how it survives a drought.

The product is mostly a 2-wheel-drive utility tractor of 41–50 horsepower, sold with a shop (ex-showroom) price of ₹6.5–8.5 lakh. It pulls implements (rotavators, cultivators, seed drills, harvesters) and trailers. For many owners it is also a haulage vehicle: it carries sugarcane to the mill, bricks from the kiln and sand to building sites. In a normal year commercial haulage is 12–20% of tractor use; one analyst quoted by Autocar Professional put it far higher in FY26. Some tractors are bought by custom-hiring centres, which rent tractor time to small farmers who cannot afford to own one; India had about 75,900 such centres in 2025.

Most of the profit is captured in Step 2 by brand and scale; the aftermarket for spares and service on an installed base of roughly 80–100 lakh tractors (indicative) is the steady annuity.

Step 1, the inputs, is where most of the cost sits. Materials and bought-out components are 69–73% of an OEM's revenue (Escorts Kubota 69.7% in FY26 and 72.8% in Q1 FY27). The big items are castings and forgings for the engine and transmission, steel bars and sheet, tyres, hydraulics, fuel-injection equipment (Bosch and Delphi supply common-rail systems on tractors above 50 HP since the TREM IV rule of January 2023), batteries, plastics and rubber. Because metal and rubber dominate the basket, steel and natural-rubber prices set the direction of gross margins. The supplier side is covered in our castings and forgings, auto components and steel products reports.

Step 2, the OEM, makes the engine, gearbox and axles, assembles the tractor and owns the brand. Most majors make their own engines; Escorts also buys Kubota engines for 10,000–12,000 of its tractors a year. Plants are large and few: Sonalika runs one plant at Hoshiarpur with a three-shift capacity of 3 lakh tractors a year; Escorts Kubota has 1,70,000 units of assembly capacity at Faridabad and ran at 77% in FY26. Tractor makers raise list prices about once a year, usually in January or April, and pass on cost inflation with a lag of one or two quarters.

Step 3, the dealer, is usually exclusive to one brand. Escorts sold through 2,072 dealers and distributors in FY26, with 92.5% of sales through dealers and its top ten dealers only 8.2% of sales. TAFE has more than 1,600 dealers. Dealers normally hold about 30 days of stock (M&M says 30–40 days), earn a margin of roughly 5–6% plus payouts from financiers and insurers, and do most of the selling of spares and service. The dealer is also the point where wholesale numbers (factory dispatches, the TMA basis) and retail numbers (registrations, the FADA basis) diverge.

Step 4, the financier, decides whether the marginal buyer can buy at all. About 95% of tractors are bought on credit, usually a loan of 4–6 years with half-yearly instalments timed to harvests. Lenders include Mahindra Finance, Shriram Finance, Cholamandalam, L&T Finance, public-sector and private banks, regional rural banks and the OEMs' own captive arms (Escorts Kubota Finance; Sonalika's Autotrac Finance; John Deere Financial India). Section 4 looks at this link in detail.

Step 5, the use, determines replacement. CRISIL estimates that 60–65% of demand is replacement, with a replacement cycle of 5–9 years depending on hours of use. Heavy haulage use shortens the cycle, which is one reason the commercial-use share matters for future volumes.

Where the value is captured. The OEM keeps most of the profit, and scale and brand decide how much. M&M's standalone farm segment earned ₹7,206 Cr of PBIT on 5,26,403 tractors in FY26, about ₹1.37 lakh per tractor including farm machinery and spares; its core tractor business alone earned about ₹1.21 lakh per tractor in Q1 FY27. Escorts Kubota's agri-machinery segment earned about ₹0.92 lakh of EBIT per tractor in FY26 (indicative, ₹1,232 Cr over 1,33,670 tractors). Component suppliers carry commodity risk on thinner margins. Dealers live on volume and service income. Financiers earn interest on loans that rise and fall with the same harvests that drive tractor sales.

In plain terms

A tractor maker buys about ₹70 of steel, castings and parts for every ₹100 it sells, spends ₹15–20 on people, plants, freight and marketing, and keeps ₹10–20 as operating profit. It sells through dealers who sell to farmers who borrow. When rain is good, all four links move together, and when it fails they all tighten at once.

The aftermarket deserves a word. India's installed base is roughly 80–100 lakh tractors (indicative: about 17 years of sales at 5–11 lakh a year). Each needs tyres, filters, clutch plates, hydraulic seals and periodic overhauls, and the OEM's genuine-parts business earns better margins than new tractors. Escorts' non-tractor revenue (spares, implements, engines, harvesters and transplanters) was 19% of its agri segment in FY26. This annuity does not stop in a drought year, which cushions the down-cycle for every major.

02

From 4 lakh to 11.6 lakh: size, growth and the export line

Sixteen years of roughly 7% compound growth, delivered in lurches of plus 20% and minus 10%.

Measured in units, the Indian domestic tractor market roughly tripled between FY10 and FY26, from about 3.9 lakh to 11.6 lakh, a compound annual growth rate of about 7%. Measured from the pre-COVID peak of FY19 (7.87 lakh), it grew about 5.6% a year. Neither number describes a single year, because the industry moves in lurches.

The long history below uses two kinds of evidence. FY19 to FY26 are now well anchored: FY23 is the exact sum of the Tractor and Mechanization Association's (TMA) monthly dispatches, 9,45,311; FY19–FY24 are chained from M&M's own industry-growth series; FY24 matches the Ministry of Agriculture's 8.75 lakh; and FY25–FY26 are TMA figures quoted by Escorts Kubota. FY10–FY18 are approximate, from widely published TMA totals that we did not re-fetch, and FY18 has a known conflict (about 7.1 lakh in TMA-based reports versus 7.29 lakh implied by M&M's series).

Three patterns stand out. First, down-years are frequent but shallow: FY13, FY15, FY16, FY20, FY22 and FY24 all fell, but the worst single-year fall in the series is about −13% (FY15). Second, the industry has never grown more than four years in a row in this series, and the longest run (FY17 to FY19) ended with a slowdown in FY20. Third, down-years cluster after weak monsoons or income squeezes: FY15 and FY16 followed the back-to-back droughts of 2014 and 2015; FY24 followed the uneven 2023 monsoon. Section 3 shows the rainfall side of that link.

The pattern also shows why FY26's +23.5% is unusual. The only comparable jump is FY21 (+26.9%), when rural India outperformed during COVID and farm incomes held up while cities shut. Both of those years were followed by declines: FY22 fell 6.4% after FY21. FY27 will not necessarily fall, but the history says the year after a 20%+ jump is rarely another good one.

Exports: a peak in FY22–FY23, not FY26

India also exports about a tenth of its tractor output. Here the commonly repeated story is wrong. Trade-press reports in 2026 said exports had crossed one lakh "for the first time" in FY26. TMA's monthly data say otherwise: exports were about 1.27–1.28 lakh in FY22 and 1,24,542 in FY23 (calendar 2022 was about 1.32 lakh), then fell to about 0.95–1.0 lakh in FY24 and 0.99 lakh in FY25 before recovering to 1.06 lakh in FY26. Export intensity, exports as a share of total dispatches, has fallen from about 12.6% in calendar 2022 to about 8.3% in FY26. Q1 FY27 exports were 29,778, up 17.7%, but the growth was in tractors above 40 HP; the sub-40 HP compact segment, where Escorts and VST are strongest, fell about 8%.

In plain terms

India sells about four times as many tractors as the US and Canada together, but each one is cheap. A ₹73,000 crore industry is far smaller by revenue than India's car or two-wheeler industries, so it is not a giant by value. What makes it attractive is that it is concentrated, profitable and almost entirely domestic in ownership of demand.

One caution on global comparisons: India is often described as having 40–45% of the world's tractor units. No fetched source supports a precise world total, because Deere and Kubota do not publish global unit sales, so this report uses the safer statement that India is the world's largest tractor market by units. The scale contrast is still stark: about 11.6 lakh domestic tractors in India against roughly 3 lakh in the US and Canada and 4–5 lakh in China (indicative), with an average Indian tractor priced at a small fraction of a North American row-crop machine.

03

Rain, MSP and farm cash: why tractors follow the monsoon

The first two forces: the weather sets farm income, and the government's support prices set what that income buys.

About half of India's net sown area depends on rain rather than irrigation, and the south-west monsoon (June to September) brings roughly three-quarters of the country's annual rainfall. A good monsoon lifts the kharif (summer) crop of rice, cotton, soybean and pulses directly, and fills the reservoirs and soil moisture that the rabi (winter) crop of wheat, mustard and gram depends on. Farm cash flow follows the harvests, and tractor demand follows farm cash flow, typically with a lag of one to two seasons.

The mechanism is not instant, which is what makes the indicator useful. A farmer who has had a poor kharif harvest still has the rabi crop to come; a farmer whose kharif was good may wait for the rabi to be sown before committing. Tractor wholesale volumes therefore tend to respond most to the rainfall of the previous season. The droughts of 2014 (about 88% of LPA) and 2015 (about 86%) produced the FY15 and FY16 down-years; the uneven 2023 monsoon (about 94%) produced FY24's 7.4% fall; the good monsoons of 2019, 2020, 2024 and 2025 preceded FY21 and FY26, the two biggest jumps in the series.

The 2026 monsoon is the main reason FY27 turns. June rainfall was only about 65% of normal; kharif sowing was 21% behind in late June before recovering to about 2% behind by early August. The season ended with a 12.6% deficit, about 87% of LPA, with the South Peninsula about 23% short and East and North-East India the driest since 1901; 17 of 36 meteorological subdivisions were deficient. IMD had forecast 90% ± 4%, so the outturn was at the bottom of its range. By October the Centre had formed a team to assess drought damage in 265 talukas of Maharashtra. Wholesale volumes in H1 FY27 were still supported by the good 2025-26 rabi harvest and higher grain procurement (M&M noted rabi wheat procurement up 19%), but the rabi crop now being sown in late 2026 depends on depleted soil moisture and reservoirs that M&M said were 7% below normal in late July.

In plain terms

The rain that fell in 2025 paid for the tractors sold in FY26. The rain that did not fall in 2026 will show up in the tractors that are not sold in late FY27 and FY28. That lag is why September 2026's fall was not a surprise to anyone watching the weather, and why the next two rabi seasons matter more than the next two months.

Terms of trade: MSP against costs

Rain sets the quantity a farmer harvests; prices set what it is worth. For wheat and paddy in the North, the government's minimum support price (MSP) and its procurement operations act as a floor. Punjab, Haryana and Madhya Pradesh, where procurement is deep, are also the states where Escorts Kubota, Sonalika, John Deere and CNH are strongest. When MSP rises faster than diesel, fertiliser, labour and tractor prices, the farmer's terms of trade improve and he can afford a bigger tractor; when it rises more slowly, affordability worsens.

The 2027-28 rabi decision on 30 September 2026 was a clear negative signal. Wheat MSP was raised by only ₹25, or 0.97%, to ₹2,610 a quintal, the smallest rupee increase in about seventeen years, which reports attributed to overflowing government wheat stocks. The previous year's increase had been ₹160 (6.6%). Mustard got a 6.7% increase, but mustard is a much smaller cash pool. On the kharif side, the 2026-27 common paddy MSP was raised 3.0% to ₹2,441 a quintal in May 2026. With steel and rubber pushing tractor prices up (Escorts raised prices 1–1.5% in April 2026 and planned another increase in Q2 FY27; M&M took about ₹15,000 a tractor in August) and diesel prices raised four times in May 2026 after the West Asia crisis, a 1% wheat MSP increase means farmers' purchasing power in Escorts' northern heartland weakens in FY27–FY28.

Two structural drivers sit underneath the cycle and explain why each trough has been higher than the last. The first is labour scarcity: rural wages have risen faster than crop prices for two decades, and migration to cities and construction work has made hired farm labour expensive and unreliable at sowing and harvest. A tractor and a rotavator replace dozens of labour-days per acre. M&M's management cited accelerating farm-labour shortages as a reason for confidence even in July 2026. The second is replacement: with 60–65% of demand coming from farmers replacing old tractors, and the large FY18–FY21 cohort now reaching replacement age, a floor exists under volumes even in poor years. Neither driver prevents a down-year; both limit how far it can go.

Shrinking landholdings cut both ways. The average Indian holding fell from about 1.08 hectares in the 2015-16 agricultural census to a reported 0.74 hectares in 2021-22, which makes outright ownership of a ₹7 lakh tractor harder to justify for the average farmer. The answer has been custom hiring, where one tractor serves many plots, and haulage income, which makes a tractor pay for itself outside the cropping season. Both increase hours of use and therefore shorten the replacement cycle. The policy side of affordability, subsidies and tax, is covered in Section 9; the credit side in Section 4.

In plain terms

Good rain gives the farmer a bigger crop; MSP decides what that crop is worth; labour scarcity decides whether he needs a machine at all. In FY27 the first two are turning against the tractor buyer while the third keeps pushing him towards mechanisation. That is why most forecasters expect a flat year rather than a collapse.

The financed tractor: why lenders matter as much as rain

The fourth force. Almost every Indian tractor is bought with a loan, so credit decides who can buy and when.

About 95% of tractors in India are bought on credit. The typical loan covers 70–80% of the on-road price, runs four to six years, and is repaid in half-yearly instalments timed to the kharif and rabi harvests rather than in monthly EMIs. That structure ties tractor demand to two things beyond the farmer's own income: the lender's appetite for rural risk, and the cost of the lender's own money.

The lenders fall into four groups. Non-bank finance companies (NBFCs) are the most important at the margin: Mahindra & Mahindra Financial Services (Mahindra Finance), Shriram Finance, Cholamandalam, L&T Finance and Tata Capital lend to farmers that banks find hard to underwrite, at higher rates. Banks (SBI, Punjab National Bank, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank) and regional rural banks lend to better-documented borrowers, often under priority-sector rules that count tractor loans as agricultural credit. And captive finance arms of the OEMs (Escorts Kubota Finance, Sonalika's Autotrac Finance, John Deere Financial India) exist to protect their parents' share when third-party lenders pull back. The NBFC side is covered in our NBFC report; Mahindra Finance has its own primer.

Where credit stands in October 2026. The cycle has been supportive. The RBI cut its policy repo rate by 125 basis points over 2025, which helped FY26. On 7 October 2026 it raised the repo rate to 5.50%, against a background of higher fuel and freight costs after the West Asia crisis. Asset quality has held: Mahindra Finance, the largest tractor lender and the leader in used-tractor finance, reported gross stage-3 (non-performing) assets of 3.45% in June 2026 with loan assets up 13%. No industry-wide tractor delinquency series is published; an older India Ratings study of securitised tractor pools found 90-day-plus delinquency of about 6.45% for a 2015 vintage, a drought-era cohort, which gives a sense of how far stress can rise in a bad cycle.

The risk for FY27–FY28 is the sequence. A weak monsoon cuts kharif income; some instalments due after the kharif harvest slip; NBFCs raise down-payment requirements and slow approvals for new borrowers in the worst-hit districts; marginal buyers drop out. That chain played out in FY15–FY16. It moves more slowly than the rainfall data but faster than the official crop estimates, so lenders' quarterly commentary on collection efficiency in rural books is one of the better early-warning signals.

In plain terms

A farmer does not decide to buy a tractor alone; his lender decides with him. A one-point rise in interest rates adds about ₹1,800 to each half-yearly payment, which few farmers notice. A lender who wants a bigger down payment, or who stops lending in a drought district, can take a buyer out of the market overnight.

Captive finance: OEMs build their own lenders

Because third-party lenders retreat exactly when an OEM most needs sales, the majors have been building captive finance companies. Escorts Kubota Finance (EKFL) financed 10–12% of Escorts' sales in its active states in Q1 FY27, and more than 15% in July 2026; the company plans to cover 40–50% of its dealers by the end of FY27 and go pan-India in FY28. Escorts put ₹70 Cr of fresh equity into EKFL in FY26, taking its investment to ₹130 Cr. Sonalika has Autotrac Finance. John Deere Financial India, the oldest captive, earned about ₹706 Cr of revenue and ₹152 Cr of profit in FY25 (from a press report). M&M owns 52% of Mahindra Finance, which lends across tractors, cars and small businesses.

A captive arm gives an OEM three things: it keeps lending in districts where outside lenders hesitate, it can run interest-rate subvention schemes at festival time without paying a third party, and it collects data on buyers' repayment that helps dealers target the next sale. The cost is balance-sheet risk: a captive's bad loans belong to the group. For Escorts, with ₹9.8k Cr of liquid assets, the capital is not the constraint; underwriting skill in a weak monsoon year is.

First, tractor demand is more sensitive to NBFC asset quality and approval rates than to the repo rate; watch rural collection efficiency at Mahindra Finance, Shriram and Chola. The million-tractor year and the hangover Deep dive

How four tailwinds stacked into FY26's record, how much of it was borrowed from FY27, and what September 2026 is telling us.

FY26 was the best year the Indian tractor industry has had. Domestic wholesale reached 11,60,231 tractors, up 23.5%; November 2025 alone saw about 1.26 lakh tractors dispatched to dealers, the strongest month on record, and October 2025's total dispatches including exports were 1,73,635. Every major OEM sold more tractors than ever. This section takes the year apart, because the parts that came from genuine demand and the parts that came from timing behave very differently in FY27.

Four tailwinds, stacked

1. Rain. The 2025 monsoon came in at 108% of LPA, with North-West India at 127%. That followed a 108% monsoon in 2024. Two good years in a row filled reservoirs, lifted the 2025-26 rabi crop and raised procurement payouts in the North. This is the genuine, income-driven part of the boom, and it supported demand right through Q1 FY27.

2. The GST cut. At its 56th meeting on 3 September 2025 the GST Council cut the rate on tractors and specified farm machinery from 12% to 5%, and on key parts (tyres, tubes, hydraulic pumps and agricultural diesel engines) from 18% to 5%, effective 22 September 2025. Ex-showroom prices fell by ₹40,000–60,000 a tractor, about 6% (ICRA). Farmers who had been planning to buy in late 2025 or early 2026 had every reason to buy immediately, and farmers who had not planned to buy at all found a bigger tractor suddenly affordable. Demand jumped in October and November 2025.

3. State subsidies. Gujarat's annual tractor subsidy was paid out on a large scale in FY26 and Q1 FY27; Maharashtra supported purchases in H2 FY26; Bihar offered 40–80% subsidies across 91 categories of machinery. Escorts' management described the Gujarat effect as a matter of "timing and quantum" rather than a new scheme: the subsidy comes every year, but its size and month vary.

4. Pre-buying ahead of TREM V. Until mid-2026 the rulebook said that all tractors would have to meet the TREM V emission standard (equivalent to the EU's Stage V, with diesel particulate filters) from April 2026, which ICRA estimated would add 15–20% to the price of a sub-50 HP tractor. Escorts' FY26 annual report refers to buying ahead of this change. Dealers stocked up and some farmers bought early. As Section 9 explains, the final rules (notified in mid-2026) moved most of the cliff to 2032, so this pre-buying was partly unnecessary in hindsight, but it happened.

The RBI's 125 basis points of rate cuts in 2025 sat under all four. The combination explains why CRISIL's April 2025 forecast of 9.75 lakh was beaten by about 1.85 lakh tractors.

The bear and bull readings of the hangover. The bear reading is that FY26 pulled forward perhaps half a year of demand (GST plus TREM pre-buying), that the 2026 monsoon will cut rabi incomes, that a 1% wheat MSP increase leaves northern farmers worse off in real terms, and that FY28 could be a genuine down-year if the 2027 monsoon also disappoints. The bull reading is that dealer stocks are normal, that replacement demand from the FY18–FY21 cohort and labour scarcity provide a floor, that the GST cut permanently lowered the price of a tractor (and let farmers trade up to more horsepower), and that the TREM cliff has moved to 2032. Both readings agree that FY27 growth will come in the low single digits; they disagree about FY28.

In plain terms

FY26 was a real boom with a tax-cut sugar rush on top. The real part (two good monsoons, rising mechanisation) does not reverse in a year. The sugar rush does, and it shows up as ugly month-on-month comparisons until October 2026's festive season and the following months are measured against the post-cut base. The test of the industry is FY28, after a weak monsoon has worked through farm incomes.

For Escorts Kubota specifically, the hangover year started better than the boom year ended. It lost 0.9 points of share in FY26 because the boom was led by the South and West, where it is under-represented (Section 10). In H1 FY27 its northern markets held up better than the rest of the country and its share recovered. The Q2 FY27 results on 2 November 2026 will show what that share recovery cost in margin: Q2 tractor volumes were 34,017 (+0.4%), construction-equipment volumes 1,764 (+53.9%), and management had warned of a further 1.5–2% of cost inflation on top of Q1's 5%.

Where ₹100 goes: steel, rubber and a lot of spare capacity

The supply side: what a tractor is made of, what it costs to make, and how much room the factories have.

A tractor maker is, before anything else, a buyer of metal. Materials and components absorb about 70% of every rupee of revenue, so the prices of steel, pig iron and scrap (for castings), aluminium, copper and natural rubber set the direction of margins, and the lag between a cost increase and a price increase sets the size of each quarter's squeeze.

Escorts Kubota's FY26 standalone accounts give the cleanest public breakdown. On ₹11,472.8 Cr of continuing revenue, materials were 69.7%, employee costs 7.0%, and other expenses (freight, advertising, warranty, power, royalty, dealer support) about 10.1%, leaving an EBITDA margin of 13.2%. Depreciation took about 2.2%, interest 0.2%, and other income (largely treasury yield) added back 4.9%, for continuing profit after tax of about 12% of revenue. A leading OEM in a good year, M&M's standalone farm business or Sonalika, spends a similar share on materials but has lower overheads per tractor, which is how it reaches 17–20% operating margins.

The industry carries meaningful spare capacity even at the FY26 peak, roughly 20–35% across the majors (indicative). Castings and forgings makers, gear and axle suppliers, and farm-tyre makers such as Balkrishna Industries and CEAT sell to tractor OEMs on contracts that pass through metal and rubber prices with a lag. When a tractor OEM says commodity inflation will "reverse from Q4", it is betting that suppliers' cost indices fall with steel and rubber prices.

One giant, five challengers: who owns India's tractor market

The fifth force. Shares have barely moved in fifteen years, the leader earns a fifth of revenue as profit, and the challengers are all cash-rich.

India's tractor market has six serious players and a tail. M&M has held roughly 40–45% of it for more than fifteen years, the single most stable fact in the industry. Below it, TAFE, Sonalika, Escorts Kubota, John Deere and CNH share most of the rest, and the remaining small makers together hold about 1.3%. Three of the six are controlled by or partly owned by foreign tractor companies; none of the six is a recent entrant.

The FADA retail series counts tractor registrations on the government's VAHAN database, which misses farm tractors in states where registration is not enforced and splits some brands (for instance, it reports TAFE's Massey Ferguson and Eicher separately). In plain terms

Six companies sell almost every tractor in India, the leader has not changed in a generation, and every challenger is rich enough to survive a bad year. That is a recipe for a stable, profitable industry rather than a cut-throat one. The risk to that comfort is not a new entrant; it is a long drought or a rule change that forces everyone to raise prices at once.

GST, TREM and the price of a tractor Deep dive

The third force. Two policy decisions moved tractor prices more than a year of commodity inflation, and the next one is scheduled for 2032.

Tractor demand is unusually sensitive to policy because the product's price is set by three things the government controls: the tax rate, the emission standard the engine must meet and the subsidies states pay. Between September 2025 and July 2026 the first two changed decisively, and the effects run through FY26, FY27, FY28 and FY32.

GST 2.0: from 12% to 5%

Before September 2025, tractors carried 12% GST while many of their parts (tyres, hydraulic pumps, diesel engines) carried 18%, an inverted structure that left OEMs claiming refunds. The 56th GST Council meeting on 3 September 2025 cut tractors and specified agricultural machinery to 5%, and the key parts from 18% to 5%, effective 22 September 2025. ICRA estimated a price reduction of about 6%; dealers reported savings of ₹40,000–60,000 a tractor. The change was permanent, so it lowered the price of a tractor for every future buyer. But because it was announced three weeks ahead and took effect in the festive season, it also concentrated purchases: October and November 2025 were the biggest months in the industry's history, and September 2026 had to be compared with them.

TREM: the emission ladder, now fixed in law

Tractors have their own emission standards, Tractor Emission Regulations (TREM), which run behind the "Bharat Stage" standards for cars and trucks. TREM I came in October 1999, TREM II in June 2003, TREM III in October 2005 and TREM IIIA in 2010–11. TREM IV applied from January 2023 to tractors above 50 HP only; it required common-rail injection and exhaust after-treatment and raised prices by 10–12%. Its effect was immediate: buyers shifted down into the 41–50 HP band, and the above-50 HP segment shrank to about 2% of domestic volume.

TREM V, equivalent to EU Stage V and requiring a diesel particulate filter, was originally scheduled for April 2024 and then April 2026 for all tractors. ICRA estimated it would add 15–20% to the price of a sub-50 HP tractor, split roughly 65% exhaust after-treatment, 18% advanced combustion, 12% electronics and sensors and 5% tooling, R&D and certification. The Tractor and Mechanization Association lobbied in August 2025 for a further delay for 25–50 HP tractors. A draft re-phasing was published in early March 2026, and the final rules were notified as the Central Motor Vehicles (Eleventh Amendment) Rules, 2026, reported on 2 July 2026. VST's chief executive confirmed the dates "as per the current notification" on 13 August 2026.

Who wins and loses from the emission ladder

Emission rules favour OEMs that make their own modern engines or can buy them from a technology parent. M&M (with Mitsubishi Mahindra in Japan), Escorts (with Kubota, which already supplies engines for 10,000–12,000 Escorts tractors a year), Sonalika (with Yanmar), John Deere and CNH all have access to Stage V engine technology through global platforms. The small Indian makers holding about 1.3% of the market are most exposed: certification, after-treatment sourcing and warranty risk are fixed costs that weigh more on a few thousand tractors a year. The 2028 IIIAA step and 2032 Stage V step are therefore likely to push the "others" share lower still.

For demand, the schedule implies two pre-buy windows: a modest one before April 2028 (IIIAA is an interim step with a smaller cost) and a large one before April 2032. Each will pull volume forward and leave a hangover, as FY26 did. Investors should expect FY28 and FY32 to look stronger, and FY29 and FY33 weaker, than the underlying farm economy alone would suggest. Construction equipment has its own, separate emission ladder (CEV/BS-V): Escorts passed on price increases of 6–7% for BS-III to BS-V models and 3.5–4.5% for BS-IV to BS-V from January 2025, and introduced BS-V backhoe loaders and compactors from September 2026.

In plain terms

Every time the government raises the emission bar, two things happen: farmers rush to buy the old, cheaper model before the deadline, and small tractor makers struggle to afford the new engines. The rush shows up as a boom followed by a slump; the struggle shows up as the big companies' share creeping higher.

Subsidies and the rest of the policy toolkit

Tractors themselves are subsidised mainly by states, through annual schemes whose size and timing vary (Gujarat's was large in FY26 and Q1 FY27). The central Sub-Mission on Agricultural Mechanisation (SMAM) subsidises machinery and custom-hiring centres at 40% for general farmers and 50% for small and marginal, women and SC/ST farmers. The Agriculture Infrastructure Fund had sanctioned ₹80,224 Cr for more than 1.5 lakh projects by January 2026, and the agriculture ministry's FY27 budget is ₹1,40,561 Cr. Other rules matter at the margin: priority-sector lending classifies tractor loans as farm credit; safety norms under the motor vehicle rules govern rollover protection, brakes and lights; minimum-wage increases (Haryana's raised Escorts' costs about one percentage point in Q1 FY27); and the new labour codes (Escorts booked a one-off ₹39.3 Cr in FY26). The construction-equipment production-linked incentive scheme announced in the 2026 Budget targets imported equipment such as tunnel-boring machines and does not cover Escorts' products.

The policy calendar for investors is therefore: GST is settled at 5%; TREM has known dates in 2028 and 2032; MSP decisions arrive in May–June (kharif) and September–October (rabi); state subsidy budgets arrive with state budgets in February–March; and RBI policy meets every two months. Fertiliser and pesticide policy, which also shape farm cash flows, are covered in our fertilisers and agrochemicals reports.

South, West and 45 HP: the two mix shifts

Forces seven and eight. Demand is moving to regions and horsepower bands where the challengers are thinnest.

Two quieter shifts explain most of the share changes of the past three years. Geographically, growth has been led by the West (Maharashtra, Gujarat) and the South, while the North (Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan) and Central India (Madhya Pradesh), the traditional tractor heartland, have grown more slowly. And in horsepower, the market has consolidated into the 41–50 HP band.

The regional shift

CRISIL estimates that North and West India together account for about 75% of tractor volume, South and East about 25%. Within that, FY26 growth was strongest in the West: trade-press analysis put the West at about 40% of volume with growth of up to 28%, against moderating growth in the North and Central regions, which still account for more than a third. In Q3 FY26 Escorts reported that "other regions" (outside its North/Central strongholds) grew 34.2% while its core markets lagged. In Q1 FY27 the pattern reversed for a quarter: the industry grew 19%, the South fastest at about 33%, while Escorts' North/Central markets grew 22% against 15.4% for the rest of the country.

The horsepower shift

The second shift is into the 41–50 HP band. ICRA's exhibit for FY24 shows the domestic market at roughly 10% below 30 HP, 27% at 31–40 HP, 60% at 41–50 HP and only 2% above 50 HP. In FY19 the 41–50 HP band was below half of the market. By Q1 FY27 M&M estimated that 40–50 HP tractors were about 69–70% of industry volume. Three things drove it: TREM IV made above-50 HP tractors 10–12% dearer from January 2023, pushing buyers down; heavier implements (seed drills, balers, larger rotavators) need more power, pushing buyers up from 31–40 HP; and the GST cut let farmers buy more horsepower for the same rupees. 4WD tractors are also growing faster than the market, though no clean industry series exists.

Eight decades of tractors: joint ventures, droughts and Japanese capital

The cycle and the history: what has happened before, and what each episode teaches about the next.

India's tractor industry was built by Indian business houses partnering with foreign tractor makers, consolidated around a few strong brands in the 1990s and 2000s, and has been reshaped in the 2020s by Japanese capital. Through all of it, the cycle has been set by the monsoon and by policy, and the industry has never lost money as a whole for long.

In plain terms

Tractor down-cycles are real but short and shallow compared with trucks or cement. The industry's worst modern years, FY15 and FY16, came after two droughts in a row. Several lessons from the history apply directly to FY27–FY28. One weak monsoon is survivable; two are not. FY24's −7.4% after one uneven monsoon contrasts with FY15–FY16's −22% after two droughts. Escorts' FY23 margin trough came during a volume increase. Ownership changes take years to show up in operations. Reading a tractor maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

The numbers that tell you whether a tractor company is winning, how each is built, what good looks like, and where the definitions trip people up.

A tractor maker's results look simple: units sold, revenue, margin. In practice almost every headline number has a hidden basis (wholesale or retail, domestic or total, standalone or consolidated, with or without treasury income), and the companies define their own KPIs differently. This section builds the profit equation, explains each native KPI with a worked example in rupees, compares the companies where data exist, and ends with a ten-minute checklist for any quarterly result.

The equation: volume, share, price, cost

Every tractor maker's profit can be written as one chain. Revenue is the industry's domestic volume multiplied by the company's market share, plus its exports, multiplied by the net price it realises per tractor, plus non-tractor revenue (spares, implements, engines, and for Escorts construction equipment). Operating profit is that revenue multiplied by the contribution margin left after variable costs, less fixed costs. Net profit adds treasury income and deducts tax. Management controls some terms and not others, and the split matters for judging results.

KPI 1: Domestic volume and market share

What it is. The number of tractors a company dispatched to Indian dealers in the period, and that number as a share of the industry total. Formula: company domestic wholesale ÷ TMA industry domestic wholesale. Worked example: Escorts' 35,457 domestic tractors in Q1 FY27 divided by the industry's 3.39 lakh gives 10.5%; a year earlier 28,848 over 2.86 lakh gave 10.1%, so share rose 0.4 points even though Q1 is seasonally weak for a North-heavy maker.

What drives it. Regional mix (a North-heavy maker gains when the North outgrows), product gaps (a missing 4WD model in the core band costs share), dealer depth and finance availability, and timing (festive dispatches and subsidy payouts move volume between months and quarters). Benchmarks: a gain of 0.5 points a year or more is good, ±0.3 points average, a loss of 0.5 points or more weak. KPI 2: Realisation per tractor

What it is. The average revenue the OEM earns per tractor, net of GST, dealer margin and discounts. Formula: tractor revenue ÷ tractors sold. Most companies do not disclose tractor-only revenue, so analysts estimate it from segment revenue. Worked example: Escorts' FY26 agri revenue of ₹9,779.6 Cr divided by 1,33,670 tractors is ₹7.32 lakh per tractor including non-tractor revenue; taking the reported 81% tractor share gives about ₹5.93 lakh per tractor (indicative). What drives it. List-price increases (usually once or twice a year), HP and 4WD mix (a 4WD 45 HP tractor sells for more than a 2WD 35 HP one), export mix (Escorts' export revenue of ₹635.6 Cr over 6,676 tractors is about ₹9.5 lakh per unit, though it includes component exports), discounts and scheme support, and tax (the GST cut lowered the shop price but not the OEM's net realisation, because GST is excluded from revenue). Benchmarks: realisation growth of 4–6% a year (price plus mix) is good, 2–4% average, below 2% weak. In a cost-inflation year like FY27 the test is whether realisation keeps up with material cost.

When the material-cost share of revenue jumps three or four points in a quarter, as it did at Escorts in Q1 FY27, the company has been caught between steel and the farmer. The question for the next results is how much of the gap the October and January price lists have closed.

KPI 6: Dealer inventory days

What it is. The stock of new tractors sitting at dealers, expressed as days of retail sales. It is the bridge between wholesale (what the factory books as sales) and retail (what farmers buy). Formula: dealer stock ÷ average daily retail sales; analysts approximate the change in channel stock by cumulative wholesale minus cumulative retail over a period. Worked example: if a company's dealers hold 9,000 tractors and sell 300 a day to farmers, inventory is 30 days. If wholesale runs at 330 a day and retail at 300, the channel adds about 900 tractors a month, three days of stock, which is fine before a festive season and a warning after it.

What drives it. OEMs push stock before price increases, emission deadlines and festivals; dealers destock after weak harvests. Benchmarks: below 30 days is good, 30–45 average, above 45 weak (outside the pre-festive build). Escorts reported about 30 days in August 2026, M&M 30–40 days in July with Swaraj below norm. M&M argued that because OEMs are now more disciplined about channel stock, down-cycles need less destocking, one reason industry volatility has fallen.

KPI 7: HP mix and 4WD share

What it is. The share of a company's volume in each horsepower band (or simply below and above 40 HP), and the share of 4WD models. Worked example: Escorts' split moved from 36:64 (below:above 40 HP) in Q1 FY26 to 32:68 in Q1 FY27. If a tractor above 40 HP realises about ₹1 lakh more than one below 40 HP (indicative), a four-point shift in mix on 36,862 tractors adds about 1,475 higher-value units and roughly ₹15 Cr of quarterly revenue, a little under 1% of tractor revenue.

What drives it. Emission rules (TREM IV pushed buyers below 50 HP), implements (heavier implements need more power), affordability (the GST cut let farmers trade up), region (paddy areas in the South want specific 4WD tractors; the North wants 45–50 HP 2WD haulage machines) and product availability. Benchmarks: a mix at or above the industry's 40–50 HP share (about 70% in Q1 FY27) is good; within five points average; more than five points below weak. Escorts is roughly average. The definition trap: companies use different band boundaries (below/above 40 HP, 41–50 HP, below/above 50 HP), and emission rules use kilowatt bands (19–37 kW, 37–56 kW) that do not map exactly onto marketing HP.

KPI 8: Regional mix

What it is. The share of a company's volume, or its market share, by region. Companies disclose this only in calls and occasional slides. Worked example: if Escorts holds about 14% in the North and Central markets and about 6% in the South (indicative regional split; only the South figure is disclosed), and the South grows 33% while the North grows 15%, its national share falls even if it holds share in each region. That arithmetic explains most of FY26's 0.9-point fall.

What drives it. Rainfall and crop patterns by state, state subsidy timing (Gujarat in FY26), dealer networks, and products suited to local crops (paddy, sugarcane, orchards). Benchmarks: a national share within two points of the company's best regional share is good (balanced); a gap of five points or more is weak (concentrated). M&M and TAFE are balanced; Escorts and Sonalika are North-heavy. The definition trap: "North" sometimes includes Central India and sometimes Rajasthan; "West" sometimes includes Madhya Pradesh. Check the definition before comparing growth rates across companies.

In plain terms

A company can hold its ground in every state and still lose national market share, simply because the states where it is weak grew faster. Before concluding that a tractor maker is losing to rivals, check where the industry's growth came from that quarter.

Definition traps, collected

  • Wholesale vs retail. TMA wholesale (factory to dealer) and FADA retail (registrations) give different shares and growth rates; FY26 was 11.60 lakh wholesale and 10.50 lakh retail. Name the basis every time.
  • Domestic vs total. Market shares are domestic; "tractors sold" may include exports (M&M's 5,26,403 vs 5,05,930 domestic; industry 12.66 lakh total vs 11.60 lakh domestic).
  • Brand perimeter. Escorts' numbers include Kubota-brand tractors from FY24 (restated); TAFE's include Eicher; M&M's include Swaraj. Retail data may split brands that wholesale data combine.
  • Continuing vs reported profit. Escorts' FY26 reported consolidated PAT of ₹2,394 Cr includes the ₹1,004 Cr railway-sale gain; continuing PAT was about ₹1,380 Cr. Screener's FY24 and earlier include the railway division; FY25 onwards do not.
  • Standalone vs consolidated. Escorts' company KPIs are standalone (FY26 revenue ₹11,472.8 Cr), data services are consolidated (₹11,540 Cr). M&M's farm margin is 19.8% standalone and 13.0% consolidated.
  • EBITDA vs EBIT vs PBIT. M&M reports PBIT (after depreciation), rating agencies report operating margin before depreciation, Escorts reports both. Do not compare across without adjusting.
  • Realisation vs price. Realisation is net of GST and dealer margin; ex-showroom price is not. Agri segment revenue per tractor includes about 19% of non-tractor revenue.
  • Month vs quarter vs year. One month's share (Escorts 12.46% in September 2026) can be two points away from the annual figure; festive timing moves volume between quarters.
  • Calendar vs fiscal years. TMA and trade press often quote calendar-year totals; Deere's fiscal year ends in October; Kubota and CNH report on the calendar year.
  • Cash definitions. Screener's "investments" for Escorts (₹8,281 Cr) omit about ₹1,560 Cr of cash and bank deposits; the company's liquid assets are about ₹9,840 Cr (about ₹9,630 Cr excluding escrow).
  • HP and kW bands. Marketing bands (below/above 40 HP; 41–50 HP) do not match the emission rules' kilowatt bands (19–37 kW, 37–56 kW).
  • Old export claims. FY26 exports of 1.06 lakh were not a record; FY22 and FY23 were higher.
In plain terms

Most bad tractor analysis comes from mixing two numbers that look alike but measure different things: wholesale with retail, domestic with total, reported profit with continuing profit, or standalone with consolidated. Before comparing any two figures, check they share all four bases.

How to read a tractor maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

The volumes are public on the first day of each month, so a tractor maker's quarterly result is mostly a test of price, cost and mix. Pull the numbers in this order.

  1. Volumes first (1 minute). Domestic and export units for the quarter, from the monthly releases already out. Compare domestic growth with the TMA industry's growth: is the company gaining or losing share? For Escorts in Q2 FY27, tractors were 34,017 (+0.4%), domestic 32,628 (+0.9%) against an industry roughly 3% down (estimate), so share rose.
  2. Revenue per tractor (1 minute). Agri or farm segment revenue ÷ units. Is it up by at least the price increases announced (1–1.5% in April plus any Q2 increase) and the mix shift? A fall signals discounting or adverse mix.
  3. Material cost ratio (1 minute). Compare with the same quarter last year and the previous quarter. Escorts' 72.8% in Q1 FY27 is the bar; above it in Q2 means the cost squeeze worsened. A red flag is a rising ratio with flat realisation.
  4. Segment EBIT margin and EBIT per tractor (1 minute). Escorts' agri EBIT was 10.8% in Q1 FY27 and 12.6% in FY26; M&M's core tractor PBIT 19.2%. Divide segment EBIT by units to get profit per tractor and compare with ₹81,000 (Escorts Q1) or ₹1.21 lakh (M&M core).
  5. Construction equipment (1 minute, Escorts only). Volumes were up 53.9% in Q2 FY27; check whether the CE margin recovered from 5.4% in Q1 and whether pick-and-carry share held near 40%.
  6. Other income and one-offs (1 minute). Separate treasury income from operating profit, and strip exceptional items (land sales, labour-code charges, impairments). If other income is above about 40% of PBT, as in Q1 FY27, the operating story is weaker than the headline profit.
  7. Exports and the Kubota channel (1 minute). Export units (Q2 FY27: 1,389, −10.3%), the share via Kubota, and any comment on the US (Section 301 tariff, 10%) and Europe.
  8. Guidance (1 minute). Industry growth for the year (Escorts said "mid-single-digit" in August; ICRA 1–4%, CRISIL 0–2%), price increases planned, commodity outlook, and capex. A guidance cut after a weak monsoon is normal; a cut in own-share ambition is not.
  9. Channel and credit (1 minute). Dealer inventory days (red flag above about 45 outside the festive build) and comments on financier behaviour, captive finance penetration and collection efficiency in rural books.
  10. Balance sheet and capital allocation (1 minute). Cash and investments, capex spent against the year's plan (₹850–900 Cr for Escorts in FY27), any dividend or buyback signal, and related-party flows (purchases from Kubota, royalty).

The red flags, in one list: market share falling in a quarter when the company's strong regions outgrew the country; realisation falling while list prices rose; material cost above 72% for two quarters running; dealer inventory above 45 days after the festive season; other income rising as a share of profit while operating margin falls; and any change to the industry guidance that is larger than the change in the monsoon data would justify.

In plain terms

By the time a tractor company reports, you already know how many tractors it sold. The result tells you what it got for each one and what each one cost. If the price per tractor rose faster than the cost per tractor and the company held its share, it was a good quarter, whatever the headline profit says.

Japan Inc. in Indian tractors: Kubota, Yanmar and Mitsubishi Deep dive

The sixth force. Three of India's top five tractor makers have Japanese owners or partners. What they paid, what flows back to Japan, and what India gets.

Japan's three big tractor makers have each tied themselves to an Indian partner. Kubota controls Escorts Kubota. Yanmar owns 30.36% of Sonalika. Mitsubishi Mahindra Agricultural Machinery in Japan is owned by M&M, the reverse direction. The logic is the same in each case: India makes 30–60 HP tractors at a cost no Japanese, European or American plant can match, and Japanese technology (engines, compact-tractor design, production systems) is worth more in India's emission-tightening, quality-upgrading market than it is at home, where farm populations are shrinking.

Kubota and Escorts: the biggest bet

Kubota's move into Escorts happened in three steps. In March 2020 it bought 9.09% of Escorts (12,257,688 shares), while Escorts took 40% of Kubota Agricultural Machinery India, the joint venture selling Kubota tractors in India. In November 2021 Kubota announced a larger move: a preferential allotment of 9,363,726 shares at ₹2,000 a share (₹1,872.7 Cr, completed 18 February 2022) and an open offer for 37,491,556 more shares at the same price (about ₹7,498 Cr), settled on 11 April 2022. After the cancellation of shares held in an Escorts trust, Kubota held 59,112,970 shares, 44.80%, and became a joint promoter; the company was renamed Escorts Kubota. Finally, the merger of Kubota's two Indian companies into Escorts Kubota (board approval September 2022, appointed date 1 April 2023, NCLT order 21 August 2024) brought an allotment of 13,79,493 shares on 6 September 2024 and took Kubota to 54.07%.

Who sells the world's tractors from India: the export race Deep dive

The global lens. India makes tractors more cheaply than anyone, yet exports have fallen since FY23. Who exports, to where, against what tariffs, and how Indian makers compare with the world's leaders.

India exported about 1.06 lakh tractors in FY26, roughly 8% of its total dispatches. The story usually told is of an emerging global sourcing hub. The data tell a more mixed story: exports peaked at about 1.25–1.28 lakh in FY22–FY23, fell about 20% over the next two years, and FY26 was a partial recovery. Export intensity, which was about 12.6% of total dispatches in calendar 2022, was about 8.3% in FY26.

Who exports

Sonalika is India's largest tractor exporter by a distance, with about 30% of the country's exports (about 30,000 tractors in FY25, down 11%) sold in more than 130 countries under the Sonalika, Solis and Yanmar brands. M&M exported roughly 20,500 tractors from India in FY26 (the difference between its 5,26,403 total and 5,05,930 domestic), about a fifth of India's exports, mainly to the US through Mahindra USA and to South Asia and Africa. TAFE exported 12,584 in FY26, about 5.9% of its own volume, down from about 13% before FY16, mainly to Turkey, the US and Mexico. Escorts Kubota exported 6,676 (6.3% of India's total), about 60% through Kubota's network, plus about ₹160–170 Cr of components. John Deere and CNH export utility tractors and components from their Indian plants to their parents' networks; their volumes are not separately disclosed but make up most of the remainder.

Where they go, and what they face. Indian tractors sell in four kinds of market. Europe buys compact tractors below 30 HP, mostly through established channels (Kubota, Solis, VST's Fieldtrac); the EU charges no import duty on tractors, so the India–EU trade agreement changes little. VST's management estimated that Indian tractors sell about 15,000 a year in Europe's sub-30 HP segment, with the largest Indian player at about 9,000. The US buys compact and utility tractors; it is the largest destination for Indian HS 8701 exports by value (from a trade-data snippet, indicative). Africa, South-East Asia and Latin America buy utility tractors, where Indian makers compete with Chinese ones on price. And Turkey and Mexico are served by TAFE's local operations.

The product mix of exports is shifting. In Q1 FY27 industry exports rose 17.7% to 29,778, but all of the growth was above 40 HP (up about 30%); exports below 40 HP fell about 8%. That hurt Escorts and VST, whose export ranges are concentrated in compact tractors, and helped Sonalika and the MNC hubs, whose exports are utility tractors. Escorts' H1 FY27 exports fell 14.8% to 2,794, and management guided FY27 exports "more or less flattish".

The US tariff stack

US trade policy changed four times in 2026, and the current position is still under legal challenge. The normal US most-favoured-nation duty on agricultural tractors is zero. In early February 2026 the US cut its tariff on Indian goods from 50% to 18%. On 20 February 2026 the US Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, the legal basis of that stack (Deere booked a US$272 million refund). A Section 122 surcharge of 10% applied from 24 February for 150 days and lapsed on 24 July 2026. Since late July a Section 301 duty of 10% has applied to Indian goods, under a USTR notice of 23 July 2026 linked to a forced-labour investigation; it is being challenged in court. Section 232 tariffs of 25–50% on steel, aluminium and copper derivatives continue to apply to covered goods, and whether tractors count as derivatives for their metal content is not clear from available sources. VST's chief executive also cited a proposed "100% tariff" in a bill before the US Congress as a risk to its US plans.

Technology and disruption: engines first, electrons later

What could change how tractors are made, sold and used, and how soon.

Indian tractors are mechanically conservative for good reasons: farmers value simplicity, repairability in a village workshop, fuel economy and resale value over features. The technologies that will matter most over the next decade are the ones regulation forces (cleaner engines) and the ones that change how tractors are used (custom hiring and telematics), rather than the ones that change what powers them.

  • Emission technology (the certain one). Stage V for the 25–75 HP core arrives in April 2032, with an interim step in 2028. It requires common-rail injection, diesel particulate filters, catalytic after-treatment and engine electronics, adding 15–30% to the price depending on whose estimate is used (ICRA 15–20%; VST's chief executive 25–30% at today's costs, single source). OEMs with access to proven Stage V engines (through Kubota, Yanmar, Mitsubishi, Deere, CNH platforms) gain; small makers and village mechanics face a steep learning curve.
  • Electric and CNG tractors (the distant one). Escorts showed a hybrid tractor concept in 2019 and Sonalika launched an electric tractor in 2020; the government has encouraged CNG tractors. Adoption was negligible in FY26. Battery cost, long working days at high load, and the lack of rural charging make battery tractors uneconomic outside orchards, greenhouses and short-haul work near towns. VST said its US electric-tractor plans had "taken a back seat". The real effect within a decade is likely to be in compact and orchard tractors and in export markets with emission zones.
  • Precision agriculture and telematics. GPS guidance, auto-steer and telematics are standard on premium export models but rare in India's ₹6–8 lakh segment. Telematics is spreading faster than guidance because it serves the lender (tracking the asset) and the dealer (service reminders) as much as the farmer. OEMs that use the data to target replacement sales and finance offers will gain.
  • Custom hiring and tractor-as-a-service. India had about 75,900 custom-hiring centres in 2025 (about 11,100 in Punjab), plus aggregator apps that let small farmers book tractor hours. This could reduce the number of farmers who need to own a tractor, but it raises hours of use per tractor and shortens the replacement cycle. The net effect so far has been more commercial use and faster replacement, not lower sales.
  • Small-farm substitutes. Power tillers, power weeders, mini-tractors and custom-hired combine harvesters serve the shrinking average holding (about 0.74 ha, as reported). They favour VST and Kubota-type compact products and could limit growth at the bottom of the tractor range.
  • 4WD and implements. The shift to 4WD and heavier implements (seed drills, balers, larger rotavators) is the most immediate technology trend, pushing demand towards 45–50 HP and rewarding OEMs with complete 4WD ranges.
  • China. Chinese tractor makers compete with Indian ones in Africa, South-East Asia and Europe's compact segment on price. They are not a threat in India's domestic market, where Indian costs are lower, but they cap export prices.

Bull vs bear: a stalled year or the start of a downturn?

The two readings of the industry, and three illustrative FY27 outcomes for Escorts Kubota.

Almost everyone agrees that FY27 will be a low-growth year after FY26's record. The disagreement is about FY28 and beyond: whether a weak monsoon and a high base produce one flat year, as in FY24, or a two-year downturn, as in FY15–FY16; and whether Escorts Kubota can use its parent and its cash to close the gap with the leaders.

The bull case

  • Structural demand: labour scarcity, 60–65% replacement demand and the FY18–FY21 cohort reaching replacement age put a floor under volumes; the industry has compounded about 7% a year since FY10.
  • Permanent price cut: GST at 5% lowered the price of every future tractor by about 6% and let farmers trade up to 45–50 HP.
  • No emission cliff until 2032: the notified TREM schedule removes the 15–20% price shock from the next five years for the core market.
  • Disciplined, cash-rich oligopoly: dealer stocks at 30–40 days, margins in a 13–20% band through cycles, and every challenger net cash.
  • Escorts' momentum: share up to about 11.3% in H1 FY27 and 12.46% in September 2026; agri ROCE near 48%; Kubota naming India a key growth driver; a greenfield aimed at global supply.

The bear case

  • Weather and income: the 2026 monsoon at about 87% of LPA, East and South worst, drought assessments in Maharashtra; a second weak monsoon in 2027 would make FY28 a down-year.
  • Terms of trade: wheat MSP up only 1%, diesel prices raised four times in May 2026, tractor prices rising with steel and rubber.
  • Base effects: FY26 included GST-cut and TREM pre-buying; September 2026 fell 18% and H2 FY27 faces the toughest comparisons.
  • Costs and credit: commodity inflation of 4–5% per tractor not yet recovered in prices; repo raised to 5.50%; NBFCs likely to tighten in drought districts.
  • Escorts' gaps: about two-thirds of M&M's profit per tractor, weak in the South and West, exports about 5% of volume and down 15% in H1 FY27, treasury income 42% of Q1 profit, and a valuation of about 20× even after removing cash.
