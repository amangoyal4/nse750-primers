00

The one-page view: three yards, one customer, an empty in-tray

India's listed shipbuilders had their best years ever on orders signed a decade ago. The next decade's orders have not been signed yet, and the cash those orders would bring is running down.

India has three listed shipbuilders, and all three are owned by the government. Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders (MDL) in Mumbai builds the Navy's submarines, destroyers and frigates. Garden Reach Shipbuilders & Engineers (GRSE) in Kolkata builds frigates, corvettes, patrol and survey ships. Cochin Shipyard (CSL) in Kochi built India's first aircraft carrier and now also builds European cargo ships and tugs and runs India's largest ship-repair business. In FY26 they earned a combined ₹25,030 crore of revenue and ₹4,043 crore of reported profit after tax. Revenue had nearly doubled in three years, from ₹12,753 crore in FY23.

That boom came from a burst of deliveries. The P-15B destroyers, the P-17A frigates, the Kalvari-class submarines and the anti-submarine shallow-water craft were all contracted between 2005 and 2019. They reached the expensive middle of their construction, when engines, weapons and sensors are installed, in FY24–FY26. That stage of a warship's life produces the most revenue. GRSE delivered eight warships in FY26. MDL handed over the last of its four P-17A frigates on 30 April 2026. Cochin delivered more anti-submarine craft and a string of European vessels.

The problem is what came in behind those deliveries. Over FY26, MDL's order book fell from ₹32,260 crore to ₹20,535 crore and then to ₹18,218 crore by June 2026. GRSE's fell from ₹22,681 crore to ₹15,324 crore and then ₹13,596 crore, the first time in ten years it has been below ₹15,000 crore. Derive the inflow (closing book minus opening book plus revenue billed) and the two Navy yards together booked only about ₹760 crore of new work in FY26 against roughly ₹19,800 crore of revenue: a book-to-bill ratio of about 0.04×. Cochin was the exception. It took about ₹6,170 crore of commercial orders in the year to June 2026, a book-to-bill of about 1.2×, but none of it was naval.

The Navy has not stopped wanting ships. About 53% of its fleet is more than 15 years old, and 62 more ships had been approved in principle by 2025. The orders are stuck between approval and signature. The six P-75I submarines (about ₹70,000 crore or more, MDL with Germany's TKMS) cleared price negotiation in January 2026 and the Finance Ministry at the end of May 2026, but still await the Cabinet Committee on Security. GRSE has been the lowest bidder for five next-generation corvettes since May 2025, now worth ₹33,000 crore after negotiation, and that contract is also unsigned. The P-17B frigate tender was only issued on 16 September 2026, with an award expected in late 2027. A second aircraft carrier has not even reached the first approval stage.

Two other forces sit on top of the order gap. The first is the float. A shipyard is paid ahead of its costs: on Indian defence contracts it receives about 25% of the contract value before it cuts steel (10% at signing, 5% at design submission and 10% when major materials are ordered). The yards park this money in bank deposits and earn interest on it. At March 2026 the three held ₹20,579 crore of customer advances, recorded as "contract liabilities", down ₹6,866 crore in one year. All three reported negative operating cash flow in FY26. Interest on deposits was about a quarter of profit. When orders stop, the float shrinks, and the interest shrinks with it.

The second is margin quality. MDL's 17.4% FY26 operating margin is struck after a ₹634 crore addition to its provision for losses on two fixed-price contracts, and after ₹476 crore of one-off credits from liquidated-damage reversals, a contract amendment and an inventory-provision release. GRSE's 11.4% was lifted by Q4 write-backs of provisions from earlier years; its normal run-rate is nearer 8–9%. Cochin's margin fell because its high-margin carrier refits ended: its repair segment's margin fell from 39% to 26%. Management guidance across the group points to lower margins on new work: 12–15% at MDL, about 8–10% at GRSE and about 14% blended at Cochin.

The market has noticed. The three stocks trade 33–41% below their 52-week highs. On 22 October 2026 their combined market value was ₹1,30,616 crore, about 25% lower than in July. That is still about 30× trailing profit and 7.2× book value, more than twice the trailing multiples of HD Hyundai Heavy Industries (about 15×) and Huntington Ingalls in the US (about 16×). The government owns 81.22% of MDL, 74.50% of GRSE and 63.33% of Cochin, and it has sold stock in MDL (April 2025) and Cochin (October 2024 and July 2026). MDL still needs to sell about 6 percentage points more to reach the 25% minimum public float.

In plain terms

These yards are very good at finishing ships and very dependent on one buyer deciding when to order the next ones. For three years they finished a lot of ships, so revenue and profit rose. They also lived partly on the interest from money the Navy paid them in advance. Few new contracts were signed in FY26, so the advance money is being used up and the queue of work is getting shorter. The next big orders exist on paper, but they have not been signed, and every promised date in the past year has slipped.

01

How a shipyard earns: from an approval in Delhi to a ship at sea

A naval yard is a systems integrator paid in advance, on a percentage-of-completion schedule, for work that takes five to ten years. Each of those features shapes the numbers.

A warship begins as a capability requirement in the Navy's long-term plan, not as an order to a shipyard. The Defence Acquisition Council (DAC), chaired by the Defence Minister, grants an Acceptance of Necessity (AoN), which sets a cost ceiling. The Navy then issues a request for proposal (RFP) to qualified yards. It evaluates the technical bids and opens the commercial bids to find the lowest (L1) bidder. A contract negotiation committee (CNC) settles the price, and the competent financial authority approves it. For large programmes that authority is the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS). Only then is a contract signed and the first advance paid. Each step can take a year or more, and the largest programmes have spent many years in this chain.

The yard's revenue follows Step 4. Its cash follows Steps 2 and 5. Its order book depends entirely on Step 1, which it does not control.

Revenue is recognised over time under Ind AS 115, using the input (cost-to-cost) method. If a yard has spent 40% of the total cost it expects a contract to need, it books 40% of the contract value as revenue. Three consequences follow. Revenue tracks spending, not deliveries. A new contract adds little revenue at first: GRSE's chairman describes year one as "design only, 5–10% of project cost". About 20–25% of the value is booked by the halfway point of the build, and the curve then rises steeply as equipment is installed. And because margin is estimated over the whole contract, any change in the estimated cost to complete changes reported profit immediately. If the yard expects a fixed-price contract to lose money, it must book the whole expected loss at once as an "onerous contract" provision.

Where is the value captured? Less of it stays in the yard than the size of the contracts suggests. In MDL's FY26 accounts, materials consumed were 43.8% of revenue and base-and-depot spares supplied with the ships another 12.6%. Much of the cost of a warship is equipment the yard installs but does not make: gas turbines and diesel engines, gearboxes, missiles, radars, sonars and combat-management systems. Many of these are "owner-furnished" or nominated by the Navy. GRSE's chairman says the engines "are all nominated by the customers". The yard's own value-add is design integration, hull fabrication, outfitting, testing and project management. Indigenous content has risen from 42% on the P-15 destroyers to 75% on P-17A, but much of that gain goes to Indian suppliers such as BEL and BrahMos and to the MSMEs that supply 33.5% of MDL's procurement, rather than to the yard itself.

Indian yards report higher margins than Western naval primes, partly because of lower labour costs and legacy nominated pricing, and partly because of provision releases at project completion (Section 07).

Two other money streams sit alongside contract margin. The first is repair and refit. It needs docks and skilled labour but little bought-out equipment, so its margin is roughly double that of newbuilding: Cochin guides 22–24% for repair against 10–12% for shipbuilding. The second is float income. Because the customer pays ahead of cost, the yards hold large bank deposits that they did not raise from shareholders. In FY26 interest on bank deposits was ₹1,007 crore at MDL, ₹246 crore at GRSE and ₹164 crore at Cochin. Section 06 explains how that float is built and why it is now shrinking.

In plain terms

A naval shipyard is paid to assemble a very expensive kit of parts that other companies make. It earns a modest margin on the assembly. It earns extra from repairing ships, and extra again from the interest on money the Navy pays it before the work is done. Its revenue depends on how fast it builds. Its future depends on how fast the government signs.

Commercial ships work differently, and that difference explains much of Cochin's recent cash flow. A European owner ordering a cargo ship pays only 20–30% upfront and the balance at delivery. Cochin's finance director calls the cash flow "tail-ended". The yard has to fund most of the build itself, so working capital turns positive and borrowing rises. Commercial contracts are also fixed-price, often in euros or dollars, and frequently for new designs: hybrid, battery-electric, methanol or LNG-fuelled ships the yard has not built before. That is where cost overruns tend to appear. MDL's onerous-contract losses in FY26 came from a Coast Guard contract and from six hybrid multipurpose vessels for a Danish owner, not from submarines.

02

Size and the 2023–26 boom: revenue doubled, profit tripled

The three yards grew revenue at 24% a year from FY22 to FY26 and profit at 31%. Almost all of that growth came from executing contracts signed between 2005 and 2019.

Combined revenue rose from ₹10,678 crore in FY22 to ₹25,030 crore in FY26, a compound annual growth rate of 23.7%. Combined profit after tax rose from ₹1,365 crore to ₹4,043 crore, or 31.2% a year. The growth was not evenly spread. GRSE grew fastest, from ₹1,754 crore to ₹7,002 crore (41% a year), as its P-17A frigates, anti-submarine craft and patrol vessels all reached the build-heavy stage at once. MDL, the largest, compounded at 23% to ₹13,006 crore. Cochin grew only 12% a year: its FY22 base included the final aircraft-carrier billing, FY23 was a gap year after the carrier's delivery, and its FY26 growth was held back by a fall in repair revenue.

How big is this compared with the market it serves? The Navy's total budget doubled from ₹49,623 crore in FY21 to ₹1,03,548 crore in FY26, according to a December 2025 government briefing. The "Naval Fleet" line of the capital budget, which pays for new ships, was about ₹25,000 crore a year. At that time about 51 large ships worth about ₹90,000 crore were under construction across all Indian yards, public and private. A new vessel was being inducted roughly every 40 days. The three listed yards hold most, but not all, of that naval work. The unlisted Goa Shipyard (47.21% owned by MDL) and Hindustan Shipyard build patrol ships and fleet-support ships, and L&T's private yard at Kattupalli builds smaller vessels.

Measured against world shipbuilding, India barely registers. According to UNCTAD's Review of Maritime Transport 2025, built on Clarksons data, India produced 0.06% of the world's gross tonnage delivered in 2024. China produced 54.57%, South Korea 28.02% and Japan 12.56%. HD Hyundai Heavy Industries alone earned about ₩17.58 trillion in 2025, roughly ₹1.12 lakh crore, about 4.5 times the combined revenue of India's three listed yards. India's yards are naval specialists in a protected home market, not competitors in the global merchant-ship business. The ₹69,725 crore maritime package of September 2025 is an attempt to change that (Section 12).

In plain terms

Between FY22 and FY26 the business more than doubled, mainly because ships ordered years earlier reached the stage where the most money is spent on them. Profit tripled because finishing a ship is more profitable than starting one and because the yards earned interest on their cash. On a world scale, Indian shipbuilding is still tiny.

One more point on growth quality. Combined operating cash flow over FY22–FY26 was only ₹3,195 crore, against cumulative reported profit of ₹13,905 crore, a cash conversion of about 23%. In FY26 alone operating cash flow was −₹4,178 crore. This is not because the profits are fictitious. The yards collected the advances for these programmes before FY22 and have been spending them since. Profit was earned in FY22–FY26, but much of the cash had arrived earlier. Unless new advances arrive, cash flow will keep lagging profit.

03

The empty in-tray: why the Navy yards stopped replacing their books

In FY26 the two MoD yards billed about ₹19,800 crore and booked under ₹1,000 crore of new orders. The demand is real; the signatures are not.

The single most important number in Indian shipbuilding today is not revenue or margin. It is order inflow, and none of the three yards reports it directly. It has to be derived: inflow equals closing order book minus opening order book plus revenue booked in the period. The calculation is approximate. It uses standalone revenue where the order book is standalone, and the book can also move for de-scoping or price revisions. But the direction is unmistakable.

Why did the orders stop? Not because the Navy's need fell. A 2025 parliamentary analysis by PRS counted 137 ships and submarines in the fleet, 53% of them more than 15 years old (the target is 20% by 2040). It also counted 58 ships under construction and approvals for 62 more. China is commissioning more than 15 vessels a year. Pakistan is adding Chinese and Turkish ships. Operation Sindoor in May 2025 was cited in budget commentary as a reason for the 2026-27 step-up in defence capital spending. Demand is not the constraint. Four other things are.

  • The approval chain is long and serial. The big programmes need CCS approval, and the CCS acts on a few large files at a time. P-75I's negotiation concluded on 16 January 2026. The Finance Ministry cleared it at the end of May. It was reported to be heading to the CCS in August, with a contract "targeted by September 2026". That target has passed.
  • Budget headroom. The Naval Fleet capital line has been roughly flat at about ₹25,000 crore a year for three years. Its share of total defence capital fell from 15% in FY25 to 11% in FY26 and FY27, while aircraft took the increment. A single ₹70,000 crore programme equals almost three years of that line, so new signatures have to wait for room created by older contracts finishing.
  • Competition instead of nomination. Since about 2020 most surface-ship contracts have been tendered competitively among the "Category-A" yards (MDL, GRSE, Goa Shipyard and Cochin). Competitive tenders take longer to run, and losing bidders can contest the results.
  • Programme redesign. P-17B was re-tendered as an open competition; the three extra Scorpenes were reported in limbo while P-75I was pending; and the second carrier has not reached AoN.

The order gap matters for revenue, cash and profit, each with a different lag. Revenue is protected for a while, because the yards are still executing the remaining work on P-17A, the anti-submarine craft and the patrol vessels. MDL guided FY27 revenue to grow only about 5%. GRSE's chairman says FY27 will be "very healthy" but declined to say FY28 would not dip. The remaining P-17A and ASW-SWC work, 46% of GRSE's June 2026 book, completes in FY27. Cash is hit at once: no new contracts means no new advances, while the old advances are consumed as the ships are finished. Profit is hit last and hardest. When the big contracts are signed, the first two years will bring design-phase revenue of only 3–10% of value at new-project margins of 12–15% at MDL and 8–10% at GRSE, just as completion-phase margins and treasury income fade.

In plain terms

A shipyard's order book is its queue of future work. In FY26 the two Navy yards worked through their queues quickly and almost nothing new joined them. Revenue holds up for now because the remaining work is still being done. But a yard that books ₹5 of new orders for every ₹100 it bills will eventually run out of work unless the big contracts are signed. The FY28 revenue gap is the risk to watch even if they are signed soon.

How long is the runway? At trailing revenue, MDL's June 2026 book covers about 1.4 years and GRSE's about 1.8 years. Cochin's covers about 4.4 years, the only comfortable cover in the group, but much of it is low-margin commercial and export work. For comparison, Fincantieri's backlog is 6.9 times its annual revenue. MDL's book also overstates its pure shipbuilding work. Of the ₹18,218 crore, ₹7,587 crore is residual work and spares on the four P-17A frigates already delivered, ₹2,774 crore is ONGC offshore platform work, and only ₹2,170 crore is submarines (mainly the AIP plug and a mid-life refit). Its submarine line, the scarcest capability in the country, has no new boat to build until P-75I or the add-on Scorpenes are signed.

04

The pipeline that won't sign: programme by programme Deep dive

About ₹2.5 lakh crore of naval programmes are approved, tendered or negotiated. As of 22 October 2026, none of the big ones has a signed contract, and every dated promise of the past year has slipped.

Every bull case for Indian shipbuilding rests on the same list of programmes. They are large, mostly approved in principle, and genuinely needed. The question is timing, and on timing the record of the past twelve months is poor. This section goes through each programme: its value, which yard is in line for it, where it stands in the approval chain on 22 October 2026, and when it could first produce revenue. One rule applies throughout. The values quoted are AoN ceilings or programme values, not a single yard's share. P-17B's ₹70,000 crore covers seven frigates that will almost certainly be split between two yards, as P-17A was split four to MDL and three to GRSE. The MCMV mine-countermeasure programme is expected to be split eight ships to one yard and four to another. Adding a whole AoN to one yard's order book is the most common mistake in commentary on this sector.

P-75I is the single largest swing factor in the sub-sector. It is a strategic-partnership programme for six conventional submarines with air-independent propulsion, which lets a diesel-electric boat stay submerged for weeks rather than days. MDL bid with Germany's thyssenkrupp Marine Systems (TKMS); the rival L&T–Navantia bid was eliminated in January 2025. Negotiations began on 10 September 2025 and concluded on 16 January 2026, and the Finance Ministry cleared the deal at the end of May 2026. Reports in August said it would go to the CCS that month, with a contract targeted by September. On 22 October it was still unsigned and absent from MDL's order book. Our MDL primer's statement that an India–Germany inter-governmental agreement was signed in January 2026 could not be verified; the January milestone that is verified is the end of contract negotiations. If MDL booked the full ₹70,000 crore, its order book would roughly quintuple. In practice part of the value may sit with TKMS for imported systems, so treat any yard-level figure as indicative until the contract is published.

The add-on Scorpenes are the quickest revenue MDL could get. Three more Kalvari-class boats would reuse the existing production line, workforce and supplier base. MDL's chairman said in October 2025 that commercial negotiations had been completed "a few months ago" and the file was at the sanction stage, and that revenue would start "immediately" on signing. Press reports since then describe the order as in limbo while P-75I is pending. The two are linked: the Navy may not want to fund both at once. Until one is signed, MDL's submarine line has only the AIP retrofit (₹1,542 crore balance) and a mid-life refit (₹492 crore) to work on.

NGC is GRSE's whole near-term story. GRSE was declared the lowest bidder in May 2025 for five of eight next-generation corvettes; the second-lowest yard builds the other three. After price negotiation GRSE's share is about ₹33,000 crore (FY26 annual report; CARE, June 2026). That figure replaces the ₹25,000 crore pre-negotiation estimate still quoted in some places. Signing would multiply GRSE's ₹13,596 crore book about 3.4 times. The guided signing date has moved four times: "this financial year" (FY26) in August 2025, "by 31 March" 2026, "this quarter" (Q2 FY27) in August 2026, and still nothing on 22 October. Even after signing, the revenue arrives slowly. GRSE expects design-phase revenue of 3–5% of value a year in FY28–FY29, first delivery about five years after signing, and the last ships around 2034-35.

P-17B is the follow-on to the Nilgiri-class frigates: seven larger, more heavily armed ships. Its RFP was issued on 16 September 2026 to the Category-A yards (MDL, GRSE, Goa Shipyard and Cochin, according to a defence-trade report; whether L&T received it could not be confirmed). Technical bids are due about 120 days later, so around January 2027, and the award is expected in late 2027. MDL had guided in October 2025 that the RFP would come "any time next month or definitely in this calendar year", so the RFP itself was about nine months late. The AoN is ₹70,000 crore for seven ships, about ₹10,000 crore a ship. Cochin values its own interest at about ₹49,000 crore and says "other yards are more competitive". The likely outcome is an L1/L2 split between MDL and GRSE, but that is a reading of the past, not a forecast.

LPD and MCMV are the amphibious and mine-warfare programmes. They are large, need big docks, and are expected at RFP stage in 2026–27. Cochin calls the LPD "most suitable for us" because of its 310-metre dry dock. MDL has a teaming agreement with Swan Defence & Heavy Industries, owner of the 662-metre Pipavav dock, to bid under a public-private partnership. GRSE's chairman puts the MCMV AoN at about ₹32,000 crore; other sources say ₹36,000 crore. NGSV is the most concrete near-term win outside the Navy yards: Cochin is L1 for five survey vessels worth about ₹5,000 crore, which would lift its book to about ₹27,000 crore. IAC-2, the second carrier, is often described as Cochin's prize. It is not in Cochin's order book, and on 10 September 2026 Cochin's CFO said it "has not reached the AoN stage". It should be treated as a possibility for the next decade, not a pipeline item.

What if they all sign? The arithmetic below is a scenario, not a forecast. If P-75I were booked in full by MDL, NGC by GRSE and NGSV by Cochin, the combined order book would rise from ₹54,037 crore to about ₹1.62 lakh crore, about 6.3 years of trailing revenue. The advances on those contracts, at about 25% of value before steel is cut, would add roughly ₹27,000 crore to the float over two to three years. That is more than the ₹20,579 crore the yards held in March 2026. This is why signature, not revenue, is the variable that moves these stocks.

In plain terms

The orders the yards are waiting for are large enough to change everything: one submarine deal is worth more than five years of MDL's revenue. But they have been "about to be signed" for more than a year, and even after signing, a new warship brings in little revenue for its first two years. Investors who buy on the pipeline are buying a timing bet on a government committee, and that bet has lost for four straight quarters.

The lesson from the last programmes is that delays are normal, not exceptional. The six P-75 Scorpenes were contracted in 2005 and due by December 2017; the last was commissioned in January 2025, about seven years late. NGC has been at L1 for 17 months. GRSE's chairman spoke in August of about ₹80,000 crore of live RFPs and another ₹60,000 crore expected that year, contested by "five to six shipyards". His wider figure of about ₹1.5 lakh crore of defence orders "in the near future" excludes NGC. These are real numbers, but they are opportunities to bid, not orders. On present evidence, the sensible base case is that P-75I and NGC are signed sometime in FY27–FY28, P-17B is awarded in late 2027 or 2028, and LPD and MCMV follow. In every case the revenue curve lags the signature by two years.

In plain terms

AoN means "we need this". RFP means "please bid". L1 means "you are cheapest". CNC means "we agree the price". CCS means "the Cabinet says yes". Only after the last step is there a contract, an advance and an order-book entry. Most of the pipeline is still at the first two steps.

Who orders ships, and with what money

One customer, the Ministry of Defence, pays for 76–86% of the naval yards' work. The rest comes from the Coast Guard, ONGC, other ministries, European ship-owners and repair clients.

Customer concentration in this sub-sector is extreme, even at the yard that looks diversified. In FY26 MDL booked ₹11,077 crore of revenue from the Ministry of Defence, 86% of its standalone revenue, and was owed ₹2,865 crore by the ministry at year-end, up from ₹1,478 crore. GRSE's March 2026 book was about 76% Navy and Coast Guard, down from 88% a year earlier, as research ships for other ministries and German cargo ships made up a larger share. Cochin's annual report shows two customers accounting for 59.9% of shipbuilding revenue and 60.9% of repair revenue, almost certainly the Navy and the Coast Guard. When a single buyer delays, all three yards feel it at once.

The Navy is the anchor. Its total budget roughly doubled between FY21 and FY26, but much of the increase went to the revenue budget (pay, fuel, maintenance and refits) and, within capital, to aircraft. Revenue spending matters for repair: the Navy's revenue budget rose from ₹45,299 crore to ₹57,060 crore and then ₹61,216 crore over FY25–FY27, and refits and medium refits are paid from it. That supports MDL's repair line (₹1,032 crore standalone in FY26, nearly double FY25), GRSE's new repair business in leased Kolkata docks (₹273 crore), and Cochin's naval repairs. New ships come from the capital budget, and specifically from the Naval Fleet line, which has stayed near ₹25,000 crore a year. Most of each year's capital pays for contracts signed in earlier years. A new ₹70,000 crore submarine deal adds roughly ₹7,000–10,000 crore a year of new cash need, so signatures cluster when older programmes finish and free up room.

The Coast Guard is the second naval-type customer. It operates 151 ships, all built in India, and orders steadily: offshore patrol vessels, fast patrol vessels and interceptors. MDL holds a 21-vessel Coast Guard contract (₹2,849 crore, ₹2,649 crore still to execute). That is one of the two fixed-price contracts behind MDL's onerous-contract provisions. Six next-generation patrol vessels for the Coast Guard, worth about ₹2,000–2,500 crore, are out to tender. Other ministries and agencies buy research and survey ships: GRSE is the only Indian yard currently building research vessels (ocean, coastal and acoustic research ships, ₹1,448 crore in its book), and Cochin is L1 for five survey vessels. ONGC buys offshore platforms (MDL, ₹2,774 crore balance) and platform supply vessels (GRSE letters of intent for four in Q1 FY27).

Commercial and export customers are the growth area, and almost all of that is at Cochin. Its export book was ₹6,038 crore at June 2026, 29% of the consolidated book. Clients include Wilson ASA of Norway (cargo ships built at Cochin's Udupi subsidiary), Samskip (two zero-emission container ships), a German client for multipurpose vessels, a Cypriot owner for commissioning service vessels, Svitzer (electric tugs) and CMA CGM (six 1,700-TEU LNG-fuelled feeders). GRSE builds multipurpose cargo ships for a German client, a dredger for Bangladesh and ferries for West Bengal. MDL builds six hybrid multipurpose vessels for Navi Merchants of Denmark. At home, the government has announced a demand-aggregation programme under which public-sector cargo owners (ONGC, GAIL, BPCL, IOCL and others) will order ships through SCI and joint ventures from Indian yards. Cochin cites 432 vessels over ten years; the government has spoken of securing 1,000 commercial vessels over a decade. Live tenders include MR tankers, very large gas carriers, Aframax tankers and container ships. See the Shipping report for how this looks from the owners' side.

In plain terms

The Navy decides how busy MDL and GRSE are, and it also decides much of Cochin's naval and repair work. European ship-owners are a second, smaller customer base that pays less upfront and expects tighter prices. Indian state-owned cargo companies have promised to order hundreds of ships from Indian yards, but those orders are only now being tendered.

Ship repair is a demand stream with its own drivers: the size of the active fleet, Indian owners' habit of repairing abroad in Dubai, Singapore and Colombo, and foreign navies. Cochin repaired 163 ships in FY26 for ₹1,656 crore and claims about 45% of India's repair market. Its International Ship Repair Facility (ISRF) at Kochi can handle 82 medium ships a year. A ₹1,570 crore repair cluster at Vadinar in Gujarat, sized for about 40 large ships a year, was approved by the Cabinet in May 2026. US Navy master ship-repair agreements with Indian yards (L&T's Kattupalli in 2023, followed by Cochin and MDL) add a small but strategic stream. MDL's 51% stake in Colombo Dockyard, bought for ₹236.95 crore, gives it a foreign repair-and-build base with about ₹1,000 crore of revenue at about a 15% margin, according to MDL's chairman.

Exports of warships remain marginal. Defence exports need government-to-government framing. MDL has a tripartite memorandum with the Brazilian Navy on Scorpene sustainment and has done submarine inspection work for Malaysia. GRSE refits coast-guard vessels for Mauritius and the Seychelles (CGS Barracuda, PS Zoroaster). None of this moves the order book in the way a single Indian Navy contract does.

The macro backdrop in October 2026 affects demand at the margin. The West Asia crisis since February 2026 has raised freight rates and insurance costs, which helps ship-owners' finances and could speed up commercial ordering. The rupee at about ₹94.9 to the dollar makes imported engines, weapons and electronics more expensive. Fixed-price contracts without price-variation clauses absorb that cost, while it makes Indian yards slightly cheaper for European buyers. The Reserve Bank's repo-rate increase to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 lifts the yield on the yards' bank deposits, partly offsetting the shrinking balances. None of these changes the core fact that MDL's and GRSE's next five years depend on Delhi signing contracts.

The float: how a shipyard is financed by its customer Deep dive

Customer advances of ₹20,579 crore fund the build and earn interest in the bank. In FY26 the float shrank by ₹6,866 crore, operating cash flow turned negative at all three yards, and the "net-cash fortress" started to look different.

The most unusual feature of a naval shipyard's balance sheet is that the customer finances the factory. On an Indian defence contract, rating-agency documents for Cochin describe the payment schedule as 10% of the contract value on signing, 5% on submission of the design and 10% when major material is ordered. That is about 25% before any steel is cut. After that come stage payments linked to milestones such as keel laying, launch, trials and delivery. Because the yard's costs in the first two years are mostly design and long-lead orders, it holds a large surplus of cash for years. It parks the surplus in bank term deposits, which appear in the accounts as "bank balances other than cash and cash equivalents", and earns interest on it. The cash received ahead of revenue appears on the liability side as contract liabilities under Ind AS 115: money the yard owes the customer in work.

Now the profit effect. Interest on bank deposits in FY26 was ₹1,007 crore at MDL (₹1,008 crore in FY25), ₹246 crore at GRSE and ₹164 crore at Cochin, a total of ₹1,417 crore. That is about 27% of combined profit before tax. After 25% tax it is about ₹1,063 crore, or about 26% of combined PAT. The share is highest at MDL (about 29%), then GRSE (about 25%) and Cochin (about 17%). The implied yield on average balances is about 6.9% at MDL, 6.7% at GRSE and 5.9% at Cochin. Interest income held up in FY26 even as balances fell, probably partly because deposits locked in earlier at higher rates take time to mature. The sensitivity is simple: every ₹5,000 crore of deposits MDL spends without replacement removes about ₹350 crore of pre-tax income, about 11% of its FY26 PBT.

There is a second side to the float that the "net-cash fortress" description leaves out: guarantees and obligations. Advances on defence contracts are backed by guarantees from the yard. MDL had indemnity bonds of ₹24,732 crore outstanding to customers at March 2026 (₹34,875 crore a year earlier). These track the unfinished MoD work and are, in effect, the Navy's claim on ships still to be delivered. MDL also had ₹1,221 crore of bank guarantees, and in August 2026 doubled its bank limits to ₹5,000 crore, mostly non-fund-based. That fits a yard preparing to post advance and performance guarantees on new contracts. GRSE's bank guarantees were ₹6,321 crore, 2.4 times its net worth. In plain terms

A shipyard's bank balance is mostly the customer's money, held until the ships are finished. Calling it a "cash pile" is half right: the yard earns the interest, but it owes the work. When new orders stop arriving, the balance falls, and so does the interest that had been padding profit. That is what happened in FY26.

Contract economics and margin quality: what the reported margin hides

A shipyard's margin is an estimate of a seven-year contract's profit, revised every quarter. Provisions, liquidated damages and write-backs move it by several points, and FY26 had plenty of all three.

There are three kinds of naval contract, and the kind matters more than the yard. Nominated, negotiated contracts were the norm for defence PSUs until about 2020: the ministry chose the yard and the price was negotiated on cost plus a margin. The aircraft carrier, the older destroyer and submarine programmes and much of P-17A were of this kind. They give a protected but capped margin. Competitive fixed-price contracts are now the norm for surface ships. The lowest bidder wins and bears any overrun, with only limited price-variation clauses. Commercial export contracts are also fixed-price, usually in foreign currency, with small advances. Cochin's finance director put the shift plainly: the higher margins of the last three years came "mainly because we had some nominated orders like the aircraft carrier", and "we cannot expect such margins from the commercial orders or the defence order".

Under percentage-of-completion accounting, margin is booked in proportion to cost incurred, using the yard's estimate of total cost. Three things can then move reported profit sharply.

  • Onerous-contract provisions. If the estimated cost of a fixed-price contract rises above its price, the whole expected loss must be booked at once, not spread over the remaining build.
  • Liquidated damages (LDs). Late delivery triggers penalties, typically capped at 5–10% of contract value. Yards book an LD provision against revenue when a delay is expected and reverse it if the delay turns out shorter or the ministry waives it.
  • Warranty and cost provisions. These are held through the build and released at completion. MDL's finance director says margins reach 25–30% at project completion for this reason, against 12–15% on new projects.

MDL's FY26 margin is the clearest example. The reported operating margin was 17.4% (₹2,268 crore on ₹13,006 crore of revenue). Behind it, the annual report shows the provision for expected losses on onerous contracts rising from ₹521.38 crore to ₹1,059.59 crore: ₹634.25 crore of additions, less ₹96.04 crore used. The total expected loss on "certain ongoing projects" was ₹1,179.72 crore, of which about ₹120 crore had already been absorbed through revenue. MDL's chairman identified the two loss-making contracts in October 2025: the Coast Guard vessel contract and the six hybrid multipurpose vessels for Navi Merchants of Denmark, both fixed-price. FY26 also contained credits: ₹262.47 crore of excess LD reversed on the second and third P-17A frigates, ₹123.28 crore from an amendment to the P-75 submarine contract, and ₹90.46 crore from reversing an inventory-obsolescence provision.

In plain terms

A shipyard's profit in any year is partly a guess about how a seven-year job will end. When the yard expects a job to lose money, it books the whole loss at once. When a delay penalty turns out smaller than feared, it books the difference as income. In FY26 MDL booked big losses on two cargo-ship and patrol-ship contracts but also big gains from smaller penalties, GRSE was helped by releasing old cost buffers, and Cochin lost the extra profit from aircraft-carrier repairs. New contracts will start at lower margins than the finished ones they replace.

What does this mean for the profit outlook? The completion-phase cushion is ending at MDL and GRSE as P-15B, P-17A and the anti-submarine craft finish. The next programmes start with design-phase revenue at new-project margins. Treasury income is falling with the float. Employee costs face the 8th Pay Commission, which GRSE expects to add 15–20% to its wage bill. The offsets are real: the remaining P-17A LD of ₹678 crore, possible submarine LD waivers, and operating leverage if signings come quickly. But the direction of margin for FY27–FY28 is down, not up, and management guidance at all three yards says so.

Where ₹100 goes, and the docks and people behind it

More than half of a naval ship's price is bought-in equipment and material. The yard's own levers are labour productivity, subcontracting, dock capacity and how much cash it holds.

The cost structure of a naval yard is dominated by things it buys. In MDL's FY26 consolidated accounts, materials consumed were ₹5,700 crore (43.8% of revenue) and base-and-depot spares supplied with the ships another ₹1,639 crore (12.6%). "Other expenses", which hold subcontracting, power, fuel and the onerous-contract provisions, were 18.6%. That leaves an operating margin of 17.4%. Depreciation and interest take only 1.3%, and other income adds 8.8%.

Three yards, three business models

A submarine specialist with a cash pile, a fast low-margin surface-ship builder, and a commercial-and-repair yard betting on capacity. The market pays the most for the one with the lowest returns.

The three listed yards share an owner and a main customer, but they make money in different ways. Their returns on equity tell the story. GRSE earns the highest ROE (31.6%) on the thinnest margin. MDL earns 29.2% on a high margin and a large cash pile. Cochin earns 12.5% despite a respectable operating margin. The differences come from asset turnover and the capital each has tied up.

MDL is the strategic integrator. It is the only Indian yard that has built both destroyers and conventional submarines, with a submarine line going back to INS Shalki in 1992. GRSE is the fast, asset-light executor. GRSE makes little profit on each ship but turns its small capital over quickly, so it earns the best return. MDL makes more on each ship and earns interest on a big cash pile. Rules of the game: procurement, indigenisation and a government that is also a seller

The state sets the price, approves the contract, funds the docks and sells the shares. Each role shows up in the numbers.

Procurement runs under the Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020 (DAP 2020). It ranks categories in order of preference: Buy (Indian-IDDM, meaning indigenously designed, developed and manufactured), Buy (Indian), Buy and Make (Indian), Buy and Make, and Buy (Global). Naval shipbuilding falls almost entirely into the Indian categories. Submarines go through the strategic-partnership model, under which an Indian yard teams with a foreign technology partner; that is how MDL and TKMS came to bid for P-75I. A draft DAP 2026, released on 10 February 2026 with comments closing on 3 March, promises shorter timelines and wider private-sector access. We found no final notification by October 2026. For the wider procurement picture see the Aerospace & Defence report.

Indigenisation is a policy goal and a real trend. Indigenous content on MDL's destroyers rose from 42% on P-15 to 75% on P-17A. GRSE claims "80% plus" overall. Its chairman breaks this down as about 100% for the hull ("float"), 60–70% for propulsion and machinery ("move") and lower for weapons and sensors ("fight"), and says reaching 100% will take five to ten years. The defence ministry has issued positive indigenisation lists of items that must be bought in India. MDL has submitted 1,024 items for its list, indigenised 55 of 174 submarine equipment items and buys 33.5% of its procurement from MSMEs. GRSE buys 69% from micro and small enterprises. Indigenisation helps the supply base and the foreign-exchange bill more than it helps yard margins.

Can India build merchant ships? The ₹69,725 crore package against China's 55% Deep dive

India built 0.06% of the world's ship tonnage in 2024. A decade of subsidies paid out ₹621 crore. The 2025 package is six times bigger and better designed, but it still has to overcome a cost gap the government itself acknowledges.

Commercial shipbuilding is a scale business dominated by three countries. In 2024, according to UNCTAD's Review of Maritime Transport 2025 (built on Clarksons data), the world delivered 71.69 million gross tons of ships. China built 54.57%, South Korea 28.02% and Japan 12.56%: together 95%. Viet Nam (1.01%), the Philippines (0.93%), Italy (0.64%) and Germany (0.26%) followed. India's share was 0.06%, about 43,000 gross tons, less than Türkiye's and only slightly more than the United States' 0.04%. China also took 74.4% of contracted tonnage in 2024 and held 63.7% of the global orderbook at the start of 2025. In 2025 new orders fell 27% to 56.43 million compensated gross tons (CGT), and China took 63% of them, South Korea 21% and Japan about 5–6% (indicative; Chinese industry data put Japan at 6.2%).

Why so little? The 2025 package fixes some of those flaws. The Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme (SBFAS), worth ₹24,736 crore and running to March 2036, pays 15% on contracts below ₹100 crore, 20% on larger ones and 25% on green, hybrid and specialised vessels. The rates do not taper. It requires at least 30% domestic value addition, and it includes a ₹4,001 crore shipbreaking credit note worth 40% of an old ship's scrap value, valid for three years and transferable, for owners who recycle old ships in India and build new ones here. The Maritime Development Fund provides long-term equity and debt through a ₹20,000 crore investment fund (government share up to 49%) and a ₹5,000 crore interest-incentive fund; Cochin describes the latter as interest subvention of up to 3 percentage points on loans to yards and ship-owners. The Shipbuilding Development Scheme (₹19,989 crore) funds capacity: Cochin says it gives a 25% capital subsidy on yard expansion. The package targets 4.5 million gross tons of annual capacity and about ₹4.5 lakh crore of investment.

In plain terms

India's old shipbuilding subsidy was small, shrank over time and was paid only after a ship was finished, so it mostly helped tugs and ferries. The new package is about six times larger, keeps its rates steady for ten years, pays more for green ships, and also helps pay for new docks and cheaper loans. It is a serious attempt. Whether it works will show up in orders for large ocean-going ships, which have not yet appeared in size.

The targets need to be read with care. Maritime India Vision 2030 aims for a top-10 shipbuilding nation by 2030; Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047 aims for the top five. One published path takes capacity from about 0.072 million GT to 0.33 million GT by 2030 and 11.31 million GT by 2047. A minister has spoken of a 5% global share by 2030. Those numbers are inconsistent: 5% of a world output of 30–70 million GT a year would be 1.5–3.5 million GT, five to ten times the 0.33 million GT path. Treat "5% by 2030" as aspirational and the 2047 numbers as direction rather than plan.

Where do the listed yards fit? Cochin is the main beneficiary, because it is the only one with an established export franchise. Its export book was ₹6,025 crore at March 2026 (standalone shipbuilding) and ₹6,038 crore at June 2026 (consolidated). About 90% of its commercial work is export and about 40% is "green" vessels. Its FY26 orders included CMA CGM's six LNG-fuelled 1,700-TEU feeders (about ₹3,240 crore in press reports; the company classifies it only as a "mega" order above ₹2,000 crore), four fully electric tugs for Svitzer and four tugs for Polestar Maritime. It is building two zero-emission container ships for Samskip, commissioning service vessels for a Cypriot owner and multipurpose ships for a German client, and its Udupi subsidiary builds 3,800-tonne cargo ships for Wilson of Norway. With HBL it has a 40% joint venture in marine electrification aimed at India's green-tug programme, targeting about ₹640 crore of revenue in its fifth year. Cochin's chairman talked in August 2025 of revenue of ₹10,000–12,000 crore by 2030-31, more than twice FY26.

The limits are just as clear. Commercial margins are thin: Cochin guides 10–12% for shipbuilding as a whole, and first-of-kind green ships at fixed prices carry overrun risk, as MDL's Danish hybrid-vessel losses show. Export advances are only 20–30%, so growth consumes working capital. Indian yards cannot yet build the ships that make up most of world tonnage: large container ships, bulk carriers and gas carriers need docks of 300–400 metres and series production. GRSE says plainly that it "currently" lacks the physical capacity for big container ships. The Korean shortcut has proved hard. Cochin's block-fabrication joint venture with HD KSOE was dropped in September 2026, and Cochin will build a smaller facility alone. MDL has denied plans for a Thoothukudi yard. The government's demand-aggregation programme (432 vessels over ten years, according to Cochin) is the most important near-term test: if PSU owners place large tanker and gas-carrier orders with Indian yards under SBFAS, Indian commercial shipbuilding will have its first real scale orders.

In plain terms

Cochin is already a credible builder of small, specialised and green European ships, and the new package should help it grow. Building the big container ships and tankers that make up most of world shipbuilding is a different business, needing bigger docks, bigger supply chains and years of practice. The package pays for some of that, but India is starting from almost zero.

The global lens: higher margins, a fraction of the scale, twice the multiple

Indian yards report higher margins than Western naval primes and trade at about twice the earnings multiple of Korean and US shipbuilders, on a fraction of their order cover.

Global shipbuilders fall into two groups. Korean and Chinese commercial giants (HD Hyundai Heavy Industries and its parent HD Korea Shipbuilding & Offshore Engineering, Hanwha Ocean, Samsung Heavy, China State Shipbuilding) build container ships, LNG carriers and tankers in huge volumes and also build warships. Western naval primes (Huntington Ingalls in the US, Fincantieri in Italy, the privately held Naval Group in France, the newly listed TKMS in Germany, BAE Systems' maritime arm in the UK) build mainly for their own navies, under cost-reimbursable or fixed-price incentive contracts. India's three yards are naval primes in that second sense, but report margins closer to the first group's recent peak.

Two centuries, five cycles: from treasury companies to a re-rating and back

For most of their history these yards earned more from interest than from ships. The 2023–25 boom was the exception, and its drivers were a decade in the making.

The yards are old. Mazagon's first dry dock in Mumbai dates from 1774. Garden Reach began as a small workshop on the Hooghly in 1884. Mazagon Dock was incorporated as a private company in 1934 and taken over by the government in 1960, the same year GRSE was nationalised. Cochin Shipyard was incorporated in 1972, the year MDL commissioned its first warship, the Leander-class frigate INS Nilgiri. MDL's submarine division began in 1984, and INS Shalki, the first submarine built in India, was delivered in 1992.

Cycle 1, before 2014: sleepy cost-plus yards. The yards built on nomination at negotiated prices, slowly. MDL's operating margin was 3.5–6% between FY15 and FY21, while its other income, mostly interest on advances, ran at ₹448–766 crore a year, more than its operating profit in every year until FY24. GRSE had negative operating profit in FY17 and FY18. They were, in effect, treasury companies with shipyards attached.

In plain terms

For decades these were slow government yards that made more money from interest than from building ships. Then a burst of contracts signed between 2005 and 2019 all reached their busiest phase at once, profits jumped, and the shares rose many times over. Now the busy phase is ending, the next contracts are late, and the shares have fallen back by a third or more.

Reading a shipyard: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twelve numbers explain almost everything about a shipyard's results. Most are not reported directly, several are defined differently by each yard, and two of them, inflow and the float, matter more than revenue.

A shipyard's profit and loss account is the last link in a long chain. Revenue is the order book multiplied by the speed of execution. Operating profit is revenue multiplied by a contract margin that is itself an estimate, adjusted for provisions and penalties. Profit before tax then adds interest earned on customer advances. Management controls execution speed, cost and, to a degree, margin. It does not control the size of the order book, which the Ministry of Defence decides, or the interest rate on its deposits. The equation below sets this out, and the rest of this section takes each term in turn.

A worked example in round numbers (illustrative). Take a yard with a ₹20,000 crore order book that executes 40% of it a year. Revenue is ₹8,000 crore. At a 12% contract margin, operating profit is ₹960 crore. Deduct ₹80 crore of net provisions and ₹50 crore of depreciation and interest. The yard holds ₹6,000 crore of customer advances in deposits at 7%, which adds ₹420 crore. Profit before tax is ₹1,250 crore, and profit after 25% tax about ₹938 crore. Interest is about a third of that profit. Now suppose the yard signs nothing for a year. Next year's opening book is ₹12,000 crore (₹20,000 crore minus the ₹8,000 crore billed). At the same 40% execution rate revenue falls to ₹4,800 crore. The advances are being consumed as the ships are built, so deposits fall too. Profit falls faster than revenue on both counts. This is the arithmetic behind the FY26 order gap.

Which way is each term moving in FY27? The order book is falling at MDL and GRSE and roughly flat at Cochin, and it only rises when the big naval contracts are signed. The execution rate is high now, because the books are full of ships near completion, and will fall when design-phase contracts are added. Contract margins are at a completion-phase peak and guided lower on new work: 12–15% at MDL, about 8–10% at GRSE and about 14% at Cochin. Provisions are a two-way risk: MDL still holds ₹1,060 crore of onerous-contract provisions and ₹678 crore of P-17A LD that could partly reverse, while new fixed-price contracts carry fresh overrun risk. Float interest is falling with deposit balances, partly offset by the higher repo rate. Four of the five terms therefore point down for FY27–FY28 unless signatures arrive. That is the analytical core of the sub-sector's derating, and the KPIs below are the tools for tracking each term quarter by quarter.

1. Order book

What it is. The value of signed contracts not yet recognised as revenue: the unexecuted balance. Formula: opening order book + new orders signed − revenue recognised ± adjustments (de-scoping, price revisions, currency). Worked example: MDL's book was ₹20,535 crore at March 2026. It recognised about ₹2,770 crore of standalone revenue in Q1 FY27 and booked about ₹453 crore of new work, ending at ₹18,218 crore in June 2026.

What drives it. Up: contract signatures, mostly lumpy MoD awards. Down: execution. A yard that executes fast without new orders sees its book fall fastest, which is GRSE's position. Benchmarks: as years of revenue cover (book ÷ trailing revenue), above 4× is good, 2–4× average and below 2× weak for a naval yard. How the three compare (June 2026): MDL ₹18,218 crore (1.37 years), GRSE ₹13,596 crore (1.81 years), Cochin ₹22,223 crore consolidated (4.40 years).

4. Operating margin: excluding versus including other income

What drives it. Contract type (nominated or competitive), project stage (completion phases release provisions), material and equipment costs, the rupee, subcontract rates, and the share of high-margin repair. Benchmarks (ex-other income): above 15% good, 8–15% average, below 8% weak for a naval yard. How they compare: FY26 MDL 17.4%, Cochin 16.5%, GRSE 11.4%; Q1 FY27 15.2%, 17.6% and 8.2%.

5. Margin quality: provisions, liquidated damages and write-backs

What it is. How much of a period's margin comes from changes in estimates rather than from the period's work. Formula: underlying operating profit = reported operating profit + onerous-contract additions + LD charges − LD reversals − provision releases − one-off contract amendments. Worked example: MDL FY26: ₹2,268 crore + ₹634 crore − ₹262 crore − ₹123 crore − ₹90 crore ≈ ₹2,427 crore, about 18.7% (indicative).

What drives it. Fixed-price exposure, first-of-kind designs, delivery delays and their causes, the ministry's willingness to waive penalties, and auditors' views on cost-to-complete estimates. GRSE's auditor named exactly these (cost-to-complete estimates and LD assessments) as a key audit matter. Benchmarks: onerous charges below 1% of revenue good, 1–4% average, above 4% weak. MDL's FY26 net charge was about 4.1% (₹538 crore ÷ ₹13,006 crore) and its gross additions 4.9%. How they compare: MDL underlying ~18.7% vs reported 17.4%; GRSE underlying ~9% vs 11.4%; Cochin reported 16.5%, with the FY25 repair peak not repeating.

Definition trap. The same item can sit in different lines. MDL booked LD reversals in revenue ("other operating revenue") and onerous provisions in "other expenses". GRSE's write-backs were in cost lines. Underlying margin is an analyst's construct, so label it as such, and remember that completion-phase margins (25–30% at MDL, by management's own description) are not a run-rate for new work.

In plain terms

Before trusting a shipyard's margin, check what was added or released that year. MDL's 17% was dragged down by losses provided on two contracts and pushed up by smaller-than-feared penalties. GRSE's 11% was helped by releasing old cost buffers. Neither number is what a new contract will earn.

6. Customer advances (contract liabilities) and working capital

What it is. Cash received from customers ahead of revenue: the float. Formula: contract liabilities on the balance sheet; intensity = contract liabilities ÷ annual revenue; working-capital days from screener. Worked example: GRSE March 2026: ₹5,751 crore ÷ ₹7,002 crore = 82% of a year's revenue held as advances, or 2.2 times its net worth.

What drives it. Up: new contract signatures (25% before steel on defence work). Down: execution of contracts whose advances have been received. Commercial export work brings only 20–30% upfront. Benchmarks: contract liabilities above 80% of revenue good, 40–80% average, below 40% weak; working-capital days below −200 good, −200 to 0 average, positive weak. How they compare (March 2026): MDL 79%, Cochin 90%, GRSE 82%, all falling; working-capital days −187, +16 and −80.

10. Revenue per employee

What it is. Revenue divided by regular employees. Formula: revenue ÷ year-end regular headcount. Worked example: MDL FY26: ₹13,006 crore ÷ 5,770 = about ₹2.25 crore per employee.

What drives it. Subcontracting (most production labour is outsourced), the share of bought-in equipment, and the stage of programmes. Benchmarks: there is no clean benchmark, because the figure measures outsourcing as much as productivity. 11. Dry-dock capacity and concurrent platforms

What it is. How many hulls a yard can work on at once and how large they can be. Formula: company-stated concurrent platforms; dock length and capacity in metres, tonnes or deadweight; for repair, ships per year. Worked example: GRSE can build 28 platforms at once, up to about 10,000 tonnes and 200 metres; its expansion plan targets 43.

What drives it. Docks, slipways, wet basins, crane capacity, waterfront depth and the block-fabrication shops that feed them. Benchmarks: a dock of 300 metres or more is needed for large commercial ships, carriers and LPDs; India has very few. How they compare: MDL 11 submarines + 10 warships, plus a 12,000-tonne floating dock under construction; Cochin's 310-metre dock (the largest at any PSU) and an ISRF ship-lift for 82 ships a year; GRSE 28 platforms, riverine and capped by channel depth.

Definition trap. "Platforms" counts a 25-metre interceptor craft and a 150-metre frigate the same way. Capacity in compensated gross tons (CGT), which weights ship types by complexity, is the global standard; Cochin's brownfield plan targets +150,000 CGT a year. Capacity matters only if it is filled: Cochin's new dock lifted its asset base and depreciation before the orders to fill it arrived.

In plain terms

Returns look excellent at MDL and GRSE because the customer's money funds the business. Take the interest away and they are still good, but closer to ordinary. Capacity is the other limit: a yard can only build what fits in its docks, and building bigger docks costs money before it earns any.

12. Repair throughput and repair margin

What it is. The volume and profitability of ship repair and refit, the steadiest and highest-margin activity a yard can have. Formulas: ships repaired per year and revenue per ship; segment result ÷ segment revenue. Worked example: Cochin FY26: ₹1,656 crore from 163 repair projects, about ₹10 crore a ship on average (indicative, since carrier refits and small craft are mixed together); segment result ₹429 crore, or 25.9%.

What drives it. Dock availability, the Navy's refit cycle (paid from its revenue budget), carrier and submarine refits (large, rare and very profitable), commercial owners' choice between Indian and foreign yards, and foreign-navy work under agreements such as the US master ship-repair agreements. Benchmarks: repair margin above 25% good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak. How they compare: Cochin repair revenue ₹1,656 crore (−11%) at a 25.9% segment margin (39.1% in FY25); MDL ship-repair revenue ₹1,032 crore standalone (up from ₹547 crore), margin not disclosed; GRSE ₹273 crore (2.4 times FY25) from leased Kolkata docks, margin not disclosed. Cochin's ISRF can handle 82 ships a year, and management targets about ₹600 crore of ISRF revenue within two years.

Definition trap. One exceptional year can dominate the series: Cochin's FY25 repair margin was lifted by carrier refits that will not recur every year. And ownership is changing: once the ISRF moves into the 50:50 joint venture with Drydocks World, its revenue leaves Cochin's consolidated top line and only half its profit comes back as a joint-venture share. Compare repair on a like-for-like perimeter.

13. Delivery schedule performance

What it is. Whether ships are delivered on, before or after the contract date. Formula: actual delivery date − contracted delivery date, in months, ship by ship. Worked example: MDL's P-17A frigate Taragiri was delivered on 28 November 2025, 1 month and 28 days after its contract date (net of a one-month grace period). MDL had provided LDs for a five-month delay, so it reversed the excess.

What drives it. First-of-class design changes, late owner-furnished equipment (engines, weapons, sensors), supplier sanctions or export licences, labour and dock bottlenecks, and sea-trial defects. Benchmarks: ahead of schedule good, within ±6 months average, more than 12 months late weak. How they compare: MDL delivered the third and fourth P-15B destroyers 5 and 2 months ahead of schedule and the P-17A follow-on ships within months of contract; the six P-75 Scorpenes, by contrast, were about seven years late. GRSE delivered two of its three P-17A frigates ahead of schedule and eight warships in FY26. Cochin plans ten deliveries in FY27, including export vessels whose payment depends on delivery.

Definition trap. Yards report deliveries in ships, which mixes a 25-metre craft with a 6,700-tonne frigate. Schedule performance must be read against the contract date, which is rarely published. LD reversals in the accounts are often the best evidence that deliveries beat the dates assumed in earlier provisions.

Five lessons come out of the anatomy. First, the revenue curve is back-loaded: only 16% of value is recognised in the first two years, which is why a signing in FY27 does little for FY28. Second, the cash curve is front-loaded: the yard holds a surplus of ₹350–430 crore through the early years, and interest on it adds ₹167 crore, raising the contract's pre-tax return from ₹440 crore to ₹607 crore, about 38% more. Third, the surplus shrinks through the build as costs overtake milestone payments; a yard whose contracts are all in years 5–6 sees its cash fall even while profit is high. That is the FY26 picture. Fourth, the margin is fragile. A five-month delay with LDs at a 5% cap (₹200 crore) cuts the margin from ₹440 crore to ₹240 crore. A 10% cost overrun (₹356 crore) leaves only ₹84 crore. A 15% overrun (₹534 crore) turns the contract into a ₹94 crore loss, which must be provided in full as soon as it is foreseen. Fifth, the release at the end: if the yard held contingency in its cost estimate and did not need it, the margin in years 6–7 jumps. That is the completion-phase uplift MDL describes.

How does the anatomy map onto the three listed yards today? MDL's book is dominated by contracts in years 6–7 (P-17A residual work, P-15B spares), where revenue is falling, margins are high because provisions are being released, and the cash surplus has mostly been spent. GRSE has a mix: P-17A and ASW-SWC in their final year, NGOPV and research vessels in years 2–4. Cochin has a large commercial book where the cash curve is reversed: export customers pay 20–30% upfront and the rest on delivery, so the yard runs a cash deficit through the build. None of the three has a meaningful book of year-1 and year-2 naval contracts, the stage that generates the cash surplus. That is the single clearest way to see why FY26 cash flow was negative everywhere and why the next signatures matter so much.

In plain terms

On one illustrative ₹4,000 crore frigate, the yard earns about ₹440 crore over seven years from building it and about ₹167 crore more from the interest on the money paid in advance. A six-month delay penalty or a 10% cost overrun can wipe out half or more of the building profit. Most of the revenue arrives in years four to six, but most of the cash arrives in years one and two.

16. Definition traps, collected

  • Order book is the unexecuted balance, not contract value. Never add AoN, L1 or programme values to it.
  • Order-book bases differ: standalone or consolidated, with or without repair, offshore and engineering, and at different dates (Cochin has three bases in six months).
  • Inflow is derived, not reported, and needs revenue and book on the same basis.
  • Standalone versus consolidated: MDL consolidates Colombo Dockyard (51%) and takes Goa Shipyard (47.21%) as an associate; Cochin consolidates Udupi and Hooghly; GRSE has no subsidiaries. This report uses consolidated for MDL and Cochin and standalone for GRSE.
  • Revenue from operations versus "total income": press releases often headline total income, which includes interest.
  • EBITDA with or without other income: company and agency EBITDA often includes it; screener's operating profit does not.
  • Cash: use cash plus other bank balances, not "cash and equivalents" alone and never screener's "other assets"; check for liens.
  • Customer advances appear as contract liabilities; a high balance means work owed, not spare cash.
  • Provisions and LDs move margin by several points; underlying margin is an analyst's adjustment and should be labelled.
  • Pipeline values are programme ceilings for several yards, often over a decade.

17. How to read a shipyard's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Order book first. Pull the quarter-end book and compare it with the last quarter and a year ago. Derive inflow (closing − opening + revenue). Red flag: book-to-bill below 0.5× for four quarters.
  2. New signatures and letters of intent. Check stock-exchange filings for contracts signed in the quarter and whether they are in the book. Red flag: guided signings that have slipped again.
  3. Revenue from operations, not total income. Compare growth with management's guide (MDL ~5% for FY27; Cochin ~12%). Red flag: revenue growth with a shrinking book and no explanation of what comes next.
  4. Operating margin excluding other income. Compare with the last four quarters, not just the last one. Red flag: margin above the guided range with no named release, or a sudden drop with no provision disclosed.
  5. Provisions, LDs and write-backs. Look for onerous-contract additions, LD reversals and cost-provision releases in the notes. Strip them out to get an underlying margin.
  6. Other income and interest cost. Is treasury income falling? Is interest cost rising (Cochin's tripled in five quarters)? Red flag: other income above 40% of PBT with falling deposits.
  7. Contract liabilities and cash (half-yearly). At the September and March balance sheets, check advances and cash plus bank balances. Red flag: both falling with no new contracts; at GRSE, liquid funds approaching the ₹3,000 crore CARE trigger.
  8. Operating cash flow (half-yearly). Negative in a building year is normal; negative in a signing year is not.
  9. Deliveries and execution milestones. Launches, trials, deliveries against contract dates. Red flag: slipping delivery dates on fixed-price contracts, which point to LDs and overruns.
  10. Pipeline commentary with the track record attached. Note each dated promise and check it next quarter. In the past twelve months every dated naval-order promise has slipped, while revenue guidance has been met at MDL and GRSE and missed at Cochin.
In plain terms

When a shipyard reports, look at the order book before the profit, the margin before the headline, the one-offs before the margin, and the cash before the dividend. Then write down every date management promises and check it next time.

About 40% of Cochin's commercial book is green. The risk is that these are first-of-kind designs at fixed prices: MDL's hybrid multipurpose vessels for Navi Merchants became one of its two loss-making contracts. SBFAS's higher 25% subsidy for green ships helps, but it does not remove execution risk.

Modular and digital shipbuilding is where the Korean and Chinese edge lies: large covered block-fabrication halls, robotic welding, digital design-to-production links, and series production. MDL has a module workshop with a retractable roof; Cochin is building a smaller block-fabrication facility on its own after the HD KSOE joint venture fell through. Adopting these methods is how Indian yards could close part of their cost gap, but it requires steady order flow to justify the investment. Supply chains remain a risk in a different sense: dependence on imported engines, gearboxes and electronics exposes delivery dates to export licences and sanctions, which MDL names as a principal risk.

In plain terms

The big technology questions are who gets India's next submarines (MDL hopes for P-75I, but the nuclear boats are being built elsewhere), whether drones replace some small patrol and mine-hunting ships, and whether yards can build new green ships without losing money on them. None of these threatens the yards in the next two years. All of them shape which yard is most valuable in ten.

Bull versus bear: the case for and against Indian shipbuilding

Both cases agree on the facts. They disagree on how soon the government signs and how much margin the new contracts carry.

The bull case

  • The need is real and dated: 53% of the fleet is over 15 years old, 62 ships are approved, and China is commissioning more than 15 vessels a year.
  • The pipeline is negotiated, not hypothetical: P-75I has cleared negotiation and the Finance Ministry; NGC has a negotiated ₹33,000 Cr price; the P-17B RFP is out.
  • Signatures refill everything at once: order books (to about ₹1.6 lakh Cr in the illustrative scenario), advances (about ₹27,000 Cr), cash and interest income.
  • Execution is proven: GRSE delivered eight warships in FY26; MDL delivered P-15B ahead of schedule and P-17A within months of contract.
  • Policy is finally serious about commercial ships: the ₹69,725 Cr package, 25% capex support and demand aggregation favour Cochin and, in time, GRSE.
  • Upside in the provisions: ₹678 Cr of P-17A LD and about ₹100 Cr of submarine LD at MDL could be released.

The bear case

  • Every dated promise has slipped: P-75I, the add-on Scorpenes, NGC and the P-17B RFP all missed their guided dates in the past year.
  • The FY28 air-pocket: even if contracts sign in FY27, design-phase revenue is only 3–10% of value a year for two years.
  • Margins revert: completion-phase margins and write-backs end; new work is guided at 12–15% (MDL), ~8–10% (GRSE) and ~14% (Cochin).
  • The float unwinds: advances fell ₹6,866 Cr in FY26, operating cash flow was negative at all three, and interest is about 26% of PAT.
  • Supply of stock: MDL must sell about 6 points more; each OFS has come at a 7–8% discount.
  • Valuation: 30× trailing and about 40× ex-cash, twice HD HHI and HII, on a book-to-bill one-ninth of Fincantieri's.

The bull case does not require anything new to happen, only things already approved to be signed. That is its strength. Its weakness is timing: the case has been "one CCS meeting away" for most of 2026, and the revenue from a signature takes two years to show up. The bear case does not require anything to go wrong, only for the slow approval process to continue at its usual speed. Its weakness is that the moment P-75I or NGC is signed, the order-book, cash and sentiment arguments all reverse together.
