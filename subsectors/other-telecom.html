00

The one-page view: a fibre landlord bolted to a government contractor

One listed company, two very different businesses, and a market that has stopped paying for the better one.

"Other Telecom Services" is the NSE 750's label for a corner of Indian telecom that has nothing to do with mobile phones. It covers the carriers that lay fibre along someone else's corridor (a railway track, a power line, a gas pipeline) and sell bandwidth to government, banks and telecom operators, together with the state-owned integrators that take a ministry's IT project, buy the hardware and install it. Indian Railways, POWERGRID and GAIL each built one of these carriers. Only one is listed on its own: RailTel Corporation of India, 72.84% owned by the government through the Ministry of Railways. This report is about the whole sub-sector, and RailTel is the lens through which its economics become visible in a public set of accounts.

The central fact is that RailTel is two businesses sharing one balance sheet. The first is a telecom landlord: about 63,000 route kilometres of optical fibre laid beside railway tracks, 21,000 km of city fibre, a presence at 7,000-plus stations, and a small data-centre arm. It earned ₹1,501 crore of revenue in FY26 and a segment EBIT of ₹400 crore, a 26.7% margin, or roughly 39% before depreciation (an estimate built from the segment result plus depreciation). The second is a project contractor: it wins government ICT and railway signalling jobs, buys cameras, servers, switches and Kavach equipment from others, installs them, and keeps a slim fee. It earned ₹2,777 crore of revenue at a 3.9% EBIT margin. Telecom was 35% of FY26 revenue but about 84% of segment EBITDA.

Five years ago the picture was reversed. Telecom was 64% of revenue in FY21 and the company's operating margin was 24%. Since then project revenue has grown 5.6 times (a 41% compound rate) while telecom grew 1.7 times (11% a year). The operating margin fell to 15% even though the telecom segment's own margin went up. The chief executive calls it "a group of two companies in one company… it is simple mathematics". That mix shift is the first of five forces that define this sub-sector, and the rest of the report is organised around them.

  • Two businesses in one. A high-margin, sunk-cost fibre network and a low-margin, working-capital-hungry integrator. The share of revenue that comes from telecom is the single best predictor of the company's margin (section 03).
  • The order-book machine. The book went from about ₹4,750 crore in mid-2024 to ₹11,747 crore on 31 July 2026, 2.65 times trailing revenue. Contrary to the popular "captive railway" story, railways are only about 23.5% of it; state and central government ICT is the engine, and most of it is tendered (sections 04 and 08).
  • Working capital and cash. Receivables of ₹2,053 crore equal 175 days of revenue against a target of 90 agreed with the government; another ₹898 crore is unbilled. Free cash flow was ₹44 crore in FY26 and negative ₹70 crore in FY25. Bank guarantees doubled to ₹1,482 crore (section 09).
  • Railway capex, signalling and Kavach. The FY27 railway budget has ₹7,500 crore for signalling and telecom works and ₹2,066 crore for Kavach. The money is real, but the equipment makers capture 34–35% margins while the integrator earns about 5% (sections 06 and 07).
  • Telecom pricing and new lines. Bandwidth prices fall every year, retail broadband faces wireless substitution, and data centres are the margin fix that is still small: ₹202 crore of revenue from about 3 MW (section 13).

The market's verdict over the past year has been harsh. At ₹229 on 23 October 2026 the shares are 38% lower than a year earlier and sit at the bottom of their 52-week range. The market value of ₹7,349 crore is 21.2 times trailing profit of ₹346 crore and about 10.4 times FY26 EBITDA after netting off roughly ₹714 crore of cash and deposits. That is the valuation of a railway contractor such as RITES or IRCON, not of a fibre network. Listed telecom and fibre names trade at roughly twice that earnings multiple. Part of the reason is the railway-PSU basket, which has de-rated as a group; part is RailTel's own cash problem; and part is that the quarter just reported (Q1 FY27, April–June 2026) showed revenue up 20% but profit flat, because a provision for slow-paying government customers rose.

In plain terms

RailTel owns a rare asset, a fibre cable running beside most of India's railway lines, and rents capacity on it at fat margins. To grow faster it has taken on large government IT jobs that pay a thin fee and take six months or more to collect. Those jobs now make up two-thirds of its sales, so the company looks like a contractor in its accounts and is priced like one. Whether that is fair depends on three things: whether the fibre business can grow again, whether the government pays faster, and whether new lines such as data centres can lift the profitable share back above 40%.

Where the results stand. The latest reported quarter is Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), approved by the board on 30 July 2026 and discussed on a call on 31 July. Revenue was ₹893 crore (+20%), with telecom at ₹361 crore and projects at ₹532 crore; profit after tax was ₹65.8 crore against ₹66.1 crore a year earlier. 01

From trench to invoice: how a corridor carrier makes money

A fibre cable beside a railway track becomes four kinds of revenue, and a government project becomes a fifth.

RailTel was incorporated on 26 September 2000 with a mandate written into its board report to this day: to modernise the railway's own telecom system for train control, operation and safety, and "to generate additional revenues by creating nationwide broadband and multimedia network, laying optical fibre cable using the right of way along railway tracks". Indian Railways transferred its existing telecom assets and fibre in exchange for shares (₹305.94 crore of equity issued to the President of India for non-cash consideration). From that starting point the business has grown along a value chain with six layers, and the money is made unevenly across them.

Most of the margin sits in layers 2–4: a network that was built years ago and is now largely depreciated. The last mile is shared with partners, and the right of way is paid for.

Layer 1, the right of way. Laying fibre anywhere in India means digging along a road owned by a municipality, a state or the highways authority, each with its own permission process and fee. A railway corridor removes most of that friction: one owner, a continuous strip of land, and protection from the road-widening and utility works that cut highway fibre several times a year. RailTel's annual report and its credit rater, ICRA, both describe its right to lay fibre along the tracks as exclusive. It is not free: RailTel pays the railway a 7% share of its service revenue (₹46.03 crore in FY26) and, separately, an 8% licence fee to the Department of Telecommunications on licensed revenue (₹91.60 crore). The tower side of the right of way stopped being exclusive in late 2022, when the railway opened all 68 of its divisions to private tower companies (section 05).

Layers 2 and 3, the network. The cable beside the track usually carries 24 to 48 fibres. Some are used by the railway for train control and block communication; the spare ones are lit with transport equipment at stations and sold. Because the trenching was done long ago, the marginal cost of a new circuit on lit fibre is mostly a transport card and electricity, which is why incremental margins on bandwidth can exceed 60% (an indicative figure). Depreciation of ₹189 crore a year sits almost entirely in the telecom segment.

Layer 5, the last mile, is shared. RailTel does not run a door-to-door field force. Its RailWire home broadband is delivered by about 11,500 local partners who own the customer connection and keep most of the subscription; in FY26 the "RailWire expenses" line, which is mainly partner payouts, was ₹228.5 crore against roughly ₹350 crore of RailWire revenue, so RailTel kept about 35%. Enterprise last-mile links are often leased from other operators. These costs, together with network maintenance, power and the railway's 7%, make up "access and other charges" of ₹604 crore, the second-largest cost line after project purchases.

Layer 6, the customer, is the state. The managing director put it simply on the Q1 FY27 call: "our telecom customers are mostly government". The annual report confirms it for contract revenue: of ₹2,802 crore of non-lease contract revenue in FY26, government customers accounted for 98.7%. That concentration explains both the strengths of the model (sticky, security-cleared customers who rarely switch carriers) and its chief weakness (payment that arrives on the government's timetable).

How a government ICT project flows

The project business works differently. A department issues a tender, awards the work to an empanelled public-sector integrator, or nominates a central PSU under the General Financial Rules. RailTel then tenders the same scope "back-to-back" to equipment makers and sub-contractors, a quarter of it through the Government e-Marketplace (GeM) in FY26. It supplies, installs and commissions, bills against milestones, and waits for acceptance and payment, which historically has taken 160–208 days on average. A three-to-five-year maintenance tail follows, and in the best cases the project leaves behind a connectivity service that RailTel bills as recurring telecom revenue. The railway's station CCTV programme did exactly that: from Q4 FY26, the bandwidth behind the cameras is billed in the telecom segment.

In plain terms

The fibre business runs on a network that was paid for long ago: each extra circuit sold on it is almost pure profit, although the price per circuit keeps getting cut. The project business is a contracting job: RailTel buys equipment, installs it, and earns a fee of about four rupees in every hundred for managing the work and waiting for the government to pay. One is valuable because of what it owns; the other because of what it is allowed to bid for.

Where is value captured in this chain? In the passive network and in the bundle RailTel can offer a government buyer: connectivity, hosting and security clearance from one state-owned supplier. It is not captured in the hardware that flows through the project business; there the equipment makers and software platforms take the margin, and the integrating PSU is paid for execution, working capital and the right to bid. RailTel's managing director has said as much: in project work "margins are 2%, 3% or 4%. Very few even operate in 4% to 5%."

02

Size and growth: one listed P&L inside several large markets

The pools RailTel draws from run to lakhs of crore; its own revenue is ₹4,277 crore and growing at 20–25%.

There is no clean official number for "corridor telecom" or "government ICT integration" as a market. The sub-sector is best sized by the public budgets and industry pools it feeds on, then by the one listed profit-and-loss account that sits inside them. The sizes vary by orders of magnitude, and the gap between budgeted and spent money matters as much as the headline.

RailTel's own growth has been rapid by any standard: revenue compounded at 26.2% a year from FY21 to FY26, profit after tax at 19.9% (₹140 crore to ₹346 crore). The headline "total turnover" of ₹4,328 crore used in press releases includes ₹50 crore of other and finance income; the operating revenue figure of ₹4,277 crore, up 23.0%, is the one to use for margins. Guidance for FY27 has moved up. On the Q4 FY26 call (1 May 2026) the managing director guided to growth of "around 20%". The quarterly pattern is the sub-sector's seasonality in miniature. Government departments rush to accept work and spend their budgets before 31 March, so the March quarter carries close to 40% of RailTel's annual revenue and an even larger share of profit (41% of FY26 PAT). The first quarter is the weakest. Operating margin has been stuck between 14% and 16% for eight quarters, which says something useful: with projects now 60–73% of revenue in every quarter, the mix shift has stopped dragging the margin lower. It has also stopped lifting it. Any recovery now needs telecom or data-centre growth, not just fewer projects.

In plain terms

The budgets that feed this business are enormous, but the piece that reaches a fibre carrier and IT integrator is narrow, and some of it (BharatNet) is announced far more generously than it is spent. RailTel is growing at 20–25% because state governments and ministries are buying more IT, not because the railway is spending more on it.

03

Landlord vs contractor: the segment split Deep dive

Separate the two businesses and every puzzle in RailTel's accounts, from the falling margin to the low multiple, resolves.

RailTel reports two segments: "Telecom Services" and "Project Work Services". Both names hide a mix. Telecom includes the pure network businesses (NLD, ISP, IP-1) plus data centres and digital services such as Aadhaar authentication; management calls only the first three "pure telecom". Projects include both non-railway government ICT and railway signalling and telecom work. The segment results are struck after depreciation, and nearly all depreciation, property and equipment sit in telecom: the segment-asset note shows no plant in projects. That makes the telecom margin understate cash profitability and the project margin roughly equal to its EBITDA.

Inside the project line: the railway is not the engine

Almost every rupee in this sub-sector comes from a government budget. The question is which budget, and how fast it pays.

1. Indian Railways capex and safety spending

The railway's FY27 capital budget is ₹2,93,030 crore, and ₹1,14,973 crore (39%) had been spent by July 2026. Safety spending has risen to ₹1,20,389 crore in FY27 from ₹39,200 crore in FY14. 2. Kavach automatic train protection

Kavach became India's national automatic train protection system in July 2020, and the Balasore collision of June 2023 turned it into a political priority. It needs fibre beside the track, a tower every few kilometres, data equipment at stations and radio on the locomotive. Those are, in principle, RailTel's competences. But the managing director told analysts in July 2026 that he was "not seeing many Kavach tenders in this current financial year", because existing awards are still under execution. Section 07 covers who captures the money.

3. Railway passenger digital services

Station Wi-Fi now runs at 6,117-plus stations, IP video surveillance is being installed at more than 5,000, a hospital management system covers 709 railway hospitals, and e-Office runs across railway units. Wi-Fi is mostly a public-service obligation. CCTV and hospital systems create something more valuable: recurring bandwidth and hosting revenue in the telecom segment once the installation is accepted. The Ministry of Railways' ₹334.5 crore e-Office upgrade order (letter of award 23 June 2026, running to June 2031) is the clearest current example of railway connectivity and hosting work flowing to RailTel.

4. State and central government digitisation

This is the engine. Non-railway project revenue rose 41.5% in FY26 to ₹2,274 crore. The work spans e-Office (150-plus organisations live, about 45 more in progress), police and city CCTV, AI-based traffic enforcement in Karnataka, LAN and WAN rate contracts such as Haryana's HARTRON, Aadhaar authentication at recruitment exams, PSU IT and health-insurance cyber-security. 6. Data localisation and sovereign cloud

The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified on 13 November 2025, with penalties of up to ₹250 crore. CERT-In's six-hour incident reporting rule, the 2024 telecom cyber-security rules and sector regulators' data rules all push government and regulated workloads towards empanelled domestic hosts. The FY27 Budget added a tax holiday to 2047 for foreign cloud providers that serve customers from notified Indian data centres. RailTel's pitch is a government-owned, security-cleared host with its own network, with railway workloads as anchor tenants. Data-centre revenue rose 59% in FY26 to ₹202 crore.

7. Wholesale fibre demand from private carriers

Private carriers need fibre for 5G backhaul, tower fibreisation and links between AI data centres. Most of their own fibre runs along highways. On the Q1 FY27 call the managing director said "private players, fiber demand is there… our right of way is independent and completely different… most other players are along the road", but added that RailTel would rather partner on revenue share than spend its own capital, and that "it will take time". The fibre-equipment makers are seeing the boom first: HFCL and STL each reported Q2 FY27 revenue of more than ₹2,500 crore, more than double a year earlier.

8. MEA-funded international ICT

A small new line: a ~₹18 crore data centre for Ethiopia's foreign ministry, laptops for Trinidad and Tobago, BHISHM medical cubes for several countries and a scoreboard at Jamaica's Sabina Park, all funded by India's Ministry of External Affairs. Export revenue was ₹7.65 crore in FY26, the first year it appeared. RailTel is bidding in Uzbekistan, trying in Vietnam and lost in Sri Lanka.

The railway as landlord: exclusive fibre, open towers

The FY22 annual report described RailTel's right as an "exclusive seamless RoW along 67,956 km of track through 7,325 stations". The FY26 report and ICRA's February 2026 rating rationale still call the fibre right of way exclusive. The consideration is the 7% revenue share on services, which has been unchanged since at least FY21 and rose from ₹30.7 crore that year to ₹46.0 crore in FY26 (cumulative ₹521 crore). The underlying agreement between the ministry and RailTel (tenure, termination, renewal) has not been published, which is a gap for any long-term valuation.

Towers are a different story. A 2016 railway order had reserved tower sites on railway land for RailTel. In late 2022 the railway revised its land-licence-fee policy so that all 68 divisions can license private telecom companies and infrastructure providers to put towers on railway land, at 1.5% of the land's market value a year, replacing a 7% revenue-share model. So the right of way is exclusive for fibre laid along the track, but the station and trackside tower business is open to competition. RailTel's tower co-location revenue was ₹59 crore in FY26, up 10%, so the loss of exclusivity has not hurt yet, but it caps the upside of a business that private tower companies now chase on equal terms.

The railway as customer: connectivity captive, projects contested

The railway as owner

The President of India, through the Ministry of Railways, holds 23,37,85,038 shares, 72.84%, unchanged since listing. The February 2021 IPO was a pure offer for sale of 8,71,53,369 shares at ₹94 (₹819 crore, all to the government); RailTel raised no money. It became a Navratna on 30 August 2024 (the 22nd), which gives the board power to invest up to roughly ₹1,000 crore or 15% of net worth in a single project without ministry approval. On a net worth of ₹2,262 crore, 15% is about ₹340 crore, roughly the cost of one 5 MW data-centre phase. Dividend policy is set not by the board's preference but by the Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) floor, covered in section 09.

Minimum public shareholding is an open question. Listed central PSUs were exempted from the 25% public-float rule until 1 August 2026 under Rule 19A(6) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Rules. No further extension was found in October 2026 searches, yet the register still showed 72.84% in September 2026. Either a fresh exemption has been issued, or there is a compliance gap that would require the government to sell about 2.2 percentage points. Treat it as a possible supply overhang without a date.

The railway has been laying it fast, under contracts executed by Kavach integrators (including RailTel itself in its Jabalpur order), and the cable belongs to the railway's signalling and telecom plan head. Kavach: who gets paid Deep dive

A ₹40,000-crore-plus safety programme where the equipment makers earn 34% and the integrator earns about 5%.

Kavach is India's home-grown automatic train protection system. It reads the signal aspect ahead, compares the train's speed with what is safe, warns the driver and, if the driver does not act, applies the brakes. It also prevents head-on and rear-end collisions by letting trains and stations talk to each other by radio. A working Kavach section needs four layers of equipment, and each layer is supplied by a different kind of company.

How big is the pool? An illustrative sizing

Who earns what: margins along the Kavach chain

The economics differ sharply by layer. The equipment makers own the safety-certified intellectual property, sell to a regulated buyer with few approved alternatives, and earn margins that look more like a technology company's than a contractor's. The integrator supplies labour, fibre, towers and coordination, and earns a contractor's fee. RailTel's managing director says Kavach margins are "slightly better than" the 4–5% project margin. The construction PSUs that carry signalling inside composite contracts earn similar thin margins.

In plain terms

Kavach is a large, durable safety programme, but most of its value goes to the handful of companies whose equipment is certified. RailTel's role is to dig, lay fibre, raise towers and install someone else's boxes. That earns about five rupees in a hundred, and its three orders are waiting on its partner's final approval. The bigger long-term question is who controls the spare fibre that Kavach leaves behind.

The strategic upside, if any, lies in the fibre. The Jabalpur order includes laying the railway's 2×48-fibre cable. If RailTel ends up as the operator and lessor of spare Kavach fibres across the 21,794 route km now under way, it gains a second network without capital spending. If the railway keeps the fibre captive, RailTel will have been paid a contractor's fee to build a potential competitor to its own backbone. No policy on this has been published.

In plain terms

The order book has more than doubled, but it now contains longer contracts. RailTel itself expects to convert only about a third of it into project revenue this year. Book quality: what the headline hides

Four features of this book deserve attention. First, 87.9% of FY26 contract revenue was fixed-price, and the cost of hardware has been pushed up by a weaker rupee and wholesale inflation of 11.4% in September 2026. On a 4–5% margin, a 2% rise in bought-in costs on a contract bid a year earlier removes about half the profit (illustrative). Second, the book consumes guarantee capacity: performance and advance bank guarantees rose to ₹1,482 crore in FY26 (section 09). Third, it is almost entirely government, so every rupee of it will become a receivable that ages on the government's timetable. Fourth, the book does not tell you margin. A ₹500 crore supply contract and a ₹500 crore five-year bandwidth contract count the same in the headline but differ by a factor of seven or eight in EBITDA.

The sub-sector's cautionary tale is another state-owned telecom integrator. ITI, 90% owned by the government, reported FY26 revenue of ₹2,184 crore, an operating margin of 2.9%, debtor days of 486 and a profit that came from other income. An order book won through state ownership is only as good as the margin it is priced at and the speed at which the customer pays. RailTel's figures are much better on both counts, but the direction of its receivables in FY26 is the reason the market has started to discount its book.

The ECL charge is a cost of doing business, not an exception

RailTel books an expected-credit-loss (ECL) provision on ageing government debtors as an "exceptional item": ₹2.1 crore in FY23, ₹26.7 crore in FY24, ₹21.7 crore in FY25, ₹24.4 crore in FY26 and ₹7.0 crore in Q1 FY27 alone, against a ₹3.4 crore write-back a year earlier. The managing director calls it "non-cash, notional", posted on debts that pass one to two years, and reversible when the money arrives. That is accounting-correct, but it recurs every year and grows with the book, so analysts should treat it as an operating cost of selling to government. It explains Q1 FY27: pre-exceptional profit before tax rose 12% (₹96.5 crore against ₹85.9 crore), but profit after tax was flat at ₹65.8 crore. The allowance of ₹142.6 crore is 6.5% of gross receivables and smaller than the ₹170 crore sitting in credit-impaired and higher-risk buckets, which suggests ₹20–30 crore a year is a floor rather than a ceiling.

Who funds the gap: suppliers, not shareholders

ICRA describes RailTel's working-capital intensity as "negative/very low, as a majority of such receivables are backed with subcontractors' liability, along with the receipt of customer advances". In plain language, RailTel pays its sub-contractors when the government pays it. The balance sheet shows it: trade payables rose to ₹1,674 crore, and payables to micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs) more than doubled, from ₹447 crore to ₹987 crore, now 59% of all payables. The ageing note says MSME payables are aged "based on date of transaction as payment milestones are not yet achieved", which confirms back-to-back terms.

This float is shrinking from two directions. Customer advances (contract liabilities) fell from ₹372 crore to ₹231 crore in FY26, so fewer advances are funding the book. And MSME suppliers have increasingly strong statutory protection: the MSMED Act's 45-day payment rule, enforced since FY24 through the tax disallowance in section 43B(h) of the Income Tax Act, makes delayed payment to small vendors costly for the buyer. If the back-to-back float shrinks, the 175-day receivable cycle falls on RailTel's own cash.

In plain terms

RailTel has been running a relay: the government pays it late, and it pays its small suppliers late in turn. That keeps its own cash needs low. But the government now protects small suppliers' right to be paid within 45 days, and customers are paying fewer advances. If RailTel has to pay suppliers on time while still waiting six months for the government, the cash has to come from somewhere, and it has no debt to draw on today.

Guarantees: the binding constraint on project growth

The least-discussed number in RailTel's FY26 accounts may be the most constraining. Bank guarantees given to customers and government doubled to ₹1,481.66 crore (from ₹723.95 crore), equal to 65.5% of net worth. Government contracts typically need performance guarantees of 3–10% of contract value, so a ₹11,700 crore book is consistent with about ₹1,500 crore of guarantees. Another doubling of the project book would need fresh guarantee lines, which banks size against net worth. Guarantee capacity, not equity, is now the first limit on how fast the project business can grow.

The dividend floor: a rule, not a choice

The last call on cash is the dividend. RailTel's payout ratio fell from 50% in FY21 to 30% in FY26, which some coverage reads as a change of policy. It is a change of rule. Under DIPAM's 2016 guidelines a central PSU had to pay the higher of 30% of profit or 5% of net worth. RailTel paid almost exactly 5% of net worth every year from FY18 to FY24, so the net-worth leg was binding and the payout ran at 37–58% of profit. DIPAM's revision of 18 November 2024 cut the net-worth leg to 4%. With profit growing, the 30%-of-profit leg now binds: FY25 was 30.5% of profit (4.57% of net worth) and FY26 30.1% (4.61%). That is why the dividend per share stayed at ₹2.85 in FY25 despite 22% profit growth, and rose only to ₹3.25 in FY26.

Cost drivers and their direction in 2026

  • Project hardware (cameras, video recorders, servers, switches, UPS units) is imported or assembled under the Public Procurement (Make in India) preference. With the rupee near ₹94.9 per dollar and wholesale inflation at 11.4% in September 2026, contracts bid a year ago carry cost risk; the margin is too thin to absorb much.
  • Access charges depend on last-mile leasing from Jio, Airtel, BSNL and local operators and on electricity at network sites. Energy savings are offsetting part of the network's growth.
  • The licence fee is 8% of licensed revenue. The FY26 charge (₹91.6 crore) fell 8% on FY25's ₹99.5 crore (per the audited P&L note; the board's report shows ₹88.6 crore on what looks like a cash basis), as more revenue moved into data centres and ICT services that are not all licensed revenue.
  • Employees: headcount rose from 698 in FY22 to 928 in FY26, and income per employee from ₹2.29 crore to ₹4.66 crore. The constraint is hiring data-centre, cyber-security and AI talent on public-sector pay scales; the annual report cites "talent retention challenges".

The ten-year table in the annual report summarises the shift in one ratio: expenditure as a share of income rose from 72.5% in FY22 to 84.2% in FY26, and profit before tax fell from 17.5% of total income to 10.9%. Profit after tax as a share of net worth nevertheless rose from 13.7% to 15.3%, because the project business uses little capital. RailTel has become less profitable per rupee of revenue and slightly more profitable per rupee of equity, which is exactly what a mix shift towards an asset-light contractor should do.

In plain terms

Most of the money RailTel collects passes straight through to equipment suppliers, sub-contractors and partners. What it keeps comes overwhelmingly from the fibre network, which was paid for years ago. That is why selling more bandwidth matters far more to profit than winning another large installation contract.

The utility-corridor carriers

The government integration crowd

Government ICT integration is crowded and the barriers are qualifications rather than assets. Public-sector integrators include RailTel, ITI, BEL, TCIL, BECIL and C-DAC, with NICSI empanelling vendors for central ministries. Private competitors include TCS, L&T, HCL, Tech Mahindra and Wipro at the large end, telecom-equipment companies with services arms such as HFCL and STL, and a long tail of integrators such as Vindhya, Allied Digital and Orient Technologies. The bar to bid is turnover thresholds, past-work certificates, equipment-maker tie-ups and security clearance. Public-sector integrators also benefit from rules that let ministries award work to central PSUs and from state rate contracts. None of this produces pricing power: integration margins of 2–5% are the norm, as RailTel's managing director acknowledges.

Imports are shaped by policy rather than price. Telecom and ICT hardware for government and core networks falls under the "trusted source" regime introduced through the 2021 licence amendment and the Make-in-India public procurement preference. Chinese vendors are effectively excluded from government and core networks. For an integrator, that raises the cost of hardware modestly (Indian or trusted vendors cost more) but also removes the lowest-cost competitors from bids, which helps protect the thin margin.

In plain terms

Owning a corridor is rare; three state utilities do it, and RailTel is the only one an investor can buy on its own. Integrating government IT is common; dozens of public and private firms do it for a few rupees in a hundred. RailTel's listed value rests on the rare part, but most of its revenue now comes from the common part.

A pays the bills, C provides the growth

The corridor carrier (A) is where most of the sub-sector's profit is made. Its network is built and largely depreciated, so it earns high cash margins on a modest asset base. Its returns depend on how much of the spare fibre it can sell and at what price. Its weakness is price: enterprise bandwidth prices fall every year, so growth has to come from volume. The integrator (C) is the opposite: almost no fixed assets, thin margins, and returns that depend on winning, executing and collecting. Its weakness is cash, as section 09 showed. Combined, A funds the dividend and the network, and C provides revenue growth and keeps RailTel relevant to government buyers.

B and D are where the margins live, and RailTel cannot easily get there

The integrated telco enterprise arms (B) have what RailTel lacks: product breadth, mobile and fixed access of their own, global networks, and enterprise sales forces. They earn mid-double-digit to high margins and collect quickly. The Kavach equipment makers (D) own certified designs and earn 30–35% margins with returns on capital of 48–59%. RailTel could reach archetype B's economics only by building a product portfolio and a private-sector sales engine, and D's only by acquiring or developing certified equipment. Neither is in its plan.

E and F are the levers RailTel is pulling

Data centres (E) are the stated priority, "first priority today" in the managing director's words, because they carry higher margins than pure telecom (the ED Finance's statement on the Q4 call) and sell naturally to the same government customers. But they need very large capital per megawatt, and RailTel is answering that with leased partner shells, which lowers the capital need and the margin together. Retail broadband (F) runs through 11,500 partners who own the last mile, so RailTel's capital is light, but it keeps only about 35% of the subscription revenue and faces wireless substitution.

In plain terms

RailTel is four kinds of company at once. The fibre network makes most of the profit; government projects make most of the revenue; data centres and home broadband are small attempts to add better-quality growth. When investors compare it with Kavach makers or data-centre developers, they are comparing it with businesses whose economics it does not share.

The utility-corridor model has one more feature worth noting: it is easier to run inside a larger utility than as a standalone listed company. POWERTEL and GAILTEL do not report quarterly results, do not face analysts and do not pay their own dividends under DIPAM's floor; their parents absorb the lumpiness. RailTel, listed on its own, has had to grow revenue to show progress, and the project business was the fastest way to do it. That choice, more than any change in the underlying corridor economics, explains why the listed vehicle now looks like archetype C.

NLD bandwidth: sticky customers, falling prices, purchase-order risk

NLD is RailTel's largest telecom line at ₹640 crore. Its customers are central and state governments, PSUs, banks, other carriers and research institutions on the National Knowledge Network, joined recently by the Air Force, "one private operator" and Starlink, which buys backhaul and gateway connectivity. Customers rarely leave: switching a ministry's network carrier involves security reviews and migration risk. But prices fall. Industry convention is that enterprise bandwidth prices drop roughly 5–15% a year (indicative), and the managing director speaks of "continuous pressure on prices in telecom", especially "in enterprise business on the South side". Volume growth has to outrun price cuts for revenue to rise at all; in FY26 it barely did.

Q1 FY27 exposed a second weakness. NLD revenue fell to ₹144 crore from ₹151 crore a year earlier (−4.6%) because government renewal purchase orders had not arrived. "We book on accrual basis, but then POs have to be there," the managing director said. The National Informatics Centre had also decentralised NKN ordering to individual institutes. The annual report shows why this matters: RailTel books ₹1,459.5 crore of telecom revenue as operating-lease income, but only ₹184 crore of future lease payments are non-cancellable, ₹113 crore of them within a year. The bandwidth book is renewed each year, purchase order by purchase order, at prices that are reset each time.

In plain terms

Government customers rarely switch away from RailTel's network, but they also do not sign long contracts. Each year they renew, often late, and often at a lower price. That makes the fibre business steady rather than locked in, and explains why a quarter can show falling bandwidth revenue while customers keep using the service.

RailTel's data-centre business is really a government cloud and hosting business that rents much of its floor space from others. That is why it earns so much revenue per megawatt and needs little capital. It is growing quickly and carries better margins than bandwidth, but at ₹200–300 crore a year it is still too small to move the company's margin by more than a fraction of a point.

Dark fibre, towers and the corridor option

IP-1 revenue (dark fibre ₹50.3 crore, towers ₹59.0 crore) is small but strategically important, because it is the line through which private demand for the corridor would show up. Private carriers want non-highway routes for 5G backhaul and data-centre interconnect; RailTel prefers to "partner with someone who is sharing best revenue" rather than spend its own capital, and says "it will take time". Britain's Network Rail ran exactly this play in 2025, letting a fibre operator and a mobile infrastructure firm build and sell along its main lines under a long-term revenue share (section 20). A comparable deal in India would be the most direct way to raise RailTel's telecom share of revenue; none has been announced. AI and GPU services are a further option: RailTel says it is "in the process" of GPU services and small language models for the railway on air-gapped infrastructure, without a capex figure.

Rules, levies and the parent: the regulation that shapes the sub-sector

A telecom licence, a railway revenue share, government procurement law and a DIPAM rulebook all sit between revenue and the shareholder.

A corridor carrier that also integrates government projects is regulated three times over: as a telecom licensee by the Department of Telecommunications and TRAI, as a supplier by government procurement rules, and as a central public-sector enterprise by the Department of Public Enterprises and DIPAM. Each layer imposes a cost or a constraint that a private telecom company or IT integrator would not face in the same combination.

The two levies: licence fee and railway share

The licence fee and the railway's revenue share work like royalties on the sub-sector's telecom revenue. At statutory rates they add up to 15% (8% plus 7%) of the revenue each applies to. Because neither base covers the whole telecom segment (data-centre and some ICT revenue fall outside licensed revenue, and the railway's share applies to services), the actual FY26 charge of ₹137.6 crore was 9.2% of telecom segment revenue. Cumulatively RailTel has paid ₹846 crore of licence fees and ₹521 crore to the railway. TRAI and DoT have discussed lowering the licence fee for years; a cut to, say, 6% would add about ₹23 crore a year to EBITDA on FY26's base (illustrative). Our telecom equipment report covers the wider DoT policy agenda.

The new telecom law and the right of way

The Telecommunications Act, 2023 replaced the colonial-era Telegraph Act and has been brought into force through rules on right of way (harmonised fees and statutory timelines for public land) and cyber security (2024). Two questions matter for corridor carriers. First, whether the new "infrastructure" authorisation that replaces IP-1 registration carries fees or obligations on dark-fibre and tower revenue; nothing has been notified that changes RailTel's position. Second, whether the RoW Rules, which govern public land generally, could be used to open railway track corridors to others. Nothing found so far does so, and RailTel's own filings and ICRA continue to describe the fibre right of way as exclusive.

Procurement law cuts both ways

The General Financial Rules allow ministries to award work to central PSUs, and state rate contracts give empanelled public integrators a channel to repeat business. That is the institutional advantage behind RailTel's order book. But the same framework imposes fixed-price contracts, milestone-based payments, performance guarantees, liquidated damages and integrity pacts, and the Government e-Marketplace pushes procurement towards price competition. RailTel itself buys through GeM and under the Make-in-India preference, which raises hardware costs modestly. The net effect is access without pricing power.

In plain terms

Before RailTel's shareholders see a rupee of telecom profit, about nine rupees in every hundred of telecom revenue have gone to the telecom department and the railway. Before they see a rupee of dividend, a government rule has already decided it will be 30% of profit. Regulation gives RailTel a protected corridor and access to government buyers, and takes a defined share of the result in return.

Governance of a listed public enterprise

The chairman and managing director and the functional directors are appointed by the President through the Ministry of Railways; the board includes government nominee and independent directors; remuneration follows public-sector scales. CMD Sanjai Kumar, Director Finance V. Rama Manohara Rao, Director Projects, Operations and Maintenance Manoj Tandon, and Director Network Planning and Marketing Yashpal Singh Tomar lead the company. Investor communication is thin: RailTel files a results document and a call transcript each quarter but no investor presentation, so many of the operating numbers in this report come from scanned transcripts read page by page. Separate data-centre disclosure is "planned". For a company whose value depends on separating two businesses, better disclosure would itself be worth something.

Twenty-six years, five eras

From a railway telecom department to a listed Navratna: building the corridor, retail and Wi-Fi, safety and listing, the project boom, and the de-rating.

The history of this sub-sector is the history of a state asset searching for a business model. The fibre was laid for train control; selling the spare capacity was an afterthought written into a mandate. Each era since has added a new line of business, and each has left a mark on today's accounts.

2000–2010: building the corridor

RailTel was incorporated on 26 September 2000. Indian Railways transferred its telecom assets and fibre in exchange for ₹305.94 crore of equity. NLD and ISP licences followed, and the company became an implementing PSU for the National Optical Fibre Network (later BharatNet Phase I) alongside BSNL and POWERGRID, for the National Knowledge Network that links universities and research institutions, and for fibre in the North-East funded by the Universal Service Obligation Fund. The North-East project, halted for long periods by terrain and conditions, still carries a cumulative impairment of ₹121 crore.

2012–2019: retail and Wi-Fi

RailTel became a Mini-Ratna in 2012. RailWire launched on a franchise model, reaching small-town households without a field force. In January 2016 station Wi-Fi launched with Google at Mumbai Central; after Google left the programme in 2020, RailTel continued it, and it now covers more than 6,100 stations. Tier-III data centres at Secunderabad and Gurugram followed, along with government cloud empanelment. Telecom remained the core business: in FY21 it was 64% of revenue.

2020–2021: safety and listing

Kavach was adopted as the national train-protection system in July 2020. In February 2021 the government sold 8,71,53,369 shares at ₹94 in an offer for sale, raising ₹819 crore for itself, and RailTel listed on 26 February 2021. FY21's 24% operating margin, with telecom dominant, is the baseline against which the later changes should be read. The 700 MHz spectrum allotment to the railway followed in 2021.

2022–2025: the project boom

Project revenue rose from ₹507 crore in FY22 to ₹2,115 crore in FY25, driven by state-government ICT, railway station CCTV, e-Office and MEA-funded supplies. RailTel Enterprises, the subsidiary that had carried some project work, was merged in (appointed date 1 October 2022, effective 29 August 2023), which is why consolidated and standalone figures differ before FY23. Tower exclusivity on railway land ended in 2022. The Balasore collision of 2 June 2023 accelerated Kavach funding. The shares rallied with the "railway PSU" trade to a peak around ₹618 in July 2024, and RailTel became the 22nd Navratna on 30 August 2024. In November 2024 DIPAM cut the dividend floor's net-worth leg from 5% to 4%.

Reading the numbers: the corridor-carrier KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain a corridor carrier and government integrator. Here is what each means, how to compute it, what good looks like, and where it misleads.

A company that is two businesses needs two sets of measures. The telecom landlord is judged like an infrastructure owner: how much of its capacity it sells, at what price, and how much of the revenue is left after levies. The project contractor is judged like an engineering firm: how many orders it wins, how fast it executes them, what margin it keeps and how quickly it is paid. On top of both sits a public-sector layer: a dividend set by rule, guarantees that limit growth, and a valuation that the market applies to the whole railway-PSU basket. This section takes each measure in turn, using RailTel's disclosures as the worked case.

The equation, worked in rupees

Every number in this masterclass feeds one equation. Revenue is the sum of the telecom annuity and project execution. EBITDA is each multiplied by its own margin, less unallocated costs. Profit then subtracts depreciation (almost all telecom), the recurring ECL provision on government debtors and tax, and adds interest on cash. Free cash flow is profit plus depreciation, less the change in working capital and capex.

Three sensitivities fall out of this arithmetic (all illustrative). Each extra percentage point of telecom share at constant revenue moves about ₹43 crore from a 4% business to a 39% business, adding roughly ₹15 crore of EBITDA, or 0.35 points of company margin. The order of importance is therefore telecom mix, then collections, then project volume, which is the reverse of how the company is usually discussed.

In plain terms

Three numbers decide RailTel's year: how much of revenue comes from the fibre business, how quickly governments pay, and only then how many projects it completes. A big project win makes headlines but adds little profit; a percentage point of revenue shifting towards the network, or ten days faster collection, matters more.

KPI 1 · Segment revenue mix (telecom share of revenue)

What it is. Telecom segment revenue as a share of revenue from operations. The primer on our site also uses the "mismatch ratio", telecom divided by projects; the two carry the same information. Formula. Telecom ÷ (telecom + projects). Worked example. FY26: ₹1,500.7 crore ÷ ₹4,277.5 crore = 35.1% (ratio 0.54×). Q4 FY26: ₹448.9 ÷ ₹1,668.9 = 26.9% (0.37×). Q1 FY27: ₹360.8 ÷ ₹893.3 = 40.4% (0.68×).

What drives it. Up: data-centre and digital-service growth, fibre-leasing deals, slower project execution. Down: large supply contracts and the March-quarter acceptance rush. Benchmark. Good above 50%, average 35–50%, weak below 35% for a carrier with an integration arm; RailTel sits on the weak/average border. Peers. Not applicable to single-business peers; POWERTEL and GAILTEL are close to 100% carrier but do not report separately. Definition trap. The quarterly figure swings with project seasonality (27% to 45% within FY26), so compare like quarters or full years. FY21–FY22 shares are on a consolidated basis that included the former subsidiary RailTel Enterprises; treat them as ±2 points.

What it is. The value of contracted work not yet executed, and how many years of revenue it represents. Formula. Order book ÷ trailing twelve-month revenue. KPI 4 · Order inflow and book-to-bill

What it is. New orders won in a period, relative to revenue billed. A ratio above one means the book is growing. Formula. Order inflow ÷ revenue. Worked example. FY26: ₹7,363 ÷ ₹4,277 crore = 1.72×. FY24: ~₹2,870 ÷ ₹2,568 crore = 1.12×. Q1 FY27: ₹1,688 ÷ ₹893 crore = 1.89× (Q1 FY26: ₹721 ÷ ₹744 crore = 0.97×). What drives it. Peers. n/a in our dataset. Definition trap. Inflow is lumpy and a single rate contract can be "won" at a ceiling value that is never fully drawn. RailTel did not disclose FY25 inflow in primary documents; a secondary figure of ₹3,146 crore does not reconcile with the book's movement and should not be used.

KPI 5 · Execution rate

What it is. The share of the opening order book converted into revenue during the year. It tells you whether the book is moving or merely accumulating. Formula. Executed value ÷ opening book, where executed value = opening book + inflow − closing book; or, more simply, project revenue ÷ opening book. Worked example. FY26: ~₹6,616 + ₹7,363 − ~₹10,650 = ~₹3,330 crore executed, about 50% of the opening book; project revenue alone was ₹2,777 crore, 42%. FY27 guidance: ₹3,000–3,500 crore ÷ ~₹10,650 crore = 28–33%.

What drives it. Contract length (Kavach runs 30–35 months), customer acceptance speed, OEM approvals (Quadrant's RDSO approval gates the Kavach orders), and site access. Benchmark. For a mixed book, 35–50% a year is healthy; below 30% signals slowing conversion or a lengthening book. Peers. n/a. Definition trap. Because the order book includes telecom and data-centre service contracts, the executed figure from a roll-forward overstates project execution; and because two of the four inputs are verbal or secondary, the result is an estimate, not a disclosure.

In plain terms

A bigger order book is only good news if it is being worked through. RailTel's own guidance implies it will convert about a third of its book into project revenue this year, against about half last year. The book is getting longer, not just bigger.

Over the past two years RailTel turned only about half of its operating profit into cash, and almost all of what was left went on capex. The dividend was paid largely from the cash pile built earlier. That is the real reason the market discounts the profit line.

KPI 9 · NLD bandwidth revenue growth

What it is. Year-on-year growth in the core carrier line. Formula. (NLD revenue this period ÷ same period last year) − 1. Worked example. FY26: ₹639.8 ÷ ₹621.6 − 1 = +2.9%. Q1 FY27: ₹144 ÷ ₹151 − 1 = −4.6%. FY21–FY26 compound growth 8.0%. What drives it. Volume (new circuits, higher capacities for existing customers, new customers such as Starlink and the Air Force) minus price cuts of perhaps 5–15% a year (indicative), plus the timing of government purchase-order renewals. Benchmark. Good above 8%, average 3–8%, weak below 3%; FY26 was weak. Peers. Airtel Business grew about 12% in Q1 FY27 (secondary); NLD-only figures for others are not published (n/a). Definition trap. RailTel books NLD as lease income only when a purchase order exists, so revenue can dip while the service is still being used; a later catch-up then inflates another quarter. Only ₹184 crore of future lease receipts are non-cancellable, so "annuity" overstates the contractual security.

KPI 10 · RailWire subscribers, ARPU and partner share

What it is. The retail broadband franchise's size, price and split with partners. Formula. Revenue ≈ subscribers × ARPU × 12; RailTel's retention = 1 − partner payouts ÷ RailWire revenue. Worked example. FY26: 6.15 lakh × ₹474 × 12 ≈ ₹350 crore. RailWire expenses ₹228.5 crore ÷ ₹350 crore = 65% to partners, so RailTel keeps about 35% (~₹121 crore) before backbone and overheads. What drives it. Partner recruitment, small-town FTTH demand, competition from 5G fixed-wireless and bundled fibre, price increases by the national players. Benchmark. Subscriber growth good above 15% a year, average 5–15%, weak below 5% (RailWire ~8–9%); ARPU good above ₹550, average ₹400–550, weak below ₹400 (RailWire ₹474). Peers. National players do not disclose comparable wired ARPU on the same basis (n/a). Definition trap. ISP revenue (₹452 crore) is not RailWire revenue (~₹350 crore); it also includes internet leased lines. RailWire's subscriber share (~1.3% of wired broadband) mixes TRAI months and should be treated as indicative.

KPI 11 · Data-centre revenue per MW and capacity

What it is. How much revenue each megawatt of operational capacity generates, and how fast capacity grows. Formula. DC revenue ÷ operational MW. Worked example. FY26: ₹202 crore ÷ ~3 MW ≈ ₹67 crore per MW. A pure colocation provider would earn perhaps ₹8–12 crore per MW per year (indicative). Guidance: ₹300 crore FY27 and ₹500 crore FY28; Noida 5–10 MW by about May 2027. What drives it. The share of managed cloud and services in the mix, use of partner-owned shells, anchor government and railway workloads, power cost. Benchmark. For colocation, utilisation above 80% and yields at the top of the range are good; for a managed-services model, revenue growth above 40% is good, 20–40% average. RailTel grew 59% in FY26. Peers. Listed Indian DC peers do not disclose on this basis (n/a). Definition trap. RailTel's DC revenue is not a colocation yield; it includes hosting and services on leased capacity, so MW figures and revenue cannot be compared with developers'. The two calls also disagree on Noida's phase-one size.

In plain terms

RailTel's data-centre business earns far more per megawatt than a landlord renting space, because most of what it sells is cloud and hosting services on top of rented floor space. That makes the megawatt count less important than whether government customers keep moving their systems onto RailTel's cloud.

KPI 12 · Right-of-way and licence levies

What it is. The share of telecom revenue paid to the Department of Telecommunications (licence fee) and to Indian Railways (revenue share on services). Formula. (DoT licence fee + railway revenue share) ÷ telecom segment revenue. Worked example. FY26: (₹91.60 + ₹46.03) ÷ ₹1,500.69 = 9.2%. Statutory rates are 8% and 7% of their respective bases. What drives it. The share of telecom revenue that counts as licensed revenue (data centres and some ICT services fall outside), any change to the 8% rate, and the railway's 7% share. Benchmark. Lower is better; every point saved drops straight to EBITDA. A cut in the licence fee to 6% would add about ₹23 crore a year (illustrative). Peers. All licensed carriers pay the licence fee; only RailTel pays a railway share (POWERTEL and GAILTEL have their own arrangements with their parents, not disclosed). Definition trap. The FY25 licence fee is ₹99.52 crore in the audited P&L note but ₹88.56 crore in the board's report, probably accrual versus cash. Use the P&L note. And "exclusive right of way" does not mean free: these levies are the price.

KPI 13 · Dividend payout against the DIPAM floor

What it is. Dividends as a share of profit and of net worth, compared with the government's minimum. Formula. Dividend ÷ PAT; dividend ÷ net worth; floor = higher of 30% × PAT or 4% × net worth (since November 2024). Worked example. FY26: ₹104.30 ÷ ₹346.32 = 30.1% of PAT; ₹104.30 ÷ ₹2,262 = 4.61% of net worth. The 4% leg would have required ₹90.5 crore, so the 30% leg bound. FY24 under the old rule: 37.2% of PAT, 4.96% of net worth. What drives it. The rule, not the board: payout ran at 37–58% of profit while the 5%-of-net-worth leg bound (FY18–FY24), then fell to about 30% when DIPAM cut that leg to 4%. Benchmark. Above 40% good for a CPSE, 30–40% average, at the 30% floor weak. Peers. Other railway PSUs follow the same rule. Definition trap. The FY22 payout is 26.9% on the annual report's basis and 37% on screener's, because of how interim and final dividends are assigned to years. A falling payout ratio here signals a rule change, not a change in management's generosity.

Putting the KPIs together: three diagnostics

Single KPIs mislead; combinations diagnose. Three pairings are worth running every quarter.

Mix against margin. If the telecom share of revenue rises but company EBITDA margin does not, something inside telecom is wrong: bandwidth prices are falling faster than volume grows, or data-centre revenue is coming from low-margin resale. If the telecom share falls and the margin holds, project margins must be improving. In FY26 the telecom share fell four points and the margin held at about 15% because the telecom segment's own margin rose to 26.7% on the CCTV bandwidth catch-up. That combination will not repeat every year, which is why the Q1 FY27 telecom margin of 19.2% matters more than the FY26 full-year figure.

Order inflow against receivables. A rising book-to-bill with stable receivable days is healthy growth. A rising book-to-bill with rising receivable days and rising guarantees is growth financed by the balance sheet. FY26 was the second kind: book-to-bill 1.72×, receivable days up from 166 to 175, unbilled work aged one to two years up fourfold, guarantees doubled. The test for FY27 is whether the March 2027 balance sheet shows receivable days falling while the book still grows.

Profit against cash against dividend. Profit after tax, minus the rise in receivables and unbilled revenue, plus the rise in supplier payables, should roughly equal operating cash flow; capex comes off that; the dividend should be covered by what is left. When it is not, cash falls, as it did between March 2024 and March 2026. A sustainable position for this business looks something like CFO of at least 75% of EBITDA, capex near 6% of revenue, and free cash flow covering the 30% dividend. On FY26 numbers that would have meant CFO of about ₹480 crore rather than ₹316 crore, a gap of roughly 14 days of receivables (illustrative).

Run against the reference peers, these diagnostics also explain the market's ranking. The Kavach equipment makers pair high margins with high returns and, in HBL's case, modest receivable days, so their profit converts to cash. The railway construction PSUs pair thin margins with short receivable days. RailTel pairs a high-margin annuity with a thin-margin contractor and long receivable days, and so sits between the two groups on most measures. Definition traps, collected

  • "Turnover" is not revenue. The headline ₹4,328 crore includes other and finance income; revenue from operations is ₹4,277 crore. Use the latter for margins.
  • Segment results are after depreciation. Telecom's 26.7% is EBIT; its EBITDA is about 39%. Projects carry almost no depreciation.
  • Three EBITDA figures. Company ₹658 crore (15.22%), screener ₹638 crore (14.9%), P&L-derived ₹636.5 crore. State which one you use.
  • "Exceptional" means ECL. It recurs every year; treat it as an operating cost.
  • Order-book dates. ₹8,563 crore is 31 December 2025, not FY25. The annual report's "exceeding ₹10,400 crore as on date" is a stale year-end statement; ₹11,747 crore (31 July 2026) is the latest.
  • Consolidated versus standalone. RailTel reports standalone only; screener's consolidated page is frozen at September 2023 (P/E 39.2, ROCE 16.2%). FY21–FY22 segment data are consolidated, including RailTel Enterprises.
  • P/E. Screener's printed 19.8× does not reconcile; ₹229 ÷ ₹10.78 trailing EPS = 21.2×.

How to read a corridor carrier's quarterly results in ten minutes

RailTel files a results document and, a day or two later, a call transcript; there is no investor presentation. This checklist pulls the important numbers in the order that tells you most, and flags the red lines.

  1. Revenue by segment (minute 1). Find telecom and project revenue in the segment table. Compute the telecom share. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter (Q4 is always project-heavy). Red flag: telecom revenue falling year on year.
  2. Segment results (minute 2). Compute telecom and project EBIT margins. Telecom should be roughly 19–27% in a normal quarter; projects 4–5%. Red flag: projects below 3.5%, or telecom below 18% without an explanation.
  3. The pure-telecom lines (minute 3, from the transcript). NLD, ISP and IP-1 figures are given verbally. Red flag: NLD down year on year for a second quarter, which would mean renewals are slipping or prices are falling faster.
  4. Exceptional items (minute 4). This is the ECL charge. Compare profit before tax before and after it. Red flag: ECL above ₹10 crore in a quarter, or a rising trend.
  5. Profit after tax (minute 5). Check whether PAT growth is below revenue growth and why: mix, ECL, lower other income.
  6. Order inflow and order book (minute 6). Note the date the book is quoted for. Compute inflow ÷ quarterly revenue. Red flag: inflow below revenue for two quarters.
  7. Railway share and Kavach (minute 7). Railway share of the book; Kavach revenue booked; the OEM approval status. Red flag: Kavach revenue still zero by Q3 FY27.
  8. Balance sheet, half-yearly (minute 8). In September and March results, pull trade receivables, unbilled revenue and cash. Compute receivable days on trailing revenue. Red flag: telecom receivables not falling back from ₹1,287 crore; cash below ₹400 crore.
  9. New lines (minute 9). Data-centre revenue run-rate (₹75 crore a quarter needed for the ₹300 crore guidance), RailWire subscribers and ARPU, Noida progress. Red flag: DC run-rate below ₹60 crore a quarter.
  10. Guidance and dividend (minute 10). Check whether the 20–25% growth guidance and ₹3,000–3,500 crore project guidance are repeated, and the interim dividend against 30% of profit. Red flag: guidance withdrawn without a project-timing explanation.
In plain terms

When RailTel reports, start with how much of revenue came from the fibre business and whether bandwidth sales grew. Then look at the provision for slow payers and the order book's date. Twice a year, check whether receivables are falling. Those five numbers tell you more than the headline profit.

The market prices RailTel with the railway PSUs, not with telecom. On EV/EBITDA, though, RailTel's 10.4–11.6× is in line with Tata Communications and Airtel (11.7–11.8×) and below every railway PSU. The two metrics disagree because RailTel's profit after tax is depressed relative to its EBITDA by depreciation on the network, and because the telecom groups carry debt that lifts their EV.

Three cautions apply. First, size: RailTel's ₹7,349 crore market value is about 0.5% of the combined reference set including Airtel, and about 2.7% excluding it; Airtel's ₹11.8 lakh crore valuation reflects a mobile business RailTel does not have. Second, basis: RailTel is standalone and its peers consolidated; for RailTel the two are identical in substance because it has no subsidiaries, but screener's stale consolidated page would give wildly different ratios. Third, the reference peers each match only one of RailTel's businesses. Tata Communications and Airtel resemble the telecom segment, HBL and Kernex a supplier to the project segment, IRCON and RVNL the project segment's railway customers, ITI the project segment at its worst. None resembles the whole.

In plain terms

RailTel grows like a telecom company, earns returns like RITES, collects cash slowly like a contractor and is priced like a railway PSU. On an enterprise-value basis it is not expensive against telecom peers; on a price-to-earnings basis it is in line with the railway group. Which group investors choose to compare it with is, in effect, the valuation debate.

The most useful single reference row may be ITI's. It shows what a state-owned telecom integrator looks like when the project business dominates, margins fall to 2–3% and receivables reach 486 days: losses at the operating level, profit from other income, and a valuation that depends on government support rather than earnings. RailTel is a long way from that, but the direction of its receivables in FY26 is the reason investors keep the comparison in mind. The opposite reference is HBL: certified intellectual property, a 34% margin, 71 debtor days and a 59% ROCE. RailTel will not become HBL, but each step it takes towards owning more of what it sells (data-centre services, managed security, fibre leasing) moves it in that direction.

China built a signalling champion; Britain concessioned its trackside fibre; India listed a carrier. Wireless, satellites and the railway's own networks are the threats.

Most railways in the world own spare fibre along their tracks. Very few run a licensed, listed telecom operator to sell it. Comparing RailTel with its foreign counterparts clarifies what is unusual about the Indian model and which alternatives it could adopt.

The Chinese case shows the scale a national signalling market can reach: CRSC's revenue of roughly ₹45,000 crore is about ten times RailTel's, and it owns the designs it installs. India's equivalent is not RailTel but the Kavach equipment makers, which is why HBL and Kernex earn margins closer to CRSC's than RailTel does. The British case is more directly relevant. In plain terms

Other countries either built a signalling champion that owns the technology, as China did, or invited private partners to sell their railway fibre for a share of the revenue, as Britain did. India built a state carrier that does a bit of both but owns the technology for neither. Britain's model is the obvious one for RailTel to copy for its spare fibre.

Technology and disruption

  • Fixed-wireless and 5G substitution. Jio and Airtel's 5G home broadband has grown fast. The managing director calls retail broadband "very heated" with ARPU "continuously under pressure". RailWire's ~₹474 ARPU and partner model are vulnerable in towns where wireless arrives first.
  • Satellite broadband. Starlink, OneWeb and Kuiper threaten rural fixed broadband, but Starlink is now a RailTel NLD customer for backhaul and gateway connectivity, and the National Frequency Allocation Plan 2025 (effective 30 December 2025) allocates bands for low-earth-orbit services. Net effect: mildly positive for NLD, negative for RailWire in remote areas (our judgement).
  • The railway's own networks. Kavach is laying 2×48-fibre cable and 1,721 towers under railway budgets, and LTE train radio on 700 MHz would add a dedicated wireless network. If zones own and run these, RailTel's role in railway communications narrows to contractor; if RailTel operates them, it widens to annuity. The contract structure is not public.
  • Hyperscaler cloud for government. MeitY empanels AWS, Azure and Google for government workloads. RailTel's cloud niche rests on sovereignty and air-gap requirements; the managing director describes its railway AI work as "completely air-gapped… not the public cloud".
  • AI and GPU capacity. RailTel says it is "in the process" of GPU services and small language models for the railway. A few megawatts of GPU capacity cost hundreds of crore, beyond its ₹241–300 crore capex budget without partners.
  • Legacy service obsolescence. Older SDH and TDM leased-line services migrate to Ethernet and MPLS at lower prices, a structural drag on NLD revenue per circuit.
  • Cyber-security. As a government carrier and host, a breach would be a reputational risk. RailTel's RailWire threat dashboard processed "over 4.58 million threat flows with a false-positive rate of 0.02%, protecting over 2.34 lakh subscribers from IP-level blacklisting" (FY26 annual report); that is defensive capability, not an incident.

The common thread is that the corridor itself is not easily disrupted (no wireless network replaces long-haul fibre), but the services sold over it are. Retail broadband faces wireless; bandwidth faces price deflation and migration to cheaper technologies; cloud faces hyperscalers. The defences are the same in every case: sovereignty requirements, security clearance, government relationships and a physically distinct route. Those defences are strongest where the customer is the state, which is why RailTel's telecom business has held its margin while its prices fall. Bull vs bear: a scarce corridor against a slow-paying customer

The case for the sub-sector rests on an irreplaceable asset and growing state demand; the case against rests on price, cash and mix.

Both cases below are about the sub-sector as expressed through its only listed company. They are not recommendations; they set out what each side must believe and what evidence would settle the argument.

The bull case

  • An irreplaceable corridor. 63,000+ route km of fibre on an exclusive track right of way, away from highway cuts, with private demand for non-road routes rising on 5G backhaul and AI data-centre interconnect.
  • A profitable core. Telecom earns ~39% EBITDA and ~84% of segment EBITDA; a fibre-leasing partnership on the Project Reach model would add revenue without capital.
  • Visible demand. A ₹11,747 Cr order book (2.65× revenue), FY26 book-to-bill of 1.72×, and FY27 guidance of 20–25% growth; non-railway government ICT up 41.5% in FY26.
  • New lines with better margins. Data-centre revenue guided from ₹202 Cr to ~₹300 Cr (FY27) and ~₹500 Cr (FY28), with sovereignty rules (DPDP, CERT-In) pushing government workloads to domestic hosts.
  • Railway safety spend. S&T works budget up 35% in two years to ₹7,500 Cr; ₹1,007 Cr of Kavach orders and a possible role operating the railway's new spare fibre.
  • Undemanding valuation. ~21× trailing and ~17× an illustrative FY27, ~10.4× EV/EBITDA net of cash, near the illustrative sum of the parts; no debt.

The bear case

  • Mix keeps diluting. Projects are 65% of revenue at ~4% EBIT; every rupee of growth guided for FY27 is mostly project rupees, so profit grows slower than revenue.
  • The annuity is not contracted. Only ₹184 Cr of telecom lease receipts are non-cancellable; NLD grew 2.9% in FY26 and fell 4.6% in Q1 FY27 on delayed renewals and price pressure.
  • Cash does not follow profit. 175 receivable days, ₹898 Cr unbilled, receivables over a year old up 38%, FCF ₹44 Cr (FY26) and −₹70 Cr (FY25), cash down from ~₹1,006 Cr to ~₹777 Cr.
  • Guarantees and supplier credit are stretched. BGs doubled to ₹1,482 Cr with headroom cut to ₹190 Cr; MSME 45-day payment rules threaten the back-to-back float.
  • Kavach margin goes elsewhere. OEMs earn 34–35%; RailTel's partner awaits final RDSO approval and few new tenders are expected in FY27.
  • Sector and supply overhangs. Railway-PSU de-rating, a higher repo rate (5.50%), a possible government offer for sale after the MPS exemption lapsed in August 2026, and a dividend fixed at the floor.

What each side has to believe

The bull case needs the telecom share of revenue to stop falling and, ideally, to rise; that requires data centres to deliver close to guidance, NLD to return to mid-single-digit growth as renewals are processed, and some form of fibre-leasing deal. It also needs receivables to come down, so that profit growth turns into cash and the guarantee constraint loosens. None of these needs heroic assumptions; all of them need evidence that has not yet appeared.

The bear case needs the opposite: projects to keep taking share, bandwidth prices to keep falling faster than volume grows, the government's payment cycle to stay at six months or worse, and suppliers to demand faster payment. Under those conditions profit would grow at perhaps two-thirds of revenue growth, cash would keep falling and the market would continue to price the company as a contractor. The bear case also notes that the railway is building its own fibre and that tower exclusivity has already gone, so the corridor's scarcity value may erode at the edges.
