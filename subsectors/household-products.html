Indian home care: crude costs, a price war, a lost licence

A complete guide to the business of keeping Indian homes clean and mosquito-free: detergents, dishwash, household insecticides, cleaners and air care. 00

The one-page view: a brands business with crude oil in every bottle

Home care looks like a sleepy staple. In 2026 it behaved like a chemicals business, and the smallest branded player took the hardest hit.

Indian home care is the basket of products a household buys to wash clothes, clean dishes, keep mosquitoes away and keep floors, toilets and air fresh. It is worth roughly ₹75,000–85,000 crore a year at manufacturer prices (an indicative estimate built up category by category; no industry body publishes a total). Almost every household buys it, most buy it every few weeks, and a single company, Hindustan Unilever (HUL), sells more than a quarter of it. HUL's Home Care segment alone booked ₹23,672 crore of revenue in FY26 at a 19.1% segment margin.

The NSE 750 has only one listed company whose business is mostly this basket: Jyothy Labs (JYOTHYLAB), the maker of Ujala, Exo, Henko, Mr. White and Maxo. Fabric care and home care were 89% of its ₹2,944 crore of FY26 revenue. That makes Jyothy the cleanest window on the economics of the category, and this report uses it as the lens. But the report is about the whole sub-sector: the giant diversified companies (HUL, Godrej Consumer, Dabur) that run large home-care divisions, the unlisted economy detergent makers (RSPL with Ghadi, Nirma), the multinational subsidiaries (P&G Home Products, Reckitt) and the upstream chemical makers that sell them their raw materials.

Seven forces define the sub-sector in late 2026, and the rest of this report is built around them.

  • The West Asia input shock. The war that began on 28 February 2026 pushed crude above $100 a barrel. LABSA, the active cleaning ingredient in Indian detergents and dishwash bars, rose 60–65% between January and May; plastic packaging rose 50–55% in March and April. Jyothy's gross margin fell from 48.0% to 38.5% and its EBITDA margin from 16.5% to 8.4% between Q1 FY26 and Q1 FY27. Value moved upstream: Tamilnadu Petroproducts, an Indian maker of LAB (the precursor to LABSA), lifted its EBITDA margin from 11.2% to 15.9% in the same quarter.
  • A price war in dishwash that started before the war. Jyothy's dishwash segment margin fell quarter by quarter through FY26, from 20.2% to 17.2% to 14.5% to 10.2%, while crude was still cheap. HUL's Vim leads the category; Godrej Consumer has now entered with Spic and Rizz.
  • The Pril and Fa licence exit. Henkel did not renew Jyothy's 15-year licences for Pril dishwash liquid and Fa deodorants beyond 31 May 2026. Jyothy is in court over the exit terms and is building Exo as its owned dishwash platform. The lost revenue is not disclosed; our indicative back-solve puts it at ₹200–270 crore a year, and that range depends on an assumption about trade wind-down.
  • The leader trading margin for volume. HUL Home Care's underlying sales growth went from 4% in FY26 to 14% in Q1 FY27 and 22% in Q2 FY27, while its segment margin went from 19.1% to 17% to 16%. A price-follower's margin recovery now depends on the leader's pricing as much as on crude.
  • GST 2.0's asymmetry. The September 2025 rate cut took toilet soap, toothpaste and shampoo to 5% but left every home-care line at 18%: detergents, laundry soap, scouring powder, insecticides and air fresheners. Agarbatti also went to 5%, which gives unregistered mosquito incense sticks a 13-point tax edge over legal insecticides.
  • Rural demand and a deficient monsoon. The 2026 monsoon delivered 87% of its long-period average, and only 76% over the South Peninsula, Jyothy's home market. A panel tracker (NIQ, reported second-hand) saw household-and-personal-care volume fall 4.3% in April–June 2026 even as companies reported volume growth.
  • The economy-detergent leaders stumbled. RSPL (Ghadi) lost 21.6% of its revenue in FY25 after a product recall and was put on a Negative outlook by CRISIL. Nirma is carving its consumer and chemical businesses into a private vehicle while its US soda-ash arm sits in Chapter 11. Branded mid-tier players have picked up volume in powders and bars.

Put together, these forces say something uncomfortable about a category usually filed under "defensive staples". Home care is a brands business with a commodity cost base. Between half and 60% of a formulator's inputs are linked to crude, either directly (surfactants, solvents, plastic packaging) or through freight and power. In a normal year brands earn a wide spread over those inputs: Jyothy's gross margin sat between 47% and 51% for most of FY24–FY26. When inputs jump faster than prices can follow, the spread collapses, and how far it collapses depends on two things. The first is how much of a company's revenue is exposed: about 90% at Jyothy on management's own revenue-weighted measure, against roughly 37% at HUL, where home care sits beside foods, beauty and personal care. The second is who sets prices. HUL leads; Jyothy, by its own description, matches competitors "SKU to SKU".

The result is a sub-sector where the single listed specialist trades at 24.3 times trailing earnings and 20.7 times FY26 earnings, against 40.8 times for HUL and 39.8 times for Godrej Consumer, and where its share price is down 41% over a year. Jyothy's board meets on 4 November 2026 to approve its Q2 FY27 (Sep-26) results and to consider a share buyback. Those results will be the first clean read on whether the margin trough was Q1 FY27, as management implied, or later.

In plain terms

Every packet of detergent contains crude oil, in the cleaning chemical and in the plastic pouch. When oil jumped in 2026, the cost of making that packet jumped by about a third in a few months, and nobody could raise the shelf price by a third. The big, diversified companies absorbed it because detergents are only part of what they sell. Jyothy, which sells little else, saw half its operating profit disappear in one quarter.

Three things make this a sub-sector worth understanding beyond one stock. First, it is the cleanest test in Indian consumer goods of the argument between "brand" and "commodity": the 2026 data show a supplier's margin rising while its branded customer's margin halved, which is not what brand power is supposed to look like. Second, it shows how market leadership works in a mature staple: HUL can choose between margin and share, and when it chooses share, every follower pays. Third, it shows how policy shapes categories in ways that never reach the headlines, from a tax schedule that lets unregistered mosquito sticks undercut regulated ones, to a rainfall map that put the weakest monsoon over the South, where Jyothy's biggest brands live.

01

How home care makes money: from benzene to the bathroom shelf

A low-capital, high-frequency business that turns cheap chemicals into branded habits, and keeps the spread only as long as the chemicals stay cheap.

A home-care company does four things. It buys commodity chemicals and packaging. It mixes them into a formula that cleans, whitens, kills insects or smells pleasant. It puts that formula into a pack with a brand on it, usually at a fixed price point such as ₹10 or ₹99. And it pushes the pack through a chain of distributors into millions of small shops, supermarkets and, increasingly, quick-commerce dark stores. The money is made in the gap between what the chemicals cost per kilogram and what the household pays per kilogram after tax and trade margins. Manufacturing is simple and cheap: Jyothy runs 23 plants, and its fixed assets of ₹504 crore support ₹2,944 crore of revenue, a fixed-asset turnover of about 5.8 times. The hard and expensive parts are the brand (advertising and promotion run at 7–11% of revenue for branded players) and the distribution system.

The products, aisle by aisle

The basket is more varied than "detergents". Each aisle has its own chemistry, its own leader and its own price ladder, and a company can be a leader in one aisle and a minor player in the next. Jyothy is the extreme case: it owns about 84% of the fabric-whitener market with Ujala Supreme (screener's company profile), is the number-two dishwash bar brand with Exo, is number two in mosquito coils by volume with Maxo, and is a small challenger in detergent powders and liquids.

Two features of the basket explain most of its economics. The first is repeat frequency. A household runs out of detergent or dishwash bar every few weeks, so brands are rebought constantly and switching costs are low. That makes advertising and shelf presence matter more than product innovation, and it makes price points sticky: a ₹10 bar stays a ₹10 bar, so companies adjust grams rather than rupees when costs move. The second is the price ladder. Every aisle runs from an economy tier (Nirma and Ghadi powders at a fraction of the premium price per kilogram) through a mid-tier (Rin, Henko) to a premium tier (Surf excel, Ariel, matic liquids). Different companies make their money on different rungs, which is why the economy makers can earn margins similar to HUL's from the opposite end of the ladder.

Where the value sits along the chain

In a normal year, most of the value is captured by the brand owner. Retailers keep roughly 8–12% of the shelf price in general trade, distributors 4–6% (indicative industry ranges), modern trade and quick commerce more once listing fees and promotions are counted. The chemical makers upstream earn thin, cyclical margins. In 2026 that ordering flipped for a while. When LAB was scarce because West Asian supply was disrupted, the domestic LAB makers could raise prices faster than detergent makers could pass them on. Licensors sit off to the side: Jyothy paid Henkel just ₹4.57 crore of royalty in FY26 for its licensed brands, a trivial cash cost, while P&G Home Products in India paid its parent ₹410 crore in FY25.

The segment re-cut matters for anyone comparing companies. From Q1 FY27 Jyothy reports three segments: Fabric Care (including the laundry-service business), Home Care (dishwash, household insecticides, incense and floor and toilet cleaners) and Personal Care. Restated FY26 figures are Fabric ₹1,409 crore, Home ₹1,215 crore and Personal ₹321 crore. HUL's "Home Care" covers fabric wash and household care but has no insecticides or air fresheners. Godrej Consumer's "Home Care" is mostly insecticides and air fresheners with almost no detergent powder. To compare Jyothy with HUL's Home Care segment you have to add Jyothy's Fabric Care and Home Care together: ₹2,624 crore in FY26.

In plain terms

Making detergent is easy; selling it is hard. The plant is cheap, so the money a home-care company earns comes from the brand that lets it charge more than the chemicals cost, and from the trucks and salesmen that get the pack into a village shop. When the chemicals get dearer, the brand has to prove it can push the price up. In 2026 most brands could not push it up fast enough.

02

A ₹80,000 crore basket that grows with the household budget

No official market size exists, so the total has to be built up aisle by aisle. Fabric care is more than half of it; household insecticides are the only aisle with a published number.

Indian home care has no single authoritative market size. Industry bodies publish figures for a few aisles, panel trackers sell theirs, and companies quote the bits that suit them. Building the total up category by category gives an indicative ₹75,000–85,000 crore at manufacturer value (excluding GST), roughly US$8–9 billion. Two numbers in that build are anchored to a named source: the household-insecticide market of ₹8,138 crore in 2025 (up from ₹7,147 crore in 2023, according to an EY study for the Home Insect Control Association, as reported by The Week; the ₹ figure itself is secondary), and the liquid-dishwash market of about ₹3,000 crore, which Godrej Consumer cited in its Q2 FY27 presentation when it launched Rizz. The rest is triangulation from company revenues and known shares.

Three things stand out. Fabric care is the profit pool that decides the sub-sector, at well over half the total. Jyothy's main-wash detergent business, at roughly ₹1,000–1,100 crore a year (36% of its Q4 FY26 revenue, annualised), is about 2–3% of it. Second, the categories Jyothy dominates are small: the whitener aisle is about ₹300–350 crore, which is why a near-monopoly in it does not make Jyothy a large company. An earlier estimate of ₹600–900 crore for whitener double-counted Jyothy's conditioners and should not be used. Third, household insecticides, at ₹8,138 crore, is a mid-sized aisle with a large illegal fringe that the legal number excludes (Section 11).

How fast it grows

Through a full cycle the category grows at about 7–9% a year in nominal terms, made of roughly 3–5% volume and 3–4% price and mix (indicative, from HUL and Jyothy multi-year growth). FY26 was well below that. HUL Home Care's underlying sales growth was only 4% and its reported revenue rose 3.1%, because the leader cut prices in the deflationary first half while volumes grew at a high single digit. Jyothy grew 3.5% in value on 6% volume. The first half of FY27 has been a pricing-led rebound: HUL Home Care +14% and +22% in Q1 and Q2, Godrej Consumer's standalone Home Care +19% in Q2. Over a longer arc, Jyothy's revenue grew 7.6% a year from FY22 to FY26 and 7% a year over ten years (screener).

India's place in the world

India is a big market by tonnage and a modest one by value, because the price per kilogram is low. The whole Indian home-care basket is smaller than Unilever's global Home Care division (€11.6 billion in 2025, about ₹1.28 lakh crore at an indicative ₹110 per euro) and about a quarter of P&G's Fabric & Home Care segment (US$30.3 billion in the year to June 2026, about ₹2.88 lakh crore at ₹94.9 per dollar). HUL's Home Care segment is a large business by Indian standards and roughly a fifth of Unilever Home Care's global turnover. Jyothy's fabric and home care revenue is about 1% of P&G's segment.

Exports barely matter. Finished home-care products are bulky and cheap per kilogram, so they are made close to the consumer. Jyothy exports to 19 countries but does not disclose the value; Godrej Consumer serves Indonesia and Africa through local subsidiaries. The industry is domestic, which means its fortunes rest on Indian household budgets, Indian rainfall and Indian tax policy, while its costs are set by global oil and chemical markets.

In plain terms

Nobody officially measures how much Indians spend on home care, so the ₹80,000 crore figure is a careful sum of educated guesses. The useful facts are the shape, not the total: washing clothes is more than half the money, mosquito control is about a tenth, and the little blue whitener bottle that made Jyothy famous is a few hundred crore. Owning a small aisle completely does not make you big.

03

Demand, the monsoon and the volume puzzle

Everyone already buys detergent. Growth comes from upgrading formats, using more per wash and moving to new channels, and in 2026 the panel data and the company data told different stories.

Home care is a category with near-total penetration. Every household cleans clothes and dishes with something, branded or not, and the EY–HICA study puts mosquito-control use at 92–99% of urban and 64–73% of rural households (secondary). So growth does not come from first-time buyers. It comes from five sources: more households (household formation runs ahead of population growth as families split); more use per household as incomes rise; trading up the price ladder from economy to mid-tier to premium; switching formats, which usually raises spend per wash; and price.

Format migration is the real growth engine

Three format shifts are running at once. In laundry, powders are giving way to liquids as more households buy semi-automatic and fully automatic washing machines. Jyothy's liquid detergents roughly doubled in FY26, and HUL's annual report describes "rapid format upgradation to liquids". In dishwash, bars are giving way to liquids: Godrej Consumer describes liquid dishwash as a ~₹3,000 crore category growing at a double-digit rate with only mid-teens household penetration. In mosquito control, coils are giving way to liquid vaporisers and incense sticks; at Jyothy the electric (liquid vaporiser) plus aerosol and racquet share of household insecticides reached about 55% in FY26 from 50%. Each shift raises revenue per household. The catch, which Jyothy's chairperson spelled out on the Q1 FY27 call, is that liquids have become the main battlefield: after a year of promotions, liquid detergents sell for less than powders per wash, "be it even a premium liquid", and at a lower margin.

Rural incomes and the 2026 monsoon

Economy detergent bars and powders, dishwash bars and mosquito coils are rural-heavy products, so rural incomes matter, and in India rural incomes still track the monsoon. The 2026 south-west monsoon was poor. The India Meteorological Department's end-of-season statement (30 September 2026) put all-India rainfall at 759.4 mm, 87% of the long-period average, the fourth-lowest since 2001. The regional picture was worse for the South: the South Peninsula received 76% of normal, its fourth-lowest since 1901, and East and North-East India 74%, the lowest on record. Seventeen of 36 meteorological subdivisions, covering 42% of the country's area, were deficient.

The timing matters for reading company commentary. Jyothy's "rural resilient, urban subdued" description covered April–June, before the weak August. The rural effect of a poor monsoon usually shows up a quarter or two later, through lower farm incomes in the October–March period. For a South-skewed company such as Jyothy, Q3 and Q4 FY27 rural demand in Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh is a specific risk. A weak monsoon also cuts both ways for insecticides: less standing water can mean fewer mosquitoes, though Godrej Consumer reported share gains "despite weak monsoons, led by an intensifying El Niño", and extended heat delayed Jyothy's Q1 season.

The volume puzzle: companies grow, the panel shrinks

The most confusing number in the 2026 data is volume. These numbers cannot be reconciled naively, but four mechanisms explain most of the gap. First, company volume is sell-in to distributors, measured in tonnes; NIQ measures what consumers buy. Quick commerce is a mixed blessing. It lets premium liquids, conditioners and new launches reach urban households without the years of distributor building GT requires, and it rewards brands with strong search visibility. In plain terms

When a company says "our volumes grew 5%" and a market tracker says "volumes fell 4%", both can be right. The company counts the tonnes it shipped, including extra grams it added to each pack and share it took from weaker rivals; the tracker counts what households actually bought across the whole market. In 2026 the branded players grew by taking business from others, not because India was washing more clothes.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes, in a good year and a bad one

Half of every rupee buys chemicals and plastic. When that half grows to 60%, everything below it gets squeezed, and advertising is the first line to be cut.

The cost structure of a branded home-care company is simple and top-heavy. Materials, meaning raw materials and packaging net of inventory changes, take about half of revenue in a normal year. Staff, advertising and promotion (A&P), and "other expenses" (freight, contract conversion, power, field-sales costs, royalty, CSR) take most of the rest. Operating profit before depreciation (EBITDA) is what is left: 15–18% for a branded specialist in a good year. Depreciation is small because plants are cheap, and a cash-rich company earns some interest on its treasury. Jyothy publishes enough line items to build this table for three very different periods.

The inputs and what drives them

The materials line is a basket of perhaps a dozen inputs, but four dominate. Linear alkylbenzene (LAB), sulphonated into LABSA, is the single largest: it is the active cleaning ingredient in detergent powders, bars, many liquids and dishwash bars. Plastic packaging (HDPE and PP bottles, multi-layer pouches) is 15–20% of Jyothy's material purchases and follows naphtha and polymer prices. Palm-derived soap noodles feed laundry and toilet soaps; they follow Malaysian and Indonesian palm oil. How the other archetypes split ₹100

Jyothy's split is typical of a branded specialist, but not of everyone. The leader, HUL Home Care, does not publish a gross margin for its segment; an indicative structure would be a 45–50% gross margin, A&P of 9–11% and a reported segment EBIT of 16–19%. In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 Jyothy collected in a normal year, about ₹50 went on chemicals and plastic and about ₹17–18 was left as operating profit. In April–June 2026 the chemicals and plastic took about ₹62, so only about ₹8 was left, even after the company spent ₹2 less on advertising. Staff and freight costs do not shrink when inputs rise, so the whole increase lands on profit.

Two further points complete the picture. First, the gross-margin decline began well before the war. Jyothy's gross margin fell in every one of the eight quarters to Q1 FY27, and 4 of the 11.7 points it lost from Q1 FY25 to Q1 FY27 had already gone by Q4 FY26, through price cuts and extra grammage in dishwash and a mix shift toward lower-margin liquids. The war then took 6.7 points in one quarter. Second, the shock passes through with a lag in both directions. The CFO said higher-cost purchases and contracted inventory would "continue to flow through Q2", with relief "more meaningfully from October", and that the Q2 FY27 margin would be "not materially lower" than Q1's 8.4%. Crude in the bottle: the detergent cost stack and the LAB–soda ash chain Deep dive

A kilogram of detergent is mostly salt-based fillers held together by a petrochemical. When that petrochemical is scarce, its makers set the price, not the brands that sell it.

The single most useful thing to understand about Indian home care is what is inside a pack of detergent powder, because that formula decides who wins in a cost shock. A typical mid-tier powder contains 15–20% LABSA, the surfactant that lifts dirt; 25–30% soda ash, which softens water and boosts cleaning; around 40% sodium sulphate or similar filler, which adds bulk and helps the powder flow; and a few per cent of zeolite or phosphate builders, enzymes, optical brighteners and perfume. Economy powders use less LABSA and more filler; premium and machine ("matic") powders use more actives and enzymes. Detergent bars and dishwash bars are LABSA held in a calcite or filler matrix. Liquids swap fillers for water and use LABSA together with SLES, a second surfactant family made from ethylene oxide or palm-derived fatty alcohols.

Why LAB is the swing factor

LAB is not exotic, but its supply is concentrated. India has a handful of producers and imports the balance, much of it from West Asia. When the war began on 28 February 2026 and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted, two things happened at once: the feedstock cost rose with crude (Brent peaked near $118 a barrel before the 7 April ceasefire and then settled around $95), and physical supply tightened as vessels were delayed and allocations cut. Jyothy's CFO described crude as having "swung from … 60, 65 to upwards of 100" and LABSA as up 60–65% from January to early May. He added that the basket inflation of 30–35% "has never happened in the recent memory of the company".

The clearest evidence of where the value went comes from the supplier side. Tamilnadu Petroproducts, a listed LAB producer, reported Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹784 crore (+67%) and an EBITDA margin of 15.9%, up from 11.2% a year earlier. In the same quarter Jyothy's EBITDA margin fell from 16.5% to 8.4%. Nirma, which makes both LAB and soda ash, sits on both sides of that spread. For a formulator that buys LAB, "brand power" did not protect against a supplier with pricing power in a shortage. The commodity-chemicals report (Indian commodity chemicals) covers the LAB and soda-ash producers in more depth.

Soda ash: the input that went the other way

Soda ash softened the blow for powder makers. Roughly 15–18% of Indian soda-ash demand goes into detergents. Global supply has been heavy: Chinese light-grade soda ash traded around RMB 1,156 a tonne in June 2026, a deep downturn, and imports from China, the US and Turkey pushed Indian prices down. India has a minimum import price of ₹20,108 a tonne and a safeguard investigation due to conclude around 16 November 2026. For a detergent maker that buys soda ash, such as Jyothy or HUL, cheap soda ash is good news and a safeguard duty would be a small cost increase. For an integrated maker such as Nirma or RSPL, weak soda ash hurts the chemicals side: CRISIL cites "softening of realisations in the soda ash segment" as one reason RSPL's EBITDA margin fell to 13.1% in FY25. Nirma's US soda-ash subsidiary, Searles Valley Minerals, filed for Chapter 11 on 15 June 2026.

Why prices could not follow

Three features of the category kept prices from following costs. Price points are sticky: much of the volume sells in ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20 packs, and moving a ₹10 pack to ₹12 loses shoppers, so companies cut grams instead, slowly. Leadership is concentrated: no follower can price up until HUL does, and HUL priced cautiously because it was pursuing volume (Section 9). And the shock was expected to be temporary: after the 7 April ceasefire, nobody wanted to be the brand that took a 15% increase just before input prices fell back. Jyothy took about 3% in Q1 and 4–4.5% cumulatively, with Personal Care up 9–10%, Fabric about 5%, and dishwash "competitive", a polite word for flat.

The sensitivity every analyst should carry

On revenue of about ₹3,000 crore, each percentage point of gross margin is worth about ₹30 crore of EBITDA, about ₹22 crore of net profit after tax, and about ₹0.60 of earnings per share on 36.7 crore shares (ILLUSTRATIVE, holding other costs constant). An indicative recovery path: if LABSA and packaging fall back about 25% from their peak in the second half of FY27 and Jyothy holds its 4–4.5% price increase, the gross margin could return to 44–46%; with A&P restored to about 8%, EBITDA margin would be around 12–14% in H2 FY27 and 15–16% in FY28. Management's own language is "H2 substantially better than H1" and a long-run "swing back to the historical margin levels". That recovery has two conditions: crude stays below its spring peak, and HUL does not use the relief to cut prices again.

In plain terms

A kilogram of washing powder is mostly cheap white minerals, held together by one oil-based chemical that does the cleaning. When that chemical's price jumped 60%, the cost of making the powder jumped by roughly a third. To keep earning the same profit per kilogram, companies would have needed to raise prices by about 17%; they raised them by 4–5%. The firms that make the chemical earned more; the firms that sell the powder earned less.

Management's response: fewer crude-linked inputs

Jyothy says it has been working "for the last 1, 1.5 years" on reducing crude-linked raw materials, and the chairperson called 2026 "a very big lesson learned and we've paid a huge price". The options are limited. Plant-based surfactants from coconut or palm swap crude risk for palm risk, and Jyothy already flags soap-noodle inflation from June 2026. Bio-surfactants (rhamnolipids, sophorolipids) are expensive and not yet at scale. Re-formulation to use less LABSA per wash, value engineering in packaging, and shifting the mix toward Personal Care (Margo soap, which also benefits from the 5% GST rate) are the near-term levers. None of these changes the basic fact that a detergent maker that does not make its own LAB is a price-taker on its largest input.

Distribution: the moat that still matters

The single biggest structural advantage in Indian home care is distribution reach, because a low-ticket, high-frequency product sells where it is stocked. HUL reaches more than 9 million outlets. Imports, China and the organised share

Finished home-care goods are barely traded: they are bulky and cheap per kilogram. The trade exposure is in inputs. LAB and LABSA come partly from West Asia; pyrethroid actives from China; polymer resins and perfumes from global suppliers. The 2026 war showed the risk of that exposure, and the weak rupee, near ₹95 to the dollar, added to it. China's influence is indirect: its soda-ash oversupply helped Indian detergent makers in 2026 even as its chemicals capacity pressured Indian chemical producers. Fabric care and dishwash are an estimated 75–85% organised and branded; the unorganised share is higher in bars and in insecticides, where unregistered incense sticks are the fastest-growing format of all.

In plain terms

HUL sells roughly nine rupees of home care for every rupee Jyothy sells in the same aisles. The big names you rarely see in stock-market tables, Ghadi, Nirma, Tide and Harpic's owner, sell far more than Jyothy does too. That matters because the price on the shelf is set by these giants. A smaller company can win an aisle the giants ignore, as Jyothy did with whitener, but where the giants fight, it follows.

Five ways to run a home-care business

The scale leader, the niche specialist, the integrated economy maker, the insecticide specialist and the digital newcomer earn similar margins in good years by completely different routes, and suffer differently in bad ones.

The companies in this sub-sector fall into five business models. The distinction matters because each model has a different answer to the three questions that decide home-care profits: who sets the price, how exposed the cost base is to crude, and what the company spends to defend its shelf.

1. The scale leader

HUL Home Care is the reference model. When it cut prices and added grammage in FY26, followers had to match; when it priced up cautiously in H1 FY27 while pursuing volume, followers could not price ahead of it. Its segment margin, 19.1% in FY26, is at or above most global home-care businesses. Its weakness is size: it cannot grow much faster than the market except by taking share, and in 2026 it chose to do exactly that.

2. The niche specialist

Jyothy built a ₹2,944 crore business by owning categories the giants under-invested in: fabric whitener (Ujala), dishwash bars (Exo), coils (Maxo), and a portfolio of licensed and acquired detergent brands from the 2011 Henkel India deal. The model is frugal (A&P of 7–9%), regionally deep (strongest in the South), cash-generative and capital-light. Its weaknesses showed in 2026. About 90% of revenue is crude-exposed home care, against roughly 37% at HUL. It is a price-follower in contested aisles. And its media weight is too small to protect pricing in a downturn. The same exposure works in reverse when costs fall: Jyothy's EBITDA margin went from 12.7% in FY23 to 17.4% in FY24 as commodities eased, a faster recovery than any diversified peer.

3. The integrated economy maker

RSPL and Nirma sell the cheapest branded detergents in India and still earned margins comparable to HUL's in good years. RSPL's EBITDA margin was 18.3% in FY23 and 19.2% in FY24 according to CRISIL. They do it by making their own soda ash (RSPL has 1,500 tonnes a day of capacity and is adding 750 tonnes a day for ₹800–1,000 crore; Nirma also makes LAB), placing plants close to heartland markets to cut freight, and spending very little on advertising. FY25 showed the limit of the model: integration protects cost but not volume. When Ghadi volumes fell after a product recall, RSPL's margin dropped to 13.1% on lower fixed-cost absorption and weaker soda-ash prices. Section 8 is a deep dive on these players.

4. The insecticide-centric specialist

Godrej Consumer's Home Care business is built around household insecticides (Good Knight, HIT) and air fresheners (aer). Insecticides reward a different set of skills: regulatory registration (every molecule and formulation needs approval under the Insecticides Act), molecule innovation (GCPL owns a patented in-house molecule, Renofluthrin), and an installed base of liquid-vaporiser machines that generate recurring refill sales. Margins are high and seasonal. GCPL is now using this platform to enter Jyothy's other aisles: Godrej Fab in liquid detergents, Spic and Rizz in dishwash.

5. The digital newcomer

Plant-based and "eco" detergent brands sold online, and private labels on quick-commerce platforms, are small and mostly loss-making. Their significance is that they raise the innovation bar (Jyothy's annual report notes that "founder-led, digital-first brands have raised innovation benchmarks") and that quick-commerce platforms can place their own labels next to national brands at a lower price.

The lesson from the five models is that margin level says little about resilience. RSPL and HUL Home Care earned similar margins in FY24 from opposite ends of the ladder; Jyothy was only a few points behind. What differs is what each model does when something goes wrong. The leader can choose to sacrifice margin for share and still earn 16%. The specialist cannot choose: it takes the cost shock in full and must follow the leader's pricing. The integrated economy maker is protected on input costs but exposed to its own brand problems. The insecticide specialist depends on weather and regulation more than on crude. A diversified FMCG company carries home care as one division among several, which is why the market pays it a higher multiple for the same category exposure (Section 15).

In plain terms

There are several ways to make good money selling detergent. Be so big that you set the price everyone else follows. Be the cheapest and make your own raw materials. Or find small corners the giants ignore. In good years all three earn similar margins. In bad years the giant chooses how much to suffer, the cheapest player is protected on costs, and the corner-finder simply suffers.

The bottom of the ladder: Ghadi, Nirma, Patanjali and the price ladder Deep dive

India's two largest economy-detergent makers spent 2025–26 in trouble. Part of the branded players' "volume boom" is business taken from them, and it will not last for ever.

The economy end of Indian laundry is where most of the tonnes are sold, and it is run by two family-owned, unlisted companies that most equity investors never look at. Nirma, founded by Karsanbhai Patel in 1969, created the economy powder market by selling detergent at a fraction of Surf's price; HUL's answer, Wheel, launched in 1987–88, became the standard business-school case of a "fighting brand". RSPL's Ghadi, launched in Kanpur in 1987, built the biggest economy franchise in North and Central India. Both companies earn their margins from backward integration into soda ash (and, for Nirma, LAB), from regional plants close to their markets, and from spending almost nothing on advertising. Both had a bad 2025–26.

RSPL: a recall, a 22% revenue fall and a Negative outlook

CRISIL's rating rationale of 23 September 2025 is the best public window into RSPL. Revenue fell from ₹6,701 crore in FY23 to ₹5,793 crore in FY24 (−13.6%), as Ghadi ran into product issues, and then to ₹4,543 crore in FY25 (−21.6%). The cause, in CRISIL's words, was a fall in Ghadi cake and powder volumes after a product recall and revamp, plus "increased competitive intensity in the laundry care segment". EBITDA margin, 18.3% in FY23 and 19.2% in FY24, dropped to 13.1% in FY25 (EBITDA ₹594 crore) on lower absorption of fixed costs and softer soda-ash prices, and net profit fell from ₹598 crore to ₹240 crore. Q1 FY26 was still below the prior year. CRISIL revised the outlook on its AA rating to Negative and expected FY26 to be "flattish", followed by 5–8% growth.

Patanjali: the ayurvedic outsider

Patanjali sells detergent powders and dishwash bars under its own brand, with ayurvedic and swadeshi positioning, mostly in the economy and lower-mid tiers. Where the volume went

The economy makers' troubles coincided with unusually strong volumes at branded players in exactly their aisles. HUL said in its Q1 and Q2 FY27 presentations that bars and powders "sustained their step-up", and in Q2 that fabric wash posted "strong double-digit growth across liquids, powders and bars". Jyothy's Fabric Care volumes rose 17.8% in Q4 FY26 and about 10% in Q1 FY27; in Q4 FY26 management described powders as in "marginal degrowth" as users moved to liquids, but by Q1 FY27 it reported double-digit growth in powders and bars. The most plausible reading is that part of the branded volume growth of FY26–H1 FY27 was share taken from a wounded economy leader, not category growth. That has two implications. It helps explain the gap between company volumes and the NIQ panel (Section 3). In plain terms

The cheapest detergents in India are made by two family companies that also make their own raw materials. Both had a rough two years: one recalled and relaunched its main brand and lost a fifth of its sales; the other is splitting itself up while its American chemical arm went bankrupt. Branded companies such as HUL and Jyothy picked up some of those customers. That helped their 2026 numbers, but it is a one-time gain, not a sign that India is suddenly washing more clothes.

For the investor, the economy end matters for a reason that goes beyond share. RSPL and Nirma are the price floor for the whole laundry aisle. When they are strong, they cap how far mid-tier brands can raise prices before shoppers trade down. When they are weak, as in 2025–26, the mid-tier has more room. A recovering Ghadi, a reorganised Nirma under Ocular, and the soda-ash cycle (which sets their cost advantage) are therefore leading indicators for every branded detergent maker's pricing power in FY28.

The leader buys volume: HUL Home Care's margin-for-share trade

Growth went from 4% to 22% in three quarters; margin went from 19.1% to 16%. When the price-setter chooses share, every follower's recovery waits on it.

HUL's Home Care segment is the single most important business in this sub-sector, and its FY26 and H1 FY27 numbers describe a deliberate strategy. In FY26 the segment grew revenue only 3.1%, to ₹23,672 crore, with underlying sales growth (USG) of 4%. Underlying volume growth was a high single digit, so prices fell: HUL passed on deflation in the first half of the year through price cuts and grammage increases. The segment margin barely moved, at 19.1% (₹4,521 crore of EBIT) against 19.3% in FY25. Home Care was about 36.7% of HUL's continuing revenue. (Some earlier write-ups, including our own HUL primer, describe FY26 Home Care growth as about 9%; the annual report shows USG of 4%. The strong growth came in FY27.)

The economics of following

Jyothy's management described the follower's position frankly on the Q1 FY27 call. Asked why it had taken only about 3% of price in Q1 while HUL and Godrej Consumer had about 5% flowing through, it pointed to SKU-level price cuts by competitors and to its own higher MRPs in the prior year, and said its pricing is matched "SKU to SKU" with competition. Asked by an individual investor whether this was a commodity business, the chairperson said that "the moment JLL … launches … there is enough big competition who brings down their prices", and attributed the gap with diversified peers to Jyothy's "90% Home Care" exposure. On dishwash specifically, pricing was described as "competitive".

A follower's margin recovery has two conditions, not one. Input costs must fall, and the leader must not pass the fall straight back to consumers through price cuts or grammage. In FY25–FY26, when commodities were deflating, HUL did pass the fall back: its Home Care USG fell to 4% on high-single-digit volume, and Jyothy's dishwash went to −1.3% value on +6% volume. If crude eases in H2 FY27 and HUL again chooses volume, Jyothy's "H2 substantially better" guidance could be met on costs and still disappoint on margin. That caveat belongs next to every recovery forecast for the specialist.

Profit per outlet: a rough measure of the moat

One INDICATIVE way to compare the two businesses is segment profit per outlet. HUL Home Care's FY26 EBIT of ₹4,521 crore over 9 million outlets is about ₹5,000 per outlet per year. Jyothy's FY26 Fabric and Home Care result of ₹414 crore over 1.4 million directly served outlets is about ₹3,000 per direct outlet (and about ₹1,000 across all 4 million outlets it reaches). The numbers are crude, because outlet definitions differ, but they show why scale compounds: the leader earns more per shop from a wider range of products in that shop, and can spend more per shop to keep it.

In plain terms

HUL had a choice in 2026: protect its profit margin or grab customers while rivals were weak. It grabbed customers. Its home-care sales grew 22% in the September quarter while its margin slipped from 19% to 16%. That is a comfortable trade for a company that size. For Jyothy, which follows HUL's prices, it means cheaper oil will not automatically restore profits: if HUL keeps prices sharp, the savings go to shoppers.

Godrej Consumer is running a similar playbook from a different base. Its standalone Home Care grew 19% to ₹1,329 crore in Q2 FY27, with "strong market share gain across formats" in insecticides, strong double-digit growth in air fresheners, "extremely strong double-digit" volume growth in Godrej Fab, and new launches in dishwash (Spic, Rizz) and stain removal (Zap). It described the input shock as "one of the highest inflationary impacts in the last many years … approximately high-single-digit", took "steep pricing" in liquid detergents with "further sequential pricing underway", and held FY27 guidance of high-single-digit standalone volume growth and double-digit consolidated revenue and EBITDA growth. Its MD and CEO, per the 29 October 2026 release, is Aasif Malbari. The two largest diversified players are both pressing on Jyothy's aisles at the same time.

Dishwash: bars, liquids and the Pril exit Deep dive

The dishwash margin halved before the war, the premium liquid brand left in May 2026, and a third major entered the aisle. Exo now has to carry the whole franchise.

Dishwash is the aisle where the 2026 story is most concentrated. It is a ₹7,000–8,500 crore category (indicative), led by HUL's Vim. Bars still sell most of the volume, especially in ₹5 and ₹10 packs in rural and smaller-town India; liquids are about ₹3,000 crore, growing at a double-digit rate with only mid-teens household penetration, according to Godrej Consumer. Jyothy is the number-two player by value, with Exo the number-two bar; secondary sources put Exo at about 14% of bars and Pril at about 13% of liquids, and those shares should be read as industry estimates, not company disclosures. (Adding the two into "about 30% of dishwash", as one write-up did, is invalid, because they are shares of different markets.)

Two corrections to common descriptions are worth stating plainly. Jyothy is not the dishwash leader: the company itself says Exo is a "strong number two" in bars and that it is number two in dishwash by value, with Vim leading. And Pril was never Jyothy's to keep: it was a 15-year licence from Henkel, which has now ended.

The price war that came first

Jyothy's dishwash segment margin fell steadily through FY26, while crude oil and LABSA were still cheap. The segment earned 18.9% in FY25 (₹183 crore on ₹972 crore). In FY26 the quarterly margins were 20.2% (Q1), 17.2% (Q2), 14.5% (Q3) and 10.2% (Q4), for 15.7% over the year (₹150.5 crore on ₹959 crore). Revenue fell 1.3% for the year on 6% volume growth; in Q4 it was flat in value on 5% more volume, through "price reductions, grammage increases and promotional offers". The annual report's own phrase is "calibrated MRP adjustments to ensure price parity across key packs". This was a domestic price war, led by the category leader in a deflationary year, not a cost shock.

The Pril and Fa licences: what happened

In 2011 Jyothy bought 50.97% of Henkel India for ₹118.7 crore (plus an open offer and assumed liabilities), and later merged it into itself. The deal brought a mixed bag of rights. The shares fell more than 11% the next trading session. Around 15 June 2026 the board approved legal remedies against Henkel over the exit and transition arrangements; by 12 August the CFO said "the matter is before the court". He also said there was no contractual clause requiring Henkel to wait before re-entering, and Henkel is rebuilding a direct Indian presence through Schwarzkopf. The questions Exo has to answer

  • Can an owned brand recapture Pril's liquid shoppers before Vim, Rizz or a returning Pril do?   • How much more competition is coming? Godrej Consumer took Spic national in Q1 FY27 and launched Rizz liquid in West and South India in Q2 FY27, Jyothy's strongest regions. In plain terms

Jyothy rented the Pril brand from Henkel for 15 years at a very low fee, and in 2026 Henkel took it back. That removed perhaps ₹200–270 crore of annual sales, and a slightly larger slice of profit, from a company that was already losing a price war with Vim in dishwash bars. The plan is to make Jyothy's own brand, Exo, do the job Pril did. The difficulty is that Exo is known as a cheap bar, and the shoppers Pril served buy premium liquids.

The dishwash aisle therefore carries three of the seven forces at once: the price war (which pre-dated the shock and is led by the leader), the licence exit (which removed the premium end of Jyothy's ladder), and a new entrant (GCPL) with the media budget to buy trial. For the sub-sector it is also the clearest example of how format migration reshapes a category. As liquid penetration rises from the mid-teens, value shifts from a bar market Jyothy knows well to a liquid market where it has just lost its strongest brand.

The mosquito economy: legal insecticides against illegal sticks Deep dive

An ₹8,000 crore regulated market, a fast-growing unregistered fringe that pays a third of the tax, and a format shift from coils to machines and sticks.

Household insecticides (HI) are the most regulated, most seasonal and most technology-driven aisle in home care. The legal market was worth ₹8,138 crore in 2025, up from ₹7,147 crore in 2023, a growth rate of about 6.7% a year, according to an EY study for the Home Insect Control Association (HICA) as reported by The Week; a second outlet confirmed the study and its policy ask but not the rupee figure, so treat the size as a single secondary source. The same study puts mosquito-control use at 92–99% of urban households and 64–73% of rural ones. Demand peaks with the monsoon and the dengue and malaria season (July to October), and is shaped by weather in ways that do not always move together: heavy rain breeds mosquitoes, but extended heat and delayed rains, as in April–June 2026, push the season back.

Formats and their economics

Mosquito control comes in six main formats, each with different economics. Coils burn an allethrin-based compound overnight; they are cheap, rural-heavy and low-margin. Liquid vaporisers (LVs) heat a refill of transfluthrin or prallethrin in a plug-in machine; once a household owns a machine it buys refills every month or so, which makes LV revenue recurring and higher-margin. Aerosols (cypermethrin and similar) are premium and urban. Mats, racquets and repellent creams (Dabur's Odomos) fill out the shelf. Who wins the legal market

Godrej Consumer is the clear leader, with Good Knight (vaporisers, sticks, creams) and HIT (aerosols); industry estimates put it above half of the legal market, and in Q2 FY27 it reported "strong market share gain across formats; despite weak monsoons, led by an intensifying El Niño". Its edge is technical as much as commercial: it owns a patented in-house molecule, Renofluthrin, and its Goodknight incense sticks are now, in its words, "the largest branded stick in the category". It is even piloting HI sticks in Nigeria. Reckitt's Mortein is the second national brand. Jyothy's Maxo is strong in coils and small in vaporisers, which is why the segment has struggled: coils are the shrinking, low-margin, illegally-undercut end of the market. Dabur's Odomos leads repellent creams. The chemistry behind these products, the pyrethroids and their makers, is covered in the agrochemicals report.

The illegal-stick problem

The fastest-growing format in Indian mosquito control is not legal. Mosquito incense sticks made with unapproved molecules, typically dimefluthrin or meperfluthrin, are sold as ordinary agarbatti. Every legal insecticide formulation needs registration with the Central Insecticides Board and Registration Committee (CIB&RC) under the Insecticides Act, 1968, a process that involves years of safety data (reports cite a mandatory three-year safety-testing requirement). Unregistered sticks skip it. They also skip the tax: registered insecticides pay 18% GST under HSN 3808, while agarbatti pays 5% under HSN 3307 41 00. Both rates are now confirmed from the Gazette text of Notification 9/2025-Central Tax (Rate), not just press reports.

The consequence is that "HI category decline" in company commentary often means share lost to illegal sticks rather than lower spending on mosquito control. In plain terms

Many mosquito sticks sold in India use chemicals that have never been approved, and they are taxed as prayer incense at 5% instead of as insecticide at 18%. So they are cheaper to make and cheaper to sell. When a branded company says its mosquito business is shrinking, part of the reason is that shoppers moved to these sticks. The big brands are fighting back with approved sticks of their own and by lobbying for a lower tax rate.

What would change the aisle

  • A GST cut to 5% for registered HI. It would remove the tax wedge, lower shelf prices by about 11% if passed through, and favour the leader with the broadest registered range. It would cost the exchequer revenue, which is why it has not happened.
  • A dedicated HSN code and enforcement. Cheaper than a rate cut, and would let states seize mis-declared sticks more easily.
  • Weather. GCPL's Indonesian business grew 20% in Q2 FY27 on an El Niño-driven mosquito surge; a wet 2027 monsoon would help Indian HI after a dry 2026.
  • Molecule resistance and regulation. Mosquitoes develop resistance to older pyrethroids; companies with new molecules (GCPL's Renofluthrin) gain. A long-pending replacement for the 1968 Act could change registration rules for everyone.

For Jyothy, HI is small (6.7% of FY26 revenue) but strategically telling. It is the one aisle where the company was losing money until recently, and where it is a coil specialist in a market moving to machines and sticks. Its stick launch in July 2026, a year or more after Godrej's, is a test of whether a follower can enter a growing legal format fast enough to offset the shrinking one it leads.

GST 2.0 and the rule book: a tax cut that skipped the whole aisle

Toilet soap and toothpaste went to 5%. Every home-care product stayed at 18%. The asymmetry shapes portfolios, prices and the mosquito-stick trade.

India's GST overhaul of September 2025, announced after the 56th GST Council meeting on 3 September and effective from 22 September 2025, cut rates on a long list of everyday goods. For household and personal care the line was drawn sharply. Toilet soap, shampoo, hair oil, toothpaste and toothbrushes went from 18% to 5%. The whole home-care basket stayed at 18%: detergents, laundry soap, scouring powders, insecticides and air fresheners. Brokers and advisers called the omission of detergents "surprising" and "inadvertently left out" (as reported by Upstox), but the Gazette notification is unambiguous.

What the asymmetry does

The asymmetry has three effects. First, it is a portfolio-mix gift rather than a category gift. Companies with large personal-care businesses got a demand tailwind; home-care specialists did not. Jyothy said on its Q4 FY26 call that "GST rate cuts also helped improve demand particularly in Personal Care", where revenue grew 20% in Q4 FY26 as trade disruption settled from December, and an analyst on the call noted that "detergents did not have that". Jyothy's stated strategy of growing Personal Care is therefore also a tax-advantaged move. The personal-care side of the story is covered in the personal care report. Second, the transition itself disrupted trade: Jyothy's annual report notes that "nearly 60% of the FMCG portfolio underwent GST rate revisions", and distributors destocked and repriced across Q2 and Q3 FY26. Third, it hard-wires the 13-point wedge between registered insecticides and agarbatti that funds the illegal-stick trade.

The history: detergents were once a "luxury"

The current 18% is itself a cut. When GST launched on 1 July 2017, detergents were placed in the top 28% slab alongside luxury goods. The 23rd GST Council, meeting in Guwahati on 10 November 2017, moved 178 headings from 28% to 18%, explicitly including "detergents, washing and cleaning preparations" (PIB release). Since then the industry has lobbied for a further cut on the argument that detergent is a hygiene necessity. GST 2.0 did not grant it. A future cut to 5% or 12% would lower shelf prices meaningfully and favour volume, particularly in rural markets; nothing suggests one is imminent.

The rest of the rule book

  • Insecticides Act, 1968 and Insecticides Rules, 1971. Every molecule and formulation needs CIB&RC registration. This is a barrier to entry for legitimate players and the rule that illegal sticks evade.
  • Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules. Packs must show MRP, net quantity and a unit sale price per kilogram or litre. That makes grammage changes visible on the pack, though few shoppers compare.
  • Plastic Waste Management Rules and Extended Producer Responsibility. Brand owners must collect and recycle plastic packaging and use recycled content in rigid plastics: a growing cost for a pouch-heavy industry.
  • No price controls and no production-linked incentive. Home care is a free-pricing category with no state support scheme.
  • Trade remedies on inputs. Soda ash has a minimum import price (₹20,108 a tonne) and a safeguard investigation due around 16 November 2026; LAB has a history of anti-dumping action. Duties on inputs raise detergent makers' costs while protecting domestic chemical makers.
  • Environmental standards. LAB is biodegradable, unlike the branched ABS surfactants it replaced; limits on phosphate builders are periodically debated.
In plain terms

In September 2025 the government cut the tax on soap and toothpaste to 5% but kept detergents, dishwash bars, mosquito repellents and air fresheners at 18%. So a company that mostly sells detergents got no tax help, while one that sells soaps did. And because prayer incense also went to 5%, illegal mosquito sticks dressed up as incense became even cheaper than the legal ones.

Six decades, seven shocks: how the category's cycles work

From Surf versus Nirma to the 2026 war, the category's margins have followed crude and its market shares have followed the price-setter.

Indian home care has a long history of price wars and cost shocks, and the pattern repeats. HUL launched Surf in 1959, India's first branded detergent powder. Nirma, launched in 1969 by Karsanbhai Patel, undercut it so sharply that it created a mass economy market. HUL responded with Wheel in 1987–88, a cheaper brand built to fight Nirma on price without dragging Surf down. In 1983 M.P. Ramachandran founded Jyothy in Thrissur with about ₹5,000 and launched Ujala Supreme, turning fabric whitener into a branded category. RSPL launched Ghadi in Kanpur in 1987. P&G brought Ariel in 1991 and Tide in 2000, and in 2004 HUL and P&G fought a detergent price war that cut Wheel and Tide prices sharply.

Three recurring lessons

First, cost shocks hurt the narrow more than the broad. In FY22 and again in 2026, the specialist's margin fell two to three times as far as the leader's segment margin. Second, deflation is not a pure gift: in FY25–FY26 the leader used falling costs to cut prices and add grams, so the specialist's gross margin fell even in a good cost year. Third, the economy end is cyclical in its own way: Nirma and Ghadi have alternated between dominance and trouble, and each period of weakness has been an opening for branded mid-tier players. The cycle also shows in the share price. Over ten years Jyothy's net profit grew about 16% a year and its average ROE was about 26% (screener), yet its share price compounded at only about 1% a year, fell about 20% a year over three years and 41% over the last year, and sits more than 55% below its all-time high (Finshots). Holders have earned their return mostly through dividends.

In plain terms

Home-care profits go up and down with oil, with a lag of a quarter or two. When oil and chemicals get cheaper, specialist companies like Jyothy enjoy fat margins for a year or two; when they get dearer, margins halve. The biggest company in the aisle feels the same swings but much more gently, because detergents are only a part of its business and because it sets the price everyone else follows.

The 2026 cycle is unusual in one respect: it stacked three shocks in one year. A domestic price war that started in the deflationary FY26 compressed realisations; the war-driven input spike hit gross margins on top; and the Pril exit removed a premium product just as the company most needed one. Previous cycles have usually brought one of these at a time. That stacking is why Q1 FY27 is the worst quarter in Jyothy's recent record, and why the shape of the recovery, rather than its timing alone, is the question for FY28.

Reading a home-care company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twelve numbers explain almost everything about a home-care business. Each has a formula, a benchmark, a driver and a trap.

A home-care company's results look simple: revenue, gross margin, advertising, EBITDA. But each of those numbers hides choices about definitions, about which effects are stripped out, and about what management can and cannot control. This section starts with the revenue and profit equation, works through one example in round rupees, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how it is calculated, a worked example, what moves it, what good looks like, how Jyothy and the reference companies compare, and the trap in its definition. It ends with an illustrative unit P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected in one place, and a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

The equation: how revenue and profit are built

Revenue in home care is the sum, across categories, of four terms multiplied together: the number of households a brand reaches, the share of them that buy it (penetration), how much each buying household uses in a year, and the net rupees the company receives per kilogram or litre (realisation). Realisation, in turn, is the MRP after GST, after the retailer's and distributor's margins, and after promotions and extra grammage. Profit follows: gross profit is revenue minus the input basket; EBITDA is gross profit minus advertising and promotion, staff and other overheads.

A worked example in round numbers, ILLUSTRATIVE only. A detergent brand is stocked in shops serving 20 million households. Sixty per cent of them buy it, so 12 million households are customers. Each uses 6 kilograms a year. The MRP is ₹120 a kilogram; after 18% GST, a 15% combined trade margin and 5% promotions, the company receives about ₹82–83. Revenue is 12 million × 6 × ₹83 ≈ ₹600 crore. At a 48% gross margin, gross profit is ₹288 crore. Advertising at 8% (₹48 crore), staff at 11% (₹66 crore) and other overheads at 12% (₹72 crore) leave EBITDA of ₹102 crore, a 17% margin. Now raise input costs by a third on the crude-linked half of the basket and hold price: the gross margin drops toward 40%, gross profit to ₹240 crore, and EBITDA to ₹54 crore even before anyone changes the advertising budget. Every term in the revenue equation would have to rise by about 8% together just to recover the lost ₹48 crore.

In plain terms

A home-care company's sales are the number of homes that buy its products, times how much each home uses, times the price it actually receives after tax, shop margins and discounts. Its profit is what is left after paying for chemicals, plastic, advertising, staff and trucks. The company controls its advertising and its distribution. It does not control the price of chemicals, and if it is not the market leader, it does not really control its own shelf price either.

1. Volume growth

What it is. The year-on-year change in the quantity sold, as measured by the company. Formula. (Tonnes or units this period ÷ same period last year) − 1, often mix-adjusted. HUL calls it underlying volume growth (UVG) and strips out acquisitions, disposals and currency; Jyothy calls it volume growth and, from FY27, quotes it excluding Pril and Fa. Worked example. A company sells 100,000 tonnes in Q1 FY26 and 105,300 tonnes in Q1 FY27: volume growth is 5.3%, Jyothy's ex-Pril figure for Q1 FY27. What drives it. Distribution gains, format launches, share taken from weaker rivals (such as Ghadi in 2025–26), grammage increases (which add kilograms without adding packs), rural incomes and, for insecticides, the weather. Benchmark. Good: 7% or more; average 3–6%; weak below 2% (indicative). How companies compare. Jyothy: +6.4% (FY25), +6.0% (FY26), +5.3% ex-Pril (Q1 FY27). HUL Home Care: high single digit in FY26 and Q1 FY27, double digit in Q2 FY27. GCPL standalone: 7% in Q2 FY27. RSPL and Nirma: not disclosed; RSPL's revenue fell 21.6% in FY25. The trap. Company volume is sell-in tonnage, so a 10% grammage increase on a ₹10 pack shows up as 10% volume growth even if no extra packs sell. It is not comparable across companies, and not comparable with NIQ panel data, which showed HPC volume falling 4.3% in April–June 2026 (secondary).

2. Value growth and price/mix

What it is. Revenue growth, split into volume and everything else. Formula. Value growth = revenue this period ÷ last period − 1; implied price/mix ≈ value growth − volume growth. Worked example. FY26 Jyothy: value +3.5%, volume +6.0%, so price/mix ≈ −2.5 points: prices fell or the mix shifted toward cheaper products. Q1 FY27 ex-Pril: value +8.1%, volume +5.3%, price/mix ≈ +2.8 points, matching the ~3% price taken. What drives it. MRP changes, grammage changes, promotions, the shift between formats (liquids are priced differently from powders), and the shift between segments (Personal Care's 9–10% price rise lifts the blended figure). Benchmark. Good: 10% or more value growth; average 5–9%; weak below 4%. How companies compare. HUL Home Care USG: 4% (FY26), 14% (Q1 FY27), 22% (Q2 FY27). GCPL Home Care +19% (Q2 FY27). Jyothy +3.0% reported, +8.1% ex-Pril (Q1 FY27). The trap. Negative price/mix does not always mean price cuts. A company growing liquids faster than powders can show negative mix even at unchanged MRPs, because liquids now sell for less per wash. And "price cuts" in this category are often invisible: more grams at the same MRP.

6. EBITDA margin

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, as a share of revenue. Formula. (Gross profit − A&P − staff − other expenses) ÷ revenue; Jyothy reports "operating EBITDA", which excludes other income. Worked example. FY26 Jyothy: ₹449.9 crore ÷ ₹2,944 crore = 15.3%. Operating leverage matters too: staff and overheads are largely fixed in rupees, so faster revenue growth lifts the margin. Benchmark. Good: 18% or more; average 14–17%; weak below 12% (indicative). How companies compare (FY26, consolidated, screener operating margin). P&G Hygiene 27.3% (no home care), HUL 23.3%, GCPL 20.9%, Dabur 18.6%, Jyothy 15.3%, Patanjali Foods 4.4% (edible-oil heavy). Management guides that Q2 FY27 will be "not materially lower" than Q1 and H2 "substantially better". The trap. Consolidated margins of diversified companies tell you little about their home-care economics: HUL's 23.3% blends home care (19.1% segment EBIT in FY26) with higher-margin beauty and foods. HUL's Q2 FY27 margin is 22.6% on turnover (company basis) or 22.4% on revenue from operations (screener basis): same numerator, different denominator.

Operating leverage is worth a worked example because it explains why margin recoveries are fast. On Jyothy's FY26 cost base, staff and other overheads were about ₹706 crore, largely fixed in rupees. If revenue grows 10% at an unchanged gross margin of 47% and A&P stays at 7.7%, gross profit rises by about ₹138 crore, A&P by about ₹23 crore, and fixed costs by perhaps ₹35 crore (5% inflation), so EBITDA rises by about ₹80 crore, or 18%, and the margin by about 1.1 points to roughly 16.4% (ILLUSTRATIVE). The same leverage works against a company when revenue stalls, which is why the loss of Pril and Fa revenue matters for margin even after the gross-margin shock passes.

8. Distribution reach and channel mix

What it is. The number of retail outlets a company's products reach, split into those served directly by its own distributors and salesmen and those reached through wholesalers; plus the share and growth of modern trade, e-commerce and quick commerce. Formula. Count of outlets billed in a period (direct) and an estimate of total outlets stocking the brand. Worked example. Jyothy: about 4 million outlets in total, 1.4 million direct, up about 100,000 in FY26; 1.2 million in FY24 and 1.3 million in FY25. If each new direct outlet adds ₹20,000 of annual revenue (illustrative), 100,000 outlets add ₹200 crore over time. What drives it. Investment in distributors and sales staff, rural van routes, the width of the portfolio (a distributor needs enough products to make a visit worthwhile), and credit terms. Benchmark. Direct reach above 3 million is strong for a mid-cap; 1–3 million average; below 1 million weak. HUL's 9 million-plus is in a class of its own. How companies compare. HUL 9 million+; Jyothy 4 million total / 1.4 million direct; RSPL 2.8–3.0 million; others not disclosed (n/a). Channel: Jyothy MT + e-commerce + QC +26% (FY26); e-commerce + QC +25–30% (Q1 FY27); share not disclosed by anyone. The trap. "Reach" definitions vary: some companies count outlets ever billed, some count outlets billed in the last quarter, some include indirect wholesale estimates. Quick-commerce sales are not disclosed separately by any home-care company.

Channel economics differ more than the reach numbers suggest. In general trade, Jyothy collects much of its money in advance and distributors hold 15–20 days of stock, so growth there consumes little working capital. Modern trade and quick commerce pay on credit, charge listing and visibility fees, and run frequent promotions; the CFO says terms are comparable to GT on working capital, but trade spend is higher. A rising share of QC therefore tends to lift growth and lower gross-to-net realisation at the same time. For an analyst, the useful pair of numbers is direct-reach additions (a measure of investment in the rural and semi-urban shelf) and the growth gap between e-commerce/QC and GT (a measure of how fast the urban premium shelf is moving). A company that adds outlets but loses QC share, or the reverse, is building only half of the system the category now needs.

11. Working capital and cash conversion

What it is. How many days of revenue are tied up in receivables and inventory, net of payables, and how much of EBITDA turns into operating cash. Formula. Company and screener working-capital definitions differ by more than 20 days for the same company. 12. Capex intensity

What it is. Capital spending as a share of revenue, and relative to depreciation. Formula. Additions to property, plant, equipment and intangibles ÷ revenue. Worked example. Capital commitments were only ₹3.4 crore. What drives it. New formats (liquids need different lines), capacity in growing regions, automation and ESG (Jyothy has zero liquid discharge at 12 of its 23 plants and solar at four). Benchmark. 2–4% of revenue is normal for a formulator; above 5% suggests a capacity build or backward integration (RSPL is spending ₹800–1,000 crore on soda ash). How companies compare. HUL announced capacity for "fast-growing premium liquids" in FY26 but does not split segment capex; RSPL's integration capex is far heavier. The trap. Low capex is a strength only if contract manufacturers and leases are counted: some capacity sits off balance sheet, and conversion charges appear in other expenses rather than capex.

In plain terms

Home care needs very little money tied up in factories: Jyothy spends about ₹2–3 of every ₹100 of sales on new equipment, and its shops pay it quickly. That is why a company earning modest margins can still pile up cash. The cash is both a cushion and a drag: it keeps the company safe in a bad year, but it lowers the return on shareholders' money unless it is returned or used well.

Definition traps, collected

Most of the mistakes made in reading this sub-sector come from comparing numbers that look alike but are built differently. The list below gathers the traps that appear in the KPI entries above, plus a few that arise only when companies are put side by side.

  • "Home Care" means four different things. HUL's Home Care has no insecticides; GCPL's is mostly insecticides and air care; Jyothy's new Home Care excludes fabric care. Compare HUL Home Care with Jyothy Fabric Care plus Home Care.
  • Reported vs ex-Pril/Fa. Jyothy's FY26 includes Pril and Fa; FY27 does not after 31 May 2026. Every FY27 growth figure needs a label.
  • Old vs new segments. Dishwashing (₹959 crore in FY26) is no longer reported; use restated FY26 comparatives for the three new segments.
  • Company volume vs panel volume. Sell-in tonnage, inflated by grammage and share gains, is not NIQ's consumer offtake.
  • USG vs reported growth. HUL's USG strips out acquisitions, disposals and other items; reported segment revenue growth can differ (Q2 FY27: USG 22%, reported about 21% on a secondary base).
  • Gross margin source. Use the company's presentation, not 100 minus a database's material-cost ratio.
  • A&P conflict. Q1 FY26 A&P was 7.8% (presentation), not the transcript's 10.8%.
  • Margin denominators. HUL's Q2 FY27 EBITDA margin is 22.6% on turnover or 22.4% on revenue from operations.
  • PAT basis. HUL's FY26 reported PAT (₹15,059 crore on screener) includes about ₹4,407 crore of profit from the discontinued ice-cream business; continuing PAT is about ₹10,652 crore.
  • Net cash basis. Jyothy's company figure (₹997 crore at Mar-26) includes bank FDs that a database's "investments" line misses; its "borrowings" are leases.
  • Standalone vs consolidated. Jyothy has had no subsidiaries since selling its Bangladesh stake in September 2025; screener's consolidated page stops at FY25 and shows a stale P/E. Use the standalone page.
  • Legal vs total HI market. The ₹8,138 crore insecticide market excludes unregistered sticks; "category decline" can mean share lost to them.

How to read a home-care company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, reported and underlying. Find the headline growth, then the version excluding one-offs (ex-Pril/Fa at Jyothy; USG at HUL). Red flag: a big gap that management does not explain.
  2. Volume growth. Subtract it from value growth to get price/mix. Red flag: volume up, value flat or down, for two quarters running: you are watching a price war.
  3. Gross margin, year on year and quarter on quarter. Compare the move with the input trend (LABSA, crude, packaging, palm). Red flag: gross margin falling while inputs are flat or falling.
  4. A&P as a share of revenue. Red flag: EBITDA protected by cutting A&P below about 7% for more than a quarter or two while rivals spend.
  5. EBITDA margin against guidance. Jyothy guided Q2 FY27 "not materially lower" than Q1's 8.4% and H2 "substantially better". Check the words, then the number.
  6. Segment results. Which segment carried the quarter? Red flag: the largest segment's margin falling faster than the group's.
  7. Category commentary. Look for liquids vs powders, bars vs liquids, coils vs vaporisers and sticks, and any comment on illegal sticks or new entrants.
  8. Pricing actions. How much price was taken, in which segments, and whether it was MRP or grammage. Compare with what HUL and GCPL took.
  9. Channels and reach. Direct-reach additions, MT/e-commerce/QC growth, GT trends, distributor inventory days. Red flag: sales growth with rising receivables or channel stock.
  10. Cash and capital allocation. Net cash, dividends, buyback, any acquisition news, and the status of litigation (the Henkel case). Then check one external number, crude or LABSA, to judge whether next quarter's gross margin is more likely to rise or fall.
In plain terms

When a home-care company reports, read it in this order: how much more did it sell, how much of that was price, how much did the ingredients cost relative to what it charged, how much did it spend on advertising, and which business line made the money. Then look at what oil did over the last three months, because that will mostly decide the next quarter.

The global lens: India's leader out-earns the world

HUL's Home Care margin sits at or above most global home-care businesses. In mid-2026 the global majors cut prices while Indian companies raised them.

Home care is one of the most global consumer categories: P&G, Unilever, Henkel, Reckitt, Church & Dwight and Clorox sell similar products in most markets, with similar chemistry and similar cost exposures. Comparing them with India shows two things. First, Indian home care is structurally profitable for its leader. HUL Home Care's FY26 segment margin of 19.1% is above Unilever's global Home Care underlying operating margin (14.9% in 2025) and above Henkel Consumer Brands' guidance of 14–15% adjusted EBIT, and comparable with P&G Fabric & Home Care's after-tax margin (18.6% in its year to June 2026, 19.5% in July–September 2026, equivalent to roughly 25% before tax). Oligopoly pricing at the top of the market, deep distribution moats and low-cost manufacturing explain it. Second, India felt the 2026 crude shock more visibly than the developed markets did.

Valuation also differs. Indian FMCG carries a premium to most global staples, and Jyothy, at 24.3 times trailing earnings, now trades much closer to global consumer-goods multiples than to its Indian peers. For a global investor, Indian home care offers higher growth and, for the leader, higher margins than developed markets, at a higher price. The specialist offers the same category exposure at a lower price, with far more volatility.

In plain terms

Selling detergent in India is, for the market leader, at least as profitable as selling it in America or Europe. But India's detergents contain more of the oil-based cleaning chemical and come in fixed small-price packs, so when oil spiked in 2026 Indian companies were hit harder and had to raise prices while the global giants were cutting them.

Technology and disruption: liquids, bio-surfactants and the digital shelf

The threats to today's leaders are not new competitors so much as new formats, new chemistry and new channels, each of which shifts where value sits.

Home care is not a high-technology category, but small technical shifts compound into large changes in who earns the margin. Six are under way in India.

  • Liquids and concentrates. The move from powders and bars to liquids shifts value from bulk chemistry (soda ash, fillers) to formulation (enzymes, multiple surfactants, perfume) and packaging. That should favour companies with formulation science, but in India it has also become the main battlefield for promotions: Jyothy says liquids now sell for less than powders per wash. HUL is adding capacity in premium liquids; Jyothy's liquids doubled in FY26; RSPL has no liquid yet.
  • Bio-based and non-crude surfactants. After 2026 every formulator wants less crude in the bottle. Plant-based surfactants from coconut or palm swap crude risk for palm risk; bio-surfactants (rhamnolipids, sophorolipids) are cleaner but costly and small-scale. HUL cites biosurfactant work; Jyothy has worked on reducing crude-linked inputs for 12–18 months and launched what it calls a bio-enzymatic dishwash liquid.
  • Pods, capsules and dishwasher tablets. Small in India, where dishwasher penetration is only an estimated 1–2%. This is a premium niche where Reckitt (Finish) and P&G lead globally, and it would arrive first through quick commerce in metros.
  • Quick commerce and private labels. Dark-store platforms reward search visibility and promotions, charge higher trade margins and launch their own labels. They weaken the moats built through general-trade distribution and favour brands with digital marketing budgets.
  • Digital-first eco brands. Plant-based and refill-model detergents sold online are small and mostly loss-making, but they raise the innovation bar for incumbents.
  • Insecticide molecules. Mosquito resistance to older pyrethroids makes new molecules valuable (GCPL's patented Renofluthrin); unregistered molecules in illegal sticks are the disruptive force in the opposite direction.

One structural risk is specific to Jyothy and illustrates a sub-sector theme: licence dependence. A meaningful part of its portfolio came from Henkel, and two of those brands have now gone. Shoppers are slowly switching from powders and bars to liquids, and from mosquito coils to plug-in machines and sticks. In theory that lets companies charge more; in practice, liquids have become so heavily discounted that they now cost less per wash than powder. The companies with the most money for research, advertising and online promotion are best placed to win that switch.

Bull vs bear: does home care's margin come back?

The case for the sub-sector rests on crude easing and format upgrades. The case against rests on a leader buying share and a follower that cannot set its own price.

The debate for Indian home care in late 2026 is narrower than it looks. Nobody doubts that Indians will keep buying detergent, dishwash and mosquito repellent, or that the category will grow with nominal consumption. The question is whose margin recovers, and how far, after the worst input shock in recent memory. For the leader, the answer depends on its own choice between share and margin. For the specialist, it depends on crude and on that choice.

The bull case

  • Costs mean-revert: Brent fell from about $118 to about $95 after the April ceasefire; LABSA and packaging lag crude by one to three months, and Jyothy expects relief "more meaningfully from October". Each point of gross margin is worth about ₹30 crore of EBITDA.
  • Pricing has been taken: 4–4.5% at Jyothy and about 5% at HUL and GCPL; if input costs fall, part of that sticks.
  • Format upgrades: liquids in laundry and dishwash, vaporisers and sticks in insecticides raise revenue per household.
  • Economy weakness: RSPL and Nirma distracted, giving branded mid-tier players room on price and shelf.
  • Balance sheets: Jyothy's ~₹850 crore of net cash and a buyback proposal; asset-light models generate cash even in bad years.
  • Valuation: the specialist trades at 24.3× trailing and about 13–15× EBITDA, a discount of roughly 39% to the FMCG median.

The bear case

  • The leader is buying share: HUL Home Care gave up three points of margin for 22% growth and guides "commodity inflation … in the short-term"; when costs ease it may pass the savings on.
  • Dishwash is structurally weaker: the margin halved before the war; Pril is gone; GCPL has entered; Henkel may return.
  • Crude risk is two-way: a renewed Hormuz disruption would repeat 2026; the rupee near ₹95/$ adds to imported input costs.
  • Demand: NIQ (secondary) showed HPC volume −4.3% in April–June; the South Peninsula monsoon was 76% of normal.
  • Illegal sticks and GST: the 13-point tax wedge persists; registered insecticides keep losing share at the bottom.
  • Economy recovery: if RSPL returns to growth, the share gains of FY26–H1 FY27 reverse.
