Indian home care: crude costs, a price war, a lost licence

A complete guide to the business of keeping Indian homes clean and mosquito-free: detergents, dishwash, household insecticides, cleaners and air care. 00

The one-page view: a brands business with crude oil in every bottle

Home care looks like a sleepy staple. In 2026 it behaved like a chemicals business, and the smallest branded player took the hardest hit.

Indian home care is the basket of products a household buys to wash clothes, clean dishes, keep mosquitoes away and keep floors, toilets and air fresh. It is worth roughly ₹75,000–85,000 crore a year at manufacturer prices (an indicative estimate built up category by category; no industry body publishes a total). Almost every household buys it, most buy it every few weeks, and a single company, Hindustan Unilever (HUL), sells more than a quarter of it. HUL's Home Care segment alone booked ₹23,672 crore of revenue in FY26 at a 19.1% segment margin.

The NSE 750 has only one listed company whose business is mostly this basket: Jyothy Labs (JYOTHYLAB), the maker of Ujala, Exo, Henko, Mr. White and Maxo. Fabric care and home care were 89% of its ₹2,944 crore of FY26 revenue. That makes Jyothy the cleanest window on the economics of the category, and this report uses it as the lens. But the report is about the whole sub-sector: the giant diversified companies (HUL, Godrej Consumer, Dabur) that run large home-care divisions, the unlisted economy detergent makers (RSPL with Ghadi, Nirma), the multinational subsidiaries (P&G Home Products, Reckitt) and the upstream chemical makers that sell them their raw materials.

Seven forces define the sub-sector in late 2026, and the rest of this report is built around them.

  • The West Asia input shock. The war that began on 28 February 2026 pushed crude above $100 a barrel. LABSA, the active cleaning ingredient in Indian detergents and dishwash bars, rose 60–65% between January and May; plastic packaging rose 50–55% in March and April. Jyothy's gross margin fell from 48.0% to 38.5% and its EBITDA margin from 16.5% to 8.4% between Q1 FY26 and Q1 FY27. Value moved upstream: Tamilnadu Petroproducts, an Indian maker of LAB (the precursor to LABSA), lifted its EBITDA margin from 11.2% to 15.9% in the same quarter.
  • A price war in dishwash that started before the war. Jyothy's dishwash segment margin fell quarter by quarter through FY26, from 20.2% to 17.2% to 14.5% to 10.2%, while crude was still cheap. HUL's Vim leads the category; Godrej Consumer has now entered with Spic and Rizz.
  • The Pril and Fa licence exit. Henkel did not renew Jyothy's 15-year licences for Pril dishwash liquid and Fa deodorants beyond 31 May 2026. Jyothy is in court over the exit terms and is building Exo as its owned dishwash platform. The lost revenue is not disclosed; our indicative back-solve puts it at ₹200–270 crore a year, and that range depends on an assumption about trade wind-down.
  • The leader trading margin for volume. HUL Home Care's underlying sales growth went from 4% in FY26 to 14% in Q1 FY27 and 22% in Q2 FY27, while its segment margin went from 19.1% to 17% to 16%. A price-follower's margin recovery now depends on the leader's pricing as much as on crude.
  • GST 2.0's asymmetry. The September 2025 rate cut took toilet soap, toothpaste and shampoo to 5% but left every home-care line at 18%: detergents, laundry soap, scouring powder, insecticides and air fresheners. Agarbatti also went to 5%, which gives unregistered mosquito incense sticks a 13-point tax edge over legal insecticides.
  • Rural demand and a deficient monsoon. The 2026 monsoon delivered 87% of its long-period average, and only 76% over the South Peninsula, Jyothy's home market. A panel tracker (NIQ, reported second-hand) saw household-and-personal-care volume fall 4.3% in April–June 2026 even as companies reported volume growth.
  • The economy-detergent leaders stumbled. RSPL (Ghadi) lost 21.6% of its revenue in FY25 after a product recall and was put on a Negative outlook by CRISIL. Nirma is carving its consumer and chemical businesses into a private vehicle while its US soda-ash arm sits in Chapter 11. Branded mid-tier players have picked up volume in powders and bars.

Put together, these forces say something uncomfortable about a category usually filed under "defensive staples". Home care is a brands business with a commodity cost base. Between half and 60% of a formulator's inputs are linked to crude, either directly (surfactants, solvents, plastic packaging) or through freight and power. In a normal year brands earn a wide spread over those inputs: Jyothy's gross margin sat between 47% and 51% for most of FY24–FY26. When inputs jump faster than prices can follow, the spread collapses, and how far it collapses depends on two things. The first is how much of a company's revenue is exposed: about 90% at Jyothy on management's own revenue-weighted measure, against roughly 37% at HUL, where home care sits beside foods, beauty and personal care. The second is who sets prices. HUL leads; Jyothy, by its own description, matches competitors "SKU to SKU".

The result is a sub-sector where the single listed specialist trades at 24.3 times trailing earnings and 20.7 times FY26 earnings, against 40.8 times for HUL and 39.8 times for Godrej Consumer, and where its share price is down 41% over a year. Jyothy's board meets on 4 November 2026 to approve its Q2 FY27 (Sep-26) results and to consider a share buyback. Those results will be the first clean read on whether the margin trough was Q1 FY27, as management implied, or later.

In plain terms

Every packet of detergent contains crude oil, in the cleaning chemical and in the plastic pouch. When oil jumped in 2026, the cost of making that packet jumped by about a third in a few months, and nobody could raise the shelf price by a third. The big, diversified companies absorbed it because detergents are only part of what they sell. Jyothy, which sells little else, saw half its operating profit disappear in one quarter.

Three things make this a sub-sector worth understanding beyond one stock. First, it is the cleanest test in Indian consumer goods of the argument between "brand" and "commodity": the 2026 data show a supplier's margin rising while its branded customer's margin halved, which is not what brand power is supposed to look like. Second, it shows how market leadership works in a mature staple: HUL can choose between margin and share, and when it chooses share, every follower pays. Third, it shows how policy shapes categories in ways that never reach the headlines, from a tax schedule that lets unregistered mosquito sticks undercut regulated ones, to a rainfall map that put the weakest monsoon over the South, where Jyothy's biggest brands live.

01

How home care makes money: from benzene to the bathroom shelf

A low-capital, high-frequency business that turns cheap chemicals into branded habits, and keeps the spread only as long as the chemicals stay cheap.

A home-care company does four things. It buys commodity chemicals and packaging. It mixes them into a formula that cleans, whitens, kills insects or smells pleasant. It puts that formula into a pack with a brand on it, usually at a fixed price point such as ₹10 or ₹99. And it pushes the pack through a chain of distributors into millions of small shops, supermarkets and, increasingly, quick-commerce dark stores. The money is made in the gap between what the chemicals cost per kilogram and what the household pays per kilogram after tax and trade margins. Manufacturing is simple and cheap: Jyothy runs 23 plants, and its fixed assets of ₹504 crore support ₹2,944 crore of revenue, a fixed-asset turnover of about 5.8 times. The hard and expensive parts are the brand (advertising and promotion run at 7–11% of revenue for branded players) and the distribution system.

The products, aisle by aisle

The basket is more varied than "detergents". Each aisle has its own chemistry, its own leader and its own price ladder, and a company can be a leader in one aisle and a minor player in the next. Jyothy is the extreme case: it owns about 84% of the fabric-whitener market with Ujala Supreme (screener's company profile), is the number-two dishwash bar brand with Exo, is number two in mosquito coils by volume with Maxo, and is a small challenger in detergent powders and liquids.

Two features of the basket explain most of its economics. The first is repeat frequency. A household runs out of detergent or dishwash bar every few weeks, so brands are rebought constantly and switching costs are low. That makes advertising and shelf presence matter more than product innovation, and it makes price points sticky: a ₹10 bar stays a ₹10 bar, so companies adjust grams rather than rupees when costs move. The second is the price ladder. Every aisle runs from an economy tier (Nirma and Ghadi powders at a fraction of the premium price per kilogram) through a mid-tier (Rin, Henko) to a premium tier (Surf excel, Ariel, matic liquids). Different companies make their money on different rungs, which is why the economy makers can earn margins similar to HUL's from the opposite end of the ladder.

Where the value sits along the chain

In a normal year, most of the value is captured by the brand owner. Retailers keep roughly 8–12% of the shelf price in general trade, distributors 4–6% (indicative industry ranges), modern trade and quick commerce more once listing fees and promotions are counted. The chemical makers upstream earn thin, cyclical margins. In 2026 that ordering flipped for a while. When LAB was scarce because West Asian supply was disrupted, the domestic LAB makers could raise prices faster than detergent makers could pass them on. Licensors sit off to the side: Jyothy paid Henkel just ₹4.57 crore of royalty in FY26 for its licensed brands, a trivial cash cost, while P&G Home Products in India paid its parent ₹410 crore in FY25.

The segment re-cut matters for anyone comparing companies. From Q1 FY27 Jyothy reports three segments: Fabric Care (including the laundry-service business), Home Care (dishwash, household insecticides, incense and floor and toilet cleaners) and Personal Care. Restated FY26 figures are Fabric ₹1,409 crore, Home ₹1,215 crore and Personal ₹321 crore. HUL's "Home Care" covers fabric wash and household care but has no insecticides or air fresheners. Godrej Consumer's "Home Care" is mostly insecticides and air fresheners with almost no detergent powder. To compare Jyothy with HUL's Home Care segment you have to add Jyothy's Fabric Care and Home Care together: ₹2,624 crore in FY26.

In plain terms

Making detergent is easy; selling it is hard. The plant is cheap, so the money a home-care company earns comes from the brand that lets it charge more than the chemicals cost, and from the trucks and salesmen that get the pack into a village shop. When the chemicals get dearer, the brand has to prove it can push the price up. In 2026 most brands could not push it up fast enough.

02

A ₹80,000 crore basket that grows with the household budget

No official market size exists, so the total has to be built up aisle by aisle. Fabric care is more than half of it; household insecticides are the only aisle with a published number.

Indian home care has no single authoritative market size. Industry bodies publish figures for a few aisles, panel trackers sell theirs, and companies quote the bits that suit them. Building the total up category by category gives an indicative ₹75,000–85,000 crore at manufacturer value (excluding GST), roughly US$8–9 billion. Two numbers in that build are anchored to a named source: the household-insecticide market of ₹8,138 crore in 2025 (up from ₹7,147 crore in 2023, according to an EY study for the Home Insect Control Association, as reported by The Week; the ₹ figure itself is secondary), and the liquid-dishwash market of about ₹3,000 crore, which Godrej Consumer cited in its Q2 FY27 presentation when it launched Rizz. The rest is triangulation from company revenues and known shares.

Three things stand out. Fabric care is the profit pool that decides the sub-sector, at well over half the total. Jyothy's main-wash detergent business, at roughly ₹1,000–1,100 crore a year (36% of its Q4 FY26 revenue, annualised), is about 2–3% of it. Second, the categories Jyothy dominates are small: the whitener aisle is about ₹300–350 crore, which is why a near-monopoly in it does not make Jyothy a large company. An earlier estimate of ₹600–900 crore for whitener double-counted Jyothy's conditioners and should not be used. Third, household insecticides, at ₹8,138 crore, is a mid-sized aisle with a large illegal fringe that the legal number excludes (Section 11).

How fast it grows

Through a full cycle the category grows at about 7–9% a year in nominal terms, made of roughly 3–5% volume and 3–4% price and mix (indicative, from HUL and Jyothy multi-year growth). FY26 was well below that. HUL Home Care's underlying sales growth was only 4% and its reported revenue rose 3.1%, because the leader cut prices in the deflationary first half while volumes grew at a high single digit. Jyothy grew 3.5% in value on 6% volume. The first half of FY27 has been a pricing-led rebound: HUL Home Care +14% and +22% in Q1 and Q2, Godrej Consumer's standalone Home Care +19% in Q2. Over a longer arc, Jyothy's revenue grew 7.6% a year from FY22 to FY26 and 7% a year over ten years (screener).

India's place in the world

India is a big market by tonnage and a modest one by value, because the price per kilogram is low. The whole Indian home-care basket is smaller than Unilever's global Home Care division (€11.6 billion in 2025, about ₹1.28 lakh crore at an indicative ₹110 per euro) and about a quarter of P&G's Fabric & Home Care segment (US$30.3 billion in the year to June 2026, about ₹2.88 lakh crore at ₹94.9 per dollar). HUL's Home Care segment is a large business by Indian standards and roughly a fifth of Unilever Home Care's global turnover. Jyothy's fabric and home care revenue is about 1% of P&G's segment.

Exports barely matter. Finished home-care products are bulky and cheap per kilogram, so they are made close to the consumer. Jyothy exports to 19 countries but does not disclose the value; Godrej Consumer serves Indonesia and Africa through local subsidiaries. The industry is domestic, which means its fortunes rest on Indian household budgets, Indian rainfall and Indian tax policy, while its costs are set by global oil and chemical markets.

In plain terms

Nobody officially measures how much Indians spend on home care, so the ₹80,000 crore figure is a careful sum of educated guesses. The useful facts are the shape, not the total: washing clothes is more than half the money, mosquito control is about a tenth, and the little blue whitener bottle that made Jyothy famous is a few hundred crore. Owning a small aisle completely does not make you big.

03

Demand, the monsoon and the volume puzzle

Everyone already buys detergent. Growth comes from upgrading formats, using more per wash and moving to new channels, and in 2026 the panel data and the company data told different stories.

Home care is a category with near-total penetration. Every household cleans clothes and dishes with something, branded or not, and the EY–HICA study puts mosquito-control use at 92–99% of urban and 64–73% of rural households (secondary). So growth does not come from first-time buyers. It comes from five sources: more households (household formation runs ahead of population growth as families split); more use per household as incomes rise; trading up the price ladder from economy to mid-tier to premium; switching formats, which usually raises spend per wash; and price.

Format migration is the real growth engine

Three format shifts are running at once. In laundry, powders are giving way to liquids as more households buy semi-automatic and fully automatic washing machines. Jyothy's liquid detergents roughly doubled in FY26, and HUL's annual report describes "rapid format upgradation to liquids". In dishwash, bars are giving way to liquids: Godrej Consumer describes liquid dishwash as a ~₹3,000 crore category growing at a double-digit rate with only mid-teens household penetration. In mosquito control, coils are giving way to liquid vaporisers and incense sticks; at Jyothy the electric (liquid vaporiser) plus aerosol and racquet share of household insecticides reached about 55% in FY26 from 50%. Each shift raises revenue per household. The catch, which Jyothy's chairperson spelled out on the Q1 FY27 call, is that liquids have become the main battlefield: after a year of promotions, liquid detergents sell for less than powders per wash, "be it even a premium liquid", and at a lower margin.

Rural incomes and the 2026 monsoon

Economy detergent bars and powders, dishwash bars and mosquito coils are rural-heavy products, so rural incomes matter, and in India rural incomes still track the monsoon. The 2026 south-west monsoon was poor. The India Meteorological Department's end-of-season statement (30 September 2026) put all-India rainfall at 759.4 mm, 87% of the long-period average, the fourth-lowest since 2001. The regional picture was worse for the South: the South Peninsula received 76% of normal, its fourth-lowest since 1901, and East and North-East India 74%, the lowest on record. Seventeen of 36 meteorological subdivisions, covering 42% of the country's area, were deficient.

The timing matters for reading company commentary. Jyothy's "rural resilient, urban subdued" description covered April–June, before the weak August. The rural effect of a poor monsoon usually shows up a quarter or two later, through lower farm incomes in the October–March period. For a South-skewed company such as Jyothy, Q3 and Q4 FY27 rural demand in Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh is a specific risk. A weak monsoon also cuts both ways for insecticides: less standing water can mean fewer mosquitoes, though Godrej Consumer reported share gains "despite weak monsoons, led by an intensifying El Niño", and extended heat delayed Jyothy's Q1 season.

The volume puzzle: companies grow, the panel shrinks

The most confusing number in the 2026 data is volume. These numbers cannot be reconciled naively, but four mechanisms explain most of the gap. First, company volume is sell-in to distributors, measured in tonnes; NIQ measures what consumers buy. Quick commerce is a mixed blessing. It lets premium liquids, conditioners and new launches reach urban households without the years of distributor building GT requires, and it rewards brands with strong search visibility. In plain terms

When a company says "our volumes grew 5%" and a market tracker says "volumes fell 4%", both can be right. The company counts the tonnes it shipped, including extra grams it added to each pack and share it took from weaker rivals; the tracker counts what households actually bought across the whole market. In 2026 the branded players grew by taking business from others, not because India was washing more clothes.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes, in a good year and a bad one

Half of every rupee buys chemicals and plastic. When that half grows to 60%, everything below it gets squeezed, and advertising is the first line to be cut.

The cost structure of a branded home-care company is simple and top-heavy. Materials, meaning raw materials and packaging net of inventory changes, take about half of revenue in a normal year. Staff, advertising and promotion (A&P), and "other expenses" (freight, contract conversion, power, field-sales costs, royalty, CSR) take most of the rest. Operating profit before depreciation (EBITDA) is what is left: 15–18% for a branded specialist in a good year. Depreciation is small because plants are cheap, and a cash-rich company earns some interest on its treasury. Jyothy publishes enough line items to build this table for three very different periods.

The inputs and what drives them

The materials line is a basket of perhaps a dozen inputs, but four dominate. Linear alkylbenzene (LAB), sulphonated into LABSA, is the single largest: it is the active cleaning ingredient in detergent powders, bars, many liquids and dishwash bars. Plastic packaging (HDPE and PP bottles, multi-layer pouches) is 15–20% of Jyothy's material purchases and follows naphtha and polymer prices. Palm-derived soap noodles feed laundry and toilet soaps; they follow Malaysian and Indonesian palm oil. How the other archetypes split ₹100

Jyothy's split is typical of a branded specialist, but not of everyone. The leader, HUL Home Care, does not publish a gross margin for its segment; an indicative structure would be a 45–50% gross margin, A&P of 9–11% and a reported segment EBIT of 16–19%. In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 Jyothy collected in a normal year, about ₹50 went on chemicals and plastic and about ₹17–18 was left as operating profit. In April–June 2026 the chemicals and plastic took about ₹62, so only about ₹8 was left, even after the company spent ₹2 less on advertising. Staff and freight costs do not shrink when inputs rise, so the whole increase lands on profit.

Two further points complete the picture. First, the gross-margin decline began well before the war. Jyothy's gross margin fell in every one of the eight quarters to Q1 FY27, and 4 of the 11.7 points it lost from Q1 FY25 to Q1 FY27 had already gone by Q4 FY26, through price cuts and extra grammage in dishwash and a mix shift toward lower-margin liquids. The war then took 6.7 points in one quarter. Second, the shock passes through with a lag in both directions. The CFO said higher-cost purchases and contracted inventory would "continue to flow through Q2", with relief "more meaningfully from October", and that the Q2 FY27 margin would be "not materially lower" than Q1's 8.4%. Crude in the bottle: the detergent cost stack and the LAB–soda ash chain Deep dive

A kilogram of detergent is mostly salt-based fillers held together by a petrochemical. When that petrochemical is scarce, its makers set the price, not the brands that sell it.

The single most useful thing to understand about Indian home care is what is inside a pack of detergent powder, because that formula decides who wins in a cost shock. A typical mid-tier powder contains 15–20% LABSA, the surfactant that lifts dirt; 25–30% soda ash, which softens water and boosts cleaning; around 40% sodium sulphate or similar filler, which adds bulk and helps the powder flow; and a few per cent of zeolite or phosphate builders, enzymes, optical brighteners and perfume. Economy powders use less LABSA and more filler; premium and machine ("matic") powders use more actives and enzymes. Detergent bars and dishwash bars are LABSA held in a calcite or filler matrix. Liquids swap fillers for water and use LABSA together with SLES, a second surfactant family made from ethylene oxide or palm-derived fatty alcohols.

Why LAB is the swing factor

LAB is not exotic, but its supply is concentrated. India has a handful of producers and imports the balance, much of it from West Asia. When the war began on 28 February 2026 and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted, two things happened at once: the feedstock cost rose with crude (Brent peaked near $118 a barrel before the 7 April ceasefire and then settled around $95), and physical supply tightened as vessels were delayed and allocations cut. Jyothy's CFO described crude as having "swung from … 60, 65 to upwards of 100" and LABSA as up 60–65% from January to early May. He added that the basket inflation of 30–35% "has never happened in the recent memory of the company".

The clearest evidence of where the value went comes from the supplier side. Tamilnadu Petroproducts, a listed LAB producer, reported Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹784 crore (+67%) and an EBITDA margin of 15.9%, up from 11.2% a year earlier. In the same quarter Jyothy's EBITDA margin fell from 16.5% to 8.4%. Nirma, which makes both LAB and soda ash, sits on both sides of that spread. For a formulator that buys LAB, "brand power" did not protect against a supplier with pricing power in a shortage. The commodity-chemicals report (Indian commodity chemicals) covers the LAB and soda-ash producers in more depth.

Soda ash: the input that went the other way

Soda ash softened the blow for powder makers. Roughly 15–18% of Indian soda-ash demand goes into detergents. Global supply has been heavy: Chinese light-grade soda ash traded around RMB 1,156 a tonne in June 2026, a deep downturn, and imports from China, the US and Turkey pushed Indian prices down. India has a minimum import price of ₹20,108 a tonne and a safeguard investigation due to conclude around 16 November 2026. For a detergent maker that buys soda ash, such as Jyothy or HUL, cheap soda ash is good news and a safeguard duty would be a small cost increase. For an integrated maker such as Nirma or RSPL, weak soda ash hurts the chemicals side: CRISIL cites "softening of realisations in the soda ash segment" as one reason RSPL's EBITDA margin fell to 13.1% in FY25. Nirma's US soda-ash subsidiary, Searles Valley Minerals, filed for Chapter 11 on 15 June 2026.

Why prices could not follow

Three features of the category kept prices from following costs. Price points are sticky: much of the volume sells in ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20 packs, and moving a ₹10 pack to ₹12 loses shoppers, so companies cut grams instead, slowly. Leadership is concentrated: no follower can price up until HUL does, and HUL priced cautiously because it was pursuing volume (Section 9). And the shock was expected to be temporary: after the 7 April ceasefire, nobody wanted to be the brand that took a 15% increase just before input prices fell back. Jyothy took about 3% in Q1 and 4–4.5% cumulatively, with Personal Care up 9–10%, Fabric about 5%, and dishwash "competitive", a polite word for flat.

The sensitivity every analyst should carry

On revenue of about ₹3,000 crore, each percentage point of gross margin is worth about ₹30 crore of EBITDA, about ₹22 crore of net profit after tax, and about ₹0.60 of earnings per share on 36.7 crore shares (ILLUSTRATIVE, holding other costs constant). An indicative recovery path: if LABSA and packaging fall back about 25% from their peak in the second half of FY27 and Jyothy holds its 4–4.5% price increase, the gross margin could return to 44–46%; with A&P restored to about 8%, EBITDA margin would be around 12–14% in H2 FY27 and 15–16% in FY28. Management's own language is "H2 substantially better than H1" and a long-run "swing back to the historical margin levels". That recovery has two conditions: crude stays below its spring peak, and HUL does not use the relief to cut prices again.

In plain terms

A kilogram of washing powder is mostly cheap white minerals, held together by one oil-based chemical that does the cleaning. When that chemical's price jumped 60%, the cost of making the powder jumped by roughly a third. To keep earning the same profit per kilogram, companies would have needed to raise prices by about 17%; they raised them by 4–5%. The firms that make the chemical earned more; the firms that sell the powder earned less.

Management's response: fewer crude-linked inputs

Jyothy says it has been working "for the last 1, 1.5 years" on reducing crude-linked raw materials, and the chairperson called 2026 "a very big lesson learned and we've paid a huge price". The options are limited. Plant-based surfactants from coconut or palm swap crude risk for palm risk, and Jyothy already flags soap-noodle inflation from June 2026. Bio-surfactants (rhamnolipids, sophorolipids) are expensive and not yet at scale. Re-formulation to use less LABSA per wash, value engineering in packaging, and shifting the mix toward Personal Care (Margo soap, which also benefits from the 5% GST rate) are the near-term levers. None of these changes the basic fact that a detergent maker that does not make its own LAB is a price-taker on its largest input.

Only two listed companies disclose a home-care segment on a comparable basis: HUL (Home Care: fabric wash and household care) and Jyothy (Fabric Care plus Home Care). Adding them gives a "listed home-care pool" of ₹26,296 crore of FY26 revenue and ₹4,935 crore of segment profit, an 18.8% margin as a ratio of sums. Jyothy is 10.0% of that pool by revenue and 8.4% by profit. Godrej Consumer's standalone Home Care (FY26 figure not on the same basis) and Dabur's home care (not disclosed) are excluded.

Distribution: the moat that still matters

The single biggest structural advantage in Indian home care is distribution reach, because a low-ticket, high-frequency product sells where it is stocked. HUL reaches more than 9 million outlets. Jyothy reaches about 4 million, 1.4 million of them directly, through more than 10,000 channel partners. RSPL's Ghadi reaches 2.8–3.0 million through 3,700 stockists and distributors. Direct reach decides whether a company controls its shelf, its credit terms and its retail promotions, and whether it gets paid in advance: Jyothy sells much of its GT volume on advance payment.

Imports, China and the organised share

Finished home-care goods are barely traded: they are bulky and cheap per kilogram. The trade exposure is in inputs. LAB and LABSA come partly from West Asia; pyrethroid actives from China; polymer resins and perfumes from global suppliers. The 2026 war showed the risk of that exposure, and the weak rupee, near ₹95 to the dollar, added to it. China's influence is indirect: its soda-ash oversupply helped Indian detergent makers in 2026 even as its chemicals capacity pressured Indian chemical producers. Fabric care and dishwash are an estimated 75–85% organised and branded; the unorganised share is higher in bars and in insecticides, where unregistered incense sticks are the fastest-growing format of all.

In plain terms

HUL sells roughly nine rupees of home care for every rupee Jyothy sells in the same aisles. The big names you rarely see in stock-market tables, Ghadi, Nirma, Tide and Harpic's owner, sell far more than Jyothy does too. That matters because the price on the shelf is set by these giants. A smaller company can win an aisle the giants ignore, as Jyothy did with whitener, but where the giants fight, it follows.

Five ways to run a home-care business

The scale leader, the niche specialist, the integrated economy maker, the insecticide specialist and the digital newcomer earn similar margins in good years by completely different routes, and suffer differently in bad ones.

The companies in this sub-sector fall into five business models. The distinction matters because each model has a different answer to the three questions that decide home-care profits: who sets the price, how exposed the cost base is to crude, and what the company spends to defend its shelf.

1. The scale leader

HUL Home Care is the reference model. It covers every rung of the price ladder in laundry (Wheel, Rin, Surf excel, Comfort) and dishwash (Vim bars and liquids, Sun liquid at the value end), so it can fight a challenger at whatever price point the challenger chooses. It spends more on advertising in absolute rupees than any rival can, reaches more than 9 million outlets, and draws on Unilever's global formulation science. Above all it sets prices. When it cut prices and added grammage in FY26, followers had to match; when it priced up cautiously in H1 FY27 while pursuing volume, followers could not price ahead of it. Its segment margin, 19.1% in FY26, is at or above most global home-care businesses. Its weakness is size: it cannot grow much faster than the market except by taking share, and in 2026 it chose to do exactly that.

2. The niche specialist

Jyothy built a ₹2,944 crore business by owning categories the giants under-invested in: fabric whitener (Ujala), dishwash bars (Exo), coils (Maxo), and a portfolio of licensed and acquired detergent brands from the 2011 Henkel India deal. The model is frugal (A&P of 7–9%), regionally deep (strongest in the South), cash-generative and capital-light. Its weaknesses showed in 2026. About 90% of revenue is crude-exposed home care, against roughly 37% at HUL. It is a price-follower in contested aisles. And its media weight is too small to protect pricing in a downturn. The same exposure works in reverse when costs fall: Jyothy's EBITDA margin went from 12.7% in FY23 to 17.4% in FY24 as commodities eased, a faster recovery than any diversified peer.

3. The integrated economy maker

RSPL and Nirma sell the cheapest branded detergents in India and still earned margins comparable to HUL's in good years. RSPL's EBITDA margin was 18.3% in FY23 and 19.2% in FY24 according to CRISIL. They do it by making their own soda ash (RSPL has 1,500 tonnes a day of capacity and is adding 750 tonnes a day for ₹800–1,000 crore; Nirma also makes LAB), placing plants close to heartland markets to cut freight, and spending very little on advertising. FY25 showed the limit of the model: integration protects cost but not volume. When Ghadi volumes fell after a product recall, RSPL's margin dropped to 13.1% on lower fixed-cost absorption and weaker soda-ash prices. Section 8 is a deep dive on these players.

4. The insecticide-centric specialist

Godrej Consumer's Home Care business is built around household insecticides (Good Knight, HIT) and air fresheners (aer). Insecticides reward a different set of skills: regulatory registration (every molecule and formulation needs approval under the Insecticides Act), molecule innovation (GCPL owns a patented in-house molecule, Renofluthrin), and an installed base of liquid-vaporiser machines that generate recurring refill sales. Margins are high and seasonal. GCPL is now using this platform to enter Jyothy's other aisles: Godrej Fab in liquid detergents, Spic and Rizz in dishwash.

5. The digital newcomer

Plant-based and "eco" detergent brands sold online, and private labels on quick-commerce platforms, are small and mostly loss-making. Their significance is that they raise the innovation bar (Jyothy's annual report notes that "founder-led, digital-first brands have raised innovation benchmarks") and that quick-commerce platforms can place their own labels next to national brands at a lower price.

The lesson from the five models is that margin level says little about resilience. RSPL and HUL Home Care earned similar margins in FY24 from opposite ends of the ladder; Jyothy was only a few points behind. What differs is what each model does when something goes wrong. The leader can choose to sacrifice margin for share and still earn 16%. The specialist cannot choose: it takes the cost shock in full and must follow the leader's pricing. The integrated economy maker is protected on input costs but exposed to its own brand problems. The insecticide specialist depends on weather and regulation more than on crude. A diversified FMCG company carries home care as one division among several, which is why the market pays it a higher multiple for the same category exposure (Section 15).

In plain terms

There are several ways to make good money selling detergent. Be so big that you set the price everyone else follows. Be the cheapest and make your own raw materials. Or find small corners the giants ignore. In good years all three earn similar margins. In bad years the giant chooses how much to suffer, the cheapest player is protected on costs, and the corner-finder simply suffers.
