Indian water treatment: the payer matters more than the plant

00

The one-page view: three companies, one question — who pays?

India makes about three and a half times more sewage than it treats, and the Gulf needs more desalinated water than ever. The constraint is not demand. It is money, and how fast it arrives.

Water treatment looks like a simple growth story. India generates about 72,368 million litres of sewage a day (MLD) and, on the last national inventory by the Central Pollution Control Board (2020-21 data), treated only about 20,236 MLD of it. Eighteen per cent of rural households still had no tap connection when the Cabinet extended the Jal Jeevan Mission in March 2026. Gulf states that already run about half the world's desalination capacity keep ordering more, and in 2026 they found out what it means when missiles hit the plants they have. Demand for clean water is non-negotiable and non-discretionary. So why did one of the three listed specialists report a quarterly profit of ₹3 crore in the June 2026 quarter, and why did another's operating cash flow stay negative for three years running?

The answer is the theme of this report. In water treatment, the buyer is almost never the person who drinks the water. It is a funding chain: a Union mission in Delhi, a state water board, a municipal body, a multilateral lender in Tokyo or Manila, a sovereign water authority in Riyadh or Kuwait, or a refinery's capex committee. Each of them pays on a different clock. The size of an order tells you very little until you know who is paying for it and when the money will turn up. A ₹500 crore sewage plant for a cash-strapped state can be worth less to a contractor than a ₹200 crore plant for a Gulf utility that pays an advance and opens a letter of credit.

Three companies make up the NSE 750 listing of this sub-sector, and they are three different businesses. Va Tech Wabag (WABAG) is an asset-light designer and builder of large water and sewage plants with a 15–20 year operations tail, a desalination specialist, and 52% of its revenue overseas. Ion Exchange (India) (IONEXCHANG) is an integrated chemistry-plus-engineering company: it makes ion-exchange resins and water chemicals, builds industrial water systems, services them, and sells home purifiers. Enviro Infra Engineers (EIEL) is a young government sewage and water contractor that used its November 2024 IPO money to add hybrid-annuity sewage concessions and then turned about 45% of its order book towards solar, wind and battery storage.

Together they reported FY26 revenue of ₹8,005 crore (up 12.8%), operating profit of ₹969 crore (a 12.1% margin) and profit after tax of ₹701 crore. They were worth ₹19,171 crore on 22 October 2026, or 27.3 times FY26 profit. Wabag alone is 49% of the revenue, 53% of the profit and 58% of the market value. That concentration matters: a "sector" number here is mostly a Wabag number, and the three stories are moving in different directions. In the June 2026 quarter (Q1 FY27), group revenue rose 24.9% but operating profit fell 15.6% and profit after tax fell 11.7%. Wabag's profit rose on foreign-exchange gains, Ion Exchange's almost vanished, and EIEL grew revenue 49% while its profit rose only 6.5%.

Five forces shape everything else in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them.

  • Who pays, and when. The Jal Jeevan Mission's central release to states fell from ₹69,885 crore in FY24 to zero in FY26; its FY26 budget was cut from ₹67,000 crore to ₹17,000 crore at the revised estimate; contractors reported certified bills unpaid for 10–12 months. JJM 2.0, approved on 10 March 2026 with ₹8.69 lakh crore of total outlay to December 2028, restarts the tap. Multilateral and Gulf sovereign payers sit at the safe end of the spectrum; state-funded schemes at the risky end.
  • Desalination and the Gulf. Chennai's 400 MLD Perur plant (₹4,400 crore, Wabag with Metito), Saudi Arabia's 300 MLD Yanbu plant, and Kuwait's 272 MLD Doha SWRO II are the biggest tickets in the listed universe. The Middle East is 35–40% of Wabag's backlog, and in 2026 the West Asia war put drones and missiles on Gulf desalination plants in Bahrain and Kuwait.
  • The sewage gap and the annuity turn. Hybrid-annuity (HAM) sewage contracts under Namami Gange pay 40% during construction and the other 60% as performance-linked annuities over 15 years. Together with long operations-and-maintenance (O&M) contracts, they turn one-off construction revenue into income that recurs for a decade or more.
  • Industrial water and chemistry. Ion Exchange's resins and chemicals earned roughly 70% of its segment profit on under 30% of its revenue in FY26, then saw their margin fall to 9.6% in Q1 FY27 as a new resin plant at Roha started up. Refineries, solar-cell lines and chip fabs need ultrapure water; pollution rules push industry towards zero liquid discharge.
  • Working capital is the real P&L. Wabag carries ₹2,524 crore of trade receivables (234 days of revenue) but still holds ₹965 crore of net cash. Ion Exchange moved from about ₹451 crore of net cash in March 2024 to about ₹135 crore of net debt in March 2026; EIEL's unbilled work reached ₹608 crore, 53% of a year's revenue. The RBI's repo hike to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 raises the cost of carrying all of it.

The market has already drawn its conclusions. On 22 October 2026 Wabag traded at 27.8 times trailing earnings, Ion Exchange at 46.4 times a trailing profit that has collapsed to ₹98 crore, and EIEL at 16.3 times. Over the past year Wabag's share price rose 23%, Ion Exchange's fell 14% and EIEL's fell 30%. The ranking matches cash quality rather than growth: the company with net cash and hard-currency payers earns the premium, and the company with the fastest revenue growth and the slowest cash conversion earns the discount.

In plain terms

Everyone agrees India needs far more water and sewage treatment. The listed companies do not make money from that need directly. They make money from contracts, and a contract is only as good as the agency paying for it. In FY26 the biggest rural water scheme stopped paying for a year, and in 2026 a war reached the Gulf plants that provide the best-paying work. The three companies were hit in three different ways, which is why the group cannot be read as one stock.

The rest of the report follows a logical order. Sections 1–3 explain the product, the money flows and the size of the opportunity, starting with the sewage gap. Sections 4–6 are the three defining arenas: who pays (a deep dive into JJM, AMRUT and HAM), desalination and the Gulf (a deep dive), and industrial water and chemistry. Sections 7–11 cover costs, competitors, business models, regulation and history. Section 12 is the KPI masterclass, the toolkit for reading any of these companies' results, and Section 13 is a deep dive into working capital, the line that decides whether profit becomes cash. Sections 14–17 compare the companies and their valuations, and the last four sections cover technology, the bull and bear cases, what to watch and a glossary. The latest reported quarter for all three is Q1 FY27, the quarter to June 2026. 01

How a litre becomes a profit: the water-treatment value chain

The money starts with a budget, passes through an owner, a technology house, an equipment supplier and a civil contractor, and only then reaches the plant that cleans the water. Value is captured where the know-how is scarce.

A water-treatment plant is a chain of physical processes: screens and settling tanks, biological reactors in which bacteria eat organic waste, filters and membranes that strip out particles and salts, chemicals that disinfect and stabilise, and pumps that move it all. What the listed companies sell is the ability to design that chain so that it reliably meets a quality standard, to build it on time, and often to run it for years afterwards. The customer pays for a guarantee: so many million litres a day, at so many milligrams per litre of biochemical oxygen demand or salt, for so many kilowatt-hours per cubic metre.

The products differ widely. A seawater reverse-osmosis (SWRO) desalination plant pushes seawater through membranes at 55–70 bar, recovering energy with pressure exchangers; it consumes about 3–4 kWh per cubic metre and is the most technology-heavy job in the sector. A municipal sewage treatment plant (STP) is a large civil structure with a biological process inside: activated sludge, sequencing batch reactors (SBR), moving-bed biofilm reactors (MBBR) or membrane bioreactors (MBR). A water treatment plant (WTP) cleans river or reservoir water for drinking. Industrial systems produce demineralised water for boilers, ultrapure water (UPW) for semiconductor and solar-cell fabs, and zero-liquid-discharge (ZLD) trains for polluting factories. Behind all of them sit consumables: ion-exchange resins replaced every few years, membranes, antiscalants, biocides and coagulants.

What each company sells

The three listed companies sit on different parts of the chain. Wabag is a Step 3 company that wraps Steps 4–6 around its designs: it owns the process engineering (125-plus intellectual-property rights, by its own count), buys equipment, subcontracts civil works and then runs the plant. Its CFO put it plainly on the August 2026 call: "construction for us is a pass-through". Ion Exchange covers Steps 3, 4 and 6 for industry, and is the only Indian company that makes the resins and membranes inside the plant as well as designing the plant. EIEL is mainly a Step 5 company with Step 3 capability: it designs and builds government sewage plants itself, including the concrete, which is why its margins and its working capital both look so different from Wabag's.

Where the value is captured

Margins follow scarcity. Process know-how and references for hard plants (large desalination, ultrapure water, reuse) are held by a handful of firms worldwide, so design-build and equipment-and-process (EP) contracts without civil works carry the best margins. Consumables earn recurring margin from the installed base: Ion Exchange's chemicals segment earned about 25% EBIT margin in FY25 and 21% in FY26, while its engineering segment earned 5–6%. O&M earns a little more than construction and uses almost no capital. Civil construction earns least and absorbs the most working capital, because it is the part that state agencies measure, certify and pay slowly. EIEL's 24% EBITDA margin is the exception that proves the rule: it comes from in-house design on a narrow range of plants, and it is paid for with a working-capital cycle that has stretched sharply.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes

The cost structure shows the business model. At Wabag, roughly ₹76 of every ₹100 goes on cost of sales (equipment, materials and subcontracted civil work), about ₹11 on staff and overheads net of a forex gain, and about ₹13 is left as EBITDA. Below EBITDA very little leaks: depreciation is trivial because the company owns ₹66 crore of fixed assets on ₹3,944 crore of revenue, and net bank charges and interest cost under ₹1. PAT is about ₹9.4. At EIEL, materials and subcontracts take ₹67, people and other costs ₹9, and EBITDA is ₹24, but interest, depreciation on new power assets and tax take more, leaving PAT of about ₹16. Ion Exchange spends far more on people and overheads, because it runs factories, a consumer brand and a services network, and ended FY26 with only ₹7 of EBITDA and ₹4.9 of PAT per ₹100.

In plain terms

A water company is paid for knowing how to make dirty water clean, reliably, for years. Pouring the concrete is the cheap part of that knowledge and the expensive part of the balance sheet. The companies that own the process and let someone else pour the concrete keep less of each rupee of revenue as cost but carry far less risk; the ones that do the concrete themselves can earn wider margins, but wait longer for the money.

One more feature of the chain explains a lot of the industry's behaviour: the buyer usually picks the lowest qualified bid. Municipal tenders in India are mostly awarded to the lowest price among bidders who meet the technical criteria, and Gulf tenders work the same way, with an extra test for local partners. That makes references (proof that you have built this kind of plant before) the real barrier to entry, and it pushes margins towards the cost of the weakest qualified bidder. Wabag's claim to be "European quality at Indian prices" is a pitch aimed at exactly this structure: its engineers in India, Turkey and Egypt give it an overhead advantage over Spanish and French rivals in the same tenders.

02

Size: follow the budgets, not the market-research reports

No rating agency publishes a clean size for India's water-treatment market, and the market-research estimates contradict each other. The public outlays and the order books are the better measure.

Search for "India water treatment market size" and you will find a range of answers: a wastewater-services market of about $3 billion in 2025 (MarketsandMarkets), a treatment-technology market of $3 billion growing at 9.6% (Mordor Intelligence), a broad wastewater market of $6.7 billion (TechSci), and a "water market" of $17.9 billion by FY29 from an unnamed publisher. These numbers measure different things and rest on different assumptions, and none comes from CRISIL, ICRA, CARE or India Ratings. Even EIEL's IPO prospectus relied on a report it commissioned from a consulting firm, which gives only global figures. This report treats them all as low-quality and leads with the money governments and companies have actually committed.

The public pool is large. The Jal Jeevan Mission's second phase, approved by the Cabinet on 10 March 2026, carries a total outlay of ₹8.69 lakh crore to December 2028, of which central assistance is ₹3.59 lakh crore (up from ₹2.08 lakh crore approved in 2019-20). AMRUT 2.0, the urban water and sewerage mission launched in October 2021, has an outlay of ₹2.99 lakh crore including ₹76,760 crore of central money, and has been extended to March 2027. Namami Gange, the Ganga clean-up programme, has received about ₹42,500 crore of approved outlay across its two phases. Add state budgets, municipal bonds, multilateral loans and industrial capex, and the addressable construction spend on municipal and industrial water and wastewater was of the order of ₹1.5–2.5 lakh crore a year at the JJM peak in FY24, and under ₹1 lakh crore in FY26 (an INDICATIVE range built from central releases plus matching state shares).

How the three have grown

The members' combined revenue grew from ₹4,408 crore in FY21 to ₹8,005 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 12.7% a year. Profit grew faster, from ₹253 crore to ₹701 crore (22.6% a year), but unevenly: FY23 was the year Wabag wrote off about ₹289 crore of receivables and its profit fell to ₹11 crore. The growth came from very different places. EIEL's revenue rose more than ninefold from a base of ₹124 crore, almost all of it from JJM-era water-supply schemes in Madhya Pradesh and then sewage plants. Ion Exchange doubled through refinery and municipal water jobs, exports and chemicals. Wabag, the largest, grew only 6.8% a year over five years because it spent FY21–FY24 shrinking deliberately: it exited loss-making European units, bid selectively and rebuilt its balance sheet before a 19.7% jump in FY26.

Desalination: a global market concentrated in the Gulf

Outside India, the largest single market for the listed companies is desalination in the Gulf. The International Desalination Association counted about 97 million cubic metres a day of installed capacity in 2018, and the International Energy Agency reported in March 2026 that installed capacity grew about 40% between 2020 and 2025. Roughly half of global capacity sits in the Middle East and North Africa; Saudi Arabia alone was reported at more than 16 million cubic metres a day by the end of 2025. These global figures are snippet-level and INDICATIVE, but the direction is clear: Gulf states plan to keep adding capacity to 2030, Saudi Arabia has named 2027 its "Year of Water", and the 2026 strikes on Kuwaiti and Bahraini plants have made redundancy a security question. India by contrast has one city on desalination at scale, Chennai, which will reach about 750 MLD (0.75 million cubic metres a day) once Perur runs.

In plain terms

The size of the "market" is less useful here than the size of the budgets and how much of them is paid out. The government has promised ₹8.69 lakh crore for rural water to 2028 and ₹2.99 lakh crore for urban water and sewage. The three listed specialists earned ₹8,005 crore last year. They do not need more of the promise; they need more of it to be spent, on the kinds of plants where know-how wins the tender.

03

The sewage gap: 72,368 MLD made, about 20,000 treated

India's sewage problem is the largest long-run engine for the industry, and the slowest to turn into orders. Hybrid annuities and long O&M contracts are how it is being made bankable.

The Central Pollution Control Board's last national inventory, published in March 2021 from 2020-21 data, is still the reference. It found India's towns and cities generating 72,368 MLD of sewage against an installed treatment capacity of 31,841 MLD. Only 26,869 MLD of that capacity was operational, and only about 20,236 MLD of sewage was actually treated. Put differently, about 28% of urban sewage was treated; the rest went into rivers, lakes, the sea and the ground. The board has not repeated the inventory since. Newer official estimates disagree: NITI Aayog used 28% in 2022, while the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs puts the share treated at 43%. The truth is probably between the two, and both say that more than half of India's urban sewage is not treated.

The gap has three layers, and each is a business opportunity. The first is capacity: roughly 40,000 MLD more is needed just to match today's sewage, and urbanisation adds more every year. The second is utilisation: about 5,000 MLD of installed capacity was not working, and plants that run below design are often missing sewer connections, power or competent operators. The third is quality: older plants were built to discharge standards set in 2017, while the National Green Tribunal's April 2019 order asked new plants to meet much tighter limits for biochemical oxygen demand, nitrogen and phosphorus. Tighter limits favour tertiary treatment, SBR and MBR processes and the companies that can guarantee them.

The missions that pay for it

Three programmes fund most new urban sewage capacity. Namami Gange, run by the National Mission for Clean Ganga (NMCG), targets 7,000 MLD of new or rehabilitated STP capacity in the Ganga basin by December 2026; by December 2025 it had completed 3,806 MLD (58%) across 138 sewage projects, up from about 1,858 MLD at the time of EIEL's late-2024 prospectus. By August 2026, 6,815 MLD had been taken up but only 658 MLD was operational: under 10%, five years into the scheme. The first AMRUT, by comparison, had 5,396 of its 6,299 MLD operational (86%). The annuity turn: HAM and long O&M

Before 2016, most Indian STPs were built as plain construction contracts: the government paid as work was certified, and then often handed the plant to a municipal body that could not run it. Plants decayed, and capacity sat idle. The hybrid annuity model (HAM), approved for Namami Gange in January 2016, changed the incentives. The concessionaire designs, builds, finances part of and then operates the plant. NMCG pays 40% of the quoted capital cost during construction, against milestones. The remaining 60% is paid back over 15 years in quarterly annuities with interest on the outstanding balance, together with a separate O&M fee. Both the annuity and the O&M fee are linked to performance: if the plant does not meet its discharge standard, payments are cut.

In plain terms

India makes about three and a half times more sewage than it treats. The government is paying to close the gap, but slowly: of the sewage capacity its main urban mission has started since 2021, less than a tenth is running. The newer contracts pay builders over 15 years for plants that keep working, instead of paying once for plants that might not. That is good for the rivers and for the quality of the contractors' income, and it means more of their money arrives later.

The sewage gap also explains why the long-run growth case for the sector survives a bad year like FY26. A country with 11.3 crore urban households, of which only 3.5 crore are connected to sewers, cannot stop building treatment plants without its rivers getting visibly worse, and the courts (through the National Green Tribunal) have repeatedly ordered states to fix that. The question is how quickly budgets turn into paid work. That is the subject of the first deep dive.

04

Who pays, and when: JJM, AMRUT, NMCG HAM and the payment-risk spectrum Deep dive

FY26 proved that the biggest water budget in India can stop paying for a year. Every order book in this sector has to be read through the quality and the clock of the agency behind it.

An Indian water contract passes through more hands than almost any other kind of infrastructure work. The Union government announces a mission and allocates money in the budget. The Ministry releases its share to a state, often only after the state has spent and accounted for the previous instalment. The state adds its own share, which depends on its finances. A state water and sanitation mission, Jal Nigam or public health engineering department awards the work, measures progress, certifies bills and pays. If any link in that chain slows down, the contractor keeps working on its own money until the bills are cleared, or slows the work and risks penalties. The cash cycle of a water company is therefore set less by its own efficiency than by where it sits on this chain.

The year the tap ran dry: JJM in FY26

The Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) was launched on 15 August 2019 to give every rural household a functional tap connection by 2024. It was funded 50:50 between the Centre and the states (90:10 for north-eastern and Himalayan states, 100% central for Union Territories), with an original outlay of ₹3.06 lakh crore including ₹2.08 lakh crore of central money. It was spectacularly successful at connecting homes: from 3.23 crore households (17%) at launch, coverage rose to 15.80 crore of 19.36 crore (81.61%) by March 2026. A Lok Sabha answer reported in the press put it at 82.19% in August 2026 (a single-source figure, INDICATIVE).

The money did not keep pace with the ambition. Central releases rose from ₹9,952 crore in FY20 to a peak of ₹69,885 crore in FY24, as states rushed to meet the 2024 deadline and awarded vast numbers of pipe-laying contracts. Then the tap was turned off. In FY25 the budget estimate of about ₹70,000 crore was cut to ₹22,694 crore at the revised stage. In FY26, PRS Legislative Research records zero central allocation and zero release to states, with only about ₹232 crore of old unspent funds used; the FY26 budget estimate of ₹67,000 crore was cut to ₹17,000 crore at the revised estimate, a fall of 75%. The cost per household connected had meanwhile risen from under ₹47,000 (2020 estimate) to above ₹50,000 since FY24, because the last households are the most remote and the hardest to serve.

JJM 2.0: the restart, with strings

AMRUT 2.0 and NMCG: slower, and safer

Urban schemes run on a different clock. AMRUT 2.0 is a central scheme with state and municipal co-funding; by August 2026 the Centre had released ₹22,763 crore of the ₹66,060 crore it had committed, 34%. Payments therefore depend heavily on state budgets and municipal finances, and on how fast projects clear technical and financial milestones. Contractors on AMRUT and state-funded STPs typically see revenue bunch into the fourth quarter, when states spend before the financial year closes: EIEL earned 37% of its FY26 revenue in the March quarter, and 63% in FY23.

Namami Gange HAM projects sit higher on the quality ladder because the payer is NMCG, a central agency with its own budget line (₹3,100 crore in FY27, against ₹2,687 crore in the FY26 revised estimate). That does not make it fast: NMCG disbursed ₹20,430 crore against an estimated ₹26,825 crore over its life (76%), and the Public Accounts Committee criticised low utilisation and delayed utilisation certificates in 2024. But the payment is contractual, quarterly once the plant runs, and backed by central money rather than a state's spare cash.

Multilateral and Gulf sovereign money

At the safe end of the spectrum sit projects funded by multilateral lenders and Gulf governments. JICA funds Chennai's Perur desalination plant; ADB funds the Chennai ring-main looped water grid that Wabag is building on a design-build-operate basis; the World Bank funds state water and sewerage programmes, and EIEL cites its "execution experience in World Bank-funded projects". In these projects the money is ring-fenced in a loan agreement, procurement is slow and document-heavy, but payment is reliable. Gulf sovereign clients such as Saudi Arabia's Water Authority (SWA) and Kuwait's Ministry of Electricity, Water and Renewable Energy pay in currencies pegged to the dollar, often against advances and letters of credit. Their risks are of a different kind: award risk (the Yanbu cancellation described in the next section), geopolitical risk (the 2026 war) and the occasional prolonged dispute.

In plain terms

A water company's order book is a list of promises from different kinds of payer. A Saudi water authority or a Japanese-funded Chennai project usually pays on time; a state rural-water scheme in a year when Delhi sends no money may not pay for a year. Two companies with the same order book can have completely different futures depending on that mix. The single most useful question to ask about any big order is: whose money is it?

The pattern across the three companies is consistent. The specialist that avoided JJM pipes (Wabag) or ring-fenced its exposure (EIEL, after FY25) came through FY26 better than civil contractors that had piled into the scheme. The company that took a large state job late in the cycle (Ion Exchange in Uttar Pradesh) is still working it off. For investors, the payer mix belongs next to the order book in any analysis, and the KPI section later in this report shows how to estimate it from the disclosures each company makes. The next deep dive looks at the opposite end of the spectrum: the Gulf, where the payers are rich and punctual, the plants are huge, and the risks are of a different kind.

Desalination and the Gulf: big plants, good payers, a war on the doorstep Deep dive

Seawater desalination is the most technology-heavy and best-referenced work in the sector. It took Wabag from Chennai to Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, and in 2026 it put the plants of its best clients within range of drones.

Desalination is the industry's prestige product. A large seawater reverse-osmosis plant needs an intake and outfall in the sea, pre-treatment that stops the membranes clogging, high-pressure pumps and energy-recovery devices that make the process affordable, thousands of membrane elements and a remineralisation stage that makes the output drinkable. Few companies in the world can guarantee such a plant's output and energy use at scale: Veolia (with Sidem and the former Suez), IDE Technologies, Acciona, Metito, Doosan and a handful of others. Va Tech Wabag claims a place among the top three private desalination plant suppliers in Global Water Intelligence's 2024 ranking. It is the only listed Indian company with that reference list, and desalination is where its largest contracts now sit.

Chennai: India's only desalination city

Chennai turned to the sea after repeated droughts. The 100 MLD Minjur plant opened in 2010; the 100–110 MLD Nemmeli plant, built by Wabag with IDE, followed in 2013; a 150 MLD second Nemmeli plant was commissioned in 2024. The Yanbu lesson: a Gulf award is provisional until it is effective

On 6 September 2024 Wabag announced, through a stock-exchange filing, that it had won the 300 MLD Yanbu seawater desalination plant from the Saudi Water Authority for about $317 million (SAR 1,190 million, roughly ₹2,700 crore). On 16 December 2024 SWA cancelled the tender "under internal administrative procedures", and Wabag's shares fell about 19% that day. The project was re-tendered; Wabag says it was the lowest bidder "in all three calls of the tender". The effective award came in July 2025 at about $272 million (SAR 1,019 million, about ₹2,332 crore) with a 30-month execution period: the same plant, about 14% cheaper. The episode is a template for reading Gulf announcements. An award is not a contract until it is signed, and a contract is not revenue until it is effective. Kuwait's Doha SWRO II was announced as a letter of award in June 2026 but signed only on 25 August 2026. Wabag has since tightened its own definitions: in Q1 FY27 it removed two framework orders worth about ₹600 crore ("signed, stamp duty paid, waiting for effectiveness") from its backlog.

In plain terms

Desalination is where Wabag's reputation earns its keep: few firms in the world can build a city-sized plant that turns seawater into drinking water, and Gulf governments pay well and on time for it. Two numbers summarise the deep dive. First, Wabag's overseas share of revenue (52%) and backlog (49%) is the main reason its working capital and margins look better than an Indian municipal contractor's. Second, the Middle East's 35–40% share of that backlog is a concentration investors should track quarter by quarter, alongside Perur's commissioning date and the outcome of the Saudi tenders announced for the "Year of Water". Industrial water, ZLD and the chemistry business

Refineries, fabs, pharma plants and polluting factories pay commercially and buy consumables forever. Ion Exchange built a resin and chemicals franchise on that logic, then hit a wall of start-up costs in FY26.

Industrial water is a different market from municipal water. The customer is a company, the payment terms are commercial, the specifications are tighter, and the plant keeps consuming chemicals, resins and membranes for its entire life. Refineries and petrochemical complexes need demineralised water for boilers and cooling, and effluent plants to meet pollution rules. Power stations need condensate polishing. Pharma plants need purified water and use resins in drug purification. Solar-cell and semiconductor fabs need ultrapure water (UPW) polished to a resistivity of about 18.2 megohm-centimetres, and generate large, complex wastewater streams. Textile, chemical, pharma and distillery clusters in water-stressed regions are pushed by pollution control boards towards zero liquid discharge (ZLD), in which effluent is treated, recovered through RO and evaporated to a solid residue.

Recent orders show where the money is. IOCL's Panipat raw-water treatment plant, described by Ion Exchange as "one of the largest water treatment packages awarded in India", was commissioned in FY26. Wabag is building BPCL Bina's water block including ZLD, won a repeat effluent-plant order from Reliance at Jamnagar on 7 September 2026, supplies UPW and ZLD for RenewSys's solar-cell plant, and is building a 100 MLD desalination plant for Indosol's solar-glass complex in Andhra Pradesh on an EP basis. EIEL has a ₹395.5 crore CETP with ZLD for MIDC at Kolhapur and sees textile ZLD (Ichalkaranji) as a growth area. Ion Exchange has won a small semiconductor UPW project with a partner, and notes that a fab's wastewater investment is larger than its UPW investment; it last did UPW for SCL Mohali about 30 years ago.

Wabag's industrial book has shrunk

Industrial work was about 30% of Wabag's revenue until FY24-25. In FY26 industrial revenue fell 3% to ₹790 crore (20% of revenue) while municipal revenue rose 27%, and industrial is now only about 12% of the backlog (₹2,314 crore). About 90% of its traditional industrial work is oil and gas. Management wants industrial back to 20–25% of order intake and describes the new industries (UPW for solar and chips, data centres, green hydrogen) as a "decadal view", expecting one or two orders in the next 12–18 months. The shift matters for cash: industrial clients pay commercially and faster than Indian municipal bodies, so a falling industrial share tends to lengthen working capital.

Roha: the resin bet

Ion-exchange resins are polymer beads, usually polystyrene cross-linked with divinylbenzene, treated so that they swap one kind of ion for another as water passes through. Every demineralisation or softening plant uses them; pharma companies use special grades to purify drugs; they are replaced every three to seven years. The global market is dominated by DuPont (AmberLite, Dowex), Lanxess (Lewatit), Purolite (owned by Ecolab since 2021) and Mitsubishi Chemical (Diaion), with Chinese makers such as Sunresin rising. Ion Exchange estimates its share of global resin volume at about 2.5% and wants 10%. Its tool is a new plant at Roha in Maharashtra, with planned capacity of 42,600 cubic metres a year. A first block of 3,696 cubic metres a year started on 30 September 2025. Management says Phase I doubles the company's resin capacity relative to the original Ankleshwar baseline, and Phase II plus de-bottlenecking at both sites takes it to about five times. Roha is India's first non-solvent resin plant, has Water Quality Association certification for the North American market, and will add an integrated spent-acid recovery plant. Separately, pharma resins made at the FDA-approved Ankleshwar site are to grow six-fold by value within about a year.

Ion Exchange sells the plant and then the consumables that keep it working, and the consumables are where most of its profit comes from. The rules that create industrial demand

Industrial water demand is partly regulatory. Central and state pollution control boards require highly polluting industries in water-stressed areas to move to ZLD, and common effluent treatment plants (CETPs) in industrial estates such as MIDC's are being upgraded. The National Framework on Safe Reuse of Treated Water (2022) and state reuse policies in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu push industry to buy treated sewage instead of fresh water, which creates tertiary-treatment and reuse contracts of the kind Wabag runs in Chennai. Thermal power plants within 50 km of a sewage plant must use treated sewage under the 2016 tariff policy. New semiconductor fabs, of which Tata Electronics' Dholera project (with PSMC) is the largest, will each need large UPW and wastewater systems. For investors, industrial water is the part of the sector least dependent on government cash flows, and the part where Ion Exchange's chemistry and Wabag's references overlap most directly with global giants such as Veolia, Kurita, Organo and Ecolab.

Inputs, the rupee and the civil pass-through

A water contractor's costs are concrete, steel, pipes, imported membranes and pumps, people and bank guarantees. Most are fixed in the bid and only partly protected; the rupee at ₹95 to the dollar helps some and hurts others.

Most water contracts are fixed-price. The contractor quotes a lump sum (or a schedule of rates) when it bids, and then bears the risk that its costs move during a construction period of two to four years. Indian government contracts usually include a price-variation clause linked to wholesale or consumer price indices, but it covers only part of the cost basket, and Gulf design-build contracts typically have none. Cost control is therefore built into the bid: the price assumptions a company makes when it wins, and how quickly it locks in suppliers and subcontractors afterwards.

Civil materials and labour are the largest cost for municipal plants: cement, reinforcement steel, ductile-iron, mild-steel and HDPE pipes, and the workforce that pours and lays them. Civil work is typically 35–60% of a municipal STP or WTP contract (INDICATIVE). Wabag subcontracts it; EIEL does much of it in-house. EIEL cut its FY27 water EBITDA guide from 22–24% to 21–22% because of input-cost increases, and its MD explained that raw materials typically move 1–2% of revenue in a year, with the price-variation clause covering only part of that. Readers who want the contractor's-eye view of these costs will find it in the civil construction report.

Imported process equipment is priced in dollars, euros or yen: membranes (DuPont FilmTec, Toray, Hydranautics–Nitto, LG Chem), high-pressure pumps (Flowserve, KSB, Danfoss), energy-recovery devices, blowers, UV systems and instruments. The rupee's slide to about ₹94.9 to the dollar in October 2026 (after a record low near ₹96.8 earlier in the year) raises the rupee cost of that kit for contracts priced in rupees. Wabag manages this with a natural hedge (its overseas revenue is earned in dollars, euros or dollar-pegged Gulf currencies, which offsets imported equipment costs) plus packing credit in foreign currency for the gap. It has no foreign-currency loans. Pumps, motors and drives also come from Indian suppliers covered in the pumps and engines report and the electrical equipment report.

Who else builds the plants: an industry of giants, generalists and a few specialists

The three listed specialists are small players in a market dominated by diversified Indian contractors at home and by European, Israeli, Gulf and Korean firms abroad.

No official data measures market shares in Indian water treatment, and the industry's structure has to be pieced together from order books and tender results. The picture is clear enough. Municipal water and wastewater construction in India is fragmented and dominated by diversified infrastructure groups. The JJM boom was won largely by civil contractors such as Larsen & Toubro (through its Water & Effluent Treatment business), Megha Engineering & Infrastructures, Tata Projects, NCC, PNC Infratech, GR Infraprojects, Kalpataru Projects and SPML Infra, plus hundreds of state-level contractors. Several of them reported large receivable problems in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh in FY25–26. Specialist process-technology players are few: Wabag, Ion Exchange, the water businesses of Thermax and Triveni Engineering, EMS Ltd (a smaller listed sewage contractor in Uttar Pradesh), and unlisted groups such as Vishvaraj Infrastructure (HAM plants and the Nagpur reuse project) and Re Sustainability (formerly Ramky).

Among the three listed members, Wabag accounts for 49% of FY26 revenue, Ion Exchange 36% and EIEL 14%. Abroad, the competitive set changes. In Gulf desalination Wabag bids against Veolia (with its Sidem desalination arm, and the Suez businesses it absorbed in 2022), IDE Technologies, Acciona Agua, Metito (its partner at Perur), Doosan Enerbility, Hyundai E&C, Fisia Italimpianti and Cobra. Developers such as ACWA Power set the tariffs in Saudi independent water projects and subcontract the construction. In industrial water and resins, Ion Exchange competes with Veolia Water Technologies, Thermax, DuPont, Lanxess, Purolite (Ecolab) and Mitsubishi Chemical, with Chinese makers growing. In consumer purifiers ZeroB is a small brand against Kent, Eureka Forbes (Aquaguard), A. O. Smith (which bought HUL's Pureit in 2025) and Livpure.

Imports and China

India imports most of its RO membranes; Ion Exchange's Goa plant, opened in 2017 as the country's first integrated RO membrane plant, is the main domestic maker. Chinese pumps, valves and blowers are increasingly used in municipal contracts to cut costs, and Chinese EPC firms compete hard in Africa and Central Asia, where Wabag also bids. Discharge norms: tighter rules, better technology

The Ministry of Environment notified national STP discharge norms in October 2017, with limits for biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) of 30 or 20 mg per litre and total suspended solids (TSS) of 100 or 50 mg per litre depending on city class. In April 2019 the National Green Tribunal ordered much stricter limits for new plants, commonly cited as BOD of 10 mg/l, chemical oxygen demand of 50, TSS of 20, total nitrogen of 10 and phosphorus of 1 mg/l. The values are as widely reported and should be checked against the order before being relied on. Meeting them requires biological nutrient removal and tertiary filtration, which favours sequencing batch reactors, membrane bioreactors and the specialists that can guarantee them; EIEL says it uses SBR technology "to meet stringent effluent quality standards set by the NGT".

Other rules shape demand at the edges. Highly polluting industries in water-stressed areas are pushed to zero liquid discharge by central and state pollution control boards. The 2022 National Framework on Safe Reuse of Treated Water and state reuse policies create tertiary treatment contracts, and the 2016 tariff policy obliges thermal power plants within 50 km of a sewage plant to use treated sewage. Courts matter too: the National Green Tribunal has repeatedly directed states to stop untreated sewage reaching rivers, and those orders often lead to tenders.

Tax and trade

For years, water supply, treatment and sewerage works for governments enjoyed a concessional 12% GST rate. That concession was narrowed from January 2022 and the specific entry was omitted from 18 July 2022, so these works have generally carried 18% GST since then, before the GST 2.0 reform of 22 September 2025; whether GST 2.0 changed anything for them has not been verified. Resins and chemicals exporters receive RoDTEP export incentives. No anti-dumping or safeguard duty protects resins or membranes in India.

The Gulf's rules

In Saudi Arabia, the Water Authority (formerly SWCC) procures EPC desalination plants directly, as at Yanbu and Aljouf, while Sharakat (formerly the Saudi Water Partnership Company) procures independent water projects from developers. Kuwait's Ministry of Electricity, Water and Renewable Energy procures EPC and O&M contracts through the Central Agency for Public Tenders, and foreign contractors need local partners: Wabag's Doha SWRO II is a joint venture with HEISCO. Local-content requirements, tender cancellations (Yanbu) and the long gap between letter of award and signed contract are features of the system that investors have to price.

In plain terms

The government regulates this industry mostly by deciding how much to spend and how strict to make the pollution rules. More spending creates orders; stricter rules decide that those orders go to companies with better technology rather than the cheapest builder. In the Gulf, the rules are about local partners and patience: an award can be cancelled, and a signed contract can take months to follow.

A century in twenty events: from Breslau to the JJM bust

The listed companies carry the history of European water engineering, of India's mission era, and of one painful lesson about who pays.

The sector's history explains its present shape. Wabag's roots are German and Austrian: the company was founded in Breslau (now Wrocław) in 1924 and passed through Deutsche Babcock, VA Tech and Siemens before its Indian managers bought the Indian business in 2005 with backing from ICICI Venture, and then bought the Austrian parent group from Siemens in 2007. It listed in 2010. Ion Exchange was formed in 1964 as a subsidiary of Permutit of the UK and became wholly Indian in 1985, having started making resins at Ankleshwar in 1979. EIEL was incorporated in 2009, built its first major plant (an 18 MLD CETP at Balotra) for ₹16 crore, won its first hybrid-annuity project at Bareilly in 2021, and listed on 29 November 2024.

The second cycle is JJM's boom and bust. Reading the numbers: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Water companies are project businesses. Revenue is a backlog being burned, profit is a margin set at the bid, and cash is whatever the payer releases. These are the numbers that tell you which is which.

A water-treatment company's income statement is the last step in a long chain. Orders are won months or years before they produce revenue; revenue is booked as work progresses, often long before it is billed; bills are paid when the payer's budget allows. To read these companies well you need to follow the chain from order intake to cash, and to know where each company's definitions differ. This section sets out the equation that links the steps, then works through fourteen native KPIs one by one: what each is, how to calculate it, a worked example, what moves it, a good-average-weak benchmark, how the three listed companies compare and the trap in its definition. It ends with an illustrative anatomy of one project, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected in one place and a ten-minute routine for reading a quarterly result.

1. Order book and order-book cover

What it is. The order book (or backlog) is the value of contracted work not yet executed. Order-book cover divides it by annual revenue to show how many years of work are in hand. Formula: order book ÷ trailing-12-month revenue. Worked example: Wabag's ₹19,394 crore book at June 2026 divided by its trailing revenue of ₹4,097 crore gives 4.7 times. What moves it: new orders add to it; execution subtracts; currency movements re-value overseas contracts (₹577 crore of Wabag's FY26 backlog movement was forex and variations); cancellations and descoping remove orders. Benchmark: 3–5 times with at least a 30% annual burn is good; 2–3 times is average; under 1.5 times, or above 6 times with a burn under 20%, is weak, because the first means revenue will shrink and the second means the book is not converting.

How the three compare. 4. EBITDA margin, and the forex and other-income trap

What it is. Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation as a share of revenue: the profit the company makes from its work before financing and accounting charges. Formula: (revenue − operating costs) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Wabag's FY26 company-basis EBITDA was ₹524.1 crore on revenue of ₹3,944.2 crore, 13.3%; it includes a ₹46.7 crore forex gain, so the ex-forex margin is 12.1% (₹477.4 crore), almost exactly screener's operating profit of ₹478 crore. 5. O&M share of revenue and backlog

What it is. The share of revenue (or backlog) that comes from operating and maintaining plants rather than building them. It is the closest thing this sector has to recurring revenue. Worked example: Wabag earned ₹682.4 crore from O&M in FY26 out of ₹3,908.4 crore of segment revenue, 17.5%; its O&M backlog of ₹6,666 crore is 34% of its order book. What drives it: the completion of construction projects that carry O&M tails (Ghaziabad and Digha-Kankarbagh in 2026, Perur in FY28), standalone O&M wins (AMAS in Bahrain, UPJN's Agra and Ghaziabad contracts), and contract escalation. Benchmark: 20% or more of revenue is good, 10–20% average, under 10% weak.

How the three compare. Wabag 17% of FY26 revenue and 18% of Q1 FY27, against a target of 20%; O&M grew only 7% in Q1 FY27, which management says should be judged on a two-to-three-year view. 7. Receivable days and retention

What it is. How long, on average, billed money takes to arrive, and how much is held back as retention until a plant passes its defect-liability period. Formula: trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365; retention is often reported separately as a current or non-current financial asset. Worked example: Wabag's trade receivables of ₹2,524 crore at March 2026 against ₹3,944 crore of revenue give 234 days; add ₹648 crore of non-current retention and the figure rises to about 294 days (INDICATIVE). What drives it: payer quality, the Q4 bunching of government billing, disputes and arbitration (Wabag's ~₹140 crore TSGENCO award is still open despite a Supreme Court ruling in its favour), and retention terms, typically 5–10% of each running bill held for one to five years (a well-established industry norm rather than a disclosed figure). Benchmark: including contract assets, under 150 days is good, 150–250 average, above 250 weak.

How the three compare. Screener debtor days: Wabag 234, Ion Exchange 135, EIEL 53. But the ageing matters as much as the level. Wabag's billed receivables more than three years overdue rose from ₹46 crore to ₹225 crore in FY26, and those more than a year overdue reached ₹546 crore, 21% of billed receivables; its expected-credit-loss provision nearly doubled to ₹103 crore. Ion Exchange's receivables over a year overdue rose from ₹103 crore to ₹153 crore. The trap: EIEL's 53 days is an illusion, because most of what it is owed has not yet been billed (the next KPI). 8. Unbilled revenue and contract assets

What it is. Revenue recognised for work done but not yet invoiced, because the client has not measured or certified it. Under Ind AS 115 it appears as a contract asset or "unbilled revenue", usually inside other current financial assets. Formula: unbilled revenue ÷ annual revenue (or × 365 for days). Worked example: EIEL's gross unbilled revenue rose from ₹265.6 crore at March 2025 to ₹608.0 crore at March 2026; against FY26 revenue of ₹1,146 crore that is 53% of a year's revenue, or about 194 days. What drives it: the speed of measurement and certification by state engineers, the timing of milestones, and Q4 bunching (EIEL earned 37% of FY26 revenue in the March quarter). Benchmark: under 10% of revenue is good, 10–25% average, above 25% weak.

How the three compare. EIEL 53%, Ion Exchange 8% (₹232.9 crore, tripled from ₹74.4 crore), Wabag not separately analysed here (its contract assets sit within its working-capital disclosure). EIEL's management describes its working capital as "still bloated" but says it has "never" had a bad debt.

9. Net working-capital days

What it is. The net amount of money tied up in running the business (receivables, unbilled work, retention and inventory, minus payables and customer advances) expressed in days of revenue. Formula: (current operating assets − current operating liabilities) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: Wabag reports NWC of 104 days for FY26 and 108 days in Q1 FY27; on ₹3,944 crore of revenue, 104 days is about ₹1,120 crore of capital. What drives it: receivables and unbilled work on one side; advances from clients (common in the Gulf) and supplier credit on the other. Benchmark: under 90 days is good, 90–130 average, above 150 weak. Wabag's management calls 100–110 days "doing very well" and notes that its NWC has fallen from 130 to about 100 days over three years.

How the three compare. Wabag 104–108 days (company basis); EIEL 137 days on screener's working-capital measure, up from 24 in FY24 and 89 in FY25; Ion Exchange 53 days on screener, up from minus 27 in FY22, as unbilled work, inventory (₹441 crore) and overdue receivables rose. What it is. The share of operating profit that turns into operating cash flow. Benchmark: above 70% on a three-year average is good, 40–70% average, below 30% weak.

How the three compare. 11. Net cash or net debt

What it is. Cash and liquid investments minus borrowings. For a contractor it is both a safety buffer and a competitive tool, because clients and banks prefer bidders who will not run out of money mid-project. Formula: cash + bank balances + current investments − borrowings. Worked example: Wabag reported net cash of ₹950 crore at March 2026 and ₹965 crore at June 2026, excluding the project debt of its HAM project companies; including them it was ₹833.7 crore and ₹856.6 crore. What drives it: cash conversion, capex (Roha, IPP assets), acquisitions (Suyog Urja) and dividends. Benchmark: net cash is good; net debt under 1 times EBITDA is average; above 2 times is weak.

How the three compare. Wabag net cash ₹950 crore (2.0 times its FY26 operating profit), its 14th consecutive net-cash quarter. 12. Return on capital employed

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital (equity plus debt) used to produce it. Formula: EBIT ÷ average capital employed. Worked example: on screener's basis Wabag's FY26 ROCE was 21%; its own annual report, using EBIT ÷ capital employed, gives 18.6% (16.9% in FY25), and its presentation 19.4%. What drives it: margin, asset turnover (which depends on working capital and fixed assets) and idle cash (which inflates capital employed). Benchmark: above 20% is good, 14–20% average, below 12% weak. Wabag's medium-term target is above 20%.

How the three compare. Screener ROCE for FY26: Wabag 21.3%, EIEL 20.2%, Ion Exchange 14.2%. The trends diverge sharply. Ion's ROCE was 36% in FY22 and has fallen every year as Roha's capital entered the base before its profit. The trap: ROCE bases differ by source, and IPO cash or customer advances distort the denominator. EIEL's FY25 figures are flattered or depressed depending on whether the November 2024 IPO money is averaged in.

13. HAM annuity economics

What it is. The return a developer earns from a hybrid-annuity sewage plant, which combines a construction margin with a financing return on the 60% of capex it funds. Formula: construction margin on the full capex, plus interest received on the annuity balance minus interest paid on project debt, plus the O&M margin, all over roughly 17 years. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE, on EIEL's ₹130 crore Lohta bid): NMCG pays 40%, ₹52 crore, during construction; EIEL books about ₹130 crore of construction revenue at a 20–22% margin (about ₹27 crore of EBITDA) and a ₹78 crore financial asset for the unpaid 60%. That ₹78 crore is funded by about ₹20–25 crore of equity and ₹55–60 crore of project debt. After commissioning, NMCG repays it in 60 quarterly instalments of about ₹1.3 crore plus interest on the declining balance; at an assumed 9% a year, interest totals about ₹54 crore over 15 years. A separate O&M fee of 3–5% of capex a year adds ₹4–6 crore annually. If NMCG pays on time, the equity return can reach the mid-teens (INDICATIVE).

What drives it: the bid price, the construction margin, the gap between the annuity interest rate and the project-debt rate, and above all the payer's discipline over 15 years, because annuities are cut for poor performance. Benchmark: there is no published benchmark; a construction margin near the company's EPC margin plus a positive financing spread is the minimum. How the companies compare: EIEL has five HAM plants (annuity interest of ₹27.4 crore in FY26; HAM backlog ₹403 crore); Wabag holds HAM and DBFOT project companies that are equity-accounted (₹5.5 crore share of profit in Q1 FY27); Ion Exchange's Oman concession is a commercial DBOOT, not HAM. The trap: the NMCG HAM interest formula was not verified for this report; NHAI's road HAM uses bank rate plus 3% on the reducing balance, which should not be assumed to apply. And HAM receivables depress reported operating cash flow while they build up.

Definition traps, collected

  • Order book has three meanings. Wabag includes 15–20 years of O&M and excludes framework contracts; Ion covers engineering only and leaves out Oman and Hyundai; EIEL includes renewables, HAM and its own power projects. Never add them without saying so.

  • EIEL's debtor days hide its unbilled work. 53 days of trade receivables, but ₹608 crore of unbilled revenue and ₹251 crore of HAM concession receivables.
  • HAM accounting depresses operating cash flow. The financial asset for the developer-funded 60% builds inside operating activities.
  • Ion's share count includes treasury-like holdings. Staff trusts and subsidiaries hold about 16% of shares; EPS-based and market-cap-based P/E differ, and free float is smaller than it looks.
  • EIEL's EBITDA excludes other income (₹42.4 crore in FY26), and its profit now carries power-asset depreciation and interest, so PAT grows more slowly than revenue.
  • Segment bases changed. Ion moved from three to five segments in Q1 FY27; EIEL reports by project type, contract model and state; there is no common municipal-industrial split.
  • Contract values: gross versus share, award versus effective. Kuwait's $372 million is the JV value; Perur's ₹4,400 crore is the JV value; Yanbu was re-awarded at $272 million after a $317 million award was cancelled.
  • ROCE and ROE bases differ. Wabag 18.6% (annual report), 19.4% (presentation), 21.3% (screener); EIEL's ratios were distorted by mid-year IPO capital in FY25.

How to read a water-treatment company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Order intake and order book (1 minute). Is the book still above three times revenue? Was the quarter's intake above revenue? Check whether any order was removed, reclassified or is still awaiting effectiveness. Red flag: intake below revenue for three quarters in a row.
  2. Revenue against guidance and seasonality (1 minute). Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter: Indian government work bunches into the fourth quarter. For EIEL, check the quarter against its 20/20/20/40 phasing.
  3. EBITDA margin on two bases (1 minute). For Wabag, subtract the forex gain or add back the loss. For EIEL, check the water and renewables split. For Ion, go straight to segment margins. Red flag: a margin held up only by other income.
  4. Segment table (1 minute). Ion: Specialty Chemicals EBIT margin and Treatment Solutions EBIT (positive or negative?). Wabag: EPC versus O&M revenue and the municipal-industrial split. EIEL: water versus renewables revenue.
  5. Other income, finance cost and tax (1 minute). Rising finance cost means working capital or borrowings are growing; a very high tax rate (Ion's 58.7% in Q1 FY27) usually means losses in units that cannot be offset.
  6. Working capital (1 minute). Half-yearly balance sheets come with Q2 and Q4. Look at trade receivables, unbilled revenue or contract assets, retention and customer advances. Red flag: unbilled revenue growing faster than revenue.
  7. Net cash or debt (1 minute). Wabag's should stay positive; Ion's and EIEL's direction matters more than the level. Note the basis (including or excluding HAM, leases).
  8. Payer news (1 minute). Any comment on JJM releases, state payments, Gulf force majeure, arbitration (Wabag's TSGENCO) or legacy projects (Ion's UP job)? These usually appear in the call, not the release.
  9. Project milestones (1 minute). Perur commissioning; Roha utilisation against the 25% guide; EIEL's NTPC battery project procurement from Q3; HAM plants reaching COD.
  10. Guidance changes (1 minute). Any change to Wabag's 15–20% growth and 13–15% margin, EIEL's ₹2,000 crore revenue and ₹260–270 crore PAT, or Ion's comments on margin recovery. A cut in guidance after one quarter is a stronger signal than a beat.
In plain terms

To read one of these results quickly: check that the pile of orders is still growing, take out anything that is not really operating profit, look at the business segment that makes the money, and then look at whether cash and customer payments are keeping up. Most of the bad news in this sector shows up first in the balance sheet and the conference call, not in the headline revenue.

What the repo hike does

The Reserve Bank raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026. Working-capital loans and new project debt price off it, so every percentage point of higher borrowing cost adds about ₹4.2 crore a year of interest on EIEL's ₹422 crore of borrowings and about ₹4.5 crore on Ion Exchange's ₹454 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE, before tax): about 2% of EIEL's FY26 profit and about 3% of Ion's, or nearly 5% of Ion's trailing profit of ₹98 crore. Wabag, with ₹1,059 crore of cash and bank balances, earns more on its deposits. The larger effect is indirect: the cost of carrying unbilled work and retention rises for every contractor, and state governments' own borrowing costs rise, which does not make them pay faster. Higher rates widen the gap between contractors with cash and those without.

In plain terms

All three companies are profitable on paper. Only one turns a reasonable share of that profit into cash, and even it has a growing pile of old bills. The other two have spent their cushions, one on a new factory and the other on funding work that state engineers have not yet signed off. With interest rates going up, the company with money in the bank gets stronger and the companies borrowing to wait for payment get squeezed.

The practical lesson for investors is to give working capital the same weight as margins. A one-percentage-point improvement in EBITDA margin at Wabag adds about ₹39 crore of EBITDA; a 30-day cut in its receivable days releases about ₹320 crore of cash. At EIEL, converting half of its ₹608 crore of unbilled work into collected cash would by itself erase its net debt and more. At Ion Exchange, a return to positive operating cash flow matters more for the share price than any single quarter's margin. The next section puts all of these measures side by side.

The Indian names are tiny next to the global water groups, yet trade at growth-stock multiples. What each price implies, and how the global peers compare.

The three companies trade over a wide range: on screener's trailing earnings, EIEL at 16.3 times, Wabag at 27.8 times and Ion Exchange at 46.4 times. On enterprise value to FY26 operating profit, the order is the same: about 11 times, 21 times and 24.5 times. The combined group trades at 27.3 times FY26 profit. These multiples are high for contractors (Indian civil EPC companies have typically traded at lower multiples) and reflect the market's view that water treatment has a long structural runway and that the specialists' know-how is scarce. But the ranking inside the group says the market cares more about cash and payer quality than about growth or margin.

A simple way to test each price is to ask what it implies. For Wabag, if revenue grows at the midpoint of guidance (17.5% a year) for three years and the operating margin holds at 12% before forex, FY29 operating profit would be about ₹770 crore, and today's enterprise value would be about 13 times that (ILLUSTRATIVE). The market is paying a premium for delivery that is plausible given the backlog, but it leaves little cushion if receivables or the Gulf go wrong. For EIEL, management's FY27 profit guide of ₹260–270 crore would put the stock at about 11 times FY27 earnings; the discount reflects doubt about the cash behind that profit and about the renewables shift. For Ion Exchange, a return of Specialty Chemicals to an 18–20% EBIT margin on about ₹1,000 crore of revenue would produce ₹180–200 crore of segment EBIT on its own, close to the whole group's FY26 profit before tax of ₹193 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE). The market is valuing that recovery; current earnings do not support the price.

The global lens

By global standards the Indian names are small. Veolia, the world's largest water-services group after absorbing Suez, reported 2025 revenue of €44.4 billion and EBITDA of €7.05 billion (15.9%); its Water Technologies division alone had revenue of about €5.0 billion. Wabag's FY26 revenue of ₹3,944 crore is about 0.9% of Veolia's and about 8% of Veolia Water Technologies'. Xylem, the US pumps-and-treatment group that bought Evoqua in 2023, reported 2025 revenue of $9.0 billion. Japan's Kurita, which combines water chemicals with ultrapure-water systems for semiconductor fabs, guided to ¥425 billion of revenue for the year to March 2026 with an operating margin of about 12.6%. Ecolab, which owns Nalco Water and the Purolite resin business, is larger still. These are the companies Ion Exchange's integrated model most resembles (chemicals plus UPW plus engineering), but its margins are less than half of theirs.

Technology and disruption: membranes, sludge, PFAS and drones

Technology in this sector moves slowly, but it decides who qualifies for the best tenders and who keeps the long O&M tails.

Desalination efficiency. Seawater reverse osmosis now uses about 3–3.5 kWh per cubic metre thanks to isobaric energy-recovery devices and better membranes, and Saudi independent water projects have been bid at tariffs below $0.5 per cubic metre (a well-established but approximate figure). Falling costs widen the market for desalination but also squeeze EPC prices, which is why references and proprietary process tweaks matter. Wabag points to its own IP: patented flat valves that shrink filter sizes, Nereda granular-sludge technology (used at Al Haer), deammonification, ceramic membranes and research on membrane crystallisation and PFAS removal.

Compact biological treatment. Sequencing batch reactors, moving-bed biofilm reactors, membrane bioreactors and granular sludge cut the footprint of sewage plants and make the NGT's tighter discharge limits achievable. They favour companies with process design capability over pure civil contractors. EIEL uses SBR technology to meet NGT norms; Wabag's Nereda work brings granular sludge to the Gulf.

Emerging contaminants and new chemistry. PFAS ("forever chemicals") regulation in Europe and the US is creating demand for resin, RO and destruction systems; Ion Exchange lists "PFAS assurance" and Wabag lists PFAS research. Ion also lists lithium extraction from brines and battery waste and brine valorisation as emerging solutions. On the other side, an analyst raised the risk that new non-polymer adsorbents could displace polystyrene resins; Ion says demand for its resins remains strong.

Ultrapure water for chips, solar, data centres and hydrogen. Each semiconductor fab needs a large UPW system and an even larger wastewater system; solar-cell lines, data centres and electrolysers need high-purity water too. These are technology-gated markets where Kurita, Organo and Veolia lead globally; Ion Exchange and Wabag have early references (RenewSys, a small semiconductor project with a partner, Indosol's desalination plant).

Sludge to energy. Sewage sludge can be digested to biogas and upgraded to compressed biogas (CBG) for sale to oil marketing companies. Wabag has a CBG build-own-operate project in Uttar Pradesh through Ghaziabad Bio Energy, a joint venture since 16 February 2026, and a tie-up with Peak Sustainability Ventures for up to 100 CBG plants. EIEL is installing CBG plants at its Jodhpur and Jaipur sewage projects (its 100 MLD Jodhpur STP combines CBG, solar and biogas-to-power) and building an agricultural-residue CBG plant in Madhya Pradesh; CBG capacity has not been disclosed.

Digital operations. Wabag uses AI decision support at Bahrain's AMAS plant and is piloting AI for non-revenue water reduction; Ion Exchange offers IonSiTE asset-performance management and digital twins. Digital tools could reduce O&M labour, but they also make incumbents harder to replace once a plant is running.

Disruptions to watch. Decentralised and nature-based treatment (faecal-sludge plants, DEWATS) could cap demand for large centralised STPs in small towns. Brine disposal and marine impact are rising concerns for desalination. And 2026 showed that coastal desalination plants are physically vulnerable in wartime, which may push Gulf clients towards distributed capacity, hardened sites and storage, changing what they buy.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The demand case is overwhelming. The argument is about payment, margins and cash.

The bull case

  • Non-negotiable demand: 72,368 MLD of sewage against about 20,236 MLD treated; 3.56 crore rural homes still to connect; more than 6,000 MLD of AMRUT 2.0 sewage capacity under way.
  • Funding restarted: JJM 2.0 (₹8.69 lakh crore to December 2028, FY27 budget ₹67,670 crore) and AMRUT 2.0 extended to March 2027, with an O&M focus that suits specialists.
  • Gulf tailwind: Saudi Arabia's 2027 "Year of Water", Kuwait's need for redundancy after the strikes, and Wabag's pipeline of $2–3 billion in the Middle East and Africa.
  • Annuity layers growing: Wabag's ₹6,666 crore O&M book (Perur adds 20 years from FY28), EIEL's five HAM plants, Ion's Oman concession and resin consumables.
  • Visibility: combined order books of ₹28,588 crore, more than three and a half times combined revenue (mixed bases).
  • Recovery optionality: if Ion's chemicals margin returns towards 20% and Roha fills, chemicals EBIT alone could approach ₹180–200 Cr, close to the whole group's FY26 profit before tax (INDICATIVE).

The bear case

  • Budgets are not cash: FY26's JJM budget was cut 75% and central release fell to zero; AMRUT 2.0 has released 34% of its central commitment.
  • Working capital: combined operating cash flow was 17% of PAT in FY26; EIEL's unbilled work is 53% of revenue; Wabag's over-three-year receivables rose fivefold.
  • Gulf concentration: 35–40% of Wabag's backlog in a war zone; award risk shown by Yanbu; force-majeure claims pending.
  • Margin slippage: Wabag's ex-forex margin 9.0%, Ion's 4.5%, EIEL's guide cut and renewables dilution.
  • Rates and rupee: repo at 5.50% raises carrying costs for the borrowers; a weak rupee inflates imported equipment on rupee contracts.
  • Valuation: 27 times combined FY26 profit, and 46 times trailing for Ion Exchange, with recovery assumed rather than shown.

The balance between the two cases turns on a small number of observable facts. If JJM 2.0 releases flow through FY27 at close to the budgeted pace, if Wabag's receivable ageing stops worsening and Perur commissions on time, if Ion Exchange's chemicals margin climbs back into the mid-teens by the end of FY27, and if EIEL's unbilled work starts converting, the bull case holds and current multiples look justified. If FY27's revised JJM estimate is cut again, if the Gulf war escalates around Wabag's sites, or if EIEL's growth comes with more unbilled work and more debt, the bear case will dominate. The demand story is not in dispute in either case; the cash story is.

The two cases also differ by company. The bull case is strongest for Wabag, which has the payer mix, the balance sheet and the references to benefit from both Indian and Gulf spending. The bear case bites hardest on EIEL, whose growth depends on state and central government cash and whose renewables pivot adds a new set of risks. Ion Exchange sits in between: its demand is mostly industrial and export-driven, so the funding cycle matters less, but its own execution (Roha, legacy projects) matters more.

In plain terms

Nobody disputes that India and the Gulf need more water treatment. The disagreement is about whether the companies will be paid on time, whether they can hold their margins while costs rise, and whether today's share prices already assume everything goes right. The next two or three quarterly results, and the next Union Budget, will settle much of that argument.
