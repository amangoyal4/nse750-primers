00

The one-page view: same shelf, opposite engines

Two companies share the "diversified FMCG" label. One is a pure consumer business with a foreign parent; the other is a cigarette tollgate that funds a conglomerate. Almost every number in this report follows from that difference.

The NSE 750 places exactly two companies in Diversified FMCG: Hindustan Unilever (HUL) and ITC. Together they were worth ₹7.63 lakh crore at the 21 October 2026 close and reported ₹1,43,336 crore of FY26 revenue (ITC counted net of excise). They are the two largest consumer-goods companies in the country by profit and by reach, and both sell soap, food and packaged staples into the same nine million kirana shops. That is where the resemblance stops.

HUL is a pure consumer-goods house. It has four operating segments: Home Care (Surf Excel, Rin, Vim), Beauty & Wellbeing (Dove, Pond's, Lakmé, Minimalist), Personal Care (Lux, Lifebuoy, Pepsodent) and Foods & Refreshment (Brooke Bond, Bru, Horlicks, Boost, Kissan). Each earns a segment profit margin (PBIT, profit before interest and tax) between roughly 17% and 28%. No single business dominates: Home Care, the largest, is about 37% of revenue and 33% of segment profit. Unilever owns 61.9% of the company and supplies brands, technology and central services for a fee.

ITC is a cigarette cash engine wrapped in a conglomerate. In FY26 its cigarette segment earned ₹21,051 crore of PBIT, about 84% of the profit of its four reported segments. That cash funds FMCG-Others (Aashirvaad atta, Sunfeast, Bingo, Yippee, Classmate, Savlon, Fiama), an agri-commodity business, a paperboards-and-packaging business and, through consolidation, ITC Infotech. FMCG-Others is now a ₹24,210 crore business by revenue, but it earned EBITDA of ₹2,412 crore (9.96%) and a PBIT margin of only 7.4% in FY26. ITC has no promoter. British American Tobacco (BAT) is the largest shareholder at 22.91%, and it is a shareholder, not a licensor: ITC pays it no royalty.

2026 pulled the two further apart. HUL's quarterly underlying sales growth (USG, its own like-for-like measure) climbed from 2% in Q2 FY26 to 10% in Q1 FY27, its best in 13 quarters, with underlying volume growth (UVG) of 5%. ITC, meanwhile, absorbed the largest cigarette tax step-up in a decade: from 1 February 2026 cigarettes moved to a 40% GST slab plus a new specific excise duty of ₹2,050–8,500 per 1,000 sticks, and ITC's standalone excise bill went from ₹1,309 crore in Q1 FY26 to ₹10,036 crore in Q1 FY27. Cigarette PBIT fell 35% to ₹3,341 crore. Both stocks still de-rated: HUL closed 21 October 28% below its 52-week high and ITC 38% below its own.

In plain terms

"Diversified" means opposite things for these two. HUL is diversified across what a household needs: detergent, shampoo, soap, tea. Every one of those businesses is good, so a bad year in one is absorbed by the others. ITC is diversified across business models: one superb but heavily taxed product pays for several ordinary ones. When the government changes the cigarette tax, the whole of ITC moves; when palm oil spikes, only part of HUL does.

Five forces shape this report, and each has its own section:

  • One label, two models. HUL's segment margins of 17–28% against ITC's mix of a 60%-plus-margin tollgate and 4–10%-margin businesses (Section 06).
  • ITC's tax reset. The February-2026 excise and GST change, what it did to net revenue, profit per stick and volumes, and what ITC is doing about it (Section 07, deep dive).
  • HUL's volume-led recovery and its new margin band. UVG back to 5–6%, pricing back at ~5% because of the West Asia input shock, and a Capital Markets Day that widened the EBITDA range to 22–24% (Section 08, deep dive).
  • Portfolio surgery and the parent's take. HUL's ice cream demerger and Unilever's McCormick deal; ITC's hotel demerger, its paper mill purchase and ITC Infotech's merger with Happiest Minds; the roughly ₹8,400 crore a year that flows from HUL to the Unilever group (Section 09, deep dive).
  • Channels and challengers. Quick commerce, modern trade, direct-to-consumer brands and Reliance Consumer Products against two incumbents whose moat was nine million kiranas (Section 16).

01

Two money machines: how HUL and ITC turn inputs into cash

HUL is a brand-and-distribution machine financed by its suppliers. ITC's cigarettes are a vertically integrated, tax-collecting tollgate; its FMCG arm borrows ITC's farms, mills and trucks.

Both companies buy commodities, turn them into branded packs and push those packs through India's fragmented retail system. The difference lies in where the value is captured and who else takes a share of the consumer's rupee on the way.

HUL: brands, distribution and the parent

HUL's inputs are mostly crude-linked chemicals (linear alkyl benzene and its sulphonic acid for detergents, polymers for packaging), palm oil and soap noodles for Lux and Lifebuoy, tea and coffee bought at auction, milk solids and malt for Horlicks and Boost, perfumes and packaging. It makes most of them into finished goods in 26 owned factories plus contract manufacturers, producing more than 85 billion units a year. Products then move through carrying-and-forwarding agents to redistribution stockists and on to more than 9 million outlets. About 1.4 million retailers order directly on HUL's Shikhar app, which covers more than 85% of its general-trade business. Modern trade, e-commerce and quick commerce run alongside.

ITC cigarettes: the integrated tollgate

ITC's cigarette chain starts on farms. Flue-cured Virginia tobacco is grown in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka and sold through Tobacco Board auctions; ITC's leaf division buys, threshes and ages it (leaf typically matures for one to two years, which is why ITC holds 209 days of inventory). Factories at Bengaluru, Kolkata, Munger, Saharanpur and Pune make the sticks, and ITC's own paperboard mills make the shells and cartons. The product then goes through wholesalers to a vast network of paan-beedi kiosks. Advertising has been banned since COTPA 2003, so reach and shelf presence are the weapons, and tobacco cannot be sold on quick-commerce apps, which makes this the one ITC channel that digital disruption cannot touch.

ITC's other businesses: an integrated food company with B2B arms

ITC's FMCG-Others segment buys wheat for Aashirvaad through its agri arm (ITCMAARS sources about 40% of that wheat), potatoes for Bingo from seed potatoes bred by its Technico subsidiary, and spices for Sunrise. It makes the products in 12 Integrated Consumer Goods Manufacturing and Logistics facilities (ICMLs) plus contract packers, and reaches about 7 million outlets; its UNNATI ordering app serves more than 8 lakh of them directly. Paperboards sells virgin multi-layer board to other FMCG, pharma and liquor companies and to ITC's own cigarette and Classmate businesses. Agri trades and exports wheat, rice, spices, coffee and leaf tobacco, and supplies leaf to the cigarette business (inter-segment sales of ₹5,557 crore were eliminated in Q1 FY27 alone, so ITC's segment revenues do not add up to company revenue).

In plain terms

HUL's profit is made in the brand and the delivery van; the farm and the factory are commodities to it. ITC's cigarette profit is made in a regulated gate that every legal smoker must pass through, and the government collects its toll at the same gate. ITC's food business, by contrast, looks more like HUL's than like ITC's cigarettes, except that it is younger, heavier on staples and still far less profitable.

Value capture therefore differs at every step. HUL's gross margin of about 50% (50.9% in FY26 and 49.5% in Q1 FY27) pays for advertising of nearly 10% of sales and still leaves 23% EBITDA. ITC's cigarettes kept roughly 71% of net revenue as PBIT before the tax change and about 63% after, with tiny capital needs, which is why ITC's return on capital employed is 38.9% even though its working capital is positive and large. In FMCG-Others, the agri and packaging integration has not yet turned into margin: PBIT of 7.4% against HUL's 21.3% average segment margin.

02

Size, growth and the record: ₹1.43 lakh crore growing at 5%

The pair grew revenue 4.9% in FY26, and the headline hides three different stories: HUL recovering, ITC's FMCG compounding at double digits and ITC's cigarettes standing still once tax is stripped out.

There is no reliable single number for the size of India's FMCG market. Popular figures of "US-dollar 200 billion and more" include unbranded staples and loose commodities. Triangulating from listed-company revenue and panel shares gives roughly ₹7–9 lakh crore of branded home-and-personal-care and packaged food and beverage at consumer prices, an estimate rather than a statistic. On that basis HUL and ITC's FMCG-Others together, ₹88,678 crore of FY26 revenue at manufacturer prices, are around a tenth of the branded market by value (INDICATIVE).

In plain terms

The pair's 5% growth in FY26 is three numbers blended together. HUL grew about 5%, mostly by selling more, not by charging more. ITC's packaged-food and personal-care arm grew about 10%. ITC's cigarettes, once the tax that passes through its books is taken out, did not grow at all. Each needs to be read on its own basis, and none of them is the industry's "growth rate".

The record also shows where the profit growth came from. ITC's cigarette PBIT rose 5.1% in FY26, to ₹21,051 crore, on flat net revenue: cost, mix and some trade loading ahead of February's price rises did the work. HUL's clean PAT was flat (₹10,324 crore, −0.1%) because margins slipped from 24.0% to 23.3% while it reinvested in advertising and premium beauty. FMCG-Others PBIT grew 14.1% to ₹1,803 crore. Over FY20–26 ITC reports a 27% compound growth in FMCG-Others "results" (segment PBIT) against 6% for cigarettes, which tells you which business is being built and which is being harvested.

03

What drives demand: frequency, premiumisation and the price of a habit

HUL's demand is a function of rural incomes, inflation and new categories. ITC's cigarette demand barely responds to price, until the gap with illicit and bidi becomes too wide.

For mass-market consumer goods in India, growth no longer comes from adding households. HUL says nine of ten Indian homes already use its products; ITC's brands reach about 280 million households. Growth therefore comes from three places: frequency (washing clothes more often, using shampoo instead of soap on hair), premiumisation (liquid detergent instead of powder, body wash instead of a bar, serums instead of cold cream) and category creation (coffee, protein snacks, frozen food). HUL's Capital Markets Day in September 2026 put numbers on this: it plans growth roughly 40% from consumption, 40% from premiumisation and 20% from "market making" in new spaces.

Rural and urban, panel and company

About a third of HUL's sales are rural. Rural demand led urban through 2024 and the first half of 2025, then the panel data turned: NielsenIQ (NIQ) measured rural FMCG volume down about 0.8% in January–March 2026 and down 5% in April–June 2026, with home-and-personal-care volume down 4.3% nationally. Yet HUL reported 5% UVG for the same quarter and ITC's FMCG-Others grew revenue 12%. The gap is not a contradiction. NIQ's offline panel under-tracks e-commerce and quick commerce, where both companies are growing 25–57% a year, and large brands gain share when smaller rivals lose shelf space during inflation. HUL's management described demand in July 2026 as "stable", with no visible inflation-led slowdown and rural growth stepping up over several quarters.

Price, inflation and GST 2.0

The second driver is price. On 22 September 2025 the GST system was rebuilt around 5% and 18% slabs plus a 40% "demerit" slab. Most of HUL's personal-care range (soap, shampoo, toothpaste, hair oil, talc) dropped from 18% to 5%, as did coffee, malt drinks, sauces and ice cream; detergents were not in the change list and stay at 18%; tea was already 5%. ITC's biscuits, noodles, namkeens, chocolates and juices also moved to 5%, and exercise books went to nil GST. Cigarettes: a habit with a price ceiling

Cigarette demand works differently. Indian studies put the short-run price elasticity of cigarettes at about −0.3 to −0.5: a 10% price rise cuts consumption by 3–5%. Cigarettes are only 10–15% of India's tobacco consumption by volume; the rest is bidi and chewing tobacco, which are cheaper and taxed far less. So the swing factor is not the absolute price of a Gold Flake but its price relative to the alternatives: a bidi, a chewing-tobacco sachet or a smuggled stick on which no duty was paid.

In plain terms

A shampoo brand that raises prices 20% loses customers to a rival shampoo. A cigarette brand that raises prices 20% mostly keeps its smokers, because there is little legal substitute and the habit is strong. The risk is a slow leak to the illegal market, which pays no tax and therefore gets cheaper relative to legal cigarettes every time the government raises duty. That leak, not the competition from Godfrey Phillips or VST, is ITC's real volume threat.

Two other demand drivers matter to ITC alone. Its agri business depends on the wheat crop and minimum support prices, India's export rules (the wheat export ban has been in place since May 2022) and global leaf demand; in Q1 FY27 shipping disruption in West Asia and delayed leaf call-offs cut agri revenue 16.6%, though ITC puts underlying growth at 9%. And paperboard demand depends on FMCG packaging volumes and on how much cheap Indonesian board enters the country, which the government has limited through a minimum import price that ran to 30 September 2026.

Where ₹100 goes: three cost structures under one label

At HUL half the rupee buys materials and the rest funds brands and profit. At ITC's cigarettes the state takes more than two-thirds of what the smoker pays. ITC's food arm sits in between, with a margin that has not yet caught up with its scale.

The fastest way to understand a consumer company is to follow ₹100 of sales down the income statement. The three archetypes in this sub-sector produce three very different shapes, and the differences explain margins, sensitivities and valuations better than any ratio table.

HUL: a 50% gross margin that pays for everything

For every ₹100 of FY26 revenue from operations, HUL spent about ₹49.1 on raw and packing materials, leaving a gross margin of ₹50.9. Advertising and promotion (A&P) took ₹9.7, employees ₹4.9, the royalty and central-services fee to Unilever about ₹3.1, and other overheads (freight, power, contract manufacturing, IT, warehousing) about ₹9.8. That left EBITDA of ₹23.4. Depreciation (₹2.1) and lease interest (₹0.6) came off, other income (₹1.2) went on, and tax took about ₹5.9, leaving clean profit of about ₹16.0, of which roughly ₹14.9 went out as dividends. The figures are company and broker P&L lines divided by revenue, rounded; the royalty share uses the annual report's ₹2,023 crore.

In plain terms

In cigarettes, ITC's real competitor is the smuggler.

Ownership structure also shapes behaviour. HUL is a subsidiary of a global group, so its brand portfolio, research and long-term category choices are coordinated from London. The ₹10,000-crore quarter: anatomy of ITC's tax reset Deep dive

On 1 February 2026 the cigarette tax moved from GST plus compensation cess to GST at 40% plus a new per-stick excise. ITC's gross revenue jumped, its net revenue and profit fell, and its reported numbers stopped meaning what they used to.

The tax reset is the single most important event in this sub-sector in 2026, and it is easy to misread. ITC's consolidated "revenue from operations" in Q1 FY27 was ₹29,523 crore, up 27.6%, and some headlines reported exactly that. In economic terms the quarter was the opposite: standalone net revenue fell 14.4%, EBITDA fell 27.9% and cigarette PBIT fell 35.1%. The difference is the excise duty, which ITC collects on the state's behalf and books inside gross revenue before deducting it.

What changed

From July 2017 cigarettes paid 28% GST plus a compensation cess (an ad valorem 5% plus a specific amount per 1,000 sticks by length), with a relatively small central excise and National Calamity Contingent Duty (NCCD) alongside. The compensation cess, created to repay states for GST revenue losses, ended for tobacco on 31 January 2026 once the related loans were repaid. GST 2.0 had already placed tobacco in the 40% "demerit" slab, effective from that date. To keep its revenue, the Centre amended the Central Excise Act and issued Notification 03/2025-Central Excise (G.S.R. 955(E), 31 December 2025, corrected on 4 February 2026), imposing a new specific duty from 1 February 2026. Unlike GST, this excise is not creditable; unlike the cess, it passes through ITC's revenue line.

The leverage in that last scenario is the key to ITC's recovery. Take an illustrative king-size stick retailing at ₹14 after the reset: about ₹1.12 goes to the trade, ₹3.68 to GST and ₹6.00 to excise and NCCD, leaving ITC ₹3.20, of which about ₹1.20 is cost and ₹2.00 profit. The cost is volume: at an elasticity of −0.3, a cumulative 35% price rise implies roughly a 10% volume loss; the Street's −5% to −6% for FY27 assumes some of that is offset by trade-down within ITC's own range and by enforcement against smuggling.

In plain terms

The government now takes a fixed sum from every cigarette, so ITC's job is to charge enough above that sum to earn what it earned before. Because the tax does not rise when ITC raises its price, each price rise is worth much more to ITC than it looks: a one-eighth rise in the shop price can lift ITC's take per cigarette by a third. The risk is that smokers, faced with a much dearer pack, move to bidis or smuggled cigarettes.

The playbook: price, variants and patience

ITC does not hold earnings calls; its quarterly "Results Update & FAQ" serves instead, and it discloses no volume, price-increase or market-share figure. What it has said is that it called the increase "unprecedented", responded in a "strategic and calibrated" way, made more than 30 portfolio interventions in a short span (new launches included Players Magic Mix, Gold Flake Indie Paan Sleek, American Club Magic Mix Sleek, Players Deluxe Filter and Flake Premium Deluxe), staggered pricing to limit migration to illicit trade, and that outcomes were tracking "in line with internal plans". The new variants are the quiet part of the strategy: shorter or slimmer sticks drop into cheaper excise slabs, letting a smoker stay with ITC at a price he can bear.

Brokers' estimates fill the gaps (INDICATIVE): volume down 4–5% in Q1 FY27 after growing about 4.5–6.5% a quarter through FY26; Systematix expected H1 FY27 volume down 8–10% and FY27 down about 5%; Nomura and others see FY27 down about 6% and the legal market down 6–8%, with growth returning by Q4 FY27. The Street expects Q2 FY27 volumes to be a little weaker than Q1's as September's price increases bite. Smaller listed rivals fared worse in Q1: reported PAT fell 44% at Godfrey Phillips and 24% at VST, according to broker previews.

One-off reset or the start of a ratchet?

History offers a guide. ITC went through steep excise increases between FY14 and FY17 and a GST cess increase in 2017; volumes fell, the stock went nowhere for about five years, and then, once taxes stabilised from 2020, volumes recovered and the shares re-rated from about ₹200 to about ₹500. If the February 2026 reset is a one-off step to replace the cess, ITC can price its way back to its old profit per stick within four to six quarters, and FY28 cigarette PBIT can approach ₹20,000 crore again (INDICATIVE). If instead it is the first step of a World Health Organization-style ratchet, with annual increases that keep the tax share of price near 70% or above, every price rise ITC takes will be followed by another tax rise, illicit trade will grow, and cigarette profits will drift lower in real terms. The Union Budget on 1 February 2027 and any GST Council move on tobacco are the first tests.

In plain terms

ITC has survived tax shocks before by raising prices until its profit per cigarette was back where it started; smokers grumbled and mostly kept buying. The question is not whether ITC can do that again, but whether the government will let it, or will raise the tax again as soon as ITC's prices catch up.

The rulebook: one member is taxed like a vice, the other like a necessity

Tobacco control, excise and the FDI ban define ITC's core. GST rates, labelling, advertising codes and a SEBI threshold on royalties define HUL's. Paper import prices and export bans shape ITC's B2B arms.

Regulation touches both companies, but in opposite directions. For HUL, the state is mostly a rate-setter and a referee: it decides how much GST a bar of soap carries, what a label may claim and how much a subsidiary may pay its parent before minorities get a vote. For ITC's cigarettes, the state is a partner that takes the majority of the revenue, bans advertising, prohibits the next-generation products that have rescued tobacco companies abroad, and decides each February how much more it wants.

The FMCG rulebook

  • GST 2.0 (22 September 2025). Most HUL personal-care items and ITC foods fell to 5%; detergents stayed at 18%; notebooks and their paper went to nil. Pass-through was largely complete within a quarter, partly as lower MRPs and partly as larger packs.
  • Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules. MRP-inclusive pricing and mandatory pack declarations, so any grammage change must be printed. This is why "price" in an FMCG company's growth number includes grammage, and why volume figures are partly a weight measure.
  • FSSAI and the "health drink" question. Labelling rules, front-of-pack nutrition proposals and a 2024 government advisory asking e-commerce sites to stop categorising malt drinks as "health drinks" bear on Horlicks and Boost, HUL's largest food sub-category.
  • ASCI and the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act. Advertising claims are policed; the 2020 rename of Fair & Lovely to Glow & Lovely followed pressure on fairness claims.
  • SEBI LODR Regulation 23(1A). Royalty or brand payments to a related party above 5% of consolidated turnover are "material" and need minority approval. HUL, at about 3.2%, is below the line.
  • Competition. The distributors' federation (AICPDF) has complained to the Competition Commission about quick-commerce pricing; the CCI sought evidence in April 2025 and no order has been found.
  • Plastic Waste Management rules. Extended producer responsibility targets on multi-layer plastics bear most heavily on sachet-heavy portfolios such as HUL's shampoo and detergent ranges.
In plain terms

For HUL, the government mostly decides the size of the GST slice and the wording on the pack. For ITC's cigarettes, the government decides the size of the whole business: it takes about two-thirds of the price, forbids advertising, forbids the alternative products that tobacco firms sell abroad, and can change all of that in one budget speech. That is why one member trades like a staple and the other like a regulated utility with a moral overhang.

ITC's B2B arms: import prices and export bans

ITC's paperboard business has been protected since 22 August 2025 by a minimum import price (MIP) on virgin multi-layer paperboard, aimed at cheap Indonesian supplies. The MIP ran until 30 September 2026. The Directorate General of Trade Remedies has recommended an anti-dumping duty on Indonesian board, which was awaiting Finance Ministry approval at the end of July; its status after September is unverified and is one of the items to watch for the Q2 results. Paperboards PBIT fell 12.6% in FY26 (to ₹797 crore) on wood costs and imports, then rose 38% in Q1 FY27 as wood costs eased and the MIP bit.

The agri business works under export rules that can change overnight: wheat exports have been banned since May 2022 and rice exports were curbed in 2023–24, and the 10% US Section 301 tariff on Indian goods from 24 July 2026 affects agri exports to America. Leaf tobacco exports are not restricted, and India exported ₹17,192 crore of tobacco and tobacco products in what appears to be FY26, about 369 million kg, making it the world's second-largest exporter of unmanufactured leaf after Brazil.

Finally, the two companies' contingent liabilities are modest. HUL's consolidated contingent liabilities were ₹3,093 crore at March 2026 (mostly income-tax disputes, ₹2,405 crore), about 2% of net worth, with no single large case disclosed. ITC's standalone figure was ₹1,009 crore against a net worth of ₹69,929 crore. Neither balance sheet carries a regulatory claim large enough to change the investment case; the regulatory risk in this sub-sector lives in future tax rates, not past disputes.

A century in fourteen turns: how two colonial-era companies became today's giants

Both began as British companies selling to Indians. One stayed a subsidiary and kept merging; the other was Indianised, diversified and repeatedly taxed. Their cycles explain today's multiples.

HUL and ITC are among the oldest listed companies in India, and both trace their present shape to choices made decades ago. Lever Brothers began selling Sunlight soap in India in 1888 and incorporated Lever Brothers India in 1933; Hindustan Lever was formed in 1956 by merging three Lever companies. The Imperial Tobacco Company of India was incorporated in 1910, renamed India Tobacco Company in 1970 and I.T.C. in 1974, and spent the next five decades diversifying away from its single product.

Four cycles worth remembering

HUL's lost decade, 2000–2008. HUL entered the millennium as India's most admired company and spent eight years going nowhere. P&G's 2004 detergent price war forced it to cut prices in its largest category, margins compressed and the share price stagnated. The rebuild under Nitin Paranjpe and Sanjiv Mehta from 2008 to 2023, with its "Winning in Many Indias" cluster model and later the Shikhar digitisation, produced the long run of margin expansion and the premium multiple that followed. The lesson for today: a determined, deep-pocketed rival willing to sacrifice margin in HUL's largest category can hurt for years. Reliance's consumer arm is the obvious candidate.

Unilever tightens control, 2013. Unilever's open offer at ₹600 a share took its stake to about 67%, and in the same year the royalty began rising from 1.4% towards 3.15%. Minority shareholders received a tender premium but also a larger fee. The GSK Consumer Healthcare merger in 2020 diluted Unilever to 61.9% because it was paid for in HUL shares; the 2023 agreement then raised the combined royalty and service fee towards 3.45%. Each step has been within the rules, and each has increased the share of HUL's value that accrues to the parent ahead of minorities.

ITC's tax decade, FY14–FY23. Excise increases between FY14 and FY17 and the 2017 GST cess step-up pushed legal cigarette volumes down and illicit volumes up; ITC's stock went nowhere from about 2016 to 2021 and earned a "value trap" label. When taxes stabilised after 2020 (no NCCD increases in 2021 and 2022), volumes recovered, profit per stick rose and the stock more than doubled. The February 2026 reset restarts that cycle; the question in Section 07 is whether the stability that followed last time will follow this time.

The FMCG multiple, 2014–2026. Indian consumer staples re-rated from the mid-30s to 50–70 times earnings between 2014 and 2021, on falling interest rates, foreign inflows and a scarcity of high-return, low-risk compounders. HUL peaked at about 60–70 times trailing earnings. Since then the premium has compressed: HUL trades at about 41 times clean earnings, slower volume growth, the quick-commerce disruption and higher bond yields having eroded it. ITC never enjoyed that premium; its multiple has moved with the tax cycle instead, and sits at about 16.6 times today.

Reading the numbers: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Two equations, fifteen native KPIs, two illustrative anatomies, a scorecard, the definition traps and a ten-minute routine for each member's quarterly results.

A diversified FMCG analyst needs two toolkits. HUL is read like any branded consumer company: volume, price, gross margin, advertising, overheads, the parent's fee and cash conversion. ITC's cigarettes are read like a regulated tollgate: sticks, net realisation per stick, tax and cost per stick. ITC's other businesses are read like a young food company and two commodity processors. This section builds both toolkits, KPI by KPI, and shows where each member stands and where the published numbers mislead.

In plain terms

To understand HUL, watch how much more it sells and what it charges, then how much of the extra it keeps after paying for materials and advertising. To understand ITC, count the cigarettes (which nobody can do precisely), multiply by what ITC keeps per cigarette after tax, and remember that everything else ITC does is paid for by that product.

1. Underlying volume growth (UVG), underlying sales growth (USG) and price

What it is. USG is HUL's like-for-like growth in its domestic consumer business, excluding acquisitions, disposals and (in FY26) ice cream. UVG is the volume part of it, measured on packs or tonnage. Price and mix is the remainder. Formula: (1 + USG) = (1 + UVG) × (1 + price/mix); in practice price ≈ USG − UVG. Worked example: in Q1 FY27 USG was 10% and UVG 5%, so price and mix contributed about 5%; HUL said growth was "equally volume and price". What drives it: distribution gains, innovation and premium formats push volume; commodity inflation and GST changes drive price. Grammage cuts at constant MRP show up as price, not as lower volume, and grammage increases after the GST cut flattered UVG in late FY26.

Benchmarks: UVG of 6% or more is strong for a company of HUL's size, 3–6% average, below 3% weak; price of 2–4% is healthy, above 6% usually means inflation pass-through. How the members compare: HUL 5% UVG (Q1 FY27); ITC discloses no comparable volume number for FMCG-Others (revenue +12%) and no cigarette volume at all (brokers estimate −4% to −5%). The trap: UVG is company-defined and weight-based, NielsenIQ volumes are offline panel units, and ITC's "volume" is a broker estimate. The three cannot be compared directly. Reported revenue growth also differs from USG whenever the perimeter changes, as with the ice cream demerger.

2. Gross margin

What it is: revenue minus raw and packing materials (adjusted for inventory changes), as a share of revenue. Formula: 1 − materials ÷ revenue. Worked example: HUL's Q1 FY27 materials were about ₹8,760 crore on revenue of ₹17,341 crore: gross margin 49.5%, down 79 basis points. Drivers: input prices with a lag of one to two quarters (inventory and hedges), pricing, mix (liquids and premium beauty carry higher gross margins than powders and mass soaps) and savings programmes. 3. Advertising and promotion (A&P) as a share of sales

What it is: spending on media, promotions and brand building, divided by revenue. Benchmarks: 10–12% signals investment, 8–10% is maintenance, below 8% is harvesting. 4. EBITDA margin and the band

What it is: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, divided by revenue or turnover. Formula for HUL: gross margin − A&P% − staff% − royalty and fee% − other overheads%. Worked example: FY26 on turnover: ₹15,054 crore ÷ ₹63,763 crore = 23.6%; on revenue from operations, ₹15,054 crore ÷ ₹64,468 crore = 23.4% (screener's operating profit gives 23.3%). Drivers: the gross margin, and management's choice of how much of a gross-margin move to absorb through A&P and savings. Benchmarks: for a pure HPC-led FMCG company, 23% or more is good, 18–23% average, below 18% weak; for food-led FMCG, 15% or more is good and below 10% weak.

How the members compare. HUL guides to a 22–24% medium-term range (widened from 22.5–23.5% at the September 2026 Capital Markets Day). ITC's company-wide EBITDA margin (35.0% standalone in FY26; 26.9% in Q1 FY27 on the company's net revenue) is a blend dominated by cigarettes and fell mechanically when excise shrank net revenue. Its FMCG-Others EBITDA margin was 9.96% in FY26. 6. Segment PBIT margins

What it is: each reported segment's result (profit before interest and tax, after the segment's depreciation) divided by its revenue. Worked example: HUL Beauty & Wellbeing FY26: ₹4,161 crore ÷ ₹14,990 crore = 27.8%. ITC FMCG-Others: ₹1,803 crore ÷ ₹24,210 crore = 7.4%. Drivers: category gross margins, scale within each category, advertising and the depreciation on new capacity. Benchmarks: 20% or more is good for a consumer segment, 12–20% average, below 12% weak; a cigarette PBIT margin on net revenue of 70% or more is strong, 60–70% average. How the members compare: HUL's four segments run 18.9–27.8% (FY26) and 17.3–27.6% (Q1 FY27); ITC's run from about 63% (cigarettes, on net) to 7.4% (FMCG-Others and Agri) and 9.1% (Paperboards). Section 06 charts them. The trap: ITC's cigarette segment revenue is gross of excise, so its PBIT margin is 56.7% "of gross" in FY26 and cannot be cleanly stated on net for that year; its Agri segment revenue includes sales of leaf to its own cigarette business.

7. Cigarette net revenue and PBIT per stick

What it is: net cigarette revenue and cigarette PBIT divided by the number of sticks sold. Formula: net per stick = (retail price − trade margin) ÷ 1.40 − excise per stick; PBIT per stick = net per stick − cost per stick. Worked example: see the table above: about ₹3.9 net and ₹3.0 PBIT per stick in FY26, about ₹3.2 and ₹2.0 in Q1 FY27 annualised (INDICATIVE, on assumed volumes of about 70 and 66.5 billion sticks). Drivers: price increases (almost fully passed to net because excise is fixed), mix between length slabs, leaf and paper costs. Benchmarks: what matters is the trend in rupees; ITC's stated aim, as reported by brokers, is to restore pre-reset EBIT per stick. The trap: ITC never discloses volumes, so every per-stick number is an estimate; and the implied average excise per stick (company excise ÷ assumed sticks, about ₹6) is higher than most slab rates, which suggests either larger volumes than assumed or timing effects in the excise line.

In plain terms

The single number that best describes ITC's cigarette health is how many rupees of profit it makes on each cigarette. Before February 2026 that was about ₹3; in the first full quarter after, about ₹2. The whole recovery story is about getting back to ₹3 without losing too many smokers on the way.

8. Tax incidence on cigarettes

What it is: the share of the retail price of a cigarette that goes to the state as GST, excise, NCCD and (until January 2026) compensation cess. Formula: (GST + excise + NCCD + cess) ÷ retail price. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): on a ₹14 king-size stick after February 2026, GST of about ₹3.68 plus excise and NCCD of about ₹6.00 is ₹9.68, or 69% of the price. Drivers: the Budget (NCCD, excise), the GST Council (rate) and ITC's own pricing: because the excise is fixed per stick, every ITC price increase slightly lowers the tax share until the next duty rise. Benchmarks: India's pre-reset share was about 50–53% on WHO's estimate; the post-reset composite is about 69%; global anti-tobacco guidance favours higher shares still. How the members compare: only ITC is exposed; HUL's highest-taxed products (detergents at 18% GST) carry about 15%. The trap: "tax incidence" is quoted in at least three ways, as a share of retail price, as excise over ITC's gross sales (65% in Q1 FY27 against about 16% a year earlier), and as a percentage increase in tax (brokers cite about 35%), and the three are routinely confused.

9. FMCG-Others: EBITDA margin versus PBIT margin

What it is: two profit measures for the same segment. ITC's commentary quotes segment EBITDA margin; its segment table reports PBIT. Formula: PBIT = EBITDA − segment depreciation. Worked example: FY26 FMCG-Others EBITDA was ₹2,412 crore (9.96%); segment depreciation was about ₹609 crore (2.5%); PBIT was ₹1,803 crore (7.4%). Drivers: scale, mix shift to dairy, snacks and premium foods, input costs, and depreciation on the new factories. Benchmarks: for food-led FMCG, EBITDA of 15% or more is good and below 10% weak. How the members compare: ITC FMCG-Others at about 10% EBITDA sits on the weak/average boundary; HUL's Foods segment earns about 20% PBIT, so roughly 22% EBITDA. The trap: mixing the two measures. Statements such as "FMCG margin crossed 8%" usually compare a PBIT number with an EBITDA number, and a "+145 basis points" improvement quoted for FY26 was a single quarter's EBITDA move; the full-year PBIT margin rose only about 25 basis points.

Definition traps, collected

  • ITC revenue, gross or net. Gross includes excise; net excludes it. Q1 FY27 consolidated gross revenue rose 27.6% while net fell 11.1%. Never compare ITC gross with HUL revenue.
  • ITC cigarette segment revenue is gross. FY26's ₹37,100 crore includes ₹9,656 crore of excise; net was about ₹27,440 crore, about 1% lower than FY25.
  • Gross minus excise is not net, quarter by quarter, around the reset. Excise loaded into inventory makes Q4 FY26's derived net revenue smaller than its PBIT. Use ITC's stated growth rates.
  • HUL has three revenue numbers. Turnover (₹63,763 crore), revenue from operations (₹64,468 crore, adds other operating income) and USG (a like-for-like growth rate, not a level).
  • HUL has three FY26 profit numbers. ₹15,059 crore reported (with the ₹4,611 crore demerger gain), ₹10,652 crore continuing reported, ₹10,324 crore before exceptional items. Use the last for P/E and payout.
  • ITC's ₹20,286 crore is standalone, not "reported consolidated"; consolidated PAT is ₹21,018 crore.
  • Segment profit is PBIT, not EBITDA. FMCG-Others EBITDA 9.96% vs PBIT 7.4% for the same year.
  • Three volume measures. HUL's UVG (company, weight-based), NielsenIQ panel volume (offline units) and ITC cigarette volume (broker estimate only).
  • Three margin denominators at HUL. 23.6% on turnover, 23.4% on revenue, 23.3% on screener's operating profit, all FY26.
  • The royalty "3.45%" is a ceiling. FY26 actual was 3.17% (₹2,023 crore); the parent's full take including dividends was about ₹8,370 crore.
  • Tax incidence has three definitions. Share of retail price, excise over gross sales, and the percentage increase in tax.
  • FII selling at ITC is not BAT. BAT is unchanged at 22.91%; the 2026 seller was GQG Partners.
  • HUL's FY25 revenue is restated ex-ice cream on screener while FY24 is not, so the printed FY25 decline is a base effect.
In plain terms

Most of the "surprises" in this sub-sector's headlines are definitions. A 28% jump in ITC's revenue that was really a 14% fall, a 64% HUL payout that was really 93%, a royalty of 3.45% that was really 3.2%, an FII exodus that was not BAT. Fix the definitions first and the numbers start telling a consistent story.

How to read HUL's and ITC's quarterly results in ten minutes

Each member publishes in a different format. HUL issues a press release, a presentation and a transcript of its earnings call; ITC holds no call and issues a press release, a presentation and a "Results Update & FAQ" instead. The routine below takes about five minutes per company.

  1. HUL, minute 1: USG and UVG. The first line of the release. Price ≈ USG − UVG. Red flag: UVG below 3% while price runs above 5%, which signals elasticity is biting.
  2. HUL, minute 2: segment USG and PBIT. Home Care, Beauty & Wellbeing, Personal Care, Foods. Red flag: any segment margin falling more than 200 basis points without a stated investment reason (Home Care fell 219 in Q1 FY27 on crude).
  3. HUL, minute 3: gross margin and A&P. From the presentation. Gross margin down while A&P is up in rupees means HUL is defending volume; both down means it is protecting margin.
  4. HUL, minute 4: EBITDA margin against the 22–24% range, on turnover. Red flag: below 22%, or two consecutive quarters at the floor with UVG not improving.
  5. HUL, minute 5: exceptional items and the call. Strip gains (demergers, disposals) and one-offs (labour codes) to get PAT before exceptional items. In the call, listen for input-cost outlook, rural commentary and any change to fees or brand licences.
  6. ITC, minute 6: net revenue, not gross. Use the presentation's net revenue (₹16,812 crore standalone in Q1 FY27) and its growth. Ignore the gross "revenue from operations" headline.
  7. ITC, minute 7: cigarette segment PBIT and ITC's stated net segment revenue growth. Compare with Q1 FY27's ₹3,341 crore and −25%. Red flag: PBIT below ₹3,000 crore in a quarter after the September price rises.
  8. ITC, minute 8: back out a volume range. ITC will not give one; combine its net revenue growth with brokers' price estimates. Red flag: implied volume worse than −8%, or commentary about illicit trade rising.
  9. ITC, minute 9: FMCG-Others EBITDA and PBIT. Note (i) in the segment results gives EBITDA; the table gives PBIT. Track both against 9.96% and 7.4%. Then Agri (West Asia, leaf) and Paperboards (the Lalkuan mill's first quarter; MIP status).
  10. ITC, minute 10: the FAQ. Read it for pricing language, new variants, input-cost hedges and capital allocation (dividend, acquisitions, ITC Infotech approvals). Red flag: any hint of further tax change or a dividend policy below 80%.
In plain terms

For HUL, ask: did it sell more, at what price, and how much of the extra did it keep? For ITC, ask: how much did cigarettes earn after tax this quarter, did the price rises hold volumes, and is the food business inching towards double-digit margins? Everything else is detail.

Channels and challengers: who owns the shelf when the shelf is an app

For fifty years HUL's moat was nine million kiranas and the vans that served them. Quick commerce, modern trade, online-born brands and Reliance are re-pricing that moat. ITC's cigarettes are the one business none of them can touch.

Traditional trade, the corner kirana, still accounts for roughly four-fifths of Indian FMCG sales, but it has stopped growing as fast as the rest. NielsenIQ's April–June 2026 data showed modern-trade volume up 17.5% and e-commerce volume up 34.8% while rural volume fell 5%. E-commerce is now about 7% of FMCG sales nationally and about 21% in the top eight metros. Quick-commerce apps, which deliver in 10–20 minutes from dark stores, are the fastest-growing part of that and the most disruptive, because they change three things at once: who decides which brand the shopper sees, how much shelf a new brand needs, and what a distributor earns.

Quick commerce: an opportunity priced as a threat

HUL's quick-commerce business doubled in FY26 and grew 40–50% in Q1 FY27; management put it at about 3% of turnover in its Q3 FY26 commentary. HUL created a dedicated quick-commerce organisation in FY26, built price-pack architecture specifically for the channel (packs sized and priced for a basket delivered in minutes rather than a monthly kirana shop), and raised its on-shelf availability on the platforms by about 1,400 basis points. E-commerce as a whole grew more than 25% in FY26. ITC's quick-commerce sales grew 57% in FY26 and its digital plus modern-trade share of FMCG sales doubled from 17% in FY20 to 34%.

The threats are real. Quick-commerce apps give online-born brands an "infinite shelf" next to Dove and Surf Excel without the cost of reaching nine million kiranas; they sell private labels; they charge listing, placement and advertising fees that can make the channel less profitable than general trade; and their discounting squeezes the distributors who serve kiranas, whose federation has complained to the Competition Commission. The mitigants are also real. HUL's supply chain can serve dark stores at scale; its sachets and low-unit packs, the heart of rural and small-town demand, are uneconomic to ship by app; and its share of platform advertising budgets buys visibility smaller brands cannot afford. ITC's cigarettes cannot legally be sold on these apps at all, an under-appreciated protection for 84% of its profit.

In plain terms

A kirana stocks a few brands per shelf and the distributor's salesman decides which. A quick-commerce app can list hundreds and its algorithm decides which one the shopper sees first. That helps a big brand that can pay for placement and hurts it when a cheaper or trendier rival is one tap away. HUL is so far winning more than it is losing, because the channel is growing faster than its share is falling; the risk is what happens to margins once the platforms want a bigger cut.

Online-born brands: buy them or fight them

Redseer counts about 70 new-age consumer brands with more than ₹100 crore of revenue, concentrated in actives skincare, protein snacks, clean-label foods and personal care, and mostly built on e-commerce and quick commerce. Both members have chosen to buy rather than fight. HUL paid ₹2,955 crore (pre-money) for 90.5% of Minimalist, about 8.5 times its FY24 revenue, and took OZiva to 100%; ITC bought Yoga Bar, 24 Mantra, Prasuma and a stake in Mother Sparsh, a portfolio now running at about ₹1,500 crore of annual revenue. The economics of these brands are different: gross margins of 60–70% but advertising and customer-acquisition costs of 25–35% of sales, so EBITDA is thin until scale arrives. The risk is overpaying; a sibling company's Africa write-down is the cautionary tale in the Personal Care report.

Technology, inputs and tobacco's missing future

Both companies are spending on technology behind the brands. HUL cites AI across its value chain, digitally powered research and development, digital-first distribution centres and a "Liquids Lab of the Future"; it has eight World Economic Forum "Lighthouse" designations across six sites, with Haridwar and Sonepat added in Q1 FY27. ITC runs Studio Alchemy, which has produced about 40,000 AI-generated content pieces, has more than 900 patent applications and links more than 250 factories and 52 warehouses through its DigiArc system. Expect lower advertising production costs and better-targeted trade spending from both.

The structural disruption risk in tobacco is the opposite of the FMCG one: not too much innovation but too little. The 2019 ban on e-cigarettes and heated tobacco protects ITC's combustible franchise today but removes the next-generation products that re-rated Philip Morris International abroad; nicotine pouches are a regulatory grey area. Climate is a shared input risk: monsoon variability (HUL says a deficit beyond 15–20% would start to matter), Indonesia's B50 biodiesel mandate tightening palm oil supply, and tea crop shortfalls in Assam. Format shifts in detergents and personal wash (liquids, body wash) are mostly an opportunity for HUL, whose liquids business passed ₹4,000 crore in FY26.

Why 41× and 16×: the de-rating, the tobacco discount and the holding company

Both stocks fell hard in 2026, for different reasons. HUL's multiple is a bet on a decade of volume-led growth; ITC's is a bet that the cigarette profit pool will not grow, with cash and holdings thrown in.

On 21 October 2026 HUL closed at ₹1,850, 28% below its 52-week high of ₹2,553, and ITC at ₹262, 38% below its high of ₹426. HUL's market value of ₹4.35 lakh crore was about 16% below the ₹5.15 lakh crore recorded in our June primer; ITC's ₹3.28 lakh crore about 9% below. Neither had reported its September quarter. The de-rating has different causes. HUL's came despite improving volumes, and reflects input-cost fears, the wider FMCG multiple compression, foreign selling and the question of how much growth costs in margin. ITC's is a direct response to the tax reset: by early August, ICICI Securities noted, the stock had fallen about 28% since the tax increase was announced.

What HUL's 41 times pays for

At about 41 times clean trailing earnings (42 times FY26), HUL trades in line with slow-growing Indian staples such as Britannia and Dabur and well below fast growers such as Nestlé India and Tata Consumer (Section 14). Relative to its own history it is cheap: Indian staples traded at 50–70 times between 2014 and 2021 and HUL peaked at 60–70 times trailing. Relative to global consumer companies it is expensive: Unilever, P&G and Nestlé trade at roughly 18–24 times (INDICATIVE). The premium is for the runway, low per-capita consumption that can compound for decades, and for scarcity, since few listed Indian businesses combine HUL's scale, returns and predictability. ICICI Securities' HOLD rating in July valued HUL at 41 times FY28 estimated EPS of ₹53.5 (target ₹2,195), which is roughly where the market prices it on trailing numbers today, two years earlier.

Three things could compress that multiple further: volume growth falling back below 3% as prices rise, a margin print near the bottom of the new 22–24% range without a volume payoff, or evidence that quick commerce and Reliance are eroding the distribution moat. Three could re-expand it: two or three quarters of 6–7% UVG with stable margins, premium formats lifting the gross margin back above 51%, and clarity on the McCormick licence and fee base. The parent's take is a permanent drag: about ₹2,000 crore of fees comes off the top every year, and minorities do not vote on it.

In plain terms

Paying 41 times earnings means paying about 41 years of today's profit up front. Investors do that only if they believe profit will grow steadily for a very long time. HUL's job is to keep proving that, quarter after quarter, through volume rather than price. When it fails for a few quarters, as in FY24 and FY25, the multiple shrinks; it does not need to fail badly.

What ITC's 16 times leaves out

ITC's 16.6 times trailing earnings already includes a lot that is not cigarettes. Take its ₹3,28,336 crore market value and subtract net cash (about ₹24,660 crore), the ITC Hotels stake at market (about ₹12,965 crore) and ITC Infotech at its swap value (₹11,920 crore): what remains, about ₹2,78,800 crore, is the price of the cigarette, FMCG, agri and paperboard businesses. Against FY26 earnings excluding ITC Infotech's profit (₹408 crore) and an estimated ₹1,450 crore of post-tax income on the cash pile, that is roughly 14.5 times (INDICATIVE). If the consumer business were valued like a mid-sized Indian FMCG company, say ₹60,000–80,000 crore, and agri and paperboards at a broker's ~₹22,500 crore, the market would be paying roughly 11–12 times FY26 cigarette earnings after tax (INDICATIVE arithmetic), close to where BAT and Altria trade globally.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against India's two diversified giants

The bull case is volume-led growth at HUL and a priced-through tax shock at ITC. The bear case is a second input shock, a disrupted moat and a tax ratchet.

The two members do not share a single investment case, so the bull and bear columns below list the sub-sector arguments and name the member each applies to. The balance of evidence in October 2026 is mixed: HUL's operating trends have improved while its share price has fallen; ITC's operating trends have deteriorated broadly in line with what its share price already reflects.

The bull case

  • HUL volume is back. UVG of 4–6% for four quarters, USG at a 13-quarter high, rural demand stepping up and an easy September-quarter base after the GST destocking.
  • HUL has room in its margin promise. The 22–24% range lets it absorb the second input leg or reinvest without breaching guidance, and premium formats (liquids, body wash, Minimalist) lift gross margin over time.
  • ITC can price its way back. With a fixed per-stick excise, a further 12–13% price rise lifts net per stick by about a third; history (FY14–FY17, then 2020–23) shows ITC recovering profit per stick after tax shocks.
  • Cash and payouts. ₹27,803 crore of dividends in FY26, ₹28,591 crore of combined net cash, ITC's 5.5% yield and 80–85% payout policy.
  • Hidden value at ITC. About ₹20 a share in ITC Hotels and ITC Infotech, about ₹20 in net cash, and an FMCG business that is now larger than Nestlé India by revenue.
  • Valuation reset. HUL at about 41× is at the low end of its decade range; ITC at about 14.5× its operating businesses (INDICATIVE).

The bear case

  • A second input shock. Brent back at $116.8 in September, wheat up 48% and tea firm; HUL's gross margin may fall another 200 basis points (Nuvama), and soaps are already losing volume.
  • The moat is being re-priced. Quick commerce, online-born brands and Reliance's ₹1 lakh crore ambition all attack HUL's mass brands and distribution economics.
  • A tax ratchet. If the February 2026 reset is followed by further NCCD, excise or GST increases, ITC's price rises will be absorbed by the state again; illicit trade (about 26%, an industry estimate) grows with every step.
  • ITC volumes worse than modelled. A cumulative 35% price rise at an elasticity of −0.3 implies a 10% volume loss, not the Street's 5–6%.
  • The parent's take. About ₹2,000 crore of fees and ₹6,250 crore of dividends flowed to Unilever in FY26; McCormick licence terms are unknown.
  • Slow FMCG progress at ITC. PBIT of 7.4% after two decades; each 100 basis points of improvement is worth only ~₹270 crore against a cigarette business that can lose that in a week of bad tax news.

What would settle the debate? For HUL, two more quarters of UVG at 5% or better with EBITDA above 22.5% would show that the recovery is volume-led and affordable; a fall in UVG below 3% as prices rise would show the opposite. For ITC, a September-quarter cigarette PBIT above Q1's ₹3,341 crore with only a modest further volume decline would show the pricing is holding; a Budget increase in NCCD or excise on 1 February 2027 would reset the clock. Neither outcome is predictable from today's data, which is why both stocks trade well below their highs.

In plain terms

The optimist sees HUL selling more soap and shampoo every quarter and ITC slowly raising cigarette prices until its profits recover, with both paying generous dividends meanwhile. The pessimist sees costs rising again, new rivals stealing HUL's shelf space and the government raising cigarette taxes again as soon as ITC catches up. The next two quarterly results and the February budget will show which picture is closer.
