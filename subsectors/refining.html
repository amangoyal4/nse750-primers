00

The one-page view: two businesses in one ticker, moving in opposite directions

Refining earns a spread per barrel that the world sets. Marketing earns a spread per litre that, in practice, New Delhi sets. In 2026 the two went to opposite extremes.

Every Indian oil company in this report runs some mix of two very different businesses. Refining buys crude oil, mostly from the Gulf and Russia, and turns it into diesel, petrol, LPG, jet fuel, naphtha and bitumen. It earns the gross refining margin (GRM): the value of the product slate minus the delivered cost of crude, quoted in dollars per barrel and set by Asian product markets. Marketing sells those products through about one lakh petrol pumps, LPG distributors, airport depots and bulk contracts. It earns a margin of a few rupees per litre, and although petrol and diesel prices were formally freed in 2010 and 2014, the government still decides in practice when pump prices may move.

The six listed companies are Reliance Industries, Indian Oil (IOC), Bharat Petroleum (BPCL), Hindustan Petroleum (HPCL), Mangalore Refinery and Petrochemicals (MRPL) and Chennai Petroleum (CPCL). Between them they own 232.75 million tonnes a year (MMTPA) of refining capacity, 87% of India's 267.1 MMTPA on 1 April 2026. They split into three kinds. IOC, BPCL and HPCL are the state-controlled oil marketing companies (OMCs): integrated refiner-marketers that run almost all of India's petrol pumps and all of its subsidised cooking gas. MRPL and CPCL are standalone refiners, subsidiaries of ONGC and IOC respectively, that sell almost everything they make to their parent OMCs. Reliance runs the world's largest single-site refinery at Jamnagar, but it is also a telecom and retail conglomerate, and the refinery now produces less than a third of its operating profit.

FY26 was the year both engines fired. Brent spent most of the year between $60 and $70 a barrel, Asian refining margins recovered from the FY25 trough, and pump prices that had been fixed since 2022 left the OMCs a fat marketing margin until February 2026. Combined net profit of the six rose 63% to ₹1.88 lakh crore. Excluding Reliance it rose 171%, from ₹34,132 crore to ₹92,595 crore. MRPL's profit went from ₹56 crore to ₹1,925 crore; CPCL's from ₹214 crore to ₹3,103 crore. A refiner with no marketing arm has very high operating leverage to the margin.

Then the West Asia war broke the pattern. From early March 2026, strikes on Gulf infrastructure and disruption in the Strait of Hormuz pushed Brent above $100, to about $126 at the late-April peak. Refining margins exploded: Asian diesel cracks went from about $22 a barrel before the war to a record of just over $87 on 16 September. But pump prices did not move until May. The state intervened with four levers at once: it discounted the refinery transfer price at which refineries sell to marketing (from 16 March), cut excise duty on petrol and diesel by ₹10 a litre (notified on 26 March), imposed an export duty on diesel and jet fuel to keep product at home, and finally allowed four pump-price increases in May totalling about ₹7.38 a litre on petrol and ₹7.52 on diesel. Domestic LPG losses rose from about ₹80 to about ₹510 a cylinder.

Reliance has to be read separately. It is 79% of the six companies' market value and 51% of their FY26 profit, but its oil-to-chemicals (O2C) segment earned ₹60,546 crore of the group's ₹2,07,911 crore reported EBITDA in FY26, about 29%, and 31% in Q1 FY27. The valuation gap is the policy wedge made visible. The five ex-Reliance companies are worth ₹4.38 lakh crore, 4.7 times FY26 earnings. The three OMCs trade at 0.84 to 1.28 times book value and 3.1 to 3.7 times EV/EBITDA, with dividend yields of 6–7%. Reliance trades at 17 times FY26 earnings and 22 times trailing. Q1 FY27 losses distort trailing P/E ratios: HPCL's is 44 times on a trailing profit of only ₹1,671 crore, against about 4 times on FY26. The market is not saying the OMCs are bad refiners. The five forces that decide this sub-sector, and the order of this report:

  • The pricing state. For the OMCs the biggest variable is not crude but how the government splits a crude shock between consumers, the exchequer and the companies. Each ₹1 a litre on petrol and diesel is worth roughly ₹15,300 crore a year of OMC EBITDA (indicative).
  • The global crack cycle. Asian product margins drive the refining leg. Standalone refiners and Reliance take it straight; for the OMCs it is partly offset by marketing, and in 2026 partly taxed away.
  • Crude access and geopolitics. Russian discounts, US and EU sanctions, the Hormuz chokepoint (about half of India's crude and about 90% of its LPG imports) and the rupee.
  • Integration depth. How much a company refines relative to what it sells, and how much of each barrel it turns into petrochemicals, decides how a shock lands. HPCL refines about half of what it sells; MRPL and CPCL sell nothing at retail.
  • Capex and transition. About 42 MMTPA of new capacity by 2028 against 1–3% demand growth, 20% ethanol already in petrol, and electric two-wheelers taking share.
In plain terms

An Indian oil marketing company is a refinery bolted to a chain of petrol pumps whose prices the government steers. When crude spikes, the refinery usually earns more and the pumps lose money, so the two halves offset each other. In 2026 the government took part of the refinery's windfall through transfer-price discounts and export duties, and held pump prices until May, so the offset failed and the OMCs lost money. The two refiners without pumps, and Reliance with very few, kept earning. That one quarter explains more about this sector than any multi-year average.

What comes next is unusually crowded. HPCL and CPCL report Q2 FY27 on 22 October, BPCL on 23 October and IOC on 30 October; Reliance's Q2 had not been published as of 19 October. Q2 should look much better than Q1: pump prices were about ₹7.5 a litre higher from late May, crude cooled for part of the quarter, JPMorgan judged marketing margins back at pre-conflict levels by late June, and diesel cracks hit a record in September. MRPL's Q2 is the first evidence. But Brent settled at $109.86 on 16 October after renewed attacks on tankers near Hormuz, so Q3 opens with the same risk that produced Q1, and the LPG under-recovery has kept growing throughout.

01

From crude to forecourt: how a barrel becomes profit

Six links in the chain. Value is captured at three of them: buying crude well, cracking it into high-value products, and selling those products at a margin the state allows.

A barrel of crude is 159 litres of a mixture that no one can use directly. The refinery's job is to separate it by boiling point (distillation) and then to break heavy fractions into lighter, more valuable ones (cracking, coking, hydrocracking). A simple refinery turns a lot of each barrel into fuel oil and bitumen that sell below the cost of crude. A complex refinery turns more of it into diesel, petrol and jet fuel that sell above it. That difference, multiplied by millions of barrels, is the refining business.

Standalone refiners (MRPL, CPCL) stop at Step 3 and sell to the OMCs at the refinery transfer price. The OMCs run Steps 3 to 5. Reliance runs Steps 1 to 3 at world scale, exports heavily, adds petrochemicals and has a small retail arm (Jio-bp).

Step 1: crude selection is the first source of margin

Crude purchase is 85–90% of an Indian refiner's cash cost, so small differences in what is paid per barrel move profit a great deal. India imported 4.8–5.0 million barrels a day (mb/d) of crude in 2026 and relies on imports for roughly 85–88% of its needs. A complex refinery can buy cheaper heavy, high-sulphur ("sour") grades that a simple unit cannot process, and it can switch suppliers when a discount appears. A $2 a barrel discount on 1.7 mb/d is worth about ₹11,800 crore a year at ₹94.9 to the dollar. That is why Russia's share of India's crude rose from about 2% before 2022 to more than 40%, and why sanctions and waivers on Russian oil matter so much (Section 09).

Step 3: the refinery and the GRM

The gross refining margin is the core number. It is the value of all products minus the delivered cost of crude, per barrel. Reported GRM includes inventory gains or losses (when crude prices rise, stock bought earlier at a lower price is sold at the new higher product prices, flattering the margin). Core GRM strips that out. In 2026 a third version appeared: the post-SAED GRM, net of the special additional excise duty on product exports. BPCL's Q1 FY27 GRM was $41.4 a barrel before that duty and $17.0 after it. The regional benchmark is the Singapore complex GRM: about $3.8 a barrel in FY25, about $5.6 in FY26 to February, and about $30 at the March 2026 peak. A crack spread is the margin on one product: diesel price minus crude price, for example.

Step 5: marketing and the transfer price

The marketing margin is the retail selling price minus taxes (central excise and state VAT), minus the dealer's commission, minus the price at which the marketing arm buys product from the refinery. That internal price is the refinery transfer price (RTP), set at import parity: what the product would cost if imported. Standalone refiners sell to the OMCs at the same RTP. From 16 March 2026 the OMCs applied discounted RTPs, which moved margin out of refining and into marketing. CPCL's Q1 FY27 figures included a ₹385 crore retrospective product-price revision effective from that date. When pump prices are frozen below cost, the gap is an under-recovery. Today that term applies formally to domestic LPG only, and informally to petrol and diesel whenever the state holds their prices.

Pipelines, LPG, lubricants and petrochemicals

Three quieter businesses sit around the main two. Product pipelines carry fuel from refineries to depots at a regulated tariff; IOC's 20,000-plus kilometre grid is a deep moat and a steady earner. LPG is a distribution business with a social obligation: the three OMCs supply effectively all subsidised domestic cooking gas (Indane, Bharatgas, HP Gas) to about 33 crore connections, and the government decides both the cylinder price and, later and partially, the compensation for selling below cost. Lubricants (SERVO, MAK, HP Lubes) earn high margins on small volumes. Petrochemicals (polymers, paraxylene, PTA) convert part of the barrel into products whose prices are set by global chemical markets rather than by the pump-price debate. Reliance converts a high share of its barrel; IOC about 6%; BPCL 2–3% today, aiming for about 8% by 2028. The polymer side links to our plastics and chemical intermediates reports, where these are the feedstocks.

In plain terms

Money is made in three places: buying crude cheaply, turning it into diesel and petrol rather than fuel oil, and selling those fuels at a margin. The first two are decided by the company and the world market. The third is decided, in practice, by the government. A refinery's margin per litre and a pump's margin per litre are roughly the same size, about ₹5–7 in a normal year, so a ₹25 loss on each litre of diesel wipes out several good quarters of refining.

One more piece of vocabulary matters for the rest of the report. Capacity is quoted in MMTPA, million metric tonnes per annum; 1 MMTPA is roughly 20,000 barrels a day, and one tonne of crude is about 7.33 barrels. Indian refiners routinely run above nameplate (BPCL processed 41.15 MMT in FY26 on 35.3 MMTPA of capacity, 116.6%), because nameplates are set conservatively and debottlenecking adds throughput over time. Utilisation above 100% is normal here; below 95% is a warning sign. The Nelson Complexity Index measures how much secondary conversion capacity a refinery has: Jamnagar is about 21, MRPL about 11.3 and most PSU refineries 8–12.

02

A ₹20 lakh crore fuel market and six listed companies

India is the world's fourth-largest refiner and third-largest oil consumer. Demand grows slowly; capacity is about to grow fast.

India consumed 243.2 million tonnes of petroleum products in FY26, up 1.7% on the year, according to PPAC. Refining capacity was 267.1 MMTPA on 1 April 2026 (about 5.35 mb/d), up from 258.1 MMTPA a year earlier; the whole 9 MMTPA increase was HPCL's new Rajasthan refinery (HRRL). That makes India the world's fourth-largest refiner after the US, China and Russia, with about 5% of global capacity, and the third-largest oil consumer with roughly 5.5% of world demand. It also makes India a net exporter of products: 56.94 million tonnes in the first eleven months of FY26, mostly from Reliance's export refinery and Nayara at Vadinar.

At retail prices the product market is roughly ₹20–21 lakh crore a year (about $215–225 billion at ₹94.9). That is an INDICATIVE estimate: petrol (42.6 million tonnes, about 57.5 billion litres at about ₹95) is roughly ₹5.5 lakh crore; diesel (94.7 million tonnes, about 112 billion litres at about ₹88) roughly ₹9.8 lakh crore; LPG, jet fuel, naphtha, bitumen, petroleum coke and the rest add ₹5–6 lakh crore. The figure includes more than ₹3 lakh crore of central and state taxes, and it uses pump prices from before the May 2026 increases.

03

Who burns the fuel: diesel, petrol, LPG and the ethanol brake

Demand is broad, price-insensitive in the short run and slowly losing petrol to ethanol and electricity. Diesel is the anchor; LPG is the social obligation.

Fuel demand in India follows the real economy, not the stock market. Trucks, tractors, two-wheelers, kitchens and aircraft each pull a different product, and each has its own growth rate and its own long-term threat. For a refiner the product mix matters because margins differ: diesel and jet fuel usually carry the widest cracks, petrol is middling, and naphtha, fuel oil and coke often sell below crude.

Diesel: 39% of the barrel and the freight economy

Diesel moves goods. It fuels trucks, farm machinery, construction equipment, backup generators and what is left of railway diesel traction. It grew 3.6% in FY26, in line with industrial activity and infrastructure spending; the e-way bill count and cement and steel output are the best monthly read-across. In March 2026 diesel demand jumped 8.0% year on year as buyers stocked up ahead of feared shortages and farm demand peaked. The long-term threats are real but slow: LNG and electric trucks are nascent, and dedicated rail freight corridors take long-haul tonnage over a decade. Diesel is the most resilient transport fuel, and also the one with the widest crack in 2026.

Petrol: 17.5% and the ethanol diversion

Petrol fuels two-wheelers and cars. It grew 6.5% in FY26 and 7.6% in March 2026, faster than diesel, because car and two-wheeler fleets keep expanding. But every litre sold at the pump now contains 20% ethanol: India achieved E20 blending in 2025-26, from under 1.5% in 2013-14. At roughly 18.5% effective blending, about 10.6 billion of the 57.5 billion litres of petrol-pool demand is met by ethanol from sugar cane and grain rather than by refinery gasoline (INDICATIVE estimate). That diverted one to two years of petrol demand growth away from refiners. BIS has notified fuel standards for E22, E25, E27 and E30, but the government has not committed to a mandate beyond E20; E27 and E30 are standards and discussion, not policy. Reported plans point to E21 by 2027 and E25 by 2029, and to 500 flex-fuel (E85) pumps by December 2026.

LPG: 13.7% and the cylinder the state prices

India has about 33 crore domestic LPG connections, of which about 10.3 crore were given to poorer households under the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY). Volume grows with household formation and commercial use. LPG grew 6.0% in FY26, but that full-year figure hides a 12.6% fall in March 2026, when supply through Hormuz was disrupted and commercial LPG was rationed (cut, then restored to 20%, 50% and 70% of normal). From 1 October 2026 subsidised refills require Aadhaar biometric authentication, intended to stop diversion of cheap domestic cylinders to commercial users; that may trim recorded "domestic" volume and the under-recovery that comes with it. LPG is a product the OMCs must supply at a price they do not set, which is why it appears again in Sections 07 and 08.

Jet fuel, feedstocks and the structural brakes

Jet fuel grew only 2.0% in FY26 as Middle East airspace closures disrupted routes. Naphtha and petroleum coke are industrial inputs: coke competes with coal in cement kilns, naphtha feeds petrochemical crackers, and both fell about 10%. The long-run brakes on transport fuel are ethanol (already in place), electric vehicles and CNG. Electric two-wheelers took roughly 6–7% of sales in FY26 and electric cars about 3–5%, and there are more than 7,500 CNG stations (all INDICATIVE figures from industry data, not re-checked for this report). The consensus view, including the IEA's, is that Indian oil demand still grows about 3% a year to 2030 and that India is the largest single source of global demand growth, with transport-fuel growth slowing after about 2030.

Price sensitivity and the FY27 outlook

Fuel demand barely responds to price in the short run: a truck still has to deliver, and a family still has to cook. That is why the state can raise prices in a crisis without much loss of volume, and also why it is reluctant to: the burden falls on everyone, immediately and visibly. ICRA expected FY27 demand to grow 1–2%. The roughly ₹7.5 a litre of pump-price increases in May, plus commercial LPG rationing from March to May, point to the low end of that range (our estimate). Watch PPAC's monthly consumption data, especially petrol and diesel after the May hikes and LPG after Aadhaar authentication.

In plain terms

India's fuel demand is steady, broad and still growing, but slowly. Diesel tracks trucks and factories, petrol tracks two-wheelers and cars, and LPG tracks kitchens. Ethanol has already taken about a fifth of the petrol pool away from refineries, and electric scooters are starting to take more. None of this changes a quarter's profit; the price of crude and the government's response do. But it does decide whether the new refineries now being built will have a home market or will have to export.

Two final points for an investor. First, demand growth is not the lever that moves these stocks: a 2% rise in volume is worth far less than a ₹1 change in the per-litre margin. Second, the composition of demand does matter for refinery design. The new Indian capacity is being built for middle distillates (diesel and jet fuel) and petrochemicals, which is the right bet if petrol is the product most exposed to ethanol and electrification. Readers following the gas side of the 2026 shock, including the halt of Qatari LNG that hit fertiliser plants, should see our fertilisers report.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: crude is almost all of it

A refiner keeps two to six paise of each rupee in a normal year. In a war quarter with frozen pump prices, an OMC's cost of goods alone exceeds its revenue.

The cost structure of a refiner is brutally simple. Crude oil and purchased products are 85–90 rupees of every 100 of revenue. Staff cost a rupee or so. Power, repairs, catalysts, freight and the commissions paid to dealers and LPG distributors cost three or four. What is left is EBITDA of 7–10%, and after depreciation, interest and tax, a net margin of 2–6%. Margins this thin mean that small changes in the crude-to-product spread move profit by multiples.

Refinery opex: small, fixed and mostly in rupees

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 an Indian refiner takes in, about ₹86–90 goes straight back out to pay for crude. The plant itself is cheap to run once built. So profit is a thin sliver between two very large numbers, the price of crude and the price of products, and it can swing from 7% of revenue to a loss within a quarter. One consequence for investors: balance sheets in this business are dominated by working capital. Net debt figures in this sector should always be read with the crude price on the balance-sheet date in mind.

The private networks matter more than their size suggests because of how they behave in a crisis. In normal times they price at or slightly below PSU pumps. The unlisted players

Three unlisted refiners shape the market. Nayara Energy runs the 20 MMTPA Vadinar refinery in Gujarat and is 49.13% owned by Russia's Rosneft; it was sanctioned by the EU in July 2025, which complicates its exports to Europe and its access to Western services. On the retail side Shell and Jio-bp are the only meaningful private brands other than Nayara. India also imports products, chiefly LPG, of which nearly 90% came through Hormuz before the war.

In plain terms

The state owns most of India's refineries and almost all of its petrol pumps, partly directly and partly through other state companies such as ONGC and Oil India. That web means a loss at HPCL is partly offset by a gain at its parent ONGC when crude spikes, and that the government can ask its companies to absorb a shock without passing a law. Private refiners and pumps exist, but when prices are frozen below cost they step back, and the state companies carry the market.

One correction worth recording: our MRPL primer calls Mangalore "India's largest single-location refinery". It is not. MRPL is, however, one of the more complex PSU refineries (Nelson index about 11.3) and has its own petrochemical units, which is why it earns close to the larger complex refineries' margins.

Archetype B: the hedge that works only when the state allows it

Archetype C: a pure bet on the GRM

A standalone refiner has one product line: the margin between crude and products, times barrels. With fixed costs and no marketing buffer, its operating leverage is extreme. It had no marketing catastrophe. Section 15 covers why the stock is not priced as a refiner at all.

Archetypes D and E: the private players who step back

The 2026 war: one shock, two opposite P&Ls Deep dive

Record refining margins and record marketing losses arrived in the same quarter. Who ended up with which depended on the business model and on four decisions taken in Delhi.

Most oil shocks hurt or help the whole sector together. The 2026 shock did both at once, to different companies, which is why it is the best lesson this sector has offered in a decade. Refining margins went to records because the war removed Gulf product exports and diesel supply tightened worldwide. Marketing went to record losses because Indian pump prices did not move for two and a half months. Each company's result depended on how much of each business it owned and on how the state chose to split the bill.

The sequence

  • Apr 2025 – Feb 2026
    "Both engines firing". Brent $60–70, Singapore complex GRM recovers from ~$3.8 to ~$5.6, pump prices unchanged since May 2022 except a ₹2 pre-election cut (Mar 2024) and a ₹2 excise rise on 7 Apr 2025 that the OMCs absorbed. Marketing margins healthy until February (ICRA).
  • Early Mar 2026
    The US/Israel–Iran war escalates: strikes on Gulf infrastructure, Qatar LNG halted, Hormuz disrupted. Brent goes above $100. Asian diesel crack reaches $85.6 by end-March; Singapore complex GRM ~$30, the highest since 2022.
  • 14 Mar 2026
    Commercial LPG rationed (cut, then restored to 20%, 50% and 70% of normal).
  • 16 Mar 2026
    OMCs move to discounted refinery transfer prices, shifting margin from the refining leg to the marketing leg. CPCL later books a ₹385 Cr retrospective revision for this.
  • 26 Mar 2026
    Notification No. 11/2026-Central Excise, effective immediately: excise on petrol cut from ₹13 to ₹3 a litre and on diesel from ₹10 to nil, to cut OMC losses rather than pump prices. Export duties on diesel (₹18.5 a litre at the time) and jet fuel are imposed separately, reviewed fortnightly.
  • Late Mar – Apr 2026
    Peak losses: about ₹24 a litre on petrol and ₹30 on diesel (press reports). Brent peaks at ~$126 in late April; the Indian basket averages $115 in April.
  • 1 May 2026
    Export duty at ₹23 a litre on diesel and ₹33 on jet fuel; nil on petrol (PIB).
  • May 2026
    First pump-price increases since May 2022: four hikes in the second half of May (reported 15–25 May) totalling ~₹7.38 a litre on petrol and ~₹7.52 on diesel. Delhi petrol about ₹102, diesel about ₹95. Rupee hits ~₹96–97 to the dollar.

The record margin that was partly taxed away

On paper the refining legs earned a fortune. BPCL's Q1 FY27 GRM was $41.4 a barrel before the special excise duty on exports and $17.0 after it. The three OMCs' Q1 FY27 profit fell by about ₹33,000 crore year on year. What Q2 and Q3 FY27 should look like

Q2 FY27 (July–September) should be much better for the OMCs. Pump prices were about ₹7.5 a litre higher, crude cooled to the $75–80s for part of the quarter, marketing margins were back at pre-conflict levels by late June, and the diesel crack hit a record in September while the export duty on diesel was cut from ₹23 to ₹20 a litre on 16 September. MRPL's Q2 profit of ₹1,881 crore (standalone; ₹1,901 crore consolidated on screener.in), on a GRM of $14.8, is the first evidence. Q3 is the risk. Brent was back at about $110 on 16 October. If pump prices are not raised again, the marketing loss returns; and the LPG under-recovery, which kept growing even as petrol and diesel recovered, is the slow-burning problem (Section 08).

In plain terms

In the worst quarter, roughly half of the cost of keeping fuel cheap fell on the government through lower taxes and half on the three state oil companies through losses. Drivers started paying part of it from late May. The next results, out between 22 and 30 October, should show the OMCs back in profit for July to September. The quarter after that depends on whether crude stays near $110 and whether Delhi lets pump prices move again.

The pricing state: five levers that decide OMC profit Deep dive

Petrol and diesel prices are free on paper. In practice the state has five levers, used all five in 2026, and each moved OMC profit by thousands of crore.

On paper the government stepped back from fuel pricing long ago. The Administered Pricing Mechanism was dismantled in April 2002; petrol was deregulated in June 2010 and diesel in October 2014; daily dynamic pricing began on 16 June 2017. Only domestic LPG (and kerosene through the ration system, now negligible) remains formally subsidised. In practice, pump prices did not change between May 2022 and May 2026 except for a ₹2 cut before the March 2024 election, while crude moved between $60 and $126. The OMCs are majority state-owned, and the state decides when their prices move.

The 26 March excise cut was explicitly not a consumer subsidy: pump prices did not fall. In plain terms

Fuel prices in India are "free" in the same way a state company's dividend is voluntary. The government did not need a law to stop pump prices rising in March 2026; it simply did not allow them to. Instead it cut its own tax by ₹10 a litre so the companies would lose less, taxed fuel exports to keep supply at home, and let prices rise only in May. For an OMC shareholder, a ₹1 change in what the government allows per litre is worth about as much as a year's growth in fuel demand.

LPG: the under-recovery that keeps growing

Domestic cooking gas is the one product the government still formally prices below cost. The under-recovery per cylinder is the import-parity cost (linked to the Saudi contract price), plus ocean freight, bottling, inland freight, distributor commission and 5% GST, minus the regulated retail price. The Delhi price of a 14.2 kg domestic cylinder has been ₹853 since April 2025 (from memory; not re-checked), with a lower effective price for PMUY households. In Q4 FY26 the loss was about ₹80 a cylinder. With Gulf supply disrupted it rose to about ₹510 in Q1 FY27.

Compensation is discretionary and arrives late. In October 2022 the government gave a one-time ₹22,000 crore for losses from June 2020 to June 2022. In August 2025 the Cabinet approved ₹30,000 crore, paid in 12 monthly tranches from November 2025: IOC ₹14,486 crore, HPCL ₹7,920 crore and BPCL about ₹7,594 crore (BPCL's share is derived by difference). By August 2026 the industry's cumulative under-recovery had reached about ₹62,000 crore (₹59,000 crore in July), against a package that was already fully allocated. HPCL's own cumulative under-recovery rose from ₹12,799 crore in March to ₹16,405 crore on 30 June 2026, after ₹1,980 crore of compensation was recognised in the quarter; the gross Q1 FY27 loss was therefore about ₹5,590 crore, roughly 11 crore cylinders at ₹510 each.

Why the market prices all this at a discount

Three features of the pricing state explain the OMCs' low valuations. First, earnings volatility comes from policy as much as from markets: BPCL's profit went from ₹2,131 crore in FY23 to ₹26,859 crore in FY24, ₹13,337 crore in FY25, ₹25,843 crore in FY26 and a loss in Q1 FY27. Second, capital allocation follows policy: dividends go mostly to the government, capex is directed toward energy security, and the attempt to privatise BPCL in 2020–22 was abandoned. Third, the asymmetry: in a crisis the state takes the upside first (export duty, transfer-price discount) and returns it slowly (pump-price hikes, LPG compensation). The bull case says buy book value and a 6–7% yield and wait for the state to restore pricing, as it did in FY24 and FY26. The bear case says the state will always keep the upside when it matters most.

In plain terms

Every cooking-gas cylinder sold in the war quarter lost the OMCs about ₹510, and the government had already promised all of its ₹30,000 crore of compensation before the war began. The unpaid balance is now about ₹62,000 crore across the industry. For HPCL it equals a quarter of everything the company is worth on its books. Whether that money comes back is a political decision, not a commercial one.

Crude access: Russia, sanctions and the Hormuz chokepoint Deep dive

Where the crude comes from has decided a large part of Indian refining margins since 2022. In 2026 it decided whether the refineries could run at all.

Before 2022 Russia supplied about 2% of India's crude. After the invasion of Ukraine, Western buyers stepped away, Russian Urals traded at deep discounts, and Indian refiners bought heavily. By mid-2024 Russia supplied 43–44% of India's crude, more than 2 million barrels a day. Those discounts underpinned the OMCs' record FY24 margins and Reliance's export economics. Since then, sanctions, tariffs and a war have pushed the share down and back up, and the Strait of Hormuz has turned from a shipping lane into the sector's largest single risk.

The discount era, 2022–25

The discount on Russian crude was widest in 2022–23, roughly $10–30 a barrel at the peak, and had narrowed to about $2–5 by 2025 (from memory; not re-checked for this report). Even a narrow discount is worth a great deal at Indian volumes. A $2 a barrel discount on 1.7 mb/d, roughly the 2025 average Russian flow, is worth about ₹11,800 crore a year at ₹94.9 to the dollar. That is more than six times MRPL's FY26 profit, earned simply by buying from the right seller.

The squeeze, August 2025 to February 2026

Three measures cut Russian flows. Who benefits from sourcing flexibility

Complex coastal refineries can swap Gulf medium-sour crude for Russian Urals, Latin American heavy grades or US light crude as prices and sanctions change: Jamnagar, Paradip, Kochi, Mangalore, Visakh after its residue upgrade, and HRRL, which was designed for heavier crude. Six decades of policy, five profit shocks

Foreign majors gave way to state champions, a private complex refinery changed the benchmark, and every big crude spike since 2004 has ended the same way: the state decides who pays.

The history of Indian refining and marketing is a history of the state's role. Foreign oil companies built India's first refineries and pumps; the state nationalised them in the 1970s; it ran an administered pricing system for three decades; it freed prices formally between 2002 and 2017; and it has intervened informally in every crisis since. The cycle in this sector is therefore two cycles layered on top of each other: the global crack cycle, and the domestic pricing cycle.

Reading a refiner and an OMC: the KPIs that matter Deep dive

Two equations, thirteen numbers and a ten-minute routine. Most mistakes in this sector come from mixing bases: pre- and post-duty GRMs, gross and net revenue, standalone and consolidated profit.

A refiner's profit can be written as one equation in dollars per barrel. An OMC's profit needs a second equation in rupees per litre added to the first, plus an LPG term and a compensation term. Management controls only a few of the terms: throughput, opex, crude selection and some of the product slate. The world sets the cracks. The government sets the pump price, the taxes, the export duty, the transfer-price discount and the LPG compensation. Reading these companies well starts with knowing which term moved.

KPI 1 · Reported GRM ($/bbl)

What it is. The gross refining margin: the value of everything the refinery produced, minus the delivered cost of the crude it processed, divided by barrels processed. Formula. (Product realisation − crude cost) ÷ barrels; reported figures include inventory gains or losses. Worked example. A refinery that processes 100 million barrels, sells products for ₹65,000 crore and paid ₹57,000 crore for the crude has a gross margin of ₹8,000 crore; at ₹94.9 to the dollar that is about $843 million, or $8.4 a barrel. What drives it. Asian product cracks (diesel and jet above all), the crude discount the refinery secured, its complexity (how much heavy residue it upgrades), its product slate and, in 2026, the export duty and the transfer-price discount. Benchmark. For an Indian complex PSU refinery, above $10 is good, $5–9 average and below $4 weak.

KPI 2 · Core GRM and inventory gains

What it is. The GRM with inventory effects removed. Refineries hold weeks of crude and product stock. When crude rises, stock bought earlier at lower prices is sold at the new higher product prices, and the reported margin includes that one-off gain; when crude falls, the reverse. Formula. Core GRM = reported GRM − inventory gain per barrel (or + inventory loss). Worked example. A refinery reports $12 a barrel on 50 million barrels in a quarter when crude rose sharply, and discloses a ₹1,400 crore inventory gain. At ₹94.9 that gain is about $148 million, or $3 a barrel; the core GRM is about $9. What drives it. The same factors as the reported GRM, without the crude-price direction. Benchmark. Above $8 good, $4–8 average, below $3 weak. How the six compare. IOC disclosed a core GRM of $9.86 for nine months of FY26 against $8.41 reported, meaning it carried inventory losses in that period. BPCL's marketing arm booked inventory gains of about ₹842 crore in FY26 (a loss of ₹905 crore in FY25) and about ₹3,000–3,130 crore in Q1 FY27. The definition trap. Inventory effects appear in both refining and marketing, and companies disclose them inconsistently; a quarter in which crude rose sharply always flatters reported profit.

KPI 3 · Premium over the Singapore benchmark

What it is. How much more (or less) a refinery earns than Asia's benchmark complex refinery. Formula. Company GRM − Singapore complex GRM for the same period. Worked example. A company GRM of $9.2 against a Singapore average of $5.6 is a premium of $3.6 a barrel; on 110 million barrels at ₹94.9 that premium is worth about ₹3,760 crore a year. What drives it. Complexity, crude-sourcing skill (Russian discounts), a distillate-heavy yield, and location (freight to customers). Benchmark. A premium above $4 is good, $1–4 average, below $1 weak.

KPI 4 · Throughput and utilisation

What it is. Crude processed, in million tonnes, and the same figure divided by nameplate capacity. Formula. Utilisation = throughput ÷ nameplate capacity. Worked example. A refinery with 15 MMTPA of nameplate that processes 17 MMT runs at 113%. Each extra million tonnes at a $9 GRM adds 7.33 million barrels × $9 × ₹94.9, about ₹626 crore of gross margin, with almost no extra opex. What drives it. Planned shutdowns (a major turnaround takes four to six weeks every three to five years), unplanned outages and fires (HRRL had a fire in April 2026), crude availability (Hormuz), and debottlenecking. Benchmark. Above 105% good, 95–105% average, below 90% weak. How the six compare. In FY26 BPCL ran at 116.6%, MRPL 113%, CPCL 111.5%, IOC 107.4% and HPCL 106.3% (chart in Section 04). Reliance does not disclose throughput; at about 1.4 mb/d Jamnagar processes roughly 68–70 MMT a year (INDICATIVE). The definition trap. Nameplate changes when capacity is revised or a unit is shut: CPCL's utilisation is 111.5% on 10.5 MMTPA, but would look like 102% on the 11.5 MMTPA in its primer that still counts the closed Cauvery Basin unit.

KPI 5 · Distillate yield and complexity

What it is. Distillate yield is the share of output that is light and middle distillates (LPG, petrol, naphtha, jet fuel, diesel). The Nelson Complexity Index (NCI) measures how much secondary conversion capacity a refinery has relative to its crude distillation. Formula. Distillate yield = light + middle distillates ÷ crude processed. NCI = sum of each unit's capacity × its complexity factor ÷ crude distillation capacity. Worked example. If a refinery lifts its distillate yield from 74% to 76% on 26 MMT, it moves about 0.52 MMT from fuel oil (which sells below crude) into diesel and petrol; at a $25 a barrel price gap between the two (an assumption), that is about 3.8 million barrels × $25 × ₹94.9, roughly ₹900 crore a year. What drives it. Investment in hydrocrackers, cokers and residue upgradation units. HPCL's Visakh residue upgradation facility, stabilising in Q1 FY27, is a recent example. Benchmark. A distillate yield above 78% is good, 72–78% average, below 70% weak.

KPI 6 · Marketing margin (₹ per litre)

What it is. What the OMC earns on each litre of petrol or diesel sold, before its own marketing costs. Formula. Retail selling price − central excise − state VAT − dealer commission − refinery transfer price. Worked example. If petrol sells at ₹100 a litre, VAT and excise take ₹40, the dealer's commission is ₹4.5 and the transfer price is ₹51, the marketing margin is ₹4.5 a litre. If crude rises and the transfer price goes to ₹60 with the pump price unchanged, the margin becomes −₹4.5. What drives it. Almost entirely policy and crude: the pump price (frozen or raised), the excise rate (cut by ₹10 in March 2026), state VAT (usually a percentage, so it rises with the base price) and the import-parity transfer price. Benchmark. ₹4–7 a litre is good, ₹2–4 average (the industry's normative figure is about ₹3.5), below ₹1 or negative weak.

KPI 7 · LPG under-recovery and the uncompensated buffer

What it is. The loss on each subsidised domestic LPG cylinder, and the cumulative amount not yet compensated by the government. Formula. Under-recovery per cylinder = import-parity cost + freight + bottling + distributor commission + GST − retail price. Buffer = cumulative under-recovery − compensation recognised. The ratio that matters is buffer ÷ net worth. Worked example. HPCL's buffer was ₹12,799 crore in March 2026. In Q1 FY27 it recognised ₹1,980 crore of compensation and its buffer rose to ₹16,405 crore, so the gross loss in the quarter was ₹16,405 − ₹12,799 + ₹1,980, about ₹5,590 crore: roughly 11 crore cylinders at ₹510 each. What drives it. The Saudi contract price for propane and butane, freight (Hormuz), the rupee, and the frozen retail price. Benchmark. A buffer below 5% of net worth is good, 5–15% average, above 15% weak; per cylinder, nil to ₹50 good, ₹100–250 average, above ₹300 weak.

KPI 8 · Refining-to-marketing ratio

What it is. How much an OMC refines relative to how much it sells. Formula. Crude throughput ÷ product sales volume. Worked example. A company that refines 26 MMT and sells 51 MMT has a ratio of 0.51; it buys 25 MMT of product from other refiners at the transfer price. When the transfer price is discounted, it benefits on those bought-in barrels; when the marketing margin goes negative, it loses on all 51 MMT while earning refining margin on only 26. What drives it. Refinery additions (HRRL, IOC's expansions) and retail growth. Benchmark. Above 0.9 is well hedged, 0.7–0.9 average, below 0.6 marketing-exposed.

KPI 11 · Return on capital employed

What it is. Operating profit earned on all the capital, debt and equity, tied up in the business. Formula. EBIT ÷ (net worth + borrowings), screener.in definition. Worked example. Definition traps, collected

  • Gross versus net revenue. Company releases and our primers quote revenue including excise duty; screener.in and this report use revenue net of excise, 10–20% lower. BPCL's primer mixes the two on one page.
  • Three GRMs per quarter. Reported (with inventory), core (without) and, in 2026, pre- or post-export duty. BPCL's Q1 FY27 was $41.4 before the duty and $17.0 after.
  • Product versus blended marketing margins. Diesel at −₹25.2 a litre and a blended −₹5.4 can describe the same quarter.
  • Standalone versus consolidated profit. HPCL's Q1 FY27 loss was ₹11,526 crore standalone and ₹12,265 crore consolidated (HRRL is consolidated). IOC consolidates CPCL; HPCL does not consolidate MRPL.
  • Profit including minorities. Screener's profit for Reliance and IOC includes minority interests; attributable profit is lower.
  • Net debt proxies. Borrowings minus "investments" treats strategic stakes as cash and includes leases in debt.
  • Capacity bases. IOC is 70.25 MMTPA standalone or 80.75 with CPCL; HPCL 24.5 or 33.5 with HRRL; BPCL is 35.3, not 38; CPCL is 10.5, not 11.5.
  • Trailing P/E after a loss quarter. HPCL's trailing P/E of 44× reflects one bad quarter, not the earning power of the business; use FY26 or normalised earnings.
  • Exchange rate. Convert historical dollar margins at the period-average rate (about ₹87.6 for FY26) and forward sensitivities at the current rate (₹94.9).
  • Retail outlet counts. Retail outlets and "total outlets" (including CNG, EV and other formats) are different counts; BPCL's figure ranges from 22,000 to 24,600 across our primers.
In plain terms

Before comparing any two numbers in this sector, ask four questions. Is the revenue gross or net of excise? Is the profit standalone or consolidated? Is the refining margin before or after the export duty, and with or without inventory gains? And is the debt figure the company's own, or a database's estimate that counts shares in other companies as cash? Most of the confusion in coverage of these stocks comes from skipping one of those questions.

How to read a refiner's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the crude and the pump. Before opening the release, note the quarter's average Brent, the Indian basket, the rupee, and whether pump prices or excise changed. That tells you which way the marketing margin went before the company does.
  2. Read net profit on both bases. Standalone and consolidated; for HPCL, the difference is HRRL; for IOC, CPCL and joint ventures.
  3. Find the GRM and its basis. Reported and core, before and after export duty. Compare with the Singapore benchmark for the same months, not with the previous quarter's company figure alone.
  4. Find the inventory gain or loss. Subtract it from EBITDA to see the underlying quarter. A big gain in a rising-crude quarter will reverse when crude falls.
  5. Back out the marketing margin. For the OMCs, segment results (downstream) and the management call usually give petrol and diesel margins; if not, take EBITDA less refining (throughput × GRM × rupee − opex) and divide the remainder by fuel volumes.
  6. Read the LPG lines. Under-recovery for the quarter, compensation recognised, and the cumulative buffer. A rising buffer is a hidden loss; a big compensation tranche is a one-off gain.
  7. Check volumes and utilisation. Throughput versus nameplate, domestic sales growth versus PPAC's industry growth (market share), and any shutdowns.
  8. Check debt and working capital. Net debt movement versus profit; a large jump with no capex usually means crude payables or receivables moved.
  9. Check project status. HRRL utilisation, IOC's Panipat, Koyali and Barauni, BPCL's Bina, CPCL's Cauvery Basin: percentage complete, revised cost and date.
  10. Red flags. A GRM disclosure that is paused or whose basis changes (IOC paused its Q4 FY26 GRM); a rising LPG buffer without compensation; cost overruns announced in footnotes; and any "other income" or deferred-tax item large enough to turn a loss into a profit.
In plain terms

Start with what happened to crude and to pump prices in the quarter, because that predicts most of the result. Then check whether the refining margin quoted is before or after the export tax, strip out stock gains, work out what the pumps earned per litre, and see how much unpaid cooking-gas loss piled up. If those five numbers make sense together, the quarter makes sense.

Who is winning: integration, refining quality and price paid

Three maps and a scorecard. The winners of 2026 were the companies with the least exposure to the pump; the winners of the decade will be those with the best refineries and the cheapest new capacity.

"Winning" in this sub-sector depends on the horizon. Over the 2026 shock, the winners were the companies that do not sell fuel at regulated prices: MRPL, CPCL and Reliance. Over a full cycle, the winners are the companies with complex, flexible refineries running above nameplate, the least marketing exposure for their size, and capex that earns its cost. The three maps show each lens in turn.

The first map is the cleanest picture of the 2026 shock. On the right, the companies with no regulated retail exposure earned as much in the war quarter as in an average FY26 quarter, or more: MRPL about twice as much, CPCL about 1.3 times, Reliance about the same. On the left, the three OMCs are ranked by their refining-to-marketing ratio. IOC and BPCL, refining about three-quarters of what they sell, lost modestly. HPCL lost nearly three average quarters' profit.

The second map shows the valuation logic. The OMCs earned 19–26% ROCE in FY26 and trade at roughly book value: the market is pricing a through-cycle return far lower than FY26, which is consistent with the history in Section 10. Capacity outrunning demand

Demand grows by about 1–3% a year, roughly 3–7 million tonnes. The wave adds about 42 MMTPA in two to three years, most of it concentrated in late 2026. India already exports about 57 million tonnes of products a year. The new capacity will therefore depend on export margins, and those are exactly the margins the government taxed in 2026 and in 2022–24. A refinery built for exports in a country that imposes windfall export duties whenever cracks spike has its upside capped by policy and its downside left to the market. That asymmetry is the main policy risk to the returns on new capacity (our estimate of the mechanism, not a stated policy).

The wave also changes each OMC's exposure. IOC's +17.3 MMTPA lifts its refining-to-marketing ratio toward about 0.85, a natural hedge against the next pump-price freeze. HRRL lifts HPCL's from 0.51 to about 0.68 and reduces the diesel it must buy in. BPCL's Bina project raises its petrochemical share from 2–3% to about 8%. In each case, the investment makes the company less dependent on the marketing margin, which is the right direction, at a capital cost that will hold down reported returns for several years.

In plain terms

The state oil companies are adding about a sixth to India's refining capacity in two to three years, at costs that have run 6–84% over budget, into a market that grows a few per cent a year. The extra fuel will mostly be exported, and the government taxes exports whenever margins are high. The new plants make the companies less exposed to pump-price freezes, which is good, but they will earn modest returns unless refining and chemical margins stay strong.

Petrochemicals: the hedge with its own cycle

The PSUs are copying Reliance's integration because petrochemicals escape both fuel-demand risk and pump-price politics. IOC has the Paradip PX-PTA complex (₹13,805 crore, on budget) and an approved ₹61,077 crore Paradip petrochemical complex; BPCL the ₹49,000 crore Bina cracker and Kochi polypropylene; HPCL the 2.4 MMTPA of petrochemicals at HRRL; MRPL a proposed PACE expansion. The risk is timing. Chinese polymer and paraxylene capacity has compressed chemical margins worldwide, and these projects will start up into that glut. Readers can follow the polymer and intermediate cycle in our plastics and chemical intermediates reports.

Balance sheets entering the wave

FY26 was a deleveraging year: IOC cut borrowings by ₹23,798 crore, BPCL's net debt fell to about ₹10,480 crore, HPCL's standalone debt-to-equity fell from 1.38× to 0.80×. Q1 FY27's losses reversed part of that; no June or September 2026 net debt figures were available for the OMCs at the time of writing, which is a gap to fill from the Q2 releases. BPCL's Project Aspire, about ₹1.5 lakh crore over five years, is roughly equal to its market value; IOC's FY27 capex budget is about ₹32,700 crore including about ₹5,000 crore for renewables. If the 2026 pattern of policy losses repeats during the build, the OMCs will finance their capex with debt rather than profits.

Restructuring and disinvestment

Three structural options keep recurring. BPCL privatisation was launched in 2019–20 for the government's full 52.98% and called off in May–June 2022; no 2026 revival has been reported. The HPCL–MRPL merger has been discussed since 2018 and is still unannounced; it would fold MRPL's refining into HPCL's marketing and lift HPCL's refining ratio further. PSU-to-PSU sales, on the 2018 ONGC–HPCL template, remain the state's preferred route. Meanwhile government dividend guidelines (payout of at least 30% of profit or 4% of net worth) keep OMC payouts at 26–37%.

In plain terms

The OMCs entered 2026 with the strongest balance sheets in years and are spending heavily on refineries and chemical plants that will make them more like Reliance. The war quarter took away some of that cushion. If the government keeps passing crude shocks onto them while they build, the projects will be paid for with borrowing, and dividends will come under pressure.

Yield as the anchor

For many holders the OMCs are yield stocks. HPCL yields 6.95%, IOC 6.32% and BPCL 5.95% on trailing dividends (7.6% on its full FY26 dividend). Government guidelines expect PSUs to pay at least 30% of profit or 4% of net worth, and the government is the largest recipient, so dividends are relatively sticky in good years. But they are paid from profit, and a year with two or three loss quarters would cut them. The yield is a reward for carrying the policy risk, not a guarantee.

Why the standalone refiners and Reliance trade higher

MRPL's 15.8 times FY26 earnings reflects a low base year (FY26 profit was still recovering from FY25's ₹56 crore) and a free float of about 11% that limits supply of shares. CPCL's 6.9 times looks modest for a 35% ROCE business with no debt, and its stock has risen 16% since its primer while every other member fell. Both trade at about twice book because their profits do not depend on pump prices. Reliance is valued on Jio, Retail and new energy; its multiple says nothing about how the market values refining.

In plain terms

The OMCs look extraordinarily cheap on last year's profit, about four to five times. On an average year that includes a bad one, they are more like five and a half to nine times, still cheap but not absurd. Investors are paid a 6–7% dividend for accepting that the government decides how much they earn in a crisis. The small refiners cost more because their profit depends on the market rather than on the ministry.

What would re-rate the OMCs is evidence that the state's crisis behaviour is changing: faster pump-price pass-through, a rule-based LPG compensation formula, or an end to windfall export duties on new capacity. What would de-rate them further is a second loss quarter in Q3 FY27 with Brent near $110, a reversal of the excise cut without matching price increases, or LPG compensation that falls well short of the ₹62,000 crore buffer. Neither is in the price yet.

Margins and returns in context

Indian PSU refiners earned $3–6 a barrel above the Singapore benchmark in FY26, thanks to complexity, Russian barrels and distillate-heavy yields. US Gulf Coast refiners typically earn more ($10–20 a barrel in good years) because they run on cheap domestic crude and cheap natural gas. Through-cycle ROCE for Indian OMCs of about 10–15% is below US independents' 12–20%, and the gap is the pricing state: Indian marketing losses in shock years pull the average down. Indian OMCs are therefore cheaper than US refiners on earnings, but in line with Asian state refiners such as Sinopec on book value, which is the fairer comparison given similar state ownership and policy roles.

The competitive backdrop for India's exports

India's surplus product competes in Asian, African and European markets. New capacity elsewhere raises that competition: Nigeria's 650,000 barrel-a-day Dangote refinery reduces West Africa's need for imported fuel (from memory); Middle Eastern export refineries compete for the same diesel and jet buyers; and changes in China's product-export quotas swing Asian cracks quickly. The EU's January 2026 ban on products refined from Russian crude also narrows which Indian cargoes can go to Europe. A capacity wave aimed partly at exports therefore lands in a crowded market.

In plain terms

India's best refineries are as good as any in the world, and its state oil companies are priced like Asian state companies, which also answer to their governments. They look much cheaper than American refiners because American refiners are not told when they may raise prices. The cheapness is the price of that difference, not a sign that Indian refining is poor.

Transition and disruption: ethanol now, electrons later

E20 has already taken about a fifth of the petrol pool. Electric two-wheelers are next. Diesel is the most resilient product, and petrochemicals the main escape route, with their own glut.

The energy transition reaches Indian refiners through four channels: ethanol blending, which is already large; electric vehicles, which are small but growing; gas (CNG and LNG trucks), which takes some diesel; and decarbonisation of the refineries themselves. None of these threatens FY27 earnings. Together they decide whether the 42 MMTPA now being built will run for its design life on domestic demand or depend increasingly on exports.

Ethanol: E20 is done, E27 and E30 are not policy

India reached 20% ethanol blending in petrol in 2025-26, from under 1.5% in 2013-14, with ethanol capacity of about 2,000 crore litres. The BIS has notified fuel standards for E22, E25, E27 and E30, but the petroleum ministry has not committed to blending beyond E20; E27 and E30 are standards and discussion, not mandates. Reported plans point to E21 by 2027 and E25 by 2029, with 500 flex-fuel (E85) pumps by December 2026 and about 5,000 by December 2027. At E20, about 10.6 billion litres of petrol-pool demand is met by ethanol (INDICATIVE), equivalent to one to two years of petrol growth diverted from refiners. For the OMCs blending is mostly neutral (they buy ethanol at an administered price and sell it in the blend), but for refining it removes gasoline demand permanently.

Electric vehicles and gas

Electric two-wheelers took roughly 6–7% of sales in FY26 and electric cars about 3–5%; there are more than 7,500 CNG stations (all INDICATIVE, from industry data not re-checked). Two-wheelers are the largest users of petrol by number, so electric scooters affect petrol first. Diesel is more resilient: heavy trucks, farm equipment and generators have few economic alternatives yet, though LNG trucks and dedicated rail freight corridors will take share over a decade. The IEA and most forecasters put the peak of Indian transport-fuel demand after 2035. The OMCs are positioning at the pump: IOC has more than 10,000 outlets with EV charging, HPCL more than 5,500 charging points and 2,250-plus CNG stations, and BPCL targets about 7,000 "Energy Stations". Returns on forecourt charging have been low so far.

Decarbonising the refinery

Refineries use large amounts of hydrogen, today made from natural gas. Green hydrogen projects are under way: IOC's 10 ktpa unit at Panipat (December 2027), BPCL's about 5 ktpa at Bina, and Reliance's electrolyser manufacturing at Jamnagar. BPCL and HPCL target net zero for Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 2040 and IOC by 2046; BPCL aims for 10 GW of renewable capacity by 2035, and IOC has earmarked about ₹5,000 crore of FY27 capex for renewables. Compressed biogas under the SATAT scheme is a small common programme. These projects add capex with uncertain returns; the power side is covered in our power generation report. BRSR Core carbon-intensity disclosure will make refinery emissions more visible to investors.

Supply-side and product disruption

The bigger near-term disruption is on the supply side. New export refineries in Africa and the Middle East, and swings in China's export quotas, compete with India's surplus diesel and jet fuel. Petrochemical oversupply from China compresses the very margins that the PSUs' integration projects are counting on (HRRL, Bina, Paradip, MRPL's PACE). Lubricants, a high-margin niche for all three OMCs, face a long-run threat from electric vehicles, which need far less engine oil.

In plain terms

The transition is already eating into petrol through ethanol and is starting to through electric scooters. It will take much longer to reach diesel. The refiners' answer is to make more chemicals and to build charging and gas points at their pumps, but China's chemical glut means the first answer is not as safe as it looks, and charging has not made money yet. None of this changes the next few quarters; it changes how long the new refineries will have a home market.

Bull vs bear: a policy bet with a refining kicker

The bull case rests on the state restoring pricing as it has after every past shock. The bear case rests on the state keeping the upside whenever it matters.

The two sides of the argument agree on the facts. They disagree on how the government will behave over the next several years, and on whether the capacity being built will earn its cost. The case below is for the sub-sector; the archetypes differ, and the bull case is strongest where pricing risk is largest.

The bull case

  • History repeats in the investor's favour: after FY23's loss came FY24's record; after Q1 FY27's loss, Q2 should recover on ₹7.4–7.5 a litre of May price increases and a cooler July–August crude price.
  • Valuation already prices the worst: the OMCs trade at 4–5× FY26 earnings, about 1× book and 3.1–3.7× EV/EBITDA, with 6–7% yields; even on average earnings including a loss year they are 5.5–9×.
  • Refining is in a strong cycle: record diesel cracks (above $87 in September), Gulf exports disrupted, and every PSU refinery earning $3–6 above Singapore.
  • The LPG buffer is a recoverable asset: ₹62,000 Cr of industry losses is a claim the government has honoured before (₹22,000 Cr in 2022, ₹30,000 Cr in 2025).
  • Integration is rising: IOC's +17.3 MMTPA and HPCL's HRRL raise refining ratios toward 0.85 and 0.68, cutting exposure to the next freeze.
  • Standalone refiners are clean margin plays: no pump or LPG exposure, high operating leverage, and CPCL's 35% ROCE on a debt-free balance sheet.

The bear case

  • The shock is back: Brent at $109.86 on 16 October and Hormuz at two-thirds of normal flows make Q3 FY27 a second loss quarter if pump prices stay put.
  • The state keeps the upside: transfer-price discounts and export duties took the refining windfall in 2026, as the windfall tax did in 2022–24; the integrated hedge cannot be relied on.
  • LPG losses outrun compensation: ₹62,000 Cr by August against a fully allocated ₹30,000 Cr package; HPCL's buffer is 25% of its net worth.
  • Capex at poor returns: overruns of 6–84%, ₹3,600–8,800 Cr per MMTPA, and mid-single-digit returns on replacement cost at mid-cycle margins, into a market growing 1–3% a year.
  • Export economics are capped: new capacity depends on exports that the state taxes when cracks spike; China's chemical glut hits the petrochemical hedge.
  • Excise reversal risk: undoing the ₹10 a litre cut without a matching price rise would take the same amount straight back out of OMC margins.

Weighing the two, the bear case is stronger for the next quarter and the bull case for the next two years. The question for an OMC investor is not whether Q3 FY27 will be difficult, which looks likely if Brent stays near $110, but whether the government will again restore margins afterwards, as it did in FY24 and FY26. The record says it will, slowly. For the standalone refiners the case is simpler: they are a bet on Asian cracks, currently very strong, and on transfer-price terms set by their parents. For Reliance the refining case barely matters.
