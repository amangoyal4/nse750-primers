00

The one-page view: a record year that split in two

India sells more two-wheelers than any country on earth, and in FY26 it finally beat its 2019 peak. Then a fuel shock pulled the market apart.

India is the world's largest two-wheeler market by units. In FY26 makers dispatched 2.17 crore motorcycles, scooters and mopeds to Indian dealers, up 10.7%, and for the first time beat the FY19 peak of about 2.12 crore. They also exported 51.8 lakh two-wheelers (+23.4%) and sold 8.36 lakh three-wheelers at home (+12.8%). The first half of FY27 ran hotter still: 1.21 crore domestic two-wheelers, up 17.6%, and a record 16.5 lakh exports in the September quarter.

Those totals hide a market that has split in two. In September 2026 domestic scooter dispatches rose 34.1% while motorcycles rose 2.8%. Electric two-wheelers reached 11.4% of all registrations, up from 6.5% across FY26. Bajaj Auto's domestic two-wheeler sales fell 12% in the same month that its exports rose 34%. Two policy shocks did this. The September 2025 GST cut made every bike and scooter up to 350cc about 7.8% cheaper and set off the boom. The 2026 West Asia war then pushed petrol prices up for the first time in four years, and buyers who had been about to buy a commuter motorcycle started buying electric scooters instead.

Six companies in the NSE 750 make two- and three-wheelers, and they fall into two groups. Four are large, profitable incumbents that run on internal-combustion engines: Hero MotoCorp (the commuter-motorcycle giant), Bajaj Auto (exporter, autorickshaw monopolist and now owner of KTM), TVS Motor (the broadest range and the largest electric-scooter seller) and Eicher Motors (Royal Enfield). Together they booked ₹1,89,794 crore of consolidated revenue and ₹25,051 crore of profit in FY26, and are worth ₹7.89 lakh crore. Two are electric pure-plays that still lose money: Ather Energy and Ola Electric, with ₹5,925 crore of revenue, a combined net loss of ₹2,350 crore and a value of ₹0.77 lakh crore. The second-largest maker in the country, Honda Motorcycle & Scooter India, is a wholly owned Japanese subsidiary and is not listed.

Five forces define the sub-sector

  • Affordability resets decide the domestic cycle. The two-wheeler buyer is price-sensitive and mostly borrows to buy. A tax cut (GST 2.0) created the FY26 boom; a fuel-price rise reshaped FY27. Industry growth will slow as the GST effect laps from October 2026, so the investable question is who gains share in a slower, mix-shifting market (Part 04).
  • The electric transition turned from a disruption story into an incumbent story. TVS, Bajaj and Hero's Vida hold about 60% of electric two-wheeler registrations in H1 FY27; Ola, the 2023 leader with about 35%, is down to 7.7% (Part 09).
  • Premiumisation and the 350cc tax cliff shape profit per vehicle. Royal Enfield earns about ₹47,800 of EBITDA on each motorcycle; Hero earns about ₹10,300. GST is 18% up to 350cc and 40% above it, which pushes every maker to engineer bikes just under the line (Part 10).
  • Exports are the second engine. Bajaj sold more vehicles abroad than at home in Q1 FY27 for the first time; Nigeria tripled. Emerging-market currency crises can also shut a region for a year (Part 11).
  • Valuation dispersion is extreme. TVS trades at 56× earnings, Eicher at 35×, Bajaj at 24× and Hero at 19× (about 15–16× if its Ather stake is stripped out). Ather is valued at about 14× sales while Ola raises a rights issue at ₹27 a share (Part 17).

In plain terms

Indian two-wheelers are a mature, cash-rich industry that has just had its best year ever, thanks to a tax cut. The surprise of 2026 is not that people are buying electric scooters, but who is selling them: the old petrol-bike makers, using the dealer networks and service workshops they already had. The start-ups that were supposed to replace them are either barely breaking even (Ather) or raising emergency money (Ola). Meanwhile the profit pool belongs to the companies that sell expensive motorcycles or sell abroad, not to the ones that sell the most units.

01

How a two-wheeler maker earns: brand, engine and a dealer at the end of a long chain

About 70 paise of every rupee goes to bought-in materials and parts. The maker's margin comes from what it adds on top: an engine, a brand, scale and a network.

A two-wheeler maker is an assembler with three things the supplier cannot copy: its own engine or powertrain, a brand that lets it charge more than the bill of materials, and a dealer network that reaches villages a start-up cannot. All six listed companies design and build their own powertrains. Everything else on the vehicle (frames, castings, suspension, brakes, lights, wiring, tyres, batteries) is mostly bought from component makers, many of them listed and covered in our Auto Components report.

The money flows down a chain of six links. Steel, aluminium, rubber, plastics, copper and precious metals become parts at tier-1 suppliers. The maker (the OEM, original equipment manufacturer) assembles the vehicle and sells it to a dealer at an ex-factory price. The dealer adds a margin of roughly 4–6% of the ex-showroom price and earns more on service, spares, accessories, insurance and finance commissions. A financier lends to most buyers: industry estimates put the share of two-wheelers bought on credit at 55–65% (indicative). Finally the rider pays GST on top. Exports run in parallel, shipped as complete vehicles or as kits for local assembly in Africa, Latin America and Asia.

The maker keeps the most value per rupee, because it owns the engine, the brand and the dealer relationship. In electric vehicles value moves upstream to the battery cell, which India imports, mostly from China.

Four points about this chain explain most of what follows in the report.

First, the segment decides the margin, not the size of the company. Royal Enfield earns about ₹47,000–48,000 of EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) on each motorcycle and Hero about ₹10,000, yet Hero sells five times as many vehicles. The gap comes from the price point (a Royal Enfield sells for about ₹2 lakh, a Splendor for about ₹75,000) and from brand pricing power, not from cheaper production.

Second, the incumbents run on other people's money. Dealers pay for vehicles quickly; suppliers are paid slowly. TVS, for example, holds about 18 days of inventory and 13 days of receivables but takes about 64 days to pay suppliers, so its working capital is negative: suppliers finance the business. That, plus low capital needs, is why Hero, Bajaj and Eicher sit on ₹17,000–21,000 crore of investments each.

Third, spares, service and finance are hidden profit pools. Hero's parts, accessories and merchandise business brought in ₹1,689 crore in Q1 FY27, up 30%. TVS Credit made ₹913 crore of profit in FY26. Ather earns about 13% of its income from software, charging and accessories, and 93% of its buyers take its paid software pack.

Fourth, in electric vehicles the cell is the new engine. The battery pack is 30–40% of an electric scooter's cost. India imports nearly all its cells. Ather, Ola, TVS and Bajaj all design their own packs, battery-management systems and motors to keep control of cost and margin; Ola has gone furthest, building its own cell factory.

In plain terms

A two-wheeler company buys about ₹70 of parts and materials for every ₹100 it sells. What it adds is the engine, the badge and the network. A Royal Enfield badge lets the maker keep a quarter of the selling price as operating profit; a Splendor badge keeps about a seventh, but on a far larger number of bikes. An electric start-up has to build all three things from scratch, which is why it loses money until it reaches a very large scale.

02

Size, growth and a lost decade: seven years to get back to 2019

Domestic volume went nowhere between FY19 and FY25. Prices did the growing. FY26 broke the spell.

Measured by units, the Indian two-wheeler market had a lost decade. Dispatches peaked at about 2.12 crore in FY19, fell to about 1.35 crore by FY22, and did not regain the peak until FY26, when they reached 2.17 crore. The compound growth rate from FY19 to FY26 is about 0.3% a year. Measured from the COVID trough of FY21, growth is a respectable 7.5% a year, but much of that was recovery rather than new demand.

Three regulatory price shocks and a pandemic explain the slump. Mandatory five-year third-party insurance at the point of sale (September 2018) added roughly ₹4,000–5,000 to the up-front cost of a bike. Anti-lock or combined braking (April 2019) added ₹2,000–5,000. The leap to BS-VI emission norms in April 2020 required fuel injection and catalytic converters and added ₹6,000–10,000. Together with later on-board diagnostics rules, these took the price of an entry motorcycle up roughly 40–50% between FY19 and FY25 (indicative), while rural incomes, the commuter bike's core market, grew far more slowly. COVID then hit in FY21. The buyer who could afford a ₹50,000 bike in 2018 could not afford a ₹75,000 bike in 2022.

In plain terms

For seven years Indian two-wheeler makers sold no more bikes than they did in 2019, because safety, emission and insurance rules made each bike much dearer while village incomes lagged. Revenue still grew because each bike cost more. FY26 was the first year the unit count beat 2019, and a tax cut, not a structural change, did most of the work. That matters for FY27: when the tax-cut effect laps, growth slows unless something else, such as fuel prices or exports, takes over.

03

Who buys, and with what money: commuters, families, riders and couriers

The buyer is price-sensitive and usually borrows. Rural income, fuel prices and credit decide how many buy; aspiration decides what they buy.

The Indian two-wheeler is a working vehicle first. Most are bought to get to a job, a field, a shop or a school, and most are bought on credit. That makes the buyer unusually sensitive to three things: the showroom price including tax, the monthly instalment, and the running cost. Every major turn in the cycle since 2018 has come from one of those three.

Rural income and the monsoon drive the commuter motorcycle, the single largest product. Hero sells about four of every five of its vehicles in the commuter segment, and its Splendor alone is about 60% of its domestic volume. A good monsoon, firm crop prices and rural wage growth lift Hero, TVS's XL100 moped and Bajaj's Platina; a poor one hits them first. Premium motorcycles and scooters are far less exposed.

Urbanisation and women riders drive scooters. A gearless scooter is easier to ride in traffic, carries more and suits the whole family. Scooters' share of domestic two-wheeler dispatches has risen from roughly 30% in FY22 (indicative) to 41% in Q2 FY27. Honda and TVS lead; Hero has only about 10% of 125cc scooters, which is why the scooter boom has helped it less than its overall size would suggest.

Premiumisation moves buyers up the ladder: from 100cc to 125cc, from commuters to 150cc-plus sports bikes, and from there to Royal Enfield, KTM or Triumph. TVS's average realisation per vehicle rose to about ₹82,000 by Q4 FY26; Bajaj's sports motorcycles (Pulsar, Avenger, Dominar) grew about 50% at retail in Q1 FY27.

The gig and delivery economy is a new buyer. Food-delivery and quick-commerce riders cover 80–120 km a day, which makes the running-cost advantage of an electric scooter decisive for them; fleets and battery-swapping operators buy on total cost of ownership, not sticker price.

Credit is the transmission belt. Industry estimates suggest 55–65% of two-wheelers are financed (indicative), through the makers' captive lenders (TVS Credit, Bajaj Auto Credit), Bajaj Finance, HDB, L&T Finance, Shriram and banks. Entry buyers are the most credit-dependent and the first to be cut off when lenders tighten, as they did after the RBI's 2024 curbs on unsecured lending. The RBI's rate cuts in FY26 helped; its decision on 7 October 2026 to raise the repo rate to 5.50% will feed through to two-wheeler loan rates over the coming quarters. 04

GST cut, fuel shock: anatomy of a two-speed market Deep dive

A tax cut lifted every boat in FY26. A war in West Asia then sorted the boats into scooters that sailed and motorcycles that stalled.

Between September 2025 and September 2026 the Indian two-wheeler market was hit by two large, opposite-signed price shocks. The first, a GST cut, lowered the cost of buying a vehicle. The second, a fuel-price rise, raised the cost of running a petrol one. The first lifted demand for everything up to 350cc; the second redirected demand from petrol commuter motorcycles to scooters, and above all to electric scooters. Understanding how the two interacted is the key to reading FY27 results and to judging which companies have a durable advantage and which had a good year.

Shock one: GST 2.0 and the 7.8% price cut

On 22 September 2025 the government collapsed most of the GST schedule into two main slabs. For two-wheelers the change had three parts. Every motorcycle and scooter with an engine of 350cc or less moved from 28% to 18%. Two-wheelers above 350cc, which had paid 28% plus a 3% compensation cess (31% in all), moved to 40%. Electric vehicles stayed at 5%, and auto parts moved to a uniform 18%.

The price arithmetic is simple. If the pre-tax price is unchanged, the showroom price falls by the ratio 1.18 ÷ 1.28, which is 7.8%. A commuter bike that cost ₹80,000 on the road before the change (ex-showroom, Delhi, illustrative) cost about ₹73,750 after it, a saving of ₹6,250, or two to three months of instalments on a typical loan (illustrative). A Royal Enfield Classic 350, at 349cc just inside the line, fell by about ₹15,400. A 650cc twin went the other way, rising about 6.9% (1.40 ÷ 1.31). The makers passed the cut on in full, and the effect on demand was immediate: domestic dispatches rose about 21.5% in the second half of FY26 against a flat first half, and 26.4% in the March quarter.

Shock two: the 2026 oil war

The 2026 conflict involving Iran disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and pushed Brent crude from about $69 a barrel to more than $113. India's state-owned oil companies had held pump prices unchanged for four years, and the government's cut in petrol excise from ₹13 to ₹3 a litre on 26 March 2026 was absorbed by them rather than passed on. In May 2026 they raised prices four times, by about ₹7.38 a litre for petrol and ₹7.52 for diesel in all. (Some early reports cited only the first round of about ₹3; the cumulative figure is the right one to use.)

Two-wheeler buyers responded before the pump-price rise and kept responding after it. Electric two-wheeler registrations jumped 59% month-on-month in March 2026 to 1.78 lakh, as buyers anticipated what was coming. Bajaj said the conflict "slows motorcycle demand, pushes buyers towards EVs": its Chetak grew about 40% in the March quarter and more than 60% in April. An Ipsos survey found 95% of prospective buyers said fuel prices had accelerated their interest in electric vehicles. By September 2026, electric two-wheelers reached 2.03 lakh registrations, 11.4% of the market and up 85% on a year earlier.

The same war raised the makers' costs. Crude-linked plastics, synthetic and natural rubber, steel and aluminium all rose. TVS reported commodity inflation of about 4% in Q1 FY27; Hero said it expected high-single-digit cost inflation and its standalone margin fell 120 basis points quarter-on-quarter to 13.3%. Bajaj called input costs "hyper-inflating" but still widened its margin by 110 basis points, because a weaker rupee raised the value of its export dollars and its mix shifted to premium bikes and three-wheelers. Exporters to the Middle East and North Africa also faced shipping constraints.

Festive timing or structural shift?

Part of the motorcycle weakness is a calendar artefact. In 2025 Navratri, the start of the festive buying season, began on 22 September, the same day as the GST cut, so September 2025 was inflated. In 2026 Navratri begins around 11 October, so some September demand has moved into October. Motorcycles, which are bought disproportionately in rural India around festivals and harvests, are more exposed to this timing than scooters.

But the timing cannot explain everything, because scooters face the same calendar and grew 34%. A rough reconciliation suggests real substitution. Scooter dispatches in September 2026 were about 2.5 lakh higher than a year earlier; electric two-wheeler registrations were about 0.9 lakh higher. On that comparison electric vehicles alone account for roughly a third of the scooter increase (INDICATIVE: the two series come from different sources, SIAM dispatches and Vahan registrations, and SIAM's scooter line covers only its members). Commuters who in 2024 would have bought a 110cc motorcycle are buying a petrol scooter for convenience or an electric scooter for running cost, and the second group is growing fastest.

In plain terms

The tax cut made every small bike cheaper to buy, so everyone sold more. The oil shock made petrol bikes dearer to run, so buyers who were choosing between a motorcycle and a scooter increasingly chose a scooter, often an electric one. Companies that sell scooters and electric scooters (TVS, Honda, Ather, Hero's Vida) are winning; companies that depend on the domestic commuter motorcycle and have no petrol scooter (Bajaj at home) are not.

What happens when the GST base laps

From October 2026 to March 2027 every month is compared with a GST-boosted month a year earlier. If FY27 lands in the INDICATIVE 2.35–2.45 crore range, the second half of FY27 will be roughly 1.14–1.24 crore units against about 1.14 crore in the second half of FY26: growth of roughly 0–9%, down from 17.6% in the first half. The festive season of October–November 2026 will show which way it goes. In a slower market, share shifts matter more than industry growth, and the share shift under way favours scooters, electric vehicles and premium motorcycles over commuter motorcycles.

The financing side is turning less helpful too. The RBI raised its policy rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, partly in response to the inflation the oil shock produced; two-wheeler loan rates will follow with a lag, and entry buyers are the most rate-sensitive. Against that, higher fuel prices have improved the running-cost case for electric scooters by about ₹1,500 a year for a typical commuter (illustrative, Part 03).

In plain terms

FY27's first half was strong because it was compared with a weak, pre-tax-cut period. The second half is compared with the post-tax-cut boom, so growth will slow sharply, perhaps to single digits. The winners in that slower market will be those taking share, not those riding the tide.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: a materials business with a thin, defended margin

Seventy rupees go to bought-in materials and parts. A one-point rise in commodity costs takes about 0.7 points off the margin before price increases catch up.

A two-wheeler maker's income statement is dominated by one line. Raw materials and bought-out components take about ₹70–72 of every ₹100 of revenue at the mass-market incumbents: TVS reported 71.0% in Q1 FY27, and Bajaj and Hero run at 69–71% (indicative). Employees take about ₹5, and other expenses (power, freight, advertising, warranty, royalties and expensed R&D) about ₹9–11. What remains is EBITDA: about ₹13–15 at Hero and TVS, about ₹21 at Bajaj and about ₹24–25 at Royal Enfield.

Capacity: the incumbents are building, the start-ups have too much

The incumbents are adding capacity where demand is moving. TVS is raising two-wheeler capacity from 6.8 to 8.3 million units and three-wheeler capacity from 0.25 to 0.42 million, to 10.3 million in all by Q4 FY27, at a capex of about ₹3,500 crore this year; its electric-scooter line goes from 40,000 to more than 50,000 a month. Royal Enfield approved a ₹1,225 crore first phase of a new plant at Tada, Andhra Pradesh, adding 4.5 lakh motorcycles a year. Hero is doubling its electric capacity by December 2026. Bajaj's Chetak is capacity-constrained, and Q1 FY27 availability was cut 10–15% by a ransomware attack and supply and materials problems.

The start-ups face the opposite problem. Ola's Futurefactory has a nameplate capacity of about a million scooters a year; its Q1 FY27 deliveries of 39,192 imply it is running at roughly 16% of that on an annualised basis (estimate), which is why depreciation of ₹684 crore in FY26 sat on revenue of ₹2,253 crore. Ather is moving the other way, adding a second plant (Factory 3.0, Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar) from Q3 FY27 to take total capacity towards about 1.42 million units, a bet that its volumes will keep compounding.

In plain terms

Seven of every ten rupees a two-wheeler maker earns go straight out to steel, aluminium, rubber, plastic and parts suppliers. The makers protect their margin by raising prices a little every quarter. That works when demand is strong. A start-up has a different problem: it has built a factory and a brand for far more scooters than it sells, so each scooter carries a heavy share of fixed cost until volume catches up.

Imports and China: negligible for vehicles, critical for EV components

Vehicle imports barely register: customs duty on complete motorcycles has historically run at 50–100% or more (indicative), and the India–US trade deal is reported to lower duties only on large bikes above 800cc, a niche. The China exposure sits in components. India imports nearly all its lithium-ion cells, mostly from China. About 90% of the world's rare-earth magnet supply is Chinese-controlled, and China's export licensing from 4 April 2025 halved Bajaj's Chetak output in July 2025 (10,824 units against 20,384 a month earlier). In plain terms

Six companies sell almost every two-wheeler in India, but they rarely compete head-on. Hero owns the cheap commuter bike, Royal Enfield owns the big classic motorcycle, Bajaj owns the autorickshaw, Honda and TVS own the scooter. When one of them steps into another's segment, as TVS and Bajaj did in electric scooters or Hero and Bajaj did against Royal Enfield, that is when market share really moves.

Why the economics differ so much

Price point times gross margin. Royal Enfield's gross margin is about 45% on an average realisation of about ₹1.9 lakh; Hero's is about 30% on about ₹72,000 (both ESTIMATES). The same percentage of overheads leaves Royal Enfield four to five times as many rupees per bike.

Fixed-cost absorption. Hero spreads its overheads across 65 lakh vehicles a year; Ather across 2.6 lakh. That alone explains much of the start-ups' losses. It is also why the incumbents' electric arms can approach breakeven at a smaller scale than a pure-play: their dealers, workshops, brand and R&D teams are already paid for by petrol bikes.

Export mix and the rupee. Bajaj earns about half its volume abroad in dollars; when the rupee weakens, its margin rises. Hero and the pure-plays have almost no such cushion.

Policy dependence. Ola's gross margin is seven points higher with production-linked incentives than without. Royal Enfield's 650s pay 40% GST; Hero's commuters pay 18%. A change in any of these schemes moves one archetype's profit and not another's.

08

Policy is a P&L line: tax slabs, subsidies and incentives move unit economics directly

Few Indian industries have their margins set as directly by government as this one. Four schemes matter most: GST, PM E-DRIVE, PLI-Auto and the safety and emission rules.

In most industries policy is background. In two-wheelers it is a line in the income statement. A GST slab decides the showroom price; a subsidy decides how much of an electric scooter's cost the buyer pays; a production-linked incentive decides whether an electric maker has a gross margin of 23% or 30%; a safety rule can take a quarter off the profit on an entry bike. Investors who track monthly volumes but not gazette notifications miss half the picture.

PM E-DRIVE: the subsidy that shrank and survived

India's electric two-wheeler subsidy has fallen from very generous to modest in five years. Under FAME-II from June 2021 a buyer received ₹15,000 per kWh of battery, capped at 40% of the price: roughly ₹45,000 on a 3 kWh scooter. In June 2023 the rate fell to ₹10,000 per kWh capped at 15% of the ex-factory price, about ₹22,500. The interim EMPS scheme of 2024 and the first year of PM E-DRIVE paid ₹5,000 per kWh capped at ₹10,000. Since 1 April 2025 the rate has been ₹2,500 per kWh, capped at ₹5,000 per vehicle, for scooters priced up to ₹1.5 lakh ex-factory.

PM E-DRIVE, notified in September 2024 with ₹10,900 crore (since raised to ₹11,900 crore), was due to end for two-wheelers on 31 March 2026, was extended to 31 July 2026, lapsed for about ten days, and was then extended to 31 March 2028 by a gazette notification of 10 August 2026. The eligible two-wheeler target was raised from 24.79 lakh to 45.79 lakh units, with about ₹2,767 crore allocated to two-wheelers. The scheme is fund-limited: at the current pace of about 2 lakh electric two-wheelers a month, the unit cap could be reached well before March 2028 (INDICATIVE; it depends on how many past sales have already claimed). For electric three-wheelers, the L5 (passenger autorickshaw) subsidy closed on 31 December 2025 after its 2.88 lakh target was reached; the e-rickshaw and e-cart subsidy runs to March 2028 at reduced rates.

The ₹25,938 crore production-linked incentive for the auto sector (PLI-Auto, 2021) pays 13–18% of the "determined sales value" of advanced-technology products, including electric vehicles that meet a 50% domestic value-addition test, for sales between FY24 and FY28. Ola's Q1 FY27 gross margin was 30.5% including PLI and 23.3% excluding it; a seven-point swing on a thin business. The separate ₹18,100 crore cell-manufacturing scheme (PLI-ACC) awarded Ola 20 GWh of its 50 GWh; it carries penalties for missed milestones, and Ola reversed a ₹55 crore penalty provision in Q1 FY27, a reminder that the incentive is conditional.

The rules that raise costs, and the one still pending

Emission and safety rules have added cost to petrol two-wheelers for eight years (Part 05). The one still pending, anti-lock brakes on every two-wheeler, would hit the cheapest bikes hardest, because ₹4,500–5,000 of extra cost is a larger share of a ₹65,000 bike than of a ₹2 lakh one. Its status is unresolved and it should not be described as in force. The more interesting regulatory story of 2025–26 is the reverse: GST 2.0 was the first big policy move in a decade that made petrol two-wheelers cheaper, and it explains much of the FY26 recovery.

Two smaller regulatory lessons come from the electric side. In February 2025 Ola claimed about 25,000 monthly sales while the government's Vahan registration database showed about 8,600; since then analysts have treated Vahan as the only reliable electric scoreboard. And the Central Consumer Protection Authority's action over more than 10,000 service complaints against Ola showed that service quality is not just a brand issue but a regulatory one.

In plain terms

The government decides a large slice of a two-wheeler maker's profit: what tax a bike pays, how much of an electric scooter it subsidises and how much it pays makers for building them locally. In 2025–26 it cut the tax on small petrol bikes, trimmed the electric subsidy to ₹5,000 but kept it alive to 2028, and kept paying production incentives that still make up a big part of electric margins. A change to any one of those can matter more to a quarter's results than a month of sales.

Act one: the start-ups take the market (FY21–FY24)

When FAME-II's subsidy was raised to ₹15,000 per kWh in June 2021, a good electric scooter suddenly cost about the same as a petrol one. Start-ups were best placed to take advantage. Ather, founded in 2013 and part-owned by Hero, built a premium, software-led scooter and its own fast-charging grid. Act two: the incumbents arrive (FY24–FY26)

Three things changed the game. The subsidy taper (Part 08) removed the start-ups' price advantage and made cost and scale decisive. The incumbents brought their dealer and service networks: a buyer who could get a Chetak serviced at the same Bajaj workshop that had serviced his Pulsar had a reason to trust it. And Ola's direct model broke under its own volume: without workshops, service queues lengthened, more than 10,000 complaints reached the consumer regulator, and the February 2025 gap between Ola's claimed sales (about 25,000) and its Vahan registrations (about 8,600) damaged trust with investors as well as customers. Ola's share fell from about 30% in FY25 to 18.7% in June 2025 and below 6% by January 2026.

Why the incumbents won

Distribution and service. An electric scooter needs fewer services than a petrol one, but when it needs one the buyer wants a workshop nearby. The incumbents had thousands; the start-ups had to build them. Ola's October 2026 decision to onboard its first 150 dealers concedes the point.

Cost of the organisation, not the vehicle. A pure-play must pay for R&D, a brand, a network and corporate overhead from electric sales alone. An incumbent's electric arm shares those with millions of petrol vehicles, so it can reach EBITDA breakeven at a fraction of the scale (Part 13).

Finance and trust. Captive lenders (TVS Credit, Bajaj Auto Credit) and long relationships with Bajaj Finance and banks let the incumbents offer loans on electric scooters that an unknown brand struggles to match. A familiar badge also reassures buyers about resale value, which is the largest unknown in owning an electric vehicle.

Supply-chain scale. When China's rare-earth licensing hit in 2025, every maker suffered; but the incumbents had purchasing teams and supplier relationships that could find alternatives faster, and their petrol volumes kept the business running while electric output fell.

In plain terms

Selling electric scooters turned out to be less about the scooter and more about everything around it: the showroom, the workshop, the loan and the brand. The old petrol-bike makers already had all of that, so when the subsidy shrank and the start-ups' price advantage vanished, buyers went to the names they knew. Three years ago the market's question was which start-up would win; today it is whether either listed start-up can reach a profitable scale before the incumbents squeeze it further.

The two pure-plays: a survivor and a rescue

Ather took the disciplined route: premium positioning, its own software and charging grid, but sold through dealers from the start. Its family scooter, Rizta, sold more than a lakh units within 15 months. In Q1 FY27 revenue rose 89% to ₹1,217 crore on deliveries of 83,173 (+80.5%), and company-defined EBITDA turned positive at +₹9 crore. That figure includes other income; excluding it, EBITDA was −₹33 crore (−2.7% of revenue), still a large improvement from −11% in FY26. The stock market values Ather at ₹59,206 crore, about 14 times trailing sales and 3.4 times Ola. Its weakness is share: it has held 15–16% while TVS and Bajaj grew twice as fast, and its answer is a cheaper platform (EL, 2–5 kWh, launched for the 2026 festive season) and a second factory.

Ola has repaired its product margin but not its market. Its Q1 FY27 gross margin was 30.5% (23.3% excluding incentives); deliveries doubled from the March quarter to 39,192 and its share recovered to 8.4%, before slipping to 6.6% in September. But EBITDA excluding other income was −₹165 crore, −36% of revenue, and free cash flow was −₹351 crore, the ninth negative quarter in a row. Its auditors flagged going-concern uncertainty in FY26. It raised ₹780 crore in a QIP in June 2026 and filed for a ₹999.74 crore rights issue at ₹27 a share (letter of offer 7 October 2026, record date 13 October), a 22% discount to the market price. Two days later it announced its first 150 dealers, ending the direct-only model that defined it. Promoter holding is 32.96%.

In plain terms

Ather is close to covering its costs and is growing fast, but is losing share to bigger rivals. Ola makes a reasonable margin on each scooter it sells but sells too few to cover its factory and overheads, so it is burning roughly ₹350 crore a quarter and asking shareholders for more money while it rebuilds its sales network.

Royal Enfield: the near-monopoly

A note on the accounts, because our Eicher primer gets it wrong: Eicher's truck and bus joint venture with Volvo, VECV (Eicher owns 54.4%), is equity-accounted. The 350cc cliff

Since September 2025 a motorcycle of up to 350cc pays 18% GST and one above it pays 40%. For two bikes with the same pre-tax price, the bigger one costs 18.6% more in the showroom (1.40 ÷ 1.18). That single line now shapes product plans across the industry. Royal Enfield's core 350s (Classic, Bullet, Hunter, Meteor, all 349cc) got 7.8% cheaper, which explains much of its 27% Q1 growth. KTM and Triumph, built by Bajaj, re-engineered sub-350cc versions specifically for the lower slab. The bikes just above the line, Royal Enfield's Himalayan and Guerrilla 450s and new 440, Hero–Harley's X440 and Bajaj's KTM 390 and Triumph 400, now pay 40%.

A big, retro, mid-sized motorcycle is the most profitable thing an Indian two-wheeler maker can sell, and Royal Enfield sells nine of every ten. The open questions for this theme are two. Can the challengers dent Royal Enfield's pricing power? In plain terms

Bajaj bought a famous but bankrupt Austrian motorcycle company at a low price and is now nursing it back to profit. Exports and three-wheelers: the Nigeria-to-Mexico engine Deep dive

For Bajaj, exports are now bigger than India by volume. For TVS they are a quarter of the business. And the autorickshaw is going electric faster than anything else on Indian roads.

India is the world's second-largest exporter of two-wheelers by units, after China. In FY26 it shipped 51.8 lakh (+23.4%) and 4.61 lakh three-wheelers (+50.1%); in the September 2026 quarter it shipped a record 16.5 lakh two-wheelers (+27.7%). The buyers are in Africa (Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya, Uganda), Latin America (Mexico, Colombia, Peru, Brazil), South and South-East Asia (Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, the Philippines, Indonesia) and the Middle East. They want cheap, tough 100–200cc motorcycles, often for use as taxis, and autorickshaws; Indian makers build exactly that at a cost few rivals outside China can match.

Bajaj: the exporter that became bigger abroad than at home

Bajaj has been India's leading exporter for two decades, and in Q1 FY27 it crossed a line: it sold 7.32 lakh vehicles abroad and 7.06 lakh at home, the first quarter in which exports were larger. Two-wheeler exports rose 52% to 6.36 lakh and three-wheeler exports 69% to 96,168. The company called Latin America a "benchmark" quarter, said Africa had more than doubled and Nigeria tripled. Over the first half of FY27 its two-wheeler exports rose 47% to 13.1 lakh while its domestic two-wheeler sales rose 6% to 11.9 lakh. In FY26 as a whole, exports were 44% of its volume (22.5 lakh of 51.2 lakh).

Why the rupee matters as much as the market

Exporters bill in dollars and pay most costs in rupees, so a weaker rupee lifts their margin directly. Bajaj said "favourable USD/INR realisation" was one reason its Q1 FY27 margin rose 110 basis points despite what it called hyper-inflating input costs. The other side of the coin is the importing country's currency: when the naira or the Egyptian pound collapses, Indian bikes become unaffordable there overnight, and a region can disappear from the order book for four to six quarters. Export earnings are therefore a separate cycle, driven by emerging-market currencies and commodity prices (oil for Nigeria, for example), running on a three-to-four-year rhythm that is largely independent of India's domestic cycle. For a diversified maker that is useful: in September 2026, Bajaj's exports grew 34% while its domestic sales fell 12%.

The United States is not a large two-wheeler market for Indian makers, so the 10% Section 301 duty on Indian goods in force since 24 July 2026 (which replaced the IEEPA tariffs struck down in February) matters mainly for Royal Enfield's larger bikes and for Norton's planned US launch, not for the commuter export trade.

TVS, Royal Enfield and Hero abroad

TVS exported 15.8 lakh vehicles in FY26 (+33%), about 27% of its volume and roughly 26% of consolidated revenue, and 4.55 lakh in Q1 FY27. It has an Africa-specific motorcycle (HLX), a plant in Indonesia that has produced a million units, and has recently entered Zambia and launched electric scooters in Nepal. Royal Enfield exported about 1.21 lakh motorcycles in FY26 (+20%), about 10% of volume, through 3,200-plus outlets in more than 80 countries, with kit-assembly in Thailand, Brazil (about 35,000 units in FY26, +71%, and a second plant), Argentina, Colombia, Bangladesh and Nepal. Hero reported 63% growth in its global business in Q1 FY27 across 52 countries, with plants in Bangladesh and Colombia, but does not disclose export volumes; our ~5% estimate of its export share is unverified.

In plain terms

Selling motorcycles to Nigeria, Mexico and the Philippines is a second business that rises and falls with those countries' currencies, not with India's monsoon. When it works, as now, it is very profitable, especially when the rupee is weak. When a currency collapses, it can vanish for a year. Bajaj depends on it most, and that dependence is why Bajaj looked strong in September 2026 even as its home sales fell.

It is winning the electric market, but each electric three-wheeler likely earns a lower margin than a CNG autorickshaw from an 88% monopoly (ESTIMATE; Bajaj does not disclose segment margins). If electric three-wheelers keep taking share, Bajaj's three-wheeler profits could grow more slowly than its three-wheeler volumes.

In plain terms

Bajaj owns India's autorickshaw market, which runs mostly on CNG. Electric autorickshaws are cheaper to run and are spreading fast. Bajaj is the second-biggest seller of those too, so it is not losing the market, but it may be swapping a very profitable monopoly product for a more competitive, less profitable one.

Seventy years, eight shocks: how the industry got its shape

Licence raj, Japanese partners, divorces, a premium boom, a regulatory down-cycle, an electric gold rush and two policy shocks in twelve months.

Today's industry structure is the residue of eight distinct eras. Each left a mark: on who owns which segment, on which companies have global partners, and on how investors think about risk.

The scooter era (1948–1990s). Under licence-raj capacity controls, Bajaj's Chetak scooter ("Hamara Bajaj") had waiting lists measured in years. Royal Enfield's Bullet was built in Madras for the Army. Demand was rationed and makers sold everything they could build.

The Japanese joint ventures (1984–2001). Hero Honda, TVS-Suzuki, Kinetic Honda and Bajaj's tie-up with Kawasaki moved the market to fuel-efficient 100cc four-stroke motorcycles. Hero Honda's "Fill it, shut it, forget it" made it the world's largest two-wheeler maker by 2001 and made the commuter motorcycle India's default vehicle.

The divorces (2001–2011). TVS and Suzuki split in 2001; Bajaj launched the Pulsar the same year and abandoned scooters for motorcycles (a decision that explains why it had no petrol scooter to sell into the 2026 scooter boom). Bajaj Auto was demerged from Bajaj Finserv and Bajaj Holdings in 2008. Hero bought out Honda in 2010–11, which freed it to export but cost it Honda's engine technology; Honda set up alone and became Hero's fiercest rival.

The premium and export build-out (2007–2018). Bajaj bought into KTM in 2007 and scaled exports to Africa and Latin America. Royal Enfield's Classic 350 took it from about 50,000 bikes a year to more than 8 lakh. TVS partnered BMW in 2013. Hero built its own R&D centre and invested in a Bengaluru start-up called Ather.

The regulatory down-cycle (FY19–FY22). Volumes peaked at 2.12 crore in FY19, then mandatory long-term insurance, ABS/CBS, the BS-VI leap and COVID drove them down to about 1.35 crore. Rural commuters were hit hardest; premium bikes and scooters held up better.

The electric gold rush (FY21–FY25). The 2021 subsidy increase made electric scooters cost-competitive; Ola rose to about 35% of the market on direct sales and aggressive pricing; subsidy cuts in 2023–25 and a 2023 episode in which several makers had to refund subsidies for breaching local-content rules tested the model. Ola listed at ₹76 in August 2024 and Ather at ₹321 in May 2025.

The export bust and recovery (FY23–FY26). Currency crises in Nigeria, Egypt and Bangladesh cut exports; recovery began in FY25 and accelerated through FY27.

The policy shocks (2025–2026). China's rare-earth licensing (April 2025) halved Chetak output; Bajaj took control of KTM; GST 2.0 (September 2025) triggered the boom; Ola's share collapsed; the 2026 oil war split the market and pushed electric penetration to 11.4%.

The cycle has a recognisable shape. Reading a two-wheeler maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen numbers explain almost everything about a two-wheeler maker's results. Here is what each one means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, and where the definitions trip people up.

Every two-wheeler maker's profit comes from one equation. EBITDA is that revenue less the variable cost of each vehicle (mostly materials) and less fixed costs (people, R&D, advertising, overheads). The KPIs below are all ways of measuring one part of that equation, and the skill is in knowing which part the company controls.

1. Volume growth and outperformance

What it is. The year-on-year change in units dispatched, compared with the industry. Formula: company units this period ÷ same period last year − 1; outperformance = company growth − industry growth. Definition traps, collected

  • Standalone is not consolidated, and it means something different at each company. Bajaj consolidated adds KTM and Bajaj Auto Credit; TVS consolidated adds TVS Credit and overseas units; Eicher consolidated is Royal Enfield plus its share of VECV's profit (VECV is equity-accounted, so not in revenue); Hero consolidated includes its equity-accounted Ather stake. Compare margins and per-vehicle figures on the auto basis.
  • Ather's EBITDA includes other income; exclude it (FY26 −₹408 crore, not −₹257 crore).
  • Ola's gross margin includes PLI; ex-PLI it is 23.3%, not 30.5%.
  • Three volume scoreboards: SIAM dispatches, Vahan registrations, company dispatches including exports. Electric shares are Vahan; petrol shares usually dispatches.
  • "Three-wheeler" is three markets: CNG/petrol autorickshaws, L5 electric autorickshaws and L3 e-rickshaws. "61% electric" is mostly the third.
  • The GST cliff is at 350cc, not at "premium": a 349cc Classic got cheaper; a 440cc Harley got dearer.
  • Realisation per vehicle is not a price: it includes spares, accessories, apparel, services, incentives and exports.
  • One-offs in profit: Hero's Q1 FY26 consolidated profit included a ₹722 crore gain on the dilution of its Ather stake; TVS's Q1 FY27 standalone profit included about ₹150 crore of fair-value gain (core about ₹1,019 crore).
  • Net cash depends on what you count: Hero's ₹19,286 crore of investments (including the Ather stake) against ₹779 crore of borrowings is about ₹18,500 crore of net cash, not the "net debt −₹268 crore" some screens show.
  • Calendar mismatch: Bajaj Mobility (KTM) reports January–December; never set its H1 2026 beside Bajaj's Q1 FY27.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in comparing two-wheeler makers come from mixing bases: a consolidated number for one company against a standalone number for another, a factory-dispatch market share against a registration share, or a margin that includes interest income against one that does not. Fix the basis first and most of the apparent puzzles disappear.

How to read a two-wheeler maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the monthly volume releases you already have (company dispatches on the 1st, SIAM around the 15th, Vahan daily). Units, domestic vs export split and EV units are known before results; the results tell you about price and cost.
  2. Pull standalone revenue and divide by units to get realisation per vehicle. Compare with the previous quarter and a year ago. A jump without a price increase means mix: find out which.
  3. Pull materials as a % of revenue (or gross margin). A rise of more than one point usually means commodities outran price increases; check what management says about the next quarter's pricing.
  4. Calculate EBITDA margin and EBITDA per vehicle on the standalone (auto) basis. The per-vehicle figure is the one to track over time.
  5. Separate the EV business: units, revenue share, any statement on contribution or EBITDA, and the PLI receivable. Red flag: EV volumes growing while the company stops disclosing EV profitability.
  6. Check exports: growth by region, comments on Nigeria, Egypt, Latin America, and the rupee effect on margin. Red flag: a single region driving most of the growth.
  7. Strip out other income and one-offs: treasury gains, fair-value gains, dilution gains on associates, PLI catch-up payments. Then recompute profit growth.
  8. Look at the consolidated subsidiaries: the captive lender's loan growth and credit costs (TVS Credit, Bajaj Auto Credit), KTM's EBIT, Hero's share of Ather's loss.
  9. For an EV pure-play, go straight to EBITDA excluding other income, free cash flow and cash balance. Compute months of runway. Red flags: an auditor's going-concern note, a gap between claimed sales and Vahan registrations, rising receivables from government incentives.
  10. Finish with guidance and inventory: management's industry growth view, dealer inventory days before the festive season, and capex. Red flag: dealer stock rising faster than retail.
In plain terms

You already know how many vehicles a maker sold before its results arrive. The results tell you three new things: what it got for each vehicle, what each vehicle cost, and how much of the profit was real operating profit rather than interest income or one-off gains. Those three answers are enough to judge almost any quarter.

Technology and disruption: batteries, magnets, software and fuel

The disruption the industry feared has arrived and been absorbed by the incumbents. The next technology battles are over cell cost, magnet supply and software.

Five technologies will shape the next five years: the cost of battery cells, the supply of rare-earth magnets, software in the vehicle, new ways of paying for the battery, and alternative fuels for the petrol engine. In each, the advantage currently lies with scale.

Electrification by segment

Scooters are electrifying first. Electric two-wheelers were 11.4% of all registrations in September 2026, and a much larger share of scooters. Motorcycles lag, because electric motorcycles still cost more for the range and torque buyers want, and rural charging is thin. The risk to Hero's and Bajaj's commuter motorcycles is therefore slower than the risk to petrol scooters, but the 2026 fuel shock accelerated it: commuters who would have bought a 110cc motorcycle are buying electric scooters instead (Part 04). Electric motorcycles (Ola Roadster, Revolt, Ultraviolette, Royal Enfield's Flying Flea, Ather's planned Zenith platform) are a nascent segment.

Battery chemistry and cost

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) is displacing nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) chemistry in two- and three-wheelers because it is cheaper, safer and longer-lived, even though it is heavier. Pack costs were about $115 per kWh in 2025, down from $132 in 2024, according to a paid-report summary; in rupees, an assembled two-wheeler pack is ESTIMATED at ₹9,500–11,000 per kWh. On a 3 kWh scooter each $10 per kWh fall saves about ₹2,900. Sodium-ion cells, which avoid lithium entirely, are a 2028-plus option. Cell localisation is starting: Ola's 4680-format LFP cells are BIS-certified and its gigafactory runs at about 2.5 GWh, targeting 20 GWh by H2 2027; Exide commissioned a 6 GWh cell plant on 23 September 2026. Execution risk is high and Chinese cell prices keep falling, so local cells must be competitive, not just available.

Rare-earth magnets

Most electric scooter motors use neodymium-iron-boron magnets, and about 90% of the world's supply is controlled from China. China's export licensing from April 2025 exposed the single-source risk: Bajaj's Chetak output halved in July 2025. Responses include motors that use ferrite or less rare earth (Sona Comstar, covered in our Auto Components report, is one developer) and the government's ₹7,280 crore REPM scheme for 6,000 tonnes a year of domestic magnet capacity. Late-2025 trade truces reportedly eased licensing, but the 2026 supply position has not been re-verified for this report and should be treated as an open risk.

Software and the connected scooter

Ather showed that software can be a product: 93% of its buyers pay for its Atherstack Pro package, and software, charging and accessories bring in about 13% of its income. Over-the-air updates let it improve range and features after sale. Its charging connector (LECCS) became the industry standard. The incumbents are catching up with connected apps (TVS SmartXonnect, Bajaj's Chetak app), and the gap is narrowing.

Paying for the battery differently

Battery-as-a-service separates the battery from the scooter: the buyer pays for the vehicle and rents the battery monthly. TVS's Orbiter is priced at ₹49,999 on this basis, close to a petrol scooter's sticker price, shifting battery risk to a financier. Battery swapping (Bounce, Sun Mobility) suits delivery fleets that cannot wait for a charge. Both lower the upfront price barrier, and both favour makers with strong financing partners.

Alternative fuels

Bajaj's Freedom 125, launched in 2024, is the world's first CNG motorcycle, a hedge between petrol and electric that halves running cost for buyers near CNG stations. E20 petrol is now standard, and flex-fuel motorcycles that can run on high-ethanol blends have been shown. TVS has flex-fuel platforms. None is likely to change the industry's structure, but each gives the incumbents another answer to fuel-price shocks that the electric start-ups do not need.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The bull case rests on a long runway, cash-rich incumbents and a won electric transition. The bear case rests on a lapping tax boost, fuel-driven mix shifts and rich multiples.

The bull case

  • Runway: India is still under-penetrated against South-East Asia, premiumisation is early, and FY26 was the first year above the FY19 peak, suggesting a new up-cycle rather than a top.
  • The incumbents won the EV transition: ~60% of electric registrations, contribution-positive or profitable electric arms at TVS and Bajaj, and a subsidy cut that removed the start-ups' advantage.
  • Balance sheets and payouts: ₹17,000–21,000 crore of net cash at each of Bajaj, Eicher and Hero; Bajaj returned ~100% of FY26 profit; Hero yields 3.5%.
  • Exports are a second engine: record Q2 FY27 exports (+27.7%), Bajaj's exports larger than domestic, and a weak rupee lifting margins.
  • Pricing power: Royal Enfield's ₹47,800 per bike and Bajaj's widening margin through commodity inflation show the franchises can pass on costs.
  • Pure-play operating leverage: Ather is within about 10,000 scooters a quarter of breakeven excluding other income (estimate).

The bear case

  • The GST boost laps: from October 2026 every month faces a tax-cut-inflated base; H2 FY27 growth could be close to zero (indicative 0–9%).
  • Mix is moving against the commuter motorcycle: +2.8% in September vs scooters +34.1%; Bajaj's domestic two-wheelers −12%; Hero's margin below its band.
  • Costs and credit: commodity inflation of ~4% a quarter, an RBI hike to 5.50% in October 2026 and an unresolved ABS mandate that could cut entry-bike profit by about a quarter.
  • EM currency risk: roughly a quarter of Bajaj's exports go to Nigeria (estimate); a devaluation can remove a region for 4–6 quarters.
  • Valuation: 19–56× earnings against 8–15× globally; Ather at ~14× sales. Multiples can compress even if profits hold.
  • EV supply risks: imported cells, rare-earth magnets and a fund-limited subsidy that could run out before March 2028.

Weighing the two, the sub-sector's fundamentals are strong but FY27's second half is likely to look weaker than its first, and the share shifts within the market matter more than the market's growth. The bull case is strongest for companies exposed to scooters, electric scooters, exports and premium motorcycles; the bear case bites hardest on companies dependent on the domestic commuter motorcycle and on businesses whose valuation already assumes years of growth.

Three questions will decide how the sub-sector looks by the end of FY27. First, was September's motorcycle weakness festive timing or a lasting shift to scooters and electric scooters? October and November dispatch and retail data will answer it. Second, can the incumbents protect margins as commodity costs, electric losses and launch spending rise while volume growth slows? The Q2 and Q3 results will show it. Third, do the pure-plays close the gap: Ather to sustained breakeven, Ola to a stable share and a funded balance sheet? Ather's 26 October results and Ola's rights issue outcome are the first data points.

Beyond FY27, the indicators to track are the electric share of motorcycles (not just scooters), the price of cells per kWh, the supply of rare-earth magnets, and the fund balance of PM E-DRIVE. Each will decide how quickly the profit pool shifts from petrol to electric, and whether the incumbents' dominance of electric scooters extends to electric motorcycles.

In plain terms

You do not have to wait for quarterly results to know how this industry is doing. The first of every month tells you how many vehicles each maker shipped, and the government's registration database tells you daily how many electric scooters were sold. Watch whether motorcycles recover after Diwali and whether electric scooters stay above 11–12% of the market; those two numbers will set the tone for FY27's second half.
