00

The one-page view: a single cash machine, and six companies still trying to earn the word "product"

Seven companies, three different businesses, one giant. Outside Oracle Financial Services, Indian product companies earn services-like margins and now trade at services-like multiples.

India built the world's largest IT-services export industry and almost no listed software-product companies. The NSE 750 contains just seven: Oracle Financial Services Software (OFSS), Intellect Design Arena, Newgen Software, C.E. Info Systems (MapmyIndia), RateGain Travel Technologies, Aurionpro Solutions and Capillary Technologies. Together they earned ₹16,728 crore of revenue in FY26. The 18 listed IT-services companies covered in our sibling report, Indian IT Services, earned ₹8.65 lakh crore. Products are about 1.9% of services by revenue and 5.3% by market value.

The difference between the two models is simple to state. A services company sells hours: it hires an engineer, puts the engineer on a client project and bills for the time. Revenue arrives from the first month, but every extra rupee of revenue needs another person. A product company sells the right to use something it has already built: a banking system, a map, a loyalty engine. It spends years and hundreds of crores building before the first sale, but once the software works, the next customer costs far less to serve than the first. In theory that gives products higher margins, stickier customers and higher valuations. This report is largely about how far the Indian listed set has, and has not, turned that theory into numbers.

Five facts define the group, and each gets its own section later.

  • It is really one company plus six. OFSS is 46% of the group's revenue, 65% of its operating profit, 68% of its profit after tax and 70% of its market value. Every aggregate in this report is therefore shown twice: all seven, and the six without OFSS.
  • The "product premium" has nearly vanished outside OFSS. OFSS earns a 45% operating margin and a 45% return on capital employed (ROCE), and paid out 132% of its FY26 profit as dividends. The other six together earned a 20.7% operating margin, below the 22.1% of listed IT services. They trade at 29.3 times trailing earnings, almost exactly the 29.2 times of mid- and small-cap IT services.
  • Banking software is lumpy. One OFSS licence of ₹935 crore made up about 30% of its Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹3,125 crore. Intellect's quarterly profit went ₹102 crore, ₹27 crore, ₹120 crore in three straight quarters. A reader must learn to strip one-off licences out before judging growth.
  • The move from licences to subscriptions hides growth. Intellect's platform (cloud) revenue grew 141% in FY26 while its licence revenue grew 3%, and its profit has been flat for four years. Newgen's annuity share rose from 56% to 62% while headline growth slowed to 6%.
  • Growth is being bought abroad. RateGain paid about $250 million for Sojern, half with debt; Capillary bought SessionM from Mastercard for ₹16.9 crore net; Aurionpro has bought four businesses. Reported growth looks spectacular (RateGain +188% in Q1 FY27), organic growth is about 17%, and returns on capital are 3–16%.

In plain terms

If you buy "Indian software products" as a basket, you are mostly buying one subsidiary of Oracle that sells banking software, pays out more than it earns and grows about 10% a year. The other six are smaller, faster-growing, less profitable and much more varied: a banking platform, a document-workflow vendor, a map company, a travel-tech and advertising group, a fare-gate and data-centre contractor with a banking arm, and a loyalty-software company that earns most of its revenue in the United States.

The timing matters too. Global software stocks sold off sharply from late January 2026, when new AI-agent products convinced investors that seat-based and workflow software might be the first casualties of generative AI. Indian names followed. Since our stock primers were written two to four months ago, Newgen's market value is down about 60%, MapmyIndia's 52%, RateGain's 17% and Intellect's 15%, and foreign institutions have sold. Yet the group's combined revenue grew 18% in FY26 and 55% in Q1 FY27 (much of it from one OFSS deal and acquisitions). Valuations moved with global software sentiment, not with Indian earnings.

The question this report answers is not "are software products good businesses?" 01

How product money is made: build once, sell the right to use it, then charge every year to keep it running

A software product earns from four streams with very different margins. Which stream dominates explains most of the gap between a 45% margin and a 13% one.

Every company in this group owns intellectual property: code, a data set or both. What a customer actually buys is the right to use that IP, plus the help needed to make it work inside the customer's own systems. The value chain runs from engineers who build the product, through sales teams that spend one to two years winning a bank, to implementation teams that configure and migrate, and finally to the years of maintenance and subscription fees that follow. The money is made at the end of the chain, not the start.

Profit concentrates in Steps 2 and 4: a licence and its maintenance carry 80–95% gross margins. Step 3 is where margin leaks. A company whose revenue is mostly Step 3 looks, financially, like a services firm.

The four main revenue streams behave very differently. A licence is a one-time fee for the perpetual right to use the software; under Indian accounting rules (Ind AS 115) it is recognised in full when the customer gets the software, so a ₹50 crore licence lands in one quarter. Implementation is the labour to make the software work for that customer, billed over months as the project progresses. Annual maintenance (AMC, also called ATS) is a yearly fee, typically 18–22% of the original licence, for updates and support; it is recognised evenly through the year. A subscription or SaaS fee replaces the licence and the AMC with one recurring charge for hosted software, again recognised evenly. Usage fees (per booking, per vehicle, per API call), hardware and media resale complete the picture for some members.

Where the value is captured

Switching costs are the margin. Once a core banking system or a loyalty engine is live, replacing it is a multi-year project that a bank's board must approve and a regulator may scrutinise. This is why RateGain reports gross revenue retention above 90%, Capillary's net revenue retention runs at 110–113%, and OFSS's maintenance annuity grows every year as its install base widens. The customer does not leave easily, so the vendor can raise prices modestly and sell more modules over time.

Implementation is where margin leaks. Implementation and services are roughly 47% of Intellect's income (our residual estimate) but only about 13% of Newgen's Q1 FY27 revenue. OFSS reported a 64.6% margin on its products segment in Q1 FY27 against 20.2% on services. Vendors that leave implementation to system integrators, as Switzerland's Temenos largely does, keep a higher margin but lose some control of the customer relationship.

Data assets compound. MapmyIndia's roughly 30-year India map, RateGain and Sojern's travel-intent data and Capillary's 1.9 billion consumer profiles all improve with use and raise the cost of entry for a newcomer.

Distribution, not code, is the bottleneck. Oracle's global sales force is OFSS's real moat; no other Indian product company has anything like it. Newgen spent 26% of its Q1 FY27 revenue on sales and marketing and about 9% on research and development. Most of the group's acquisitions, from Brierley to Sojern to SessionM, are in effect purchases of American customer lists and sales teams.

In plain terms

A licence is like selling a house; maintenance is the annual service charge; a subscription is renting the house instead. Implementation is the interior-design work needed before anyone can live there. The house and the service charge are where the profit is. The interior work keeps a lot of people busy but earns builder-like margins, and a company that does a lot of it will look more like a contractor than a landlord.

02

An island beside services: ₹2 lakh crore of products next to a ₹13 lakh crore services industry

India's whole software-product industry is about a seventh the size of its services industry, and the listed slice is tiny even within that.

NASSCOM's 2026 strategic review puts India's technology industry at about $315 billion in FY26, of which IT services is about $149 billion and software products about $23 billion. At the FY26 average rate of ₹89.6 to the dollar, that is roughly ₹13.35 lakh crore of services against ₹2.06 lakh crore of products. Products are about 7% of the whole technology industry. The FY25 products figure was revised from $16.1 billion to $21 billion between NASSCOM's two reviews; most of that jump reflects re-estimation (including product work done inside global capability centres) rather than growth, so the underlying growth rate is closer to 9–10% than the headline revision implies.

That makes the rupee a silent driver of reported growth. The rupee averaged ₹89.6 per dollar in FY26 and about ₹95.5 so far in FY27, a fall of roughly 6%; it was about 13% weaker year-on-year in April–September 2026. For any company earning most of its revenue in dollars, euros or pounds, FY27 rupee growth will be flattered by roughly 5–10 percentage points against constant-currency growth (our estimate). Always ask whether a growth rate is in rupees or constant currency, and remember that converting a rupee figure at today's rate understates the FY26 dollar size by about 6%.

In plain terms

Most of these companies sell to foreign banks, hotels and retailers and report in rupees. When the rupee weakens, their reported revenue rises even if nothing else changes. A 15% rupee growth number in FY27 could be 8% real growth plus 7% currency. Products have one advantage over services here: much of their cost (Indian engineers) is in rupees, so a weak rupee widens margins too.

03

Why India has so few product companies, and why the best ones are not on this list

Services pay from day one, regulated buyers want references nobody has yet, and India's best product franchises were absorbed by services giants, sold to a foreign parent or listed abroad.

India's services firms have generated cash from their first month for four decades. A product company must spend for years before its first reference customer, and in banking, the largest software market these companies address, a bank will rarely buy a core system from a vendor that has never run one at a comparable bank. That chicken-and-egg problem has three consequences visible in the listed set.

  • The best franchises were absorbed. Infosys built Finacle and TCS built BaNCS inside their services businesses, where they cannot be valued separately. Citicorp's Indian software arm built FLEXCUBE, listed as i-flex Solutions in 2002, and was bought by Oracle in 2005–07; it is now OFSS, 72.39% owned by Oracle.
  • The founders who stayed independent listed late and small. Newgen (founded 1992) listed in January 2018; MapmyIndia (1995) and RateGain (2004) listed in December 2021; Capillary listed in November 2025. All four had revenue below ₹800 crore at listing.
  • The biggest new-generation names went elsewhere. Zoho stayed private; Freshworks listed on Nasdaq. India's SaaS ecosystem (estimates range from $12–13 billion of annual recurring revenue in 2022 by Bain to $7 billion by SaaSBoomi and McKinsey: definitions differ and the figures vary roughly two-fold) mostly sits outside Indian exchanges.

Imports and China, the questions that dominate manufacturing reports, barely apply. The "import" competition here is foreign software sold into India: Google Maps against MapmyIndia, Salesforce and SAP in Indian retail loyalty, and occasionally Temenos or Finastra at an Indian bank (most Indian banks run Finacle, BaNCS or FLEXCUBE). Data-localisation rules (the RBI's 2018 payments-data circular, the 2021 geospatial guidelines and the 2023 Digital Personal Data Protection Act, all recalled from public records) tilt some government and financial demand toward Indian vendors, which helps MapmyIndia most.

In plain terms

The listed product companies are survivors of a hard road, not the champions of the industry. The champions were bought (FLEXCUBE by Oracle), kept inside services firms (Finacle, BaNCS) or listed in New York (Freshworks). What remains on the Indian market is one foreign-owned franchise and six founder-led or VC-built challengers that mostly do not compete with one another.

04

Who buys, and why now: bank cores, compliance, claims, hotel rooms, loyalty points and maps

Demand comes from eight distinct sources. Three of them (bank modernisation, regulation and document AI) are slow and sticky; the rest swing with travel, advertising and government budgets.

Because the seven companies sell to such different customers, there is no single demand curve for "Indian software products". The useful exercise is to list what each buyer is actually trying to achieve, how much of that spending is discretionary, and what evidence the companies have given that demand is growing. Eight drivers matter.

1. Bank core modernisation and cloud migration

Most banks still run account and loan systems built decades ago, often on mainframes. Replacing them is risky, expensive and slow, which is precisely why it happens in long waves: a bank typically replaces its core once in 15–25 years and its digital and transaction-banking layers more often. The newer pitch is "composable" banking, where a bank replaces one module at a time (payments, lending, cash management) rather than the whole core. That suits Intellect's eMACH.ai, which it describes as roughly 700 microservices and 3,061 APIs, and Aurionpro's cash-management and lending products. Where ₹100 goes: people first, then selling, and a quiet charge called amortisation

Half or more of every rupee goes to people. What separates a 45% margin from a 15% one is how much of that labour is spent on building and maintaining product rather than implementing and selling it.

Software has no raw-material bill, so cost analysis is mostly about people. Engineers, implementation consultants, support staff and salespeople together absorb 50–65% of revenue across the group. Indian wage inflation of roughly 6–9% a year (recalled from industry surveys) is a constant headwind, and product companies now compete for engineers with global capability centres, which pay 20–40% more according to the sibling IT-services research. Newgen's employee cost was ₹190 crore on ₹357 crore of revenue in Q1 FY27, 53% of revenue. Onshore staff in the US and Europe cost several times more than Indian engineers, which is why the US-heavy businesses acquired by RateGain and Capillary raise those companies' cost bases.

The quiet charge: capitalised R&D and acquired intangibles

Two accounting choices push cost below the EBITDA line, where many readers do not look. First, Indian rules (Ind AS 38) let a company capitalise development spending once a product is technically feasible, turning an expense into an asset that is then amortised over several years. Intellect does this: it carried ₹298 crore of capital work in progress (mostly product development) at March 2026, and its depreciation and amortisation charge rose from ₹98 crore in FY22 to ₹209 crore in FY26. Second, acquisitions create intangible assets (customer lists, technology, brands) that must be amortised. RateGain's acquisition amortisation was ₹33.8 crore in Q1 FY27 against ₹6.8 crore a year earlier; Capillary's depreciation and amortisation is about 10% of revenue.

Hosting, hardware and media: the costs that dilute gross margin

Cloud hosting is the main extra cost of SaaS: roughly 10–20% of SaaS revenue (our estimate). OFSS runs on Oracle Cloud Infrastructure; the others use AWS, Azure or Google Cloud. Because hosting is billed in dollars, it partly hedges dollar revenue. Three members also carry pass-through costs that look nothing like software. MapmyIndia sells IoT and telematics devices (its inventory days were 119 in FY26). Aurionpro's fare gates and data-centre builds are hardware and construction. RateGain's MarTech business buys advertising media to resell, at gross margins well below SaaS. Each of these lowers the blended margin even when the underlying software is highly profitable.

Capacity and utilisation, the software way

A product company has no plant, but it does have an implementation bench, and that bench is its swing factor. When a project slips, the consultants are still paid. Newgen's Q1 FY27 shows the effect: implementation revenue fell 24% because of project delays in EMEA while employee cost rose 4.7%, so the EBITDA margin dropped to 15.7% from about 31.8% in the previous quarter. Intellect's operating margin was 13.7% in Q3 FY26 against 21.7% in Q4, by the company's account a matter of cost absorption rather than demand. In both cases the cost base is fixed in the short run and revenue is lumpy, so margins swing.

In plain terms

A product company's costs are mostly salaries, and salaries do not fall when a big deal slips a quarter. That is why margins at mid-size vendors jump around: Newgen went from about 32% to 16% in one quarter. The second lesson is that some costs hide below the EBITDA line. When a company capitalises its development spending or buys other companies, the bill arrives later as amortisation, which is why profit can stay flat while EBITDA rises.

Why the leverage has not shown up

Four reasons explain it, and each is visible in the company data.

  • Implementation-heavy revenue. Intellect's implementation and services residual is roughly ₹1,494 crore, about 2.9 times its licence revenue. That revenue carries services margins, so as the company wins more large deals, it adds more low-margin revenue alongside the high-margin licence.
  • Heavy and rising spending on selling. Breaking into banks in the US and Europe against Temenos, Pega or Salesforce takes sales teams. Newgen spent 26% of Q1 FY27 revenue on sales and marketing while growing 11%.
  • Acquisitions that bring lower-margin revenue. Sojern's advertising business carries lower gross margins than RateGain's SaaS lines. SessionM and Kognitiv came to Capillary as sub-scale or troubled businesses. Aurionpro's TIG adds hardware.
  • Mix toward devices and projects. MapmyIndia's growth in Q1 FY27 came from devices; Aurionpro's from data-centre projects. Both dilute margin.

None of these is permanent. A company that finishes its licence-to-SaaS move, lets partners do more implementation and digests its acquisitions should see margins rise. That is precisely the case management teams make: Capillary targets a 25–30% steady-state EBITDA margin, RateGain guides to 22.5–23.5% adjusted in FY27, Newgen aims for 23–25%. The point is that the market has stopped paying in advance for it.

OFSS: one company, 70% of the value, and a quarter that one licence rewrote Deep dive

A 72%-Oracle-owned banking-software franchise that earns 45% margins, pays out more than it earns, booked a ₹935 crore licence in one quarter and changed its chief executive the next day.

Oracle Financial Services Software is the sub-sector in all but name. Its FY26 operating profit of ₹3,477 crore was 1.85 times the combined operating profit of the other six companies (₹1,879 crore). It is the only listed Indian product company with what textbooks would call product economics: a 45% operating margin, a 45% return on capital employed, cash from operations equal to 100% of its profit after tax (76% of pre-tax EBITDA), receivables of just 64 days and almost no fixed assets or debt. Understanding OFSS means understanding three things: where the franchise comes from, how its parent takes the cash, and how to read a quarter in which one deal changes everything.

The franchise: FLEXCUBE and Oracle's sales force

OFSS began as Citicorp Information Technology Industries in Mumbai around 1989–90, became i-flex Solutions, built the FLEXCUBE core-banking system and listed in 2002. Oracle bought control in 2005–07 and renamed it in 2008. Today FLEXCUBE runs at more than 600 banks in over 140 countries, alongside a suite of digital-banking, lending, payments, treasury, analytics (OFSAA) and financial-crime products (Mantas/FCCM). Its products segment earned ₹6,942 crore in FY26 (up 12%) and its services segment ₹730 crore (up 16%). Licence to subscription: the transition that makes a good business look worse Deep dive

Selling the same software as a subscription instead of a licence cuts year-one revenue by more than half and lifts lifetime revenue. Intellect, Newgen and Capillary are at three different points on that curve.

The single most misunderstood feature of software-product accounts is what happens when a company moves from selling perpetual licences to selling subscriptions. The customer's need is identical; the software is identical; often the implementation work is identical. But the timing of revenue changes completely, and with it the growth rate, the margin and the cash profile. A company in the middle of this move will report weaker numbers than a company that has not started it, even if its business is healthier. Analysts who compare headline growth across companies at different points in the transition will reach the wrong answer.

The accounting mechanics

Under Ind AS 115, a licence that gives the customer a right to use the software as it exists is recognised at a point in time: when the customer gets the software. A ₹50 crore licence signed and delivered in June is ₹50 crore of June-quarter revenue. Maintenance (AMC) and software-as-a-service fees are recognised over time, evenly across the contract period. Implementation is usually recognised as the work is done. Contracts that bundle a licence with services require the company to allocate the price between them, which is where judgement (and audit attention) concentrates.

So if a vendor sells the same ₹50 crore of customer demand as a subscription of ₹25 crore a year, the first year shows perhaps half a year of subscription, not ₹50 crore. The difference is not lost; it is spread over the life of the contract and, typically, the lifetime value is higher.

A worked example: one bank, two routes, five years

Consider a mid-size bank buying a transaction-banking platform, with numbers calibrated to Intellect and OFSS norms. On the licence route, it pays a ₹50 crore licence at signing, ₹60 crore of implementation over 18 months (₹40 crore in year one, ₹20 crore in year two) and maintenance at 20% of the licence, ₹10 crore a year from year two, rising 4% a year. On the subscription route, implementation is the same, and the bank pays ₹25 crore a year from go-live in the middle of year two (₹12.5 crore in year two), also rising 4% a year. Gross margins are assumed at 92% for licence, 28% for implementation, 85% for maintenance and 70% for SaaS (after hosting).

Quality flags: slow collections, cash that does not arrive, and a core that has stopped growing

Newgen takes 164 days to collect a rupee of revenue, MapmyIndia 136. Aurionpro turned ₹282 crore of EBITDA into ₹56 crore of operating cash. MapmyIndia's map business grew 2%.

Product companies are supposed to convert profit into cash efficiently: customers pay up front for subscriptions or on signature for licences, and there are no factories to fund. Three companies in this group show the opposite pattern, and each has a specific cause. Spotting these flags early is the difference between owning a product company and owning a contractor with product branding.

Accounting is the regulation that moves the numbers

Ind AS 115 is why OFSS's Q1 FY27 jumped 69% and why Intellect's growth looked weaker than its business during its platform shift. The standard is sensible: a perpetual licence really is delivered at a point in time. But it means that for licence-heavy companies, quarterly revenue reflects the timing of contract signatures more than the health of the business. Ind AS 38's capitalisation rules similarly let companies choose, within limits, whether development spending hits EBITDA now or depreciation later. Two companies with identical economics can report EBITDA margins several points apart depending on how much they capitalise. When comparing, add back capitalised development (visible in investing cash flows) to get a like-for-like R&D figure.

The RBI as the gatekeeper of bank SaaS

Indian banks buying software as a service must meet the RBI's outsourcing and data-localisation rules: data must generally stay in India, the bank must retain audit and access rights, and material outsourcing needs board oversight. This raises the bar for pure multi-tenant SaaS in Indian banking, especially at public-sector banks, and is part of why India remains a licence-and-implementation market longer than the US or Europe. It favours vendors with India-hosted options and long relationships. Our Private Banks and PSU Banks reports cover these customers' technology spending and the RBI's supervisory stance.

Data, maps and sovereignty

India's data-protection law (the DPDP Act, 2023, with rules notified in November 2025 by our recall) is both a cost and an opportunity. Capillary holds personal data on loyalty members for hundreds of brands; RateGain's Sojern uses travel-intent data to target ads. Both must comply with consent and purpose-limitation rules in every market they serve. At the same time, compliance itself creates demand for consent-management and data-governance software. The 2021 geospatial guidelines opened mapping to Indian private companies and reserved some high-accuracy work for Indian-owned entities, which protects MapmyIndia's government business but also let new Indian competitors enter.

Visas, tariffs and the HIRE Act

The policies that dominate the IT-services debate (the US H-1B fee, US tariffs and the proposed HIRE Act's 25% excise on outsourcing payments) matter much less here. Product companies do most of their engineering offshore and hire sales staff locally, and US tariffs apply to goods, not services. The proposed HIRE Act, which has stalled, would hit cross-border services billing; whether software licences would be covered is unclear. The indirect effects (client budgets, the rupee) are the same as for services, and our IT Services report covers them in depth.

In plain terms

No regulator sets software prices. The rules that matter most are accounting rules, which decide when revenue and cost show up, and banking rules, which decide how Indian banks are allowed to buy cloud software. A reader who understands Ind AS 115 and the RBI's outsourcing direction understands most of the regulatory story.

Four decades, three waves: from i-flex to the "SaaSpocalypse"

India's product companies came in three waves, each shaped by a different constraint: the services boom, the long climb to listing, and the 2025–26 rush of acquisitions, IPOs and an AI-driven de-rating.

Wave one, 1989–2008: the banking-product pioneers. Citicorp's Indian software arm (CITIL) became i-flex Solutions and built FLEXCUBE; i-flex listed in 2002 as one of India's first pure banking-product companies. Infosys built Finacle and Polaris built banking products inside services businesses. Newgen (1992) and C.E. Info Systems, now MapmyIndia (1995), were founded as small domestic product firms. The defining event of the wave was Oracle's purchase of control of i-flex in 2005–07 and its renaming as OFSS in 2008: India's best product company became a subsidiary, and its cash has flowed to its parent ever since.

Wave two, 2014–2024: carve-outs, listings and profitable growth. Intellect Design Arena was demerged from Polaris in 2014 as a pure products company. Newgen listed in January 2018; MapmyIndia and RateGain in December 2021. COVID nearly broke RateGain, whose FY22 revenue was ₹367 crore at a 9% operating margin; it then rebuilt margins to 22% by FY25 and raised about ₹600 crore in a December 2023 QIP. Aurionpro raised about ₹568 crore and issued bonus shares. Intellect reinvested heavily and kept profit flat. In July 2024 MapmyIndia sent a legal notice to Ola Electric alleging breach of a map-licence agreement (Ola denied it; we found no ruling), and Google later cut its India Maps API prices.

Wave three, 2025–2026: acquisitions, a listing and the AI de-rating. RateGain completed the roughly $250 million Sojern purchase in November 2025. Capillary listed on 21 November 2025 at ₹577 and bought SessionM from Mastercard in May 2026. From late January 2026 a global software sell-off, which markets nicknamed the "SaaSpocalypse", hit seat-based and workflow software after AI-agent launches (the trigger most often cited is Anthropic's Claude Cowork plug-ins on 30 January 2026); Indian IT stocks fell 5–6% in a day on 4 February. OFSS booked its record licence and changed chief executive in July 2026. By October, 52-week ranges told the story: Newgen ₹1,042 to ₹401, MapmyIndia ₹1,894 to ₹795, Intellect ₹1,245 to ₹564, Aurionpro ₹1,247 to ₹593.

Reading a software-product company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, one equation and a ten-minute routine. Learn these and you can read any of the seven, and most global software companies, without being misled by a lumpy licence, an adjusted margin or a run-rate dressed up as ARR.

Why a separate toolkit? Because the usual industrial metrics do not work here. There is no capacity utilisation to track, no raw-material spread, no plant-level return. A software product costs almost nothing to deliver once built, so almost all of the economics sit in three places: how much of the revenue repeats without a new sale, how much of each rupee of revenue is high-margin product rather than labour-intensive services, and how quickly reported profit turns into cash. Every KPI below is a way of measuring one of those three things, and every definition trap is a way that one of them can be flattered.

Software-product companies publish fewer standard metrics than banks or hospitals, and they define the ones they do publish differently. That makes a disciplined toolkit more important, not less. This section starts with the equation that links a product company's revenue streams to its profit, works it through in rupees, and then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example, what moves it, what good looks like, how the seven compare, and the trap hidden in its definition.

The equation: five revenue streams, four margins, three cost lines

A product company's revenue in any year is the sum of five streams, each with its own driver. New licence revenue depends on how many deals sign and how big they are. Implementation revenue is typically 0.8 to 1.5 times the licence value for core banking and is recognised over 6 to 36 months. Maintenance (AMC) is roughly 18–22% a year of the cumulative licence base that is still live, multiplied by a renewal rate of about 95%. Subscription revenue is last year's ARR multiplied by net revenue retention, plus new ARR. Usage, transaction, media and hardware revenue depend on volumes and take-rates.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). Take a banking-software house with ₹1,000 crore of revenue: ₹150 crore of new licences, ₹450 crore of implementation, ₹200 crore of maintenance, ₹150 crore of SaaS subscriptions and ₹50 crore of third-party hardware and software. That is why the first question about any margin in this industry is "what was the mix?"

In plain terms

A product company's margin is mostly decided by what it sold, not how efficiently it worked. A quarter heavy in licences looks brilliant; a quarter heavy in implementation looks ordinary. Before praising or blaming management for a margin, find out the mix of licence, maintenance, subscription and services in that period.

KPI 1: Recurring revenue share

What it is. The share of revenue that repeats without a new sale: maintenance (AMC or ATS), subscriptions and SaaS, and support contracts. Formula: (AMC + subscription + support) ÷ total revenue. Worked example: in the ₹1,000 crore company above, recurring revenue is ₹200 crore of AMC plus ₹150 crore of SaaS, or 35%. What moves it: selling subscriptions instead of licences, growing the installed base (which grows AMC), and fewer large implementation projects. It falls when a big licence or project lands. Benchmarks: above 70% is good, 50–70% average, below 50% weak for a mature product company. How the seven compare: Capillary 94.5% (subscription, Q1 FY27); Newgen 62% (FY26) and about 71% (Q1 FY27); Intellect 36% on platform plus AMC. OFSS, MapmyIndia, Aurionpro and RateGain do not disclose a comparable figure. The global benchmark, Temenos, reports ARR above 90% of product revenue. The trap: companies label different bundles "recurring". Newgen's annuity includes maintenance on old licences, Intellect's "licence-linked" includes one-time licences, and a year with a big licence deal lowers the ratio even though nothing bad has happened.

KPI 2: Annual recurring revenue (ARR) and its growth

What it is. The annualised value of contracted recurring revenue at a point in time. Formula: sum of active subscription contracts' annual values (or monthly recurring revenue × 12). Worked example: a vendor with 400 customers paying an average of ₹2.5 crore a year has ARR of ₹1,000 crore; if 40 new customers sign at ₹2 crore, existing ones expand by net 10%, ARR next year is ₹1,000 × 1.10 + ₹80 = ₹1,180 crore, 18% growth. What moves it: new customers, expansion and price rises (up); churn and downgrades (down); acquisitions (up, but inorganic); currency (for rupee reporting). Benchmarks: above 25% growth good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak for a scaling SaaS company. How the seven compare: only Capillary reports contracted ARR: ₹1,026.6 crore at June 2026, up 43% from ₹719.6 crore, of which organic growth was about 17%. A call summary puts March 2026 ARR at about ₹765 crore (single source, unverified). The trap: RateGain's "annualised revenue run-rate" of ₹3,140 crore is Q1 FY27 revenue × 4. It includes usage and media revenue and is not contracted ARR. Always ask whether ARR is contracted, and how much of its growth was acquired.

KPI 3: Net and gross revenue retention (NRR, GRR)

What it is. NRR compares this year's revenue from last year's customers with what those same customers paid last year, including upsell, downgrades and churn. GRR is the same but ignores upsell, so it can never exceed 100%. Formula: NRR = (opening ARR + expansion − contraction − churn) ÷ opening ARR; GRR = (opening ARR − contraction − churn) ÷ opening ARR. Worked example: opening ARR of ₹500 crore, ₹90 crore of upsell, ₹15 crore of downgrades and ₹10 crore lost to churn gives NRR of 113% (₹565 crore ÷ ₹500 crore) and GRR of 95%. What moves it: product breadth (more modules to sell), usage-based pricing, price escalators, customer health and competition. Benchmarks: NRR of 115% or more good, 105–115% average, below 100% weak; GRR above 92% good, 85–92% average, below 85% weak. How the seven compare: Capillary's NRR was 113% (TTM, June 2026; organic 111%, acquired customers 120%) and 110% for FY26 (organic 114%, acquired 94%). RateGain says gross retention is above 90%. Nobody else discloses either. The trap: acquired cohorts distort the figure in both directions: Capillary's acquired customers retained at 94% in FY26 and 120% in the TTM to June. Ask for organic NRR. As the cohort example in section 09 shows, the gap between 113% and 92% NRR is worth 52% more five-year revenue per cohort.

KPI 4: Licence intensity and the implementation-to-licence ratio

What it is. How much of revenue comes from one-time licences, and how much services work each rupee of licence drags along. Formulas: licence share = licence ÷ revenue; implementation ratio = implementation and services revenue ÷ licence revenue. Worked example: Intellect's FY26 licence revenue was ₹517 crore and our residual estimate of implementation and other services is about ₹1,494 crore, a ratio of about 2.9. For every rupee of licence it earned roughly ₹2.9 of services revenue at services margins. What moves it: deal type (full core replacement versus a module), whether partners do implementation, and the move to SaaS (which lowers licence share). Benchmarks: an implementation ratio below 1.0 is good (partners implement), 1.0–1.5 average, above 2 services-heavy. How the seven compare: Intellect ~2.9 (estimate). Newgen's Q1 FY27 implementation of ₹46.4 crore against licence of ₹57 crore gives about 0.8 for that quarter. OFSS's services segment is about 10% of revenue, but implementation inside its products segment is not split out. The trap: Intellect's "licence-linked revenue" (₹1,667 crore, 53% of FY26 income) adds licence, platform and AMC together. It is a useful measure of product revenue, but because it includes one-time licences it is not a recurring measure, and it rose 34% partly because platform deals replaced licences.

KPI 5: Visibility: pipeline, order book, RPO and deal wins

What it is. Measures of revenue not yet recognised. A pipeline is opportunities being pursued (not signed). An order book is signed but unexecuted contract value. Remaining performance obligations (RPO) is the audited accounting version of the order book, required under Ind AS 115. Formula for coverage: pipeline ÷ next year's new-business target, or order book ÷ trailing revenue. Worked example: Aurionpro's order book of more than ₹1,950 crore against FY26 revenue of ₹1,411 crore is coverage of about 1.4 years. What moves it: wins (up), execution (down), cancellations and currency. Benchmarks: pipeline coverage above 3 times the new-business target is good, 2–3 times average, below 2 times weak; order-book coverage above 1.5 years is comfortable for project businesses. How the seven compare: Intellect: pipeline ₹13,000 crore (up 15%, 29 deals above ₹50 crore), 19 strategic wins in Q1 FY27 and 59 new customers in FY26. OFSS: RPO ₹7,345 crore (up 16%). Aurionpro: order book above ₹1,950 crore, 23 new logos in Q1 FY27. MapmyIndia: ₹1,754 crore open order book. Newgen: 10 new logos and 4 large deals in Q1 FY27. The trap: pipeline is not order book. MapmyIndia's own communications call ₹1,754 crore an "open order book" in the press release and a "pipeline" in the chairman's quote; the two have very different meanings and the conflict is unresolved.

KPI 6: AMC as a percentage of licence

What it is. The annual maintenance fee charged as a percentage of the original licence. Formula: annual AMC ÷ licence value. Worked example: a vendor that signs ₹100 crore of licences a year for five years at an AMC rate of 20%, with 95% annual renewal, builds an AMC stream of roughly ₹90 crore a year by year six (₹20 crore from each year's cohort, minus attrition), almost as large as a full year's new licence sales. What moves it: contractual rate (typically 18–22%), escalators (often 3–5% a year), renewal rates and the age of the installed base (very old versions may be dropped). Benchmarks: 20–22% good, 16–20% average, below 15% weak. How the seven compare: no company discloses its contractual rate; the 18–22% norm is an industry convention (INDICATIVE). Intellect's AMC revenue (₹570 crore) already exceeds its annual licence revenue (₹517 crore), which is what an accumulated base looks like. The trap: AMC is recurring but not necessarily growing. If new licence sales slow (or move to SaaS), AMC growth slows a few years later, and a "harvest" deal like OFSS's Q1 FY27 licence, if it involves the customer taking over support, could reduce it.

KPI 7: R&D and sales & marketing intensity

What it is. How much of revenue is reinvested in building the product and in selling it. Formulas: R&D % = (expensed R&D + capitalised development) ÷ revenue; S&M % = sales and marketing cost ÷ revenue. Worked example: Newgen's Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹357 crore carried roughly ₹32 crore of R&D (about 9%) and roughly ₹93 crore of sales and marketing (26%). What moves it: product cycles (a new platform raises R&D), entry into new geographies (raises S&M), and scale (both should fall as a percentage as revenue grows). Benchmarks: R&D of 12–20% sustained is good for a product company; 8–12% average; below 7% suggests under-investment and above 25% a cash burn. S&M of 15–25% is fine with growth above 20%; above 35% with growth below 15% is weak. How the seven compare: only Newgen publishes both (about 9% R&D, 26% S&M); its 26% selling cost alongside roughly 11% growth scores weak on our scale. Global benchmarks: Temenos and Pega have historically spent roughly 20% and 25% of revenue on R&D (recalled, INDICATIVE). The trap: expensed R&D understates true investment at companies that capitalise; Intellect's ₹298 crore of capitalised development in progress is R&D that never touched its EBITDA.

KPI 8: EBITDA margin, and which EBITDA

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation and amortisation as a share of revenue. Formula: (revenue − operating costs) ÷ revenue, excluding other income. Worked example: MapmyIndia's FY26 EBITDA is ₹169 crore on screener (35.7%) and about ₹176 crore on the company's basis (37%); the difference is classification, not performance. What moves it: mix (licence vs services vs hardware), utilisation of implementation staff, wage inflation, the rupee, capitalisation choices and acquisitions. Benchmarks: above 30% good for a mature product company and above 20% for scaling SaaS; 15–30% and 10–20% average; below 15% and 10% weak. How the seven compare (FY26, screener): OFSS 45.3%, MapmyIndia 35.7%, Newgen 25.8%, Aurionpro 20.0%, Intellect 19.2%, RateGain 18.9%, Capillary 12.7%. Adjusted Q1 FY27: RateGain 24.6%, Capillary 17.2%. The trap: four different "EBITDAs" appear in this group: screener operating profit (used in our peer table), company EBITDA including other income (Intellect's ₹703 crore is 22.2% of total income), adjusted EBITDA excluding acquisition-linked costs (RateGain, Capillary), and quarterly margins that swing with mix (Newgen 31.8% to 15.7% in one quarter).

KPI 9: Debtor days (DSO)

What it is. How many days of revenue are owed by customers at year-end. Formula: trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365 (unbilled revenue should be added where disclosed). Worked example: Newgen's 164 days on ₹1,574 crore of revenue implies receivables of about ₹707 crore (₹1,574 × 164 ÷ 365); at OFSS's 64 days, Newgen would hold about ₹276 crore and have roughly ₹430 crore more cash. What moves it: milestone billing on licences and projects (up), government and Gulf customers (up), subscription billing in advance (down), and revenue recognised before invoicing. Benchmarks: below 75 days good, 75–110 average, above 120 weak. How the seven compare: OFSS 64, Intellect 82, Capillary 90, RateGain 94, Aurionpro 98, MapmyIndia 136, Newgen 164 (FY26). The trap: screener's debtor days use trade receivables; companies that hold large "unbilled revenue" or contract assets look better than they are. A rising trend matters more than the level.

KPI 10: Cash conversion (CFO ÷ EBITDA) and free cash flow

What it is. How much operating profit turns into operating cash, and what is left after capital spending. Formulas: CFO ÷ EBITDA; free cash flow = CFO − capital expenditure (including capitalised development). Worked example: Aurionpro's FY26 EBITDA of ₹282 crore produced ₹56 crore of CFO (20%), and after capital spending free cash flow was −₹115 crore; OFSS's ₹3,477 crore of EBITDA produced ₹2,638 crore of CFO (76%, equal to its profit after tax) and ₹2,587 crore of free cash flow. What moves it: tax (CFO is after tax, EBITDA before), working capital (receivables, project inventory), billing terms (annual-in-advance subscriptions help) and capitalised R&D (which sits in investing, not operating, cash flow, flattering CFO). Benchmarks: above 75% good, 55–75% average, below 45% weak (our benchmark, allowing for 25–28% tax). How the seven compare (FY26): Capillary 161% (low base), Intellect 87%, OFSS 76%, RateGain 68% (its Q1 FY27 free-cash-flow conversion was 78.8% on the company's basis), Newgen 57%, MapmyIndia 55%, Aurionpro 20%. The trap: companies define "cash conversion" differently (free cash flow ÷ EBITDA, CFO ÷ PAT, or CFO ÷ EBITDA), and even research sources disagree: our research file lists ratios of 105% for OFSS and 38% for Aurionpro on an unreproducible basis. Compute it yourself from the cash-flow statement, compare like with like, and look at two or three years, because one year's working-capital swing can mislead.

KPI 11: The Rule of 40

What it is. A shorthand used by software investors that balances growth against profitability. Formula: revenue growth % + EBITDA margin %; a score of 40 or more is considered healthy. Worked example: Intellect grew 21.5% in FY26 with a 19.2% margin, a score of 40.7; Newgen grew 5.9% with a 25.8% margin, a score of 31.7. What moves it: anything that raises growth without cutting margin, or vice versa; acquisitions inflate the growth half. Benchmarks: above 40 good, 30–40 average, below 30 weak. How the seven compare (FY26): OFSS 57, Intellect 41, Aurionpro 40, MapmyIndia 38, Capillary 36, Newgen 32. RateGain scores 88 on reported FY26 numbers but about 42 on Q1 FY27 organic growth (17.5%) plus adjusted margin (24.6%). The trap: use organic growth and a consistent margin basis. With acquired growth, the rule rewards buying revenue; with adjusted margins, it rewards excluding costs.

KPI 12: Client concentration

What it is. The share of revenue from the largest customers. Formula: top-5 (or top-10) customers' revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Capillary's top five customers were 34% of revenue in Q1 FY27, about ₹87 crore of the ₹256.6 crore quarter; a year earlier they were 40%. What moves it: large deals (up), broadening the base and acquisitions of many small customers (down). Benchmarks: top-5 below 20% good, 20–35% average, above 35% weak. How the seven compare: only Capillary discloses it in our sources. Banking-software vendors are often heavily concentrated in a few large banks per year (OFSS's single ₹935 crore deal was 30% of a quarter). MapmyIndia depends on a handful of car makers. The trap: concentration by revenue understates it for lumpy licence businesses, where a single deal can be most of a quarter's growth.

KPI 13: Payout ratio and capital allocation

What it is. The share of profit returned as dividends (and buybacks). Formula: dividends ÷ PAT. Worked example: OFSS paid about ₹400 a share on EPS of ₹303 in FY26, a payout of 132%. What moves it: the reinvestment need, acquisition plans, ownership (a parent wanting cash) and the cash pile. Benchmarks: there is no universal "good" level; it should match the reinvestment opportunity. Paying out more than 100% with no growth plan is a weak sign; paying nothing while making value-destroying acquisitions is worse. How the seven compare (FY26): OFSS 132%, Intellect 28%, Newgen 28%, MapmyIndia 14%, Aurionpro 10%, RateGain and Capillary 0%. The trap: a high payout at a subsidiary may reflect the parent's preference, not a lack of opportunities; a zero payout at an acquirer is only justified if its acquisitions earn more than its cost of capital, which in this group they have not yet shown.

KPI 14: Organic growth and constant-currency growth

What it is. Revenue growth excluding acquisitions (organic) and excluding currency moves (constant currency). Formula: organic growth = (this year's revenue − acquired revenue) ÷ last year's revenue − 1, or the combined-entity method (both years including the acquired business). Worked example: RateGain's Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹785 crore was up 187.6% on Q1 FY26's ₹273 crore, but on a combined-entity basis (adding Sojern to both periods) growth was 17.5%. If the rupee is 6% weaker against the dollar, roughly 6 points of a dollar earner's rupee growth is currency. What moves it: demand, pricing, wins, churn. Benchmarks: organic growth above 15% good, 8–15% average, below 8% weak for this group. How the seven compare: RateGain 17.5% and Capillary about 17% organic in Q1 FY27; OFSS about 18% ex-deal (our estimate); Intellect 20% and Newgen 11% (no material acquisitions); MapmyIndia 15% headline but 2% in map data; Aurionpro 6%. The trap: companies choose the organic method that flatters them; the combined-entity method includes the acquired business's own growth, which may itself have been bought.

KPI 15: Deferred revenue and remaining performance obligations

What it is. Deferred revenue (also called contract liabilities or unearned revenue) is cash billed or received before the service is delivered; remaining performance obligations (RPO) is the total contracted revenue not yet recognised, whether billed or not. Formulas: deferred revenue appears on the balance sheet; RPO is disclosed in the notes under Ind AS 115; the useful ratio is RPO ÷ trailing revenue and its growth. Worked example: a SaaS vendor that bills a ₹24 crore annual subscription in advance on 1 April records ₹24 crore of deferred revenue and recognises ₹2 crore a month; by 30 June, ₹18 crore is still deferred. OFSS's RPO of ₹7,345 crore in Q1 FY27 against trailing revenue of ₹8,945 crore covers about ten months. What moves it: multi-year signings and advance billing (up), recognition as work is delivered (down), and the mix of licence (recognised at once, so little RPO) versus subscription and services (recognised over time, so more RPO). Benchmarks: RPO growing faster than revenue is good; RPO growing slower than revenue for several quarters is a warning that new signings are not keeping pace. How the seven compare: only OFSS's RPO (up 16%) appears in our sources; Capillary's ARR and Aurionpro's order book are the nearest substitutes elsewhere. The trap: a big perpetual licence lifts revenue but adds little to RPO, while a long SaaS contract does the reverse, so a company shifting to subscriptions can show RPO accelerating while revenue growth slows. Read the two together.

In plain terms

Remaining performance obligations are the revenue a company has already signed for but not yet booked. When that number grows faster than revenue, the future is filling up; when it lags, the company is living off past signings. For licence-heavy businesses it understates visibility; for subscription businesses it is the best single gauge of next year.

Definition traps, collected

  • Total income vs revenue. Intellect headlines total income of ₹3,161 crore (including other income and currency gains); revenue from operations was ₹3,038 crore. Its quarterly tables in our primer mix the two bases.
  • Company EBITDA vs screener operating profit. Intellect ₹703 crore vs ₹584 crore; MapmyIndia ₹176 crore vs ₹169 crore. State the basis every time.

  • ARR vs run-rate. Capillary's ₹1,026.6 crore is contracted ARR; RateGain's ₹3,140 crore is one quarter × 4.
  • Licence-linked vs recurring. Intellect's licence-linked revenue includes one-time licences.
  • Annuity. Newgen's annuity includes maintenance on old licences as well as SaaS.
  • Order book vs pipeline. MapmyIndia's ₹1,754 crore is called both in its own communications.
  • Organic vs reported growth. RateGain +188% vs 17.5%; Capillary +43% vs ~17%.
  • NRR, organic vs blended. Capillary's 113% blended hides an organic 111%.
  • Rupee vs constant currency. A 6% weaker rupee adds about 6 points to a dollar earner's rupee growth.

In plain terms

Almost every number in a software company's results release has a cousin with a similar name and a different meaning. Before comparing two companies, check that you are comparing the same cousin: revenue not total income, contracted ARR not run-rate, organic not reported, screener EBITDA not adjusted.

How to read a software-product company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue, and the one-offs inside it (minute 1). Pull reported revenue and growth, then look for any single licence, project or acquisition named in the release. Subtract it. For OFSS's Q1 FY27 that turns +69% into about +18%. Red flag: a big jump with no explanation of the drivers.
  2. Organic and constant-currency growth (minute 2). Find management's organic figure for acquirers (RateGain 17.5%, Capillary ~17%) and estimate the currency effect for dollar earners. Red flag: no organic figure disclosed in a quarter with acquisitions.
  3. Revenue mix (minute 3). Licence, implementation, AMC, subscription, usage, hardware. A rising recurring share is good news even in a slow quarter (Newgen Q1 FY27: annuity ~71%). Red flag: growth coming only from devices, projects or media.
  4. Recurring metrics (minute 4). ARR, NRR, GRR, or the company's nearest equivalent. Red flag: ARR growing while organic NRR falls.
  5. Visibility (minute 5). Pipeline, order book, RPO, wins and large deals. Compare with last quarter. Red flag: a falling RPO or order book after a big revenue quarter.
  6. Margin and its basis (minute 6). Screener-basis EBITDA margin, then the company's adjusted figure, and the reasons for any gap. Red flag: a widening gap between adjusted and reported.
  7. Below the line (minute 7). Depreciation and amortisation, other income, exceptional items, tax. Red flag: amortisation rising faster than EBITDA, or "exceptional" items appearing every year.
  8. Cash (minute 8). Operating cash flow, debtor days or receivables, deferred revenue, capitalised development. Red flag: receivables rising faster than revenue (Newgen, MapmyIndia) or CFO well below EBITDA (Aurionpro).
  9. Balance sheet and capital allocation (minute 9). Net cash or net debt, acquisition loans, deferred consideration, dividends. Red flag: debt not falling after an acquisition, or payouts above 100% without a cash pile to support them.
  10. Guidance and people (minute 10). Has guidance been raised, held or cut? Any changes in the chief executive, finance chief or key sales leaders? Red flag: guidance withdrawn, or senior departures around a weak quarter.
In plain terms

Ten minutes is enough if you go in order: strip the one-offs, find organic growth, check the mix, look at recurring metrics and backlog, test the margin basis, read below the EBITDA line, follow the cash, check the balance sheet, and finish with guidance and management changes. Most surprises in this group would have been spotted at steps 1, 6 or 8.

No single ranking captures "winning" in a group this varied. The three positioning maps in this report (the earnings-quality map in section 11 and the two below) and the scorecard at the end of this section each answer a different question: is the company priced for what it earns, is it growing without destroying returns, and does its profit turn into cash?

The first map shows the market paying for margin in a rough line: the higher the margin, the higher the multiple. The exceptions tell the story. A simple way to value what remains

A useful rough framework is: value ≈ recurring revenue × a recurring multiple + non-recurring gross profit × a lower multiple + net cash. Take Newgen as an ILLUSTRATIVE case: FY26 annuity revenue of ₹968 crore at 4 times sales is ₹3,872 crore; the remaining ₹606 crore of licence and implementation revenue at a 40% gross margin is ₹242 crore of gross profit, worth perhaps ₹1,200 crore at 5 times; add net cash of ₹1,076 crore and the total is about ₹6,150 crore, against a market value of ₹6,937 crore. On these (arbitrary but plausible) multiples the market is paying roughly fair value for Newgen's annuity and a little more for growth. In the 2026 market, Indian recurring software revenue is implicitly valued at roughly 2.5–5.5 times sales (Intellect 2.6×, Newgen 3.7×, RateGain 5.5× on FY26 or 3.2× on FY27 guidance, Capillary 5.6×), while OFSS trades at about 10.9 times sales on the strength of its margin and payout.

In plain terms

The share prices fell because investors worried that AI would shrink software, not because these companies reported bad results. Some of the fall is justified by company problems (Newgen's slow collections, MapmyIndia's flat core), some by genuine uncertainty about AI. But the banking-software names now trade at multiples close to mid-sized IT-services firms, which means the market is no longer paying anything extra for owning a product.

GenAI: the threat the market has priced, and the product cycle it has not

AI agents could hollow out workflow software and implementation work. They could also run on top of the systems of record and domain data that these companies own. So far, AI revenue is immaterial everywhere.

The 2026 sell-off was a bet on one side of a genuinely two-sided question. To assess it, separate the specific ways AI could hurt these companies from the specific ways it could help, and then look at what the companies have actually shown.

Four ways AI could hurt

  • Agentic AI compresses seat- and workflow-based software. If an AI agent can read a loan application, check it against rules and route it, the value of a low-code workflow tool (Newgen) or a loyalty rules engine (Capillary) may shift to the model layer and to whoever owns the agent. This is the market's main thesis, and it is why Newgen fell furthest.
  • AI lowers the cost of building software. Banks and enterprises can build more in-house, and challengers (including cloud-native core-banking vendors such as Thought Machine, Mambu and 10x) can build faster. A cheaper build weakens the "it took us 20 years to write this" moat.
  • AI travel agents may bypass the channels RateGain feeds. If consumers book through AI assistants that talk directly to hotels or to a few large platforms, the online travel agencies and metasearch sites that RateGain's distribution business connects could matter less (our assessment). RateGain's answer is "Agentic ARI" and AI price-visibility tools.
  • Implementation services deflate. The same AI-driven productivity gains squeezing IT-services pricing (covered in our IT Services report) will compress implementation revenue, which is roughly 13–47% of members' revenue. That hurts revenue, though perhaps not margin, since implementation is the lowest-margin stream.

  • Installed bases are distribution. Selling an AI module to 600 existing FLEXCUBE banks or 415 Capillary brands is far easier than winning new customers.

AI could make some software less valuable, especially tools that route documents or run simple rules, and it will cut the price of implementation work. It could also make software with unique data and regulatory track records more valuable, because AI agents need somewhere trustworthy to run. Today nobody in this group can show material AI revenue, so the market is pricing fear rather than evidence.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian software products

The bull case rests on recurring revenue, owned IP and a de-rated starting point; the bear case on services-like margins, bought growth, weak cash at three companies and AI.

The bull case

  • A de-rated starting point. The three BFSI challengers trade at 12–14 times EBITDA and the ex-OFSS group at about 29 times trailing earnings, roughly the same as mid-sized IT services, with no premium for owned IP.
  • The transition is visibly working. Newgen's annuity is about 71% of Q1 FY27; Intellect's platform revenue rose 141%; Capillary's subscription share is 94.5% with 113% NRR. As transitions complete, reported growth and margins should improve.
  • Demand has non-discretionary pillars. Core modernisation, AML and compliance, data-protection rules and insurance digitisation are multi-year and partly forced by regulators. Intellect's ₹13,000 crore pipeline and OFSS's ₹7,345 crore RPO back that up.
  • Acquirers are executing. RateGain raised FY27 guidance, repaid 38% of its loan in nine months and is growing 17.5% organically; Capillary bought ~$32 million of ARR for ₹16.9 crore.
  • OFSS is a cash compounder. A 45% margin, 3.9% yield and about ₹5,400 crore of cash, with ex-deal growth of about 18% in Q1 FY27.
  • Products own what AI needs. Systems of record, domain data and regulated workflows; every member has an AI product to sell into an existing base.

The bear case

  • No operating leverage. The six non-OFSS companies doubled revenue from FY22 to FY26 at a flat ~21% margin, below IT services; implementation and selling costs absorb the product economics.
  • Growth is bought. Organic growth at RateGain and Capillary is about 17%; ROCE is 14% and 3%, and "adjusted" figures exclude real costs (₹80–90 crore a year of RateGain deferred consideration until FY29).
  • Cash quality is weak at three. Newgen's debtor days are 164, MapmyIndia's 136, and Aurionpro converted a fifth of its FY26 EBITDA into operating cash, with negative free cash flow.
  • Lumpiness and concentration. One OFSS deal was 30% of a quarter; Intellect's quarterly profit swung four-fold; Gulf geopolitics can freeze a region's deals.
  • AI may be a real threat. Workflow software, loyalty rules and OTA-linked distribution are exactly what agents could bypass; implementation revenue will deflate; AI revenue is immaterial so far.
  • Governance and control risks. OFSS's capital allocation is set by Oracle and its CEO just changed; Capillary lost ₹33.4 crore to fraud at an acquired unit; Newgen also has a new CEO.

Weighing the two, the evidence through Q1 FY27 supports a narrower conclusion than either side. The bull case is strongest where a company has already shown recurring revenue and cash together: OFSS, and to a lesser degree Intellect (net cash, 87% cash conversion, a deep pipeline) and RateGain (record FCF conversion, debt falling). The bear case is strongest where one of the quality flags is unresolved: Newgen and MapmyIndia on collections and growth, Aurionpro on cash, Capillary on profitability and controls. The sub-sector's re-rating, if it comes, is likely to be company by company, triggered by two or three quarters in which margins rise and cash follows, rather than by a change in the AI narrative.

In plain terms

The optimist sees owned software, recurring revenue and low prices. The pessimist sees services-like margins, bought growth, slow cash and an AI threat. Both are right about different companies. The way to tell them apart is the same three numbers every quarter: recurring share, organic growth and cash conversion.
