India's diversified pharma majors: the windfall ends, the engines must carry

A complete guide to the six multi-engine drugmakers — Sun Pharma, Cipla, Dr. Reddy's, Zydus, Lupin and Glenmark. How a company that sells branded medicines in India, generics in America, patented specialty brands, consumer health products and biosimilars under one roof actually makes its money; why the end of one US cancer generic cut the group's American sales by a tenth in a single quarter; where the cash is being recycled, from GLP-1 pens to a ₹1 lakh crore acquisition; and how to value a company whose separate engines deserve multiples as far apart as 2× and 30×.

00

The one-page view: six portfolios that just lost their best product

Each of the six is five or six businesses with opposite economics under one roof. FY26–27 is the year the most profitable of those businesses, the US windfall, stopped paying.

Sun Pharma, Cipla, Dr. Reddy's, Zydus Lifesciences, Lupin and Glenmark are often lumped together as "Indian pharma". They should not be. A diversified major does not run one business. It runs a portfolio of profit engines: branded medicines sold to Indian doctors, plain and complex generics sold to American wholesalers, patented specialty brands, branded generics in Russia, South Africa and Brazil, European generics and over-the-counter brands, bulk drug ingredients sold to other drugmakers, and, at some of them, consumer health, biosimilars and orthopaedic implants. No single engine is more than about 45% of revenue at any of the six. That is the definition this report uses, and it is what separates them from the India-branded franchises (Torrent, Mankind, Alkem), the export generic houses (Aurobindo) and the API makers (Divi's, Laurus).

Size is not the test. Aurobindo is as large as Dr. Reddy's but sells three-quarters of its output in the US and Europe and almost nothing in India, so it belongs with the exporters. Glenmark is the smallest of the six at ₹16,983 crore of FY26 revenue, but it has four balanced engines plus an innovation arm, so it belongs here. Together the six booked ₹1,92,414 crore of revenue in FY26, ₹50,045 crore of EBITDA (26.0%) and ₹31,370 crore of profit, and were worth ₹9.04 lakh crore on 5 October 2026. Sun alone is 30% of the revenue and 47% of the market value.

The investment case for each major is an argument about three things. The first is mix: how much of the profit comes from durable engines (India branded, emerging-market brands, specialty) and how much from transient ones (a US generic sold while only two or three companies are allowed to make it). The second is capital allocation: where the cash thrown off by the US business is being recycled. The third is compliance: whether the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lets the American engine keep launching products. Every number in this report hangs off one of those three questions.

Five reasons FY26–27 is the hinge

  • The gRevlimid cliff. Generic versions of Bristol Myers Squibb's myeloma drug Revlimid (lenalidomide) were sold in the US under volume caps from March 2022, at prices far above normal generic levels. Unlimited entry arrived on 31 January 2026 and the windfall vanished. Dr. Reddy's North America sales fell 41% in dollars in Q1 FY27; Cipla's fell 28%. We estimate the six lost $1.0–1.2 billion of very high-margin revenue (indicative; no company discloses it).
  • Specialty is scaling, and is now taxed. Sun's innovative brands earn $351 million a quarter. In April 2026 the US imposed a 100% Section 232 tariff on patented drug imports; Sun bought a 0% rate until January 2029 by signing a most-favoured-nation pricing deal.
  • GLP-1 generics open outside the US. Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) went generic in India and Brazil in March 2026 and in Canada from January 2026. Dr. Reddy's won the first G7 approval but lost 3–4 million pens to an ingredient quality problem.
  • Capital allocation turned aggressive. Sun is buying Organon for $11.75 billion; Zydus did five deals in eighteen months and moved into net debt; Lupin, Dr. Reddy's and Glenmark bought or licensed their way into new engines.
  • FDA status is still binary. Sun's Halol plant has been under import alert since December 2022, Zydus's Baddi plant received a warning letter in June 2026, and Dr. Reddy's biosimilar future hangs on one Hyderabad site that drew seven observations in June.
In plain terms

For three years, a handful of Indian companies were among the few allowed to sell a copy of a $9 billion cancer drug in America, and they earned something close to pure profit on it. That arrangement ended in January 2026. The question for each of the six now is what replaces that profit, how quickly, and whether the money it generated was spent well. The answers differ sharply, which is why their valuations range from 15 to 34 times earnings.

01

Ten engines, one P&L: how a diversified major earns

The same molecule can earn a 15% margin in an American pharmacy and 40% in an Indian one. A major's profit is the sum of very different businesses, and so is its risk.

Every major starts in the same place: a chemist makes an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API), a formulation plant turns it into a tablet, inhaler or injection, and a regulator approves it for sale. From there the paths diverge completely. The value is not made in the factory. It is made in the channel the product is sold through, and the price that channel will bear.

Steps 1 and 2 are shared across engines, which is the point of being diversified: one plant network and one R&D budget feed several markets. Steps 3 and 4 decide the margin, and they differ by engine.

Three features make the economics of a major unusual. First, the engines have opposite profiles. The India branded business grows 8–15% a year at a 30–40% contribution margin and almost never shrinks, because Indian doctors keep prescribing a brand they trust. Plain US generics shrink 4–10% a year in price, earn 10–20%, and need a constant stream of new launches just to stand still. Complex or limited-competition US generics earn 40–70% while only two or three companies can make them, then collapse to plain-generic economics when the window closes. Specialty brands lose money for years and then earn 25–40% once a field force is paid for.

Second, the engines share costs. A single plant in Goa or Gujarat may make tablets for India, the US and South Africa. A single R&D centre in Hyderabad develops products for all of them. That makes segment profit hard to see from outside: of the six, only Zydus reports segments with profit, and even its "Pharma" segment bundles India, the US and emerging markets. Analysts estimate engine margins from what the companies say on calls and from what pure-play peers earn. The margins below are therefore indicative.

Third, the engines feed each other. The US generics cash funds the R&D and acquisitions that build the next engine. The India business gives a launch pad for in-licensed or home-grown innovations (Ilumya, saroglitazar, Ryaltris) that pure branded players rarely have. Emerging-market brands provide a second home for products first built for India. A major is valued on whether this recycling is working.

The cash-cow ladder

Sun's management describes its strategy as a four-rung ladder, and the frame works for all six. Rung one is US and global generics: cash-generating but eroding. Rung two is emerging-market brands. Rung three is India brands. Rung four is innovative or specialty medicine, reached either by building it through R&D or by buying it through licensing and acquisition. The cash from the bottom rung pays for the climb. Each major has chosen a different top rung, and the choice explains most of the strategic differences between them.

In plain terms

A major makes medicines once and sells them through several very different shops. In India it is a brand business, where a doctor's habit is worth money every year. In America it is mostly an auction, where three huge buyers push prices down every year, interrupted by brief periods when only a few sellers are allowed in. The companies use the American cash to buy or build something more durable. Judging a major means judging each shop separately and then asking whether the cash is being moved to the right one.

The typical major's EBITDA splits roughly 35–45% from India and emerging-market brands, 30–50% from the US, and 10–20% from everything else (indicative). FY24–26 broke that pattern. At Dr. Reddy's, Lupin and Cipla, one or two limited-competition US products produced 25–60% of profit at the peak. That concentration is the single most important fact about the group's recent earnings, and the reason the next two sections spend time on size and mix before the deep dives.

02

₹1.92 lakh crore and how it grew: a decade of compounding, one windfall

The six grew revenue about 10% a year from FY20 to FY26. Profit grew faster, then stalled in Q1 FY27 as the windfall ran out.

Between FY20 and FY26 the six majors grew combined revenue from roughly ₹1,07,700 crore to ₹1,92,414 crore, a compound rate of about 10.2% a year. That is faster than global generic majors (2–5% a year) and roughly in line with the Indian pharmaceutical market (IPM). In dollar terms the group is about $22 billion of revenue at ₹87.5, which is larger than Teva's $17.3 billion but spread across six companies that each earn $1.9–6.7 billion.

The growth came from three places. India branded sales compounded at low double digits. Emerging markets and Europe grew through acquisitions (Dr. Reddy's NRT purchase almost doubled its European revenue in two years) and through inflation-linked pricing in markets such as Russia. And the US contributed lumpy windfalls: gRevlimid from 2022, Lupin's complex products from FY24, Zydus's gAsacol HD and gMirabegron. Margins: a rise, a peak, and the first step down

Profitability tells the more dramatic story. On company-reported numbers, the group's EBITDA margin climbed from the low 20s in FY22–23 to the high 20s in FY25 as the US windfalls compounded. FY26 blended 26.0% on the screener basis (which deducts one-offs) and about 27% on company definitions. Q1 FY27 showed the turn: Dr. Reddy's dropped to 12.5%, Cipla to 16.7% and Zydus to 24.1%, while Sun held at 28.9% and Lupin, still inside its own windfall, ran at 29.8%. In plain terms

The six have grown about 10% a year for six years, which is respectable for companies of this size. But the margin gains of FY24–26 were not mostly the result of the steady businesses improving. They came from a few American products sold while competition was restricted. Every FY27 guidance number in the table above is, in effect, a forecast of how much of that profit can be replaced.

Two other context numbers matter. The six sold about ₹58,000 crore of medicines in India in FY26, about 23% of the ₹2.53 lakh crore IPM by revenue (and 26–27% on monthly audited sales, a different basis). And they sold about $7.0 billion in the US (roughly ₹61,000 crore), not counting about $0.28 billion of Glenmark licensing income booked in its North America line. Those two numbers are the backbone of the group: India is the stable floor, the US the swinging profit pool.

Reading the shapes

  • Sun is the most balanced and, underneath, the most transformed. Its US line ($1.90 billion, 28.6%) is total US sales, not generics: specialty brands now exceed $1 billion of it, which leaves US generics including Taro below $0.9 billion, under 15% of the group. Its specialty business ($1.42 billion, about ₹12,430 crore, ~21% of sales) cuts across the US and RoW blocks.
  • Cipla is the most India-heavy: One-India (prescription brands, trade generics and consumer health) is 45%. Africa, mostly South Africa, is a genuine second home at 15%. Its US business, at 24%, is now the swing factor rather than the core.
  • Dr. Reddy's is the most US-generics-exposed at 34% of FY26 (and around 45% at the FY25 peak, approximate). It is also the most geographically spread: emerging markets (Russia-led) 20%, Europe 16.5% after the NRT deal, and a 10% third-party API and services arm (PSAI) that no other major has at that scale.
  • Zydus has the highest US share (about 42%, estimated) but also the most adjacencies: a listed consumer company (Zydus Wellness, 14.6% of group revenue on the reported segment, not the ~20% sometimes quoted) and a medtech arm (2.9%).
  • Lupin is US-heavy (41%) because its complex products boomed; its India share fell from about 35% in FY20 to 29%.
  • Glenmark is the most evenly balanced across four engines, once the AbbVie licensing income is separated out. We estimate India at ~₹5,250 crore, core North America ~₹3,220 crore, licensing ~₹2,430 crore, Europe ~₹2,950 crore and emerging markets ~₹2,650 crore. These are estimates built by annualising the Q1 FY26 base disclosed on the Q1 FY27 call; the split in Glenmark's primer sums to only ₹14,200 crore and understates India.
In plain terms

If you want to know how badly a US problem will hurt a major, look at the slate-blue block. A 10% fall in American sales costs Dr. Reddy's or Zydus three or four times as much revenue as it costs Cipla's India-heavy model, and at Sun most of the US block is now patented brands that behave differently from generics. If you want to know what will hold the valuation up, look at the oxblood block.

Where demand comes from: seven markets, seven growth rates

The majors do not sell into one market. They sell into seven, each with its own buyer, its own price-setter and its own reason to grow or shrink.

Because the six are portfolios, "demand" has to be read engine by engine. A doctor in Lucknow writing a prescription for an inhaler, a buyer at a US purchasing group running a quarterly bid, a dermatologist in Texas deciding whether to try a new alopecia pill, a Russian pharmacy chain and a German health insurer's tender office are all customers of the same company. What follows is a short tour of each pool, with the demand signal that matters most for the majors.

India: steady, price-led, chronic-tilted

The Indian pharmaceutical market (IPM) was worth more than ₹2.52 lakh crore on a moving annual total to June 2026 (Pharmarack), growing about 9–11% in FY26 and about 12% in Q1 FY27 (IQVIA 12.2%). The growth is mostly price and new launches rather than volume: IPM volume rose only about 2% in Q1 FY27, against Sun's 5.4%. Chronic therapies (cardiac, diabetes, respiratory, central nervous system, dermatology) grow 1.2–1.5 times as fast as acute ones, because patients stay on them for years. That is why every major is pushing its chronic share up: Cipla reports 60.4% and targets about 65%, Lupin about 65–67%, Zydus 54.2%. For the mechanics of detailing, MR productivity and price control, see the branded formulations report.

US generics: huge volume, shrinking value per pill

Generics fill 89% of US prescriptions but take only 11.6% of US drug spending, about $106 billion (Association for Accessible Medicines, 2026). Volume grows in low single digits. Value depends on three things the majors do not control: how many branded drugs lose exclusivity in a given year (the loss-of-exclusivity, or LOE, wave), how often shortages let generic makers raise prices, and how many rivals file for the same product. Plain oral tablets now face 4–10% annual price erosion. The complex generics pool, which covers inhalers, injectables, peptides, long-acting products and ophthalmics, is cited at about $54 billion and erodes much more slowly because fewer companies can make the products.

US specialty: patients, payers and formularies

Specialty brands sell on patient-level demand: psoriasis (Ilumya), alopecia areata (Leqselvi), acne (Winlevi), cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (Unloxcyt), dry eye (Cequa). Demand exists; access is the constraint. Pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) decide which drugs are on formulary, and infused products need hospital and cancer-centre approval. Unloxcyt is billed under Medicare Part B, which brings it into the scope of US drug-price negotiation over time. For an Indian major, the specialty question is not whether patients need the drug but whether the company can afford the years of sales-force and rebate spending needed to win formulary access.

GLP-1 and obesity: the largest new pool

Global semaglutide sales exceed $30 billion a year. The molecule's patents and data exclusivity began expiring outside the US, EU and Japan in 2026: India and Brazil in March, Canada from January (data exclusivity). The US, EU and Japan follow around 2031–32. In India, GLP-1 sales reached ₹2,333 crore on a moving annual total to August 2026, but 62% of that was Lilly's tirzepatide (Mounjaro, now India's largest single brand) rather than semaglutide. Section 10 covers this race in depth.

Emerging markets, Europe and biosimilars

  • Emerging markets grew fastest in FY26: Dr. Reddy's Russia sales rose 34% to ₹3,480 crore, Lupin's emerging markets 35%, Sun's 13.6% in dollars. Part of that is real volume and launches, part is pricing in inflationary economies and currency effects. Cipla is #3 in South Africa with about 8.5% share. Brazil, the Philippines, Vietnam and Malaysia are growing at double digits.
  • Europe is two markets: tendered generics (Germany, where insurers award contracts to the lowest bidder) and branded niches the majors are buying into — Glenmark's respiratory and dermatology brands (Ryaltris, Winlevi), Dr. Reddy's Nicotinell (#1 or #2 in 14 of the top 17 nicotine-replacement markets) and Lupin's VISUfarma eye-care brands.
  • Biosimilars ride a 2025–32 wave of biologic patent expiries, including Keytruda, Orencia, Prolia/Xgeva, Eylea and Stelara. Dr. Reddy's chief executive expects biosimilars to surpass generics in the US by the early 2030s.

Chemistry: integrated, but still tied to China

Majors are 50–80% backward-integrated: they make most of the APIs in their own products. But many KSMs and solvents still come from China, so a Chinese price spike or a freight disruption flows into costs within a quarter or two. In 2026 a war in the Middle East raised solvent, API and freight costs; Cipla put the hit at about 1–2% of revenue and Dr. Reddy's at about 1% of EBITDA. The API economics behind this are covered in the API and intermediates report. The new bottleneck is peptides and devices. GLP-1 drugs need synthetic or recombinant peptide APIs made to tight purity limits, and pens, cartridges and autoinjectors made at scale. Dr. Reddy's semaglutide API failure in Q1 FY27 cost it ₹240 crore and 3–4 million pens. Sun says it makes semaglutide API and formulation in-house and has secured device components.

People and compliance

India field forces are large: Cipla has about 12,000 representatives and plans no major additions; Sun had more than 11,000 in 2022 and has expanded since (no current count is disclosed). US specialty requires a separate, far more expensive sales force: Sun added representatives for its 2025–26 launches. Compliance is a permanent cost line. A warning letter or "official action indicated" (OAI) finding at a key plant typically costs ₹50–300 crore or more in remediation consultants, extra quality staff and lost launches, and takes 18–36 months to clear.

Capital: plants, and now American plants

Each major runs 15–45 plants across India and abroad. Capex runs at roughly 3–7% of sales: Dr. Reddy's spent about ₹2,300 crore in FY26 (~7% of sales) and guides ₹1,800 crore for FY27; Zydus guides ₹1,500–1,600 crore; Lupin spent ₹1,062 crore. Sun and Cipla spend an estimated 3–5% of sales. What has changed since 2025 is where the capital goes. US-based plants, once a cost disadvantage, have become a hedge against tariffs and a qualification for "made in America" procurement preferences.

The gRevlimid cliff: anatomy of a windfall Deep dive

For four years a legal settlement let a few Indian companies sell a cancer drug in America at near-branded prices. When it ended, it took a large share of the group's profit with it.

Revlimid (lenalidomide) is Bristol Myers Squibb's blockbuster treatment for multiple myeloma, a blood cancer. Before generics, its US sales ran at roughly $8–9 billion a year. Rather than fight every generic challenger to the end in court, the originator (Celgene, later acquired by BMS) settled. The settlements allowed named generic companies to sell limited volumes from March 2022, with the permitted percentage rising each year, and unlimited volumes from 31 January 2026. Natco, partnered with Teva, held the first-to-file position. Dr. Reddy's, Cipla, Zydus, Sun, Aurobindo, Alvogen and others held their own settlement slots.

During the window, only a few companies could sell, so they did not compete on price the way generic makers normally do. A product that would usually sell at a few percent of the brand price sold at a large fraction of it. Because the manufacturing cost of a capsule is trivial, the contribution margin was close to the selling price: an estimated 85–95%. The end has an accounting tail. When the price collapses, generic sellers pay shelf-stock adjustments (SSA) to wholesalers and pharmacies to compensate them for inventory bought at the old price. Dr. Reddy's booked ₹453 crore of SSA in Q4 FY26, which left its net lenalidomide revenue for that quarter close to zero.

The margin arithmetic

Why did one product matter so much? Take a company with ₹28,000 crore of revenue and a 26% EBITDA margin, or ₹7,280 crore of EBITDA. Remove a $200 million product earning 90% contribution. At ₹87.5 that is ₹1,750 crore of revenue and ₹1,575 crore of EBITDA, or 5.6% of the original revenue base. EBITDA falls 22% to ₹5,705 crore; revenue falls to ₹26,250 crore; the margin drops from 26.0% to 21.7%. That is almost exactly Cipla's FY25 to FY26 move (about 26% to about 21%). For Dr. Reddy's, whose exposure was roughly three times larger, the move from about 28% in FY25 to 15.4% in Q1 FY27 (excluding the semaglutide provision) is the same arithmetic at a bigger scale.

In plain terms

A normal generic earns a modest profit for many years. gRevlimid earned an enormous profit for four years and then almost nothing. When a single product earns close to its entire selling price as profit, losing $200 million of sales costs about ₹1,575 crore of operating profit, more than most Indian mid-caps earn in a year. That is why Dr. Reddy's quarterly profit fell 69% even though its revenue barely moved.

Dr. Reddy's: the company the cliff hit hardest

Dr. Reddy's had the largest settlement position among the six and the most US-generics-heavy mix. Its North America sales fell 22% in FY26 to ₹11,374 crore and a further 41% in Q1 FY27 to $236 million. Q1 FY27 profit was ₹443 crore, down 69%, with the semaglutide ingredient problem adding a ₹240 crore provision. The company argues that its base business, excluding lenalidomide, is growing at double digits, including in North America. If so, the $164 million year-on-year decline in quarterly US sales implies roughly $180–200 million of lenalidomide in Q1 FY26 alone (calculated). Management's FY27 guidance, "double-digit growth excluding lenalidomide", is an explicit invitation to look through the hole. In plain terms

Every major lives with this pattern: a few exceptional products, a long tail of ordinary ones. The skill is not avoiding cliffs, which is impossible in generics, but having the next exceptional product ready before the current one ends, and not mistaking the windfall years for the normal level of profit. Investors now watch every major for the next cliff date as closely as they watch for the next launch.

Three lessons carry beyond lenalidomide. First, windfall profits should be valued as a windfall: a few years of cash, not a perpetuity. Section 20 shows that the market applies this rule harshly to Lupin today. Second, the cash from windfalls is only as valuable as what it is spent on. Dr. Reddy's put its gRevlimid cash into NRT, Stugeron, biosimilars and net cash; Zydus put its into five acquisitions; Cipla mostly kept its as cash. Third, the replacement is slower than the loss. One product ended on one date; the replacements need dozens of approvals, several plant inspections and, for semaglutide and abatacept, a perfect supply chain.

The specialty pivot: Sun's brands, the Organon leap and Glenmark's cheque Deep dive

Indian majors spent twenty years failing at patented medicine. Sun finally made it work by buying rather than inventing, and is now making the largest bet in Indian pharma history.

Every Indian major has wanted a specialty business, because patented brands earn 85–90% gross margins, grow for a decade and do not erode the way generics do. Until recently, almost none succeeded. This section explains why, then looks at the three live specialty stories: Sun's innovative portfolio and its Organon acquisition, Glenmark's out-licensing of ISB 2001 to AbbVie, and Zydus's home-grown new drugs.

Why Indian majors struggled at specialty

  • Small, under-funded discovery programmes. Ranbaxy, Dr. Reddy's and Glenmark all ran new-drug research in the 2000s. Most molecules failed or were returned by licensing partners. A single global drug programme can cost more than an Indian major's entire annual R&D budget.
  • Under-scaled US sales forces. Selling one or two brands to US doctors requires the same fixed field-force cost as selling ten. Lupin's earlier US branded lines and Dr. Reddy's Promius dermatology unit (wound down around 2019–20) never reached scale.
  • Buying sub-scale brands at full prices, then watching them erode.
  • The FDA crisis years (2013–19) diverted management attention to remediation just as specialty needed it.
  • A rule of thumb: a US specialty launch needs roughly $100–300 million of cumulative pre-profit spending and four to six years to break even (indicative). Few Indian majors could absorb that while defending a generics business.

What worked was narrower. In-licensing approved or late-stage assets rather than discovering them (Sun's Ilumya from Merck, Odomzo from Novartis, Leqselvi via Concert, Unloxcyt via Checkpoint). Focusing on one call point, so one field force sells several brands to the same doctors (Sun in US dermatology). Patience funded by an India cash engine. And out-licensing early clinical assets to big pharma instead of trying to commercialise them (Glenmark–AbbVie; Zydus's partnering model for saroglitazar). Every specialty deal in the M&A list in section 14 follows the first rule: approved or late-stage, never discovery.

What Organon changes for Sun: it roughly doubles revenue (to about $12–13 billion combined; the primer quotes ~$12.4 billion), makes Sun a US and European biosimilars marketer overnight, adds a women's health franchise and global distribution, and turns a net-cash company into one with about 2.3 times net debt to EBITDA. Glenmark: ISB 2001 and a cheque that is mostly still to be booked

In July 2025 Glenmark's innovation arm, Ichnos Glenmark Innovation (IGI), licensed ISB 2001, a trispecific antibody that engages BCMA, CD38 and CD3 to direct T-cells against multiple myeloma, to AbbVie for $700 million upfront and up to $1.925 billion in total, plus tiered double-digit royalties. Glenmark keeps the India and emerging-market rights. It received about ₹5,950 crore, used ₹1,300 crore to repay debt, and has held zero gross debt since. No milestones beyond the upfront had been received by August 2026. More than 160 patients have been dosed in the Phase 1 TRIgnite trial and a combination study has started. The next assets are ISB 2301 (an NK/T-cell engager for solid tumours, IND expected later in 2026), ISB 2302 and ISB 2501, with IGI planning to spend about $70 million over two to three years.

The accounting is where most commentary goes wrong. Glenmark's primer describes the upfront as "booked largely as income" and roughly a third of annual revenue. In Q1 FY27 North America grew 41.1% reported but 19.8% excluding that deferred income. Working backwards, about ₹2,300–2,600 crore, roughly 40% of the upfront, was recognised in FY26, and about ₹3,400–3,600 crore remains to be recognised at about ₹150–170 crore a quarter (calculated). The GLP-1 generics race: the next window opens outside America Deep dive

Semaglutide went generic in India, Brazil and Canada in 2026. It is the next limited-competition window, but this time the winners are decided by peptide chemistry and pen capacity, not patent lawyers.

Novo Nordisk's semaglutide, sold as Ozempic for diabetes and Wegovy for obesity, is one of the largest drugs in the world, with more than $30 billion of annual sales. Its protection runs to about 2031–32 in the US, EU and Japan, but expired much earlier elsewhere. In 2026, for the first time, Indian majors could sell generic semaglutide in large markets. That makes it the most important new product family for the group, and the clearest test of which majors can execute in complex peptides.

Its main value to the majors is as a learning ground for peptide injectable pens before the US semaglutide market opens in the 2030s.

What decides the winners

  • API control. Making semaglutide at scale and purity is hard. Dr. Reddy's failure shows the cost of getting it wrong. Sun and Dr. Reddy's make their API in-house.
  • Device and fill capacity. Pens, cartridges and autoinjectors are a separate supply chain and the binding constraint in 2026.
  • Regulatory first-mover status. Dr. Reddy's in Canada, Sun in South Africa and Brazil. A first approval is only valuable with supply behind it.
  • Channel. In India, cardio-diabetes field-force strength decides share; Torrent leads, not the majors.
  • Price discipline. With 25+ Indian brands within six months, India will not be a high-margin market.
In plain terms

Semaglutide is the next big product that only a few companies can make well, but the race is being won on manufacturing, not on lawsuits. Dr. Reddy's got the first approval in Canada and then could not supply it. India turned into a price war with two dozen brands. The money in 2026–29 is in countries like Canada and Brazil where only a handful of makers can deliver, and later in the US, where the market opens around 2031–32.

How big is the prize? The best available frame is an estimate of $0.5–1.0 billion a year of aggregate Indian GLP-1 generic revenue by FY29 (indicative, from the pharmaceuticals dossier), across all Indian companies, not just the six. Among the majors, Dr. Reddy's and Sun are best positioned outside India; Zydus and Lupin have Indian share and supply partnerships; Cipla has chosen the innovator's tirzepatide instead. Set against the $1.0–1.2 billion of gRevlimid revenue lost, GLP-1s alone will not fill the hole by FY29. They are one of several replacements, alongside complex respiratory, biosimilars and specialty.

Four ways the majors' India businesses differ from the pure branded players

  • Less chronic-pure. Torrent earns about 75% of its India sales from chronic therapies; Cipla (60%), Lupin (65–67%) and Zydus (54%) are lower, and Sun and Dr. Reddy's do not disclose. Chronic franchises grow faster and are stickier.
  • Less disclosure on productivity. None of the six publishes revenue per medical representative (PCPM) the way Mankind and Torrent do, and the field-force counts that exist are dated. Do not compute PCPM for the majors from the numbers in this report: Sun's implied figure at its 2022 headcount would be implausibly high, which simply means the current headcount is larger.
  • "Global brands at home". Ilumya, Ryaltris, saroglitazar, biosimilars and in-licensed oncology (Glenmark's Tevimbra and Brukinsa from BeOne) give the majors innovation-led launches in India that pure branded players mostly lack.
  • India is the ballast, not the growth story, except at Cipla, where One-India is 45% of sales. At the others it is 18–33%.
In plain terms

India is the part of a major that investors are most willing to pay for, because it grows every year and rarely surprises. That makes it the floor of the valuation. The flip side is that an Indian slowdown, a price-control order or a shift of patients to trade generics hurts a major's share price more than its revenue share suggests, because it is the engine holding the multiple up.

All six grew at or above the market in Q1 FY27, which is unusual and worth noting. Some of it is launches (semaglutide, Ilumya, Yurpeak, in-licensed oncology); some is the shift to chronic therapies. Lupin's weak FY26 India growth (+7%) followed a portfolio change and recovered to +13.9% in Q1 FY27. The risk to watch is regulatory rather than competitive: an expansion of the NLEM to more chronic molecules or a stronger push on trade generics would cap price growth across the board. India's GLP-1 shift is the other watch-point, because diabetes and cardiac brands are among the majors' largest domestic franchises.

Washington and the inspector: tariffs, MFN pricing and FDA status

US policy has split the majors' portfolios in two: generics are exempt from tariffs, patented brands are not. And one bad inspection can still freeze the launches meant to fill the cliff.

Two US authorities shape the majors' American engine. The first is trade policy. In 2026 the US put tariffs on patented drug imports and created an escape hatch for companies that accept "most-favoured-nation" (MFN) pricing. The second is the FDA, whose inspections decide which plants can supply the US and whether new products can be approved from them.

Section 232: the rules

  • A Section 232 proclamation on 2 April 2026 imposed a 100% ad valorem tariff on patented pharmaceutical products and their APIs.
  • Effective dates: 31 July 2026 for large (Annex III) companies; 29 September 2026 for all others.
  • Preferential tiers for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland/Liechtenstein and the UK; 100% elsewhere. One source records India among 20 partners with zero duty on specified specialty medicines (rare disease, fertility, cell and gene therapy, ADCs); legal summaries do not mention it. This is unresolved and should be checked against the proclamation text.
  • Generics, their ingredients and biosimilars are excluded. The Commerce Department is to report on generics within a year, around April 2027.
  • MFN escape hatch: companies with MFN pricing agreements pay 0% from 29 September 2026 to 20 January 2029.
  • Sun signed an MFN agreement on 2 September 2026: MFN pricing for US Medicaid and for future innovative launches in exchange for more than two years of tariff relief. The stock fell 2.3% on the news.

Four decades of cycles: founders, windfalls and crackdowns

The six were built by founders under India's process-patent regime, went to America for growth, were humbled by the FDA and by price deflation, and are now in their third reinvention.

All six were founded or shaped by individual entrepreneurs who built through the 1970 Patents Act, which protected only manufacturing processes and so allowed Indian chemists to make any drug by a new route. That regime created the chemistry skills that later made India the world's generic pharmacy. The history since has run in recognisable cycles: a US gold rush, a compliance crackdown, a price-erosion bust, a windfall and, now, a diversification push.

  • 1935–1984
    Founders: Cipla (K.A. Hamied, 1935); Cadila (1952; split in 1995, Zydus under Pankaj Patel); Lupin (D.B. Gupta, 1968); Glenmark (Gracias Saldanha, 1977); Sun (Dilip Shanghvi, 1983, with five psychiatry products); Dr. Reddy's (Anji Reddy, 1984).
  • 2001
    Cipla offers HIV triple therapy at about $1 a day, transforming its reputation in emerging markets. Dr. Reddy's wins a 180-day exclusivity on fluoxetine, proving the US Para IV model.
  • 2005
    India adopts product patents under TRIPS; reverse-engineering of new drugs ends. Growth shifts to the US.
  • 2005–2013
    US gold rush: Para IV and first-to-file filings; Sun takes control of Taro (2010); new-drug programmes at Glenmark, Dr. Reddy's and Zydus. Zydus's saroglitazar (2013) is India's first home-grown NCE.
  • 2008–2015
    The Ranbaxy saga: Daiichi buys Ranbaxy (2008), import alerts follow, a $500m US plea (2013). Sun buys Ranbaxy for about $4 bn in stock (2014–15), becoming India #1 and inheriting its compliance problems.
  • 2013–2019
    Compliance crackdown: Dr. Reddy's warning letter on three sites (Nov 2015), Sun Halol (OAI 2014, warning letter Dec 2015), Lupin Goa and Indore (2017), Zydus Moraiya (2019), Glenmark Goa (2019). Approvals freeze for one to three years at each.
  • 2015–2019
    Price-erosion bust: US buyers consolidate into three groups; oral-solid prices fall 10–15% a year in 2017–18; Nifty Pharma falls 40–50% from its April 2015 peak. Lupin's margin collapses; 2015–16 US acquisitions under-earn. Ilumya approved (Mar 2018).
  • 2019–2021
    Restructuring and COVID: Lupin exits Japan; Zydus Wellness buys Heinz India; Dr. Reddy's exits Promius; Zydus's ZyCoV-D becomes the first DNA vaccine; Indian pharma re-rates.
  • Mar 2022 – Jan 2026
    The gRevlimid windfall. Sun's Halol plant gets a warning letter and import alert (Dec 2022). Margins and ROCE at Dr. Reddy's, Cipla and Zydus peak in FY24–25.
  • 2023–2025
    Specialty and diversification build: Concert, Taro buyout, Checkpoint (Sun); NRT (Dr. Reddy's); GLS sale and the AbbVie deal (Glenmark); Lupin's complex US surge; Amplitude and Agenus plants (Zydus); VISUfarma (Lupin).
  • 2026
    The hinge: gRevlimid full entry (31 Jan); semaglutide generic in India, Brazil, Canada; Section 232 tariffs (2 Apr); Sun–Organon (26 Apr); Zydus–Assertio (May); Zydus Baddi warning letter (2 Jun); Sun MFN deal (2 Sep).

Indian pharma has been through this before. Every few years the American market either hands the majors a windfall or takes one away, and every few years the FDA finds problems at someone's plant. The companies that do best are the ones that use the good years to build something that does not depend on the next good year. The history suggests the 2026 cycle will be judged the same way.

Reading a diversified major: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twelve numbers explain almost everything about a multi-engine drugmaker. The trick is knowing which engine each one describes, and where the companies define it differently.

A diversified major publishes dozens of numbers every quarter. Most analysis goes wrong because it treats the company as one business and reads a group margin or a group growth rate as if it described all of it. This section starts with the equation that ties the engines together, works through it in rupees, and then takes each of the native KPIs in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example, what moves it, what good looks like, how the six compare, and the definition trap that catches people out.

The equation, and who controls which term

Group revenue is the sum of the engines. Each engine has its own driver. India grows with field-force productivity and new launches; the US with launches, erosion, limited-competition windows and the exchange rate; emerging markets with local growth and currencies; Europe and consumer often with acquisitions; licensing with deal timing. Group EBITDA is each engine's revenue times its contribution margin, minus the shared costs of R&D and the corporate centre. And value, the number shareholders care about, is each engine's profit times the multiple the market gives that kind of profit, plus net cash and whatever the pipeline is worth.

Three lessons follow. First, the loss of one $200 million limited-competition product, about ₹1,400 crore of contribution, outweighs the combined growth of India, emerging markets, Europe, specialty and complex launches. Third, specialty takes years to matter: a $50 million increase in a launch-phase brand adds only about ₹160 crore of contribution.

In plain terms

A major's profit is a weighted sum, and the weights are very uneven. One exceptional product can carry more profit than five ordinary engines put together. When that product ends, the rest of the business can grow nicely and the company still reports lower profit. Always ask which engine a change in profit came from before judging it.

KPI 1 · US quarterly run-rate, in dollars

What it is. The latest quarter's US (or North America) sales, expressed in dollars, read as an annual pace. Formula: quarterly US sales in $ × 4, compared with the same quarter last year in $. Worked example: Dr. Reddy's reported $236 million for Q1 FY27; × 4 = $944 million a year, against FY26 of about $1.3 billion and a Q1 FY26 quarter of about $400 million. In rupees the same quarter looked better because the rupee had weakened by 6–8%.

What moves it. Up: new launches, especially first-to-file and complex products; shortages that let generic makers raise prices; specialty brand growth. Down: price erosion on the base portfolio (4–10% a year), competition arriving in a limited-competition product, FDA actions that stop shipments, and exclusivity roll-offs. Benchmarks: good is at least $350 million a quarter and growing; average $150–350 million; weak is below $150 million or falling more than 15% a year. How the six compare: Sun $427 million (−9.7%), Lupin $366 million (+30% constant currency), Zydus $327 million (−2.6%), Dr. Reddy's $236 million (−41%), Cipla $162 million (−28%), Glenmark about $99 million core (+19.8%).

KPI 5 · EBITDA margin: reported, core and ex-windfall

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, amortisation, interest and tax as a share of revenue. For the majors it must be read three ways: as reported; excluding one-offs (licensing income, shelf-stock adjustments, provisions); and excluding the current windfall product. Formula: EBITDA ÷ revenue; core margin = (EBITDA − one-off income + one-off charges) ÷ (revenue − one-off revenue). Worked example: Glenmark's Q1 FY27 EBITDA was ₹805 crore on ₹4,018 crore of revenue, 20.0%. Dr. Reddy's Q1 FY27 was 12.5% reported; adding back the ₹240 crore semaglutide provision gives 15.4%.

What moves it: mix above all (a windfall product can add 5–6 points; a specialty launch year can subtract 2–3), plus price erosion, input costs (the 2026 solvent and freight shock cost Cipla 1–2% of revenue), launch spending and currency. Benchmarks: good at or above 27%; average 20–27%; weak below 20%. But read the benchmark through the archetype: 25% is strong for a generics-led diversifier and weak for Sun.

KPI 9 · Launches per year and price erosion

What it is. The number of new US products launched in a year, set against the annual price decline on the existing portfolio. Together they say whether the US base is growing or shrinking. Formula: launches = count of new US products; erosion = like-for-like price change on products sold in both periods. Worked example: a $700 million base portfolio eroding 6% loses $42 million a year; to stand still, launches must add $42 million of first-year sales. At an average $3–5 million a launch for ordinary generics, that is 8–14 launches just to stay flat. Benchmarks: launches: good at least 30 a year, average 15–30, weak below 15; erosion: good below 4%, average 4–7%, weak above 7%. How the six compare: Sun launched 37 US products in FY26; Dr. Reddy's 25 (27 targeted for FY27); Zydus targets 30–40+ (11 in Q1 FY27); Lupin plans 50+ over several years; Glenmark launched 9 in Q1 FY27; Cipla expects about four significant launches in the rest of FY27. On erosion, Zydus uses 4–6% a year and Lupin guides high-single to low-double digits for FY27. Definition trap: few companies disclose erosion directly, and "launches" mixes $1 million products with $100 million ones. Ask management for the value of launches, not just the count.

In plain terms

A US generics portfolio is a leaky bucket. Every year the price of what is already in it drips away by 4–10%, and new launches have to refill it. A company that launches 30 products a year is not necessarily growing; it may just be keeping the bucket level. Growth only comes when a few launches are big and hard to copy.

KPI 10 · FDA status

What it is. Whether any plant that supplies a material share of US sales or US pipeline carries a live warning letter (WL), import alert (IA) or "official action indicated" (OAI) classification. Formula: qualitative; score each key site clean (no action or VAI), minor (483 observations pending), or impaired (OAI, WL or IA). Worked example: Sun's Halol plant has been under import alert since December 2022 and was classed OAI again in September 2025; only products in shortage can ship from it, and new approvals from it are frozen. What moves it: inspections, the company's corrective and preventive action (CAPA) programme, and FDA reinspection timing, which the company does not control. Benchmarks: good is clean; average is one minor site; weak is a key site under WL or IA. How the six compare: weak — Sun (Halol; Mohali and Dadra OAI) and Zydus (Baddi WL, Jarod OAI); average — Cipla (Indore WL, reinspection pending), Dr. Reddy's (biologics site 483), Glenmark (Indore WL; Monroe cleared); average to good — Lupin (Somerset 483 with three observations). Definition trap: a Form 483 is a list of observations at the end of an inspection, not a sanction; an OAI classification or warning letter is. KPI 12 · Return on capital employed

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital (equity plus debt) tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ average capital employed. Worked example: a major earning ₹6,000 crore of EBIT on ₹30,000 crore of capital employed has a 20% ROCE. What moves it: margins, asset turnover, idle cash (which earns little and drags ROCE down), and acquisitions carried at full price with goodwill. Benchmarks: good above 20%; average 13–20%; weak below 13%.

Definition traps, collected

  • "US" means different things. Sun's ~$1.9 billion is total US including specialty; Dr. Reddy's reports North America (with Canada); Glenmark's North America includes licensing income. Never add Sun's US specialty to its US total.
  • Rupee growth is not dollar growth. With the rupee at ₹92–95 in Q1 FY27 against ₹87.5 in FY26, rupee growth flatters US performance by 8–10 points.
  • Three EBITDA numbers. Screener, company-reported and company-adjusted can differ by 3–4 points (Dr. Reddy's FY26: 19.2% vs 22.8%).
  • Licensing income is revenue but not recurring. Glenmark's AbbVie money is about 40% booked in FY26 and the rest deferred over several years; both reported growth and margin are inflated.
  • Shelf-stock adjustments sit inside revenue. Dr. Reddy's ₹453 crore SSA in Q4 FY26 reduced reported revenue, not costs.
  • Primary vs secondary India growth. Company numbers are sales to distributors; IPM data is sales to chemists.
  • Segment shares in primers can be stale. Dr. Reddy's North America was 33.9% of FY26 revenue, not 38%; Zydus's consumer segment 14.6%, not ~20%.
  • Gross-margin proxies are not company figures. Screener's raw-material proxy overstates gross margin by 10 points or more for some companies.
  • Net cash depends on what counts as cash. Strategic investments and bank deposits move the figure by thousands of crores.
  • A 483 is not a warning letter. Observations are a step; OAI, WL and import alerts are sanctions.
  • Exceptional items are frequent. Glenmark's FY26 profit was struck after ₹2,266 crore of exceptionals; Dr. Reddy's after ₹228 crore of impairments and the SSA; Sun's Q1 FY27 after ₹162 crore of Organon costs. Trailing P/E ratios mix these in.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading a major come from comparing numbers that look alike but are not: a rupee number with a dollar number, a company margin with a screener margin, a primary sale with a secondary sale, a one-off with a recurring item. Before comparing any figure across the six, check that both sides are on the same basis.

How to read a diversified major's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. US sales in dollars, first. Pull the quarter's US or North America figure in $ and compare with the same quarter last year. Note what management says caused the change: launches, erosion, a specific product, or currency. A fall of more than 15% in $ is a red flag unless it was guided.
  2. Strip the one-offs. Find any licensing income, shelf-stock adjustment, inventory provision, litigation charge or deal cost. Recompute revenue growth and EBITDA margin without them. This is the number to compare with guidance.
  3. Check the margin against guidance and against the archetype. Is the underlying EBITDA margin inside the company's own FY27 range (Cipla 18.5–20%, Zydus >24%, Lupin ~25%, Glenmark 21–22% including deferred income)? If not, does management explain why the rest of the year will be better?
  4. India growth versus the IPM. Compare India growth with IQVIA or Pharmarack market growth for the same quarter. Below 0.9× for two quarters is a red flag for the valuation floor.
  5. Specialty and new engines. For Sun, innovative sales in $ and their growth; for Dr. Reddy's, semaglutide pens and biosimilar milestones; for Zydus, consumer and medtech; for Glenmark, core North America excluding licensing; for Lupin, the US after tolvaptan competition.
  6. Gross margin. A drop of more than 2 points without a named one-off usually means mix deterioration (the high-margin product is fading) or input-cost pressure.
  7. The pipeline numbers. Filings, approvals and launches in the quarter; any first-to-file wins; any changes to the count of pending ANDAs. Compare launches with the full-year target.
  8. FDA and regulatory updates. Any new 483, OAI, warning letter, import alert or reinspection. Check whether the affected plant supplies the launches that are supposed to fill the next cliff.
  9. Cash and capital allocation. Net cash or net debt at quarter end, any acquisition or buyback, capex against guidance. A rising net debt without a named deal is a red flag.
  10. Guidance changes and the next cliff date. Did management change any FY27 number? What is the next known exclusivity loss, patent expiry or competitor approval? Red flags: guidance cut without explanation, vaguer language on a key product, or "one-off" charges that recur for a third quarter.
In plain terms

Ten minutes is enough if you go in the right order: US in dollars, then clean out the one-offs, then check India against the market, then look at what is supposed to replace the last windfall. Everything else is detail. The questions that move the share price are nearly always the same: is the hole filling, and is the cash being spent well?

Sum of the parts: what the market is paying for each engine Deep dive

Value each engine at what a pure-play peer would fetch, add the cash, and see what is left. The residual tells you what the market believes about the uncertain engine.

A multi-engine company is hard to value with a single multiple, because its engines deserve very different ones. India branded franchises trade at 20–43 times EBITDA (Torrent 42.7×, Mankind 29.4×, Alkem 20.1×). Global generic majors trade at 6–13 times. Specialty pharma is often valued on sales. The left-over, or residual, is the cleanest way to see what the market is assuming about the engine whose future is most uncertain.

Four things stand out. The Indian six earn similar margins to the global majors at lower selling prices, thanks to lower costs and the India mix. The disruption list

  • GLP-1s reshape cardio-metabolic franchises. Diabetes and cardiac brands are among the majors' largest in India. Weight-loss injections, and later oral GLP-1s (oral semaglutide, orforglipron), could shrink demand for older drugs and commoditise faster than expected.
  • Biosimilar interchangeability and a lighter US approval pathway lower the cost of developing biosimilars. Good for Dr. Reddy's, Zydus and Lupin, but it lowers barriers for everyone else too.
  • AI-assisted discovery and Chinese biotech licensing. Big pharma is sourcing early-stage molecules from Chinese biotechs at low prices. That competes directly with Glenmark-style out-licensing and puts a ceiling on what an Indian innovation arm can charge.
  • Continuous manufacturing and US onshoring erode India's cost advantage at the margin for tariff-sensitive products.
  • Pricing policy: US MFN pricing and Inflation Reduction Act negotiation reaching Part B (Unloxcyt) and Part D drugs; NLEM expansion and trade-generic substitution in India.
  • Unannounced FDA foreign inspections (from May 2025) raise the chance that a plant is found out of compliance without warning.
In plain terms

Compared with the world's big generic companies, the Indian majors are smaller, grow faster, owe less and cost more. The extra price is mostly for the Indian business. The changes to watch are the ones that hit that Indian business (GLP-1s replacing older chronic drugs, price controls) or the cost advantage behind the US one (onshoring, tariffs, surprise inspections).

Bull vs bear, and what to watch

The case for the group rests on durable India and specialty engines bought cheaply with windfall cash. The case against rests on a hole that is not yet filled and deals not yet proven.

The bull case

  • The cliff is mostly behind them. gRevlimid ended in January 2026; Zydus has absorbed its cliffs, Dr. Reddy's and Cipla are in the trough, and FY27–28 comparisons get easier.
  • Durable engines keep compounding. All six grew India at or above the IPM in Q1 FY27; EM and Europe grew double digits; India is ~30% of combined revenue.
  • Many replacement shots. Complex respiratory, peptides, semaglutide outside the US (and in the US from ~2031), biosimilars (abatacept), first-to-files (Dr. Reddy's 22).
  • Cheap assets bought with windfall cash. Organon at ~6× EBITDA; NRT, VISUfarma, Assertio and Amplitude at modest prices; Glenmark monetised research for $700m upfront.
  • Strong balance sheets outside Sun and Zydus, and a tariff regime that exempts generics and biosimilars, with Sun's specialty protected to 2029.
  • Valuation is not stretched for the group at ~17× EV/EBITDA, with Lupin and Dr. Reddy's priced for little US value.

The bear case

  • The hole is not filled. Group US sales fell ~10% in $ and profit ~10% in Q1 FY27; Lupin's own cliff starts this year; replacements are many small products with more competitors.
  • Execution risk is concentrated. Dr. Reddy's semaglutide supply and one biologics site; Sun's innovative growth slowing to 12.8%; Cipla's margin below guide.
  • Deals are unproven and leverage is rising. Sun to ~2.3× net debt/EBITDA; Zydus to ~0.7×; the 2015–16 US acquisitions destroyed value.
  • Policy risk is growing. A possible generics tariff after the April 2027 review; MFN pricing; NLEM expansion in India.
  • FDA overhangs persist at Sun (Halol), Zydus (Baddi, Jarod), Cipla and Glenmark (Indore).
  • GLP-1s are a mixed blessing: India became a price war, and new obesity drugs may erode older chronic franchises.

The balance between these cases differs by company, which is the point of a multi-engine report. For Sun the debate is Organon and the specialty growth rate. For Dr. Reddy's it is three binary events in six months. For Lupin it is how fast erosion arrives. For Cipla it is US launches. For Zydus it is FDA clearance and deal integration. For Glenmark it is whether IGI can produce another deal.
