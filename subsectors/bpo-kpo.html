00

The one-page view: five companies that rent out skilled hours, and a technology that sells the same work by the answer

Fast growth, high margins and a rich multiple, sitting on a pricing model that GenAI is starting to rewrite.

India's listed business-process outsourcers do work that most investors never see: they code a US hospital's claims and chase the ones an insurer refuses to pay, they key in and check a health insurer's claims and answer its members' calls, they collect overdue credit-card debt for British banks, they write and approve the promotional material a drug company sends to doctors, and they reconcile the trades of global investment banks. The client pays in dollars or pounds; the work is done mainly by Indian and Philippine staff paid in rupees and pesos. The gap between the two is the business.

Five companies make up this sub-sector in the NSE 750. Firstsource Solutions (₹9,556 Cr of FY26 revenue) is the diversified customer-experience and back-office operator, split roughly evenly between US healthcare, banking and financial services, and telecom, media and retail. Sagility (₹7,193 Cr) runs claims, enrolment, member service and payment-integrity work for US health insurers. eClerx (₹4,117 Cr of operating revenue) is a knowledge-process shop for investment banks, cable operators and consumer brands. Indegene (₹3,510 Cr) produces and runs the commercial and medical content of global drug makers. IKS Health (Inventurus, ₹3,194 Cr) runs the revenue cycle and clinical documentation of US doctors and health systems, and in July 2026 bought TruBridge, a US software and billing company for rural hospitals.

Together they reported ₹27,570 crore of revenue in FY26, up 23.0%, an operating profit (EBITDA excluding other income) of ₹6,126 crore, a 22.2% margin, and reported net profit of ₹3,428 crore, up 33.5%. At the 21 October 2026 close the market valued them at ₹1,00,990 crore, 29.5 times FY26 profit. That is a premium multiple for services companies: the large global outsourcers trade at a fraction of it. The question this report answers is whether the premium is earned, and what could take it away.

Five forces explain almost everything that happens to these stocks, and the report is built around them.

  • GenAI is repricing the "seat". Most of this industry has historically been billed per full-time equivalent (FTE): the client pays for a person-hour. AI agents now sell customer-service resolutions for between $0.50 and $2.00 each, and global voice-centric outsourcers such as Concentrix and Teleperformance are shrinking. Whether a vendor is paid per person, per transaction or per outcome decides whether AI is a cost-saver it keeps or a price cut it gives away (section 07).
  • US healthcare administration is the profit pool, and US health policy is the macro variable. Roughly half of the group's revenue comes from running the paperwork of American insurers and hospitals; adding Indegene's drug-industry work takes it to about 62%. Medicare Advantage rates, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's Medicaid cuts and drug-pricing deals matter more to these companies than Indian GDP (section 08).
  • The rupee is a margin tailwind that has partly been sold forward. The rupee averaged about ₹88.3 to the dollar in FY26 and stands at ₹95.0 today. But eClerx has hedged its FY27 dollars at ₹91.16 and Firstsource its next twelve months at ₹92.7, so the ₹ growth rate (26% in Q1 FY27) is roughly double the constant-currency rate (12–15%), and the profit benefit arrives late (section 09).
  • Roll-ups, goodwill and client concentration define the risk. Sagility carries ₹6,405 crore of goodwill from its carve-out from Hinduja Global Solutions; Firstsource's goodwill equals about 97% of its equity; IKS has bought AQuity and TruBridge; Indegene spent about ₹1,015 crore on three agencies in FY26. Sagility's three largest clients are 60% of revenue, and Indegene's largest alone is 31% (section 10).
  • Who owns the stock, and a new kind of US policy risk. Three of the five listed in 2024 largely through private-equity and pre-IPO investors selling shares. EQT still holds 50.95% of Sagility. Meanwhile, the US risk has shifted from a stalled 25% outsourcing tax (the HIRE Act) to a concrete proposal from the Federal Communications Commission to cap the share of telecom customer-service calls handled offshore (sections 11 and 12).

IKS earns 12% of the group's revenue but carries 28% of its market value, because it combines the highest margin (34%) with outcome-linked pricing and a stated goal of ₹3,000 crore of EBITDA by FY30. In plain terms

These companies sell skilled hours of Indian and Philippine staff to American and British organisations. For twenty years that was a simple, steady business: more clients meant more staff and more revenue. AI agents can now do part of that work for a fee per task, so the vendors that will do well are the ones paid for results rather than for headcount, and the ones whose work needs medical, regulatory or financial knowledge that a general AI tool lacks. The weak rupee is helping profits, but less than it seems, because much of it has been locked in at older rates.

Nothing in this sub-sector is cyclical in the way steel or cement is. These stocks fall on technology scares (the 2023 GenAI sell-off), on client-specific shocks (Firstsource lost 10–17% on 7 August 2026 after a contract termination and a regulatory indemnity charge) and on supply of stock from private-equity sellers. They rise on delivered growth and margin. The next test arrives within weeks: Sagility reports Q2 FY27 on 22 October 2026, Indegene on 29 October, and IKS, Firstsource and eClerx must report by 14 November. IKS's numbers will be the first to include TruBridge, so its reported revenue will roughly double from one quarter to the next, its margin will dilute and its balance sheet will move from about ₹265 crore of net debt to an indicative ₹5,000–5,500 crore.

01

How the money is made: billing a foreign client for work done in India

Five value chains, one economic engine: a dollar price for a rupee cost, protected by domain knowledge and compliance.

Business-process outsourcing (BPO, or business-process management, BPM, in industry jargon) means running a defined piece of a client's operations: answering calls, processing claims, collecting debts. Knowledge-process outsourcing (KPO) is the same contract structure applied to work that needs judgment and specialist training: medical coding, clinical documentation, regulatory writing, trade reconciliation, analytics. The five members sit along that spectrum, from Firstsource's contact centres at one end to Indegene's medical writers and IKS's certified coders at the other.

The economic engine is identical for all five. A client in the US or UK hands over a process. The vendor transitions it (often taking over the client's staff, systems and procedures), then delivers it from lower-cost locations: India for most, the Philippines for voice and some healthcare work, and nearshore centres in Mexico, Jamaica, Colombia, South Africa or Romania for clients who want the same time zone or accent. The vendor bills in dollars or pounds and pays most of its staff in rupees or pesos. An Indian claims processor or coder might cost a vendor ₹6–15 lakh a year all-in, against $45,000–90,000 for a US employee doing the same job (indicative ranges). That gap is the arbitrage, and everything else is about how much of it the vendor keeps.

The vendor that owns the whole chain, and increasingly the software it runs on (IKS now owns an electronic health record through TruBridge), controls the data and the outcome metric, so it can price on results.

On the other side of the same transaction sits the insurer. Sagility's clients are US health plans, mostly large Blue Cross Blue Shield plans and national Medicare Advantage insurers. A plan collects premiums, enrols members, receives claims from hospitals and doctors, adjudicates them (most automatically, the rest manually), pays them, and then checks for overpayment and fraud. The outsourcer's work is the manual queue, the member and provider call centre, the enrolment and eligibility updates, and payment integrity. The reason this exists at scale is a US rule: under the Affordable Care Act's medical-loss-ratio requirement, insurers must spend at least 80–85% of premium on medical care and quality, leaving 15–20 cents of each premium dollar for administration and profit. Every administrative dollar saved is a dollar of insurer profit, so plans push their paperwork to the cheapest competent vendor.

The other three value chains follow the same pattern with different clients. Firstsource's banking work runs from mortgage origination support and servicing to early- and late-stage collections for UK and US banks; its telecom and media work is mainly customer service and sales for UK and US providers. Indegene's chain starts with a drug's brand plan and ends with thousands of promotional and medical assets per brand, each passing through medical-legal-regulatory review before it reaches doctors through email, web and sales-rep channels; in parallel it processes adverse-event reports for pharmacovigilance. eClerx's capital-markets work begins after a trade is executed: confirmation, settlement, reconciling breaks between systems, maintaining reference data and producing regulatory reports, usually inside the client's own platforms.

Where the value is captured matters more than the service list. Labour arbitrage is the base layer and the most contestable: any vendor can hire in Pune or Manila. Domain knowledge and compliance form the second layer: HIPAA privacy rules for patient data, payer and regulator audits, pharmaceutical promotional-review rules and bank operational-risk standards all create switching costs, which is why Firstsource's top five clients have stayed for more than 22 years on average. Technology and platforms are the third layer, and the one that explains the margin spread: IKS (34% operating margin) and eClerx (26%) run their own workflow platforms and automation, while a contact-centre operator competing on hourly rates earns about half that. Outcome ownership, where the vendor is paid a share of the money it collects or saves, is the highest-value layer, and it is the layer AI makes more attractive.

In plain terms

Half of this group's revenue comes from doing the paperwork of the American health system, on both sides of the bill: IKS helps doctors get paid, Sagility helps insurers decide what to pay. Another eighth comes from drug companies. That makes US healthcare rules and budgets the most important outside force on these companies, far more than anything in the Indian economy.

Three features of the contracts shape everything that follows. First, they are long: master service agreements typically run three to five years with statements of work renewed annually, and the work is embedded in the client's systems, so churn is low but price is renegotiated at each renewal. Second, the billing unit varies. FTE billing (a rate per person-hour) still dominates payer operations and voice work; transaction billing (a price per claim, per chart or per asset) is common in coding, documentation and content; outcome billing (a percentage of collections, recoveries or savings) is common in RCM and payment integrity. Indegene says about 60% of its revenue is "outcome-aligned"; IKS's RCM is heavily collections-linked; none of the five discloses its full pricing mix. Third, the client usually expects an annual productivity give-back, a small price reduction or efficiency commitment each year, which is why volume growth and new services, not price, drive revenue. Sagility's management said in July 2026 that pricing is moving "in the opposite direction" as insurers are squeezed.

02

Size, growth and share: a $59 billion industry in which the listed five hold about 5%

The listed specialists are growing two to three times as fast as India's BPM industry, while the voice-heavy Philippine industry has cut its targets.

India's technology industry earned about $315 billion in FY26, up 6.1% on a restated FY25 of $297 billion, according to NASSCOM's Strategic Review 2026. Business-process management was $59 billion of that, up about 7% from $55 billion, roughly 19% of the industry. In rupees at the FY26 average of about ₹88.3 to the dollar, Indian BPM is a ₹5.2 lakh crore industry. The five listed members, with about $3.1 billion of combined FY26 revenue, are roughly 5% of it.

The listed five are a small, unusual slice of that industry. Most Indian BPM revenue sits elsewhere: with the global capability centres (GCCs) that multinationals run for themselves, with the BPM arms of the IT majors (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra), with multinational BPM companies that deliver from India (Genpact, EXL, WNS, now owned by Capgemini, Concentrix, Teleperformance, Conduent, Sutherland), and with private-equity-owned healthcare specialists (Omega, Access, AGS, GeBBS, Ventra). The listed Indian names are specialists: four of the five are concentrated in one or two verticals, and three of them did not exist as listed companies before 2024.

Their growth has been well above the industry's. Combined revenue grew from about ₹11,400 crore in FY22 to ₹27,570 crore in FY26, a compound rate of about 25% a year. That number needs three adjustments. Sagility's FY22 includes only eight months after its carve-out from Hinduja Global Solutions; IKS consolidated AQuity from October 2023; and the rupee fell from about ₹74 to ₹88 to the dollar over the period. Even so, constant-currency or dollar growth in FY26 was 23.6% at Sagility, 17.9% at eClerx, about 18% at Indegene, 13.6% at Firstsource and roughly 13–15% at IKS, two to three times the industry's 7%. The listed specialists are taking share.

The captive centres are the biggest structural fact in the industry. NASSCOM and Zinnov count 2,117 GCCs in India with about 3,728 units and roughly 2.36 million employees, earning an estimated $98.4 billion in FY26, up from $64.6 billion in FY24. That figure includes IT and engineering work, not only business processes, but it shows that multinationals increasingly prefer to own their Indian operations. For the listed vendors this cuts both ways. A bank or insurer that builds its own centre in Hyderabad is work lost to outsourcing; but GCCs also buy specialist services (a captive may contract eClerx for a reconciliation tower or Indegene for medical writing) and use vendors to build and run units they later take over.

The Philippine revision is the clearest official evidence of what AI is doing to voice work. The Philippines' IT-BPM industry, about $40 billion and 1.9 million workers in 2025, is dominated by contact centres. In September 2026 its industry association cut its 2028 target from $59 billion and 2.5 million jobs to a best case of $50.5 billion and 2.14 million, and a downside of $43.3 billion and 1.85 million. India's BPM mix is more back-office and knowledge work, which helps explain why its growth (7%) is holding up better, and why the Indian listed specialists, who do even less pure voice work, are growing faster still.

In plain terms

The five listed companies are small fish in a big pond, which is good news: they can keep growing fast simply by winning work from others, without needing the whole industry to boom. The bad news is that the pond itself is being reshaped. Big companies increasingly run their own Indian offices, and call-centre work is being automated, so the listed firms have to stay in the kinds of work that are hard to automate and hard to take in-house.

Looking forward, the members' own guidance points to low-double-digit constant-currency growth in FY27: Firstsource 10–13%, Sagility "low double digit" organic, IKS's legacy business "faster than 12%", Indegene better than FY26 organically, and eClerx in the "top quartile" of its peers. Reported rupee growth will be higher because of TruBridge (roughly $300 million of annual revenue joining IKS from July 2026), Indegene's acquisitions and a rupee that is about 7% weaker than its FY26 average. An INDICATIVE FY27 range for combined revenue is ₹33,000–35,000 crore, with organic constant-currency growth of perhaps 11–14%; this is an estimate built from guidance, not a forecast any company has made.

Market sizes for the end-markets these firms serve come from third parties and should be read as INDICATIVE. IKS's investor deck, citing Zinnov, puts US healthcare spending at about $5 trillion, the addressable pool of provider-side services at about $260 billion, and the outsourced part at about $34 billion in calendar 2023, growing 8–12% a year. Indegene's deck sizes life-sciences operations spend at more than $135 billion, of which marketing and sales operations are about $55 billion, regulatory and medical affairs $24 billion and pharmacovigilance $21 billion, with outsourcing growing 9–14% a year. Even the largest member is a low single-digit share of its addressable pool, so none of them is constrained by market size; they are constrained by their ability to win and deliver work.

03

Who buys, and why now: insurers under cost pressure, drug makers under price pressure, banks under regulation

Demand for outsourcing rises when clients' margins are squeezed, which is why bad news for clients is often good news for vendors, until it is not.

Outsourcing demand has an unusual relationship with the client's business cycle. When a US insurer's medical costs rise faster than premiums, or a drug maker's US prices are cut, the first response is to cut administrative cost, and the cheapest way to do that is to move more work offshore. Indegene's chief executive put it bluntly: "Whenever the pharma industry is under pressure, those times are better for us." The limit is that a squeezed client also pushes harder on price, delays new programmes and occasionally cancels one, as Firstsource discovered in Q1 FY27.

US health insurers: three years of margin pain, and a better 2027

US health insurers have had a hard three years. Medical cost trends, especially in Medicare Advantage (MA), the private alternative to traditional Medicare that now covers about half of Medicare beneficiaries, outran the rates the government paid them. The effect on outsourcers was two-sided: insurers pushed more work to cheaper vendors, which helped Sagility grow 23.6% in constant currency in FY26, but they also cut scope where they could. Firstsource said in August 2026 that several MA clients had "recalibrated program scope" after rate and utilisation-management changes, and one transformational payer engagement was wound down after a leadership change at the client.

The policy signal for 2027 is better. In its final rate notice of 6 April 2026, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) raised MA payments for 2027 by 2.48% on average, more than $13 billion, against just 0.09% proposed in the January advance notice; including the trend in risk scores, plans get roughly 5%. CMS also kept its existing risk-adjustment model rather than recalibrating it, but excluded diagnoses from "unlinked" chart reviews, which trims one source of coding-driven revenue for insurers. Insurer shares rallied on the day. For Sagility, whose three largest clients are national and Blue plans, a healthier 2027 should loosen administrative budgets from calendar 2027, which falls in FY28 for the Indian vendors.

Medicaid and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act: more verification work, poorer hospitals

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed on 4 July 2025, is the largest change to US Medicaid in a decade. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that its health provisions will leave 11.8 million more people uninsured by 2034 and cut federal Medicaid spending by about $1 trillion over ten years. From 31 December 2026, expansion adults must show 80 hours a month of work or qualifying activity, and eligibility must be re-verified every six months. Provider taxes, which states use to fund their share of Medicaid, are capped more tightly from FY2028, and state-directed payments are cut. A $50 billion rural health fund cushions part of the blow over five years.

For this sub-sector OBBBA cuts both ways. Payer operations gain work: more frequent eligibility checks, more redeterminations, more appeals, more member calls, all of which Sagility and Firstsource's payer teams process. Provider RCM gains complexity, since a hospital needs more front-end eligibility checking and more denial management. But uncompensated care rises as people lose coverage, and the hospitals hit hardest are rural and community hospitals, which are exactly TruBridge's customers. IKS has bought a franchise whose clients face a funding squeeze, at the same moment that squeeze makes outsourcing more attractive to them. Firstsource's management summarised the effect on providers in August 2026: "transactional volume is increasingly being repriced as a technology service."

In plain terms

US health policy decides how much money flows through the system and how many forms have to be filled in to move it. More rules and tighter budgets mean more paperwork for these vendors to do, which helps volumes. But if their clients, especially small rural hospitals, are short of money, they will push for lower prices, and some may not survive. The 2027 Medicare Advantage rate increase is good news for insurers and their vendors; the Medicaid cuts are mixed news.

Drug makers: price cuts, tariffs and the shift from sales reps to digital

Indegene's demand comes from the commercial and medical budgets of global drug makers. Three forces are at work. The first is structural: about 60% of US physicians now restrict access for sales representatives, each of whom costs a company $250,000–300,000 a year, so promotion is shifting to digital channels that need far more content. The second is pricing pressure: by 31 August 2026 the US government had signed 26 "most-favoured-nation" pricing agreements covering about 89% of the branded drug market, Medicare's negotiated prices for the first ten drugs took effect in January 2026, and Section 232 tariffs of up to 100% on imported patented drugs took effect for large makers on 31 July and all branded makers on 29 September 2026 (companies with MFN deals are exempt). The third is a patent cliff that forces cost programmes across the industry.

Price pressure squeezes promotional budgets, but it also pushes drug makers to replace in-house teams and expensive agencies with a lower-cost, technology-heavy vendor, which is Indegene's pitch. Its management reported that eight of the top 25 biopharma companies grew by double digits in Q1 and that biopharma M&A exceeded $100 billion in the first half of 2026, both supportive. No hard data on 2026 promotional budgets was found, so the net effect remains a judgement call rather than a number.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: people first, then everything else

Two-thirds of the cost base walks out of the door every evening, which makes wages, attrition and the rupee the three numbers that move margins.

The inputs are people, seats (desks in leased buildings), telephony and software, and the sales and management overhead needed to win and run contracts. The single largest difference between the volume model and the others is onshore cost. Wages: a 6–9% annual drift, and a new regulatory shock

Indian BPM wage increments typically run at 6–9% a year, and the companies absorb them in the first quarter of the fiscal year (April–June), which is why Q1 margins usually dip. eClerx said its Q1 wage hit is typically 300–350 basis points, recovered over the year through operating leverage; its Q1 FY27 operating EBITDA margin fell to 23.0% from 25.7% in Q4. In FY27 a new kind of wage shock appeared: statutory minimum-wage increases in Karnataka (from 22 May 2026) and Telangana (from 1 June 2026). With 65–70% of its staff in Bengaluru and Hyderabad, Sagility says the increases, plus the pay-parity raises they trigger above the minimum, will cost about 120 basis points of FY27 margin, and that mitigation through new sites in Indore and Tamil Nadu will take 12–18 months. In plain terms

A BPM company's costs are mostly salaries. If Indian wages rise 8% and prices stay flat, margins shrink unless the company gets more done per person. 07

GenAI and the seat: when the client can buy the answer instead of the hour Deep dive

The same productivity gain is a 40% revenue cut, a margin windfall or a revenue increase, depending only on how the contract is written.

For twenty-five years, Indian BPM grew in a straight line with headcount. A client handed over a process, the vendor staffed it, and revenue rose roughly one-for-one with people. Generative and "agentic" AI (software agents that can read a document, take an action in a system and escalate exceptions) break that link. They can draft a clinical note, code a chart, answer a member's question, resolve a simple claim or reconcile a trade break at a fraction of the cost of a person. The question is not whether that happens; it is who keeps the savings.

The pricing physics

Three contract structures exist side by side in this industry, and AI affects each differently. Under FTE pricing, the client pays a rate per person-hour, so revenue is proportional to headcount: a 30% productivity gain becomes, at the next renewal, a 30% revenue cut unless the vendor finds new scope. Under transaction pricing, the client pays per claim, per chart or per call, so revenue tracks volume: the vendor keeps any productivity gain until the client renegotiates the unit price. Under outcome pricing, the client pays a share of money collected, recovered or saved: the vendor keeps the productivity gain and also earns more if AI improves the result.

Sagility is mostly priced per FTE or per transaction, but its payment-integrity work is contingency-based and it owns BirchAI, a conversational-AI company. Firstsource has made AI its corporate identity: UnBPO (February 2025), an Agentic AI Studio, a stake in Lyzr.ai and the "Intelligence That Operates" platform with its Kairos agents; it says voice-AI agents are now standard in its eligibility and early-out work. Indegene argues that GenAI deflates content prices but lets a scaled, tech-heavy vendor take share from agencies; its revenue per employee rose from $67.5k to $77.1k in a year. eClerx won its first large agentic-AI deal (a hi-tech client) in Q4 FY26, runs its Roboworx CogniFlows tools internally and has trained more than 3,000 staff on agentic AI.

The clearest early signal of who is winning will not be in the AI announcements but in three numbers: revenue per employee at stable margins, headcount growth against constant-currency growth, and the share of revenue that is not priced per FTE. A vendor whose headcount keeps growing in line with revenue is not yet capturing AI productivity; one whose revenue falls at renewal while headcount falls with it is giving the productivity away. The first evidence of the second outcome in this group was Firstsource's comment in August 2026 that provider transactional volume is "increasingly being repriced as a technology service".

In plain terms

The worry is real but uneven. Simple call-centre work is already getting cheaper as software does it, and the global companies that rely on it are shrinking. Most of what these five Indian companies do is more specialised and regulated, and so far they are still growing at 12–15%. The proof that they are winning would be revenue rising faster than staff numbers while margins hold; that has just begun to show at IKS and eClerx and has not yet shown at Sagility.

US healthcare admin: getting paid versus deciding what to pay Deep dive

Half the group's revenue comes from the administrative machinery of American healthcare, on both sides of the same claim.

The United States spends about $5 trillion a year on healthcare, and a large slice of that goes on administration: deciding who is covered, documenting what was done, coding it, billing it, disputing it and paying it. Estimates of total administrative cost run to roughly a trillion dollars a year (an indicative figure without a single authoritative source). The CAQH Index, which tracks only automatable transactions such as eligibility checks and claim status, found that electronic processing avoided $258 billion of cost in 2024 and that a further $21 billion of savings remains available. Indian vendors live in the gap between what the system could automate and what it actually does by hand.

Two sides of the same claim

On the provider side, a hospital or physician group is trying to get money in. IKS's job, and that of Firstsource's provider-RCM unit, is to document the visit, code it correctly, submit a clean claim, fight denials and collect the cash. The metrics are the provider's: denial rate, days in accounts receivable, net collection rate. Provider vendors are hurt when their clients' finances worsen (Medicaid cuts, lower reimbursement, site-neutral payment) and helped by complexity: every new prior-authorisation rule or denial algorithm creates more work.

On the payer side, an insurer is trying to control money going out. Sagility's job, and that of Firstsource's payer unit, is to enrol members, adjudicate claims accurately, answer member and provider queries, run utilisation management and find overpayments. The metrics are the insurer's: administrative cost per member, claims accuracy, auto-adjudication rate, savings recovered. Payer vendors are driven by insurer margins, enrolment volumes in Medicare Advantage and Medicaid, and the medical-loss-ratio rule that caps how much of each premium dollar insurers can keep for administration and profit.

The provider side: IKS, TruBridge and rural America

IKS's core clients are large physician enterprises and integrated health systems. The rupee and the hedge book: a tailwind that has already been sold forward Deep dive

A 7% weaker rupee should have lifted margins by several points; instead margins fell, and the hedge books explain part of why.

Every member bills mostly in dollars (Firstsource also in pounds, Indegene partly in euros) and pays most of its costs in rupees and pesos. When the rupee weakens, the same invoice converts into more rupees while most costs stay put, so margins rise. The rupee averaged about ₹88.3 to the dollar in FY26, the members' Q1 FY27 revenue implies about ₹94.5, and it stood at ₹95.0 on 21 October 2026, about 1.6% off its record closing low of ₹96.53 on 19 May 2026. On paper this is a large tailwind. In practice much of it has been sold forward, absorbed by wages, or passed back to clients.

The mechanics

Take a vendor whose revenue is all in dollars and whose costs are 75% of revenue, with about a fifth of those costs in dollars (onshore staff, US software) and the rest in rupees. A 1% fall in the rupee raises rupee revenue by 1% and rupee costs by only about 0.15–0.2% (the dollar-denominated part), so EBITDA rises by roughly 0.8% of revenue, about 0.4–0.6 percentage points of margin once one allows for the costs that also rise with the rupee indirectly. Across a 7% move, that is several points of margin before hedges. A useful rule of thumb from Indian IT services is 25–35 basis points of margin per 1% of rupee depreciation; BPM, with a higher rupee cost share, sits at the upper end or above (INDICATIVE).

Why the hedge books matter so much now

Indian outsourcers hedge to protect margins from a strengthening rupee, typically selling forward 50–80% of the next 12–24 months of expected net dollar inflows. When the rupee falls sharply, those contracts lose money against spot, and the companies realise the loss as the contracts mature. eClerx says about 80% of its dollar receivables are hedged and that "most of our hedge book is ~₹89 for FY27". In plain terms

A cheaper rupee should make these companies more profitable, because they earn dollars and pay rupees. Three implications follow for reading results. First, compare constant-currency growth, never rupee growth, across companies and over time. Second, check where each company books its hedge result before comparing margins; eClerx's operating revenue and Firstsource's revenue are depressed by hedge losses, Sagility's adjusted EBITDA is not. Roll-ups, goodwill and concentration: how the cohort was built and where it is fragile Deep dive

Acquisitions are now the fastest source of growth; they leave behind goodwill, earn-outs, amortisation and, often, a client book dominated by a few names.

Three of the five members were assembled by acquisition, and a fourth is buying at pace. Sagility is a carve-out from Hinduja Global Solutions, topped up with bolt-ons. IKS doubled its size with AQuity in 2023 and is doubling again with TruBridge. Indegene spent about ₹1,015 crore on three agencies in FY26. Firstsource has bought something almost every year since 2022. Only eClerx has grown mainly organically, returning cash instead. Acquisitions bring growth, capabilities and clients; they also bring goodwill that can be impaired, intangibles that depress reported profit for years, earn-outs that create surprise charges, and debt.

Sagility: a carve-out that will amortise until FY32

Sagility carries goodwill of ₹6,405 crore at March 2026: ₹5,170 crore from the HGS carve-out, ₹489 crore from the Devlin, BirchAI and BroadPath bolt-ons, and ₹746 crore of currency translation (goodwill on a dollar-denominated business rises in rupees as the rupee falls). On top sit ₹2,041 crore of other intangibles, against equity of ₹9,659 crore. The go-forward schedule in its deck shows carve-out intangibles amortising at about ₹156.5 crore a year from FY27 to FY32, plus acquisition intangibles of ₹47.3 crore in FY27 falling to ₹25 crore by FY32. That is why its reported FY26 profit (₹925 crore) was 18% below its adjusted profit (₹1,131 crore), and why the gap will persist until FY32, not FY30 as earlier reports implied. Its reported return on capital of 13.4% reflects the purchase price; on an "adjusted" basis that excludes goodwill and intangibles, the company reports about 59%.

In plain terms

Much of this sector's growth has been bought. Who owns the stock: private equity on the way out, founders and DIIs on the way in

The 2024 IPO cohort was mostly an exit for existing holders; how fast the rest comes to market sets the supply of shares.

The three 2024 listings raised little or no money for the companies. Indegene raised ₹760 crore of fresh capital alongside an offer for sale; Sagility (about ₹2,100 crore) and IKS (₹2,498 crore at ₹1,329 a share) were 100% offers for sale. That matters because the remaining stakes are large, and each sale adds supply of stock at the moment the market is deciding what these businesses are worth.

Sagility's promoter is Sagility B.V., controlled by EQT, the Swedish private-equity firm that absorbed Baring Private Equity Asia in 2022. At listing in November 2024 it held 82.39%. Rules from three capitals: Washington sets demand, Bengaluru sets costs, Brussels sets data rules

The biggest regulatory risks come from where the clients are, not from India; the newest one is a proposed US cap on offshore customer-service calls.

Indian regulation of this industry is light: there is no licence, no price control and no production incentive scheme. Exports of services are zero-rated for GST, most profits are taxed at the 25.2% new-regime rate, and transfer-pricing safe harbours fix the margin Indian subsidiaries earn on cost. The rules that move these companies' revenue and costs are written in Washington, London and Brussels, with one important exception: Indian state minimum-wage and labour law, which in FY27 has started to bite.

The FCC proposal: the first concrete onshoring rule

For two years the political risk most often cited for Indian outsourcers was the HIRE Act, a bill from Senator Bernie Moreno that would impose a 25% excise tax on payments to foreign service providers. It has gone nowhere: no co-sponsors, no hearing, a blocked unanimous-consent attempt, and an estimated near-zero chance of passage. The H-1B fee, which mattered more to IT services than to BPM, was vacated by a federal court in June 2026. The policy risk that has actually advanced is narrower and more concrete. On 27 March 2026 the Federal Communications Commission released a notice of proposed rulemaking that would limit the share of customer-service calls telecom, wireless, VoIP, cable and satellite-TV providers can handle offshore, with 30% given as an illustrative cap, and would require offshore agents to meet an English-proficiency standard, disclose their country, transfer callers to a US agent on request and keep sensitive data such as Social Security and card numbers onshore. The FCC also asked whether the rules should extend to every call covered by the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which would reach far beyond telecom.

The direct exposure in this group is limited. Firstsource's communications, media and technology vertical is about 21% of revenue, but much of it serves UK clients, outside the rule's reach. eClerx's CMT vertical is about 26%, but its cable and telecom work is largely back-office provisioning and order management rather than customer calls; its chief executive nevertheless named the proposal as a risk on the May 2026 call, and NASSCOM is lobbying against it. The wider significance is that the proposal targets exactly the voice segment that AI is already deflating, and it shows a US regulator is willing to set offshore quotas. If the scope were widened to all TCPA-covered calls, collections and healthcare member-service calls could come into play.

Tariffs do not apply, and why that matters

The US tariff saga of 2025–26 has been about goods. The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026; a temporary 10% tariff under Section 122 ran to 24 July 2026, and since then India has faced a 10% Section 301 tariff on top of normal duties. None of these applies to services. An Indian company processing a US insurer's claims or a bank's reconciliations pays no tariff, and there is no customs border for a data stream. That is one reason the HIRE Act was framed as an excise tax rather than a tariff, and one reason the policy debate has moved to sector rules like the FCC's. Our IT services report discusses the same point for software services.

India: labour law is the live issue

India's four Labour Codes, effective from 21 November 2025, changed the definition of wages for gratuity and other benefits; Firstsource booked a one-off charge of about ₹98 crore in Q3 FY26 for their effect. More important for FY27 are state minimum wages. Karnataka raised its minimum wages from 22 May 2026 and Telangana from 1 June 2026. For a company with two-thirds of its staff in Bengaluru and Hyderabad, as Sagility has, the direct increase on entry-level pay is compounded by raises for staff just above the new minimum to preserve pay differentials. Sagility put the FY27 cost at about 120 basis points of margin and is shifting new hiring to Indore and Tamil Nadu. eClerx (Mumbai, Pune, Chandigarh) and IKS (Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad) are less exposed to Karnataka; any company with a large Hyderabad base faces some of it. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act and its 2025 rules add compliance work but mostly as a processor for foreign clients.

The UK and Europe matter mainly through conduct and data rules. The UK Financial Conduct Authority's Consumer Duty, in force since July 2023, raises the standard for how collections and customer-service operations treat vulnerable customers, which increases compliance cost for Firstsource's UK book but also raises barriers to entry. The EU AI Act imposes obligations on high-risk AI systems, including some credit and collections uses, and GDPR governs transfers of European personal data to India for processing. Indegene, with about a quarter of revenue in Europe, and eClerx, with about 16%, operate under these rules. None is an existential risk; all add cost and favour scaled, compliant vendors over small ones.

This industry does not follow the economic cycle; it de-rates on technology scares and client shocks and re-rates on delivered growth.

All five members were founded within eight years of each other, between 1998 and 2006, in the first wave of Indian offshoring. Three kinds of shock recur. The first is the technology scare. Reading a BPM company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, what each one means, how the five compare, and the traps that make the same word mean different things at different companies.

A BPM company's accounts look simple: revenue, employee cost, other costs, profit. The numbers that tell you whether the business is getting stronger or weaker sit underneath, in the investor deck and on the earnings call: growth at constant exchange rates, revenue per person, how many staff leave, how concentrated the clients are, where the hedges are struck. This section explains each of them, with the formula, a worked example, the drivers, a benchmark, how the five members compare, and the definition trap that catches people out.

The revenue and profit equation

Every BPM company's revenue is the sum of three kinds of billing, and its profit is what is left after paying mostly rupee salaries out of mostly dollar revenue. The flow below shows the equation. Management controls headcount, utilisation, pricing structure, offshore mix and overheads. It does not control the exchange rate, wage inflation in the cities where it hires, or the client's decision to automate, insource or cut scope.

After ₹110 crore of depreciation and amortisation (₹60 crore of it acquired intangibles), ₹30 crore of interest and tax at 25.2%, profit is about ₹387 crore (15.8%), or ₹432 crore "adjusted" if acquired-intangible amortisation is added back. The profile is close to Sagility's.

Three sensitivities from the same example show what moves profit. A 2% weaker rupee adds ₹49 crore of revenue and ₹7 crore of onshore cost, so EBITDA rises ₹42 crore, about 1.2 points of margin, before hedges. An 8% offshore wage increase adds ₹60 crore of cost and removes 2.4 points of margin unless priced through. Automating 10% of FTE-priced volume without recovering it in price removes ₹245 crore of revenue but only about ₹110 crore of cost (the freed staff can be redeployed only gradually), cutting EBITDA by ₹135 crore, about four points of margin. The last sensitivity is the AI risk in one number.

In plain terms

A BPM company earns roughly: number of people doing billable work, times hours, times the dollar rate, converted to rupees, minus mostly rupee salaries. If salaries rise 8% and nothing else changes, margins fall about two and a half points. If the rupee weakens 2%, margins rise about one point. If clients automate a tenth of the work and pay less for it, margins fall about four points. Every KPI below is a way of watching one of those levers.

1. Constant-currency revenue growth

What it is: revenue growth recalculated as if exchange rates had not moved, so that it measures the business rather than the rupee. Formula: this year's revenue in each currency converted at last year's average rates, divided by last year's reported revenue, minus one. Companies that bill in dollars often report dollar growth instead, which is the same idea for a single-currency business. Worked example: a vendor with $100 million of revenue last year at ₹88 (₹880 crore) and $112 million this year at ₹94 (₹1,053 crore) reports rupee growth of 19.7% but constant-currency growth of 12%.

Drivers: volume growth at existing clients, new logos ramping up, pricing (usually slightly negative, through productivity give-backs), and acquisitions (which should be separated as "organic" versus "inorganic"). 2. Operating margin, and five different "EBITDA"s

What it is: the share of revenue left after operating costs, before depreciation, interest and tax. Formula: EBITDA ÷ revenue. This report uses screener.in's "operating profit" (sales minus operating expenses, excluding other income) for every combined figure, because it is the only basis available for all five. Worked example: a company with ₹1,000 crore of revenue, ₹750 crore of operating costs and ₹20 crore of treasury income has an operating margin of 25.0%; if it reports EBITDA including the treasury income, it shows 27.0%.

Drivers: offshore mix, pricing model, utilisation, wage inflation, the rupee (net of hedges) and the cost of acquired onshore businesses. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for healthcare platforms and KPO, above 27% good, 20–27% average, below 20% weak; for volume CX, EBIT above 13% good, 10–13% average. 3. Revenue per employee

What it is: how much revenue each person on the payroll generates in a year. Formula: revenue (usually in dollars) ÷ average headcount over the period. Worked example: $469 million of revenue and 22,639 employees gives about $20,700 per head; if revenue grows 15% and headcount 5%, revenue per head rises about 9.5%.

Drivers: pricing model (outcome and transaction pricing raise it as automation spreads), onshore mix (onshore staff bill far more), skill mix, utilisation and acquisitions of higher-revenue businesses. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for offshore BPM, above $30,000 good, $18,000–30,000 average, below $18,000 weak; but the benchmark only makes sense within an archetype. And a rising figure can mean either AI productivity or a shift to onshore work: check the margin at the same time.

4. Headcount growth and non-linearity

What it is: the gap between revenue growth and headcount growth, the cleanest available sign of whether automation is raising output per person. Formula: constant-currency revenue growth minus headcount growth, in percentage points. Worked example: revenue up 12% in constant currency, headcount up 4%: non-linearity of +8 points. If headcount rose 15%, it would be −3 points.

Drivers: automation inside fixed-price or outcome contracts (positive), shifts from onshore to offshore (negative for revenue per head, positive for margin), acquisitions of businesses with different revenue per head, seasonal hiring and contractor conversions. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): above +5 points good, 0 to +5 average, below zero weak. How the five compare: Q1 FY27 shows IKS about +8, Firstsource +5.4, eClerx +4.7 and Sagility −3.3; Indegene's +12 is distorted by acquisitions (section 07 charts the values). The trap: one quarter proves little; Sagility's figure is depressed by the CareSeed deal and by open-enrolment hiring, and Indegene's inflated by BioPharm. Track it over four quarters and alongside margins.

In plain terms

For twenty years, if a BPM company wanted 15% more revenue it hired about 15% more people. If AI is working in the company's favour, revenue should now grow faster than the number of staff while profit margins hold. That is the single most useful thing to check each quarter, and it is just starting to show at IKS and eClerx.

5. Attrition

What it is: the share of employees who leave in a year. Formula: leavers over the last twelve months ÷ average headcount; "voluntary" excludes dismissals. Worked example: a company with an average of 30,000 staff and 9,000 leavers over a year has 30% attrition; if each replacement costs ₹80,000 to recruit and train and is unproductive for six weeks, the annual cost is several percent of salary cost.

Drivers: the type of work (voice and transactional roles churn fastest), the local job market (Bengaluru and Hyderabad compete with GCCs and IT firms), pay rises and minimum-wage changes, and seasonal hiring (open-enrolment hires at Sagility leave after the season). Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for KPO and healthcare, below 15% good, 15–25% average, above 25% weak; for voice CX, below 30% good, 30–45% average. How the five compare: Firstsource 33.1% TTM total (from 28.9% a year earlier), Sagility 28.6% voluntary in Q1 FY27 (38.1% in the seasonal Q4), eClerx 18.1% offshore voluntary, Indegene 15.7% TTM voluntary; IKS does not disclose. The trap: the definitions differ (total vs voluntary, quarterly annualised vs TTM, offshore only vs all), so cross-company comparisons are approximate.

8. Deal wins: total and annual contract value

What it is: the value of new contracts signed, the leading indicator of future revenue. Drivers: pipeline, win rates, the size of deals the company is credible for, and client budgets. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): ACV growth above 20% good, 5–20% average. How the five compare: eClerx discloses the most: new-deal ACV of $169.9 million in FY26, up from $82.4 million in FY23, and $40–41 million in Q1 FY27 (+25%). 9. Offshore mix

What it is: the share of delivery (people, hours or revenue) performed in low-cost locations. Formula: offshore and nearshore delivery headcount ÷ total delivery headcount, or offshore revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: moving 1,000 roles from the UK (₹45 lakh a year) to India (₹7 lakh) saves ₹380 crore of cost; if the client pays 40% less per role, revenue falls ₹180 crore, so EBITDA rises ₹200 crore while revenue shrinks.

Drivers: client willingness, regulation (licences, data rules, the FCC proposal), language needs and time zones. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for a CX/BPM company, rising offshore share is good for margins; for a pure offshore KPO, onshore revenue of 15–20% is typical for client-facing roles. How the five compare: Firstsource's offshore and nearshore share of delivery rose from 35.0% in Q1 FY25 to 43.5% in Q1 FY27; eClerx earns about 18% of revenue onshore and has 18,305 of its 22,499 staff in offshore delivery; Sagility, IKS and Indegene do not disclose a comparable figure but deliver mostly from India and the Philippines. The trap: offshoring cuts revenue per head and can cut revenue outright, so a company can report falling revenue per employee and rising margins at the same time.

10. Utilisation

What it is: the share of delivery staff time that is billable. Formula: billable FTE ÷ total delivery FTE. Worked example: 10,000 delivery staff at 80% utilisation bill 8,000 FTE; raising utilisation to 85% adds 500 billable FTE without hiring, worth about ₹70 crore of revenue at $16 an hour and almost all of it profit.

Drivers: bench size during ramp-ups, training time, attrition (new hires are not billable in training) and how much of the work is priced on outcomes (where "utilisation" matters less). Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for FTE-billed BPM, above 85% good, 78–85% average, below 78% weak. How the five compare: only eClerx discloses it: 75.5% in Q1 FY27, up from 72.8% a year earlier. The trap: utilisation is meaningless for outcome-priced work and easily flattered by excluding trainees from the denominator.

11. Days sales outstanding (DSO)

What it is: how many days of revenue are waiting to be collected. Formula: trade receivables (including unbilled revenue) ÷ revenue × 365, or × 91 for a quarter. Worked example: ₹2,000 crore of annual revenue and ₹440 crore of receivables gives 80 days; cutting it to 65 days releases about ₹82 crore of cash.

Drivers: client payment terms, billing in arrears, milestone billing on outcome contracts, and disputes. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): below 60 days good, 60–80 average, above 80 weak. How the five compare: IKS about 65 days (FY26), Firstsource 67–69 days normalised, Indegene 67 days net (up four days in Q1), eClerx 79 days (from 86), Sagility 80 days. 12. Hedge rate versus spot

What it is: the average rate at which a company has sold its future foreign-currency revenue, compared with today's exchange rate. Formula: (hedge rate ÷ spot rate) − 1, for each maturity bucket. Worked example: $156 million hedged at ₹91.16 when spot is ₹95.0 means the company will receive about ₹60 crore less than it would at spot, roughly 1.5% of eClerx's FY26 operating revenue, spread over FY27.

Drivers: the company's hedging policy (share of exposure and tenor), the rupee's path, and forward premiums (interest-rate differentials make forward rates weaker than spot, which partly offsets the hedge loss). Benchmark: there is no good or bad; what matters is knowing the gap. How the five compare: eClerx's FY27 book is about 4% below spot, its FY28 book about 1% above; Firstsource's dollar book about 2.4% below and its sterling book about 6% below; the other three do not publish rates. The trap: where the result lands differs (revenue at eClerx and Firstsource; below adjusted EBITDA at Sagility; through profit and loss at Indegene until Q1 FY27), which changes reported margins without changing the business.

13. Goodwill ÷ equity and the reported-to-adjusted profit ratio

What it is: two measures of how much of a company's balance sheet and earnings come from acquisitions. Formulas: goodwill ÷ shareholders' equity; reported PAT ÷ adjusted PAT. Worked example: a company with ₹4,000 crore of equity and ₹3,000 crore of goodwill (75%) that writes off a third of the goodwill loses a quarter of its book value overnight, with no cash leaving the company.

Drivers: acquisition prices, currency translation (goodwill on foreign businesses rises in rupees as the rupee falls), amortisation schedules for acquired intangibles, and earn-out revaluations. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): goodwill below 30% of equity is low, above 70% high; reported profit above 95% of adjusted is clean, below 85% means large ongoing acquisition charges. How the five compare: Firstsource 97% goodwill-to-equity, Sagility 66% (plus ₹2,041 crore of other intangibles), IKS about 47% before TruBridge, Indegene about 36%; reported-to-adjusted profit runs from 82% at Sagility to 100% at eClerx (section 10). The trap: "adjusted" profit excludes different things at each company (ESOP cost at some, acquisition costs at others, hedge losses at Sagility). Use reported profit for valuation comparisons, and treat adjusted profit as management's view of run-rate earnings.

14. Net cash and cash conversion

What it is: cash and investments minus borrowings, and how much of operating profit turns into operating cash flow. Formulas: net cash = cash + investments − borrowings (this report excludes lease liabilities); cash conversion = operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: ₹1,000 crore of EBITDA and ₹750 crore of operating cash flow is 75% conversion; the rest went into tax, receivables and other working capital.

Drivers: acquisitions, buybacks and dividends (for net cash); receivables, tax and upfront client payments (for conversion). Benchmark (INDICATIVE): net cash is good, net debt up to 1.5× EBITDA average, above 2× weak; conversion above 80% good, 65–80% average. How the five compare: at March 2026, excluding leases, Indegene had net cash of ₹1,482 crore, eClerx ₹1,046 crore, Sagility ₹326 crore and IKS ₹125 crore, while Firstsource had net debt of ₹1,553 crore (about 1× operating profit). IKS's position changes to an INDICATIVE ₹5,000–5,500 crore of net debt after TruBridge. Cash conversion: IKS's operating cash flow of ₹674 crore was 62% of FY26 operating profit; Firstsource's normalised free cash flow is about 94% of profit; eClerx's free cash flow was ₹756 crore against ₹706 crore of profit. How to read a BPM company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Constant-currency (or dollar) revenue growth, year on year and quarter on quarter. This is the real growth. Compare it with guidance (Firstsource 10–13%; Sagility low double digits; IKS legacy above 12%). Red flag: a quarter-on-quarter decline outside a known seasonal pattern.
  2. Organic versus acquired. Strip out acquisitions closed within the last year (CareSeed, TruBridge, BioPharm). Red flag: the company stops disclosing the split.
  3. Margin on the company's own basis, then on screener operating profit. Note the bridge management gives (wages, FX, acquisition costs, hedges). Red flag: margin down in a quarter when the rupee weakened, without a clear one-off.
  4. Exceptional items and adjusted-profit add-backs. Read what they are: programme terminations, indemnities and earn-out revaluations (Firstsource Q1 FY27) are business events, not accounting noise. Red flag: recurring "one-offs".
  5. Headcount against revenue. Compute non-linearity. Red flag: headcount growing faster than constant-currency revenue for several quarters.
  6. Client metrics. Top-5 and top-10 shares, clients above $1 million and $10 million, new logos. Red flag: a sudden drop in a top client's share, or management mentioning "scope recalibration" or "insourcing".
  7. Deal wins and pipeline. ACV, number of large deals, pipeline size. Red flag: strong wins with flat revenue for several quarters (wins replacing losses).
  8. Attrition and utilisation. Red flag: attrition jumping several points, or utilisation falling during a ramp.
  9. Cash and balance sheet. Operating cash flow against EBITDA, DSO, net debt, hedge book and the cash-flow hedge reserve. Red flag: DSO rising for two quarters, or large upfront payments to clients.
  10. Guidance and tone on AI and pricing. Listen for pricing direction ("opposite direction" at Sagility), the share of non-FTE pricing, and how the company describes AI's effect on renewals. Red flag: talk of "productivity pass-through" without new scope.

Applied to the Q2 FY27 season that starts on 22 October 2026, the checklist points to a few specific numbers: Sagility's constant-currency organic growth against its low-double-digit guide and whether its adjusted margin holds 24–25% after the minimum-wage hit; Indegene's margin, which management expects to be stable before an inflection in Q3; IKS's first consolidated quarter with TruBridge, where revenue, margin, interest cost and net debt will all reset; Firstsource's EBIT progress towards 12.25–12.75% and any further programme terminations; and eClerx's revenue per head and hedge drag. Who is winning: margin, concentration and AI positioning

Three maps and a scorecard show that no member wins on every measure, and that the market pays most for margin and pricing protection.

"Winning" in this sub-sector has three dimensions: earning a high margin on hard-to-replace work, growing without becoming dependent on a handful of clients, and being paid in a way that lets the company keep AI productivity gains. The maps below place the five on each, and the scorecard pulls them together. The first map compares what investors pay with what each company earns.

The first map says the market is paying for two different things. For IKS it is paying for margin and the promise of more: management's FY30 target of at least ₹3,000 crore of EBITDA, from about ₹1,800 crore combined with TruBridge today. For Indegene it is paying for recovery: profit fell 1.5% in FY26 on acquisition costs, and the stock trades on the expectation that margins return to 19–20% in the second half of FY27. Sagility and eClerx sit in the "lower multiple, high margin" corner, for opposite reasons: Sagility because of concentration and supply of stock, eClerx because its growth (15% in constant currency) is solid rather than spectacular and its client base is mainly banks and telecom companies with their own captive centres.

The second map shows the trade-off between growth and concentration. In this industry growth often comes from expanding inside existing large clients, which raises concentration. IKS is the clearest case: its top-ten share went from 43% to 54% in a year while its client count fell from 650-plus to 600-plus, a deliberate land-and-expand strategy that makes its revenue look more like Sagility's than like a broad RCM vendor's. Firstsource has moved the other way, reducing its top-ten share from 51.5% to 39.2% in two years by adding new logos and verticals. Its growth is slower, but a single client loss hurts less, which was the lesson of Q1 FY27: even a programme termination cost it only a sliver of revenue.

The third map is a judgement, not a measurement, because none of the five except Indegene discloses its pricing mix. It captures the logic of section 07: a company is safest where its work is hard to automate or where it is paid per outcome, and most at risk where the work is easy to automate and billed per person. Firstsource sits in the riskiest quadrant on this view, which is why it has been the most aggressive in repositioning around agentic AI. IKS is highly exposed but largely outcome-priced, so automation should raise its margin if it moves faster than competitors such as AI-native scribes and EHR vendors.

Valuation is where the comparison is least flattering. The global CX majors trade at single-digit to low-teens multiples of earnings, and Genpact has historically traded at around 11–13 times forward earnings (INDICATIVE, not refreshed for this report). In plain terms

Around the world, call-centre companies are shrinking and cheap; specialist data and healthcare operators are growing and expensive. That premium is safe only as long as they keep growing well into the teens.

Finally, the competitive geography matters. The Philippines remains India's main rival for voice work and some healthcare processing, and its own targets have been cut. Nearshore centres in Latin America, the Caribbean, South Africa and Eastern Europe serve clients who want time-zone or language alignment; Sagility (Jamaica, Colombia) and Firstsource (Mexico, South Africa, Romania, Trinidad) use them. India's advantage is depth: the largest pool of English-speaking graduates trained in US healthcare coding, pharmacovigilance and financial operations, and the GCC ecosystem that keeps adding to it. Technology and disruption: AI-native rivals, EHR vendors and the human in the loop

The threat is less a generic chatbot than specialist software firms selling the same outcome without the staff.

Section 07 explained the pricing mechanics of AI. This section maps the specific technologies and competitors that matter for each member, and the defences they are building. The common thread is that AI does not usually replace a whole process; it replaces the first draft, leaving a human to validate, handle exceptions and carry liability. The vendors that own that human-in-the-loop layer, and price it per outcome, can turn AI into margin.

IKS ("AI drafts, human validates"), Firstsource (Kairos agents with human oversight), eClerx (Roboworx CogniFlows) and Indegene (AI-embedded content factory with medical-legal-regulatory review) all describe versions of this model.

The specialist threats, by member

  • IKS: ambient scribes and autonomous coding. Abridge, Microsoft's Dragon Copilot (formerly DAX), Suki and Ambience listen to the doctor-patient conversation and draft the clinical note directly; autonomous-coding companies such as Fathom and CodaMetrix code charts without a human for routine cases. These attack IKS's documentation and coding revenue directly. IKS's chief executive argues that expanding from a scribe to a full pre-, peri- and post-visit platform takes years; IKS invested in Abridge and sold part of its stake for $10 million in August 2026.
  • IKS and Firstsource provider RCM: the EHR vendors. Epic and Oracle Health are adding AI billing and denial-management features inside the record system, a form of insourcing through software. Owning TruBridge's EHR is partly IKS's defence: it controls the platform for its rural hospital clients.
  • Sagility and Firstsource payer: voice AI and auto-adjudication. Member and provider service calls are the most automatable payer work, and insurers' own claims systems keep raising auto-adjudication rates. Sagility owns BirchAI for conversational AI and is discussing outcome-based contracts; Firstsource says voice agents are already standard in parts of its work.
  • Firstsource CX: per-resolution AI agents. Software vendors sell AI resolutions directly to Firstsource's telecom, media and retail clients at $0.50–2.00 each. This is the threat that has shrunk Concentrix and Teleperformance.
  • Indegene: GenAI content and platform vendors. Generative tools draft promotional and medical content, and life-sciences software platforms such as Veeva are adding AI agents inside the review workflow. Indegene says GenAI is "driving price deflation" but that its scale lets it take share from agencies and contract research organisations.
  • eClerx: reconciliation software and LLM exception handling. Banks have automated trade operations for a decade; T+1 settlement and LLM-based exception handling accelerate that. eClerx's first large agentic-AI deal (Q4 FY26) shows it can sell automation as well as be displaced by it.

Cybersecurity and data risk

The other technology risk is security. The February 2024 ransomware attack on Change Healthcare, a UnitedHealth unit that processes a large share of US claims, disrupted payments to providers for weeks and showed how concentrated the system's plumbing is. Every member handles sensitive data (patient records, insurance claims, bank and trade data, pharmacovigilance cases) under HIPAA, GDPR and client security audits. That is a barrier to entry for small rivals and a tail risk for every incumbent: a breach at a vendor can end a client relationship and bring regulatory penalties. Firstsource's Q1 FY27 indemnity of a client's regulatory penalty (₹28.4 crore, for a contractual performance matter) shows that operational failures can carry direct costs.

In plain terms

The real competition is not a general-purpose chatbot but specialist software companies that sell the result these firms deliver, such as a finished medical note or a resolved customer query, without needing hundreds of staff. The Indian firms' defence is to adopt the same tools, keep the expert human checking the output, and charge per result. Those that manage it should see profit rise even if headcount stops growing.

The investment response is visible across the group. Firstsource has built an AI brand and partnerships; IKS has absorbed engineering teams from smaller acquisitions (ThinkDTM, ARAI) and reports rising EBITDA per employee; eClerx has trained more than 3,000 staff on agentic AI; Indegene is carrying the cost of GenAI engagements ahead of revenue; Sagility is negotiating outcome-based managed services. NASSCOM's first estimate puts AI-related revenue across India's technology industry at $10–12 billion in FY26. For investors, the tell will be in the KPIs, not the press releases: rising revenue per head, stable or higher margins, and a growing share of non-FTE pricing.

Bull vs bear: specialists riding a paperwork boom, or a seat business about to be repriced

Both cases rest on the same facts; they differ on how fast AI moves through contracts and how long US healthcare budgets stay loose.

The case for this sub-sector is that its members are domain specialists in the parts of outsourcing that are still growing, with high margins, light balance sheets (IKS aside) and a weak rupee that will feed through as hedges roll. The case against is that they trade at premium multiples on a billing model that AI is undermining, with heavy client concentration, acquisition-built balance sheets and owners who want to sell.

The bull case

  • Share gains in growing niches: constant-currency growth of 12–15% (and 18–24% in FY26) against 7% for India's BPM industry and negative growth at global voice-CX majors.
  • The paperwork keeps growing: US healthcare's administrative arms race, OBBBA verification work, CMS prior-authorisation deadlines and pharma's shift to digital all add volume.
  • Better payer budgets in 2027: the +2.48% Medicare Advantage rate (about 5% all-in) should ease the pricing pressure Sagility and Firstsource describe.
  • AI as margin, not deflation: outcome-linked pricing at IKS and parts of Indegene and Sagility lets vendors keep productivity gains; early non-linearity is visible at IKS and eClerx.
  • The rupee arrives in FY28: hedge books roll from ₹89–93 to rates near or above ₹95, lifting margins a year after the currency moved.
  • Clean balance sheets: four of five are net cash or near it ex-leases; Sagility repays its promoter loan in FY27; eClerx returns cash.

The bear case

  • The seat is being repriced: AI resolutions at $0.50–2.00 and Firstsource's "volume repriced as a technology service" show FTE revenue shrinking at renewal; no member discloses its pricing mix.
  • Premium multiples: 29.5× FY26 profit for the group and 39× for IKS, against single-digit to low-teens for global peers; convergence would hurt.
  • Concentration: Sagility's top three are 60% of revenue, Indegene's largest client 31%, IKS's top ten 54% and rising; one programme decision moves a year.
  • Healthcare budget risk: Medicaid cuts hit TruBridge's rural hospitals; payer clients are pushing prices down; the 2028 MA notice could disappoint.
  • Acquisition risk: IKS takes on ~₹5,000–5,500 Cr of indicative net debt; Firstsource's goodwill is 97% of equity; Sagility amortises to FY32 and carries an HGS-indemnified lawsuit.
  • Supply of stock and policy: EQT holds 51% of Sagility, Brighton Park 8.7% of Indegene; the FCC offshore-call proposal shows voice work is a political target; Karnataka and Telangana wage rules raise costs.

Weighing the two, the sub-sector's fundamentals are better than its global reputation: it is growing at mid-teens in constant currency, its margins are holding through a wage shock, and it is concentrated in the most defensible parts of outsourcing. The valuation already assumes much of that. In plain terms

The optimist sees specialist firms winning business in the parts of outsourcing that are still growing, with a weak rupee about to help profits. The pessimist sees companies paid by the hour just as AI cuts the hours, priced well above their global rivals, and dependent on a few very large clients. Both are describing the same companies; the next few quarters of results will show which description fits better.
