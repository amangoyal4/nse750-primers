00

The one-page view: five ways to sell skilled hours to America

Five listed companies, four business archetypes, one shared question: what does AI do to a business that has always billed for people?

India's listed business-process management (BPM) group is small, young and unusually profitable. Five companies sit in the NSE 750 under this label: Inventurus Knowledge Solutions (IKS Health), Sagility, Firstsource Solutions, Indegene and eClerx Services. Together they earned ₹27,671 crore of revenue in FY26, up 23% in rupees, and ₹3,428 crore of reported profit after tax. On market prices from late July to early October 2026 they were worth roughly ₹1.02 lakh crore, about 30 times that profit. Three of the five listed only in 2024, all three largely as private-equity or founder exits.

The group is not one business. IKS runs the back office of US doctors and hospitals: documentation, medical coding, claims and collections. Sagility does the mirror-image job for US health insurers: enrolment, claims processing, appeals and payment checking. Firstsource is a diversified customer-experience and operations company that serves US and UK healthcare, banks, mortgage lenders and telecom groups. Indegene builds and runs the marketing, medical-information and drug-safety operations of global pharmaceutical companies. eClerx handles data-heavy operations for investment banks, cable companies and retailers. What unites them is the delivery model: skilled staff, mostly in India and the Philippines, doing work that a client in the US or Europe would otherwise do at three to five times the cost.

That model has been compounding for twenty-five years. Its new problem is that the thing being sold, a trained person's hour, is exactly what generative AI and AI agents now automate. The global contact-centre industry is already shrinking: Concentrix, the second-largest listed operator, reported a 1.2% revenue decline and a $1.05 billion goodwill write-down in its latest quarter; Teleperformance's first-half revenue fell 1.7%; and the Philippine industry body cut its 2028 revenue target from $59 billion to $50.5 billion in its best case, citing AI. Yet the five Indian names grew 12–15% in constant currency in Q1 FY27. The gap between those two facts is the investment case, and the risk.

Five forces shape the sub-sector, and this report is organised around them.

  • GenAI is repricing the "seat". Under per-person billing, a 30% productivity gain becomes a 30% revenue cut when the contract renews. Under transaction or outcome pricing the vendor keeps much of the gain. Contract structure, not technology alone, decides who wins (Section 07).
  • US healthcare administration is the profit pool, and US health policy is the macro variable. About half of group revenue comes from US payers and providers, and close to three-fifths once Indegene's US pharma work is added. Medicare Advantage rates, Medicaid cuts and drug-pricing deals matter more to these stocks than Indian GDP (Section 08).
  • The rupee flatters growth, and margins absorb less of it than expected. Q1 FY27 revenue rose 26% in rupees but only 12–15% in constant currency. The 7% fall in the rupee since the FY26 average did not lift margins, because wage rises, acquisition costs, AI investment and hedge losses absorbed it (Section 09).
  • Concentration and acquisitions drive risk. Indegene's largest client is 31% of revenue; eClerx's top ten are 60%. Acquisitions (IKS's $557 million TruBridge deal above all) now add more growth than organic wins, and they widen the gap between reported and adjusted profit (Section 10).
  • Private-equity exits set the supply of stock. EQT cut its Sagility stake from 82% to 51% in twelve months; Carlyle has left Indegene entirely. The 2024 cohort trades at 22–42× trailing earnings, against single-digit multiples for global call-centre peers (Sections 11 and 18).

In plain terms

These five companies rent out trained Indian (and Filipino) professionals to American and European companies that need paperwork, phone calls and data processed. They have been growing fast and earning good margins. The worry is that AI software can now do a large part of that paperwork. So far the five have kept growing, partly because their work is specialised (US healthcare billing, drug-safety reporting, bank trade processing) and partly because the falling rupee makes every dollar they earn worth more at home. The question for an investor is how much of the growth is durable once both of those supports fade.

What follows is built to be read in order, but each section stands alone. Sections 01–06 explain the business: what is sold, to whom, at what cost and in what industry structure. Sections 07–10 are deep dives into the four forces that move the numbers. Section 14 is a masterclass on the key performance indicators (KPIs) that matter in this business and their definition traps. Sections 15–18 compare the five companies with each other and with global peers. The last three sections set out the bull and bear cases and what to watch, including the Q2 FY27 results calendar: Sagility reports on 22 October 2026, Indegene on 29 October, and the other three by the 14 November deadline.

01

How the money is made: renting judgement by the hour, the claim and the outcome

Every member sells the same thing in a different wrapper: a client's process, moved offshore, run to a service level and billed per person, per transaction or per result.

A business-process outsourcer takes over a defined piece of a client's operations, runs it from its own centres with its own staff and systems, and is paid against a contract with service levels. A knowledge-process outsourcer (KPO) is the same thing for work that needs domain judgement: a medical coder who knows ICD-10, a pharmacovigilance associate who can classify an adverse event, an analyst who can resolve a failed trade. The industry calls the whole space BPM, business-process management. The distinction between BPO and KPO is mostly about how much judgement each transaction needs, and therefore how much each hour can be billed for.

The money comes from three pricing models, and the mix between them decides how each company fares as AI arrives. Per FTE (full-time equivalent, one person-year of work) is the oldest: the client pays for a team of a given size, say 200 coders at an hourly rate. Revenue rises and falls with headcount. Per transaction pays a fixed price per claim processed, chart coded or call handled; the vendor keeps any efficiency gain until the price is renegotiated. Per outcome pays a share of value created: a percentage of the money collected for a hospital, of overpayments recovered for an insurer, or of debts collected for a bank. Firstsource's own investor material lays out all three. Most of the industry is still FTE-priced.

Each member sits on a different value chain. In provider RCM (IKS), a patient visit generates a clinical note, the note is coded into billing codes, the claim goes to an insurer, the payment or denial comes back, and the vendor posts the cash and fights the denials. US payers deny roughly one claim in eight on first submission (industry estimates range from 10% to 15%), which is why denial management is a business in itself. The vendor that controls the whole chain, and now increasingly the hospital's electronic health record (EHR) software as well, captures the most value because it owns the data and the outcome measure. That is the logic behind IKS's purchase of TruBridge, a US software and RCM company serving rural and community hospitals.

In payer operations (Sagility), the flow runs the other way. Premiums come in, members are enrolled, claims arrive, most are adjudicated automatically and the rest are "pended" for manual review: that queue of exceptions is the outsourcer's work. After payment, payment-integrity teams look for overpayments. The economic engine is the US medical-loss-ratio (MLR) rule under the Affordable Care Act: insurers must spend at least 85% of large-group premiums (80% for individual and small-group plans) on care and quality, which leaves 15–20 cents of every premium dollar for administration and profit. Every dollar of admin cost saved goes straight to the insurer's margin.

In pharma commercialisation (Indegene), the chain starts with an approved drug and a brand plan, runs through thousands of promotional assets per brand, each of which must pass medical-legal-regulatory review, and ends in omnichannel campaigns aimed at doctors and patients, with drug-safety case processing running in parallel. In capital-markets KPO (eClerx), the work follows a trade from confirmation through settlement, reconciliation of breaks, reference-data upkeep and regulatory reporting, usually inside the client's own systems. Firstsource spans several chains at once: US healthcare payers and providers, UK and US banks and collections, mortgage processing and telecom customer service.

Value is captured in layers. The base layer is labour arbitrage: an Indian delivery FTE in voice or transactional work costs roughly ₹6–12 lakh a year all-in, a coder or KPO analyst ₹10–20 lakh, and a clinical or medical-writing professional ₹15–30 lakh, against $45,000–90,000 for a US equivalent (INDICATIVE ranges; industry norms, not sourced company data). Offshore healthcare BPM typically bills $12–25 an hour against fully loaded US in-house costs of $35–60 (also INDICATIVE). Arbitrage alone, though, is competed away. The second layer is domain and compliance: HIPAA, SOC 2 audits, payer and regulator reviews and pharma review standards create switching costs that keep clients for decades. The third is technology and IP, where platforms and automation let a vendor do more per head. The top layer is outcome ownership, where fees are tied to collections, recoveries or savings. That top layer is the one AI makes more valuable rather than less, which is why it matters so much in Section 07.

In plain terms

The cheapest part of this business to copy is the low wage. The hardest parts are knowing the rules (US medical billing codes, drug-safety regulations, bank settlement rules) and being paid for results rather than for time. The companies with the highest margins in this group are the ones furthest from "we supply people" and closest to "we get your money in" or "we run your process on our platform".

Two features of the model explain why investors have liked it. First, it is asset-light: a delivery centre needs leased office space, laptops and network links, but capital spending is only about 2–4% of revenue, so most profit converts to cash (eClerx turned about 76% of FY26 EBITDA into operating cash flow; Indegene's FY26 operating cash flow of about ₹651 crore was roughly 162% of reported profit). Second, it is sticky: once a process has been transitioned, re-tendering is costly and risky for the client, so revenue tends to recur for years. The weaknesses mirror the strengths: growth needs people, people cost more every year (wage increments of about 6–9% a year are typical), and the client holds the bargaining power at every renewal.

02

A ₹5 lakh crore industry in which the listed five are a rounding error

India's BPM industry earns about $59 billion a year; the five listed members earn about $3.1 billion of it. Most of the market belongs to captives, IT majors and global firms.

NASSCOM's Strategic Review for 2026 put India's technology industry at about $315 billion of revenue in FY26, up 6.1% on a restated FY25 of $297 billion, with exports of about $246 billion. Within that, BPM contributed about $59 billion, up roughly 7% from about $55 billion, or around 19% of industry revenue. Converted at the FY26 average rate of about ₹88.3 to the dollar, that is roughly ₹5.2 lakh crore. The other segments were IT services (about $149 billion), engineering research and development services (about $63 billion), software products ($23 billion) and hardware ($21 billion). Our IT services, ER&D services and software products reports cover those.

A warning on vintages applies. NASSCOM's 2025 review had FY25 at $282.6 billion; the 2026 review restated it to $297 billion. Mixing the two vintages produces growth rates that never happened. The industry employed about 59.5 lakh people directly in FY26, an increase of 1.35 lakh or 2.3% against revenue growth of 6.1%. NASSCOM calls that gap "non-linearity": revenue growing faster than headcount, the first macro sign of automation.

In plain terms

India's outsourcing industry is big, but the five listed companies in this report are a small slice of it, about one rupee in twenty. They are growing much faster than the industry because each has picked a profitable niche, mostly US healthcare, and because three of them have been buying other companies. The large in-house centres that global firms run in India are growing faster still, which is both a threat (clients doing the work themselves) and an opportunity (selling specialist services into those centres).

03

Who buys, and why now: the end-markets behind the growth

US health insurers, US doctors and hospitals, global drugmakers, investment banks, telecoms and retailers each buy for different reasons and cut for different reasons.

Outsourcing demand is a function of the client's cost pressure, its process complexity and its willingness to move work offshore. The five members sell into six end-markets, and they are not equally healthy. By our INDICATIVE estimate, about half of group revenue (IKS's ~100%, Sagility's ~100% and Firstsource's ~34% in healthcare, together about ₹13,600 crore of ₹27,671 crore) comes from US healthcare, and close to three-fifths once Indegene's US pharma work (about 72% of its revenue) is added. That makes US health policy, rather than the Indian economy, the main macro variable for this sub-sector.

US health insurers: margin squeeze, then relief

US payers have had three hard years: medical costs have risen faster than premiums, squeezing margins across the industry, and that pushed insurers to outsource more administration. The most outsourcing-intensive line is Medicare Advantage (MA), the private alternative to traditional Medicare, which needs risk-adjustment coding, quality (Star ratings) work, prior-authorisation reviews and appeals. In January 2026 the federal health agency CMS proposed an almost flat 2027 MA payment increase (+0.09%); its final notice on 6 April 2026 gave +2.48%, about $13 billion, or roughly 5% including risk-score trends, and delayed a planned change to the risk model. Insurer shares jumped on the day. For Sagility, whose clients are mostly MA-heavy plans, better payer margins in calendar 2027 should loosen admin budgets in its FY28.

US doctors and hospitals: complexity pays, cuts hurt

Providers outsource to get paid. Coding rules, prior-authorisation requirements and denial rates rise every year, which increases the work, but provider margins are thin and getting thinner. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed on 4 July 2025, cuts federal Medicaid spending by about $1 trillion over ten years; the Congressional Budget Office expects 11.8 million people to lose coverage by 2034 from its health provisions. Work requirements of 80 hours a month for expansion adults must be in place by 31 December 2026, with eligibility re-checked every six months. Provider-tax limits tighten from FY2028. For outsourcers this cuts both ways. More eligibility checks, redeterminations and appeals mean more work for payer operations and for patient-access teams. But more uninsured patients mean more uncompensated care, weaker hospital finances and harder price negotiations, and rural hospitals, TruBridge's core customers, are the most exposed. A $50 billion rural health fund over five years softens the blow.

Global drugmakers: pressure as a selling point

Indegene's clients are the world's large pharma companies, and their US pricing environment changed sharply in 2026. By 31 August 2026, 26 most-favoured-nation (MFN) pricing agreements covered about 89% of the US branded market; Section 232 tariffs of up to 100% on patented drug imports took effect for 17 large drugmakers on 31 July and for all branded makers on 29 September 2026, with MFN-deal companies exempt. Medicare's first negotiated drug prices took effect on 1 January 2026. Lower net prices squeeze sales and marketing budgets. Indegene's chief executive argues the opposite effect dominates: "Whenever the pharma industry is under pressure, those times are better for us," because clients outsource to cut cost. Structural shifts help too: about 60% of US physicians restrict sales-rep access, and a rep costs about $250,000–300,000 a year, so digital and content operations replace field calls. No hard data on 2026 promotional budgets was found. Indian drugmakers face the same US regime; see our pharma diversified majors report.

Banks, telecoms and retailers

Banks and capital-markets firms (about 41% of eClerx, 33% of Firstsource) buy to cut cost and to keep up with regulation: the US moved to next-day (T+1) settlement in May 2024 and the UK and EU plan to follow around October 2027. Firstsource's mortgage business rises and falls with US refinancing, which picks up when rates fall. Telecom and cable clients (about 21% of Firstsource, 26% of eClerx) are mature and cost-cutting, and their customer-service work is the most exposed to voice AI. Retail and luxury (eClerx's fashion and luxury book, Firstsource's retail CX through Ascensos) are cyclical; fashion and luxury was eClerx's soft spot in FY26.

04

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: a people business with a thin layer of everything else

Staff costs take 55–60 rupees of every hundred; the rest of the margin story is onshore mix, selling costs and how much is billed per hour.

The cost structure of a BPM company is simple to describe and hard to change. People are the product, so employee costs dominate: roughly 60–70% of revenue at a volume customer-experience business and 50–55% at a high-margin healthcare or knowledge platform (INDICATIVE ranges built from our primers; Firstsource describes people as about two-thirds of its cost base). Facilities, telephony, cloud and software licences add another 6–9%. Selling, general and administrative costs (sales teams, account management, corporate overhead, listed-company costs) take 13–21%. What is left is EBITDA, earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.

The differences between members come from three places. The first is onshore mix: an American nurse doing utilisation review, a UK collections agent or a US mortgage processor costs several times an Indian equivalent, and some work must be done onshore for licensing or client reasons. Firstsource carries the most onshore cost, which is why its revenue per head is high but its margin is the lowest. The second is selling and creative cost: Indegene runs agency-style creative teams and acquired US and European agencies, so its SG&A is the highest. The third is price per unit of work, which depends on how specialised the work is and whether it is billed by the hour or by the result.

05

Industry structure: fragmented, global, and owned by private equity

No listed Indian player has more than 2% of the market. The competition is captives, IT majors, global BPM firms and a private-equity roll-up machine.

BPM is a fragmented global industry with low formal barriers to entry and high practical ones. Anyone can open a delivery centre; very few can pass a large US insurer's security audit, hold HIPAA business-associate agreements, run a Medicare Advantage appeals process to the regulator's deadlines or process a drug's adverse-event cases to European standards. The result is a market with thousands of small firms and a few dozen that matter in each niche. The five listed Indian members compete in different niches against different rivals, so "market share" only means something within an archetype.

GenAI and the death of the seat Deep dive

AI agents now sell for $0.50–2.00 per resolved query. Whether that destroys or enriches an outsourcer depends on how its contracts are priced.

The defining question for every BPM company in 2026 is what happens when software can do a large share of the work its people do. The question is no longer theoretical. AI customer-service agents are sold at $0.50–2.00 per resolution: HubSpot cut its price to $0.50 in April 2026 from $1.00, Intercom's Fin charges $0.99, and Zendesk $1.50 on a committed plan or $2.00 pay-as-you-go. An offshore human-handled contact costs roughly $2–6 (INDICATIVE). Large language models now draft clinical notes, suggest medical codes, write first drafts of promotional copy and triage failed trades.

The pricing physics: same AI, three different outcomes

The mechanics are easiest to see in a worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE). Take a 100-FTE offshore claims-processing team billed at $22 an hour for 1,900 hours a year: $41,800 per FTE, or $4.18 million (about ₹39.7 crore at ₹94.9) a year. Each FTE costs the vendor about ₹9 lakh plus 25% overheads, ₹11.25 lakh or roughly $11,850, so the team costs about ₹11.2 crore and earns a gross profit of about ₹28.4 crore ($2.99 million), around 72% before selling and corporate costs. Now an AI agent automates 40% of the transactions.

  • If the contract is FTE-priced, the client simply asks for a 60-person team at renewal. Revenue falls 40% to about ₹23.8 crore ($2.51 million); gross profit falls to about ₹17.1 crore ($1.80 million). The vendor has created the productivity and given all of it away.
  • If it is transaction-priced at about $4.00 a claim on roughly 1.045 million claims, revenue stays at ₹39.7 crore until the unit price is renegotiated. The vendor redeploys 40 people elsewhere and pays about $0.15 million for the AI tools, so gross profit rises to about ₹31.5 crore ($3.32 million), up 11%.
  • If it is outcome-priced, say 4% of $105 million of claims dollars recovered, revenue is ₹39.9 crore ($4.2 million). If AI lifts recoveries by 10%, revenue rises 10% to about ₹43.8 crore ($4.62 million) while the vendor needs fewer people.

In plain terms

If you are paid by the hour and a robot makes your team twice as fast, your client will soon want to pay for half the hours. If you are paid by the job done, you keep the savings until the client asks for a lower price per job. If you are paid a share of the money you recover, a faster robot makes you richer. The same technology can halve one outsourcer's profit and raise another's, depending only on what the contract says.

So far: augmentation, not replacement

The surprising fact of 2025–26 is how little of this shows up in the Indian members' numbers. None of the five yet shows clear AI-driven "non-linearity", meaning revenue growing much faster than headcount. eClerx's FY26 dollar revenue rose 17.9% while its delivery headcount rose 16.9%, almost perfectly linear. Sagility's Q1 FY27 constant-currency growth of 15.2% came with headcount up 18.5% (inflated by the CareSeed acquisition). The only early hint is eClerx's Q1 FY27: dollar revenue up 15.2% with heads up 10.4% and down 0.6% on the quarter. Surveys point the same way. In a Gartner survey of 321 customer-service leaders, only 20% had reduced agent staffing because of AI; 55% kept staffing stable while handling more volume. The industry is at the stage where AI raises output per head inside fixed-price and outcome deals, but cannibalises FTE-billed deals at renewal, and renewals take two to five years to roll through a book.

There is a further twist. AI does not only replace work; it creates work. Payers using AI to deny claims faster create more appeals for providers; providers using AI to code more aggressively create more payment-integrity reviews for payers. CAQH, the US healthcare-admin benchmarking body, reports that more than half of health plans and a quarter of providers already use AI in administrative workflows. That "AI arms race" has so far increased the total volume of US healthcare admin work, not reduced it, which is one reason the healthcare-heavy members are still growing while the global call-centre industry shrinks.

In plain terms

AI is already shrinking the global call-centre business. India's listed outsourcers have avoided the damage so far because most of their work is specialised paperwork rather than phone calls, and because in US healthcare both sides are using AI against each other, which creates more work, not less. The danger is concentrated in contracts that pay per person: those will shrink as each comes up for renewal over the next few years.

US healthcare's admin-cost machine: payer vs provider Deep dive

One side is paid to get money in, the other to control money going out. The same policy change can help one and hurt the other.

The United States spends about $5.3 trillion a year on health care (2024, per the national health expenditure accounts), and administration is commonly estimated at 15–30% of that, roughly $950 billion to $1 trillion across providers and payers (an INDICATIVE figure: McKinsey put it at about $950 billion, or a quarter of spending, in 2021; it was not re-verified for this report). A 2019 JAMA study estimated $266 billion a year of waste from "administrative complexity" alone. That machine, which exists because thousands of insurers and hundreds of thousands of providers argue over millions of claims a day, is the profit pool for three of the five members.

The machine has two sides, and the members sit on opposite ones. Providers (doctors, physician groups, hospitals) are paid to get money in: their outsourcers are judged on how many claims are paid first time, how quickly, and how much of what is owed is actually collected. Payers (health insurers, Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid managed-care organisations) are paid to process and control money going out: their outsourcers are judged on claims accuracy, auto-adjudication rates, appeal turnaround and the overpayments they recover. IKS sits almost entirely on the provider side; Sagility about 89% on the payer side; Firstsource's healthcare book (₹3,209 crore, 34% of its FY26 revenue) spans both.

The payer side: an admin budget capped by law

The medical-loss-ratio rule explains why US insurers outsource so aggressively. If at least 80–85 cents of every premium dollar must go on care, every dollar of administration that can be moved to Chennai at a third of the cost is a dollar of extra margin. That pressure intensified in 2023–25, when medical costs outran premiums and insurers' margins fell for nearly three years; outsourcing was one of the few levers they controlled. Sagility's 23.6% constant-currency growth in FY26 (the fastest among listed global healthcare BPM firms) and its client-group count rising from 82 to 109 in Q1 FY27 (26 of them via the CareSeed acquisition) reflect that push.

The 2027 Medicare Advantage rate notice is a mixed signal for payer outsourcers. Better rates mean healthier insurers, which could ease the pressure that drove outsourcing; but healthier insurers also spend more on growth projects, quality programmes and Star-rating work, which is exactly what CareSeed does. On balance it is positive for volumes in calendar 2027. The more structural risks on this side are concentration (Sagility's top three clients were about 80% of revenue when EQT bought it and are still in the low 60s per cent) and captives, since most large US insurers run their own Indian operations.

The provider side: paid on what is collected

Provider RCM is more attractive in pricing and harder in demand. Contracts tied to a percentage of collections let the vendor keep its productivity gains, which is why IKS earns about 34% EBITDA, the highest in the group. But the clients are financially weaker. Hospitals and physician groups operate on single-digit margins, and the OBBBA's Medicaid cuts (about $1 trillion over ten years, 11.8 million fewer insured by 2034 per the Congressional Budget Office) will raise uncompensated care. Rural and community hospitals, TruBridge's customer base, are the most exposed, partly cushioned by the $50 billion rural health fund. A vendor paid a share of collections earns less when there is less to collect.

The rupee, the margin and the hedge book Deep dive

Revenue is billed in dollars and pounds; most costs are paid in rupees and pesos. A falling rupee should lift margins. In FY27 it has not, and the reasons matter.

Every member bills almost entirely in foreign currency and pays most of its costs in rupees. A weaker rupee therefore does two things at once: it inflates reported revenue growth, and it should widen margins. The rupee has fallen hard. It averaged about ₹88.3 to the dollar in FY26 (implied by Firstsource's and Sagility's own rupee and dollar revenue), about ₹94.5 in Q1 FY27 and stood near ₹94.9 on 19 October 2026, close to a record low near ₹96.8. That is roughly 7% weaker than the FY26 average. The Reserve Bank raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, partly to steady the currency during the West Asia crisis that began on 28 February 2026.

The mechanics of the margin tailwind

The theory is straightforward. If 60–70% of costs are in rupees or pesos and revenue is in dollars, a 1% fall in the rupee lifts rupee revenue by 1% but rupee costs by only 0.3–0.4% (the dollar-denominated onshore share). For a business with a 25% EBITDA margin, that adds about 0.4–0.6 percentage point of margin per 1% of depreciation, before hedges and before clients ask for some of it back (INDICATIVE rule of thumb; the IT-services rule of thumb is 25–35 basis points). Applied to the ILLUSTRATIVE 10,000-FTE vendor used in Section 14, a move from ₹94.9 to ₹96.8 (about 2%) adds ₹49 crore of revenue and ₹7 crore of onshore cost, so EBITDA rises by ₹42 crore and the margin by about 1.2 points.

Hedging explains why the tailwind should arrive later rather than never. Most Indian BPM and IT companies hedge 50–70% of their next twelve months' net exposure, and eClerx historically hedges 12–24 months forward. Firstsource (RPSG, about 53.66%) and eClerx (founders, 54.52%) have stable controlling holders. eClerx's share count is shrinking: it bought back 13.75 lakh shares at ₹2,800 (₹385 crore, completed August 2025) and up to 6.67 lakh at ₹4,500 (₹300 crore, approved 24 October 2025), then issued a 1:1 bonus.

Supply interacts with valuation. The cohort trades at 22–42× trailing earnings, well above global peers. A premium multiple meeting a motivated seller is a combination that tends to cap rallies: blocks are priced at a discount to the market and absorb demand that would otherwise lift the price. As long as EQT holds half of Sagility, that stock's multiple is likely to stay at a discount to its growth. Indegene shows what happens when the overhang clears.

In plain terms

Private-equity funds own shares to sell them. Sagility's main shareholder, EQT, has already sold about a third of the company since listing and still owns about half, so more selling is likely. Each sale tends to hold the share price back for a while. Indegene's private-equity investor has sold out completely, which removes that pressure. Watching who still has shares to sell is as important as watching the results for these 2024 listings.

Rules of the game: the regulators are in Washington, London and Brussels

The rules that shape these businesses are mostly foreign: US health and privacy law, UK conduct rules, EU data and AI law. India's role is tax, labour and data protection.

An Indian BPM company is regulated mainly by its clients' regulators. A US insurer that sends member data to Chennai remains responsible for it under US law, so it passes the obligations down through contracts, audits and business-associate agreements. The most important rules therefore sit in the US, where four-fifths or more of the group's revenue originates, and they fall into three groups: rules that set clients' budgets (the medical-loss-ratio rule, Medicare Advantage rates, Medicaid funding, drug pricing), rules that set how the work must be done (HIPAA, prior-authorisation deadlines, collections conduct rules, AI-voice consent), and rules that could restrict offshoring itself (the HIRE Act, H-1B fees, state call-centre bills).

The most-feared rule is also the least likely. The HIRE Act, introduced by Senator Bernie Moreno in September–October 2025, would impose a 25% excise tax on payments by US companies to foreign service providers and deny a tax deduction for them. If enacted it would upend the economics of every member. It has had no co-sponsors, no hearings and a blocked unanimous-consent attempt; tracking services put its odds of passage near zero. It belongs on the watch list as a low-probability, high-impact risk, most dangerous if attached to a must-pass bill. Services are also outside the scope of US tariffs: the IEEPA tariffs were struck down by the Supreme Court on 20 February 2026, a 10% Section 122 tariff ran until 24 July 2026, and since then a 10% Section 301 tariff on Indian goods has applied on top of most-favoured-nation rates (under legal challenge). None of it applies to services exports. Recurring "Keep Call Centers in America" bills and state limits on offshore handling of Medicaid data are more likely to bite at the margin, for Firstsource and for Sagility's Medicaid managed-care work.

The H-1B fee matters less than headlines suggested. BPM delivery barely uses H-1B visas; the fee mainly affects IT services companies that place Indian engineers at US client sites (see our IT services report). It was vacated by a Massachusetts federal court in June 2026, and the First Circuit refused to stay that ruling.

UK, EU and the Philippines

In the UK, where Firstsource earns a large part of its roughly 31% European revenue, the Financial Conduct Authority's Consumer Duty (July 2023) raised conduct standards for collections and customer service, making compliance capability a selling point. In the EU, the AI Act's obligations for general-purpose AI began in August 2025, with high-risk-system obligations from August 2026–27 (timing under review); that matters for AI in credit and collections and for AI-generated pharma content, relevant to Indegene's ~26% European revenue. GDPR rules on cross-border data transfer apply to any EU data processed in India. In the Philippines, the CREATE MORE Act (November 2024) lets IT-BPM firms in economic zones keep incentives while allowing up to 50% work-from-home, relevant to Sagility's and Firstsource's Manila operations.

India: tax, labour and data

India's own rules are mostly about cost. Special Economic Zone tax holidays have largely expired, and units set up after July 2020 get none, so most profit is now taxed at about 25.2% under the new corporate regime. Exports of services are zero-rated for GST (the GST 2.0 rate changes of 22 September 2025 do not alter that). Transfer-pricing safe harbours for IT-enabled services set the margins Indian subsidiaries must report. The four Labour Codes, made effective on 21 November 2025, changed the definition of wages for gratuity and social-security contributions; Firstsource booked a one-off FY26 charge for them, and others may have absorbed smaller amounts. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and its 2025 rules phase in compliance to 2027. There is no production-linked incentive scheme and no price control.

Twenty-six years, five shocks: how the industry got here

BPM is not cyclical in the commodity sense. It de-rates on technology scares and client shocks, and re-rates on delivered growth.

The listed members were all founded in the first wave of Indian outsourcing. Indegene started in Bengaluru in 1998; eClerx in Mumbai in 2000; Firstsource began in 2001 as ICICI OneSource, ICICI Bank's captive; IKS was founded in 2006. Firstsource and eClerx listed in 2007. The industry then went through five recognisable shocks, each of which reshaped the group.

The financial crisis (2008–12) hit banking clients and volume work, consolidated the industry and handed Firstsource to a new owner: the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group bought control from ICICI in 2012. Post-crisis regulation then created demand for the reconciliation, reporting and reference-data work that eClerx built its franchise on. US healthcare reform (2010–19) created the healthcare specialists. The Affordable Care Act's coverage expansion and medical-loss-ratio rule, and electronic health record mandates, multiplied documentation, coding and claims work; Hinduja Global Solutions built its healthcare arm and IKS grew around Indian physician-support teams. COVID (2020–21) proved work-from-home delivery, lifted healthcare and banking volumes and triggered the private-equity deals that created today's cohort: HGS sold its healthcare arm to Baring PE Asia, and Carlyle and Brighton Park invested in Indegene.

The GenAI scare (2023), after ChatGPT's launch in November 2022, de-rated Indian IT and BPM stocks on fears of automation, even before any revenue effect. It did not stop the 2024 listings, but it set the frame in which every result is now read. The bifurcation (2025–26) is the current phase: voice-heavy CX is shrinking (Concentrix, Teleperformance and TTEC down; the Philippines cutting targets) while domain-data and healthcare operations grow (EXL, Sagility, IKS, eClerx). The listed Indian group has so far been on the right side of that split.

Three lessons from the history are useful today. First, client-specific shocks matter more than macro cycles: a single programme termination at Firstsource in Q1 FY27 produced ₹71.7 crore of exceptional charges and a 10–17% one-day fall, larger than any move caused by Indian interest rates or GDP. Second, technology scares de-rate before they hurt revenue: the 2023 GenAI sell-off came two to three years before any measurable deflation, and multiples tend to recover when growth is delivered. Third, ownership changes come in waves: private equity bought in 2020–22 and sold in 2024–26, and the next wave will depend on whether listed valuations stay above private-market prices.

In plain terms

This industry does not boom and bust with the economy the way steel or cement do. Its share prices fall when a big client leaves or when investors fear a new technology, and recover when the companies keep delivering growth. Since 2023 the fear has been AI; the five Indian companies have kept growing through it so far, while the global call-centre giants have started to shrink.

Reading a BPM company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number a BPO or KPO company reports, what it means, what good looks like, how the five compare, and where the definitions trap the unwary.

A BPM company's results look simple: revenue, a margin, headcount, a few client numbers. They are harder to read than they look, because the same word means different things at different companies, because currency moves distort every rupee figure, and because the most important numbers (how much revenue is not billed per person, how much productivity the company keeps) are rarely disclosed at all. This section builds up from the revenue equation to the individual KPIs, then puts them together in one illustrative delivery centre and a ten-minute checklist for reading quarterly results.

The equation: what management controls and what it doesn't

Revenue in BPM is the sum of three streams. The people stream is billable FTEs multiplied by utilisation, billable hours and the hourly rate. The transaction stream is volume multiplied by price per transaction. The outcome stream is the base on which a fee is earned (money collected, overpayments recovered) multiplied by the fee percentage. Most companies are still dominated by the first stream, so the first flow below shows it. The second flow takes revenue down to EBITDA. Management largely controls utilisation, the cost per FTE and overheads; it partly controls rates and headcount (both are negotiated with clients); it does not control the exchange rate, client volumes or the client's decision to automate.

In plain terms

A BPM company's revenue is people × how busy they are × hours × price × the exchange rate. Its profit is that revenue minus mostly salaries. Management can keep its people busy and control costs; it cannot control the rupee or what clients decide to automate. A 2% move in the rupee is worth about one margin point; losing 10% of people-priced work to AI is worth about four.

Constant-currency revenue growth

What it is. Revenue growth restated at the previous period's exchange rates, so that it measures volume and price rather than currency. Formula: (current-period revenue in each currency × prior-period rates) ÷ prior-period revenue − 1. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a company earned $100 million last year at ₹88.3 (₹883 crore) and $112 million this year at ₹94.5 (₹1,058 crore). Rupee growth is 19.8%, dollar growth 12%. If all revenue is in dollars, constant-currency growth is 12%; the other 7.8 points are the rupee. Companies with pound or euro revenue must also strip out cross-currency moves (dollar versus pound).

What drives it: new clients, scope expansion with existing clients, pricing, and acquisitions (which should be reported separately as inorganic). Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): above 15% is good, 8–15% average, below 8% weak. How the five compare: Sagility grew 23.6% in constant currency in FY26 and 15.2% in Q1 FY27 (14.9% organic); Firstsource 13.6% and 12.3%; eClerx 17.9% in dollars in FY26 and 15.2% in Q1 FY27; Indegene about 18% in dollars in FY26; IKS 12% in dollars in Q1 FY27. FY27 guidance converges on low double digits: Firstsource 10–13% CC, Sagility "low double-digit" organic CC. The trap: companies quote whichever growth rate looks best. Rupee growth is not organic growth, dollar growth is not constant-currency growth when part of revenue is in pounds, and growth including acquisitions is not organic growth. Always ask which of the three a number is.

Non-linearity: revenue growth minus headcount growth

What it is. The gap between constant-currency revenue growth and headcount growth, in percentage points. It is the single best public test of whether AI is changing the economics. Formula: CC revenue growth − headcount growth. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a company grows revenue 15% in constant currency while headcount grows 10%: non-linearity is +5 points, meaning each person produces about 4.5% more revenue than a year ago (1.15 ÷ 1.10 − 1).

What drives it: automation, a shift to transaction or outcome pricing, mix shift to higher-value work, and acquisitions (which can distort both numbers). Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): above +5 points good, 0 to +5 average, below 0 weak. What it is. Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation as a share of revenue, the standard measure of operating profitability. Formula: EBITDA ÷ revenue. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a company with ₹1,000 crore of revenue, ₹720 crore of operating costs and ₹30 crore of share-based pay reports EBITDA of ₹250 crore (25%) but "adjusted EBITDA" of ₹280 crore (28%) if it excludes the share-based pay.

What drives it: offshore mix, utilisation, wage cycles, pricing, scale in SG&A, the rupee and hedges, and acquisition integration costs. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): for healthcare and KPO platforms, above 27% good, 20–27% average, below 20% weak; for volume CX on an EBIT basis, above 13% good, 10–13% average, below 10% weak. What it is. The share of revenue from the largest client, the top five and the top ten. Formula: revenue from the top N clients ÷ total revenue. Offshore mix and geography

What it is. The share of effort or headcount delivered from low-cost locations (India, the Philippines, nearshore hubs) rather than from the client's country. Formula: offshore FTE ÷ total delivery FTE (or offshore revenue ÷ total). Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): an onshore US role might bill about ₹85 lakh a year ($90,000) and cost about ₹71 lakh ($75,000), a 16% margin; the same work done offshore might bill ₹19 lakh and cost ₹11 lakh including overheads, a 42% margin. Moving 100 roles offshore cuts the vendor's revenue on them from about ₹85 crore to ₹19 crore and its profit from ₹14 crore to ₹8 crore, but lifts the margin from 16% to 42%. Offshoring lowers revenue per role and raises margin; the vendor wins only if the lower price brings it more volume.

What drives it: licensing and regulatory needs (US nurses for utilisation review, UK collections staff), client preference, language and time zone. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): 80%+ offshore good for healthcare and KPO, 60–80% average, below 60% weak. How the five compare: most do not disclose a precise mix. The trap: "offshore" sometimes includes nearshore centres (Mexico, South Africa, Romania, Jamaica, Colombia) that cost two to three times India.

Attrition

What it is. The share of employees who leave over twelve months. Formula: leavers in the last twelve months ÷ average headcount (voluntary attrition counts only resignations). Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a 10,000-person operation with 30% attrition loses 3,000 people a year. If recruiting and training a replacement costs about ₹1 lakh and a new hire is half as productive for three months, the direct cost is about ₹30 crore a year, before the hit to service quality.

What drives it: wage competition (GCCs and IT firms hire the same people), the nature of the work (night-shift voice work churns most), career paths and the hiring market. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): for KPO and healthcare, below 15% good, 15–25% average, above 25% weak; for voice CX, below 30% good, 30–45% average, above 45% weak. How the five compare: Firstsource 29.7% (FY26, total, good for a voice-heavy mix); eClerx 18.1% offshore voluntary in Q1 FY27 (down from 21.7%); Indegene 15.7% trailing voluntary to June 2026 (16.35% in its FY26 sustainability report); Sagility and IKS not disclosed in the sources available (the attrition chart is in Section 04). The trap: voluntary and total attrition are different numbers, and companies choose which to report.

Utilisation

What it is. The share of delivery staff who are billable at any time; the rest are in training, on the bench, ramping up or in support roles. Formula: billable FTE ÷ total delivery FTE. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): in the 1,000-FTE centre described below, 85% utilisation produces 17 lakh billable hours and ₹187 crore of revenue; at 80% the same staff produce 16 lakh hours and ₹176 crore, and because costs barely change, EBITDA falls from ₹50.3 crore to ₹39.3 crore.

What drives it: hiring ahead of demand (which lowers it), training cycles, client ramp-downs and transitions (new accounts are often non-billable for weeks). Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): above 85% good, 78–85% average, below 78% weak; healthy BPM operations run at 80–88%. How the five compare: none of the five discloses utilisation consistently, which is itself a data gap; investors have to infer it from revenue per head and margin trends. The trap: very high utilisation is not always good; it can mean the company has no bench to absorb new wins, which shows up later as delayed ramps.

Billing rate per hour and pricing-model mix

What it is. The price charged per billable hour, and the share of revenue earned under FTE, transaction and outcome pricing. Formula: people-priced revenue ÷ billable hours; pricing mix = revenue by model ÷ total. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a billable FTE working 1,900 hours at $16 an hour earns $30,400, or about ₹28.8 lakh at ₹94.9. A 5% rate cut at renewal on a 1,000-FTE account at 85% utilisation costs about $1.3 million, or ₹12.3 crore, a year, almost all of it straight off EBITDA.

What drives it: skill (clinical and regulatory work bills highest), location, competition at re-tender, and productivity give-backs that clients demand at each renewal. Offshore healthcare BPM typically bills $12–25 an hour against $35–60 for fully loaded onshore US staff (INDICATIVE industry norms). Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): a rising share of transaction and outcome pricing (above a third of revenue) is good; mostly FTE-priced books are the most exposed to AI. How the five compare: none discloses a pricing-mix figure. Qualitatively, IKS's collections-linked RCM and Sagility's payment-integrity contingency fees are the most outcome-like; Indegene's per-asset and project pricing is not per seat; Firstsource's collections work includes contingency fees but most of its CX book is FTE-priced; eClerx is mostly FTE and project. The trap: a "transaction-priced" contract with a productivity-sharing clause behaves like an FTE contract at renewal.

In plain terms

The single most useful number a BPM company could publish is how much of its revenue is paid per result rather than per person. None of the five publishes it. Until they do, investors have to piece it together from what management says about contracts and from whether revenue per employee is rising.

Deal wins, TCV and ACV

What it is. Total contract value (TCV) is the value of new contracts signed over their full term; annual contract value (ACV) is the value per year. Deal wins are leading indicators: they become revenue one to three quarters later, after transition. Formula: ACV = TCV ÷ contract years. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a three-year contract worth ₹300 crore has an ACV of ₹100 crore. If a company with ₹4,000 crore of annual revenue signs ₹1,000 crore of ACV in a year and loses 15% of its existing book to attrition and price give-backs (₹600 crore), its net new revenue is about ₹400 crore, or 10% growth.

What drives it: sales coverage, references, the client's own budget cycle and the share of large deals. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): ACV growth above 20% good, 5–20% average, below 5% weak. How the five compare: eClerx disclosed ACV of $40–41 million in Q1 FY27, up 25%, with an annual run-rate above $500 million; Firstsource signed four large deals and 12 new logos in Q1 FY27 and reported a pipeline above $1 billion in FY26; Sagility added 27 client groups in Q1 FY27 (26 of them through CareSeed); Indegene had 105 active clients, 53 above $1 million (FY26) and 12 above $10 million (Q1 FY27); IKS reported 600+ clients in Q4 FY26 and expects to settle at 500–600 after pruning small accounts (our primer's "900+" appears to count older, smaller accounts). The trap: client counts are defined differently at every company: "client groups", "active clients", "clients above $1 million". Compare each company with itself.

DSO, working capital and cash conversion

What it is. Days sales outstanding (DSO) measures how long clients take to pay; cash conversion measures how much of EBITDA becomes operating cash. Formulas: DSO = receivables (including unbilled revenue) ÷ revenue × 365; cash conversion = operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a company with ₹1,000 crore of revenue and ₹200 crore of receivables has a DSO of 73 days. Every ten extra days ties up about ₹27 crore of cash, which on a ₹250 crore EBITDA business is more than a tenth of a year's operating profit.

What drives it: client payment terms (large US payers and pharma companies often impose 60–90 days), billing efficiency, unbilled revenue on milestone projects, and disputes. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): DSO below 60 days good, 60–80 average, above 80 weak; cash conversion above 80% good, 65–80% average, below 65% weak. How the five compare: DSO could not be sourced for any member this time (a gap). On cash conversion, eClerx improved to about 76% of EBITDA in FY26; Indegene's FY26 operating cash flow of about ₹651 crore was about 96% of adjusted EBITDA (162% of reported profit); Firstsource's Q1 FY27 operating cash flow of ₹355.6 crore was about 106% of EBIT. The trap: unbilled revenue (work done, not yet invoiced) is often reported separately from receivables and can hide a rising DSO.

Net collection rate and days in A/R (provider RCM)

What it is. The two outcome measures by which hospitals and physician groups judge an RCM vendor such as IKS. The net collection rate is the share of collectible money actually collected; days in accounts receivable (A/R) is how long, on average, it takes to collect. Formulas: net collection rate = payments ÷ (charges − contractual adjustments); days in A/R = outstanding A/R ÷ average daily charges. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a physician group is owed ₹100 crore after contractual discounts and collects ₹96 crore: a 96% net collection rate. If its outstanding A/R is ₹40 crore and it bills ₹365 crore a year (₹1 crore a day), A/R days are 40. A vendor paid 5% of collections earns ₹4.8 crore; lifting the collection rate to 98% raises the client's cash by ₹2 crore and the vendor's fee by ₹0.1 crore, which is why outcome pricing aligns interests.

What drives it: clean claims, denial management, payer mix (Medicaid pays slower and less), coding accuracy and follow-up discipline. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE, US industry norms): net collection rate above 96% good, 92–96% average, below 92% weak; A/R days below 40 good, 40–50 average, above 50 weak. How the five compare: none of the listed members publishes client-level collection metrics; IKS cites outcomes in case studies, not in results. The trap: the gross collection rate (payments ÷ gross charges) is a meaningless number, because list charges are not what anyone expects to be paid; always ask for the net rate.

Reported vs adjusted PAT

What it is. Reported profit after tax is the statutory figure; adjusted (or normalised) profit adds back items the company considers non-recurring or non-cash, mainly amortisation of acquired intangibles, share-based pay and one-offs. Formula: adjusted PAT = reported PAT + after-tax add-backs; the quality ratio = reported ÷ adjusted. Worked example: in the 10,000-FTE example above, ₹60 crore of acquired-intangible amortisation reduces reported profit to ₹385 crore; adding it back after tax gives about ₹430 crore, so reported is about 90% of adjusted.

What drives it: the size and age of acquisitions, the amortisation period, share-based pay and exceptional items. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): reported above 95% of adjusted good, 85–95% average, below 85% weak. How the five compare: eClerx 100%; IKS and Firstsource about 89%; Indegene about 88%; Sagility 82%. In Q1 FY27 Firstsource's reported ₹165.9 crore compared with ₹222.2 crore adjusted, after ₹71.7 crore of pre-tax exceptionals; IKS reported ₹193.7 crore against ₹215.3 crore adjusted; Sagility about ₹216.8 crore against ₹269.7 crore adjusted. The trap: valuing on adjusted profit makes Sagility look cheap (about 18× instead of 22×); our Sagility primer's "~56× reported P/E" is wrong, as FY26 reported profit was ₹924.8 crore, not about a third of adjusted.

Definition traps, collected

  • Four margin bases. IKS and eClerx report EBITDA; Sagility and Indegene adjusted EBITDA; Firstsource EBIT. Compare like with like or not at all.
  • eClerx's "revenue" includes other income. FY26 ₹4,217.4 crore and Q1 FY27 ₹1,170.2 crore are total revenue; operating revenue in Q1 FY27 was ₹1,152.4 crore. FY25 operating revenue was ₹3,365.9 crore; comparing it with FY26 total revenue gives a spurious 25% growth rate instead of 22.6%.
  • Three growth rates. Rupee, dollar and constant-currency growth differ by 5–12 points in FY26–Q1 FY27. Indegene's Q1 FY27 +39.7% includes three acquisitions.
  • IKS's history breaks twice. FY24 includes AQuity only from October 2023 (FY24 revenue ~₹1,817 crore per the IPO prospectus, INDICATIVE, not the ~₹2,050 crore in our primer); from Q2 FY27 TruBridge roughly doubles revenue. FY26-based multiples are stale from Q2 FY27.
  • Headcount means different things. All employees (Sagility), "people" (Firstsource), delivery staff (eClerx), total and delivery (Indegene), an undated global team (IKS).
  • Client counts are not comparable. Client groups, active clients, clients above $1 million, and IKS's 600+ (not 900+).
  • Seasonality. Sagility's Q4 (Jan–Mar) is seasonally strong (US open enrolment and Medicare Advantage enrolment) and Q1 seasonally weak; a QoQ dip in Q1 is not a slowdown.
  • Reported vs adjusted profit. Gaps of 11–18% at four members; earn-out revaluations now pass through exceptionals (Firstsource Q1 FY27).
  • Hedge losses inside revenue. eClerx's implied ~₹91.5 to the dollar in Q1 FY27 against ~₹94.5 for peers suggests hedge losses booked in operating revenue (INDICATIVE inference).
  • Net debt bases differ. Sagility's ₹2,168 crore (Mar-25) and "$71.5 million TTM Dec-25" cannot both be right on one basis; IKS's post-TruBridge debt is an estimate.

How to read a BPO/KPO company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Constant-currency (or dollar) revenue growth, year on year and quarter on quarter. This is the true volume-and-price signal. Compare it with guidance (Firstsource 10–13% CC; Sagility low double digits). Red flag: rupee growth headlined, CC buried.
  2. Organic vs inorganic. Strip out acquisitions (TruBridge at IKS from Q2 FY27; BioPharm, Warn and Cake at Indegene; CareSeed at Sagility). Red flag: no organic figure given.
  3. Headcount, QoQ and YoY, on the company's own definition. Compute non-linearity (CC growth minus headcount growth). Red flag: headcount growing faster than CC revenue for two quarters.
  4. Margin on the company's own basis, against guidance and against the same quarter last year. Q1 carries wage hikes; Q4 often carries seasonal peaks. Red flag: margin below the guided range with no one-off explanation.
  5. Reported vs adjusted profit and the exceptional items. Read the exceptionals note: contract terminations, indemnities and earn-out revaluations tell you about client risk. Red flag: exceptionals in consecutive quarters.
  6. Client metrics. Top-client and top-ten shares, clients above $1 million or $10 million, new logos. Red flag: a falling count of large clients, or a rising top-client share without a new large win.
  7. Deal wins and pipeline. ACV or TCV, large deals, ramp timing. Red flag: "program terminations" or "client-specific softness" language.
  8. Attrition. Voluntary, trailing twelve months, and its direction. Red flag: a sharp rise ahead of the wage cycle.
  9. Cash and balance sheet. Operating cash flow against EBITDA, DSO if given, net cash or debt, and the hedge book (amount and average rate). Red flag: profit growth with flat or falling cash flow.
  10. Management's AI language, checked against the numbers. Any disclosure of revenue from transaction or outcome pricing, AI-led deals or productivity give-backs. Red flag: "AI-led wins" with no change in revenue per head.
In plain terms

When a BPM company reports, start with growth in constant currency, not in rupees, and take out acquisitions. Then compare that growth with headcount growth to see whether AI is helping. Then check the margin against what management promised, read the small print on one-off charges, and look at whether big clients are staying. Those five checks take ten minutes and tell you most of what matters.

Technology beyond the chatbot: the other disruptions

AI agents are the headline. AI-native rivals, EHR vendors, content automation, settlement reform and cyber risk matter as much to individual members.

Section 07 dealt with the pricing physics of AI. This section looks at the specific technologies and competitors that threaten (or help) each member, because the AI risk is not one risk but several, and they arrive at different speeds in different niches.

Four stages of automation in a BPM process
Stage 1 · 2015–22
Rules and RPA
Bots move data between screens; humans do every judgement. Vendors resell it as productivity.
→
Stage 2 · 2023–25
GenAI copilots
Models draft notes, codes, emails, content; humans review. Output per head rises.
→
Stage 3 · 2025–27
Agents with human QA
Agents complete whole tasks; humans handle exceptions and audit. Seat count falls.
→
Stage 4 · later
Autonomous process
Software owns the outcome; the vendor sells a platform and guarantees results.
ILLUSTRATIVE staging. Simple voice queries are already at Stage 3; clinical documentation and coding are moving from Stage 2 to 3; payer appeals, drug-safety cases and complex trade breaks are mostly at Stage 2. Vendors that reach Stage 4 with outcome pricing keep the value.

AI-native competitors in healthcare documentation and coding are the most direct threat to IKS. Ambient-AI scribes (Abridge, Microsoft's Dragon Copilot built on Nuance DAX, Suki, Ambience) listen to the doctor-patient conversation and draft the clinical note, the job IKS's documentation teams were built to do. Autonomous-coding companies (Fathom, CodaMetrix) target medical coding. IKS's answer is a hybrid ("AI drafts, humans validate and code") that it argues delivers higher accuracy and audit protection; it was also an investor in Abridge, selling its stake for $10.0 million in August 2026.

EHR vendors moving into revenue-cycle management are the second provider-side threat. Epic, Oracle Health and athenahealth are adding AI billing and coding features inside the software hospitals already use, which amounts to insourcing via software. IKS's purchase of TruBridge is partly a defence: owning the EHR of rural and community hospitals puts IKS inside the workflow rather than outside it.

Pharma content automation threatens and helps Indegene. Generative AI can draft promotional assets within medical-legal-regulatory workflows, and Veeva and others are adding AI agents to the tools pharma companies use. If content becomes cheap to produce, per-asset pricing falls; if regulatory review remains the bottleneck, the value shifts to whoever runs the review and compliance process. Indegene's revenue per employee (about $75,000) is the number that will show which way it goes.

Capital-markets automation works on eClerx: next-day settlement (T+1 in the US since May 2024, the UK and EU around October 2027), reconciliation software, distributed-ledger pilots and language-model exception handling all reduce manual breaks. eClerx's response is its own automation platform, Roboworx CogniFlows, used by about 15,000 staff, and its first large agentic-AI client win in Q4 FY26.

Voice AI threatens Firstsource's communications and retail customer-service work and Sagility's member and provider call centres. Firstsource has moved fastest to cannibalise itself (UnBPO in February 2025, an Agentic AI Studio, a stake in Lyzr.ai, the "Intelligence That Operates" platform with Kairos in March 2026, and AI collections agents live at a large US financial-services client); Sagility uses BirchAI's conversational models.

Cyber and data-security risk is the tail risk that applies to all five. The February 2024 ransomware attack on Change Healthcare disrupted US claims processing for weeks and showed how much of the healthcare system runs through a few vendors. Security credentials are a barrier to entry for smaller rivals, but a breach at any member could cost it clients overnight.

Bull vs bear: specialists that AI needs, or seat sellers with a premium multiple

The same evidence supports both cases. Which one wins depends on contract structure, US health budgets and how fast non-linearity shows up.

The bull and bear cases for the sub-sector rest on the same facts read differently. The bull reads twelve to fifteen per cent constant-currency growth through the most AI-exposed period in the industry's history as proof that domain-specialist work is resilient; the bear reads it as a lag before contract renewals pass the productivity gains to clients. Both sides agree that FY26 was a strong year and that the rupee flattered it.

The bull case

  • Specialists, not seat sellers: most of the group's revenue is US healthcare, pharma and capital-markets work where regulation, audit and liability keep humans in the loop; growth of 12–15% CC in Q1 FY27 while Concentrix and Teleperformance shrank shows it.
  • The AI arms race creates work: payers and providers automating against each other has so far increased US admin volume; CAQH counts $21 billion of further savings still to capture.
  • Outcome pricing and owned software: IKS's collections-linked fees and TruBridge EHR, Sagility's payment-integrity contingencies and eClerx's platform let productivity gains stay with the vendor.
  • Policy wind for payers: the 2027 MA rate rise (~5% all-in) and Medicaid redetermination work support payer outsourcing into FY28.
  • A delayed currency tailwind: hedge books rolling at ₹94–95 should lift margins in FY28; balance sheets are mostly strong (ex-IKS net cash) with buybacks at eClerx.
  • Supply overhang clearing: Carlyle is out of Indegene; EQT has already sold 31 points of Sagility.

The bear case

  • Renewal-cycle deflation: FTE-priced contracts (most of the industry) pass AI productivity to clients at renewal over two to five years; no member yet shows clear non-linearity to prove otherwise.
  • Growth is slowing and flattered: FY27 guidance converges on low double digits; half of Q1 FY27's 26% rupee growth was currency and acquisitions.
  • Premium valuation: about 30× reported earnings against single-digit to low-teens for global peers, with growth converging.
  • Concentration and client shocks: Indegene's top client is 31%, eClerx's top ten 60%; Firstsource's Q1 FY27 termination shows how fast a client can cost 10–17% of market value.
  • Leverage and integration: IKS's TruBridge adds about ₹5,000 crore of net debt (INDICATIVE) and doubles exposure to rural hospitals facing Medicaid cuts.
  • Sellers and earnings quality: EQT still owns 51% of Sagility; reported profit is 82–89% of adjusted at four members.

The balance of evidence in October 2026 favours the specialists over the generalists: the healthcare and data names have grown through the AI shock so far, and the clearest damage is in voice CX, which is a minority of the group's revenue. But the bear case's central point, that FTE-priced revenue will be repriced at renewal, has not been disproved; it simply has not arrived yet in the numbers. The resolution will come through three measurable things: the gap between constant-currency growth and headcount growth, any disclosure of the share of revenue that is not FTE-priced, and margins once the hedge books have rolled. Valuation is the swing factor. At about 30× reported earnings, the group needs low-to-mid-teens earnings growth for several years to justify its price; a slowdown to global-peer growth rates would leave the multiple exposed.

For individual stocks the cases differ. Sagility combines the best policy wind with the lowest multiple and the heaviest seller overhang; IKS the best margin with the highest multiple and new leverage; Indegene the cleanest ownership with the highest concentration; eClerx the cleanest earnings with a concentrated, cost-cutting client base; Firstsource the most AI exposure with a self-cannibalisation strategy that is either early or expensive.

In plain terms

Optimists say these companies do specialised work that AI will help rather than replace, and that US healthcare paperwork keeps growing. Pessimists say most contracts still pay per person, so clients will demand lower prices as AI makes staff more productive, and that the shares already price in years of strong growth. So far the optimists have been right on growth; the pessimists' test comes as contracts renew over the next few years.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • ACV / TCV: annual / total contract value of new deals signed.
  • Adjusted EBITDA / adjusted PAT: company-defined profit excluding items such as share-based pay, acquisition amortisation and one-offs.
  • A/R days: days in accounts receivable; how long a provider takes to collect what it is owed.
  • BPM / BPO / KPO: business-process management (the industry), business-process outsourcing (transactional work), knowledge-process outsourcing (judgement-heavy work).
  • CC (constant currency): growth restated at last year's exchange rates.
  • CMS: Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the US federal health-insurance agency.
  • CX: customer experience; contact-centre and customer-service work.
  • DSO: days sales outstanding; receivables ÷ revenue × 365.
  • EHR: electronic health record software used by hospitals and doctors.
  • FTE: full-time equivalent; one person-year of billable work.
  • GCC: global capability centre; a multinational's own captive operation in India.
  • HIPAA / BAA: the US health-privacy law and the business-associate agreement a vendor signs to handle patient data.
  • Medicare Advantage (MA): private health plans that replace traditional Medicare for about half of US beneficiaries.
  • MLR (medical-loss ratio): the share of premium a US insurer must spend on care and quality (80–85%).
  • Net collection rate: payments collected ÷ collectible amount after contractual discounts.
  • Non-linearity: revenue growing faster than headcount.
  • OBBBA: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of July 2025, which cut federal Medicaid funding.
  • OFS: offer for sale; existing shareholders selling shares in an IPO or later.
  • Payment integrity: checking insurer claims for overpayments, often paid as a share of savings.
  • RCM: revenue-cycle management; everything a provider does to get paid.
  • Utilisation: billable staff ÷ total delivery staff.
