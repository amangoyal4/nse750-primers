00

The one-page view: four miners, one giant and a very large landlord

Three unrelated businesses share this label. One company is most of the money, and the state takes more of its ore than its owners do.

"Industrial minerals" in the NSE 750 means four companies and three quite different businesses. NMDC, the Government-controlled iron-ore miner, digs about one tonne in every six that India produces. MOIL, also state-owned, and Sandur, a family-controlled Ballari miner, sell manganese ore into the ferro-alloy furnaces that every steel plant depends on; Sandur also mines iron ore and has bolted on alloys, coke and a speciality-steel maker. Ashapura is barely a domestic miner at all: about four-fifths of its revenue comes from shipping Guinean bauxite to Chinese alumina refineries, with India's largest bentonite (swelling clay) business as a smaller, steadier core.

Read any "sector" number with care, because it is mostly an NMDC number. In FY26 NMDC was 73% of the group's ₹43,866 crore of revenue, 85% of its ₹8,791 crore of profit after tax and 78.5% of its ₹76,776 crore market value. Strip NMDC out and what remains is a ₹11,795 crore revenue group earning an 18.5% operating margin, with net debt rather than net cash. This report shows that ex-NMDC line wherever it changes the answer.

Five forces explain almost everything that happens to these companies, and the report is built around them.

  • Who owns the rent. NMDC mines iron ore at Bailadila for roughly ₹810 a tonne. In FY26 it then paid ₹2,206 a tonne to the state in royalty, a 150% "additional royalty" on its extended leases, the District Mineral Foundation levy and the exploration trust levy: ₹11,082 crore in all, more than its own ₹10,737 crore of company-defined EBITDA. A 2024 Supreme Court ruling let states add mineral taxes on top; Karnataka has a ₹15,786 crore claim pending against NMDC; and the MMDR Amendment Act 2026 now tries to shut that door (Section 7).
  • Volume against the railway and the market. NMDC mined a record 53.16 million tonnes in FY26 and targets 60 in FY27 and 100 by FY30. Its constraint is evacuation: one hill railway carrying 28–30 mtpa out of Bailadila, a slurry pipeline commissioned only on 27–28 September 2026, and a line doubling due in December. In H1 FY27 it produced 27.27 mt and sold 22.23 mt (Section 8).
  • The cycles have split. India mined 313 mt of iron ore in FY26 against a steel-sector requirement of roughly 300 mt. In H1 FY27, hot metal output (the ore-consuming step) grew 2.5% while hot-rolled coil prices rose 27% and the seaborne 62% Fe price fell about 17% from April. Steelmakers are earning the margin; miners are holding the stock (Section 3).
  • The manganese squeeze. India imported 6.46 mt of manganese ore in calendar 2025, far more than MOIL's 1.91 mt. The landed price of South African and Gabonese ore sets MOIL's and Sandur's prices, and MOIL's profit moves 30–70% on 5–10% price changes (Section 9).
  • Clay at home, bauxite abroad. Ashapura earns about $6.3 a tonne of EBITDA on Guinean bauxite worth $60–72 a tonne delivered to China. Its returns depend on ports, freight and a Guinean tax shield that lasts "one or two years" more (Section 10).

The market's verdict is blunt. On 22 October 2026 NMDC traded at ₹68.5, MOIL at ₹220, Sandur at ₹162 and Ashapura at ₹434, each within one per cent of its 52-week low. The four together were valued at 8.7 times FY26 earnings and about 6.4 times enterprise value to operating profit. NMDC alone had fallen 26% from about ₹92 on 1 June 2026. No single news event explains that fall. The evidence points to a combination: soft seaborne prices, five million tonnes of unsold ore in six months, ₹11,160 crore owed to NMDC by two state-owned steel companies, and the Karnataka contingent claim. Two facts frame everything that follows. First, the geology is good. NMDC's Bailadila ore grades 64–65.5% iron, above the 62% seaborne benchmark, at a time when only 11% of India's output reaches 65% Fe. Its company-reported reserves exceed 2,300 million tonnes, and its cash mining cost is a fraction of an Australian producer's. MOIL has 122 mt of reserves and resources. Second, almost none of that advantage reaches the shareholder by default. It is shared, under rules the shareholder does not write, with the Union and state governments, with railways, with the steelmakers who buy the ore, and in Ashapura's case with Guinean ports and Chinese refineries. Valuing these companies means following that sharing out.

In plain terms

Owning NMDC means owning the right to dig some of the best iron ore in India and keep a minority share of what it sells for. In FY26 the state took about ₹39 of every ₹100 of NMDC's ore revenue (ex-trading) before costs; NMDC kept about ₹33 as operating profit; and then paid tax and a 41% dividend, 61% of which also went to the Government. The geology is excellent. The sharing rules decide the return.

01

How a tonne becomes money: lease, dig, sort, move, sell

The value chain is short. Value is captured at the lease and at the railhead, not at the plant.

Mining is a short value chain. A company holds a lease from a state government, digs rock out of the ground, crushes and screens it into saleable sizes, moves it to a buyer, and is paid by the tonne. There is no brand and little product differentiation beyond grade and size. Three numbers decide the economics: how many tonnes can be moved and sold, what each tonne fetches, and how much of that price is owed to someone else before the miner sees a rupee.

Iron ore, NMDC's business, is the clearest example. At Bailadila in Chhattisgarh, the Kirandul and Bacheli complexes blast and shovel haematite in mechanised open pits. The ore is crushed and split into lump (10–40 mm, about 65.5% Fe), which can be charged straight into blast furnaces and sponge-iron kilns, and fines (below 10 mm, about 64% Fe), which must first be sintered or pelletised. NMDC then sells free-on-rail at the mine. The buyer pays the freight, so NMDC's price competes with every other source of ore once rail costs are added. In Karnataka, NMDC's Donimalai and Kumaraswamy mines sell mostly through court-monitored e-auctions.

Manganese works differently because the ore is scarcer and the buyers are smaller. MOIL runs about ten mines in Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh, most of them underground shafts in the Balaghat–Nagpur belt. It sells graded ore at prices it revises monthly to silico-manganese and ferro-manganese smelters in central India. Those smelters cannot use Indian medium-grade ore alone: their furnaces need a blended feed of about 33–37% manganese. So they mix MOIL or Sandur ore with high-grade ore imported from South Africa, Gabon or Australia. Imported ore lands at Visakhapatnam, Paradip or Kakinada and costs at least ₹1,500 a tonne more to bring inland. That freight gap is the domestic miner's only natural protection.

Ashapura's chain has two branches. In Gujarat's Kutch district it mines bentonite and other clays, dries, activates and grinds them, and sells them to foundries, iron-ore pellet plants, drillers, civil contractors and pet-litter makers. Bentonite is the binder that holds iron-ore pellets together, so even this clay business is tied to the steel chain. In Guinea, Ashapura mines bauxite near Boffa, barges it down river, loads it onto large ocean-going bulk carriers at its own port and jetty, and ships it to refineries in Shandong. Port throughput and ocean freight decide the profit; the geology matters much less.

Where is value captured? Mostly at the lease. In FY26 NMDC sold its ore for an average of about ₹5,657 a tonne excluding the one-off steel trading (Motilal Oswal's net sales realisation). It spent roughly ₹1,600 a tonne on mining, processing, staff and logistics. The remaining ₹4,050 or so is economic rent: a return on scarce, high-grade ore that the company did not create. The state took ₹2,206 of it, before income tax. The shareholder's operating profit was about ₹1,843 a tonne (screener operating profit ÷ tonnes sold). For manganese, value goes to whoever owns medium-grade ore close to central India's smelters, but the import price sets the ceiling. For Ashapura's bauxite, value is captured in logistics: port capacity, barging and freight contracts.

In plain terms

A mine is a licence to sell rock that nature made. The miner's own contribution — digging, sorting and moving the rock efficiently — is worth real money but is the smaller part of the price. The bigger part is rent on the deposit itself, and in India the lease-owner, the state, has steadily raised its share of that rent.

02

313 million tonnes: a record year and a surplus

India mines more iron ore than ever, with capacity far above output. Manganese is the opposite story: domestic supply falls well short.

India mined a record 313 million tonnes of iron ore in FY26, up 7.9% from about 290 mt. The Government of India confirmed the figure in its August 2026 backgrounder on the MMDR Amendment Act, matching BigMint's estimate. At realised prices of ₹4,500–5,500 a tonne, the domestic market is worth roughly ₹1.4–1.7 lakh crore a year (an indicative range, not a reported figure). India is the world's fourth-largest producer, behind Australia (about 950 mt), Brazil (about 440 mt) and China, against global output of roughly 2.5 billion tonnes.

Capacity is not the constraint. Environmental-clearance (EC) capacity rose 10.1% to 521 mtpa in FY26, so the industry ran at only 60.1% utilisation, down from 61.3%. Merchant miners, who sell to third parties, produced 188 mt (up 13%) on 289 mt of EC capacity. Captive mines, owned by steelmakers for their own furnaces, produced a flat 125 mt on 232 mt of capacity. Never compare EC capacity with production: EC is a legal ceiling, and the gap reflects rail, forest clearances, state production caps and, increasingly, demand.

Geography is concentrated. Odisha produced 158 mt, about half the national total, on 278 mt of capacity. Chhattisgarh, home to NMDC's Bailadila, produced 54 mt (up 23%). State matters because each state sets its own forest fees, transit permits and, after the 2024 Supreme Court ruling, potentially its own mineral taxes.

Quality is drifting down, and that matters for NMDC. The share of Indian output grading 65% Fe or better fell to 11% in FY26 from 20% in FY17. Ore of 60–62% Fe rose to 20% of output from 14%, and sub-58% grades to 20% from 13%. Odisha's newer auctioned mines and Karnataka's legacy dumps are lower grade. High-grade, low-alumina ore has become scarce at home, which is why coastal steel mills now import it, and why NMDC's Bailadila deposits are worth more than their tonnage suggests.

Manganese is the mirror image: India is short of ore. National output, from the Ministry of Mines' monthly series, runs at roughly 4.0–4.3 mt a year (an indicative annualisation; no official FY26 total was found). MOIL's 1.91 mt makes it about 45% of domestic production. Imports were 6.46 mt in calendar 2025, up from 6.21 mt, and well above the 5.5 mt five-year average MOIL's management cites. Total consumption is therefore about 10.7 mt (indicative), and MOIL supplies only about 18% of what Indian furnaces use. India's reserves are modest: 75 mt of reserves and 504 mt of total resources (IBM, 1-Apr-2020). MOIL alone holds 53.47 mt of reserves plus 68.50 mt of resources, a combined 121.97 mt as of April 2025.

Ashapura's markets are harder to size. Bentonite is a "minor mineral" regulated by the states, so there is no national production series. Gujarat's output is reported as "NA" in the Indian Minerals Yearbook. India holds about 583 mt of bentonite resources, 73% of them in Rajasthan and 25% in Gujarat. Ashapura claims about 35% of the domestic market, over 60 mt of reserves in Kutch and a 12–14% share of the global bentonite market (company claims). Guinea exports about 200 mt of bauxite a year, mostly to China. Ashapura's ~8 mt makes it a mid-sized exporter there, around 4% of Guinea's shipments. The company's own claim of "~15% of the global bauxite export market" looks inflated and should not be repeated without that caveat.

In plain terms

India has more iron ore than it can sell at good prices, and less manganese than its furnaces need. That one asymmetry explains why NMDC's problem in 2026 is finding buyers and railway paths, while MOIL's problem is competing with a ship from South Africa.

03

Steel decides demand, and the steel and ore cycles have split

Steel uses about 98% of India's iron ore and 90% of its manganese. In 2026 the steelmakers took the margin while the miners took the stock.

Demand for everything these companies sell, except Ashapura's bauxite, comes from steel. Steel takes about 98% of India's iron ore and about 90% of its manganese. Bentonite goes into iron-ore pellets and foundry moulds for auto castings. So the starting point is India's steel cycle. In FY26 India made 168.4 mt of crude steel (up 10.7%) and consumed 163.7 mt of finished steel (up 7.6%). Installed capacity reached 222.9 mtpa by September 2026, against a National Steel Policy target of 300 mtpa by 2030–31. Every tonne of hot metal from a blast furnace needs roughly 1.5–1.6 tonnes of iron ore, which puts the steel sector's FY27 ore requirement at about 300 mt.

That is the first problem: supply already exceeds demand. India mined 313 mt in FY26, and BigMint expects more than 330 mt in FY27, against a requirement near 300 mt. Exports, once the release valve, are closing. India's exports of ore and pellets fell 15% to 25.78 mt in FY26, with China taking 24.21 mt of it. In September 2026 exports fell to just 0.7 mt, the lowest in about four years. A 30% export duty on lump and fines of 58% Fe or more makes high-grade exports uneconomic unless seaborne prices are high. NMDC's chairman put it plainly on the June 2026 call: "With a 30% export duty, it doesn't make too much of a sense."

The second problem is that steel growth has moved away from the ore-consuming step. In H1 FY27 (April–September 2026), crude steel output rose only 2.9% to 85.1 mt and hot metal, the blast-furnace step that actually eats ore, rose 2.5% to 48.3 mt. Finished-steel consumption grew 7.5%, and India turned a net importer of finished steel in the half (4.14 mt imported against 3.55 mt exported). Meanwhile hot-rolled coil sold for ₹76,085 a tonne in September 2026, up 26.8% year on year, helped by the safeguard duty on flat-steel imports. Steelmakers were enjoying high prices without needing much more ore.

The seaborne market added pressure. The benchmark 62% Fe fines price, delivered to China, was $109.95 a tonne at the end of April 2026. It slipped to about $99–100 in mid-July and $96.6 in late September, and fell to $90.95 on the Singapore exchange on 5 October, the lowest since September 2024. China's crude steel output fell 3.6% year on year in July. By the end of September only about 7% of Chinese mills were profitable and Beijing was again calling for output cuts. When China buys less, Australian and Brazilian ore seeks other homes, and Indian coastal mills can import it.

Indian mills did import. Iron-ore imports almost doubled to 12.35 mt in FY26 from 6.49 mt, the highest since 2018. JSW Steel alone brought in 10.02 mt, mostly Brazilian fines landed on the west coast at Jaigarh and on the east coast at Krishnapatnam. At the coast, mills are short of high-grade, low-alumina ore, and the landed price of Brazilian fines sets a new ceiling on what Indian high-grade ore can charge there. NMDC owns exactly that grade, but delivers it by rail into central and eastern India. Its plans for a Visakhapatnam blending yard and a doubled hill railway are, in effect, bids to displace those imports at the coast.

In plain terms

In 2026 steel was a good business and iron ore was not. Steel prices rose by a quarter while the furnaces that consume ore grew only 2.5%. NMDC dug 23% more and sold no more, and the extra ore went onto stockpiles at the mine. When the miner's customers make more money without needing more of the miner's product, the miner has no pricing power.

Manganese demand follows the same steel volume but with its own lag. Manganese is used at roughly 10 kg of alloy per tonne of steel, and India's 300 mt steel ambition implies about 11 mt of manganese ore a year by 2030, roughly 37 kg of ore per tonne of steel (MOIL's estimate). The near-term driver is the margin of central India's silico-manganese smelters. That margin swings with power costs and with Chinese alloy export prices; when it is thin, smelters cut back and push hardest on ore prices.

Ashapura's demand drivers are outside India. Chinese alumina refineries import about 200 mt of bauxite a year, roughly 70% of it from Guinea. Ashapura's management says four new Chinese refineries will add 20–30 mt of bauxite demand, but alumina prices were "under pressure" in August 2026. Guinea also expects to introduce export quotas before the end of 2026. Those could support prices but cap volumes for smaller exporters. In India, bentonite demand tracks foundry output (auto castings), pellet-making (pellets use about 0.5–1% bentonite by weight), drilling and civil works. Its export business to the Middle East and Europe was hurt by the 2026 freight spike that followed the West Asia war.

In plain terms

Three different customers set the demand side: Indian blast furnaces for iron ore, Indian alloy smelters for manganese, and Chinese refineries for bauxite. Only the first is truly domestic, and in 2026 it was the weakest of the three for ore volumes.

At NMDC the state is the largest cost. At MOIL it is labour. At Sandur it is steel raw materials. At Ashapura it is freight and ports.

The four members share a label but not a cost structure. The quickest way to see the difference is to follow ₹100 of revenue through each company's profit and loss account. For NMDC, the largest line is not mining at all. It is the bundle of royalty and levies the company pays to the Union and state governments because it holds extended Government leases. For Sandur, consolidated, the largest line is raw materials for its Arjas steel mills. For Ashapura it is the Guinean logistics chain: barging, port handling, ocean freight and Guinean royalties.

NMDC is the largest merchant seller, but captive steel-company mines, Odisha's auctioned leases, imported ore and seaborne giants all shape its price.

Who owns the rent: royalty, the 150% premium, the 8:1 judgment and Section 9D Deep dive

At NMDC the state already takes more than the shareholder. The fight since 2024 has been over whether states can add yet another layer.

Every mineral in India belongs, in law, to the state. A mining company buys the right to extract it and pays for that right in several layers. Understanding those layers is the single most useful thing an investor in this sub-sector can learn, because a change in any one of them moves NMDC's profit more than a year of cost-cutting. This section explains the layers, what NMDC actually paid in FY26, how the Supreme Court reopened the question in 2024, why Karnataka's claim against NMDC has reached ₹15,786 crore, and what the MMDR Amendment Act 2026 does and does not change.

The layers of the state's take

The first layer is royalty. For iron ore it is 15% of the average sale price (ASP) that the Indian Bureau of Mines publishes each month, state by state. For manganese ore the statutory rate is about 5% of ASP (a rate stated from memory, not re-checked against the MMDR Second Schedule). Royalty goes to the state where the mine sits. The second layer, for leases granted before 2015, is the District Mineral Foundation levy: 30% of royalty, spent on mining-affected districts (10% of royalty for auctioned leases). The third is the National Mineral Exploration Trust levy, 2% of royalty, renamed the National Mineral Exploration and Development Trust (NMEDT) by the 2026 Act.

The fourth layer is the one most investors miss. When the MMDR Act was amended in 2015, privately held merchant leases granted before then were set to lapse in March 2020 and be re-auctioned. Government companies such as NMDC had their leases extended instead. The 2021 amendment fixed the price of that extension: an "additional amount" equal to 150% of royalty, which works out to 22.5% of ASP. NMDC's FY26 annual report books it as "Additional Royalty (22.50% premium)". It is the largest single cost line in the company.

The 8:1 judgment: royalty is not a tax

For decades the working assumption was that royalty under the Union's MMDR Act was the states' main return from minerals, and that it limited their power to tax mining further. On 25 July 2024 a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court overturned that assumption by an 8:1 majority in Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India, with Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissenting. The Court held that royalty is a contractual payment, not a tax. It held that the Constitution's State List lets states tax mineral rights (Entry 50) and mineral-bearing land (Entry 49), and that the MMDR Act does not cap that power. On 14 August 2024 the Court let states recover such taxes back to 1 April 2005, payable in twelve annual instalments starting 1 April 2026. It waived interest and penalties on demands for the period before 25 July 2024. Review petitions were dismissed in October 2024; the Centre filed a curative petition.

For a miner, this created a new and unbounded liability: any state could legislate a mineral-rights tax, a land tax, or both, retrospectively to 2005. Odisha and Jharkhand had no such tax on the day of the judgment. Jharkhand later introduced a mineral-bearing-land tax, with iron ore reportedly rising from ₹100 to ₹400 a tonne (an indicative, single-source figure). The Government's backgrounder noted that some states had begun taxing mineral-bearing land "at rates as high as 20 percent". Large steelmakers disclosed big contingent amounts: Tata Steel ₹17,350 crore and JSW ₹4,690 crore. Coal India and other PSUs were estimated at ₹70,000–80,000 crore (Zerodha's analysis, an indicative estimate).

Karnataka's claim against NMDC

Karnataka moved fastest against iron ore. In December 2024 its legislature passed the Karnataka (Mineral Rights and Mineral Bearing Land) Tax Bill. It levies a land tax of ₹100 a tonne on iron ore, bauxite, manganese and other minerals from April 2005, and a rights tax from January 2015 that, for non-auctioned iron-ore mines, equals three times royalty. Because it touches a subject on which central law also operates, the Governor reserved it for the President's assent. It has been awaiting that assent since.

NMDC's two Karnataka mines, Donimalai and Kumaraswamy, are exactly the non-auctioned iron-ore leases the Bill targets. NMDC therefore discloses the potential liability as a contingent, not as a provision. The amount was ₹13,975 crore on 1 April 2025, ₹15,482 crore on 31 March 2026 and ₹15,786 crore on 30 June 2026. It grows by roughly ₹1,200–1,500 crore a year. The FY26 increase of ₹1,507 crore works out to about ₹97 a tonne on Karnataka's 15.6 mt of output, close to the Bill's ₹100 a tonne land-tax rate (an indicative inference, not a company statement). The claim equals about 26% of NMDC's market value and about two years of NMDC's FY26 profit.

The MMDR Amendment Act 2026: Section 9D

The Union responded with legislation. Parliament passed the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Bill 2026 (the Rajya Sabha on 13 August 2026), and the President assented on 17 August 2026, making it Act 20 of 2026. Its new Section 9D says state governments cannot impose any tax, cess or other levy on mineral rights or mineral-bearing land, except under conditions the Centre prescribes by rule. Any such levy not deposited or recovered before the section commences is "deemed invalid at all material times", notwithstanding any court order. Amounts already collected are not refundable. The Act came into force on 22 August 2026 according to one secondary source; the Gazette notification itself was not seen, so treat the commencement date as indicative until confirmed.

  • What 9D blocks: new state taxes on mineral rights and mineral land, including Karnataka's retrospective Bill and Jharkhand's land tax. If 9D commenced before Karnataka's Bill receives assent, NMDC's ₹15,786 crore contingent should fall away.
  • What 9D does not touch: royalty, the 150% additional amount, DMF, NMEDT and auction premiums. The Government's own backgrounder celebrates these as the system working. NMDC's existing ₹2,206 a tonne is unchanged.
  • What else the Act does: removes the 50% cap on captive-mine sales; allows a one-time lease-area expansion for deep-seated minerals; lets lessees add critical minerals such as lithium, graphite, nickel and cobalt to a lease without extra payment; permits sale of old mine dumps; renames NMET as NMEDT; and creates an authority to regulate mineral exchanges.
  • What it leaves to the states: minor minerals, including clays such as bentonite. Ashapura's Indian franchise gets no 9D protection.
  • What comes next: constitutional challenges by mineral-rich states are likely, because 9D overrides a nine-judge bench on a State List subject. The Mines Minister said no fiscal-impact assessment was made.

The net effect is easy to state and easy to overstate. Section 9D removes the risk that NMDC's state take rises further through new state taxes. It does nothing to the existing take, which already exceeds NMDC's own EBITDA. The additional royalty alone, ₹5,875 crore in FY26, was 79% of NMDC's standalone PAT. Even if 9D survives every challenge, the ceiling on NMDC's margin stays where it is. For NMDC's shareholders, the most valuable policy change would be a cut in the 150% additional amount, and nothing suggests one is coming.

A simple sensitivity shows how this shapes earnings. Because royalty, additional amount, DMF and NMEDT together are about 42% of ASP, roughly 42% of any rise in the price NMDC charges flows to the state, provided IBM's ASP follows NMDC's price. A ₹100 a tonne increase across about 55 mt of FY27 sales adds ₹550 crore of revenue but only about ₹320 crore of operating profit and ₹240 crore of PAT, about 3% of FY26 PAT (an illustrative calculation). The state also absorbs 42% of any price cut, which cushions the downside. That symmetry is one reason NMDC's earnings have been steadier than the ore price.

In plain terms

The 2026 Act shuts the door on the state taking a bigger slice of NMDC's tonne. It does not give back any of the slice the state already takes, which at NMDC is the larger part of the profit pool. Both bullish and bearish readings overstate the Act if they forget that.

In plain terms

Because the state takes about 42% of the price, NMDC's profit swings less than the ore price in both directions: when the price rises ₹100, NMDC keeps about ₹58 before tax and the state takes about ₹42. That makes NMDC a lower-beta play on iron ore than its tonnes suggest.

MOIL's recent history is a lesson in why volume growth is not the same as value growth in a price-taking business. The 3.5 mt plan and the shafts

MOIL wants to produce about 3.5 mt a year by 2030, nearly double FY26, lifting its share of India's requirement from about 20% to about 32%. That requires environmental clearance to rise from 36.33 LT to 50 LT (about 5 mt of EC in total), and five shaft-sinking projects worth ₹664 crore: three at Dongri Buzurg and one each at Kandri and Chikla. At Balaghat, its deepest mine, a high-speed shaft to 650 m, with the ore body proven to 1,000 m, should take output from about 3.5 LT to as much as 8 LT in five to six years. Capex runs at about ₹325 crore a year, more than MOIL's FY26 operating cash flow of ₹157 crore, so free cash flow was negative ₹169 crore. MOIL is also forming a 51% joint venture with Madhya Pradesh's state mining corporation (MOIL MPSMCL Mining, shares issued 1 August 2026) and awaits a lease with GMDC in Gujarat.

The near-term target looks out of reach. Management set 25 LT for FY27, up 31%. H1 FY27 production was 9.61 LT, up 1.8%, so H2 would need about 15.4 LT, roughly 60% above H2 FY26 (an indicative calculation). September's 1.81 LT was up 19% year on year, which helps, but not enough. MOIL also faces a structural grade issue. About 1.3 mt of its output is "prime" ore near the 33–37% furnace blend and about 0.5 mt is lower grade. It has been upgrading and blending the low grades, and sales of its lowest grades rose about 50%. That keeps tonnes moving but pulls the average price down.

In plain terms

MOIL sells a product its customers can also buy from a ship, at a price set by that ship's owner, from mines whose costs do not fall when prices do. When the price rises 10% its profit rises 70%; when the price falls its profit collapses. Digging more of lower grades has not changed that, which is why five years of record output came with lower profits.

Sandur: the miner that is also its own customer

Sandur sits on both sides of the squeeze. It is India's second-largest manganese miner, with 0.599 mtpa of capacity and low-phosphorus ore from semi-mechanised Ballari mines. In FY26 it produced 0.59 mt, sold 0.34 mt externally (up 93%) and used 0.61 LT in its own ferro-alloy furnaces, which can make 1,25,000 tpa of ferro-manganese and 95,000 tpa of silico-manganese. In Q1 FY27 it sold 0.97 LT of manganese ore (up 99%) at ₹7,954 a tonne and 16,292 tonnes of ferro-alloys (up 162%) at ₹71,005 a tonne.

That integration is a partial hedge. Making one tonne of silico-manganese takes roughly 2.0–2.2 tonnes of manganese ore plus coke, quartz and 3,500–4,500 kWh of power (illustrative input norms). When ore prices rise, Sandur's mines earn more and its furnaces earn less; when ore prices fall, the reverse. The segment data show which side has been winning. In FY26 ferro-alloys earned ₹10 crore of PBT on ₹377 crore of revenue (2.7%), after losing money in FY25, while mining earned 52%. Sandur's standalone EBITDA margin (mines, alloys, coke and power) was 43% in Q1 FY27, against 26% for the consolidated group including Arjas steel.

In plain terms

For Sandur, a tonne of manganese ore is worth about ₹8,000 sold raw and helps make a ₹71,000 tonne of alloy if smelted. That sounds like a ninefold mark-up, but most of the extra price pays for coke, power and labour. In FY26 the furnaces kept less than 3% of their revenue as profit. Most of Sandur's margin is still made in the mine.

The rulebook beyond tax: leases, caps, duties and clearances

Who may mine, how much, for how long and where it may be sold are all set by the state, usually by several arms of it at once.

Section 7 dealt with how much the state takes. This section covers the other rules, which decide whether a tonne can be mined, moved and sold at all. Some are national: the MMDR Act's lease and auction regime, export duties and customs duties. Others are local: Karnataka's court-imposed production ceiling, its e-auction system, mining-plan caps set for individual mines, and forest and environmental clearances. A miner can lose more volume to these rules than to any market downturn. NMDC's Kumaraswamy mine, for example, ran under an 8.6 mt plan cap in FY26 against 10 mt of rated capacity.

Prices themselves are not regulated. NMDC and MOIL both set prices administratively each month, guided by international prices, competitor offers, demand and logistics, but no statute fixes them. A mining company's output and life are set mostly by permits, not machines. A court caps Karnataka at 50 mt; a mining plan caps Sandur at 4.45 mt; an export duty decides whether NMDC's best ore can leave India; and a lease date decides how many years of a 2,300 mt deposit NMDC actually owns. Read the permits before the production guidance.

Seven decades, six shocks: how the cycle has run

Booms came from China, busts mostly from Indian policy. The members' histories are shaped as much by courts and duties as by ore prices.

All four companies are old. Sandur was incorporated in 1954 by the Ghorpade family, the former rulers of Sandur. NMDC was set up by the Government of India in 1958, and in 1968–77 built the Bailadila mines and the Kirandul–Kottavalasa railway to supply Japanese steelmakers. Manganese Ore (India) Ltd, now MOIL, was formed in 1962 by the Union and the governments of Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh; it listed in December 2010. Ashapura was founded in 1960 and incorporated in 1982. Their histories since then have been shaped by six shocks, of which only two came mainly from markets.

The pattern is clear. Booms arrive from outside, almost always from Chinese steel demand. Busts are often made in India: court bans that closed whole districts, an export duty imposed overnight to fight inflation, a lease-expiry date that took 300 mines offline, a Supreme Court ruling that let states tax back to 2005. For an investor, that means the policy calendar matters as much as the ore price. It also means the members' worst years were not their weakest markets. NMDC's FY23 profit fell 41% because the Government closed the export window, not because China stopped buying.

Each member wears its history differently. The current downswing is the sixth shock, and it is unusual in two ways. It is not a Chinese demand collapse, since China's buying has weakened but not crashed, and it is not an Indian policy shock, since the 2026 Act, if anything, reduces policy risk. It is an Indian supply response. Record merchant output, extra captive ore and imported high-grade fines arrived just as Indian steelmakers shifted growth towards finished-steel processing rather than more hot metal. In plain terms

Indian mining's worst years have usually been policy accidents, not market crashes: a ban, a duty, a lapse date or a court ruling. The 2026 slump is different. India is simply producing more ore than its steelmakers want, and the biggest producer still plans to add more.

Reading a miner: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number that matters in Indian mining, how it is built, what good looks like, how the four compare, and where the definitions trap the unwary.

A mining company's results are simpler than a bank's or a software company's. Almost everything flows from a short equation: how many tonnes were sold, at what price per tonne, less what each tonne cost to mine and what the state took from it. The complications are in the definitions. "Price" can mean a posted list price, an average realisation or a broker's net sales realisation, and NMDC's list prices have switched between including and excluding royalty twice since 2021. "Volume" can mean production or sales, and in 2026 the two parted company. "EBITDA" can include or exclude other income, trading revenue and inventory credits. This section works through the equation and then each native KPI in turn.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). Take a merchant iron-ore miner that sells 50 mt a year at ₹5,600 a tonne. Revenue is ₹28,000 crore. Mining, processing, staff and other operating costs come to ₹1,600 a tonne, or ₹8,000 crore. EBITDA is ₹28,000 − ₹11,750 − ₹8,000 = ₹8,250 crore, or ₹1,650 a tonne and a 29% margin. NMDC's FY26 numbers are close to this example: about 50 mt at ₹5,657, levies of ₹2,206 a tonne and operating profit of ₹1,843.

In plain terms

A miner's profit is tonnes sold times the gap between price and what each tonne costs, including what it owes the state. At NMDC the state's slice of each tonne is bigger than the miner's own cost, so the price per tonne and the levy rules matter far more than squeezing mining costs.

1. Production and sales volume (mt, LT)

What it is. Production is run-of-mine ore dug and processed in the period; sales (despatches) are tonnes delivered to customers. Iron ore is quoted in million tonnes (mt); MOIL quotes manganese in lakh tonnes (LT; 1 LT = 0.1 mt). Formula: revenue from ore = tonnes sold × average realisation; production matters only once sold. Worked example: NMDC sold 50.24 mt in FY26 at a net sales realisation of about ₹5,657 a tonne, giving about ₹28,420 crore of ore revenue (indicative). Drivers: mine capacity and plan caps, evacuation (rail, pipeline, road), permits, customer demand, and the monsoon. Q2 (July–September) is the seasonal trough for open-cast mining and Guinean shipping.

Benchmarks: sales growth above 10% a year is strong for a mature miner, 3–10% average, below 3% weak. How the members compare: NMDC grew sales 13% in FY26 but 0% in H1 FY27; MOIL's sales were flat in FY26 (15.89 LT vs 15.88 LT) and up 4% in Q1 FY27; Sandur's iron-ore sales were 4.10 mt in FY26 and up 13% in Q1 FY27, with manganese sales nearly doubling; Ashapura's bauxite more than doubled to about 8 mt. The trap: companies headline production. NMDC's "record" Q1 FY27 production of 15.12 mt sat beside sales of 11.73 mt. Always read sales first.

3. Realisation per tonne (NSR)

What it is. The average revenue the miner actually received per tonne sold, across all grades, sizes and customers. 4. List price versus realisation: the price-basis trap

What it is. NMDC posts monthly list prices for Bailadila lump (65.5% Fe, 10–40 mm) and fines (64% Fe, below 10 mm), free-on-rail at the mine. They are the most-quoted iron-ore prices in India. The trap is the basis. NMDC's notices from 2021 to April 2023 quoted prices excluding royalty, DMF and NMET. By October 2024, and still in January and October 2025, they quoted prices including those levies. In January 2026 NMDC "reverted to a base-price model", excluding them again. So the October 2025 lump price of ₹5,550 (inclusive) and the September 2026 price of ₹5,400 (exclusive) are not comparable. Grossed up by about 1.20 for royalty, DMF and NMEDT (assuming ASP is close to the list price), ₹5,400 exclusive is about ₹6,450–6,500 inclusive: above October 2025, not below it (an indicative conversion).

Worked example: lump at ₹5,400 + 15% royalty (₹810) + DMF at 30% of royalty (₹243) + NMEDT at 2% of royalty (₹16) ≈ ₹6,470 a tonne inclusive-equivalent. Fines at ₹4,500 ≈ ₹5,390. Against October 2025's ₹5,550 and ₹4,750 inclusive, that is a rise of 17% for lump and 14% for fines. This is consistent with Q1 FY27 realisation rising 17% quarter on quarter. Drivers: NMDC sets its list price looking at seaborne prices, competitors' offers, demand and logistics. Comparison: MOIL's monthly list is grade-wise and its basis is stable. Benchmark: realisation should run at or above the posted list price on a gross-of-levy basis; NMDC's Q1 FY27 ₹5,548 against list prices of ₹5,250–5,700 exclusive fits that.

5. Lump/fines mix and grade (% Fe, % Mn)

What it is. Iron ore is sold by size and iron content. Lump (10–40 mm) goes straight into furnaces and commands a premium; fines need sintering or pelletising. Grade is the share of iron (% Fe) or manganese (% Mn) in the ore. Formula: blended realisation = lump share × lump price + fines share × fines price; lump premium = lump realisation ÷ fines realisation − 1. Worked example: in Q1 FY27 NMDC realised ₹6,540 for lump and ₹4,991 for fines, a 31% premium. Its blended ₹5,548 implies a lump share of about 36% of tonnes sold (an indicative back-calculation: (5,548 − 4,991) ÷ (6,540 − 4,991)).

Drivers: geology, crushing practice, and demand from DRI kilns (which need lump or pellets) versus sinter plants. In manganese, the furnace blend of 33–37% Mn sets the value of each grade. Benchmarks: a lump premium of 30%+ is strong, 20–30% normal, below 20% weak. Grades of 64%+ Fe and 44%+ Mn are premium. Comparison: NMDC's Bailadila ore (64–65.5% Fe) sits in a niche that is only 11% of Indian output; Sandur's iron ore realises ₹3,095 a tonne, a sign of much lower grade; MOIL's mix is about 1.3 mt of "prime" ore near the furnace blend and 0.5 mt of lower grades. The trap: a rising lump share flatters realisation without any price change. Compare lump and fines prices separately.

What it is. The cost of mining and processing a tonne, excluding statutory levies, depreciation and interest. Formula: (operating cost − royalty and levies) ÷ tonnes produced or sold; state which. Drivers: strip ratio, haul distances, diesel and power prices, wage settlements, contractor rates and scale.

7. Statutory levies per tonne (royalty, additional amount, DMF, NMEDT)

What it is. Everything the miner pays the state per tonne for the right to the mineral, before income tax. Formula: (royalty + additional amount or auction premium + DMF + NMEDT) ÷ tonnes sold. For an extended Government iron-ore lease, about 42.3% of IBM's ASP; for an auctioned lease, royalty plus DMF at 10% of royalty plus the bid premium, which in Odisha often exceeds 100% of ASP. Worked example: NMDC's FY26 total of ₹11,082 crore ÷ 50.24 mt = ₹2,206 a tonne: royalty ₹781, additional royalty ₹1,169, DMF ₹234, NMEDT ₹21. In Q1 FY27 it was ₹2,980 a tonne.

Drivers: the statutory rates, IBM's monthly state ASP (which lags market prices), and lease type. Benchmarks as a share of ASP: below 20% good (royalty-only captive lease), 20–45% average (extended PSU lease at about 42%), above 60% weak (auctioned at a premium above 100%). Comparison: NMDC about 39% of ex-trading revenue; Sandur about ₹1,090 a tonne of ore sold (₹482 crore ÷ 4.44 mt, indicative, includes other taxes); MOIL about ₹620 a tonne (₹99 crore ÷ 1.59 mt), or 6.7% of revenue. The trap: NMDC books levies as both revenue and expense, so its realisation and its margin both depend on levy accounting. 9. Strip ratio and mining cost

What it is. In an open pit, the strip ratio is tonnes of waste rock removed per tonne of ore; in an underground mine, the equivalent pressures are depth, haulage distance and dilution. Formula: waste moved ÷ ore mined. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a pit with a strip ratio of 1.5 that pays ₹150 a tonne to move material spends ₹375 a tonne of ore on haulage alone (2.5 tonnes moved × ₹150). If the ratio rises to 2.5 as the pit deepens, that becomes ₹525, a ₹150 increase that comes straight out of EBITDA per tonne. Drivers: deposit shape, pit depth, and how much low-grade material must be set aside.

Benchmarks: below about 1.5 is low for iron ore and above 3 high, though this varies widely by deposit (indicative ranges). Comparison: none of the four members discloses a strip ratio. The nearest proxies are cost per tonne and depth: NMDC's Bailadila mining cost fell from about ₹1,000 to about ₹810 a tonne as volumes rose; MOIL's Balaghat shaft goes to 650 m on an ore body proven to 1,000 m, and deeper mining raises cost per tonne. The trap: falling cost per tonne during a volume ramp can come from spreading fixed costs, not from easier geology. Check whether it holds when volume flattens.

10. Reserves, mine life and lease expiry

What it is. Reserves are economically mineable tonnes under a recognised code; resources are a wider, less certain inventory. Mine life is reserves divided by annual output. Formula: reserves ÷ production; then compare with the years left on the lease. Worked example: Sandur holds 137 mt of iron-ore reserves and may mine 4.45 mtpa, about 31 years of ore; its leases run to 2033, about 7 years. MOIL's 53.47 mt of reserves cover about 28 years at 1.91 mt a year, or 64 years including resources (121.97 mt). NMDC reports access to over 2,300 mt of 64%+ Fe reserves, about 43 years at FY26 output, against Bailadila leases that expire in 2035–2037.

Drivers: exploration (MOIL added 16.07 mt in FY25), price (higher prices turn resources into reserves), and law. Benchmarks: more than 30 years of reserve life is good, 15–30 average, below 15 weak. But the binding figure is the shorter of reserve life and lease life. The trap: a long reserve life is worth little if the lease ends first and renewal terms are unknown. NMDC's 2021 extension cost 150% of royalty for the life of the lease.

11. Capacity, EC limits and evacuation

What it is. Three different ceilings: rated mine capacity (what the equipment can do), the legal limit (EC capacity or the mining plan, MPAP), and evacuation capacity (what rail, pipeline, road or port can carry). The lowest one binds. Formula: utilisation = output ÷ the binding limit. Worked example: NMDC produced 53.16 mt against 64.8 mtpa of EC (82%), but Bailadila's rail evacuation of 28–30 mtpa was the real limit for its Chhattisgarh ore. The slurry pipeline adds 15 mtpa and the line doubling about 10 more. Drivers: clearances, rail projects, port expansions and court caps.

Benchmarks: 85%+ utilisation of the binding limit is efficient; 60–85% average; below 60% means the limit is elsewhere (usually demand). Comparison: Sandur runs at about 98% of its legal iron-ore and manganese caps, so its mining growth is capped. MOIL uses about 52% of its EC, because its shafts, not its permits, limit output. Ashapura ships about 8 mt through ports that can export about 18 mt. The trap: "capacity" in a press release may be EC, rated or evacuation capacity. India's 521 mt of iron-ore EC tells you nothing about how much ore can reach a customer.

13. Capex per tonne of capacity

What it is. The capital needed to add one tonne of annual capacity, including mines, beneficiation and evacuation. Formula: project capex ÷ annual tonnes added. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): NMDC's guided capex of about ₹27,000–36,000 crore for FY27–FY30 (₹6,000 crore, then ₹7,000–10,000 crore a year), set against roughly 35 mtpa of new capacity (65 to 100), implies ₹7,700–10,300 per tonne of annual capacity. At EBITDA of ₹1,800 a tonne, that is a pre-tax payback of about four to six years, before any price decline. The ₹5,427 crore slurry system cost about ₹3,620 per tonne of annual transport capacity (15 mtpa). MOIL's plan to add about 1.6 mt by 2030 at about ₹325 crore a year implies roughly ₹8,000–10,000 per tonne (indicative).

Drivers: greenfield versus brownfield, beneficiation needs, rail and port spend, and land and forest costs. Benchmarks (indicative): a payback under five years at mid-cycle prices is good, five to eight years average, longer weak. The trap: payback must fit inside the lease. NMDC's ₹30,000 crore of expansion capex will be five to seven years into payback when the Bailadila leases expire in 2035–37. Treat capacity bought without lease certainty as partly an option.

14. Net cash and dividend payout

What it is. Net cash is cash and equivalents minus borrowings; payout is dividends as a share of profit. Together they show what a miner does with its rent. Formula: net cash = cash equivalents − borrowings; payout = DPS ÷ EPS. Worked example: NMDC held ₹11,387 crore of cash equivalents and ₹6,407 crore of borrowings at March 2026, net cash of about ₹4,980 crore (JM Financial uses ₹5,243 crore). Its FY26 dividend was ₹3.50 a share (₹2.50 interim plus ₹1.00 final; FY25 ₹3.30), about ₹3,077 crore and a 41% payout. Screener's default "net debt" of ₹5,035 crore subtracts only investments, not cash, and is wrong.

Benchmarks: net cash is ideal for a cyclical miner; net debt below 1.5× EBITDA is acceptable; above 2.5× is weak. Payout above 40% is generous, 20–40% average, below 20% low. Comparison: MOIL has had zero borrowings in every year FY21–FY26 and ₹711 crore of cash (down from ₹2,830 crore in March 2015 after buybacks and dividends), with a 41% payout (₹5.33 a share). Sandur is net cash standalone (₹93 crore) but carries consolidated net debt: ₹911 crore on screener's March 2026 basis, and ₹375–940 crore at June 2026 depending on how its presentation's ambiguous 0.27 debt-equity label is read (about ₹375 crore is the likelier reading; indicative). It pays out 4%. Ashapura has net debt of ₹1,115 crore (2.0× EBITDA) and pays out 7%. The trap: "net-debt-free" claims are often standalone; check the consolidated balance sheet.

In plain terms

The two state miners hand back about two-fifths of their profit each year and owe little. The two family companies keep almost everything and borrow to grow. Neither choice is wrong, but investors pay very differently for them, and a cyclical miner with debt has less room when the cycle turns.

15. Import parity price (manganese)

What it is. The cost of landing an equivalent tonne of imported ore at the buyer's furnace, which caps what a domestic miner can charge. Formula: seaborne $/dmtu × Mn grade × exchange rate + duty + port and inland freight. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): Comilog's September 2026 offer of $4.90 a dmtu for 44.5% ore is $218 a tonne, about ₹20,690 at ₹94.9 per dollar. Adding 2.5% duty and at least ₹1,500 of extra inland freight gives about ₹22,700 a tonne delivered to central India, or about ₹510 per unit of manganese. Drivers: seaborne offers, the rupee, duty (nil for ≥46% Australian ore since June 2026) and freight.

Sensitivity (ILLUSTRATIVE): South32's $0.30 a dmtu cut on 37% ore in September is $11 a tonne, about ₹1,050. That is most of MOIL's ₹1,500 freight advantage on comparable grades, wiped out by one month's offer. A 5% weaker rupee does the opposite, adding about ₹1,000 a tonne to the landed price of 44% ore. Benchmark: a domestic price at or below parity minus the freight gap is sustainable; above it, smelters switch to imports. Comparison: MOIL's October 2026 increase of 5% came as seaborne offers fell, so its premium to parity widened. The trap: compare per manganese unit, not per tonne; a tonne of 30% Mn ore is not cheap just because its price per tonne is half that of 44% ore.

16. Freight per tonne (Ashapura)

What it is. For a seller paid on a delivered (CIF) basis, ocean freight is a cost inside its price. For a seller paid on an FOB basis, freight comes off the buyer's price and hence off what the seller can charge. Either way, a long-haul bulk exporter's margin depends on it. Formula: EBITDA per tonne = delivered price − freight − port and barging − mining − royalties and duties. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): the Chinese index for Guinean bauxite rose from about $61 to about $72 a tonne in 2026, and management said that rise was "predominantly" freight. Ashapura's unit EBITDA rose only from $5.9 to $6.3, so almost all of the $11 went to shipowners and costs. At 10 mt a year, every $1 a tonne is about ₹95 crore of EBITDA. At FY27's guided 9–11 mt and $6.3 a tonne, Guinean EBITDA would be about ₹540–660 crore.

Drivers: Capesize charter rates, bunker fuel (spiked by the 2026 West Asia war), voyage length and port efficiency. Benchmarks: a freight-exposed exporter earning more than $10 a tonne has a cushion; $5–10 is normal; below $5 leaves little. Comparison: NMDC and MOIL sell free-on-rail or at the mine, so the buyer pays freight; their exposure is indirect, through competitiveness against imports. The trap: a higher delivered price can mean higher freight, not a better market. Judge Ashapura on dollars per tonne, not on revenue.

19. Definition traps, collected

  • NMDC FY26 revenue includes one-off trading. ₹32,071 crore consolidated includes about ₹3,000–3,900 crore of HR coil traded for NMDC Steel; ex-trading revenue was about ₹28,420 crore (+19%, indicative broker figure), not +34%.
  • Three NMDC EBITDAs. Company EBITDA ₹10,737 crore includes ₹1,488 crore of other income; screener operating profit is ₹9,260 crore; Motilal Oswal's ex-trading figure is ₹9,030 crore. NMDC's FY25 EBITDA was about ₹8,150 crore (34%) on screener's basis, not the ~₹9,850 crore (41%) sometimes quoted.
  • List-price basis. Exclusive of levies in 2021–April 2023 and from January 2026; inclusive from at least October 2024 to October 2025. 2026 prices are not cuts from 2025.
  • Realisation versus revenue per tonne. NMDC's "average domestic realisation" is gross of levies; JM's ₹6,378 FY26 "realisation" includes trading.
  • Inventory credits. NMDC's Q1 FY27 41% margin included a ₹1,094 crore stock-build credit.
  • Net cash. Screener's default NMDC "net debt" ignores ₹11,387 crore of cash; net cash is about ₹4,980 crore. Sandur's "net-debt-free" is standalone only; its consolidated borrowings were ₹999 crore.
  • Growth by acquisition. Sandur's +62% FY26 revenue is mostly the first full year of Arjas Steel.
  • Consolidated versus standalone pages. MOIL's consolidated screener page is stale (data to FY20); use standalone.
  • PAT bases. Ashapura's Q1 FY27 PAT is ₹115 crore (company) or ₹108 crore (screener). Its 6% tax rate reflects a Guinean investment-recovery provision.
  • Manganese tonnes are not comparable. MOIL reports all grades in lakh tonnes; Sandur consumes part of its ore captively; MOIL's ₹9,749 and Sandur's ₹7,954 reflect different grades, not efficiency.
  • Production bases. India's 313 mt is production; 521 mt is EC capacity; ~300 mt is the steel sector's requirement. Never mix them.

20. How to read a mineral miner's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Tonnes sold, then tonnes produced. Pull both and divide. A ratio below 0.85 means stock is building; find out why (rail, auctions, demand).
  2. Average realisation per tonne, by product. Compare with last quarter and last year, and with the list-price notices of the quarter. For NMDC, check the price basis (inclusive or exclusive of levies).
  3. Royalty, levies and additional amount per tonne. If they rise faster than realisation, the state's share is growing (IBM ASP lag or a rate change).
  4. Change in inventories. Remove any stock-build credit from EBITDA before judging the margin.
  5. Other sales and trading. Strip one-offs (NMDC's HR-coil trading) from revenue and margin.
  6. EBITDA per tonne. Compute it on screener's basis (excluding other income) and compare with the FY26 base (NMDC ₹1,843, MOIL ~₹2,612).
  7. Other income and receivables. Rising interest income from overdue customers is not quality income. Check NMDC Steel and RINL dues, and Ashapura's debtors.
  8. Capex and borrowings. Compare quarterly capex with guidance (NMDC ₹6,000 crore in FY27) and watch whether borrowings rise while cash is high.
  9. Contingent liabilities. Read the auditor's emphasis paragraph: NMDC's Karnataka claim, Dantewada compensation, transit-pass penalty; any new state levy for Sandur.
  10. Guidance and the next month's price. Check the monthly production release and price notice that follow the quarter. Red flags: sales ÷ production below 0.85 for two quarters, levies per tonne rising faster than price, receivable days above 100, and a cut in volume guidance (Ashapura cut its FY27 range to 9–11 mt in August).
In plain terms

Start every results season with two numbers: tonnes sold and price per tonne. Then ask how much of that price the state kept and whether the profit includes ore that was dug but not sold. Those four answers explain most quarters for all four companies.

Sandur is the best-run mine with the shortest lease. Ashapura has the most growth and the thinnest margin. Choosing between them means choosing which risk you are paid to carry.

India's largest iron-ore miner, 60.79% owned by the Government, mining 64–65.5% Fe ore at Bailadila (Chhattisgarh) and in Karnataka. FY26 revenue of ₹32,071 crore includes one-off HR-coil trading for NMDC Steel; ex-trading it was about ₹28,420 crore (+19%, indicative). Screener operating profit was ₹9,260 crore; company EBITDA of ₹10,737 crore includes other income. Its edge is world-class, cheap-to-mine ore (about ₹810 a tonne at Bailadila). Its burden is the state's take: ₹11,082 crore of levies in FY26, including ₹5,875 crore of "additional royalty". Q1 FY27 brought record production of 15.12 mt but sales of only 11.73 mt, and H1 sales were flat at 22.23 mt. It paid ₹3.50 a share for FY26 (₹3.30 for FY25) and yields about 5.2%.

India's largest manganese-ore miner, owned 64.68% by the Union and the governments of Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh (the Union alone about 53%). It mines mostly underground in the Balaghat–Nagpur belt and also makes EMD (India's only producer), ferro-manganese and wind power. FY26 was a down year: production a record 19.07 LT but sales flat at 15.89 LT, revenue ₹1,470 crore (−7%) and PAT ₹267 crore (−30%). Q1 FY27 PAT then rose 70% to ₹87.6 crore on a 10% higher realisation, before price cuts in July and August and a 5% rise in October. Its levies are light (6.7% of revenue, no additional amount); its problem is grade and cost in a market priced off South African and Gabonese ore, with a ₹1,500 a tonne inland-freight moat. Management targets 3.5 mt by 2030 and 25 LT in FY27; H1 FY27 production of 9.61 LT (+1.8%) makes the FY27 target look out of reach. It has no debt and ₹711 crore of cash, but free cash flow was −₹169 crore in FY26.

India's largest bentonite and specialty-clay company and a fast-growing exporter of Guinean bauxite to China. Promoters (the Shah family) own 48.09%. FY26 revenue nearly doubled to ₹5,237 crore as Guinean shipments rose from 3.5 mt to about 8 mt. Guinea made about ₹4,200 crore of revenue and ₹561 crore of EBITDA; India ₹998 crore and ₹112 crore. Profit is lightly taxed because Guinea levies no income tax until the investment is recovered, "at least the next one or two years"; group current tax was ₹29 crore on ₹445 crore of PBT. Sandur's arc: from mine to MedTech

Sandur deserves a further word, because its strategy is the sub-sector's most open question. For most of its 72 years it was a mine. Over the past decade it added ferro-alloy furnaces, a coke plant with waste-heat power, and in 2024 Arjas Steel's two SBQ mills at Tadipatri (Andhra Pradesh) and Mandi Gobindgarh (Punjab). Arjas has since added a Garret coiler and a new ingot-casting facility. It also plans a 1.0 mtpa integrated steel plant in Karnataka, at the environmental-clearance stage. In 2026 it adopted a "Royal Sandur Group" brand and began forming MedTech, hospitality and academy companies.

The FY26 segment numbers are the strongest argument for caution. Mining's ₹863 crore of PBT exceeded the whole group's ₹827 crore. The downstream layers together, after ₹212 crore of finance cost largely tied to the Arjas purchase, added roughly nothing (indicative), while steel holds two-thirds of segment assets. The argument for the strategy is that the mine's life is legally capped at 2033, so turning ore rent into durable manufacturing businesses before then is rational. The market's verdict so far is sceptical: the stock fell 41% from its 52-week high to ₹162 on 22 October 2026, the day the MedTech land lease was announced.

In plain terms

Sandur's mine earns more than the whole company. Everything built on top of it has so far roughly paid for its own debt and no more. The family's bet is that by 2033, when the leases end, those other businesses will stand on their own.
