00

The one-page view: a grid boom measured in MVA, priced in multiples

Demand is long and real; the open question is how long supernormal margins and customer-funded working capital survive the capacity now being built.

India's heavy electrical equipment makers build the hardware that moves electricity: the power transformers and reactors that step voltage up and down, the switchgear that isolates faults, the converter stations that carry bulk power over direct current, and, at the generation end, the boilers, turbines and generators that make it. Thirteen of them sit in the NSE 750. Together they reported ₹1,05,611 crore of revenue over the latest four quarters and are valued at ₹7,96,675 crore, about 66 times their combined trailing profit.

Three things make this cycle unusual. First, it is led by substations, not wires. India added a record 1,13,013 MVA of transformation capacity in FY26, while it built only 12,139 circuit-km of new lines, less than half the FY16 peak. Each kilometre of new line now carries about nine times as much transformer capacity as it did a decade ago, so the equipment makers have had a far bigger boom than the line builders. Second, the boom arrived when global supply was short. Large transformers in Europe and North America now take two to three years to deliver, and Indian 765 kV units take 20 to 24 months. That scarcity let suppliers raise prices, collect large advances and pick their orders, which is why GE Vernova T&D India's EBITDA margin went from 3.7% in FY23 to 27.1% in FY26. Third, thermal power has returned. The government now plans at least 97 GW of new coal and lignite capacity by FY35, and BHEL, the near-monopoly domestic supplier of boilers, turbines and generators, is sitting on an order book of about ₹2.6 lakh crore, seven times its annual revenue.

The listed universe splits into four groups. Five MNC subsidiaries (Hitachi Energy India, Siemens Energy India, Siemens Ltd, GE Vernova T&D India and Schneider Electric Infrastructure) own the high-voltage technology and increasingly export to their parents. Two domestic heavy engineers (BHEL and Thermax) execute large power and industrial projects. Four transformer and grid-component makers (Transformers & Rectifiers, Atlanta Electricals, Voltamp and Quality Power) sell units into utility and industrial tenders. Two rotating-machine specialists (Triveni Turbine and TD Power Systems) make industrial steam turbines and generators, much of it for export.

The report is organised around five forces. Substation intensity explains why equipment demand outran line-building. Scarcity pricing explains the margin explosion, and the supply response now under way (Atlanta quadrupled capacity in 18 months; Siemens Energy India is doubling transformer output and approving a second plant; four China-linked makers were cleared to bid central tenders from June 2026) explains why that pricing has probably peaked. Thermal's second wind has turned BHEL into an execution story. Parent-controlled economics shape the MNC subsidiaries, which now export a quarter to a half of their output to group companies. And execution and evacuation risk sits between the national plan and the profit and loss account: only 12% of competitively bid transmission projects were finished on time, and the FY26 line target was cut by 37% before the year ended.

In plain terms

India decided to build a much bigger grid at exactly the moment the world ran short of the big transformers and switchgear that grids need. The handful of companies able to make them could name their price, take cash up front and still have queues. That is why profits tripled and why the shares trade at 60–150 times earnings. Now every one of them is adding capacity, Chinese-linked factories in India are being let back into public tenders, and the government's own buyer says prices are easing. Demand will keep growing; the question for an investor is how much of today's margin survives once supply catches up.

The key numbers to carry through the report are these. The National Electricity Plan for transmission envisages more than ₹9.15 lakh crore of investment to FY32, with 1.91 lakh circuit-km of lines, 1,270 GVA of transformation and 33 GW of HVDC links; a newer CEA plan to FY36 adds about ₹7.9 lakh crore. The listed group's latest disclosed order books add up to roughly ₹4.14 lakh crore, about 3.9 times its trailing revenue, though BHEL alone accounts for almost two-thirds of that. Aggregate trailing P/E is 65.9 times, or 79.8 times once Siemens Ltd's one-off motors-sale gain is removed. The cheapest names (TARIL at 30 times, Voltamp at 33 times) are domestic transformer makers whose margins are most exposed to a price reset; the dearest (Schneider at 149 times, Hitachi Energy India at 114 times) are subsidiaries whose margins are either depressed or still expanding.

01

How a rupee of grid capex becomes equipment revenue

Value pools where few suppliers are qualified: at 765 kV, in HVDC converter stations and in gas-insulated switchgear, not in steel towers or small distribution transformers.

Every piece of equipment in this report sits somewhere between a power station and a factory or home. Electricity leaves a generator at 11–25 kV, is stepped up by a generator transformer to 400 or 765 kV for long-distance transmission (or converted to direct current at ±500–800 kV for the longest links), passes through switchyards and pooling stations full of circuit breakers, isolators and reactors, and is stepped down again in stages, to 220 kV, 132 kV, 33 kV and finally 415 volts, by a chain of power and distribution transformers. Each step needs equipment, and each class of equipment has a different number of qualified makers.

Money flows right to left: a tariff approved by CERC or won in a competitive bid funds the asset owner, who pays the EPC contractor, who pays the equipment maker, who pays for copper and steel. Pressure at Step 5 (aggressive bids) travels all the way back to Step 3.

The money comes from the right-hand end. An asset owner, either Power Grid Corporation (PGCIL) under the regulated-tariff route or a developer such as Adani Energy Solutions that won a project in tariff-based competitive bidding (TBCB), is paid an annual transmission charge for 25–35 years. It spends that capital through EPC contracts and direct equipment orders. The equipment maker earns a one-time sale, plus some service and spares income over the life of the asset. Where the asset owner bid aggressively for the tariff, it squeezes its suppliers: GE Vernova T&D India said in August 2026 that its order inflow was hit by "lower realisations in the TBCB market".

Value is captured unevenly along the chain. At the top are the technology-gated nodes: HVDC converter stations, 765 kV and ±800 kV transformers and reactors, gas-insulated switchgear (GIS) of 400 kV and above, and FACTS devices such as STATCOMs. Only a handful of firms can supply them (Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy and GE Vernova, with BHEL in consortia), so margins are the highest in the group. Next come the qualified transformer makers, who convert copper and steel into units on a materials base of about 70% of revenue and earn 15–19% EBITDA when pricing is firm. Below them are the EPC and boiler-turbine-generator integrators, BHEL and Thermax, who carry heavy site labour, sub-contracting and erection costs and long cash cycles. The asset owners themselves earn regulated EBITDA margins of around 85%, but on enormous capital; they are customers, not members of this sub-sector, and the line-building EPC contractors that sit at Step 4 are covered in our civil construction report.

Two refinements matter. First, the ranking inside the "systems" tier is not fixed: Hitachi Energy India sits below two transformer makers on FY26 margin because it is still converting low-margin orders booked in 2022–23 and is now absorbing HVDC projects that carry lower gross margins in their early stages. Hitachi Energy India paid ₹241.85 crore of royalty to its parent in FY26, about 3% of revenue, and GE Vernova T&D India paid a technology licence fee of ₹117.8 crore, almost double the previous year. These payments are a legitimate charge for technology, but they mean the listed subsidiary keeps less of the value its products create than the margin tables imply.

In plain terms

The money in this industry is made where few companies are allowed to bid. Anyone with a shed and a winding machine can make a small distribution transformer, so those sell on price. Only three global groups can build an ±800 kV converter station, and only a short list of factories is approved by PGCIL for 765 kV transformers, so those carry fat margins. A company's place on that ladder of approvals matters more than its size.

02

Size, growth and the numbers that frame the industry

A domestic market of roughly ₹1.4–1.7 lakh crore a year, growing in the mid-teens, inside a broader electrical-equipment industry of about ₹4 lakh crore.

There is no single official figure for the size of India's heavy electrical equipment market, so it has to be built up from pieces. The broadest anchor is the Indian Electrical & Electronics Manufacturers' Association (IEEMA), which put domestic power-equipment production at about ₹2.6 lakh crore in FY23, up 18% on the year, equal to roughly 45% of all capital-goods output and about 1% of GDP. IEEMA's production index rose about 17% in FY24 and 14% in FY25, then slowed to single and low-double digits by quarter in FY26. Rolling those growth rates forward gives an indicative industry of ₹3.4–3.5 lakh crore in FY25 and ₹3.8–4.0 lakh crore in FY26, but that includes cables, meters, low-voltage switchgear, motors and much else outside this sub-sector, and IEEMA's index is volume-weighted rather than a rupee measure.

A narrower, bottom-up estimate of the heavy-electrical core is more useful. Transmission investment under the national plan runs at roughly ₹0.9–1.0 lakh crore a year, of which perhaps half to 60% is equipment (transformers, reactors, switchgear, HVDC and protection) rather than towers, conductors and civil work: about ₹50,000–60,000 crore a year. The distribution reform scheme (RDSS, ₹2.83 lakh crore over about five years) adds ₹15,000–20,000 crore of transformers, switchgear and meters. Thermal boilers, turbines and generators, at about 10 GW a year and an equipment share of ₹4–5 crore per MW, add ₹40,000–50,000 crore. Industrial turbines, boilers, motors and medium-voltage switchgear add another ₹30,000–40,000 crore. The total is an indicative ₹1.4–1.7 lakh crore a year of domestic demand in FY26–27.

India's place in the world is still small. Global power-transformer capacity is about 4,700 GVA across some 400 plants, according to Rystad Energy; India's listed transformer capacity runs to a few hundred GVA (TARIL 75 GVA, Atlanta 63 GVA, BHEL 30 GVA, Siemens Energy India heading to 30 GVA, plus Hitachi, GE Vernova, CG Power and others), which puts the country at perhaps 8–10% of global capacity on an indicative basis. India's transmission spending of roughly ₹1 lakh crore a year is about 2% of global grid investment, which the IEA puts at roughly US$550 billion in 2026. It is a minor market in global terms but one of the fastest-growing, and its factories are increasingly being used by multinational groups to supply the much larger Western shortage.

In plain terms

The listed companies sell roughly ₹1 lakh crore a year, into a home market worth an indicative ₹1.4–1.7 lakh crore a year that has been growing at about 15–20% a year. The fastest growth is in transmission equipment; the slowest is in equipment for factories' own power plants, which depends on private industrial investment that has been hesitant in 2026.

03

The MVA supercycle: why substations, not line-km, drove this boom Deep dive

Line additions are lower than a decade ago; transformation capacity added is at a record. That gap is the single most important fact in this sub-sector.

Most commentary on Indian transmission talks about circuit-kilometres of line. For equipment makers, that is the wrong measure. A circuit-km of line is towers, conductor and insulators, which are the business of line EPC contractors. The equipment in this report is counted in MVA (mega volt-amperes), the rated capacity of transformers at substations and converter stations. The Central Electricity Authority (CEA) publishes both series every March for the 220 kV-and-above network, and they tell completely different stories.

Transformation capacity moved the other way. It hovered between 57,000 and 87,000 MVA a year for a decade, then jumped 31% to a record 1,13,013 MVA in FY26, about 90% of a revised target of 1,26,007 MVA. In the year the line target was missed by a fifth, the substation target was nearly met. The national network stood at about 1,486 GVA of AC transformation at 220 kV and above by July 2026, and the FY27 target is 1,58,339 MVA, another 40% higher; 35,670 MVA was added in the first four months, 23% of the target in a third of the year.

The composition matters as much as the total. At 765 kV, the highest AC voltage in the Indian grid, additions ran at 18,000–21,000 MVA a year from FY22 to FY25 and then jumped to 54,000 MVA in FY26, more than in any year in the series. A boom concentrated at 765 kV is a boom concentrated in the most profitable, least contestable part of the market.

Why the intensity tripled

Three engineering changes explain the shift. The first is renewable pooling. Solar and wind parks are built in clusters of hundreds of megawatts, often thousands, in a few desert districts. Each cluster needs a pooling substation that collects power at 33 kV or 220 kV and steps it up to 400 or 765 kV, often with several large interconnecting transformers (ICTs) and reactors. A pooling station can carry several thousand MVA of transformation but sit at the end of a relatively short line. The second is augmentation: much of FY25–26's capacity was added inside existing substations, as utilities installed extra ICTs to relieve congestion without building new corridors. That adds MVA with no line-km at all. The third is voltage upgrading: a single 765 kV circuit carries roughly three to four times the power of a 400 kV circuit, so the grid can move more power with fewer kilometres, but each end of that line needs much larger transformers.

For investors, the consequence is that the line-km statistic badly understated what was happening to equipment demand. Between FY16 and FY25, line additions fell by two-thirds while transformation additions rose by 38%. Equipment makers had a boom; line builders had a slump. It also means the right leading indicator for the companies in this report is CEA's monthly MVA addition, not ckm.

Can supply keep up? Yes in aggregate, no at the top

Aggregate transformer capacity is not the constraint. Listed capacities alone add up to several lakh MVA a year: TARIL 75,000 MVA, Atlanta 63,060 MVA, BHEL 30,000 MVA, Voltamp about 20,000 MVA after its new plant, Siemens Energy India heading from 15,000 to 30,000 MVA, plus Hitachi Energy, GE Vernova, CG Power, Toshiba, Hyosung and China-linked makers with Indian plants. A dossier estimate is that qualified 220–765 kV capacity is three to four times the grid's annual MVA addition, though much of it also serves industry, renewables developers and export. The binding constraints in FY24–26 were narrower: approvals to bid at 400 and 765 kV (a new entrant needs two to three years after building a plant), short-circuit testing slots at the government's laboratory at Bina, specific components such as bushings and on-load tap changers, particular grades of CRGO steel, and skilled winders. Those constraints are what produced the pricing power, and they are exactly what the current investment wave is aimed at.

In plain terms

India has not been building many more kilometres of power line. What it has been building is far more and far bigger substations, especially at 765 kV, to collect solar and wind power and push it across the country. Substations are where the transformers and switchgear go, so the companies in this report had a much bigger boom than the line statistics suggest. The plan needs another 40% step-up from here. Volume growth looks secure; what is less secure is the price, because the shortage was in qualified high-voltage capacity, and that capacity is now being built.

The implication for the five forces is direct. Force 1, substation intensity, gives the equipment makers more runway than the line-EPC firms: the plan requires transformation additions to rise by roughly 40% from FY26 levels and to stay there for six years. But pricing power in 220–400 kV equipment should shift back toward buyers first, because that is where new capacity and new approvals are arriving fastest. 765 kV and HVDC should stay tight the longest. The monitorable is PGCIL's own commentary, which in August 2026 described transformer and GIS pricing and availability as "easing", and the first central tenders in which the newly exempted China-linked makers bid.

04

Plans versus the ground: ₹9.15 lakh crore on paper, 12% on time

Demand drivers are long and stacked; the gap between plan and commissioning is where order books swell, revenue lags and working capital gets tied up.

The demand case for grid equipment rests on seven drivers, and it helps to separate the ones already turning into orders from the ones still on paper. The first and largest is renewable evacuation. India targets 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 and more than 600 GW by 2032. Of the 38.5 GW of generation added in the first nine months of FY26, every megawatt was non-fossil. Much of it sits in western Rajasthan and Kutch, far from load centres, and the grid has not kept pace: Crisil Ratings estimates that 35–37 GW of renewable capacity faces a high risk of curtailment in FY27, and projects connected under temporary access (T-GNA) saw about 39% curtailment between November 2025 and February 2026. This is demand that is already late. NEP-T plans to lift inter-regional transfer capacity from 119 GW to 143 GW by 2027 and 168 GW by 2032, and to add 47 GW of battery storage and 31 GW of pumped storage, each needing its own substations.

The second driver is load growth. Peak demand reached 270.8 GW in May 2026. The Ministry of Power projects 289 GW in FY27 and 388 GW by FY32, a rise of more than 40% in five years. Data centres are a new and fast-moving part of that: a parliamentary reply estimated that AI data centres could add 26.3 GW of demand by FY32, double the ministry's own March 2026 estimate of 13.56 GW, and Wood Mackenzie expects India's data-centre capacity to grow from 2.2 GW in 2025 to 12 GW in 2030. Data centres are now among Hitachi Energy India's leading order segments, 17% of Thermax's Q1 FY27 bookings and a growing slice of Schneider's order bank.

The procurement route matters for suppliers. Since 2021, inter-state schemes above ₹500 crore default to TBCB: developers bid a levelised annual transmission charge, and the lowest bid wins. The execution gap

The constraint is not money or ordering; it is physical delivery. ICRA found in July 2026 that of the TBCB projects commissioned by March 2026, only 12% met their scheduled commercial operation date. The rest were late by between two months and three years, with a median delay of more than ten months. Land acquisition, right-of-way disputes over tower footings and forest and wildlife clearances were the main causes. CEA's own targets tell the same story: FY25 achieved 58% of a revised line target and FY26 79% of a target that had been cut by 37%.

Substations have consistently done better than lines, which makes sense: a substation needs one plot of land, while a 765 kV line needs thousands of tower sites and the consent of the farmers who own them. For equipment makers, this execution gap has three effects. Order books swell faster than revenue, because orders are placed on award but deliveries are scheduled to site readiness. Working capital rises, because finished equipment sits in inventory or is billed but not paid until erection and commissioning, and 5–10% retention money is held until the asset is accepted. And firm-price contracts are exposed to commodity moves for longer. When analysts call this an order-book-driven sector, they should add that the order book is only as good as the speed at which the customer's site is ready.

In plain terms

The government's plan is enormous, and most of the money is already allocated to someone. The hold-up is getting land and permissions for towers and substations. That does not cancel orders, but it slows the rate at which they turn into revenue and cash. A company with a big order book and a customer stuck in a land dispute is carrying inventory, not earning profit.

There is a quieter risk in the plan numbers too. The FY27–36 plan lists fewer line-km (1.375 lakh ckm over ten years) than NEP-T did for FY23–32 (1.91 lakh ckm), even though its transformation figure is large. It reinforces the substation-heavy pattern but also shows how plans are reshaped as realities intrude. Investors should treat each successive plan as a statement of intent, track CEA's monthly MVA additions as the evidence, and watch whether the 23% of the FY27 MVA target achieved in the first four months accelerates into the usual second-half surge.

Thermal's second wind: BHEL's ₹2.6 lakh crore book and the execution test

Coal is back in the plan; BHEL holds most of the orders; the investment case now turns on how fast a seven-year backlog can be built and at what margin.

For a decade after 2012, new coal power in India all but stopped. Stressed private projects, cancelled coal blocks and falling plant-load factors meant few new thermal orders; BHEL booked only 1,320 MW of thermal orders as late as FY23, according to a UBS estimate quoted in the press. That changed when peak demand began to outrun the ability of solar and wind to supply evening and night load. A written reply in the Rajya Sabha in March 2026 set out the new pipeline: 39,545 MW of coal and lignite capacity under construction (including 4,845 MW of stressed projects), 22,920 MW awarded and due to start, and 24,020 MW in planning, against a ministry plan for at least 97,000 MW of new coal and lignite capacity to reach about 3,07,000 MW by FY35. A broker note adds that the "97 GW target now extends to 110 GW+", and about 37 GW of existing plants will cross 35 years of age by FY32, making them candidates for replacement or renovation.

A 7.2-times order book is not a sign of strength on its own; it is a measure of how long BHEL will take to deliver. The industrial end of thermal is not booming

The thermal revival is a utility story. The industrial end of power generation, steam turbines up to 100 MW for sugar mills, steel plants, refineries and cement kilns, and the generators that go with them, is soft at home. TD Power, which makes generators for turbine and engine makers, booked 93% of its Q1 orders from exports, mostly for US gas turbines and data-centre gensets. Thermax, the industrial boiler and EPC company, grew revenue only 3.7% in FY26 and took a ₹91 crore cost-to-complete provision on one industrial infrastructure project in Q1 FY27, cutting its quarterly profit by 86%. The comeback of coal has helped BHEL; it has not lifted the whole generation-equipment chain.

06

HVDC: the oligopoly at the top of the grid Deep dive

Three global groups, lumpy ₹20,000–25,000 crore projects, back-ended revenue and dilutive early margins: the most valuable and least predictable orders in the sector.

High-voltage direct current (HVDC) links move bulk power over long distances. Alternating-current lines lose more power over long distances and cannot easily be controlled; a DC link converts AC to DC at one end, sends it over a single high-voltage corridor with lower losses, and converts it back at the other end, with precise control over how much power flows. India uses HVDC to carry bulk renewable power from Rajasthan and Gujarat to load centres in the north and south, to feed dense cities (the 1,000 MW Kudus–Aarey link into Mumbai, India's first city in-feed, built by Hitachi Energy), and to stabilise a grid with a rising share of variable generation.

There are two technologies. Line-commutated converters (LCC) use thyristor valves; they are proven at ±800 kV and 6 GW and are the default for bulk point-to-point transfers such as Khavda–Nagpur. Voltage-source converters (VSC) use IGBT transistors; they need less space, can support weak grids and restart a blacked-out network, and are used for offshore wind and city links. India's first VSC link was Pugalur–Thrissur at ±320 kV. GE Vernova T&D India has won a 2.5 GW VSC scheme at Khavda, and Siemens Energy India points out that VSC links need fewer STATCOMs and synchronous condensers than LCC ones, which shifts some FACTS demand.

Why HVDC dilutes margins at first

The second distortion is in margins. HVDC packages carry a large bought-out content (cables, civil works, some imported valves and control systems from the parent) and are recognised as revenue on a percentage-of-completion basis, so the early years carry engineering and procurement cost before the high-value equipment is delivered. Hitachi Energy India's material cost rose to 59.4% of revenue in Q1 FY27 from 55.6% a year earlier, which management attributed partly to HVDC entering the mix. GE Vernova T&D India estimates that its HVDC ramp-up dilutes gross margin by about 2–2.5 percentage points, roughly half of which is offset at the EBITDA level by spreading fixed costs over more revenue. Its HVDC backlog is back-ended, with meaningful revenue from FY29.

The third feature is scarcity of global slots. HVDC factories, engineering teams and valve lines are booked by European offshore-wind and interconnector programmes as well as Indian ones, and parents decide which projects their subsidiaries bid for and where the valves are made. GE Vernova T&D is adding HVDC valve lines at Noida as part of about ₹1,000 crore of capacity investment, and Hitachi Energy India booked a European offshore-wind HVDC order in Q1 FY27, a sign that Indian factories are being written into parents' global HVDC supply chains. For the domestic listed universe, the HVDC theme reaches beyond the three subsidiaries: Quality Power supplies reactors and FACTS equipment and says it is "targeting two HVDCs", and BHEL earns its terminal share through the Hitachi consortia, which sit in its industry segment.

In plain terms

An HVDC link is a ₹20,000–25,000 crore project in which only three companies in the world can build the expensive converter stations at each end. Winning one is transformative for a listed subsidiary, but the order arrives in one quarter, the revenue trickles in over four or five years, and the early years earn thinner margins. A good HVDC quarter can make orders look spectacular and the next year look like a collapse. Investors should always ask for orders "excluding HVDC".

What could go wrong

The risks are mostly about timing. Projects slip: Leh–Kaithal was removed from PGCIL's book in Q4 FY26 and has to be re-tendered. Awards can be delayed by right-of-way and forest issues on the DC corridor, which is not the OEM's scope but still holds up the converter work. Margins can also disappoint if a subsidiary prices aggressively to win a reference project, or if commodity and currency moves hit the imported content of a fixed-price contract. And there is concentration risk: Hitachi's two big LCC projects are each single-customer exposures to POWERGRID and an Adani entity. On the other side, the pipeline is long. All three HVDC-capable subsidiaries say a greenfield project is close to award, and the ~21-project frame implies roughly two to three awards a year for the rest of the decade.

Where ₹100 goes: copper, CRGO steel and approval-gated capacity

A transformer maker spends about ₹74 of every ₹100 on materials and conversion; a systems MNC about ₹60; BHEL about ₹71 plus a heavy payroll. That cost structure decides who wins when prices move.

The fastest way to understand the economics of this sub-sector is to follow ₹100 of revenue through three typical income statements. Copper and CRGO: the two inputs that decide margins

Two commodities dominate a transformer's cost. Copper goes into the windings; IEEMA's standard price-variation formula weights it at 29% of a transformer's price. CRGO (cold-rolled grain-oriented) electrical steel forms the core; IEEMA weights it at 23%, with steel plates for the tank at another 9%. Copper on the London Metal Exchange averaged about US$13,150 a tonne in 2026 to July, 38.6% higher than a year earlier, and reached about US$14,535 by late August. India consumes 4.0–4.5 lakh tonnes of CRGO a year but makes only 40,000–50,000 tonnes; about 90% is imported from China, Japan, Korea and Russia. A quality-control order since 2020 restricts which foreign mills can sell, and in June 2026 the trade-remedies authority (DGTR) began an anti-dumping investigation into CRGO and amorphous steel from those four countries, on the petition of JSW JFE Electrical Steel. A duty would raise costs for every transformer maker that does not make its own steel.

Price-variation (PV) clauses linked to IEEMA's indices pass most of these moves through on public-sector orders, adjusting the invoice for changes in copper, CRGO, steel, oil and wages between order and delivery. Firm-price orders, common with private developers, TBCB EPC contractors and industrial customers, do not. The 2021 commodity spike showed what that means: copper rose about 52% in 15 months, CRGO mills cut allocations to India by 50–60% as they switched to electric-vehicle steel, and TARIL's material cost reached 82% of sales in FY22 while GE T&D India's operating margin went negative. In 2026 the pattern is repeating at the margin: Voltamp, whose orders are mostly fixed-price, saw its PBILDT margin fall to 14.8% in Q1 FY27 from 17.2%, and Schneider's fell to 5.2% as legacy fixed-price orders booked before December 2025 were executed at old prices. Hitachi Energy India says 60–65% of its orders carry pass-through; TARIL says its orders are covered by PV clauses.

Approval-gated capacity, lead times and the capacity wave

The Ministry of Power describes transformer manufacturing as "an approval-based market". A new 400 kV or 765 kV factory must pass PGCIL's vendor qualification and type tests, including short-circuit testing at the Central Power Research Institute's laboratory at Bina, before it can bid; that takes two to three years after the plant is built. Lead times stretched as demand outran approved capacity: the ministry quotes 11–15 months for 220 kV units, about 15 months for 400 kV and 20–24 months for 765 kV, and Atlanta's chairman says deliveries that once took four to six months now take eight to fifteen in several segments. Globally, Rystad Energy reports two to three years for power transformers and high-voltage breakers in Europe and North America, more than 24 months for units above 100 MVA, and three to six years for some large units.

In plain terms

About three-quarters of what a transformer maker charges goes on copper, special steel and other bought-in parts. If it signed a fixed price and copper jumps, its profit shrinks fast; if its contract lets it pass the increase on, it barely notices. And because a new factory costs relatively little, every company is now building one. The shortage that made the last three years so profitable was a shortage of approved high-voltage factories, and that is the shortage the industry is now fixing.

Scarcity pricing is peaking: how the margin boom was built and how it unwinds Deep dive

Three years of supernormal margins came from lead times, approvals and advances. Each of those three supports is now weakening, though not at the same speed.

Between FY23 and FY26 the listed grid-equipment makers went from single-digit margins and sub-10% returns to some of the most profitable industrial businesses in India. GE Vernova T&D India's screener operating margin went from 4.4% to 28%, and its ROCE from negative in FY21 to 77% today. Hitachi Energy India's margin went from 6% to 16%; Schneider's ROCE from 3% in FY21 to 41% in FY26. The mechanism was simple. When a 765 kV transformer takes two years to deliver and only a handful of factories are approved to bid, buyers compete for slots rather than suppliers competing for orders. Prices rise, suppliers insist on price-variation clauses or advances, they decline marginal orders, and the mix shifts toward the highest-voltage, highest-margin products.

Support 1: lead times are plateauing

Rystad Energy reported in June 2026 that "growth in lead times and prices has largely stagnated over the past year" and that "peak lead times could ease within the next 12 months". Transformer and switchgear prices rose only 1–3% on average in 2025, after switchgear had risen 45–50% since 2020. Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, HD Hyundai Electric, Eaton and CG Power are together investing roughly US$4 billion in new capacity. In India, the capacity wave described in the previous section lands between late 2026 and 2028.

Support 2: competition is returning to public tenders

On 24 June 2026 the Department of Expenditure exempted four China-linked makers with Indian plants (TBEA Energy India, Nanjing Electric India, New Northeast Electric India and Taikai Electric India) from the 2023 procurement order that requires bidders from countries sharing a land border with India to register, for two years. The exemption covers extra-high-voltage power transformers, GIS and glass insulators, and was requested by the Ministry of Power itself. The order says it "may not be considered as a precedence", but it opens central PSU tenders to suppliers with a large cost advantage. Direct imports of transformers from China remain blocked, and bidders must meet local-content rules of about 60–70% for GIS. Hitachi Energy India's management told analysts it sees "no material impact"; GE Vernova T&D India said the effect is "yet to be assessed". Power Grid, the largest buyer, said in August 2026 that "transformer/GIS pricing and availability [are] easing, aided by four Chinese OEMs cleared to bid".

Why a small price cut hurts a lot

In a business where materials and conversion take three-quarters of revenue, the arithmetic of a price cut is brutal. Take an ILLUSTRATIVE domestic transformer maker with ₹2,400 crore of revenue and a 15% EBITDA margin, so ₹2,040 crore of costs. If it cuts prices on new orders by 5% and costs stay flat, revenue on those orders falls to ₹2,280 crore and EBITDA to ₹240 crore: a 10.5% margin, down 4.5 points. If copper also rises 20% on the 40% of its book that has no price-variation clause, costs rise by about ₹40 crore and the margin falls to about 8.8%. Margins earned during the shortage are leveraged to price, and a modest price reset removes a third or more of profit.

What holds margins up

Not everything points down. Volume growth continues: the FY27 MVA target is 40% above FY26's record, so new capacity has somewhere to go. The 765 kV and HVDC end will stay tight longest, because qualification there takes years and the China-linked exemption is limited and temporary. Exports absorb capacity: the MNC subsidiaries now ship 25–55% of their output or orders abroad, and domestic makers are starting to follow. Backward integration can protect margin even if price softens: TARIL expects 80–85% of its raw-material needs to be met in-house once its ₹900–1,000 crore programme (conductors, insulation, bushings, fabrication, CRGO processing) is complete in FY28. And operating leverage helps when new plants fill up.

The dossier's central estimate, which should be read as an indicative view rather than a forecast, is that domestic transformer-maker margins drift back toward the low-to-mid teens over FY27–29 unless exports take up the slack, while HV-systems margins hold in the high teens to low twenties on the strength of HVDC, GIS and exports. Voltamp's own annual report already warns that "with the planned capacity additions across the industry and increasing competition, margins are expected to get normalized".

In plain terms

The boom in profits came from a shortage. Three things kept it going: very long waits for equipment, very few approved suppliers, and buyers who could not afford to haggle. Waits have stopped getting longer, Chinese-owned factories in India are being allowed into government tenders, and buyers who won projects with tight bids are pushing back. None of this ends demand, but it chips away at price, and in a business where most of the cost is copper and steel, a few percent off the price takes a large share off the profit.

Who supplies what: an oligopoly at the top, a crowd at the bottom

Concentration rises with voltage. Three companies make up more than half the listed value, and several important competitors sit outside this sub-sector.

Industry structure in heavy electrical equipment is best read voltage by voltage. At the top, HVDC converter stations are an oligopoly of three global groups (Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy and GE Vernova), with BHEL as a local consortium partner. Just below, 765 kV transformers and reactors are made by BHEL, the three MNCs, TARIL (which describes itself as the only Indian company to qualify on its own with PGCIL for both 765 kV transformers and reactors, a company claim), CG Power, Toshiba's Indian unit and, through Indian plants, China-linked makers such as TBEA. At 220–400 kV, perhaps 15–20 makers are qualified, including Atlanta (400 kV approval received April 2026; 765 kV approval expected), Voltamp (to 220 kV, 250–315 MVA after its new unit), Shilchar, Indo Tech and Bharat Bijlee. Important competitors outside the sub-sector

Several major competitors are not in this report's universe. CG Power is a large listed transformer and motor maker classified elsewhere; it is adding substantial transformer capacity and won a roughly ₹900 crore US data-centre transformer order. ABB India is often mentioned alongside these names, but its power-grid business went to Hitachi in 2020, and what remains (low- and medium-voltage products, motors and automation) is outside this sub-sector; it is context, not a member. Unlisted or foreign-owned rivals include Toshiba Transmission & Distribution Systems India, Hyosung India, TBEA Energy India and other China-linked units, plus smaller listed firms such as Shilchar, Indo Tech and Bharat Bijlee. On the EPC side, L&T, KEC International and Kalpataru build substations and lines and buy much of this equipment; they are covered in our civil construction report.

China's role deserves its own note. China supplied about 37% of India's electrical-equipment imports in FY23, according to IEEMA. Since 2020 a Ministry of Power order has required prior government permission for power-system imports from "prior reference" countries and mandatory testing of imported equipment for malware and standards compliance, and a 2023 procurement order requires bidders from land-border countries to register. Direct transformer imports from China are effectively blocked; Chinese-origin makers compete through Indian factories. The June 2026 exemption is the first formal crack in that wall for central tenders, and its effect on prices will show first in 400–765 kV transformers and GIS.

In plain terms

The higher the voltage, the fewer the suppliers and the better the margins. At the very top, three foreign groups and BHEL share the market. In the middle, a couple of dozen approved factories compete hard. At the bottom, hundreds of small firms sell distribution transformers at thin margins. Indian investors can only buy a few of these competitors on the stock market, which is part of why the listed ones command high valuations.

Parent and child: the economics of the MNC listed subsidiaries

India gets global demand without global capital risk. In return, the parent sets transfer prices, royalties and the export tap, and minority shareholders vote on related-party deals.

Five of the thirteen members are subsidiaries of global groups: Hitachi Energy India (71.31% owned by its parent), Siemens Energy India (75%), Siemens Ltd (75%, held by Siemens AG), GE Vernova T&D India (51%) and Schneider Electric Infrastructure (75%). Their histories are a map of global industry consolidation. And they have strong balance sheets, mostly net cash, with customer advances funding growth.

Who funds the cycle: advances, inventory and the ₹38,600 crore BHEL ties up

The same order can be financed by the customer, the supplier or the shareholder. Working capital is where the differences between business models show up in cash.

Every heavy-equipment order has a cash cycle. The buyer may pay an advance at signing (typically 10–20% on large orders when the supplier has pricing power). The maker then buys copper, steel and components, often months before dispatch, and carries them as inventory. On dispatch or on reaching a project milestone it bills the customer, but public utilities and EPC contractors pay on cycles of around three months, and 5–10% of the contract value is typically retained until the equipment has been erected and commissioned. Performance bank guarantees, often 10% of value, are held for the warranty period. The sum of these timing gaps, net of what the maker owes its own suppliers, is working capital.

Rules of the game: planning, procurement and protection

The state plans the grid, decides how projects are bid, sets who may supply and indexes most public contracts to commodity prices. Each lever moves margins.

Almost all demand in this sub-sector is shaped by public institutions. The Central Electricity Authority writes the national transmission plans (NEP-T for FY23–32 and the 900-GW plan for FY27–36). The National Committee on Transmission approves inter-state schemes and decides whether each goes to competitive bidding or regulated nomination. Bid process coordinators owned by REC and PFC run the tariff-based bids. The Central Transmission Utility (CTUIL) grants connectivity to generators under the 2022 General Network Access regulations, and the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) adopts the winning tariffs or sets regulated returns. State regulators and state transmission utilities do the same within states.

Seven decades, three cycles: from BHEL's monopoly to the grid supercycle

The first boom was in generation and ended in a bust; the second is in transmission. Fifteen years after its peak, BHEL's revenue is still below FY11 in nominal terms.

Indian heavy electrical equipment has been through three distinct eras. In the monopoly era, from the 1950s to the 1990s, BHEL (incorporated in 1964) supplied almost everything: boilers, turbines, generators, transformers and switchgear, with ABB, Siemens and English Electric serving niches. The first power boom followed the Electricity Act of 2003, which opened generation and transmission to private investment. Ultra-mega power projects and the 11th Five-Year Plan's 78.7 GW target produced a wave of coal plant orders. Chinese makers (Shanghai Electric, Dongfang, Harbin) won a large share of private orders, and global groups formed supercritical joint ventures in India after bulk tenders in 2008–10. BHEL peaked in FY11 with revenue of ₹43,337 crore, PAT of ₹6,011 crore, order inflow of ₹60,476 crore and an order book above ₹1.64 lakh crore, while expanding capacity from 15 GW to 20 GW a year.

Then came the bust. Coal-block cancellations in 2014, about 40 GW of stressed private plants, falling plant-load factors and price wars in transmission equipment left the industry with surplus capacity for most of a decade. Line additions fell to 11,664 ckm in FY20. The 2021 commodity spike destroyed what margin was left on fixed-price orders, and GE T&D India's operating margin went negative in FY22. The grid supercycle began around FY23, with renewable-evacuation planning, record substation additions, the first TBCB HVDC awards and the return of thermal ordering.

The history carries two lessons for the current cycle. The first is that order books are not earnings. Reading a heavy-electrical company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Orders become backlog, backlog becomes revenue at an execution rate, revenue becomes profit at a margin set by commodity clauses, and profit becomes cash only if working capital allows. Every native KPI measures one link in that chain.

In plain terms

A heavy-equipment company is a queue. Orders join the back, the factory works through it at a certain speed, and each finished order earns a margin that depends on what copper and steel cost by the time it is built. Profit becomes cash only after the company has paid for materials and waited for the customer to pay. Most of the KPIs below simply measure the length of the queue, the speed of the factory, the margin per order or the cash stuck in between.

Order book (backlog)

Definition. The unexecuted value of firm orders at the end of the period. MNCs call it "order backlog"; BHEL "order book"; TARIL "unexecuted order book". Formula. Opening book + inflow − revenue ± price-variation, currency and scope adjustments − cancellations. Worked example. TransformCo's book goes from ₹3,000 crore to ₹3,200 crore. GE Vernova T&D's went from ₹21,456 crore to ₹20,930 crore in Q1 FY27 because inflow (₹1,137 crore) was smaller than revenue (₹1,836 crore). Drivers. Inflow and execution speed; for BHEL also the timing of large EPC awards. Good, average, weak. The absolute level means little; what matters is its ratio to revenue and its quality (customer, margin, price protection).

How the companies compare. Latest disclosed books total about ₹4.14 lakh crore at mixed dates: BHEL ~₹2,60,000 crore, Siemens Ltd ₹46,670 crore, Hitachi ₹32,222 crore, GE Vernova T&D ₹20,930 crore, Siemens Energy India ₹19,331 crore, Thermax ₹14,045 crore, TARIL ₹7,237 crore, Atlanta ₹3,117 crore, TD Power ₹2,207 crore, Triveni ₹2,180 crore, Schneider ~₹2,100 crore, Quality Power ₹1,945.5 crore and Voltamp ~₹1,590 crore. The definition trap. Some books include orders where the company is lowest bidder (L1) but has not received the contract; some exclude unexecuted framework agreements; BHEL's ₹2.6 lakh crore is a broker figure, not a company filing; Voltamp's comes from a rating-agency report. Definition traps, collected

Almost every headline number in this sub-sector has a definitional catch. The list below gathers the ones that most often mislead.

  • Three fiscal calendars. Siemens Energy India runs October–September, so its "Q3 FY26" is the June 2026 quarter. Siemens Ltd moved to April–March after an 18-month year from October 2024 to March 2026; never compare its 18-month revenue (₹24,846 crore) with a 12-month figure. TARIL has already reported the September 2026 quarter. Compare companies on trailing four quarters, as this report's peer table does.
  • One-off gains in trailing profit. Siemens Ltd's TTM PAT of ₹3,277 crore includes a ₹2,099 crore gain on selling its low-voltage motors business; its continuing-operations TTM PAT is about ₹1,476 crore, and the group's aggregate P/E rises from 65.9× to 79.8× without the gain.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. Triveni, TARIL's deck and Quality Power include other income; Atlanta excludes it; Hitachi strips out FX and commodity timing; Siemens Energy India reports EBIT. Use one basis.
  • Revenue definitions. Thermax's ₹10,694 crore (company) vs ₹10,774 crore (screener, including other operating income); Quality Power's "total revenue" includes other income.
  • Minority interests. Quality Power's consolidated PAT of ₹186 crore includes about ₹65 crore belonging to minority holders of Endoks and Mehru; owners' PAT is about ₹121 crore, and screener's 87.8× P/E is on owners' earnings.
  • HVDC and mega-orders. Always ask for inflow excluding HVDC (Hitachi) or excluding the largest EPC (BHEL's Meja).
  • Order-book sources and bases. BHEL's ₹2.6 lakh crore is from a broker note; Voltamp's from a rating agency; TARIL's is standalone; some books include L1 orders.
  • Working capital without contract assets. BHEL's screener 86 days vs 164 days including contract assets; TARIL's screener 80 days vs management's 170.
  • Cash that is not in the bank. GE Vernova T&D's ₹2,930 crore "available cash" includes lending to a group company through a cash pool; screener shows about ₹1,500 crore.
  • Mislabelled filing lines. TARIL's Q2 FY27 consolidated statement has a row labelled "Exceptional items" that is actually EBITDA, not added to profit.
  • Treasury income inside PAT. Voltamp's other income has been 14–36% of PBT in recent quarters, so its earnings move with bond and fund marks.
  • Export share basis. Orders, revenue and order book give different answers; Hitachi's orders-based 30–37% is about 25% of revenue.
In plain terms

Before comparing two companies in this sector, check four things: which twelve months the figures cover, whether "EBITDA" includes interest and other income, whether profit includes a one-off gain or belongs partly to minority shareholders, and whether the order book is the company's own number. Most apparent bargains and most apparent disasters in the data disappear once those four are aligned.

How to read a heavy-electrical company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the calendar first. Which quarter is this for this company (October–September for Siemens Energy India), and is the comparison year-on-year on the same basis?
  2. Order inflow, with and without the big one. Read the headline, then strip out HVDC, mega-EPC, related-party and method changes. Note the export and domestic split.
  3. Order book and book-to-bill. Did the book grow? Is four-quarter book-to-bill above 1×? What share is private, central and state?
  4. Revenue and execution rate. Divide revenue by the opening book. Is execution speeding up or stalling, and does management blame factory capacity or customer sites?
  5. Gross margin before EBITDA. Gross margin shows pricing and commodities; EBITDA adds operating leverage. A gross-margin fall with a stable EBITDA margin usually means mix (HVDC, domestic) or commodities offset by volume.
  6. The basis of "EBITDA". Does it include other income or FX? Restate it on revenue less operating costs before other income.
  7. Commodity commentary. What share of the book has price-variation cover, and are any legacy fixed-price orders still being executed?
  8. Working capital and cash. Look at receivables, inventory, contract assets and customer advances on the half-year balance sheet; compare operating cash flow with operating profit.
  9. Capacity and approvals. Which plants started, which are delayed, which voltage approvals arrived (400 kV, 765 kV), and what the capex plan now totals.
  10. Parent and policy items. Related-party approvals, royalty changes, cash pooling, China-linked competition in tenders, and any change in guidance on margin or growth.
In plain terms

Ten minutes is enough if you read in this order: orders (without the one big order), the length of the queue, how fast the queue is moving, the margin before and after copper, and whether the cash actually came in. Everything else, including the share price reaction on the day, is detail around those five facts.

Who is winning: growth, visibility and capital, mapped

Three maps and a scorecard. GE Vernova T&D and Siemens Energy India lead on quality; Atlanta and TARIL on momentum; BHEL on visibility; the industrial names lag.

No single measure identifies the winners in this sub-sector, so the three maps below look at the group from three angles: whether growth comes with margin, whether the market pays for order visibility, and who funds growth with customers' money rather than shareholders'. The scorecard then combines nine dimensions.

The first map shows that growth and margin rarely come together. GE Vernova T&D (44.6% growth, 26.2% margin) and Siemens Energy India (31% growth, 21% margin) occupy the top-right quadrant alone. Atlanta and TD Power grew fastest but at high-teens margins; TARIL and Hitachi Energy India sit near the middle, with margins still rising. The four companies in the bottom-left (Thermax, Schneider, Siemens Ltd and, on margin, BHEL) are those most exposed to commodity costs on fixed-price work or to project execution.

The second map undercuts a common assumption: that the market pays for a long order book. It does not. BHEL, with 7.2 years of cover, trades at 63 times; TARIL, with 2.7 years, at 30 times; Schneider, with less than a year, at 149 times. What the market pays for is margin quality, parentage, export exposure and a scarce listing, and it discounts companies where it fears that the order book is priced too cheaply (BHEL's legacy orders) or that margins will be competed away (TARIL and Voltamp's 220–400 kV transformers).

The third map is the most useful for judging the quality of growth. Companies in the left half grow on their customers' and suppliers' money: Hitachi Energy India and Thermax run negative working capital, and GE Vernova T&D and Siemens Energy India very little. The right half needs shareholders' money to grow, which is why TD Power, Quality Power and TARIL are raising capital or borrowing. ROCE above 30% with low working capital (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy) is the strongest combination; low ROCE with high working capital (BHEL) the weakest. Hitachi Energy India's ROCE is depressed by its QIP cash rather than by weak operations.

Global lens: Indian subsidiaries at two to three times their parents' multiples

The world is short of transformers and switchgear, global capacity is now growing fast, and India is both a modest market and an increasingly important factory for the West.

The Indian cycle is part of a global one. The IEA estimates global grid investment at about US$550 billion in 2026, up roughly 20%, with battery storage another US$110 billion; Rystad Energy's broader measure of grid capex is US$650 billion, up 5%, with transmission investment growing about 6% a year to 2029. Data centres are a large new load: their electricity use rose from 145 TWh in 2020 to 539 TWh in 2025 on Rystad's figures. The West is structurally import-dependent for grid equipment. Imports supply 76% of US demand for 10–100 MVA transformers and 70% above 100 MVA, mostly from Mexico, China, Korea and Europe, and Europe imports about 42% of its power transformers from outside the region. European awards for 50–100 MVA units have cleared at about US$30,000–35,000 per MVA.

That shortage is why Indian factories matter to multinational groups. GE Vernova, Hitachi Energy and Siemens Energy are using their Indian subsidiaries as export hubs, routing current transformers, GIS bays, transformers and HVDC work to projects in North America, Europe and the Middle East. Indian domestic makers are following more slowly: TARIL has a ₹150 crore US order and a new UK subsidiary, Atlanta targets 15% of revenue from exports, CG Power (outside this sub-sector) won a large US data-centre order, and TD Power sells most of its generators to US gas-turbine makers. India's direct trade data still show modest exports of finished large transformers (₹1,260 crore of power transformers in FY25, down 41% in the first nine months of FY26), because most of the export flow is components, switchgear, instrument transformers, engineering services and group-captive supply booked under other product codes. HV switchgear exports were ₹3,926 crore in FY25 and rose 27% in 9M FY26.

Technology and disruption: VSC, SF6-free switchgear, storage and the Chinese cost curve

The technology is evolving toward electronics, digital control and storage; the main disruptive threat is not a new product but a cheaper competitor.

Five technology shifts are reshaping what the grid buys. The first is VSC HVDC. Voltage-source converters, built on IGBT transistors, are taking share from thyristor-based LCC for new links because they need less space, can support weak grids, can restart a blacked-out network and suit offshore wind and city in-feeds. India still uses ±800 kV LCC for its largest bulk links, but GE Vernova T&D's 2.5 GW Khavda VSC award and Hitachi's Mumbai in-feed show the direction. VSC moves value toward power electronics and control software, which parents tend to supply from global centres, and away from conventional converter transformers. The second is gas-insulated and SF6-free switchgear. GIS saves land at congested substations and pooling stations; European rules on fluorinated gases are pushing the industry toward alternatives to SF6 (clean-air, vacuum and dry-air designs). IEEMA's MV-HV GIS index rose 38.7% in Q1 FY24 and 8.4% in FY25.

The third is grid-forming and stability equipment. As renewables replace spinning generators, the grid loses inertia and voltage support. STATCOMs, synchronous condensers and FACTS devices fill the gap: PGCIL won India's first TBCB synchronous-condenser project at Fatehgarh-II in 2026, Siemens Energy India booked ±300 MVAr STATCOMs, and Quality Power's Endoks makes STATCOMs in Turkey. The fourth is battery storage as a grid asset. CERC now lets transmission licensees own batteries on regulated returns, NEP-T plans 47 GW of battery storage, and Hitachi Energy India took its first 165 MW / 330 MWh battery order in Q1 FY27; Quality Power is building a battery power-conversion plant in Turkey. The fifth is digital substations and efficient transformers: process-bus protection, condition monitoring, dynamic line rating, amorphous-core and ester-fluid transformers, and high-temperature low-sag conductors that raise the capacity of existing corridors. 1,200 kV AC is under evaluation by PGCIL.

The main disruptive threat is commercial rather than technological: the Chinese cost curve. China-linked makers such as TBEA, with plants in India, can build 400–765 kV transformers and GIS at materially lower cost, and the June 2026 exemption gives four of them two years of access to central tenders. If they win meaningful share in PGCIL tenders, domestic 400–765 kV prices will reset quickly. A second, subtler threat is that the highest-value parts of new technology (VSC valves and control, digital protection software, battery power conversion) accrue to the global parents rather than the listed Indian subsidiaries, which may remain assemblers of hardware with software and IP fees flowing abroad. A third is quality: CEA has found transformer failure rates of up to 10%, and a new entrant's early failures can cost it approvals.

The technology shifts also create openings for domestic companies. Instrument transformers, reactors, STATCOM components and insulators are niche products where Quality Power, Mehru and others already supply the MNCs. Backward integration into CRGO processing, copper conductors, pressboard and bushings (TARIL's programme) lowers cost and dependence on imports. And the move to storage and grid-forming equipment widens the market beyond conventional transformers at exactly the moment transformer pricing is likely to soften.

In plain terms

The grid is becoming more electronic: more power converters, more batteries, more software. That favours the foreign groups that own the electronics and code, and shifts some value from the factory floor to the parent's design centre. The biggest near-term threat to Indian makers is not a new invention but cheaper Chinese-owned factories in India being allowed to bid.

Bull vs bear: a decade of demand against a few years of excess margin

Both cases are strong. The bull rests on volumes that are visible to FY32; the bear on prices, margins and multiples that assume the shortage persists.

The bull case

  • Visible volume to FY32 and beyond: NEP-T needs about 1.54 lakh MVA a year for six years against FY26's record 1.13 lakh; the FY27–36 plan adds 8.28 lakh MVA; 33 GW of HVDC and ~21 HVDC projects are in the frame.
  • Demand already late: 35–37 GW of renewables at curtailment risk, peak demand heading from 271 GW to 388 GW by FY32, data centres adding up to 26 GW.
  • Thermal is back: 97 GW+ of new coal to FY35 and 37 GW of ageing plants; BHEL holds ~₹2.6 lakh Cr of orders and is finally executing profitably.
  • Exports as a second engine: the West imports 42–76% of its large transformers; parents are routing work to Indian factories, and domestic makers are qualifying in the US and Europe.
  • Strong balance sheets: the group has ₹12,533 Cr of gross debt against much larger cash; the MNCs are customer-funded.
  • Oligopoly at the top: 765 kV and HVDC remain tight; qualification takes years.

The bear case

  • Scarcity pricing is fading: lead times have plateaued, ~200 GVA of global capacity arrives in 2026, Indian makers are adding tens of thousands of MVA, and PGCIL says prices are easing.
  • China-linked competition: four makers may bid central EHV tenders until June 2028.
  • Margin leverage to price: a 5% price cut takes an illustrative transformer maker from 15% to 10.5% EBITDA; first cracks are visible (GE Vernova gross margin −709 bp, Voltamp and Schneider squeezed).
  • Execution gap: 12% of TBCB projects on time; targets cut by a third; BHEL converts its book at 13% a year.
  • Valuation: about 80× trailing earnings ex one-offs, two to three times the parents' multiples, on peak-cycle margins.
  • Equity supply: QIPs (TD Power, Quality Power), promoter sell-downs (Atlanta must sell ~12 points by 2028) and earlier OFSs.

The two cases are less contradictory than they look, because they are about different variables. The bull case is about volume, and on volume it is hard to argue: the plans, the curtailment data, the peak-demand projections and the thermal pipeline all point to a decade of rising equipment demand. The bear case is about price and multiple. The margins of FY25–26 were earned in a shortage, the shortage is being fixed, and the shares are valued as if the margins were permanent. Both can be right at once: volumes keep rising while margins fall back toward mid-cycle levels and multiples compress. That is the classic pattern in capital-goods cycles, and it means the best-performing stocks from here may be those whose margins are least exposed to price (HVDC, exports, PV-covered books) and whose valuations already assume some normalisation.
