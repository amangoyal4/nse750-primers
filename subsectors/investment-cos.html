India's holding companies: the discount is the asset class

A complete guide to the six listed investment and holding companies in the NSE 750: what sits inside each wrapper, why ₹100 of Bajaj Holdings stock buys roughly ₹185 of shares, why one wrapper trades at well under half its contents while another trades at three times its book, how three different accounting regimes make reported profit nearly useless, and which rule changes, from the RBI's Tata Sons ruling to the tax on buybacks, are moving discounts for the first time in a decade.

00

The one-page view: six wrappers, one discount

These are not operating businesses. They are boxes of shares, and the price of the box rarely matches what is inside it.

An Indian investment company is a listed company whose main assets are shares in other companies. Its "revenue" is mostly dividends, its costs are a rounding error, and its value is whatever its holdings are worth on the stock market that day. The six members of this sub-sector have a combined market value of ₹2,36,151 crore on 19 October 2026. If you add up what they own, marking listed stakes at market and unlisted ones at a stated basis, and strip out the Bajaj cross-holdings that would otherwise be counted twice, you get roughly ₹3.26 lakh crore. On a matched basis the market prices the group at about 69–70 paise per rupee of contents. That gap of roughly 30 paise, and why it varies so widely by company, is what this report is about.

The gap is anything but uniform. Bajaj Nivesh (formerly Maharashtra Scooters) trades at about 39% of its look-through net asset value (NAV): a ~61% discount. Bajaj Holdings & Investment (BHIL), the apex box of the Bajaj group, trades at about 54% of its NAV: a ~46% discount, or ~38% once you deduct the capital-gains tax that would be due if the stakes were ever sold. TSF Investments, the Sundaram Finance family's auto-component holding company, is somewhere between a 20% and 40% discount, depending on what you think its unlisted stakes are worth. Tata Investment Corporation (TIC) trades almost exactly at NAV, about 5% below pre-tax and about 5% above post-tax. Then there are two premium cases. Lloyds Enterprises trades about 17% above the value of its listed stakes plus its gold-mine investment at cost. Authum Investment & Infrastructure trades at 3.2 times book value, a premium of about 220% for its manager's record of buying distressed assets cheaply.

Five forces explain this ladder, and they organise the rest of the report.

  • The discount is the asset class. A passive family holding company is valued as its contents minus a discount, and over one to three years the change in that discount moves the share price more than anything happening inside the box. A minority shareholder's return is NAV growth, plus dividends, plus or minus the change in the discount.
  • Catalysts come from regulators, not activists. Promoters hold 51–73% of every member, so no outsider can force a break-up. What has moved discounts recently is regulation: the RBI's rule that the largest non-bank lenders must list, which brought Tata Capital to market in October 2025 and has now put Tata Sons back on the path to listing; the RBI's core investment company (CIC) rules, which are pushing BHIL to sell non-group shares; SEBI's special call auctions for illiquid holding companies; and tax changes on buybacks and capital gains.
  • Reported profit is not earnings. Three accounting regimes coexist. Profit booked from associates is real but not cash; gains on holdings carried at fair value through other comprehensive income (OCI) never reach the profit line; mark-to-market gains on trading books swing profit violently. Bajaj Nivesh's reported P/E is 50.5×; on the profits its holdings actually earn, it is about 10×.
  • Concentration is single-stock risk wearing a holdco label. 88% of BHIL's gross assets sit in two Bajaj companies; half of Bajaj Nivesh is Bajaj Finance; nearly all of Lloyds Enterprises' listed value is two Lloyds companies. Only TIC is genuinely diversified.
  • Passive wrappers versus active allocators. BHIL, Bajaj Nivesh, TIC and TSF hold and pay out. Authum and Lloyds Enterprises deploy capital aggressively, use borrowed money and do deals. The market gives the passive group a discount and the active pair a premium, and that premium depends entirely on trust in the people allocating the capital.

In plain terms

Buying a holding company is buying a box of shares at a price the market sets for the box, not for its contents. For the Bajaj boxes that price is roughly half the value of what is inside, and has been for most of the last two decades. The question for an investor is never "are the contents good?" (they usually are) but "will the gap between box and contents shrink, stay or widen, and how much cash will reach me while I wait?"

Timing matters for reading this report. No member had reported July–September 2026 (Q2 FY27) results by 19 October. Bajaj Nivesh reports on 22 October and BHIL on 3 November. Every figure here is therefore FY26 (the year to March 2026), Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) or a market price on 19 October 2026, and we say which. The macro backdrop has turned less friendly: the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October, and the Nifty was around 22,780 at the end of September. Holding-company discounts have historically widened in risk-off markets; BHIL's reached about 66% in the COVID crash of early 2020.

01

What a holding company is, and how a rupee of value reaches you

Three legal wrappers, four business models and one leaky pipe between an operating company's profit and a minority shareholder's pocket.

Strip away the names and an Indian investment company is a balance sheet with almost nothing on it except shares. The company may have been created to hold a family's controlling stakes after a demerger (BHIL in 2008, TSF in 2018), set up as a joint venture that outlived its factory (Maharashtra Scooters, now Bajaj Nivesh), floated as a pooled investment vehicle eighty-nine years ago (TIC, 1937), or converted from a dormant shell into an aggressive investor (Authum, Lloyds Enterprises). What they share is that the value of the listed share depends on other companies' share prices.

Three legal wrappers

Indian law sorts these companies into three regulatory boxes, and the box matters because it decides how much they can borrow, what they must hold and whether the RBI supervises them.

  • Core Investment Company (CIC). An RBI category for group holding companies: at least 90% of net assets must be in group companies, and at least 60% in their equity. A CIC must register with the RBI only if its assets exceed ₹100 crore and it raises public funds. Otherwise it is an "exempt" or "unregistered" CIC. TSF and Bajaj Nivesh are unregistered CICs; BHIL's board approved re-categorisation as one in FY26. Tata Sons, TIC's unlisted parent, is a registered CIC in the RBI's Upper Layer, which is the source of the Tata listing story in Part 06.
  • NBFC – Investment and Credit Company (NBFC-ICC). Since February 2019 the RBI has merged its old asset-finance, loan and investment-company categories into one ICC licence. Under Scale-Based Regulation, a non-deposit-taking ICC with assets above ₹1,000 crore sits in the Middle Layer. TIC and Authum are both registered NBFC-ICCs. TIC's primer still uses the older label "NBFC-ND-SI Investment Company", which no longer exists as a category.
  • Ordinary company. If financial assets are not more than half of total assets and financial income is not more than half of gross income, the company is not an NBFC at all. Lloyds Enterprises, whose income is mostly steel-trading revenue, sits here: it holds large share stakes but is regulated like any trading company.

Value leaks at three points: what the operating company retains, what the holdco retains, and the discount the market applies to the wrapper. Only the last one is usually large.

The pipe is leaky by design. The operating company retains most of its profit, which is fine because that builds value inside the box. The holdco then decides what to pass on. Since dividend distribution tax was abolished in April 2020, section 80M of the Income-tax Act lets a domestic company deduct dividends it receives to the extent it pays them out again, so passing through is tax-efficient and hoarding is penalised. Finally, the minority shareholder pays tax at their own slab rate and receives a share price that is NAV minus the discount. For BHIL, Bajaj Nivesh and TSF that discount is a far bigger leak than anything in the first three steps.

In plain terms

Four of these companies are passive: they own shares, collect dividends and pass most of them on. Two are active: they borrow, buy distressed assets or restructure their group, and their share prices reflect how much investors trust the people making those decisions. The passive four trade below their contents; the active two trade above.

Running costs are trivial. BHIL spent ₹162 crore in FY26 to look after about ₹2.3 lakh crore of holdings, or 0.07%. Bajaj Nivesh spent about ₹4 crore. An active mutual fund charges ten to twenty times as much (see the asset managers report for how fund fees work). So the economic cost of owning a holding company is not its expenses but the discount: the shareholder owns assets they cannot get at, managed by a controller they cannot remove, with tax due if anything is ever sold. The discount is the price of those three constraints.

The controller, meanwhile, gets something valuable. The Bajaj family controls Bajaj Auto and Bajaj Finserv, two companies worth ₹5.6 lakh crore together, through 51.46% of BHIL. That is control of a large industrial and financial group at about half the cost of owning the stakes directly. This is why promoters create holding companies and why they rarely dismantle them: the structure that frustrates the minority investor is exactly what makes it useful to the family.

02

Size, and what actually compounds

Revenue is a meaningless number here. NAV, dividends and the discount are the three things to measure, and they have moved in different directions.

Any measure of this sub-sector's size has to state its definition, because the obvious ones mislead. "Revenue" on a data screen is dividend income for BHIL, Bajaj Nivesh and TIC, a lending-and-investment line for Authum, and consolidated operating sales of subsidiaries for Lloyds Enterprises and TSF. Adding those together gives ₹7,234 crore, which tells you nothing. The meaningful pools are market value, look-through NAV, profit and cash paid out.

Why the NAV has to be netted

BHIL's NAV includes its 51% stake in Bajaj Nivesh at market value, ₹7,175 crore. Bajaj Nivesh's own NAV of ₹36,261 crore counts every share it owns, including its 3.04% of BHIL, worth ₹3,797 crore. If you add the two NAVs, the Bajaj Nivesh stake inside BHIL is a claim on assets already counted in Bajaj Nivesh's NAV, and the BHIL stake inside Bajaj Nivesh is a claim on assets already counted in BHIL's. Subtracting both (₹10,972 crore) leaves a net NAV of about ₹3.26 lakh crore. To keep the ratio like-for-like, the same ₹10,972 crore has to come out of combined market cap as well, because BHIL's market value includes what it owns in Bajaj Nivesh, and vice versa. On that matched basis the group trades at ₹2,25,179 crore ÷ ₹3,25,897 crore = 0.69 times NAV. On the gross basis it is ₹2,36,151 crore ÷ ₹3,36,869 crore = 0.70. Either way the group sits at a discount of roughly 30%. Excluding BHIL the ratio is 1.06 (₹1,11,238 crore ÷ ₹1,05,437 crore), a small premium created entirely by Authum; excluding Authum as well, it falls back to 0.71.

What compounds: NAV, not revenue

For a holding company the useful growth measure is NAV per share plus dividends paid. On that basis FY26 was a split year. BHIL's disclosed NAV per share moved from ₹21,244 (September 2025) to ₹19,388 (March 2026), then back to ₹21,062 (June 2026); we estimate about ₹20,800 on 19 October. Bajaj Nivesh's reserves, which mark its Bajaj shares at fair value, fell 10% in FY26, from ₹30,851 crore to ₹27,623 crore, as Bajaj Finance and Bajaj Auto prices weakened over the year. TIC's reserves fell 6%, from ₹31,040 crore to ₹29,171 crore, tracking weakness in TCS, Tata Motors and Tata Steel. Authum's net worth was flat at ₹14,728 crore after a strong FY25. TSF grew reserves 8%. Lloyds Enterprises grew reserves 61%, but much of that came from issuing new shares rather than from profit.

The wider universe is bigger than these six. India has roughly 30–40 listed holding companies, including Kalyani Investment (Bharat Forge), Pilani Investment (Aditya Birla), Bombay Burmah (Britannia), JSW Holdings, Nalwa Sons, Summit Securities, Kama Holdings (SRF), TVS Holdings, Rane Holdings and Elcid Investments (Asian Paints). No study has sized the whole universe recently. The best anchor is a 2012 Business Standard study of the fifteen largest, which found them averaging a 54.5% discount, from 30% at Maharashtra Scooters to 83.6% at Bombay Burmah, with TIC alone at a 19.3% premium. Fourteen years on, the shape of that ladder is strikingly similar, except that Maharashtra Scooters, then the narrowest discount, is now the widest.

In plain terms

The six members own roughly ₹3.26 lakh crore of shares (after removing the Bajaj double count) and the market values them at about ₹2.25 lakh crore on the same basis. They earned about ₹13,100 crore of attributable profit in FY26 but paid out only about ₹2,870 crore in dividends, and most of that profit was never received as cash in the first place.

Anatomy of the holdco discount Deep dive

Why ₹100 of shares sells for ₹54, which part of the gap is rational, and which levers have actually closed it.

Every passive holding company in India trades below the value of what it owns, and has done for as long as anyone has measured. The 2012 study of the fifteen largest found an average discount of 54.5%. BHIL's discount averaged about 58% between 2015 and 2019 and touched 66% in the COVID crash. Today it is about 46%. The persistence is the puzzle: if the shares inside are worth ₹100, why will nobody pay more than ₹54 for a claim on them? The answer is that a minority claim on a holding company is not the same thing as owning the shares directly, and the gap between the two has several separate causes, some of which are permanent.

The five components of the discount

Analysts usually break a holdco discount into rational and residual parts. For Indian promoter holdcos, five components matter.

  • Latent capital-gains tax. If the holdco ever sold a stake, it would pay long-term capital gains tax at an effective ~14.56% for a company (12.5% plus surcharge and cess, since July 2024). BHIL's strategic stakes cost about ₹22,025 crore and are worth about ₹2.31 lakh crore, so the embedded gain is about ₹2.09 lakh crore and the tax that would fall due is about ₹30,490 crore. That alone explains roughly 13 points of BHIL's 46-point discount. It is the most defensible part of the gap: a direct shareholder in Bajaj Auto does not face that tax, a BHIL shareholder does, at least in theory.
  • No control, no takeover route. The Bajaj family owns 51.46% of BHIL. No outsider can buy the company, break it up or force a distribution. The minority holder's only exit is to sell the BHIL share to another minority holder.
  • Dividend leakage. Before 2020 every rupee of dividend passing through a holdco was taxed twice. Section 80M now removes the holdco-level tax on dividends that are passed on, but anything retained still sits in the box earning a market return on which the minority holder has no claim except through the discounted share price.
  • Illiquidity and thin float. Free floats are 27–49% across the six, with low institutional ownership outside BHIL and Authum. Bajaj Nivesh has about 25,000 shareholders. Thin trading makes large investors demand a bigger discount to buy and a smaller one to sell.
  • "It never closes." The last component is self-reinforcing. Because Indian holdco discounts have not closed in decades, investors price them as permanent, which keeps them permanent. This is the residual that only a credible catalyst can shift.

Tata Sons, Tata Capital and Tata Investment: from mania to a real catalyst Deep dive

An RBI listing rule turned a speculative trade into a live event. What it means for TIC is narrower than the market once believed.

Tata Investment Corporation is the odd member of the group: a diversified portfolio of mostly Tata shares that trades near its NAV rather than at a deep discount. For two years its share price has been driven less by that portfolio than by a single question: will Tata Sons, the unlisted apex company of the Tata group, be forced to list? The answer changed in September 2026, and with it the logic of owning TIC.

The rule that started it

In October 2021 the RBI introduced Scale-Based Regulation for non-bank finance companies, sorting them into Base, Middle, Upper and Top layers by size and risk. Companies designated to the Upper Layer must list their shares within three years. Tata Sons, which is registered as a core investment company, was placed in the Upper Layer in September 2022, setting a listing deadline of about September 2025. Tata Capital, the group's lending arm, was also an Upper Layer company.

Tata Sons chose not to list. It repaid its debt, sold a block of TCS shares worth about ₹9,362 crore, and applied to surrender its CIC registration, which would take it out of the Upper Layer altogether. The September 2025 deadline passed with the application pending. Tata Capital, meanwhile, complied: it listed in October 2025 through an IPO of about ₹15,512 crore at ₹326 a share, and traded at ₹318 on 5 October 2026 (the NBFC report covers it).

This is where the report must correct TIC's primer. The primer states that TIC holds a small unlisted stake in Tata Sons. Our research found no evidence for this. Three kinds of profit, none of them earnings Deep dive

Associate share is real but not cash, fair-value gains skip the P&L, mark-to-market swings it, and consolidation invents revenue growth. Translate everything to NAV and cash.

A holding company's profit line looks like any other company's, and that is the trap. Under Indian accounting standards (Ind AS) a holdco can account for the same kind of asset, a stake in another company, in four different ways, and each produces a different "profit". Two members report far more profit than they receive in cash, two report less profit than their holdings earn, and one reports profit that swings by a factor of thirty from one quarter to the next. A reported P/E is therefore almost useless for comparing these six companies.

Equity method: profit you never receive

When a holdco owns 20–50% of a company and has significant influence, it books its share of that company's profit as "share of profit of associates". BHIL's consolidated FY26 profit attributable to shareholders was ₹9,637 crore. Fair value through OCI: profit that understates the economics

Bajaj Nivesh and TIC carry most of their holdings at fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI). Under that election, dividends and interest go through the P&L, but changes in the shares' market value bypass it and go straight to reserves. So Bajaj Nivesh's FY26 profit of ₹311 crore is almost exactly its dividend income of ₹313 crore, while its reserves fell by ₹3,228 crore as Bajaj share prices fell. TIC reported ₹434 crore of profit, while its reserves fell ₹1,869 crore. Reported P/Es of 50.5× (Bajaj Nivesh) and 71.2× (TIC) therefore look absurdly high, because the denominator is only the dividend slice of what the holdings earn.

FVOCI also creates seasonality. Bajaj Finance and Bajaj Auto pay their final dividends in the July–September quarter, so Bajaj Nivesh earned ₹267 crore in Q2 FY26 and only ₹4 crore in each of the next two quarters. Its Q1 FY27 profit was ₹3.3 crore. Its Q2 FY27 result, due on 22 October, should again be large. Anyone comparing quarters year-on-year without knowing this would see wild swings that mean nothing.

The two active allocators: Authum and Lloyds Enterprises Deep dive

One buys distressed assets with borrowed money and trades at 3.2× book; the other is a group restructuring vehicle funded partly by pledged shares. Both premiums rest on trust.

The four passive members hold what their families gave them and pay out most of what they receive. Authum and Lloyds Enterprises are different. They deploy capital, take on debt, buy and restructure assets, and their share prices carry premiums rather than discounts. The premium is the market's estimate of what the people running the vehicle will add, and it is only as durable as the market's trust in them. That makes governance and disclosure the central analytical questions for these two, more than the value of any single holding.

Authum: a listed special-situations book

Authum Investment & Infrastructure was incorporated in 1982 and has been controlled by the Dangi family since about 2017–18, through Alpana Dangi (about 65%) and Mentor Capital. Sanjay Dangi is described as an advisor. It is a registered non-deposit-taking NBFC and, by size, sits in the RBI's Middle Layer. It listed on the NSE on 23 April 2026, having been BSE-only, and issued four bonus shares for every one held (record date 13 January 2026; its primer wrongly dates this to January 2024).

The rules that move discounts

RBI categories decide what a holdco may own and borrow; SEBI decides how it is priced; the tax code decides what leaks. Each has shifted since 2019.

In most industries regulation shapes costs and competition. For holding companies it shapes something more direct: the size of the discount. A rule that forces a listing reveals a hidden value. A tax that falls on monetisation sets a floor under the discount. A tax change on buybacks removes the tool other countries' holdcos use to narrow it. India's last seven years have seen changes on all three fronts.

RBI: what a holdco may be

The RBI created the core investment company category in January 2011 and consolidated the rules in a 2016 Master Direction, revised after the Tapan Ray Working Group reported in 2020. The essentials are simple. A CIC must hold at least 90% of its net assets in group companies and at least 60% in their equity. It must register only if its assets exceed ₹100 crore and it accesses public funds. A registered CIC's adjusted net worth must be at least 30% of its risk-weighted assets, and its outside liabilities may not exceed 2.5 times that net worth. A group may have no more than two layers of CICs. Registered CICs need board-level risk and audit committees and now sit inside Scale-Based Regulation, with the largest in the Upper Layer.

These rules explain two of the six members' recent behaviour. BHIL, historically an RBI-registered investment NBFC, has been re-categorising itself as an unregistered CIC, and to meet the 90% group test it has been selling non-group shares (realising about ₹2,069 crore of gains in the first nine months of FY26). Bajaj Nivesh, already an unregistered CIC, changed its name and objects. Both moves make the boxes purer, and so easier to value.

The second RBI lever is Scale-Based Regulation (announced October 2021, effective October 2022). Upper Layer NBFCs must list within three years of designation. That rule brought Tata Capital to market and, after the RBI's September 2026 refusal to let Tata Sons surrender its registration, has put Tata Sons back on the path to a listing (Part 06). Investment and credit companies (ICCs), the category TIC and Authum hold, have existed since February 2019, when the RBI merged three older NBFC categories.

SEBI: how a holdco is priced

In June 2024 SEBI introduced a special call auction for "investment holding companies" that trade below half of their book value. Once a year, eligible stocks go through an auction with no price band, letting buyers and sellers discover a price that thin daily trading never reaches. The first auction, on 28 October 2024, re-priced Elcid Investments, a holder of Asian Paints shares, from about ₹3.5 to ₹2,36,250 a share. Maharashtra Scooters was among the stocks deemed eligible that month. The framework fixes price discovery for illiquid, forgotten holdcos. It does little for a liquid one such as BHIL, whose discount reflects investor judgement rather than an absence of trading.

Two other SEBI rules bear on the members. Minimum public shareholding requires at least 25% of every listed company to be held by the public; Authum's promoter was at 74.95% in June 2025, the limit, and has since fallen to 68.81%. And the takeover code requires an open offer if a promoter group holding between 25% and 75% raises its stake by more than 5% in a year, which would matter if the Bajaj family ever tried to consolidate BHIL and Bajaj Nivesh by buying shares.

Tax: the most under-appreciated driver

  • Dividends. Dividend distribution tax was abolished from 1 April 2020; dividends are now taxed in the recipient's hands at slab rates. Section 80M lets a domestic company deduct dividends received to the extent it distributes them at least a month before its tax-return due date. Passing dividends through a holdco is therefore tax-neutral; retaining them is not.
  • Buybacks. From 1 October 2024 the whole amount a shareholder receives in a buyback is taxed as a deemed dividend at their slab rate, and the cost of the shares becomes a capital loss. Before that, the company paid about 23.3% buyback tax on the distributed amount and the shareholder was exempt. For high-bracket resident shareholders, tendering into a buyback is now often worse than selling in the market.
  • Capital gains. From 23 July 2024 long-term gains on listed shares are taxed at 12.5% without indexation, and short-term gains at 20%. For a company, surcharge and cess take the effective long-term rate to about 14.56%. This is the permanent, rational floor of a holdco discount if investors assume the holdings will one day be sold.
  • Demergers. A demerger that meets the conditions of section 2(19AA) is tax-neutral. That is why the Bajaj split of 2008, the Sundaram Finance Holdings demerger of 2018 and Lloyds' planned realty demerger all used the route.

Ninety years of wrappers: how the six were built

From a 1937 investment trust to a 2026 rename, each member is the residue of a group decision, and the defining events are demergers, rules and manias.

Indian holding companies were not designed as investment products. Each is the by-product of something else: a group pooling its savings, a demerger leaving a residual company, a joint venture whose factory faded, or a shell put to new use. Their history is therefore a history of group decisions and of the rules that shaped them.

1937 to the 1990s: the first pooled vehicles. Tata Sons floated The Investment Corporation of India in 1937; it was renamed Tata Investment Corporation in 1995. Maharashtra Scooters was set up in 1975 as a joint venture between Bajaj Auto and the Western Maharashtra Development Corporation. In this era investment companies carried shares at historical cost, so their books bore no relation to market value and discounts were invisible to all but the most diligent investors.

2007–08: the demerger era. Bajaj Auto Ltd split into three companies, effective 20 February 2008: a new Bajaj Auto for the motorcycle business, Bajaj Finserv for financial services, and the residual company, renamed Bajaj Holdings & Investment, which kept the family's strategic stakes and cash. BHIL became the apex box of the group.

2011–16: regulation arrives. The RBI defined core investment companies in 2011 and issued its Master Direction in 2016. The 2012 Business Standard study found the fifteen largest holdcos averaging a 54.5% discount, with TIC alone at a premium.

2018–20: fair value and a new member. Ind AS reached NBFCs from FY19, putting listed investments at fair value and making TIC's NAV visible in its accounts. Sundaram Finance demerged its non-financial investments into Sundaram Finance Holdings, which listed on the NSE in March 2018. BHIL took Maharashtra Scooters to 51% in 2019 when the state corporation exited. Dividend distribution tax was abolished in April 2020, and the COVID crash took BHIL's discount to about 66%.

2021–24: rules, manias and distressed buyers. Scale-Based Regulation (October 2021) and Tata Sons' Upper Layer designation (September 2022) set up the Tata listing question, and TIC roughly tripled in 2023–24 as a proxy for it. Authum bought Reliance Commercial Finance (reported October 2022) and then RHFL's loan assets. Shree Global Tradefin became Lloyds Enterprises (September 2023). In 2024 the government raised long-term capital gains tax to 12.5%, made buyback proceeds taxable as dividends, and SEBI ran its first special call auction for holdcos.

2025–26: clean-up and catalysts. Tata Sons' listing deadline lapsed (September 2025), Tata Capital listed instead (October 2025), and TIC split its shares 1:10. Sundaram Finance Holdings became TSF Investments and took control of Axles India. BHIL sold a block of Bajaj Finserv shares in June 2025, then put ₹16,331 crore into the Bajaj insurers when Allianz exited in January 2026. Authum issued 4:1 bonus shares and listed on the NSE. The RBI rejected Tata Sons' attempt to leave the Upper Layer, and Maharashtra Scooters became Bajaj Nivesh.

Add up what the box owns at today's prices, subtract what it owes, and divide by the number of shares: that is NAV per share. Compare it with the share price and you have the discount. Everything else in this section is a refinement of those two numbers or a measure of how much cash leaks out while you hold.

1. Look-through NAV and NAV per share

What it is. The value of everything the holdco owns, marked to market, less its debt, divided by its shares. "Look-through" means following the chain to the operating companies, and in a layered group like Bajaj you can choose how far to go: BHIL's first-layer NAV marks Bajaj Finserv at its market price; a second-layer look-through would value Bajaj Finserv's own holdings instead.

Formula. NAV = Σ (stake × investee market cap) + unlisted at stated basis + cash − debt. NAV per share = NAV ÷ shares outstanding.

Worked example. BHIL on 19 October: Bajaj Finserv ₹1,06,394 crore + Bajaj Auto ₹97,150 crore + insurers ₹16,331 crore (at the January 2026 deal price) + Bajaj Nivesh ₹7,175 crore + treasury ₹4,392 crore − borrowings ₹10 crore = ₹2,31,432 crore, or ₹20,796 per share on 11.129 crore shares (INDICATIVE).

What drives it. Almost entirely the share prices of the holdings. Retained cash adds a little; dividends paid out reduce it rupee for rupee.

Benchmark. There is no "good" NAV, only good NAV growth: over a cycle, NAV per share growth plus dividends above about 12% a year is strong, 8–12% average and below 8% weak.

How the members compare. Only BHIL and TIC publish NAV. BHIL's disclosed NAV per share was ₹21,244 (September 2025), ₹19,388 (March 2026) and ₹21,062 (June 2026). TIC publishes a post-tax NAV, ₹569.90 at March 2026. For the other four, NAV must be built from filings: Bajaj Nivesh about ₹31,730 a share; Lloyds Enterprises' listed stakes and gold about ₹59 a share; TSF anywhere from ₹272 (book) to about ₹675 (top of the fair-value range); and for Authum, book value per share (₹173 at March 2026) is the fair-value NAV.

The trap. NAVs are stale the moment they are published. BHIL's June figure was ₹21,062; Bajaj Finserv has since fallen about 15% and Bajaj Auto has risen, leaving our October estimate about 1% lower. Always recompute with current prices, and always state whether unlisted holdings are at deal price, book or a multiple.

2. Discount to NAV, pre-tax and post-tax

What it is. How far the share price sits below (or above) the value of what the company owns. It is the single most important number for a passive holdco.

Formula. Discount = 1 − market cap ÷ NAV. A negative discount is a premium. Post-tax discount uses NAV minus latent capital-gains tax.

Worked example. Holdco X above: 1 − 17,000 ÷ 31,500 = 46% pre-tax; 1 − 17,000 ÷ 27,860 = 39% post-tax.

What drives it. Payout (higher narrows), catalysts such as listings, demergers and simplification (narrow), disclosure (narrows), market mood (risk-off widens), complexity and cross-holdings (widen), governance concerns (widen) and liquidity (thin float widens).

Benchmark (passive holdcos, pre-tax). Below 25% is tight and usually means a catalyst is priced in; 25–45% is the normal range; above 45% is wide and carries value-trap risk.

How the members compare. Bajaj Nivesh ~61%, BHIL ~46% (~38% post-tax), TSF ~20–40%, TIC ~5.5% (and a ~5% premium to post-tax NAV). Lloyds Enterprises and Authum trade at premiums.

The trap. Discounts are computed against different NAVs: market-only listed stakes (Lloyds), listed plus unlisted at a deal price (BHIL's insurers), book as a floor (TSF), or a published post-tax figure (TIC). Two discounts are comparable only if their NAVs are built the same way. TIC's primer quoted peer discounts of "~70–80%" for Maharashtra Scooters and "~50%" for BHIL, which contradict the sibling primers and our calculations.

Why the post-tax version matters. Pre-tax discounts flatter holdcos with old, low-cost holdings and penalise those with recent purchases. BHIL's strategic stakes cost about ₹22,025 crore against a market value above ₹2.3 lakh crore, so roughly 13 points of its discount is tax that would be due on any sale. Bajaj Nivesh's holdings were bought so long ago that almost their entire value is gain, so its post-tax NAV is about 85% of its pre-tax NAV. TIC publishes its NAV after notional tax, which is why it appears at a premium on one basis and a discount on the other. When comparing a holdco's discount with history or with peers, use the post-tax figure if the holdings are likely ever to be sold, and the pre-tax figure if the controlling family intends to hold forever and investors believe it. For the Bajaj boxes the second assumption is closer to reality, which is one reason the market does not simply price them at their post-tax NAV.

3. Market cap ÷ NAV, and how to aggregate it

What it is. The same information as the discount, expressed as a multiple: 0.54× means ₹54 of price per ₹100 of NAV. It is the more useful form for combining several holdcos.

Formula. Price-to-NAV = market cap ÷ NAV. For a group, it must be the ratio of sums: Σ market cap ÷ Σ NAV, on matched bases.

Worked example. All six: ₹2,36,151 crore ÷ ₹3,36,869 crore (gross) = 0.70; net of the Bajaj cross-holdings on both sides, ₹2,25,179 crore ÷ ₹3,25,897 crore = 0.69. Excluding BHIL: ₹1,11,238 crore ÷ ₹1,05,437 crore = 1.06. Excluding BHIL and Authum: ₹64,127 crore ÷ ₹90,709 crore = 0.71.

What drives it. The same factors as the discount, plus, for a group figure, the weights: one large company (BHIL) or one premium outlier (Authum) can swing the aggregate.

Benchmark. Above 0.75 is narrow for a passive holdco, 0.55–0.75 normal, below 0.55 wide.

The trap. Never average the six ratios. The simple average of 0.39, 0.54, 0.68, 0.945, 1.17 and 3.20 is about 1.15, which would suggest the group trades at a premium. It does not: weighted by value, it trades at about 0.70.

4. Look-through P/E

What it is. The holdco's market cap divided by its share of the profits its holdings earn, whether or not those profits are paid out or booked. It puts FVOCI and equity-method holdcos on the same footing.

Formula. Look-through P/E = holdco market cap ÷ Σ (stake × investee PAT). Compare it with the weighted P/E of the underlyings.

Worked example. Bajaj Nivesh: 3.05% × ₹19,332 crore + 2.47% × ₹10,574 crore + 2.37% × ₹9,801 crore + 3.04% × ₹9,637 crore ≈ ₹1,377 crore of look-through earnings. Market cap ₹14,069 crore ÷ ₹1,377 crore ≈ 10×, against reported 50.5×.

What drives it. The underlying P/E multiplied by (1 − discount). A holdco at a 46% discount on underlyings at 30× earnings has a look-through P/E of about 16×.

Benchmark. Look-through P/E ÷ weighted underlying P/E below 0.6 is cheap, 0.6–0.85 average, above 0.85 means little discount is being captured.

How the members compare. Bajaj Nivesh ~10× against Bajaj underlyings at roughly 30× (ratio ~0.33, cheap). BHIL ~16× against Bajaj Auto at 24.1× and Bajaj Finserv at ~27–28× (ratio ~0.62, average). Lloyds Enterprises ~48× on its listed stakes against Lloyds Metals at 21.5× and Lloyds Engineering at 65.8×. TSF's reported 18.2× already includes associate profits. TIC not computable without a current portfolio list; Authum not meaningful.

The trap. Look-through P/E ignores whether the profits are ever paid out, and for layered groups it can double count (Bajaj Nivesh's earnings include its share of BHIL's earnings, which include BHIL's share of Bajaj Nivesh's). Use it to compare, not to value.

5. Dividend pass-through ratio

What it is. The share of dividends a holdco receives that it passes on to its own shareholders. It is the cash link between the operating companies and the minority holder.

Formula. Pass-through = dividends paid ÷ dividends received (or ÷ standalone income where receipts are not disclosed).

Worked example. Bajaj Nivesh FY26: received ₹313 crore, paid about ₹252 crore (about ₹220 a share) = 81%. TIC: paid ₹172 crore on ₹403 crore of dividend and interest income = 43%.

What drives it. Board policy, cash needs (Bajaj Nivesh's new renewable object could compete for cash; BHIL funded the insurer purchase partly from its treasury book), and tax: since 2020, s.80M makes pass-through tax-neutral and retention costly.

Benchmark. Above 80% good, 50–80% average, below 50% weak.

How the members compare. Bajaj Nivesh 81% (good). BHIL roughly 100% of estimated strategic dividends including the FY26 centenary special, ~75% excluding it (average to good; ESTIMATE, as BHIL does not disclose receipts). TIC 43% of income (weak). TSF paid 47% of profit, but associate dividends received are not disclosed. Authum ~0. Lloyds not meaningful (8% payout).

The trap. Payout ratio (dividends ÷ PAT) is not pass-through. BHIL's payout ratio is 23% because its PAT is mostly non-cash associate share; its pass-through of cash actually received is far higher.

Necessary, not sufficient. The two members show that pass-through alone does not set the discount. Bajaj Nivesh has passed on 81–87% of its dividend income for four years and its discount has still widened from about 55% to 61%, because it is a second-layer box with a thin float and a holding in its own parent. TIC has cut its pass-through to about 43% and still trades near NAV, because it is diversified, transparent and carries the Tata Sons story. What pass-through does reliably is set the floor for a patient holder's return: even if the discount never moves, a holdco that passes on everything it receives delivers the underlying dividend stream at a discounted entry price. A holdco that retains cash and reinvests it in the same group shares delivers that return only if the market eventually recognises the reinvestment in the share price.

6. Dividend yield and the holdco yield identity

What it is. Dividends per share ÷ share price. For a holdco, it links three other KPIs.

Formula. Holdco yield ≈ look-through yield × pass-through ÷ (1 − discount).

Worked example. If the underlyings yield 1%, the holdco passes on 100%, and the discount is 46%, the holdco yields 1% × 1.00 ÷ 0.54 = 1.85%. The discount amplifies the yield: a buyer of the wrapper gets more dividend per rupee than a buyer of the underlyings, provided the wrapper passes it on.

What drives it. Underlying payout, pass-through and discount. A widening discount raises the yield, which is one of the few self-correcting forces in holdco pricing.

Benchmark. Above 2% good, 1–2% average, below 1% weak.

How the members compare. TSF 2.79%, Bajaj Nivesh 1.83%, BHIL 1.29% on screener's trailing basis (about 1.74% on the FY26 ₹195 including the special), TIC 0.54%, Lloyds 0.21%, Authum 0.02%. Bajaj Auto itself yields 1.44%: BHIL's yield is below its main holding's because of the mix of Bajaj Finserv (which pays very little) and the trailing basis.

The trap. Trailing yield includes one-offs. BHIL's FY26 included a ₹50 centenary special; its FY27 interim was a plain ₹65. Use the regular dividend for a forward yield.

7. Top-holding concentration

What it is. The share of NAV (or gross assets) in the largest single holding, and in the top two.

Formula. Concentration = largest holding ÷ GAV.

Worked example. BHIL: Bajaj Finserv ₹1,06,394 crore ÷ GAV ₹2,31,442 crore = 46%; top two (with Bajaj Auto) 88%.

What drives it. Portfolio design, and relative share-price moves: a holding that outperforms becomes a bigger share of NAV.

Benchmark. Below 25% good (diversified), 25–50% average, above 50% weak (single-stock risk).

How the members compare. Bajaj Nivesh 51% (Bajaj Finance); Lloyds Enterprises 51% of listed stakes plus gold (Lloyds Engineering); BHIL 46% (Bajaj Finserv; 88% top two); TSF an ESTIMATED 35–45% (Brakes India, unlisted); Authum about 19% (Welspun Corp, of a ~₹13,100 crore investment book, early 2026); TIC below 15% (estimate).

The trap. Concentration at the first layer understates it in a layered group. BHIL's 46% in Bajaj Finserv is really mostly Bajaj Finance, and Bajaj Nivesh's holdings are all Bajaj; combined, nearly all of both boxes is three related companies.

Why concentration widens the discount. A concentrated holdco competes with its own main holding. An investor who wants Bajaj Finance can buy Bajaj Finance directly, with full liquidity, no second layer of governance and no latent holdco tax. The only reason to buy it through Bajaj Nivesh instead is the discount, so the discount has to be wide enough to compensate for everything the wrapper takes away. A diversified holdco such as TIC offers something a single purchase cannot: a ready-made basket, rebalanced by someone else. That is part of the reason TIC trades near NAV and the concentrated Bajaj boxes do not. Concentration also changes how a holdco reacts to news: when Bajaj Finserv fell about 15% between August and October 2026, BHIL's NAV absorbed most of the shock, while TIC's NAV moves with a broad Tata basket.

8. Price-to-book, for fair-value books only

What it is. Market cap ÷ net worth. Useful only where book value is itself close to market value.

Formula. P/B = market cap ÷ shareholders' equity.

Worked example. Authum: ₹47,111 crore ÷ ₹14,728 crore = 3.20×. Bajaj Nivesh: book value ₹24,180 a share (Bajaj shares at fair value, net of deferred tax) against a price of ₹12,310 = 0.51×.

What drives it. For a fair-value book, P/B is close to price-to-NAV after tax. For an equity-method or cost book, it reflects accounting history.

Benchmark (fair-value books). Below 0.8× cheap, 0.8–1.5× average, above 1.5× premium.

How the members compare. Meaningful: Bajaj Nivesh 0.51×, TIC 1.05×, Authum 3.20×. Not meaningful: BHIL 1.71× (screener's book of ₹6,573 a share is equity-method; the company's own cost-basis book is ₹2,425 a share; NAV is ₹21,062), TSF 1.47× (associates near cost), Lloyds 2.49× (investments largely at cost).

The trap. BHIL has three "book values": ₹6,573 (screener), ₹2,425 (company, cost basis) and ₹21,062 (NAV). A P/B of 1.71× sounds expensive for a stock at a 46% discount to NAV.

9. ROE on book versus return on market NAV

What it is. Two ways of measuring how well the box is compounding. Book ROE uses accounting profit and book equity. Return on market NAV uses the change in the market value of the holdings plus dividends paid.

Formula. Book ROE = PAT ÷ average equity. Return on market NAV = (closing NAV − opening NAV + dividends paid) ÷ opening NAV.

Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE). A holdco opens the year with NAV of ₹10,000 crore, closes at ₹10,800 crore and pays ₹100 crore of dividends: return on market NAV = (800 + 100) ÷ 10,000 = 9%. If its equity-method book was ₹4,000 crore and it booked ₹500 crore of associate profit, its book ROE is 12.5%. The two can diverge for years.

What drives it. Book ROE: associate earnings, dividends and, for FVTPL books, marks. Market-NAV return: share prices of the holdings.

Benchmark. Return on market NAV above 15% good, 8–15% average, below 8% weak.

How the members compare. Book ROE: Authum 12.8%, BHIL 11.9%, TSF 8.2%, Lloyds about 10% (our calculation; screener shows an implausible 0.33%), TIC 1.44%, Bajaj Nivesh 1.06%. Market-NAV return for FY26, approximated by the change in fair-value reserves plus dividends: Bajaj Nivesh about −10%, TIC about −5.5%. BHIL's NAV per share rose about 7% between March and October 2026, before dividends (INDICATIVE).

The trap. Bajaj Nivesh's and TIC's book ROEs of 1–1.5% look dreadful only because FVOCI keeps price gains out of profit while putting them into equity. Their real economic return in FY26 was negative, for a different reason: their holdings fell.

10. Leverage: borrowings, pledges and net debt to NAV

What it is. How much the holdco owes against what it owns, and whether it has pledged holdings to borrow.

Formula. Net debt ÷ NAV = (borrowings − cash) ÷ NAV. Pledge ratio = shares encumbered ÷ shares held.

Worked example. Lloyds Enterprises holds 48.33 crore Lloyds Engineering shares, of which 11.25 crore are encumbered: 23% of its stake. On 13 August 2026 it pledged 6 crore of them for a ₹219 crore loan from Tata Capital. At ₹94.9 those 6 crore shares are worth about ₹569 crore, 2.6 times the loan. If Lloyds Engineering fell 30%, cover would drop to about 1.8 times (ILLUSTRATIVE); lenders typically demand more shares or repayment when cover falls below an agreed threshold, which can force selling into a falling market.

What drives it. Deal activity (Authum), consolidation of operating subsidiaries (TSF's Axles, Lloyds' Engineering arm) and group funding needs.

Benchmark. Net cash good; net debt up to 10% of NAV average; above 10% weak. Any pledge of core holdings is a flag.

How the members compare. BHIL, Bajaj Nivesh and TIC are net cash. TSF about 2% of mid-point NAV (₹235 crore). Lloyds Enterprises about 9% of listed stakes plus gold (₹798 crore consolidated), with pledges. Authum about 23% of investments (₹3,598 crore).

The trap. Consolidated borrowings mix holdco debt with subsidiaries' operating debt. TSF's ₹235 crore is mostly Axles India's; Lloyds' ₹798 crore includes Lloyds Engineering's. What matters for the holdco shareholder is standalone debt and pledges, which require reading the filings.

Leverage and mark-to-market together. Debt matters most where holdings are marked through the P&L. Authum carried ₹15,473 crore of investments against ₹3,598 crore of borrowings and ₹14,728 crore of book at March 2026. A 10% fall in the investment portfolio would remove about ₹1,547 crore of value, roughly 10.5% of book, before any change in the debt; a 10% rise would add the same. Without the borrowing, the same portfolio would be smaller and each move proportionally milder. This is ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic, since not every investment is a listed share and part of the book is loans. For the passive, debt-free members the question does not arise: a 10% fall in their holdings moves their NAV by at most 10%, and nothing forces them to sell.

11. Opex to NAV: the visible cost of the wrapper

What it is. Holdco operating expenses as a share of NAV, the equivalent of a fund's expense ratio.

Formula. Opex ÷ NAV.

Worked example. BHIL: ₹162 crore ÷ ₹2,31,432 crore = 0.07%.

What drives it. Scale, staff, and whether the holdco runs operating businesses (which should be excluded).

Benchmark. Below 0.10% good, 0.10–0.30% average, above 0.30% weak.

How the members compare. Bajaj Nivesh 0.01%, BHIL 0.07%, TIC 0.13%. Not meaningful for Authum (an active investor and lender), Lloyds or TSF (which consolidate operating businesses).

The trap. Opex is the cost you can see. The discount is the cost you cannot. At 0.07% a year, BHIL's expenses would take more than six centuries to add up to its 46-point discount.

12. RBI category: what the licence tells you

What it is. The regulatory box the holdco sits in: registered CIC, unregistered (exempt) CIC, NBFC-ICC, or not regulated as a finance company.

How to read it. An unregistered CIC (TSF, Bajaj Nivesh, BHIL after re-categorisation) must keep at least 90% of net assets in group companies and cannot raise public funds, so it is structurally a pure, low-leverage group holdco. An NBFC-ICC (TIC, Authum) can invest outside the group and lend, so its portfolio and leverage can change. A company outside RBI regulation (Lloyds Enterprises) can do almost anything a trading company can, including pledge-funded investment.

Benchmark. There is no good or bad category, but a change of category is a signal: BHIL's move toward CIC status means more group purity and fewer non-group bets; a holdco moving the other way would be taking on more freedom and more risk.

The trap. Labels age. TIC's primer calls it an "NBFC-ND-SI Investment Company", a category abolished in 2019. Check the current RBI list, not the annual report boilerplate.

Where the six sit. Unregistered or exempt CIC: TSF, Bajaj Nivesh and BHIL (re-categorising from an investment NBFC during FY26). NBFC investment-and-credit company in the Middle Layer by size: TIC and Authum. Not RBI-regulated: Lloyds Enterprises. The registered CIC in the background is Tata Sons, TIC's parent, whose Upper Layer status is the reason it must now list. The category map lines up almost exactly with the archetypes: the three group holdcos are CICs, the two portfolio investors are ICCs, and the hybrid is outside the system altogether.

In plain terms

The RBI category is a quick test of what a holdco is allowed to become. Unregistered CICs are locked into owning their group; investment-and-credit NBFCs can lend and buy outside it; an unregulated trading company can borrow against its shares. The more freedom a structure has, the more the share price depends on trusting the people using it.

Definition traps, collected

  • "Revenue" is not revenue. It is dividend income (BHIL, Bajaj Nivesh, TIC), an NBFC line (Authum) or a consolidated subsidiary's sales (Lloyds, TSF). Never sum it or compare its growth.
  • Profit has three meanings. Associate share (non-cash), FVOCI dividend-only profit (understated) and FVTPL marks (volatile). BHIL's ₹9,637 crore and Bajaj Nivesh's ₹311 crore are not comparable numbers.
  • Pre-minority versus attributable. Screener shows BHIL at ₹9,789 crore (before Bajaj Nivesh's minority); the company reports ₹9,637 crore attributable.
  • Book value has three meanings. Equity-method (BHIL ₹6,573), cost-basis (BHIL's own ₹2,425) and fair value net of deferred tax (TIC ₹578, Bajaj Nivesh ₹24,180). P/B works only for the last.
  • NAV is pre-tax unless labelled. TIC publishes post-tax; BHIL publishes pre-tax. A 14.56% tax on embedded gains separates them.
  • Discounts use different NAVs. Listed-only (Lloyds), deal price for unlisted (BHIL's insurers), book floor (TSF), published post-tax (TIC).
  • Cross-holdings double count. BHIL and Bajaj Nivesh own each other; net ₹10,972 crore before adding their NAVs or market caps.
  • Payout ratio is not pass-through. A 23% payout of non-cash profit can be 100% of cash received.
  • Quarterly profit is seasonal. Bajaj Nivesh earns most of its year in July–September, when the Bajaj companies pay final dividends.
  • Consolidated debt is not holdco debt. TSF's and Lloyds' borrowings include subsidiaries' operating debt; pledges sit in separate disclosures.

How to read a holding company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Recompute NAV first (two minutes). List the stakes from the latest shareholding filings, multiply by today's prices, add cash and unlisted holdings at a stated basis, subtract debt. If the company publishes NAV (BHIL, TIC), compare and note the date difference.
  2. Compute the discount, pre- and post-tax. Compare with last quarter and with the company's own two-year range. A move of more than five points is the story of the quarter, whatever the profit line says.
  3. Split profit into its pieces. Associate share, dividends and interest, realised gains, mark-to-market, exceptional items. Ignore the headline growth rate until you know which piece moved.
  4. Check seasonality. For FVOCI holdcos, compare the same quarter last year, never the previous quarter. Bajaj Nivesh's July–September quarter carries most of its year.
  5. Find the cash. Read standalone results for dividends received and paid. Compute pass-through. Note any change in dividend policy, special dividend or record date.
  6. Read the OCI line. For Bajaj Nivesh and TIC, other comprehensive income shows the real gain or loss on the portfolio. For BHIL, the NAV disclosure does the same job.
  7. Check leverage and pledges. Standalone borrowings, any new pledge or release of pledged shares (Lloyds), and any change in the share of debt to investments (Authum). Red flag: borrowing rising faster than NAV.
  8. Scan corporate actions. Stake purchases or sales, demergers, renames, category changes, warrant conversions, preferential subscriptions. These are what move discounts.
  9. Read the governance notes. Auditor qualifications, contingent liabilities, regulatory orders, tax searches, subsidiaries with negative net worth. For the active allocators, these matter more than the profit line.
  10. Write one sentence. "NAV per share moved X%, the discount moved Y points, cash pass-through was Z%." If you cannot fill in all three, you have not yet read the results.
In plain terms

For a holding company, the quarter's real news is almost never in the profit figure. It is in three numbers you usually have to compute yourself: how much the holdings are worth now, how far the share price sits below that, and how much cash came out. Get those three and you have read the results; miss them and the headline profit will mislead you.

Start at the right-hand block, not the left. The left-hand columns are what a stock screen shows, and for holdcos they mislead. Bajaj Nivesh's 99% operating margin and 50.5× P/E, TIC's 1.4% ROE and Lloyds' 92.4× P/E are accounting outcomes, not measures of quality or value. The native columns tell the story: Bajaj Nivesh and BHIL at deep discounts to NAV with high pass-through; TIC at roughly NAV with low pass-through; TSF at a wide but uncertain discount with the best yield; Lloyds and Authum at premiums with almost no payout.

Read the discount with its basis. The six NAVs are built six different ways, as the notes explain. BHIL's and Bajaj Nivesh's are the firmest: almost everything they own is listed, so the main judgement is the insurers' value inside BHIL. TIC's is a pre-tax proxy that matches the company's own post-tax figure closely. Authum's is book, which for an FVTPL investor is close to fair value. Lloyds' excludes the trading business and land, so its "premium" partly reflects real assets left out of the NAV. TSF's is a range, and the discount could be anywhere from about 20% to about 40%.

Use the combined rows to see the shape of the group. All six together trade at about 0.70 times NAV, a discount of about 30%, whether or not the Bajaj cross-holdings are netted. Taking BHIL out leaves a basket at a small premium (1.06×), but that is entirely Authum: removing it as well brings the remaining four back to 0.71×. The passive boxes, in other words, trade at about 70 paise in the rupee whichever way they are grouped, and the sub-sector's only premiums belong to the two companies whose managers actively reallocate capital.

Do not add BHIL and Bajaj Nivesh as if they were separate pools. BHIL's profit already includes 51% of Bajaj Nivesh's, and each company's NAV includes the other's shares. The combined NAV is shown both gross and net for this reason, and the net figure deducts the cross-holdings from market cap as well, so the ratio compares like with like.

For a holdco, "winning" means NAV growth plus cash paid out plus a narrowing discount. Three maps show who is positioned for which.

Three lessons carry over. First, a long record of steady dividend growth and low costs is the most reliable discount-narrower; Investor AB took decades to earn its near-NAV pricing, and TIC's comparable position owes more to its diversified portfolio and Tata following than to its payout, which has been falling. Second, buybacks funded by selling holdings work, but only when sustained and large: Prosus's open-ended programme narrowed its discount by perhaps fifteen points. India's 2024 decision to tax buyback proceeds as dividends in shareholders' hands makes that tool less effective for resident investors. Third, regulatory pressure can matter, as Korea's Value-Up programme suggests; in India the equivalent has come not from a holdco-specific policy but from the RBI's listing rule for large finance companies.

Berkshire Hathaway is the global model for the two Indian premium cases. It shows that investors will pay above book for an allocator with a long, trusted record of compounding capital. It also shows how much trust that takes: decades of disclosure, conservative leverage and a governance record beyond question. Authum's 3.2× book is a bet that a five-year record of distressed-asset buying can become something similar, made while the company faces a tax search and rising debt.

In plain terms

Family holding companies trade at discounts almost everywhere. India's passive boxes are at the wide end, alongside Italy's Exor and the Korean groups. The tools that have closed gaps elsewhere, large buybacks and simplification, are either taxed more heavily in India or not yet being used.

Disruption and wrapper risk

A holdco has no technology of its own to disrupt. It inherits its holdings' risks and adds two of its own: the discount, and the people who control the box.

Holding companies have no products, plants or customers, so the usual technology questions do not apply directly. What applies is the disruption facing what they own, plus risks that come from the wrapper itself.

Inherited disruption

  • Electric vehicles. Bajaj Auto's position in the shift from petrol to electric two- and three-wheelers drives about 42% of BHIL's gross assets and 19% of Bajaj Nivesh's. At TSF, Turbo Energy's turbochargers serve diesel engines, a technology EVs would make obsolete; Brakes India and Wheels India are largely powertrain-neutral.
  • Fintech and credit competition. Bajaj Finance, which is about 51% of Bajaj Nivesh's NAV and the largest part of BHIL's look-through value, faces digital lenders, banks' retail push and regulatory tightening on unsecured credit (see the NBFC report).
  • AI and IT services. TCS and other Tata technology holdings inside TIC's portfolio face the question of how generative AI changes demand for outsourced IT; Tata-group stock weakness was the main reason TIC's reserves fell 6% in FY26.
  • Commodity and mining cycles. Lloyds Metals' iron-ore and pellet economics, and the new gold production at Geomysore, drive Lloyds Enterprises' value.
  • Interest rates. The RBI's 7 October repo increase to 5.50% raises funding costs for Authum's leveraged book and for the lenders inside the Bajaj boxes, and higher rates generally lower the multiples investors pay for all of them.

Other wrapper risks

  • Disintermediation. Index funds and ETFs make it cheap and easy to own the underlyings directly. Every rupee that moves into a Nifty fund or a Bajaj Finance holding is a rupee that does not need a holdco, which removes part of the reason to own one.
  • Tax change. The 2024 increases in capital-gains tax and the buyback change both widened the rational floor of discounts. A future change to s.80M would make pass-through more expensive overnight.
  • Regulatory reclassification. CIC tests, Upper Layer designations and NBFC category rules can force sales, listings or structure changes on timetables the company does not control.
  • Governance shocks. At Authum, an Income Tax search (from 20 July 2026), a subsidiary with negative net worth and a ₹25 crore SEBI penalty under appeal; at Lloyds, borrowing against pledged shares. These are the risks most likely to hit a premium valuation hardest.
  • Controller decisions. A cash-rich box can choose to spend rather than distribute. Bajaj Nivesh's new renewable-energy object is a reminder that the controller, not the minority, decides how retained cash is used.
In plain terms

Owning a holdco means owning two risks at once: the risk in the companies inside, and the risk that the market's discount on the box changes. For the deepest-discount boxes, the second risk is as large as the first. For the premium boxes, the main risk is a loss of trust in the people running them.

Bull versus bear

The case for and against owning India's holding companies as a group, stated as plainly as possible.

The bull and bear cases for this sub-sector are unusually clear, because they turn on a small number of variables: the quality of a few underlying companies, the discount, payout policy and, for two members, trust in the allocator. Both sides agree on the facts; they disagree on whether the discount is an opportunity or a permanent feature.

The bull case

  • Great assets at a discount: ₹100 of BHIL buys about ₹185 of Bajaj shares and insurers; ₹100 of Bajaj Nivesh buys about ₹258. The underlyings are among India's best-run companies.
  • Discount as a margin of safety: at 46–61%, the Bajaj boxes already price a lot of bad news; the 2015–19 average for BHIL was about 58% and the COVID trough about 66%.
  • Cash is flowing: BHIL paid ₹195 for FY26 including a special; Bajaj Nivesh passes on 81%; TSF yields 2.8%. s.80M makes pass-through tax-neutral.
  • Regulators are now the catalyst: the RBI forced Tata Capital to list and has put Tata Sons on the same path; CIC rules push BHIL toward purity; SEBI auctions fix price discovery in illiquid holdcos.
  • Debt-free and almost costless: four of the six have no meaningful borrowing and opex below 0.15% of NAV.
  • Two genuine compounders: Authum's net worth rose ten-fold in five years; TSF owns quality unlisted suppliers no other listed vehicle offers.

The bear case

  • Discounts that never close: Indian holdcos averaged 54.5% in 2012 and the Bajaj boxes are at 46–61% today. No minority investor can force change while promoters hold 51–73%.
  • Concentration: the Bajaj boxes are mostly two or three related companies; a Bajaj Finance setback hits both and widens their discounts at the same time.
  • Profits are mostly not cash: associate share, FVOCI and FVTPL make reported earnings unreliable; BHIL's cash income is about a fifth of its reported profit.
  • Tools are blocked: buybacks are now taxed as dividends; capital-gains tax rose in 2024; no merger of the Bajaj boxes has been proposed.
  • Premiums rest on trust: Authum faces a tax search, a negative-net-worth subsidiary, 3.4× higher debt and a selling promoter; Lloyds borrows against pledged shares.
  • Catalysts may disappoint: TIC does not own Tata Sons shares as far as can be verified, and its premium has already gone; a Tata Sons listing could be delayed or fought.

Weighing the two, the bull case is strongest for investors who can hold through cycles and value cash yield over re-rating: Bajaj Nivesh and BHIL deliver the Bajaj dividend stream at a large discount, and TSF delivers the Sundaram group's at a smaller but uncertain one. The bear case is strongest for anyone counting on the discount to close on a predictable timetable, because nothing in India's history or current rules guarantees that. For the active allocators, the arguments are about people rather than structure, and the evidence on governance should carry at least as much weight as the record of returns.

In plain terms

The optimist sees excellent companies on sale at half price, with cash flowing out and regulators starting to force change. The pessimist sees half-price sales that have lasted for decades, profits that are mostly accounting, and two expensive vehicles whose appeal depends on trusting their managers through a tax search and rising debt.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, defined for holding companies.

  • Associate (equity method). A company in which the holdco owns 20–50% and has significant influence. The holdco books its share of the associate's profit, whether or not it receives any cash (Ind AS 28).
  • Core Investment Company (CIC). RBI category for group holdcos with at least 90% of net assets in group companies and 60% in their equity. Registration is required only above ₹100 crore of assets with access to public funds; otherwise "exempt" or "unregistered".
  • Discount to NAV. 1 − market cap ÷ NAV. A negative discount is a premium.
  • FVOCI. Fair value through other comprehensive income: price changes go to reserves, only dividends reach profit.
  • FVTPL. Fair value through profit and loss: every price change is profit or loss.
  • GAV. Gross asset value: the holdings before deducting holdco debt.
  • IHC special call auction. SEBI's annual auction (since 2024) for investment holding companies trading below half of book value, run without a price band.
  • Latent tax. The capital-gains tax a holdco would pay if it sold its holdings; about 14.56% of the embedded gain for a company on listed shares.
  • Look-through. Following ownership down the chain to the operating companies (BHIL → Bajaj Finserv → Bajaj Finance).
  • Look-through P/E. Holdco market cap ÷ its share of its holdings' profits.
  • NAV. Net asset value: holdings at market (unlisted at a stated basis) plus cash minus debt; pre-tax unless labelled post-tax.
  • NBFC-ICC. Non-bank finance company, investment and credit company: the RBI category (since 2019) for TIC and Authum.
  • Pass-through ratio. Dividends paid ÷ dividends received.
  • Pledge / encumbrance. Shares offered as security for a loan; the lender can sell them if the borrower defaults or the cover falls too low.
  • Scale-Based Regulation (SBR). The RBI's 2021 framework sorting NBFCs into Base, Middle, Upper and Top layers; Upper Layer companies must list within three years.
  • Section 80M. Income-tax provision letting a company deduct dividends received to the extent it distributes them, making pass-through tax-neutral.
  • Sum of the parts (SOTP). Valuing a company by adding up separately valued pieces.
  • Upper Layer. The RBI's tier for the largest and most systemically important NBFCs, including Tata Sons.
