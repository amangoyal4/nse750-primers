00

The one-page view: a ₹15 lakh crore order book that pays slowly

Demand is the government's capital budget. Profit is booked as work is done. Cash arrives when someone signs the bill. Almost everything that matters in this sector happens in the gaps between those three.

Indian civil construction is the business of turning the state's capital budget into roads, railway lines, transmission towers, tunnels, metro stations, water plants and buildings, and then waiting to be paid for them. The 22 contractors in the NSE 750 earned a combined ₹4.91 lakh crore of revenue in FY26 and held a combined order book of roughly ₹15.3 lakh crore at the end of June 2026, a little over three years of work. That order book has never been larger. Yet FY26 was one of the sector's hardest years in a decade.

The contradiction has three parts. First, the tide is still rising but more slowly. The Union government's capital spending went from ₹3.36 lakh crore in FY20 to ₹12.22 lakh crore budgeted for FY27, but annual growth has dropped from about 30% in FY21–24 to roughly 9–10%. When the whole tide rose 30% a year, nearly every contractor grew. At 9–10%, winners are decided by which segments a company happens to be in.

Second, those segments moved in opposite directions. National highway construction fell to about 9,380 km in FY26, the lowest in eight years, after the 2024 general election froze awards. Jal Jeevan Mission water projects slowed because states stopped paying. At the same time India added a record 44.6 GW of solar capacity, transmission tenders hit a record 45 schemes in FY25, and Gulf oil companies and sovereign giga-projects kept ordering. The listed basket split accordingly: thirteen companies grew revenue in FY26 and nine shrank. Excluding L&T, combined revenue grew just 1.0%.

Third, the order book is not cash. Contractors book revenue as work progresses under percentage-of-completion accounting, but the client pays only when it certifies the bill, holds back about 5% as retention for years, and disputes variations through arbitration that can take a decade. India Ratings found that its rated EPC contractors converted only ₹29 of every ₹100 of FY26 operating profit into operating cash flow, against a historical norm of about ₹65. Among the 18 listed contractors where a comparable figure can be calculated, eight reported negative operating cash flow in FY26.

The five forces that define the sector

  • The government budget, and its mix, is demand. Almost every rupee of order inflow traces back to a Union, state or PSU budget, or to a regulated tariff. With aggregate capex growth down to about 9–10% a year, segment exposure (transmission, rail, urban, international) now matters more than the size of the tide.
  • Working capital, not margin, decides returns. A contractor earning 10% EBITDA but waiting 150 days for payment can destroy value; one earning 7% and collecting in 18 days (L&T's FY26 net working capital was about 4–5% of sales) compounds. Most of India's construction failures in 2008–13 were working-capital failures that looked like margin stories.
  • Lowest-price bidding pins margins to the weakest bidder. Government contracts go to the L1 (lowest) bid, and a long tail of contractors means margins revert to whatever the most desperate bidder accepts. Durable margins come only from complexity (tunnels, marine, HVDC), vertical integration (tower and conductor plants) or client relationships (railway nomination).
  • Balance-sheet capacity limits growth. A contractor can only bid what its bank-guarantee lines, net worth and the NHAI bid-capacity formula allow. Hybrid-annuity road projects also need equity of roughly 10–12% of project cost (indicative), which is why road developers keep building, selling to infrastructure funds and re-bidding.
  • The energy transition and the Gulf are the new engines. Transmission (a ₹9.15 lakh crore plan to 2032), renewables (54.6 GW added in FY26) and Gulf hydrocarbon and giga-project spending drive the strongest order books. They also bring fixed-price, commodity and geopolitical risk.

L&T is a category of its own. It contributes 58% of the 22 companies' revenue, 51% of their order book and 70% of their market value. Its numbers also come wrapped around a listed IT services group and a listed lender, which distort reported margins and debt. Throughout this report, combined figures are shown both with and without L&T, and L&T's construction economics are shown on the company's own basis (group EBITDA margin of 9.0% in Q1 FY27, core engineering and construction about 7–7.8%), not on the screener figure of 12.7%.

The rest of the basket is a set of very different businesses that happen to share a label. Railway PSUs (RVNL, Ircon) and NBCC run cost-plus or fee models with thin margins. Transmission specialists (KEC, Kalpataru, Transrail, Techno) make their own towers and conductors. Road developers (Dilip Buildcon, PNC, H.G. Infra, Ashoka, KNR, Welspun) own hybrid-annuity concessions that inflate their consolidated margins to 17–26% when their actual construction margin is 8.5–13%. Specialists such as Afcons, Cemindia and HCC build tunnels, marine works and dams that few competitors can bid for. Comparing any of these on raw screener numbers produces nonsense, and much of this report is about knowing which number to use.

In plain terms

An Indian contractor is paid to take three risks for the government: the risk that the job costs more than the bid, the risk that the job takes longer than planned, and the risk that the client pays late. In a boom all three look small. In FY26 the third one dominated: the work got done and the profit got booked, but for most mid-sized contractors the cash stayed with the client. The companies that thrived were the ones whose clients paid (Gulf oil companies, the central grid, private developers) or whose balance sheets were strong enough to wait.

01

How a contractor earns: margin on paper, cash on certification

An EPC contractor sells a promise to build something at a fixed price by a fixed date. The margin is set at the bid; everything after that is about protecting it and collecting it.

EPC stands for engineering, procurement and construction. The contractor designs the asset (or completes a client's design), buys the materials and equipment, hires the labour (mostly through subcontractors), builds the asset and hands it over. The work spans roads and highways, railway lines and electrification, metros, power transmission lines and substations, solar and wind farms, water supply and irrigation schemes, buildings and factories, tunnels, dams, ports and marine works, and for a few companies, oil and gas plants, defence equipment and nuclear components.

The money flows from a budget to an agency to a contractor, and the contractor earns its margin in the middle. A ministry or state allocates funds; an agency such as NHAI, a railway PSU, a state transmission company or a municipal body prepares the project and tenders it; the contractor wins, builds and bills; the agency certifies and pays; and once the asset is complete it is operated, maintained and often sold to an infrastructure investment trust (InvIT) or a toll-operate-transfer buyer.

The contractor's margin is fixed at Step 3 and earned at Step 4, but its cash depends on Step 5. The best franchises either win Step 3 without a price war (complexity, nomination) or shorten Step 5 (advances, reliable clients).

Value is captured unevenly along this chain. A pure EPC contractor earns a builder's margin, typically 7–13% EBITDA, on a base that requires a lot of working capital. Its return on capital is excellent when clients pay on time and poor when they do not. An asset owner, such as a hybrid-annuity road developer or a tariff-based transmission licensee, earns a 12–16% equity return (indicative) on a long-dated annuity or toll stream, and usually sells the asset once it is built. Suppliers upstream (cement, steel, construction equipment) and owners downstream (InvITs) often earn better risk-adjusted returns than mid-tier contractors, which is one reason the sector has historically traded at lower valuations than capital-goods makers.

How a contract turns into profit

The price is fixed when the bid is submitted. The contractor estimates total cost, adds a margin and bids; if it is the lowest (L1), it wins. Government contracts in India usually include a price-variation clause that adjusts the bill for movements in steel, cement, bitumen, fuel and labour indices, with about 15% of the price left unindexed (indicative). Private, international and many renewable contracts are fixed-price, so cost inflation lands entirely on the contractor.

Revenue is recognised under Ind AS 115 on a percentage-of-completion basis, normally by the cost-to-cost method: if 40% of the estimated total cost has been spent, 40% of the contract price is booked as revenue. Profit is therefore an estimate that depends on management's forecast of the remaining cost. If that forecast rises, the cumulative correction hits the current quarter, and a contract expected to lose money must be provided for in full immediately. RVNL's ₹54 crore onerous-contract provision in Q4 FY26 and Afcons's first quarterly loss since 2010 in the same quarter were examples of this mechanism.

Cash follows a different clock. The contractor raises a running-account bill each month; the client's engineer measures the work and certifies it; the client pays the certified amount minus retention (typically 5%) and minus recovery of any mobilisation advance (often 10% of the contract value, paid up front and recovered from bills). Retention is released only after a defect-liability period of one to five years. Disputed variations become claims, and claims become arbitration. Every gap between work done and money received sits on the balance sheet as receivables, unbilled revenue (contract assets) or claims.

02

Size and the capex tide: still rising, no longer surging

Union capital spending has risen 3.6 times since FY20. The re-rating it fuelled was about the pace of growth, and the pace has halved.

The demand for civil construction in India is, to a first approximation, the government's capital budget. Union capital expenditure was ₹3.36 lakh crore in FY20. It rose to ₹4.26 lakh crore in FY21, ₹5.93 lakh crore in FY22, ₹7.40 lakh crore in FY23 and ₹9.49 lakh crore in FY24. The FY27 budget, presented in February 2026, allocates ₹12.22 lakh crore, about 9% more than FY26's ₹11.21 lakh crore budget estimate. Including grants to states for creating capital assets, "effective capex" is ₹17.15 lakh crore, about 4.4% of GDP and more than 32% of total Union expenditure.

The Union budget is only part of the picture. State governments together spend roughly as much on capital works as the Centre, and they are the source of most water, irrigation, urban and state-road contracts. Since FY24 the Centre has lent states about ₹1.3–1.5 lakh crore a year in 50-year interest-free capex loans, which counts within the ₹17.15 lakh crore effective figure. Railway capex (₹2.78 lakh crore of gross budgetary support in FY27, up from ₹2.52 lakh crore), PSU spending by PGCIL, NTPC and the oil companies, and a reviving private capex cycle in data centres, factories and renewables add to the pool. Altogether India spends roughly ₹18–20 lakh crore a year on construction-heavy investment (indicative), and the construction sector's nominal output is perhaps ₹25–30 lakh crore a year, around 8–9% of gross value added (indicative). India is usually ranked the world's third-largest construction market after China and the United States, with roughly 4–5% of global output (indicative).

The near-term outlook is modest. ICRA (March 2026) expects sector revenue to grow 6–8% in FY27 on a roughly 10% rise in order inflow, led by a recovery in road awards and water, with diversified contractors growing 8–10%. India Ratings' review of FY26 is a reminder of how far reality can fall below promise: its rated EPC portfolio grew revenue only 2.8% against double-digit management guidance, and fresh tender awards fell about 18%, with roads and water leading the decline. For the listed basket, FY26 combined revenue grew 7.0% (to ₹4,90,755 crore from ₹4,58,657 crore), but that was almost all L&T; the other 21 grew 1.0%.

In plain terms

A government that spends 9% more each year is still a growing customer, just not the explosive one that lifted every contractor's stock in 2021–24. In a slower market, contractors stop being a single trade. The question is no longer "is capex rising?" but "which line of the budget is rising, which state is paying, and which contractor is positioned there?"

03

Six end-markets moving in opposite directions

Roads stalled, water froze, rail plateaued, while the grid, solar, buildings and the Gulf boomed. A contractor's FY26 was decided by its order-book mix two years earlier.

Roads: an eight-year low

The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways' budget was flat at ₹2.72 lakh crore in FY26 after compounding 19% a year over FY21–24. National highway construction fell from 12,349 km in FY24 to 10,660 km in FY25 and about 9,380 km in FY26, roughly 26 km a day and the lowest in eight years. Awards have stagnated at around 7,500–8,500 km a year since FY24, and only 2,257 km had been awarded by December 2025. The ministry is now shifting from the Bharatmala programme to access-controlled corridors (11,000 km targeted by FY27), and NHAI is reducing its debt through toll-operate-transfer sales, which partly explains the slower awarding. The road-heavy contractors took the hit: Dilip Buildcon's consolidated revenue fell 21%, PNC's 21%, Ashoka's 25% and KNR's 43%. Section 09 covers roads in depth.

Railways and metro: big budget, flat growth, harder margins

Railway capex has risen from about ₹54,000 crore in FY14 to ₹2.52 lakh crore in FY25–26, but it has plateaued, and FY27's ₹2.78 lakh crore is a 10% increase. The work is shifting from new lines and gauge conversion towards signalling (Kavach is commissioned on 2,633 route-km of a ~68,000 route-km network), station redevelopment (1,300-plus Amrit Bharat stations), high-speed rail (the 508 km Mumbai–Ahmedabad corridor) and rolling stock. India has about 1,000 km of metro in operation and roughly 900 km under construction across some two dozen cities (approximate). For the railway PSUs, a growing share of this work is now competitively bid rather than nominated, which has compressed margins; Section 12 covers this.

Transmission: record tenders, constrained execution

The Central Electricity Authority's transmission plan calls for about ₹9.15 lakh crore of investment over 2022–32 to integrate 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030, adding about 1.91 lakh circuit-km of lines and 33 GW of HVDC links. FY25 saw a record 45 inter-state transmission schemes awarded under tariff-based competitive bidding, with ₹19,786 crore of annual tariff. Awards then paused (two schemes in Q1 FY26), and execution became the bottleneck: only 47.5% of the line-addition target was met in the first nine months of FY26, while 90% of the transformation-capacity target was met. KEC's transmission revenue rose 24% to ₹15,883 crore, Kalpataru's revenue 22% and Transrail's 30%.

Renewables: a record year with oversupply risk

India added 54.6 GW of renewable capacity including large hydro in FY26 (+85%): 44.6 GW of solar, 6.1 GW of wind and 3.7 GW of large hydro. The previous solar record was 23.8 GW in FY25. Waaree Renewable Technologies roughly doubled its revenue to ₹3,331 crore and Sterling and Wilson posted a record ₹7,548 crore. The risks are visible: module oversupply, more than 40 GW of awarded capacity reported without signed power-sale agreements (2025), curtailment and transmission lag. Section 10 covers the grid and solar together.

Water: the payment freeze

The Jal Jeevan Mission, launched in August 2019 to give every rural home a tap connection, had an original outlay of ₹3.6 lakh crore and has been extended to 2028 (total funding reported at about ₹8.69 lakh crore, indicative). In FY25–26 central releases lagged, some states diverted funds and others froze payments; a late-2025 scrutiny drive put about ₹7,000 crore of orders at risk of a freeze. NCC executed only ₹1,300–1,500 crore of its JJM book in nine months against a plan of ₹4,000–5,000 crore "because payments simply did not arrive". ICRA sees a ₹3 lakh crore operations and maintenance opportunity under JJM 2.0, which would help contractors once payments resume.

Buildings, data centres and the Gulf

Building contractors had a better year. Ahluwalia's revenue rose 11% on hospitals, campuses, Central Vista and private residential towers; Cemindia's order book now includes 13% data centres; NBCC's PMC book reached ₹1.28 lakh crore. India's data-centre capacity of roughly 1.3–1.5 GW (2025) is projected to reach 4–5 GW by 2030 (indicative). Abroad, the Gulf hydrocarbon and giga-project boom is the single largest marginal driver of L&T's order book: the Middle East accounted for 42% of L&T's Q1 FY27 inflows and 37% of its order book. The 2026 West Asia crisis is the main risk to that engine; it already disrupted KEC's Q1 FY27 supply chain and pushed up bitumen prices for road builders at home.

Where ₹100 of a contract goes: materials, subcontractors and a thin top line

About ₹90 of every ₹100 a contractor bills is spent before it earns a rupee. The question is how much of the remaining ₹10 survives cost overruns, interest and late payment.

A typical diversified mid-cap Indian contractor spends roughly half of its revenue on materials and equipment (steel, cement, bitumen, aggregates, conductors, transformers, solar modules), about a quarter on subcontractors who supply most of the site labour, and the rest on its own staff and site overheads such as fuel, machinery hire, power, insurance and bank-guarantee commissions. What is left is an EBITDA margin of about 9–12%, which ICRA puts at 10.1–10.8% for its construction sample and India Ratings at 10.2% for FY26, a multi-year low. After depreciation on plant and machinery, interest (including guarantee and letter-of-credit charges) and tax, the contractor keeps roughly ₹3–6 of every ₹100 as profit.

The price-variation clause, and why it never fully protects

Most central-government contracts adjust the bill using a formula: each indexed input (steel, cement, bitumen, fuel, labour, plant) is given a weight, and its weight is multiplied by the change in the relevant wholesale or consumer price index since the bid. A fixed portion of the price, typically around 15% (indicative), is not indexed at all. Take an illustrative ₹1,000 crore road contract in which bitumen is 12% of cost. If bitumen rises 20% halfway through, the contractor's cost rises by about ₹24 crore on the work still to be done. The formula reimburses most of that, but late (the index lags the market by one to three months), imperfectly (the index is not the contractor's actual purchase price) and only on indexed portions. A contractor that bid at a 10% margin can see 1–2 percentage points disappear in a year of volatile commodities, and on fixed-price private or international work it absorbs the whole movement. ICRA flags the rising share of fixed-price contracts and incomplete pass-through as a margin constraint.

Capacity is a balance-sheet concept

A contractor's capacity is not a factory. It is limited by four things. The first is bank-guarantee lines: clients demand a performance guarantee of about 3–5% of contract value (temporarily cut to 3% as COVID relief) plus guarantees against mobilisation advances, and a contractor whose limits are nearly used up cannot bid. Insurance surety bonds have been allowed as a substitute since 2022 but uptake is low. The second is net worth and track record, which set eligibility under NHAI's bid-capacity formula: available bid capacity = 2.5 × A × N − B, where A is the highest annual turnover in the last five years, N is the construction period in years and B is the value of existing commitments. An illustrative contractor with ₹5,000 crore of peak turnover bidding a 2.5-year project has a gross capacity of ₹31,250 crore; if it already has ₹20,000 crore of work in hand, it can bid only about ₹11,250 crore more. The third is equipment and skilled supervisors. The fourth is working-capital lines, which is why a contractor whose receivables balloon also loses bidding headroom.

In plain terms

A contractor keeps about ₹3–4 of profit from every ₹100 it bills, after spending ₹90 on materials, labour and site costs. That ₹3–4 is wiped out by a cost overrun of 4% or a year's delay in payment. This is why the industry's history is full of companies that looked profitable for years and then collapsed: the cushion is thin and the risks compound.

The listed sector is L&T plus twenty-one mid- and small-caps, competing with thousands of unlisted contractors, PSU in-house builders and, abroad, Chinese state giants.

The rulebook: the lowest bid wins, disputes take a decade

Procurement rules decide who wins and at what price. Contract models decide who carries which risk. Dispute rules decide how long a contractor waits for money it is owed.

Public works in India are procured under the General Financial Rules 2017, Central Vigilance Commission guidelines and the Department of Expenditure's Manual for Procurement of Works. The dominant method is the L1 system: among technically qualified bidders, the lowest price wins. Quality-cum-cost-based selection is permitted for some works but used sparingly. The L1 system is transparent and hard to corrupt, but it has a well-known side-effect: when awards dry up, contractors with idle equipment and overheads bid below cost to keep crews busy. In FY24–25, L1 road bids 15–25% below NHAI's own estimates were common (indicative). Those bids are being executed in FY26–27, which is part of why road margins have fallen.

The contract model allocates risk. Under EPC, the authority pays the whole cost in milestones and the contractor carries construction risk. Under BOT-toll, the developer funds the project and recovers it through tolls, carrying traffic risk. Under the Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM), introduced in January 2016, NHAI pays 40% of the bid project cost during construction and the developer funds the remaining 60%, which NHAI repays in semi-annual annuities over 15 years with interest at the bank rate plus 3%, plus operations and maintenance payments. In transmission, tariff-based competitive bidding (TBCB) awards a scheme to the developer quoting the lowest annual tariff, and that developer then hires an EPC contractor. Railway PSUs also receive nominated work without bidding, on a cost-plus basis.

Disputes: where margins go to wait

When a client changes the scope, delays handing over land or refuses to pay a price variation, the contractor files a claim. If talks and dispute-resolution boards fail, the claim goes to arbitration, which takes years, and the losing side, usually the government agency, often challenges the award in court, which takes more years. Since 2016, agencies have been instructed to release 75% of an award against a bank guarantee while they challenge it, which eased liquidity in 2016–19. In 2023 the Vivad se Vishwas II scheme offered contractors a settlement of up to 65% of the net arbitral amount (for awards up to 31 January 2023) or up to 85% of court-upheld awards; it targeted about 500 cases worth roughly ₹1 lakh crore. PNC Infratech, for example, settled ₹234.99 crore with NHAI under it. A further NHAI one-time settlement is reported but its details are not verified.

The accounting rules matter as much as the legal ones. Under Ind AS 115 a contractor may recognise revenue from variations and claims only when it is "highly probable" that a significant reversal will not occur. Aggressive companies book claims early and carry them as contract assets for years; conservative ones wait for an award. The forensic test, covered in the KPI section, is whether contract assets are growing faster than revenue.

In plain terms

The rules make the government a price-taker at the bid and a price-setter afterwards. It picks the cheapest contractor, then decides when to certify bills, whether to accept variations and whether to contest awards. Contractors with strong balance sheets can afford to wait; weak ones settle at a discount or fail. That asymmetry is why the sector has periodically needed government relief (the 75% award release, Vivad se Vishwas, reduced guarantees) to stay solvent.

A century of booms and busts, and two lessons the sector keeps relearning

Every Indian construction cycle has ended the same way: contractors bid aggressively into a boom, took risks they could not price, and ran working capital on money they had not been paid.

The PSU era (to 1991). Public works were built by the Central Public Works Department, state public-works departments and public-sector companies such as NBCC (1960) and Ircon (1976). The private pioneers predate independence: HCC was founded by Walchand Hirachand in 1926 (it held its hundredth AGM in August 2026), L&T in 1938, Gammon India in 1922 and Afcons in 1959.

The first highway boom (1998–2007). The National Highways Development Project and the Golden Quadrilateral, the rural-roads programme (PMGSY, 2000) and Delhi Metro's first phase created India's modern contracting industry. A wave of contractors listed in 2006–08, including IVRCL, NCC, Punj Lloyd, Era Infra, Gammon, HCC and Simplex, and the government shifted highways to build-operate-transfer toll concessions.

The bust (2008–2013). Developers bid aggressive premiums for toll roads, then hit land-acquisition and environmental delays, high interest rates and traffic below forecast. Roughly ₹2 lakh crore of road projects stalled, and India's "twin balance sheet" problem of over-indebted companies and over-exposed banks followed. HCC went into corporate debt restructuring in 2012 after its Lavasa township venture; Gammon, IVRCL and Punj Lloyd later ended up in insolvency proceedings; GMR's and GVK's highway portfolios and Era Infra and Madhucon were casualties. The lessons were clear: do not take traffic risk at peak bids, and do not finance working capital on unpaid claims.

Repair (2014–2019). The government funded roads directly through EPC, then introduced the Hybrid Annuity Model in January 2016 to bring private capital back without traffic risk. The 2016 instruction to release 75% of arbitral awards rescued liquidity. Bharatmala was approved in October 2017 (34,800 km for ₹5.35 lakh crore, though the CAG later flagged cost escalation). Mid-sized road contractors with clean balance sheets (KNR, PNC, Dilip Buildcon, H.G. Infra, Ashoka) re-rated. The IL&FS default of September 2018, about ₹91,000 crore of debt including a large road portfolio, froze infrastructure credit. Ircon listed in 2018 and RVNL in April 2019 at ₹19 a share.

COVID and the capex boom (2020–2024). Labour fled sites in 2020; the government cut performance guarantees to 3% and released retention money. Then Union capex nearly tripled, from ₹3.36 lakh crore in FY20 to ₹9.49 lakh crore in FY24. Highway construction hit a record 36.5 km a day in FY21. PSU construction stocks were re-rated dramatically: RVNL rose from below ₹30 to above ₹600 by mid-2024 (approximate). L&T's order book passed ₹5 lakh crore on Middle East megaprojects.

The FY25–26 squeeze. The 2024 general election froze awards for about three months and NH awards fell to 7,538 km in FY25. FY26 brought the JJM payment freeze, lumpy railway receivables, the West Asia crisis and higher bitumen prices; NH construction fell to an eight-year low and India Ratings' contractors converted 29% of profit to cash. Roads after the HAM boom: an eight-year low and road builders leaving roads Deep dive

The hybrid-annuity model rebuilt India's highway industry after 2016. The award drought of FY24–26 has now pushed the six road developers in this basket to shrink, sell their concessions and diversify into mining, power and water.

Highways were the defining construction segment of the last decade, and in FY26 they were the weakest. National highway construction peaked at 13,327 km in FY21 (36.5 km a day, a record helped by counting changes) and was still 12,349 km in FY24. It then fell to 10,660 km in FY25 and about 9,380 km in FY26, the lowest in eight years. NHAI on its own built 5,313 km against a 4,640 km target, so the shortfall came from other agencies and from the absence of new work rather than from NHAI's execution.

From BOT to HAM and back again

Indian highway contracting has cycled through four models. In 2005–12 developers bid for build-operate-transfer toll roads, took traffic risk and paid premiums to NHAI; when land, clearances and traffic disappointed, the model stalled and several developers failed. In 2013–15 the government fell back on EPC, paying the full cost itself, which its budget could not sustain. In January 2016 it introduced the Hybrid Annuity Model, which split the difference: NHAI keeps traffic and toll risk and pays 40% of the bid project cost during construction; the developer funds 60% and is repaid through 30 semi-annual annuities over 15 years, with interest on the outstanding amount at the bank rate plus 3%, plus separate O&M payments. CRISIL counted 306 HAM projects awarded in FY16–FY23 to 62 sponsors, covering 12,700 km with a bid project cost of ₹3.35 lakh crore. Since 2024 the ministry has tried to revive BOT-toll for large corridors with better termination and traffic-risk terms, with limited uptake so far.

Margins fell, and road builders are leaving roads

The standalone margins have also fallen sharply. KNR's standalone EBITDA margin dropped from 18.7% in FY25 to 8.5% in FY26 as its Telangana irrigation client stopped paying and road revenue dried up; H.G. Infra's fell from 15.7% to 13.0% and then to 8.5% in Q1 FY27, when it reported a consolidated loss of ₹45 crore. The causes are the ones the L1 system predicts: fewer awards, aggressive bids from FY24–25 being executed now, bitumen inflation linked to the West Asia crisis and execution delays. Business Standard reported Q4 FY26 revenue declines of 40% at KNR and 31% at H.G. Infra.

The more striking response is diversification. Roads are now only 17% of Dilip Buildcon's ₹27,691 crore order book, behind coal mining, renewables and irrigation, and it also has ₹1.03 lakh crore of long-dated coal-mining contracts that sit outside the reported book. KNR's record ₹15,234 crore book is 45% mining. PNC's book is about 40% non-road, including 15% coal mining. H.G. Infra's was about 47% non-road (rail, metro, solar, battery storage, transmission) at March 2026, though about ₹6,000 crore of its ₹14,502 crore June book is not yet executable. Ashoka's is a third transmission. Welspun Enterprises is now about 60% water and wastewater. A group of companies that were valued as highway specialists in 2018–23 has become a set of diversified contractors with mining and power exposure, and investors are still working out what that is worth.

In plain terms

A HAM road developer earns money three times: a construction margin when it builds the road for its own project company, an annuity spread while it owns the road, and a gain when it sells the road to a fund. Consolidated accounts mix all three. When awards dry up, the first stream shrinks, the third one (asset sales) props up reported profit, and the consolidated income statement looks healthier than the construction business underneath it.

In plain terms

Road awards have been flat at roughly 7,500–8,500 km a year for three years. If the corridor programme lifts awards in FY27, the road developers have the bidding capacity and freed-up equity to benefit quickly; if it does not, their order books will continue to fill with mining, irrigation and power work at different margins and different risks than the roads that made their reputations.

The grid and solar super-cycle: orders are easy, execution is the constraint Deep dive

India's energy transition needs transmission lines faster than anyone can build them. That has given T&D contractors record books, and moved the bottleneck from winning work to doing it.

The fastest-growing part of Indian construction is not civil at all in the traditional sense. It is the build-out of the electricity grid and the renewable plants that feed it. The Central Electricity Authority's transmission plan for 2022–32 calls for about ₹9.15 lakh crore of investment to integrate roughly 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030: about 1.91 lakh circuit-km of new lines, 1,270 GVA of transformation capacity and 33 GW of high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) links. In April 2026 the CEA published a further roadmap for integrating 900 GW of non-fossil capacity; its investment figure was not available for this report.

How a transmission scheme reaches a contractor

Inter-state schemes are planned by the CEA and the National Committee on Transmission, then bid out by bid-process coordinators (subsidiaries of PFC and REC) under tariff-based competitive bidding. The developer quoting the lowest annual tariff wins a special-purpose company that will own the line for about 35 years. That developer, such as Power Grid, Adani Energy Solutions, Sterlite Power or a fund-backed platform, then hires EPC contractors for the towers, lines and substations and buys HVDC converters from the few global suppliers. Indian listed T&D contractors mostly sit at the EPC stage; some (Techno, Kalpataru) once owned transmission assets and have sold them.

Solar EPC: a record year, two very different contractors

India added 44.6 GW of solar capacity in FY26, nearly double FY25's 23.8 GW record, plus 6.1 GW of wind and 3.7 GW of large hydro, for a total of 54.6 GW of renewable capacity. Solar EPC contracts come in two forms: full EPC including modules (high revenue, low percentage margin, because modules are passed through) and balance-of-system only (the contractor builds everything except the modules, earning a higher margin on smaller revenue).

Waaree Renewable Technologies is the balance-of-system specialist of the Waaree group. Its revenue doubled to ₹3,331 crore at a 19.2% EBITDA margin, with ROCE of 83%, on commercial-and-industrial and utility projects using its parent's modules. Management itself guides a sustainable margin of 14–16%, so the current level is above what the company expects to keep. In June 2026 it bought 55% of Associated Power Structures, a transmission-tower and EPC company, for about ₹1,225 crore, more than its entire March 2026 net worth; its order book of more than ₹5,300 crore now includes about ₹2,400 crore of solar, ₹200 crore of battery storage and the acquired transmission book.

Sterling and Wilson Renewable Energy is one of the world's largest pure-play solar EPC contractors, now backed by Reliance New Energy. FY26 revenue was a record ₹7,548 crore and it commissioned about 4.5 GW, roughly 15% of India's utility-scale solar for the year, but its operational EBITDA margin was only about 5.9%. Its consolidated FY26 loss was ₹296 crore, after a ₹478 crore exceptional charge in Q2; its standalone loss was ₹2,510 crore after a ₹2,802 crore write-off of its exposure to its overseas subsidiary following an unfavourable arbitration, which cut standalone net worth from ₹3,003 crore to ₹452 crore. Its international share is rising again (39% of the June 2026 book, including a 1 GW solar and 600 MWh storage project in Egypt), the business that previously caused its losses.

The risks are concentrated. Module oversupply is pushing down equipment prices, which helps balance-of-system contractors but squeezes full-EPC revenue. The energy transition is a genuine multi-year order boom for grid contractors, but it does not pay all of them equally. How the trap works

A contractor's profit is recognised as work progresses, but cash depends on a sequence of approvals. The path from winning a tender to collecting the last rupee runs through a letter of award, an "appointed date" (for highways, typically when 80% or more of the land is handed over), a mobilisation advance, monthly running bills, certification by the client's engineer, payment, and finally the release of retention money after the defect-liability period. Any delay at any step moves money from the cash line to the balance sheet. Three things went wrong at once in FY26.

  • States stopped paying. Jal Jeevan Mission receivable cycles exceeded six months in several states. NCC executed only ₹1,300–1,500 crore of its JJM book in nine months against ₹4,000–5,000 crore planned, and its Uttar Pradesh JJM receivables peaked at about ₹1,700 crore. KNR's Telangana irrigation dues are about ₹1,300 crore. Kalpataru has about ₹1,600 crore of water receivables stuck.
  • Central payments became lumpy. RVNL's trade receivables tripled from ₹1,782 crore to ₹5,371 crore and its operating cash flow was −₹1,889 crore; ₹3,400 crore of Ministry of Railways dues arrived only in April 2026, after the year closed.
  • Mobilisation advances shrank. When order inflow slows, so do the up-front advances (often 10% of contract value) that finance the early stages of a project. The advances already received keep being recovered from bills, so a contractor with a slowing book loses a source of free working capital exactly when its collections weaken.

The arithmetic of the trap is unforgiving. For a contractor with ₹6,560 crore of revenue, every ten extra days of working capital ties up about ₹180 crore, roughly two-thirds of a year's profit at a 4% net margin, while a full percentage point of lost margin costs only ₹66 crore. That is why, in this sector, collections matter more than margins, and why the companies with the highest valuations (L&T, Cemindia) are the ones whose clients pay up front.

In plain terms

When a client pays late, a contractor has to borrow to pay its own suppliers and workers. The profit is still in the income statement, but it has been converted into a loan to the client. If enough clients do this at once, a profitable contractor runs out of money. In FY26, eight listed contractors had more cash going out than coming in from their operations; their profits were real only in the sense that the state still owed them.

In plain terms

The best early sign of trouble is not a fall in profit but a rise in unbilled revenue and receivables faster than revenue. It means the company is doing work, booking it, and not being paid, or booking work the client has not yet agreed to. The second-best sign is a falling order inflow, because it shrinks mobilisation advances, a quiet source of free funding.

Railway PSUs and NBCC: from nominated work to open competition Deep dive

Three government-owned builders were re-rated in 2021–24 as if they were growth companies. Their FY26 numbers show what happens to a cost-plus model when the client starts asking for bids.

RVNL, Ircon and NBCC are government companies whose original job was to execute projects for their parent ministries without bidding. RVNL was created in 2003 to build railway lines for the Ministry of Railways; Ircon has built railways in India and abroad since 1976; NBCC manages construction for government departments. Nominated work is priced cost-plus: the PSU earns a fixed project-management margin, typically about 5–9% of cost (indicative), and subcontracts most of the execution. The model is asset-light and low-risk but cannot produce high margins.

Two things changed. First, in 2021–24 investors re-rated the PSUs on the assumption that the railway capex boom would lift them indefinitely. RVNL listed at ₹19 a share in 2019 and traded above ₹600 by mid-2024 (approximate); NBCC and Ircon multiplied too. Second, the Railway Board began shifting work from nomination to open competition. By FY26 RVNL's order book was roughly half competitive (about ₹46,000 crore) and half legacy or nominated (about ₹43,000 crore); Ircon's nominated share had fallen to about 46%. On competitive work the PSUs bid against private contractors under the L1 system, and their margins converge with private ones or fall below them.

L&T is India's largest engineering and construction company and the anchor of this basket. Why L&T earns a premium

L&T's construction margin is lower than that of many mid-caps, yet it trades at 29 times earnings and 4.7 times book while most mid-caps trade at 10–20 times earnings. Four things explain the gap. Reading a contractor: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every contractor's results can be read through two equations: one that turns an order book into revenue, and one that turns profit into cash. The KPIs below are the terms of those equations.

The two equations: revenue from the order book, cash from the profit

A contractor's revenue in any year comes mostly from work it already has. Revenue equals the opening order book multiplied by the share of it executed during the year (the execution rate), plus the small portion of new orders won and started within the same year. The closing order book equals the opening book plus new orders minus revenue, plus or minus scope changes and cancellations. Profit is revenue multiplied by the margin. Cash is profit minus the increase in working capital, tax and capital spending. Management controls some terms (which bids to make, at what margin, how fast to execute) and not others (how many projects the government awards, when clients pay, what steel and bitumen cost).

The first half of the equation drives the income statement. The second half drives the balance sheet. Markets price the first; contractors fail on the second.

An illustrative worked example makes this concrete. "IndiaBuild", a diversified mid-cap contractor, starts FY27 with an order book of ₹20,000 crore and executes 30% of it during the year, producing ₹6,000 crore of revenue. It wins ₹8,000 crore of new orders, of which about 7% is executed in the same year (orders arrive through the year and take three to six months to mobilise), adding ₹560 crore. Revenue is ₹6,560 crore. The closing order book is 20,000 + 8,000 − 6,560 = ₹21,440 crore. Its book-to-bill is 8,000 ÷ 6,560 = 1.22, and its order-book cover is 21,440 ÷ 6,560 = 3.3 years.

At an 11% EBITDA margin, EBITDA is ₹722 crore. After depreciation of ₹164 crore (2.5% of revenue), interest of ₹197 crore (3%) and tax of ₹90 crore (25%), PAT is ₹271 crore, a 4.1% net margin. Now the cash. Last year, on revenue of ₹5,800 crore and 90 days of net working capital, IndiaBuild had ₹1,430 crore tied up (5,800 × 90 ÷ 365). This year late payments push working capital to 110 days on revenue of ₹6,560 crore, or ₹1,977 crore. The increase of ₹547 crore comes out of cash. Operating cash flow is therefore 722 − 90 − 547 = ₹85 crore, only 12% of EBITDA. After ₹200 crore of plant and machinery capex, free cash flow is −₹115 crore, and after paying ₹197 crore of interest, net debt rises by about ₹312 crore in a year in which the company reported a ₹271 crore profit.

In plain terms

In the IndiaBuild example, letting clients pay 20 days later cost twice as much cash as the company earned in profit. Every ten days of extra working capital ties up about ₹180 crore, roughly two-thirds of a year's PAT; a one-point drop in margin costs ₹66 crore, about a quarter of PAT. For a contractor, collections matter more than margins.

Order book (backlog)

What it is. The unexecuted value of signed contracts: what the company has agreed to build but not yet billed. Formula. Closing order book = opening order book + order inflow − revenue recognised ± scope changes and cancellations. Worked example. IndiaBuild's ₹20,000 crore opening book plus ₹8,000 crore of inflow, minus ₹6,560 crore of revenue, gives ₹21,440 crore. What moves it. Award pace and the company's win rate push it up; execution and cancellations pull it down; currency movements change the rupee value of international books. How the listed names compare. L&T's ₹7.79 lakh crore is half the basket's ₹15.3 lakh crore. Among the rest, NBCC (₹1.27 lakh crore), RVNL (₹93,492 crore), NCC (₹81,214 crore) and Kalpataru (₹66,607 crore) lead.

The definition traps. Companies define the order book differently. Some include L1 positions (contracts where they are the lowest bidder but have not signed): Kalpataru discloses ₹7,300 crore of L1 separately, Techno ₹2,100 crore, Transrail includes about ₹400 crore in its figure. Some include very long-dated contracts: Power Mech's ₹55,398 crore includes about ₹39,200 crore of 25-year mine developer-operator contracts, so its executable book is about ₹16,229 crore; Dilip Buildcon keeps its ₹1.03 lakh crore of mining contracts outside its ₹27,691 crore book. Some include work that cannot start yet: about ₹6,000 crore of H.G. Infra's ₹14,502 crore June book awaits appointed dates and land. And some books include "zombie" orders, such as JJM projects where funding has stopped. The executable book is the number that matters, and for the basket it is roughly ₹14.9 lakh crore excluding Power Mech's mining contracts.

Order inflow and inflow growth

What it is. The value of new contracts signed in a period, and its year-on-year change. Worked example. IndiaBuild's ₹8,000 crore inflow is 33% higher than an illustrative ₹6,000 crore the year before. Drivers. Government award pace by segment, the company's bidding appetite and pricing discipline, international wins and currency. Benchmarks. Inflow growth above 15% is good, 5–15% average and below zero weak; ICRA expects about +10% for the sector in FY27. How they compare. FY26 inflows ranged from records (L&T ₹4,35,590 crore, +22%; KEC ₹25,280 crore; Dilip Buildcon ₹18,548 crore against only about ₹2,100 crore in FY25) to near-zero (H.G. Infra about ₹300 crore during NHAI's award freeze; Afcons ₹4,125 crore). The trap. Inflow is lumpy. Afcons booked ₹13,219 crore in Q1 FY27 alone after a weak FY26; one quarter says little. Also check whether inflow includes the company's own HAM projects (where the client is effectively its own SPV) or group companies (about a quarter of Cemindia's book is Adani-linked).

Book-to-bill

What it is. Order inflow divided by revenue for the same period. Above 1.0 the order book is growing; below 1.0 the company is eating into its backlog. Worked example. 8,000 ÷ 6,560 = 1.22 for IndiaBuild. Drivers. Inflow (above) and execution speed: a company that executes quickly needs more inflow just to stand still. Benchmarks. Above 1.2× good, 0.9–1.2× average, below 0.8× weak. The trap. A high ratio can mean a company is winning long-dated work it will execute slowly (Dilip Buildcon's 2.06× includes multi-year irrigation and mining) or under-pricing to win; a low ratio for one year may simply reflect a lumpy award landing in the next quarter.

What it is. The split of the order book by segment (roads, rail, T&D, water, buildings, mining) and by client (central government, state government, PSU, private, international). Worked example. If IndiaBuild's ₹21,440 crore book were 40% state-government water, then ₹8,576 crore of its backlog would depend on state budgets. Drivers. Bidding choices and where the money is. Benchmarks. Largest segment below 40% of the book is good; above 70% (a single-segment company) is concentrated. State-government share below 25% is good; above 50% carries high receivable risk. How they compare. Client mix is rarely disclosed: Ahluwalia's is (private 61.7%, central 28.3%, state 9.3%), Ircon's is 77% railways, PNC's about 90% NHAI and Cemindia's about a quarter Adani-linked. Segment mix is in the chart in Section 03. The trap. "Government" is not one client. A central ministry, a cash-strapped state irrigation department and a state utility pay on very different schedules.

EBITDA margin: standalone, consolidated and company basis

What it is. Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation divided by revenue. Formula. (Revenue − materials − subcontracting − staff − site and other operating costs) ÷ revenue. Worked example. IndiaBuild's ₹722 crore on ₹6,560 crore is 11.0%. Drivers. Bid pricing, commodity pass-through, execution efficiency, provisions for onerous contracts, and mix (balance-of-system solar earns more than full EPC; specialist tunnels more than plain roads). Benchmarks. For civil EPC, above 12% good, 9–12% average, below 8% weak; for T&D, above 9% good, 7–9% average, below 6% weak; for rail PSUs and PMC, above 8% good, 5–8% average, below 5% weak. The sector averaged 10.2% in FY26 (India Ratings).

The traps. This is the most abused number in the sector. Screener's EBITDA excludes other income, while some companies include it (Ircon reports 14.1% including ₹513 crore of other income against 8.5% excluding; Cemindia 11.9% against 10.1%). Some company bases differ for other reasons (Kalpataru 8.3% vs 9.0% on screener; KEC 7.1% vs 8.0%; Afcons 11.7% vs 10.4%; Transrail 11.9% vs 13.7%). Road developers' consolidated margins include annuity income (Section 09). L&T's screener margin includes its lender's spread. The chart below uses the construction-relevant basis for each company.

Net working capital days

What it is. The money tied up in running the business, expressed as days of revenue. Formula. (Trade receivables + contract assets or unbilled revenue + retention + inventory − trade payables − customer advances) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. IndiaBuild's ₹1,977 crore on ₹6,560 crore is 110 days. Drivers. Client payment speed, certification delays, retention, the size of mobilisation and customer advances, and how hard the company leans on its own suppliers. Benchmarks. Below 45 days good, 60–120 days average, above 150 days weak; as a share of sales, below 10% good, 15–25% average, above 30% weak. L&T's core business ran at about 4% of sales in FY26. The trap. Screener's working-capital days and company-defined NWC differ (Kalpataru reports 80 days on its own basis against 38 on screener). Negative working capital can mean strength (NBCC and L&T are funded by client advances) or stretched suppliers. Receivable days, unbilled revenue and retention

What they are. Receivable days are billed but unpaid invoices as days of revenue (receivables ÷ revenue × 365). Unbilled revenue, or contract assets, is revenue booked under percentage of completion but not yet certified or invoiced. Retention money is the 5% or so of each bill the client holds until the defect-liability period ends. Worked example. An illustrative contractor with ₹6,560 crore of revenue and ₹1,200 crore of receivables has 67 receivable days; if it also has ₹2,000 crore of contract assets, the true money waiting to be collected is 178 days. Drivers. Client discipline, certification speed and, for unbilled revenue, how aggressively the company books variations and claims. Benchmarks. Billed receivables below 60 days good, 60–100 average, above 120 weak; contract assets below 15% of revenue good, 15–30% average, above 35% or rising faster than revenue weak. How they compare. HCC has 207 debtor days, NBCC 144, Techno 137 (debtor days chart in Section 11). NCC's standalone unbilled revenue of ₹7,414 crore was about 38% of annualised revenue in June 2026. The trap. Unbilled revenue is the single best forensic red flag in this sector. A company that keeps booking revenue its client has not certified will show healthy profits and low debtor days while its contract assets swell.

In plain terms

A bill the client has accepted but not paid is a receivable. Work the contractor says it has done but has not yet billed, or has billed but the client has not accepted, is unbilled revenue. The first is a payment delay. The second may be a payment delay, a paperwork delay or a disagreement about whether the work is worth what the contractor booked. Rising unbilled revenue deserves the most suspicion.

Cash conversion (CFO to EBITDA)

What it is. Cash generated from operations divided by EBITDA. Worked example. IndiaBuild's ₹85 crore of operating cash on ₹722 crore of EBITDA is 12%. Drivers. Every working-capital item above, plus tax paid. Benchmarks. Above 70% good, 40–70% average, below 30% weak. The sector's historical average is about 65%; India Ratings' FY26 figure was 29%; the 18 listed contractors with comparable data managed about 9% in aggregate (chart in Section 11). The trap. Cash flow can be flattered by one-offs: HCC's 140% includes about ₹720 crore of arbitration awards; PNC's ₹4,593 crore includes flows linked to selling its road SPVs. Look at it over three years, not one.

Net debt to EBITDA and interest cover

What they are. Net debt (borrowings, including leases and often acceptances, minus cash and liquid investments) divided by EBITDA; and EBITDA divided by interest expense. Worked example. IndiaBuild with ₹1,500 crore of net debt and ₹722 crore of EBITDA is at 2.1×; its interest cover is 722 ÷ 197 = 3.7×. Drivers. Working capital above all, then capex and HAM equity. Benchmarks. Net debt to EBITDA from net cash to 0.5× good, 0.5–1.5× average, above 2.5× weak; interest cover above 5× good, 3–5× average, below 2× weak. India Ratings' portfolio was at 1.2× and 3.0× in FY26.

How they compare. On company figures, KEC carries ₹6,722 crore of net debt including acceptances, about 4× its EBITDA, with interest cover around 2.1×; Dilip Buildcon is about 4× at group level (₹7,244 crore) but about 2.6× on its standalone ₹1,880 crore; HCC is about 3.1× (₹1,995 crore). At the other end, Techno (₹2,230 crore net cash), NBCC, Ahluwalia and Waaree RTL (pre-acquisition) have net cash; Kalpataru (₹915 crore), Cemindia (₹430 crore), Transrail (₹267 crore) and Power Mech are lightly geared. The traps. Screener borrowings can omit acceptances and other debt-like items (KEC, HCC); road developers' consolidated debt is mostly non-recourse SPV debt serviced by annuities (H.G. Infra's ₹4,763 crore, KNR's ₹1,748 crore with a debt-free parent); L&T's includes its lender; PSU "investments" are often JV equity, not cash. And a year-end snapshot can be distorted by a single payment, as RVNL's ₹3,400 crore April receipt shows.

ROCE and ROE

What they are. Return on capital employed is EBIT divided by (net worth + net debt); return on equity is PAT divided by average shareholders' equity. Worked example. IndiaBuild's EBIT is 722 − 164 = ₹558 crore; on capital employed of ₹3,500 crore, ROCE is 16%. Drivers. Margin multiplied by asset turnover, and asset turnover is mostly working capital: the same 11% margin produces very different ROCE at 60 days and at 150 days. Benchmarks. Pre-tax ROCE above 20% good, 13–20% average, below 10% weak; ROE above 18% good, 12–18% average, below 10% weak. The traps. Tiny equity bases inflate ratios (Waaree RTL's 83% ROCE). One-off gains distort them (Ashoka's 26% ROCE). Screener's ROE for three road developers appears to strip out one-off items and is wrong as a measure of reported returns: PAT divided by average equity gives 13.0% for PNC (screener 2.4%), 23.5% for Dilip Buildcon (screener 5.6%) and 49% for Ashoka (screener 0.7%), but those recalculated figures are themselves inflated by asset-sale gains; Ashoka's standalone ROE is closer to 7–8% (estimate).

HAM equity committed and asset recycling

What it is. The equity a road developer must still inject into under-construction hybrid-annuity projects, usually compared with its net worth; and the value it realises when it sells completed ones. Formula. Equity required ≈ bid project cost × 60% × equity share of the developer's funding, partly offset by the parent's EPC margin. Worked example. On a ₹2,000 crore project, 60% is ₹1,200 crore; at a 20% equity share that is ₹240 crore, 12% of project cost (indicative). Three such wins a year need about ₹700 crore. Benchmarks. Equity still to be committed below 20% of net worth good, 20–40% average, above 50% weak. How they compare. None of the six developers disclosed remaining committed equity in the sources used for this report; Dilip Buildcon says its next 11 assets need less than ₹81 crore more. Sale multiples are disclosed: PNC about 1.7× equity invested, KNR about 2.7×. The trap. The equity-recycling model only works while buyers pay above book. If InvIT appetite or prices fall, developers are left holding equity they cannot recycle, which caps how much new work they can bid.

Arbitration claims

What it is. Money the contractor says it is owed for scope changes, delays or price variations, at stages from claim to award to court-upheld judgment. Worked example. A ₹60 crore claim settled under Vivad se Vishwas at 65% yields ₹39 crore, a ₹21 crore write-down if the full amount was booked. Benchmarks. Claims under 10% of net worth are routine; above 50% the company's solvency depends on courts (HCC's historically exceeded its net worth). How they compare. HCC has about ₹1,700 crore of awards in its favour and realised ₹720 crore in FY26; PNC booked a ₹176 crore NHAI award in Q1 FY27. Few others quantify claims. The trap. Awards realised against bank guarantees can be clawed back if the government wins its court challenge; and awards inflate the quarter in which they land (PNC's Q1 FY27 standalone margin of 24.7% falls to about 13% without the award).

In plain terms

An arbitration award is a court-approved IOU from the government. It is worth something, often a lot, but the government decides when to pay it and may appeal for years. Treat awards as a possible future bonus, not as part of a contractor's normal earnings.

Anatomy of one ₹1,000 crore contract, from award to final retention

To see how the KPIs fit together, follow one illustrative ₹1,000 crore government contract over its life. It runs for three years, with ₹250 crore of work in year one, ₹450 crore in year two and ₹300 crore in year three, at an 11% EBITDA margin (₹890 crore of cost, ₹110 crore of EBITDA). The client pays a 10% mobilisation advance (₹100 crore) up front and recovers it from bills, holds back 5% of every bill as retention until a two-year defect-liability period ends, and in the base case pays certified bills 60 days after the work.

In the base case the contractor is never out of pocket. The advance and prompt payments mean its cumulative cash is positive in every year, peaking at ₹110 crore when retention is released in year five, exactly matching its EBITDA. Now introduce a delayed-payment scenario. From year two the client pays 180 days late; the contractor incurs ₹60 crore of extra cost on a scope change the client disputes, and books it as a claim; retention is released two years late. The booked EBITDA is still ₹110 crore. But cumulative cash swings to −₹146 crore at the end of year two, which the contractor must borrow, costing roughly ₹25 crore of interest at 10%. The claim is eventually settled at 65% (₹39 crore), forcing a ₹21 crore write-down. The lifetime profit falls from ₹110 crore to about ₹64 crore, and the cash arrives two years later.

Anatomy of one hybrid-annuity road project

A HAM project adds a second layer of economics on top of the EPC contract. In this illustrative ₹2,000 crore project, NHAI pays ₹800 crore during construction; the developer's SPV borrows ₹960 crore and its parent invests ₹240 crore of equity, roughly matched by the ₹240 crore of EBITDA the parent's EPC arm earns building the road at 12%. Once the road opens, NHAI repays the ₹1,200 crore over 15 years (simplified here to ₹80 crore a year), pays interest on the outstanding balance at the bank rate plus 3% (assumed 9.5%), and pays an O&M fee (assumed to leave about ₹5 crore a year of margin). The SPV services its debt at an assumed 8.5% over 15 years. The equity receives the difference: about ₹53 crore in the first year, falling to about ₹24 crore in the last, roughly ₹580 crore over 15 years for ₹240 crore invested, an equity IRR of about 13% (illustrative). Selling the SPV after a year or two of operation at 1.2–1.6× equity invested (indicative) would return ₹290–385 crore early and free the capital for a new bid.

Definition traps, collected

  • Order book with or without L1. Some companies include contracts they are lowest bidder on but have not signed. Compare signed books only.
  • Executable vs headline order book. Long-dated mining and O&M contracts (Power Mech's ~₹39,200 crore; Dilip Buildcon's ₹1.03 lakh crore kept outside), unappointed projects (~₹6,000 crore at H.G. Infra) and stalled JJM work inflate the headline.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. Ircon (14.1% vs 8.5%) and Cemindia (11.9% vs 10.1%) report including it; screener excludes it.
  • Consolidated vs standalone for HAM owners. Consolidated margins (19–26%) include annuity income; standalone (8.5–14.6%) is the construction business.
  • One-off gains in PAT. Asset-sale gains (Ashoka ~₹2,250 crore, Dilip Buildcon ₹1,396 crore of other income) and arbitration awards (PNC ₹176 crore in Q1 FY27) are not run-rate.
  • Gross revenue in PMC. NBCC books the full project cost as revenue while earning a fee, so its margin cannot be compared with a contractor's.
  • Consolidated revenue below standalone. H.G. Infra's consolidated revenue (₹5,235 crore) is lower than standalone (₹5,667 crore) because EPC billed to its own SPVs is eliminated.
  • Net debt definitions. Acceptances (KEC), debt-like items (HCC), non-recourse SPV debt (road developers), lender borrowings (L&T) and JV equity counted as "investments" (RVNL, Ircon) all change the answer.
  • Working capital: screener vs company. Screener's days and company NWC differ; debtor days exclude unbilled revenue.
  • Standalone vs consolidated losses. Sterling and Wilson lost ₹296 crore consolidated and ₹2,510 crore standalone in FY26; both are correct.
  • Fiscal-year confusion. Ircon's ₹10,760 crore is FY25 revenue, not FY26 (₹9,071 crore); HCC's ₹3,872 crore is trailing twelve months, FY26 was ₹3,970 crore.

How to read a contractor's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Order inflow and the order book (minute 1). Did inflow beat revenue (book-to-bill above 1)? Is the closing book reconciled to opening + inflow − revenue? An unexplained fall, like RVNL's in Q1 FY27, needs an answer.
  2. Executable book (minute 2). Strip out L1, long-dated mining or O&M, and projects awaiting appointed dates. Divide by trailing revenue for real cover.
  3. Revenue against guidance (minute 3). Compare year-to-date revenue with the full-year guide; contractors are often second-half weighted, but a large H2 dependence (Techno ~60%) is a risk.
  4. Construction margin on the right basis (minute 4). Use standalone for HAM owners, exclude other income, and separate onerous-contract provisions or one-off awards.
  5. Other income and exceptional items (minute 5). If other income is more than a fifth of PBT, find out why (treasury, asset sales, awards). RVNL's exceeds its EBITDA.
  6. Working capital (minute 6). Receivables, contract assets and retention against revenue. Rising faster than revenue is the first red flag.
  7. Cash flow and net debt (minute 7). At half-year and year-end, compare operating cash flow with EBITDA, and net debt with the previous quarter on the company's definition.
  8. Interest cost (minute 8). Rising interest with flat debt means more guarantee and LC charges or higher rates; interest above 40% of EBITDA (KEC) leaves little for shareholders.
  9. Segment and client signals (minute 9). Which segments grew, which states are paying, how much is international and where.
  10. Guidance and management tone (minute 10). Changes to inflow, revenue and margin guidance (Ahluwalia cut its inflow guide to ₹4,000–5,000 crore; Ashoka its growth to 10–15%) say more than the quarter itself.

Red flags to stop on: contract assets growing faster than revenue for two or more quarters; negative operating cash flow in a year of record profit; a falling execution rate on a rising order book; repeated "one-time" provisions; promoter pledges rising; and order wins from related parties forming a growing share of inflow.

In plain terms

If you have time to check only three numbers in a contractor's results, check order inflow against revenue, the change in receivables plus unbilled revenue against the change in revenue, and operating cash flow against EBITDA. The first tells you whether the business is growing; the second whether it is being paid; the third whether its profits are real yet.

In plain terms

Almost every headline number in this sector comes in two or three versions: with and without L1 bids, with and without other income, consolidated and standalone, screener and company. None is wrong; each answers a different question. The mistake is mixing them in one comparison.

How to read the table

Start with the two aggregate rows. The 22 companies earned ₹4.91 lakh crore of revenue and reported ₹32,240 crore of profit, and the market values them at ₹7.34 lakh crore, about 23 times reported earnings. Strip out L&T and the picture changes: the other 21 grew revenue just 1.0%, and their ₹13,286 crore of reported profit includes about ₹3,200 crore of one-off road-asset gains. On adjusted earnings the ex-L&T group trades at roughly 22 times, much closer to L&T's own 29 times than the raw 16 times suggests (estimates).

Then read across the columns in pairs. Growth against order cover shows who is running down a book (KEC at 1.5 years needs inflow; H.G. Infra's 0.06 book-to-bill in FY26 explains its weak Q1 FY27) and who has years of work but cannot execute it (NBCC, RVNL). Construction margin against debtor days separates profitable contractors that get paid (Cemindia: 11.9% and 47 days) from profitable ones that do not (HCC: 14.2% and 207 days; Techno: 14.2% and 137). ROE against P/B is the valuation test: Transrail earns 20% on equity at 2.7 times book; RVNL earns 9% at 4.2 times; the road developers trade below book because the market does not trust their reported returns.

Finally, check the footnotes for every company you compare. The column most prone to misreading is EBITDA, which mixes four bases. The second is net debt, where the difference between screener and company figures is large for KEC (₹4,866 crore vs ₹6,722 crore), HCC (₹363 crore vs ₹1,995 crore) and NCC (₹2,015 crore vs ₹3,513 crore). The third is PAT for the road developers, where Ashoka's ₹2,576 crore is mostly a one-off and its standalone business earned ₹320 crore.

In plain terms

The cheapest-looking stocks in this table (PNC at 5.8 times earnings, Ashoka at 0.45 times book) are cheap partly because their profits contain asset-sale gains that will not recur, and partly because the market doubts how quickly they can rebuild a road order book. The most expensive (RVNL at 45.6 times, Afcons at 43.3 times, HCC at 38.7 times) are expensive for three different reasons: a PSU premium, depressed earnings and a turnaround bet. A low or high multiple in this sector is a question, not an answer.

Who is winning: growth, margin and what the market pays

The winners of FY26 were grid, solar and specialist contractors with clean balance sheets. The market has rewarded some of them, kept paying for others whose numbers have weakened, and written off the road builders.

Three maps summarise the competitive picture. The first plots FY26 revenue growth against construction margin: the top-right quadrant holds companies that grew and earned a rich margin, the bottom-left those that shrank on thin margins. The second plots the same growth against the price-earnings multiple, to show whether the market pays for growth. The third plots price-to-book against return on equity, the most basic test of whether a valuation is justified by returns.

The first map shows how cleanly the FY26 split followed segment exposure. Every company in the top-right quadrant except Cemindia is a power-sector contractor; every company in the left half except Afcons, NCC and Ircon is a road developer or HCC. Margin was not the problem for the road developers: PNC and H.G. Infra kept standalone margins of 12.6–13.0% even as revenue fell. The problem was volume. The bottom-right quadrant holds the companies whose growth did not translate into margin: KEC and Kalpataru (large books, 7–8% margins), Sterling and Wilson (record revenue, 5.9% margin), and the PSUs.

The second map is the more surprising. If the market paid for growth, the dots would rise from left to right; instead the highest multiples sit near or left of the zero-growth line. RVNL's multiple reflects a PSU premium and the hope that bidding margins recover to 5–7%. Afcons's and HCC's reflect depressed or recovering earnings: Afcons earned only ₹251 crore after a Q4 loss, and HCC's earnings are low relative to its order book and claims. Meanwhile Transrail, which grew 30% at 11.9% margins with 34% ROCE, trades at about 15 times. The basket is not pricing FY26 growth; it is pricing ownership (PSU, Adani, L&T's franchise) and expected recoveries.

The third map is the cleanest test. Cemindia (28% ROE, 8.3 times book), NBCC (20%, 6.8 times), Transrail (20%, 2.7 times) and Waaree RTL (68%, 9.0 times) are priced roughly in line with their returns, although Cemindia's and Waaree RTL's returns depend on a parent group's orders or supply. L&T (15.9%, 4.7 times) carries a premium for quality and collections. RVNL (9.0%, 4.2 times) is the clearest outlier. The left side of the map, where Ashoka, PNC, KNR, H.G. Infra, Dilip Buildcon and NCC trade at 0.45–1.0 times book, is the market's verdict on road developers and state-dependent contractors: it values them at roughly the book value of their assets and gives no credit for future returns.

Technology and disruption: machines, the grid and owner-builders

Construction technology changes slowly, but three shifts are already visible: mechanisation against labour scarcity, new asset categories such as storage and data centres, and owners who would rather build for themselves.

Mechanisation and precast. Indian construction has long relied on cheap migrant labour. Rising wages (the 35–40% NCR minimum-wage increase that halved Ahluwalia's Q1 FY27 margin), four new labour codes in force since November 2025 (which forced Afcons to take a one-time provision) and labour shortages at Gulf and domestic sites (cited by KEC) are pushing contractors towards machines: slip-form pavers, precast segmental bridges, tunnel-boring machines and modular construction. Ahluwalia is investing in mechanisation; Dilip Buildcon's owned fleet of about 10,000 machines is its competitive edge; Afcons and Cemindia spend heavily on tunnel-boring machines and marine plant. The trade-off is capital intensity: mechanised contractors need more fixed assets and must keep them busy, which makes award droughts more painful.

Digital project controls. Building information modelling, drone surveys for progress certification, sensors on equipment and NHAI's data lake for milestone tracking can shorten the certification cycle that drives working capital. A faster, less disputable measurement of work done is, in effect, a working-capital technology, and it is the most valuable digital tool for a contractor whose cash is stuck in unbilled revenue.

Grid technology. HVDC converters and high-end substations are supplied by Hitachi Energy, Siemens and GE Vernova; Indian contractors integrate rather than make them, although BHEL is building domestic HVDC capability. Transformer and reactor shortages are part of why transmission execution lags targets. Contractors with in-house towers and conductors (Transrail, KEC, Kalpataru, and now Waaree RTL through APS) control more of their own supply chain.

New asset categories. Battery storage, pumped hydro (the CEA has identified more than 50 GW of potential), offshore wind and green hydrogen are creating new EPC categories. L&T has won ultra-mega European offshore wind orders; Sterling and Wilson sees up to 20% of future orders coming from storage; H.G. Infra and Dilip Buildcon have moved into battery storage and renewables; Techno and Cemindia are building data centres. These categories are less tendered, less understood and often fixed-price, so early margins can be good or bad.

The structural disruption to watch is not a technology but an ownership pattern. When a project owner (Adani in ports and airports, Reliance in solar, large transmission developers) buys or builds its own contractor, the work it controls leaves the open market. For the captive contractor this guarantees volume, and Cemindia's order book has grown about 60% in a year; for independent contractors it shrinks the pool they can bid for, and for minority shareholders of the captive one it raises the question of whether related-party contracts are priced at arm's length. At the same time, InvITs and private-equity platforms are capturing the long-term returns on completed roads that developers once hoped to keep, which turns developers into builders with a short holding period.

In plain terms

The contractors best placed for the next decade are those that can do new kinds of work (grid, storage, data centres, offshore) without signing fixed-price contracts they cannot price, and that are not squeezed out when their biggest potential clients decide to build for themselves.

Bull vs bear

The sector's long-term demand is not seriously in doubt. The argument is about how much of that demand becomes cash for shareholders, and at what price.

Both cases start from the same facts: record order books, a slower but still rising government budget, a booming grid and solar segment, a road segment at a cyclical low and a cash-conversion problem at its worst in years. The bull case says FY26 was the bottom of a payment and awards cycle that is already turning. The bear case says the sector has repeated its old habit of booking profits it has not collected, and that valuations still assume the 2021–24 boom.

The bull case

  • Demand is structural. Union capex of ₹12.22 lakh crore (₹17.15 lakh crore effective), a ₹9.15 lakh crore transmission plan, 500 GW of non-fossil targets, railway modernisation, urban transit, water (JJM 2.0) and a reviving private capex cycle in data centres and factories.
  • The books are full. About ₹15.3 lakh crore of orders, roughly three years of revenue, with record inflows at L&T, KEC, Dilip Buildcon, Cemindia and Afcons.
  • The road trough is ending. The 11,000 km corridor programme, ₹1.15 lakh crore of NHAI bids invited and freed-up developer equity after asset sales set up an awards recovery; Dilip Buildcon guides 30–40% growth.
  • Cash is turning. RVNL received ₹3,400 crore in April; NCC's UP receivables are down about 40% from the peak; Telangana has proposed instalments to KNR; ICRA expects JJM receivable cycles below 60 days.
  • Balance sheets are cleaner than in 2008–13. Kalpataru, Cemindia, Transrail, Power Mech and Welspun are lightly geared; road developers have sold assets at 1.7–2.7× invested equity; HCC has cut debt 38%.
  • Valuations outside a few names are modest. NCC at 11×, Transrail at 15×, KEC at 16×, and several road developers below book.

The bear case

  • Profit is not cash. India Ratings' contractors converted 29% of EBITDA to cash in FY26; the 18 comparable listed contractors about 9%; eight reported negative operating cash flow.
  • Growth is slowing at the top. Capex growth has halved to about 9–10%; ex-L&T revenue grew 1% in FY26; the fiscal deficit target leaves little room.
  • Margins are under pressure. Sector EBITDA at a multi-year low of 10.2%; aggressive L1 bids from FY24–25 being executed now; labour codes and wage jumps on fixed-price work; KEC's Q1 margin of 5.8%.
  • Concentration risks. L&T's 37% Middle East exposure during the West Asia crisis; Transrail's 20% Bangladesh; Cemindia's Adani-linked book; KNR's dependence on one state.
  • Earnings quality. Road developers' profits lean on one-off sale gains; arbitration awards inflate quarters; PSU profits lean on other income.
  • Valuation outliers. RVNL at 45.6× earnings for a 9% ROE, Afcons at 43×, HCC at 39×; governance overhangs at Afcons (pledge), HCC (pledge, thin promoter) and PNC (2024 case).

What decides between them is mostly measurable within a year. If FY27 operating cash flow recovers towards half of EBITDA, receivables at NCC, RVNL and KNR keep falling, road awards exceed 10,000 km and margins hold at or above 10%, the bull case is largely right and the cheaper names will re-rate. If cash conversion stays near FY26 levels, the bear case is right, and the contractors with the weakest balance sheets (high net debt, interest cover below 2×, pledged promoters) will need equity or asset sales. The segments matter too: the grid and solar contractors face execution and margin risk rather than demand risk, while the road developers face a demand question that only the government can answer.

In plain terms

Nobody serious argues that India will stop building roads, railways and power lines. The real argument is whether contractors will be paid on time for building them, and whether the prices investors are paying already assume they will. Watch the cash, not the orders.

What to watch: the indicators, their thresholds and the calendar

Eight indicators tell you, months before company results do, whether the sector's cash problem is easing and which segments will grow next.

Contractors report quarterly, but most of what drives their results is visible earlier in government data. The indicators below are ordered from the broadest (the budget) to the most company-specific (cash conversion). The thresholds are our own judgement of what would signal improvement or deterioration, set against the FY26 baselines in this report; they are indicative, not official targets.

1. Union capex run-rate

The Controller General of Accounts publishes monthly Union spending. The FY27 budget is ₹12.22 lakh crore; spending at least half of it by the end of September (about ₹6.1 lakh crore) would suggest the budget is on track and front-loaded, which helps contractor collections. Below about 45% by September would raise the risk that the government trims capex in the revised estimate, as it did in FY25 (₹10.18 lakh crore revised against ₹11.11 lakh crore budgeted). The indicator matters because contractors are paid out of this spending; slippage shows up first as slower payments, then as fewer awards.

2. Railway capex utilisation

Railways had spent ₹1.14 lakh crore, 39% of the FY27 allocation, by July 2026, which is ahead of a straight-line pace. If utilisation stays above roughly 60% by October and 85% by January, RVNL and Ircon should see better collections than in FY26. A slowdown in the second half would show up in RVNL's receivables, which tripled last year.

3. National highway awards and construction

MoRTH and NHAI publish monthly awards and construction. The FY26 baselines are about 9,380 km built (about 26 km a day) and awards stuck at 7,500–8,500 km a year. A recovery in FY27 would mean awards above about 10,000 km and construction back above 28 km a day; another year of awards below 8,000 km would extend the road developers' trough into FY28 revenue. Watch the mode mix too: a revival of BOT-toll would change developers' equity needs and risk, while more HAM would bring back equity recycling.

4. Transmission awards and execution

Track inter-state TBCB schemes awarded and SPVs transferred, and CEA's monthly line and substation additions against target. FY25 set a record of 45 schemes; about 25 schemes worth roughly ₹1 lakh crore were in the bid shelf. Twenty or more awards in FY27 would sustain T&D order books; fewer than ten would mean the boom is pausing. On execution, line additions consistently above about 70% of target would show the bottleneck easing; the FY26 nine-month figure was 47.5%.

5. Renewable additions and power-sale agreements

MNRE's monthly capacity additions and the backlog of awarded capacity without signed power-sale agreements tell you whether the solar EPC boom is sustainable. FY26 added 44.6 GW of solar. Additions above about 35 GW with a shrinking PSA backlog would support Waaree RTL and Sterling and Wilson; a rising backlog or growing curtailment would signal that developers will slow new EPC orders.

6. State payments: JJM 2.0 and the problem states

The approval and funding schedule of JJM 2.0 and the release of central funds to states are the key event for water contractors. At company level, watch NCC's UP JJM receivables (₹1,043 crore at June 2026; below ₹800 crore would confirm the turn), KNR's Telangana instalments (₹80–90 crore a month proposed) and Kalpataru's stuck water dues (about ₹1,600 crore). ICRA expects JJM receivable cycles to come down below 60 days by September 2026; if that has not happened by the Q2 FY27 results, the recovery is slower than expected.

7. Company cash conversion and contract assets

The single most important company metric for FY27 is operating cash flow against EBITDA. The FY26 baseline is 29% for India Ratings' portfolio and about 9% for the 18 comparable listed contractors. A recovery above about 50% for the year would confirm that the working-capital cycle has turned; another year below 30% would mean the sector's balance sheets keep weakening. Alongside it, contract assets (unbilled revenue) growing slower than revenue for two consecutive half-years would show that booked profits are being converted.

8. Margins and input costs

Watch the sector EBITDA margin (10.2% in FY26) and the company-level signs of stress: KEC's margin recovering above 7%, Ahluwalia's back towards 8–9%, RVNL's towards its 5–7% target. On the input side, bitumen (linked to crude and the West Asia situation), steel, aluminium and zinc determine how much of the margin price-variation clauses can protect. A sustained rise in these without a matching pass-through would compress FY27–28 margins on fixed-price work.
