00

The one-page view: an industry that sells content, not cars

Twenty-nine listed suppliers, three engines of growth, one powertrain question and a valuation that assumes most of it goes right.

India's auto component makers produced ₹7.60 lakh crore of parts in FY26, 12.7% more than a year earlier and more than double the ₹3.40 lakh crore of FY21, according to the Automotive Component Manufacturers Association (ACMA). They sell to four kinds of buyer: Indian vehicle makers (the OEMs, or original equipment manufacturers), the replacement market of distributors, retailers and garages (the aftermarket), foreign vehicle makers and suppliers (exports), and a growing set of customers outside the car industry in rail, defence, aerospace and energy storage. The first channel dominates. Two of every three rupees of component sales go straight to an Indian assembly line.

The business looks like a bet on how many vehicles India builds, and partly it is. The defining fact of the past five years, though, is that component sales have grown faster than vehicle production. In FY26 India built a record 3.47 crore vehicles, roughly 12% more than in FY25, while ACMA's supplies to Indian OEMs rose 16.3%. The four-point gap is content per vehicle: a passenger-vehicle market where SUVs now outsell everything else, more airbags, disc brakes and LED lamps, more electronics, and the first electric-vehicle parts. A supplier's revenue is the product of three numbers: how many vehicles its customers build, how many rupees of its parts go into each vehicle, and what share of those parts it wins. Most of what separates the winners in this report is the second and third number.

Five forces shape the industry, and the rest of this report is organised around them:

  • Content per vehicle beats volume. Vehicle production grows at high single digits over a cycle; supplies grow four to six points faster as premium features, safety rules and electronics add rupees to every vehicle. The first question about any supplier is how much of its product sits in each vehicle and whether that amount is rising.
  • The domestic cycle sets the direction, and GST 2.0 reset it. Indian vehicle demand is cyclical: two-wheeler sales peaked in FY19, collapsed to FY22 and only passed the old peak in FY26. The tax cut of September 2025 turned a flat first half of FY26 into a booming second half, which also makes FY27 comparisons harder.
  • Powertrain exposure decides the long-term value. Electric vehicles delete pistons, fuel injectors and exhausts, leave lamps, brakes and seats untouched, and add motors, controllers, high-voltage wiring and battery cooling. With EVs only 8.3% of vehicle sales, today's earnings still come from engines, but valuations already sort companies into losers, agnostics and gainers.
  • Exports are a share-gain story held hostage by trade policy. Global vehicle output has been flat for a decade, so Indian exporters grow only by taking business from Western and Chinese suppliers. A 25% US tariff on half of India's US-bound parts, a weak American truck cycle and a stalled US electric-car programme slowed export growth to 5% in FY26, while Europe grew 9%.
  • The growth content is imported. India became a net importer of components again in FY26, with a deficit of about $1.4 billion. China supplies 36% of imports and around 90% of the world's rare-earth magnets. Localising electronics, magnets and battery cells is the strategic project of the next five years.

The 29 companies in this report are the auto-component members of the NSE 750. They range from Samvardhana Motherson, a ₹1.26 lakh crore global supplier with more than 300 subsidiaries in about 45 countries, to Jamna Auto, a ₹2,612 crore maker of truck leaf springs. Excluding Tube Investments, whose consolidated accounts are dominated by its 56% stake in CG Power, the other 28 earned ₹3.46 lakh crore of revenue in FY26, up 13.9%, at a blended EBITDA margin of 12.0% and ₹20,404 crore of net profit. Seven companies are subsidiaries or affiliates of foreign groups, or were founded as joint ventures with them; the rest are Indian family companies, several of them first-generation.

That last number is the tension in the sub-sector. Japanese, European and North American suppliers trade at 13 to 16 times earnings; India's listed suppliers trade at roughly three times that. Part of the gap is justified: Indian suppliers earn EBITDA margins of 10–25% where global peers earn operating margins of 3–7%, they return 15–30% on capital, and they sit in the only large vehicle market still growing at double digits. Part of it is a scarcity premium for a handful of foreign-owned subsidiaries and for the few listed EV-content plays. The rest is an assumption that today's double-digit growth continues for a decade. Section 18 works through what that assumption requires.

In plain terms

An Indian component maker gets paid for each vehicle its customers build, so it rises and falls with the vehicle cycle. What has made the good ones exceptional is that each vehicle now carries more of their product than it did five years ago, and that they keep winning more of each customer's parts list. The threats are the shift to electric drive, which makes some parts obsolete, and trade barriers abroad. Investors are paying a high price for the content story and need it to keep running.

01

From steel bar to assembly line: how a component maker earns

A vehicle is a few thousand parts bought from a pyramid of suppliers. Money is made by winning a slot on a model, holding it for the model's life and charging more for each successive generation of the part.

A typical Indian car contains several thousand parts, and a vehicle maker builds only a few of them itself: the body shell, usually the engine, and final assembly. Everything else arrives from suppliers arranged in tiers. Tier-1 suppliers deliver finished systems or modules straight to the assembly line: a headlamp, a wiring harness, a seat, a brake assembly, an air-conditioning unit. They buy machined parts and sub-assemblies from tier-2 suppliers, who in turn buy castings, forgings, stampings and moulded parts from tier-3 workshops. Most of the companies in this report are tier-1s; a few, like the forgers, sell both to tier-1s and directly to vehicle makers.

The programme: how a supplier wins and keeps business

Component business is won model by model. When a vehicle maker plans a new model, typically three to four years before launch, it sends a request for quotation (RFQ) to two to five qualified suppliers for each system. The winner receives a nomination, designs or adapts the part with the vehicle maker's engineers, builds tooling and validates the part through a formal approval process (PPAP, the production part approval process). Revenue starts at the start of production (SOP), ramps to a peak in the second or third year and lasts for the model's life, usually five to seven years, sometimes longer with facelifts. When a supplier talks of "order wins" or "booked business", it means the expected lifetime revenue of such nominations.

Three features follow. First, revenue is sticky once won: changing a validated supplier mid-life costs the vehicle maker re-testing and risk, so it rarely happens without a quality failure. Second, the incumbent has an edge at the next generation, because it already knows the vehicle, holds the tooling and has a record with the customer's quality team. Third, the price falls every year. Most contracts carry an annual price reduction of 1–3% that the supplier must offset by cost engineering (value analysis and value engineering, or VAVE). Raw-material costs, by contrast, are usually passed through, with a lag, through indexation clauses. A supplier's margin therefore depends on how well it engineers costs down faster than the price-downs and how quickly its contracts reset for metal prices.

Four ways to sell the same part

The same company can sell into four channels with very different economics. OEM supply is the volume base, priced by negotiation with a buyer who has many alternatives and an annual price-down habit. Aftermarket sales of replacement parts go through distributors and retailers to garages; prices are set by the brand, not by an OEM purchasing team, and the margin per part is usually higher, though no published industry series quantifies the premium, so it is directional only. Exports go either to foreign vehicle makers or to global tier-1s who integrate the part into their own system. Non-auto sales use the same metallurgical and machining skills for railway wheels, artillery shells, aircraft parts, semiconductor equipment and energy-storage batteries.

Where the value is captured

Margins in the sub-sector follow technology and scarcity rather than size. The highest margins belong to suppliers whose parts are hard to make and hard to qualify: precision driveline gears and EV motors (Sona), pistons and rings (SPR Auto), forged and machined engine parts for global customers (Sansera, Bharat Forge), bearings (Schaeffler) and air brakes (ZF CV). The lowest belong to assemblers whose bill of materials is mostly bought-in metal or components: wiring harnesses, where copper is most of the cost, sheet-metal parts, where steel is two-thirds, and air-conditioning, where compressors and heat exchangers are bought or licensed. Size helps buying power but does not create margin; Motherson, by far the largest, earns one of the lower margins, partly because it carries Europe's cost base.

In plain terms

A supplier wins the right to supply a part for one car or motorcycle model, usually for five to seven years, and then has to deliver it cheaper every year. It makes money if it can cut its own costs faster than the customer cuts the price, pass on metal-price rises quickly, and win the next model. Companies whose parts are difficult to make keep more of each rupee; companies that mostly assemble bought-in material keep less, but can still earn good returns if they need little machinery.

One more distinction matters for reading this report: the difference between a company's products and its customers' powertrains. A lamp maker sells the same product whether the car is petrol or electric. A piston maker's product disappears in a battery vehicle. A wiring-harness maker sells more in an electric vehicle. Sections 04 and 10 return to this, because it is the axis on which investors now price the sector.

02

The ₹7.6 lakh crore puzzle: what the headline number measures

ACMA's turnover is net of imports. Read gross, the industry sold ₹9.83 lakh crore; read net, it made ₹7.60 lakh crore in India. The difference is the industry's most important quality signal.

ACMA publishes four numbers each July. For FY26 they were supplies to Indian OEMs of ₹6,62,893 crore, aftermarket sales of ₹1,08,453 crore, exports of ₹2,12,176 crore and an industry turnover of ₹7.60 lakh crore. The first three add up to ₹9.83 lakh crore, which is more than the turnover. The reason is that the turnover figure nets out imported components. ACMA also reports imports of ₹2,24,287 crore, and 9.83 minus 2.24 gives 7.59, within rounding of the headline. The same arithmetic works for FY25: ₹5.70 + ₹1.00 + ₹1.92 − ₹1.88 lakh crore of imports = ₹6.74 lakh crore, against a reported ₹6.73 lakh crore.

The netting makes sense once the flow is clear. An imported electronic control unit enters India, is fitted into a module by an Indian supplier and is sold to a vehicle maker. It then appears in the OEM-supply line, because the supplier invoiced it. Counting it as Indian output would overstate what the industry actually makes, so ACMA subtracts imports to arrive at a figure for components produced in India. The three channel numbers are gross sales; the turnover is domestic production. They answer different questions, and mixing them produces nonsense, such as an OEM channel that is 87% of the industry and channels that add up to 129%.

In plain terms

Indian component companies sold about ₹9.8 lakh crore of parts in FY26, but ₹2.2 lakh crore of what they sold had been imported first. So the industry actually made ₹7.6 lakh crore of parts in India. Two-thirds of sales went to Indian vehicle makers, a fifth abroad and a tenth to the repair market. When the import share rises, more of the value in an Indian vehicle is being made somewhere else.

Doubling in five years, from a low base

ACMA's turnover has grown from ₹3.40 lakh crore in FY21 to ₹7.60 lakh crore in FY26, a compound rate of 17%. That figure flatters, because FY21 was the COVID trough. Measured from the previous peak in FY19 (₹3.95 lakh crore), growth is about 9.7% a year in rupees, roughly nominal GDP growth plus a little. The FY19–FY21 decline (axle-load norms, the NBFC credit freeze, the BS-VI price jump and COVID) wiped out three years of growth, and the recovery since has been powered by both volume and content. In dollars the industry grew from $57 billion in FY19 to $86 billion in FY26.

The trade balance: a quality problem hiding in a growth number

India was a net importer of components for most of its history and briefly turned exporter: a $700 million surplus in FY22, $300 million in FY24 and $453 million in FY25. In FY26 the balance swung back to a deficit of about $1.4 billion, because imports grew 13% while exports grew 5%. Exports slowed because of US tariffs and the US truck and EV downturns, discussed in section 12. Imports accelerated because the new content going into Indian vehicles, in electronics, sensors, control units, magnets, motors and the cells inside battery packs, is largely made abroad. ACMA says imports are driven by "demand for advanced technologies and specialised components", and China supplies 36% of them, around $9 billion.

The deficit matters for investors because it reveals where Indian suppliers are not yet competitive. A vehicle that is getting more expensive because it carries an imported infotainment unit, an imported battery cell and an imported motor magnet adds sales to an Indian tier-1's top line but little to its value added. The companies that localise those parts, through technology joint ventures, cell plants and magnet capacity, will capture the margin that is currently earned in Shenzhen, Suzhou, Stuttgart and Osaka.

What the number leaves out, and what comes next

ACMA's scope excludes tyres, batteries, paints and consumables. That puts the core lead-acid business of Exide and Amara Raja, two of the larger companies in this report, outside the ₹7.60 lakh crore, even though both supply vehicle makers and the replacement market. It also excludes the non-auto revenue of companies like Bharat Forge and Sansera. The industry total is a guide to the automotive opportunity, not the sum of the listed companies' revenue.

For FY27, ACMA expects growth of 8–10%; CareEdge Ratings expects 8–9%, taking its gross measure from ₹9.83 lakh crore to ₹10.68 lakh crore. The slowdown reflects the high base created by the GST-driven second half of FY26. ACMA's long-range ambition is a $200 billion industry with $100 billion of exports by 2030. That would require about 19% a year in dollars from FY26 and a fourfold rise in exports, and is better read as a target than a forecast.

In plain terms

The industry has doubled since the pandemic, but measured from its 2019 peak it has grown at about 10% a year, which is solid rather than spectacular. The worrying detail is that imports are growing faster than exports. The newest and most valuable parts in Indian vehicles, the electronics and electric-drive components, are still mostly made abroad.

03

Three engines of demand: vehicles, content and the rest of the world

FY26 was a record in every vehicle segment, with a tax cut doing the heavy lifting in the second half. Each segment runs on its own cycle, and each supplier rides a different mix of them.

Every segment of the Indian vehicle market set a record in FY26, according to the Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers (SIAM). Total production reached 3.47 crore vehicles, up from about 3.10 crore in FY25. Two-wheeler domestic sales rose 10.7% to 2.17 crore, passing the FY19 peak for the first time. Passenger vehicles rose 7.9% to 46.4 lakh, commercial vehicles 12.6% to 10.8 lakh and three-wheelers 12.8% to 8.4 lakh. Vehicle exports rose 24% to 66.5 lakh units, with passenger-vehicle exports at a record 9.05 lakh.

GST 2.0: the tax cut that made FY26

On 22 September 2025 the government rebuilt GST on vehicles. Small cars (petrol up to 1,200cc, diesel up to 1,500cc, under four metres) and two-wheelers up to 350cc moved from 28% to 18%. Larger vehicles moved to a flat 40% with the compensation cess (1–22%) abolished, which also lowered their effective rate. Commercial vehicles and buses fell from 28% to 18%. All auto parts moved to a uniform 18%, down from 28% for many. Electric vehicles stayed at 5%. Ex-showroom prices of small cars fell by roughly 10–13%. Combined with repo-rate cuts, the change lowered the cost of owning a vehicle at a stroke, and SIAM credits it with the growth of the third and fourth quarters.

The effect is visible in the quarterly numbers: two-wheeler sales grew about 21.5% in the second half after a near-flat first half, and fourth-quarter growth ran at 13–27% across segments. For suppliers, the parts-rate cut has a second, slower effect. When branded replacement parts paid 28% and grey-market parts often paid nothing, the tax gap pushed garages toward unbranded parts. A uniform 18% narrows that gap and should help organised aftermarket players formalise share over time, though this is an expectation rather than a measured result.

Four segments, four cycles

Two-wheelers are a rural and semi-urban purchase that tracks the monsoon, crop prices and rural wages. Domestic sales peaked at about 2.12 crore in FY19, then fell for three years as BS-VI emission rules raised prices, insurance costs jumped and COVID hit, bottoming at about 1.35 crore in FY22. FY26's 2.17 crore is the first year above the old peak. Suppliers that depend on two-wheelers (Endurance, Belrise, ASK, Fiem, Gabriel, Varroc) spent four years growing mainly through content and share. The GST cut on bikes up to 350cc was the swing factor in FY26.

Passenger vehicles are the richest segment for content. SUVs now make up more than half of sales, and each SUV carries more harness, more lighting, more air-conditioning capacity and more electronics than the hatchback it replaced. The entry hatchback segment shrank from FY19 to FY25 and revived after the GST cut. For a supplier, a shift from small cars to SUVs raises revenue even when total units are flat.

Commercial vehicles follow the investment and freight cycle. Sales peaked at about 10.1 lakh in FY19, then collapsed when revised axle-load norms in July 2018 raised the payload of existing trucks, the IL&FS default froze vehicle finance, BS-VI raised prices and COVID halted freight. FY26's 10.8 lakh is a new record. Jamna Auto and ZF CV ride this cycle almost one for one. Exporters face a separate one: the US Class 8 heavy-truck market, which fell sharply in FY26 and took Bharat Forge's standalone revenue down 5% and weighed on Ramkrishna Forgings.

Tractors and off-highway machines matter to Gabriel, Sansera, Ramkrishna, Banco, Craftsman and Bosch's power-solutions arm. Domestic tractor sales were about 9.4 lakh in FY25; FY26 figures were not available for this report.

The export engine and the non-auto engine

Global vehicle production has been stuck at roughly 90–93 million a year since 2017. Indian exporters therefore grow by taking share: from Western suppliers whose costs are too high (Europe's energy crisis accelerated this), from Chinese suppliers whom Western buyers want a second source for (the "China+1" motive), and by following Indian customers abroad. The US takes about 26% of India's component exports and Europe about 31%; section 12 covers how tariffs have reshaped that. Separately, several companies are using the same forging, casting and machining skills to sell to railways (Sona bought Escorts Kubota's railway business; Ramkrishna has a ₹12,227 crore, 20-year rail-wheel contract), defence (Bharat Forge's defence order book is ₹11,196 crore), aerospace and semiconductor equipment (Sansera's ₹5,750 crore backlog) and energy storage (Exide, Amara Raja). Non-auto revenue is valued highly because it diversifies away from the vehicle cycle and the engine.

The aftermarket: a slower, steadier engine

India's aftermarket grew 9% to ₹1.08 lakh crore in FY26, after 6% in FY25. It depends on the size and age of the vehicle parc rather than on new sales, so it cushions downturns: when new-vehicle sales fall, owners keep vehicles longer and replace more parts. ACMA lists the drivers as the growing used-vehicle base, formalisation of repair, rural demand and e-commerce. Among listed companies the aftermarket is largest for Banco (whose Dutch subsidiary NRF is almost entirely European aftermarket), Exide and Amara Raja (where replacement batteries are the profit engine), Bosch (about 18% of revenue), Jamna, Gabriel and ZF CV (12–23% non-OEM).

In plain terms

FY26 was the best year Indian vehicle makers have had, helped by a tax cut that made small cars and motorcycles cheaper from September 2025. Suppliers enjoyed it, but each one rides a different part of the market: motorcycle-parts makers depend on rural incomes, car-parts makers on the shift to SUVs, truck-parts makers on freight and investment, and exporters on American and European truck and car makers. Next year will look slower simply because the comparison is so strong.

Content per vehicle: how the same motorcycle came to carry three times the parts bill Deep dive

Premium mixes, safety rules, electronics and electric drive add rupees to every vehicle. This is the growth engine that kept the industry expanding through a four-year volume slump, and the first thing to measure in any supplier.

Content per vehicle (CPV, also called kit value) is the rupee value of one supplier's parts in one vehicle. A lamp maker that supplies a ₹1,500 set of headlamp, tail lamp and indicators for a commuter motorcycle has a two-wheeler CPV of ₹1,500 on that model. If the next generation of the bike moves to LED lamps with a digital cluster, the same supplier's kit may be worth ₹3,000–4,000, with no change in the number of bikes sold. Across the industry, this is why ACMA's OEM supplies have grown faster than vehicle production: by about two points in FY25 (supplies +10%, production +8%) and about four points in FY26 (+16.3% against roughly +12%).

CPV rises for five reasons, and each has a different durability.

  • Premiumisation. SUVs now make up more than half of passenger-vehicle sales. An SUV's wiring harness is worth about 1.5 times a hatchback's, according to MSWIL, and it carries larger lamps, more air-conditioning capacity, alloy wheels and often a sunroof. In two-wheelers, the shift toward 125cc-plus and premium bikes brings disc brakes, LED lamps and digital clusters. Premiumisation tracks incomes and credit and can stall in a downturn, but it has been persistent for a decade.
  • Safety regulation. Combined or anti-lock braking became mandatory on two-wheelers from April 2019, dual front airbags on new cars from January 2022, Bharat NCAP crash ratings started in October 2023, and air-conditioned cabins became mandatory for N2 and N3 trucks from October 2025, more than doubling Subros's truck-AC revenue to about ₹260 crore in FY26. ZF CV's content per truck rises with each step from ABS to electronic braking, stability control and emergency braking. Regulation-driven content is the most durable kind: once mandated, it does not reverse.
  • Emission rules. The leap from BS-IV straight to BS-VI in April 2020 raised the content of fuel injection, sensors and after-treatment in every engine. Tenneco and Bosch benefited. Real-driving and on-board diagnostic rules (2023 for cars, 2025 for two-wheelers) added more. This content is tied to the engine and fades with electrification, though hybrids keep it alive.
  • Electronics and comfort. Instrument clusters have moved from analog dials (a few hundred rupees) to LCD and full-colour TFT screens (several thousand), and Pricol holds 55–60% of the Indian cluster market. Switches gain functions, cars gain cameras, connected-vehicle units and infotainment. Much of the electronic content is imported, which is why the trade deficit widened.
  • Electric drive. An electric two-wheeler carries roughly three times the electrical content of a petrol one: Uno Minda puts its kit at about ₹37,600 for an electric two-wheeler against about ₹11,900 for a combustion one, and Minda Corp (with its Flash Electronics associate) at ₹35,000–40,000 against ₹10,000–12,000. But the electric kit replaces content other suppliers lose, so it is a transfer between companies, not pure industry growth.

In plain terms

Share of wallet: the third multiplier

Content is what a vehicle could carry; share of business is how much of it one supplier wins. Two suppliers can sit on the same model, each with 50% of the volume, and either can gain at the next model. Dominant shares are common at the level of a single customer: Fiem's wallet share at HMSI has been close to 100% on mirrors and around three-quarters on some lamps (historical, indicative figures); Subros supplies more than 70% of Maruti's air-conditioning; MSWIL supplies harnesses for nine of India's ten best-selling cars. High wallet share is a double-edged asset. It locks in volume and engineering intimacy, but it also caps growth from that customer and concentrates risk, because a single lost platform hurts.

The CPV trap: when content growth is really metal prices

A rising revenue per vehicle is not always rising content. When aluminium, copper or steel prices rise and are passed through to the customer, revenue per vehicle goes up with no extra product or value added. ASK Automotive's Q1 FY27 revenue grew 52%, but revenue excluding alloy pass-through and a closed wheel-assembly line grew 25.3%. MSWIL's FY26 growth of 23% includes copper inflation. The cleaner measures are revenue growth stripped of commodity pass-through, or volume of parts shipped per vehicle, which few companies publish. Section 14 sets out how to adjust for it.

In plain terms

If a supplier's revenue per vehicle rises because metal got more expensive, it has not really sold more: it has just passed on a higher bill. Good analysis separates the two. Real content growth comes from better products, new features and new parts in each vehicle; inflation growth disappears when metal prices fall.

The investment conclusion from CPV is that suppliers should be judged against the vehicle segments they serve, not against the market as a whole. A two-wheeler supplier growing at 15% in a year when two-wheeler production grew 11% gained about four points of content and share. A truck-parts supplier growing 10% when truck production grew 13% lost ground, whatever the headline. Section 14 turns this into a KPI ("outperformance over production") and shows how the listed companies compare.

Why pass-through protects rupees but not percentages

Take a wiring-harness maker with ₹100 of quarterly revenue, ₹67 of materials (of which ₹40 is copper), ₹24 of other costs and ₹9 of EBITDA, a 9% margin. Copper rises 15%, adding ₹6 to costs. In the first quarter, before the price resets, EBITDA falls to ₹3, a 3% margin. In the next quarter the customer pays the extra ₹6, revenue becomes ₹106 and EBITDA returns to ₹9. Profit in rupees is restored, but the margin is now 8.5% (9 ÷ 106), because revenue is inflated by metal on which the supplier earns nothing. This example is illustrative, but it is what MSWIL reported: copper rose 38% year on year by the fourth quarter of FY26, and its margin fell from about 9.8% to 7.6% in Q1 FY27, with sharp wage increases in the NCR adding to the pressure while it negotiated cost recovery with customers.

The same mechanism runs in reverse. When metal prices fall, the supplier keeps the old, higher price for a quarter or two and margins briefly widen. A supplier's margin is therefore partly a commodity reading, and the useful comparison is margin over a full commodity cycle, or rupee EBITDA per unit, not one quarter's percentage. The replacement market adjusts more slowly than OEM contracts, which means battery and aftermarket-heavy companies can lag further behind a cost spike. Exide absorbed about ₹150 crore of commodity headwinds in FY26 (sulphur prices rose about five times) before price increases offset them.

Working capital and capex

A domestic supplier typically collects from vehicle makers in 45–60 days, holds 30–45 days of inventory and pays its own suppliers in 60–75 days, leaving net working capital of 20–45 days of sales. These are indicative norms. Exporters hold stock in warehouses near their foreign customers and run 60–100 days. Aftermarket businesses extend credit to distributors and hold wide catalogues: Banco's European subsidiary NRF pushes its inventory to 263 days. At the other end, Motherson, Varroc, Lumax and Minda Corp run negative working capital by paying suppliers later than they collect.

Capacity is the other balance-sheet lever. After the FY26 volume surge, most listed tier-1s were running at an estimated 70–85% utilisation, and capex plans for FY27 are large: Motherson about ₹6,000 crore, Sansera a ₹3,500 crore multi-year capacity plan, Craftsman ₹1,500 crore, ASK about ₹700 crore (1.3 times its FY26 EBITDA), Lumax about ₹300 crore. One rating agency puts projects worth ₹70,300 crore across the auto and auto-ancillary sector commissioning over FY27–29; the attribution and scope of that figure are uncertain, so treat it as indicative. New plants depress returns for two to three years before they fill.

In plain terms

Most of what a component maker charges goes to buy metal, plastic and electronics. Contracts let it pass higher metal prices to its customers, but a quarter or two late, so its profit margin dips when metals rise and bounces when they fall. Wages are the other cost that bit in 2026, with several states raising minimum wages by roughly a third. A supplier that adds a lot of its own engineering to each part, like a forger, is less exposed to metal prices but more exposed to power costs and to running its plants below capacity.

Oligopolies in a crowd: how the industry is structured

No supplier holds more than about 2% of the industry, yet most important products are controlled by two or three companies. Foreign groups, family promoters and a new wave of IPOs share the field.

ACMA has about 1,100 members, who together account for more than 90% of organised-sector turnover, and below them sits a long tail of small, unorganised tier-3 workshops. No company comes close to dominating the whole industry. Motherson's Indian revenue is perhaps ₹20,000 crore (an estimate; most of its ₹1.26 lakh crore is earned overseas), under 3% of ₹7.60 lakh crore. Concentration lives at the level of products, where a few suppliers hold most of the market because qualification is slow, tooling is customer-specific and scale lowers cost.

The supply of new paper matters for valuations. China: the supplier behind the supplier

China supplies 36% of India's $25.4 billion of component imports, by far the largest share, followed by Germany and Japan. Indian dependence is greatest in electronics (control units, chips, displays), rare-earth magnets, lithium-ion cells and some EV motors and controllers. Chinese companies also enter as technology partners: several Indian tier-1s have licensed Chinese EV technology, and Exide's cell plant uses technology from SVOLT. Direct Chinese investment is restricted by Press Note 3 (2020), which requires government approval for investment from countries sharing a land border with India. The result is a two-sided relationship: China is both the main source of the new content and the main competitor for it.

In plain terms

There are hundreds of component makers in India, but for any particular part, a car or motorcycle maker usually chooses between two or three qualified suppliers. That is why the leading companies can earn steady profits in a crowded industry. Many of the strongest competitors are unlisted subsidiaries of Japanese, German and American groups, and China supplies much of the advanced electronics that Indian companies assemble.

1. The global consolidator

Samvardhana Motherson is a model of one. It has grown through more than 50 acquisitions, typically distressed or orphaned European and Japanese businesses bought cheaply and integrated into its procurement and manufacturing system: Visiocorp mirrors (2009), Peguform bumpers and cockpits (2011), PKC wiring (2017), Reydel interiors (2018) and many since. More than 70% of its revenue is earned outside India, and its EBITDA margin (9.5% reported) reflects Europe's cost base more than India's. Returns are mid-teens at best on screener's measure, leverage rises with each deal and falls in between, and the investment case rests on whether the next acquisitions earn their cost of capital. Section 13 covers the machine in detail.

2. The foreign-owned licensees

Bosch, Schaeffler India, ZF CV and Tenneco Clean Air India are Indian arms of global groups. They make the parent's products under licence, pay a royalty or technical fee for it (about 2.4% of revenue at Bosch, 2.3% at Tenneco in FY25, about 4% reported at ZF CV), sell part of their output to the parent group as exports and carry net cash. Their margins and returns are high, but the parent decides which technologies come to India, which export markets the subsidiary may serve and at what transfer prices. Investors pay a scarcity premium for them: there are few listed ways to own Bosch or Schaeffler technology in a fast-growing market, and their median price-earnings ratio is about 49 times.

3. The domestic system tier-1s

The largest group comprises Indian family companies that make systems for Indian vehicle makers: lighting and switches (Uno Minda, Fiem, Varroc), clusters (Pricol), locks and harnesses (Minda Corp), harnesses (MSWIL), plastics and shifters (Lumax), suspension and castings (Endurance, Gabriel), braking and aluminium (ASK), sheet metal (Belrise) and air-conditioning (Subros). Their margins are moderate (9–15%) but their assets turn over two to three times a year in the better cases, so returns on capital of 18–40% are common. They grow by adding products to the same customers, often through joint ventures with global technology owners (Uno Minda has more than 15; Lumax has partners from Japan, Italy, Germany and Israel). Their main risk is concentration: many depend on one or two two-wheeler makers or on Maruti.

4. The precision metal-workers

Forgers, casters and machinists turn steel and aluminium into engine, axle and driveline parts: Bharat Forge, Ramkrishna, Sansera, Craftsman, Sona, SPR Auto, Jamna and Banco. Their own process adds most of the value, so margins are high (15–25%), but they need heavy presses, furnaces and machining centres, so asset turnover is low (0.6–1.6 times) and returns depend on keeping plants full. Many are export-led and therefore exposed to the US and European truck cycles and to tariffs. The market pays the highest median multiple for this group, about 63 times, because several have credible non-auto diversification (defence, aerospace, rail, semiconductors) and one, Sona, is the purest EV-content play in the list.

5. The battery makers

Exide and Amara Raja share India's lead-acid starter-battery market and lead in industrial batteries for telecom towers, inverters and data centres. Replacement batteries sold through thousands of dealers are the profit engine; OEM supply is thinner-margin. Both are using the cash to build lithium-ion cell plants without government incentives, which depresses consolidated returns today (Exide's consolidated ROCE is 8.5% against about 29% for the lead-acid core). The market values them at the lowest multiples in the sub-sector, about 15 times earnings for Amara Raja, which says investors doubt the lithium spending will earn its cost of capital. Section 11 covers the bet.

The two hybrids

Tube Investments of India is a precision-tube, chain and cycle maker (₹8,556 crore standalone revenue, pre-tax return on invested capital of about 44%) that also owns 56.29% of CG Power, a motors, transformers and railway-traction company it rescued in 2020. Where Tube Investments appears in comparisons, this report either uses its standalone figures or excludes it, and says which.

JBM Auto is a sheet-metal and tooling supplier to Maruti, Tata and Mahindra (about 62% of revenue) that has also become India's largest electric-bus maker (₹2,300 crore, about 38% of FY26 revenue, with an order book of more than 10,000 buses). It operates many of those buses itself on 10–12-year per-kilometre contracts with state transport undertakings, which makes it capital-intensive and leveraged (net debt about 3.7 times EBITDA) and gives it 131 days of receivables. It is not a pure component maker, and its numbers should be read with that in mind.

In plain terms

Component makers come in five kinds: one global buyer of other companies' factories, a handful of Indian arms of foreign giants, a large group of Indian family firms that assemble systems for Indian vehicle makers, a group of metal specialists that forge and machine heavy parts often for export, and two battery makers. Each kind earns money differently. The metal specialists have the fattest margins but need the most machinery; the assemblers have thin margins but need little capital; the foreign-owned arms combine good returns with a parent who calls the shots.

Production subsidies have flowed mainly to vehicle makers and cell builders. The policies that matter most to component makers are the quieter ones: emission and safety mandates, the tax schedule and trade agreements.

Government policy touches the component industry through five channels: production-linked incentives, electric-vehicle demand subsidies, tax rates, emission and safety rules, and trade. The incentive schemes attract the headlines, but for most listed suppliers the regulatory mandates have done more, because they force content into every vehicle regardless of who builds it.

PLI-Auto: an incentive that reached the vehicle makers

The production-linked incentive for automobiles and components was designed to pull advanced technology into India, with incentives of 13–18% of eligible incremental sales for vehicle makers and 8–13% for component makers, conditional on high domestic value addition. In practice the disbursements have gone mainly to vehicle makers building electric two-wheelers and other advanced vehicles. Of the ₹2,321.94 crore paid out by December 2025, five vehicle makers received ₹2,000 crore: Bajaj Auto ₹625.65 crore, Tata Motors ₹403.18 crore, Ola Electric ₹366.78 crore, TVS Motor ₹320.51 crore and Mahindra ₹283.82 crore. ACMA's leadership has said only two of its roughly 1,100 members had received benefits. The 50% domestic-value-addition test is hard to meet when the most valuable parts of an electric drivetrain are imported, which is the trade-deficit problem again.

Mandates: the policies that move content

Emission and safety rules matter more to most listed suppliers because they apply to every vehicle sold. The BS-VI leap of April 2020 forced every diesel engine to carry particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction, and every petrol engine more precise fuel injection and sensors; that one rule change underpins Tenneco's clean-air business and much of Bosch's power-solutions revenue. The 2019 two-wheeler braking rule created much of ASK's and Endurance's braking growth. The October 2025 truck-cabin air-conditioning mandate turned truck AC into Subros's fastest-growing line. Commercial-vehicle safety rules phased in from April 2026 (advanced emergency braking, lane-departure warning, endurance braking) add to ZF CV's content per truck.

The next rule to watch for passenger vehicles is CAFE-III, the corporate average fuel-efficiency standard proposed for FY28, which caps fleet-wide carbon emissions. Tighter limits reward hybrids and EVs and could raise content for motor, power-electronics and thermal suppliers while capping the life of pure petrol powertrains. For two-wheelers, a move to compulsory ABS on all models would be the largest single content event; a draft was reported in June 2025 but its status could not be confirmed for this report.

The rare-earth magnet scheme

The Cabinet approved a ₹7,280 crore scheme on 26 November 2025 to build 6,000 tonnes a year of integrated sintered rare-earth permanent magnet capacity in India, with ₹6,450 crore of incentives linked to sales over five years and ₹750 crore of capital subsidy. It follows China's April 2025 licensing of rare-earth exports, which halved some Indian EV output (section 10). Private projects are emerging: Midwest Advanced Materials targets 500 tonnes by the end of 2026 and 5,000 tonnes by 2030. Magnet capacity takes years to build and qualify, so the scheme is a medium-term hedge rather than a near-term fix.

In plain terms

Most of the government's manufacturing subsidy for the auto sector has gone to vehicle makers, not part makers. What has helped part makers more are rules that force every new vehicle to be cleaner and safer, because each rule adds parts that someone has to supply. The rules to watch next are stricter fuel-economy limits for cars from 2027–28 and a possible requirement for anti-lock brakes on every motorcycle.

Seven decades, five shocks: how the industry got here

A licence-era base, a Japanese template, a globalising decade, a five-year bust and the strongest recovery in the industry's history. The shocks have come from policy and credit more often than from demand.

The industry's history explains its shape today. Its oldest listed member was founded in 1951; its newest listed in 2025. In between, five distinct shocks redrew the competitive map, and each left a lesson that still governs how the companies are run and valued.

  • 1951–1963
    Motor Industries Company (MICO, now Bosch Ltd) is set up in Bengaluru (1951). Gabriel India (1961) and Shriram Pistons & Rings (1963, now SPR Auto) follow under the licence regime.
  • 1983–1986
    Maruti Udyog starts production with Suzuki. Suzuki pushes Japanese technology partners into joint ventures with Indian families: Subros with Denso, Sona with Koyo, Motherson with Sumitomo Wiring (1986). The modern tier system is born.
  • 1991–2002
    Liberalisation and de-licensing (1993) bring Hyundai, Ford, Honda and Toyota. The 2002 Auto Policy allows 100% foreign ownership in vehicles and components.
  • 2004–2011
    Indian suppliers go global by acquisition: Bharat Forge buys Germany's Carl Dan Peddinghaus (2004); after the financial crisis Motherson buys Visiocorp (2009) and Peguform (2011) cheaply.
  • Jul 2018 – FY21
    The long bust: revised axle-load norms (Jul 2018), the IL&FS default and NBFC credit freeze (Sep 2018), insurance-cost increases, the BS-VI leap (Apr 2020) and COVID. ACMA turnover falls from ₹3.95 lakh crore (FY19) to ₹3.40 lakh crore (FY21).
  • 2021–2022
    The semiconductor shortage stretches car waiting periods to 6–12 months. PLI-ACC and PLI-Auto are approved. Sona, Craftsman and Sansera list. MSWIL is demerged from Motherson and listed (Mar 2022).
  • 2023–2024
    SUVs pass half of car sales. Bharat NCAP starts (Oct 2023); ASK lists (Nov 2023). PM E-DRIVE replaces FAME-II (Sep 2024).
  • 2025
    The year of shocks: China licenses rare-earth exports (4 Apr), the US imposes a 25% Section 232 tariff on auto parts (3 May), US reciprocal tariffs on India reach 50% on other goods (Aug), then GST 2.0 cuts vehicle taxes (22 Sep). Belrise (May) and Tenneco Clean Air India (Nov) list.
  • 2026
    US–India interim trade framework (Feb); the US Supreme Court strikes down IEEPA tariffs (20 Feb); record FY26 production of 3.47 crore vehicles (Apr); ACMA reports a return to a trade deficit (Jul); Exide commissions India's first large private lithium-ion cell plant phase (23 Sep).

Era by era

The licence years (1950s–1983) produced a handful of protected suppliers making old designs for a tiny market. Their legacy is a few of today's listed names and a culture of family control. The Maruti template (1983–1991) created the modern industry. Suzuki brought Japanese supplier management, just-in-time delivery and quality discipline, and pushed its Japanese partners to form joint ventures with Indian families. Subros, Sona and Motherson's wiring business all date from that template, as does the habit, still visible, of Japanese partners holding strategic stakes.

Liberalisation and globalisation (1991–2011) brought global vehicle makers and their global suppliers to India and sent the ambitious Indian suppliers abroad. Bharat Forge bought forging plants in Germany, Sweden and the US to become a global forging company. Motherson used the 2008–09 crisis to buy European mirror and bumper businesses at distressed prices, a playbook it has repeated since. Not every overseas acquisition worked: several Indian suppliers lost money on European assets, and Bharat Forge's European operations still require restructuring today.

The long bust (2018–2021) was the deepest downturn in the industry's modern history and was mostly made in Delhi and Mumbai rather than by consumers. New axle-load norms let existing trucks carry more and killed demand for new ones; the IL&FS collapse froze the shadow banks that finance most two-wheelers and trucks; compulsory multi-year insurance raised the upfront price of two-wheelers; the leap to BS-VI raised two-wheeler prices by 10–15%; then COVID shut factories. Two-wheeler domestic sales fell from about 2.12 crore to about 1.35 crore. Suppliers that survived in good shape were those with content growth, aftermarket businesses and low debt.

The recovery (2022–2026) combined a volume rebound, a structural shift to SUVs, a stream of safety and emission content and the first EV programmes. ACMA turnover more than doubled from the FY21 trough. The period ended with a cluster of external shocks in 2025 (rare earths, US tariffs) followed by a domestic tax boost, which is why FY26 shows record domestic demand alongside slowing exports.

Four lessons the cycles taught

  • Policy shocks matter more than demand shocks. Axle-load norms, credit freezes, emission leaps and tax changes have moved volumes more than incomes have. Investors in this sector need to read the regulatory calendar as closely as the macro data.
  • Content cushions the cycle. Component turnover cleared its FY19 peak in FY22, four years before two-wheeler volumes did, because each vehicle carried more. Suppliers on the right side of content trends survived the bust in better shape.
  • Overseas acquisitions are hard. Motherson has made the buy-and-fix model work at scale; many others have not. European assets bring customers and technology but also high costs, unions and restructuring charges that recur, as Bharat Forge's ₹358 crore exceptional charge in Q1 FY27 shows.
  • Leverage kills in a downturn. The suppliers that struggled most in 2019–21 were those that had borrowed for capacity at the peak. Today's QIP wave (Belrise, Craftsman, SPR, Bharat Forge) is partly a lesson learned: companies are raising equity while valuations are high to keep balance sheets light.
In plain terms

India's parts industry was built on a Japanese model in the 1980s, went global in the 2000s, nearly halved in volume terms during a five-year slump that ended in 2021, and has since doubled. Most of its downturns were caused by government rules and credit crunches, not by people suddenly refusing to buy vehicles. Companies that kept adding content to each vehicle and kept debt low came through best.

The EV sort: losers, agnostics and gainers Deep dive

Electric drive deletes the engine, keeps the chassis and adds a battery, motor and power electronics. Penetration is still low, so the sort shows up first in valuations and order books, and only slowly in earnings.

An electric vehicle removes about a third of a conventional vehicle's parts by value and adds a new set, dominated by the battery. For a component maker, the question is which side of that line its products sit on. The answer sorts the 29 companies into three groups: those whose products disappear (pistons, rings, valves, fuel injection, exhausts, clutches, radiators), those whose products carry over unchanged (lamps, seats, mirrors, brakes, suspension, body parts, cabin air-conditioning, the 12-volt battery) and those whose content grows (motors, controllers, converters, chargers, high-voltage harnesses, thermal management, battery packs and cells, reduction gears and EV differentials). CareEdge estimates that electronics are about 23% of an EV's cost against less than 10% in an ICE vehicle, and the battery 40–50%.

Where penetration stands

EVs were 8.27% of the 29.63 million vehicles registered in FY26 (Vahan data), up from 7.52% in FY25, with more than 2.45 million units sold against about 1.7 lakh in FY20. The average hides very different segments. Three-wheelers are already largely electric if e-rickshaws are counted (roughly 55–60% of all three-wheelers), while the share among regulated L5 passenger and cargo three-wheelers is lower (an estimated 25–35%). Electric two-wheelers were 6.54% of the market in FY26, about 1.4 million units and 57% of all EVs sold. Electric cars are the laggard: about 4% in FY26 (an indicative estimate) rising to about 6% in January–July 2026, and electric buses were 4.8% over the same months.

Why ICE suppliers still grow: the arithmetic of a slow transition

An ICE-only supplier is not shrinking yet, because the market it serves is still growing. Consider an illustrative two-wheeler market growing 6% a year. If the electric share rises from 6.5% to 25% over five years, total volumes rise by about 34% (1.06 to the fifth power) and combustion volumes from 93.5 units in every 100 today to about 100, an increase of 7%. If the electric share reaches 40% instead, combustion volumes fall to about 80, a decline of 14%. The difference between those two outcomes, roughly 20 percentage points of volume for an engine-parts maker, is what investors argue about. In the meantime, hybrids (whose cess was abolished under GST 2.0 and which carry a full engine and after-treatment system) extend the runway for engine and exhaust suppliers, and the replacement market for engine parts keeps growing with the size of the existing fleet.

In plain terms

Electric vehicles are still only about one in twelve vehicles sold in India, and in cars only about one in twenty. So companies that make engine parts are still growing, and will for some years. But investors value a company on its whole future, and they are already paying more for makers of electric-vehicle parts and less for makers of engine parts. The engine-parts companies are using today's profits to buy their way into other businesses before the decline arrives.

The magnet shock: when the gaining side depends on China

The EV-gaining side carries its own risk: the motors at its heart need neodymium-iron-boron magnets, and China makes about 90% of the world's supply. On 4 April 2025 China introduced export licences and end-use documentation for seven medium and heavy rare-earth elements and the magnets made from them. Shipments to India slowed sharply. Bajaj Auto's Chetak electric scooter output fell to 10,824 units in July 2025 from 20,384 a year earlier, and other EV and motor makers reported disruptions; ACMA flagged the issue as a concern. The same magnets are used in conventional vehicles too, in power-steering, wiper and window-lift motors.

Two more threats to the gainers: insourcing and US policy

EV makers have taken more of the drivetrain in-house than ICE makers ever did. Ola Electric, Ather and Tata build their own battery packs, and several make their own motors or controllers, which shrinks the addressable market for independent suppliers. Chinese EV-component makers enter through licensing deals and joint ventures with Indian tier-1s. And export-oriented EV suppliers depend on foreign EV programmes: the end of the US federal EV tax credit on 30 September 2025 slowed American EV launches, and Sona's order book has been tilting toward hybrid programmes (EV share 70% in March 2026, 64% in June, including a ₹640 crore hybrid driveline win). Being on the gaining side of the sort is necessary for the EV premium, but it is not sufficient: the gainer also needs to hold on to the content against the vehicle maker and against China.

In plain terms

Making electric-vehicle parts is not a free pass. The magnets inside electric motors come almost entirely from China, which showed in 2025 that it can turn off supply. Electric-vehicle makers also like to build their own batteries and motors, leaving less for suppliers. The companies most likely to profit are those that design around Chinese bottlenecks and sell something vehicle makers cannot easily make themselves.

Lead to lithium: the battery makers' bet-the-franchise pivot

Exide and Amara Raja are using a lead-acid duopoly's cash to build lithium-ion cell plants without government subsidy. The first gigawatt-hours are arriving now; the returns will take years.

Exide (about 40% of India's automotive battery market) and Amara Raja (an estimated 30–35%) share one of the most stable franchises in the sub-sector. Every vehicle, electric or not, carries a 12-volt starter or auxiliary battery, and most automotive batteries are sold as replacements through dealer networks that took decades to build. Both companies also lead in industrial batteries for telecom towers, home inverters, UPS systems, railways and data centres. The business throws off cash, and lead prices, which are about 65–70% of battery cost, are passed through to vehicle makers by indexation and to replacement buyers with a lag.

The threat is that lithium-ion takes over the uses where lead-acid is weakest: traction batteries for electric two- and three-wheelers (already lithium), home and telecom backup, and grid storage. Lead-acid starter batteries erode more slowly, because EVs still carry a 12-volt system. Both companies decided to make lithium-ion cells themselves rather than import them, without the PLI-ACC incentive that went to Ola, Reliance and Rajesh Exports, and against Chinese cell makers who dominate global supply on cost.

The economics of a cell plant, roughly

Neither company has published unit economics, but a rough illustration shows why the plants dilute returns. Assume a cell price of about ₹5,000 per kWh, an assumption broadly in line with recent global lithium-iron-phosphate pricing but not sourced to either company, so treat it as illustrative. Unless cell margins are much higher than lead-acid margins, which Chinese competition makes unlikely, the lithium business will earn lower returns than the core for several years. Tariffs, Europe and China+1: the export book under pressure Deep dive

India exports $24 billion of components, a quarter of it to the US. Eighteen months of US tariff changes slowed growth to 5%, Europe became the largest market, and the companies that build abroad rather than ship from India were the least affected.

Exports are 22% of the industry's gross sales and the channel with the most strategic upside, because global vehicle production is flat and every export rupee is share taken from someone else. Engine components and drive-transmission and steering parts make up more than half of what India ships; suspension, braking and body parts follow. In FY25 North America took about 32% of exports, Europe 29.5% and Asia 26%. In FY26 Europe grew 9% to $7.36 billion, about 31% of the total and now the largest region, while the US share was about 26%, roughly $6.2 billion, with Germany 9%, Thailand 4% and the UK 3%.

Whether the preferential quota promised in February has been implemented, and how large it is, could not be confirmed for this report. That is the single most important open question for Indian exporters to the US.

What a 25% tariff does to a contract

A tariff is paid by the importer, usually the US vehicle maker or tier-1, but the cost gets shared. Take an illustrative forged axle beam sold to a US truck maker at $100. A 25% tariff makes it cost the buyer $125. The buyer can absorb the $25, pass it to truck buyers, push the Indian supplier to cut its price, or move the order to a supplier in Mexico or the US. In practice, existing programmes are rarely re-sourced mid-life because re-qualification takes a year or more, so the first-year effect is a negotiation over who absorbs the cost. The bigger effect is on new programmes: a 25% handicap against a Mexican competitor under the US-Mexico-Canada agreement is hard to overcome on price alone. The tariff therefore threatens the export order book more than current revenue.

In plain terms

About half of what Indian companies ship to the US for vehicles pays a 25% import tax, and most of the rest pays 10–12.5%. Customers rarely switch suppliers for parts already in production, so the damage so far has been slower growth and harder price talks rather than lost business. The bigger risk is that new contracts go to suppliers in Mexico or the US, and that will only show up in order books over the next two to three years.

Who is exposed, and who builds where it sells

The distinction that matters is between companies that export from India and companies that manufacture overseas. Motherson earns more than 70% of its revenue outside India, but most of it is made in the country or region where it is sold, including large plants in the US and Mexico, so US tariffs on Indian goods touch it far less than its overseas share suggests. The same applies to Banco's European aftermarket subsidiary NRF and Endurance's German operations. The companies that ship from India into the US are the ones in the tariff's path.

Europe: the market that is opening

While the US closed, Europe opened. European exports grew 9% in FY26, the fastest of any major region. Three forces are behind it. European suppliers face high energy and labour costs and are moving production to cheaper locations; Schaeffler is building India as a sourcing hub, including bearing production relocated from Europe. Trade agreements are improving access: the India–UK agreement took effect in July 2026, and ACMA expects an India–EU agreement to be concluded. And European vehicle makers want second sources outside China. Companies with European plants or customers (Endurance, whose European revenue rose 29% in FY26, Sona with 24% of revenue in Europe, Banco through NRF, Motherson) benefit. The risk is that European vehicle production itself is weak, and that restructuring of Europe's own suppliers creates cheap distressed capacity that competes on price.

China+1: real, but slow

The "China+1" idea is that global vehicle makers and tier-1s want a second source outside China for parts they currently buy there. For Indian suppliers this is the main route to export share gains, because they compete on cost and engineering with Chinese suppliers rather than with Western ones. The evidence is visible in programme wins (Sona's North American EV contracts, Sansera's semiconductor-equipment orders, Ramkrishna's $220 million North American order of February 2024), but it shows up slowly in revenue because every new programme takes two to four years from nomination to peak. Trade policy cuts both ways: tariffs on China push buyers to India, while tariffs on India push them to Mexico. India's relative tariff position against China and Mexico, more than its absolute rate, decides how much of the China+1 opportunity it captures.

In plain terms

Indian exporters grow only by taking business from someone else, because the world is not building more vehicles. American tariffs have made that harder in the US, especially for forgings sent to truck makers, so growth has shifted to Europe, where local suppliers are expensive and trade deals are improving access. Companies that own factories abroad, like Motherson, are largely insulated; companies that ship from India, like Bharat Forge and Ramkrishna, are exposed.

Consolidators and licensees: Motherson's machine and the foreign-owned subsidiaries Deep dive

The largest company grows by buying other people's factories; four of the most valuable are controlled by foreign parents that license them technology. Both models need reading on their own terms.

Motherson: a ₹1.26 lakh crore acquisition engine

Samvardhana Motherson International is India's largest component company and one of the world's top 25 auto suppliers. It began in 1986 as a wiring-harness joint venture with Sumitomo Wiring Systems for Maruti and has since made more than 50 acquisitions, most of them distressed or non-core European and Japanese businesses bought at low multiples and then integrated into Motherson's procurement, manufacturing and customer system. Today it makes bumpers, cockpits, door panels and other polymer modules, mirrors and vision systems, wiring harnesses, integrated assemblies and, increasingly, non-automotive products (aerospace parts, consumer-electronics components, medical and logistics) in more than 300 subsidiaries in about 45 countries.

In FY26 Motherson reported revenue of ₹1,26,104 crore (+10.9%), EBITDA of ₹12,033 crore (a 9.5% margin) and net profit attributable to shareholders of ₹3,860 crore. Normalised for ₹379 crore of pre-tax European restructuring provisions and smaller items, attributable profit was ₹4,258 crore, up 17%. One definition issue needs explaining once: Motherson also publishes an "economic" EBITDA of ₹14,472 crore, a 10.1% margin on "economic revenue" of ₹1,43,288 crore. The economic figures include 100% of the joint ventures and associates that the consolidated accounts carry only as a share of profit (₹17,184 crore of revenue and ₹2,439 crore of EBITDA). Both are company figures; they describe different perimeters. This report uses the reported consolidated ₹12,033 crore and 9.5% for comparisons with other companies, and dividing the economic EBITDA by the reported revenue, which some summaries do, overstates the margin.

The licensees: Bosch, Schaeffler, ZF CV and Tenneco

Four companies in the list are controlled by foreign parents: Bosch Ltd (Robert Bosch GmbH holds 70.5%), Schaeffler India (Schaeffler AG, 74.1%), ZF Commercial Vehicle Control Systems India (ZF Friedrichshafen, 60.0% after a 5:1 bonus issue) and Tenneco Clean Air India (Tenneco, owned by Apollo Global since a 2022 take-private, 74.8%). They share five features that investors need to read differently from Indian-promoted companies.

  • Royalty and fees. The subsidiary pays the parent for technology. Bosch paid ₹478 crore of royalty and technical-service fees in FY26, about 2.4% of revenue, up 29% from ₹372 crore (about 2.1%) while revenue grew 10.8%; it separately paid Robert Bosch GmbH ₹561 crore of professional and other charges. Tenneco's royalty was 2.3% of sales in FY23, 4.7% in FY24 (management calls that year a one-off project) and 2.3% in FY25, with 2.5% guided for the long run. ZF CV's royalty is reported at about 4% of sales, a figure from the primer that could not be verified against FY26 accounts; Schaeffler's rate was not sourced. A rising royalty transfers margin from minority shareholders to the parent.
  • Related-party trade. Bosch bought ₹4,252 crore of goods from its parent in FY26 and sells about three-quarters of its exports to it. ZF CV exports about 43% of output to the ZF network, Schaeffler about 15%. Transfer prices on both sides shape the subsidiary's margin.
  • Parent's technology roadmap. The parent decides which products are localised in India and which export markets the subsidiary serves. Schaeffler India's €300 million (about ₹2,700 crore) e-axle order and its role as a sourcing hub for bearings moved out of Europe are examples of parent decisions that favour the subsidiary.
  • Net cash. All four carry net cash: Bosch about ₹8,300 crore or more, ZF CV about ₹2,200 crore, Schaeffler about ₹1,800 crore and Tenneco about ₹500 crore. Idle cash depresses return on equity (Bosch's ROE is 14.6% against a ROCE of 21.5%) and inflates reported profit through interest income (Bosch's ₹1,410 crore of other income was 39% of FY26 pre-tax profit, flattered by a one-off gain on selling its building-technologies business).
  • Small free float and parent selling. With 60–75% held by the parent, free floats are small, which supports a scarcity premium. Parents also monetise: Tenneco's November 2025 IPO was entirely an offer for sale by the parent (₹3,600 crore, 58.8 times subscribed, qualified institutions 166 times), preceded by dividends of about 172% of FY26 profit that hollowed out book equity and inflate its reported 61% ROCE and 44% ROE.

Why the market pays a premium anyway

Despite these frictions, the licensees trade at 32–56 times earnings, and Bosch at 47 times EV/EBITDA, among the highest in the sub-sector. Investors pay for access to global technology in a fast-growing market, for balance sheets that cannot fail, for steady dividends (Schaeffler pays out about 48%) and for the possibility that the parent eventually buys out minorities at a premium. The premium is not a free lunch: when the parent raises the royalty, keeps cash idle, or sells shares, minority holders bear the cost. The cleanest test is to compare growth in the royalty with growth in revenue each year, and dividends with free cash flow.

Several Indian-promoted companies have a lighter version of the same relationship through technology joint ventures. Uno Minda runs more than 15 joint ventures with Japanese, European and other partners; Lumax's best product lines sit in 50:50 joint ventures, so ₹58 crore of its ₹337 crore FY26 profit belongs to partners; Subros was founded with Denso and Suzuki, which still hold strategic stakes, and Denso supplies its compressor technology. Those arrangements bring products to market quickly, but they leak profit to partners and complicate the accounts.

In plain terms

Bosch, Schaeffler, ZF CV and Tenneco are the Indian arms of German and American groups. They make excellent products with their parents' technology, carry piles of cash and are very profitable. But the parent takes a fee for the technology, sets prices for what it buys and sells, and can sell shares when it likes. Investors accept those terms because there are few other ways to own world-class auto technology in India's market.

If the company employs ₹1,700 crore of capital (revenue twice capital employed), its pre-tax return on capital is 275 ÷ 1,700 ≈ 16%.

Now move each dial for the following year: passenger-vehicle production +6%, two-wheeler production +8%, commercial vehicles +5%, kit values +7% everywhere from LED and premium mix, passenger-vehicle share up one point (25% to 26%), aftermarket +9% and exports +5%. Passenger revenue becomes 1,650 × 1.06 × 1.07 × 1.04 ≈ ₹1,946 crore; two-wheelers 1,009 × 1.08 × 1.07 ≈ ₹1,166 crore; commercial vehicles about ₹135 crore; aftermarket ₹382 crore; exports ₹263 crore. Total revenue rises to about ₹3,890 crore, up 15%, from production growth of 6–8%. That is the industry's FY26 pattern in miniature: content and share turned high-single-digit volume growth into mid-teens revenue growth. If polymer and aluminium prices rose 10% in the same year, the one-quarter pass-through lag would cut that year's EBITDA margin by roughly 50–70 basis points even as rupee profit grew.

In plain terms: three dials, and only one fully in management's hands

A supplier cannot control how many cars or motorcycles its customers sell. It can partly control how much of its product goes into each vehicle, by developing better products, and it can win a bigger share of each customer's orders, slowly, one model at a time. When results improve, ask which dial moved. Growth from content and share tends to last; growth from a good year for vehicle sales, or from higher metal prices passed on to customers, can reverse.

Up: premium mix, safety and emission mandates, new product lines, electric drive (for gainers), metal inflation. Down: downtrading, lost platforms, deflation in metals, electric drive (for losers). Benchmark. Content growth above 7% a year is good, 3–7% average and below 3% weak. The listed companies. Only Uno Minda and Minda Corp publish rupee kit values (both two-wheeler; see section 04). Capacity utilisation: why the forgers' margins swing

What it is. Output ÷ installed capacity. Formula. For forgers, tonnes shipped ÷ tonnes of press capacity; for assemblers, units ÷ line capacity. Worked example. If variable costs (steel, power, consumables) are 60% of revenue and fixed costs ₹735 crore, EBITDA is ₹735 crore, a 20% margin. Drivers. Customer volumes, new-plant ramp-ups, export cycles. Benchmark. 75–85% good; 60–75% average; below 60% weak; above 90% signals a need for new capex.

Material cost and the pass-through lag

What it is. Raw materials and bought-out parts as a share of revenue, and the delay before changes in their cost reach the selling price. Formula. Material cost ÷ revenue; lag in quarters from the contract terms. Worked example. Section 05's harness example: a 15% copper rise cut the margin from 9% to 3% for one quarter, and even after full pass-through left it at 8.5%, because revenue was inflated by metal. Drivers. Product type (assembly versus process value-add), contract indexation, the replacement channel's slower repricing. Benchmark. Pass-through within a quarter on indexed contracts is good; one to two quarters average; longer or discretionary weak. Material cost as a share of sales ranges from about 42% (Sansera) to 81% (Belrise); section 05 charts it.

The definition trap. Only proxies exist for most companies. "Gross margin" computed as 100 minus material cost ignores conversion costs and badly overstates the true gross margin for machining-heavy businesses: Sansera's proxy is 58% when manufacturing costs take a further 18% or so of revenue. Use material cost as a share of sales to understand commodity sensitivity, and do not compare it across business models as if it were a margin.

Royalty and technical fees: the parent's share

What it is. Payments to a parent or technology partner for the right to make its products. Formula. Royalty plus technical fees ÷ revenue, from the related-party note. Worked example. A ₹10,000 crore subsidiary paying 2.5% sends ₹250 crore to its parent; at a 15% EBITDA margin, a rise to 3.5% removes ₹100 crore, about 7% of EBITDA. Drivers. New technologies licensed, parent policy, tax considerations. Benchmark. Below 1.5% good; 1.5–3% average; above 3% or rising faster than revenue weak. Listed companies. Bosch 2.4% (₹478 crore, up 29%), Tenneco 2.3% in FY25 with 2.5% guided, ZF CV about 4% (unverified); Schaeffler and the joint-venture partners of Subros, Lumax and Uno Minda not sourced (section 13). The definition trap. Royalty is not the only channel: professional fees, purchase prices from the parent and export prices to it move value just as effectively and are harder to spot.

R&D intensity: buying the next generation

What it is. Research and development spending as a share of revenue. Formula. R&D expense (and capitalised development) ÷ revenue. Worked example. A ₹4,000 crore electronics supplier spending 4% invests ₹160 crore a year, enough for a few hundred engineers and validation labs; at 1%, ₹40 crore barely covers adapting a partner's designs. Drivers. Product complexity, in-house versus licensed technology, EV ambitions. Benchmark. Above 4% good for electronics and EV players; 1.5–4% average; below 1.5% weak unless technology is licensed. Listed companies. Only Bosch's figure (₹601 crore, 3.0% of gross sales) was sourced for this report; Varroc reports 750-plus R&D engineers across seven centres. Most others are n/a in the sources used. The definition trap. Companies that license technology report low R&D but pay royalties; companies that capitalise development costs show higher margins today and higher amortisation later.

Net debt to EBITDA: the shock absorber

What it is. How many years of operating profit it would take to repay net borrowings. Formula. (Borrowings − cash − liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example. In plain terms: whose cash, and whose debt

Three lessons follow from the arithmetic. First, a single programme on its own earns a pre-tax return of only about 14%, barely above a reasonable cost of capital. Second, the economics improve sharply with incumbency: if the supplier wins the successor model and reuses most of the line, needing only ₹20 crore of new capex instead of ₹60 crore, the second programme's pre-tax IRR rises to roughly 39% on the same revenue. That is why suppliers fight to keep platforms and why the order-book "re-win rate" matters more than any single win. Third, price-downs are lethal if not matched by cost engineering: a 2% annual price cut from year two, with no offsetting saving, reduces revenue by about ₹56 crore over the programme's life, essentially its entire pre-tax surplus, and by year five takes ₹17.5 crore off a peak EBITDA of ₹30 crore. The suppliers with high returns are those whose engineering teams find savings faster than their customers demand them.

In plain terms: the money is in the second model, not the first

Winning a part on a new car means spending money for three or four years before earning it back. One model on its own barely pays for itself. The supplier makes its real return when it keeps the business for the next model, reusing its machines and know-how. That is why incumbency and re-wins matter so much, and why a supplier that loses a big platform can see its returns fall for years.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA with or without other income. Endurance, Subros, Lumax, Pricol and SPR report higher margins than the excluding-other-income basis; differences reach two points.
  • Economic versus reported. Motherson's "economic" revenue and EBITDA include 100% of joint ventures; its reported figures do not. Do not divide one by the other.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. Exide's widely quoted ₹17,269 crore revenue and ₹1,111 crore profit are standalone; consolidated (with the cell subsidiary) is ₹17,995 crore and ₹860 crore. Tube Investments' consolidated numbers are mostly CG Power. MSWIL reports standalone only.
  • Profit attributable versus total. Profit including minorities overstates what shareholders own at Tube Investments (₹1,118 crore total, about ₹637 crore attributable), Lumax (₹337 crore, ₹279 crore) and Uno Minda (₹1,284 crore, about ₹1,192 crore).
  • Year-ends. Schaeffler India reports on a calendar year; its "FY" figures here are for calendar 2025.
  • One-offs in profit. Bosch's FY26 profit includes a gain on selling a business; Bharat Forge's Q1 FY27 loss of ₹90 crore follows a ₹358 crore exceptional charge; Ramkrishna's FY25 profit of ₹415 crore was inflated by a negative 80% tax rate, so its fall to ₹72 crore in FY26 is not like-for-like; Minda Corp's trailing profit includes a ₹106 crore one-off gain.
  • Pass-through inflation. Revenue and content per vehicle rise with metal prices; margins fall in percentage terms. Ask for volume or net-of-pass-through growth.
  • Order books. Lifetime, peak-annual and multi-decade figures are not comparable, and none is a firm contract.
  • EV share. Battery-electric only, or including hybrids, CNG and non-auto? Rupee figures settle it.
  • Net debt. Strategic stakes, joint-venture investments, leases and short-term loans move it between "net cash" and "net debt".
  • Gross margin. Only proxies (100 − material cost) exist; they overstate gross margin for machinists and should be read as material cost as a share of sales.
  • Per-share data after capital events. Bonus issues (ZF CV 5:1, Motherson 1:2, MSWIL 1:2), QIPs (Belrise, Craftsman, SPR, likely Bharat Forge) and Gabriel's merger scheme change share counts; book value and P/B may be pre-event.
In plain terms: one company, several sets of numbers

The same company can report two revenues, three profits and four margins depending on whether it counts its subsidiaries, its joint ventures, one-off gains or interest on its cash. None of these is wrong, but comparing one company's flattering version with another's conservative version produces false conclusions. Before comparing, put both on the same footing.

How to read an auto component company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth against the customers' production (2 minutes). Pull the quarter's revenue growth and compare it with the production growth of the company's main segments and customers (SIAM or the customers' own numbers). A gap above five points is content or share gain; a negative gap is lost share or mix.
  2. Strip out pass-through and acquisitions. Look for the company's "net of commodity" growth, alloy or copper pass-through, and any acquired revenue. ASK's 52% became 25%; Pricol's 50% was half bought.
  3. EBITDA margin on a consistent basis. Recompute it excluding other income and compare with the same quarter a year ago (not the previous quarter: festive and fiscal-year-end seasonality matter). Then explain the change: metal prices (check LME copper, aluminium and lead for the quarter), wages, mix, utilisation, one-offs.
  4. Material cost as % of sales. A two-point jump with stable selling prices means pass-through is lagging; expect a recovery next quarter if contracts are indexed. A rise without metal inflation signals price-downs or mix erosion.
  5. Segment and channel mix. Note the shares of two-wheeler, passenger, commercial, aftermarket and exports. Watch for rising dependence on a weakening segment or a fading aftermarket (Lumax's slipped from 10% to 8%).
  6. Order wins and EV share. Record new business won (lifetime or peak annual, and which), the EV share of wins and of revenue, and any RFQ pipeline. Compare with the previous four quarters; one big win can distort.
  7. Exports and tariffs. For exporters, read US and Europe revenue separately and any comment on tariff sharing with customers, the Section 232 quota and the US truck cycle.
  8. Below EBITDA. Check depreciation (new plants), interest (debt-funded expansion), tax rate (deferred-tax credits flatter) and exceptional items (restructuring, gains on sales). Recompute profit excluding them.
  9. Balance sheet and cash. At half-years, check receivable and inventory days, net debt on a consistent basis, capex against guidance and any equity raised. Rising debtor days with flat sales is a red flag.
  10. Guidance and governance. Note management's outlook for margins and growth, capex plans, royalty changes (for subsidiaries), related-party transactions and promoter shareholding or pledges. Unexplained changes in promoter holdings (as at Ramkrishna and Gabriel in 2026) deserve a filing-level check.

Red flags, in one list: revenue growing faster than receivables can explain; margins rising while material cost falls only because metal prices dropped; order wins announced as lifetime totals without a peak-annual equivalent; a rising royalty at a subsidiary; profit lifted by other income or tax credits; capex guidance raised in the same quarter as a margin miss; and any unexplained movement in promoter holdings.

The first map shows how unusual FY26 was. Most companies cluster in a band of 10–25% growth and 9–18% margin. Above the band sit Sona and SPR, which combined fast growth with margins around 20% or more. To the right sit the acquirers, Craftsman, Lumax and Pricol, whose growth was bought. Below and to the left sit the companies that had a hard year: Exide (4.4% growth, lithium losses), Ramkrishna (5.1%, US trucks), ZF CV (7.5%, export weakness) and Amara Raja (7.5%, lead costs). Motherson and MSWIL sit at the bottom on margin by business model, not by weakness: MSWIL grew 23%.

The second map is the most important for understanding the sector. It splits the companies into two families. In the upper left are the capital-light assemblers, with margins of 9–14% and returns of 20–39%. In the lower right are the capital-heavy metal-workers, with margins of 15–24% and returns of 6–15%. The foreign-owned subsidiaries and Banco sit at the upper right, combining both. A company moving from one family to the other changes its economics: SPR's acquisitions of interiors businesses lower its margin but may raise its turnover; Craftsman's shift into aluminium did the same; Sona's rail acquisition added capital before it added return.

The third map is the valuation puzzle in one picture. If the market paid for current returns, the dots would rise from left to right. Instead the top-left quadrant, high multiples on modest returns, holds Bharat Forge, Ramkrishna, Sansera, Sona, Craftsman and JBM: companies whose value depends on defence, aerospace, rail, EV or e-bus growth that has barely reached the profit line. The bottom-right quadrant, high returns on low multiples, holds Banco, Fiem, Jamna, Subros and, at the edge, MSWIL and Tenneco: companies the market sees as concentrated on one customer, one segment, one region or one engine-linked product. Either the market is right about their futures, or the bottom-right is where the sector's value lies. The scorecard below helps decide company by company.

Who is winning, in four sentences

The clearest winners of the last two years are the domestic content compounders that combine growth, high returns and a neutral-to-positive EV position: Uno Minda, MSWIL, Endurance, Pricol, Lumax and ASK. The best-positioned for the EV sort is Sona, whose exposure to US EV policy and governance questions after its founder's death has kept its returns and valuation volatile. The metal-workers diversifying into defence, aerospace and rail (Bharat Forge, Sansera, Ramkrishna) are winning orders but not yet profits, and their valuations already assume success. The overlooked group, Banco, Fiem, Jamna and Subros, earns high returns at low multiples because each carries a visible concentration, and each would re-rate if it reduced it.

In plain terms

The winners so far are the Indian companies that keep adding more of their products to each car and motorcycle without needing heavy machinery to do it. The companies the market likes most are not necessarily the most profitable today, but those with a story about defence, aircraft, rail or electric vehicles. A handful of very profitable but cheap companies are cheap because each depends heavily on one customer or one kind of vehicle.

Five reasons for the premium

  • Growth. India's component industry grew about 10% a year from its FY19 peak and 17% from the FY21 trough; global vehicle production has been flat since 2017. An Indian supplier is compounding content and volume at the same time; a global one is fighting for share in a stagnant market.
  • Margins and returns. Lower labour costs, a fragmented domestic vehicle market where no single buyer dictates terms to the extent that the big Western OEMs do, high-margin export niches in precision forging and machining, and the absence of legacy restructuring give Indian suppliers higher margins. Returns on capital of 15–30% compare with single digits for many Western peers.
  • Balance sheets. Fourteen of the 29 have net cash, many after recent equity raises. Global peers such as ZF carry heavy debt from past acquisitions.
  • Scarcity. There are few listed ways to own Bosch, Schaeffler or ZF technology, or EV content in a fast-growing market. Free floats are small (parents hold 60–75%), and domestic institutions own large stakes (DIIs hold 42% of Sona, 33% of Bharat Forge and Craftsman, 31% of Sansera).
  • Domestic money. Indian equities as a whole trade at a premium to global markets, supported by steady inflows from domestic savers. Auto components inherit that premium on top of their own.

Is 20% a year plausible? The sector's earnings grew faster than that from the FY21 trough, helped by recovery, operating leverage and falling interest costs. Going forward, revenue growth of 10–14% (production 6–8%, content 3–5%, some share gains and non-auto) plus modest margin gains would deliver earnings growth in the mid-teens. Reaching 20% for a decade needs either a faster content shift (EVs, electronics, safety), sustained export share gains, or successful diversification into defence, aerospace and rail at higher margins. Some companies will do it; the sector as a whole would be doing something it has not done over a full cycle.

Motherson: the inverse case

Motherson shows what happens when an Indian supplier imports a Western cost base. Its 9.5% EBITDA margin and 13% screener ROCE look much more like its global peers than like the Indian domestic tier-1s, because most of its revenue comes from European and North American plants. It trades at 36 times, below the Indian median but more than twice Magna's 13 times. The market is paying for Indian management of global assets, with the acquisition machine as the growth engine. If that machine slows, Motherson's multiple has the furthest to converge toward global levels.

In plain terms

Indian parts makers are more profitable than their famous Japanese, German and American rivals, and they sell into a market that is still growing. That justifies a higher valuation. But the gap is huge: Indian shares cost about three times as much per rupee of profit. For today's prices to work out, profits need to grow about 20% a year for ten years. Some companies will manage it; the whole sector would need to do better than it ever has across a full cycle.

Technology beyond the battery: software, sensors, lightweighting and the insourcing threat

Electric drive is the biggest technology shift, but not the only one. Where the vehicle's value migrates over the next decade decides whether Indian suppliers become system owners or stay metal-benders.

Five technology trends besides electrification will redistribute value among suppliers. Each creates winners among the 29 and threatens others, and in each, the question is whether Indian companies own the technology, license it, or simply manufacture someone else's design.

The software-defined vehicle

Today a car carries dozens of small electronic control units, each running one function. The industry is moving toward a few powerful central computers ("domain" or "zonal" controllers) that run many functions in software and receive updates over the air. Value migrates from many small boxes and their wiring toward the central computer, its software and its chips. Bosch, Continental, Aptiv and a handful of chip and software companies own that architecture globally. Indian tier-1s mostly supply the parts around it: switches, sensors, lamps, harnesses and clusters. The risk for them is becoming suppliers of commodity hardware into someone else's system. Pricol's move from analog dials to full TFT displays and connected-vehicle units, Uno Minda's controllers and Lumax's telematics with Ituran are steps up the value chain, but the core vehicle computer remains largely foreign.

Zonal architecture also changes the wiring harness. Fewer, shorter, more data-heavy harnesses could reduce copper content per vehicle even as high-voltage harnesses add content in EVs. For MSWIL, the net effect over a decade is uncertain, which is one reason its multiple is lower than its returns would suggest.

Safety electronics and ADAS

Advanced driver-assistance systems (automatic emergency braking, lane-keeping, adaptive cruise control) add cameras, radars, sensors and the computing to interpret them. India's Bharat NCAP ratings and commercial-vehicle safety rules from April 2026 are pushing them into mass vehicles. In trucks, ZF CV supplies the braking and stability systems that ADAS acts through. In cars, the sensors and software come mainly from global suppliers, with Indian companies supplying cabling, housings and mechanical parts. ADAS is a content gain for the industry but, for now, mostly for the foreign-owned or unlisted suppliers.

Lightweighting

Electric vehicles carry heavy batteries, and fuel-economy rules punish weight in conventional ones, so vehicle makers are replacing steel with aluminium in castings, wheels, knuckles and housings. That is good news for India's aluminium casters: Craftsman (aluminium now 59% of revenue), ASK (aluminium lightweighting 45%), Endurance (castings 42%), and Sansera's Swedish aluminium-forging plant. Steel-stamping suppliers such as Belrise and JBM face substitution at the margin, though body structures will remain largely steel in Indian price segments for years.

Hybrids, CNG and other powertrains

The transition is not a straight line from petrol to battery. Strong hybrids, favoured by Maruti and Toyota and given a tax boost when GST 2.0 abolished the cess on them, keep a full engine and exhaust while adding a motor, battery and power electronics: good for engine-parts makers and EV-parts makers at the same time. Sona's ₹640 crore hybrid-driveline win and Tenneco's after-treatment business both benefit. CNG remains large in Indian cars and three-wheelers, supporting Lumax's Greenfuel business. CAFE-III fuel-economy norms, if finalised for FY28, will reward hybrids and EVs and accelerate the decline of pure petrol and diesel powertrains.

Insourcing and Chinese competition

The two largest threats to the EV-gaining side are the vehicle makers and China. EV start-ups and some incumbents build their own battery packs, motors and controllers, partly to control the most valuable part of the vehicle and partly because supplier capability lagged when they started. Chinese component makers bring cost and scale, entering India through licensing and joint ventures with Indian tier-1s, since Press Note 3 restricts direct Chinese investment. If those two forces take the battery and much of the electronics, the EV transition could shrink, not expand, the addressable market for many Indian suppliers. The counter-trend is localisation pressure: PM E-DRIVE's phased-manufacturing rules, the REPM magnet scheme and US and European wariness of Chinese supply chains all push value back toward local or non-Chinese suppliers.

Manufacturing technology and labour

Finally, the factory itself is changing. State minimum-wage increases of 26–40% in 2026 hit labour-intensive assembly in the NCR, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh. Automation, robotics and new plants in lower-cost states are the response, and Sona's new robotics and "physical AI" vertical (July 2026) is an attempt to sell that capability. Suppliers that automate early will protect margins; those that rely on cheap labour will see the advantage that underpins the Indian margin premium erode.

In plain terms

Cars are becoming computers on wheels, lighter and more varied in what powers them. The most valuable new parts, the central computers, sensors and batteries, are mostly designed abroad, and some vehicle makers want to build them themselves. Indian parts makers win where they make the lighter metal parts, the wiring and the displays, and lose where they only assemble someone else's design. Rising wages are also pushing them to automate.

Bull vs bear: the case for the sub-sector, and the case against

The structural story is strong; the price already reflects most of it, and the next two years bring a high base, a cost squeeze and unresolved trade policy.

The bull case

  • Content keeps compounding. Premiumisation, safety mandates (ABS, airbags, truck AC, CV safety electronics), electronics and EV content add four to six points of growth above vehicle production, as FY25 and FY26 showed.
  • A record domestic market with room to grow. India builds 3.5 crore vehicles a year, car ownership is low, and GST 2.0 made small cars and bikes cheaper. Two-wheeler volumes have only just regained their FY19 peak.
  • Structural share gains abroad. China+1 sourcing, Europe's cost problems and new trade agreements with the UK and the EU give exporters a decade of share-gain opportunity; Europe grew 9% in FY26 despite weak production.
  • Diversification is working. Defence (Bharat Forge, ₹11,196 crore book), aerospace and semiconductors (Sansera, ₹5,750 crore backlog), rail (Ramkrishna, Sona) and energy storage reduce dependence on the engine and the truck cycle.
  • Strong balance sheets. Fourteen companies have net cash; recent QIPs have deleveraged Belrise, SPR and Craftsman. Motherson's leverage is the lowest in its history.
  • EV is a net gain for the best-positioned. Kit values three times higher for electricals suppliers, 1.75–2 times for harnesses and 2.5–3 times for thermal systems.

The bear case

  • Valuation. At about 46 times earnings, the sector needs roughly 20% annual profit growth for a decade; global peers trade at 13–16 times. Several stocks trade above 60 times depressed or unproven earnings.
  • A high base and a cost squeeze. H2 FY26's GST boom makes FY27 comparisons hard (forecast growth 8–10%), and Q1 FY27 showed margins narrowing at most assemblers on copper, lead and 26–40% wage increases.
  • The engine's long goodbye. SPR, Sansera, Tenneco, Bosch and Banco depend on combustion products whose terminal value falls as EV penetration rises, and their diversification is still small or low-margin.
  • Tariffs and foreign cycles. A 25% US tariff on half of US-bound parts, an unimplemented quota, the US truck cycle and slower US EV launches all weigh on exporters and on new export order books.
  • The growth content is imported. A widening trade deficit, Chinese control of magnets and cells, and vehicle makers insourcing EV components cap the share of EV value Indian suppliers can capture.
  • Concentration and governance. Many suppliers depend on one or two customers (Maruti, Honda, TVS, Bajaj); foreign parents control royalties and transfer prices; unexplained promoter-holding changes (Ramkrishna, Gabriel) and Sona's family dispute show governance risk is not abstract.

The balance between the two depends on the time horizon. Over five to ten years, the bull case's structural points (content, domestic growth, export share and diversification) are well supported by the evidence in this report. Over the next 12–24 months, the bear case's cyclical and cost points are more immediate, and the valuation leaves little cushion if they bite. The most defensible positions are in companies where the structural story is strongest and the price assumes least: capital-light content compounders with diversified customers, and high-return companies whose discount reflects a concentration they are visibly reducing.

In plain terms

The long-term story for Indian parts makers is good: more content per vehicle, a growing home market, chances to win business abroad and new markets in defence and rail. The short-term problems are a tough comparison after a tax-cut boom, rising wages and metal costs, American tariffs and shares that already cost a lot. Investors need to decide which of those matters more over their own time horizon.
