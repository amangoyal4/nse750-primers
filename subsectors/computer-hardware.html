00

The one-page view: a boom built on someone else's chip

India is buying computing power faster than ever, almost all of it imported, and one listed Indian company sits in the middle of the trade.

The Indian computer-hardware industry is not one business but a stack of them. At the bottom sit chips nobody in India makes: NVIDIA's graphics processors, Intel and AMD's server CPUs, memory from SK hynix, Samsung and Micron. Above them sit the people who turn those chips into working machines: global brands such as Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro, Taiwanese contract manufacturers that build for the hyperscalers, Indian contract assemblers working under production-linked incentives, and a single listed Indian company that designs its own boards, chassis and software and sells complete systems: Netweb Technologies. Around them sit the distributors who move the boxes and the GPU clouds that rent them out by the hour.

This report is about that whole stack as it operates in India. It uses Netweb as its lens because Netweb is the only listed company in the NSE 750 whose business is designing and building servers, supercomputers and AI systems. Everything we can measure about the industry from a listed balance sheet comes through that one company, so the report reads the industry's economics off Netweb's filings and checks them against the dossier's primary sources and a set of clearly marked reference companies: the electronics manufacturers Dixon, Kaynes and Syrma SGS, the distributors Redington and Rashi Peripherals, the GPU cloud E2E Networks, and the global server makers.

Five forces define the industry today. The first is the AI and GPU demand wave: worldwide server revenue grew 52% in the April–June 2026 quarter to $166.3 billion (about ₹15.8 lakh crore at ₹94.9 per dollar, indicative), and GPU servers are now more than half of that. The second is the government, research and defence order book: the IndiaAI Mission (₹10,372 crore), the National Supercomputing Mission and sovereign clouds for agencies such as NIC and CERT-In are the anchor buyers of high-end computing in India, and they buy in lumps. The third is pass-through economics: a GPU-heavy server is mostly NVIDIA's value, so the more AI a builder sells, the lower its gross margin percentage; profit margins then depend on spreading overheads, not on pricing power. The fourth is working capital and funding: the builder pays for GPUs and memory long before the customer pays for the system, so growth eats cash, and the money comes from customer advances, bank lines and equity. The fifth is the supply-side chokepoint: GPU allocation from NVIDIA, a memory price super-cycle, US export controls and global competitors that are now assembling in India under the same incentive schemes.

Netweb's numbers show all five forces at once. Revenue rose 90% to ₹2,184 crore in FY26 and 172% to ₹820 crore in Q1 FY27 (Jun-26), and AI systems went from 15% of revenue in FY25 to 62% in Q1 FY27. Yet gross margin fell from 23.1% in FY25 to 20.9% in FY26, the operating EBITDA margin slipped from about 13.8% to 13.0% (13.3% on the company's adjusted basis), the cash-conversion cycle lengthened from 73 to 96 days, the company moved from ₹124 crore of net cash in March 2026 to ₹200 crore of net debt in June 2026, and it then raised ₹1,200 crore of equity in August 2026. That sequence is the industry in miniature: volume up, percentage margin down, cash absorbed, capital raised.

The market is pricing that miniature as a scarcity. At ₹25,782 crore of market value on 23 October 2026, Netweb trades at about 99 times trailing earnings and 34 times book, against 7–16 times forward earnings for Dell, HPE and Supermicro. The premium is not for a better business model; on gross margin, Netweb sits between Supermicro and the enterprise-heavy global brands. It is for being the only listed Indian company that designs and builds high-end computers at a time when the state wants sovereign compute and prefers to buy it from local suppliers. Whether that premium holds depends on the five forces above, which is why the report is organised around them.

In plain terms

India's appetite for computing is huge and growing, but almost every valuable part inside an Indian-built server is imported. The Indian builder earns its living by designing the system around those parts, assembling it, installing it, keeping it running and, increasingly, financing the gap between paying its suppliers and being paid by its customers. When AI demand surges, its revenue jumps because GPUs are expensive; its profit grows more slowly because most of that price belongs to NVIDIA; and its need for cash grows fastest of all.

Three things follow for anyone reading this industry. First, revenue growth on its own says little; the useful numbers are gross margin, the mix between GPU boxes and design-heavy HPC or private-cloud solutions, and the cash cycle. Second, the order book matters more than the income statement, because one sovereign order can be worth a full year's revenue: Netweb's two "strategic" orders of September 2025 totalled ₹2,184 crore, by coincidence exactly its FY26 revenue. Third, policy is both the moat and the risk. Make-in-India procurement rules and the IT hardware incentive scheme favour local builders today, but the same rules are pulling Dell, HP, Lenovo, Foxconn and Indian contract manufacturers into Indian assembly, and the state's own C-DAC designs servers that Indian EMS firms build.

Every company figure in this report therefore stops at Q1 FY27 (Jun-26), plus the August 2026 equity raise. The final section lists the specific Q2 numbers to check, including how much of the remaining strategic order (about ₹1,195 crore on the dossier's indicative arithmetic) was delivered by September.

01

How a server earns money: from silicon to a signed handover

A server is a box of imported chips. The money is made in choosing them, fitting them together, making them work at scale and standing behind them for five years.

Computer hardware covers a wide range of products, from a ₹40,000 laptop to a ₹4 crore eight-GPU AI server and a ₹100-crore-plus supercomputer. They share a supply chain but not an economic model. Client devices (laptops, desktops, tablets) are a volume business dominated by global brands and Chinese and Taiwanese contract manufacturers; Indian participation is mostly assembly under incentives. Data-centre hardware (general-purpose servers, storage, AI servers, supercomputers and private-cloud stacks) is a project business: fewer units, much higher ticket sizes, a design and integration layer, and long support contracts. This report concentrates on the second, because that is where Indian design content exists and where the listed member operates, but it uses the first to explain imports and policy.

The value chain runs in seven steps. Silicon comes first and is entirely imported: GPUs and accelerators, CPUs, high-bandwidth memory sitting inside the GPU package, system memory and enterprise solid-state drives. In an AI server, silicon is 60–80% of the bill of materials; in a plain server it is 30–50% (indicative). Second come boards, power supplies, cooling, chassis and networking. Netweb designs its own motherboards and chassis and runs a surface-mount line in Faridabad; global brands use Taiwanese ODMs. Third is system design, integration and manufacturing: putting the parts together, burning them in, and testing whole racks. Fourth is software and middleware: cluster managers, schedulers, private-cloud stacks and AI workbenches. Fifth is the channel, direct to government and large enterprises or through distributors and system integrators. Sixth is deployment and multi-year support. Seventh is the end user.

Most of the value sits in Step 1 and leaves the country. An Indian builder's margin is earned in Steps 3 and 5, which is why design content, software and support decide its profitability.

Who captures the profit? NVIDIA first: its data-centre business earns gross margins of roughly 70%, and in a shortage the memory makers capture the next slice; conventional DRAM contract prices rose about 80% quarter-on-quarter in April–June 2026 and about 40% in July–September (TrendForce, as reported). System builders keep a thin layer. Supermicro, the purest global AI-server maker, earned a 10.8% gross margin and a 7.1% operating margin in its year to June 2026. Dell's server division earned an operating margin of about 15% in May–July 2026. Netweb earns a 13–15% EBITDA margin. Distributors keep 2–3%, volume contract manufacturers 3–6%, high-mix contract manufacturers 10–16%. GPU clouds earn 50–77% EBITDA margins but carry all the capital and utilisation risk.

Why does an Indian designer-OEM earn four to six times a distributor's margin on much the same silicon? Three reasons. It adds work the customer would otherwise pay someone else for: designing a cluster for a specific workload, writing or integrating the software, and running it on site. Government and research buyers value domestic design and long support and are allowed, under Make-in-India procurement rules, to prefer local suppliers. And Netweb's ticket sizes, from a few crore to a few hundred crore, are too small for the hyperscaler-style procurement that squeezes global ODMs to 3–6% margins. Each of these reasons is also a vulnerability, which later sections examine.

The node example shows the core arithmetic of the business. Gross profit is ₹15 per ₹100, and after integration, logistics, warranty and overheads the builder keeps about ₹10.5 of EBITDA. Compare a ₹100 crore HPC cluster for a research institute, also illustrative: bought-in silicon and components of about ₹68 crore, gross profit of ₹32 crore, design, software, on-site team and a five-year support provision of ₹14 crore, and EBITDA of ₹18 crore, an 18% margin. The cluster earns almost twice the margin of the GPU box because a larger share of its value is the builder's own work. That gap, between box-like AI deals and solution-like HPC and private-cloud deals, is the single most important thing to understand about the economics of this sub-sector.

In plain terms

The builder of an AI server is closer to a specialist contractor than to a chip company. The expensive materials are bought from one dominant supplier at prices the builder cannot set; what the builder sells is the design, the assembly, the testing, the installation and the promise to fix it. The more the job looks like a complex project rather than a delivery of boxes, the more of the price the builder keeps.

Support is the quiet part of the model. A supercomputer or private cloud runs for five years or more, and the same institution usually buys the next generation from the vendor it trusts to keep the current one running. Netweb's annual report says 302 customers have been with it for more than six years and the average tenure of its top-10 customers is 7.7 years; 75.6% of FY26 revenue came from repeat customers, and 98.9% in Q1 FY27. Sale of services was still small at ₹51.4 crore in FY26 (2.4% of revenue), so support works mainly as a door-opener for the next system rather than as a large annuity. For the reader of quarterly numbers this means the repeat-customer share is a better test of franchise strength than the services line.

02

The size of the pie: a global super-cycle and India's thin slice

The world's server market is running at record levels. India's share is small, hard to measure, and mostly imported.

The global server market has never been larger. IDC's tracker put vendor revenue at $166.3 billion in the April–June 2026 quarter, up 52% on a year earlier and up 36% on the previous quarter; the earlier record was $125.3 billion in October–December 2025. GPU-accelerated servers were $87.4 billion of that, 52.6% of the market. The number of GPU servers shipped actually fell about 11%, but their average selling price rose about 44% to roughly $170,000, because each server now carries more expensive Blackwell-generation GPUs and memory. Even ordinary, non-accelerated servers became a third more expensive on average, almost entirely because memory and SSD prices rose. Annualised, the market is running at about $665 billion, or roughly ₹63 lakh crore at spot rates (indicative).

Two features of the global picture matter for India. First, branded OEMs gained share from the ODMs in 2026: ODM Direct fell from 60.6% to 53.9% of revenue because demand broadened from four or five American hyperscalers to "neoclouds", sovereign buyers and enterprises, who buy from brands rather than straight from Taiwanese factories. India's demand is exactly that kind: sovereign, neocloud and enterprise. Second, the five largest branded OEMs together hold only about 30% of the market, so this is not a market where one brand sets the price; NVIDIA's reference designs do.

The 1Lattice numbers are useful for direction rather than precision. They say AI infrastructure nearly tripled in two years, from ₹4,150 crore in FY24 to ₹11,540 crore in FY26, and could reach ₹28,290 crore by FY30; that HPC grows at a steady 11–12% a year; and that private cloud and hyper-converged infrastructure is the largest pool, at ₹45,670 crore, growing near 20%. Its private-cloud share is much smaller, about 1%, because that market is dominated by software-led stacks from Nutanix, VMware and Red Hat running on global-brand hardware.

In plain terms

Nobody publishes a reliable "India server market" figure. The import statistics are the most solid evidence: in FY26 India brought in about ₹43,000 crore of processing units such as rack and GPU servers (at an approximate average exchange rate) and about ₹1.19 lakh crore of computers of all kinds. Netweb's entire revenue is roughly a twentieth of the first number. The pool is large; the domestic share of it is small.

The PC market sits alongside. India shipped about 15.9 million traditional PCs in calendar 2025, a record, with commercial buyers slightly ahead of consumers; HP, Lenovo and Dell lead, though the vendor shares in circulation may mix periods and are best treated as indicative. Laptops are the largest single line in India's computer imports, $6.73 billion in FY26, and the part of the market that the government's incentive and import-monitoring schemes most directly target. They are relevant here because the same global brands that dominate laptops also sell servers, and because the incentive scheme that covers laptops also covers servers.

For investors, the size discussion comes down to a ratio. Imported processing units of roughly ₹43,000 crore, an IDC-tracked infrastructure market of ₹58,000–63,000 crore a year and an AI-infrastructure pool of ₹11,540 crore (all indicative) are each many times the revenue of India's one listed designer-OEM. Market size is not what limits a domestic builder; the limits are access to GPUs, the share of demand that is open to local design (mainly government and sovereign buyers), and the balance sheet needed to fund large orders. Those limits are the subject of the rest of the report.

03

The AI wave arrives in India: GPUs, data halls and neoclouds

India's AI build-out is smaller than America's but moving fast, and it turned an HPC boutique into an AI-server company in eighteen months.

The first force is demand for AI computing, and its arrival in India is visible in three places: data-centre capacity, GPU clouds and the revenue of the one listed builder. Data-centre capacity is the venue: without power, cooling and floor space, GPUs cannot be installed. PIB puts India's installed capacity at about 1,575 MW in August 2026, up from 375 MW in 2020; a February 2026 PIB backgrounder cited "cloud data centre capacity" of about 1,280 MW and expected four- to five-fold growth by 2030. CBRE expects about 2 GW by the end of 2026 and more than 3 GW by the end of 2028. The bases differ (installed versus IT load, cloud versus all), so the useful reading is the trend: capacity has roughly quadrupled in six years and is expected to triple or quadruple again by 2030.

Very little of the hyperscaler money will reach an Indian system builder directly. Hyperscalers buy custom racks from Taiwanese ODMs or global brands under global contracts, and Netweb's management said on its Q4 FY26 call that it is not targeting hyperscalers because "India is not a hyperscaler-driven market" but "primarily more of a sovereign" one. The hyperscaler build-out matters for a different reason: it creates the powered, liquid-cooled floor space that sovereign buyers, GPU clouds and enterprises then fill with their own hardware, and it competes with them for power, land and GPUs.

A rule of thumb helps to translate megawatts into hardware. One megawatt of AI IT load holds roughly 700–800 Blackwell-class GPUs, worth about ₹350–450 crore of servers and networking at current prices, so every 100 MW of AI capacity represents ₹35,000–45,000 crore of hardware (indicative). That is 15–20 times Netweb's FY26 revenue. Even a modest share of India's AI halls would be a large business for a domestic builder; the question is how much of it is open to one.

The GPU clouds, or "neoclouds", are the second sign of the wave. Yotta, Jio, Tata Communications, CtrlS, Sify, Cyfuture and the listed E2E Networks buy GPU servers and rent them out by the hour to start-ups, research labs, model builders and, through the IndiaAI Mission, subsidised users. E2E's revenue shows how fast the business can grow when GPUs arrive: from ₹70 crore in the quarter to December 2025 to ₹224 crore in Q2 FY27 (Sep-26), when it earned a 77% operating margin and ₹73 crore of profit. E2E has already reported Q2 FY27, which makes it the freshest data point in this report.

In plain terms

India's AI build-out has three layers: global cloud giants building giant halls with their own suppliers, Indian GPU clouds buying servers to rent out, and the government paying for sovereign computing power. Two cautions. First, much of 2026's industry "growth" is price, not volume. Second, management's description of the next leg is a shift "from one-off training projects to continuous, inference-driven enterprise workloads". The rest of the report weighs these crosscurrents.

The sovereign-AI order book: how IndiaAI turns subsidies into server orders Deep dive

The state does not buy most of its AI computing directly. It pays GPU clouds per hour, and the clouds buy the servers. One distributor-routed basket of ₹2,184 crore shows how lumpy that makes a builder's book.

The IndiaAI Mission, approved by the Cabinet in March 2024 with an outlay of ₹10,372 crore (₹10,371.92 crore precisely), is the single most important policy for India's AI hardware market. It has seven pillars: compute, foundation models, datasets (AIKosh), applications, skills, start-up financing and safe and trusted AI. Compute is the largest, at roughly ₹4,563 crore as reported. The design of the compute pillar is what matters for hardware makers. The government does not buy GPUs and run them itself, at least not mainly. It runs tenders to empanel private GPU-cloud providers, who buy and operate the hardware and bid an hourly rate per GPU; the government then subsidises users, so that a start-up or researcher pays about ₹65 per GPU-hour, which PIB describes as roughly one-third of the global average.

That structure splits the risk. The cloud provider bears the capital cost and the utilisation risk. The government bears a subsidy per hour actually used. The hardware vendor is paid when the servers are delivered and installed, by the cloud provider or the distributor that finances it. For a builder like Netweb, the mission therefore creates demand indirectly, through the providers' purchases, and directly where agencies build their own infrastructure: Netweb's milestones include NIC's government-owned on-premise AI cloud on NVIDIA Grace Blackwell "under the IndiaAI Mission" and CERT-In's sovereign cyber-secure compute of 650+ units, both in 2026.

Utilisation of the subsidised channel has been low. By August 2026, 237 projects had used 93.18 lakh GPU-hours, according to PIB. In plain terms

IndiaAI works like a rental scheme. Private companies buy the GPUs and the government pays part of the rent when a researcher or start-up uses one. The hardware maker is paid up front by the company buying the GPUs, so it does well when providers are expanding. Its risk is indirect: if the GPUs are not rented out enough, the providers slow their buying or pay their bills late.

In plain terms

One distributor placed two orders worth as much as Netweb's entire FY26 revenue, for GPU systems tied to India's sovereign AI programme. Those orders drove the revenue jump, the customer concentration, the customer advances and the dip in gross margin. Most of the money should have been delivered by September 2026. The test now is whether new orders of similar size keep arriving once that one is finished.

Two further risks sit inside the sovereign book. One is timing: execution cycles have lengthened from 8–12 weeks to 16–20 weeks because orders are larger, more complex and supply-constrained, so a delay of a few weeks can move hundreds of crore between quarters. The other is the channel: when a sovereign order comes through a distributor, the builder's counterparty is the distributor, which concentrates credit exposure on one balance sheet and leaves the builder one step removed from the end user. Neither has caused a problem so far, but both explain why concentration is the main risk named in Netweb's own annual report: "dependence on a limited number of large customers".

Government, research and defence: the anchor buyer that buys in lumps

Supercomputers, sovereign clouds and secure compute are bought by a small number of public institutions, on budget calendars, with a preference for domestic design, and the state now designs some servers itself.

Before AI, India's high-end computing market was a research market. The National Supercomputing Mission, launched in 2015 by the Department of Science and Technology and MeitY and implemented by C-DAC and IISc with an outlay of about ₹4,500 crore, has so far deployed 40 supercomputers with a combined 68 petaflops of capacity, according to a PIB backgrounder of 4 October 2026, against a target of 50 systems and more than 123 petaflops; it serves more than 16,000 researchers. Thirteen of the systems exceed one petaflop, twelve are between half and one petaflop, and fifteen are smaller, per a secondary summary of the same release. For scale, the world's fastest system in 2026 delivers about 2.19 exaflops, roughly 32 times India's entire mission fleet.

The state as a server designer

The most underappreciated competitor in Indian HPC is the state itself. C-DAC has designed its own Rudra servers, and PIB says they are "indigenously designed… with technology transferred to Indian EMS partners for manufacturing". Secondary summaries put about 6,000 Rudra servers in service and 1,500 more being built. The PARAM Shakti facility at IIT Madras, running PARAM Rudra, was inaugurated in January 2026. Under Make-in-India procurement rules, a C-DAC design manufactured by an Indian electronics contract manufacturer can meet local-content norms without a private designer-OEM in the loop. The EMS partners are not named by PIB, and this report does not attribute the work to any listed company. The implication is clear enough: in HPC, the domestic preference that shields a local builder from Dell and HPE does not shield it from a state-designed alternative.

In plain terms

The government is the most loyal buyer of Indian supercomputers, and it now also designs its own. A research centre that wants a domestic machine can buy one from a private designer or use the state's design built by a contract factory. That keeps the private builders disciplined on price and limits how much of the "Make in India" advantage they can keep.

The moat that is a rule: Make-in-India procurement, PLI and import monitoring

Three policies tilt Indian demand toward local builders. The same policies are pulling the global brands into Indian factories, which narrows the gap they were meant to create.

The third force is policy. Three instruments matter for Indian computer hardware: the Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India) Order, which governs what government buyers may purchase; the Production-Linked Incentive scheme for IT hardware, which pays manufacturers for incremental Indian production; and the Import Management System, which requires authorisation (but not a quota) for imports of laptops, tablets, all-in-ones, ultra-small form-factor PCs and servers. Together they create a preference for local manufacture. None of them makes GPUs, CPUs or memory in India, so the preference applies to the design, assembly and integration layers only.

Public procurement: the real moat

The Make-in-India procurement order classifies suppliers by local content: Class-I at 50% or more, Class-II at 20–50%, and non-local below 20%. For procurements below ₹200 crore, global tenders are generally not allowed and only Class-I and Class-II suppliers may bid, with purchase preference for Class-I. MeitY's notification for electronic products, dated 7 September 2020, specifies how local content is counted for listed electronic items. The dossier could not find a server-specific threshold or any official estimate of how much government spending is ring-fenced this way, so claims that the protected pool is "several thousand crore a year" should be treated as unsourced. Netweb holds Class-I status; its value-add in boards, chassis, software and integration is what lets a GPU-heavy system count as local.

The weakness of a local-content rule is that it measures where value is added, not who owns the design. A global brand that assembles servers in India with locally made boards, power supplies and enclosures can climb toward Class-I or Class-II status on some products. Dell assembles servers at Sriperumbudur through Flex, HPE builds servers with VVDN in Manesar, and Lenovo makes servers in Puducherry and announced AI-server production in India in 2025. As those operations deepen, the moat narrows from "foreign versus Indian" to "designed in India versus assembled in India". GPUs complicate the arithmetic further: if a ₹4 crore AI node is 70% imported GPU, reaching 50% local content requires an unusually high share of the remaining value to be Indian, which is one reason sovereign AI orders have been routed in ways that sit outside strict tender rules.

PLI IT Hardware 2.0: generous on paper, slow in practice

The Cabinet approved PLI IT Hardware 2.0 on 17 May 2023 with ₹17,000 crore over six years, targeting ₹3.35 lakh crore of incremental production, ₹2,430 crore of incremental investment and 75,000 direct jobs. Twenty-seven companies were approved in November 2023, including Dell, HP, Lenovo, Acer, ASUS, Foxconn, Optiemus, Dixon through Padget Electronics, Sahasra, VVDN, Kaynes, Syrma and Netweb. Import monitoring: a speed bump, not a wall

On 3 August 2023 the government restricted imports of laptops, tablets, all-in-ones, ultra-small PCs and servers under HS 8471. In plain terms

India's laptop problem and its server problem are different problems. Laptops still come mostly from China, though less so than often claimed. Servers come mostly from American, Singaporean and South-East Asian supply chains run by Dell, HPE, Supermicro, Lenovo and Taiwanese contract makers. A domestic server builder is therefore not competing with cheap Chinese boxes; it is competing with the world's largest server companies.

Two consequences follow. First, the import-monitoring regime and "trusted source" policies mainly bite on laptops, not servers; the server market was already sourcing outside China. Second, the realistic addressable pool for an Indian designer-OEM is the imported processing-unit bill of roughly ₹43,000 crore (FY26, at ~₹87 per dollar, indicative), about twenty times Netweb's FY26 revenue. That pool is supplied by global brands and ODMs that are themselves now assembling in India.

The data come with caveats. HS 847150 also includes some desktop processing units and other machines, so not all of it is servers. GPU baseboards and modules imported as parts fall under HS 847330 or semiconductor headings and are not in the number, and Netweb imports components rather than finished servers, so its own import bill sits under 8473, 8542 and 8523 rather than 8471. Domestic production under PLI is also excluded. The import figures are therefore a floor for India's spending on finished processing units, not a full market size, and they should be read as a trend rather than a share calculation.

For the reference companies, the import data frame the opportunity. Dixon's ambition in IT hardware, assembling laptops and all-in-ones for HP and others through Padget Electronics, is a bet on replacing part of the $6.73 billion laptop import bill. Distributors such as Redington and Rashi Peripherals move a large share of whatever is imported or locally assembled. Netweb's opportunity sits in the $4.96 billion processing-unit line and in the GPU components behind it. Readers interested in the electronics-manufacturing side, where Dixon, Kaynes and Syrma compete, can find the full treatment in the consumer electronics and EMS report; the telecom equipment report covers the networking gear that sits next to servers in every data hall.

Chokepoints: GPU allocation, a memory super-cycle and export rules

In 2026 the builder that ships is the builder that secured parts. Supply, not demand, sets the pace, and the price of supply is rising.

The fifth force is the supply side. A server builder buys almost all of its value from a handful of suppliers, and in 2026 every one of them is constrained. GPUs come overwhelmingly from NVIDIA, which holds roughly 85–90% of AI-accelerator value and allocates scarce parts among hyperscalers, brands and smaller OEMs. Memory comes from three companies, SK hynix, Samsung and Micron, which are in the middle of a price super-cycle. CPUs come from Intel, AMD and, in Grace systems, NVIDIA itself. Networking, optics, substrates, liquid cooling and power equipment are also short; Dell named semiconductors, substrates, optics, cooling and power as constraints in its May–July 2026 results. CRISIL says Netweb's top three suppliers account for 65–70% of its procurement, with a significant share imported from Taiwan.

GPU allocation

A GPU server cannot be built without a GPU baseboard, and NVIDIA decides who gets them. Allocation favours customers with scale, forecast visibility and the ability to prepay. Netweb's mitigants are its NVIDIA OEM partnership, which covers Grace CPU Superchip and GH200 MGX systems (2023) and the Blackwell generation including GB200, B200, B300 and GB300 systems, and a "chip-agnostic" design approach that also uses AMD and Intel accelerators. CRISIL credits the company with access to NVIDIA, Samsung, AMD and Intel roadmaps nine to twelve months ahead. Still, a builder with under 0.1% of world server revenue is a small customer for NVIDIA, and in a shortage small customers wait. The lengthening of Netweb's execution cycle from 8–12 weeks to 16–20 weeks is partly an allocation effect.

The memory super-cycle

Memory is where 2026's supply shock is sharpest. Conventional DRAM contract prices rose roughly 80% quarter on quarter in April–June 2026 and roughly 40% in July–September, as reported from TrendForce; TrendForce's own forecast for October–December is a further 10–15% for conventional DRAM and 15–20% for NAND flash, with server DRAM still undersupplied and enterprise SSDs the only category where price growth is accelerating. It forecasts high-bandwidth memory prices up about 121% in 2027 and enterprise-SSD bit demand up more than 80% in 2026, with "most incremental volume already committed". IDC's server data show the effect: the average price of a non-accelerated server rose about 33% in a year, from roughly $9,800 to $13,000, mostly from memory and SSD content.

Export controls: benign for India today, a tail risk

The United States controls exports of advanced AI chips, and India's access depends on how those rules are drawn. On 13 January 2025 the outgoing US administration issued the "AI Diffusion" interim final rule, which sorted countries into three tiers; India was in Tier 2, with country caps of roughly 50,000 advanced GPUs for 2025–27 unless purchases ran through validated end users. On 13 May 2025 the Bureau of Industry and Security announced the rule's rescission two days before compliance began. As of August 2026, however, the rule had not been formally removed from the Code of Federal Regulations, BIS was not enforcing it, and a replacement tied to the US AI Action Plan was planned but had not been published. Since 15 January 2026 licence review for certain advanced chips to China and similar destinations has been case-by-case rather than presumption of denial. There are no India-specific caps in force.

For Indian buyers the practical effect today is compliance rather than caps: end-use certification, know-your-customer checks and scrutiny of possible diversion. Supermicro's August 2026 disclosure is a reminder that enforcement is active and that a hardware vendor's own controls are part of the risk. A renewed country-cap regime would limit India's GPU imports and could push NVIDIA's allocation further toward hyperscalers. India's alignment through Pax Silica and its Tier-2 history make that less likely, but it is the clearest single policy event that could cap the market.

In plain terms

An Indian server builder depends on three kinds of goodwill it does not control: NVIDIA deciding to sell it enough GPUs, three memory makers deciding the price of memory, and the US government deciding India can import advanced chips freely. In 2026 all three are tight but workable. Each could change faster than any domestic policy.

Currency and competition

Netweb buys in dollars and sells mostly in rupees. Exports are only about 4–5% of revenue (foreign-exchange earnings were ₹60.7 crore, 2.8% of FY26 revenue) and "not a focus", so US tariffs on Indian goods barely matter to it, but the rupee does: at ₹94.9 per dollar on 19 October 2026, near a record low of about ₹96.8, every imported part costs more in rupees. The company hedges payables; in FY26 it booked an FX loss of ₹18.1 crore in other expenses and a derivative fair-value gain of ₹9.5 crore in other income, the pair behind its adjusted EBITDA. New contracts are bid at current exchange rates, so the risk is concentrated in orders already priced. The last constraint is competition: Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro have 50 to 150 times Netweb's scale, better GPU allocation and global financing, and all are deepening Indian assembly. In enterprise and neocloud deals outside the procurement preference, a domestic builder competes on price, speed and service, which caps the margin it can earn.

Pass-through economics: why a GPU boom compresses gross margin Deep dive

Netweb's gross margin fell by more than two points as AI took over its revenue. Its EBITDA margin barely moved. The reason is operating leverage, not pricing power, and that matters for what happens next.

The third force is the arithmetic of selling someone else's chip. When a builder sells a GPU-dense system, most of the price is the GPU baseboard, which it buys at a price set by NVIDIA and passes through with a small markup. The builder's gross profit in rupees rises with every order, but its gross margin as a percentage of revenue falls, because the pass-through share of revenue rises. The same thing happens when memory prices rise: revenue inflates, gross profit grows less than proportionately, and the gross-margin percentage falls. The global evidence is unambiguous. Supermicro, the purest AI-server builder among the large global players, earned a GAAP gross margin of 10.8% in its year to June 2026 and an operating margin of 7.1%. Dell's server division earned about 15% operating margin at peak AI volumes in May–July 2026, helped by a richer mix of storage and traditional servers.

Netweb's numbers now show the same effect from primary sources. The company reports gross margin, defined as revenue less materials consumed and the change in finished-goods and work-in-progress inventory, in its annual report KPI table: 23.11% in FY25 and 20.85% in FY26, a fall of 2.26 percentage points. On the same definition, the quarterly figures derived from the Q1 FY27 results statement show the shape: 25.6% in Q1 FY26 (June 2025, before the strategic orders), an average of about 19.9% in Q2 and Q3 FY26 as the B200 orders began to ship, 20.4% in Q4 FY26 and 21.2% in Q1 FY27.

In an illustrative GPU-dense AI system, the GPU pass-through is 65–70% of revenue and other materials 15–20%, leaving a gross margin of 12–18%; design, integration and service costs of 3–5% leave an EBITDA margin of roughly 9–13%. Its 20.85% gross margin is roughly double Supermicro's 10.8%, which reflects its HPC and private-cloud content and its smaller, more design-heavy deals. It is below the 25–30% typical of enterprise-heavy server and storage mixes at Dell or HPE (indicative), which reflects its GPU weight. Its 13% EBITDA margin is close to Dell ISG's roughly 15% operating margin and above Lenovo ISG's record 9.1% and Supermicro's 7.1%. The lesson from global peers is that AI-server margins converge downward as GPU content rises and competition for the same NVIDIA reference designs intensifies. A domestic builder can resist that pull only to the extent that its revenue stays weighted toward work the customer cannot buy as a box.

In plain terms

Every AI server maker in the world faces the same squeeze: the GPU is most of the price and someone else owns it. The ones that keep better margins are those that sell more of their own work, such as design, software, storage, installation and support. For an Indian builder, the HPC and private-cloud businesses are what keep the AI business from turning it into a low-margin box shifter.

Working capital is the business: who funds the gap between NVIDIA and the customer Deep dive

A server builder pays for GPUs and memory months before it is paid for the system. In this industry growth consumes cash, and cash comes from customer advances, supplier credit, banks and shareholders, not from margins.

The fourth force is the balance sheet. Every order a hardware builder wins has to be financed from the day it orders parts to the day its customer pays. That gap, measured by the cash-conversion cycle, is the defining financial characteristic of the business. Netweb's cycle, measured by the company on average balances, was 73 days in FY25, 84 days in FY26 and 96 days in Q1 FY27. Management says the cycle "typically operates from 90 to 110 days". In Q1 FY27 it was made up of 78 days of receivables plus 110 days of inventory minus 92 days of payables.

Four decades of cycles: from a denied Cray to a sovereign GPU tender

Indian high-end computing has moved through denial, the PC-brand era, mission-led supercomputing and now an AI super-cycle, each with its own winners.

The industry's history explains its current shape. In the first phase, from the late 1980s, India built supercomputers because it could not buy them. After the United States declined to sell a Cray in 1987, the government set up C-DAC in 1988 and C-DAC delivered PARAM 8000 in 1991. That origin created a lasting institutional preference for domestic high-end computing and a state body that both buys and builds. In the second phase, through the 1990s and 2000s, Indian PC brands such as HCL Infosystems, Wipro and Zenith competed in the domestic market and lost to global brands and Chinese ODM supply. Netweb began in this era: the CMD says he founded the business in 1996, and the company was incorporated in 1999; it deployed a supercomputer at IMSc Chennai in 2004 and PARAM Yuva II with C-DAC in 2013.

The third phase, from 2015 to 2022, was the mission and Make-in-India turn. The National Supercomputing Mission (2015), the procurement order (2017, revised 2020) and the first IT hardware PLI (2021) created a protected, growing demand pool for domestic builders. Netweb commissioned its Faridabad manufacturing facility in 2019, qualified for the IT-hardware PLI in 2021 and the telecom PLI in 2022, and grew revenue from ₹143 crore in FY21 to ₹445 crore in FY23. The fourth phase began in 2023 with the AI shock: the ChatGPT-driven GPU shortage, India's laptop and server import controls, PLI 2.0, AIRAWAT, and Netweb's IPO in July 2023 at ₹500 a share. It has since been amplified by the IndiaAI Mission, Blackwell, the memory super-cycle and the sovereign orders of September 2025.

Reading a hardware maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain an Indian server and AI-systems builder. Most are disclosed only in presentations, annual-report notes and call transcripts, and several are defined in ways that trap the unwary.

A hardware builder's income statement is the last place its story shows up. Orders are won months before they become revenue; gross margin depends on what was in the order; cash depends on who paid first. The right way to read the business is as an equation that starts with orders and ends with cash, with each term mapped to a disclosed KPI. Some terms management controls: which tenders it bids for, how it prices new bids, how many engineers it employs, how much inventory it buys ahead. Others it does not: NVIDIA's prices and allocation, memory prices, the exchange rate, the timing of government budgets and the mix of orders the market offers it.

The term most investors watch (revenue) is the one most driven by things outside the company: the timing of sovereign orders and the price of GPUs and memory.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the terms interact. Take an illustrative builder that starts the year with an order book of ₹2,500 crore, wins ₹3,000 crore of new orders and ends with ₹2,000 crore in hand. It executes ₹3,500 crore of revenue. At a 21% gross margin it earns ₹735 crore of gross profit. Its operating costs are ₹262.5 crore, or 7.5% of revenue, leaving ₹472.5 crore of EBITDA, a 13.5% margin. Now the cash: revenue grew by ₹1,300 crore over the prior year, and at a 96-day cash cycle that growth ties up about ₹342 crore of extra working capital (₹1,300 crore × 96 ÷ 365). After roughly ₹115 crore of tax, operating cash flow is only about ₹15 crore, and after ₹20 crore of capex, free cash flow is slightly negative. A business reporting a 13.5% margin and record profits can generate no free cash in a growth year. That is not a sign of trouble; it is how the model works.

In plain terms

Profit in this business arrives when a system ships; the cash to pay for it leaves when the parts are ordered. In a year when sales jump, almost all the profit is reinvested in stock and unpaid invoices. Reading the KPIs in the right order, from orders to margin to cash, tells you whether that reinvestment is healthy or a warning.

1. Revenue mix: six product lines, three pillars

What it is. The split of revenue between product lines. Netweb reports six: AI systems, HPC and supercomputing, private cloud and hyper-converged infrastructure, high-performance storage, data-centre servers, and software, services, spares and others. Analysts, and management on calls, group them into three pillars (AI, HPC, private cloud) plus a small remainder. Formula. Segment revenue ÷ total revenue from operations. Worked example. In FY26 AI systems were ₹947.8 crore of ₹2,183.6 crore, or 43.4%; HPC ₹530.8 crore (24.3%); private cloud and HCI ₹517.0 crore (23.7%); storage ₹32.8 crore, data-centre servers ₹32.1 crore and software, services and spares ₹123.2 crore (together 8.6%). In Q1 FY27 AI rose to ₹510.6 crore of ₹819.7 crore, or 62.3%.

2. Order book, L1 and order-book-to-revenue

What it is. Netweb discloses three layers of forward demand. The order book is firm purchase orders not yet executed. Order-book coverage = order book ÷ trailing twelve-month revenue. Worked example. At 30 June 2026 the order book was ₹2,506.9 crore and L1 ₹848.0 crore, a total of ₹3,354.9 crore; trailing revenue was ₹2,702 crore. Coverage was 0.93 times on the order book alone and 1.24 times including L1. With execution cycles of 16–20 weeks, the firm book covers roughly four to five months of revenue at the current run-rate (indicative).

3. Pipeline conversion

What it is. The share of the bid pipeline that becomes firm orders, and how long that takes. Formula. Orders won from a pipeline cohort ÷ that cohort's value, over a stated period. Worked example. Management guides about 60% conversion over 18–24 months. Applied to the June 2026 pipeline of ₹10,410 crore, that implies about ₹6,250 crore of orders over 18–24 months, or roughly ₹3,100–4,200 crore a year (illustrative arithmetic). Adding the ₹3,355 crore order book and L1, the visible book supports FY27 revenue in the ₹3,300–4,000 crore range if execution holds; this is an analyst-style estimate, not guidance, and an analyst on the Q1 FY27 call referred to ₹4,000 crore without contradiction. What drives it. Win rates in tenders, the share of pipeline that is sovereign versus enterprise, and whether projects are delayed or cancelled. Benchmark. Above 60% is good, 40–60% average, below 40% weak; Netweb's 60% is a guidance figure, not an audited history. Definition trap. Conversion percentages are only meaningful if the pipeline definition is stable; it changed in June 2026. Investors should track orders actually announced and the change in the order book plus revenue, which gives orders booked in the quarter: Q1 FY27 orders booked ≈ closing book ₹2,506.9 crore − opening ₹2,097.6 crore + revenue ₹819.7 crore ≈ ₹1,229 crore (indicative).

4. Gross margin and GPU-mix dilution

What it is. The share of revenue left after materials. Formula. (Revenue − materials consumed − change in finished-goods and work-in-progress inventory) ÷ revenue; this is the company's own definition in its annual report. Worked example. FY26 revenue ₹2,183.6 crore minus cost of goods ₹1,728.3 crore leaves ₹455.3 crore, a 20.85% gross margin, against 23.11% in FY25. Q1 FY27: ₹819.7 crore minus ₹646.2 crore leaves ₹173.5 crore, 21.2%. What drives it. Mainly the share of GPU-heavy AI systems in revenue (each extra ten points of AI share costs roughly one point of gross margin on the illustrative mix model in Section 9), then component prices, FX and pricing on new bids. Benchmark. For a designer-OEM, above 25% is good, 18–25% average and below 18% weak; for a pure AI box, above 15% is good. Netweb is average. Comparison. Supermicro 10.8% (FY26); Netweb 20.85%; enterprise-heavy global mixes 25–30% (indicative). The Indian reference companies do not disclose comparable gross margins on the same definition. Definition trap. Gross margin is not in the company's quarterly presentation; quarterly figures must be derived from the results statement, and screener's "material cost" line may treat inventory changes differently. Never compare a "gross margin" across companies without checking whether it subtracts only materials or also manufacturing labour and freight.

5. EBITDA margin: reported versus adjusted

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and other income. Formula. Netweb's "operating EBITDA" = profit before tax + depreciation + finance cost − other income. Worked example. FY26: ₹276.5 crore PBT + ₹14.2 crore D&A + ₹13.0 crore finance cost − ₹18.8 crore other income = ₹284.8 crore, 13.04% of revenue. FY25 was ₹159.0 crore, 13.84%. The margin fell by 0.8 points in FY26. The company also reports an "adjusted operating EBITDA" of ₹290.1 crore (13.3%) that adds back ₹5.24 crore of hedging gains booked in other income, because the matching FX losses on payables sit in operating expenses.

6. Working-capital days and the cash-conversion cycle

What it is. How long cash is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Formula. Receivable days = average receivables × 365 ÷ revenue; inventory days = average inventory × 365 ÷ revenue (the company uses revenue as the base); payable days = average payables × 365 ÷ revenue; cash-conversion cycle = receivable + inventory − payable days. Worked example. Q1 FY27: 78 + 110 − 92 = 96 days. At ₹3,300 crore of annual revenue, each 10 days of cycle ties up about ₹90 crore of cash (₹3,300 crore × 10 ÷ 365). What drives it. Customer payment terms and advances, inventory policy (buying memory ahead of price rises), supplier credit and the timing of deliveries within a quarter. Benchmark. Cycle below 60 days good, 60–100 days average, above 100 days weak; receivables below 75 days good; inventory below 60 days good; payables above 90 days good. Netweb is average on the cycle, good on payables, weak on inventory. Comparison. On year-end balances: Dixon −7, Redington 34, Syrma 48, Rashi 60, Netweb 121, Kaynes 193 days. Definition trap. Company figures use average balances; screener and CRISIL use year-end balances, which are much higher after a Q4 delivery surge (Netweb FY26: 84 days average, 121 days year-end; CRISIL gross current assets 268 days). Always say which basis.

In plain terms

Netweb's cash is tied up for about three months on every order: it holds parts for nearly four months, waits about two and a half months to be paid, and pays its own suppliers after about three. Ten extra days on that cycle locks up roughly ₹90 crore at today's sales. The cycle, not the margin, decides how much new money the company needs to grow.

7. Cash conversion: operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA

What it is. How much of operating profit turns into operating cash. Formula. Cash flow from operations ÷ operating EBITDA. Worked example. FY26: ₹171.5 crore ÷ ₹284.8 crore = 60%. FY25: −₹13.2 crore ÷ ₹159.0 crore = −8%. What drives it. The change in working capital, which in turn depends on revenue growth, the cash cycle and, above all, customer advances; in FY26 a ₹410 crore advance on the strategic basket turned what would have been an outflow into a positive number. Benchmark. Above 70% over three years is good, 40–70% average, below 40% weak. Netweb's FY25–FY26 average is weak; FY26 alone is average. Comparison. Supermicro's FY26 operating cash flow was −$6.8 billion against $2.2 billion of net income, deeply negative; E2E's FY26 free cash flow was −₹1,140 crore after GPU capex.

8. R&D as a share of revenue

What it is. Spending on research and development, which for a designer-OEM covers board and chassis design, system software and new platforms. Formula. R&D expenditure (revenue plus capital) ÷ revenue. Worked example. The FY26 Board's report gives R&D expenditure of ₹13.14 crore including capital spending, about 0.6% of revenue. The R&D team numbered 110 at 30 June 2026 per the annual report (19.3% of the 569-person workforce), or 125 per the Q1 FY27 presentation; total employee cost was ₹80.3 crore, 3.7% of revenue. What drives it. New platform generations (Blackwell to Rubin), software products (Tyrone Cluster Manager, Kubyts, Skylus), and new areas such as physical AI and quantum simulators. Benchmark. Above 3% good, 1.5–3% average, below 1.5% weak. On the statutory number Netweb is weak. Comparison. Not disclosed on a comparable basis by the reference set. Definition trap. CRISIL's rationale says Netweb invests "2–3% of annual revenues" in R&D, which conflicts with the statutory ₹13.14 crore. The likely reconciliation is that much engineering work sits in employee cost rather than the narrowly defined R&D line. Use the statutory figure and say so; do not quote 2–3% without attributing it to CRISIL.

9. Customer concentration

What it is. The share of revenue from the largest customers. Formula. Revenue from the top five (or ten) customers ÷ total revenue; also the number of customers above 10% of revenue (an accounting-standard disclosure). Worked example. Top-10 share 76.4% and top-5 62.2% in FY26; 87.7% and 81.2% in Q1 FY27. Three customers each exceeded 10% of FY26 revenue, against two in FY25. If one ₹500 crore customer delays an order, a quarter of ₹820 crore loses 60% of its revenue. What drives it. The size of sovereign and strategic orders relative to the base business. Benchmark. Top-10 below 50% good, 50–70% average, above 70% weak. Netweb is weak and getting weaker through Q1 FY27. Comparison. CRISIL's own estimate is top-5 at 65–70%. Definition trap. Repeat-customer revenue (98.9% in Q1 FY27) sounds like stickiness but here mostly reflects one large repeat order. And a distributor that channels a sovereign order counts as one customer even though the end user is the state.

Concentration matters for valuation as well as for risk. A business whose top five customers provide four-fifths of revenue deserves a higher discount rate than one with a broad base, because a single cancellation or payment delay changes the year. The reference companies show the range: distributors such as Redington and Rashi sell to tens of thousands of resellers, while contract manufacturers such as Kaynes and Syrma typically depend on a few dozen brand customers. A designer-OEM sits nearer the contract manufacturers, and the sovereign AI cycle has pushed it further in that direction. The figure to watch is not the number of new customers, which rose to 131 in FY26, but the revenue share outside the top ten, which fell from about 34% in FY25 to about 12% in Q1 FY27.

10. Government versus enterprise share

What it is. The share of business from government, public-sector, research and defence buyers. Formula. Government revenue ÷ total. Worked example. The FY26 annual report shows government at 42.8% and non-government at 57.2%, on its KPI page for "customers"; the CMD described it as "50% government, 50% enterprise" on the Q4 FY26 call. What drives it. Mission funding (NSM, IndiaAI), budget timing and procurement rules. Benchmark. 30–50% is healthy (an anchor, not a dependence), 50–70% average, above 70% weak. Netweb is in the healthy band. Definition trap. The annual report's KPI page does not make explicit whether the split is by revenue or by number of customers; and sovereign orders routed through a private distributor count as non-government. The economic exposure to state spending is therefore higher than 42.8%.

11. PLI incentive accrued

What it is. Income from the production-linked incentive scheme. Formula. PLI income ÷ revenue. Worked example. FY25: ₹5.94 crore on ₹1,149 crore of revenue, about 0.5%, booked as other operating revenue. FY26: nil, with no PLI receivable at year-end, despite Netweb being an approved PLI 2.0 beneficiary for servers since 24 November 2023. What drives it. Eligible incremental sales over a base year, the incentive rate (about 5% on average) and the timing of government approval of claims. Benchmark. For eligible manufacturers, 1–4% of revenue is typical; for Netweb it is immaterial. Definition trap. Some companies book PLI as other operating revenue (inside EBITDA), others as other income (outside it). A jump in PLI can flatter EBITDA margin without any operational change; a nil year, as at Netweb in FY26, deserves an explanation.

In plain terms

The incentive scheme that headlines say favours Indian server makers paid Netweb about ₹6 crore in FY25 and nothing in FY26. For this company the government's help comes through what it buys, not what it pays out in incentives.

12. Capacity utilisation and asset turnover

What it is. How much of the plant's capability is being used, and how much revenue each rupee of fixed assets generates. Formula. Utilisation = output ÷ capacity (management estimate); gross fixed-asset turnover = revenue ÷ gross fixed assets. Worked example. The CMD put utilisation at "around 65% to 70%" on the Q4 FY26 call and said the existing facilities can support "₹3,000-plus crores" of turnover without major capex. Trailing revenue of ₹2,702 crore is already about 90% of that comfort level. Gross fixed-asset turnover was 33 times in FY26 and about 44 times annualised in Q1 FY27; net fixed assets were only ₹60.9 crore at June 2026. What drives it. Revenue and the density of systems built (a GPU rack is worth many times a CPU server per square metre). Benchmark. For an assembler, 70–85% utilisation is healthy; for a designer-OEM, the constraint is engineers and parts more than floor space. Definition trap. "Capacity" here is not a disclosed physical figure; the company calls itself "capability-driven". A capex announcement for a new platform (management mentioned NVIDIA's Vera Rubin) would be the signal that revenue is approaching the plant's real limit.

13. Return on capital employed

What it is. Operating profit relative to the capital tied up in the business. Formula. EBIT ÷ average capital employed (equity plus debt, less cash on some definitions). Worked example. FY26 ROCE was 37.5% on the company's basis (capital employed ₹1,005.5 crore at March 2026) and 48.2% annualised in Q1 FY27. What drives it. Margin times capital turnover; in this model, working capital is almost all of the capital, so the cash cycle matters as much as the margin. Benchmark. Above 30% good, 18–30% average, below 18% weak.

The anatomy also shows which levers move the result most. In plain terms

On a ₹500 crore AI order, about ₹345 crore goes to NVIDIA, ₹75 crore to other suppliers, ₹25 crore to the builder's own work, ₹13 crore to banks and depreciation and ₹11 crore to tax. 17. How to read a hardware maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its year-on-year growth (minutes 0–1). Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter; government seasonality and lumpy sovereign deliveries make sequential comparisons misleading. For Q2 FY27 the base is ₹304 crore.
  2. The AI share of revenue (minute 1). From the presentation's offering split. Rising AI share means bigger revenue and lower gross margin; falling AI share means the opposite. Compare with the order-book mix (about 40–45% AI at June 2026).
  3. Gross margin, derived (minutes 1–3). Revenue minus materials consumed minus the change in inventories, from the results statement. Below 19% would be a new low; above 22% would suggest the HPC and cloud mix is returning.
  4. Operating cost ratio (minute 3). Employee cost plus other expenses as a share of revenue. If it rises while revenue is flat, margin pressure is coming.
  5. Operating EBITDA margin, reported and adjusted (minute 4). Check against the 13–14% guide and the 11% CRISIL trigger; note any hedge adjustment.
  6. Order book, L1 and orders booked (minutes 4–5). Closing book minus opening book plus revenue gives orders booked in the quarter. A book that falls while revenue is high means the company is eating its backlog.
  7. Customer concentration (minute 6). Top-5 and top-10 shares; whether the number of customers above 10% has changed.
  8. Working-capital days and net cash (minutes 6–8). Receivable, inventory and payable days; the cash cycle; customer advances; borrowings and free cash. Red flags: receivables above 110 days, inventory above 120 days, or net debt returning despite the equity raise.
  9. Finance cost and other income (minute 8). Finance cost rose from ₹1 crore to ₹12 crore a quarter in a year; after the QIP it should fall and other income should rise as surplus cash earns interest.
  10. The call (minutes 9–10). Listen for three things: any new large order and its channel, any change in the 13–14% margin band or the 60% conversion guide, and comments on GPU allocation and memory prices. Note whether management gives or withholds revenue guidance.

The checklist is ordered so that each step explains the next: revenue and mix set gross margin, gross margin and costs set EBITDA, the order book says whether revenue will continue, concentration says how fragile it is, and working capital says what it costs in cash. A quarter in which revenue, margin and cash all improve at once is rare in this business; when revenue jumps, expect working capital to absorb the profit, and judge management by whether the cash cycle returns to the 90–110 day band it describes as normal.

Technology and disruption: racks, liquid, inference and the state's own chips

Every NVIDIA generation changes what a builder must be good at. The shift to rack-scale systems narrows differentiation; the shift to inference and agentic AI may widen it again.

Six technology shifts matter for the Indian server industry. The first is rack-scale AI. NVIDIA's GB200 and GB300 NVL72 systems are designed as whole racks, with NVLink switch trays and reference designs (MGX) that every builder follows. When the rack is specified by the chip vendor, the builder's differentiation narrows to integration, liquid cooling, deployment speed and service. Netweb has an NVIDIA OEM partnership for GB200 and Grace systems and launched Make-in-India GB200 systems in February 2026, which is necessary but not differentiating; Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro have the same access, earlier allocation and global references.

The second is power density and cooling. A Blackwell rack draws roughly 120–140 kW and needs direct liquid cooling, while most Indian colocation halls were designed for 6–15 kW per rack. Netweb's new 15,000 square-foot dense-GPU line supports more than 150 kW per rack, and it has a partnership with Vertiv for liquid-cooled AI systems. The constraint for India's AI build-out is therefore as much the data hall as the server: GPUs ordered for halls that are not ready sit in inventory. That links the hardware cycle directly to the data-centre construction cycle covered in Section 3.

The third is the product cadence. NVIDIA now releases a major generation roughly every year: Hopper, Blackwell, Blackwell Ultra and then Rubin (Vera Rubin) in 2026–27. The fourth is the shift from training to inference. Training giant models needs dense GPU clusters; serving them to users, and running AI agents, needs more, smaller and cheaper inference systems, edge devices and, according to TrendForce, more CPUs: the ratio of CPUs to accelerators is moving from one to eight toward one to two or one to one under agentic AI, and server-CPU shipments are forecast to rise 31% in 2026 and more than 40% in 2027. That shift could favour builders with a general-purpose and HPC heritage; Netweb's "Spark" personal petascale AI system and its edge products are aimed at it. It also favours volume, which is where global brands and Indian contract assemblers are strongest.

The fifth is software. Netweb's software stack (Tyrone Cluster Manager, the Kubyts container platform launched in 2023, Skylus and Skylus.ai for private cloud and AI workspaces, GPU aggregation tools) is what turns hardware into a solution. Broadcom's repricing of VMware has pushed enterprises toward container platforms and alternative private-cloud stacks, which management cites as an opportunity. Software is still a small share of revenue (the software, services and spares line was 5.6% in FY26), and the competition is global; the software products report covers India's listed software companies. The sixth is long-dated: domestic silicon. C-DAC's indigenous processors, Semicon India 2.0's ₹1,27,500 crore programme and the electronics-component incentive scheme could, over five to ten years, localise memory modules, SSD assembly, PCBs and packaging, raising local content for Indian builders. None of it will make leading-edge GPUs in India within the forecast horizon.

In plain terms

The more the chip maker designs the whole rack, the less room a builder has to stand out, and the more it competes on speed, cooling, installation and service. The move to running AI models for millions of users, rather than training them, may bring back demand for the kind of flexible, CPU-rich systems that Indian research computing grew up on. Netweb is building for both, but neither shift is under its control.

Two newer areas carry option value but no revenue guidance. Netweb has started research on quantum simulators and emulators and on physical-AI platforms such as digital twins for manufacturing and defence. Chinese open-weight models, which lower the cost of training, could delay some enterprise hardware buying; management argues they widen the market by making AI affordable to more Indian enterprises. Neither is likely to change the FY27 numbers. The technology risks that could change them are nearer: a delay in liquid-cooled hall capacity, a Rubin transition that strands Blackwell inventory, or a shift of sovereign demand from owned hardware to rented cloud capacity.

Bull versus bear: sovereign scarcity against a cyclical box business

The bull case rests on India's sovereign compute ambitions and a domestic preference that only one listed company can fully use. The bear case rests on the economics every AI-server maker shares.

The bull case

  • Sovereign demand is policy-backed and growing: IndiaAI's GPU pool rose from 18,417 to 45,000+ in about eighteen months, with a stated ambition of 100,000; NSM 2.0 aims at pre-exascale and exascale systems by 2028; NIC and CERT-In are building their own sovereign clouds.
  • Domestic preference is real: Make-in-India procurement favours Class-I local suppliers, and Netweb is the only listed designer-OEM with Class-I status, HPC references and an NVIDIA OEM partnership.
  • Funding is solved: ₹1,200 Cr of equity and ₹2,420 Cr of bank lines can finance roughly ₹13,000 Cr of revenue at a 96-day cycle (illustrative), five times FY26.
  • Margins have held: EBITDA stayed at 13–15% through a quadrupling of AI revenue, and the 40–45% AI share of the order book points to some gross-margin recovery.
  • Inference and agentic AI broaden demand toward CPU-rich and general-purpose systems, the company's heritage.

The bear case

  • One basket made the year: ₹2,184 Cr of strategic orders from one distributor drove FY26–Q1 FY27; top-5 customers were 81% of Q1 revenue, and replacing that volume is unproven.
  • Pass-through economics: gross margin fell from 23.1% to 20.9%; EBITDA held only through operating leverage, which reverses if revenue plateaus while headcount grows.
  • Cash, not profit: true FY26 operating cash flow was ₹171.5 Cr (60% of EBITDA), helped by a ₹410 Cr advance; the cycle lengthened to 96 days and the company needed equity.
  • The moat is a rule: global brands are localising, C-DAC's own Rudra servers are built by EMS firms, and the state increasingly rents GPU-hours rather than buying hardware.
  • Valuation: about 99× trailing earnings against 7–16× forward for Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro; QIP buyers at ₹4,790 are about 9.5% under water.

Both cases are about the same few numbers. The order book plus L1 of ₹3,355 crore at June 2026 and the ₹10,410 crore pipeline support FY27 revenue in roughly the ₹3,300–4,000 crore range if execution holds, an illustrative analyst-style range rather than guidance. In plain terms

The optimist sees India deciding to build its own AI computing and one listed company best placed to supply it. The pessimist sees a business that buys most of what it sells from NVIDIA, needs more cash every time it grows, depends on a few huge orders and is priced as if none of that mattered. Both can be right for a while; the next four quarters' order flow, margins and cash cycle will show which matters more.

The macro backdrop adds to both sides. The rupee at about ₹94.9 per dollar, near a record low, raises the rupee cost of every imported GPU; RBI's repo rate was raised to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which lifts the cost of working-capital lines; the West Asia crisis since 28 February 2026 has disrupted freight and energy prices; and US tariffs (10% under Section 301 on top of MFN rates since July 2026, under legal challenge) barely touch a business with exports of 4–5%. On balance, macro conditions raise costs at the margin but do not change the demand drivers, which are policy and AI capital spending rather than the domestic consumer.
