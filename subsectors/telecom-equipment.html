00

The one-page view: four companies, two opposite cycles

Fibre is in the strongest boom in a decade; telecom gear is waiting for the government's next order. One sector label covers both.

"Telecom equipment" in the NSE 750 is four companies that share a customer list but not a business. HFCL and Sterlite Technologies (STL) draw glass into optical fibre, wrap it into cable and, increasingly, sell the connectors that plug it into AI data centres. Tejas Networks designs the boxes that light the fibre: optical transport, broadband access and 4G/5G radios. ITI, owned by the government since 1948, assembles equipment and runs BharatNet construction contracts. In 2026 these businesses sit in opposite phases of their cycles, and averaging them hides more than it shows.

Start with the money. Together the four reported FY26 revenue of ₹12,981 crore, down 37% from ₹20,600 crore in FY25. That fall is entirely one company: Tejas billed ₹8,923 crore in FY25 while shipping radios for BSNL's indigenous 4G network and ₹1,103 crore in FY26 after the shipments ended. Strip Tejas out and the other three grew 1.7%. Combined operating profit (screener basis, before other income) was ₹715 crore, a 5.5% margin, and reported profit after tax was a loss of ₹231 crore. Even that figure flatters the group, because ITI's ₹293 crore profit came from an exceptional gain of about ₹832 crore on selling land and buildings in Bengaluru.

Then look at the last two quarters. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) HFCL and STL together grew revenue 102% year on year to ₹3,825 crore and lifted their operating margin from 8.5% to 20.9%. HFCL's Q2 FY27 (July–September) revenue rose another 33% on the quarter to ₹2,542 crore, at a company-reported EBITDA margin of 25.9%. Over the same period Tejas lost ₹202 crore and ₹207 crore, and ITI lost ₹32 crore in Q1 with its auditor refusing to give a conclusion on the numbers. The sub-sector's trailing-twelve-month (TTM) operating profit of ₹2,009 crore is made up of ₹2,415 crore from the fibre makers and a loss of ₹406 crore from the rest.

Five forces explain almost everything in this report. First, the AI data-centre fibre super-cycle. After a glut in 2023–25 that halved Chinese fibre prices, AI clusters began consuming fibre at a rate the industry had not planned for. TrendForce estimates 2026 demand at 577 million fibre-kilometres, a 180 million shortfall, 92% industry utilisation and spot prices 2.8–5 times 2025 levels, with the shortage lasting to at least 2028. HFCL and STL now sell most of their output abroad, much of it to US hyperscalers. Second, the BSNL indigenous 4G campaign and its cliff. Tejas built the radios for about one lakh BSNL sites, then lost seven-eighths of its revenue when the order finished; an add-on order for roughly 20,000 more sites is now shipping, and there is still no 5G order. Third, BharatNet and the government as customer. The ₹1.39 lakh crore Amended BharatNet programme, with 16 packages and ten-year maintenance contracts, is the domestic floor for fibre demand, but government work comes with slow payment and thin margins. Fourth, ITI, a state company kept going by grants and land sales, whose valuation has little to do with its earnings. Fifth, trade walls: US tariffs (10% Section 301 on Indian goods against 12.5% on China and Vietnam), EU anti-dumping and countervailing duties on Indian cable, and India's own Trusted Source rules that keep Chinese gear out of telecom networks.

In plain terms

Two of these companies make glass threads that the AI boom suddenly cannot get enough of, and their profits have jumped four- to six-fold in a year. The other two make or install network equipment for the government, and their revenue depends on when BSNL and BharatNet place the next order. The sector average is meaningless: read HFCL and STL as commodity manufacturers at the top of a price cycle, and read Tejas and ITI as government contractors between orders.

The tension for an investor is timing. The fibre shortage is real and well documented, but every previous fibre shortage, including 2017–18, produced a glut within about two years, and the capacity now being announced (HFCL's expansion to 43.1 million fkm of fibre by July 2028, STL's plan to add 50%, Corning's roughly $2 billion of 2026 capex, YOFC's equity raise) is scheduled to arrive right when TrendForce expects the shortage to end. On the equipment side the question is whether BSNL's next phase and any 5G upgrade arrive before Tejas's balance sheet runs out of room. The rest of this report explains how each business makes money, what each number means, and what to watch to tell which of these outcomes is coming.

01

Glass, silicon and tenders: how the money is made

Three value chains run through this sub-sector. Each one captures value at a different link, and each has a different enemy.

The fibre chain starts with chemistry. Silicon tetrachloride and germanium tetrachloride are burned in oxygen to deposit ultra-pure glass soot on a rod or inside a quartz tube; the soot is sintered into a solid glass cylinder called a preform, the size of a person's leg. The preform is the scarce, capital-heavy and patent-heavy step: it takes 18–24 months to add preform capacity, and HFCL's management says preform is 65–70% of the cost of a fibre and about 45% of the cost of a cable. The preform is then heated at the top of a draw tower and pulled into a strand 125 microns wide at 2,000–3,000 metres a minute, coated in UV-cured acrylate, and wound on spools. A cable plant colours the fibres, places them in loose tubes or flat ribbons, adds strength members and an HDPE sheath, and ships cable drums. A connectivity plant cuts cable into pre-terminated assemblies with multi-fibre connectors for data-centre racks.

Each fibre maker chooses how much of this chain to own. STL is integrated from preform to connectivity, with glass and fibre at Shendra in Maharashtra, cable plants at Waluj, Aurangabad, Silvassa and Dadra, a cable plant in Dello, Italy, and a cable and interconnect plant at Lugoff, South Carolina. HFCL draws fibre and makes cable at its Indian plants, buys its preform today, and plans to make its own from October 2028; management says in-house preform is at least 30% cheaper than buying it, which would cut raw-material cost by 10–12%. Most other Indian cablers (Birla Cable, Vindhya Telelinks, Aksh, Polycab's fibre line) buy fibre, which leaves them squeezed between fibre suppliers and cable buyers when prices move.

The second chain is silicon to network. Tejas designs optical transport systems (DWDM, OTN and packet-optical gear that moves 100G–800G signals over long distances), GPON broadband access equipment and IP routers, and 4G/5G radio access network (RAN) equipment. Almost all the chips inside (merchant silicon from Broadcom and Marvell, FPGAs, RF front-ends, optical modules) are imported and priced in dollars. What Tejas owns is the hardware design, the firmware, the network-management software and 748 patent filings at September 2026. Manufacturing goes to contract electronics makers; for BSNL 4G, TCS integrated Tejas radios with a core network from the state research lab C-DOT, and ITI assembled part of the hardware.

The third chain is a tender. The Digital Bharat Nidhi (the renamed Universal Service Obligation Fund) pays for BharatNet; BSNL, as project management agency, splits the work into packages; project implementation agencies (PIAs) such as ITI, HFCL's consortium, Polycab, NCC and STL Networks bid for them, buy fibre, ducts and routers, hire civil contractors to trench and lay, and bill on milestones once BSNL accepts the work. The contract then runs for about ten years with operation and maintenance (O&M). Cash goes out at the start and comes back late. ITI's FY26 debtor days of 486 and payable days of 2,670 (screener) show a contractor that is owed money by the state and in turn owes money to its own suppliers for years.

In plain terms

The same word, "telecom equipment", covers a glass factory, a chip-design house and a construction contractor. The glass factory makes money when glass is scarce. The design house makes money when a big customer is buying and loses it when that customer pauses. The contractor makes money only if the government pays on time. Ask which of the three you are looking at before reading any number.

Two further points matter for the rest of the report. First, the members overlap on purpose. HFCL is a portfolio of all three chains (fibre and cable, telecom products such as Wi-Fi and 5G fixed-wireless CPE, turnkey EPC, and a growing defence arm); its Q2 FY27 telecom products segment was ₹2,322 crore of ₹2,542 crore. STL used to run an EPC arm too, but demerged its Global Services business (network deployment, system integration and BharatNet EPC) into STL Networks with effect from 31 March 2025; STL Networks is separately listed and not a member of this group, while the STL Digital IT-services arm stayed inside STL as a segment worth about 4% of revenue. Second, the units differ by chain: fibre is counted in fibre-kilometres, equipment in sites or ports, and EPC in gram panchayats connected. A reader who mixes them, for example by adding fibre capacity to cable capacity, will get every capacity number wrong.

02

A sector with no trend, only campaigns and cycles

Group revenue in FY26 was within 1% of FY22. Between those years it swung by two-thirds in one year and lost a third in the next.

The four companies together reported ₹12,576 crore of revenue in FY22 and ₹12,981 crore in FY26, a rise of 0.8% in four years. Nothing about that flat line describes what happened. STL peaked at ₹6,925 crore in FY23 on the Jio and BharatNet fibre boom, fell to ₹4,083 crore in FY24 as the glut hit and then lost its services arm in the demerger. Tejas went from ₹920 crore in FY23 to ₹2,471 crore in FY24 and ₹8,923 crore in FY25 as BSNL's 4G shipments ran, then fell back to ₹1,103 crore. ITI nearly tripled in FY25 to ₹3,616 crore on BSNL and BharatNet billing, then dropped 40%. HFCL drifted from ₹4,743 crore to ₹4,065 crore before the fibre upturn took it to ₹4,949 crore in FY26 and a TTM ₹7,492 crore by September 2026.

Outside India the market is far bigger and better measured for fibre than for equipment. Estimates of global 2026 fibre demand do not agree, because sources define it differently: TrendForce says 577 million fkm, the research institute cited in YOFC's 2026 interim report says more than 670 million fkm, and HFCL's FY26 annual report says 750 million fkm of annual optical-cable demand. Some count bare fibre and some cable; some include China's domestic market in full. The cleaner statistic is CRU's split, cited by YOFC: data-centre fibre demand growing at about 20.7% a year from 2025 to 2030, with data centres becoming more than half of global demand, against about 2.5% a year for telecom. India's trade data show the shape of the opportunity. In calendar 2025 India exported $512.8 million of optical fibre and cable (HS 9001.10 plus 8544.70), up 47%, against $131.8 million of imports, so it is a net exporter of the product at almost four to one. Exports to the United States rose from $80.4 million to $214.3 million. In network electronics the picture is reversed: India imported $3.84 billion of switches, routers, base stations and similar gear (HS 8517.62) in 2025 and exported $1.46 billion. In plain terms

India already makes more fibre than it uses and sells the surplus abroad, increasingly to America. It still buys most of the electronic boxes that sit at either end of that fibre. That is why the two fibre companies can grow with world demand, while Tejas, the one designer of boxes, has to win against Nokia, Ericsson and Ciena one contract at a time.

The domestic market for equipment has no reliable size estimate; the dossier found none in any primary source. Two proxies give a sense of scale. Network-gear imports of $3.8 billion (about ₹33,000 crore at ₹88/$) are one floor. Telecom PLI participants reported cumulative sales of more than ₹96,240 crore over about four and a half years to September 2025, roughly ₹21,000 crore a year, though most of that is the Indian arms of Nokia, Ericsson, Samsung and contract manufacturers rather than listed Indian designers. Against either proxy, the four listed members are small: their combined TTM revenue of ₹16,692 crore is mostly fibre and cable, and much of that is now exported.

03

Who buys, and why the buyer changed in 2026

For twenty years Indian telecom gear was bought by Indian telcos and the Indian state. In 2026 the marginal buyer of Indian fibre is an American data-centre operator.

There are six demand pools, and they move independently. The newest and largest for fibre is AI and hyperscale data centres. An AI training cluster links thousands of accelerators with optical cables, and the number of fibres per rack has risen with each chip generation. STL's management, at its September 2026 investor meet, put the figure at about 1,000 fibres per rack for legacy data-centre designs, about 4,000 for Nvidia's Hopper generation, about 16,000 for Blackwell and about 64,000 for the coming Vera Rubin generation. Because every server must talk to many others, interconnect paths scale faster than server count. TrendForce estimates AI data centres at 18–20% of global fibre demand in 2026, rising to 30–35% in 2027; CRU says data-centre optical cable demand grew 63% in 2026.

In plain terms

In 2023 the question for an Indian fibre maker was how much Jio and BharatNet would buy. In 2026 it is how much a few US cloud companies will take, and for how long. Three cautions apply to every demand number in this section. Hyperscaler deals are allocations, not orders: STL's October 2026 agreement is a "potential value" at "prevailing selling prices", with annual volume allocations, purchase orders released periodically and capped liabilities on both sides if either falls short. Government programmes are budgets, not cash: BharatNet's outlay is spread over about a decade and paid on acceptance. And telco capex is lumpy: Jio's spending fell by a third in two years. The companies that will do best through the next cycle are those whose order books blend these pools rather than depend on one.

Where ₹100 goes: germanium, helium, people and interest

The same fibre plant can earn ₹10 or lose ₹3 on every ₹100 of sales. The difference is price, not cost.

A fibre and cable maker's costs fall into four buckets. Raw materials come first: glass chemicals (silicon and germanium tetrachloride), quartz tubes, helium, acrylate coatings, and for cable the polymers (HDPE, PBT), aramid yarn, glass-reinforced plastic rods and steel armour. Germanium is the costliest dopant and China dominates its supply; Beijing has required export licences since August 2023 and banned exports to the US in December 2024. Helium cools fibre as it leaves the draw furnace, and the 2026 West Asia war, with its closure of the Strait of Hormuz from 28 February, disrupted Qatari helium shipments. STL says it is diversifying germanium and helium suppliers and testing lower-germanium processes. The second bucket is people; the third is power, freight and plant overheads; the fourth, below EBITDA, is depreciation and interest, which are large because preform and draw capacity is expensive and was mostly financed with debt.

China, multinationals and the unlisted: who else is in the room

Four listed Indian companies are a small part of a market set by Chinese fibre makers and European radio vendors.

Indian optical fibre cable is a moderately crowded market. HFCL describes itself as India's largest OFC supplier; STL is the largest integrated Indian fibre maker and says it holds 9% of the global OFC market outside China (8% in FY26). Behind them sit Birla Cable and Vindhya Telelinks (both in the MP Birla group), Aksh Optifibre, Polycab's OFC line, Finolex Cables (which has commissioned preform capacity and is doubling fibre), Apar, Universal Cables, UM Cables, ZTT India (owned by China's Zhongtian) and Aberdare (owned by Italy's Prysmian). No audited market-share data exist for India; the EU's anti-dumping investigation, which named most of these exporters, is the closest thing to an industry census. For wider coverage of cable makers, see our cables report.

China is the swing producer. YOFC, Hengtong, FiberHome, ZTT and Futong together dominate world preform and fibre volume, and in 2023–25 their overcapacity collapsed prices worldwide: China Mobile's 2025/26 cable tender cleared about 35% below its 2023/24 award, and Russian buyers saw wholesale Chinese fibre at ¥16 a kilometre in early 2025. YOFC's H1 2026 results show the scale of the turn: revenue up 53.6% to RMB 9,809 million (about ₹12,850 crore), gross margin 53.4% against 28.3% a year earlier, and profit up almost ninefold.

"Glass is the new GPU": how AI made HFCL and STL hyperscaler suppliers Deep dive

In twelve months the fibre glut became a shortage, two Indian companies quadrupled their profits, and the question moved from "how bad" to "how long".

At the start of 2025 the global fibre industry was in its second glut in six years. Chinese overcapacity had driven bare-fibre prices to levels where few makers outside China earned a return, and STL lost ₹123 crore in FY25. By the end of 2025 hyperscalers building AI training clusters were signing multi-year supply contracts for fibre they had not planned to buy. CRU recorded an 80% rise in Chinese G.652.D prices between November 2025 and January 2026. In plain terms

The AI fibre boom is real, but the industry has seen this film before. When everybody's new factories open in 2028, prices will tell you whether data-centre demand has kept growing fast enough to absorb them. Until then, the two Indian makers are earning extraordinary margins, and the share prices (STL up about 9.5 times from its 52-week low, HFCL about 4.2 times) assume much of that lasts.

What would extend the cycle? Three things. Next-generation fibre (hollow-core, multi-core, ultra-low-loss G.654.E) could shift demand to products that only a few makers can produce, which STL is developing and HFCL is less visibly working on. Co-packaged optics inside AI clusters would raise fibre counts further. And trade walls could fragment the market so that Chinese capacity cannot clear the American shortage: US buyers are being steered away from Chinese fibre, Indian fibre pays a 10% US tariff against 12.5% plus legacy duties on Chinese goods, and US local-content rules favour plants on US soil. What would shorten it? A pause in hyperscaler capex, faster Chinese preform additions, or a hyperscaler choosing to take less than its allocation, which the capped-liability terms of STL's agreement explicitly allow.

Why contractors lose money on government networks

The economics of a package explain the gap between the programme's size and the members' profits. A worked example with round numbers shows the mechanism. Take a ₹1,000 crore package. Bought-in cable, ducts and routers cost about ₹550 crore; civil sub-contracting (trenching, laying, restoration) about ₹300 crore; project overhead about ₹80 crore. That leaves ₹70 crore, a 7% gross margin. In plain terms

BharatNet is a very large, very safe customer that pays very slowly. A contractor that builds a village network finances it for a year or two out of its own pocket, then maintains it for a decade at small annual fees. That is fine for a company with cheap capital and a fast-moving site team; it is ruinous for one already borrowing at high rates and arguing with the customer about acceptance.

The ten-year O&M model changes the risk in a subtle way. Under the old model, a contractor built and left; under the new one, it carries the network's uptime for a decade, earns a modest annuity and is exposed to penalties if villages go dark. That gives order books a long, low-yield tail (HFCL's ₹5,070 crore of O&M will bill over seven to ten years) and makes the order-book-to-revenue ratio look better than near-term revenue will be. It also ties up bank guarantees for longer. The model should, if it works, lift the share of active connections from the reported 45%, which is the programme's real test. Satellite broadband is a long-run substitute for the hardest last-mile villages, and Starlink was authorised in India in 2025; but for middle-mile and backbone links, fibre remains the only practical option.

For investors the BharatNet read-across is mixed. It underwrites domestic fibre volumes for years and gives ITI and HFCL visible order books. But it is low-margin, working-capital heavy work, and it is where both companies' weakest numbers come from: ITI's 486 debtor days and HFCL's turnkey losses. The cleanest exposure is as an equipment or cable supplier to PIAs, Tejas's routers and the fibre makers' cable, rather than as a PIA. HFCL's board appears to agree: its restructuring review is examining whether the turnkey/EPC and O&M business belongs in the same company as the export fibre business.

In plain terms

The programme is good for fibre volumes and bad for contractors' cash. Read an order-book figure that contains BharatNet work as part annuity, part financing burden, not as a pile of high-margin revenue waiting to be billed.

Trade walls: tariffs, duties and the rules on who may sell

Policy decides which of these companies may sell to whom, and at what toll. In 2026 the walls mostly helped Indian fibre and protected Indian gear.

Five sets of rules shape this sub-sector: India's Trusted Source regime, India's production-linked incentive and public-procurement preferences, US tariffs and local-content rules, EU trade remedies on Indian cable, and China's export controls on germanium. Each favours or penalises a different member. The common thread is that Indian suppliers benefit from being "not Chinese" in a world where both the US and India are building walls against Chinese telecom technology, and pay for it when the EU treats them like the Chinese makers it already taxes.

India: Trusted Source, PLI and procurement preference

From 15 June 2021, under an amendment to telecom licences, operators may deploy only "trusted products" from "trusted sources" designated by the National Cyber Security Coordinator. Huawei and ZTE have not been designated, which has effectively excluded them from new Indian network deployments; the Telecommunications Act 2023 gives the government wider powers over equipment standards and trusted sources, and all gear must pass Mandatory Testing and Certification (MTCTE). The rule helped Ericsson, Nokia and Samsung most, but it also made indigenous designers like Tejas eligible for BSNL's 4G network, which a 2020 directive had already restricted to indigenous technology. Public procurement rules give "Class-I local suppliers" (at least 50% local content) purchase preference in government tenders.

The telecom and networking PLI scheme, notified in February 2021 with an outlay of ₹12,195 crore effective April 2021, pays 4–7% on incremental sales of locally made equipment, with an extra 1% for design-led manufacturers added in June 2022. By 30 September 2025 participants had invested more than ₹4,646 crore, sold more than ₹96,240 crore and exported ₹19,240 crore, employing 29,574 people. Disbursement has lagged far behind: ₹1,162 crore by March 2025, paid to only 21 of the 42 shortlisted firms (secondary), and about ₹1,586 crore cumulatively by mid-2025 (secondary). Tejas booked ₹467.70 crore of PLI income in FY25, a large part of that year's profit, and none in FY26 because it missed the scheme's minimum threshold; its ₹278.78 crore PLI receivable was collected. An industry complaint is that some participants "white-labelled" imports.

US tariffs on Indian goods changed four times in a year. In August 2025 the IEEPA "reciprocal" tariffs hit India at 25% plus a 25% penalty. On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs. The European Union: duties that narrowed HFCL's edge

The 1999–2001 telecom bubble pulled HFCL into the Ketan Parekh "K-10" episode and its collapse. In the 2000s Sterlite Optical (STL) was demerged from Sterlite Industries in 2000–01 and began building fibre; Tejas was founded in 2000; ITI was referred to BIFR in 2004.

Reading the KPIs: a masterclass for telecom-equipment numbers Deep dive

Order books that are not orders, capacities in the wrong unit, margins on different definitions and receivables that are really loans to the state. How to read each number, and how each company scores.

This section explains the native numbers of the sub-sector one at a time: what each measures, how it is calculated, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, what good and weak look like, how the four listed companies compare, and the trap that catches most readers. It starts with the equation that every fibre-and-cable maker's profit follows, because two of the four members now earn almost all the group's profit from that equation, and ends with an anatomy of one typical plant, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected in one place and a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

The fibre-and-cable profit equation

Revenue for a fibre and cable maker is the volume it sells, measured in fibre-kilometres (fkm), times the price it gets per fkm, plus connectivity products sold on top. Volume is capacity times utilisation. Profit is revenue less raw materials (roughly half of revenue), less conversion costs (people, power, freight, plant overheads), less depreciation and interest, less tax. Management controls capacity (with a two-year lag), mix and conversion costs. It does not control the market price per fkm or the cost of germanium and helium, and only partly controls utilisation, which depends on demand.

At a blended export, data-centre-heavy realisation of about $20 per fkm, or ₹1,898 at ₹94.9, revenue is about ₹10,000 crore. With materials at 50% (STL's Q1 FY27 ratio) and other operating costs at 25%, EBITDA is ₹2,500 crore, a 25% margin close to HFCL's Q2 FY27 figure. Depreciation of about ₹350 crore (FY26's ₹157 crore plus ₹1,800 crore of new capex at roughly 10%) and interest of about ₹250 crore leave pre-tax profit of about ₹1,900 crore, and after 25% tax, PAT of about ₹1,425 crore. Now change only the price: at $8 per fkm, a 2024-like level, revenue is about ₹4,000 crore, EBITDA 10–12% and PAT perhaps ₹100–200 crore. The same plant at the same utilisation earns seven to fourteen times less. This example is ILLUSTRATIVE: HFCL does not disclose volumes or realisations in this form.

Order book and book-to-bill

What it is. The order book is the value of signed but unexecuted orders. Book-to-bill, as used here, is the order book divided by trailing twelve-month revenue: the number of years of revenue the book would cover at the current run-rate. (Some analysts use book-to-bill to mean quarterly order inflow divided by quarterly revenue; we call that the inflow ratio below.) Formula: book-to-bill = order book ÷ TTM revenue. Worked example: HFCL's ₹28,911 crore book at September 2026 against ₹7,492 crore of TTM revenue gives 3.9 times, about four years of work at today's pace.

What moves it. New orders raise it; execution lowers it; cancellations, re-scoping and currency moves change it without any business event. Long O&M contracts raise it artificially because they bill slowly. Benchmarks: good is above 3 times with no single customer above 30%; average 1.5–3 times; weak is below 1.5 times or more than 70% from one customer. How they compare: ITI 6.6 times (but see the trap), HFCL 3.9 times, STL 3.3 times, Tejas 1.6 times with 96% of the book in India, mostly BSNL through TCS.

Capacity utilisation

What it is. Output as a share of capacity. Formula: utilisation = fkm produced ÷ fkm capacity. Worked example: a plant with 50 million fkm of capacity producing 35 million fkm runs at 70%. Because conversion costs are mostly fixed, raising utilisation from 70% to 90% lifts EBITDA margin sharply even at a constant price: STL's annual report tied its target of about 20% EBITDA to "total manufacturing capacity utilisation" reaching "the 70%+ milestone", which implies FY26 utilisation was below 70%.

What moves it. Demand, product changeovers (high-count ribbon cable runs slower per fkm than standard cable), preform availability and plant uptime. Benchmarks: above 85% is good; 70–85% average; below 65% weak. TrendForce puts global industry utilisation at 92% in 2026. How they compare: HFCL does not disclose a percentage; CARE says its ~₹10,159 crore export contract alone uses over half its capacity. STL was below 70% in FY26 and targets 70%+ by Q4 FY27. The trap: companies rarely state utilisation on the same basis (fibre draw versus cabling versus connectivity), and a company can run its cable lines at 95% while its draw towers idle for lack of preform.

R&D intensity and patents (Tejas)

What it is. Research spending as a share of revenue, and the stock of patents it produces. For an equipment designer this is the product pipeline. Formula: R&D spend (expensed plus capitalised) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Tejas does not give a clean R&D total in our sources, but its ₹403 crore of FY26 depreciation, much of it amortisation of capitalised development, plus expensed engineering costs suggests an R&D bill above half of FY26 revenue (INDICATIVE). In FY25, on ₹8,923 crore of revenue, the same bill was a single-digit percentage. Patent filings rose from 676 (371 granted) at March 2026 to 722 (380 granted) at June and 748 filings at September 2026. STL cites 785+ patents; HFCL reports an R&D headcount of 253.

What moves it. Product cycles (5G-Advanced and 6G need new radio silicon), the revenue denominator, and accounting choices on capitalisation. Benchmarks: 8–15% of revenue sustained is healthy for an equipment maker; above 30% means a sub-scale designer; below 5% means it is harvesting old designs. The trap: capitalised R&D moves cost from the P&L to the balance sheet and back again later as amortisation. Tejas's capital commitments for intangibles fell from ₹437 crore to ₹118 crore in FY26, which reduces future amortisation but may signal a slower pipeline. Patent counts measure activity, not commercial value.

PLI income

What it is. Incentive income under the telecom production-linked incentive scheme, usually booked in "other operating revenue". Formula: PLI = 4–7% (+1% design-led) × eligible incremental sales above a base year, subject to investment and sales thresholds. Worked example: Tejas booked ₹467.70 crore of PLI in FY25, about 5% of that year's ₹8,923 crore revenue and more than its ₹447 crore PAT; excluding it, FY25 revenue from sales was about ₹8,455 crore and the profit would have been close to zero or negative. In FY26 it booked nothing, because it missed the minimum threshold.

What moves it. Meeting the year's incremental-sales and investment thresholds, the base year, and government disbursal speed (only ₹1,162 crore disbursed scheme-wide by March 2025). Benchmarks: PLI should be a bonus, not the profit; a company whose profit depends on it is weak. The trap: PLI sits above the operating line, so it inflates EBITDA margin. Compare a company's FY25 margin with and without it before judging its "normal" profitability.

In plain terms

Tejas's best year was helped by a government subsidy worth more than its whole profit. The subsidy stopped the next year. When a company's good year includes a scheme payment, take it out before deciding what a good year looks like.

BSNL sites and RAN revenue per site

What it is. The number of 4G sites shipped or installed, and the equipment value per site; for Tejas and ITI the BSNL programme is measured in sites. Formula: revenue per site = order value ÷ sites. Worked example: the original Tejas APO of ₹7,492 crore (press reports) over about one lakh sites is about ₹7.5 lakh of RAN equipment per site; the TCS LoI of ₹1,537 crore for 18,685 sites is about ₹8.2 lakh; ITI's ₹856 crore for 7,613 sites (secondary) is about ₹11.2 lakh, with broader scope. Benchmarks: none published; the useful comparison is sites ordered against the one-lakh installed base and the 5.08 lakh 5G base stations already in India's private networks. Definition traps, collected

  • ITI's FY26 profit is a land sale. ₹293 crore reported PAT includes a ~₹832 crore exceptional gain; ex-gain the year lost about ₹539 crore (INDICATIVE), and the auditor disclaimed the accounts with a going-concern doubt.
  • Order book is five things. L1, LoI/APO, firm PO, hyperscaler allocation (PAL/LTSA) and multi-year O&M; always print the date and composition.
  • fkm, ckm and rkm. Fibre-km = cable-km × fibres per cable; route-km is a trench. Never add fibre capacity to cable capacity.
  • Company EBITDA is not screener operating profit. HFCL's includes other income; STL's company ₹397 crore vs screener ₹385 crore in Q1 FY27.
  • Consolidated vs standalone. HFCL's Q1 FY27 consolidated PAT was ₹245.64 crore against ₹179.21 crore standalone; a ₹362.92 crore Q2 figure in one report is on an unknown basis.
  • STL before and after the demerger. FY24 and earlier include the Global Services (EPC) arm; FY25 onward is continuing operations. STL Digital stayed in STL.
  • Tejas's history. Use FY23 ₹920 crore / PAT −₹36 crore and FY24 ₹2,471 crore / +₹63 crore (screener); the campaign multiple was 3.6× (FY24 → FY25), not 7×.
  • PLI in revenue. Tejas's FY25 revenue included ₹467.70 crore of PLI; FY26 none.

In plain terms

Before trusting any number in this sector, ask three questions: on what basis (company or screener, consolidated or standalone), as of what date (FY26, Q1 FY27 or Q2 FY27), and does it include something one-off (land, PLI, a demerged arm)? Most of the mistakes in published coverage of these companies come from skipping one of the three.

How to read a telecom-equipment company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue, then the segment table (2 minutes). Split products from EPC/turnkey and defence. For HFCL, telecom products versus turnkey; for STL, Optical Networking versus Digital. Red flag: growth coming from the low-margin segment.
  2. Operating margin on one basis (1 minute). Compute revenue less operating expenses yourself, or use screener consistently; note whether the company's EBITDA includes other income. Red flag: a margin rise explained only by "other income".
  3. Exceptional items and other income (1 minute). Land sales (ITI), PLI (Tejas), fair-value gains, refunds. Strip them out before computing earnings growth.
  4. Order book: value, date, composition (1 minute). Product versus O&M versus network work; L1 and LoIs separated; export and government share. Red flag: a sudden drop without explanation (ITI's March-to-June fall).
  5. Order inflow (30 seconds). Closing book − opening book + revenue. Red flag: inflow below revenue for two quarters.
  6. Volume and price clues (1 minute). Capacity additions, utilisation remarks, realisation per fkm, attach rate, data-centre share. Red flag: revenue growth with flat volumes late in a price cycle.
  7. Working capital (1 minute). Receivables and inventory against the previous quarter; debtor days on TTM revenue. Red flag: receivables growing faster than revenue (HFCL in H1 FY26) or ageing beyond a year (ITI).
  8. Cash flow and net debt (1 minute). Operating cash flow (half-yearly for most Indian companies), capex, company-basis net debt including cash. Red flag: net debt rising while EBITDA is negative (Tejas).
  9. Audit and governance notes (30 seconds). Limited-review conclusion, emphasis of matter, related-party ceilings (Tejas–TCS ₹4,800 crore), contingent liabilities (STL's Prysmian verdict). Red flag: a disclaimer, as at ITI.
  10. Guidance against delivery (1 minute). HFCL's ≥80% growth implies H2 FY27 revenue of at least ₹4,452 crore; STL's 23% FY27 margin and FY29 targets; Tejas's "quarterly positive EBITDA". Red flag: guidance raised while working capital deteriorates.

The checklist works because it follows the equation. Revenue and segment tell you which business model is driving the quarter; margin, exceptional items and order book tell you whether the drive is real; working capital, cash and audit notes tell you whether the profit will turn into cash. In this sector the last three steps catch most of the bad surprises, because government customers and campaign receivables delay cash far more than they delay revenue recognition.

Who is winning: exporters, the patient and the stranded

Three maps and a scorecard. The winners in 2026 are the companies that sell abroad, hold little debt and are paid on commercial terms.

The first map plots each company's export share against its latest-quarter EBITDA margin. It is the shortest summary of 2026: the two companies that sell most of their output abroad are the two that make money, and the two that depend on Indian government customers do not. HFCL and STL sit in the top-right corner, Tejas in the bottom-left and ITI on the left edge at roughly zero margin. The correlation is not accidental. Export buyers in 2026 are hyperscalers and foreign telcos competing for scarce fibre at boom prices and paying on commercial terms; the domestic government buyer is between campaigns and pays after acceptance tests.

The second map asks whether the market pays for quality. It plots FY26 ROCE against enterprise value as a multiple of TTM revenue. ITI is the outlier: the lowest-return business after Tejas trades at the highest multiple of sales, about 10.7 times, because of its float. STL and Tejas sit at similar EV/sales multiples (about 7.6 and 7.9 times) from very different starting points, STL with a positive 8% ROCE and rising, Tejas at −15%. On this measure HFCL is the best value of the four, though FY26 ROCE understates both fibre makers' current returns.

The third map looks at visibility and its source: book-to-bill against the domestic share of revenue. A long order book is valuable when it comes from paying commercial customers; it is less valuable when it is mostly government work with long acceptance cycles. ITI has the longest book relative to revenue (6.6 times) and is entirely domestic; Tejas has the shortest book (1.6 times) and is almost entirely domestic; HFCL and STL have three to four years of cover from mostly foreign, commercial buyers. The map makes visible why the market treats the fibre makers' order books as near-cash and ITI's as a question.
