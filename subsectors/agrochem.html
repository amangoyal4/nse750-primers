00

The one-page view: two businesses that rarely peak together

One label covers a rural consumer business run on the Indian monsoon and an export business run on Brazilian credit and Chinese prices. Read them separately or misread both.

"Indian agrochemicals" sounds like one industry. It is at least two, and the six listed companies in this bucket of the NSE 750 sit in different places on that map. The first is domestic branded crop protection: insecticides, herbicides and fungicides packed into branded bottles and sachets and sold through a long chain of distributors and village retailers to roughly a hundred million farm households. Its revenue is set by rain, sown area, crop prices and dealer credit, and it pays 18% GST. Bayer CropScience, Sumitomo Chemical India and Rallis India live mostly here, as do PI Industries' domestic arm and UPL's Indian business.

The second is export and global crop protection: generic active ingredients and formulations sold into Brazil, the United States and Europe, molecules made under contract for the global patent holders, and registrations that unlock regulated markets. Its revenue is set by distributor inventory in São Paulo and Iowa, by the price of Chinese technical-grade material, by registrations, by the patent calendar and by the rupee. UPL, Sharda Cropchem and PI's contract manufacturing (CSM) business live here. The two halves rarely peak together. In FY24 the global half crashed while Indian farm demand held; in Q1 FY27 the global half steadied while the Indian monsoon failed.

Four facts shape the rest of this report. First, the FY24 crash was almost all UPL. Combined profit after tax of the six fell from ₹7,338 crore in FY23 to ₹1,094 crore in FY24 and has since recovered to ₹5,638 crore in FY26. The other five together earned more in FY24 than in FY23, and their profit has risen every year from ₹2,034 crore in FY21 to ₹3,418 crore in FY26 at EBITDA margins of 18–20%. Second, the destocking is over but the deflation is not. The 2023–25 inventory flush in Brazil, the US and Europe has run its course; the global majors now describe channel stocks as normal. But distributors buy just in time, farmers are squeezed, and generic prices are still drifting down: Corteva's crop-protection prices fell 4% in Q2 of calendar 2026 and Syngenta's Latin American sales fell 11% in the first half. Chinese technical prices did turn up in September 2026, glyphosate to RMB 28,250 a tonne and glufosinate to RMB 60,000 a tonne (+12% in a month), but on cost policy (the end of export VAT rebates on 1 April and an anti-below-cost-selling notice on 10 September), not on demand.

Third, the domestic half has just had a bad monsoon. Rainfall ended the season at 87% of the long-period average, with June at 65%, the South Peninsula at 76% and the East and North-East at 74%. Q1 FY27 domestic sales were already soft (Sumitomo +1%, Bayer −4%, PI's domestic arm +3% on volume +12% and falling prices). Q2, the main kharif spraying quarter, has not been reported by anyone yet. Rallis reports on 22 October, Sumitomo on 26 October and Bayer on 3 November.

Fourth, valuations have reset and the gap between the cheapest and the dearest is huge. Combined market value is ₹1,23,513 crore, and every one of the six is down between 13% (Sharda) and 39% (PI) over the past year. Sumitomo still trades at about 34 times trailing earnings and Bayer at about 22 times with a 4.2% dividend yield; PI at 25–30 times and 16.8 times EV/EBITDA; UPL at about 6 times EV/EBITDA before any adjustment for its large minority shareholders; and Sharda at about 10 times earnings and 5.3 times EV/EBITDA despite a 30% return on capital. Explaining that spread, which is mostly about who owns the molecule and who carries the receivable, is the purpose of this report.

01

From molecule to spray tank: where a farmer's ₹1,000 goes

The value chain runs from a patent lab to a Chinese intermediate plant to an Indian formulator to a village shop. Margin pools sit at both ends, not in the middle.

A pesticide reaches a farmer through seven steps, and the vocabulary matters because each step has a different owner and a different margin. The active ingredient (AI), also called the technical (TC), is the pure molecule: glyphosate 95% TC, for example. It is made by multi-step synthesis from key starting materials (KSMs) and intermediates, a large share of which come from China. The technical is then turned into a formulation: mixed with solvents, emulsifiers and carriers into the emulsifiable concentrate, suspension or granule that a farmer actually sprays, and branded and packed. In the Indian domestic market the margin lives in the formulation and the brand, not in the molecule.

Products split into insecticides, herbicides, fungicides and plant-growth regulators. India is insecticide-heavy: older estimates put insecticides at about two-thirds to three-quarters of value (one research firm cites 72.5% in 2022). The world is herbicide-heavy, at roughly 40% or more. Rising rural wages are pulling India toward herbicides, because a herbicide replaces paid hand-weeding: herbicides are already 19–22% of Sumitomo's sales and its fastest-growing family.

Every molecule has a life-cycle. A patented molecule is sold by its discoverer (Bayer, Syngenta, Corteva, BASF, FMC, Sumitomo, Kumiai). When the patent and the data protection lapse, generics (UPL, Sharda, Chinese makers) enter. Indian marketers call newer, often in-licensed molecules sold at a premium "specialty". Nothing can be sold without a registration: in India from the Central Insecticides Board and Registration Committee (CIB&RC), under section 9(3) for a molecule new to India (full data package) or 9(4) for a "me-too" of an already registered product; abroad, country by country, from the EU, the US EPA or Brazil's three regulators. Finally, CSM (custom synthesis and manufacturing) is making an innovator's molecule or intermediate to its specification under a multi-year contract. PI Industries is the Indian leader.

Value is captured at the ends: by whoever owns the molecule (Step 1) and by whoever owns the brand or the registration that reaches the farmer (Steps 4–5). The technical-making middle earns what the Chinese cost curve allows, except under CSM contracts.

The order in which value is captured is roughly this. Patent holders first. Then contract manufacturers with process know-how and a reputation for protecting a client's IP: PI earns a 57–58% gross margin and 22–28% EBITDA. Then branded domestic formulators with specialty molecules: Sumitomo and Bayer earn 15–22% EBITDA. Then registration-house generics in regulated markets: Sharda earns about 20% EBITDA before roughly 9% of sales goes in amortising its registrations. Last come commodity technical exporters who take the Chinese price: 10–15% EBITDA in a normal year and losses in a glut.

In plain terms

When a farmer pays ₹1,000 for a branded insecticide, roughly ₹650 reaches the company and about ₹114 of that is operating profit. The rest goes to the tax office, the dealer chain and the chemical supply chain. The company that owns a patented molecule or a strong brand keeps more of the ₹650; the company selling a commodity molecule keeps whatever China leaves it.

The farmer's purchase is discretionary and timing-sensitive. A spray is bought at sowing or on a pest outbreak, and a missed window is a lost sale, not a deferred one. That is the deepest difference from fertiliser, which a farmer buys almost regardless of conditions and whose price the government sets (see the Fertilizers report for that side of the farm economy). Pesticide demand flexes with pest pressure, which itself depends on rainfall, humidity and the crop mix, and the channel amplifies every swing because dealers will not hold stock they fear they cannot sell.

02

Size and growth: a ₹35–45k crore home market and three lost years

Nobody agrees how big the Indian market is. Everybody can see that the six listed companies sold no more in FY26 than in FY23.

Estimates of the Indian crop-protection market vary by a factor of almost two because the definitions differ: some count technical-grade consumption, some count formulations at company realisation, some at farm-gate prices, and some exclude seed treatment or bio-products. Research firms sit at the low end. Mordor Intelligence puts the market at $2.59 billion in 2025, rising to $3.21 billion by 2030. Ken Research gives $3,285 million for 2025 with a 5.49% annual growth rate to 2031, and technical-grade chemical pesticide consumption of 45.3 thousand tonnes in 2024-25. IMARC puts the Indian pesticides market at ₹260 billion (₹26,000 crore) in 2024.

A bottom-up check gives a larger number. Bayer's ₹5,675 crore of domestic revenue (including corn seed), Sumitomo's ~₹2,500 crore of domestic sales, Rallis's ~₹1,900 crore of domestic crop care, PI's ₹1,302 crore of domestic revenue, UPL's Indian crop-protection business, Coromandel's crop-protection arm and Dhanuka's ~₹2,000 crore, plus the unlisted Indian arms of Syngenta, FMC, Corteva and BASF, point to roughly ₹35,000–45,000 crore at company-realisation level in FY26. That is the dossier's own INDICATIVE estimate, not a published figure; no authoritative FY26 number from FICCI or the Crop Care Federation was found.

The export side is at least as large as the home market. India became the world's second-largest agrochemical exporter after China around FY23, with exports of roughly $5.4–5.5 billion that year, then fell by about a fifth in FY24 as the global destock hit. Exports in April–July 2025 were $1,434 million, with the US, Brazil and Japan the main buyers, and India exports roughly half of what it produces. These export figures are INDICATIVE: the FY23 level and the FY24 fall are from industry-body figures quoted from memory, IMARC's narrower definition gives only $3.3 billion for FY25, and no full-year FY26 export figure was found. India's share of global agrochemical exports is roughly 8–10% (INDICATIVE).

The usual structural argument is under-use: India applies roughly 0.4–0.6 kg of active ingredient per hectare, against about 2.5 in the US, 6–10 in Brazil, around 11 in Japan and 10–13 in China, and a world average near 2.6. These per-hectare figures are INDICATIVE and dated (older FAO and FICCI data, not re-checked). They explain why every company deck says the market has room to grow; they do not explain why it has not grown faster, which is mostly the fragmentation of Indian farms, the cost of credit and the discretionary nature of the spend.

Three years of standing still

For the six listed companies the growth record is plain. Combined revenue rose from ₹55,002 crore in FY21 to a peak of ₹75,731 crore in FY23, fell 15% to ₹64,525 crore in FY24, and recovered to ₹75,631 crore in FY26: slightly below the FY23 peak. The five-year compound growth rate of 6.6% hides a boom in FY22–23, a bust in FY24 and a slow climb back. Strip out UPL and the five others grew 7.8% a year, but they too were flat in FY26 (₹23,792 crore against ₹23,583 crore in FY25), because PI's 16% fall offset Sharda's 22% rise.

The ex-UPL line is not as smooth inside as it looks, and the reason is a timing offset that is itself instructive. PI Industries, the contract manufacturer, peaked in FY24–25 (PAT ₹1,682 crore and ₹1,660 crore) because its innovator clients were still buying patented molecules while generic prices collapsed. In plain terms

04

Rain, acreage and the 87% monsoon: the domestic demand engine

Indian pesticide demand is set by when it rains, where, on which crops, and whether the farmer has cash or credit when the pests arrive. 2026 failed the first two tests in the South and East.

The domestic half of this sub-sector is driven by four things in roughly this order: the timing and spread of the south-west monsoon, the area sown under each crop, crop prices (which decide whether a farmer can afford to protect a crop), and credit in the dealer chain. Insecticide demand rises with humidity and pest pressure; herbicide demand rises with labour cost and with direct-seeded rice; fungicide demand rises in wet, warm spells. The monsoon touches all of them, and the channel magnifies its effect, because a dealer facing a dry July does not reorder.

The 2026 monsoon ended on 30 September at 87% of the long-period average (759.4 mm), the fourth lowest since 2001 and the thirteenth lowest since 1901. It was an El Niño year, and the India Meteorological Department's late-May forecast of 90% (±4) proved slightly optimistic. The shape mattered as much as the total. June, when kharif sowing and early spraying begin, delivered only 65% of normal; July recovered to 101%; August fell back to 84% and September to 92%. Regionally the North-West (94%) and Central India (97%) were close to normal, and the core monsoon zone was at 94%, but the South Peninsula received 76% and the East and North-East 74%: the rice, chilli and cotton belts of the South and the paddy lands of the East.

In plain terms

A weak monsoon hurts pesticide sales twice: fewer sprays on a stressed crop, and dealers who refuse to stock up because they fear being left with unsold product at season-end. 2026 rainfall was 87% of normal, and the South and East were near three-quarters. Beyond the weather: the structural drivers

Over a decade, five slower forces matter more than any single monsoon. Labour scarcity is moving India from hand-weeding to herbicides; every major has been launching rice and soybean herbicides, and Rallis's herbicide-tolerant direct-seeded rice ("Dhaanya") is a seed-plus-chemical answer to the same problem. Formalisation shifts farmers from unbranded and spurious products (industry bodies have long claimed 20–30% of the market is illegal or spurious, an old and unverified estimate) toward registered brands. Low per-hectare use leaves headroom. Drones and precision spraying change how much product is used per acre and favour newer formulations. And corn demand from ethanol blending and poultry feed is pulling up hybrid corn seed, which matters for Bayer's DEKALB and UPL's Advanta.

Crop prices sit underneath all of this. UPL's management said in August that commodity prices "have shown signs of stabilization and even some recovery" but that "grower profitability remains under pressure". Rice and wheat have the support of minimum support prices and procurement; cotton and soybean prices were weak in 2024–25. A farmer who earns less per quintal sprays less, and switches from a ₹1,200 specialty insecticide to a ₹500 generic. That is why specialty-heavy portfolios suffer most in a bad farm year, and why PI's domestic volumes could rise 12% while its revenue rose only 3%.

Credit closes the loop. Dealers buy on 60–120 days of credit and are offered season-end "liquidation" schemes; unsold stock comes back as returns. When the Reserve Bank raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, it raised the cost of that dealer credit at the worst time of the year. A company that pushed stock into the channel in June to hit a quarterly number will see it either as receivables in September or as returns in December.

In plain terms

Long-run demand for Indian crop protection should grow faster than the farm economy because labour is getting dearer (good for herbicides), farmers are moving to branded products, and Indian farms still use far less chemistry per hectare than China or Brazil. In any given year, though, rainfall, crop prices and dealer credit decide the number.

05

Brazil, America, Europe: the export demand side

For UPL, Sharda and PI the Indian monsoon is a footnote. Their year is decided by Brazilian soybean credit, American generic competition and European weather and registrations.

The export half of the sub-sector sells into three very different markets. Brazil is the largest crop-protection market in the world, roughly $15–20 billion a year (INDICATIVE, from memory). Farmers buy on harvest credit, often 180–360 days and sometimes paid in grain ("barter"), through distributors who themselves carry heavy inventory and debt. When soybean prices fall or distributors over-order, the whole chain destocks at once. In 2023–24 several distributors came under balance-sheet stress and some filed for judicial recovery. China supplies more than half of Brazil's imported technical volume, so Chinese prices set the floor there too.

The United States is a large, increasingly generic market where UPL earns about 14% of its revenue and Sharda about a quarter of its agrochemical revenue. Trade policy is now part of the picture: the IEEPA tariffs were struck down on 20 February 2026, and a 10% Section 301 duty on Indian goods (12.5% for China and Vietnam) has applied since 24 July 2026 and is under legal challenge. Europe has the highest registration barrier in the world, which is a moat for whoever already holds registrations and a risk whenever the EU withdraws a molecule. In 2026 a summer heatwave cut herbicide and fungicide volumes; sugar-beet area fell about 7%; Sharda's European sales fell 11% in Q1 FY27 while UPL's grew only 4%.

The rupee is the last export driver. UPL's management described it moving "from around 86 to nearly 95" year on year, and it has barely moved since March 2026. 06

China sets the floor: costs, supply and where ₹100 goes

The marginal tonne of almost every generic molecule is made in China. When Beijing changes its tax rebates or its pricing rules, Indian margins move within weeks.

China is the world's dominant maker of generic technicals, with roughly 40% or more of global technical production (INDICATIVE). India buys its key starting materials and intermediates there: India's organic-chemical imports from China were $11.12 billion in 2024, and agrochemical inputs are a large part of that. The two countries are mirror images. India exports formulations and generic technicals and imports the building blocks; China exports both. So Chinese prices set the floor for Indian technical makers' realisations, the cost line for Indian formulators and Sharda, and the competitive price in Brazil.

Through 2024 and the first half of 2026 that floor kept sinking. Chinese capacity additions had collapsed technical prices after the 2021–22 spike: glyphosate 95% technical, which peaked at roughly RMB 80,000 a tonne in 2021, was in the low RMB 20,000s by 2024 (both INDICATIVE figures from memory). Chinese pesticide output was up 23% year on year in early 2026 and the Chinese pesticide price index fell another 4.38% month on month in July 2026. Then, in September, prices turned.

Destock over, deflation not: the 2021–26 cycle Deep dive

Channels have emptied. Farmers are squeezed, distributors buy only what they need this week, and generic prices are still edging down outside China. A volume-light recovery is not a boom.

Every agrochemical cycle has the same three ingredients: Chinese supply, distributor inventory and farm income. The 2021–26 cycle had all three in extreme form, and the sequence matters because each phase hit a different listed company.

  • 2020–2022 · Boom
    COVID supply fears and China's energy-control and environmental shutdowns in the second half of 2021 send technical prices to records; glyphosate reaches roughly RMB 80,000 a tonne (INDICATIVE). Distributors in Brazil, the US and Europe over-order. Six-company PAT peaks at ₹7,338 crore in FY23; UPL's revenue reaches ₹53,576 crore at a ~19% margin.
  • FY24 · Crash
    New Chinese capacity and weaker demand collapse technical prices (glyphosate in the low RMB 20,000s by 2024, INDICATIVE). Channels destock. Six-company revenue −15%, PAT −85% to ₹1,094 crore. UPL loses ₹1,878 crore at a 10% EBITDA margin; Sharda's PAT falls to ₹32 crore; Indian agrochem exports fall about a fifth.
  • FY25 · Stabilisation
    Volumes return at lower prices. Combined PAT recovers to ₹3,983 crore. UPL back to a ₹820 crore profit; PI still near its peak (₹1,660 crore).
  • FY26 · Split recovery
    UPL (PATMI ₹1,921 crore), Sharda (record ₹681 crore), Bayer, Rallis and Sumitomo recover or set records. PI's PAT falls 20%: contract manufacturing is the last link to suffer, as innovators destock their own patented chains.
  • Calendar 2026 · Volume-light
    Inventories "normalized" (Corteva) but buying is just in time (UPL, FMC). Corteva's CP prices −4% in Q2, with further declines guided for H2 in Latin America. Chinese output +23% early in the year, then a September price firming on cost policy. The West Asia war adds input-cost push from March.

The destock was a classic bullwhip. When distributors expect prices to rise they over-order; when prices start to fall they stop buying and sell from stock, so manufacturers' sales fall much more than farmers' actual use. In FY24 farmers in Brazil and the US were still spraying, but UPL's customers were selling them product bought a year earlier at higher prices. UPL's sales fell 20% and its EBITDA margin halved, while Indian domestic demand, served by companies whose dealers had not over-ordered, held up.

Who wins from a Chinese price floor, and who does not

If the September floor holds, the winners are Indian technical makers that sell into the same markets as China: UPL and Rallis's B2B arm gain on realisations and on stock bought before the rise. The losers are those who buy Chinese material and cannot reprice quickly: Sharda with a lag of a quarter or two, and domestic formulators selling into a weak monsoon. If the floor fails, because Chinese producers ignore the notice once export orders dry up, the 2026 pattern of falling generic prices in Latin America resumes. Either way, a price floor set by cost policy does not create demand; it only changes who keeps the margin.

In plain terms

The 2023–25 problem was too much stock in the shops; that is fixed. The 2026 problem is that shops buy only what they need this week, farmers have little money, and generic prices outside China are still slipping. UPL has grown margins by raising prices first and selling cheaper old stock, which will not repeat for ever.

In plain terms

Three of six Q1 FY27 profit numbers need adjusting before you use them: Sumitomo's includes an insurance claim, Bayer's a one-off sale of marketing rights, and Sharda's fell only because last year's included forex gains. Patents, generics and contract manufacturing: PI's own down-cycle Deep dive

The patent cliff moves value from innovators and their contract makers to generic sellers. India's CSM leader is living through that shift while carrying India's first home-grown insecticide.

A new crop-protection active ingredient costs around $300 million and takes 11–12 years to bring to market, and patent life after launch is often only 8–10 years; data protection extends exclusivity in some markets (ten years in the EU). These are INDICATIVE industry figures from AgbioInvestor-style estimates. When protection lapses, Chinese technical makers enter first, then Indian and other formulators, and prices typically fall 30–60% over a few years (INDICATIVE). The 2021–28 window is a heavy one: the diamide insecticides (chlorantraniliprole now trades as a Chinese technical at RMB 265,000 a tonne), SDHI fungicides, several herbicides such as mesotrione, and pyroxasulfone, where expiry is contested.

The pyroxasulfone question

PI's largest franchise, by wide agreement though never disclosed by molecule, is pyroxasulfone, a pre-emergence herbicide it makes for Japan's Kumiai. Its patent position is now contested. Kumiai says it still holds several North American patents covering intermediates, processes and compositions, has been enforcing them since 2024, and in January 2026 won an Australian process patent running to 2040 after an opposition by ADAMA. Against that, China's Anhui Jiuyi is producing pyroxasulfone at 600 tonnes a year and US generic formulations are adding price competition. The outcome is legal and commercial, and it is the single largest swing factor for PI's export business. A price war in pyroxasulfone would hit both the volume PI supplies and the price Kumiai can pay.

India's first own molecule

On 21 September 2026 PI disclosed that Pioxaniliprole, described as "India's first discovered insecticide", had received registration for domestic launch. It is the first new chemical entity in crop protection discovered in India, and a break from a history in which Indian companies made, formulated or in-licensed other people's molecules. The Indian MNC arms remain importers of IP: Bayer's and Sumitomo's margins depend on their parents' pipelines and royalty terms. Commercial scale for Pioxaniliprole will take years and there is no disclosed revenue target; the significance is strategic. Other Indian CSM exposure is smaller and also under pressure: Rallis's B2B business fell 19% in Q1 FY27, while Sumitomo's network exports rose 26%. For PI's pharma-style peers in contract chemistry, see the Custom Synthesis report.

In plain terms

PI makes patented molecules for the global giants under long contracts, which normally means steady, high margins. But when those giants cut their own stocks and lose patents, PI's orders fall too, and because its costs are mostly scientists and plants, profit falls faster than sales. That is what FY26 and Q1 FY27 show. The order book and ₹3,794 crore of net cash are the cushion; the herbicide pyroxasulfone is the risk.

In plain terms

"China+1" was supposed to make Indian contract manufacturing immune to the cycle. It does not. In a destock, innovators cut contract orders first, because they are holding patented stock too. CSM lags the generic cycle by about a year on the way down and, probably, on the way up.

Working capital is the quality test Deep dive

Crop protection is sold on credit and stocked for a season. The companies that turn profit into cash are the ones the market trusts; UPL's balance sheet breathes in and out with Brazilian harvests.

Every pesticide sale is a loan. In India dealers get 60–120 days and season-end "liquidation" schemes, and unsold stock comes back as returns. In Brazil farmers buy on harvest ("safra") credit of 180–360 days, sometimes settled in grain, and distributors' balance sheets sit between the manufacturer and the farmer; the 2023–24 downturn pushed several of them into stress, and AgroGalaxy filed for judicial recovery in 2024 (a detail from memory, unverified). Inventory is just as seasonal: product has to be in the channel before the pest arrives, and seeds and imported formulations are bought months ahead. So a company's profit and its cash can diverge sharply, and the size of the gap is the best single test of the quality of its earnings.

Seeds and biologicals: the growth layers Deep dive

Hybrid seed and biological products have no Chinese generic equivalent, are rebought every season and are growing faster than chemistry. They are why UPL, Bayer and Rallis look different from pure pesticide makers.

Three of the six companies own seed businesses that sit inside their reported numbers. Biologicals: small, fast and helped by tax

Biologicals (microbial, botanical, peptide and pheromone products) and biostimulants are the fastest-growing niche in crop protection. The rulebook: a 1968 Act, a draft that never passes, and trade wars both ways

Registration is the industry's moat and its bottleneck. The rest of the rulebook (bans, GST, anti-dumping duties, foreign tariffs) shifts margins between companies more than it changes total demand.

Indian crop protection is still governed by the Insecticides Act of 1968 and the 1971 Rules, administered by the Central Insecticides Board and Registration Committee (CIB&RC). Nothing can be made, imported or sold without registration: under section 9(3) for a molecule or formulation new to India, which needs a full data package on chemistry, efficacy, toxicity and residues, or under 9(4) for a "me-too" registration of something already registered, which needs much less. States license manufacturing and dealers. The system is criticised for slow approvals, which protects incumbents with large registration portfolios and frustrates companies trying to bring new molecules in.

Trade policy works in both directions. India's Directorate General of Trade Remedies has put anti-dumping duties on several Chinese inputs (pretilachlor, PEDA, MIPA), which protects Indian makers of those chemicals and raises costs for formulators who import them. China has retaliated with duties of 48.4–166.2% on Indian cypermethrin from May 2025. The United States' 10% Section 301 duty since July 2026 applies to UPL's and Sharda's American sales, though much of PI's CSM output goes to innovators' plants outside the US. Europe's registration regime is not a tariff but works like one: rising dossier costs and withdrawals of approved molecules are the main risk to a registration-led model.

The drone rules matter more than they look. Since 2022 the CIB&RC has issued interim procedures and crop-by-crop label extensions for applying registered pesticides by drone, and the "Namo Drone Didi" scheme aimed to put 15,000 drones with women's self-help groups over 2023–26 (status not re-verified; INDICATIVE). Drones cover ground faster and use less water, which favours concentrated suspension and oil-dispersion formulations and products with drone labels. A company that gets drone labels early gains a channel advantage.

In plain terms

India still regulates pesticides under a 1968 law, and the new law has been in draft since 2008. The feared mass ban of 27 molecules ended with four banned. Chemical pesticides still pay 18% GST while listed biologicals pay 5%. The trade rules cut both ways: India taxes some Chinese inputs, China taxes Indian cypermethrin, and the US taxes Indian goods at 10%. None of this changes how much farmers need; it changes who earns the margin.

In plain terms

A registration is a licence to sell one product in one country. Getting one takes years and money, so holding thousands of them, as Sharda does, is a moat. But a regulator can also withdraw approval for a molecule, and every registration for it becomes worthless overnight. That two-sided risk is why the market prices registration-led businesses cautiously.

Six decades, five shocks

A tragedy, a patent law, a wave of global mergers, a Chinese boom and bust, and a 2026 that combined a weak monsoon, a war and a policy-made price floor.

The modern Indian industry was built by the patent law of 1970, which protected processes but not products and let Indian chemists make any molecule by a new route. United Phosphorus (UPL, founded in 1969 by R.D. Shroff), Rallis, Excel Crop Care, Gharda and many others grew by reverse-engineering global molecules for the domestic market and then for export. The first shock, in 1984, was moral and regulatory: the methyl isocyanate leak at Union Carbide's carbaryl pesticide plant in Bhopal, the worst industrial disaster in history, which has shaped safety regulation and public suspicion of the industry ever since.

The second shock was the switch to product patents in 2005 under India's WTO obligations. Generic Indian companies could no longer copy patented molecules; they shifted to off-patent generics and exports, while multinationals expanded in India by in-licensing and launching patented products. It also created the opening for contract manufacturing: if you cannot copy a molecule, you can make it for its owner. PI Industries' export boom in the 2010s, led by pyroxasulfone for Kumiai, came from that.

The third was the global consolidation of 2016–2019. Sumitomo Chemical bought control of Excel Crop Care in 2016 and merged it into Sumitomo Chemical India by 2019–20. Bayer bought Monsanto for about $63 billion in 2018, and the National Company Law Tribunal approved the merger of Monsanto India into Bayer CropScience on 13 September 2019 (effective 16 September). UPL bought Arysta LifeScience for about $4.2 billion in February 2019 and became a top-five global crop-protection company, with the debt that still shapes its balance sheet. The fourth was the Chinese boom and bust of 2021–24 described in Section 9. The fifth is 2026 itself: an 87% monsoon, a West Asia war, a weak rupee and a Chinese price floor set by policy rather than demand, all arriving while the global channel was only just back to normal.

Reading an agrochem company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers, what each one means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, where the six companies sit, and the traps in the way they are reported.

Every agrochemical company, whatever its model, runs on one equation. Revenue is volume times net realisation, by channel (branded domestic, technical export, CSM, seeds), translated at the exchange rate. Subtract materials, whose price China and crude oil largely set, and you get gross profit. Subtract the costs of selling, of plants and of scientists, and you get EBITDA. Then comes the step most investors skip: subtract the increase in working capital (receivables plus inventory minus payables, often 20–40% of sales) and capital expenditure (plants, or registration dossiers at Sharda), and only then do you have cash. Management controls some of these terms and not others, and the KPIs below are simply ways of measuring each term.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). A domestic formulator sells 50 lakh packs a year at a net realisation of ₹400 each: revenue ₹200 crore. Materials at 60% cost ₹120 crore; selling, staff and other opex cost ₹45 crore; EBITDA is ₹35 crore, a 17.5% margin. At 120 days of working capital it has about ₹66 crore tied up, so if revenue grows 10% it needs another ₹6.6 crore of working capital; with ₹5 crore of capex, pre-tax free cash flow is about ₹23 crore, two-thirds of EBITDA. Now test the two sensitivities. If price falls 5% with volume unchanged, revenue drops to ₹190 crore but materials stay at ₹120 crore: EBITDA falls to ₹25 crore, down 29%. If volume falls 5% at unchanged price, materials fall too (₹114 crore): EBITDA is ₹31 crore, down 11%. A price cut hurts about two and a half times as much as the same volume cut. That is why the volume/price split is the first thing to read in any results.

In plain terms

Sales equal how much you sell times what you get for it, converted at the exchange rate. Take away materials and running costs and you have operating profit. Take away the extra money lent to dealers, the extra stock and the new plants, and you have cash. In this industry the last step can swallow half the profit, so always finish the equation.

1. The revenue bridge: volume, price and FX

What it is. The split of revenue growth into volume (more tonnes), price/mix (more rupees per tonne, including a shift to costlier products) and currency. Formula: revenue growth ≈ volume growth + price/mix growth + FX effect. UPL and the global majors publish the bridge; PI publishes volume; the domestic names rarely do. Worked example: UPL's Q1 FY27 revenue rose 10%: price +3%, volume −3%, and the rest from a weaker rupee. So roughly a tenth of growth came with three per cent fewer tonnes sold. Drivers: volume rises with rain, channel restocking and new registrations; price with Chinese technical prices, brand strength and mix; FX with the rupee. Benchmarks: good is volume +5% or more with price flat or rising; average is volume 0–5% with price within ±3%; weak is both negative.

How the six compare. UPL: volume −3%, price +3%. PI's agchem exports: volume −8% and an implied price effect of about −4%. PI domestic: volume +12% but an implied price of about −8%. Corteva for comparison: volume −2%, price −4%. Sumitomo, Bayer, Sharda and Rallis do not disclose a bridge (n/a). Definition trap: "price" in a company bridge usually includes mix, so a shift from generics to specialty products shows up as "price" even if no list price changed; and FX gains on export revenue can make a falling-volume year look like growth.

Worked example: UPL's company-reported FY26 EBITDA of ₹9,588 crore on ₹51,839 crore is 18.5%; screener's operating profit of ₹9,078 crore gives 17.5%. Drivers: gross margin, operating leverage (fixed plants and scientists at PI), selling intensity and royalties (MNC arms). Benchmarks: CSM good above 25%, average 20–25%, weak below 20%; branded good above 18%, average 13–18%, weak below 13%; global generic good above 18%, average 13–18%, weak below 12%.

How the six compare. FY26 on screener's basis: PI 25.8%, Sumitomo 20.7%, Sharda 20.1%, UPL 17.5% (company 18.5%), Bayer 15.4%, Rallis 12.5%. Q1 FY27 reorders them: Rallis 18% and Bayer 20% (both seasonal highs), Sumitomo 22%, PI 22%, Sharda 17% (company 16.6%), UPL 13% on screener (company ~14.7%). Definition trap: Sharda's company EBITDA strips out forex gains that screener counts as operating, so the two can move in opposite directions (Q1 FY27: company +25%, screener −13%); Sharda's EBITDA is also flattered against peers because registration spending is capitalised and amortised below EBITDA. 6. Debtor days

What it is. How long customers take to pay. Formula: trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: Sharda's 166 debtor days on ₹5,268 crore of revenue imply roughly ₹2,400 crore of receivables (5,268 × 166 ÷ 365); at 100 days it would hold about ₹1,440 crore, freeing nearly ₹1,000 crore. Drivers: customer geography (Brazil's harvest credit, European distributor terms), competitive pressure to extend credit, factoring and securitisation (which move receivables off the balance sheet), and channel health. Benchmarks: domestic good below 75 days, average 75–100, weak above 100; export good below 110, average 110–150, weak above 150.

How the six compare. Bayer 72, Rallis 78, Sumitomo 83 (down from 128 in FY20), PI 88 (up from 43–64), UPL 126, Sharda 166. 8. Net working capital days and channel inventory

What it is. The net amount of money tied up in running the business, in days of sales, plus the related but unreported question of how much stock sits with distributors. Formula: (receivables + inventory − payables) ÷ revenue × 365; working capital as a share of sales is the same number divided by 365. Worked example: UPL's company net working capital of 110 days at June 2026 is about 30% of a year's sales, roughly ₹15,600 crore on FY26 revenue (calculated); the 24-day rise year on year is about ₹3,400 crore of extra cash tied up. 10. Net debt ÷ EBITDA, and net cash

What it is. Borrowings minus cash and liquid investments, divided by a year's EBITDA: how many years of operating profit would repay the debt. Formula: (gross debt − cash − liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA. Drivers: acquisitions (Arysta), the seasonal working-capital build, capex, dividends and equity raises (UPL's 2024 rights issue). Benchmarks: good is below 1 times or net cash, average 1–2.5 times, weak above 2.5 times.

11. Return on capital employed (and ROE)

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) earned on the capital (equity plus debt) tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), averaged over the year; ROE is net profit ÷ equity. Worked example: the ILLUSTRATIVE ₹1,000 Cr company later in this section earns ₹155 crore of EBIT on ₹518 crore of capital employed, about 30% pre-tax. Drivers: margin times asset turnover. A branded, asset-light business (Sumitomo's fixed assets are only ₹577 crore on ₹3,238 crore of revenue; Bayer's ₹458 crore on ₹5,675 crore) turns capital fast; a CSM maker building plants ahead of demand (PI's sales to net fixed assets fell from 1.75 to 1.30 times) turns it slowly; acquisition goodwill (UPL's Arysta) depresses it for years. Benchmarks: good above 25%, average 15–25%, weak below 15%.

How the six compare (FY26, screener). Sharda 30.2% (ROE 23.2%), Bayer 29.1% (23.7%), Sumitomo 22.1% (16.2%), PI 15.0% (11.2%), Rallis 14.1% (10.2%), UPL 10.1% (5.6%). Definition trap: Sharda's ROCE is high partly because it capitalises registrations at cost and amortises them, but owns almost no plants; it is not comparable with a manufacturer's ROCE. Large net-cash balances (PI, Sumitomo) dilute ROE without saying anything about the operating business.

12. Registrations, and registration capex against amortisation

What it is. For a registration house, the count of product-country registrations held and pending does the job that plant capacity does for a manufacturer, and the balance between spending on new dossiers and amortising old ones decides whether reported profit reflects cash. Formula: registrations held, pending, and pending ÷ held; registration capex ÷ registration amortisation. Worked example: Sharda held 3,016 registrations and had 1,027 pending at June 2026 (pending is 34% of held). It spent about ₹505 crore on dossiers in FY26 against guided amortisation of ₹450–500 crore, a ratio of about 1.0–1.1 times; amortisation is about 9% of sales. Drivers: regulators' speed, the company's choice of molecules and markets, and EU renewals and withdrawals. Benchmarks: registrations growing with pending above 25% of held is good; flat is average; shrinking or withdrawals is weak. Capex at or below amortisation (≤1.0 times) means the company is harvesting; above 1.2 times means reported profit is flattered by capitalisation.

How the six compare. Only Sharda reports this. Its net registration intangible rose from about ₹577 crore (FY22) to about ₹986 crore (FY26), with ₹247 crore more under development. The others expense most registration costs or do not disclose (n/a). What it is. The value of signed but unexecuted contract-manufacturing orders, relative to revenue: a measure of how many years of CSM sales are visible. 14. Launches, pipeline and R&D intensity

Formula: count of launches (separating new-to-India 9(3) molecules from me-too 9(4) products); R&D expense ÷ revenue. 15. Valuation: P/E, EV/EBITDA and the minorities trap

What it is. What the market pays for a rupee of profit (P/E) or of operating profit including debt (EV/EBITDA). Formula: P/E = market cap ÷ net profit attributable to shareholders; EV/EBITDA = (market cap + net debt + minority interests) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: UPL's market cap of ₹44,853 crore plus about ₹15,000 crore of March net debt, divided by company EBITDA of ₹9,588 crore, gives about 6.2 times, but that leaves out the minority shareholders who own large stakes in UPL Corp and Advanta, so the true multiple is higher. On P/E, UPL is 20.2 times total profit but 23.3 times profit attributable to its own shareholders (₹1,921 crore). Drivers: growth, return on capital, balance-sheet risk, float and governance. Benchmarks: not quality measures; compare against a company's own history and its cash conversion.

How the six compare (19 October 2026). EV/EBITDA: Sumitomo ~27.6, Bayer 18.3 (on gross debt), PI 16.8, Rallis 9.8, UPL ~6.2 (before minorities), Sharda 5.3. P/E on FY26: Sumitomo 36.1, PI 24.9, UPL 23.3 (PATMI), Bayer 23.1, Rallis 21.6, Sharda 9.3. Definition trap: screener's P/E for UPL (22.7) uses total profit including minorities; Bayer's EV uses gross debt because its cash is not separated, which overstates EV; Sharda's earnings include forex gains that come and go. 18. Definition traps, collected

  • Consolidated versus standalone. Bayer's and Rallis's figures here are standalone because their consolidated screener pages are stale (2006 and FY22). For Rallis that means Metahelix is included (it was folded into the parent), but any future subsidiary would not be.
  • UPL's three profits. Net profit including minorities (₹2,220 crore FY26), PATMI attributable to UPL shareholders (₹1,921 crore), and screener's quarterly figure (−₹73 crore in Q1 FY27 against PATMI of +₹10 crore). Press reports have quoted Q4 FY26's ₹1,294 crore as Q1. Use PATMI for P/E.
  • UPL's two EBITDAs. Company ₹9,588 crore (18.5%) against screener ₹9,078 crore (17.5%).
  • Sharda's forex. Company EBITDA excludes forex gains; screener includes them; reported PAT swings with them (Q1 FY27 PAT −38% while PBT ex-forex rose 16%).
  • Capitalised registrations. Sharda's EBITDA excludes about 9% of sales of registration amortisation; compare on EBIT or free cash flow.
  • One-offs in Q1 FY27. Sumitomo's ₹26.9 crore insurance claim; Bayer's ₹63.9 crore from divesting marketing rights.
  • PI's segments. Pharma sits inside exports; the primer's 80/19/3–4% split double-counts it.
  • Sumitomo's revenue. The primer's ~₹3,160 crore (+0.3%) differs from screener's consolidated ₹3,238 crore (+2.8%); this report uses ₹3,238 crore.
  • Seasonal snapshots. Debtor days, net debt and working capital at 31 March are the low point for UPL; June and September are the highs.
  • "Price" includes mix; "specialty" has no standard definition; a "launch" can be a pack size.
  • EV inputs. UPL's EV excludes minority interests (understated); Bayer's uses gross debt (overstated); Sumitomo's uses a lower bound for net cash.
  • Screener working-capital days for UPL (5) are not meaningful; use the company's 110 days.
In plain terms

Before comparing any two numbers across these six companies, ask four questions: is it consolidated or standalone; does it include minorities, forex or a one-off; is it a March or a June snapshot; and does the company capitalise or expense its registration and development spending? Half the apparent differences disappear once you do.

19. How to read an agrochem company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth and the bridge (1 minute). Find volume, price/mix and FX. Volume up with price flat is healthy; revenue up only on FX or price while volume falls (UPL Q1 FY27) needs a second look.
  2. Domestic versus export (1 minute). Split the growth. Domestic tells you about the monsoon and the channel; export about Brazil, the US, Europe and China.
  3. Gross margin against last year and last quarter (1 minute). A fall means input costs (China, crude, the rupee) are not being passed on; a rise may be cheap old inventory and will reverse.
  4. EBITDA margin and its basis (1 minute). Company or data-provider figure? Forex in or out? Compare with the same quarter last year, never the previous quarter, because of seasonality.
  5. One-offs (1 minute). Scan other income and exceptional items: insurance claims, sales of brands or rights, forex. Restate PBT without them.
  6. Profit attributable to shareholders (30 seconds). For UPL, PATMI, not total profit.
  7. Working capital (1.5 minutes). In half-year and annual results, compute debtor and inventory days against the same date last year. Rising debtors with falling sales is the classic red flag (PI FY26).
  8. Cash flow and net debt (1 minute). Operating cash flow against EBITDA; for UPL, net debt and net debt to EBITDA against the same quarter last year and against the <1.5× target.
  9. The native KPI (1 minute). PI's order book and export volume; Sharda's registrations and gross margin against its ~35% guide; Sumitomo's specialty share; Rallis's B2B versus B2C; Bayer's corn-seed commentary; UPL's Advanta growth.
  10. Guidance and channel commentary (1 minute). Did guidance change (UPL 7–11% revenue, 10–14% EBITDA; PI low single digit; Sharda 10–15%)? What does management say about channel inventory, pricing and the next season? Red flags: "pricing under pressure", "postponement of sales", a guidance cut in the first half.
In plain terms

In ten minutes you can tell whether a quarter was good: check if volumes grew, whether prices held, whether margins moved because of costs or because of old cheap stock, whether profit was flattered by a one-off, and whether the cash came in. The last two catch most of the surprises in this industry.

Technology and disruption: biology, drones, resistance and rules

Nothing here will change FY27 numbers, but each trend decides which business models are worth more in 2030.

Biologicals and peptides are the most direct substitute for chemistry. Microbial, peptide and pheromone products can take share from chemical insecticides and fungicides, especially in tightly regulated markets such as the EU, where chemical molecules keep losing approval. Every company in this group is building a line: PI's Harpin and peptide platform, UPL's NPP, Sumitomo's Valent biorationals and Barrix pheromones, Rallis's soil and plant health range. India's 5% GST for listed bio-pesticides helps. The risk for the chemistry-heavy models is gradual erosion, not a cliff.

Drones and precision spraying change the volume of product applied per acre and favour concentrated, low-volume formulations (suspension concentrates, oil dispersions) with drone labels. They improve coverage and timing, which could raise efficacy per rupee and shift spending to premium products. They also reduce wastage, which over time could reduce volume. Interim CIB&RC approvals for drone application have been extended crop by crop since 2022 (status not re-verified).

Gene editing and traits could cut insecticide use, as Bt cotton did for bollworm sprays two decades ago. India remains cautious: no GM food crop is commercial and GM mustard is in court. Approval of herbicide-tolerant traits would shift demand from hand-weeding and selective herbicides toward broad-spectrum herbicides paired with traits, which would favour companies with both seeds and chemistry (Bayer, UPL/Advanta, Rallis).

Resistance is the opposite force: weeds resistant to ALS inhibitors and glyphosate, and insects resistant to older chemistries, need new modes of action. That is the commercial case for new molecules such as PI's Alcor (for ALS-resistant weeds) and Pioxaniliprole. Regulatory withdrawal can remove a molecule's registrations everywhere at once, a risk concentrated in registration-led models (Sharda) and in Europe. Spurious products and e-commerce cut both ways: online sales and traceability can squeeze counterfeit products and help brands, but enforcement against spurious sellers remains weak. Precision agriculture and digital advisory (Bayer's digital tools, PI's farmer platforms) aim to lock in farmers before they reach the dealer.

In plain terms

Over the next decade, farmers will spray more precisely (drones), use more biological products (helped by lower GST), and need new molecules as pests and weeds become resistant to old ones. Bull vs bear

The case for the sub-sector rests on a cycle that has bottomed and companies that held margins through it. The case against is that prices have not recovered, demand is weak at home and the multiples still assume growth.

The bull case

  • The destock is over: channel inventories are normal, and any restocking from just-in-time levels adds volume.
  • China has a floor: VAT-rebate removal and the anti-below-cost notice lifted technical prices in September (glyphosate RMB 28,250, glufosinate RMB 60,000), helping Indian technical makers.
  • Ex-UPL resilience: five companies grew profit every year from ₹2,034 crore to ₹3,418 crore through the "crash", at 18–20% margins and with net cash.
  • UPL deleveraging: net debt/EBITDA from above 4 times (estimate) to 1.6 times at March 2026; the Advanta listing and reorganisation could crystallise value and cut debt.
  • PI's cushion and options: a ~$1.2 billion order book, ₹3,794 crore of net cash, India's first own insecticide, and biologicals growing 50%+.
  • Structural demand: labour costs pushing herbicides, low per-hectare use, corn for ethanol and feed, and formalisation away from spurious products.
  • Valuations reset: every stock is down 13–39% in a year; Sharda at ~9 times earnings and Bayer at a 4.2% yield.

The bear case

  • Price deflation continues: Corteva guides further generic price declines in Latin America; China's floor rests on policy, not demand, and overcapacity remains.
  • A weak monsoon: 87% of normal, the South and East near three-quarters; Q2 FY27 will show kharif damage and rabi depends on reservoirs.
  • No aggregate growth: six-company revenue in FY26 was below FY23; ex-UPL revenue grew 0.9% in FY26 and fell 1.7% in Q1 FY27.
  • PI's patent cliff: pyroxasulfone generics, a two-year CSM decline, falling asset turns and FY26 cash conversion of 27%.
  • UPL's balance sheet and minorities: ₹23,649 crore of net debt at the June peak, $500 million due in December, NWC up 24 days, management departures.
  • Trade and regulation: China's duty on Indian cypermethrin, a 10% US Section 301 duty, EU withdrawals, and an Indian pesticides law still in draft after 18 years.
  • Multiples still high in places: Sumitomo at ~34 times trailing earnings and ~27.6 times EV/EBITDA on flat revenue.

Weighing the two, the evidence favours a slow, uneven recovery rather than either a boom or a second bust. The inventory problem that caused FY24 has been fixed, and the companies outside UPL never lost their margins. But the price side of the equation is still negative outside India, the Chinese floor is administrative, and the domestic half has just had its weakest monsoon in years. The near-term numbers (Q2 and Q3 FY27) are likely to be weak for the domestic names and mixed for exporters; the medium-term case depends on volumes returning in Brazil and on PI's order book converting.

Within the group the bull and bear cases fall unevenly. The bull case is strongest for companies with balance-sheet room and exposure to a restock (UPL if it delevers, PI if exports turn, Sharda if Europe recovers). The bear case is strongest where the multiple is highest relative to growth (Sumitomo) and where the risk is concentrated in one molecule or one balance sheet (PI's pyroxasulfone, UPL's debt). Bayer's yield and Rallis's net cash give them some downside protection, but neither has shown much growth.

In plain terms

The optimist says the worst is over: shops have run down their stock, China has stopped dumping, and five of the six companies stayed profitable throughout. The pessimist says prices are still falling outside India, the monsoon was poor, and nobody has grown much for three years. Both are partly right, which is why the next two quarters matter so much.
