00

The one-page view: four business models, one stalled pair count

India buys almost three billion pairs a year and still two-thirds of them from the informal sector. The four listed members are trying to win that market with four different machines, and in 2026 none of them is winning it on volume alone.

Footwear looks like the simplest consumer business there is. Everybody needs it, it wears out, and India, at roughly two pairs per person per year against a world average of 3.2, is plainly under-shod. Yet the four listed footwear companies in the NSE 750 grew their combined revenue by only 4.3% a year between FY23 and FY26, and the three that existed throughout (Bata India, Relaxo Footwears and Campus Activewear) grew by just 1.2% a year together. In the twelve months to 23 October 2026 their share prices fell between 22% and 46%. The gap between the obvious long-term opportunity and the disappointing recent arithmetic is the subject of this report.

The first thing to understand is that the four are not four versions of the same company. Relaxo is a mass manufacturer: it made and sold 17.5 crore pairs in FY26, mostly rubber and EVA chappals and sandals, through about 630 distributors and 70,000 small shops, at an average of just ₹153 a pair. Bata India is a brand-retailer with roughly 2,000 exclusive stores, of which about 750 are now franchised, selling close to 50 million pairs a year at around ₹700 each (indicative); it buys about 84% of what it sells as finished goods. Campus Activewear is a sports-and-athleisure brand that assembles all of its 26 million pairs in its own plants and sells about half of them directly to consumers, mostly online, at ₹683 a pair. Redtape, demerged from Mirza International in 2023, is a fashion brand that sells shoes, clothes and accessories through 702 mostly franchised stores and the big marketplaces, and buys about 96% of its goods ready-made, three-quarters of them from Bangladesh, Myanmar and Nepal.

Five forces shape every number in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them.

  • Two policies, one wall. The BIS quality-control orders (QCOs) now make certification compulsory for anyone who makes or imports footwear for India, and a 12 June 2026 amendment gave holders of old, uncertified stock until 31 July 2027 to sell it. GST 2.0 cut the tax on every pair priced up to ₹2,500 to 5% from 22 September 2025. Add an effective customs wall of about 38.5% and the cost of being informal or an importer has risen sharply.
  • Pairs versus price. Mass volumes are still shrinking. Relaxo sold 2% fewer pairs in Q1 FY27 (Apr–Jun 2026) while its realisation rose 10% after price increases of 15–18%, forced by raw materials and a 25–30% jump in Haryana wages. Campus grew volume 11.7%. The first question about any footwear company is now how many pairs it sold, not how many rupees it earned.
  • The margin that is not there. Bata reports a 20.6% EBITDA margin because Ind AS 116 moves store rent below the EBITDA line. Before that accounting change the same FY26 business earned ₹342 crore, or 9.7%. After its Hush Puppies licence payments, 8.3%.
  • A fast grower on someone else's factory. Redtape produced 78% of the group's revenue gain over FY23–FY26, but it is an importer-brand whose other income is mostly marketplace rebates, whose inventory runs at 173 to 306 days depending on the basis, and whose auditor has added an emphasis of matter about a September 2025 income-tax search.
  • Premiumisation into the GST sweet spot. Products priced between ₹1,000 and ₹2,500 got the largest tax cut (18% to 5%), and that is precisely where Campus, Bata's Power and Relaxo's Sparx want to grow. Alongside sit the channel shift to franchising, online and quick commerce; a polymer shock after the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026; small, mostly indirect exposure to export markets and US tariffs; and a valuation reset that leaves Metro Brands, the premium multi-brand retailer, worth 84% of all four members combined.

In plain terms

India is short of shoes but not short of shoemakers. Most pairs are still made in small workshops that pay little tax and certify nothing. Policy in 2024–26 has started to make that harder, which should eventually push volume to branded companies. So far the branded companies have mostly raised prices to cover dearer plastics and wages, rather than winning new customers, and investors have stopped paying for the long-term story until they see the pairs.

The timing of this report matters. Every member's latest reported quarter is Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026). Bata reports Q2 FY27 (July–September) on 3 November 2026, with its call on 4 November; Relaxo, Campus and Redtape have not announced dates and are expected in early-to-mid November (indicative). Diwali falls on 8 November 2026 and the festive season starts later than in 2025, when it began in late September, so some festive buying and trade stocking moves out of the September quarter and into the December quarter. Metro Brands, which has already reported, estimated that shift at 250–350 basis points of Q2 growth. Expect soft Q2 headlines and judge the year on the first half, or on Q2 and Q3 together.

01

How a pair earns: from polymer pellet to a shop shelf in Bareilly

A pair of footwear passes through five hands before it reaches a foot. Who owns which step decides whether a company looks like a factory, a shop or a trading house.

Start with the product. Indian footwear spans an enormous price range. At the bottom are moulded open footwear: hawai chappals, flip-flops and slippers made of rubber, EVA (ethylene-vinyl acetate) or PVC in one to three components, selling for ₹99 to ₹400. Above them sit sandals and floaters (₹300 to ₹1,500), then closed sports and athleisure shoes with a moulded sole and a mesh, knit or synthetic upper (₹500 to ₹3,000 for Indian brands, ₹3,000 to ₹15,000 for global ones), formal and casual leather shoes (₹1,000 to ₹6,000), school shoes and kids' footwear. Redtape and, in a small way, Campus also sell clothing and accessories.

Now follow a pair from raw material to customer. For non-leather footwear, which is most of what India buys, the bulk of the material is petrochemical: EVA for slipper soles and midsoles, polyurethane (PU) systems for comfort soles and synthetic leather, PVC compound for mass sandals and school shoes, natural and synthetic rubber for hawai chappals and outsoles, polyester mesh and knit for sneaker uppers, plus adhesives, solvents and packaging. Leather footwear starts with hides and finished leather from the tanning clusters of Kanpur, Agra, Ambur–Vaniyambadi and Kolkata. The second step is component manufacture: soles are injection- or compression-moulded and uppers are cut, stitched or knitted. The third is assembly, either by direct injection (cheap and suited to mass product) or by the stuck-on, cemented method used for higher-priced sneakers. Assembly is the most labour-intensive step; footwear and leather together employ about 4.42 million people in India, according to the Council for Leather Exports.

Gross margin is set mainly by Steps 4 and 5, not by the factory. A brand that owns the customer relationship earns the retail margin; a maker selling through distributors gives a third of the shelf price away.

The fifth step, distribution, is where the four members differ most. General trade means a distributor selling to a multi-brand outlet (MBO), the small family shoe shop found in every market town; it is Relaxo's backbone (74% of FY26 revenue) and just over half of Campus's. Exclusive brand outlets (EBOs) can be company-owned or franchised; Bata runs about 1,250 itself and 750 through franchisees. Large-format stores and shop-in-shops add counters in department stores and malls. Marketplaces (Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra, Ajio, Nykaa Fashion) and quick-commerce apps take a commission. And direct-to-consumer (D2C) websites and apps keep the full price but must pay for traffic. About 250,000 customers a day walk into Bata stores alone.

The value split on a typical pair shows why channel choice matters more than manufacturing skill. Consider a pair with an MRP of ₹1,000 sold through general trade. GST at 5% takes about ₹48 of the GST-inclusive price. The retailer keeps 25–35% of the MRP for rent, staff and profit, and the distributor 5–8%. The brand is left with roughly 55–65% of MRP as its net realisation, out of which it must pay for materials, conversion, freight, advertising and its own overheads. Through its own store the brand keeps the retail margin but pays the rent and the staff; through a marketplace it pays a commission of 20–35% plus the costs of returns and delivery.

There is a second, less obvious point about who makes what. Of the four members, only two are manufacturers in any meaningful sense. Relaxo's Q1 FY27 profit and loss account shows ₹311 crore of materials consumed against just ₹23 crore of finished goods bought in, so about 93% of its inputs are raw materials for its own nine plants. Campus assembles all its pairs in its own plants in Baddi and Dehradun and makes about half of its soles and over a third of its uppers. Bata, despite its famous factory town at Batanagar, bought ₹1,239 crore of finished goods in FY26 against ₹233 crore of materials, so about 84% of its merchandise inputs arrive ready-made. Redtape bought ₹1,261 crore of finished goods against ₹52 crore of materials: about 96% traded. CRISIL adds that roughly three-quarters of Redtape's product is imported from Bangladesh, Myanmar and Nepal, with its Unnao plant covering the rest, including contract manufacturing.

Bata and Redtape are, in accounting terms, mostly buyers and sellers of finished shoes. That changes how the quality-control orders, input costs and margins hit them.

This matters for three reasons that recur through the report. First, a manufacturer's gross margin looks high because wages and power sit outside the cost of materials; a trader's gross margin includes the supplier's labour and profit inside the purchase price. Relaxo's 66.7% gross margin and Redtape's 47.5% are therefore not comparable. Second, a crude-oil spike hits a maker's material bill directly and within a quarter, while a sourcing brand feels it through supplier price negotiations, with a lag set by its inventory. Third, the quality-control orders protect domestic makers but impose compliance costs on anyone who imports, which, it turns out, includes Redtape and many of the global brands that Metro Brands sells.

In plain terms

Two of the four listed "footwear makers" mostly buy shoes from other people's factories and sell them under their own name. That is not a criticism; it is the asset-light model most global brands use. But it means the footwear sector on the stock market is really three businesses at once: a factory business (Relaxo, Campus), a store business (Bata, Metro) and a trading-and-marketplace business (Redtape), and each makes its money at a different step of the chain.

02

2.8 billion pairs: the world's second-largest footwear market, and still mostly informal

India makes and wears more footwear than any country except China, but per person it buys barely two pairs a year, and two-thirds of the value is sold outside the organised sector.

Start with volume, because footwear is a pairs business before it is a rupee business. The World Footwear Yearbook 2026, published by the Portuguese industry association APICCAPS, puts world production in 2025 at 24.6 billion pairs, up just 0.1%. Asia made 88.7% of it. China alone made 13.7 billion pairs (55.6%), India about 3.0 billion (12.2%, second), Vietnam 1.7 billion (6.7%) and Brazil about 3.4%. On consumption, China used 4.8 billion pairs, India about 2.8 billion and the United States 1.95 billion. India is therefore number two on both measures, and broadly self-sufficient: it produces a little more than it consumes and exports the difference, mostly leather shoes to Europe and the US.

The rupee size of the market is far less certain, and any report that quotes a single precise number is overstating what is known. CRISIL, cited by ICICI Direct and 5paisa, estimated the market at about ₹92,000–95,000 crore in FY22 and ₹1.38–1.45 lakh crore in FY25, a 15–17% compound growth rate that is flattered by the rebound from a Covid-depressed base. In plain terms

The size of the prize is large but fuzzy: somewhere between ₹1.4 and ₹1.9 lakh crore, two-thirds of it informal. The four listed companies together sell about one rupee in every twenty. Over the last three years they did not grow as fast as the market, so the "formalisation" that everyone expects has so far helped other organised players more than the listed ones.

Two cautions on all of these numbers. First, the market-growth rates quoted by consultants in the 15–17% range include the post-Covid rebound and inflation; with volumes flat, nominal growth of 6–8% a year in FY26 is more plausible. Second, the members' "market share" mixes definitions: Bata's revenue includes accessories and wholesale to franchisees, Redtape's includes 34% apparel, and Relaxo's includes 5% exports. Treat the share figures as orders of magnitude.

03

Who buys, and why: replacement, upgrading and the Diwali calendar

Footwear demand has two engines that ran at different speeds in 2023–26: a rural, replacement-led mass market that stalled, and an urban, upgrade-led market that kept growing.

The mass market is a replacement market. A ₹150 hawai chappal or a ₹350 sandal is bought because the old one wore out, and the decision is driven by rural wages, food inflation and the monsoon (open footwear sells in the summer and the rains). Between FY23 and FY25 that engine sputtered. Relaxo, the purest play on it, saw revenue fall from a peak of ₹2,914 crore in FY24 to ₹2,790 crore in FY25 and ₹2,702 crore in FY26, and called value-footwear demand "muted" throughout. Its own disclosures show pairs sold falling from 17.8 crore in FY25 to 17.5 crore in FY26, and Q4 FY26 (January–March 2026) was the first quarter of volume growth after eight quarters of decline. The 2026 shocks (dearer fuel and LPG after the West Asia war began on 28 February, food inflation and the RBI's rate rise to 5.50% on 7 October 2026) have not helped.

The upgrade market behaves differently. Urban and semi-urban buyers moving from a ₹500 pair to a ₹1,500 pair, from formal leather to sneakers, and from one pair to several kept spending. Industry data cited by ICICI Direct has mid-premium footwear (₹1,500–3,000) growing about 13% a year against about 8% for mass-economy. Campus's share of revenue from products priced above ₹1,500 rose from 47.3% in FY24 to 48.9% in FY25 and 56.2% in FY26. Bata's sneaker volumes roughly doubled in FY26 (+109%) to about 12.7% of its pairs, and Campus's sneaker share of volume went from about 4% in FY24 to 12–13% in FY26. Casualisation (the slow death of the polished office shoe) is a secular trend; Redtape rebuilt itself around sneakers and apparel for the same reason.

Three further demand drivers are under-used. Women and children are under-served by the mass brands: Relaxo's revenue mix is 70% men, 25% women and 5% kids, whereas Metro Brands, a premium retailer, sells 42% to women, 33% to men and 11% to children. Every member now has a women's push (Bata ran a ladies campaign with the actor Taapsee Pannu in Q1 FY27). School shoes are a large, seasonal and sticky category: Campus's school-shoe volumes rose about 50% in Q1 FY27 and accounted for roughly 40% of its volume growth. And price points matter more than ever because of GST: after 22 September 2025, a pair priced up to ₹2,500 carries 5% GST, so brands now pack their ranges below that line.

Seasonality and the calendar complicate every quarterly comparison. The December quarter is the strongest for most brands because it carries Diwali and the start of the wedding season; Campus earned ₹589 crore in Q3 FY26 against ₹343 crore in Q1. Metro Brands, which reported Q2 FY27 on 15 October 2026 with revenue up 12.2%, said the shift cost about 250–350 basis points of growth; Campus also noted that marketplace sale events moved from late September into early October. A weak Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) print from any member should be read alongside Q3 before concluding anything.

04

Two policies, one wall: quality orders, GST 2.0 and the price of staying informal Deep dive

Between 2023 and 2026 the state changed the economics of the informal shoemaker and the importer at the same time. The wall is real; its effect on the listed companies is slower and more uneven than the headlines suggest.

For most of its history Indian footwear competed on a tilted field. A branded maker paid GST, registered its factories, met labour rules and printed an MRP; a workshop in Agra or a trader importing from Guangzhou could do none of those things and still sell to the same small shop. Three policy levers have now moved in the same direction. The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) quality-control orders make product certification compulsory for anyone who makes, imports or sells footwear. GST 2.0 cut the tax on footwear priced up to ₹2,500 a pair to 5%, which removes most of the gain from evading it. And customs duty keeps the landed price of finished imports roughly 38.5% higher before any quality rule applies. Together they raise the cost of being informal or an importer. How much that helps the four members depends on which side of the wall each one stands.

The quality-control orders: five years to arrive

The Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT) first notified footwear QCOs in October 2020, covering about 24 products against Indian Standards such as IS 6721 for PVC sandals and IS 15844 for sports shoes. Enforcement was deferred repeatedly through 2021–23 and finally began on 1 July 2023 for large and medium manufacturers and importers, with small and micro units exempted or phased later. In March 2024 DPIIT replaced the original orders with two new ones, the Footwear made from Leather and other Materials (Quality Control) Order, 2024 and the Footwear made from All Rubber and all Polymeric Material and its Components (Quality Control) Order, 2024, with revised standards including IS 6721:2023 for sandals and slippers, effective 1 August 2024. Every factory supplying India, domestic or foreign, now needs a BIS licence and the product must carry the Standard Mark.

Two different deadlines are easily confused, and many headlines did confuse them. The first is the date from which manufacture and import must be certified, which for large and medium firms passed in 2023–24. The second is the legacy-stock deadline, before which a retailer may still sell uncertified pairs made or imported before the rules applied. It is this second date that the 12 June 2026 amendments pushed from 31 July 2026 to 31 July 2027, according to the PIB release of 9 July 2026. The Press Trust of India reported that the rubber/polymeric amendment also gives micro and small non-leather units one more year to comply, which is consistent if the original order tied both to the same date. "QCO deferred to 2027" therefore means the clean-shelf effect is delayed by a year, not that the regime has been softened.

The best evidence that the wall bites comes from the companies on the wrong side of it. Metro Brands, a reference peer that sells many global brands, told investors on 16 October 2026 that "BIS implementation challenges affecting selected external brands have constrained supply readiness" for its Foot Locker and MetroActiv formats, and that FILA footwear is now made locally because of BIS concerns. Redtape, which imports about three-quarters of its product from Bangladesh, Myanmar and Nepal, said in August 2026 that it still holds "some non-BIS inventory" and hoped for "one more extension"; CRISIL notes that most of its suppliers are already certified. Campus, a domestic assembler, calls the QCO an "import wall" that keeps cheap Chinese and Vietnamese sneakers and importer-brands off its shelves.

Shops were given until July 2027 to clear old, unmarked stock, so the full effect arrives a year later than planned.

GST 2.0: the 13-point cut in the band that matters

Footwear's GST history explains why the September 2025 change was so important. From 2017 to 2021 footwear priced up to ₹1,000 a pair paid 5% and everything above paid 18%. From 1 January 2022 the lower band was raised to 12%. Then the 56th GST Council meeting on 3 September 2025 rationalised rates so that, in the words of the PIB explainer, "footwear priced up to ₹2500 per pair now attracts just 5% GST", with effect from 22 September 2025 under Notification 9/2025-Central Tax (Rate). Leather and leather job work also fell from 12% to 5%. The threshold applies to the transaction value per pair, not the printed MRP.

For organised players the more important effect is on evasion. An informal maker that under-invoices or sells for cash gains whatever tax it avoids; at 12% or 18% that advantage was large enough to pay for a lot of inefficiency, while at 5% it is much smaller. Relaxo's management said on its Q4 FY26 call that the cut "significantly enhanced the competitiveness of organised branded players" and drove a "strong recovery in distributor restocking", and Q4 FY26 was its first quarter of volume growth after eight quarters of decline. One wrinkle cuts the other way. Polymer inputs and, reportedly, footwear components (HSN 6406) carry 18%, so makers selling at 5% accumulate input-tax credit: an inverted duty structure. Refunds are available for goods inputs under Section 54(3) of the CGST Act, but not for input services, and the cash is tied up until refunded. Customs: a 38.5% wall that changed its bricks, not its height

The third lever is the oldest. Finished footwear (HS 6401–6405) attracted 35% basic customs duty plus a 10% social welfare surcharge on that duty, about 38.5% in all. The Union Budget of February 2025 cut the tariff rate to 20% but added an 18.5% Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess and scrapped the surcharge, effective 2 February 2025. The effective incidence is unchanged at roughly 38.5% of the assessable value, before IGST is added on top. The same Budget removed the 10% duty on wet-blue leather and extended duty-free input schemes for exporters, measures aimed at the export clusters rather than the domestic brands.

In plain terms

That should move business from small, unregistered makers to large registered ones over several years. Pairs versus price: why Relaxo shrank, Campus grew and Bata stalled Deep dive

Revenue is pairs multiplied by price. In 2025–26 the four members found growth almost entirely in the second term, and the companies that grew pairs did so in different ways.

The simplest equation in footwear is also the most revealing: revenue equals pairs sold times net realisation per pair. Apply it to each member and the four business models separate immediately. Relaxo sold 17.5 crore pairs in FY26 at ₹153 each, about ₹2,680 crore against reported revenue of ₹2,702 crore (the gap is other operating revenue and rounding). Redtape does not publish annual pairs, but its Q1 FY27 deck gives 6.1 million pairs in the quarter. Relaxo sells about three and a half times Bata's pairs at about a fifth of the price; Campus sells half Bata's pairs at a similar price and has grown ten times faster.

In plain terms

Most of the sector's 2026 growth is inflation passed on to customers, not more shoes sold. Relaxo charges about 10% more per pair and sells slightly fewer pairs; Campus sells more pairs but much of that is school shoes; Bata and Redtape are growing in low single digits. A footwear recovery worth paying for is one where the pair count rises, and the evidence for that is so far limited to Campus.

Why have mass volumes been so weak when India is so under-shod? Four reasons, in rough order of importance. Rural and lower-income budgets were squeezed by food inflation in 2023–25 and fuel prices in 2026, and a chappal purchase is easy to postpone by a few months. The informal sector competes hardest at exactly this price point and, until GST 2.0, had a 12% tax advantage. Online-native and regional brands (Walkaroo, VKC, Paragon, Lehar, Asian) took share in sandals and value sneakers. And the listed companies themselves have been pushing customers up the price ladder, deliberately trading pairs for rupees. The policy changes in section 04 work on the second and third reasons; nothing in the companies' control works on the first.

In plain terms

When a footwear company reports revenue growth, split it into pairs and price before reacting. Growth from more pairs means the brand is winning customers. Growth from price means it is passing on costs, which protects margins for a while but can lose customers to cheaper rivals if competitors do not follow.

Polymers, wages and prices: the 2026 cost shock

Most of what goes into a non-leather pair is a crude-oil derivative. When the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026, the input bill jumped within weeks; a step-up in factory wages followed.

For a mass non-leather maker, the material bill is dominated by petrochemicals. No company discloses an exact split, but industry practice suggests something like this: EVA for soles and slippers about 25–35% of material cost, PU systems and foam 15–20%, PVC compound 10–15%, natural and synthetic rubber and TPR 10–15%, textile and synthetic-leather uppers 15–20%, and adhesives, solvents, chemicals and packaging about 10%. Roughly 60–70% of the total is directly linked to crude oil and naphtha. India imports much of its EVA, and Chinese and Korean supply sets the price of several inputs.

How sensitive are margins to polymer prices? A rough rule for a mass maker: if materials are about a third of revenue and 60–70% of them are crude-linked, a 20% rise in polymer prices adds about 4–5 percentage points of cost to every ₹100 of sales before any price action (33 × 65% × 20% ≈ 4.3). On Relaxo's 14% operating margin that is close to a third of the profit. For Campus, with a gross margin around 54–56%, the hit is roughly 3–4 points; for Bata, which buys most product finished and feels the increase through supplier prices with a lag, roughly 2–3 points. The margin that isn't there: Ind AS 116, franchising and Bata's true store economics Deep dive

Bata reports a 20.6% EBITDA margin. Before lease accounting it earned 9.7%, and after paying for the Hush Puppies licence 8.3%. Understanding that gap is the key to the stock and to every store-led retailer.

On 1 April 2019 Indian companies adopted Ind AS 116, the leasing standard. Before it, store rent was an operating expense, deducted before EBITDA. After it, a long-term lease is treated as if the company had borrowed money to buy the right to use the store: a "right-of-use" asset appears on the balance sheet alongside a lease liability, and the annual rent is replaced in the profit and loss account by depreciation on that asset and interest on the liability, both of which sit below EBITDA. Nothing about the business changes; the cash paid to landlords is the same. But reported EBITDA rises by roughly the amount of rent, and a company with no bank debt suddenly shows large "borrowings". Bata's reported operating margin jumped from about 17% in FY19 to 28% in FY20, and screener's borrowings line went from zero to ₹1,249 crore, with no change in how many shoes it sold.

For a company like Bata, whose biggest fixed cost is rent on about 1,250 company-run stores, the post-116 EBITDA margin is therefore close to meaningless as a measure of operating profitability. The annual report allows the old measure to be reconstructed. In FY26 Bata's standalone EBITDA was ₹706.3 crore (20.1% of ₹3,515.5 crore of revenue). The cash-flow statement shows lease payments of ₹364.2 crore (₹246.8 crore of principal and ₹117.4 crore of interest). Deduct them and pre-Ind AS 116 EBITDA is ₹342 crore, a margin of 9.7%. FY25 on the same basis was ₹380 crore (10.9%), so the underlying figure fell about 10% in FY26. Short-term and variable leases (₹96 crore in FY26) are already inside EBITDA, so the total rent-type cash is about ₹460 crore, roughly 13.1% of sales.

Redtape: the fast grower with someone else's factory Deep dive

Redtape delivered almost four-fifths of the group's revenue growth over three years and a third of its profit. Four questions decide what that growth is worth: who makes the product, what the other income is, why there is so much stock, and what the tax search finds.

Redtape Ltd was carved out of Mirza International, the Kanpur leather exporter, with an appointed date of 1 January 2022; the NCLT approved the scheme in February 2023 and the shares listed on 11 August 2023, one for one, with a three-for-one bonus following in FY25. It took with it the domestic branded business: RED TAPE, Bond Street and later Mode, OZARK and, from Q1 FY27, the sports brand Sprandi. Its first full year, FY23, brought ₹1,468 crore of revenue; FY26 brought ₹2,419 crore, an 18.1% compound annual growth rate that no other member came close to. Its FY26 reported profit of ₹241 crore (+42%) was the largest of the four, and its 24% ROCE the highest. At 23.3 times earnings it is also the cheapest. Each of those attractive numbers comes with a qualification.

Question 1: who makes the shoes?

Redtape describes itself as an "asset-light business model with strategic vendor partnerships", and its accounts agree. In FY26 it bought ₹1,260.6 crore of finished goods and consumed only ₹51.7 crore of raw materials: about 96% of what it sells is traded. CRISIL's November 2025 rating rationale adds that its integrated facility at Unnao in Uttar Pradesh accounts for about a quarter of products, including domestic contract manufacturing, and that about three-quarters is imported from Bangladesh, Myanmar and Nepal. Earlier descriptions of an "own-factory" moat, including our own primer, overstate the manufacturing. The model has real advantages (no plant to fill, flexible sourcing, fast design cycles) and specific risks: the quality-control orders require every foreign vendor to hold a BIS licence, the company still held some uncertified stock in August 2026, and the 2024 unrest in Bangladesh appears to have prompted a defensive inventory build. Purchases from Mirza International itself rose to ₹53.1 crore in FY26 from ₹15.0 crore.

Question 2: what is the other income?

Redtape's FY26 other income of ₹132 crore (consolidated) was about 41% of its pre-tax profit, an unusually large share that once looked like a quality problem. The annual report resolves it. Of ₹133.0 crore of standalone other income, ₹86.9 crore was "rebate and discount: online portals" and ₹18.0 crore rebates from suppliers, together about 79%. The rest was claims (₹6.5 crore), foreign-exchange gains (₹9.4 crore), gains on ending leases (₹6.6 crore) and interest and other items (₹5.6 crore). In FY26 Redtape reclassified these rebates out of selling expenses and purchases into other income and restated FY25. That is why screener shows FY25 other income jumping to ₹151 crore and FY25 operating margin falling to 10.9%: on the old basis FY25's margin would have been about 16.8% (estimated), close to CRISIL's 17.1%.

Question 3: why so much stock?

Redtape's inventory rose from ₹764 crore in March 2024 to ₹1,221 crore in March 2025 and was ₹1,152 crore in March 2026, equal to 48% of a year's revenue and 40% of the four members' combined inventory on 23% of their revenue. CRISIL noted that 18–20% of the March 2025 stock was more than a year old. Two inventory-days figures circulate and both are right. Screener shows 306 days because it divides inventory by the cost of goods sold; the company reports 173 days (June 2026), with a target of 150, because it divides by sales. For a business with a gross margin around 45%, cost-based days are roughly 1.75 times sales-based days (1,152 ÷ 2,419 × 365 ≈ 174 days on sales). Compare like with like: on the screener basis Redtape's 306 days compares with Bata's 164, Relaxo's 184 and Campus's 207. Inventory is funded partly by short-term bank borrowing: ₹385 crore of bank debt at March 2026 (₹371 crore short-term), net ₹375 crore after cash, plus ₹334 crore of leases. Redtape is the only member with material bank debt. Operating cash flow recovered to ₹174 crore in FY26 from just ₹4 crore in FY25, and free cash flow to ₹89 crore from −₹104 crore. CRISIL rates it A/Stable (the outlook was cut from Positive in November 2025) with bank lines of ₹615 crore and a downgrade trigger of an operating margin below 12%.

Question 4: what will the tax search find?

The Income Tax Department searched Redtape's registered office and the homes of some directors and employees between 11 and 16 September 2025. The trade-off for FY27 is now explicit. Redtape grows fast because it does not have to build factories or most of its stores: it designs, sources from overseas vendors and sells through franchisees and online marketplaces. Its low valuation reflects those questions, not a lack of growth.

In plain terms

"Other income" usually means interest on cash. At Redtape it mostly means money the marketplaces pay back for selling a lot of shoes on their sites. That is real, recurring business income, but it shrinks if Redtape sells less online, which is exactly what it chose to do in 2026.

Masstige at ₹1,500–2,500: Campus and the GST sweet spot

The fastest-growing slice of Indian footwear is the branded sneaker priced between a chappal and a Nike. GST 2.0 made it 11% cheaper overnight, and the quality orders keep cheap imports out of it.

"Masstige", mass prestige, is the industry's word for branded product priced within reach of a mass customer: in Indian footwear, roughly ₹1,000 to ₹3,000. It is where the casualisation trend, the GST cut and the quality-control orders all point at once. Campus is the purest listed play. Its share of revenue from products priced above ₹1,500 rose from 47.3% in FY24 to 48.9% in FY25 and 56.2% in FY26, while its share below ₹1,050 fell from 26.9% to 20.8%. In Q1 FY27 the above-₹1,500 share was 53.8%, up from 52.7% a year earlier, with the sub-₹1,050 band rising because of school shoes. (A different "58.4%" figure for sneakers above ₹1,500 has circulated; the company's own decks use the revenue-share measure, so this report uses 56.2% for FY26 and 53.8% for Q1 FY27.)

Campus's operating model is built for this band. It runs six owned assembly plants (Baddi I and II and Dehradun for assembly; Haridwar I and II, Paonta Sahib and Pantnagar for uppers; Ganaur for soles), makes 37.2% of its uppers and 49.6% of its soles in-house, sources about 90% of raw material domestically, and turns a design into product in 60–90 days against an industry norm of 90–120. It has also been rebuilding its capacity for higher prices. Assembly capacity fell from about 36 million pairs to 31.1 million after it closed 4–5 million pairs of obsolete direct-injection PVC capacity, used for cheap product, and shifted to the stuck-on method used for sneakers. FY26 production of 26.0 million pairs therefore implies about 84% utilisation (indicative). About ₹230 crore of capex over FY26–27, funded internally, adds upper and assembly capacity; CRISIL upgraded the outlook to A+/Positive in October 2025.

Management's FY27 guidance, given on the Q1 call in August 2026, is the most specific in the sector. Revenue should grow at a "mid-double-digit" rate, roughly half from ASP (+6–7% from Q2 onward, helped by the 8% MRP increase and a reversal of the Q1 accounting effects) and half from volume ("high single digits for sure"). The EBITDA margin, on the company's basis, which includes other income, is guided at 17–19%, and the CEO said he was "100% confident". The equivalent screener figure for FY26 was 16.5% excluding other income (17.5% on the company basis). Campus plans 80–120 new stores, with 90–100 "achievable", after opening 18 franchise EBOs in Q1, the most in six to eight quarters, and expects sneaker volumes to grow about 30%. Its new lifestyle range, Élan by Campus, priced at ₹1,500–2,500 and fronted by the actor Jim Sarbh, sits squarely in the 5% GST band and was in about 100–110 of its own stores by August.

The weak spot is cash. Operating cash flow fell to ₹135 crore in FY26 from ₹235 crore, and free cash flow to ₹46 crore from ₹176 crore, as inventory (₹474 crore against ₹403 crore) and direct-to-consumer working capital grew; working-capital days have risen from 69 in FY22 to 109. Campus is "low debt", not net cash: it had ₹36 crore of short-term bank borrowing against ₹4 crore of cash in March 2026 (net bank debt ₹32 crore), plus ₹200 crore of lease liabilities. And its FY26 lease payments (₹117 crore against ₹39 crore in FY25) were inflated by terminations and modifications, so a pre-Ind AS 116 margin should not be computed from them; on FY25's lease cash it would have been about 12.9% (indicative).

The masstige band is also where the competition is fiercest. Global brands are pricing entry ranges at ₹2,000–3,000; Asian Footwears and other online-native brands sell value sneakers below ₹1,000; Relaxo has extended Sparx from ₹999–1,700 towards ₹2,500; Bata's Power brand grew in Q1 FY27 while North Star was a drag and is being relaunched; Redtape's Sprandi launched in late September. The GST cliff at ₹2,500 caps the band's upper price for everyone, which compresses differentiation. Campus's advantage is speed and price-point engineering, not a protected position.

In plain terms

The sweet spot in Indian footwear is the branded sneaker or sandal priced between ₹1,000 and ₹2,500: aspirational enough to command a brand premium, cheap enough for a mass buyer, and now taxed at only 5%. Campus is built to sell exactly that product. The risk is that everyone else, from Nike's cheaper ranges to online start-ups and Relaxo's Sparx, has noticed the same thing.

Stores, screens and shelves: how footwear reaches the customer

The choice of channel decides how much of the shelf price a brand keeps, how much capital it ties up and who carries the stock. All four members changed their channel mix in 2025–26.

Each member leans on a different channel. Relaxo is a general-trade business: 74% of FY26 revenue came through distributors and small shops, 12% from its own and franchised stores (429 EBOs at June 2026), 9% from "new channels" (e-commerce and modern trade) and 5% from exports. Campus is split between trade (52.1% in FY26) and direct-to-consumer (47.9%: 36.8% online, 11.0% through its 305-plus EBOs and 2,350-plus large-format store counters). Bata sells mainly through its own and franchised stores and 17,000-plus multi-brand outlets in 1,678 towns, with digital at 14% of turnover in Q1 FY27; it does not disclose the split between company stores, franchise wholesale and MBO distribution. Redtape sells through its 702 stores, with e-commerce at about 22% of Q1 FY27 revenue (30% in FY26).

Ninety years in one timeline: from Batanagar to the BIS mark

Indian organised footwear has gone through four eras: the Bata monopoly, the rubber-chappal boom, the sports-shoe brands and the policy-driven formalisation now under way.

The industry's history explains its shape. Bata, founded in Zlín in what is now Czechia in 1894, came to India in 1931–32 and built the company town of Batanagar near Kolkata in 1934; for four decades it was organised Indian footwear, and it listed as Bata India in 1973. The second era belonged to mass rubber and plastic: Relaxo was founded in Delhi in 1976 by the Dua brothers, grew on Hawaii chappals, listed in 1995 and moved up-market with Flite and Sparx in the 2000s. Mirza International, founded in Kanpur in 1979, launched the RED TAPE brand in 1996 alongside its export business. The third era, from about 2005, brought organised sports shoes at Indian prices: Campus Activewear was incorporated in 2008, attracted private-equity capital from TPG and QRG in 2017 and listed in May 2022; Metro Brands listed in December 2021.

Three shocks then reshaped the economics. Ind AS 116 in April 2019 changed how every retailer reports profit. Covid in FY21 halved Bata's revenue to ₹1,708 crore and pushed it to a ₹89 crore loss, while Relaxo, selling cheap essentials made from cheap inputs, hit a record 21% operating margin and ₹292 crore of profit. The 2022 input-cost spike after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, together with the increase in GST on footwear under ₹1,000 from 5% to 12% on 1 January 2022, cut Relaxo's margin to 12.3% in FY23. The fourth era, formalisation by policy, began with the enforcement of the quality orders in 2023–24 and GST 2.0 in September 2025, and has run straight into the 2026 West Asia cost shock and a sharp de-rating.

Reading a footwear company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about a footwear company. Each one has a formula, a benchmark and a trap, and the four members define half of them differently.

The equation: pairs, price, margin and the fixed costs in between

Every footwear business, whether it makes chappals or sells sneakers in malls, runs on the same equation. Revenue is the number of pairs sold multiplied by the net realisation per pair, summed across channels. Gross profit is revenue multiplied by the gross margin, which depends on what the pair cost to make or buy and how much was given away in discounts. Take away the largely fixed costs of running the business (staff, advertising, rent, freight, overheads and, for some, fees to a parent) and what is left is EBITDA. Since 2019 Indian accounts have moved long-term rent below EBITDA, so there are two EBITDAs: the reported, post-Ind AS 116 one and the economically meaningful pre-116 one that still deducts rent.

Because the costs after gross margin are largely fixed, small changes in pairs or price swing EBITDA several times as much. That operating leverage is why a flat year is so damaging for a store-heavy business.

A worked example with round numbers shows the leverage (ILLUSTRATIVE). A brand sells 1 crore pairs at a net ₹500 each: revenue ₹500 crore. At a 55% gross margin, gross profit is ₹275 crore. Staff cost ₹50 crore (10%), advertising ₹20 crore (4%), store rent ₹50 crore (10%) and freight, power and other overheads ₹75 crore (15%), of which about a third varies with sales. Pre-116 EBITDA is ₹80 crore, a 16% margin; if all the rent is on long leases, reported EBITDA is ₹130 crore, or 26%. Now sell 5% more pairs at the same price: revenue rises ₹25 crore, gross profit ₹13.75 crore, variable overheads about ₹1.25 crore, so EBITDA rises about ₹12.5 crore, or 16%. Raise the price 5% instead without losing any pairs: revenue rises ₹25 crore with no extra cost of goods, so EBITDA rises about ₹23.75 crore, or 30%. And a 10% rise in the cost of goods (₹22.5 crore) with no price response cuts EBITDA by 28%. This arithmetic is why Relaxo raised prices 15–18% in 2026, and why investors fear that price increases which lose customers will unwind just as fast.

In plain terms

A footwear company's profit is a thin slice on top of a big pile of fixed costs. Selling a few more pairs, or charging a little more for each, makes the slice much thicker; selling a few fewer, or paying more for plastic, makes it much thinner. That is why the first two numbers to look at every quarter are pairs and price per pair.

KPI 1: Volume (pairs sold)

What it is. The number of pairs sold in the period, the purest measure of whether a brand is winning customers. Formula. Reported directly by the company, or derived as revenue ÷ average realisation. Worked example. Relaxo sold 4.2 crore pairs in Q1 FY27 against 4.3 crore a year earlier: −2.3%. What drives it. Distribution reach (number of shops, distributors, stores), product range and price points, rural and urban incomes, the monsoon and festive calendar, competitors' prices and, in 2025–26, the GST cut and restocking. Benchmarks. Good: above 8% a year; average: 3–8%; weak: below 3% or negative. How the members compare. Campus +11.7% in Q1 FY27 (good, though about 40% came from school shoes); Redtape about +3–4% (average); Bata +2.3% (weak to average); Relaxo −2.3% (weak). Relaxo and Campus publish pairs every quarter; Bata gave "close to 50 million a year" and a quarterly percentage; Redtape published a pairs figure for the first time in Q1 FY27 (6.1 million).

The definition trap. A "pair" is not always a pair of the same thing. KPI 2: Net realisation per pair (ASP)

What it is. The average rupees the company receives per pair, net of GST and trade discounts; Relaxo calls it realisation, others ASP. Formula. Revenue from footwear ÷ pairs sold. Worked example. Campus FY26: ₹1,774 crore ÷ 26.0 million pairs = ₹683. What drives it. Price increases, mix between brands and price bands (Relaxo's Sparx realises about ₹390 a pair against Hawaii's ₹78), channel mix (a pair sold online at full price realises more than one sold to a distributor), discounting and marketplace accounting. Benchmarks. Good: growth above 6% a year from mix rather than list-price increases; average: 2–6%; weak: below 2% or negative. How the members compare. Relaxo's realisation fell 2% in FY26 (₹156 to ₹153) and rose 9.9% in Q1 FY27 (₹151 to ₹166) on price increases; Campus rose 6.9% in FY26 (₹639 to ₹683) and 0.4% in Q1 FY27; Bata's is about ₹700 (derived, indicative); Redtape's derived footwear figure is about ₹440 (indicative).

The definition trap. ASP growth from a price increase is not the same as ASP growth from mix. Relaxo's +10% in Q1 FY27 is almost all price; Campus's flat ASP hid an 8% MRP increase offset by Flipkart and Myntra netting their charges from revenue (−2.5%), school-shoe mix (−2%) and a franchise accounting change. A company can also lift ASP by selling fewer cheap pairs, which shows up as falling volume. Read ASP and volume together, never separately.

In plain terms

Average price per pair tells you whether the company is selling dearer shoes or just charging more for the same ones. The first is a sign of a stronger brand; the second protects margins only as long as customers keep paying.

KPI 3: Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after the cost of the goods sold. Formula. (Revenue − materials − purchases of finished goods − change in inventory) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Bata Q1 FY27: gross profit ₹536 crore on revenue ₹979 crore = 54.8%. What drives it. Input costs (polymers, leather, the rupee for imported goods), sourcing terms, full-price sell-through versus markdowns, channel mix (franchise and wholesale sales carry lower gross margin than own-store retail) and, for manufacturers, how much production is absorbed into inventory. Benchmarks. For a brand-retailer: good above 57%, average 52–57%, weak below 52%. For a manufacturer on a materials basis: good above 62%, average 55–62%, weak below 55%.

KPI 4: EBITDA margin, before and after Ind AS 116

Gross margin, operating leverage on fixed costs, store productivity, and how many stores are leased rather than franchised. Benchmarks. Post-116 for a maker: good above 17%, average 13–17%, weak below 13%; for a retailer: good above 25%, average 18–25%, weak below 18%. If a company rents its shops, the accounting rules now let it leave most of the rent out of the profit figure everyone quotes. To compare a shop-heavy company with a factory-heavy one, put the rent back in. Bata's 20.6% becomes 9.7%, while Relaxo's 14% barely moves.

KPI 5: Rent-to-sales

What it is. Total rent and lease cost as a share of revenue, the biggest fixed cost of a store-led business. Formula. (Lease payments on long leases + short-term and variable rent) ÷ revenue; at store level, store rent ÷ store sales. Worked example. Bata FY26: lease cash ₹364.2 crore + short-term and variable leases ₹96.0 crore ≈ ₹460 crore, or 13.1% of ₹3,515.5 crore. Long-lease cash alone is 10.4%. What drives it. Store count, location (high street versus mall), rental escalations (typically 5–15% every three years in five-to-nine-year contracts), and above all same-store sales, because the rent is fixed while sales move. Benchmarks. At store level, good below 10% of sales, average 10–15%, weak above 15%; at company level the figure depends on how much of revenue comes from stores. How the members compare. Only Bata's can be computed from primary data (13.1% all-in). Campus's FY25 lease cash of ₹39 crore was about 2.4% of revenue, but its FY26 figure was distorted by terminations. Relaxo (lease liabilities ₹233 crore) and Redtape (₹334 crore, mostly franchised stores) are low. Metro Brands says rents are "elevated" and has slowed openings.

The definition trap. Under Ind AS 116 rent is not a line in the profit and loss account: it is split into depreciation of the right-of-use asset and interest on the lease liability, both below EBITDA, while short-term and variable rents stay in other expenses. To rebuild rent you need the cash-flow statement (principal and interest repaid on leases) plus the short-term and variable lease notes. A one-off spike in lease cash from terminations, as at Campus in FY26, will overstate it.

KPI 6: Store count and franchise mix

What it is. The number of exclusive brand outlets and how many are run by franchisees rather than the company. Formula. Store count at period-end; franchise share = franchise stores ÷ total exclusive stores. Worked example. Bata June 2026: about 750 franchise ÷ about 2,000 exclusive outlets ≈ 37.5% (approximate split given by management). What drives it. Capital available, the brand's pull with franchisees (their return on investment), rent levels and the strategy for new towns. Benchmarks. No universal good or bad: a franchise share above 50% marks an asset-light model; net store additions of 5–10% a year with stable same-store sales is healthy expansion. How the members compare. Bata about 2,000 exclusive outlets (≈1,250 company-run, ≈750 franchised) plus about 370 shop-in-shops and 17,000-plus multi-brand outlets; Redtape 702 stores, 70.5% franchised; Relaxo 429 EBOs; Campus 305-plus EBOs and 2,350-plus large-format counters; Metro Brands (reference) 1,053, primarily company-run.

KPI 8: Same-store sales growth (SSSG, like-for-like)

What it is. Sales growth in stores open for at least a full year, which strips out the effect of new openings. Formula. (Sales this period in mature stores ÷ sales in the same stores a year earlier) − 1. Worked example. A chain whose 1,000 mature stores sold ₹2,000 crore last year and ₹2,100 crore this year has SSSG of 5%. What drives it. Footfall, conversion, pairs per bill, price, assortment and the festive calendar. Benchmarks. Good: above 8%; average: 3–8%; weak: below 3%. For a chain with fixed rents, SSSG below about 4–5% (rent escalation plus wage inflation) means store profits are shrinking. How the members compare. None of the four publishes a consistent SSSG series. Redtape's Q4 FY26 SSSG was reported at about 17.8% (primer); it gave no figure for Q1 FY27 ("Q1 is not very good for everybody"). Bata's franchise stores have run like-for-like growth "close to high single digits" for four quarters, and its ZBM stores outperformed control stores by 4.6%. Relaxo and Campus are not store-led.

The definition trap. Companies choose the store base, the period (some exclude stores refurbished or relocated) and whether online orders fulfilled from stores count. Without a published, consistent definition, SSSG claims from calls should be treated as indicative.

In plain terms

For a store chain, the most important question is whether the same shops sell more this year than last. New shops can make total sales grow even when every existing shop is struggling, and with rent fixed, a struggling shop quickly turns from profit to loss.

KPI 9: Channel mix (trade, own stores, online, direct-to-consumer)

What it is. The share of revenue from each route to market. Formula. Channel revenue ÷ total revenue; D2C usually means own stores plus own website plus marketplace sales where the brand is the seller. Worked example. Campus Q1 FY27: trade 53.9%, online D2C 32.9%, offline D2C 13.2%, so D2C 46.1%. What drives it. Strategy, marketplace terms and discounting, store openings and franchise conversions. Benchmarks. For a brand-maker, D2C above 45% is high; 25–45% average; below 25% low. Higher D2C means higher gross margin but higher selling costs and working capital. How the members compare. Campus D2C 47.9% (FY26); Relaxo 74% general trade, 12% own retail, 9% new channels; Bata digital 14% of turnover; Redtape online 22% in Q1 FY27 (30% in FY26); Metro online 14.2%.

The definition trap. Marketplace accounting differs. Campus's revenue fell about 2.5% when Flipkart and Myntra began netting their charges from its sales; Redtape books marketplace rebates in other income; Bata's "digital" includes B2B sales to marketplace sellers (41% of digital). Online growth figures are not comparable across companies without these footnotes.

KPI 10: Inventory days and the cash conversion cycle

What it is. How long stock sits before it is sold, and how long cash is tied up in the whole trading cycle. Formula. Inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365 (screener basis) or ÷ sales × 365 (some companies). Cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days. Worked example. Redtape March 2026: ₹1,152 crore ÷ ₹2,419 crore of sales × 365 ≈ 174 days on sales; about 306 days on the screener (cost) basis. What drives it. Range breadth (styles and sizes), seasonality, sourcing lead times, channel (own stores and sale-or-return franchises carry more stock), and ageing stock that should be written down. Benchmarks. Screener basis: good below 150 days, average 150–220, weak above 220. Cash conversion cycle: good below 100 days, average 100–160, weak above 160.

Definition traps, collected

  • Two EBITDAs. Post-Ind AS 116 (reported, rent excluded) versus pre-116 (rent deducted). Bata 20.6% versus 9.7%; the gap at the others is 1–4 points.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. Screener excludes it; Campus's and Redtape's own EBITDA include it; Campus's 17–19% FY27 guidance is on its own basis.
  • Gross margin of makers versus traders. Relaxo's 66.7% excludes factory wages; Redtape's 47.5% includes its suppliers' wages and profit. Not comparable.
  • Inventory days on sales versus cost. Redtape 173 days (company, on sales) and 306 days (screener, on cost) are the same stock.
  • Stores. Bata's ~2,000 exclusive outlets, Redtape's 702 "stores" (incl. 132 shop-in-shops) and Campus's 305-plus EBOs count different things; never add them.
  • Pairs and ASP. A Relaxo pair (₹153) and a Campus pair (₹683) are different products; Redtape's pairs basis is undefined; Bata's ~₹700 includes accessories.
  • Marketplace accounting. Netting of platform charges (Campus), rebates in other income (Redtape) and B2B digital sales (Bata) change revenue and margins without changing the business.
  • Franchise conversions. Outright-sale franchising lowers gross margin and moves stock off the books; sale-or-return keeps it on and delays revenue.
  • Exceptional items in "other income". Screener folds Bata's FY25 land gain (₹133.95 Cr) and FY26 VRS into other income; reported PAT growth (−59%) and underlying (about −25%) differ hugely.
  • GST threshold. The ₹2,500 limit applies to transaction value per pair, not MRP, and footwear GST changed in both 2022 and 2025, so year-on-year price comparisons across September 2025 need care.
  • Restated history. Redtape's FY23–FY24 margins are on a different rebate basis from FY25–FY26; Redtape's FY22 is a three-month stub, so no five-year growth rate exists.

How to read a footwear company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, then split it. Find pairs (deck or call) and realisation. Revenue growth = volume + price/mix. Price-led growth after cost inflation is defensive; volume-led growth is what you want. Red flag: revenue up, pairs down for a second quarter.
  2. Check the calendar. Was Diwali or the wedding season in this quarter or the last? For Q2 FY27 (Jul–Sep 2026), the later festive season (Diwali on 8 Nov 2026) shifts sales into Q3. Compare half-years when festivals move.
  3. Gross margin and its drivers. Compare year on year, not with peers. Ask whether the change came from price, input costs, full-price selling, channel mix or inventory absorption. Red flag: gross margin up while inventory jumps (absorption) or receivables jump (channel stuffing).
  4. Operating profit on one basis. Take screener-style OP (excluding other income) and, for store-heavy companies, deduct lease payments to get pre-116 EBITDA. Note the company's own "EBITDA" basis and guidance basis.
  5. Fixed costs. Employee costs (Relaxo +17% in Q1 FY27 on wages), A&P (Bata +25%), and rent. Rising faster than revenue means negative operating leverage.
  6. Other income and exceptionals. How much of PBT came from other income, and is it treasury, rebates or one-offs? List every exceptional item (VRS, labour codes, forex on licences, land sales) and compute PAT before them.
  7. Inventory and receivables. From the half-year balance sheet (Q2 and Q4): inventory days and receivable days versus a year ago. Red flag: inventory growing faster than sales for two halves, or ageing stock disclosures.
  8. Channel and network. Store openings and closures, franchise share, online share and any change in marketplace terms. Red flag: net closures, or online share swinging sharply without explanation.
  9. Guidance against delivery. Campus guides 17–19% EBITDA (company basis) and mid-double-digit growth; Relaxo guides +100 bp margin and 4–5% volume over two years; Bata and Redtape give no numbers. Track each quarter against the stated path.
  10. Balance sheet and governance notes. Net bank debt (excluding leases), lease liabilities, contingent liabilities, related-party payments (Bata's group fees, Redtape's purchases from Mirza), auditor remarks (Redtape's emphasis of matter) and changes in management. Red flag: new emphasis of matter, rising related-party flows, or senior exits around results.
In plain terms

In ten minutes: find out whether the company sold more pairs, whether it got more per pair, whether it kept more of each rupee after the cost of the shoes, and whether its stock and debts grew faster than its sales. Then check that the profit did not come from a one-off. Almost every surprise in this sector has come from one of those five places.

The valuation reset: from compounder multiples to "show me the pairs"

In twelve months the four members lost between a fifth and nearly half of their market value. They are still not cheap by global standards, and the market now pays only for growth it can see.

Until 2024 Indian footwear stocks were priced as consumer compounders: under-penetrated market, rising incomes, formalisation, therefore 50–100 times earnings. The 2025–26 numbers broke that frame. Over the twelve months to 23 October 2026 Bata fell 46%, Relaxo 37%, Campus 33% and Redtape 22%; Relaxo's share price has fallen about 27% a year over five years. Market values have fallen even since our primers were written in July–August 2026: Bata's ₹15,500 crore figure in its primer was already stale (HDFC Securities had ₹8,600 crore on 3 June) and it now stands at ₹8,147 crore; Relaxo has gone from ₹8,555 crore on 1 June to ₹6,950 crore; Campus from ₹6,784 crore on 21 August to ₹5,782 crore; Redtape to ₹5,753 crore. Retail shareholders have been leaving Relaxo and Campus and arriving at Bata as its yield rose.

For the sector, two consecutive quarters of positive pairs growth at Relaxo and Bata after their 2026 price increases, which would show that the policy changes are finally moving volume to organised brands. For Redtape, closure of the tax search without a material demand, inventory falling towards the 150-day target and offline growth replacing online. In plain terms

Footwear shares used to be priced as if India's shoe market would grow fast for decades and the big brands would take most of it. After two years of weak volumes the market has cut those prices sharply, but they are still well above global norms. From here, share prices will follow evidence: more pairs sold, real profit after rent, and clean accounts.

The global lens: exports, US tariffs and a sector one-fortieth the size of Nike

India is the world's second-largest footwear producer but a minor exporter, and the four members barely export at all. The trade war matters to them through second-order effects.

The Council for Leather Exports' 2026 overview, using DGCI&S data, puts India's exports of leather, footwear and accessories at US$4,750 million in FY26, down 1.6% from US$4,829 million. (A figure of US$4.89 billion quoted by the CLE chairman in September 2026 is a slightly different cut; an earlier report of US$5.7 billion for FY25 is wrong or out of scope.) Footwear is just over half: leather footwear US$1,947 million (−3.0%), non-leather footwear US$262 million (+3.6%) and components US$253 million (+3.8%), US$2,462 million in all. The rest is leather goods, garments, finished leather and saddlery. Against India's US$628.8 million of footwear imports, the country is a clear net exporter, but a small one: Vietnam exports several times as much footwear, and China ships about 9 billion pairs a year, 61% of world trade by volume. World footwear exports are worth about US$172 billion, at an average of US$11.65 a pair.

Technology and disruption: data, machines and new channels

The technology that matters in footwear is unglamorous: merchandising software, moulding machines and marketplace algorithms. Each is shifting advantage between the members.

The most measurable change is in merchandising. Bata's "Zero Base Merchandising" rebuilds each store's range from sales data rather than history, on a merchandising platform from Blue Yonder; by June 2026 it covered 775 company stores and about 80% of company-store revenue, and ZBM stores beat control stores by 4.6% in turnover, 6.1% in volume and 3% in bills. Lines per store are now 68% of the level two years ago, heading for about 60%. The financial results are visible in full-price sales (about 73% of sales two years ago, 84.3% a year ago, 88% in Q1 FY27), stock turns (2.54 times against 1.88) and inventory (down 37% from Q1 FY24). A second phase, "Project Elevate", reports results in Q2 FY27. Campus's 60–90-day design-to-shelf cycle and 250–270 annual launches are the same capability applied to a maker.

In the factory, the shift is towards automated injection moulding of EVA and PU, one-shot sole moulding and knitted uppers, which cut the labour content of a pair just as Haryana wages rose 25–30%. Both manufacturers are reshaping capacity rather than adding it: Relaxo cut its stated capacity from 10.5 to 9.1 lakh pairs a day while upgrading two plants for "fashion-forward" product, and Campus shut 4–5 million pairs of old direct-injection PVC capacity while spending about ₹230 crore on stuck-on assembly and uppers. Scale makers can afford the machines; small workshops largely cannot, which reinforces the formalisation trend.

The disruptive threats come from three directions. First, online-native D2C brands (Asian, Neeman's and others) compete on design speed and price, and the marketplaces themselves shape which brands customers see; Redtape's 2026 retreat from heavy discounting and Campus's loss of revenue to platform charges show how much power the platforms hold. Quick commerce is beginning to list slippers and basic footwear, a natural fit for impulse and replacement purchases of cheap open footwear, though its scale for the members is not yet measurable. Second, global brands could move further down-market into the ₹2,000–3,000 band now that GST favours it, squeezing Campus and Bata's Power. Third, policy could reverse: the quality-control orders could be diluted under trade-deal pressure, and the EU's chemical and deforestation rules raise compliance costs for exporters. Footwear is not yet under India's plastic extended-producer-responsibility rules, a possible future cost for polymer-heavy makers.

In plain terms

The technology that is changing Indian footwear is better software for deciding what to put in each shop, better machines that need fewer workers, and online shops that decide which brands people see. The big listed companies can afford the first two; the third gives a lot of power to Flipkart, Amazon and Myntra.

Bull versus bear: the case for and against Indian footwear

The long-term case rests on under-penetration and formalisation; the near-term case against rests on weak pairs, cost inflation and accounts that need careful reading.

The two cases are about different time horizons. The bull case is structural and slow: India buys fewer pairs per person than almost any large economy, two-thirds of the market is informal, and policy has finally turned decisively against informality and imports. The bear case is cyclical and immediate: volumes in the mass market are still falling, the 2026 price increases may cost customers, and several of the members' headline numbers flatter the underlying business. Both can be true at once, which is why the sector de-rated without collapsing.

The bull case

  • Under-penetration: about 1.9 pairs a head against 3.2 for the world; each extra 0.1 pair is roughly 145 million pairs, more than half of what the four members sell.
  • Policy wall: BIS certification for every maker and importer, 5% GST up to ₹2,500 and ~38.5% customs make informality and imports dearer; organised share is projected to rise from ~35% towards 45% by 2034 (indicative).
  • Pricing power shown: Relaxo raised prices 15–18% and Campus 8% in 2026 with limited volume loss so far; gross margins rose at every member in Q1 FY27.
  • Self-help at Bata: franchising, full-price selling, supplier consolidation (~200 bp target) and a new CEO; net cash ₹532 Cr and a 3.9% yield.
  • Masstige growth: the ₹1,500–3,000 band grows ~13% a year; Campus guides mid-double-digit growth and 17–19% margins.
  • Valuations reset: 22–46% price falls have brought multiples down; any return of pairs growth would show up quickly through operating leverage.

The bear case

  • Pairs still falling: Relaxo −2.3% in Q1 FY27 after two years of decline; listed incumbents grew 1.2% a year over FY23–26 and lost share.
  • Price-led growth is fragile: 2026 growth is mostly inflation pass-through; if informal rivals do not follow, volumes could fall further.
  • Headline numbers flatter: Bata's 20.6% margin is 9.7% after rent; Redtape's other income is rebates tied to marketplace volume; company EBITDA bases differ.
  • Governance and leakage: Bata's group fees (~2.6% of sales, technical fees +31%); Redtape's tax search with an emphasis of matter and related-party purchases.
  • Working capital: growth requires stock (Redtape 306 days, Campus 207); Campus's free cash flow fell to ₹46 Cr.
  • Competition and policy risk: global brands moving down to ₹2,000–3,000, D2C natives, marketplace power, possible QCO dilution in trade deals; multiples of 23–47× still above global peers.

Weighing them, the evidence in late 2026 favours patience over conviction. The policy changes are real and the members' gross margins and pricing show they have some power, but the decisive variable, organised pairs growth, has appeared so far only at Campus. The next two quarters, distorted by the festive shift, will show whether Relaxo's and Bata's price increases held their customers. For the wider consumer context, see the diversified FMCG report; for the retail channel, speciality retail.

In plain terms

The optimist says Indians will buy many more branded shoes over the next decade and the rules now push them towards the listed companies. The pessimist says that has not happened yet, that the recent growth is mostly higher prices, and that some of the profit figures look better than the businesses. Both are describing the same companies over different time frames.
