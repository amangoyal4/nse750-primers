00

The one-page view: a digital market owned by gardens, and a specialist that sells results

India's advertising has already gone digital. The fight now is over who captures the digital rupee and how they get paid for it.

Indian advertisers spent about ₹1.5 lakh crore on advertising in calendar 2025, according to the FICCI-EY media report, and 63% of it, ₹94,700 crore, went to digital media, which grew 26% while linear television shrank. That headline settles the old argument about whether digital would overtake TV. It did, several years ago. The questions that matter for an investor in 2026 are different: who keeps the digital rupee, how is it priced, and what happens to the companies in the middle when privacy rules, artificial intelligence and a handful of giant platforms change the terms.

The first answer is blunt. Google and Meta booked about ₹64,100 crore of gross Indian ad revenue in FY25 (₹34,742 crore and ₹29,392 crore), roughly 68% of the digital total. Add Amazon, Flipkart and the quick-commerce apps, whose ad sales FICCI-EY puts at ₹22,000 crore including point-of-sale media, and the share left for everyone else, the independent ad networks, exchanges, demand-side platforms and on-device specialists, is small and contested. These independents survive where the gardens do not reach: ads inside other companies' apps, handset and telecom inventory, connected TV outside YouTube, vernacular audiences on cheap phones, and contracts priced on results rather than views.

Affle 3i (NSE: AFFLE), formerly Affle (India), is the only listed Indian company of scale in that space, and so it is this report's lens. It does not sell impressions. It sells converted users: an app install, a sign-up, a first order. It buys ad space by the thousand impressions on its own account and bills the advertiser a fixed price per verified conversion, its CPCU (cost per converted user). In FY26 it billed 456 million conversions at an average ₹58.9, giving revenue of ₹2,709 crore (+19.5%), EBITDA of ₹610 crore (22.5%) and profit after tax of ₹455 crore. In Q2 FY27 (Jul–Sep 2026) revenue rose 21.2% to ₹783.6 crore and the EBITDA margin reached a record 23.2%.

Six forces define the sub-sector, and the report is organised around them. First, digital has overtaken everything, and growth has moved inside digital, to commerce media, connected TV and small-business self-serve. Second, the walled-garden duopoly of Google and Meta, now joined by retail and quick-commerce media, decides what is left for independents. Third, privacy and signal loss: Apple's App Tracking Transparency (2021), Google's abandoned cookie phase-out and retired Privacy Sandbox (2025), and India's Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, whose consent duties bite in May 2027. Fourth, advertiser-vertical cycles: India banned real-money-gaming advertising in August 2025, and e-commerce and fintech budgets swing with funding. Fifth, AI-driven performance advertising has split the global industry: AppLovin earned an 84% adjusted EBITDA margin in Q3 2026 while The Trade Desk reported its first revenue decline since listing. Sixth, emerging-market currency and the M&A roll-up: Affle bills largely in dollars to emerging-market clients, and on 1 November 2026 it agreed to buy Perion Network for about US$420 million in cash.

Affle's position in that landscape is unusual. Its margin sits with Digital Turbine's, well below AppLovin's, yet the market values it at ₹23,155 crore, 46.6 times trailing earnings and roughly 31 times trailing EBITDA, a higher EBITDA multiple than AppLovin (21.6×), The Trade Desk (9.4×) or Digital Turbine (8.5×) on 13 November 2026. That premium reflects Indian-market scarcity: there is no other listed Indian adtech compounder, and domestic funds now own 18.7% of the company. It also explains the Perion deal. Affle is paying roughly four times Perion's adjusted EBITDA, net of Perion's cash, and asking its own rating to absorb the result.

Three points from the research change the usual reading of this company, and they recur throughout. First, India is only about a fifth of Affle's revenue. Its "India & Emerging Markets" bucket was 73.7% of revenue in Q2 FY27, but its Indian entity's domestic billing was only about ₹538 crore in FY26, which makes Affle's share of Indian digital advertising roughly 0.6% (an indicative figure). Second, the gross-margin dip of FY26 was a return to old levels, not a collapse: gross margin was 37.2% in FY22, rose to 39.1% in FY25 and fell back to 38.0% in FY26 as Affle bought premium inventory and the dollar spiked. Third, Perion is not a low-gross-margin business but a high-cost one: its contribution after traffic-acquisition costs was 46.2% of revenue in 2025, above Affle's, but only 22% of that reached adjusted EBITDA, against about 59% at Affle.

In plain terms

Most of India's online ad money goes to Google, Meta and the shopping apps, because they own the audiences and the data. Affle lives off what is left: it places ads inside thousands of other apps and on phones and TVs, and it only gets paid when someone actually installs, signs up or buys. That makes it a bet on its own targeting skill. It has been good enough to grow about 20% a year with steady margins, and the stock market prices it as a rare compounder. It is now using that rating to buy Perion, a bigger, shrinking, Israeli-American ad company, at a much lower price.

Readers who want the operating side of the platforms should read our internet platforms report; the television side of the ad market is in TV broadcasting; the real-money-gaming ban is covered from the operators' side in gaming; and the messaging rails that carry many of the same brands' customer communications are in CPaaS.

01

How an ad rupee becomes a sale: agencies, gardens, exchanges and the outcome network

Advertising technology is a relay race of auctions. Each runner takes a cut, and the runner who predicts the finish keeps the most.

An advertiser, say a quick-commerce app that wants first orders in Lucknow, can spend its rupee in several ways. It can buy directly from a walled garden, Google's search and YouTube, Meta's Facebook and Instagram, Amazon's or Flipkart's sponsored listings, using their self-serve auctions priced per thousand impressions (CPM), per click (CPC) or per action (CPA). It can hand the budget to a media agency (WPP Media, Publicis, Dentsu, Omnicom, Madison), which plans and buys media for a fee of roughly 2–10% of billings or, increasingly, buys inventory as principal and resells it at a margin. Or it can go to the open programmatic market, where a demand-side platform (DSP) bids for impressions inside other publishers' apps and websites through exchanges and supply-side platforms (SSPs) in auctions that take about a tenth of a second.

Inside the open market there are several specialist roles. DSPs such as The Trade Desk, Google's DV360 and Amazon DSP take roughly 15–20% of client spend. SSPs, exchanges and mediation platforms such as Google AdMob, AppLovin MAX, Unity, InMobi and Digital Turbine's exchange sell publishers' inventory for a revenue share. On-device specialists such as Digital Turbine, InMobi's Glance and Affle's Appnext place apps and content on handsets through deals with manufacturers and telecom operators. Measurement and anti-fraud firms (AppsFlyer, Adjust, Branch, IAS, DoubleVerify) confirm which conversions were real. And outcome networks, Affle, AppLovin, Moloco, Liftoff and InMobi in part, take the conversion risk themselves: they promise the advertiser a price per install or purchase and keep whatever is left after buying the impressions.

An outcome network buys at Step 2 by the impression and sells at Step 5 by the conversion. Its margin is the gap between the two, which exists only if its prediction beats the auction.

The billing basis is the single most important thing to understand about any adtech company, because it decides what "revenue" means. An agency reports net fees, so ₹100 of client billings may become ₹3–10 of revenue. The Trade Desk reports its take rate, roughly a fifth of the gross spend that flows through its platform. AppLovin reports most of its network revenue net of what it pays publishers. Affle reports gross: the full CPCU bill is revenue, and the ad space and data it buys, about 62–63% of revenue, sits below as "inventory and data costs". Google's Indian subsidiary shows the extreme case: ₹34,742 crore of gross ad billings in FY25, of which ₹32,048 crore was paid on to Google Asia Pacific in Singapore, leaving ₹2,694 crore of "net advertising revenue" in India. Comparing revenue across these models is meaningless; comparing gross profit or EBITDA is not.

Where the value is captured. Audience owners with log-in data capture most of the profit: they also control measurement and the identifiers that make targeting work. Among independents, the company that owns the best prediction model and some captive supply wins. AppLovin owns MAX, the mediation layer most mobile games use to auction their ad slots, and AXON, its bidding model; that combination took its adjusted EBITDA margin to 84%. The Trade Desk owns a strong buying platform but no supply and no consumer log-ins, and it is being squeezed between Amazon's DSP and the agencies' own principal trading. Affle sits between the outcome-network and on-device models: it buys from exchanges, but it also owns supply through Appnext's handset integrations and, since June 2026, the AdColony software development kit (SDK) it bought from Digital Turbine.

In plain terms

Every online ad passes through a chain of middlemen, and each takes a slice. Companies report that slice in very different ways: some count the whole ad budget as revenue, some count only their fee. Affle counts the whole bill, so its revenue looks large and its margins look thin next to a software platform. The fair comparison is how much each company keeps after paying for the ad space.

Why outcome pricing matters. Under CPM pricing the advertiser carries the risk that the ad does nothing. Under CPCU the network carries it. That shifts bargaining power: an outcome network can win clients who distrust impression-based billing, especially in emerging markets where ad fraud is common and marketing teams are judged on installs and first transactions. But it also makes the network's gross margin a direct read of its prediction quality. If conversion rates fall because signals are lost, the network still pays for the impressions. This is why Affle invests in fraud filtering (its mFaaS platform), why it insists on direct technical integration with every advertiser's app (it says all of its revenue comes from integrated clients) and why its gross margin, not its revenue growth, is the number to watch every quarter.

The chain also explains the sub-sector's habit of acquisitions. Owning more of the chain removes a toll. Affle has bought buy-side platforms (Vizury's commerce business in 2018, RevX in 2019, mediasmart in 2020, Jampp in 2021, YouAppi in 2023), supply (66.67% of Appnext in 2020, later 100%; the AdColony assets in 2026) and now Perion, which brings search monetisation, display, connected TV, retail media and digital out-of-home screens in the US, Europe and Israel. Each step is an attempt to keep more of the ₹100.

02

A ₹1.5 lakh crore market, gone digital: four publishers, four definitions

The size of India's ad market depends on who is counting. Pick one source per chart and never compute growth across two.

India's advertising market has at least four published sizes for 2025, and they do not contradict each other; they define the pie differently. FICCI-EY, in its March 2026 report, puts total advertising at ₹1.5 lakh crore, up 13.5%, equal to 0.41% of GDP, with digital at ₹94,700 crore (+26%), or 63% of the total. Pitch Madison's February 2026 report has two definitions: an "expanded" market of ₹1,55,105 crore (+12%) that adds small-business self-serve digital and retail media, of which digital is ₹93,156 crore or 60%, and a "legacy" market of ₹1,15,291 crore (+7%) in which digital is only 46%. dentsu-e4m counts digital at ₹71,621 crore, 59% of a pie of a little over ₹1.2 lakh crore. WPP Media, the widest lens because it counts gross platform revenue including long-tail and commerce spend, forecasts ₹2.0 lakh crore (US$22.5 billion) for 2026, growing 8.8%.

Why India went digital so fast. Three things compounded. Reliance Jio's launch in September 2016 collapsed mobile data prices and put several hundred million Indians online on cheap smartphones. UPI made online transactions measurable, so advertisers could see which ad led to which payment. And the business models that grew on top of that, e-commerce, food and grocery delivery, fintech, online gaming and streaming, were natively digital buyers that judged marketing by installs and first transactions rather than reach. The result is that digital's share in India is now close to what it is in far richer markets, even though India's total ad spend, at about 0.4% of GDP, is low by international standards. WPP's India leadership frames that low ratio as the long-run opportunity.

Television is shrinking in the linear sense, not disappearing. Pitch Madison has linear TV ad revenue falling 5% to ₹32,855 crore in 2025, with volumes down 10%. On FICCI-EY's basis linear TV fell about 10% to roughly ₹26,000 crore, while total TV including connected TV was stable at ₹36,200 crore, which implies connected TV of about ₹10,000 crore. Pitch Madison counts connected TV at about ₹6,000 crore, roughly double the year before. Either way the big screen is being bought more like a digital channel, programmatically and with household-level targeting, and that opens a door for adtech companies, Affle included, that never sold a TV spot. The TV broadcasting report covers the broadcasters' side; WPP flags the 2027 IPL media-rights auction as the next inflection for TV budgets.

In plain terms

Depending on who you ask, India spends ₹1.15 to ₹2 lakh crore a year on advertising. The difference is mostly about whether you count millions of small businesses boosting posts on Instagram and the ads inside shopping apps. On every count, more than half of the money is now digital, growing at 20–26% a year, while traditional TV slowly shrinks.

Growth ahead. WPP expects a return to "stable, double-digit growth starting in 2027". FICCI-EY projects the whole media and entertainment sector at ₹3.3 lakh crore by 2028, compounding at more than 7% a year from ₹2.78 lakh crore in 2025. Within advertising, digital should keep growing in the high teens or low twenties for several years, driven by commerce, video and connected TV rather than by any further shift out of print or TV, which are now too small to supply much more share. For the independents the more relevant number is programmatic spend, which dentsu-e4m puts at ₹30,081 crore in 2025 (+19%, 42% of its digital total) and expects to compound at about 19% to 2027. That is the auction pool that Affle, InMobi, Vertoz and the global DSPs fish in, although a large part of it also runs through Google's own buying tools and exchange.

03

Where the digital rupee goes: video, social, search and the commerce cross-cut

Inside digital, the money is moving from search and social toward video, commerce media and connected TV.

dentsu-e4m gives the clearest breakdown of Indian digital advertising by format. Of its ₹71,621 crore for 2025, social media took ₹21,057 crore (29%), online video ₹20,004 crore (28%) and paid search ₹16,581 crore (23%), leaving about ₹14,000 crore (around 20%) for display and other formats. Video is growing fastest of the big three, about 22% in 2026 against 19% for social, and dentsu-e4m expects it to overtake social in 2027. Two other figures cut across these formats rather than adding to them: e-retail media of ₹17,601 crore (25% of digital, +56%), which is counted by the seller, and programmatic of ₹30,081 crore (42%, +19%), which is counted by buying method. They must never be stacked on top of the format split.

Commerce media is the structural story. When a shopper searches for "atta" on Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart, Amazon or Flipkart, the brands that appear first have usually paid for the slot. That inventory has three advantages no open-web ad can match: the shopper is already in a buying mood, the platform sees the purchase directly (closed-loop measurement), and the brand's trade-marketing budget, money once spent on retailer discounts and shelf space, can be moved into it. FICCI-EY counts e-commerce and point-of-sale ads at ₹22,000 crore in 2025, up 50% and already equal to 85% of linear TV ad revenue. Pitch Madison has quick-commerce ads alone at ₹4,000 crore, up 202%. WPP expects Indian commerce media to grow 29% in 2026 and about 22% in 2027, and globally it estimates commerce ads passed TV in 2025 at US$178 billion, 15.6% of all advertising.

In plain terms

The fastest-growing ad space in India is inside shopping apps. Brands pay to appear at the top when you search for bread or shampoo on Blinkit or Amazon, and the app can prove the ad led to a sale because it also takes the order. That money is growing two to three times as fast as digital overall, and almost none of it reaches independent ad-tech companies, which do not own a shop.

Connected TV is the second structural story, and it is more open. India had roughly 40 million connected-TV homes in 2025, up from about 30 million a year earlier, on FICCI-EY's count. Connected-TV inventory is sold through streaming apps, smart-TV makers' home screens and programmatic exchanges, and much of it can be bought by DSPs. Affle markets "conversion-first CTV", which links a TV ad to a household's phones so that a later install or purchase can be attributed to the TV exposure, and it collected 18 awards at a CTV industry event in 2026. Whether that becomes material revenue is unproven; the company does not disclose CTV revenue.

What this means for independents. The three fastest-growing pools, commerce media, small-business self-serve and short video, are all controlled by platforms with their own audiences and their own auctions. The pools independents can reach, open in-app inventory, handset and telecom placements, connected TV outside YouTube and JioHotstar, and programmatic display, are growing too, but more slowly and with more competition. That is why the independent model in India is a niche, outcome-priced and emerging-market-heavy business rather than a direct rival to the gardens. The next section quantifies the gardens.

04

Walled gardens vs independents: where the ad rupee goes Deep dive

Google and Meta take about two-thirds of Indian digital advertising, commerce platforms take a fast-growing fifth, and independents compete for the rest.

The single most important structural fact about Indian digital advertising is how much of it two American companies collect. Their Indian subsidiaries file accounts with the Registrar of Companies, and the press reports the gross ad billings those accounts reveal. Google India's gross ad revenue was ₹34,742 crore in FY25, up 11.3% from ₹31,221 crore. Meta's Indian gross ad revenue was ₹29,392 crore, up 29% from ₹22,730 crore. Together that is ₹64,134 crore, up 18.9%, against FICCI-EY's ₹94,700 crore of calendar-2025 digital advertising, or about 68%. The years do not match exactly (fiscal versus calendar) and the definitions differ, so treat the share as indicative, but no plausible adjustment takes it below 60%.

Why the gardens win. Three advantages reinforce each other. In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 Indians spend on digital ads, about ₹68 goes to Google and Meta and another ₹20-odd to shopping apps. Affle earns about 60 paise of that ₹100 in India. Its business is mostly abroad, in other emerging markets and, increasingly, in the US and Europe. The Indian ad boom helps it only indirectly.

Retail and quick-commerce media is the new garden. Quick-commerce apps added a third type of walled garden in 2024–25. Their ad income is high-margin because the inventory is their own search results and category pages, and it is growing faster than their order volumes. Pitch Madison has quick-commerce ads tripling to ₹4,000 crore in 2025 and reaching about ₹6,000 crore in 2026. Brands fund it partly from trade-marketing budgets that used to go to distributors and kirana schemes. For an independent outcome network, the effect is double-edged: the same e-commerce and grocery apps are among the biggest buyers of app-install and re-engagement campaigns (Affle's "E" and "G" verticals), but they increasingly spend their own advertising money inside other gardens and sell ads in their own. The internet platforms report covers the platforms' side of this trade.

The global mirror. The same pattern exists everywhere. Globally, WPP estimated digital and social advertising at US$413 billion in 2025, and commerce ads at US$178 billion. Independent adtech companies live in the gaps: AppLovin built an 84%-margin business by owning mobile-game mediation, a gap Google and Meta never closed; The Trade Desk built a large business buying open-web and connected-TV inventory for agencies, and is now feeling Amazon's DSP push into the same space. The Indian independents are far smaller and more dependent on the emerging-market long tail. The important strategic question for any of them is which gap they own and how long the gardens will leave it open.

Can regulation shift the balance? Possibly, at the margin. The Competition Commission of India fined Google ₹1,337.76 crore over Android and ₹936 crore over Play billing in October 2022, and the appellate tribunal largely upheld the Android order in March 2025. An ex-ante Digital Competition Bill was drafted in 2024 and has since been reworked. In the United States, a court found in April 2025 that Google had monopolised the publisher ad-server and ad-exchange markets; the remedies phase has run through 2025–26. A forced separation of Google's exchange from its ad server would help independent exchanges globally. A Delhi High Court ruling in May 2026 that Google infringed trademarks by letting rivals bid on brand keywords is under appeal. None of these has yet changed the Indian share split, and none is likely to in the next year.

05

The advertisers and their cycles: e-commerce, fintech, gaming and a ban

Performance budgets come from a few app categories funded by venture capital, and they move with funding cycles and with regulation.

Brand advertising, the kind that buys reach for soap and cars, is broad-based and moves with consumer demand. Performance advertising, the kind outcome networks sell, is concentrated in a handful of app-first categories that judge every rupee by installs, sign-ups and first transactions. Affle groups its clients into four letters: E (e-commerce, entertainment, edtech), F (fintech, foodtech, FMCG), G (gaming, groceries, government) and H (healthtech, hospitality and travel, home utilities). Its BRSR says 100% of FY26 revenue came from these verticals. In India and other emerging markets the ranking is E, then F, then H, then G; in developed markets E and G lead, and health is gaining.

E-commerce and quick commerce are the largest and most dependable buyers. They need a constant flow of new users and re-engaged lapsed users, and festive seasons (Diwali sales, year-end) concentrate spending in the October–December quarter. dentsu-e4m reports e-retail ad spend up 56% in 2025, and quick-commerce ads up 202% on Pitch Madison's count. Fintech buys account openings, KYC-completed users and first transactions. It is regulation-sensitive: the Reserve Bank's tightening of unsecured digital lending in 2023–25 and payments-aggregator rules cut some budgets, and Affle flagged "certain areas of impact in FinTech" in its Q1 FY27 call. Entertainment and OTT buy subscribers and viewers, and increasingly connected-TV reach.

In plain terms

Fantasy-sports and rummy apps used to be among India's biggest online advertisers. In August 2025 the government banned them from advertising at all. Affle lost perhaps 4–6% of its revenue, by our rough estimate, and kept growing about 20% because its other categories grew faster.

Funding cycles are the deeper rhythm. Performance budgets come disproportionately from venture-funded companies chasing growth: e-commerce, fintech, edtech, gaming and delivery apps. When capital is cheap they spend aggressively on user acquisition; when it is scarce, marketing is the first line cut, often within a quarter. Its claim to resilience rests on two features: outcome pricing, which keeps it in budgets that are judged on return, and breadth across about 130 countries.

Developed markets are the other demand lever. Management says developed markets are "more than 50%" of global ad spend, against 26–28% of Affle's revenue, and it wants at least 20% growth there "consistently". In plain terms

Who holds the pie: gardens, unlisted networks, global DSPs and a small listed tail

A two-and-a-half-player oligopoly sits on top of a long tail of specialists, most of them unlisted or foreign.

The structure of Indian digital advertising is a "two-and-a-half" oligopoly: Google and Meta, plus the commerce platforms as a group, with JioStar as a large video and sports seller. Below them is a long tail of specialists. Most are unlisted or are subsidiaries of foreign companies, which is why the listed universe is so thin. Affle is the only listed Indian pure-play mobile adtech company of scale. The others that a reader will meet in the trade press are either private, foreign, or listed small-caps with very different businesses or governance records.

InMobi and Glance are the most important unlisted Indian players. InMobi, founded in Bengaluru in 2007, became India's first unicorn in 2011 when SoftBank invested US$200 million. It runs a mobile ad network and exchange; its subsidiary Glance provides lock-screen content on Samsung, Xiaomi and other phones, a direct competitor to Affle's Appnext handset integrations. Google and Jio Platforms invested in Glance in 2020–21. InMobi has been preparing an Indian listing for two years: it is reverse-flipping its domicile from Singapore to India, appointed banks including JPMorgan, Jefferies, Kotak and Axis for a reported US$1 billion offering in July 2026, and talked of a US$4–5 billion valuation. In plain terms

If you want to invest in Indian ad-tech on the Indian stock market, there is effectively one choice. The giants are foreign, the best-known Indian rival, InMobi, is still private, and the few other listed names are tiny, are not really ad-tech, or have had their accounts questioned by the regulator. That scarcity is part of why Affle's shares trade at a high multiple.

Concentration in the open market. Even within open programmatic, the structure is concentrated. Google's DV360 and AdX sit on both sides of many auctions; AppLovin's MAX mediates a large share of mobile-game inventory; Amazon's DSP is the fastest-growing buyer of connected TV globally. An independent like Affle competes for inventory against these and against other outcome networks with better-funded models. That is why owning some supply, as AppLovin does with MAX and Digital Turbine with handset deals, has become the main strategic objective for mid-sized players. Affle's purchase of the AdColony SDK and brand from Digital Turbine for US$4.7 million in June 2026, without customer contracts, was a cheap step in that direction; its value depends on how many apps adopt the SDK.

That has two consequences. Affle's Indian tax rate is not its group tax rate (18.6% effective in FY26). Why AppLovin won. AppLovin owns two things at once. MAX is the mediation platform that most mobile-game developers use to auction their ad slots, which gives AppLovin a view of a vast share of in-game ad auctions and a direct pipe to that supply. The company sold its own games business in 2025 to become a pure ad platform and has been pushing the same engine into e-commerce and web advertisers. Because it reports most network revenue net of publisher payouts, its margin is not directly comparable with Affle's gross-revenue margin, but even on a gross basis it would be far higher.

Privacy, consent and regulation: ATT, the Sandbox U-turn, DPDP and the gaming ban Deep dive

Three privacy regimes, a category ban and a tax change set the signal supply and the size of the pool. They are often conflated; they should not be.

Targeted advertising runs on signals: device identifiers, cookies, app events and log-ins that let a buyer predict who will convert and prove afterwards that they did. Every privacy rule changes which signals exist, and so which business models win. Five separate developments matter for this sub-sector, and the first job is to keep them apart. Apple's App Tracking Transparency (ATT) is in force. Google's Chrome cookie phase-out was abandoned, and most of its Privacy Sandbox replacement was retired. Android's advertising ID still exists. India's Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) regime is being phased in. And India's Online Gaming Act removed an advertiser category outright.

ATT is the precedent. When Apple made tracking opt-in in April 2021, most iPhone users declined, and the industry lost the deterministic link between an ad seen in one app and an install or purchase in another. Meta, which depended on that link more than anyone, said the change cost it about US$10 billion of revenue in 2022. Mobile networks shifted to modelled, probabilistic attribution and to Apple's own aggregated SKAdNetwork reports. Two kinds of business did relatively well: platforms with first-party log-in data, which could model conversions internally, and outcome-priced networks with direct integrations into advertisers' apps, which see the conversion event themselves. Affle argues it belongs to the second group: all of its revenue comes from clients whose apps are technically integrated with its platform, and it bills on a conversion it can verify.

The cookie U-turn. In plain terms

Ad companies track you so they can show you relevant ads and prove the ad worked. Apple made that much harder on iPhones in 2021. Each rule makes tracking harder for outsiders and easier, relatively, for the giants that already know who you are.

India's DPDP regime. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and its Rules, notified on 14 November 2025, phase in over 18 months. The Data Protection Board was constituted immediately. From about 14 November 2026, Rule 4 allows the registration of consent managers, entities with a net worth of at least ₹2 crore that let users give, manage and withdraw consent across services. From about May 2027, the substantive duties apply: clear notice, consent for processing, security safeguards, breach reporting, data retention and erasure, duties for "significant data fiduciaries" and verifiable parental consent for children's data. Section 9 of the Act bars tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising directed at children (under 18). Penalties run up to ₹250 crore per breach.

The gaming ban as regulation by category. The Online Gaming Act is a different kind of rule: it does not restrict how ads are targeted, it bans an entire advertiser category and makes every link in the chain liable, including ad networks, with prison terms of up to two years. The Supreme Court hearing could restore part of the category if the court finds the ban disproportionate, but no relief has been granted so far. For Affle, the ban cost an estimated 4–6% of revenue (ILLUSTRATIVE) and has already passed through the year-on-year comparisons.

Three decades, five cycles: from SMS to AI, with a roll-up at every downturn

Indian mobile advertising has moved from telecom value-added services to app installs to AI-priced outcomes, and every downturn has ended in consolidation.

The SMS and VAS era (2000s). Indian mobile marketing began as telecom value-added services: ringtones, SMS campaigns and operator portals. Affle's corporate vehicle was incorporated in 1994; Anuj Khanna Sohum and his co-founders took control in 2006 and pivoted it to mobile marketing. InMobi was founded in 2007. Revenues were small and controlled by operators, and measurement was crude.

The smartphone app boom (2010–15). App-install advertising, priced per install (CPI), emerged as Android phones spread. InMobi became India's first unicorn in 2011. Affle built its "mobile audience as a service" platform and its first outcome-priced products. Globally, Google and Facebook built the self-serve app-install products that still dominate.

Jio and the next billion (2016–19). Reliance Jio's launch in September 2016 collapsed data prices, and Indian mobile users and data consumption exploded. E-commerce, fintech and gaming app spending boomed. India introduced the 6% equalisation levy the same year. Affle restructured through Singapore, bought Vizury's commerce business (2018) and RevX (2019), and listed in August 2019 through a ₹460 crore IPO at ₹745 a share, as India's first listed pure-play mobile adtech company.

In plain terms

Reading an adtech company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen numbers, how each is built, what moves it, what good looks like, and where each one misleads.

Adtech companies are hard to read because the same words mean different things from one company to the next. "Revenue" can be gross media billings or a thin fee. "Margin" can be measured on gross or net revenue. "Customers" can be advertisers, agencies or apps. This section builds the reading kit from the ground up, using Affle 3i, the sub-sector's one listed member, as the worked case and the global and Indian reference companies as benchmarks where they disclose comparable figures. Where a company does not disclose a metric, the charts say n/a rather than guess.

The equation: what an outcome network controls and what it does not

An outcome network's profit comes from a short chain. It bills conversions × average price per conversion. It pays for impressions × price per thousand impressions (CPM), plus data. The difference is gross profit. Subtract people and overheads to get EBITDA. The terms management controls directly are the price it agrees with advertisers (within limits set by competition and the client's return targets), which campaigns it accepts, how well its model picks impressions, and its operating costs. The terms it does not control are auction prices for inventory, advertisers' budgets, exchange rates, and the privacy and platform rules that decide how much signal its model gets.

A worked example in round numbers. Suppose a network bills 10 crore conversions in a quarter at ₹60 each: revenue is ₹600 crore. It buys the impressions and data for ₹375 crore, 62.5% of revenue, so gross profit is ₹225 crore (37.5%). Staff and overheads cost ₹85 crore, leaving EBITDA of ₹140 crore, a 23.3% margin. If the model got 5% worse, needing 5% more impressions for each conversion, inventory cost would rise to about ₹394 crore and EBITDA would fall to about ₹121 crore, a 13% drop from a 5% change in model accuracy. That leverage is why gross margin is the first number to check every quarter.

In plain terms

Affle's profit is a simple sum: how many paying results it delivers, times the price per result, minus what it paid for the ad space, minus its staff and running costs. The price per result is agreed in advance; the cost of the ad space is not. So a small slip in how well it picks which ads to buy makes a much bigger dent in profit.

1. Converted users: the volume line

What it is. The number of verified conversions (installs, registrations, first transactions or other agreed actions) an outcome network bills in a period. Affle calls them converted users and discloses them every quarter. Formula: conversions billed = CPCU revenue ÷ average CPCU. Worked example: Affle billed 456.0 million conversions in FY26, up 16.1% from 392.8 million; in Q2 FY27 it billed 128.9 million, up 18.2%. What drives it: client budgets, the number of integrated advertisers, the reach of the network (Affle claims more than 4 billion connected devices on a trailing basis), the categories it serves and how much inventory it can buy at a price that still converts profitably. It falls when a category disappears (real-money gaming in India from August 2025) or when a funding winter cuts install budgets.

5. Inventory and data cost: the price of the raw material

What it is. What the network pays publishers, exchanges, handset makers, operators and data providers for the impressions and signals it uses. Formula: inventory and data costs ÷ revenue. 7. EBITDA margin, and how much gross profit reaches it

Putting the KPIs together: FY26 in one paragraph each

Growth. Revenue rose 19.5% to ₹2,709.3 crore. CPCU revenue rose 19.1%, made of 16.1% more conversions and a 2.4% higher average CPCU; the remaining revenue (under 1%) was non-CPCU. Volume did about five-sixths of the work. In Q2 FY27 the balance shifted: conversions +18.2% and CPCU +4.5%, so price and mix contributed about a fifth of growth, helped by developed-market mix and currency.

Margins. Gross margin fell 1.1 points to 38.0% as inventory and data cost rose to 62.0% of revenue. Employee and other costs fell 2.3 points to 15.5% of revenue, so EBITDA margin rose 1.2 points to 22.5%. Below EBITDA, D&A rose to 4.6% of revenue, other income fell 16.5% on exchange losses, and the effective tax rate rose to 18.6%, so PAT grew 19.1% to ₹454.9 crore, in line with revenue.

Cash and balance sheet. Operating cash flow was ₹502.3 crore after ₹106.7 crore of tax, about 100% of EBITDA before tax and 110% of PAT. Capex was about ₹211 crore, leaving free cash flow of about ₹291 crore. Borrowings fell to ₹11.9 crore and cash and liquid investments reached ₹1,792 crore. Receivable days rose from 48 to 52, and the auditor's opinion was unmodified. On every KPI except gross margin and ROE, FY26 was a good year; on gross margin it was a deliberate step back, and on ROE the drag is the cash and goodwill the company carries.

Definition traps, collected

  • Gross versus net revenue. Affle and Perion book gross; AppLovin mostly net; The Trade Desk books its take rate; agencies book fees. Compare gross profit and EBITDA, never revenue or revenue margins across models.
  • "India & EM" is not India. Domestic Indian billing is about 20% of Affle's revenue (INDICATIVE); the rest of the 73.7% is other emerging markets billed from Singapore and Dubai.
  • The market split is by billing entity, unaudited. The audited accounts show one segment and only an India/other-countries geography split.
  • Q4 FY26's 71.6/28.4 split is a quarter, not the year. No full-year FY26 split was disclosed; use H1 FY27 (73.0/27.0) or Q2 FY27 (73.7/26.3).
  • Average CPCU is a mix statistic. It rises with developed-market share, premium inventory and a stronger dollar; it is not a like-for-like price.
  • CPCU share averages hide quarters. FY26's 99.2% included one quarter at 97.7%.
  • Contribution ex-TAC is not gross margin. Perion's 46% looks better than Affle's 38%, but it converts only 22% into adjusted EBITDA.
  • Adjusted EBITDA excludes stock compensation at US peers. Affle's company EBITDA does not.
  • Reserves are not cash. Affle's book equity of ₹3,652 crore (Mar-26) is not its treasury; cash and liquid investments were ₹1,792 crore.
  • Working-capital days can jump on a reclassification. Affle's 25 → 82 days was mostly deposits moved into "other financial assets"; receivable days went 48 → 52.
  • Low-tax years flatter cash conversion. FY25 taxes paid were ₹4.3 crore against ₹106.7 crore in FY26.
  • Reported profit and normalised profit differ. Affle's FY22 PAT was ₹214–215 crore reported and ₹183.4 crore normalised by the company.

How to read an adtech company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its YoY growth. Compare with management's ~20% organic guide. Check the base quarter for one-offs (Q2 FY26 had extra non-CPCU revenue). Red flag: growth below 15% for two quarters without a named cause.
  2. Conversions and average CPCU. Split growth into volume and price/mix. Healthy: volume doing most of the work. Red flag: falling conversions masked by a rising CPCU.
  3. Inventory and data cost, then gross margin. The single most important line. Compare with the prior quarter and the same quarter last year; management's recovery timeline points to FY28. Red flag: gross margin below 36% without a currency explanation.
  4. Employee and other costs as % of revenue. They have been falling; check they still are. Red flag: opex rising faster than revenue while gross margin is flat.
  5. EBITDA margin. Inside the 22–25% band? Red flag: below 21%.
  6. D&A, other income and tax. D&A jumped to ₹41.2 crore in Q2 FY27; other income swings with currency; the effective tax rate has run at 18–19%. These explain most gaps between EBITDA and PAT growth.
  7. Market mix. India & EM vs DM share and growth. After Perion closes, expect developed markets to become the majority.
  8. Cash flow and receivables. OCF/PAT against the 80–85% guide; receivable and contract-asset growth against revenue growth. Red flag: receivables growing twice as fast as revenue.
  9. Balance sheet. Cash and liquid investments, borrowings, goodwill. After Perion, track net debt (Affle says it will be net-debt free post deal) and new goodwill.
  10. Corporate actions and disclosures. Warrant conversions (about ₹489 crore still due), the Bobble write-down decision, acquisitions and any change in segment reporting. Red flag: a change in KPI definitions without a reconciliation.

CPCU unit economics: volume, price, inventory and the margin squeeze Deep dive

"Paid only when it works" is a promise to the advertiser and a risk for the network. This section takes the CPCU model apart, conversion by conversion.

Cost per converted user is Affle's billing unit and its whole business model: more than 99% of revenue in every recent quarter but one. The advertiser sets a target cost for an outcome, for example ₹60 for a first order on a quick-commerce app, and integrates its app with Affle's platform so that conversions can be verified. Affle then buys impressions across exchanges, handset and operator inventory and its own supply, optimises bids with its models, filters fraudulent installs and clicks with its mFaaS system, and bills only for verified conversions. The margin is the gap between the CPCU it bills and what it spent on impressions and data to produce each conversion.

Per conversion, the arithmetic is thin. In FY26 Affle earned ₹59.4 of revenue per conversion (₹58.9 on CPCU revenue alone), spent ₹36.8 on inventory and data, kept ₹22.6 of gross profit, spent ₹9.2 on staff and overheads and earned ₹13.4 of EBITDA per conversion, about ₹10.0 after tax. In Q2 FY27 the figures were ₹60.8 of revenue, ₹38.0 of inventory, ₹22.8 of gross profit and ₹14.1 of EBITDA. The structure has barely changed; what changed is that each conversion now costs about ₹1.2 more to produce and sells for about ₹1.4 more.

In plain terms

Until last year, almost all of Affle's growth came from delivering more customers to its clients at roughly the same price each. Since then, a growing share has come from a higher average price per customer, mostly because more of its business is in richer countries and because the dollar rose. That is fine, but it is not the same as clients agreeing to pay more.

One campaign, worked through (ILLUSTRATIVE). An Indian quick-commerce app agrees to pay ₹60 per first order. Affle bids on impressions at an effective ₹40 per thousand, or 4 paise each. If one impression in 1,100 converts (about 0.09%), each conversion costs 4 paise × 1,100 = ₹44, and Affle keeps ₹16, a 27% gross margin. If the model improves so that one in 700 converts, the cost falls to ₹28 and the margin rises to 53%. If it worsens to one in 1,600, the cost is ₹64 and every conversion loses money. A 30% swing in conversion rate moves this campaign from a loss to a margin above 50%. The same campaign in a developed market might carry a CPCU of ₹300 or more and a CPM of ₹200 or more, with fewer impressions per conversion; the CEO's rule of thumb is that emerging-market campaigns need about five times as much traffic per rupee.

Why the gross margin fell, and why management says it will recover. Gross margin peaked at 39.4% in Q4 FY25 and fell to 36.7% in Q4 FY26 before recovering to 37.5% in Q2 FY27. The supply lever. AdColony's SDK is management's main margin tool. Affle bought the SDK, platform, brand and domain from Digital Turbine under an asset purchase agreement dated 15 June 2026, through its Dubai subsidiary, for US$4.7 million in cash (about ₹44 crore), without customer contracts. The aim is to activate 100,000 apps and 500 million developed-market devices on the SDK, so that Affle buys more inventory directly from app developers rather than through exchanges, cutting a layer of fees. Management said in August 2026 that "within this financial year, we will see better margin expansion, especially in developed markets". That claim is testable in the Q3 and Q4 FY27 gross margins.

The fraud and integration moat. Because Affle bears the cost of every impression and earns only on verified conversions, fraud is its cost, not the advertiser's. Install farms, click injection, SDK spoofing and AI-generated bot traffic all inflate impressions without producing real users. Affle's mFaaS fraud-detection platform, covered by several of its patents, filters these in real time; its requirement that every client be technically integrated gives it first-party conversion data the model can learn from. Both features are hard to copy quickly and are a large part of why the model has held margins through ATT and the gaming ban. Neither guarantees immunity from a better model bidding up the same inventory.

In plain terms

On each customer it delivers, Affle makes about ₹13–14 of operating profit out of about ₹60 billed. The margin depends almost entirely on how few ads it has to buy to win that customer. It has been paying more for better ad space and losing a little on currency, and it expects owning more ad space directly, through the AdColony software it bought cheaply in June, to bring margins back up.

How the model compares with CPM, CPC and CPI pricing. Google and Meta sell mostly on auctions priced per impression or click, with automated bidding toward a target cost per action; the advertiser carries the residual risk. AppLovin sells largely on return-on-ad-spend targets: the advertiser sets the return it wants and AXON bids to deliver it, a model close to CPCU but with far more data. CPI networks bill per install, a shallower outcome. CPCU sits at the deep-outcome end, which earns client trust (about three-quarters of Affle's revenue is billed directly to advertisers) and makes gross margin the shock absorber. That trade-off is the essence of the model: steady revenue and client loyalty in exchange for margin volatility when signals or inventory prices move.

The premium is real, but it is fragile in a specific way: it rests on consistency, and the Perion deal will make the reported numbers much less consistent for at least a year.

Emerging-market currency is the hidden variable. Affle bills most clients in dollars but adjusts prices to keep each client's return constant in local currency, and it buys much inventory in dollars. When the dollar strengthens against emerging-market currencies, as it did in Q3–Q4 FY26, Affle absorbs part of the move in its gross margin; only about a fifth of its business is dollar-to-dollar. After Perion, Affle will earn most of its revenue in dollars and euros, which will reduce the emerging-market squeeze but add translation volatility to rupee-reported results.

In plain terms

Investors abroad value ad-tech companies at 8 to 22 times their operating profit. Indian investors value Affle at about 31 times, because it is the only one they can buy at home and it has been very consistent. Currency moves quietly affect its margins too: when the dollar jumps against the currencies of its emerging-market clients, Affle takes part of the hit.

The global scale gap. AppLovin's quarterly adjusted EBITDA (US$1.84 billion, about ₹17,360 crore) is roughly 26 times Affle's annual EBITDA. Its competitive claim is therefore not scale but specialisation: emerging-market consumer data built over a decade, outcome pricing, direct integration and fraud filtering, and now owned supply. The global lens says those are real advantages in the pools where AppLovin and the gardens are weakest, and insufficient where they are strong.

What the macro backdrop adds. GST 2.0, in force since 22 September 2025, supported consumer spending and so festive-season e-commerce advertising. For Affle the most direct macro links are emerging-market consumer demand (its e-commerce, fintech and delivery clients), the dollar, and global interest rates, which drive the venture funding behind many of its app-first advertisers.

Technology and disruption: the model arms race, agentic AI and the new screens

The technologies that will decide the sub-sector over five years are prediction models, the ad surfaces AI creates and destroys, and the screens beyond the phone.

The model arms race. AppLovin's results are the proof that, in performance advertising, a better prediction model captures the economics. Its gains came partly at the expense of other networks bidding for the same gaming inventory, and it is now pushing the same engine into e-commerce and web advertisers, the categories that make up Affle's "E" and "F" verticals. Affle's response is its own AI stack: ConvergeAI for campaign optimisation, Niko and OpticksAI, agentic tools that run campaigns with less human input, and what it calls "human versus non-human data distillation", filtering bot-generated signals out of training data. Its moat is a decade of emerging-market consumer conversion data and direct integration with every client. The risk is that an AXON-class model trained on far more data generalises well enough to win in emerging markets too.

Generative and agentic AI change the ad surface. Two effects pull in opposite directions. AI assistants and AI-written search answers may reduce the number of searches that end in an ad click and the number of apps people discover through ads, which threatens search-monetisation businesses such as Perion's and, over time, app-install volumes. At the same time, AI makes it cheap to generate thousands of ad creatives and to run campaigns automatically, which lowers the cost of performance marketing and helps platforms that own both the model and the inventory. Agencies are most exposed to the second effect, intermediaries without models to the first. Affle's capitalised technology spending shows the investment it is making to stay on the right side: depreciation and amortisation has quadrupled since FY22.

Walled-garden encroachment. Google's Performance Max and App campaigns, Meta's Advantage+, Amazon's DSP and the quick-commerce apps' retail media all sell automated, outcome-targeted campaigns with closed-loop measurement on their own inventory. They are competing for the same "pay for results" budget that outcome networks sell. Every improvement in the gardens' automation narrows the reason for an advertiser to use an outside network for in-garden reach; outside networks' value lies in reach the gardens do not have.

Signal loss, continued. ATT is done; Chrome cookies survived; India's DPDP consent duties arrive in May 2027; future restrictions on the Android advertising ID remain possible. Outcome-priced models with direct, first-party integrations are relatively resilient because they observe conversions themselves rather than relying on cross-app tracking. But resilience is relative: any reduction in usable signals raises the impressions needed per conversion, which, under fixed CPCU prices, comes straight out of gross margin.

In plain terms

The big technology question is whether Affle's own AI can keep up with AppLovin's and with Google's and Meta's automated ad tools. The second question is whether AI chatbots reduce the number of searches and app downloads that ads are built on. Affle is spending more each year on technology, which shows up as rising amortisation, but it is a small player in a race the giants are funding with billions.

Ad fraud is an arms race of its own. Install farms, click injection, SDK spoofing and, increasingly, AI-generated bot traffic produce impressions and fake conversions. In a CPM model the advertiser bears that cost; in a CPCU model the network does, because it pays for impressions and earns only on verified conversions. Affle's mFaaS fraud-detection platform, covered by several of its 40 patents, is therefore a direct margin defence rather than a feature. The same logic applies to attribution: a network that cannot prove a conversion cannot bill for it.

Connected TV and digital out-of-home move budgets to bigger screens. India had about 40 million connected-TV homes in 2025 and connected-TV ad spend roughly doubled. Programmatic CTV is one of the few fast pools that independents can buy. Affle markets "conversion-first CTV" with household sync to mobile, and Perion brings CTV and digital out-of-home businesses in the US and Europe, including "CTV-to-cart" and "DOOH-to-cart" concepts. Affle also talks of "autonomous or agentic intelligent connected devices" as a future ad surface; that is unproven commercially. Signpost India, the listed airport and transit screen owner, shows the physical side of out-of-home: high reported margins but weak cash conversion.

On-device inventory faces scrutiny. Pre-installed apps, lock-screen content and app recommendations at phone set-up, monetised by Glance, Digital Turbine and Affle's Appnext, depend on deals with handset makers and operators and on user consent. Regulators in several countries have questioned pre-installs, and DPDP's consent rules will apply. Digital Turbine's US$3.5 million revision of revenue-share expense in Q1 FY27 shows that the accounting is also error-prone. The CPaaS report covers the adjacent issue of operator-controlled messaging channels.

Bull vs bear: a steady compounder buying scale, or a premium rating meeting a hard integration

The case for and against the sub-sector, through its one listed member.

Both cases start from the same facts. Indian and emerging-market digital advertising is growing in the high teens to mid-twenties; the gardens take most of it; Affle has grown about 20% a year organically with stable operating margins and full cash conversion; and it is about to double its revenue by buying a shrinking, cash-rich, high-cost company in the developed world. The disagreement is about whether the model's resilience and the deal's price outweigh the integration risk and the valuation.

The bull case

  • Structural growth: Indian digital ad revenue grew 26% to ₹94,700 Cr in 2025 and emerging markets beyond India are growing too; Affle has kept ~20% organic growth through a category ban and a currency squeeze.
  • Outcome pricing wins in a privacy-constrained world: direct integration and verified-conversion billing are relatively resilient to signal loss, and advertisers judged on results keep budgets with networks that deliver them.
  • Margin levers are in place: AdColony SDK supply, AI-assisted operations and the end of the premium-inventory investment phase point to gross-margin recovery by ~FY28 and EBITDA toward 23–25%.
  • Perion is cheap and fundable: ~4× adjusted EBITDA net of its cash, paid from cash on hand, with a 46% contribution margin and an obvious cost-out opportunity; Affle says EPS-accretive in year one and net-debt free post deal.
  • Clean numbers: unmodified audit, no promoter royalty, ~100% pre-tax cash conversion and 52 receivable days, in an industry where those are rare.

The bear case

  • Valuation: ~31× EBITDA and 46.6× earnings, above AppLovin's multiple with a fraction of its growth and margin; any wobble in consistency can de-rate the stock.
  • Integration risk: Perion is ~10× Affle's previous largest deal, loss-making on a GAAP basis, shrinking, partly dependent on Microsoft search, and back-ended in its 2026 plan.
  • AI-native competition: AXON-class models moving into e-commerce and emerging markets would bid up the same inventory and compress gross margin further.
  • Regulation and categories: DPDP consent duties from May 2027, the gaming ban, fintech advertising curbs; the next category shock is unknowable.
  • Accounting and disclosure gaps: no client-concentration disclosure, a pending Bobble write-down (₹134.6 Cr), rising amortisation, and KPIs that stop being comparable once Perion is consolidated.

How to weigh them. The bull case is mostly about the operating business, which has a long record; the bear case is mostly about price and the deal, which have none. On the evidence to Q2 FY27, the organic business is doing what management says: growth of 20–21%, EBITDA margin at a record 23.2%, gross margin off its trough. The open questions are whether gross margin returns to 38–39% by FY28 as guided, whether Perion's cost base can be cut quickly without losing clients, and whether the market keeps paying a scarcity premium once the reported numbers become a blend of two very different businesses.

In plain terms

The optimist sees a steady, well-run company buying a bargain. The pessimist sees an expensive share price and a big, messy acquisition. Both can be right at once: the business can keep doing well while the share price falls if investors decide they were paying too much for consistency that the deal temporarily breaks.

For the sub-sector as a whole, the bull case is that India's digital ad market keeps compounding at 20%-plus, connected TV and programmatic open new pools to independents, and an InMobi listing widens the investable universe. The bear case is that the gardens and commerce platforms absorb nearly all incremental spend, privacy rules raise the cost of every outside signal, and AI concentrates the remaining economics in one or two global model owners. The evidence so far is that both are happening: the pie is growing fast enough for a well-run specialist to grow, while the share of the pie available to specialists is not growing.
