00

The one-page view: a fee on trading that the state keeps narrowing

Retail broking earns a fee on India's appetite for trading. The regulator decided that appetite was hurting people, and spent two years turning the tap down.

An Indian discount broker collects a small fee on a very large flow. Each time a client places an order, the broker collects about ₹20. Each rupee of idle client cash earns it interest. Each rupee it lends a client to buy shares earns it about 15%. For five years after the pandemic the traffic on that road grew faster than almost anything in Indian finance: demat accounts went from about 4 crore to roughly 23.5–24 crore, and India became the world's largest equity-options market by number of contracts traded. The busiest lane, by far, was index options, where young traders with small accounts placed thousands of short-dated bets.

Most of those bets lost money. SEBI's study of FY26 found that 87.7% of individual F&O traders lost money, with a combined net loss of ₹91,685 crore. In that year they paid about ₹25,000 crore in transaction costs, of which about 44% was brokerage. That brokerage, roughly ₹11,000 crore (INDICATIVE, derived from SEBI's percentages), is the single biggest profit pool in Indian retail broking. It is also the pool the regulator has been trying to shrink. Between October 2024 and December 2025 SEBI introduced eight separate F&O measures, ended the exchange rebates that brokers used to pocket, and the government raised the securities transaction tax (STT) twice. The second hike, announced in the Budget on 1 February 2026 and effective from 1 April 2026, took STT on futures to 0.05% and on options premium to 0.15%. None of our six company primers mentions it, and it is the most recent turn of the tap.

The result is a business in transition. Participation fell: NSE active clients dropped 7.1% in FY26, from 4.92 crore to 4.57 crore, the first annual fall of the app era, and the number of individuals trading F&O fell from 106 lakh to 88 lakh. But activity per survivor held up. NSE's options premium turnover fell only 7.7% in FY26 and was up 16% year on year in the June 2026 quarter, while BSE took a growing share. The traders who stayed are larger and more professional. For brokers paid per order, that is worse than it sounds, because fewer people placing larger orders means fewer ₹20 tickets.

The industry's answer to a shrinking F&O pool is diversification, and the biggest and riskiest leg of it is the margin trading facility (MTF), under which a broker lends a client money to buy shares for delivery. The industry MTF book reached a record ₹1.36 lakh crore in June 2026, up 58% in a year. Groww's book grew 161% to ₹4,351 crore in the year to September 2026. That swaps volume risk for credit and funding risk. The other leg is annuity income: fund management, private wealth and distribution. Motilal Oswal now earns 55% of its operating profit from asset and private wealth management, which is why it trades at 31× earnings when IIFL Capital, with a cyclical full-service mix, trades at 19×.

Meanwhile scale is concentrating in one firm. Groww holds 29.16% of NSE active clients and took more than 63% of the industry's net additions in September 2026. It gained clients through FY26 while Zerodha lost 9.95 lakh and Angel One lost 8.15 lakh. The two are now about 36,000 clients apart in the race for second place. The stock market has priced this: Groww's market value of ₹1.26 lakh crore is 51% of the six listed brokers combined and 4.6 times Angel One's.

Five forces define this sub-sector, and the report is built around them:

  • The regulator owns the F&O tap and has turned it down at every lever: who may trade, how often contracts expire, how big a contract is, and what each trade costs in tax (sections 04–05).
  • Participation fell, activity per participant did not, which hurts per-order brokers more than exchanges, and produced an FY26 profit reset followed by an FY27 rebound off a low base (sections 06, 07 and 13).
  • Scale is consolidating to one discount winner, Groww, and the market pays a large premium for it, while unlisted Zerodha remains the profit benchmark that keeps prices low (sections 08–09).
  • Diversification into balance-sheet and annuity income: MTF for the discounters, fund management and wealth for the full-service houses. The quality of that diversification, more than size, sets the multiple (sections 10–12).
  • The market cycle still drives the full-service houses, whose IPO fees, cash volumes and own-capital books move with the Nifty, which closed at 22,780 on 28 September 2026, about 13% below its peak (sections 12–13).
In plain terms

Indian brokers made most of their money from young people betting on options, and most of those people lost. The government and SEBI decided to make that betting harder and more expensive. Brokers are now trying to earn the same money in other ways, mainly by lending clients money to buy shares and by selling them mutual funds and wealth products. The broker that has done this best, and kept winning new clients, is Groww, and the stock market values it at roughly half the whole listed group.

The six listed members are very different businesses that share a licence. Groww (Billionbrains Garage Ventures) and Angel One are mass discount platforms. Motilal Oswal is a full-service broker that has turned itself into an asset and wealth manager with a large own-capital book. IIFL Capital Services is a full-service capital-markets house now passing to Fairfax control. Choice International is a small-cap hybrid of broking, government advisory and lending. Share India is a proprietary-trading house with a broking front. Readers interested in the fund-management side should also read our Asset Management Companies report, which covers Motilal Oswal AMC, Groww AMC and the mutual-fund flows that brokers increasingly distribute, and our Wealth & Other Financial Services report, which covers Nuvama and the wealth houses that compete with Motilal and IIFL for the same rich clients.

01

Four kinds of rupee: how a broker actually earns

"Revenue" at a broker mixes fees for a service, interest on a balance sheet, fund fees and trading profits. They carry different risks and deserve different multiples.

The single most useful idea for reading any broker is that its revenue line adds together four very different kinds of rupee. The first is agency income: brokerage, distribution commissions, fund-management fees, investment-banking fees and depository charges. These are fees for a service and carry no market risk of their own, though their volume moves with markets. The second is spread income: interest on margin-trading loans, interest from an NBFC or housing-finance subsidiary, and interest earned on client money parked as fixed deposits. This is earned on a balance sheet and carries credit and funding risk. The third is annuity income, the recurring part of agency income tied to assets rather than transactions: mutual-fund trail, portfolio-management and private-wealth fees, AMC fees on assets under management. The fourth is principal income: the profit or loss from trading the firm's own capital, which is central at Share India and appears as the "treasury" book at Motilal Oswal.

Investors pay very different prices for each. A rupee of AMC fee income is recurring and capital-light, so the market capitalises it at a high multiple. A rupee of options brokerage is high-margin but policy-exposed. A rupee of MTF interest needs capital and can turn into a loss in a crash. A rupee of proprietary trading profit can reverse next quarter. This is why six companies with the same SEBI licence trade between 11× and 72× earnings.

Who keeps what from one options trade

Under SEBI's "true-to-label" rule, effective 1 October 2024, everything except brokerage is a pass-through. The exchange charge, STT, stamp duty, SEBI's fee and GST are collected from the client and handed on. The broker keeps only its ₹20 per order. That has a striking consequence: the broker earns the same ₹40 on a round trip whether the client trades ₹10,000 of option premium or ₹1 lakh. On the small ticket, brokerage is more than half of the client's total cost. On the large ticket, the state's STT dominates. SEBI's aggregate figure, brokerage about 44% and STT about 27% of individual traders' costs, sits between the two because the trader base is a mix of small and larger tickets.

In plain terms

A discount broker's costs are mostly fixed: the app, the servers, the compliance team. Once those are paid for, every extra active client is nearly pure profit. That is why the biggest app keeps 45 paise of every rupee and the third-biggest keeps 18. Angel One also borrows heavily to lend to clients, and that interest bill takes another 8–9 paise.

Two cautions apply to every number in this report. First, Motilal Oswal's screener revenue of ₹9,381 crore includes treasury gains and housing-finance interest; its net operating revenue was about ₹5,908 crore, and that is the right base for mix and cost ratios. Second, Share India's revenue blends client commission with proprietary trading gains, and the company does not split them. A cost-to-income ratio means something different at each.

In plain terms

Before comparing any two brokers, ask which of the four rupees each one earns. Two companies with the same revenue can be one fee business and one trading business, and the market will price them a world apart.

02

The pools: clients, traders, turnover and leverage

Every headline number in broking has two or three definitions. Name the basis, or the comparison is meaningless.

Broking has no single "market size" in the way cement has tonnes or hospitals have beds. It has several pools, each measured differently, and the industry's commentary routinely mixes them. The comparable measure of clients is the NSE active client: anyone who traded at least once on NSE in the trailing twelve months, published monthly by the exchange. It was 4.61 crore in September 2026. It is far smaller than the number of demat accounts, about 23.5–24 crore across CDSL and NSDL (INDICATIVE; published totals conflict), because most accounts are dormant. And it is larger than the number of people who trade derivatives: 87.5 lakh individuals traded F&O at least once in FY26.

How big is the listed slice?

The six listed members earned ₹24,280 crore of revenue and ₹5,996 crore of profit in FY26, and were worth ₹2.47 lakh crore on 19 October 2026. Those revenue totals are not additive in an economic sense, because Motilal's includes treasury gains and housing-finance interest and Share India's includes proprietary trading, so they are a size picture only. A more useful cross-check is against the F&O brokerage pool. Groww's F&O income in FY26 was roughly ₹2,550 crore (about 55% of ₹4,645 crore, using the primer's approximate mix), Angel One's roughly ₹2,260 crore, and unlisted Zerodha reported brokerage income of ₹2,738 crore. Together that is about ₹7,550 crore, or around 70% of the roughly ₹11,000 crore that individual F&O traders paid in brokerage. That is plausible: the top three hold about 58.5% of NSE active clients and a larger share of F&O orders.

In plain terms

India has about 24 crore demat accounts but only about 4.6 crore people who actually trade on NSE in a year, and fewer than 90 lakh who trade options. The brokers' money comes overwhelmingly from that last, small group. When people quote huge "turnover" numbers for Indian derivatives, they usually mean the face value of contracts, which is not money anyone pays.

03

Who trades, and why: seven demand drivers

A structural shift of household savings into markets, layered over a cycle of sentiment and a policy regime that is now pushing the other way.

Demand for broking comes from two very different behaviours. One is investing: buying shares and mutual funds to hold, which is driven by the slow financialisation of Indian household savings and rewards brokers mainly through distribution and AMC income. The other is trading: frequent, often leveraged, short-term bets, which drives the order counts that discount brokers are paid on. The first is structural and policy-supported. The second is cyclical and, since 2024, policy-restricted. Seven drivers matter.

1. Financialisation of savings (structural, positive)

Demat accounts rose from about 4 crore before 2020 to roughly 23.5–24 crore. Monthly SIP inflows hit a record ₹32,490 crore in September 2026, and India has about 6.2–6.3 crore unique mutual-fund investors (both from our AMC report). The broker app has become an on-ramp for mutual funds: Groww holds 14.8% of the MF market by its own measure and processed ₹14,347 crore of SIP inflows in Q2 FY27, up 27.5%. Motilal Oswal counts more than 1 crore unique investor PANs, about 16% of all mutual-fund investors. This driver keeps growing in down-markets and it feeds the annuity lines.

2. Market returns and sentiment (cyclical)

Activity follows returns. FY26 was a weak year: the US–Iran conflict from 28 February 2026, record foreign selling in March and a Nifty that ended FY26 down 3.3%. The Nifty closed at 22,780 on 28 September 2026, about 13% below its peak. Kotak Institutional Equities noted that the active retail investor base shrank in FY26 and that the weighted average NAV of equity funds had fallen below June 2024 levels by March 2026. Poor returns push marginal traders out, and that showed up directly in NSE active clients.

3. Regulation of derivatives (policy, negative)

Section 04 covers this in full. In short, quarterly unique F&O individuals fell from 61.4 lakh in Q1 FY25 to 42.7 lakh in Q4 FY25 as the curbs took effect, and annual unique traders fell 18% in FY26.

4. The demographics of the trader base

SEBI's FY26 study describes who trades F&O. Forty-three percent of individual F&O traders are under 30. About three-quarters earn less than ₹5 lakh a year. Thirty-five percent held no equity portfolio at all at the end of FY26: they trade derivatives without owning shares. This is the cohort most sensitive to both losses and regulation, and the one the discount apps were built to reach.

5. Leverage appetite

When options became harder to trade, retail leverage moved to the cash market. The industry MTF book rose 58% in the year to June 2026, to ₹1.36 lakh crore. Brokers welcomed the shift because MTF earns about 15% against a funding cost of about 8% (INDICATIVE). Section 10 covers it in depth.

6. The primary-market cycle

IPOs bring new demat accounts, application volumes and investment-banking fees. The regulator's tap: eight measures, two tax hikes and no rebates Deep dive

Between October 2024 and April 2026 every lever on the F&O pool was pulled: who trades, how often contracts expire, how big a contract is, and what each trade costs.

The defining fact about Indian retail broking in 2024–26 is that its largest profit pool was regulated downwards on purpose. SEBI's own research kept showing that nine in ten individual derivatives traders lost money. The response was not a single rule but a sequence of measures, each aimed at a different lever, plus two increases in the securities transaction tax and an end to the hidden rebates that brokers had been earning from exchanges. Read together, they are a deliberate redesign of the product that paid for the discount-broking boom.

There are four levers. Who trades: the measures do not yet bar anyone, but higher contract sizes and upfront premium collection price out the smallest accounts. How often: cutting weekly expiries to one per exchange removed most "expiry days", which were the busiest trading days for small option buyers. How big: a ₹15 lakh minimum contract value replaced the old ₹5–10 lakh. What it costs: STT on options premium went from 0.0625% to 0.15% in eighteen months, and futures STT quadrupled from 0.0125% to 0.05%.

The policy direction has been one way throughout, and SEBI has described its FY26 loss study as "descriptive", which leaves room for more.

In plain terms

SEBI did not ban options. It made each contract bigger, cut the number of weekly "lottery days" from several to one per exchange, made traders pay for options upfront, and let the government raise the tax on every trade. Each step removed some small traders. Small traders are the ones who place lots of ₹20 orders.

The Budget-2026 STT hike: the turn of the tap no primer mentions

The most recent and least discussed measure is fiscal, not regulatory. In the Union Budget on 1 February 2026 the government raised STT on futures from 0.02% to 0.05% of the value sold, on options from 0.10% to 0.15% of the premium sold, and on exercised options from 0.125% to 0.15%, all effective from 1 April 2026, the start of FY27. It was the second hike in eighteen months. Futures STT is now four times its level of September 2024; options STT is 2.4 times. None of our six broking primers, written between July and September 2026, records it, and it belongs at the centre of any FY27 analysis.

True-to-label: the rebate pool that vanished

Before October 2024, exchanges charged brokers transaction fees on a volume slab: the more a broker traded in a month, the lower its rate. Brokers billed clients a flat rate at the top of the slab and kept the difference. For a large discount broker this rebate was meaningful pure profit. SEBI's "true-to-label" circular of 1 July 2024 required exchanges to charge one uniform rate from 1 October 2024, and required brokers to pass exactly that rate to clients. Zerodha's "net transaction charges" income fell from about ₹400 crore to zero in FY26. Angel One responded within a month by starting to charge for equity delivery, ₹20 or 0.1% per order (minimum ₹5), from 1 November 2024. Groww also charges for delivery, ₹20 or 0.05–0.1% per order. Zerodha remains the free-delivery broker, which is why it anchors price expectations across the industry. Two of our primers (Groww and Angel One) still describe delivery trading at those brokers as free; that is out of date.

The quieter rules: float, MTF and finfluencers

Three other rules shape the economics. Client-fund upstreaming, phased in during 2023, ended the practice of brokers holding client cash overnight; money must go to the clearing corporation as cash or as fixed deposits lien-marked to it. Float income survived, but in a standardised form that favours brokers large enough to negotiate deposit rates. The MTF framework caps a broker's margin-funding exposure at five times its net worth, which is why fast-growing MTF books require fresh equity or bonds. And SEBI's August 2024 rules on finfluencers barred brokers from paying unregistered social-media promoters, which removed a cheap acquisition channel used heavily in 2021–23.

What could come next

The regulator's remaining options are well known in the market: an eligibility or suitability test before a client may trade F&O (for example, a minimum portfolio, income or experience), further changes to the expiry calendar, minimum holding periods, or higher margins. Each would land on the same cohort, young traders with small portfolios and high turnover, that SEBI's studies identify as the biggest losers and that discount brokers identify as their best-paying clients. No such consultation was open at the time of writing, but the FY26 study published in August 2026 gives SEBI the evidence it would cite. For brokers, the practical consequence is that F&O revenue deserves a lower multiple than annuity revenue, whatever its current margin.

In plain terms

The tax on futures is four times what it was two years ago, and the tax on options is nearly two and a half times. The broker does not collect that tax, but its clients pay it, and clients who pay more per trade trade less. The industry worried that the April 2026 increase would cut volumes by a fifth or more. In the first quarter it did not, but the risk has not gone away, and SEBI could add another rule at any time.

Participants fell by a fifth. Money traded barely moved, and BSE took a big share from NSE. Per-order brokers caught the worst of it.

NSE active clients, a broader measure including cash-market investors, fell 7.1%.

Why did money hold up when people did not? Three reasons. First, the ₹15 lakh contract size and upfront premium priced out the smallest tickets, but the larger traders who remained simply traded larger contracts. Second, proprietary and institutional desks, which supply much of the premium turnover, were affected mainly by position-limit rules, not by contract size. Third, BSE's growth added new premium rather than only redistributing it, as traders who had used multiple weekly expiries moved to the Thursday Sensex expiry.

For a broker paid ₹20 an order, this is the worst version of the outcome. An exchange earns on premium, so a smaller number of larger traders producing the same premium leaves its revenue roughly intact. A broker earns on orders, and a larger contract carries the same ₹20 as a small one. The exchanges and depositories, BSE, CDSL and MCX, are listed separately and fall outside this sub-sector; their share prices also fell on the curbs, but their revenue proved sturdier than the brokers' because of exactly this difference in how they are paid.

The cash market tells a separate, more cheerful story. Cash ADTO was about ₹1.52 lakh crore in May 2026, up 28.3% year on year, after a 19% fall in the first half of FY26. Part of the recovery was MTF: borrowed money buying delivery shares. Groww's own stock ADTO was ₹14,430 crore in Q2 FY27, up 42.5%, and its derivatives premium ADTO ₹12,322 crore, up 28.7%. Groww grew faster than the market on both because it kept taking share.

In plain terms

The rules drove out about a fifth of options traders, but those who stayed traded bigger, and BSE grew, so the money changing hands in options barely fell. Exchanges, paid on the money, were fine. Brokers, paid per order, lost tickets. That is why broker profits fell harder in FY26 than the turnover figures suggest they should have.

Jane Street and the liquidity question

The July 2025 order against one foreign trading firm showed how much retail options trading depends on the professionals on the other side.

On 3 July 2025, SEBI issued an interim order barring Jane Street, a global proprietary trading firm, from Indian markets over alleged manipulation of index levels around Bank Nifty and Nifty expiries. Jane Street deposited about ₹4,843.5 crore in escrow on 14 July 2025 and was allowed to resume trading around 21 July. The episode lasted less than three weeks, but it is the clearest natural experiment the industry has had on the role of liquidity providers.

The volume effect was immediate. F&O volumes fell nearly 20% in the following week. NSE index-options daily turnover fell about 17.4% week on week, and weekly index-options turnover fell 21.4% on the first expiry after the order. Options premium turnover slid about 25% over two weeks. Listed brokers and market infrastructure sold off on 4 July 2025: Angel One fell 6.35% to ₹2,617 (about ₹262 on a split-adjusted basis), Nuvama fell 6–7%, and BSE and CDSL fell as well.

Brokers do not pay market-makers and India bans payment-for-order-flow, but brokers depend on them all the same: the liquidity is the product their clients are buying.

The ecosystem survived. SEBI's FY26 study shows proprietary and foreign algorithmic desks still captured about ₹58,000 crore of gross trading profit across the year. But the regulatory response, intraday position-limit monitoring from April 2025 and delta-based position limits phased in from July to December 2025, was aimed at precisely this group. For most brokers that is a second-order risk. For Share India, whose proprietary algorithmic desk sits on the same side of the trade as the global firms, it is first-order: rules that thin retail flow or cap intraday positions hit its trading income directly, as well as its client commissions. Our Wealth & Other Financial Services report covers Nuvama's exposure to the same episode through its institutional and clearing businesses.

Groww has twice as many active clients as Angel One, runs them far more cheaply, borrows almost nothing, and is still winning most new clients. To justify the gap on today's numbers, Groww has to roughly double its profit.

In plain terms

A quick test for any broker headline: is the growth coming from more clients, from each client trading more, or from earning more on each client in new ways? Groww is growing on all three. Angel One is growing on the third, and its client numbers are flat to down.

MTF: the new profit engine and the new risk Deep dive

As options volumes were curbed, retail leverage moved into the cash market. Brokers now earn a lending spread, and carry a lender's risks.

The margin trading facility lets a client buy shares for delivery with part of the money lent by the broker. The client puts up margin, typically a quarter to a third of the position, and the broker funds the rest at an interest rate of about 14–18% a year. The shares are pledged to the broker as collateral, and only stocks on an approved list are eligible, with haircuts by risk category. SEBI caps a broker's total MTF exposure at five times its net worth, which is why a fast-growing book needs fresh capital. The broker funds the loans with its own equity, bank lines, commercial paper and bonds, at a cost of about 7.5–9%.

MTF has become the industry's fastest-growing line. The industry's average MTF book was ₹0.86 lakh crore in June 2025 and an all-time high of ₹1.36 lakh crore in June 2026, up 57.6% in a year, according to CareEdge. One unverified report puts the combined NSE and BSE book at about ₹1.52 lakh crore in late August 2026. The growth is not a straight line: the book reportedly fell by ₹9,663 crore in March 2026 during the market sell-off, as clients cut positions or were forced to.

That would not sink the big brokers, but it would come at the worst possible moment.

Each archetype also has a characteristic failure mode. The mass platforms fail if regulation cuts order counts faster than they can add clients or new revenue. The converted incumbent fails if it keeps losing share while paying more to acquire clients. The full-service manufacturer fails if its own fund performance slips, because performance drives flows into the AMC, PMS and AIF products that now earn most of its profit. The capital-markets house fails in a long bear market, when IPOs, block trades and retail volumes all dry up together. The hybrid fails if one of its three legs, government advisory with its slow-paying clients, or a small NBFC, has a bad year. And the proprietary trader fails if its models stop working or the flow it trades against thins out.

The market's multiples line up with how recurring and how policy-safe each model's income is, more than with growth or size. The two archetypes with the most annuity income in their mix, Motilal's asset and wealth franchise and Groww's diversifying platform, trade at 31× and 47×. The two with the most cyclical or principal income, IIFL and Share India, trade at 19× and 11×. Angel One, with the highest F&O share, trades at 24×. Choice, at 72×, is the exception, priced for its government advisory order book and the growth of a small company rather than its broking. Section 18 returns to valuation.

In plain terms

A discount broker makes a little from each of millions of clients. A full-service broker makes a lot from each of a few lakh. A proprietary trader makes money from trading itself. The market pays most for income that recurs and that SEBI cannot easily cut, such as fees on money managed, and least for income from the firm's own trading.

Three exits from F&O, and one firm that doubled down

Motilal Oswal manufactures funds, IIFL Capital sells distribution and deals under a new owner, Choice advises governments. Share India went the other way.

The full-service and hybrid members answered the F&O squeeze differently from the discounters. Rather than lending more to the same traders, they built businesses whose income does not depend on order counts at all. Each found a different exit, and each exit carries its own risks.

Motilal Oswal: from broker to asset and wealth manager

Motilal Oswal Financial Services, founded as a stockbroker in 1987 by Motilal Oswal and Raamdeo Agrawal, now earns most of its profit from managing money. Broking has become a funnel into the asset and wealth franchise rather than the profit centre.

Four decades, one reset: from sub-brokers to the FY26 profit shock

Branch networks, then listings, then apps, then a retail boom, then the regulator. FY26 was the reset year; FY27 is the rebound off a low base.

Indian broking has had four eras. From 1987 to about 2005 it was a full-service, branch-and-sub-broker business. Motilal Oswal (1987), Choice (incorporated 1993), India Infoline (1995) and Angel Broking (1996) built networks of offices and franchisees charging 0.3–0.5% of trade value, and research and relationship managers justified the price. From 2007 to 2015 came listings and the first diversification: Motilal Oswal listed in 2006–07, Zerodha was founded in 2010 with a flat-fee, free-delivery model that would eventually reprice the whole industry, and Fairfax took about 26% of the IIFL group in 2015.

From 2016 to 2019 cheap mobile data turned investing into an app. Groww launched in 2017 as a commission-free mutual-fund app. IIFL split into three listed companies in 2019. Angel began its digital pivot the same year, cannibalising its own full-service model. Then came the retail boom of 2020–2024. Pandemic lockdowns, zero-cost apps and a rising market took demat accounts from about 4 crore to more than 18 crore. Angel Broking listed in October 2020 and rebranded as Angel One in 2021. Groww added stockbroking in 2020 and overtook Zerodha on active clients in late 2023. Weekly options on several indices turned every weekday into an expiry day somewhere, and India became the world's largest options market by contracts traded. SEBI's first study, in January 2023, showed 89% of individual F&O traders lost money in FY22.

The turn came in 2024, the reset in 2025 and 2026. The market peaked around September 2024. True-to-label, STT hike #1 and SEBI's six-measure circular all landed on 1 October 2024, the contract-size and expiry rules on 20 November 2024. In 2025 came the upfront-premium rule, the May position-limit circular, the Jane Street order, the FY25 loss study and the move of NSE's weekly expiry to Tuesday. Broker profits fell to their trough in the June 2025 quarter. Groww's IPO in November 2025 was the year's bright spot. In 2026 came the Budget STT hike, the US–Iran conflict and the March sell-off, the first annual fall in NSE active clients, Fairfax's move on IIFL, a record MTF book, SEBI's FY26 study and NSE's IPO filing.

Three things drive the FY27 rebound, and only one is durable. The first is base effect: the June and September 2025 quarters were the trough, after the curbs and the Jane Street episode. A broker's revenue is the number of active clients, times how often they trade, times what it earns per trade, plus what it earns lending to them and managing their money. The regulator has been cutting "how often they trade". Brokers have been answering by lending more and selling more funds. Profit then depends on how much of the cost base is fixed: when revenue recovers, profit recovers much faster.

KPI 1: NSE active clients and active-client share

What it is. The number of a broker's clients who traded on NSE at least once in the trailing twelve months, published monthly by NSE, and that number as a share of the industry total. Formula: broker's NSE active ÷ industry NSE active. Worked example: Groww's 1.34 crore active clients ÷ 4.61 crore industry = 29.16% (September 2026). What drives it: acquisition spend, app quality, word of mouth, product breadth (a client who buys mutual funds on the app may also try stocks) and churn; industry-wide, market sentiment and regulation. Benchmarks: above 14% is scale tier; 3–14% is a viable national player; below 3% is sub-scale for a discount model. How members compare: Groww 29.16%; Angel One 14.65%; Choice about 0.5% (on its 2.42 lakh active accounts, INDICATIVE); Motilal Oswal and IIFL do not disclose an NSE active figure, though both declined in FY26 by NSE's data and both sit in the "each under ~3%" group. Section 08 charts the shares.

The trap. "Clients" means at least five things. Angel One reports 3.998 crore registered clients; Groww reports 1.7 crore active users and 2.4 crore transacting users, which include mutual-fund-only users; Choice reports 12.94 lakh demat accounts; Motilal reports 1.58 crore total clients (unverified). Only NSE active clients are comparable across firms, and even they exclude BSE-only traders.

KPI 2: Share of net active additions

What it is. A broker's monthly net change in NSE active clients divided by the industry's net change. It is the best leading indicator of share. Formula: Δ broker NSE active ÷ Δ industry NSE active. Worked example: in September 2026 Groww added a net 1,00,961 clients, more than 63% of the industry's net additions of roughly 1.6 lakh. What drives it: the same forces as KPI 1, but at the margin, where brand momentum matters most. Benchmarks: above 40% is dominant; 10–40% is holding or gaining share; negative while the industry grows is losing. Members: Groww above 63% (September 2026) and +1.15 lakh in Q1 FY27 while the industry lost 2.57 lakh; Angel One +36,956 in September, about 23% of the industry's net additions (INDICATIVE, derived). The trap: when the industry total is shrinking, the ratio can exceed 100% or turn negative and becomes meaningless; then compare absolute net additions instead.

Net additions matter more than the stock of clients because broking has strong word-of-mouth effects. A new investor usually opens an account on the app a friend or colleague already uses, so the broker taking most of the net additions this year is likely to take most of them next year as well. That is why the monthly NSE release moves Groww's and Angel One's share prices, and why a single month in which Angel One passes Zerodha for second place would be read as news even though the two are separated by less than 1% of their client bases.

KPI 4: Revenue per order

What it is. What the broker earns per order, on two bases: broking revenue only, or all revenue. Formula: revenue ÷ orders. KPI 8: MTF book, yield and spread

What it is. The size of the margin-funding loan book, the interest rate earned on it, and the gap between that rate and the cost of funding it. Formula: yield = MTF interest income ÷ average MTF book (annualised); spread = yield − marginal cost of funds. Worked example: Groww's MTF income of about ₹156 crore in Q2 FY27 × 4 ÷ an average book of about ₹4,060 crore ≈ 15.3% yield; against a funding cost of about 8%, the spread is about 7 percentage points (INDICATIVE). What drives it: client demand for leverage (which rises in bull markets), the broker's pricing, its funding mix (equity is free at the margin; commercial paper and bank lines are not) and RBI rules on bank lending to brokers. Benchmarks: spread above 6.5 points good; 4.5–6.5 average; below 4.5 weak. Members: books of Angel ₹7,450 crore, Motilal ~₹6,100 crore, Groww ₹4,351 crore, Share India ₹465 crore; IIFL and Choice do not disclose. The trap: some firms report an average book, others period-end; Motilal's "loan book" of ₹7,388 crore (June 2026) may combine MTF with housing loans. Never compare a period-end book with an average one.

KPI 9: MTF book as a multiple of net worth

What it is. How far a broker's lending has grown relative to its equity, measured against SEBI's five-times cap. Formula: MTF book ÷ net worth. Worked example: Angel One's ₹7,450 crore average book ÷ ₹6,667 crore net worth ≈ 1.1×. What drives it: book growth versus retained profit and fresh capital. Benchmarks: 1–2.5× healthy; 2.5–3.5× stretched; above 3.5× close to the cap and dependent on fresh capital. Members: Angel ~1.1×, Motilal ~0.5×, Groww ~0.4×, Share India ~0.2× (INDICATIVE; section 10 charts them). The trap: net worth also supports other businesses, an HFC at Motilal, an NBFC at Choice and Share India, and exchange margins for prop trading, so a low ratio does not mean the capital is free. IIFL's ratio will fall sharply when Fairfax's ₹2,000 crore arrives.

In plain terms

Brokers lend to clients at about 15% and borrow at about 8%. The rule says a broker can lend at most five times its own capital. All six listed brokers are far below that, which means the risk is not running out of room but losing money in a crash on loans that looked safe the day before.

KPI 10: Float income

What it is. Interest earned on clients' idle cash and margin, which must be placed with the clearing corporation as cash or as fixed deposits lien-marked to it. Formula: average client funds × deposit yield retained by the broker. Worked example: ₹50,000 of idle margin at about 6.5% earns about ₹3,250 a year (ILLUSTRATIVE, Client A). What drives it: the size of client balances (which rise with options margins and fall when traders leave), interest rates, and the broker's negotiating power with banks. Members: mostly undisclosed. Groww's "net interest and float" was roughly 12% of FY26 revenue (approximate, primer); Zerodha's interest income, mostly on client money, was ₹2,269 crore in FY26 against brokerage of ₹2,738 crore (INDICATIVE). The trap: float is often buried inside "interest income" alongside MTF interest. A falling rate cycle or a fall in client balances cuts it without any change in trading.

KPI 15: Segment margin as a proprietary-trading signal

What it is. Where a broker does not split client commission from its own trading profit, the margin of the combined segment reveals how much principal income it contains. Formula: segment EBIT ÷ segment revenue. Worked example: Share India's broking-and-trading segment earned about ₹189 crore of EBIT on ₹427 crore of revenue in Q1 FY27, about 44%. What drives it: trading profits (volatile), commission rates and costs. Benchmarks: above 40% suggests the prop book is working; 25–40% mixed; below 25% looks like pure agency broking. Members: only Share India reports such a segment. The trap: a high margin in one quarter can reverse completely in the next; do not annualise it.

Share India's own quarterly profit shows why. On the screener basis its PAT ran at ₹84 crore, ₹93 crore and ₹89 crore in the first three quarters of FY26, dropped to ₹58 crore in the March 2026 quarter, when markets sold off, and then jumped to ₹124 crore in Q1 FY27. A broker earning only commission would not swing that much. Over several quarters, the average segment margin is a better guide to the prop book's contribution than any single print.

KPI 16: Market value per active client, P/E and P/B

What it is. How the market prices each client, and each rupee of profit and book value. Formula: m-cap ÷ NSE active clients; m-cap ÷ trailing PAT; m-cap ÷ net worth. Worked example: Groww's ₹1,26,068 crore ÷ 1.34 crore active clients ≈ ₹94,000 per client; Angel One's ₹27,511 crore ÷ 67.55 lakh ≈ ₹40,700. What drives it: expected profit per client, growth in clients, and the quality of revenue. Benchmarks (P/E, trailing): agency platforms 25–50×; full-service 18–32×; prop-led 8–14×. Members: P/E Choice 72.1×, Groww 46.9×, Motilal 31.0×, Angel 23.8×, IIFL 19.1×, Share India 11.4×; P/B from 10.9× (Groww) to 1.6× (Share India). The trap: value per client is only meaningful for client-count businesses. For Choice (~₹6.8 lakh per active account) and IIFL (~₹2.6 lakh), most of the value sits in businesses unrelated to the broking client count, and for Share India it is not meaningful at all.

The link makes a useful sanity check: a broker trading at a high P/B with an ordinary ROE, as Choice does (9.8× book on 16.1% ROE), is being priced for a large rise in returns, not for what it earns today.

That arithmetic is the industry's strategic problem in one line.

A full-service cohort looks completely different. One lakh full-service clients, at IIFL's roughly ₹26,700 of retail revenue each, would bring in about ₹267 crore a year, more than four times the discount cohort, but with research, relationship managers and branch costs that push cost-to-income to around 65%. Its profit depends less on order counts and more on the value of the clients' portfolios, the distribution products they buy and the market's level. That is why a full-service house's profit tracks the market's level and the deal calendar as much as it tracks the F&O rulebook: IIFL Capital's 21% profit fall in FY26 came mostly from weaker retail volumes and a soft market, not from options order counts.

In plain terms

A cohort of one lakh active clients can earn a discount broker around ₹19 crore a year in profit. Nearly half its revenue comes from options orders, so a 20% cut in options activity takes away more than a fifth of the profit. To make that up from lending, the broker would have to more than double what it lends to the same clients.

Definition traps, collected

  • Turnover. Premium, notional and cash ADTO differ by orders of magnitude. Industry options premium ADTO is derived, not reported.
  • Market share. Share of active clients, of retail turnover, of derivatives, of MF flows and of MTF are all quoted as "market share". Name the base.
  • MTF book. Average or period-end; MTF alone or combined with loans against shares or housing loans.
  • Pricing. "Zero brokerage" usually means free delivery at Zerodha only. Groww and Angel One both charge for delivery (Angel ₹20 or 0.1% since 1 Nov 2024).

How to read a broker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the NSE active client data, not the results. NSE publishes monthly; check the broker's active clients, share and net additions for the quarter. Red flag: falling share while the industry grows.
  2. Pull revenue and split it into the four rupees. Broking (by segment if disclosed), interest (MTF and float), fees (distribution, AMC, IB) and anything principal. Red flag: total revenue up only because of interest or other income.
  3. Check order counts and the F&O share. Orders per client and the derivatives share of revenue tell you how exposed the quarter was to regulation. Red flag: rising F&O share after a new curb.
  4. Read the MTF book and finance cost together. Book growth, yield, finance cost and any credit loss. Red flag: book growing much faster than net worth, or finance cost growing faster than MTF income.
  5. Compute cost-to-income. Opex plus depreciation over revenue, compared with the same quarter last year. Red flag: costs rising faster than revenue in a rising market.
  6. Separate other income and treasury. At IIFL, other income was a large part of Q1 FY27 PAT; at Motilal, treasury can swing profit by ₹1,000 Cr. Red flag: core profit flat while headline profit jumps.
  7. Check the annuity lines. AMC and wealth AUM, net flows, distribution AUM, SIP flows. These carry the multiple. Red flag: AUM growth that comes only from market moves, with weak net flows.
  8. Read the regulatory section of the commentary. STT, SEBI circulars, penalties, inspections, key-person exits. Red flag: unexplained penalties or a risk officer resigning (IIFL, October 2026).
  9. Recompute the per-client numbers. Annualised revenue and PAT per NSE active client; compare with the peer set. Red flag: revenue per client rising only because the client count is falling.
  10. Finish with valuation. P/E on the right profit basis (operating PAT at Motilal), P/B, and m-cap per active client for the discounters. Ask what growth the price assumes, as section 09 does for Groww.
In plain terms

The fastest way to read a broker's quarter is to ask four questions in order. Did it win or lose active clients? How much of its revenue came from options? Is its lending growing faster than its capital? And is the profit coming from the business or from one-off investment gains? Everything else is detail.

Why 11× to 72×: what the market pays for

The widest multiple range of any financial sub-sector we cover. It is explained by revenue quality, scale and growth, not by size.

On 19 October 2026 the six listed brokers traded between 11.4 times (Share India) and 72.1 times (Choice) trailing earnings, and between 1.6 and 10.9 times book value. That range is wider than in banks, NBFCs or asset managers. The dispersion has a clear logic once revenue is split into its four kinds: the market applies a high multiple to recurring fee income, a medium multiple to agency income from trading, a lower one to spread income that carries credit risk, and a low one to principal trading income. Scale and share gains add a premium on top; a pending deal can anchor a price.

Four valuation stories

The platform premium (Groww, 47×). The market pays for share gains, the industry's lowest cost ratio and highest ROE, and a mix moving away from derivatives. Section 09 shows the price assumes trailing profit roughly doubles. The premium is vulnerable to a further F&O step and to any month in which Groww's share of net additions falls sharply.

The annuity premium (Motilal, 31×; about 26× operating PAT). Over half of operating profit comes from managing money, a business the market values at 19–41× in our AMC report. The treasury book is better valued at net asset value than on its volatile earnings, which is why the operating-PAT multiple is the more useful one.

The cyclical discount (Angel One 24×, IIFL 19×). Angel is priced for its F&O dependence and leverage despite the best operating leverage in an upturn; IIFL for its cyclicality, with its price anchored near Fairfax's ₹350 offer. Both would re-rate if annuity income grew to dominate their mix.

The outliers (Choice 72×, Share India 11×). Choice is priced for its government order book, its 46% profit growth and small-company scarcity value. Share India is priced as a trading business, with a pledge overhang.

The unlisted market leader is the best reference point for what a mature discount broker can earn. The global lens: India's brokers against the world's

India has the world's busiest options market by contracts, low revenue per client, high returns and a ban on the model that funds US zero-commission trading.

India became the world's largest equity-derivatives market by number of contracts traded during 2021–24, according to industry data. On notional or premium value the United States remains larger, because Indian contracts are small and short-dated. Indian retail brokers are entirely domestic: none has a meaningful export business, and foreign retail brokers are negligible in India. The global comparison is therefore about business models rather than competition.

The closest analogue to Groww is Robinhood in the US: app-first, young users, options-heavy. The difference is how they are paid. Robinhood earns much of its revenue from payment for order flow, selling its clients' orders to market-makers. India bans payment for order flow, so Indian brokers charge clients directly (the ₹20 order fee) and earn from float and margin lending. That makes Indian brokers' revenue more visible to clients and to the regulator, and arguably more exposed to direct rule changes, but it also avoids the conflict of interest that has drawn regulatory scrutiny of PFOF in the US.

Where India differs most is revenue per client. Technology and disruption: the circular is the biggest threat

The largest disruption risk in Indian broking is regulatory product design. Price wars, super-apps, retail algos and AI come next.

In most industries "disruption" means a new technology or entrant. In Indian broking, the most powerful disruptive force of the last two years has been a regulator's circular: one document in October 2024 repriced the profit pool more than any app launch. That risk has not gone away. SEBI could introduce eligibility or suitability tests for F&O, change the expiry calendar again, impose minimum holding periods or raise margins, and the government could raise STT a third time. Every one of these would land on the same order counts that pay discount brokers.

Price wars and new entrants

Zerodha's free equity delivery anchors price expectations across the industry. A new wave of app brokers, Dhan, INDmoney, PhonePe's Share.Market, Sahi and others, compete on user experience and are often cross-subsidised by a parent's other businesses; Sahi grew 11.1% in September 2026. Jio Financial, with its BlackRock joint venture already holding ₹15,258 crore of mutual-fund assets in March 2026, is a potential broking entrant with an enormous distribution base. The risk for listed brokers is that the ₹20 order fee comes under pressure in the segments where it is still charged, especially delivery, where Angel One and Groww charge and Zerodha does not.

Super-apps and banks

Payments companies (PhonePe, Paytm) and banks want to own the "money app": the place where a customer saves, pays, borrows and invests. Bank brokers have lost activity share for a decade, but they keep a distribution advantage in mutual funds and wealth. If investing becomes a feature of a super-app rather than a destination, the standalone broking apps would face a higher cost of acquiring each client.

Retail algorithmic trading

SEBI's 2025 framework for retail algorithmic trading opens a new product line. Share India's uTrade Algo, with about 72,500 subscriptions of which about 6,500 are paid, is the listed example. Algos could raise order counts per client, which helps per-order revenue, but they also concentrate risk and could invite further rules.

AI and robo-advice

AI-driven advice and model portfolios could shift value from execution to advice and asset management. That favours brokers with their own AMC or wealth business (Motilal, Groww AMC, Angel's wealth arm) over pure execution platforms, and it could compress the research advantage of full-service houses.

Outages and cyber risk

Platform failures on busy days carry exchange penalties and client churn. In October 2026 MCX levied about ₹28.1 lakh on IIFL Capital over abnormal trades, a reminder that operational risk shows up as regulatory cost.

After that come cheaper competitors, super-apps from payment companies and banks, and trading robots for retail clients. The brokers best protected are those that earn from managing money rather than from counting trades.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian brokers

Structural financialisation and operating leverage on one side; a regulator that keeps tightening and a new credit risk on the other.

The bull and bear cases for the sector rest on the same facts read differently. The bull sees a profit pool that survived two years of curbs, a structural rise in household investing, and brokers that have built new engines (MTF, AMC, wealth) faster than the old one shrank. The bear sees a pool still dependent on a loss-making customer the regulator is protecting, valuations that already price a rebound, and a credit risk that has not yet been tested in a sharp fall.

The bull case

  • The pool survived the curbs. Industry options premium turnover rose about 8% in FY26 (INDICATIVE) and NSE's was up 16% in Q1 FY27, the first quarter under the new STT rates.
  • Structural financialisation. ~24 crore demat accounts, record SIPs of ₹32,490 Cr a month, and brokers as the MF on-ramp.
  • Operating leverage. Mostly fixed costs: Angel One's Q2 FY27 PAT rose 58% on 19% revenue growth.
  • New engines. MTF at ₹1.36 lakh Cr (+58%), Groww AMC +145%, Angel wealth +160%, Motilal asset + PW at 55% of operating profit.
  • Consolidation. Groww takes most net additions; scale leaders keep widening the cost gap.
  • Cheap options in the group. Angel One at 24×, IIFL at 19× with Fairfax's capital arriving, Share India at 11×.

The bear case

  • The regulator is not finished. 87.7% of individual traders still lose; the FY26 study gives SEBI cover for eligibility tests or more expiry changes.
  • STT keeps rising. Two hikes in eighteen months; the effect on volumes may yet appear in Q2–Q3 FY27.
  • Participation is shrinking. NSE active clients −7.1% in FY26; new F&O entrants −39%.
  • MTF credit risk. A record book built in a falling market; a fast 20–40% drop in mid-caps would test brokers' liquidation systems.
  • Base effects fade. FY27's +50–60% quarterly growth compares with the 2025 trough and will normalise in FY28.
  • Valuation. Groww at 47× and Choice at 72× price in a doubling of profit or sustained 30% growth.

The sector's investment case therefore depends on which way three things break: whether SEBI's next step is a pause or another curb, whether the industry's MTF book proves to be a sound credit business in its first real test, and whether the annuity businesses grow fast enough to carry the multiple once the FY27 base effects have washed through. Company by company, the balance differs. Groww's case rests on continued share gains and diversification; Angel One's on operating leverage and a cheaper starting multiple; Motilal's on fund performance and flows; IIFL's on what Fairfax does with its new capital; Choice's on converting its order book; Share India's on its trading models and its pledge.
