Indian telecom towers: two landlords, one swing tenant

A complete guide to the business that owns the steel under India's mobile networks: how a tower company turns a rented slot into cash, why the industry shrank to two big landlords, why a loss-making, government-backed operator decides how fast it grows, what the energy bill and lease accounting do to its reported profit, and how to read the one listed company, Indus Towers, against Brookfield's Altius and the world's towercos.

00

The one-page view: a rent business that lives on its tenants' health

India's towers are owned by two landlords of almost equal size. What separates their fortunes is how many tenants each tower carries, and whether the weakest tenant can keep paying and building.

A telecom tower company owns the steel, the land lease, the power connection, the diesel generator and the batteries that sit under a mobile network. It does not own the radios. Operators rent a slot on the tower, pay a monthly fee for it, and pay separately for the electricity their equipment burns. The tower costs roughly ₹20–25 lakh to build and lasts for decades. The first tenant pays most of the cost back slowly; the second and third tenants, who need almost no extra steel, are close to pure profit. That one fact, the economics of sharing, explains nearly everything about this industry: why operators sold their towers, why tower companies fight over tenancies rather than towers, and why a tenant's bankruptcy is the worst thing that can happen to a landlord.

India has about 8.43 lakh mobile towers (DoT count at 31-Oct-2025) carrying 31.44 lakh base stations. Two companies own most of the independent ones. Indus Towers, the only listed equity in this sub-sector and a subsidiary of Bharti Airtel since 19-Nov-2024, had 2,70,480 macro towers and 4,40,153 co-locations (tenancies) at 30-Sep-2026, a closing tenancy ratio of 1.63. Altius Telecom Infrastructure Trust, a Brookfield-sponsored infrastructure investment trust (InvIT), had 2,58,111 sites and 3,15,351 tenancies at 31-Mar-2026, held in three companies: Summit Digitel (Reliance Jio's former tower estate), Elevar Digitel (American Tower's former India business, bought on 12-Sep-2024) and Crest Digitel (in-building and micro sites). Between them the two landlords hold about 5.23 lakh sites and 7.43 lakh tenancies. Everyone else, BSNL's captive estate and a tail of private independents, holds the rest.

The customers are just as concentrated. Three private operators, Airtel, Jio and Vodafone Idea (Vi), hold 92.7% of India's 1,300 million mobile subscribers (Sep-2026); BSNL and MTNL hold the rest. Each operator has a different relationship with the landlords. Airtel controls Indus with 51.26% of its shares and supplies roughly 55% of its revenue (an INDICATIVE estimate derived from annual-report related-party data). Jio's towers sit inside Altius under a 30-year anchor contract, and Jio rents some Indus towers too, at roughly 12–13% of Indus revenue on broker estimates (INDICATIVE). Vi, 49.00% owned by the Government of India since 2025 and still loss-making, is the one operator that rents from everyone and owns little, which makes it the tenant whose survival and capital spending decide how fast the whole industry fills its towers.

The money arithmetic is simple to state. Indus earned ₹32,493 crore of revenue in FY26, but only ₹20,928 crore of it was rent; the other ₹11,550 crore was electricity billed back to tenants, at a small loss. Its reported EBITDA was ₹17,978 crore (55.3% of revenue), but that figure excludes ₹4,113 crore of land rent paid to site owners, because Indian accounting (Ind AS 116) moves lease costs below EBITDA. After land rent, Indus's cash operating profit was ₹13,863 crore, or 42.7% of revenue and about 66% of rent. Capital spending took ₹8,817 crore, leaving company-defined free cash flow of ₹3,743 crore, which Indus paid out almost in full as a ₹14-a-share dividend in August 2026, its first since FY22.

Five forces shape the sub-sector, and this report is organised around them:

  • Vi's survival and funding. Vi's unpaid rent forced Indus into big provisions in FY23 and produced a ₹5,077 crore write-back in FY25 once it paid. Now the government owns 49.00%, Vi's adjusted gross revenue (AGR) dues have been cut from ₹87,695 crore to ₹64,046 crore with no meaningful payment until FY32, and Vi has raised over ₹10,500 crore and lifted quarterly capex to ₹3,946 crore (Q2 FY27). It is now the sector's main source of new tenancies.
  • Two landlords and the renewal cliff. Airtel moves expiring tenancies from rival towers to its own subsidiary; Jio's master contract with Indus has expired and is "in discussions". Indus's book of future lease receipts has shrunk from ₹1.28 lakh crore (Mar-25) to ₹1.19 lakh crore (Sep-26).
  • Tenancy economics. Rent per tenancy has been stuck near ₹41,000 a month for five years because 2.5% escalators are offset by renewal discounts. Growth therefore comes only from more tenancies, and the tenancy ratio is the master variable.
  • Energy. Electricity is 37% of costs and billed to tenants at a loss (−3.6% to −4.8% margins). The 2026 West Asia diesel shock tested that; solar on 49,001 sites is the defence.
  • Airtel control and capital allocation. Indus runs almost no debt (0.97× net debt to EBITDA including leases, against 3.4–6.4× at global towercos), pays out its free cash flow with no formal policy, owes most of its receivables to its own parent, and is building towers in Africa for Airtel Africa.

Where does that leave the sub-sector in November 2026? Indus's Q2 FY27 (Jul–Sep 2026) results, reported on 27-Oct-2026, showed revenue of ₹8,515 crore (+4.0% year on year), EBITDA of ₹4,638 crore (54.5%) and profit of ₹1,798 crore (−2.2%, or +6.4% excluding a Vi write-back in the base quarter). More telling, the tenancy ratio rose to 1.63 from 1.62, the first rise in about six quarters, on 7,903 net co-location additions, most of them second tenants on existing towers, most of them Vi. Altius's adjusted revenue grew 3.7% in the same quarter, Indus's rent 3.8%: the two landlords are growing at the same modest pace. The stock trades at 14.7 times trailing earnings and about 6.7 times EBITDA, a fraction of the 18–25 times paid for American towercos, because the market doubts the length and quality of the rent stream. The rest of this report explains why, and what would change the verdict.

In plain terms

A tower is a building with only one kind of tenant. If you can get a second and third tenant into a building you have already paid for, the extra rent is almost all profit. India's tower companies built a lot of single-tenant towers in 2023–25, so the share of towers with a second tenant fell and rent per tower fell with it. The next few years depend on filling those towers, and the tenant most able to fill them is also the one with the weakest balance sheet.

01

How a tower earns: steel, a slot and two monthly bills

The towerco owns the passive shell and rents slots on it under ten-year contracts. Rent is the profit engine; electricity is a costly service sold at cost or below.

A tower company sells space and uptime. Its product is a slot on a structure that is powered, secured and maintained around the clock, so that an operator can hang its antennas and radios on it. The operator owns everything that transmits: antennas, remote radio units, baseband, the fibre or microwave backhaul. The towerco owns everything else: the steel lattice or mast, the shelter or outdoor cabinet, the grid connection, the diesel generator (DG set), battery banks, rectifiers, increasingly solar panels, and the lease on the land or rooftop. Indian licensing made this split formal. Tower companies register with the Department of Telecommunications as Infrastructure Provider Category-I (IP-I) firms and were historically barred from active equipment; the Telecommunications Act, 2023 is moving the sector to an authorisation regime, and Indus argues that infrastructure providers could later offer shared radio networks too.

The products differ mainly in size and how many tenants they can hold. Indus's quarterly report describes the range: a ground-based tower on 2,500–4,000 square feet of land, 30–50 metres tall, built for two or three tenants; a rooftop tower on 300–1,000 square feet, 6–21 metres; a ground-based mast, a slim pole on a small plot; and "lean" structures such as smart poles and feather sites, 3–12 metres, for one or two tenants. Alongside those sit in-building solutions (shared antenna systems in malls, airports and metro tunnels), small cells on street furniture, fibre to the site, and energy management. The contract is what turns steel into an annuity. Each customer signs a Master Service Agreement (MSA) with the towerco, and a service contract for each tower it occupies. Indus offers every customer on a tower the same terms, a legacy of its origin as a joint venture between Bharti, Vodafone and Idea. The MSA sets a standard rent per tenancy, a loading charge for extra antennas or equipment, an annual escalator of 2.5% (stated in the FY26 annual report), exit penalties, and pass-through of municipal levies and electricity tariff differences. New tenancies and renewals run for a minimum of ten years, management said on the Q2 FY27 call. At 30-Sep-2026 the weighted average residual life of Indus's contracts was 5.89 years and the minimum lease payments still receivable were ₹1.19 lakh crore. Altius's book is longer: a weighted average lease expiry of about 15.4 years (Sep-26), because 55% of its tenancies sit under Jio's 30-year non-cancellable MSA.

The 2.5% escalator is real, but renewal discounts and accounting straight-lining offset it. Indus's rent per tenancy was ₹44,264 a month in FY22 and ₹41,036 in Q2 FY27.

The cost base is mostly fixed. Once a tower stands, the towerco pays ground rent to the land or roof owner (about ₹14,000 per tower per month at Indus on our INDICATIVE arithmetic from lease repayments), field maintenance, security and a small head office. Electricity is the large variable cost, but it is recharged. That leaves a structure in which the first tenant on a new tower barely covers ground rent, maintenance and a return on the steel, and each extra tenant adds rent with little added cost. Who captures the value? Mostly the towerco, through sharing. Land owners capture ground rent, which rises with renewals. Operators capture savings: a shared tower costs each of them a fraction of a captive one, which is why every Indian operator except BSNL eventually sold or spun off its towers. Tower and site rentals run at about 8–12% of an Indian operator's revenue, as our telecom operators report notes, and sit below EBITDA in their accounts. Energy, by contrast, destroys a little value for the towerco today, but it is also a competitive weapon: a landlord that can run a site on less diesel and more solar charges tenants less for power, and Indus pitches exactly that when it bids for tenancies.

In plain terms

A tower company sends each tenant two bills a month. The rent bill, about ₹41,000 per slot at Indus, is where the profit is. The electricity bill, about ₹24,000 per slot, is meant to cover the power the tenant's equipment uses, and at present it slightly undercharges. Everything a towerco does to grow, build, upgrade or win tenants is aimed at sending more rent bills from the same steel.

Two features make the Indian version of the business unusual. Rent per tenancy is among the lowest in the world: ₹41,036 a month is about US$432 at ₹94.9 to the dollar, against roughly US$2,000–2,500 for a macro tenancy in the United States and US$1,000–1,500 in Africa (INDICATIVE reference ranges). And the customer base is tiny: three private operators and one state operator, where an American towerco has three national carriers plus cable, fixed-wireless and government users. Low rents are offset by low costs, so margins on rent are at global norms. A tiny customer base is not offset by anything; it is the reason Indian towers trade at a deep discount.

02

8.43 lakh towers, sized: a ₹60,000 crore bill, a ₹36,000 crore rent pool

India's tower estate is the world's second-largest after China's. Its rent pool is small in dollars, growing in low single digits, and split mostly between two owners.

The official count comes from the Department of Telecommunications. Its 2025 year-end review, published by PIB on 19-Dec-2025, put India's mobile towers at 8.43 lakh and mobile base transceiver stations (BTS) at 31.44 lakh on 31-Oct-2025, about 3.7 base stations per tower. The PM Gati Shakti national master plan maps a near-identical ~8.40 lakh towers. No 2026 total has been published. The DoT figure includes BSNL and MTNL sites, operator-captive towers and probably some rooftop masts and poles, so it is not a pure count of independent macro towers, and it is a year older than the latest company figures. Shares computed against it should be read as INDICATIVE.

Market size depends on whether energy is counted. Indus reports total revenue that includes electricity billed back to tenants; Altius reports both an Ind AS revenue line that includes pass-through and site-rent reimbursements and an "adjusted revenue" that strips them out. On a gross basis, Indus's ₹32,493 crore plus Altius's ₹24,165 crore equals ₹56,658 crore in FY26; adding BSNL, the private independents and IBS operators gives an INDICATIVE India passive-infrastructure billing of ₹59,000–62,000 crore (about US$6.2–6.5 billion). On a rent-only basis, Indus's ₹20,928 crore of sharing revenue plus Altius's ₹12,262 crore of adjusted revenue is ₹33,190 crore, and the INDICATIVE national rental pool is ₹35,500–37,500 crore.

Against the world, India's estate is large and its rent pool small. China Tower runs roughly 2.1 million sites (INDICATIVE). India's 8.43 lakh towers are the second-largest national estate. India's tower industry bills about ₹60,000 crore a year, but more than a third of that is electricity passed through to tenants. The rent that actually generates profit is about ₹36,000 crore, and two companies collect almost all of it. The industry grows when operators add tenancies, which they have done at 4–5% a year, not when they talk about 5G.

03

Who needs more steel: data, 5G loading and three-and-a-half tenants

Tower demand follows operators' network spending, not GDP. In 2026 the spender that matters most is Vodafone Idea, not Airtel or Jio.

The end demand is relentless. India had 1,300.0 million mobile wireless subscribers at Sep-2026 (753.9 million urban, 546.1 million rural), according to TRAI data cited in Indus's Q2 FY27 report. Data volume grew 30% year on year in Q1 FY27 and usage per subscriber 18%; 5G carried 44% of all data, up from 32% a year earlier, and 5G subscribers reached 463.1 million in Jun-2026. Altius, citing Analysys Mason, expects average usage to rise from about 26 GB per user per month (Q3 FY26) to about 53 GB by FY30 (approximate, read from a chart; INDICATIVE). More traffic needs more capacity, and capacity is added in three ways: more spectrum and better radios on existing sites, more equipment ("loading") on existing towers, or more sites closer together (densification). Only the last two pay a towerco, and only the last creates new tenancies.

That distinction matters because India's 5G rollout so far has been a loading story. Of the 5.93 lakh 5G base stations installed by Sep-2026, most went onto towers that already carried the same operator's 4G equipment. "5G is actually loading on the same tower," Indus told analysts in October 2026. Loading earns extra rent through loading charges and capex-linked rate revisions, but it does not raise the tenancy count. The next phase, densification and in-building coverage to carry 5G's higher frequencies indoors, does need new sites. That is why the tower count matters for the long run even though the tenancy ratio decides near-term returns.

The customers each pull in a different direction:

  • Vodafone Idea is the volume driver. It plans ₹45,000 crore of capex over three years to FY29 (coverage parity in 17 priority circles, then five more, then urban 5G). The demand risks are as specific as the drivers. Operator consolidation has historically been the towerco's worst enemy: India went from a dozen operators to three-and-a-half, and every merger or exit cancelled tenancies. Active sharing, in which two operators share one radio network, would cut tenancies per site; a Vi–BSNL tower-sharing pact already exists on a small scale. A failure of Vi's bank funding would stall the second-tenant fill. And a decision by Jio to move tenancies from Indus to its own Summit estate would hit Indus's rent directly. None of these is a forecast; each is a lever that the Section 20 watch-list tracks.

In plain terms

Mobile data grows 20–30% a year in India, but most of that growth is handled by better radios on towers that already exist. Tower companies earn the most when an operator that is not yet on a tower decides to move in. In 2026 that operator is mostly Vi, which is spending again because the government has taken a 49% stake and banks are lending. Airtel is building little of its own, and Jio prefers its own landlord.

One more structural point completes the demand picture. In India the operator chooses whether to build or rent, and the answer has swung with the operators' balance sheets. When capital was scarce (2017–2022), operators deferred capex and the towercos added towers slowly. When an operator had capital and needed coverage fast (Airtel and BSNL in FY23–FY25), towercos built single-tenant towers on order, which raised the tower count but diluted the tenancy ratio. The healthiest demand for a towerco is a well-funded challenger that must catch up on coverage on existing towers. That is Vi today, which is why the sector's fortunes and Vi's funding are, for the next two to three years, the same story.

The supplier base is fragmented and mostly domestic. Capacity in this industry means slots, not towers. Indus's standard towers are designed for two or three tenants. At an average of 1.62–1.63, it is using roughly 55–80% of nominal slot capacity (INDICATIVE, depending on whether the design maximum is taken as two or three). In plain terms

Of every ₹100 a tower company bills, about ₹37 goes straight back out on electricity, ₹13 to the people who own the land under the towers, ₹8 on running costs and ₹27 on building and upgrading towers. Two landlords: Indus, Altius and the Jio renewal Deep dive

India's towers belong to a listed, net-cash, Airtel-controlled company and a levered, Brookfield-sponsored trust built on Jio's contract. They serve the same tenants under opposite structures, and their frontier is moving.

The Indian tower industry used to have eight to ten independent landlords. In 2016 Indus, Bharti Infratel, American Tower's Indian arm (which had just bought Viom Networks), Reliance Infratel, Tower Vision, Ascend and others competed for tenancies. Ten years later, two remain at scale. Bharti Infratel merged with Indus in November 2020. Jio's tower company, Reliance Jio Infratel, was sold to a Brookfield-sponsored InvIT in 2019–2020 for roughly ₹25,215 crore (INDICATIVE) and became Summit Digitel. American Tower sold its India business to the same trust in a deal that closed on 12-Sep-2024, for a total consideration of about ₹18,200 crore including roughly US$320 million tied to Vi securities and receivables (from AMT filings; INDICATIVE), booking a US$1.2 billion loss, mostly currency translation; the estate was renamed Elevar. In March 2025 Airtel sold its last 10,093 macro towers to Indus. The result is a duopoly in which each landlord owns about a quarter-million sites.

The frontier: Airtel moves in, Jio may move out

The competitive frontier between the landlords runs through their tenants' choices. Airtel, which controls Indus, has been moving tenancies off rival towers as their contracts expire. On the Q1 FY27 call Indus described part of its growth as "movement of their expired tenancy portfolios to Indus", and said its order book for the next three to four quarters mixed "network expansion and moving of towers of tenancies". Elevar, the former ATC estate, was the only large third-party host of Airtel equipment, so it is the most likely loser (an INDICATIVE inference; neither company names the counterparty). For Indus, a moved tenancy is a second tenant on an existing tower, which is the most profitable revenue it can win. For Altius, it is the risk on Elevar's 45% of tenancies that sit under contracts of up to ten years, with about 4.9 years remaining on average.

Jio is the mirror image. Indus disclosed in 2026 that one major customer's master contract had expired and was "in discussions"; analysts on the Q1 FY27 call identified it as Jio ("the Jio contract is still not renewed"). Many Indus tenancies were signed in late 2016 and early 2017, when Jio launched, and come up for renewal in late 2026 and early 2027, Jefferies notes. Jio owns a landlord of its own through its 30-year anchor contract with Summit, so it has a credible alternative for any tenancy it chooses to move. Management's line has not changed in two quarters: "As of now, we continue to operate the tenancy portfolio with all our customers… that is something that is in discussions." On the Q4 FY26 call it added that "there is no broad contract expiry" because contracts are per tower, and that the expired bucket is "a very small portion of the entire portfolio".

In plain terms

Each big operator now has a favourite landlord. Airtel owns Indus and moves its equipment there when contracts elsewhere expire. Jio's own towers sit in Altius and it can move equipment there when its Indus contracts expire. Vi rents from everyone. So the two landlords' market shares are shaped less by price competition than by where their owners and anchors choose to put their radios.

Vi, the swing tenant: from bad debt to growth engine Deep dive

Vodafone Idea nearly broke the tower industry once. With the government as its largest owner and banks lending again, it is now the source of most new tenancies, and its funding is the sector's biggest single dependency.

Every tower company in India has the same weak tenant. Vodafone Idea, formed by the August 2018 merger of Vodafone India and Idea Cellular, lost subscribers for most of a decade, ran up adjusted gross revenue (AGR) dues to the government after the Supreme Court's October 2019 ruling, and for long stretches could not pay its landlords in full. Vi is still loss-making: in Q2 FY27 (Jul–Sep 2026) it lost ₹5,975 crore on revenue of ₹11,884 crore, and its total equity was minus ₹44,298 crore at Sep-2026. But it is also the only operator spending heavily on new sites on other people's towers, and that makes it the swing tenant: the one whose behaviour moves the industry's tenancy ratio, receivables and growth rate.

The Vi ledger at Indus, 2021–2026

The receivables story is now history, and its arc explains Indus's distorted profits. Vi under-paid rent through 2021–2022. Indus provided heavily, including ₹2,298 crore in Q3 FY23 alone (per our primer), which pushed FY23 EBITDA down to ₹9,767 crore, a 34.4% margin, and profit to ₹2,040 crore; Indus stopped paying dividends after FY22. Vi resumed full monthly payments from January 2023. Doubtful receivables at Indus peaked at roughly ₹5,400–5,700 crore around March 2024 (the primer's ₹5,386 crore is consistent with the deferred-tax arithmetic in the FY26 annual report; INDICATIVE). Vi's ₹18,000 crore follow-on offer in April 2024 and Vodafone Group's sale of its Indus shares in stages through December 2024 funded the catch-up. As Vi paid, Indus wrote back provisions: the cash-flow statement shows a net reversal of ₹5,077 crore of allowances in FY25, which inflated FY25 EBITDA to ₹20,845 crore (69.2% margin) and profit to ₹9,932 crore. Smaller write-backs of ₹90–230 crore a quarter continued into Q2 FY26.

The Airtel subsidiary: anchor tenant, controller and slow payer

Indus began as a three-way joint venture. It was incorporated in November 2007 with Bharti Infratel holding 42%, Vodafone 42% and Idea 16%, to share towers in 15 telecom circles. The 2020 merger of Bharti Infratel into Indus, effective 19-Nov-2020, left Airtel with 36.73%, Vodafone with 28.12% and Providence with 3.25%, and paid Vi ₹3,764 crore in cash for its 11.15%. The broader related-party question is about capital allocation. Airtel's interests and those of Indus's 48.74% minority overlap heavily (both want Indus profitable and its towers full) but diverge at the edges. The protections are the usual ones. Related-party transactions above materiality thresholds need approval from Indus's independent directors and, at the largest scale, from minority shareholders. Indus's biggest customer is also its owner. That guarantees Indus a steady flow of Airtel's equipment, including tenancies Airtel pulls back from rival towers. Nothing improper has been disclosed; the point is that minority investors depend on Airtel's choices.

What EBITDA really is: energy at a loss, land rent below the line Deep dive

A towerco's headline margin mixes a profitable rent business with a loss-making electricity resale and moves land rent out of sight. Rebuilding the numbers changes how Indus compares with the world.

First, 36% of revenue is electricity billed to tenants at roughly cost, which inflates the denominator and, because the electricity is resold at a small loss, slightly deflates the numerator. Second, since FY20 Ind AS 116 has required lessees to capitalise long-term leases, so the ₹4,113 crore of land and rooftop rent Indus paid in FY26 appears in its P&L as depreciation and interest, not operating cost. Strip the energy out of revenue and put land rent back into costs, and the business looks very different.

Why the pass-through loses money

The energy line is meant to be neutral. Each tenant reimburses the electricity and diesel its equipment consumes, under a formula set in the MSA. In practice it runs at a loss. Indus's energy margin, (energy reimbursement − energy cost) ÷ energy reimbursement, was −3.6% in Q2 FY27, −4.6% in Q1 FY27 and −4.8% in Q2 FY26, per management; for FY26 as a whole it was about −3.9% on our arithmetic (₹11,550 crore billed against ~₹11,996 crore of cost; INDICATIVE). That is a drag of roughly ₹450 crore a year on EBITDA. The causes are structural. Reimbursement formulas assume a grid-versus-diesel mix and a consumption norm; when grid supply fails and generators run longer, or when diesel prices rise faster than the formula resets, the towerco absorbs the difference. Settlement disputes from past periods add noise: Q1 FY27 included "past period settlements". And seasonality matters: management says H1 margins are structurally weaker because of the summer cooling load and monsoon disruption to grid supply, with H2 "usually better".

Why invest in energy efficiency if the savings flow back to tenants? Because energy is the competitive weapon in a market where rents are standardised. An operator choosing between hosting its radios on an Indus tower or an Elevar tower compares the total monthly cost of the slot, and electricity is about 37% of it. Indus claims lower unit energy cost than "other providers", and pitched exactly this when asked about the Jio renewal: "much lower operating costs than a single tenant would be". Energy efficiency also cuts the size of the loss on the pass-through, improves uptime (99.97% in Q2 FY27) and lifts the ESG score that lenders and some funds track (CRISIL ESG score up from 57 to 63). Grid policy is helping: the Ministry of Power has advised states to treat towers as a priority consumer category, CEA metering rules (2026) mandate smart meters, and green-energy open access is now operational in all states. One legacy dispute remains: a discom's 2016 reclassification of towers from industrial to commercial tariff, decided for the industry by APTEL in 2020 and stayed by the Supreme Court; customers have undertaken to reimburse the differential if Indus loses.

In plain terms

Towercos resell electricity to their tenants and lose about 4 paise on every rupee doing it. That loss is small, about ₹450 crore a year at Indus, but it swings with diesel prices and the weather. The bigger trick is accounting: rules that started in 2019 move land rent out of operating costs, so the "EBITDA" in the headline is about ₹4,100 crore a year higher than the cash the towers actually throw off. When you compare Indus with American towercos, use the figure after land rent and divide by rent alone: about 64–66%, which is normal.

What the accounting does to the rest of the P&L

Ind AS 116 also explains why Indus, a company with almost no bank debt, reports interest of about ₹1,893 crore a year: it is mostly interest on ₹20,952 crore of lease liabilities (Sep-2026). In Q2 FY27 lease interest was ₹417 crore, offset by ₹77 crore of net finance income on Indus's own cash. Depreciation of ₹1,944 crore in Q2 FY27 includes right-of-use depreciation on those leases. Two further accounting items distort comparisons. Revenue equalisation, the straight-lining of fixed escalators over a contract's life, reduces cash relative to reported revenue in the later years of a contract; it was a ₹473 crore drag in FY26's cash flow and ₹93 crore in Q2 FY27, because the bulk renewals of FY21–FY22 are now in their fifth or sixth year. And the 2013 Indus scheme's general reserve absorbs extra depreciation on merged fair-valued assets (₹79.5 crore in FY26), a small recurring flattering of reported profit.

The practical rule for an investor is to never mix bases. Net debt including leases (₹17,516 crore at Sep-2026) goes with reported EBITDA: 0.97 times trailing EBITDA, and an EV/EBITDA of about 6.7 times at the 13-Nov-2026 price. Net cash excluding leases (₹3,436 crore) goes with lease-adjusted EBITDA: an EV of about ₹1,01,035 crore and about 7.3 times trailing lease-adjusted EBITDA (INDICATIVE). Mixing net cash with reported EBITDA, which some screens do, makes Indus look cheaper than it is.

Rules of the road: registration, right of way and who pays the tax

Tower regulation is light and mostly favourable: faster permits, priority power and a tax win. The bigger policy lever is the one applied to the tenants.

Indian towercos are lightly regulated compared with their tenants. They do not hold spectrum or pay licence fees on revenue; they register with DoT as Infrastructure Provider Category-I firms, a regime dating from 2000, which allows them to supply passive infrastructure, dark fibre, right of way and ducts to licensed operators. The Telecommunications Act, 2023 replaces licences with authorisations and is being phased in through new rules. The industry body, formerly TAIPA, renamed itself the Digital Infrastructure Providers Association (DIPA) and lobbies on permits, power and electromagnetic-field rules. Two policy gaps are worth flagging. There is no statutory payment-security mechanism for towercos: when Vi fell behind, Indus's protection was contractual (exit penalties, the ability to stop service) plus a merger-era arrangement under which Vodafone Group backed Vi's dues to Indus with Indus shares. The policy lever that matters most for towers is applied to their tenants. The September 2021 reform package, the equity conversions that made the government Vi's largest shareholder, and the 2026 AGR reassessment all strengthened the weakest tenant without touching the towercos. Tariff policy is in operators' hands: there have been three rounds of tariff increases (December 2019, November–December 2021 and July 2024) and none since, which caps the cash operators can spend on networks. The Reserve Bank's repo rate, raised to 5.50% on 7-Oct-2026, affects levered landlords such as Altius far more than net-cash Indus.

In plain terms

The government mostly helps tower companies indirectly. It has made permits faster and cheaper, nudged states to give towers reliable power, and, most importantly, rescued Vodafone Idea, the tenant most likely to fail. What it has not done is give towercos any legal protection if a tenant stops paying; that protection comes only from contracts and from the tenant's health.

Four cycles in twenty years: build, collapse, consolidate, refill

Tower demand follows operator spending and operator consolidation. Each wave of mergers cut tenancy ratios; each rollout built towers first and tenancies later.

The Indian tower industry has lived through four distinct cycles, and each left a mark on today's structure. From 2000 to 2007 towers were captive: each operator built its own. Bharti created Bharti Infratel in 2006, and Bharti, Vodafone and Idea formed Indus in November 2007 to share towers in 15 circles, the first big bet on sharing. The 2008 licence wave brought a dozen-plus operators and a tower land-grab; Bharti Infratel listed in December 2012. Then the tenants began to disappear: the 2012 cancellation of 2G licences, followed by exits of Telenor, MTS, Videocon and others, removed tenancies faster than towers.

The defining shock came with Jio's launch in September 2016. Free voice and cheap data triggered a price war that drove Aircel, Reliance Communications, Tata Teleservices and Telenor out of the market between 2017 and 2019, and pushed Vodafone and Idea into their August 2018 merger, which cancelled overlapping tenancies. Towercos negotiated exit penalties but lost the tenancies for good. The October 2019 Supreme Court AGR verdict imperilled Vi and opened the receivables crisis of 2021–2023. Meanwhile the landlords consolidated: Jio sold its tower company to Brookfield's InvIT (2019–2020), Bharti Infratel and Indus merged (November 2020), American Tower sold to the same InvIT (September 2024) and Airtel sold its last towers to Indus (March 2025).

The third cycle, 2023–2025, was a build boom that diluted returns. The lesson of the cycles is that tower demand does not follow GDP or even data traffic; it follows operator capex and operator count. A consolidation wave cuts tenancy ratios and can wipe out years of growth; a rollout wave builds towers first, dilutes ratios, and pays off only when later tenants arrive. The industry is now as consolidated as it can be on the landlord side (two big owners) and close to its floor on the tenant side (three private operators plus BSNL). In plain terms

Every time Indian mobile operators merged or went bust, tower companies lost tenants they could never replace. Every time operators built out coverage, tower companies built lots of new towers with one tenant each, which made returns look worse before they got better. Today there are only four operators left and two big landlords, so the industry's fortunes rest on the four all staying in business and spending.

Reading a towerco: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain a tower company. Most are disclosed only by Indus; Altius and the global towercos supply the yardsticks. Each one has a trap.

A tower company's results look simple: a few lines of revenue, a high margin, a tower count. The difficulty is that almost every headline number has two or three definitions, and the company's own presentation does not always use the most useful one. This section works through the native KPIs one at a time, starting from the equation that links them, and ends with a tower-level P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected in one place, and a ten-minute routine for reading a quarterly report. Indus, the only listed member, discloses most of these metrics in its 40-page quarterly report; Altius discloses a subset; the global towercos use their own conventions.

The equation: towers × tenancy × rent, less energy and land

Every towerco's revenue and profit can be written as a chain of multiplications and subtractions. Rental (sharing) revenue equals towers times the tenancy ratio times rent per tenancy, times twelve for a year, adjusted for loading charges, escalators, renewal discounts and accounting straight-lining. Total revenue adds energy reimbursement. Reported EBITDA subtracts energy cost and other operating costs; lease-adjusted EBITDA also subtracts land rent. Free cash flow subtracts capex, tax and the change in working capital. The flow below shows the chain and marks which terms management controls.

In plain terms

A tower company's profit is the number of towers, times how many tenants each one has, times the rent per tenant, minus the cost of land and upkeep. The company decides how many towers to build. The tenants decide whether to move in or out, and rent per tenant barely changes. So the tenancy ratio is the number that tells you whether the business is getting better.

1. Towers

What it is. The count of macro sites the towerco owns and operates at the end of the period: ground-based towers, rooftop towers and ground-based masts. Indus began FY26 with 2,49,305 towers and ended with 2,64,514, a net addition of 15,209, about 1,270 a month. Drivers. Operator coverage orders (Airtel and BSNL in FY23–FY25), acquisitions (Airtel's 10,093 towers in March 2025), rural programmes, and decommissioning after operator mergers. Benchmark. Growth matters less than what follows it: towers added for a single tenant dilute returns until a second tenant arrives. Indus's 9.3% a year growth over FY22–FY26 was high by global standards; American Tower's site count is roughly flat after disposals and SBA's US count barely grows. Comparison. What it is. Tenancies terminated in the period. Indus disclosed 472 co-location exits in Q2 FY27. Formula. What it is. The profit or loss on reselling electricity and diesel to tenants. Formula. (Energy reimbursement − energy cost) ÷ energy reimbursement. Worked example. Q2 FY27: (₹3,070 crore − ₹3,183 crore) ÷ ₹3,070 crore = −3.7%; management quoted −3.6% on its precise figures. A move to zero would add about ₹450 crore a year. Drivers. 7. Reported EBITDA versus lease-adjusted EBITDA

What it is. Reported EBITDA under Ind AS 116 excludes land rent; lease-adjusted EBITDA deducts it. Formula. Lease-adjusted EBITDA = reported EBITDA − repayment of lease liabilities including interest. Worked example. FY26: ₹17,978 crore − ₹4,113 crore = ₹13,863 crore. Q2 FY27: ₹4,638 crore − ₹1,133 crore = ₹3,506 crore (41.2% of revenue). Drivers. Land-rent renewals (usually upward), the number of sites, and decommissioning. Benchmark. Lease payments at 12–14% of revenue are typical for Indus; a rising share means land costs are outrunning rent. Comparison. Altius reports "cash EBITDA" that also deducts the revenue-equalisation reserve: ₹8,301 crore in FY26. Global towercos' "adjusted EBITDA" deducts ground rent (US GAAP straight-line). Vi reports "cash EBITDA" after its lease rentals. Trap. Reported EBITDA flatters both towercos and telcos. Any EV/EBITDA comparison must say whether EV includes lease liabilities; with leases, use reported EBITDA (Indus about 6.7 times); without, use lease-adjusted EBITDA (about 7.3 times; INDICATIVE).

FY26: ₹13,863 crore ÷ ₹20,928 crore = 66.2%. Q2 FY27: ₹3,506 crore ÷ ₹5,441 crore = 64.4%. Drivers. Tenancy ratio (more rent on the same costs), energy margin (the loss falls into this margin), land rent and O&M. Benchmark. Good above 70%, average 60–70%, weak below 60% (INDICATIVE). Comparison. SBA 68.8% and American Tower 65.8% (Q3 2026), Altius 69.3% (Q2 FY27), IHS about 58% and Helios 55% (both carry more power revenue). Trap. Global margins are on revenue that includes small amounts of power and services; Indus's is on rent alone but absorbs the energy loss. The comparison is close, not exact. A one-off write-back (FY25) can push the margin far above its sustainable level.

In plain terms

Strip out the electricity resale and put the land rent back, and Indus keeps about 64–66 rupees of every 100 rupees of rent as cash operating profit. That is the same as large American tower companies. The business is not less profitable than its global peers; it simply charges much lower rents on much cheaper towers.

9. Capex per tower and maintenance capex

What it is. The cost of building a new tower, and the recurring spend needed to keep the existing estate running. Formula. Growth capex ÷ net towers added (a rough proxy); maintenance capex as a share of revenue. Worked example. Management's guide is ₹20–25 lakh per new macro tower; the Airtel purchase worked out to about ₹20 lakh a tower (₹2,043 crore including lean co-locations). Nomura's Q2 FY27 calculation of growth capex ÷ net towers was about ₹67 lakh, because growth capex also funds tenancy upgrades, 5G loading, solar and work in progress. FY26 maintenance capex was ₹2,182 crore, 6.7% of revenue. Drivers. Steel and equipment prices, tower design (lean towers are cheaper), upgrade intensity, battery and solar programmes, and the age of the estate. Benchmark. Capex below 20% of revenue is good, 20–30% average, above 30% weak; maintenance below 5% good, 5–7% average, above 7% weak (INDICATIVE). Comparison. Indus capex was 27.1% of revenue in FY26 and 28.2% in Q2 FY27; Altius does not disclose a comparable split. Trap. "Capex per tower" computed from total capex overstates the cost of a tower and understates the cost of a tenancy upgrade. And maintenance capex rose 57% in FY26 as the estate aged, which cuts into free cash flow permanently if it persists.

11. Receivable days by customer

What it is. How many days of billing are outstanding, overall and by customer. Formula. Trade receivables ÷ annual billing × 365; by customer, use related-party balances and estimated billing. 12. Net debt including and excluding leases

13. ROCE versus pre-tax ROE: the definition trap

What it is. Indus defines return on capital employed as trailing EBIT ÷ average capital employed, where capital employed is equity plus net debt including lease liabilities. Formula. ROCE = LTM EBIT ÷ average (equity + net debt incl. leases); pre-tax ROE = LTM PBT ÷ average equity. Worked example. Sep-2026: pre-tax ROCE 19.2% standalone, 19.1% consolidated; pre-tax ROE 25.3%; post-tax ROE 18.8%. Drivers. EBIT growth versus the capital base; the FY25 write-backs lifted LTM ROCE to 29.1% before they rolled out of the window. Benchmark. Pre-tax ROCE above 20% good, 15–20% average, below 15% weak (INDICATIVE). Helios reports a ROIC of 14.4%. Comparison. Screener shows ROCE 19.5% and ROE 19.1%. Trap. On both the Q1 and Q2 FY27 calls the CFO read out pre-tax ROE as "pre-tax return on capital employed" (25.4% and 25.3%). The quarterly report's table and glossary make the definitions clear. Use 19.1–19.2%. The step-down from 29% is the write-back year leaving the window, not a deterioration.

14. Residual contract life and minimum lease receipts

What it is. The weighted average remaining term of the tower contracts, and the undiscounted rent the towerco is contractually entitled to receive. Formula. Weighted residual life = Σ(remaining years × contracted rent) ÷ Σ contracted rent; minimum lease receipts = sum of future contracted rent. Worked example. Indus Sep-2026: 5.89 years and ₹1,19,230 crore. At about ₹21,800 crore of annual rent, that is roughly 5.5 years of current rent. Drivers. Renewals (minimum ten years) lengthen it; expiries without renewal shorten it; new tenancies add to it. Benchmark. Above 10 years good, 6–10 average, below 6 weak (INDICATIVE). Comparison. Altius's weighted average lease expiry is about 15.4 years (Sep-26) because of Jio's 30-year MSA; US towercos typically have 7–10 years of non-cancellable terms plus renewals (INDICATIVE). Trap. Undiscounted receipts can look huge and still be shrinking. Indus's fell from ₹1,28,132 crore (Mar-25) to ₹1,22,054 crore (Mar-26) and ₹1,19,230 crore (Sep-26), and the over-five-year bucket fell 13% in FY26, a hard-data signal that some contracts (most likely Jio's) are not being renewed long-term.

In plain terms

Indus has contracts that guarantee it about ₹1.19 lakh crore of future rent, or about five and a half years of today's rent. That sounds safe, but the total has been falling even while Indus adds tenants, which tells you that some old contracts are running off without being renewed for the long term. Altius, by contrast, has about 15 years of contracts because of Jio's 30-year deal.

In plain terms

The ladder also explains the valuation debate in one line. If a tower is worth its cash profit capitalised at, say, 10–12% (the range implied by the Altius valuer's WACCs), a single-tenant tower is worth roughly ₹18–21 lakh, slightly less than it cost to build; a two-tenant tower is worth ₹51–61 lakh and a three-tenant tower ₹84–101 lakh (ILLUSTRATIVE). A towerco that builds 15,000 single-tenant towers a year at ₹22.5 lakh each invests ₹3,375 crore to create assets worth less than their cost until someone moves in.

Definition traps, collected

  • "Revenue" includes about 36% energy pass-through. Compare Altius's ₹12,262 crore adjusted revenue with Indus's ₹20,928 crore sharing revenue, never with its ₹32,493 crore total.
  • The energy "pass-through" loses money. −3.6% to −4.8%; about ₹450 crore a year.
  • Three EBITDAs. Reported (Ind AS 116, before land rent), lease-adjusted (after), and screener's operating profit. Global "adjusted EBITDA" matches lease-adjusted.
  • Net cash versus net debt. Net cash ₹3,436 crore excluding leases; net debt ₹17,516 crore including them. Pair each with the matching EBITDA.
  • FY25 and FY23 are distorted. FY25 includes ~₹5,077 crore of Vi write-backs (69.2% EBITDA margin); FY23 includes heavy provisions (34.4%). Use management's normalised growth (FY26 EBITDA +11.4%, PAT +13%).
  • Four tenancy ratios. Indus closing and average (macro only), Altius's site-based 1.2×, global "tenants per tower".
  • Tower counts are not additive. Indus macro, Altius sites incl. in-building, Cellnex points of presence, DoT all towers.
  • The Airtel tower purchase was 10,093 macro towers + 2,179 lean co-locations for ₹2,043 crore, completed 24-Mar-2025, not "~16,000 towers in 2024".
  • "ROCE 25%" on recent calls is pre-tax ROE; ROCE is 19.1–19.2%.
  • Screener FCF ignores lease payments and doubles the company figure.
  • Rent per tenancy does not escalate in practice: renewal discounts and revenue equalisation offset the 2.5% escalator.
  • Customer mix is estimated. Airtel ~55% (from related-party data) and Jio ~12–13% (broker estimate) are INDICATIVE; Indus discloses neither.
In plain terms

Almost every tower-company number has a twin that means something different: revenue with or without electricity, EBITDA before or after land rent, debt with or without leases, a return on capital that is really a return on equity. The safe habit is to ask "which one?" every time, and to compare companies only when the answer is the same.

How to read a towerco's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Towers and co-locations (minute 1). Pull closing towers, co-locations, net additions and exits from the KPI table. Net co-location adds above ~7,500 and more than two tenancies per net new tower mean second-tenant fill; exits above ~1,000 a quarter are a red flag (watch for Jio).
  2. Closing tenancy ratio (minute 2). Rising means filling existing steel; falling means single-tenant building or exits. Check whether a rise came from decommissioning.
  3. Sharing revenue and rent per co-location (minute 3). Use sharing revenue, not total revenue. Compare its growth with co-location growth; a gap below co-location growth means renewal discounts are biting.
  4. Energy margin (minute 4). Calculate (energy reimbursement − energy cost) ÷ reimbursement. Remember H1 is seasonally weaker. Worse than −5% signals a fuel or grid problem.
  5. Reported and lease-adjusted EBITDA (minute 5). Note both; compute lease-adjusted EBITDA ÷ sharing revenue. Below 60% is a warning.
  6. One-offs (minute 6). Check the cash-flow line "allowance for doubtful receivables" and management's "ex write-back" growth. Strip write-backs and exit penalties before judging growth.
  7. Capex and FCF (minute 7). Capex split into growth and maintenance; company FCF after lease payments. Maintenance creeping above 7% of revenue is a structural cost.
  8. Receivables (minute 8). Trade receivables versus last quarter and versus revenue growth. A rise of more than ~₹500 crore in a quarter needs an explanation; check who owes it in the annual report.
  9. Balance sheet (minute 9). Net debt with and without leases; net debt ÷ EBITDA; dividend paid. Note the CFO's ROCE wording against the report's table.
  10. Contract book and commentary (minute 10). Weighted residual life and minimum lease receivables (falling = renewals not replenishing); management's words on expired contracts, the largest customer's funding and order-book visibility.

For Altius the same routine works with substitutions: use adjusted revenue in place of sharing revenue, cash EBITDA in place of lease-adjusted EBITDA, distributions per unit in place of FCF, and net debt to asset value (44.9% at Sep-2026, against a 70% cap) alongside net debt to cash EBITDA. Altius discloses no energy margin, exits or rent per tenancy, so its quarterly read is necessarily coarser; the weighted average lease expiry (15.4 years) and the Summit/Elevar split are the numbers that carry most information.

Applied to Q2 FY27, the routine gives a clear read. Co-location additions were strong (7,903) and mostly second tenants; the tenancy ratio rose for the first time in six quarters; rent grew 3.8%, slower than co-locations (5.9%) because of renewal discounts and a one-off in the base; the energy margin improved to −3.6%; lease-adjusted EBITDA was 64.4% of rent; there were no meaningful write-backs left in the current quarter; capex was ₹2,399 crore and FCF ₹1,377 crore; receivables fell; net debt including leases rose to 0.97 times after the dividend; and the contract book shrank again with no update on the expired Jio contract. Good operations, an unresolved renewal.

Start with Indus's two debt numbers. Net debt of ₹17,516 crore and 0.97 times EBITDA include ₹20,952 crore of lease liabilities; excluding them Indus has ₹3,436 crore of net cash. Either way it is the least levered towerco in the table by a wide margin: every global peer runs 3.4–6.4 times, and Altius about 5.2 times. The EV/EBITDA of 6.7 times uses the matching basis (EV including leases, reported EBITDA). That is less than half the 17.8–25.2 times paid for the American towercos and below Helios's indicative 8–9 times. Section 17 explains the gap.

Next, the margins. Indus's 55.3% and Altius's 41.2% are both Ind AS figures on gross revenue that includes electricity pass-through; Altius's lower number reflects heavier pass-through and site-rent reimbursements (₹11,900 crore of its ₹24,165 crore). On the comparable basis, cash EBITDA after land rent as a share of rent, Indus earns about 64–66% and Altius about 68–69%, while American Tower and SBA earn 65.8% and 68.8%. The African towercos sit lower, at 55–58%, because their revenue includes more power. Indus is not a low-margin towerco; it is a low-rent one.

Indus's 2,70,480 towers make it larger than any single global listed towerco by site count except Cellnex's points of presence, and its 1.63 tenancy ratio is above IHS and Altius but well below Helios's 2.26. Indus generates ₹4,000–5,000 crore of free cash a year, pays it all out, carries almost no debt and is spending some of it building towers for Airtel in Africa. Shareholders are asking for more.

Capital allocation is where the sub-sector's structure becomes a live debate. Indus has no formal dividend policy. On the Q4 FY26 call (1-May-2026) management said the board considers distributing year-end free cash flow "subject to the working capital requirements", with no minimum payout, aiming for "steady and progressive distribution". Why has Indus not levered up? Three reasons are plausible, though management has not given one. First, Indian equity investors and rating agencies reward the net-cash position after the Vi scare, and the AAA ratings depend partly on it. Second, Indus's contract book is short (5.89 years) and its counterparties are weaker than American towercos', so the stability that justifies 5–6 times leverage elsewhere is less certain. Third, Airtel, which consolidates Indus, may prefer that group leverage sit at the parent, where it is reducing debt with Indus's dividends. An InvIT, Altius's structure, would let Indus distribute more and borrow more tax-efficiently, but would require moving towers into a trust, with Airtel's agreement. Altius's planned listing will give the market a direct read on how Indian tower cash flows are priced inside such a structure.

The strategic logic is mixed for Indus's minority shareholders. Indus earns about ₹4,000–5,000 crore of spare cash a year and has started handing almost all of it to shareholders. It has almost no debt, while tower companies elsewhere borrow five or six times their annual profit, so some investors want it to borrow, buy back shares or copy Altius's trust structure. It has not said whether it will. Meanwhile it is building towers in Africa for Airtel's African business, which may pay off but serves the parent's interests at least as much as the minority's.

The global lens: stalled America, growing Africa, consolidating everywhere

Global towercos run the same equation with longer contracts, higher rents and far more debt. Their 2026 results show US growth has stalled while emerging markets grow, and the industry is selling assets to simplify.

The tower business was invented as a stand-alone industry in the United States in the 1990s, when carriers sold their towers to independent owners such as American Tower, Crown Castle and SBA, which then leased slots to every carrier. The model spread to Latin America, Africa, Europe and India. Its defining features are the same everywhere: long non-cancellable leases with fixed or inflation-linked escalators, high incremental margins on extra tenants, and heavy use of debt against contracted cash flows. What differs is the length and quality of the contracts, the level of rent, the number of operators and the cost of capital. India sits at the extreme on all four: short residual contracts, the lowest rents, three-and-a-half operators and a high cost of equity.

On rent per tenancy, India is in a category of its own. The gap is offset by cost: an Indian tower costs ₹20–25 lakh (about US$21,000–26,000), its land rent about ₹14,000 a month (INDICATIVE), and its staffing is lean (70 towers per Indus employee). That is why margins on rent converge (Indus 64–66%, AMT 65.8%, SBA 68.8%) even though absolute rents differ several-fold. What does not converge is contract quality. US towercos typically hold 7–10 years of non-cancellable terms with renewal options and 3%-plus escalators (INDICATIVE); Helios has US$5.9 billion of contracted revenue, about six years of revenue at its current run-rate; Altius has a weighted average lease expiry of about 15 years. Indus has 5.89 years of weighted residual life and effective rent escalation near zero.

China Tower, the world's largest towerco by far with roughly 2.1 million sites (INDICATIVE), offers one more comparison: a state-orchestrated monopoly formed in 2014 from the three state operators' towers, with a tenancy ratio around 1.7–1.8 and an EV/EBITDA multiple in the mid-single digits (both INDICATIVE). It shows that a towerco whose tenants are also its owners, and whose pricing is negotiated with them, tends to trade like a utility rather than a growth asset. Indus, controlled by its largest tenant and priced through MSAs with three customers, is closer to that model than to American Tower's.

In plain terms

Tower companies everywhere make the same kind of margin on rent. The difference is how long their contracts last, how healthy their tenants are, and how much they borrow. American towercos have long contracts and strong tenants but have stopped growing; African ones grow fast but in risky currencies. India's landlords grow at a European pace with the shortest contracts and lowest rents of all.

Why 6–7× EBITDA: what the market is paying for, and what it is not

Indus trades at a third of the American towercos' multiple. Contract tenor, counterparty risk, flat rent, control and capital structure explain most of the gap; on free cash flow it is not obviously cheap.

At ₹396 a share on 13-Nov-2026, Indus was worth ₹1,04,471 crore: 14.7 times trailing earnings, 2.6 times book value, a 3.54% dividend yield and about 6.7 times trailing EBITDA on an enterprise value of ₹1,21,987 crore that includes lease liabilities. Investors who think the Jio renewal will cost tenancies, or that Vi's build will stall, see little margin of safety at 6–7 times EBITDA; investors who think the tenancy ratio is heading back towards 1.7 see a cheap annuity with a growing coupon.

In plain terms

Indus looks very cheap next to American tower companies, but it is priced roughly in line with tower companies in emerging markets, and the reasons are real: shorter contracts, weaker tenants, rents that do not rise, and a controlling shareholder who is also the biggest customer. On the cash it actually generates after building new towers, the share price already assumes the business keeps growing.

Two catalysts could move the multiple directly. A listed Altius would establish a traded yield for Indian tower cash flows; if it lists at a yield close to its FY26 distribution yield on NAV (about 9%, INDICATIVE: ₹15.6 DPU ÷ ₹170.77), Indus's 3.5% dividend yield and 4.5% FCF yield will be compared with it daily. And a decision by Indus to lever up, through buybacks, a special dividend or an InvIT, would shift value from the lower-return cash pile to shareholders and narrow the capital-structure part of the gap. The opposite catalysts are equally specific: a Jio exit wave, a stalled Vi loan, or a dilutive African acquisition.

Active sharing is the threat to watch. Today each operator puts its own radios on a shared tower, so three operators on one tower means three tenancies. If two operators shared one radio network, as is common in Europe, a tower would carry one tenancy where it had two. The Indian precedent is small: Vi says it has "some relationship with BSNL where we do the tower sharing… no other tower sharing". A deeper Vi–BSNL network pact, or a government push to share radio networks in rural areas, would cut tenancies. Indus's response is to propose that infrastructure providers themselves run shared radio networks, citing European regulator BEREC's estimate that active sharing cuts capex by 33–35% and opex by 25–33%; that would require a regulatory change under the Telecommunications Act, 2023 that has not yet been made.

Densification is the opportunity. 5G's higher frequencies travel shorter distances and penetrate buildings poorly, so carrying 44% of traffic today and more tomorrow eventually needs more sites closer together. So far operators have avoided it by loading 5G radios onto existing towers. When densification comes, the question is whether it lands on macro towers, where Indus earns ₹41,036 a tenancy, or on small cells and poles, where it earns ₹16,867 and competes with municipalities and fibre owners. The Q2 FY27 signal was encouraging for macro towers: lean additions fell to 13 because operators were "moving towards the macro or bigger towers".

Satellites are a smaller issue in India than headlines suggest. Direct-to-device satellite services can cover remote areas that towers cannot reach economically, but they carry a fraction of a terrestrial cell's capacity. The towers at risk are the marginal rural single-tenant sites that earn the least anyway. Energy technology is the quiet positive: lithium-ion batteries, solar and smart meters lower the cost of running a site, shrink the energy loss and make Indus's sites cheaper for tenants than a rival's, which is how a landlord competes when rents are standardised.

In plain terms

The thing most likely to hurt tower companies is operators deciding to share radios, because then two tenants become one. The thing most likely to help is 5G needing many more sites, as long as those sites are proper towers rather than street poles. Satellites and new tower designs matter much less.

Bull vs bear: a refill story against a renewal cliff

The bull case rests on Vi's build filling single-tenant towers; the bear case on Jio's renewals and Vi's funding. Both are visible in the same quarterly numbers.

The sub-sector's investment case reduces to a tug of war between tenancy additions and tenancy losses, with the balance sheet as a cushion. The bull reads Q2 FY27 as the start of a multi-year refill: Vi is funded and building, Airtel is moving tenancies onto Indus, the tenancy ratio has turned, and a towerco with net cash and a 4.5% free-cash-flow yield is being priced as if none of that matters. The bear reads the same quarter as the calm before a renewal cliff: the long end of the contract book is shrinking, the largest independent tenant has its own landlord, rent per tenancy has not risen in five years, and the swing tenant still loses ₹5,500 crore a quarter before exceptionals.

The bull case

  • Vi refills the towers. ₹45,000 Cr of capex to FY29, funding past ₹10,500 Cr, orders over ₹13,200 Cr; each new Vi site on an existing tower is a high-margin second tenancy. +0.01 tenancy ≈ ₹133 Cr of EBITDA.
  • Counterparty risk has collapsed. Government owns 49.00% of Vi; AGR dues cut to ₹64,046 Cr and deferred to FY32–FY41; Indus's doubtful-debt allowance down to ₹197 Cr.
  • Airtel in-sourcing. The parent moves expiring tenancies from Elevar to Indus, adding second tenants on existing towers.
  • Energy defences work. Energy margin improved to −3.6% through a diesel shock; each point back towards zero adds ~₹123 Cr.
  • Balance-sheet optionality. 0.97× leverage against 3.4–6.4× globally; ₹46,000–64,000 Cr of theoretical debt capacity (ILLUSTRATIVE); buybacks now tax-efficient.
  • Cheap on EBITDA. 6.7× against 17–25× for US towercos, with margins on rent at the global norm.

The bear case

  • The Jio renewal. Master contract expired; ~50,000 tenancies at stake (INDICATIVE); broker scenarios from ~5,000 exits to 25% non-renewal; Jio has its own landlord in Summit.
  • A shrinking contract book. Future lease receipts ₹1.28 → ₹1.22 → ₹1.19 lakh Cr; >5-year bucket −13% in FY26; 5.89 years of residual life.
  • Flat rent. ₹41,000 per tenancy for five years; renewal discounts offset escalators, so growth needs tenancies.
  • Vi's funding could stall. The SBI-led consortium has not closed; orders run out in two to three quarters at the Q2 pace; Vi still loses ~₹5,500 Cr a quarter.
  • Related-party drift. Airtel owes 72% of receivables (~69 days), steers Africa capex and decides payouts; no formal dividend policy.
  • Active sharing or consolidation. A Vi–BSNL network pact or a fourth-to-third operator merger would cut tenancies with nowhere left to consolidate.
