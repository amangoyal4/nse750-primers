00

The one-page view: lenders without deposits, valued on whether their returns last

Five forces explain almost everything about Indian NBFCs. Here they are before the detail.

A non-banking financial company (NBFC) does the main thing a bank does, lend money, without the thing that makes banking cheap: deposits that can be withdrawn on demand. India's NBFCs lent about ₹48.4 lakh crore at March 2025, roughly a quarter as much as the banks and about 14% of GDP, and they have grown faster than banks for most of the past decade. The 25 NBFCs in the NSE 750 manage about ₹46 lakh crore of loans, earned about ₹1.21 lakh crore of profit in FY26 (leaving out Sammaan Capital's one-off ₹7,145 crore write-down), and the stock market values them at about ₹22 lakh crore, roughly 18 times those profits.

Those totals hide five very different businesses, from a ₹5.5 lakh crore consumer lender to state-owned power financiers, gold pawnbrokers, a credit-card issuer and small lenders making ₹3 lakh loans against village homes. Averages across them mislead. What ties them together are five forces:

  • Funding is destiny. An NBFC borrows its raw material from banks, bond investors and mutual funds. Its credit rating and its parent decide what that money costs and whether it keeps arriving in a panic. Every crisis since 2018 (IL&FS, DHFL, COVID) sorted lenders by the quality of their funding, not their loans. Banks supply 43.5% of NBFC borrowing today.
  • Credit cost, not margin, drives the cycle. Net interest margins move slowly within each type of lender; loan losses swing by one to four percentage points of the book. Microfinance arrears went from 6.3% to 2.0% in twelve months. The lenders with premium valuations are the ones that kept losses inside a narrow band through every downturn.
  • The RBI is an active co-pilot. The regulator sets risk weights, bans products, imposes gold-loan rules, forces large NBFCs to list, and in 2026 redrew the line between the middle and upper layers. Growth rates are partly decided in Mumbai, and the old regulatory gap between NBFCs and banks keeps closing.
  • Collateral moves. A large share of NBFC loans is backed by assets whose prices change: gold, used trucks, tractors. NBFC gold loans grew about 78% in FY26, mostly because gold got dearer, not because more gold was pledged. That flatters growth now and reverses if the price falls.
  • The state is a lender. PFC, REC, IRFC and IREDA hold roughly ₹16–17 lakh crore of loans, about 30% of system NBFC credit (indicative). They borrow at near-sovereign rates and lend to state power utilities and the railways. Their returns are set by policy, dividends and discom finances, and PFC is absorbing REC in a merger targeted for 1 April 2027.

Above the five forces sits the fact investors care about most: the market prices these lenders on how long their returns will last, not on how high they are today. Bajaj Finance earns a 20% return on equity and trades at 5.3 times book value. PFC earns about 21% and trades at roughly book value. Muthoot earns 27% and trades at 2.7 times. The gap is explained by growth runway, concentration and the market's guess at each lender's cost of equity, and section 12 works through the arithmetic.

In plain terms

An NBFC is a shop that buys money wholesale and sells it retail. Its profit is the gap between the two prices, minus the cost of running the shop, minus the loans that never come back. The best NBFCs have found customers banks will not serve well, such as a first-time truck owner or a family pawning a necklace, and they charge enough to cover a higher cost of money. The market pays up only for lenders that have shown they can keep doing that through bad years, which is why two lenders with the same return can be valued five times apart.

01

How an NBFC earns: buy money wholesale, lend it where banks will not

What separates an NBFC from a bank, where the money flows, and how ₹100 of interest income is split in four kinds of lender.

Legally, an NBFC is a company registered under the Companies Act that the RBI has licensed under Chapter IIIB of the RBI Act, 1934. Registration has been compulsory since the 1997 amendment. In practice, it is a lender that cannot take demand deposits, cannot issue cheque books, has no access to the RBI's repo window, and is not required to keep cash with the RBI or buy government bonds. It must hold more capital than a bank (a 15% capital ratio against a bank's 11.5% including buffers) and it must fund itself in the wholesale market.

That combination sounds like a handicap, and on funding it is. A large bank pays about 5–6% for its deposits; a typical private NBFC pays about 7.5–9.5% for its borrowings (indicative composite of FY26 disclosures). PFC, with a near-sovereign rating, paid 7.50% in FY26. The NBFC has to make that gap back somewhere: by charging more, by running leaner, by taking less risk, or by serving a customer the bank cannot reach. The successful ones do one or more of those things very well.

The value chain: five steps from a bond to a loan and back

The money moves through five stages. First the NBFC raises funds: equity from its promoter, the market or a strategic investor (MUFG, IHC and Bain in the past year), then bank term loans, bonds (non-convertible debentures, NCDs) bought by mutual funds, insurers and provident funds, commercial paper (CP) for short-term money, overseas borrowing (ECBs), securitisation, and, for a few, public deposits. The government lenders also sell tax-saving 54EC bonds. Then it originates loans through branches (Muthoot has more than 5,000), dealers and manufacturers, apps, agents, co-lending tie-ups with banks or, in PFC's and REC's case, a government mandate to finance state utilities. It underwrites using bureau data, cash-flow checks and collateral valuation; it services and collects; and it recycles capital by selling pools of loans to banks.

Value concentrates at the two ends. Step 1 (a cheaper cost of funds, set by rating and parentage) and Steps 3–4 (lower loan losses) decide most of the difference between a 1% and a 5% return on assets.

Where is value captured? Mostly in two places. The first is underwriting and collection, which shows up as credit cost: about 0.3% of the book a year for a gold lender, 1.5–2% for a diversified retail lender, and 4–8% for microfinance or cards at the peak of a bad cycle. The second is the cost of funds, which depends on rating and parentage. A AAA conglomerate or bank-owned NBFC borrows 100–250 basis points cheaper than an A-rated standalone (indicative). Distribution reach is the third lever: Bajaj Finance's franchise of 124 million customers lets it sell a second and third loan at a fraction of the cost of finding a new borrower.

Where ₹100 of interest income goes

The clearest way to see the archetypes is to split ₹100 of income into its uses. A government infrastructure lender spends almost 60% on interest because it borrows nearly all of its book and earns a thin spread, but its running costs are tiny: a few hundred staff in Delhi lending thousands of crores at a time. A gold lender spends a third on interest and runs thousands of branches with vaults, appraisers and guards, but almost never loses money on a loan because the gold sits in its safe. A vehicle financier pays more for money and loses more to bad loans, so it keeps the smallest slice as profit.

In plain terms

Every NBFC is solving the same sum with different numbers. A power-sector lender borrows at 7.5%, lends at 10% and spends almost nothing on staff, so it keeps about a third of its income despite a thin gap. A gold lender borrows at 9%, lends at 18% and spends heavily on branches, but its losses are tiny because the collateral is in its vault. A truck financier sits in between on price and pays for it with higher losses. None of these models is "better"; each is a different answer to the question of where a lender without deposits can earn its keep.

02

₹48 lakh crore and counting: a second credit engine growing faster than the banks

How big the NBFC system is, how fast it grows, what it lends against, and how the 25 listed names line up by size.

The authoritative figure comes from the RBI's Report on Trend and Progress of Banking: NBFC advances of ₹48.39 lakh crore at March 2025, up 19.4% from ₹40.53 lakh crore a year earlier. That counts every NBFC, including the government-owned PFC, REC and IRFC. It is about 14% of GDP and about a quarter of what the scheduled commercial banks lend. The FY26 edition of the report is due around December 2026, so the March 2026 figure is not yet published; extrapolating 17–20% growth gives about ₹56–58 lakh crore, which this report labels INDICATIVE.

Three different "sizes" circulate, and they are not in conflict. The RBI counts all NBFCs. CRISIL tracks a narrower universe of mainly private retail and wholesale NBFCs plus housing-finance companies, excluding the government infrastructure lenders, and expects it to pass ₹50 lakh crore by March 2027 at 18–19% growth. ICRA's "NBFC-Retail" excludes housing-finance companies and wholesale lending and expects 16–18% growth in FY27. Always ask which universe a number describes before comparing it with another.

The composition matters as much as the size. At March 2025, lending to industry was ₹22.91 lakh crore, and 56.1% of that, roughly ₹12.9 lakh crore, was to the power sector, almost all of it through PFC and REC. Retail lending was ₹16.32 lakh crore. Industry plus retail made up 81.3% of the total, and services lending was the fastest-growing slice at 29.8%. So the system is two quite different things bolted together: a state-directed power and infrastructure financier, and a private retail and small-business lending industry.

Faster than the banks, for a reason

NBFCs have grown at about 20% a year for most of the past decade, against 11–15% for banks. The RBI's Financial Stability Report put credit growth at upper- and middle-layer NBFCs at 21.3% year on year in September 2025, while bank credit grew 14.5% in FY26. NBFC lending to MSME services grew 32.5% and to MSME industry 26.2%. Part of this is genuine market-making: NBFCs reach borrowers banks do not. Part is substitution: when the RBI raised risk weights on unsecured loans in November 2023, banks slowed, NBFCs shifted to gold and property-backed loans, and both regrouped.

In plain terms

India's NBFCs lend roughly ₹50–60 lakh crore, about a quarter as much as its banks, and they have grown half as fast again as the banks for a decade. But a quarter of that is really the government lending to its own power utilities through PFC and REC. The private half is a collection of specialists: truck and tractor financiers, pawnbrokers, consumer lenders and small-business lenders. When someone quotes "NBFC growth", ask which half they mean.

03

Who borrows from an NBFC, and why they keep coming

Seven demand drivers, from a thin household-debt base to the state's power and railway capex, and the risks that could cut them short.

The demand case starts with a simple fact: Indian households do not owe much. Household debt is about 41–42% of GDP (RBI Financial Stability Reports, 2024–25), against roughly 60% or more in China and 70% or more in the US and UK. Mortgages are a smaller share than in rich countries, so non-mortgage credit, where NBFCs over-index, has room to deepen as incomes rise and credit histories lengthen.

Seven drivers

  • Vehicles. Vehicle finance is expected to grow 16–17% a year to about ₹11 lakh crore by FY27 (CRISIL). The drivers are the GST rate cuts in the second half of FY26, which lifted demand; premiumisation; and a shift to used vehicles among price-sensitive buyers. NBFCs finance an estimated 55–60% of commercial vehicles and a much higher share of used trucks and tractors (indicative), because a used truck bought by a first-time owner-driver with no tax return is a loan most banks do not want to write.
  • The gold price. Indian households hold an estimated 25,000 tonnes or more of gold, and only a few percent of it is pledged. NBFC gold loans grew about 78% in FY26, but ICRA notes that the tonnage pledged "remained muted": the growth was mostly the price. Gold is now 17.4% of NBFC retail portfolios, the biggest cyclical swing factor in FY26–27 earnings for Muthoot, Manappuram, IIFL, SBFC, Fedbank and Capri, and a new entry point for Tata Capital and Bajaj Finance.
  • MSME formalisation. GST registration, more than 5 crore Udyam registrations, account-aggregator cash-flow data and the Unified Lending Interface widen the pool of small firms a lender can underwrite. NBFC MSME lending grew at a 32% compound rate in FY21–24, against 20.9% for private banks and 10.4% for public-sector banks (CARE).
  • Consumption and digital commerce. Point-of-sale finance for phones and appliances, and personal loans through apps, are the entry product for millions of first-time borrowers. Bajaj Finance added a record ₹36,969 crore of loans in Q1 FY27 and booked 16 million new loans in the quarter.
  • Public capital spending. Distribution reform (RDSS), the 500 GW non-fossil power target for 2030, transmission and pumped hydro drive PFC, REC and IREDA. Railway capex once drove IRFC, but the Ministry of Railways has funded capex from the budget since FY24, so IRFC is diversifying into power, fertiliser, freight corridors and ports.
  • Substitution toward secured credit. After the 2023–25 stress in unsecured and microfinance loans, lenders and borrowers both moved to gold and property-backed loans, which gained share in FY26 (ICRA).
  • Strategic capital chasing the opportunity. MUFG, Abu Dhabi's IHC and Bain Capital have put about ₹53,000 crore into three Indian NBFCs in eighteen months. Foreign owners are buying the growth runway, and their money lets the lenders grow without hitting capital limits.

The risks to demand

Three things could cut this short in FY27. The West Asia conflict has raised oil prices and inflation and led to GDP downgrades (ICRA, April 2026). An El Niño year would hit rural cash flows, which matters most for tractor, microfinance and small-ticket lenders; M&M Finance's tractor disbursements jumped 45% in Q1 FY27 on a strong rural cycle, and that can reverse. And interest rates have stopped falling: the RBI held the repo rate at 5.25% in August 2026, bond yields have risen 20–90 basis points since June, and ICRA flags possible rate rises in the second half of FY27. Higher rates do not kill demand for a ₹50,000 gold loan, but they squeeze the lender's margin and slow big-ticket borrowing.

There is also a quieter risk: over-borrowing. The 2023–25 microfinance stress happened because too many lenders chased the same borrowers. The industry's self-regulatory guardrails now cap a microfinance borrower at three lenders and ₹2 lakh of total debt, and 98% of clients are within them. Demand for credit is not the constraint in India; the constraint is how much debt a borrower with an irregular income can carry, and the lenders who misjudge that write it off two years later.

In plain terms

Indians borrow far less than people in richer countries, so there is plenty of room for lending to grow. NBFCs get the customers banks find awkward: the self-employed, the rural, the thin-file and the collateral-rich but paperwork-poor. Demand is rarely the problem. The problem is lending to people who cannot repay, and the reminder of that came in microfinance as recently as 2024.

04

Funding is destiny: from the IL&FS freeze to MUFG's cheque Deep dive

A lender without deposits lives or dies by its liabilities. Who lends to NBFCs, what it costs, how the rate cycle passes through, and why foreign banks are now buying in.

Every NBFC crisis in living memory began on the liability side. In September 2018 IL&FS, an AAA-rated infrastructure group with about ₹91,000 crore of debt, defaulted. Mutual funds, which had been rolling over NBFC commercial paper every few weeks, stopped. Lenders that had funded five-year loans with three-month paper had to sell assets or shrink, and some (DHFL, Altico, Reliance Capital, Srei) did not survive. The loans were not the trigger. The trigger was that the money stopped arriving.

That is the first thing to understand about an NBFC: it has no lender of last resort. A bank in trouble can borrow from the RBI against government bonds; its depositors are insured and mostly stay put. An NBFC in trouble depends on banks and bond investors choosing to keep lending to it. So the quality of its liability franchise, meaning its credit rating, its parent, the tenor of its borrowings and the spread of its lenders, decides both what it pays for money in good times and whether it survives bad ones.

Who lends to NBFCs

Banks are the biggest single source: 43.5% of NBFC borrowings at the latest RBI count (FSR, June 2026). Bank funding improved sharply in FY26, especially in the second half, and covered about 45% of NBFC-Retail's incremental needs, much better than in the two prior years (ICRA). The rest comes from bonds and debentures, commercial paper (about ₹5.4 lakh crore outstanding across all issuers in Q1 FY27), overseas borrowing, securitisation, subordinated debt and, for a handful, public deposits. ICRA estimates NBFC-Retail needs ₹4.2–4.4 lakh crore of fresh funding in FY27 and expects non-bank sources to stay tight.

In plain terms

Two lenders making the same loan to the same truck owner can earn very different profits because one pays 7% for its money and the other pays 9.5%. That gap comes almost entirely from who stands behind the lender: a bank, the Tata or Bajaj name, the government, or nobody. This is why a rating upgrade or a big-name shareholder can be worth more to an NBFC than a year of good lending.

How the rate cycle passes through

The RBI cut the repo rate by 125 basis points between February and December 2025, from 6.50% to 5.25%, and held it at 5.25% in August 2026 (unanimously). JM Financial estimates that its NBFC coverage saw the cost of funds fall about 80 basis points between Q3 FY25 and Q4 FY26, a pass-through of about 62%. Then the direction changed: in Q1 FY27, 19 of 23 companies saw funding costs rise, by about 10 basis points on average, and yields on government securities, corporate bonds and NBFC debt rose 20–90 basis points from June 2026. The one-year MCLR at banks is up about 30 basis points since March.

Why only 62% pass-through, and why the lag? An NBFC's liabilities reprice at different speeds. Bank loans linked to MCLR or an external benchmark reset in 3 to 12 months. Fixed-rate bonds reprice only when they mature and are refinanced, which for a two-to-five-year bond book means a few percent of the stock each quarter. Commercial paper reprices almost immediately but is a small slice. Deposits reprice as they renew. So funding costs follow the repo rate with a lag of several quarters, in both directions.

The asset side matters as much. Vehicle loans and most personal loans are fixed-rate for their life, so a vehicle financier's margin is squeezed when rates rise and widens when they fall, with a lag. That is why Shriram, Chola and M&M Finance saw margins expand through FY26 and why the turn in rates is a headwind for FY27–28. Loans against property, home loans and infrastructure loans are mostly floating, so those lenders pass changes on, though competition decides how fully. Gold loans are short (6–12 months), so they reprice fast in both directions. Muthoot's margin collapse in Q1 FY27, from 13.4% to 10.4%, was the reverse case: yields fell faster than funding costs as competition and rate cuts passed through, while its funding costs rose.

ALM: the 2018 lesson

The fault line in 2018 was the asset-liability mismatch (ALM): long loans funded with short money. A housing-finance company lending for 15 years and funding a fifth of its book with 90-day commercial paper must refinance that paper four times a year. When buyers vanish, it has no way to repay except by selling loans at a discount or begging banks. After IL&FS and DHFL the RBI imposed a liquidity-risk framework and, from 2020, a liquidity coverage ratio, phased to 100% by December 2024 for NBFCs with more than ₹10,000 crore of assets. The industry also shrank its reliance on commercial paper. Today CP is 4% of Muthoot's borrowings, 4% of Tata Capital's and 1.9% of HDB's. Shriram's liquidity coverage ratio is 262.5% and Piramal's 553%, both swollen by fresh capital.

Gold lenders have the best natural match: their loans are repaid in under a year, so a funding freeze would shrink them quickly but not break them. Vehicle and MSME lenders sit in the middle with two-to-five-year loans. The government lenders have the longest loans (10–20 years) but borrow long too, and their quasi-sovereign standing means the market keeps buying their paper in a panic.

Foreign strategic capital: buying a cheaper liability franchise

The cleanest recent case study of funding as destiny is Shriram Finance. Japan's MUFG paid ₹39,618 crore for 20% at ₹840.93 a share, allotted in April 2026. On top of new equity, Shriram got two things. First, a CRISIL upgrade to AAA in FY26, which narrows its funding gap to AAA peers: its incremental cost of borrowing is already 7.77% against a book average of 8.56%. Second, enormous capital: its capital ratio rose to 34.17% and leverage fell to 2.14 times, against 3.82 times in March. It used part of the money to repay borrowings, which fell from ₹2,50,690 crore to ₹2,32,639 crore.

The same logic runs through the other deals. Abu Dhabi's IHC committed ₹8,850 crore to Sammaan Capital, became promoter in May 2026, and Sammaan's ratings went to AA+. Bain Capital's ₹4,385 crore into Manappuram bought joint control from April 2026. The new owner does not just bring equity; it changes what lenders think the NBFC will be worth in a crisis, and that changes what they charge it. The catch is the short-term arithmetic: unused capital dilutes return on equity. Shriram's ROE fell to 12.8% in Q1 FY27 from about 16% for FY26 because it is sitting on MUFG's money.

An NBFC's real competitive weapon is often not its loans but its lenders. If banks and bond buyers trust it, it pays less for money and keeps getting money when others do not. Concentration and control: 9,000 licences, 170 lenders that matter, and who owns them

Industry structure, market shares where they can be measured, the unlisted competition, and the five kinds of owner behind the listed names.

India has more than 9,000 registered NBFCs, but almost all of the credit sits in a few. The RBI's FY26 stress test covered 174 NBFCs (11 in the upper layer, 163 in the middle layer) that account for about 95% of upper- and middle-layer advances. Within those, the top ten (PFC, REC, Bajaj Finance, IRFC, Shriram, Tata Capital, Chola, Muthoot, L&T Finance and M&M Finance) probably hold 55–60% of all NBFC credit, and the government trio of PFC, REC and IRFC alone about 30% (both INDICATIVE).

The rest of the 9,000 are mostly small: investment companies, core investment companies that hold group shares, peer-to-peer platforms, account aggregators, and thousands of tiny lenders in the base layer. They matter to the system mainly as a regulatory headache, which is why the RBI's scale-based rules exist.

Market share where it can be measured

Gold is the one product where the listed names can be set against a reliable system total. ICRA puts NBFC gold-loan AUM at ₹4.0 lakh crore at March 2026. Muthoot's standalone gold book of ₹1.63 lakh crore in June 2026 is about 41% of that, the same share the industry dossier gives. Manappuram and IIFL each hold roughly 14–15%. Bajaj Finance, Capri and Fedbank have built meaningful gold books within the past three years.

What makes each model work

Diversified consumer. Bajaj Finance is a category of one in the listed set: it originates millions of small loans at stores and online, then cross-sells personal loans, gold loans and mortgages to the same customers. Its yields are high but not extreme; its edge is volume and a decade of credit data on 124 million people, which keeps both acquisition cost and credit cost lower than a newcomer could manage. Its ROA of 4.3–4.7% at modest leverage gives an ROE of about 20% while growing loans 22–25% a year.

Vehicle finance. The oldest NBFC franchise. Lend against trucks, tractors and cars, often used, often to self-employed borrowers with patchy records; repossess and resell when needed. Yields are moderate, losses are meaningful (gross NPAs of 2–5% are normal), and rural cycles and the monsoon matter. Within the group, Chola runs the highest leverage and gets the highest ROE; Sundaram runs the most conservative book; Shriram is the used-CV specialist; M&M Finance is the rural and tractor lender whose bad loans peaked at about 15.5% in mid-2021.

Gold loans. The highest-quality collateral in Indian lending: liquid, valued daily, held in the lender's vault. Loans are small (₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh), short, and repaid or renewed quickly; when a borrower defaults, the lender auctions the gold. Credit cost is a fraction of a percent. Yields are high because the service, money in twenty minutes with no questions about income, is valuable. Leverage stays low because the RBI requires gold-heavy NBFCs to hold Tier-1 capital of at least 12%.

Government infrastructure. Big tickets (hundreds or thousands of crores) to state power utilities, generators, transmission companies and railway projects. Thin spreads (2.4–2.6% for PFC and REC), near-zero running costs, and leverage of six to eight times. The risk is not small defaults but concentration: a single state's distribution companies can be a large slice of the book. IRFC is a special case: it leases rolling stock and project assets to the Ministry of Railways at its own borrowing cost plus a margin of about 30–40 basis points, so it has almost no credit risk and a 1.5% margin.

Bank-promoted. HDB and Fedbank borrow on their parents' names and lend to customers a step riskier than the parent bank wants on its own balance sheet: MSME, used vehicles, gold, loans against property. Their returns are mid-pack; their edge is cheap funding and parent distribution. Cards (SBI Cards) are a separate animal: 100% unsecured, with fee income almost as large as interest income, very high acquisition and servicing costs, and credit costs that swung from about 9% to 6.5% within a year.

Conglomerate. Tata Capital, Aditya Birla Capital and L&T Finance are large, diversified, AAA-rated and still working toward the returns their owners promise: ROAs of 2.3–2.5% against targets of 2.5–3.2%. Jio Financial is a holding company with a huge equity base and a young lender, Jio Credit, that is growing at more than 160% a year from a small base.

Small-ticket MSME. Five-Star lends ₹3–5 lakh to micro-entrepreneurs in the South against their own homes, at yields above 22%. Opex is high (about 7% of the book) because underwriting is in person, but credit losses stay low because borrowers will not risk the family house. The result is an ROA above 8%, the highest in the group, but with a 52% capital ratio the ROE is only about 15%. SBFC and Capri run the same playbook at larger tickets with gold counters attached.

Turnaround. Piramal (the old DHFL book plus a wholesale real-estate legacy), Poonawalla (the 2021 rescue of Magma Fincorp) and Sammaan (the former Indiabulls Housing) have each spent years shrinking or cleaning legacy books. Their retail engines are growing fast, but returns on equity are still in single digits or low double digits, and their valuations swing on whether investors believe the clean-up is finished.

RBI as co-pilot: risk weights, bans, gold rules and a new upper layer

How scale-based regulation works, what changed in 2026, and why the sector's growth rate is partly set in Mumbai.

For most of their history NBFCs were lightly regulated cousins of banks: lower capital rules, later bad-loan recognition, few limits on concentration. That gap has been closing for a decade, and since 2023 the RBI has used its tools more actively on NBFCs than on almost any other part of finance. It has changed risk weights, banned specific lenders from specific products, written detailed rules on how gold must be weighed and auctioned, forced the largest NBFCs to list, and in 2026 redrew the boundary of its top tier to pull in the government lenders. For an investor, regulatory risk is not a footnote here; it is one of the main inputs.

Who regulates what

The RBI regulates NBFCs, housing-finance companies (moved from the National Housing Bank in August 2019), microfinance lenders and asset-reconstruction companies. Its Department of Regulation writes the rules; its Department of Supervision inspects. SEBI governs listed equity and debt; the Ministry of Corporate Affairs governs the Companies Act. For PFC, REC, IRFC and IREDA, the government is also the owner, acting through the Ministries of Power, Railways and New & Renewable Energy, so policy can arrive as a shareholder instruction rather than a regulation.

Announced in October 2021 and effective from October 2022, scale-based regulation (SBR) sorts NBFCs into layers, with rules tightening as size and systemic importance rise.

A draft in April 2026 and final rules reported in June 2026 replaced the parametric score with a flat test: total assets of ₹1 lakh crore or more, reviewed every three years. The detail of the final text comes from a single secondary source, so it should be read as "finalised in June 2026, per press reports".

The implication is largest for PFC, REC and IRFC. A state lender that lends heavily to a handful of state utilities now faces upper-layer discipline on single-group exposure. That matters even more after PFC absorbs REC, because the merged balance sheet of about ₹11.6 lakh crore concentrates the same state exposures in one entity. IREDA, with total assets of ₹93,802 crore at March 2026 and a book growing about 19% a year, is likely to cross ₹1 lakh crore during FY27. On a simple reading of reported loan books, roughly a dozen of the 25 listed names are above the line, but the RBI's formal list under the new test has not been published, so this is INDICATIVE.

Capital, provisioning and the microfinance rulebook

NBFCs must hold a capital ratio of 15%, with Tier-1 of at least 10%; gold lenders with at least half their assets in gold loans need Tier-1 of 12%, and microfinance NBFCs 15%. Listed NBFCs have provisioned under the Ind AS 109 expected-credit-loss model since FY19, sorting loans into Stage 1 (performing), Stage 2 (30–90 days overdue or significantly riskier) and Stage 3 (credit-impaired). A November 2021 clarification that an NPA can be upgraded only once all arrears are cleared caused a one-time jump in reported bad loans at vehicle financiers in FY22, and it is why Chola and Sundaram still report two NPA numbers: Stage 3 under accounting rules and GNPA under the RBI's stricter definition. The 2022 harmonised microfinance framework capped household income at ₹3 lakh and repayments at 50% of monthly income; the industry's own guardrails since 2025 limit a borrower to three lenders and ₹2 lakh of total microfinance debt.

In plain terms

The RBI is not a referee standing at the edge of the pitch; it is closer to a co-driver. When it thinks a type of lending is getting reckless it makes it more expensive (risk weights) or simply stops a lender from doing it (embargoes), and when the danger passes it eases off. For NBFC investors that means a regulator's speech or circular can matter as much as a quarter's results, and the old advantage of being "not a bank" keeps shrinking.

Three decades of scares: deposit runs, IL&FS, DHFL, COVID and the microfinance hangover

The cycle that shaped today's sector, and the lessons each crisis left behind in the rulebook and in valuations.

Indian NBFCs have a crisis roughly every five to seven years, and each one has a recognisable shape. Something breaks on the liability side (deposits, commercial paper, bank lines) or in a crowded corner of the asset side (microfinance, developer loans, unsecured personal loans). The weak are exposed, the RBI tightens, the strong take share, and a few years later a new boom begins in a different product. Knowing the pattern is the best protection against assuming today's good numbers are permanent.

1990s: the deposit-taking free-for-all

In the 1990s thousands of NBFCs took public deposits with little supervision, often promising high rates to fund risky lending. The collapse of CRB Capital in 1997 triggered an amendment to the RBI Act that made registration compulsory, set minimum net owned funds and curbed deposit-taking. The number of deposit-taking NBFCs shrank drastically, and the survivors with public-deposit licences (Bajaj, Shriram, Sundaram, M&M) still treat them as a prized, sticky source of funding.

2000s–2010: professionalisation, then Andhra Pradesh

The vehicle financiers built national franchises, and Bajaj Auto Finance reinvented itself as a consumer lender in 2007–08, becoming Bajaj Finance in 2010. Microfinance boomed and, in October 2010, broke: after reports of coercive collection and over-lending, Andhra Pradesh passed an ordinance that effectively stopped repayments in the state. The Malegam Committee followed, and in 2011 the RBI created the NBFC-MFI category with income and indebtedness caps. The lesson, relearned in 2023–25, was that over-lending to borrowers with irregular incomes ends in a political as well as a credit crisis.

2013–2018: the golden run and the hidden mismatch

When the RBI's asset-quality review of 2015 forced public-sector banks to recognise their bad loans, those banks stopped lending, and NBFCs and housing-finance companies stepped in. They grew 18–20% a year or more, funded heavily by mutual funds buying their commercial paper and bonds. Many funded long loans (housing, developer finance, loans against property) with short money. It worked while money was abundant.

2018–2020: IL&FS, DHFL and the pandemic

In September 2018 IL&FS defaulted on about ₹91,000 crore of debt; the government superseded its board on 1 October. Mutual funds stopped rolling NBFC paper, spreads spiked and lenders with gaps between their loans and liabilities had to sell assets. In June 2019 DHFL defaulted, with creditor claims of about ₹87,000–90,000 crore, and in November 2019 became the first financial firm referred to the bankruptcy code under a special window. Piramal's ₹34,250 crore resolution plan completed in September 2021, with creditors recovering roughly 37–46% depending on the measure. Altico, Reliance Capital, Reliance Home Finance and Srei also failed; Indiabulls Housing began a multi-year shrink that ended only with Sammaan's 2026 write-down. Then COVID brought a six-month moratorium (March–August 2020), special liquidity windows for NBFCs and a partial credit guarantee for their paper. NBFCs over-provisioned and wrote back in FY22–23.

2021–2023: consolidation and re-regulation

Scale-based regulation arrived. HDFC Ltd, India's largest housing-finance company, merged into HDFC Bank in July 2023, removing the biggest NBFC-type lender from the sector. Shriram Transport, Shriram City and Shriram Capital merged into Shriram Finance in December 2022. Jio Financial was demerged from Reliance and listed in August 2023.

2023–2025: the unsecured and microfinance hangover

The RBI's November 2023 risk-weight increase, together with visible over-lending in microfinance and small-ticket personal loans, set off a two-year credit cycle. Microfinance arrears (portfolio at risk of 31–180 days) rose to 6.3% in March 2025 from about 2% in 2023. Credit costs rose at diversified lenders: Bajaj's reached 2.09% in FY26 and Poonawalla took a one-time ₹666 crore provision on its small personal-loan book that produced an FY25 loss. Then the repo cuts, the February 2025 risk-weight rollback and the industry's tighter guardrails started the healing: arrears were down to 2.0% by March 2026 and the microfinance portfolio grew quarter on quarter for the first time in seven quarters.

The pattern across these crises is consistent. In plain terms

NBFCs do not usually fail because of one bad loan. They fail because a whole group of lenders gets into the same thing at the same time, funded by money that can disappear overnight. Every time that has happened, the lenders with a big parent and patient funding have bought market share cheaply from the ones without. Today's numbers are the best in years, which is exactly when the next crowded trade tends to be forming.

Reading a lender like a credit committee: the ROA tree and the KPIs inside it Deep dive

One equation, seventeen numbers, a ₹1,000 crore loan book run for three years through a bad patch, and a ten-minute routine for quarterly results. Each KPI with its formula, a worked example, its drivers, a benchmark and the trap inside it.

The equation: the ROA tree, from yield to return on equity

Every lender, from IRFC to Five-Star, can be written as one chain of percentages of its loan book. Multiply by how many rupees of loans each rupee of equity supports and you have the return on equity (ROE). Analysts call this the ROA tree, and every KPI in this section is a branch of it.

In plain terms

An NBFC's whole business fits on one line: what its loans earn, minus what its money costs, minus what it spends to run itself, minus the loans that go bad, minus tax. What is left, as a share of the loans, is the ROA. Multiply by how much it has borrowed against its own capital and you get the return to shareholders. Every number below is one piece of that line.

AUM and AUM growth

What it is. Assets under management: loans on the balance sheet plus loans sold (securitised or assigned) that the lender still services. AUM growth is the lender's top line. Formula: (AUM at end − AUM a year earlier) ÷ AUM a year earlier. Worked example: Bajaj Finance's AUM rose from about ₹4,41,000 crore (implied) to ₹5,46,944 crore in the year to June 2026, +24%; ₹36,969 crore of that was added in the single quarter. What drives it: disbursements (new loans) minus repayments. A gold book churns several times a year, so it can grow or shrink fast; an infrastructure book runs off over 10–20 years, so it moves slowly. Disbursement growth above AUM growth means the book is building; disbursements falling while AUM still grows means the book is ageing.

Benchmark: for a retail lender, 18–25% with stable credit cost is good; 12–18% is average; below 10% or above 35% are both worrying (the second because fast growth hides bad loans until the book seasons). For infrastructure lenders, 8–12% is normal. How the 25 compare: the fastest growers in Q1 FY27 were Jio Credit (from a small base), Capri, Manappuram, Muthoot and IIFL, all powered by gold; the slowest were SBI Cards (+3%), HDB (+11.4%), Five-Star (+10%) and the government lenders. The trap: in gold, AUM growth in FY26 was mostly the gold price, not more borrowers. Muthoot's gold AUM rose 44% while the gold it held fell 6%.

Yield on AUM

What it is. What the loans earn. Formula: interest income ÷ average AUM (or average loans). Worked example: Five-Star earned a 22.46% yield in Q1 FY27; on an average book of about ₹13,450 crore that is about ₹3,020 crore of interest a year. What drives it: product mix (unsecured and small-ticket loans yield more), competition, the rate cycle for floating loans, and fee-like charges booked as interest. Benchmark: only within archetype: gold 17–20%, small-ticket MSME 20–24%, consumer 15–17%, vehicle 14–17%, infrastructure 9.5–10.5%. How they compare: Five-Star 22.46%, Muthoot 17.93% (down from 20.16% in FY26), SBFC 17.90%, Capri 17.0%, IIFL 16.53%, SBI Cards 16.0%, HDB 14.19%, PFC 9.96% (FY26), REC 9.55%. The trap: a sharply rising yield is not good news unless the mix change is deliberate; it usually means the lender is moving down the credit curve. Yields falling while funding costs rise, as at Muthoot, is the combination that crushes margins.

Cost of funds

What it is. What the borrowed money costs. Formula: interest expense ÷ average borrowings. Worked example: a lender with ₹8,000 crore of average borrowings and ₹680 crore of annual interest expense has a cost of funds of 8.5%. What drives it: the repo rate and bond yields (with the lags described in section 4), the credit rating, the parent, the funding mix (deposits and bank lines versus bonds), and tenor. Benchmark: below 7.5% is good, 7.5–9% average, above 9.5% weak. How they compare: from SBI Cards at 6.5% to Muthoot at 9.28% (chart in section 4). The trap: companies use different denominators. Shriram reports a "cost of liabilities" that includes deposits; M&M reports interest cost as a share of total assets (5.8%), which is lower than cost of borrowings because part of the assets is funded by equity. Compare like with like, and watch the incremental cost (Shriram 7.77% against 8.56% on the book) for the direction of travel.

NIM, and why it is wider than the spread

What it is. Net interest margin: what is left from interest after paying for money, as a share of the book. Formula: (interest income − interest expense) ÷ average AUM or average interest-earning assets. The related spread is yield minus cost of funds. Worked example: PFC in FY26 had a yield of 9.96% and a cost of funds of 7.50%, a spread of 2.46%, but a NIM of 3.55%. The roughly 1.1-point difference exists because part of the book is funded by equity, which carries no interest: a lender with 15% of its book funded by equity earns the full yield on that slice. What drives it: everything in yield and cost of funds, plus leverage (less leverage means a higher NIM for the same spread).

Benchmark: gold above 11%, consumer above 9.5%, vehicle above 8%, infrastructure above 3.5% are good. How they compare: Five-Star 19.97%, SBI Cards 10.8%, Manappuram ~10.5%, L&T Finance 10.47% (including fees), Muthoot 10.41%, Bajaj 9.5%, Shriram 9.04%, Chola 8.2%, PFC 3.54%, REC 3.34%, IRFC 1.48%. The trap: NIM is the most inconsistently defined number in the sector. L&T and Poonawalla include fees; M&M divides by total assets; IIFL's 4.7% on total AUM rises to about 7% on on-book loans because 35% of its AUM is off-book, earning fees rather than interest. A low-leverage lender shows a high NIM partly because it has lots of equity, not because it lends well.

Operating expenses: opex/AUM and cost-to-income

What it is. The cost of running the lender: staff, branches, technology, marketing, collections. Formulas: opex ÷ average AUM; and cost-to-income = opex ÷ net total income (NII plus fees). Worked example: Chola spent ₹1,787 crore in Q1 FY27 against net income of ₹4,930 crore: cost-to-income 36.2%, and about 2.9% of its average AUM annualised. What drives it: ticket size (a ₹3 lakh loan costs nearly as much to underwrite as a ₹30 lakh one), branch expansion, field collections, technology, and operating leverage as books mature. Benchmark: retail opex below 3% of AUM is good, 3–4.5% average, above 5% weak unless yields are very high; government lenders run below 0.2%. Cost-to-income below 30% is good, 30–40% average, above 45% weak.

How they compare: Shriram's cost-to-income of 25.5% and Sundaram's 30.7% are the leanest private names; Bajaj is at 33.4%; Chola, Tata Capital and M&M at about 36%; HDB at 39.9%; Capri at 44.2%; Piramal at 52.5% and SBI Cards at 58.7% are the heaviest. Credit cost

What it is. The share of the book lost to bad loans in a year, as provisions plus write-offs. It is the single biggest swing factor in NBFC profits. Formula: (provisions + write-offs) ÷ average AUM, annualised. Worked example: Chola's loan losses were ₹922 crore in Q1 FY27; annualised (₹3,688 crore) against an average book of about ₹2.49 lakh crore that is 1.48%. What drives it: the product (secured or unsecured, collateral quality), the borrower segment, the economic and rural cycle, underwriting discipline, collections capacity, and provisioning policy (management overlays can be built in good years and released in bad ones). Benchmark: gold below 0.5%, vehicle below 1.5%, consumer below 2% and infrastructure near zero are good; vehicle above 2.5%, consumer above 3% and microfinance above 5% are weak. ICRA expects 2.0–2.2% for NBFC-Retail in FY27.

How they compare: SBI Cards has the highest at 6.5%, but that is down from about 8.6% in FY26. L&T Finance (2.54%), Poonawalla (2.40%) and HDB (2.32%) are the next highest. Bajaj's 1.54% includes a ₹296 crore management overlay; without it, 1.31%. Muthoot's is 0.12%, and PFC, REC and IRFC are running net write-backs. The trap: a falling credit cost can be a released overlay, a recovery from a written-off account, or a change in write-off policy rather than better loans. And credit cost on a fast-growing book looks low because new loans have not had time to go bad: divide this year's losses by the book of two years ago to see the seasoned rate.

PPOP: the shock absorber

What it is. Pre-provision operating profit: what the lender earns before any loan losses. Expressed against the book, it tells you how much credit cost the lender can absorb before it starts losing money. Formula: (NII + fees − opex) ÷ average AUM; or PPOP ÷ credit cost as a cover ratio. Worked example: Bajaj's Q1 FY27 PPOP was ₹10,137 crore, about 7.7% of average AUM annualised, against a 1.54% credit cost: cover of about five times. HDB's lending PPOP of ₹1,726 crore is about 5.7% of its book against a 2.32% credit cost: cover of about 2.5 times. What drives it: NIM, fees and efficiency. Benchmark: PPOP more than twice credit cost and above 5% of AUM for retail lenders is good; 1.5–2 times average; below 1.3 times weak. The trap: a lender with a fat PPOP can look safe while lending into a segment whose losses could triple; the cover ratio needs a stress number, not the current one. The ₹1,000 crore example later in this section shows this.

GNPA and Stage 3

What it is. Gross non-performing assets: loans more than 90 days overdue, as a share of gross loans. Under Ind AS the closest equivalent is Stage 3 (credit-impaired). Formula: 90+ days-past-due loans ÷ gross loans. Worked example: Shriram's Stage 3 of 4.64% on its ₹3.14 lakh crore book means about ₹14,560 crore of loans are credit-impaired. What drives it: the segment (used trucks and small businesses carry structurally higher arrears than gold), the cycle, the speed of write-offs, and recognition rules. Benchmark: below 2% good, 2–4% average, above 5% weak, read by archetype: a used-CV lender at 4.5% can be healthy; a gold lender at 4.5% would be in trouble. The NBFC system was at 2.4% in March 2026.

How they compare: Shriram (4.64%), IREDA (3.76%), Five-Star (3.46%), M&M (3.45%) and Chola (3.29% Stage 3; 4.50% under RBI norms) are highest; Bajaj (0.96%), REC (0.23%) and IRFC (nil) lowest. The trap: Chola and Sundaram report two numbers. Their Stage 3 follows accounting rules; their RBI-norm GNPA is higher because of the 2021 rule that a loan cannot be upgraded until all arrears are cleared. Always check which one a table uses. And a lender that writes off bad loans fast shows a lower GNPA than one that holds them: look at write-offs too.

NNPA and provision coverage

What it is. Net NPA is the bad-loan pile after subtracting the provisions already set aside against it; the provision coverage ratio (PCR) is the share of Stage 3 loans already provided for. Together they tell you how much of the known damage has already passed through the profit and loss account. Formulas: NNPA = (Stage 3 loans − Stage 3 provisions) ÷ net loans; PCR = Stage 3 provisions ÷ Stage 3 loans. Worked example: Shriram's gross Stage 3 of 4.64% on a ₹3.14 lakh crore book is about ₹14,560 crore. With 51% coverage, about ₹7,400 crore is already provided for and about ₹7,150 crore remains on the balance sheet at book value, which is 2.33% of net loans. If those loans eventually recover 60 paise in the rupee, the remaining exposure is fully covered; if they recover 40 paise, Shriram still has to book about ₹1,300 crore of further losses.

What drives it: the collateral (a repossessed truck or a pledged necklace recovers far more than an unsecured personal loan, so secured lenders can carry lower coverage), the speed of write-offs, and management's provisioning stance. Benchmark: NNPA below 1% is good, 1–2% average, above 2.5% weak; PCR above 60% good, 45–60% average, below 40% weak, read against the collateral. How they compare: PFC carries the thickest cover at 86%, followed by L&T Finance (~69%), IREDA (68%), Bajaj (60%) and SBI Cards (60%). Manappuram (~31%), Fedbank (~38%) and Five-Star (40%) are lowest; for the two gold-heavy lenders that is defensible, for Five-Star it means its rising Stage 3 is carried mostly on property collateral. The trap: some presentations quote a "PCR" that includes Stage 1 and 2 provisions or technically written-off loans. IIFL's deck shows 94%; the simple Stage 3 calculation gives about 50%. Always recompute it as 1 − NNPA ÷ GNPA.

Stage 2: the early warning

What it is. Loans that are 30–90 days overdue, or that the lender judges to have suffered a significant increase in credit risk, under the Ind AS expected-loss rules. Stage 3 tells you where the book has been; Stage 2 tells you where it is heading. Formula: Stage 2 loans ÷ gross loans. Worked example: Five-Star had ₹1,224 crore in Stage 2 out of ₹13,722 crore in June 2026, 8.9% of the book, and 12.4% of its loans were more than 30 days overdue. If even a quarter of the Stage 2 pile rolls into Stage 3, GNPA rises by more than two percentage points. What drives it: the same forces as Stage 3, about two quarters earlier, plus seasonality (rural books slip before harvests and recover after them). Benchmark: below 4% good, 4–7% average, above 8% or rising for three quarters weak.

How they compare: only a handful disclose it cleanly. Bajaj is about 0.9%, Poonawalla about 1.0%, Muthoot about 1.1%, HDB 1.88%, Chola 2.72% (up from 2.47% in March), SBI Cards about 3.6%, M&M about 4.85% and Five-Star 8.9%. Shriram, Tata Capital, L&T Finance, Aditya Birla Capital, PFC and REC did not disclose a Stage 2 figure in the sources used. Where Stage 2 is missing, the monthly collection-efficiency number and the 1+ or 30+ days-past-due ratio do the same job: SBFC's 1+ DPD rose from 8.12% to 9.18% in a year, the same direction as its credit cost. The trap: Stage 2 includes judgement calls, so two lenders with the same arrears can report different numbers; and a lender that writes off quickly can keep Stage 2 low while losses run through the profit and loss account.

Return on assets (ROA)

What it is. Profit after tax as a share of the assets that produced it: the purest single measure of how good a lending franchise is, because it is independent of how much the lender has borrowed. Formula: PAT ÷ average total assets (some companies divide by AUM). Worked example: HDB earned ₹785 crore in Q1 FY27, or about ₹3,140 crore annualised. On its average loan book of about ₹1.20 lakh crore that is 2.6%; HDB reports 2.50% because it divides by total assets, which include cash and investments as well as loans. What drives it: every line in the ROA tree above: yield, cost of funds, fees, opex, credit cost and tax. Benchmark: for retail lenders above 3% good, 2–3% average, below 1.5% weak; for gold lenders above 5%; for infrastructure lenders above 2.5%. The NBFC system earned 2.3% in FY26 (RBI FSR), against about 1.3–1.4% for banks; ICRA expects 2.5–2.7% for NBFC-Retail in FY27.

How they compare: the high-yield secured lenders lead: Five-Star 8.11%, Muthoot 6.09%, Bajaj 4.7%, SBFC 4.53% and Capri 4.1%. The government lenders sit at 2.6–3.4% except IRFC (1.5%), whose cost-plus lease model caps its margin. Turnaround names (Piramal's growth business 1.9%, Poonawalla 1.98%, Sammaan about 1.7%) and Jio Credit (about 1.4%) are at the bottom. The trap: bases differ more than for any other ratio. Chola's 3.7% is pre-tax (about 2.7–2.8% after tax); Five-Star's 8.11% is on AUM; Piramal's 1.9% covers only its growth business. Off-book loans make ROA on assets look higher than ROA on AUM, because the fee income arrives without the asset: IIFL keeps 35% of its AUM off its balance sheet.

Leverage

What it is. How many rupees of assets each rupee of shareholders' money supports. It is the multiplier that turns a modest ROA into a respectable ROE, and the amplifier that turns a bad year into a capital problem. Formulas: debt ÷ equity (D/E), AUM ÷ equity, or total assets ÷ equity. Worked example: Chola's borrowings were 6.93 times its equity in March 2026, so its assets were roughly eight times equity. A post-tax ROA of about 2.7% times eight gives about 21%, which is Chola's reported ROE. What drives it: the 15% capital floor (which caps leverage at roughly six to seven times for a book with 100% risk weights), rating-agency tolerance, the product's risk weight, growth, and the timing of equity raises.

Benchmark: for retail lenders, 4–6 times assets to equity is good, 6–7 times average, above 7 times weak; below 2.5 times is lazy capital that depresses ROE. Infrastructure lenders run 6–8 times. How they compare (D/E, March 2026 unless noted): IRFC 7.43, Chola 6.93, REC 6.10, IREDA 5.59, Tata Capital 5.3, HDB 5.0, IIFL 4.97, PFC 4.85 standalone (7.62 consolidated), M&M 4.82, Sundaram 4.68, Fedbank 4.67, ABCL 4.24, L&T Finance 3.94, Bajaj 3.82, Manappuram 3.60, Muthoot 3.59, Capri 3.35, Piramal 2.84, SBI Cards 2.80, Shriram 2.14 (after MUFG), SBFC 1.92, Five-Star 1.11 and Jio Financial's group 0.16. The trap: companies use the word "leverage" for different ratios (SBFC's "3.25×" is assets to equity; its debt to equity is 1.92); a fresh equity raise drops leverage overnight and with it ROE; and loans sold or co-lent off the balance sheet do not show up at all.

Return on equity (ROE)

What it is. What shareholders earn on the capital they have in the business, and the number the stock market ultimately prices. Formula: PAT ÷ average equity, or equivalently ROA × average assets ÷ average equity. Worked example: Muthoot's 6.09% ROA multiplied by assets of about 4.4 times equity gives its 26.6% ROE. Bajaj gets to 20.4% with a lower ROA (4.7%) and similar leverage (about 4.3 times). What drives it: ROA and leverage; equity raises (which dilute ROE until the capital is deployed); and dividend policy, because capital paid out stops earning. Benchmark: above 18% good, 13–18% average, below 12% weak. Lenders with a cost of equity around 13–15% need an ROE above that just to be worth their book value, which is why the line between 13% and 15% matters so much in section 12.

How they compare: Muthoot (26.6%), Chola (21.2%), PFC (20.7%), Bajaj (20.4%) and REC (20.1%) lead; IIFL (19.5%) and Capri (19.1%) are close behind. The large mid-pack sits at 13–17%. At the bottom are the turnaround and capital-heavy names: Poonawalla about 9.5% and Piramal about 6.4% (both annualised), Sammaan about 5%, and Jio Financial 1.2%. The trap: ROE is distorted by capital events. Shriram's 12.8% is diluted by MUFG's ₹39,618 crore, much of it not yet lent out (its FY26 ROE was about 16.4%); Jio's 1.2% reflects a ₹1.34 lakh crore net worth mostly held in a Reliance stake and cash, not a weak loan book; Sammaan's FY26 ROE of about −35% is one quarter's write-down. Read ROE alongside ROA and leverage, never alone.

CRAR and Tier-1 capital

What it is. The capital-to-risk-weighted-assets ratio: the cushion of capital the RBI requires against the riskiness of the book. NBFCs must hold at least 15%, with Tier-1 (equity and retained profit) of at least 10%; upper-layer NBFCs must also keep core equity (CET1) of 9%; gold lenders with half their book in gold need Tier-1 of 12%. Formula: (Tier-1 + Tier-2 capital) ÷ risk-weighted assets. Worked example: ₹10,000 crore of personal loans at a 125% risk weight counts as ₹12,500 crore of risk-weighted assets and needs ₹1,875 crore of capital at the 15% floor; the same ₹10,000 crore of gold loans at 100% needs ₹1,500 crore. That ₹375 crore difference is why the November 2023 risk-weight increase slowed unsecured lending. What drives it: retained profit, equity raises, Tier-2 bond issues, growth and risk weights.

Benchmark: above 20% (Tier-1 above 17%) good, 17–20% average, below 16% weak. The NBFC system stood at 24.6% in March 2026. How they compare: Five-Star (51.9%) and SBFC (32.0%) hold far more than they need; Shriram jumped to 34.2% on MUFG's money. Most of the large lenders sit between 18.5% and 23.5%. The trap: look at Tier-1, not the headline. Chola's CRAR of 19.81% includes Tier-2 debt; its Tier-1 of 14.81% is the lowest among the large private names and explains its regular capital raises. Consolidated and standalone ratios can differ sharply too: IIFL is 24.3% consolidated but 17.1% for the standalone parent that holds the gold book. And very high capital is not purely a strength; it caps ROE, as Five-Star's 14.5% ROE on an 8% ROA shows.

Liquidity: ALM gaps, LCR and the borrowing mix

What it is. Three measures of whether the lender can keep paying its own creditors if new money stops arriving. The asset-liability (ALM) gap compares the loans falling due in each time bucket with the borrowings falling due; the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) compares high-quality liquid assets with the net cash that could leave in a 30-day stress; the borrowing mix shows how dependent the lender is on any one source. Formulas: cumulative inflows − outflows by maturity bucket; LCR = liquid assets ÷ 30-day stressed net outflows (100% required for large NBFCs since December 2024); each funding source ÷ total borrowings. Worked example: Shriram reported an LCR of 262.5% in Q1 FY27; Piramal held ₹6,925 crore of liquid assets, an LCR of 553%; Chola kept ₹22,765 crore of cash, about 9% of its AUM. A lender with ₹1,000 crore of debt maturing in the next three months and ₹600 crore of loan collections due has a ₹400 crore gap that it must cover from cash or new borrowing.

What drives it: the tenor of the products (gold loans run for months, home loans for decades), the tenor of the funding, and the cost of holding idle cash. Benchmark: positive cumulative gaps in every bucket up to one year is good; commercial paper above 10% of borrowings, or any single source above 60%, is weak. How they compare: SBI Cards draws 62% of its money from bank working-capital lines and 14% from commercial paper, the highest CP share in the group; Bajaj gets half from money markets (mostly long-dated bonds); Shriram's 31% public deposits are the stickiest money any private NBFC has. The trap: excess liquidity costs money (it earns less than it costs), so very high LCRs depress margins, and liquidity always looks best the quarter after a capital raise.

The segment KPIs: gold LTV, collection efficiency and ticket size

Gold LTV. The loan as a share of the market value of the pledged gold. Formula: loan outstanding ÷ market value of collateral. Worked example: a ₹1 lakh loan against jewellery worth ₹1.56 lakh is at 64%. If gold falls 15%, the jewellery is worth about ₹1.33 lakh and the LTV jumps to 75%, the cap for loans above ₹5 lakh, with interest still accruing. Benchmark: below 60% good, 60–68% average, above 70% weak. NBFCs averaged below 60% in FY26; banks about 55% (ICRA). Muthoot is at about 64% (65–66% including accrued interest), Manappuram 65.6% (up from 57.3% in March), IIFL 70% and Fedbank about 60%. Collection efficiency. Collections ÷ amounts billed in the month; above 99% good for vehicle and MSME lenders, below 95% weak. Five-Star reported 97.9%. The trap: many lenders exclude arrears from the denominator, which flatters the number. Ticket size. The average loan. A rising ticket means a lender is moving towards bigger, more competitive borrowers: SBFC's average MSME ticket rose from ₹9.38 lakh to ₹10.51 lakh in a year. Under the new gold rules, loans above ₹2.5 lakh need an income assessment, which makes larger gold tickets slower to write.

In plain terms

A bad year for a lender usually does not mean a loss; it means the year's profit is used up paying for bad loans. Whether it tips into a loss depends on how much the lender earns before those losses. Our example book earned ₹59 crore before its ₹42 crore of bad-loan costs, so it stayed profitable. A lender earning half that would not have.

Definition traps, collected

  • NIM has at least four definitions. On average AUM, on average loans, on total assets (M&M), or including fees (L&T Finance, Poonawalla, Aditya Birla Capital). The gap between definitions can be a full percentage point.
  • ROA can be pre-tax. Chola's 3.7% is pre-tax; after tax it is about 2.7–2.8%. Five-Star reports on AUM, Piramal on its growth business only.
  • Two bad-loan numbers. Chola and Sundaram report Stage 3 (accounting) and a higher RBI-norm GNPA (4.50% against 3.29% at Chola). Tables that mix them are wrong.
  • Consolidated against standalone. PFC consolidated includes all of REC (GNPA 0.66% consolidated, 1.11% standalone); Muthoot's and Manappuram's ratios are standalone while their AUM is consolidated; IIFL's CRAR is 24.3% consolidated and 17.1% standalone.
  • "AUM" is not always loans on the balance sheet. IIFL keeps 35% off-book and Capri 20%; SBI Cards' "AUM" is card receivables; Aditya Birla Capital's total lending includes its housing subsidiary.
  • Cost of funds has different denominators. Shriram's "cost of liabilities" includes deposits; M&M reports interest cost on total assets (5.8%), which is not comparable with a cost of borrowings.
  • PCR can include more than Stage 3. IIFL's deck shows 94%; the simple calculation gives about 50%.
  • Cost-to-income can include credit cost. Five-Star's 45.9% does; nobody else's does.
  • ROE moves with capital events. Shriram (MUFG), Poonawalla (QIP), Piramal (QIP), Sammaan (write-down) and Jio (holding-company equity) all show ROEs that say more about their capital than their lending.
  • Growth can be price. Gold-loan AUM grows when gold gets dearer even if no new customer walks in: Muthoot's gold AUM rose 44% while its tonnage fell 6%.
  • Multiples can be stale. Screener's Shriram P/B (2.77×) uses pre-MUFG book value; its Piramal P/E (106×) uses pre-merger share counts. The corrected figures are 2.10× and about 27×.

How to read an NBFC's quarterly results in ten minutes

Results arrive as a press release, an investor presentation and the audited statements. In this order, the presentation gives you almost everything in ten minutes.

  1. AUM and disbursements (1 minute). Year-on-year and quarter-on-quarter growth, and the segment table. Is growth coming from the segments management says it is focusing on? Red flag: disbursements falling while AUM still grows, or a sudden jump in one unsecured product.
  2. Price versus volume (30 seconds, gold lenders). Tonnes of gold held and the gold price per gram. If AUM growth is above the gold-price change, the lender is adding customers; if below, it is losing them.
  3. Yield, cost of funds, NIM (1 minute). Note the definition. Red flag: yield falling and cost of funds rising in the same quarter, as at Muthoot in Q1 FY27 (17.9% and 9.28%), which cut NIM by three points.
  4. Opex and cost-to-income (30 seconds). Compare with guidance. Branch expansion should show up here first.
  5. Credit cost (1 minute). Annualise it, and check for overlays or write-backs. Bajaj's 1.54% includes a ₹296 crore overlay; strip it out to see 1.31%. Red flag: credit cost above guidance two quarters running.
  6. Stage 2, Stage 3 and PCR (1.5 minutes). Direction matters more than level. Red flag: Stage 2 and Stage 3 rising together while PCR falls, as at Chola in Q1 FY27 on two of the three.
  7. Write-offs and recoveries (30 seconds). A falling GNPA with rising write-offs is not an improvement.
  8. ROA and ROE (30 seconds). Check the basis (pre-tax? on AUM?) and any capital raised during the quarter.
  9. CRAR, Tier-1 and liquidity (1 minute). How long can the lender grow before it needs capital? What is the LCR and the CP share?
  10. Borrowing mix and incremental cost (1 minute). Is the incremental cost below the book average (Shriram 7.77% against 8.56%)? That is next year's margin.
  11. Guidance and one-offs (1 minute). Compare with what was promised last quarter, and look for one-time gains in "other income": Piramal's FY26 profit included ₹1,377 crore of other income in a single quarter.

If three or more of the red flags appear together, the next quarter usually brings a credit-cost surprise. If none appear and incremental funding costs are falling, the quarter after that usually brings an upgrade to guidance.

How to read across the rows

Start with the last two columns and work backwards. The market's price for each lender (P/B) is a verdict on the columns to its left, and the interesting rows are the ones where the verdict looks out of line with the numbers. Bajaj and Chola earn 20–21% returns on equity and trade at 4.5–5.3 times book; PFC and REC earn the same 20–21% and trade at about book. Muthoot earns the highest ROE in the table, 26.6%, and trades at under ten times earnings, the cheapest P/E of any private lender. Poonawalla earns under 10% on its equity and trades at about three times book. None of these is a mistake by the market; each is a judgement about how long the current return will last, and section 12 unpacks it.

Then read growth against credit cost. Fast growth with low credit cost (Muthoot, Manappuram, Capri, Fedbank) is mostly the gold price at work. Fast growth with high credit cost (L&T Finance, Poonawalla) is a lender pushing into unsecured or rural lending and paying for it. Slow growth with high credit cost (SBI Cards, HDB) is the hardest combination to re-rate. Slow growth with near-zero credit cost (PFC, REC, IRFC) is the government lenders' world, where the constraint is mandate, not risk appetite.

Finally, read capital against ROE. Five-Star, SBFC and Shriram hold 32–52% CRAR, so their ROEs understate how profitable their lending is; Chola's Tier-1 of 14.8% means it will keep raising capital, so its ROE is closer to its ceiling. The NNPA column tells you whose capital is already partly spoken for: Chola (2.95% on the RBI norm), Shriram (2.33%) and Five-Star (2.10%) carry the most unprovided bad loans relative to their books.

Who is winning: growth that stays clean, and margins that funding can defend

Two positioning maps, a growth league table and a 25-company scorecard show who is gaining ground, who is buying growth with risk, and who is standing still.

"Winning" for a lender means three things at once: growing the book faster than the system without a matching rise in losses, earning a margin that its funding can sustain when rates turn, and doing both without running short of capital. Very few of the 25 tick all three boxes at once. The maps below place each company on the two trade-offs that matter most.

Growth against credit cost

The first map plots how fast each lender grew its book in the year to June 2026 against what it lost on bad loans. The bottom-right corner, fast and clean, is where a lender wants to be, but read the names carefully: Muthoot, Manappuram and Fedbank sit there largely because gold got dearer, not because they took share. The genuinely hard-won positions are Bajaj, Chola and Tata Capital, growing 22–24% at credit costs of 1.0–1.5% across books of ₹2.5–5.5 lakh crore. L&T Finance and HDB sit top-left of the cluster: L&T because its rural-business and personal-loan books still cost 2.5% a year, HDB because it is growing only 11% while losing 2.3%. SBI Cards is off the chart at 6.5% credit cost and 3% growth.

Margin against the cost of money

The second map asks whether a lender's margin comes from cheap funding or from high yields. Lenders on the left borrow cheaply because of a AAA parent (SBI, HDFC Bank, L&T, Tata) or a quasi-sovereign owner (PFC, IREDA). Lenders on the right pay 8.8–9.3% because they lack one, and must earn high yields to compensate: Five-Star at the top, gold lenders in the upper right, Piramal and IIFL lower down. The upper-left corner, cheap money and a wide margin, is the strongest place to be, and it is thinly populated: SBI Cards, L&T Finance and, just inside, Bajaj. When rates rise, the lenders on the right feel it first, because their spread over the cost of money is already being earned from riskier customers.

Two ROEs, two multiples: why one 20% return is worth five times book and another one times Deep dive

The arithmetic of price-to-book, what today's prices imply about each lender's future, and why the state's financiers, the gold lenders and the consumer franchises are priced so differently.

On 5 October 2026 Bajaj Finance earned a 20.4% return on equity and traded at 5.30 times book value. PFC, on a standalone basis, earned 20.7% and traded at 0.98 times; REC earned 20.1% at 0.92 times. Muthoot earned 26.6%, the best in the group, at 2.69 times book and under ten times earnings. Across the 25, the market pays about 2.2 times book and 18 times FY26 profit. The spread between these multiples is the clearest statement the market makes about Indian NBFCs, and it can be decoded with one formula.

The formula behind the gap

A lender's book value is the capital it has to lend. If it earns exactly its cost of equity (the return shareholders demand for the risk) on that capital, it is worth book value and no more. If it earns more, and can keep growing that capital, it is worth more. The standard way to write this is the justified price-to-book: P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (CoE − g), where g is the long-run growth rate of the book and CoE the cost of equity.

Take ₹100 of book. An ILLUSTRATIVE Bajaj-like lender earning 20% ROE, growing 11% for the long run, with a 13% cost of equity, is worth (20 − 11) ÷ (13 − 11) = 4.5 times book, or ₹450. Raise long-run growth to 12% and the answer becomes 8 times. An ILLUSTRATIVE PFC-like lender earning 18%, growing 8%, with a 15% cost of equity, is worth (18 − 8) ÷ (15 − 8) = 1.4 times. Three lessons follow. First, when growth gets close to the cost of equity, small changes in either swing the value enormously, which is why premium multiples are fragile. Second, a higher cost of equity, the market's charge for concentration, policy risk or a thin shock absorber, crushes value even when ROE is high. Third, this year's ROE matters less than whether it lasts.

The formula can also be run backwards. Rearranged, the ROE a price implies is g + P/B × (CoE − g). The table applies it to ten of the 25 using assumptions this report has chosen to be reasonable for each archetype; change the assumptions and the answers move, so treat them as ILLUSTRATIVE.

Why the state's lenders trade at book

PFC and REC earn about 20% on equity, have near-zero credit cost and pay dividend yields of 5.8% and 6.3%. On the arithmetic above, the market is either expecting their ROE to fall to about 15% or charging them a cost of equity above 20%. Six reasons explain why:

  • Concentration. Their borrowers are a few dozen state distribution companies, generators and transmission utilities. PFC's standalone book is 33% distribution and 32% conventional generation. Since June 2026 the RBI's revised rules make government NBFCs eligible for the upper layer and remove their exemption from concentration limits (per press reports of the final rules), which bites hardest on a merged ₹11.6 lakh crore book.
  • A thin shock absorber. PPOP is about 3.6% of the book. One large state utility at 5–10% of the book, defaulting with a 50% loss, would cost 2.5–5% of AUM: a year's profit or more. Credit cost is near zero because the last cycle's thermal-power NPAs have been resolved and are now producing write-backs, which will not repeat.
  • Stalled growth. PFC's Q1 FY27 disbursements fell 44% and its standalone book shrank by about ₹10,000 crore in the quarter; REC added only about 3% in FY26 and cut yields to 9.55%; IRFC's book is flat because the railways now fund capex from the budget.
  • The owner. The government sets dividends, directs lending to policy priorities and sells stock: it took its IRFC holding from 86.36% to 82.90% in two quarters.
  • The merger. REC is to be absorbed at 88 PFC shares per 100 REC shares; the boards approved the scheme on 28 June 2026 and the President and the Ministry of Power have signed off, with shareholder, creditor, exchange, SEBI and NCLT approvals pending and 1 April 2027 the target. The fact pack's own calculation, which assumes PFC's 52.63% of REC is cancelled and about 110 crore new PFC shares go to REC's minorities, puts the government's stake in the merged PFC at about 42%, below 51%; how the scheme handles that is not yet clear. At ₹322 for PFC, swap parity values REC at about ₹283, yet REC trades at ₹296, about 4.5% above parity.
  • Look-through value. PFC's REC stake is worth ₹40,782 crore at market, 38% of PFC's own m-cap. Strip it out and the market values standalone PFC at about ₹65,500 crore, roughly 3.3 times its FY26 standalone profit.

The IRFC anomaly, mostly corrected

IRFC long looked like the opposite puzzle: a lender earning 13% on equity with a 1.5% margin, trading at 3 times book near its 52-week high and about 6 times at its July 2024 peak. The explanation was a tiny free float (the government owns 82.9%) held heavily by retail investors (11.9% public, 1.2% foreign institutions) and a cost-plus lease with the Ministry of Railways that carries almost no credit risk. On a near-sovereign cost of equity of 9.5% and 5% growth, the justified P/B is about 1.8 times. At ₹76.5, its 52-week low, IRFC trades at 1.70 times, so the premium has largely gone. What remains is a different question: with the railways no longer drawing new money, IRFC's growth has to come from lending to power, fertiliser, ports and metro projects, which brings the credit risk its valuation used to exclude.

Paying for duration: Bajaj and Chola

Bajaj's 5.3 times book is a bet on two decades, not two years. On a 13% cost of equity, the price implies about 11% growth in book value forever at today's 20% ROE. The supporting evidence is a 124 million-customer franchise, 24% AUM growth, and an underlying credit cost of 1.31% once the ₹296 crore overlay is removed; the evidence against is that foreign investors trimmed their holding from 21.7% to 20.2% over the year, and that the multiple leaves little cushion if unsecured lending relapses as it did in FY26, when credit cost reached 2.09%. Chola's 4.5 times rests on the same logic with more leverage: its Tier-1 of 14.8% means growth will keep requiring new equity, and Stage 2 and Stage 3 both ticked up in Q1 FY27.

Muthoot at under ten times earnings

Muthoot's FY26 profit doubled to ₹10,607 crore and its ROE reached 30.6%. The market's response was to pay 9.6 times trailing earnings, against 23–29 times for Bajaj and Chola. The reason is visible in Q1 FY27: yield fell from 20.2% to 17.9%, cost of funds rose from 8.8% to 9.3%, and NIM dropped three points to 10.4%; tonnes of gold held fell 6% while the gold price did all the work. The stock fell about 14% after those results and sits near its 52-week low of ₹2,671. The price implies an ROE of about 21%, close to Muthoot's pre-boom level. That is the market saying FY26 was a peak.

In plain terms

A lender's share price is a guess about how long its current profits will last. Bajaj's price assumes its 20% return lasts for decades. PFC's price assumes its 20% return will shrink, or that something could go badly wrong with the state utilities it lends to. Neither is a forecast of next year; both are views on the next ten.

What moves a multiple

NBFC multiples change for five reasons, roughly in order of how often they matter. Credit cost through a downturn: a lender that keeps losses inside its guided band in a bad year earns a permanent re-rating, while one that breaks it loses years of premium. The growth runway: SBI Cards fell 37% in a year not because of losses, which improved, but because its receivables grew only 3%. The cost of money: a rating upgrade or a strategic shareholder (MUFG at Shriram, IHC at Sammaan) lowers funding costs and lifts the justified multiple. Capital events: QIPs below the market, promoter sell-downs and government offers for sale add supply. Regulation: the 2023 risk-weight increase and the 2024 IIFL gold embargo each re-priced whole segments overnight.

For the discounted names, the catalysts are specific: completion of the PFC–REC merger with a clear answer on the government's stake; evidence that state utilities' finances (helped by the 2022 late-payment surcharge rules) hold up; and for Muthoot, a stable NIM at a lower gold price. For the premium names, the risks are equally specific: an unsecured-credit relapse at Bajaj, a CSEL or Stage 2 problem at Chola, or a capital raise priced at a discount.

In plain terms

A cheap lender gets re-rated when it proves its profits are not about to fall. An expensive lender gets de-rated when a single bad year shows its losses can rise like everyone else's. That is why a quarter of rising bad loans hurts the premium names far more than the cheap ones.

The gold rush: a price boom dressed as a lending boom Deep dive

How a 47% rise in the gold price doubled the pawnbrokers' profits, why the margin squeeze has already started, what the new RBI rules change, and what a falling gold price would do.

Gold loans were the fastest-growing corner of Indian lending in FY26. ICRA puts NBFC gold-loan AUM at ₹4.0 lakh crore in March 2026, up about 78% in the year, and the whole organised gold-loan market (banks, small finance banks and NBFCs) growing about 50%, on its way to an estimated ₹30 lakh crore by FY28. Gold is now 17.4% of NBFCs' retail lending, according to the RBI's June 2026 Financial Stability Report. Indian households hold an estimated 25,000 tonnes or more of gold, of which only a few percent is pledged, so the runway looks long. But most of FY26's growth did not come from new borrowers or more gold. It came from the price.

Price, not tonnes

Muthoot, which holds about 41% of all NBFC gold loans, publishes the tonnes of gold it holds as security and the price per gram, so the split can be measured directly. In the year to June 2026 the gold price rose 47%, Muthoot's gold-loan AUM rose 44%, and the gold in its vaults fell 6%, from 209 tonnes to 197. Customers pledged less gold and borrowed more against it. Manappuram, smaller at 67 tonnes, did add gold (up 7% in the quarter) but doubled its gold AUM mostly the same way.

In plain terms

When gold gets dearer, the same necklace supports a bigger loan. Muthoot's loan book grew 44% in a year even though customers handed over 6% less gold. The growth was real money lent, but it came from the gold price, and a falling gold price would run the same machine in reverse.

Why a pawnbroker earns 6% on its assets

A gold loan is the closest thing in Indian retail credit to a riskless loan. The collateral is worth roughly 1.5 times the loan, can be valued in minutes, sits in the lender's own vault and can be auctioned within weeks if the borrower stops paying. Most loans are bullet loans repaid in under a year, so the book reprices quickly. The result is a credit cost of 0.12–0.35% of the book at Muthoot, against 1.5–2.5% for vehicle and consumer lenders, on yields of 17–20%. What the business does cost is branches: Muthoot runs 5,029 of them standalone (7,654 across its group), each with a vault, appraisers and security, and spends about 2.3% of its book a year on operations; Manappuram spends 4.6%. What it cannot do is lever much: gold lenders with half their book in gold need 12% Tier-1 capital, and rating agencies dislike bullet-loan funding mismatches, so leverage stays around 3.6 times. That combination gives a 6% ROA and a 25–30% ROE at the peak, and explains section 9's point that a gold lender's PPOP can absorb dozens of times its normal credit cost.

The squeeze has started

Q1 FY27 showed how quickly the economics can turn. Muthoot's yield fell from 20.2% in FY26 to 17.9% as competitors cut rates and the gold rules took effect; its cost of funds rose from 8.8% to 9.3%; its NIM fell from 13.4% in the March quarter to 10.4%. The stock fell about 14% after the results. Competition is the main cause. Banks' retail gold loans more than doubled in FY26 (ICRA), lent at an average LTV of about 55% and funded by deposits costing far less than an NBFC's 9%. ICRA notes that competitive intensity "stays high with entry of larger players", and that the new rules' shift away from bullet loans can mean yield moderation or churn. Manappuram has taken the opposite path, letting its LTV rise from 57.3% to 65.6% in a quarter to defend volumes, which uses up the cushion that makes the business safe.

The new rulebook, effective 1 April 2026

The RBI's Directions on lending against gold and silver, finalised in June 2025 after a 2024 review found irregular practices across lenders, took effect at the start of FY27. Their main provisions: tiered LTV caps of 85% for loans up to ₹2.5 lakh, 80% for ₹2.5–5 lakh and 75% above ₹5 lakh, with accrued interest counted for bullet loans; an assessment of repayment capacity for loans above ₹2.5 lakh; bullet tenors of up to about twelve months; only jewellery, ornaments and bank-minted coins as collateral; and standardised valuation and auction procedures. For the specialists, the effect is mixed. The higher cap on small loans is a relaxation for books dominated by small tickets, like Muthoot's and Manappuram's; the income test makes larger loans slower to write; and standardised processes favour organised lenders with appraisal systems over local pawnbrokers. Q1 FY27 was the first quarter under the rules, so their full effect on yields is not yet visible.

Everyone wants a gold counter

Gold's economics have drawn in almost every diversified lender. Bajaj's gold book more than doubled to ₹21,152 crore and it plans to go from 1,701 gold branches to 2,700–2,800 by March 2027. L&T Finance's grew 182% to ₹3,829 crore across 343 branches. Shriram wants gold to go from 2.5% to 5% of its book. Tata Capital entered in Q1 FY27 by buying 88.6% of Yogloans, with ₹708 crore of AUM and 160 branches. Among the smaller lenders the shift is more dramatic: gold overtook mortgages at Fedbank in the June quarter (53% of AUM), is now 48% of Capri's book (with 1,000 gold branches and 20.2 tonnes in custody) and 22% of SBFC's, where it shares the cost of 220 existing branches. Manappuram plans 500 new gold branches in FY27.

IIFL's 2024 embargo: what happens when the regulator says stop

On 4 March 2024 the RBI ordered IIFL Finance to stop sanctioning, disbursing, assigning or securitising gold loans after inspectors found lapses in assaying purity and weight, LTV breaches, cash disbursals above the legal limit and poor auction practices. The gold book fell about 40% in a quarter. Fairfax, IIFL's largest institutional investor, pledged a $200 million liquidity line, and a ₹1,271 crore rights issue followed in May. The embargo was lifted on 19 September 2024 after a special audit, but FY25 consolidated profit fell to ₹578 crore and the standalone company made a loss of ₹410 crore. Two years on, gold is 51% of IIFL's AUM, above its pre-ban share, and its Q1 FY27 ROE was 19.5%. The episode carries two lessons: the regulator can switch off a gold lender's core business in a day, and the business recovers quickly once it is switched back on. IIFL still trades at 12 times earnings and 1.8 times book, the cheapest P/E among the private lenders after Muthoot, which suggests the market has not forgotten the first lesson.

Foreign capital and the IPO wave: who bought in, who listed, and what they paid

A listing rule that produced ₹34,500 crore of IPOs in thirteen months, strategic stakes bought by Japanese, Emirati and American capital, and how each deal has fared since.

Between September 2024 and August 2026 the ownership of Indian NBFCs changed more than in the previous decade. Three upper-layer lenders listed because the RBI told them to; a Japanese megabank bought a fifth of the largest retail NBFC; an Abu Dhabi conglomerate took control of a scarred mortgage lender; an American private-equity firm became joint owner of a gold lender; and a string of QIPs refilled the capital of the fastest growers. Section 4 explained why strategic equity matters for funding. This section records who paid what, and what has happened since.

The listing mandate

Scale-based regulation requires any NBFC placed in the upper layer to list within three years. Three of the 2022 list were unlisted and had to go to market by September 2025: Bajaj Housing Finance (₹6,560 crore, September 2024; a housing finance company, outside this report's 25), HDB Financial (₹12,500 crore in July 2025, of which ₹10,000 crore was HDFC Bank selling and ₹2,500 crore new money) and Tata Capital (₹15,512 crore at ₹326 a share, listed on 13 October 2025: ₹6,846 crore new shares and ₹8,666 crore sold by existing holders). Together they raised about ₹34,572 crore in thirteen months. Piramal took a different route: Piramal Enterprises merged into its upper-layer lending subsidiary, which began trading as Piramal Finance on 7 November 2025, a listing without an IPO. Tata Sons, also on the list, chose instead to seek deregistration as a core investment company.

The mandate's after-effects are still working through. Tata Capital's promoters own 85.4%, so they must sell down towards the 25% minimum public shareholding, and the stock (₹318) already trades below its IPO price. The RBI dropped its proposed restriction on banks and their NBFC subsidiaries doing the same business in October 2025, which removed the risk that HDFC Bank would be forced to cut its 74% of HDB. And the June 2026 redraw of the upper layer, to any NBFC with assets of ₹1 lakh crore or more, including government-owned ones, brings IREDA (₹93,802 crore of assets in March 2026) close to the line; it is already listed, so the listing rule is moot, but the capital and concentration rules are not.

What MUFG bought

MUFG's ₹39,618 crore is the largest foreign investment in an Indian NBFC, and its logic is about liabilities. Before the deal, Shriram borrowed at roughly 140 basis points more than AAA-rated peers; with a Japanese megabank owning a fifth, and a CRISIL AAA upgrade, that gap should close as old borrowings roll off. Q1 FY27 already showed it: incremental borrowing cost was 7.77% against 8.56% across the book. Shriram used part of the cash to repay debt (borrowings fell from ₹2.51 lakh crore to ₹2.33 lakh crore), which cut interest costs and lifted the quarter's profit by 60%, and book value per share rose 48.5% to ₹461.78. The cost is temporary: ROE fell to 12.8% because the new capital is not yet lent out, and CRAR of 34% is far more than the business needs. The deal fits a wider Japanese pattern, after SMBC's purchase of 24.99% of Yes Bank in 2025. For other NBFCs it sets a benchmark: a strategic shareholder can be worth a full notch of rating and more than a percentage point of funding cost.

What IHC and Bain bought

IHC bought control of a lender after its bad news. Sammaan cleaned out its legacy wholesale book in a single quarter, reclassifying ₹14,953 crore of loans to fair value and selling tranches to an asset reconstruction company, then handed control to an Abu Dhabi conglomerate whose backing lifted its ratings to AA+. What IHC owns is a ₹56,000 crore, mostly mortgage, book with a low NNPA but unproven earning power; the market still prices it at 0.8 times book. Bain bought into a gold lender at the start of the gold boom, at ₹236 a share, and took joint control in April 2026; six independent directors resigned in May and the board was reconstituted, with V.P. Nandakumar staying as managing director. Its immediate tasks are visible in the numbers: fixing the Asirvad microfinance subsidiary (which lost ₹579 crore in FY26 and is now back in profit) and deciding how far to run gold LTV, which rose to 65.6% in Q1 FY27.

    Piramal Finance raises ₹2,100 crore by QIP at ₹2,110 and proposes ₹1,750 crore of promoter warrants.

The pattern in the returns is instructive. The two strategic investors who bought before a re-rating, Bain and MUFG, are in profit. The two raises priced into a strong market, Tata Capital's IPO and Piramal's QIP, are under water, and in both cases more stock is coming (Tata's promoter sell-down, Piramal's warrants). For investors, the supply calendar matters almost as much as the earnings calendar.

In plain terms

Foreign banks and investors are paying billions to own Indian lenders mainly to lower what those lenders pay for money. A lender backed by a Japanese megabank or a Gulf conglomerate can borrow more cheaply, which feeds straight into profit. Meanwhile, the RBI's rule that big NBFCs must list has put three large new lenders on the stock market, and some of their owners still have shares to sell.

Why Indian lenders earn more

Three reasons explain most of the gap. Nominal rates are higher. India's nominal GDP grows about 10–11% a year and the repo rate is 5.25%; loans priced at 15–20% are normal in a way they are not in a 4% rate economy, and the spread between what a good borrower pays and what a lender's funding costs is wider in absolute terms. Credit is under-penetrated. Household debt is about 41–42% of GDP in India, against roughly 60% or more in China and 70% or more in the US and UK (INDICATIVE, from earlier RBI and BIS data), so lenders compete for new customers rather than refinancing each other's, and pricing holds up. Leverage is lower. The 15% capital floor holds most NBFCs at four to seven times assets to equity, far less than a deposit-funded bank, so more of the ROE has to come from ROA. A US card lender with Bajaj's ROA would be extraordinary; an Indian NBFC with Capital One's would not survive its cost of funds.

What the comparison does and does not justify

It justifies a premium: Bajaj trades at about five times book against Capital One's 1.1–1.4 times because it grows its book at about 25% a year with similar or higher ROE, and the closest valuation analogues are the two high-growth, high-return franchises in the table, American Express and Nu Holdings. It does not justify assuming the gap is permanent. The global pattern is that consumer-lending returns fall as a market matures, as banks with cheaper deposits move in, and as regulators cap rates and fees; India is already seeing all three, in banks doubling their gold loans, in the RBI's 2023 risk-weight increase on consumer credit, and in its 2024 actions against lenders charging what it called usurious rates. The infrastructure lenders have a different comparison: they resemble policy lenders such as Germany's KfW or the China Development Bank, which mostly earn near-zero returns, yet PFC and REC are listed and earn about 20% on equity. The market prices them closer to Indian public-sector banks, at about book value, because it doubts those returns will persist under a government owner.

In plain terms

India's best non-bank lenders are among the most profitable consumer lenders in the world, because interest rates are higher, fewer people already have loans, and the rules stop them borrowing as much as banks do. Those advantages are real but not guaranteed: as India's credit market matures and banks compete harder, returns are likely to drift towards those of lenders elsewhere.

Technology and disruption: banks fight back, data-rich newcomers arrive, and the power book meets the energy transition

Six forces that could move market share and margins over the next five years, who gains, who is exposed, and the signal to watch for each.

NBFCs grew by doing what banks would not: lending to thin-file borrowers, against gold, at the shop counter, in villages. Technology and regulation are eroding parts of that advantage while opening new ones. The threats are less about a single fintech replacing a lender than about the cost of acquiring and underwriting customers falling for everyone, which rewards whoever already owns the customer or the cheapest money.

Banks competing back

The clearest threat is the oldest: banks. Banks' retail gold loans more than doubled in FY26, at average LTVs of about 55% and funded by deposits costing several percentage points less than an NBFC's borrowings; ICRA expects the competition to keep pressure on NBFC gold yields. In vehicles, private banks are aggressive in new cars. Co-lending rules now let banks use NBFCs as originators for their own balance sheets. The NBFC response is to retreat to segments banks cannot underwrite well, such as used trucks, small-ticket property loans to cash-income borrowers and rural tractors, and to own the customer relationship even when the loan sits on a bank's books.

Digital lending and the fintech settlement

The RBI's 2022 digital lending guidelines and the 5% cap on default-loss guarantees (since June 2023) ended the model in which an app found borrowers and a lender quietly took the risk. Fintechs had to partner openly with regulated lenders or become NBFCs themselves (Navi, Kissht and KreditBee did). Meanwhile public digital infrastructure (the account aggregator system for sharing bank-statement data, the Unified Lending Interface, credit lines on UPI) lowers the cost of underwriting for everyone. The winners are incumbents with large customer bases and data: Bajaj's 124 million customers, the 23.9 million at HDB, and potentially Jio's telecom base.

Jio Financial as the wild card

Jio Credit grew AUM 163% to ₹30,667 crore and disbursements 173% in a year, borrowing at 7.07%, among the lowest costs for a new NBFC, with a parent sitting on ₹1.34 lakh crore of net worth and joint ventures with BlackRock and Allianz. If it chose to, it could price consumer and merchant loans below what standalone lenders need to earn their cost of capital. So far it has not; its ROA is about 1.4% and its book is 6% of Bajaj's. The signal to watch is whether its pricing or its growth starts to show up in Bajaj's and the small-ticket lenders' yields.

AI in underwriting and collections

The most immediate effect of technology is on operating cost, the second-largest line in a retail lender's ROA tree. L&T Finance credits its AI underwriting stack (Project Cyclops) with its retail turnaround; Manappuram writes 90% of its gold loans online; Five-Star collects 86% of its instalments digitally; speech analytics in collections, automated gold assaying and machine-read KYC all cut cost per loan. A point off opex-to-AUM is worth a point of ROA, so this is where technology pays first, but it also lowers the barrier for any well-funded entrant.

Co-lending 2.0 and the asset-light model

From 1 January 2026 the RBI's revised co-lending rules extend the model beyond priority-sector loans to all regulated lenders and loan types, with each partner keeping at least 10% of each loan. That lets NBFCs originate and service loans that sit mainly on banks' balance sheets, earning fees rather than spread. IIFL already keeps 35% of its AUM off-book and Capri 20%. The effect is a higher ROE on less capital, but thinner economics per loan and a dependence on bank partners' appetite, and it makes reported AUM growth a less reliable guide to profit.

The energy transition and the state lenders

For PFC and REC the disruption is in the power system itself. Conventional generation, mostly coal, is ₹1.88 lakh crore of PFC's ₹5.80 lakh crore standalone book, about 32%, against ₹90,135 crore (16%) in renewables; REC's mix is similar. The 500 GW non-fossil target by 2030 is a growth opportunity (IREDA's entire purpose, and a rising share of PFC and REC lending), but it also raises the long-run risk on the thermal book and on state distribution companies that must absorb the transition's costs.

Bull vs bear: the healthiest balance sheets in a decade, entering a turn in rates and gold

The case for the sector and the case against, set side by side, and the three variables that will decide which one wins over FY27–28.

Indian NBFCs enter the second half of FY27 in better shape than at any point since before IL&FS: system bad loans of 2.4%, capital of 24.6%, a microfinance cycle that has turned and fresh foreign equity in several of the weakest names. But the forces that produced FY26's profits, falling rates and a soaring gold price, have both stopped, and some cracks are visible in small-ticket and vehicle books. Both cases are real.

The bull case

  • A long runway. Household debt is about 41–42% of GDP; NBFC credit grew 19.4% in FY25 and ICRA expects NBFC-Retail AUM to grow 16–18% in FY27.
  • The strongest balance sheets in a decade. GNPA 2.4%, NNPA 0.8%, CRAR 24.6% and ROA 2.3% for the system in March 2026; microfinance arrears down from 6.3% to 2.0% in a year.
  • A credit cycle that has turned. Bajaj's underlying credit cost is 1.31%, SBI Cards' fell from about 9% to 6.5%, M&M's Stage 3 is at an eight-year low, and Q1 FY27 profits rose at 23 of the 25.
  • Funding moats being bought. MUFG, IHC and Bain have put about ₹53,000 crore into three lenders; banks funded about 45% of NBFC-Retail's incremental needs in FY26, far more than in the two prior years.
  • Cheap pockets of quality. PFC and REC earn about 20% on equity at about book value with 6% dividend yields; Muthoot earns 26.6% at under ten times earnings; IIFL earns 19.5% at 12 times.
  • A settled rulebook. The 2025 risk-weight rollback, the completed listing wave and the new gold rules remove several regulatory unknowns.

The bear case

  • Rates have turned. The repo-cut cycle ended at 5.25%; bond yields are up 20–90 basis points since June 2026 and ICRA flags possible hikes in H2 FY27. Funding costs rose for 19 of 23 lenders tracked by JM Financial in Q1, and fixed-rate vehicle books reprice slowly.
  • Gold could reverse. Much of FY26's growth and profit was the gold price. Muthoot's NIM already fell three points; LTVs are creeping up (Manappuram 65.6%, IIFL 70%); a 20% price fall would cut gold AUM by close to 20%.
  • Concentration and policy at the state lenders. Utility exposures, removed concentration exemptions, stalled disbursements and a merger that could take the government's PFC stake to about 42% (fact-pack calculation).
  • Cracks in small-ticket and vehicle books. Five-Star's Stage 3 doubled in fifteen months; SBFC's credit cost and arrears are rising; Chola's Stage 2 and 3 ticked up; Shriram sits at 4.64%; IREDA's impairments jumped.
  • Premium multiples assume decades. Bajaj at 5.3 times book and Chola at 4.5 times price in about 20% returns for a generation; Poonawalla trades at 50 times trailing earnings.
  • Supply. Tata Capital's promoters must sell about 10% of the company; the government keeps selling IRFC and IREDA; Piramal has warrants pending; private-equity holders are exiting Five-Star.

The three variables that decide it

Most of the difference between the two cases comes down to three things that no NBFC management controls. The cost of money: if the RBI holds rates and bond spreads settle, the lag in funding costs works in lenders' favour through FY27; if rates rise, the lenders with fixed-rate books and market funding (vehicle financiers, gold lenders without AAA ratings) feel it first. The gold price: a stable price lets gold lenders grow on tonnage and new branches; a falling one shrinks their books and their profits even without bad loans. The rural and small-business cycle: the monsoon, diesel prices, freight demand and small-business cash flows decide whether the early cracks at Five-Star, SBFC and Chola stay contained. Lenders with a wide PPOP, AAA or parent-backed funding and no single concentrated risk are best placed whichever way these break; that describes only a minority of the 25.

In plain terms

NBFCs have never been healthier in this generation, but last year's tailwinds, cheaper money and dearer gold, have stopped blowing. The next two years will reward lenders with cheap, dependable funding and disciplined lending, and expose those whose recent profits came mostly from the gold price or from lending to riskier borrowers in a good year.
