00

The one-page view: a services business that shrank while manufacturing boomed

Three listed companies sell Indian scientists' time to the world's drug developers and try to keep the molecules they touch. In 2026 the theme held up and the numbers did not.

A CRDMO (contract research, development and manufacturing organisation) is a company that does drug research for hire, then tries to stay with the molecule as it moves into clinical trials and, if it is approved, into commercial supply. India has three listed companies whose business starts in research and grows into manufacturing: Syngene International, Sai Life Sciences and Anthem Biosciences. Together they earned ₹8,055 crore of revenue in FY26 and were worth ₹94,206 crore on 9 October 2026.

The defining fact of 2026 is a surprise. In the April–June 2026 quarter (Q1 FY27), India's manufacturing-led contract developers, the six companies covered in our CDMO & CMO report, grew revenue by 25.5% and widened their EBITDA margin from 19.4% to 26.1%. Over the same three months the three research-led CRDMOs shrank. Their combined revenue fell 10.6%, from ₹1,911 crore to ₹1,708 crore, and combined EBITDA fell 25%, from ₹518 crore to ₹390 crore. Syngene's revenue fell 15.8% and it reported a small loss. Anthem's fell 22.6%. Only Sai Life grew, by 11.7%, and even that was slower than its 29% growth in FY26.

Both declines were company-specific, and that is the first lesson of this report. Syngene lost the drug-substance volumes it made for Zoetis's canine arthritis antibody, Librela, because Zoetis now holds enough inventory for "the next couple of years". Anthem had shipments to its large customers pushed into later quarters and kept its guidance of about 20% growth for FY27. Neither event says global demand for outsourced research has collapsed. Both say something about this business that investors tend to forget in good years: a CRDMO's revenue is concentrated in a few clients and a few molecules, so one decision in a client's boardroom can move a whole year. Anthem's five largest customers were 70.9% of its FY25 revenue.

The second lesson is that research services and manufacturing are different businesses even when they sit inside one company. Research is billed by the scientist-year (the FTE model) or by the project (fee-for-service). It is a people business with staff costs near a third of revenue, so a revenue drop flows almost straight through to profit. Manufacturing is billed by the kilogram under multi-year supply contracts, carries materials as its biggest cost and earns higher margins once plants are full. The value of a CRDMO comes from following the molecule: winning a programme cheaply in discovery and keeping it through development into a decade of commercial supply. The share of revenue already in that last stage is the best single predictor of margin in this group: Anthem (about 71% development and manufacturing) earned 39.3% EBITDA in FY26, Sai (about 65%) 28.9%, and Syngene (about 60% research) 24.6%, falling to 12.3% in Q1 FY27.

The third lesson concerns China. The usual Indian pitch is that US legislation (the BIOSECURE Act, signed in December 2025) and Western caution will push work from China to India. So far China is winning more work, not less. WuXi AppTec grew revenue 38.9% in the first half of 2026, though its discovery-biology unit grew only 11.2%, and Pharmaron grew 17.9% with bookings up more than 30%. India still has a cost edge. An undated industry estimate puts the loaded cost of a scientist-year at $60,000–75,000 in India against $90,000–100,000 in China and $250,000–270,000 in the US (indicative). But Indian research wages are rising 8–10% a year while contract prices rise 3–5%, and Syngene's staff costs have climbed from about 27% of revenue in FY24 to 33% in FY26 and 38% in Q1 FY27.

The fourth lesson is about price. The market has sorted these three companies by margin, growth and capacity runway, not by size or history. Syngene has the most revenue (₹3,739 crore), the most scientists (5,700+) and the longest record (listed 2015), and it is the cheapest: about 14 times FY26 EBITDA on a company-basis enterprise value, 2.9 times book, near its 52-week low. Anthem has the least revenue and is the most valuable, at about 57 times EBITDA and 82 times earnings. Sai sits at about 50 times EBITDA and 86 times earnings. The two premium names are priced for many years of 15–20% compounding at high margins; the third is priced as a turnaround.

In plain terms

These companies rent out Indian chemists and biologists to Western drug developers, then try to turn that relationship into a long manufacturing contract if the drug succeeds. When it works, a ₹2 crore research project becomes a ₹200 crore-a-year supply contract. When one large client stops buying, the fixed cost of labs and scientists stays and profit collapses. 2026 has shown both halves at once.

01

What a CRDMO sells, and where this report draws the line

The same company can sell a chemist's afternoon and a tonne of patented API. The line we draw is how a client first walks in, not what the company does today.

A new medicine passes through roughly five stages before it reaches a pharmacy, and outsourced suppliers exist at every one. Discovery chemistry designs and makes candidate molecules in milligram-to-gram quantities. Discovery biology tests them in assays and animal models to see whether they hit the target. DMPK (drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics, sometimes written ADME for absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion) studies how the body handles them. Safety assessment, or toxicology, produces the package a company needs to file a US Investigational New Drug application (IND) and enter human trials, usually under GLP (good laboratory practice) rules. Then process research and development works out how to make the molecule at kilogram and then tonne scale, and writes the chemistry, manufacturing and controls (CMC) section of the regulatory file. Finally, commercial manufacturing supplies the approved drug's active ingredient for as long as it sells.

A CRO (contract research organisation) sells the early stages. A CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organisation) sells the later ones. A CRDMO tries to do all of them, so it can carry a molecule from first synthesis to commercial supply. WuXi AppTec in China popularised the term for its integrated model, and all three Indian companies in this report now use it.

Value per molecule rises by roughly an order of magnitude at each step, but only about one molecule in ten that enters Phase I reaches approval. Whoever writes the CMC in Stage 3 usually keeps Stage 4.

The hinge of the whole model is Stage 3. The company that develops the manufacturing process writes it into the regulatory file, and once a drug is approved with a named supplier and process, moving production requires new validation batches, stability data and a filing supplement that can take 12–24 months. That regulatory lock-in is what turns a cheap, competitive research relationship into an expensive, sticky manufacturing one. Discovery work has no such lock-in: no regulator names the company that synthesised the first analogues. Its stickiness is commercial, built on teams, data systems and the client's trust that its intellectual property is safe.

Where this report stops

All three companies in this report manufacture. Syngene's CDMO line has been between 22% and 41% of quarterly sales; Sai's about 60–65%; Anthem's development and manufacturing about 71%. So the boundary with our CDMO & CMO report (Divi's, Gland, Neuland, Piramal Pharma, OneSource, Akums) is drawn on business model rather than activity. The companies here enter through research, billing scientists and projects, and convert some of that work into manufacturing. The CDMO report's companies enter through manufacturing. Facts that matter to both, such as BIOSECURE, WuXi's results, ICRA's market sizing and the biotech-funding cycle, are used consistently across the two reports.

Clinical CROs, the companies that run human trials (IQVIA, ICON, Fortrea, Parexel, Syneos, Medpace, and in India Veeda, Lambda and Jeevan), are outside this report. They sell trial-site management, patient recruitment and monitoring, and their economics are different. Syngene has a small clinical and bioanalytical arm, inherited from its Clinigene acquisition, and is mentioned where relevant. Biocon, which owns 52.59% of Syngene, is covered in our specialty & biologics report, and the Chinese-sourced starting materials that Indian CRDMOs buy are covered in the API & intermediates report.

Who the clients are

Three kinds of client buy from Indian CRDMOs. Big pharma, the top 20–25 global drug companies, uses dedicated FTE centres and late-stage development and manufacturing, and outsources chemistry that is demanding but routine. Sai says 19 of the top 25 pharma companies are active customers and that large pharma has risen from about 28% of its revenue in FY22 to about 49%, and more than 90% of its CDMO revenue. Syngene's named clients include Bristol Myers Squibb (BMS), GSK, Zoetis and Merck KGaA, with dedicated centres for Amgen, Baxter and Herbalife. Emerging and virtual biotechs, funded by venture capital, outsource almost everything and are the most cyclical buyers; about 65% of Sai's customers use its integrated discovery-to-development offer, mostly biotechs. Non-pharma research, in nutrition, animal health, consumer goods and agrochemicals, is a Syngene speciality, and it is through animal health that Syngene acquired its Zoetis exposure.

Geographically the work is overwhelmingly export. Anthem sent about 85% of FY25 revenue to roughly 50 countries, with Europe about 57% and North America about 26%. Sai and Syngene are weighted to the US and Europe. That makes the rupee a direct driver of reported revenue, and, as Syngene showed with a ₹50 crore hedge loss in Q1 FY27, a source of accounting noise when companies hedge forward.

In plain terms

Discovery work is easy to win and easy to lose because no regulator records who did it. Manufacturing work is hard to win and hard to lose because the regulator records exactly who makes the drug and how. A CRDMO is a bet that enough easy-to-win relationships will turn into hard-to-lose ones.

02

Three ways to bill a scientist, and a fourth to bill a reactor

How a CRDMO prices its work decides who carries the risk, how margins move, and why three companies in one sub-sector have EBITDA margins from 25% to 39%.

The pricing model is the first thing to understand about any contract research business, because it decides who bears the cost of an idle scientist or an empty reactor. There are five in use, and every Indian CRDMO runs a blend.

FTE (full-time equivalent). The client rents a named team for a year at a fixed rate per scientist. The client directs the science; the CRO supplies people, labs and management. Rates are reset annually, with escalators typically 3–5% a year in dollar terms. Risk sits mostly with the client, who pays whether or not the experiments work; the CRO's risk is unfilled seats and the client cutting headcount at budget time. Margins are predictable, typically in the mid-20s at EBITDA. Syngene's dedicated centres, the BMS centre with about 700 scientists in particular, are the archetype.

FFS (fee-for-service). A fixed price per deliverable: synthesise 50 analogues, run this assay panel, complete this toxicology study. The CRO carries productivity risk and keeps the gain if it works faster, so margins range more widely (15–40%) and can benefit from automation and AI tools. FFS is common with biotechs and for DMPK, safety and process campaigns.

Risk-sharing. Discounted fees in exchange for milestone payments or royalties if the drug succeeds. This is rare in India. Syngene has historically refused to own client IP, and clients own everything the CRO produces.

Commercial supply. A price per kilogram under a multi-year supply agreement, sometimes with a capacity reservation. This is manufacturing economics: materials are the biggest cost and margins of 35–45% are possible when plants are full. The risk is the client drug's own sales, its patent life, and exposure to a single molecule.

Own products. Anthem also sells its own fermentation-derived ingredients (probiotics, enzymes, peptides, vitamin analogues) business-to-business. Its Specialty Ingredients arm earned ₹354 crore in FY26 at a gross margin of about 51% in Q1 FY27. This keeps fermentation tanks busy between custom campaigns but is priced closer to a commodity.

Three archetypes in one sub-sector

The blend of these models gives three distinct archetypes. Syngene is research-led and broad: about 60% of FY26 revenue in research services (59% in Q4 FY26, 78% in Q1 FY27 after its CDMO line collapsed), with dedicated centres, discovery services, small-molecule manufacturing at Mangaluru and biologics in Bengaluru and Baltimore. Sai Life is an integrated small-molecule CRDMO weighted to manufacturing: CRO ₹775 crore (35%) and CDMO ₹1,417 crore (65%) in FY26, with 33–34 commercial molecules. Anthem is manufacturing-heavy: early research about 11%, development and manufacturing about 71%, and specialty ingredients about 17% of FY26 revenue, with a fermentation platform that the other two do not have.

In plain terms

Selling scientists by the year is steady but caps your margin, because you must keep paying them and their pay keeps rising. Selling kilograms of a patented drug ingredient is lumpier but far more profitable once the plant is full. The three listed companies sit at different points on that spectrum, and their margins line up almost exactly in that order.

03

A ₹30,000 crore slice of a $190 billion world

India's whole CRDMO industry is 2–3% of the global market. The three listed companies are about a quarter of India's slice, and their growth has gone from fast to flat to negative in three years.

Market sizes for outsourced pharma research vary widely because publishers draw the boundary in different places. The most useful anchor is ICRA's February 2026 estimate, which we also use in the CDMO report: the global CRDMO market (research, development and manufacturing for hire) was about US$180–200 billion in 2025. China holds an estimated 18–21% of it and India 2–3%.

Inside that total, the pure research slice is much smaller and slower. Estimates of the drug-discovery outsourcing market for 2025 range from US$8 billion (Fortune Business Insights, growing 8.6% a year to US$16.9 billion by 2034) to about US$20 billion for a broader "drug discovery services" definition growing 7%. A sensible working range is US$8–20 billion growing 7–9% a year. Preclinical safety and DMPK adds perhaps US$6–8 billion (Charles River's safety-assessment segment alone runs at about US$2.4–2.5 billion a year). Clinical CROs, outside this report, are a far larger US$60–80 billion. The important point for investors is that discovery is the slowest-growing part of the CRDMO pie; development and manufacturing are where the money compounds, which is why every company in this report is trying to move along the chain.

India: ICRA's ₹30,000–35,000 crore

ICRA sizes the Indian CRDMO market at US$3.4–4.0 billion in calendar 2025, or about ₹30,000–35,000 crore at ₹87 to the dollar. It projects US$7–9 billion by 2030 and US$14–18 billion by 2035, a 15–16% compound annual growth rate. Its sample grew only 8–10% in FY26, which it blames partly on US National Institutes of Health (NIH) funding cuts and weaker venture funding in 2025. Other estimates run higher (JPMorgan US$8.2 billion for 2024; BCG and the industry body IPSO US$22–25 billion by 2035), but those include generic API supply contracts and domestic contract manufacturing, so they should not be mixed with ICRA's figure.

The three listed companies' FY26 revenue of ₹8,055 crore (about US$0.93 billion at ₹87) is roughly 23–27% of ICRA's Indian market. Add the CDMO revenue of the six manufacturing-led companies in our sibling report (about US$2.0–2.2 billion) and the unlisted Aragen (about US$0.25 billion), and listed and near-listed players come to US$3.2–3.4 billion, which suggests ICRA's market is essentially the organised listed universe plus the CDMO arms of Laurus and Cohance.

There is no clean public figure for India's research-only market. Built from the bottom up, Syngene's research services (about ₹2,200–2,400 crore), Sai's CRO (₹775 crore), most of Aragen's ₹2,178 crore, and the discovery units of Jubilant, Aurigene, TCG Lifesciences, Piramal and others, we estimate it at roughly US$1.0–1.5 billion, an INDICATIVE figure with no published source. The captive research centres that multinationals run in Hyderabad and Bengaluru are excluded, though they employ thousands of the same kind of scientist.

The three companies' growth: fast, then flat, then negative

Combined revenue grew 12.7% in FY25 and 12.2% in FY26, to ₹8,055 crore, a two-year compound rate of 12.4%. That average hides a sharp divergence. Sai and Anthem both compounded at about 22% a year from FY24 to FY26. Syngene, the largest, grew just 3.5% a year. Over a longer span Syngene compounded at about 13% a year for a decade, from about ₹1,105 crore in FY16 to ₹3,739 crore in FY26, but nearly all of that growth came before FY24.

Who pays for research: big pharma's budgets and the biotech funding tap

Discovery revenue follows US biotech funding with a lag of two to four quarters. In 2026 the tap reopened, then a September rate rise put a hand back on it.

Demand for outsourced research comes from two very different wallets. Big pharma spends from operating budgets that move slowly and are planned a year ahead. Biotechs spend from money raised in venture rounds and public offerings, which can double or halve in a year. A CRDMO with many biotech clients rides the funding cycle; one weighted to big pharma rides corporate cost programmes and patent expiries. All three Indian companies have both, in different proportions.

The biotech funding cycle, and why it leads

US biotech funding peaked in 2021, collapsed in the 2022–23 "funding winter" as interest rates rose, partly recovered in 2024–25 and rebounded in 2026. The patent cliff cuts both ways for a CRDMO. Pharma companies must refill their pipelines, which means more external discovery and more in-licensing of molecules from biotechs that themselves use CROs. Mergers add another twist. Anthem disclosed that a large biotech client was acquired by a big pharma company, and that the acquirer is now sending it enquiries: a merger that widened its door. Four smaller drivers

  • New modalities. Peptides and oligonucleotides (together "TIDES"), antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), RNA therapies and biologics are where pharma pipelines are growing fastest. WuXi's TIDES unit grew 44% in the first half of 2026. All three Indian companies are building capability (section 10), but new modalities were only 4–5% of Sai's Q1 FY27 revenue, so the payoff is years away.
  • AI in discovery. Faster design-make-test cycles mean fewer compounds made per drug candidate, which deflates headcount-based chemistry. It also creates new services. The net effect is unknown (section 19).
  • Non-pharma research. Syngene's nutrition, animal-health, consumer and agrochemical clients diversify demand, but animal health is also where its Librela problem came from.
  • The rupee. At about ₹94–95 to the dollar in mid-2026, against an FY26 average of about ₹87, a weaker rupee lifts reported revenue. In plain terms

A US biotech raising money this quarter is an Indian CRO's revenue next year. When US interest rates rise, the money dries up and the smallest clients cancel first. The 2026 rebound helped Sai's research arm grow a quarter; September's rate rise is the first reason in two years to watch that tap again.

Follow the molecule: how a ₹5 crore research client becomes a ₹150 crore supply contract Deep dive

The whole CRDMO model rests on converting cheap, competitive discovery relationships into long, sticky manufacturing ones. Here is how the conversion works, how often it fails, and who is converting.

Every CRDMO's investor presentation contains a version of the same picture: a funnel of hundreds of early programmes narrowing into a handful of commercial molecules. The idea is called "following the molecule". The company wins a client in discovery, often at thin margins, does good work, gets asked to scale up the chemistry for clinical trials, writes the manufacturing process into the regulatory file, and becomes the commercial supplier when the drug is approved. Discovery is the customer-acquisition cost; commercial supply is the annuity.

Attrition: why the funnel is so wide

The funnel has to be wide because most molecules die. The largest public study, by the BIO industry association with Informa and QLS for 2011–2020, found that only about 52% of drug candidates passed Phase I, about 29% passed Phase II, about 58% passed Phase III and about 91% of filed applications were approved. A worked example of the conversion economics

Take an ILLUSTRATIVE programme with round numbers. A US biotech pays a CRDMO ₹5 crore a year for three years of discovery chemistry and biology: ₹15 crore of revenue at about 25% EBITDA. Its candidate enters Phase I, and the CRDMO wins process development and clinical supply, worth perhaps ₹10 crore a year for six years through Phase III: ₹60 crore more. If the drug is approved, the CRDMO becomes commercial supplier at ₹150 crore a year for ten years, at 35–40% EBITDA: ₹1,500 crore of revenue.

Now apply the odds. With an 8% chance of approval from Phase I, the expected commercial revenue is about ₹120 crore (8% × ₹1,500 crore), already eight times the discovery revenue that started the relationship. The expected development revenue is lower than ₹60 crore, because many molecules fail in Phase I or II and stop billing. The lesson is that the discovery work's real value is the option it creates, which is why CRDMOs accept thin margins at the top of the funnel and why a single approval can change a company's revenue for a decade. The corollary is that a CRDMO needs many shots: a company with 150 early programmes and 14 late-stage ones can expect a few approvals a year; one with 20 cannot.

Why the molecule does not always follow

Conversion is not automatic, and investors should not assume it. Four things break the chain. First, the molecule fails, which is the usual outcome. Second, the client is acquired and the acquirer moves manufacturing to its own plants or its preferred suppliers. Third, a large pharma client may deliberately split work, using an Indian CRO for discovery and a Western or Chinese CDMO for commercial supply, or dual-sourcing the active ingredient for security. Fourth, the CRDMO may lack the right capacity or modality when the molecule is ready: a company with no fermentation, no peptide plant or no high-potency suite cannot keep a molecule that needs one. Sai's decision to stay out of biologics and fermentation, and Syngene's choice to buy biologics capacity, are both bets on which molecules will need following.

WuXi AppTec shows where the model leads at scale: its small-molecule development and manufacturing revenue grew 72.7% in the first half of 2026 while its discovery biology grew only 11.2%. The research is the entry fee; the money is downstream. India's CRDMOs are trying to build the same engine at roughly one-ninth of WuXi's revenue.

In plain terms

Count the molecules, not the scientists. A CRDMO adding three or four commercial contracts a year, as Sai is, is converting its research into durable revenue. One that cannot tell you how many molecules it has in late-stage trials is asking you to take the conversion on trust.

The one-client problem: Librela, Anthem's top five and the arithmetic of concentration Deep dive

Concentration, not demand, is the defining risk of this sub-sector in 2026. A veterinary antibody cut Syngene's quarter in half; a shipment delay cut Anthem's by a quarter.

The flip side of following the molecule is that success concentrates revenue. A CRDMO that keeps a big commercial molecule ends up with one client that matters far more than the rest. If that molecule's sales falter, or the client builds inventory and then stops ordering, the CRDMO has no regulatory or contractual means of forcing the volume back. In 2026 this risk moved from footnote to headline at two of the three companies.

The Librela case: anatomy of a lost volume

Syngene's relationship with Zoetis, the world's largest animal-health company, began in 2011. In July 2022 the two signed a ten-year agreement for Syngene to manufacture the drug substance for bedinvetmab, sold as Librela, a monoclonal antibody for osteoarthritis pain in dogs, in a contract described as worth "up to US$500 million over 10 years". Syngene built biologics capacity around it. Librela became a large product for Zoetis, but in 2024 the US label was updated following reports of adverse events in dogs, and Zoetis's Librela sales weakened through 2025 (company reports; we have not re-verified Zoetis's figures for this report). Zoetis built inventory and then stopped drawing it down from Syngene.

Why a lost client hurts a people business so much

The damage from a lost client depends on how much of the cost base can shrink with it. At Syngene, revenue fell ₹139 crore year on year in Q1 FY27 (₹875 crore to ₹736 crore) and operating EBITDA fell ₹115 crore (₹206 crore to ₹91 crore): a decremental margin of about 83%, meaning 83 paise of every lost rupee of revenue came straight out of profit. Part of that was a ₹50 crore forex hedge loss; stripping it out, EBITDA fell from ₹211 crore to ₹141 crore, a decremental margin of about 50%. That is still severe, because biologics plants and scientific teams cannot be shrunk in a quarter. Staff costs fell just 3.5% while revenue fell 16%.

Anthem's revenue fell ₹122 crore (₹540 crore to ₹418 crore) and its EBITDA fell about ₹40 crore (₹191 crore to ₹151 crore), a decremental margin of about 33%. A manufacturing-heavy company whose materials are 35–43% of sales flexes better than a people-heavy one, because when shipments stop the materials bill stops too. Sai grew, adding ₹58 crore of revenue and about ₹27 crore of EBITDA, an incremental margin of about 47%: the same operating leverage working in its favour.

Any single commercial molecule above about 15% of revenue deserves a line in the investment case of its own. By that rule Anthem is in the weak band, Sai in the average one, and Syngene's problem was not its overall concentration but the type: one commercial molecule in a business otherwise built on diversified research.

In plain terms

When a CRDMO tells you a client relationship is "long-term", ask two questions: is the revenue billed per scientist or per kilogram, and does the contract guarantee any volume? A long FTE contract is close to a salary. A long supply contract with an "up to" value is closer to a promise to answer the phone.

India vs China in research services: a narrowing people spread Deep dive

India's founding advantage is that a scientist-year costs less. That gap is real, but it is shrinking from both ends, and China is using the BIOSECURE years to grow faster, not to retreat.

Contract research began in India as wage arbitrage. A Western drug company could rent an Indian PhD chemist, fully loaded with lab space, reagents and management, for a fraction of the cost of one at home. The arbitrage still exists. The question for investors in 2026 is how much of it survives Indian wage inflation, Chinese price competition and AI tools that reduce the number of chemists a programme needs.

What a scientist-year costs

The only public comparison we found is an undated industry article from the expert network GLG. It puts the fully loaded annual cost of a contract scientist at about US$250,000–270,000 in the US, US$180,000–190,000 in Europe, US$90,000–100,000 in China and US$60,000–75,000 in India. Treat these as INDICATIVE: the source is undated, no current rate card is public, and actual FTE prices vary with seniority and scope. They imply that India is roughly 25–35% cheaper than China and 70–75% cheaper than the US on headline rates.

The spread inside an Indian CRO

The arbitrage is easiest to see inside Syngene's own accounts. Its FY26 staff cost of ₹1,230 crore across about 8,300 employees works out to roughly ₹14.8 lakh (about US$17,000) per employee per year. Revenue per employee was about ₹45 lakh. After materials, overheads and depreciation it converts into an EBITDA margin of about 25% and a pre-tax margin of about 10%.

The spread is under pressure because the two sides grow at different rates. Indian pharma R&D wages are rising an estimated 8–10% a year, with high attrition among mid-level chemists who are courted by rival CROs and by multinationals' captive research centres. FTE contracts typically carry escalators of 3–5% a year in dollars. Unless productivity, mix or a weaker rupee closes the gap, costs gain three to five points a year on prices. Syngene's staff cost has risen from about 27–28% of revenue in FY24 to 33% in FY26 and 38.2% in Q1 FY27, though the last figure is inflated by the revenue collapse.

The way out is to sell something worth more than the chemist's time.

China is not retreating

The expectation behind much of the Indian CRDMO re-rating since 2024 was that geopolitical pressure would push work from China to India. BIOSECURE: what the law does and does not do

The BIOSECURE Act was signed into US law on 18 December 2025 as part of the FY26 National Defense Authorization Act. It bars US federal agencies from procuring, or funding through grants and loans, "biotechnology equipment or services" from "biotechnology companies of concern", and it extends to federal contractors who use such services in performing a federal contract. Companies of concern are entities on the Pentagon's 1260H list with a biotechnology nexus, plus those on a list the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) is due to publish around December 2026. It does not ban private contracts, and WuXi is not named in the final text. WuXi AppTec was added to the 1260H list in June 2026, but a US court enjoined that designation on 8 August 2026.

Is discovery work easier to move than manufacturing?

In one sense yes, in another no. Discovery has no regulatory lock: no drug application names the company that made the first analogues, so a client's next programme can go anywhere. Manufacturing, by contrast, is named in the regulatory file and moving it takes 12–24 months of validation and a filing supplement. But discovery is commercially sticky in a different way. Building a team of 50–200 chemists takes six to eighteen months; the client must trust the new vendor with its most valuable intellectual property; data systems must be integrated. And the capacity gap is stark: WuXi Chemistry and Pharmaron each employ more than 20,000 scientists, while India's largest, Syngene, has about 5,700.

Scientists, labs and reactors: the supply side and the capex wave

A CRDMO's capacity is people plus square feet plus kilolitres. All three are expanding at once, with Sai and Anthem stepping up capex sharply and Syngene sitting on a quarter of a year's revenue in unfinished assets.

Three inputs determine what a CRDMO can sell. Scientists are the core of research capacity: Syngene employs 5,700+ scientists among more than 8,300 staff. Sai has about 3,400 employees on an undated third-party count and plans to hire more than 700 in FY27. Anthem's figure is genuinely uncertain: IPO coverage cites "1,015 scientific staff" in March 2025, while other sources say 600+ or 300+. We use about 1,000 and flag the conflict. Mid-level chemists change jobs frequently, and the captive research centres of multinationals in Hyderabad and Bengaluru compete for the same people.

Laboratories are the second input. Syngene has more than 3 million square feet across Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Mangaluru and Baltimore. A modern lab fit-out costs an INDICATIVE ₹4,000–6,000 per square foot before equipment. Reactors and bioreactors are the third, measured in kilolitres (KL) for chemical synthesis and fermentation, and in litres of single-use bioreactor volume for biologics. An API reactor block costs an INDICATIVE ₹1–2 crore per KL including infrastructure.

Materials are a modest cost for research and a large one for manufacturing. Syngene's materials were 24.6% of FY26 revenue (reagents, consumables, biologics media). Anthem's run at 35–43% of quarterly sales because commercial supply consumes key starting materials and solvents; backward integration into its own starting materials lifted its CRDMO gross margin by 964 basis points to 68% in Q1 FY27. Power and utilities are about 2–3% of revenue, and animal facilities (vivariums) add a regulated cost for companies doing in-vivo pharmacology and toxicology.

Anthem spent only ₹207 crore in FY26 (9.8% of revenue) while generating ₹844 crore of operating cash flow, much of it from releasing inventory (down from ₹340 crore to ₹125 crore). Modalities: biologics, fermentation, peptides and ADCs as the manufacturing tail

Which kinds of molecule a CRDMO can make decides which molecules it can follow. The three have made very different bets, and only one of them is already paying.

A "modality" is the kind of molecule a drug is: a small chemical molecule, a peptide, an oligonucleotide, an antibody, an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC), a cell or gene therapy. Each needs different equipment and skills. A CRDMO that cannot make a client's molecule at the next stage loses it, however good its discovery work was, so capability across modalities is the practical limit on following the molecule.

Syngene: a biologics bet with an empty first chapter

Syngene is the only one of the three with antibody manufacturing. It has about 50,000 litres of single-use bioreactor capacity across Bengaluru, including the Unit 3 multimodal facility it bought from Stelis Biopharma in FY24, and the Baltimore Bayview plant in the US that it acquired from Emergent BioSolutions in March 2025. It added an ADC discovery lab and a GMP bioconjugation suite in FY26. Its one commercial antibody was Librela; most other molecules in its biologics plants are still in clinical trials, and Baltimore will not be operational until later in FY27, with customers evaluating it and commitments expected after it starts.

Biologics plants are unforgiving when they are empty. Depreciation and specialist staff are fixed, so a half-full plant can lose money while a full one earns very high margins. At scale, Samsung Biologics earns operating margins around 45% and Lonza a CORE EBITDA margin of 34.8% (first half 2026). Syngene's biologics arm is a long way from that scale, and Baltimore carries an extra diligence point: as Emergent's Bayview site it was where the 2021 contamination of COVID-19 vaccine batches led US regulators to halt production. Syngene must requalify it under its own quality systems before clients will commit.

Anthem: fermentation keeps the tanks busy

Anthem's distinctive capability is fermentation and biotransformation: using microbes and enzymes to make molecules. With about 142 KL of fermentation capacity (100 KL more in Unit IV), it makes peptides, enzymes, probiotics and molecules for RNA interference, lipid and oligonucleotide programmes. Few Indian peers have this. To keep fermenters full between custom campaigns, Anthem sells its own fermentation products business-to-business through Specialty Ingredients (₹354 crore in FY26, about 17% of revenue), mostly to domestic buyers on month-to-month terms. That arm grew just 4.6% in FY26 and fell 11.5% in Q1 FY27 when a supply-chain disruption linked to the Middle East conflict hit it. A semaglutide API (the active ingredient in the GLP-1 obesity and diabetes drugs) is awaiting approval from India's drug regulator, CDSCO, for the generic market that opened in India in March 2026.

Sai: small molecules, peptides, and nothing else, deliberately

Sai has chosen to stay a small-molecule company. It has said it will not enter antibodies or proteins and has no fermentation in its capex plan. Its new-modality bets sit at the chemical end: a dedicated peptide development lab for a top-tier pharma client, a GMP pilot plant soon, and a commercial peptide plant operational in 2028 for less than ₹300 crore of capex; and a payload-linker-conjugation centre of excellence for ADC chemistry close to opening. New modalities were only 4–5% of its Q1 FY27 revenue, all in discovery and development. The bet is coherent: oral small-molecule GLP-1 drugs such as orforglipron, if they take share from injectable peptides, would favour exactly the chemistry Sai and Anthem already do.

Regulators, laboratories and IP held by contract

A CRDMO is regulated as a factory, a laboratory and an animal facility at once, and the thing clients value most, the safety of their intellectual property, is protected mainly by contract.

Regulation in this sub-sector comes in four layers, and each matters at a different stage of the value chain.

Manufacturing (GMP). Any plant that makes clinical or commercial material is inspected against good manufacturing practice by the regulators of the markets it supplies: the US FDA, Europe's EMA and national agencies, Japan's PMDA, Australia's TGA, and India's CDSCO under the revised Schedule M. A US FDA inspection ends either with no observations or with a Form 483 listing them; the company responds; the FDA then issues an establishment inspection report (EIR) classifying the site as NAI (no action indicated), VAI (voluntary action indicated) or OAI (official action indicated). OAI can lead to a warning letter and, in serious cases, an import alert that blocks shipments.

Laboratory studies (GLP). Preclinical safety studies that support an IND must follow good laboratory practice. India's National GLP Compliance Monitoring Authority, under the Department of Science and Technology, certifies facilities, and India has been a full adherent to the OECD's Mutual Acceptance of Data system since 2011. That is essential: it means Indian GLP toxicology data is accepted across OECD countries, so a US biotech can file an IND on studies run in Bengaluru.

Animal research. In-vivo pharmacology and toxicology are regulated by the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA, formerly CPCSEA) under the Breeding of and Experiments on Animals (Control and Supervision) Rules, 1998, with an Institutional Animal Ethics Committee at each facility. Clinical trials fall under the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019, relevant here only to Syngene's small clinical and bioanalytical arm, which signed a memorandum with the Translational Health Science and Technology Institute (BRIC-THSTI) in Q1 FY27 for first-in-human and Phase I work.

Intellectual property. For a discovery CRO, the client's IP is the product. India's Patents Act, 1970 has been TRIPS-compliant since 2005, but a CRO's clients own whatever it invents for them, so the relevant protection is contractual: master service agreements, firewalled teams, restricted IT systems and information-security audits. India has no standalone trade-secrets statute, so a leak would be pursued as a breach of contract or confidence. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 covers personal data, not chemical structures. In practice Indian CROs have a clean record with Western big pharma, and that record of trust is the asset that makes switching from China possible at all. Data integrity, following ALCOA+ principles (attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate and more), is the commonest theme in FDA observations at Indian sites.

Tax and trade

Two policy details affect profits. Syngene's main Bengaluru campus sits in the Biocon Park special economic zone. Section 10AA income-tax holidays for SEZ units taper over 15 years, and no new units have qualified since March 2020, so effective tax rates for older CRO units rise as holidays lapse. In plain terms

For research work, what keeps a client is trust that its secrets stay secret, and that rests on contracts and security, not on any Indian law. For manufacturing work, what keeps a client is a clean FDA record, because one bad inspection at a shared plant can stall every client's drug made there at once.

Three decades, four cycles: from wage arbitrage to the BIOSECURE re-rating

India's CRDMOs were founded in a decade, grew up in China's shadow, boomed with COVID, froze with the biotech winter and re-rated on geopolitics. 2026 is the first test of whether the re-rating matches the revenue.

The industry's history falls into four cycles, each with a lesson that still applies.

The founders' era (1993–2005). Biocon incorporated Syngene in 1993 as a contract research arm, and it began operating in Bengaluru in 1994. Krishna Kanumuri founded Sai Dru Syn Laboratories in Hyderabad in 1999 with two fume hoods. GVK Bio, now Aragen, started CRO work in 2001–02; Jubilant's discovery business, now Jubilant Biosys, also began in the early 2000s; Advinus was set up in 2005 by the Tata group with scientists (later sold to Eurofins). Ajay Bhardwaj, a former president of Biocon, founded Anthem in Bengaluru in 2006–07 with Ganesh Sambasivam and K. Ravindra Chandrappa. The founding logic in every case was the people arbitrage.

The CRAMS wave and the dedicated centre (2005–2012). Western pharma, restructuring after a decade of mergers, began outsourcing chemistry to India under the label CRAMS (contract research and manufacturing services). In 2009 Syngene commissioned the Biocon Bristol Myers Squibb R&D Centre (BBRC), a ring-fenced team working only for BMS, and created the dedicated-centre model in India. Abbott nutrition and Baxter centres followed in 2012–13, along with the Clinigene acquisition. The first bust came quickly: pharma restructuring in 2008–12 created outsourcing pull and cancelled programmes at the same time.

The China decade and the listings (2013–2019). WuXi AppTec and Pharmaron scaled far faster than any Indian company, and WuXi listed in Hong Kong in 2018. Indian CROs grew at roughly 10–15% a year. Syngene listed in August 2015, India's first listed pure-play research services company. In 2018 private equity arrived in force: TPG bought Tata Capital's stake in Sai, and True North invested in Anthem.

Boom, winter and re-rating (2020–2026). The COVID years brought record biotech funding (peaking in 2021) and record discovery demand; Syngene grew in the 20s. In 2022 Syngene signed its ten-year Librela contract. Then rising rates froze biotech funding in 2022–23, small biotechs cut discovery first, and Chinese CROs cut prices. In January 2024 the BIOSECURE Act was introduced, and Indian CRDMOs re-rated on the "China+1" story; Sai listed in December 2024 and Anthem in July 2025, both at full valuations. The law was signed in December 2025. Then 2026 delivered the reality check: Syngene's Librela loss and guidance cut, Anthem's deferred shipments, and WuXi growing almost 40%.

Sixteen numbers explain almost everything about a contract research and manufacturing company, and at least six of them are routinely quoted on inconsistent bases. Here is what each one means, how to compute it, what good looks like, and where the traps are.

A CRDMO's results look simple: revenue, EBITDA, profit. Underneath, the revenue comes from two different engines (people billed by the year and plants billed by the kilogram), the costs behave differently in each, and the companies disclose different things. This section builds the revenue and profit equation first, then works through each native KPI, then shows a model P&L for a single dedicated research centre, and ends with a scorecard, the definition traps and a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

In plain terms

Syngene's year is, roughly, 4,000 billed scientists at about ₹59 lakh each plus a manufacturing business, minus a staff bill that rises every year whether or not clients do. Change any one input by a few percent and profit moves by several times as much.

KPI 1 · Revenue per scientist

What it is. The revenue a company earns for each scientist it employs: the purest measure of how well it prices and uses its people. Formula: annual revenue ÷ scientist headcount (average for the year if available). Worked example: Syngene, ₹3,739 crore ÷ 5,700 scientists = ₹65.6 lakh, about US$75,000 at ₹87. What moves it: up with price rises, higher-value services (AI-enabled design, safety studies, ADCs), more manufacturing revenue per head, and a weaker rupee; down with idle scientists, price cuts and a shift to low-end chemistry. Benchmark: good above ₹70 lakh (above about US$80,000), average ₹50–70 lakh, weak below ₹50 lakh, for a research-led company.

How the three compare. Only Syngene discloses scientists. Sai's revenue per employee on an undated headcount of about 3,400 is about ₹64 lakh, an ESTIMATE that mixes scientists with plant staff. Anthem's "scientific staff" count conflicts across sources (1,015, 600+, 300+); on 1,015 its revenue per scientific employee would be about ₹2.1 crore, which is not comparable because most of its revenue comes from reactors, not lab benches. The definition trap: per scientist and per employee are different numbers (Syngene's per-employee figure is ₹45 lakh), and in a manufacturing-heavy company the ratio measures asset productivity as much as people productivity. Watch the trend, not the level: if revenue per scientist rises while headcount stalls, AI and automation are helping; if both stall, they are deflating the business.

KPI 2 · Scientist headcount and billable utilisation

What it is. The number of scientists, and the share of them billed to clients in a period. Formula: billable utilisation = billed scientist-days ÷ available scientist-days. Worked example: a 1,000-scientist research unit billing 920 at ₹60 lakh a year earns ₹552 crore; at 850 billed it earns ₹510 crore, ₹42 crore less, and almost all of that loss falls to EBITDA because the 150 idle scientists are still paid. What moves it: client renewals, the pace of new programmes, ramp time for new hires (six to eighteen months for a full team), and attrition. Benchmark: headcount growing at least as fast as revenue with revenue per head rising is good; flat is average; shrinking headcount with falling revenue per head is weak. Billable utilisation above 90% is good, 80–90% average, below 80% weak (INDICATIVE; no company discloses it).

How the three compare. Syngene's headcount is flat at 5,700+ scientists and it is restructuring: it booked termination benefits of ₹30 crore in Q4 FY26 and ₹13.5 crore in Q1 FY27. Sai plans to hire more than 700 people in FY27 and says new discovery capacity is "already sold out". Anthem's count is unreconciled. The definition trap: companies quote "scientists", "scientific staff", "R&D professionals" and "employees" interchangeably, and none reports billable utilisation. Headcount announcements are an input, not an output.

KPI 3 · FTE share versus fee-for-service

What it is. The share of research revenue earned from annual FTE contracts rather than per-project fees. Formula: FTE revenue ÷ research revenue. Worked example: a research business of ₹2,000 crore with ₹1,100 crore on FTE has a 55% FTE share; that ₹1,100 crore renews annually with modest price escalators, while the ₹900 crore of FFS must be re-won project by project but can earn higher margins on efficient delivery. What moves it: new dedicated centres raise it; biotech project wins lower it. Benchmark: 40–60% is a healthy blend of stability and upside; below 20% means everything is spot work; above 80% means little pricing upside and heavy exposure to a few clients' budget decisions.

How the three compare. None discloses it. Our INDICATIVE estimate is 50–60% of Syngene's research revenue, mostly FFS and integrated work at Sai (with at least one large end-to-end FTE engagement), and immaterial at Anthem. The definition trap: "dedicated centre" revenue is FTE, but not all FTE revenue is in dedicated centres; large-pharma research teams in shared buildings are also billed per head.

In plain terms

FTE work is like salaried employment for the CRO: steady, but the raise is capped. Fee-for-service is like freelancing: you can earn more if you work faster, but you must keep finding the next job. A good CRO wants a mix, and none of the three tells you what its mix is.

KPI 4 · Client count and large clients

What it is. The number of active clients, and ideally the number paying more than US$1 million a year. Formula: clients billed in the trailing twelve months; clients with revenue above a threshold. Worked example: two CROs each earn ₹2,000 crore; one has 400 clients with 40 above US$1 million, the other 150 clients with 25 above US$1 million. The first is more diversified and has more clients that could graduate into manufacturing; the second probably has deeper relationships per client. What moves it: business development reach (US sites help: Sai's Boston discovery centre targets 30–35% revenue growth a year over four to five years), biotech funding and pharma consolidation. Benchmark: more than 300 active clients with more than 50 above US$1 million is good; 150–300 average; below 150 weak.

KPI 6 · The molecule funnel: commercial molecules and new contracts

What it is. The number of molecules a CRDMO supplies commercially, the number in late-stage trials, and how many new commercial supply contracts it signs each year: the direct measure of "following the molecule". Worked example: Sai has 14 late-stage molecules and expects four new commercial contracts in FY27; if each contract reaches ₹30–60 crore of annual revenue at maturity (ILLUSTRATIVE), FY27's wins alone could add ₹120–240 crore of annual revenue over two or three years, 5–11% of FY26 revenue. What moves it: clinical success at clients, approvals, and whether the CRDMO has the right capacity when a molecule is ready. Benchmark: more than 30 commercial molecules, more than 10 late-stage and three or more new commercial contracts a year is good; fewer than 10 commercial molecules is weak.

KPI 8 · EBITDA margin, and its three bases

KPI 14 · Order visibility and guidance arithmetic

What it is. How much of next year's revenue is already contracted or scheduled, and whether management guidance adds up. Formula: disclosed backlog ÷ next-year revenue, or "share of shipments visible". Worked example: Sai guides 15–20% growth for three to five years from FY27 and a 40/60 first-half/second-half split. If its first half is about ₹1,100 crore, a 40/60 split implies FY27 revenue of about ₹2,750 crore, growth of about 25%, above the long-term range. Either the second quarter dips below the first, or the long-term guide is conservative; both readings should be held until Q2 results on 5 November 2026. Benchmark: more than 60% of next year visible is good; undisclosed visibility with lumpy revenue is weak.

How the three compare: Anthem says 60% of FY27 shipments are visible; Sai has cited returning customers above 90% of revenue; Syngene has a BMS centre contracted to 2035 but no disclosed backlog. The definition trap: "visibility" may include orders the client can still defer, as Anthem's Q1 showed; and a "10-year, up to US$X" contract is a ceiling, not a backlog.

In plain terms

Whenever a company gives a full-year number after a weak first quarter, do the subtraction: what must the remaining quarters average, and has the company ever delivered that? For Syngene and Anthem in FY27, the answer is "a lot, and only in their best quarters".

KPI 15 · Gross margin and backward integration

What it is. The share of revenue left after materials: a measure of pricing power and of how much of the product the company makes itself. Formula: (revenue − cost of materials consumed, adjusted for inventory change) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Anthem's Q1 FY27 gross margin was 65.0% against 57.3% a year earlier; its CRDMO segment reached 68%, up 964 basis points, because it now makes more of its own key starting materials instead of buying them, and its Specialty Ingredients arm ran at about 51%. Syngene's FY26 materials were 24.6% of revenue, a gross margin of about 75%, typical of a research-led business where people, not chemicals, are the main cost. What moves it: mix between research and manufacturing, the share of pass-through raw materials in supply contracts, backward integration, and input prices (many building blocks come from China). Benchmark: for a manufacturing-heavy CRDMO, above 60% is good, 50–60% average, below 50% weak; for a research-led one, the mid-70s are normal and the staff line matters more. The definition trap: gross margin rises mechanically when revenue shifts toward research, which uses few materials, so a rising gross margin during a manufacturing slump (Syngene) is not good news. Read it alongside the mix.

KPI 16 · Regulatory record and the lead indicator

Regulatory record. The latest US FDA classification at each commercial site: NAI or VAI is good, VAI with an unresolved Form 483 average, OAI, a warning letter or an import alert weak. All three are in the good band today: Syngene's Bengaluru biologics site was classified VAI in June 2025 after a five-observation Form 483; Sai received EIRs for its Bidar and Hyderabad sites in October 2024; Anthem's Harohalli unit received US FDA approvals in 2023 and 2024 and its Bommasandra unit in 2025. The trap is that a CRDMO's record is only as good as its newest site: Syngene's Baltimore plant, with its Emergent-era history, must be requalified before it earns, and every new block at Sai and Anthem will need its own pre-approval inspection.

The lead indicator. For research revenue, the best external signal is US biotech funding: venture rounds and IPO proceeds per quarter, and the direction of the XBI index. More than US$8 billion of venture funding a quarter with the index rising is good for discovery demand two to four quarters later; US$5–8 billion is average; below US$5 billion is weak. In 2026 the first half was strong (US$16.3 billion of venture funding), the third quarter's private financings were US$5.0 billion plus US$2.7 billion of IPOs, and the XBI fell from its August high after September's rate rise: an average reading, turning cautious. A second external signal is Charles River's safety-assessment book-to-bill, which reached 1.19× in Q2 2026, a sign that IND-enabling work is recovering.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA including other income. Anthem's 43.4% (FY26) and 39.6% (Q1 FY27) headlines appear to include treasury income; operating margins were 39.3% and 36.0%.
  • Hedge losses inside operating EBITDA. Syngene's operating EBITDA includes forex hedge losses (₹61 crore FY26, ₹50 crore Q1 FY27); ex-hedge Q1 margin was 19.1%, not 12.3%.
  • Net cash on two bases. Syngene's net cash is ~₹1,565 crore on the company balance sheet excluding leases, but ₹512 crore on screener's basis, which treats ₹449 crore of leases as debt and omits ₹604 crore of bank deposits.
  • P/E on an unclear basis. Screener displays 47.4× for Syngene; consolidated trailing PAT of ₹221 crore gives 63.5×, FY26 reported PAT 44.2×, pre-exceptional PAT 36.9×. We quote 44× FY26 and ~63× trailing.
  • A quarter's mix quoted as a year's. Syngene's 59/41 research/CDMO split is Q4 FY26 only.
  • Molecule counts. "Commercial" versus "active in CMC"; "late-stage" versus "Phase 3/pre-registration": Sai's own counts move between 11 and 14 and between 33 and 34.
  • "Up to" contract values. Syngene's Librela contract was "up to US$500 million over 10 years": a ceiling with no volume floor.
  • Headcount labels. Scientists, scientific staff, R&D professionals and employees are different numbers; Anthem's ranges from 300+ to 1,015 depending on the source.
  • Client counts. Active in a year versus served over three years.
  • Utilisation. Installed, validated or block-level, and mechanically lower after each capacity addition.
  • Capex. Guided, spent, or derived from balance-sheet movements; Anthem's Unit IV figures range from about ₹700 crore to ₹1,200 crore across sources.
  • ROCE. Depressed by cash and idle work in progress, flattered during debt-funded builds; brokers use their own definitions.

How to read a CRDMO's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue by engine (two minutes). Pull total revenue year on year and quarter on quarter, then the research and CDMO lines (Anthem: CRDMO and Specialty Ingredients). Compare with guidance. Red flag: a CDMO swing of more than 20% without a named reason.
  2. Operating EBITDA on one basis. Take revenue minus operating costs, excluding other income. Separate any forex hedge result. Red flag: a headline margin several points above your calculation.
  3. Gross margin. One minus materials over revenue. Rising gross margin with falling revenue (Anthem Q1 FY27) means mix and backward integration; falling gross margin means pricing or pass-through.
  4. Staff cost. Absolute growth versus revenue growth, and the ratio. Red flag: staff growing faster than revenue for more than two quarters outside a known hiring plan.
  5. Client events. Read the commentary for named clients, destocking, deferrals, attrition and new logos. Red flag: "timing" used for the same client two quarters running.
  6. The funnel. Commercial molecules, late-stage count, new commercial contracts. Red flag: a funnel number that stops being disclosed.
  7. Capacity. Blocks commissioned, utilisation, and the movement in capital work in progress. Red flag: CWIP that does not fall after a plant is "operational".
  8. Cash and working capital. Operating cash flow, capex, net cash or debt, debtor days. Red flag: rising debtor days with falling revenue (Anthem 89 → 101).
  9. Rupee and hedges. Note the quarter's average rate, the hedge result and the hedge book's size. A weak-rupee quarter with lower EBITDA usually means hedges.
  10. Guidance arithmetic. Subtract the year-to-date from the full-year guide, divide by the quarters left, and compare with the company's best quarter. Red flag: a required run-rate above anything the company has delivered.
In plain terms

Three questions get you most of the way: which engine (scientists or reactors) drove the quarter, what is the margin on a basis you can compare, and which named client or molecule moved? If the company will not answer the third, assume the answer is "one big one".

Five reasons Sai and Anthem trade at 50–57× EBITDA

Scarcity. There are three listed research-led CRDMOs in India, plus the manufacturing CDMOs in our sibling report, for a very large and growing pool of domestic mutual-fund and insurance money. Domestic institutions raised their holdings in Anthem from 7% to 13.5% in nine months and in Syngene from 24.6% to 28.0%, and hold 32% of Sai. Scarce assets with steady domestic buying trade at scarcity prices.

Narrative. The China+1 and BIOSECURE story, and exposure to new modalities, give investors a reason to expect growth above the industry's. Section 7 argues that the evidence so far supports a slow share gain rather than a sudden shift, but the narrative premium is real in the price.

Growth and margin together. Sai guides 15–20% revenue growth a year for three to five years at 28–30% EBITDA margins. Anthem guides about 20% for FY27 at 38–41% margins, with Unit IV adding about 80% to installed volume from FY28. Brokers' target prices for Anthem (BOBCAPS ₹900 at 57× June-2028 earnings of ₹15.8 a share; PL ₹850 at 55× FY28 earnings) already assume 50–57× forward multiples persist; PL's own figures put Anthem at about 35× FY28 EBITDA and about 50× FY28 earnings at the price it wrote on.

Returns on capital. Anthem earns a 30% ROCE with net cash and about 44% on operating capital excluding cash and work in progress; Sai about 20% and rising. High and rising returns justify a premium on almost any valuation method.

The comparison set. Indian investors compare these companies with India's other pharma "quality" names, which also trade at high multiples, not with WuXi at 15–20 times earnings. Geography sets the multiple as much as fundamentals do.

Why Syngene de-rated

Syngene's multiple fell because almost everything that supports a premium went the wrong way at once. Growth stalled at 3% in FY26 and turned negative in FY27. Margins halved in Q1 FY27. AI, automation and disruption: a tool that can shrink the billable head

Artificial intelligence threatens the unit that research CROs bill, the scientist-year, while offering a way to bill more per scientist. Which effect wins is the biggest unknown in this report.

AI-led discovery. Generative chemistry, gigascale virtual screening and protein-structure prediction let drug designers test far more ideas on a computer before making anything in a lab. That reduces the number of compounds synthesised per drug candidate. Because FTE chemistry is billed per head, fewer syntheses can mean fewer heads. "AI-native" biotechs such as Recursion, Isomorphic and Insilico run lean, highly automated laboratories and outsource less routine chemistry. This is the core threat to a research model built on renting chemists.

AI as a service line. The same tools can raise the value of each scientist if the CRO owns them. Syngene's stated strategy is "CDMO and AI-led advanced services"; in Q1 FY27 it launched Syn.AI for gigascale virtual screening and de novo molecular design, and appointed Dr Vijay Chandru, an AI and computational-biology specialist, as an independent director. A CRO that sells design insight rather than synthetic hands can charge more per head and win earlier in a programme. The net effect on revenue is genuinely unknown.

Bull vs bear: a real structural shift, priced unevenly

The case for Indian CRDMOs is strong over a decade. The case against is mostly about concentration, China and price over the next two years.

The two cases below are for the sub-sector as a whole. They apply with different force to each company: the bull case is strongest for Sai and Anthem's operating models, the bear case is strongest for their valuations, and Syngene is the one where the bear case is most visible in the numbers and least visible in the price.

The bull case

  • Structural outsourcing: a patent cliff of US$139–400 billion forces pharma to refill pipelines and turn fixed R&D cost into variable cost, and biotechs outsource almost everything.
  • A cost edge that still exists: an Indian scientist-year costs an INDICATIVE 25–35% less than a Chinese one and 70–75% less than an American one, with clean IP records and OECD-accepted GLP data.
  • Conversion is visible: Sai's 33–34 commercial molecules, 14 late-stage and three new commercial contracts in one quarter; Anthem's ten molecules taken from discovery to commercial supply.
  • BIOSECURE's long tail: new programmes are already being placed with Indian vendors as second sources, and the law's full effect by ~2033 favours India in development and manufacturing.
  • Quality economics: Anthem's 39% margin and 30% ROCE match global leaders; Sai's FY26 showed strong operating leverage.
  • Funding rebound: US biotech venture funding of US$16.3 billion in H1 2026 and Charles River's best bookings in four years point to recovering early-stage demand.
  • Syngene's optionality: net cash, the BMS centre to 2035 and under-used capacity priced at about 14× EBITDA.

The bear case

  • Concentration: Anthem's top five clients are 71% of revenue; Syngene showed what one client can do. Quarterly revenue at both swung 16–23% in 2026.
  • China is winning more, not less: WuXi grew 38.9% and Pharmaron 17.9% in H1 2026, and China out-invests India five to ten times a year.
  • The people spread is narrowing: wages rising 8–10% against price escalators of 3–5%; Syngene's staff ratio from ~27% to 33–38%.
  • AI may shrink the billable head faster than CROs can sell AI-led services.
  • Funding and rates: the September 2026 rate rise and the XBI's slide recall the 2022–23 winter, when discovery was cut first.
  • Valuation: 50–57× EBITDA and 82–86× earnings assume roughly 16–19% EBITDA growth for four years and full new plants; supply from True North and Anthem's promoter group continues.
  • Execution load: Sai's debt-funded capex, Anthem's Unit IV and Syngene's Baltimore requalification all land in FY27–28.

Our reading is that both cases are right on different timescales. The structural shift toward Indian research and manufacturing partners is real and has a decade to run, and the companies converting research into commercial supply will capture most of it. But 2026 has shown that the path is lumpy, concentrated and contested by a Chinese industry that is growing faster than India's. For the two premium names, the operating case is strong and the valuation already assumes it. For Syngene, the operating case is weak today and the valuation assumes it stays that way.
