Indian hotels: scarce rooms, rich multiples, a widening split

A complete guide to the business of India's listed hotel companies: how a room-night becomes profit, why five years of demand outrunning new supply has pushed room rates to records, why palace hotels are still gaining pricing power while midscale owners are being squeezed by a tax change, who keeps the money when a hotel does well (the owner or the brand), and what the seven companies in the NSE 750 are worth at 11 to 34 times their operating profit.

00

The one-page view: a supply-gap business in the middle of its best cycle

Demand has outrun new rooms for five years. The question now is which segment's gap closes first, and who keeps the money while it lasts.

India's listed hotel sector is seven companies worth a combined ₹2.13 lakh crore. One of them, The Indian Hotels Company (IHCL, the Taj group), is 51% of that value on its own. Together they earned ₹23,757 crore of revenue from operations in FY26 and ₹8,751 crore of operating profit (EBITDA, before other income), a 36.8% margin that few Indian industries can match. They run roughly 77,000 rooms between them, but own only a minority of those rooms: most are hotels that someone else owns and pays a listed brand to manage.

The industry is in the strongest up-cycle in its history. Across India's branded hotels, occupancy was 63–65% in calendar 2025 at an average room rate (ARR) of about ₹8,500–8,700, giving revenue per available room (RevPAR, the industry's single best number) of ₹5,400–5,600, according to HVS ANAROCK. In April–June 2026, the first quarter of FY27, same-store RevPAR rose another 11–13% on the year. ICRA expects premium-hotel room rates of ₹8,600–8,800 in FY27, occupancy of 72–74% in that segment, and industry revenue growth of 7–9%.

The reason is supply. Premium rooms in India's twelve key hotel cities are growing at 5–6% a year against demand growth of 8–9%, and ICRA expects that gap to last another two to three years. A hotel takes three to five years to plan, approve and build, so today's room rates are set by decisions taken in 2020–2022, when nobody was building. Brand signings hit a record of about 64,000 rooms in calendar 2025, but only about 14,000 rooms actually opened. The next wave of supply is visible; it is just slow.

Five forces shape everything else in this report:

  • The supply–demand gap and how long it lasts (Part 04, deep dive). It is closing at different speeds in different segments: first in midscale and tier-2 cities, where hotels are cheap to build; last in palace luxury, where they cannot be built at all.
  • Fixed costs with borrowed money on top (Part 05). About 60% of a hotel's costs do not move with occupancy, so most of each extra rupee of room revenue becomes profit. Owners with debt get that effect twice. The Leela's profit went from ₹48 crore to ₹403 crore in FY26 mostly because its interest bill fell from ₹459 crore to ₹204 crore.
  • Owner versus operator (Part 08, deep dive). The market pays about 34 times EBITDA for IHCL, which earns growing management fees on hotels it does not own, and 11–17 times for companies that own the buildings and borrow to do it. Almost every listed company is moving toward fees. Lemon Tree is literally splitting itself in two.
  • Palaces up, midscale squeezed (Part 09, deep dive). The Leela's room rate rose 30% in July–September 2026 to ₹25,046; Lemon Tree's rose about 2% in April–June. A September 2025 GST change cut the tax on rooms up to ₹7,500 to 5% but took away the input tax credit hotels used to claim, which hurts midscale owners' margins.
  • Shocks absorbed by domestic demand (Part 11). Five external shocks in eighteen months, including the West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026, each dented a quarter. None broke the trend, because domestic guests fill more than 80% of branded room-nights.

Where does that leave the cycle? The evidence points to mid-to-late, not peak. The top end is still accelerating on price; midscale is growing on occupancy rather than price, which is what a maturing cycle looks like; Chalet's business hotels lost 1.2 points of occupancy in Q1 FY27 as its own new rooms and competitors' opened. Q1 FY27's double-digit growth also sits on an easy comparison: the same quarter a year earlier was hit by the India–Pakistan hostilities of May 2025. The more useful framing is dispersion. Segments and cities are now moving at different speeds, and the gap between the best and worst performers is the story of FY27.

The valuation picture has two layers. The seven together trade at about 24.7 times FY26 EV/EBITDA (enterprise value, which adds net debt to market value, divided by operating profit). Strip out IHCL and the other six trade at 19.3 times. IHCL's 34 times is mostly a price for its brand and fee machine, not for hotel buildings. The Leela, at 29 times, is the exception: an owner priced like a brand, because its palace rates are rising faster than anyone else's. SAMHI, the most indebted owner, trades at about 11 times and roughly ₹1 crore of enterprise value per room, which is below what it would cost to build an upscale hotel today.

In plain terms

A hotel company makes money when more people want rooms than there are rooms to sell. In India that has been true for five years, because almost nothing was built during and after COVID while travel boomed. Prices have risen every year since. The listed companies are making record profits, and investors are paying high prices for their shares on the bet that the shortage lasts. The risk is the usual one in hotels: everybody has now signed up to build, and when those rooms open, rates stop rising. Palace hotels are safest because nobody can build another palace; cheaper business hotels are most exposed.

01

How a room-night becomes money

A hotel sells the same perishable product 365 nights a year, plus food, events and, increasingly, its own name.

A hotel room is the most perishable product in the economy. A room unsold tonight cannot be sold tomorrow. Everything about hotel economics follows from that: the obsession with occupancy and rate, the dependence on distribution (whoever can fill the room tonight has power), and the high fixed costs of a building, a staff and a brand that exist whether or not a guest arrives.

A listed Indian hotel company earns money in five ways, and the mix decides its margin, its capital needs and its valuation.

  • Rooms. The engine. Room revenue is 45–60% of a full-service hotel's revenue and 70% or more at a midscale hotel with limited food. ITC Hotels' rooms were 47.9% of revenue (excluding branded residences) in Q1 FY27. Rooms are the highest-margin line: once the building, staff and power are paid for, an extra room sold costs little more than laundry, amenities and a distribution commission.
  • Food, beverage and banqueting. Restaurants, bars, weddings and conferences: 35–45% of revenue at luxury and upscale hotels. ITC Hotels' F&B revenue was ₹361 crore in Q1 FY27, 40.2% of the total and up 11%. F&B carries food costs (about a quarter of F&B revenue) and much heavier staffing, so its margin is lower, but Indian weddings make banqueting a business in its own right. Weddings and conferences (MICE: meetings, incentives, conferences, exhibitions) were 19% of ITC Hotels' room-nights in Q1 FY27.
  • Management and franchise fees. Money for running or lending a brand to a hotel owned by someone else. IHCL earned ₹685 crore of fees in FY26 (+22%) and ₹168 crore in Q1 FY27 (+26%); ITC Hotels ₹49 crore in Q1 FY27 (+35%); Lemon Tree ₹169.7 crore in FY26, of which a growing part comes from hotels owned by third parties (₹22.8 crore in Q1 FY27). Fees need almost no capital and carry margins of 80% or more.
  • Adjacent businesses. IHCL's TajSATS airline and institutional catering earned ₹1,219 crore in FY26 at a 24.2% margin; EIH runs flight kitchens, airport lounges, Nile cruisers and a charter airline; IHCL also has Qmin (food), amã (homestays) and Chambers (a members' club). These lower the consolidated margin but diversify the income.
  • Real estate. Chalet rents about 2.4 million square feet of offices next to its hotels (rent run-rate ₹29 crore a month in June 2026) and sold apartments worth ₹738 crore in FY26; ITC Hotels is selling branded residences in Hyderabad. These are lumpy and can swamp the hotel numbers in a given year.

An integrated Indian group (Taj, Oberoi, ITC) does Steps 2, 3 and part of 4 itself on its owned hotels and only Step 3 on managed ones. A pure owner such as SAMHI does only Step 2 and pays someone else for Step 3. The money a guest pays is divided along this chain, and the divisions are the subject of Part 08.

Most investors focus on Step 2 and Step 3. The owner bears the capital cost and keeps whatever profit is left after paying the operator. The operator puts up little or no capital, takes a slice of revenue and a slice of gross operating profit, and can grow as fast as owners will sign contracts with it. A typical Indian hotel management agreement pays the operator a base fee of 2–4% of total revenue plus an incentive fee of 6–10% of gross operating profit, sometimes only after the owner has earned a minimum return. A franchise, where the owner runs the hotel and borrows only the brand and booking system, pays 4–6% of room revenue plus loyalty and reservation charges. These are typical ranges, not disclosed contract terms; actual agreements vary and are confidential.

Where the value is captured

Three positions in the chain earn above-average returns. The first is location plus brand at the very top: owners of irreplaceable buildings (the Taj palaces, the Oberoi Vilas resorts, the Leela palaces in Delhi, Bengaluru and Udaipur, ITC's Grand Chola and Maurya) charge ₹17,000–25,000 or more a night because there is no new supply to compete with them. The Leela's Q2 FY27 average daily rate (ADR, the same thing as ARR) was ₹25,046. EIH's all-domestic ARR, including managed hotels, was ₹17,868 in Q1 FY27.

The second is brand plus distribution at scale. An operator with a loyalty programme (IHCL's Neu programme claims about 16 million members; Marriott Bonvoy is far larger globally), a reservation system and a reputation can sign hotels faster than it could ever build them. IHCL's fees were about 20% of its FY26 EBITDA (₹685 crore of ₹3,477 crore on the company's own EBITDA basis) and are growing faster than the rest of the business.

The third is cheap capital at the owner level, which earns only an 8–12% pre-tax EBITDA yield on what it would cost to build a hotel today. That is acceptable only if the owner bought the asset cheaply (SAMHI's model of buying under-performing hotels and fixing them) or owns land acquired long ago (Chalet's land came from the K Raheja group). An owner who pays full replacement cost and borrows heavily makes a thin return even in a good year. Online travel agents (OTAs) such as MakeMyTrip, Booking.com and Agoda take a growing toll, particularly at midscale and economy hotels where direct booking is weakest.

In plain terms

A hotel has three kinds of money-makers attached to it. Whoever owns the building gets most of the profit but has to pay for the building. Whoever's name is on the door (Taj, Marriott, Lemon Tree) gets a small cut of every rupee without buying the building. Whoever sold the room online (MakeMyTrip, Booking.com) takes a commission. The listed Indian groups play all three roles in different proportions, and the proportion largely decides what their shares are worth.

One more structural point matters for reading the numbers. Integrated groups report consolidated figures that blend owned hotels (full revenue, full costs), leased hotels (full revenue, with rent as a cost), managed hotels (fee only) and non-hotel businesses (catering, real estate). Two companies with the same "revenue" can therefore run very different numbers of hotels. IHCL's ₹9,689 crore of revenue comes from more than 380 operating hotels, most of which contribute only a fee; SAMHI's ₹1,248 crore comes from 31 hotels, every one of them owned. When this report compares companies per room, it uses owned rooms for owners and operating rooms for brands, and says which.

02

Size: a big country short of branded rooms

About two lakh branded rooms serve a country of 1.45 billion people. The listed seven earn roughly a third of the branded market's revenue.

India's branded hotel industry is small for the size of the country. Industry estimates put total branded room inventory at roughly 1.9–2.1 lakh rooms (INDICATIVE: an estimate from HVS and Horwath HTL industry reports, not re-verified for this report). The United States has about 5.6 million branded rooms and China more than 2.5 million. That is under one branded room for every 6,000 Indians, against about one for every 60 Americans (INDICATIVE). The unbranded sector, from family-run guesthouses to dharamshalas, is several times larger by room count: the Federation of Hotel & Restaurant Associations of India (FHRAI) says about 90% of India's hotels charge less than ₹7,500 a night.

The premium end is where the listed companies play. ICRA counts premium room inventory in twelve key cities as having crossed 1,00,000 rooms in FY23 and expected about 1,20,000 by the following year. The branded pipeline signed up to 2029 is about 1,05,000 rooms (Horwath HTL, 2025), roughly half today's branded base, although signings convert to openings slowly and some never open.

The arithmetic is simple: two lakh rooms, sold on 64% of nights at about ₹8,600, produce roughly ₹40,000 crore of room revenue a year. With food, beverage and other income at 40–45% of a branded hotel's total, the branded market is roughly ₹70,000–75,000 crore. The listed seven report ₹23,757 crore, about a third, and that figure includes IHCL's catering, Chalet's offices and apartments, and EIH's flight kitchens. Because managed hotels book their revenue in the owner's accounts, the listed brands influence a much larger share of the market than their revenue suggests: IHCL's 380-plus operating hotels generate an "enterprise revenue" well above its consolidated revenue.

Growth: 21% a year for two years, partly flattered

Combined revenue of the seven grew from ₹16,120 crore in FY24 to ₹23,757 crore in FY26, a 21.4% two-year compound rate. Two adjustments are needed before that figure means anything. ITC Hotels' FY24 is a carve-out from ITC Ltd, before the demerger became effective on 1 January 2025, so its jump is accounting rather than growth. Chalet's FY26 includes ₹738 crore of apartment sales recognised at handover. Strip both effects out and the underlying hotel revenue of the group grew in the low-to-mid teens, still strong for an industry whose prices are tied to inflation over the long run.

The penetration gap, and why it is not a forecast

The long-run case for Indian hotels rests on under-penetration: a country with one of the world's five largest economies, one of the three largest domestic air markets and an eighth-ranked travel and tourism economy (WTTC) has a fraction of the branded rooms of its peers. That case is real but slow. Branded supply grows only as fast as land, approvals and capital allow, which has historically been 5–7% a year, and penetration does not by itself set room rates. What sets rates is the gap between demand growth and supply growth in each city in each year, which is why the industry swings between shortage and glut while the long-run penetration story barely changes.

In plain terms

India has very few proper branded hotel rooms for its size, and that is the reason the business can grow for decades. But it does not mean prices rise every year. Prices rise when demand grows faster than new rooms open, and that happens in waves. Right now India is in a wave where rooms are short. The listed companies earn about a third of what branded hotels collect, and they run or brand many more hotels than that number suggests.

Growth for FY27 looks lower than FY26 but still healthy. ICRA's 7–9% industry revenue growth assumes rates up 3–5% and occupancy edging higher. Company guidance is consistent with that: IHCL has maintained its double-digit revenue growth guidance for FY27; SAMHI guides 9–11%; Lemon Tree expects a stronger second half. The aggregate of the seven grew only 5.0% in Q1 FY27, but that is distorted by Chalet's apartment sales a year earlier (95 units handed over in Q1 FY26 against one in Q1 FY27). Excluding Chalet, Q1 FY27 revenue grew 14.9% and operating profit also grew 14.9%.

03

Who fills the rooms: weddings, flights, pilgrims and offices

Domestic travellers buy more than four of every five branded room-nights. That fact explains why the 2025–26 shocks barely dented the cycle.

Branded hotel demand in India is overwhelmingly domestic. SAMHI, which owns business hotels in the big IT cities, says domestic travellers were 81–82% of its room-nights in Q1 FY27, and industry estimates put the domestic share across branded hotels at 80–85%. Foreign tourists matter disproportionately to a few luxury circuits (Rajasthan, Agra, Kerala, Goa in winter) and to the January–March quarter, but they are a minority of the business.

Six demand engines drive the domestic number.

  • Air travel. India's airports handled about 420 million passengers in calendar 2025, roughly 5% more than in 2024, of which 338.9 million were domestic (HVS ANAROCK). Air connectivity is the single best predictor of hotel demand in a city: a new airport or route creates room-nights almost immediately, and an airline disruption removes them just as fast.
  • Leisure and religious travel. Domestic tourist visits were about 4.5 billion in 2025, and the Ministry of Tourism targets more than 9.5 billion by 2030. Spiritual tourism is a large segment on its own: the Maha Kumbh in early 2025 drew more than 663 million visitors. Hotels near temples, in hill stations and on short-drive circuits from metros have been the fastest-growing segment since COVID.
  • Weddings and MICE. Indian weddings are a hotel business: several hundred guests, several nights, banqueting priced per plate. Weddings plus conferences were 19% of ITC Hotels' room-nights in Q1 FY27 (23% a year earlier). The wedding calendar, set by auspicious dates, can move revenue between quarters.
  • Corporate travel. Contracted corporate rates were 18% of ITC Hotels' room-nights in Q1 FY27. The growth of global capability centres (GCCs) and IT parks in Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune drives demand for business hotels near office corridors, the core market of Chalet and SAMHI.
  • Events. The G20 summit in 2023 and the BRICS summit in Delhi in Q2 FY27 lifted city-hotel rates sharply; The Leela says its city hotels gained four percentage points of occupancy in Q2 FY27, helped by BRICS. Commonwealth Games 2030 in Ahmedabad is the next large event on the horizon.
  • Inbound tourism. Foreign tourist arrivals (FTAs) were 10.93 million in 2019, fell to 1.53 million in 2021, recovered to 9.95 million in 2024 and then fell about 9% to roughly 9.0 million in 2025, before the West Asia war cut inbound travel by a further 15–20% (PHDCCI estimate, April 2026).

Seasonality: two strong quarters, two weak

Indian hotels earn most of their profit between October and March. The wedding season, winter leisure travel, inbound tourists and the conference calendar all concentrate in the third and fourth fiscal quarters. April to September is the trough: summer heat on the plains, the monsoon, school holidays that favour hill stations over cities. Because costs are largely fixed, margins swing far more than revenue does. In plain terms

Most people in Indian hotels are Indians: families on holiday, wedding guests, pilgrims and people travelling for work. Foreign tourists matter for a handful of palace and heritage hotels in the winter, but not for the business as a whole. That is why the fall in foreign visitors in 2025 and 2026 did not stop the boom. The year has two halves: hotels make most of their money between October and March and much less in summer and the monsoon, so a single quarter's profit tells you little.

One demand risk deserves its own line: aviation. Hotels in India depend on flights more than in most countries, because road and rail journeys between the major cities are long. The December 2025 airline disruption, which HVS linked to softer occupancy that month, and the closure of West Asian airspace in 2026 both showed the dependence. When an airline cuts capacity, hotels feel it within weeks. The flip side is that every new airport (Navi Mumbai, Noida's Jewar) is a demand catalyst for the hotels around it, which is why the owners of airport-adjacent land, Chalet at Mumbai's Sahar among them, value those sites so highly.

04

The supply gap and its half-life Deep dive

Demand growing at 8–9% against supply at 5–6% explains the boom. Record signings explain why it will not last forever, and why each segment will feel the end at a different time.

Every hotel cycle is a supply story. Demand for rooms moves with the economy, air travel and events, and in India it has grown at high single digits for most of the past two decades. Supply moves in lumps, three to five years after the decision to build, and it is the gap between the two that sets room rates. When demand runs ahead, occupancy rises first; once hotels are full on peak nights, revenue managers raise rates; higher rates then flow almost entirely into profit because the costs of the building and staff are already paid. When supply catches up, the sequence runs in reverse, and it runs faster than it rose.

India's current gap was created by a building drought. The long bust of FY09–FY14 left owners with heavy debt and little appetite to build; COVID froze what projects remained. When travel came back in 2022 and 2023, it came back into a stock of rooms that had barely grown. ICRA estimates that premium room inventory in the twelve key cities is growing at a 5–6% compound rate between FY25 and FY28 against demand growth of 8–9%, and expects the gap to persist for another two to three years. In luxury, industry estimates put demand growth at 10–11% against supply of about 6%.

The seed of the next glut is already planted

Brands signed a record of about 64,118 rooms across 586 hotels in calendar 2025, up from 44,744 rooms in 456 hotels in 2024, according to HVS ANAROCK. Openings in 2025 were only about 14,199 rooms in 176 hotels. Signings ran at roughly 4.5 times openings. Those numbers need careful reading. A signing is a management or franchise contract, not a building; many signings are conversions of existing unbranded hotels (which add branded supply but not physical rooms), and a meaningful share of greenfield signings are delayed for years or never built. Even so, the direction is clear. Owners and developers have responded to record rates exactly as they always do, and the room-count growth that ICRA sees as 5–6% a year through FY28 is likely to rise after that.

The national average hides the mechanism that matters for investors: supply arrives first where it is cheapest and quickest to build. In plain terms

When a hotel is busy, it first fills its empty rooms and then raises prices. Raising prices is where the real money is. Right now the expensive palace and city hotels are still raising prices fast, while the mid-priced hotels are mostly filling more rooms and barely raising prices. That is what usually happens when new hotels are starting to open in a segment: the cheaper end, where hotels are easiest to build, feels the competition first.

Is the cycle peaking? The case it is not

  • Industry growth re-accelerated. Same-store RevPAR rose 11–13% in Q1 FY27 (HVS ANAROCK), IHCL's domestic like-for-like RevPAR 14%, and The Leela's 30% in Q2 FY27.
  • Supply cover. ICRA sees the demand–supply gap lasting two to three more years, and forecasts premium ARR of ₹8,600–8,800 in FY27, up 3–5% from ₹8,200–8,500 in FY26.
  • Guidance. IHCL maintains double-digit FY27 revenue growth guidance; SAMHI guides 9–11%; Lemon Tree expects a stronger second half and double-digit fee growth from Q2.
  • Occupancy still rising at most listed owners: SAMHI +5.1 points, Lemon Tree +3.2, ITC Hotels +3, EIH owned hotels +3.

The case it is late

  • Deceleration through FY26. IHCL's same-store RevPAR growth slowed to 9% in FY26 from the high teens in FY24; Chalet's FY26 RevPAR rose 5% as occupancy fell to 67.2%.
  • An easy comparison. Q1 FY27's double-digit growth is measured against a quarter hurt by Operation Sindoor in May 2025; ITC Hotels also noted April 2026 softness from air-travel uncertainty.
  • Occupancy-led growth. Rate growth narrowing to about 2% in midscale is a classic late-cycle sign that price has run ahead of what guests will pay.
  • Record signings and cost inflation. About 64,000 rooms signed in 2025, plus energy, food and staff costs rising, flagged by brokers in Q1 FY27 commentary.
  • Inbound weakness. Foreign arrivals fell about 9% in 2025 and a further 15–20% after the West Asia war began.

Our reading is mid-to-late cycle, not peak. The aggregate still has two to three years of supply cover, but the segments have separated. The luxury top end is accelerating on rate; upscale business hotels are holding rate but losing occupancy where new rooms open; midscale is growing on occupancy and being squeezed by GST. The spread between the best and worst performers is now the most important number to track, more than the industry average.

How the last cycle ended

The analyst's standing lesson from Indian hotels is that cycles end in a supply glut funded by cheap debt. In the mid-2000s boom, rates and RevPAR peaked around FY08. Developers responded with a wave of projects in Delhi NCR (timed for the 2010 Commonwealth Games), Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune. The 26/11 attacks of November 2008 and the global financial crisis then cut demand just as the new rooms opened. Occupancy and rates fell for the better part of five years. The most leveraged owner, Hotel Leelaventure, never recovered; its palaces and brand were sold to Brookfield in 2019 for about ₹3,950 crore. That history is why investors watch signings, debt and city-level openings more closely than the national average.

Two differences make a repeat less likely in the short run. Today's leading companies are far less indebted: IHCL, ITC Hotels and EIH have net cash, and The Leela used most of its IPO money to repay debt. And much of the new supply is being signed as management contracts or franchises, which means the listed brands earn fees on it rather than competing with it from their own balance sheets. The risk has moved to the owners of upscale and midscale hotels in the cities with the largest pipelines, and to the private developers financing them.

In plain terms

The good times in hotels last as long as it takes developers to build new hotels, which is about three to five years. Developers have now started building in record numbers. So the shortage that has made hotels so profitable will start to ease, probably first for mid-priced hotels in smaller cities and last for luxury hotels in big cities, where land is scarce and building is slow and expensive. The listed brands are better protected than last time because many of the new hotels will carry their names and pay them fees.

Fixed costs, double gearing: why profits move faster than rooms

About 60% of a hotel's costs do not change when occupancy does. Add borrowed money and a small change in RevPAR becomes a large change in profit.

A hotel is mostly a fixed-cost business. The building has to be lit, cooled, cleaned, insured and maintained whether it is full or empty; the core staff has to be paid; the brand and reservation system cost the same. Industry rules of thumb put roughly 60% of a hotel's operating costs as fixed in the short run (INDICATIVE). The consequence is operating leverage: when RevPAR rises, most of the extra revenue becomes operating profit, and when it falls, most of the lost revenue comes straight out of it.

The margin varies predictably by model. A brand-owner such as IHCL, ITC Hotels or EIH pays no operator fee on its own hotels, so owned-hotel EBITDA margins run at 45–48% (IHCL's standalone business earned 45.1% in FY26 and 41.8% in the seasonally weak Q1 FY27). Lemon Tree runs at about 48% despite low room rates, because its hotels serve limited food and run lean staffing ratios. SAMHI's 34–35% reflects franchise and management fees paid to Marriott, IHG and Hyatt, a select-service mix and recently acquired hotels still being improved. Consolidated margins of the diversified groups are lower again because of catering and other businesses: IHCL's consolidated operating margin was 33% in FY26.

Operating leverage in one example

Take an owned hotel with ₹100 crore of revenue, ₹40 crore of EBITDA, ₹10 crore of depreciation and ₹15 crore of interest, so ₹15 crore of profit before tax (ILLUSTRATIVE). Revenue rises 10%, to ₹110 crore. Because most costs are fixed, about 65% of the extra ₹10 crore drops through to EBITDA: EBITDA rises ₹6.5 crore, or 16%. Depreciation and interest do not change, so profit before tax rises by the same ₹6.5 crore, which is 43%. A 10% revenue gain becomes a 16% EBITDA gain and a 43% pre-tax profit gain. Run the same arithmetic in reverse for a 10% fall and pre-tax profit drops by 43%. This is the arithmetic that made hotel stocks fall so hard in FY09–FY14 and rise so fast since FY23.

Leverage across the seven

The balance sheets divide neatly. In plain terms

A hotel costs almost the same to run whether it is full or half-empty. So when more guests come and pay more, nearly all the extra money is profit. If the owner also borrowed to build the hotel, the interest bill stays the same too, so profit after interest jumps even more. This is wonderful on the way up and painful on the way down. The Leela showed the other side of it: by paying off most of its debt with IPO money, it turned a small profit into a large one in a single year without its hotels doing anything dramatic.

Who owns India's branded rooms

A global brand is the largest operator, Tata's IHCL is the largest Indian one, and much of the capital behind the boom comes from private equity and property developers.

Branded hotels are roughly 10–15% of India's hotel rooms (INDICATIVE), and within that branded segment a handful of operators dominate. Marriott International is the largest brand system in India by rooms, with about 150 or more hotels and over 30,000 rooms (INDICATIVE, from industry reports, not re-verified), followed by IHCL. Then come ITC Hotels, Accor, IHG, Hyatt, Hilton, Radisson, Lemon Tree and Wyndham. Aggregators such as OYO, Treebo and FabHotels add tens of thousands of rooms at the economy end, but they are a different, franchise-of-convenience business.

The seven listed companies between them run about 77,000 operating rooms. Well under half of these are owned by the listed companies themselves: about 35–40% on our research estimate, closer to half if leased hotels and Lemon Tree's Fleur portfolio are counted as owned. The rest are managed or franchised hotels owned by families, developers and funds, from which the listed company earns a fee.

Six ways to be a hotel company

Seven listed companies, six business models. What a company owns decides its margin, its return on capital and the multiple the market pays.

"Hotel company" covers businesses that have very little in common beyond the product. At one end, IHCL is a brand and management company that also owns trophy hotels and runs an airline-catering business; at the other, SAMHI owns every one of its 31 hotels, borrows to do it and pays Marriott, IHG and Hyatt to run or brand them. Between them sit luxury owner-operators, a private-equity-controlled palace owner, a property developer's hotel arm and a midscale chain in the middle of splitting itself in two. Owner or operator: who keeps the RevPAR Deep dive

Owners capture the cycle; operators capture the growth in hotel count. The market pays more for the second, and almost every listed company is moving toward it.

The single most important structural question in Indian hotels is how the money a guest pays is split between the company that owns the building and the company whose name is on it. The split decides return on capital, growth, risk and valuation, and it is changing: brands are signing more managed and franchised hotels than they are building, and owners are being separated from brands (Lemon Tree and Fleur), listed on their own (Chalet, SAMHI, Juniper, Ventive, Brigade Hotel Ventures) or recapitalised by private equity.

Who gains when rates rise

Now raise the hotel's room rate by 10%, to ₹9,900, holding occupancy. Room revenue rises ₹5.75 crore. Almost all of it reaches GOP; the operator's base fee takes 3%, the incentive fee 8% of the GOP increase, and the FF&E reserve 3%. The owner's EBITDA rises about ₹4.9 crore, or 12%, and its ROCE rises from 8.2% to about 9.5%. The operator's fees rise about ₹0.6 crore, or 9%. In percentage terms the two gain similarly; in rupees the owner gains eight times as much, because it owns the residual. Run occupancy down 10 points instead (to 60%) and room revenue falls ₹8.2 crore; with about 65% flow-through, owner EBITDA falls about ₹5.3 crore (13%) while the operator loses a much smaller absolute amount.

That asymmetry explains three facts about the listed sector. In an up-cycle, owners' EBITDA grows faster than operators': The Leela's operating profit rose 24.5% in FY26. Operators' fee growth is steadier and comes mostly from new hotels: IHCL's fees grew 22% in FY26 and 26% in Q1 FY27, ITC Hotels' 28% and 35%. And the market pays more for a rupee of fees than for a rupee of owner EBITDA, because the fee rupee needs no capital to grow and falls less in a downturn. It also explains the timing of the listing wave. Owner-side companies listed in 2023–2025 because their private-equity backers wanted to sell at the point where owner cash flows were highest.

In plain terms

If you own the hotel, you keep most of the profit when room prices rise and take most of the pain when they fall. If you only manage the hotel, you take a small, steady cut either way, but you did not have to pay for the building, so you can manage hundreds of hotels with very little money. Owning is a bet on the cycle. Managing is a bet on signing more hotels. Investors currently pay much more for the second bet.

Luxury rates are rising faster than at any point in the cycle, while a tax change has quietly cut midscale owners' margins. The sector is splitting in two.

GST 2.0 and the ₹7,500 cliff

On 22 September 2025, as part of the "GST 2.0" rate rationalisation, the tax on hotel rooms with a tariff of ₹7,500 a night or less was cut from 12% to 5%, but without input tax credit (ITC). Rooms above ₹7,500 stayed at 18% with ITC. Hotels moving into the 5% band also had to reverse ITC balances standing on that date. Earlier history: GST on rooms began in 2017 with a 28% top slab on rooms above ₹5,000; a September 2019 cut set rooms of ₹1,001–7,500 at 12% and above ₹7,500 at 18%; rooms up to ₹1,000 were exempt. Restaurant service inside hotels follows a related rule: in "specified premises" (hotels where any room's declared tariff in the previous year exceeded ₹7,500, or that opt in), restaurants charge 18% with ITC; elsewhere 5% without ITC.

Who is exposed, and the cliff itself

The exposure maps directly onto the rate ladder. Lemon Tree (ARR ₹6,361–6,875) and SAMHI (about ₹6,500–6,600) sell most of their rooms below ₹7,500, as do IHCL's Ginger and Gateway brands and ITC's Fortune. The luxury owners (ARR above ₹11,000) are largely unaffected on rooms, though the "specified premises" rule affects their restaurants. Within IHCL, the exposure sits in the faster-growing new businesses, which is worth watching in its segment disclosures.

The cliff creates a pricing distortion. A room at ₹7,500 costs the guest ₹7,875 with 5% GST. A room at ₹7,501 attracts 18% and costs ₹8,851. One rupee more in tariff costs the guest ₹976 more. Hotels with rates near the line therefore have a strong incentive to bunch just below it, which caps their rate growth unless they can jump well above it. For an upscale-select hotel trying to move its tariff from ₹7,000 to ₹8,000, a 14% increase, the guest sees the tax-inclusive price rise 28%, from ₹7,350 to ₹9,440. This is an analyst inference about behaviour, not a measured effect, but it is consistent with midscale ARR growth stalling at 2–3% in Q1 FY27.

In plain terms

The government cut the tax guests pay on cheaper hotel rooms, which sounds good for hotels. But it also stopped those hotels from deducting the tax they pay on their own bills, such as rent, electricity and outsourced staff, so their costs went up. For a mid-priced hotel that can mean about five points less profit margin. Expensive hotels charging more than ₹7,500 a night were not affected. Add the fact that luxury hotels can raise prices because nobody can build new palaces, and you have a sector where the top end is doing much better than the middle.

The split has a valuation consequence. The market already pays 29 times EBITDA for The Leela, an owner, because its rate growth is so strong, and 15–16 times for Lemon Tree and 11 times for SAMHI, where rate growth has stalled and costs have risen. If the divergence persists into the second half of FY27, the gap may widen further; if the GST Council restores input credit or midscale rates recover once occupancy is high enough, the cheaper owners have the most room to re-rate. Q2 and Q3 FY27 results for Lemon Tree and SAMHI, due in late October and November, will show which way it is going.

In plain terms

Investors are paying a lot for luxury hotels and much less for mid-priced ones because the luxury end is where prices are rising. If the mid-priced hotels start raising prices again, or the tax rule is changed back, their shares are the ones with the most catching up to do. If not, the gap between the two halves of the sector will keep growing.

The rules: tax, land, licences and the slow brake on supply

Hotels are lightly regulated on price and heavily regulated on everything needed to open one. That combination protects incumbents.

Nobody in India regulates what a hotel charges for a room. Rates are set by revenue managers night by night, and the only price-related rule is the tax bracket a tariff falls into. What is regulated, intensely and mostly at state and municipal level, is building and running a hotel: land use, building plans, fire safety, pollution consent, liquor licences, food safety, lifts, signage, and dozens of local permissions. Those approvals take years in the large cities, and they are one reason supply responds so slowly to high rates.

Tax is the policy lever that most directly moves hotel economics, and it has moved three times in eight years. Infrastructure status is the second lever. Hotels with a project cost of more than ₹200 crore, excluding land, are on the government's harmonised list of infrastructure sub-sectors, which lets lenders treat them as infrastructure loans with longer tenors and, often, lower rates. Recent budgets have proposed extending the benefit, including to hotels in the 50 destinations the Union Budget 2025-26 proposed developing with the states; whether those extensions have been notified is not confirmed in our research. For large luxury projects (Chalet's Ritz-Carlton Hyderabad, the Leela pipeline) the status makes debt cheaper; for the midscale hotels that dominate the pipeline it does little.

The most important regulation is the one nobody writes down: time. A metro hotel needs land-use conversion, building-plan approval, environmental and fire clearances, and then a liquor licence and a dozen operating permits before it opens. Three to five years from land purchase to first guest is normal; longer is common. That delay is why the supply response to the current boom will arrive only in FY28 and later, and why existing hotels in the best locations earn a scarcity rent. Liquor licensing is a state matter and can make or break a hotel's F&B economics; Bihar and Gujarat, for example, restrict alcohol, which changes what a full-service hotel can earn there.

What could change

  • GST Council on input credit for the 5% band. The single most valuable policy change for Lemon Tree, SAMHI and IHCL's Ginger. Watch Council meeting agendas.
  • A hotel REIT framework in practice. India's REITs hold offices and malls; no hotel REIT has listed. A listed hotel REIT would give owners such as Chalet, SAMHI or Fleur a way to recycle capital and could re-rate owned hotels.
  • E-visa fees and inbound promotion. Fee waivers and marketing could help the inbound recovery once West Asian airspace normalises.
  • State tourism policies. Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Goa offer capital subsidies, stamp-duty waivers and industry status to hotels in some form; generous state incentives accelerate supply in those states.
In plain terms

The government does not tell hotels what to charge, which is unusual in India. What it controls is tax and, through the states, every permit needed to build and run a hotel. Getting those permits takes years, which slows down new hotels and protects the ones that already exist in good locations. The tax rules have changed three times since 2017, and the latest change helped guests but hurt the profit of mid-priced hotels.

Five shocks in eighteen months, and why none broke the cycle

Hostilities, a tax change, an airline meltdown, a regional war and a two-year slide in foreign tourists. Each dented a quarter; domestic demand absorbed them all.

Between May 2025 and mid-2026, Indian hotels absorbed more external shocks than in any comparable period outside COVID. Operation Sindoor, the India–Pakistan hostilities of May 2025, emptied hotels in the north and west for several weeks. GST 2.0 changed the tax economics of most rooms in September 2025. An airline disruption softened December 2025 occupancy, which HVS linked to air-travel problems that month. The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 cut India's air links to the Gulf and Europe. And foreign tourist arrivals, already down about 9% in 2025, fell a further 15–20% (PHDCCI estimate). Through all of it, industry RevPAR kept rising: by 11–13% in Q1 FY27.

120 years, three busts: the history behind today's structure

Indian hotels went from family-owned palaces to a listed sector of brands and owners. Each era left a company or a scar that still shapes the stocks.

Indian hospitality's history explains why its leaders are what they are. The founding families built trophy hotels that remain the industry's most valuable assets. Liberalisation brought global brands and the first boom; the long bust that followed broke the most indebted owner and taught a generation of investors to fear supply and debt. The asset-light turn, the post-COVID recovery and the 2023–2026 listing wave produced the seven companies in this report.

1903–1990s: the founding families

Jamsetji Tata opened the Taj Mahal Palace in Bombay in 1903, the start of what is now IHCL. M.S. Oberoi built the Oberoi group from the 1930s; the Oberoi Inter-Continental in Delhi opened in 1965. ITC entered hotels in 1975 as Welcomgroup. Capt. C.P. Krishnan Nair opened the first Leela, in Mumbai, in 1986. For most of the century the industry was luxury-only, owner-operated and capital-heavy, serving foreign tourists, diplomats and a narrow Indian elite.

2003–2008: liberalisation and the first boom

The IT and outsourcing boom created business travel on a new scale, and room rates spiked in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Mumbai and Delhi. IHCL launched Ginger in 2004 to serve the budget traveller; Lemon Tree was founded in 2002 by Patu Keswani to build a midscale brand. Global chains entered by management contract, starting the separation of owner from brand that defines the industry today. RevPAR peaked around FY08.

FY09–FY14: the long bust

Three blows arrived together: the 26/11 attacks of November 2008, which targeted the Taj Mahal Palace and the Oberoi in Mumbai; the global financial crisis; and a wave of new supply in Delhi NCR (built for the 2010 Commonwealth Games), Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune. Occupancy and rates fell for most of five years. Leveraged owners failed. Hotel Leelaventure's debt crisis ended with Brookfield buying the Leela palaces and brand in 2019 for about ₹3,950 crore. IHCL carried heavy losses from international acquisitions. The lesson analysts still cite is that hotel cycles end in supply funded by cheap debt.

FY15–FY20: recovery and the asset-light turn

Little new supply was built, and occupancy recovered. IHCL's "Aspiration 2022" strategy (2018) committed it to growth through management contracts. Lemon Tree listed in April 2018 and Chalet in February 2019. The 2019 GST cut helped. In 2010, Reliance had bought about 14% of EIH as a "white knight" against ITC, raising its stake to about 18.5% by 2012, which created EIH's unusual shareholder register.

FY21: COVID

Revenue collapsed. EIH's revenue fell to about ₹497 crore in FY21 with a loss of about ₹94 crore. Chalet's office rents kept cash coming. IHCL raised equity through a rights issue. The building stopped completely, and that pause is the origin of today's supply gap.

In plain terms

The big Indian hotel names are old: the Taj opened in 1903 and the Oberoi group grew from the 1930s. The industry has had two good booms and one long bust in the last twenty years. The bust, from 2009 to 2014, happened because too many hotels were built with borrowed money just as a terror attack and a global crisis hit. The current boom started after COVID, when nobody had built anything for years. The owners who survived the last bust are the reason today's leaders carry so little debt.

History also explains the valuation hierarchy. The trophy assets built by the founding families (Taj, Oberoi, the Leela palaces, ITC's Grand Chola and Maurya) cannot be replicated, so the companies that own them can raise prices in any decent year. The midscale chains born in the 2000s (Ginger, Lemon Tree) compete in a segment where new supply is easy to add, so their rates are more cyclical. The owners born in the 2010s (SAMHI) are capital vehicles whose value depends on buying cheaply and managing leverage. The cycle treats each generation differently.

Reading a hotel: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number a hotel company reports, what it means, what a good one looks like, how the seven compare, and how each can mislead.

Hotel companies report a small set of operating numbers that, read together, explain almost everything about their results. Occupancy and room rate tell you about demand and pricing power; RevPAR combines them; margins and flow-through tell you how much of the revenue becomes profit; EBITDA per room, fee share, pipeline and capex per room tell you about the business model; ROCE and leverage tell you about the capital. The difficulty is that every company defines most of these numbers slightly differently, over a different set of hotels, in a business with strong seasons. This section takes each metric in turn and ends with a ten-minute method for reading a quarterly result.

The equation: how a room becomes revenue and profit

The whole business reduces to one chain of multiplication and subtraction. Room revenue is the number of rooms (keys) times 365 nights times the share of nights sold (occupancy) times the average price per room sold (ARR). Total revenue adds food, beverage and other income, conventionally expressed by dividing room revenue by the rooms' share of total revenue. Owner EBITDA is total revenue times the gross operating profit margin, less any operator fees and the furniture and equipment reserve.

In plain terms

Count the rooms, multiply by the nights in a year, multiply by the share of nights that were sold, and multiply by the average price. That is room revenue. Add food and drink and you have total revenue. Take away the running costs, and any fee paid to the brand, and you have the hotel's profit before interest and depreciation. 1. Occupancy

What it is. The share of available room-nights that were sold. Formula: room-nights sold ÷ room-nights available. A 300-room hotel open for a 30-day month has 9,000 room-nights; selling 6,750 of them is 75% occupancy.

What drives it. Demand in the micro-market, competing supply, seasonality, the corporate and group contracts the hotel has signed, its distribution reach, and its pricing strategy. Occupancy and rate trade off: a hotel can fill rooms by cutting price, so occupancy on its own is not a measure of quality. Luxury hotels deliberately run at 65–75% to protect rate; select-service business hotels run at 75–80% because their rates are lower and their costs leaner. New hotels start at 40–50% and take two to three years to stabilise.

Benchmarks. Good: 75% or higher for a city hotel. Average: 65–75%. Weak: below 65%. Resorts run lower (Chalet's resorts were at 51% in Q1 FY27; The Leela's at 53% in H1 FY27) and should be judged against other resorts. In plain terms

Occupancy tells you how full the hotel is; the room rate tells you what guests paid. A hotel can always fill up by cutting prices, so a high occupancy on its own is not impressive. The room rate is where real pricing power shows, and it matters more for profit because a higher price costs the hotel almost nothing extra to deliver.

3. RevPAR (revenue per available room)

What it is. Room revenue divided by every room available, sold or not. It combines occupancy and rate in one number and is the single most-used measure of hotel performance. Formula: occupancy × ARR, or room revenue ÷ room-nights available. A hotel at 75% occupancy and ₹10,000 ARR has RevPAR of ₹7,500; 9,000 available room-nights produce ₹6.75 crore of room revenue.

What drives it. Everything that drives occupancy and rate. Its value is that it cannot be gamed by trading one against the other: cutting price to fill rooms raises occupancy but usually lowers RevPAR. Same-store or like-for-like (LFL) RevPAR compares only hotels open in both periods and is the right measure of underlying growth; reported RevPAR mixes in new hotels, which start with low occupancy and drag it down.

Benchmarks. For growth in this cycle: good ≥10% a year, average 5–10%, weak below 5%. For level, segment-relative, as with ARR. The all-India branded RevPAR was ₹5,400–5,600 in calendar 2025.

4. RGI or RevPAR index (market share)

What it is. A hotel's RevPAR relative to a defined competitive set of similar hotels nearby, usually supplied by STR (CoStar). Formula: own RevPAR ÷ comp-set RevPAR × 100. A hotel earning ₹12,500 RevPAR in a comp set averaging ₹10,000 has an RGI of 125, meaning it earns 25% more per room than its rivals.

What drives it. Brand strength, location within the city, product quality and renovation, distribution and revenue management. RGI above 100 means the hotel is taking more than its fair share of the market's revenue. It is the best single test of whether a brand earns a premium, and the best early warning when a hotel loses ground to a newer competitor.

Benchmarks. Good: above 115. Average: 100–115. Weak: below 100. How the seven compare: EIH reports an RGI of 125 and says 13 of its 15 owned hotels are first or second in their STR comp sets. ITC Hotels says its India portfolio earns 1.3 times industry RevPAR (a comparison against the whole industry, not a comp set, so not the same measure). The Leela claims 1.7–1.8 times the luxury segment. The others do not disclose an RGI. Definition trap: the company chooses its own comp set, and a generous choice of weaker rivals inflates the index; an index against the whole industry (ITC) is not comparable with an index against a luxury comp set (EIH).

5. TRevPAR and the F&B share

What it is. Total revenue per available room: all hotel revenue (rooms, F&B, banqueting, spa, other) divided by room-nights available. It captures what RevPAR misses, which at an Indian full-service hotel is 40–55% of revenue. Formula: total hotel revenue ÷ room-nights available. Our 200-room example with ₹92.9 crore of revenue and 73,000 available room-nights has TRevPAR of about ₹12,700 against RevPAR of ₹7,000.

What drives it. Banqueting and weddings, restaurant reputation, conference space, and the hotel's location (a city hotel with large ballrooms earns far more F&B than an airport hotel). F&B revenue carries lower margins than rooms (food costs about a quarter of F&B revenue, and staffing is heavy), so a rising F&B share can lift TRevPAR while diluting margin.

Benchmarks. Rooms share of revenue: full-service luxury 45–55%; upscale business 55–70%; select-service and midscale 75–85%. How the seven compare (our calculations from FY26 or Q1 FY27 data): ITC Hotels' rooms were 47.9% of Q1 FY27 revenue excluding residences, and its owned-hotel TRevPAR was roughly ₹16,400 against RevPAR of ₹8,380. Chalet's hospitality revenue of ₹1,731 crore over 3,389 rooms implies TRevPAR of about ₹14,000 against FY26 RevPAR of ₹9,226, so rooms are about two-thirds of its hotel revenue. SAMHI's implies about ₹7,000 against RevPAR of ₹5,490, rooms about 80%. Definition trap: few companies report TRevPAR; calculated figures depend on whether fees, residential sales and non-hotel businesses are stripped out.

6. GOP margin, EBITDA margin and flow-through

What they are. Gross operating profit (GOP) is hotel revenue less the hotel's direct operating costs, before operator fees, property costs and corporate overhead; it is the measure on which incentive fees are paid. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation at the company level. Flow-through is the share of incremental revenue that becomes incremental EBITDA. Formulas: GOP ÷ revenue; EBITDA ÷ revenue; ΔEBITDA ÷ Δrevenue.

Worked example. Our 200-room hotel with ₹92.9 crore revenue and ₹44.6 crore GOP has a 48% GOP margin. If next year revenue rises ₹9.3 crore (10%) and EBITDA rises ₹6.0 crore, flow-through is 65%.

What drives them. Rate versus occupancy (rate flows through at 80–90%, occupancy at 50–65%), the F&B share (lower margin), staffing ratios (luxury hotels employ 1–1.5 staff per room; midscale 0.4–0.6), energy costs (5–8% of revenue), distribution costs (OTA commissions of 15–20% on the bookings they bring), operator fees for owners, and the consolidation of lower-margin businesses such as catering. Margins also swing heavily with season (Part 03).

Benchmarks. Owned hotel EBITDA margin: good ≥42%, average 32–42%, weak below 32%. Consolidated diversified groups: good ≥33%, average 25–33%, weak below 25%. Flow-through on rate-led growth: good above 70%; on occupancy-led growth, 50–60% is normal.

7. EBITDA per key

What it is. Hotel EBITDA divided by rooms, measuring how much profit each room generates a year. For owners use owned keys; for brands, fee EBITDA per managed key is the equivalent. Formula: owner EBITDA ÷ owned keys. The 200-room example earning ₹41.8 crore makes about ₹21 lakh per key.

What drives it. RevPAR, the F&B contribution, margin and operator fees. It is the bridge between operating performance and valuation: dividing the price paid for a hotel by its EBITDA per key gives the multiple paid per room. Benchmarks: good above ₹20 lakh, average ₹10–20 lakh, weak below ₹10 lakh, for upscale and luxury owners. Midscale hotels earn less per key but cost far less to build, so judge them against capex per key (KPI 10).

8. Fee income share and fee per managed key

What they are. The share of a company's EBITDA that comes from management and franchise fees, and the fee revenue earned per managed or franchised room. 9. Pipeline and openings

What it is. Signed hotels not yet open, usually in keys or hotels, as a share of the operating base; and the rate at which signings convert to openings. Formula: pipeline keys ÷ operating keys. ITC Hotels has 7,962 pipeline keys against 14,372 operating, about 55%.

What drives it. Brand appeal to owners, the strength of the development team, and the cycle: signings surge when rates are high. The pipeline is a forecast of fee growth for brands and of capex for owners. Its quality matters as much as its size: conversions of existing hotels open within months; greenfield projects take three to five years and some never open. Benchmarks: good above 60% of operating base, average 30–60%, weak below 30%; a conversion rate (openings ÷ signings over three years) below a third is a warning.

11. ROCE (return on capital employed)

What it is. Operating profit after depreciation (EBIT) divided by the capital tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + net debt), ideally excluding capital work in progress (CWIP) so that hotels under construction do not depress the ratio. In the worked example, EBIT of ₹30.6 crore on ₹375 crore of capital is 8.2%.

What drives it. The age and cost basis of the assets (old hotels on cheap land earn high ROCE), the fee share (fees need no capital), margins, and the stage of the capex cycle. New hotels and acquisitions at full prices depress ROCE for years. Benchmarks: good above 18%, average 10–18%, weak below 10%.

12. Net debt to EBITDA, lease-adjusted

Definition traps, collected

  • Revenue is not always revenue from operations. IHCL's headline FY26 "₹9,971 crore" is total income; revenue from operations is ₹9,689 crore. Its Q1 FY27 "₹2,419 crore" compares with ₹2,339 crore from operations. EIH's ₹3,106 crore is total income against ₹2,940 crore from operations. Lemon Tree's ₹1,453 crore against ₹1,444 crore. Use revenue from operations for comparisons.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. IHCL ₹3,477 crore (34.9%) vs ₹3,196 crore (33.0%); EIH ~₹1,190 crore vs ₹1,024 crore. Chalet and ITC Hotels also headline margins excluding residential or real-estate sales.
  • PAT with or without minorities, and one-offs. IHCL ₹2,247 crore total vs ₹2,084 crore attributable. Lemon Tree ₹288 crore total vs about ₹227 crore attributable (estimate: EPS ₹2.87 × ~79 crore shares), because Warburg owns 41.09% of Fleur. SAMHI's ₹567 crore includes deferred-tax recognition and impairment reversals (a −112% tax rate). Chalet's ₹645 crore includes residential sales. The Leela's jump is mostly lower interest.
  • RevPAR and ARR scope. Every company defines its own portfolio; growth rates are more comparable than levels.
  • Keys are not owned keys. Portfolio keys include the pipeline; operating keys include managed and franchised rooms. Use owned keys for EV per key and operating keys for brand scale.
  • Net debt with or without leases and cash. Screener borrowings include lease liabilities and its "investments" may miss cash. Use company net debt in prose and state both in tables.
  • Targets reported as facts. ITC Hotels' 15,000 managed keys is a 2031 target, not today's 8,600-plus; IHCL's 700 hotels and ₹15,000 crore revenue are 2030 targets.
  • Seasonality and periods. Compare a quarter with the same quarter a year earlier; Q1 and Q2 are troughs. Watch for figures quoted for one quarter but labelled as a year (our Leela primer did this).
  • Base effects. Q1 FY27 growth is measured against a quarter weakened by Operation Sindoor; Chalet's Q1 FY27 consolidated revenue fell 43% because of apartment handovers a year earlier, not hotel weakness.
  • Reported versus comparable after GST 2.0. SAMHI's reported EBITDA fell while comparable EBITDA rose 12.1%; other midscale owners may show similar gaps.
In plain terms

Hotel companies are not trying to mislead, but they each count things their own way. One includes interest earned on its cash in "revenue", another leaves out apartment sales from its margin, a third counts hotels that are not yet built in its room total. Before comparing any two numbers, check that they are measured the same way. When in doubt, use revenue from operations, operating profit without other income, and growth rates rather than levels.

How to read a hotel company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the season and the base (1 minute). Which quarter is it, and what happened in the same quarter last year? Q1 and Q2 are troughs; Q1 FY26 was hit by Operation Sindoor. Never compare a quarter with the previous one.
  2. Pull same-store RevPAR growth and split it (2 minutes). How much came from occupancy and how much from rate? Rate-led growth is better for profit and signals pricing power; occupancy-led growth with flat rates is a late-cycle sign. Red flag: RevPAR growth below the industry's (HVS) for two quarters running.
  3. Check revenue from operations, not total income (1 minute). Strip out residential or real-estate sales (Chalet, ITC Hotels) and note any acquisitions that inflate growth.
  4. Read the operating margin and the flow-through (1 minute). Calculate change in EBITDA ÷ change in revenue. Below 50% in a rate-led quarter is a red flag for cost inflation (staff, energy, F&B), as at EIH in Q1 FY27.
  5. For brands, read fee income and its growth (1 minute). Fee growth above 20% (IHCL +26%, ITC +35% in Q1 FY27) means managed openings are converting. Check whether fees are from third parties or from the group's own hotels.
  6. Check openings, signings and the pipeline (1 minute). Signings show brand appeal; openings show conversion. A pipeline that grows while openings stall is a warning.
  7. For owners, check leverage and interest (1 minute). Net debt ÷ trailing EBITDA, interest cost, and any equity raise or asset sale (SAMHI's ₹750 crore authorisation). Red flag: rising net debt with falling occupancy.
  8. Reconcile profit to operations (1 minute). How much of the PAT change came from interest, tax, other income, exceptional items or minorities? The Leela's FY26 and SAMHI's Q4 FY26 are the examples to remember.
  9. Note GST and other one-offs (30 seconds). Midscale owners may report "comparable" figures; use reported numbers for valuation and note the gap.
  10. Read the outlook line (30 seconds). Guidance on the second half, wedding dates, the event calendar, new supply in key cities, and inbound trends. IHCL's commentary is the sector's bellwether.
In plain terms

To read a hotel company's results quickly: compare the quarter with the same quarter last year; check whether room revenue grew because prices rose or because more rooms were filled; make sure apartment sales or interest income are not flattering the numbers; see how much of the extra revenue became profit; check fees and new hotels for brands and debt for owners; and read what management says about the busy season ahead.

The listing and deal wave: capital recycling at the top of the cycle

Seven listings in two years, private-equity exits and entries, and a run of brand acquisitions. Owners are selling into strength; brands are buying reach.

When hotel cash flows are at their best, the people who own hotels want to sell some of them, and the people who run hotels want to buy brands and contracts. India's 2023–2026 deal wave fits that pattern exactly. Private-equity owners listed their portfolios (SAMHI, Ventive, The Leela); conglomerates separated their hotel businesses (ITC Hotels; Lemon Tree's Fleur); developers listed their hotel arms (Brigade Hotel Ventures); and the brand-owners bought smaller Indian chains (IHCL with Clarks and Brij; ITC Hotels with Zuri). For context on how demergers have reshaped India's conglomerates more widely, including ITC's separation of its hotels, see our Diversified Conglomerates report; for hotels as annuity assets inside developers' portfolios, see the Real Estate Developers report.

The Lemon Tree–Fleur scheme, step by step

The most consequential corporate action in the sector is Lemon Tree's composite scheme of arrangement, approved by its board on 9 January 2026. It has three parts. First, two subsidiaries (Carnation Hotels and Hamstede Living) merge into Lemon Tree, and four other wholly owned subsidiaries merge into Fleur Hotels. Second, Lemon Tree demerges its hotel ownership and development business into Fleur; Lemon Tree shareholders receive 20 Fleur shares for every 311 Lemon Tree shares, and Fleur will list on the NSE and BSE about 12–15 months after the restructuring. Third, Warburg Pincus (through Coastal Cedar Investments) buys the 41.09% of Fleur held by the Dutch pension investor APG, and invests up to ₹960 crore of new money in tranches. The CCI approved in May 2026; NCLT approval was pending at the time of writing.

At 24.7 times FY26 EV/EBITDA for the seven, and 19.3 times excluding IHCL, Indian hotel stocks are expensive by global standards. Why the premium

Four reasons are usually given, and they are reasonable up to a point. Growth: Indian RevPAR has been rising 8–14% a year against low single digits in the US, and Indian hotel EBITDA has been compounding in the high teens. Scarcity of supply: Part 04's gap, and the fact that luxury land in Indian metros cannot be added. Under-penetration: two lakh branded rooms for 1.45 billion people gives a long runway for brand signings. Scarcity of listed paper: domestic funds wanting consumer-services exposure have few hotel stocks to choose from, and IHCL is the only large, liquid one, which supports its multiple.

The premium also carries the cyclical risk. Multiples set at the high point of a cycle, on near-record margins, compress when growth slows. A simple illustration (ILLUSTRATIVE, not a forecast): IHCL at 34 times FY26 EBITDA is about 17 times FY31 EBITDA if EBITDA compounds at 15% a year for five years (34 ÷ 2.01). If it compounds at 10%, the FY31 multiple is about 21 times (34 ÷ 1.61); at 5%, about 27 times (34 ÷ 1.28). The market is paying today for something like the first path. The same arithmetic for the six excluding IHCL, at 19.3 times, gives about 9.6 times FY31 EBITDA at 15% growth and 12 times at 10%.

Technology and disruption: who owns the guest

The real technology fight in hotels is over distribution: whether the guest books with the hotel or through an intermediary that takes a cut.

Hotels are not usually disrupted by new technology in the way that retailers or media companies are. A hotel room cannot be downloaded. The disruption is in distribution, pricing and alternative supply: who finds the guest, how the price is set, and whether the guest chooses a hotel at all. Five forces matter.

  • OTA power and direct booking. Online travel agents (MakeMyTrip, Booking.com, Agoda) charge commissions of roughly 15–20% of the room rate (INDICATIVE). They brought 17% of ITC Hotels' owned-hotel bookings in Q1 FY27; at midscale and economy hotels the share is much higher. Loyalty programmes (IHCL's Neu with about 16 million members, Marriott Bonvoy) and brand websites and apps (23% of ITC's bookings) are the defence. Every point of business moved from OTA to direct adds roughly 0.1–0.17 points to room-revenue margin (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Aggregators and alternative stays. OYO, Treebo and FabHotels compete at the economy end with franchise-lite models; Airbnb and villa platforms compete for family and group leisure. IHCL's amã homestays brand is an explicit hedge. Pressure is greatest on midscale leisure hotels, least on luxury and business hotels.
  • Revenue management and AI pricing. Dynamic pricing software, demand forecasting and centralised procurement are margin levers that the larger chains (Lemon Tree, Chalet, IHCL) have invested in; they matter most in an occupancy-led phase, when getting the last rupee of rate out of a full hotel is the job.
  • Energy and climate. Power and water are 5–8% of revenue. ITC Hotels has 24 LEED Platinum and 13 LEED Zero Water hotels and a 52.4 MW renewables target; lower energy cost is a margin advantage, and heat and water stress are rising risks for resorts.
  • Aviation as a single point of failure, and remote work. The December 2025 airline disruption and the 2026 airspace closures showed how dependent hotels are on air capacity. Video-conferencing caps the growth of routine corporate travel, offset so far by the expansion of global capability centres in the IT cities.

The scale effect is clear. So hotel chains spend heavily on loyalty programmes and apps to get guests to book directly. The big chains are better at this than small ones, which is one reason small hotels are joining big brands.

Bull vs bear

The bull case is a supply gap with years left to run and a structural shift to fees. The bear case is record signings, a split cycle and multiples that assume no slowdown.

The bull case

  • Supply cover: demand growing 8–9% against premium supply of 5–6%, with ICRA seeing the gap lasting two to three more years; luxury supply effectively fixed.
  • Growth re-accelerated: Q1 FY27 industry RevPAR +11–13%, IHCL +14%, The Leela +30% in Q2; IHCL guiding to double-digit revenue growth.
  • Fees compound: IHCL fees +26%, ITC +35%; pipelines of 55–96% of operating bases, almost all capital-light; brand consolidation (Clarks, Brij) cheap.
  • Strong balance sheets: IHCL, ITC Hotels and EIH hold net cash; The Leela de-levered; the debt-fuelled bust of FY09–FY14 is unlikely to repeat at the listed leaders.
  • Domestic resilience: more than 80% of room-nights are domestic, and five shocks in eighteen months failed to dent the trend.
  • Long runway: about two lakh branded rooms for 1.45 billion people.

The bear case

  • The next glut is signed: about 64,000 rooms signed in 2025, 4.5 times openings, and a 1.05 lakh-room pipeline to 2029.
  • Late-cycle signs: midscale ARR +2%, Chalet occupancy −1.2 points, FY26 deceleration at IHCL, Q1 FY27 flattered by an easy base.
  • Policy squeeze: GST 2.0 removed input credit for rooms up to ₹7,500, cutting up to ~5 points from midscale owners' margins.
  • Valuation: 24.7× EBITDA for the sector and 34× for IHCL, above global leaders, on near-record margins.
  • Inbound and aviation exposure: foreign arrivals −9% in 2025 and −15–20% since the war; luxury circuits and Q4 most exposed.
  • Supply of stock: Brookfield's Leela sell-down, the Fleur listing and further PE exits; a 31.9% promoter pledge at Chalet.

Weighing the two, the bull case is stronger for the luxury owners and the fee-earning brands, and weaker for the midscale and upscale-select owners. The supply argument favours segments that cannot be built quickly; the fee argument favours companies whose growth does not depend on their own capex; the policy argument currently works against the midscale. The bear case is strongest on valuation: the sector's multiples assume the cycle continues for several more years with little interruption, while the hotel industry's history is that cycles end abruptly when supply arrives and demand stumbles at the same time.

The most useful way to hold both views is segment by segment. The palace and luxury city hotels (Taj, Oberoi, Leela, ITC) have the most durable pricing power but the highest exposure to inbound shocks. The fee businesses (IHCL, ITC, Lemon Tree after the scheme) have the steadiest growth but are already richly valued. The upscale business-hotel owners (Chalet, SAMHI) are the most exposed to new supply in Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune, with leverage on top. The midscale owners carry the GST squeeze. A downturn would hit the last two groups first.

In plain terms

The optimists say the hotel shortage has years to run, the big brands can keep signing hotels without spending much money, and Indian travellers keep the rooms full whatever happens abroad. The pessimists say developers have already signed up to build a record number of hotels, mid-priced hotels have stopped raising prices, a tax change has cut their profits, and share prices already assume everything goes right. Both are partly true; which side wins depends on which part of the sector you own.
