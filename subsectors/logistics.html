00

The one-page view: five businesses, one diesel bill

The listed "logistics solution providers" share a label and a fuel bill. Almost everything else about them, from assets to margins to what drives the share price, is different.

The five companies in this report all move goods for other people, and all of them are paid more when the economy ships more. That is where the resemblance ends. Container Corporation of India (CONCOR) is a state-controlled operator of container trains and inland terminals. Delhivery is the largest third-party parcel and part-truckload network in the country. Blue Dart Express, 75% owned by Germany's DHL Group, runs its own fleet of eight night freighters for time-definite express. BlackBuck sells FASTag toll payments, GPS trackers, loads and loans to small truck owners and owns no trucks at all. TVS Supply Chain Solutions (TVS SCS) runs warehouses and factory-floor logistics for manufacturers, and earns 72% of its revenue outside India.

Together they reported ₹37,383 crore of revenue in FY26 (up 10.1%), ₹4,487 crore of operating profit (a 12.0% margin) and ₹1,923 crore of reported profit after tax. That profit pool barely existed four years ago: in FY22 the five together made ₹92 crore, because Delhivery, BlackBuck and TVS SCS were losing money. Almost all of the improvement since then has come from those losses ending, not from the two old profit-makers. CONCOR's and Blue Dart's profits were flat to down over the same period.

Five forces explain most of what happens to these companies, and the report is organised around them.

  • The modal shift is promised, and road is still winning. The 1,506-km Western Dedicated Freight Corridor (WDFC) to JNPA was formally completed on 8 September 2026. Yet in the first half of FY27, JNPA's container volume rose 17.6% while the boxes it sent by rail rose 0.3%, so rail's share at the port fell to about 12.7% (a press figure, indicative). CONCOR's own share of rail container traffic has slid from about 74% in FY20 to 54.5% in FY26, and the discounts it gives customers rose 27.5% last year.
  • Parcels are a density business, and the question is who owns the density. Delhivery bought Ecom Express in 2025 and now delivers about 322 million express parcels a quarter. The biggest shippers, Meesho, Flipkart and Amazon, run their own networks (Valmo, Ekart, Amazon Transportation Services), and a newly listed rival, Shadowfax, says it holds 28–30% of third-party express.
  • Fuel, wages and the speed of pass-through. Diesel rose 8.6% in ten days in May 2026, and the new Labour Codes landed in the same year. Contracts indexed to fuel recover the cost with a lag of one to two months; the companies that carry the cost in the meantime show it in their margins.
  • Asset intensity versus return on capital. CONCOR earns the highest operating margin and turns its assets over only 1.1 times a year; BlackBuck turns them over 5.5 times. Lease accounting (Ind-AS 116) makes the asset-light companies look far more profitable at the EBITDA line than they are in cash.
  • Formalisation of the trucker economy. Most of India's trucks belong to owners with a handful of vehicles. FASTag, e-way bills and GST have pushed them onto digital rails; BlackBuck monetises that shift, and Delhivery has just received a lending licence to compete for the same customers.

The market's verdict is visible in the price. On 21 October 2026 the five were worth ₹87,094 crore together, 45 times their FY26 profit. CONCOR, the only member with a decade of uninterrupted profits, trades at 26 times; the other four together trade at about 81 times, because investors are paying for a margin path rather than today's earnings. Three of the five (CONCOR, Delhivery and Blue Dart) closed within 1–4% of their 52-week lows that day. The cheap one is cheap because its volume growth has stalled; the expensive ones are expensive because their profits are only just arriving.

In plain terms

Indian logistics is a large, slow-growing pot of money (about ₹24 lakh crore a year in FY24) that is mostly spent on small, unorganised truckers. The five listed companies take about 1.5% of it. Each has found a different corner where scale or a scarce asset gives it pricing power: railway land and trains for CONCOR, parcel density for Delhivery, its own aeroplanes for Blue Dart, the trucker's phone for BlackBuck, and a long contract inside a customer's factory for TVS SCS. The question for each is whether that corner earns a decent return on the money it ties up, and the answer differs a lot from company to company.

Status as of 22 October 2026: none of the five has reported its Q2 FY27 (September quarter) results, so Q1 FY27 (June 2026) is the latest reported quarter for all of them. CONCOR has already filed its Q2 physical volumes: 1.45 million TEU, up just 0.5%, with export-import boxes down 0.07%. BlackBuck is the only member that has announced a Q2 board date (29 October 2026); the others usually report between late October and mid-November, and Delhivery's outer deadline is 14 November. CONCOR's most recent earnings call was its Q4 FY26 call on 26 May 2026; it did not hold a Q1 FY27 call.

01

Five ways to move freight, and where each one earns

A box from a factory in Ludhiana, a ₹400 kurta ordered on a phone and a pallet of auto spares follow very different paths. Each member owns a different stretch of one of them.

Logistics is the business of moving goods and holding them in between. A shipper pays for a chain of steps: picking the goods up (first mile), moving them a long distance (line-haul), sorting or storing them at a node (hub, terminal or warehouse), and delivering them (last mile). Every step can be done by the shipper itself, by an unorganised trucker, or by an organised provider. The five members are organised providers who have chosen very different steps to own.

CONCOR's margin is made in Steps 2 and 3: rail freight revenue less the haulage paid to Indian Railways (a 27.16% rail freight margin in FY26), plus terminal charges. Step 1 is where it loses customers to road.

The other four chains look like this:

  • Express parcel (Delhivery, and Blue Dart's e-commerce arm). Seller pick-up → first-mile centre → automated sort centre (Delhivery has 48 of them, with 73 sorters) → line-haul on about 20,663 mostly leased trucks a day or in aircraft bellies → destination processing centre → one of 4,266 express delivery centres → a rider at the door, who may collect cash on delivery. Returns run the chain backwards. Revenue is parcels × yield per parcel; profit depends on how full every node is.
  • Air express (Blue Dart). Pick-up → hub → a night flight on Blue Dart Aviation's six Boeing 757-200 and two Boeing 737 freighters (over 500 tonnes of payload a night) → metro hub → ground delivery to more than 19,000 PIN codes. The customer pays a premium for a guaranteed next-morning delivery, which is why air yields are several times ground yields.
  • Part-truckload or PTL (Delhivery). Business-to-business freight of 50 kg to 3 tonnes per consignment, consolidated on shared trucks through freight service centres. Revenue is kilograms × realisation per kg (₹11.32 in FY26).
  • Trucker services (BlackBuck). A small fleet owner is signed up through one of 10,000+ physical touchpoints, buys a FASTag and recharges it, fits a GPS tracker, finds loads on the app and may borrow to buy a truck. BlackBuck earns fees, float, subscriptions and interest; it never owns a truck.
  • Contract logistics (TVS SCS). Under a multi-year contract, TVS runs a customer's warehouse, feeds its assembly line, manages its spare parts or forwards its freight, with its own people (16,601 permanent employees) in 435 warehouses covering 25.1 million sq ft. It bills on cost-plus, per-transaction or gain-share terms.

Three patterns run through all five chains. First, value is captured where something is scarce: land next to a railway line, a slot on a night flight, a dense parcel network, a habit on a trucker's phone. Where nothing is scarce, as in plain truck hire, the organised players earn little and the unorganised owner earns less. Second, handoffs cost money and time. Rail is cheaper than road per tonne-kilometre (NCAER puts rail at ₹1.96 and air at ₹72), but every extra lift from truck to train and back adds cost and hours, which is why rail only wins on long hauls. Third, the shipper always has the option to do it in-house. Meesho did exactly that with Valmo and cost Ecom Express more than half its revenue; shipping lines are doing it with inland rail; manufacturers re-tender contract logistics every few years.

In plain terms

Every logistics business is a chain of pick-ups, long hauls, sorting and deliveries. The money sits in the link that is hardest to copy. For CONCOR that is the terminal next to the railway line; for Delhivery it is the sheer number of parcels flowing through the same sorting centres; for Blue Dart it is owning the aircraft; for BlackBuck it is being the app a trucker opens every day; for TVS SCS it is being so embedded in a customer's factory that switching is painful.

02

A ₹24 lakh crore bill, half the size everyone quoted

The first systematic measurement of India's logistics cost reset the debate, and the five listed companies take a very small slice of it.

For a decade, policy speeches said India spent 13–14% of GDP on logistics, against 8–9% in developed countries. The figure came from consultants using different methods: Armstrong & Associates put it at 13% of GDP for 2016, and a CII study at 10.9% of gross value added for 2015. In September 2025 the first systematic estimate, by NCAER for the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT), and later confirmed in a PIB explainer of 27 November 2025, put the figure at 7.97% of GDP in FY24, or ₹24.01 lakh crore, down from 8.84% in FY23 and 8.79% in FY22. As a share of non-services output it was 9.09%. The method combined a survey of more than 3,500 stakeholders with data from MoSPI, the RBI and GSTN.

The new number changes the argument. India's problem is less that logistics is too expensive in total and more that the cost falls unevenly. The same study found that logistics eats about 17% of output value for firms with turnover under ₹5 crore, against about 7.6% for firms above ₹250 crore; that fuel is 42.1% of road-freight cost; and that rail costs ₹1.96 per tonne-km against ₹72 for air. Small firms pay more because they ship in small lots, cannot fill a truck and do not have contracts that index fuel. The fault lines are modal mix (too much road), speed and fragmentation, which are exactly the corners the five members work in.

No one has re-estimated the total for FY26. At nominal GDP growth of about 10–11% a year, the FY26 bill is roughly ₹26–27 lakh crore (an indicative estimate). The five members' FY26 revenue of ₹37,383 crore is therefore about 1.4–1.5% of the national logistics spend. Most of the rest is paid to millions of truck owners, brokers, warehouse landlords, Indian Railways, airlines and shipping lines, and to the in-house logistics arms of manufacturers and marketplaces.

In plain terms

India does not spend an unusually large share of its economy on logistics; it spends it badly. Too much freight goes by truck, small firms pay more than twice the share big firms do, and handoffs between road, rail and port waste time. The listed companies are a small, organised slice of a huge, fragmented market. Their growth comes from taking share from the unorganised part, not from the market itself growing quickly.

03

What fills the network: trade, e-commerce, factories and trucks

Each member rides a different demand engine. In 2026 those engines diverged: parcels and air cargo boomed while rail containers and export trade stalled.

Demand for logistics is derived demand: nobody ships for the sake of shipping. The five members draw on six engines, and knowing which engine powers which company explains most of their recent results.

1. Export-import trade (CONCOR, TVS SCS forwarding)

India's merchandise exports barely moved in FY26, from US$437.7 billion to US$441.8 billion (+0.9%), while imports rose 7.45% to US$775 billion. US tariffs, which reached 50% on many Indian goods in August 2025, hit textiles and marine products in particular; the IEEPA tariffs were struck down by the US Supreme Court on 20 February 2026, a temporary 10% Section 122 tariff ran until 24 July 2026, and since then a 10% Section 301 tariff sits on top of normal duties, under legal challenge. CONCOR's EXIM handling still grew 8% in FY26, with exports +3% (auto parts +17%, buffalo meat +19%, aluminium ingots +22%) and imports +5.8% (auto parts +38%, solar parts +92%, polymers +23%). Then the West Asia war from 28 February 2026 disrupted the Strait of Hormuz until a gradual reopening from late June, stopped CONCOR's own Middle-East container service and pushed some sea freight into the air.

2. E-commerce and quick commerce (Delhivery, Blue Dart, Shadowfax)

Redseer counts 10–11 billion express shipments in FY25, more than half of them e-commerce, and projects 24–29 billion by FY30, about 20% a year. The third-party slice of that pool, the part not delivered by marketplaces' own fleets, barely grew in FY25 (+3%, the year Meesho moved most of its volume to Valmo) and then grew about 24% in FY26, according to an index in Shadowfax's Q1 FY27 presentation. Quick commerce takes some small-ticket parcels away, but also creates line-haul demand to restock dark stores, which Delhivery serves; it deliberately does not run dark stores or 10-minute delivery itself. For the online retailers that generate these parcels, see our e-retail report.

3. Manufacturing, autos and China+1 (TVS SCS, Blue Dart B2B, Delhivery PTL)

Contract logistics follows factory output. TVS SCS's FY26 customers were 32% industrial, 25% automotive, 14% consumer and 12% technology. Its India business grew 12.4% in FY26 and 43.9% in Q1 FY27, with Indian forwarding up 84% in the quarter as more manufacturing moves to India. Blue Dart's fastest-growing vertical in Q1 FY27 was auto, in ground B2B. See our auto components report for that demand engine.

4. Trucks on highways (BlackBuck, Delhivery FTL)

GST 2.0 on 22 September 2025 cut GST on commercial vehicles from 28% to 18%, lowering heavy-truck prices by ₹2.8–4.65 lakh at Tata Motors. That supports truck sales, telematics devices and vehicle loans; BlackBuck had its best-ever quarter for new GPS devices in Q1 FY27. Toll transactions are the best real-time proxy for highway freight: BlackBuck processed 17.84 crore toll transactions in Q1 FY27 (+12%), while the national commercial-vehicle toll pool grew only in the high single digits. Our commercial vehicles report covers the truck cycle.

5. Bulk moving into boxes (CONCOR domestic)

CONCOR's domestic rail business carries 14–15 million tonnes a year: cement in tank containers, food grain, steel, tiles, gunny bales. It is trying to win bulk cement from open wagons and trucks into tank containers (target at least 1 million tonnes in FY27; 500 tank containers in the fleet and 3,000 more approved). The cement and iron & steel reports describe those shippers. Domestic demand was hit in Q4 FY26 when gunny-bale traffic collapsed for want of jute from a neighbouring country and Morbi tile factories shut on a gas shortage during the West Asia crisis.

6. The high-frequency gauges: e-way bills, rail loading, air cargo

E-way bills, which every consignment above ₹50,000 must generate, are the best monthly freight indicator. Press reports citing GSTN show a record 140.6 million in March 2026 (+13%) and about 139 million in August 2026 (+7.7%); growth slowed after the GST 2.0 base effect. Indian Railways loaded 1,670 million tonnes in FY26 (+3.25%) and 833.7 million tonnes in April–September 2026 (+4.1%, press). Air cargo grew 6.2% in FY26 and 11.6% in April–August FY27.

In plain terms

In 2026 the demand picture split in two. Anything linked to Indian consumers spending online, or to domestic factories, grew fast: parcels, domestic air cargo, Indian contract logistics, e-way bills. Anything linked to exports and to the rail network grew slowly or not at all: CONCOR's export-import boxes were flat in the September quarter, and rail freight grew about 4%. That split explains why the parcel and contract companies reported 15–29% revenue growth in Q1 FY27 while CONCOR reported 0.3%.

04

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: five very different cost stacks

A rail operator pays most of its revenue to Indian Railways, a parcel network to truckers and riders, a contract logistics firm to its own staff. The cost stack decides how each one reacts to a fuel spike or a slow quarter.

The quickest way to understand a logistics company is to follow ₹100 of its revenue down the income statement. The five members have almost nothing in common at this level. Their biggest costs are paid to different people (Indian Railways, independent truck owners, aircraft lessors, employees), behave differently when volume falls, and are passed on to customers at different speeds. Reading the five stacks

CONCOR pays more than half of every rupee straight to Indian Railways. In FY26 its standalone rail freight expense was ₹5,011 crore, 55.3% of operating income, and it was flat year on year (−0.2%) even as rail freight income rose 1.9%. That gap is the double-stack saving: on a double-stack train CONCOR pays only 50% haulage for the upper-deck box. Road transport (₹380 crore), handling (₹310 crore) and other terminal charges (₹160 crore) take another 9%. Then comes the most contested line in the sector: the land licence fee (LLF) paid to Indian Railways for the land under its terminals, ₹398.6 crore in FY26 (+6.6%), or 4.4% of standalone operating income and about a fifth of consolidated operating profit. Employees are a small cost: CONCOR has only 1,238 staff and outsources most operations, which makes it a ₹7 crore-of-revenue-per-employee business. Below the operating line it has large other income (₹394 crore) from about ₹4,500 crore of cash, deposits and investments, so its ₹21.5 operating margin becomes a ₹13.7 profit margin.

Delhivery spends ₹71 of every ₹100 on freight, handling and servicing: payments to the owners of leased line-haul trucks, to partner delivery agents (82,768 of them in Q1 FY27), to warehouses and to airlines for belly space. Staff costs are ₹15. What is left, about ₹6 of EBITDA, is less than its depreciation and lease interest; Delhivery's FY26 profit of ₹153 crore (1.5% of revenue) came almost entirely from ₹340 crore of treasury income on its IPO cash. After paying ₹417 crore of actual lease rent, its own "adjusted EBITDA" was 4.4%.

Blue Dart carries a heavier fixed cost: aircraft, hubs and 13,300+ employees. About ₹58 goes on the network (fuel, ATF, ground transport, airport and handling charges, aircraft maintenance), ₹17–18 on staff, ₹9 on other expenses, leaving 15.5% operating profit on screener's basis (16.05% before the Labour Code exceptional, on the company's basis). Depreciation, including aircraft right-of-use, takes ₹8.7, and its profit margin ends near ₹4.

TVS SCS is a people-and-subcontractor business. Employees (₹22.7), freight and forwarding (₹27.3), sub-contracting and casual labour (₹14.1) and pass-through material it buys for customers (₹18.6) take ₹93 of every ₹100. Its 7.1% EBITDA is then consumed by right-of-use depreciation on leased warehouses (₹3.7), other depreciation (₹1.5) and finance cost (₹1.4), leaving an adjusted pre-tax profit of under ₹1.

BlackBuck is the outlier. On net revenue its direct costs are ₹7.4 per ₹100, a 93% contribution margin, because a FASTag recharge or a GPS subscription costs almost nothing to deliver once the platform exists. Its costs are people, technology, marketing and its investment in Superloads, together ₹60, leaving ₹33 of adjusted EBITDA. This is a software cost structure attached to a freight market.

In plain terms

Four of the five members are middlemen with thin slices: they collect ₹100 from a customer and pay ₹78–93 of it onward to railways, truckers, riders, airlines, landlords and staff. A small change in what they can charge, or in what they must pay, moves profit a lot. The fifth, BlackBuck, keeps most of each rupee it collects but collects only a small fee on the trucker's spending. That is why one is judged on margin and the others on volume and pricing.

The corridor is built; where is the cargo? Deep dive

India spent two decades and tens of thousands of crores building a freight railway to its biggest box port. In the first half of FY27 the port's container volume rose 17.6% and its rail volume rose 0.3%.

The rail bull case for Indian logistics has always rested on one project. The Western Dedicated Freight Corridor (WDFC) is a 1,506-km freight-only railway from Dadri, near Delhi, to JNPA, near Mumbai, built for long, heavy, double-stacked container trains that do not have to wait for passenger services. Together with the Eastern corridor (Ludhiana to Sonnagar, fully commissioned by 2024), the network runs to about 2,843 km. Japan's JICA has lent ¥620.8 billion (about ₹37,658 crore) for it. The final 102-km link from New Saphale to JNPA had its trial run on 31 March 2026, and on 8 September 2026 the Prime Minister dedicated the last 326 route-km (costing ₹20,703 crore). The corridor is finished.

The Eastern corridor shows what dedicated track can do. According to the PIB, it cut wagon turnaround from 15–16 days to 2–3 days and transit from more than 60 hours to 35–38 hours. If the Western corridor did the same for containers between the northern hinterland and Mumbai, the economics of rail against road would shift decisively, and CONCOR, which carries about 60% of the rail boxes that move through JNPA, would be the biggest beneficiary.

What management promised

On its Q4 FY26 call on 26 May 2026, CONCOR's chairman guided to FY27 handling volume growth of 9.5% (EXIM +8%, domestic +15%) and an EBITDA margin of 24–25% on the company's standalone basis. He said the share of JNPA's containers moving by rail, the rail coefficient, was 15.12% in FY26 and should reach about 19% in FY27 (possibly 20–21%), and 30–35% in about three years. He also warned that the shift from road to rail "will not happen overnight", and that JNPA serves hinterlands such as Hyderabad, Nagpur and Bengaluru that the corridor does not reach.

What has happened since

The volume data for the first half of FY27 are poor. CONCOR's handling volume rose 4.48% in H1 FY27 (EXIM +4.57%), about half the guided rate. The second quarter was worse: on 21 October CONCOR filed Q2 volumes of 14,48,379 TEU, up 0.53%, with EXIM boxes down 0.07% and domestic boxes up 2.43%. The port itself was booming. JNPA handled 4.66 million TEU in H1 FY27, up 17.6%, including a record 834,697 TEU in September. But the boxes it sent by rail rose only 0.3% to 590,004, and the number of trains fell 5.2% to 3,446. On JNPA's own figures, reported by the trade press, rail's share of its containers fell to about 12.7% from about 14.8% (single-source, indicative).

Four explanations, and how much each one explains

1. Much of the port's growth never wanted a train. The fastest-growing terminal at JNPA in H1 FY27 was NSFT, now run by CMA CGM with JM Baxi, whose volume more than doubled (+123.5%). Part of the port's surge was transshipment and diversion of cargo during the West Asia disruption: boxes that arrive by sea and leave by sea, which never touch the rail network. When the rail-borne numerator stays flat while the denominator jumps, the rail coefficient falls even if nothing has gone wrong inland. This is the most benign explanation, and probably a large part of the story, but it cannot explain why CONCOR's EXIM volume across all its gateways was flat.

2. New services take time to settle. Shippers have built road supply chains over years. Moving to rail means changing warehouse locations, inventory buffers, customs arrangements and truck contracts for the first and last mile. The corridor's final link opened for trials only in March, and formally in September. On this view, the volume will come, with a lag of several quarters.

3. Private operators are taking the increment. CONCOR's rail tonnage grew 2.8% in FY26 while all container rail tonnage grew 5.6%. Private container train operators, many tied to shipping lines or port owners, are adding rakes "month by month", in management's words, and shipping lines are integrating inland (forwarding, customs, first and last mile) and steering volume to operators they hold stakes in. Even if the rail coefficient rises, CONCOR's share of the gain is not guaranteed.

4. Road is still faster door to door. CONCOR's chairman put it plainly: rail point-to-point is still cheaper than road, but "the challenge… is the transit time". A truck leaves the factory gate and reaches the port; a rail box needs a truck to the depot, a wait for a full train, the train, and often a truck at the other end. Direct port delivery and direct port entry, which let boxes skip inland depots, grew strongly; CONCOR's own annual report calls them a "serious threat on modal shift… from rail to road". Better highways have cut the time advantage of short-lead rail. And the diesel price rise of May 2026, which should have helped rail, coincided with this stall, which suggests that cost is not the binding constraint. Time and handoffs are.

In plain terms

The government built a fast freight railway to Mumbai's port, and in its first months of full operation the share of port boxes going by train fell. Some of that is because the port's growth came from ships swapping cargo with other ships, which never needed a train. The rest is because a train journey still involves more trucks, more waiting and more paperwork than a truck going straight from factory to port. The track was the easy part; changing how thousands of shippers organise their supply chains is the hard part.

The price of holding share

CONCOR's financial statements show what it is doing to hold its volume. Handling volume rose 9.6% in FY26, but revenue rose only 2.2%. Three things explain the gap. The average EXIM haul shortened by about 3 km, so each box earned slightly less. The land question that was supposed to be settled

The second big finding in CONCOR's FY26 annual report concerns land. Investors have treated the land licence fee as fixed since the Cabinet's September 2022 decision to lease railway land for cargo activity at 1.5% of market value for up to 35 years. The parcel war after Ecom Deep dive

Delhivery bought its largest rival's network at a fraction of its old value. The real contest now is between the third-party networks and the marketplaces that can deliver their own parcels.

A parcel network is a machine with a high fixed cost and a very low cost for the next parcel. Sort centres, line-haul trucks running on schedule and delivery centres in every district cost roughly the same whether they handle 80% or 100% of their capacity. The company that sends the most parcels through the same nodes earns the most per parcel. That is why Indian parcel logistics has consolidated, and why the companies that generate the most parcels, the marketplaces, have every incentive to build their own networks.

How Ecom Express ended up inside Delhivery

Ecom Express, founded in 2012, was for years one of the three largest third-party e-commerce carriers, with more than a quarter of 3PL business-to-consumer e-commerce shipments in FY24 by its own draft prospectus. About 52% of its revenue came from Meesho. When Meesho moved most of its orders to its own logistics layer, Valmo, Ecom lost more than half its business, shelved its IPO and was sold. Delhivery agreed to buy it in April 2025, the Competition Commission approved the deal on 17 June 2025, and Ecom became a subsidiary on 18 July 2025. Delhivery's FY26 annual report gives the final terms: 99.87% for ₹1,369.6 crore, wholly owned from 10 December 2025 after it bought the last 0.13%. The fair value of Ecom's identifiable net assets was ₹328 crore, so goodwill was ₹1,041.7 crore, 76% of the price. Delhivery also took on ₹118.7 crore of Ecom's borrowings and ₹116.7 crore of lease liabilities. The price was roughly 80% below Ecom's last private valuation of about ₹7,000 crore.

Blue Dart's different choice

Blue Dart has deliberately stayed out of the volume war. About 70% of its revenue is business-to-business and 60% comes from air express; e-commerce is "a growth driver… not a completely focus area", in its CFO's words. Its 403.4 million domestic shipments in FY26 (all products, +7.1%) grew in line with domestic air cargo, but in Q1 FY27 its shipments grew only about 2% while the 3PL express pool kept compounding, so it is ceding share in e-commerce while protecting margins through price. In plain terms

Delivering parcels pays only if the vans and sorting machines are full. Delhivery bought Ecom cheaply to fill its network and is now the biggest outsourced carrier, with Shadowfax a fast-rising second. The danger is that the biggest online sellers keep building their own delivery arms, as Meesho did. The good news for the outsourced carriers is that the rest of online retail has kept growing and kept outsourcing, so the pool they compete for grew about a quarter in FY26.

The scorecard for this force, then: consolidation has given Delhivery scale and, probably, some pricing discipline; the outsourcing pool is growing again; and the biggest risk, in-sourcing by a large customer, has already hit once and could hit again if Flipkart, Amazon or a newly listed Meesho decide to expand their captive arms. Fuel, wages and the speed of pass-through

The West Asia war and the Labour Codes tested every member's contracts in the same year. The winners were not the ones with the lowest costs but the ones that could reprice fastest.

Fuel is about 42% of the cost of moving freight by road, by the NCAER estimate, and labour is the largest cost in every part of logistics that involves sorting, loading or delivering by hand. In 2026 both moved sharply. The West Asia war began on 28 February and disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz until a gradual reopening from late June. The government cut the special additional excise duty on diesel from ₹10 a litre to nil on 26 March 2026, but told oil companies not to change pump prices, so the cut cushioned their losses rather than consumers. Then came four pump-price increases in ten days: Delhi diesel went from ₹87.67 to ₹90.67 on 15 May, ₹91.58 on 19 May, ₹92.49 on 23 May and ₹95.20 on 25 May, a cumulative rise of ₹7.53 a litre or 8.6%, and the first revision in four years.

Labour was the second shock. BlackBuck: a fintech wearing a logistics badge Deep dive

BlackBuck owns no trucks and moves no freight in its core business. It sells payments, devices, data and loans to small truck owners, and it is priced like a platform.

India has millions of trucks and most of them belong to people who own one to five. These owners pay tolls, buy diesel, look for loads, chase payments and borrow to buy vehicles, mostly in cash and through brokers. BlackBuck (listed in November 2024 as Zinka Logistics Solutions and since renamed) started in 2015 as a freight marketplace, the gross-revenue model behind its ₹867 crore of FY21 revenue. It then pivoted to selling services to the trucker, starting with the payment every truck makes on every highway trip: the FASTag toll.

Management said the national toll pool grew only "a very small single digit" in Q1 FY27 and the commercial-vehicle pool "a little bit of a higher single digit", so BlackBuck's 16% was a share gain.

Thin margins, big balance sheets: what EBITDA means in logistics Deep dive

Delhivery has four EBITDAs, CONCOR four margins and TVS SCS a 7% EBITDA that becomes 2.6% once rent is paid. Comparing logistics companies starts with putting them on one basis.

Since April 2019, Indian accounting (Ind-AS 116) has required companies to put almost every lease on the balance sheet. The rent on a leased warehouse, hub, aircraft or truck no longer appears as an operating expense. Instead the company records a right-of-use asset, depreciates it, and records interest on a matching lease liability. Operating cash flow is unchanged, but EBITDA rises by the full amount of the rent. For a business that owns its assets, such as CONCOR, the change is small. For businesses that lease most of what they use, such as TVS SCS, Delhivery and Blue Dart, it is very large.

TVS SCS: from 7% to under 3%

TVS SCS reported adjusted EBITDA of ₹773 crore in FY26 (7.0%; the annual report's reconciliation gives ₹770.7 crore). Below that line sat ₹403.4 crore of right-of-use depreciation and ₹82.9 crore of lease interest, together ₹486 crore. Take them out and the cash EBITDA, the figure comparable with pre-2019 accounts, was roughly ₹287 crore, about 2.6% of revenue (ILLUSTRATIVE, calculated). After other depreciation of ₹167 crore and ₹75 crore of non-lease finance cost, adjusted pre-tax profit was ₹99.3 crore, 0.9% of revenue.

In plain terms

EBITDA is the most quoted and least reliable number in logistics. Accounting rules let companies that rent their warehouses and trucks report the rent below EBITDA, so a company that rents everything looks more profitable than one that owns everything, even if both pay the same cash. The fix is simple: subtract the rent (or all depreciation) and compare what is left. On that basis, CONCOR and BlackBuck make real operating profits, Blue Dart a modest one, and TVS SCS and Delhivery very little.

Who writes the rules: railways, tolls, taxes and labour

Logistics is lightly regulated as a service and heavily shaped by the state as an owner of track, land, highways and tax systems. Each member has one policy lever that matters more than all the others.

No regulator sets parcel prices or contract-logistics fees in India. The state shapes the industry in other ways: it owns the railway track and much of the land under rail terminals, builds and tolls the highways, runs the tax systems that track every consignment, sets fuel taxes and labour law, and owns CONCOR itself. The national frame: Gati Shakti and the National Logistics Policy

Two policies set the direction. PM Gati Shakti (October 2021) is a GIS-based master plan that coordinates infrastructure planning across more than 16 ministries. The National Logistics Policy (17 September 2022) set three goals: cut logistics cost to global benchmarks by 2030, take India into the top 25 of the World Bank's Logistics Performance Index (LPI) by 2030, and build data-driven decision support. Its tools include ULIP (a unified interface that lets companies query government logistics data), the Logistics Data Bank that tracks containers by RFID, and the LEADS ranking of states. India ranked 38th of 139 in the 2023 LPI, up from 44th in 2018, with international shipments 22nd and infrastructure 47th (government and press figures, single-source, indicative); no newer edition has been published. LEADS 2025, released in May 2026, introduced a four-tier scheme in which Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Mizoram and Delhi were "Exemplars" (press, indicative). Following the NCAER study, the logistics-cost goal is less about the total and more about mix and speed.

Railways: the plan versus the numbers

The National Rail Plan aims to raise rail's share of freight to 45% by 2030 and average freight train speed to 50 km/h. The latest official baseline is about 27% (a 2024 parliamentary reply; other estimates range from 18% to 28%), and there is no FY26 figure. The trend is not encouraging: Indian Railways' loading grew 3.25% in FY26, slower than the economy, and containerised rail tonnage (+5.6%) grew more slowly than port boxes (+6.5%). The Gati Shakti Cargo Terminal policy (December 2021) has added private terminals on or near railway land, with 115 of 132 approved terminals commissioned by August 2025, and the newer exclusive container rail terminal policy repurposes railway terminals for domestic containers; about 13 CONCOR locations are notified under it. Both widen access to rail and add competition for CONCOR's nodes. The Dedicated Freight Corridors, the plan's centrepiece, are covered in Section 7.

Roads, tolls and taxes

FASTag has been mandatory since 15 February 2021, which created BlackBuck's core business. NHAI's changes for private cars, such as the ₹3,000 annual pass from August 2025, do not apply to commercial vehicles; a move to satellite-based tolling could. GST 2.0 (22 September 2025) cut GST on commercial vehicles from 28% to 18% and changed the goods-transport-agency options (18% with input credit or 5% without); warehousing and 3PL services stay at 18%. Diesel taxes moved twice in 2026: the special additional excise duty on diesel was cut from ₹10 a litre to nil on 26 March, absorbed by oil companies, and pump prices then rose four times in May.

Labour

The four Labour Codes, operational in FY26, widen the wage base for statutory contributions and extend social security to gig and platform workers. The one-off cost was visible in FY26 (Blue Dart ₹44 crore, TVS SCS ₹14.3 crore); the recurring cost is now in the base and will show in each annual price round. For Delhivery, whose last mile relies on partner agents, the gig-worker provisions are the bigger long-term question.

The state as owner

The government owns 54.8% of CONCOR. Its 2019 decision to sell 30.8% with management control has not progressed, and land certainty, its prerequisite, has not arrived (Section 7). Ownership also shows in CONCOR's 53% dividend payout, its PSU pay structure, and the slower pace at which it can price aggressively against private operators.

In plain terms

The government does not set what a logistics company can charge, but it owns or controls most of what they depend on: the railway track, the land under rail terminals, the highways and their tolls, the fuel taxes and the tax system that tracks every truckload. A single circular from Indian Railways on land or haulage, or from the toll authority on FASTag, can move a member's profits more than a year of good execution.

Four decades of cycles: monopolies, start-ups, shocks and a reset

Indian logistics has been reshaped by three waves: liberalisation of rail containers, venture-funded e-commerce networks, and the formalisation that GST and FASTag forced. Each wave created one of today's members.

The five members were born in different decades and still carry the marks of their origins. Blue Dart was founded in 1983, before India had private airlines, and built its own aircraft because there was no other way to guarantee next-morning delivery. CONCOR was created by the Ministry of Railways in 1988 as the sole container train operator, a monopoly that lasted until 2006. Delhivery (2011), BlackBuck and Shadowfax (2015) were children of the venture-funded e-commerce boom. TVS SCS grew out of a century-old automotive group and bought its way into European contract logistics before listing in 2023.

The cycles that recur

Rail containers follow trade and policy. CONCOR's earnings have been knocked off course by policy more often than by the economy: the FY21 land licence fee shock cut its operating margin by ten points, and haulage-charge decisions by Indian Railways have done the same in earlier decades. Trade shocks matter too: the 2025 US tariffs hit textile and marine exports, and the 2026 West Asia war stopped its Middle-East service. Its revenue has compounded at only 4% a year for a decade, and its share price has fallen 5% a year over five years.

Express parcels follow e-commerce funding and customers' make-or-buy decisions. The 2015–2022 venture boom funded both the marketplaces and the delivery companies; price wars kept parcel networks loss-making for years (Delhivery lost about ₹1,000 crore in each of FY22 and FY23). The defining event of the last cycle was a customer decision, not a demand shock: Meesho's move to Valmo broke Ecom Express. The current phase, consolidation into fewer, profitable 3PLs, began with Delhivery's first profitable year in FY25.

Contract logistics follows manufacturing and Europe. TVS SCS's revenue fell 8% in FY24 as European customers cut volumes, then recovered. Its cycle is driven by auto and industrial production in Europe as much as India.

Trucking follows freight rates, diesel and credit. Small truck owners are squeezed when diesel rises faster than freight rates and when credit tightens; that is the environment BlackBuck's users faced in April 2026.

Reading a logistics company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every logistics business reduces to volume times yield, minus a network that costs roughly the same whether it is full or not. The KPIs below tell you which of those terms is moving, and the traps tell you when a number means something other than it seems.

Logistics companies publish an unusual number of operating statistics: boxes, parcels, kilograms, tonnes, truckers, toll rupees, square feet. They are more useful than the income statement for understanding a quarter, because they show volume and price separately. They are also easy to misread, because each company counts differently. This section starts with the equation that links them, works through each native KPI with a worked example and the listed companies' numbers, builds the profit-and-loss account of one typical terminal, and ends with a checklist for reading results.

Then: EBITDA − lease rent = cash EBITDA; − depreciation − interest + other income − tax = PAT. Management controls yield and, slowly, the fixed network; it does not control fuel, haulage or the economy, and controls volume only at the margin.

Three features of this equation drive everything else. First, yield is a mix number as much as a price number: Delhivery's yield per parcel fell 14% in a year because it bought a network of lighter parcels, not because it cut prices. Second, the fixed part of the network creates operating leverage: once hubs and staff are paid for, each additional unit adds revenue but little cost. Third, variable costs are largely passed through by contract, but with a lag, so short-term margins swing with fuel even when long-term economics do not change.

A worked example in round numbers

Take an ILLUSTRATIVE parcel network that handles 100 crore parcels a year at ₹60 each: revenue ₹6,000 crore. Variable cost (line-haul and last-mile payments) is ₹40 a parcel, ₹4,000 crore. Semi-fixed cost (hubs, staff, technology) is ₹1,500 crore. EBITDA is ₹500 crore, an 8.3% margin. If lease rent is ₹300 crore, cash EBITDA is ₹200 crore (3.3%).

Now push each lever. Volume +10% at the same price and fixed cost: revenue ₹6,600 crore, variable cost ₹4,400 crore, EBITDA ₹700 crore, up 40%. Yield −₹2 (a 3% price cut) at the same volume: revenue ₹5,800 crore, EBITDA ₹300 crore, down 40%. Diesel adds ₹1 a parcel to variable cost and is passed through after a month: in the lag month EBITDA falls by about ₹8 crore, then recovers, while the margin percentage stays slightly lower because both revenue and cost are higher. The lesson: a 3% price cut wipes out the benefit of 10% more volume, which is why consolidation and pricing discipline matter so much in parcels.

In plain terms

A logistics company makes money when its network is full and its prices hold. Because most of its costs are fixed in the short run, a small rise in volume lifts profit a lot, and a small cut in price destroys profit even faster. When you read a result, find out first whether volume or price moved, and then whether costs moved with them or stayed put.

What it is. Running container trains with two layers of boxes, possible on the DFC and on some electrified routes with raised wires. CONCOR ran 6,396 double-stack rakes in FY26 (+1.5% from 6,302). Formula. Double-stack share = double-stack train runs ÷ total train runs; the saving comes from haulage: CONCOR pays 50% haulage for the upper-deck box. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): if a single-stack box costs ₹10,000 in haulage, a train carrying 90 boxes on the lower deck and 90 on the upper pays ₹9 lakh + ₹4.5 lakh = ₹13.5 lakh for 180 boxes, or ₹7,500 a box, a 25% saving per box. If CONCOR passes half the saving to the customer, realisation falls ₹1,250 a box while margin rises ₹1,250. Drivers. DFC availability, terminal capability, balanced flows (the upper box needs cargo too), CONCOR's pricing choice. Benchmark. Good: double-stack runs rising more than 10% a year; average: flat; weak: falling. FY26's +1.5% is weak for a year in which the corridor neared completion. Trap. More double-stacking lowers revenue per TEU; do not read falling realisation as weak pricing without checking it.

In plain terms

Stacking a second layer of containers on a train nearly halves the cost of the extra boxes. CONCOR can keep that saving as margin or hand it to customers as a lower price to win boxes from trucks. In FY26 it did a bit of both, which is why its revenue per box fell while its rail margin rose. Whether it wins the extra volume that would justify the lower price is the corridor question.

7. Service EBITDA, reported EBITDA and adjusted EBITDA

What it is. Three layers of profit (Section 11 adds a fourth). Service EBITDA is a business line's profit before corporate overheads; reported EBITDA is after all operating costs but after Ind-AS 116 (lease rent excluded); adjusted EBITDA, in Delhivery's definition, adds back ESOP and one-offs and deducts actual lease rent. Formula. Adjusted EBITDA = reported EBITDA + ESOP + one-offs − lease rent paid. Worked example: Delhivery FY26: ₹640 crore + ₹86.4 crore + ₹147.8 crore − ₹417.1 crore = ₹457.1 crore (4.4%). In Q1 FY27 the same bridge gave ₹76 crore (2.6%) against a service EBITDA of 13.1%; corporate overheads of about ₹272 crore (9.4% of revenue) explain most of the gap. Drivers. Network utilisation (service EBITDA); overhead discipline and scale (the gap between service and adjusted); lease intensity (the gap between reported and adjusted). Benchmark. For an asset-light express network, group EBITDA after Ind-AS 116: good above 10%, average 5–10%, weak below 5%; Delhivery 6.1%, Shadowfax about 5%. Trap. Never compare one company's service or segment margin with another's group EBITDA; never compare a post-Ind-AS EBITDA with a pre-2019 one.

In plain terms

Ask "EBITDA before or after what?" every time. Before head-office costs, a parcel business can show 14%; after them, 6%; after paying the rent on its hubs, 4%. All three are true. Only the last one is close to the cash the business generates before it reinvests.

Definition traps, collected

  • Handling versus originating TEU. CONCOR's 5.58 million is handling (all boxes through its terminals); trains carry about 65–70% of that.
  • Three CONCOR shares. National rail share (54.5%), share of a port's rail boxes (60% at JNPT) and a port's rail coefficient (15% at JNPT) are different numbers.
  • Four CONCOR margins. 30.9% "operating", 27.2% rail freight, 24.3% standalone EBITDA, 21.5% consolidated operating margin.
  • Four Delhivery EBITDAs. Service 14.4%, ex-integration ~7.5%, reported 6.1%, adjusted 4.4% (FY26).
  • Delhivery PAT. FY26 reported PAT is ₹153 crore; ~₹300 crore only before integration costs; "₹321 crore" figures in circulation are wrong on any reported basis.
  • Gross versus net revenue. BlackBuck's Superloads and TVS SCS's forwarding are booked at gross freight value; use net revenue or segment margins.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. Blue Dart's standalone margin (10.4%) excludes its airline; use consolidated (16.1% pre-exceptional).
  • Blue Dart's 404 million. It is all domestic shipments (403.4 million), not e-commerce shipments.
  • BlackBuck's tax. The big deferred-tax credit was in FY25 (₹247.5 crore); FY26's was ₹7.8 crore; the low tax rate persists for a few years.
  • TVS SCS one-offs and geography. FY26 PAT includes a ~₹180 crore share of JV profit from an InvIT transfer and a ₹105.6 crore exceptional loss; operational PAT was ₹69 crore. FY26 geography was India 28% / abroad 72%.
  • Borrowings include leases. Screener's debt for all five includes lease liabilities, and its net debt ignores bank deposits.
  • Market sizes. The 7.97%-of-GDP logistics cost is for FY24; FY26 figures are estimates. India's largest container port is Mundra (8.47 million TEU), not JNPA.

How to read a logistics company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volume first (1 minute). Pull the native volume (TEU, parcels, kg, operators, new wins) and its year-on-year growth. Compare with guidance and with the market (JNPA, air cargo, e-way bills). Red flag: volume growth below the market's for two quarters.
  2. Yield next (1 minute). Divide revenue by volume. If yield fell, ask whether mix (lighter parcels, double-stacking, shorter leads) or price explains it. Red flag: falling yield with falling margin.
  3. Revenue on the right basis (1 minute). For BlackBuck use net revenue; for TVS SCS split ISCS and GFS; for Delhivery note acquisition effects in the base. Red flag: headline growth driven by a gross-revenue business.
  4. Margin on one basis (1 minute). Use the same EBITDA definition as last year and as peers; note service versus group, standalone versus consolidated. Red flag: management switching to a more flattering definition.
  5. Fuel and wages (1 minute). Check the call for pass-through lags, surcharge changes and labour comments. Red flag: margin falls blamed on "timing" two quarters running.
  6. Below EBITDA (1 minute). Subtract lease depreciation and interest, or use adjusted EBITDA after rent. Red flag: EBITDA up, cash EBITDA down.
  7. One-offs and tax (1 minute). Find exceptional items, JV gains, deferred-tax credits and integration costs; restate PAT without them. Red flag: a recurring "one-off".
  8. Other income (1 minute). Compare treasury income with PAT; at Delhivery it was larger than PAT in FY26. Red flag: PAT growth coming from interest on cash.
  9. Cash and capital (1 minute). Check cash from operations against EBITDA, capex against plan, debtor days and new leases. Red flag: debtor days rising or impairments on receivables climbing (TVS SCS ₹56 crore in FY26).
  10. Guidance and policy (1 minute). Note any change to volume and margin guidance (CONCOR +9.5%, Delhivery 20–30% express growth and 16–18% express margin, TVS SCS mid-teen growth and ISCS 9%) and any policy news (LLF, haulage, FASTag, Labour Codes). In plain terms

Read a logistics result in this order: how much moved, at what price per unit, what it cost, and whether the profit is real cash. Most surprises in this sector come from the second and fourth steps: a price per unit that is falling for reasons management calls "mix", and a profit that depends on lease accounting, interest on cash or a one-off gain.

Technology and disruption: who could take the margin away

The biggest disruptions in Indian logistics have come from customers and regulators, not from new machines. Automation, AI and cleaner trucks matter, but mostly as ways to defend margins.

The defining disruption of the last five years was not a technology but a decision: Meesho choosing to build Valmo. It is worth ranking the threats by how much margin they could remove from the five members, rather than by how novel they are.

1. Captive logistics by marketplaces

The largest single risk for the parcel networks. Valmo went from 2% to 65% of Meesho's orders in about two and a half years and broke Ecom Express. Ekart and Amazon's network already handle most of their parents' parcels, and Ekart sells capacity to others. A newly listed Meesho, or a decision by another large platform to insource, could remove a large customer from Delhivery or Shadowfax in a few quarters. Delhivery argues that the trend may be reversing and the 3PL pool's 24% growth in FY26 supports that, but this is the risk that cannot be managed from inside the network.

2. Quick commerce

Ten-minute delivery from dark stores substitutes for some express parcels (electronics accessories, beauty, grocery). It also creates demand: dark stores need restocking by truck, which Delhivery serves through PTL and FTL. Delhivery has chosen not to run dark stores or the last mile ("not a super high margin industry"); Shadowfax has embraced hyperlocal (49 million orders in Q1 FY27), and the market has rewarded it with a rerating. Whether quick commerce takes share from express or adds a separate pool is not yet clear.

3. Automation and AI

Automation is a margin defence, especially with labour short and the Labour Codes raising wage costs. Delhivery has 48 automated sort centres and 73 sorters, with a rated sort capacity of 10.4 million shipments a day at March 2026, and its own mapping system. TVS SCS runs control towers and warehouse robotics. BlackBuck uses AI to match loads and price Superloads, and cut its headcount 7% while revenue grew 53%. The risk for the incumbents is that automation lowers the scale needed to compete, which would help challengers.

4. Cleaner trucks and fuels

CONCOR runs 230 LNG trucks and five electric reach-stackers. Delhivery's partner fleet had about 3,000 CNG and electric vehicles at the end of FY26, up from about 1,600, and Blue Dart has 599 electric vehicles in its delivery fleet. Electric two- and three-wheelers already lower last-mile costs; electric heavy trucks are still nascent. The direct effect on margins is small for now, but lower-carbon options matter to the multinational customers of Blue Dart and TVS SCS.

5. Rail productivity

Double-stacking on the DFC and longer trains could cut rail cost per TEU by 20–30% (an indicative estimate). That would be the single biggest technology gain for CONCOR, but only if the first and last mile shrink too; its chairman says "we have to minimise first mile, last mile". The gain may be passed to customers to win volume from road rather than kept as margin.

6. Payments and tolling technology

NHAI is piloting satellite-based (GNSS) tolling, and RBI and NPCI rules govern FASTag issuers' economics. A shift that bypasses FASTag issuers, or that compresses issuer fees, would hit BlackBuck's core directly; tolling is more than 60% of its revenue. This is the tail risk in BlackBuck's valuation.

7. Data platforms, cyber and weather

Government platforms such as ULIP and NETC concentrate logistics data; outages or breaches would ripple through every member. And weather is a recurring operating risk: Delhivery cited weather disruption in Q1 and early Q2 FY27.

In plain terms

The machines matter less than the customers. A parcel network can buy the best sorting robots and still lose a third of its volume if its biggest customer decides to deliver its own parcels. The technology risks worth watching are the ones that change who controls the volume (marketplaces' own fleets, quick commerce) or who controls the payment (satellite tolling), not the ones that make a warehouse more efficient.

Bull vs bear: what has to go right, and what could go wrong

The bull case is that India's freight is formalising and the listed leaders will take the margin that scale brings. The bear case is that the volume is real but the profit keeps leaking to customers, competitors and the state.

The two cases below are for the sub-sector as a whole; each member's own case is in its primer. They are framed around the five forces, because those forces will decide which case wins.

The bull case

  • Volume tailwinds are real: express parcels are growing about 20% a year, the outsourced pool grew 24% in FY26, domestic air cargo grows 7%, Indian contract logistics grew 44% in Q1 FY27 at TVS SCS, and e-way bills are at record highs.
  • Consolidation brings pricing discipline: Delhivery and Shadowfax together carry about three-quarters of outsourced express (indicative), and both say yields will hold.
  • The corridor is finished: once shippers rebuild supply chains around it, JNPA's rail coefficient could move from 15% towards 30%, worth about ₹1,050 crore of revenue to CONCOR (illustrative).
  • Operating leverage is visible: Delhivery's express margin rose from 15% to 19% in a year as volume filled the network; BlackBuck grew revenue 53% with fewer staff; TVS SCS's new contracts are accretive.
  • Balance sheets are strong: CONCOR, Delhivery and BlackBuck hold large net cash; only TVS SCS carries meaningful financial debt.
  • Formalisation: FASTag, GST, e-way bills and GST 2.0's truck tax cut keep pushing small truckers and shippers onto organised platforms.

The bear case

  • The modal shift may not come: JNPA's rail share fell in H1 FY27 despite the corridor; CONCOR is buying volume with 27.5% higher rebates and still losing share.
  • Land is unresolved: CONCOR's land licence fee sits under a 6%-of-value regime with no agreements and an auditors' Emphasis of Matter; disputed demands are part of ₹2,345 crore of contingent liabilities.
  • Customers can insource: Valmo showed how fast a marketplace can take its volume in-house; the next large insourcing decision could reverse the 3PL recovery.
  • Profits are thin and partly not operational: Delhivery's profit is smaller than its treasury income; TVS SCS's FY26 profit relied on a one-off; BlackBuck's tax rate is temporarily low; lease accounting flatters EBITDA everywhere.
  • Costs are rising: diesel, the Labour Codes and labour shortages squeeze margins until price rounds catch up; the repo rate is back up to 5.50%.
  • Valuation: 45 times combined earnings, 81 times excluding CONCOR, against 13–20 times for global peers.

Where the balance lies today: the volume part of the bull case is happening in parcels, air and Indian contract logistics, and not happening in rail containers. The margin part is unproven everywhere except BlackBuck. On valuation, the market has already moved: three of the five stocks were within 1–4% of their 52-week lows on 21 October 2026, and the combined market value of ₹87,094 crore is well below the primers' summer levels (CONCOR ₹36,300 crore to ₹32,507 crore, Delhivery ₹37,000 crore to ₹29,044 crore, Blue Dart ₹12,000 crore to ₹10,412 crore, TVS SCS ₹5,900 crore to ₹5,047 crore). Foreign investors have sold CONCOR and Delhivery heavily since 2023 and domestic funds have bought.

The swing factors for the next year are specific and checkable: whether CONCOR's EXIM volume and realisation recover in H2 FY27; whether Delhivery's express service margin reaches 16–18% in H2 FY27 as guided; whether Blue Dart's price-led growth holds without further share loss; whether BlackBuck's Superloads losses stay contained as its vehicle-finance arm reaches break-even; and whether TVS SCS's ISCS margin reaches 9% from Q2 FY27.

In plain terms

The optimist sees a country whose freight is moving from millions of small truckers onto a few organised networks, with a new freight railway waiting to be used. The pessimist sees companies whose biggest customers, competitors and landlord (the government) keep taking the profit that scale should produce. The next two or three quarters of margins, more than volumes, will show which view is closer to the truth.
