00

The one-page view: a toll on the unlocked 13%

India sells most of its electricity under long contracts that need no middleman. Traders and exchanges fight over what is left, for a fee measured in paise.

About four-fifths of India's electricity never changes hands in a market. A coal, hydro or solar plant signs a power purchase agreement with a state distribution company (a discom), usually for 25 years, and the power flows under that contract at a tariff set by a regulator or a tender. CERC's March-2026 data put that long-term share at 82.29%. Power trading is the business of handling the remainder: the surplus a generator cannot sell under contract, the shortfall a discom faces on a hot evening, the hydropower Bhutan exports in the monsoon, the solar glut at noon that has nowhere to go.

That remainder is not small in absolute terms. In FY25 India moved 238.35 billion units (BU) through short-term channels, including the Deviation Settlement Mechanism (DSM, the penalty account for plants and discoms that miss their schedules). That was 13.03% of gross generation of 1,829.70 BU, up from 9.6% in FY10. The market value of power sold through traders and exchanges was ₹89,681 crore. But the fee earned for intermediating it is tiny. Exchanges charge up to 2 paise per unit from each side of a trade; traders realised an industry-wide average of 1.9 paise per unit on bilateral trades in FY25 and 1.3 paise in March 2026. One paisa on one billion units is ₹1 crore, so the whole intermediation pool, all traders' margins plus all exchange fees, is probably worth only ₹1,200–1,500 crore a year (INDICATIVE estimate).

Inside that pool, the money has moved decisively from traders to exchanges. In FY10 bilateral trades through traders were 40.5% of short-term volume and exchanges 10.9%. In FY25 traders had 11.0% and exchanges 60.3%. The Indian Energy Exchange (IEX) alone traded 141.1 BU in FY26 on ₹616 crore of revenue and an 84% operating margin. The traders survived by becoming the exchanges' biggest customers: PTC India, the largest trader, did 56.3% of its FY26 volume on exchanges and about 60% in H1 FY27, at a short-term margin of only 0.69 paise in Q2 FY27 (Sep-26).

This report is about the whole industry, the traders, the exchanges and the regulator that caps both, and it uses PTC India as its lens because PTC is the only listed member of this sub-sector. IEX is listed too but belongs to our exchanges report; it appears here as a reference peer. The eight forces below shape everything else in the report.

  1. Disintermediation by exchanges. Exchanges took the short-term market; traders kept volume by routing clients' bids onto those exchanges at sub-one-paisa margins. Volume grows; blended margin falls.
  2. A regulated fee ceiling nobody reaches. CERC caps trading margins at 0–7 paise per unit (2 paise on back-to-back deals without payment security). Competition, not the cap, sets the price: 1.3 paise industry-wide in March 2026.
  3. Market coupling and exchange competition. A single national day-ahead price, drafted by CERC in April 2026 but not yet notified, would end IEX's 98–99% share of day-ahead trading and could revive PXIL and HPX, in which PTC owns 22.6%.
  4. Scarcity, price caps and record peaks. Demand hit 270.8 GW on 21-May-2026 and 269 GW in September; the West Asia gas shock made gas power almost unaffordable; the ₹10 per unit exchange cap now binds on most evenings. Scarcity raises the value traded, not the trader's fee.
  5. Discom finances and the Late Payment Surcharge Rules. Legacy dues fell from ₹1.40 lakh crore (Jun-2022) to ₹3,330 crore (Mar-2026). Good for credit risk; bad for a trader's late-payment surcharge income, which at PTC fell from ₹268 crore in FY25 to ₹104 crore in FY26.
  6. Renewables and storage change what is traded. The real-time market grew 41% in FY26 as solar swamps the middle of the day; batteries will compress the intraday spread but create a new store-and-sell trade.
  7. Cross-border trade as the residual franchise. PTC's government-nominated Bhutan, Nepal and Bangladesh business earns about 7 paise a unit. FY26 was a record 11.3 BU; FY27 has reversed as Bhutan consumes more of its own hydropower.
  8. Ownership, governance and the holding-company discount. The government wants NTPC as PTC's sole promoter, shareholders blocked the article changes that would have given NTPC control, PTC is trying to sell its listed lender, and cash equals about 59% of PTC's market value.

In plain terms

A power trader is a broker and a guarantor rolled into one. It finds a buyer for a generator's spare power, or spare power for a buyer, and stands between them so the generator gets paid even if the discom is slow. It is paid a few paise on every unit for that, out of a unit price of ₹4–10. The revenue line looks huge because the full price of the power passes through its books; the business is in the paise.

Three numbers frame the investment question. First, PTC's FY26 trading margin of ₹310.5 crore came from 92.8 BU at 3.35 paise, and about 84% of it, on ICRA's figures, came from the 38% of volume sold under long-term, medium-term and cross-border contracts that cannot easily be replaced. Second, PTC's standalone profit of ₹397 crore leaned heavily on two sources that are shrinking: late-payment surcharge (₹104 crore net) and interest on cash (₹159 crore). Third, the market values the whole company at ₹4,117 crore against ₹2,431 crore of cash and a PFS stake worth ₹1,080 crore at market, which leaves about ₹600 crore for the trading business itself (ILLUSTRATIVE sum-of-the-parts, section 17). Whether that is cheap depends on how fast the paise erode and what management does with the cash.

The report runs in four movements. Sections 1–9 explain the market: how a unit changes hands, how big the pool is, why exchanges won, how CERC regulates margins, and the scarcity, renewables and discom-finance forces now at work. Sections 10–13 are the analytical core: the earnings stack of a trader, the KPI masterclass, the cross-border franchise and the history. Sections 14–18 compare PTC with its reference peers, profile it, and work through the holding-company and governance puzzle and the search for new businesses. Sections 19–22 cover the global lens, the bull and bear cases, what to watch, and a glossary.

01

How a unit of power changes hands

There are eight routes from a generator to a consumer, and each pays an intermediary a different fee, from nothing to eight paise.

Electricity cannot be stored cheaply in bulk (yet), so every unit generated must be consumed at the same instant. That physical fact shapes the whole market. A discom needs enough contracted capacity to meet its peak, but its demand swings by the hour, the season and the monsoon. A generator wants to run its plant flat out, but its contracted buyer may not want all of it. The gap between what is contracted and what is actually needed, hour by hour, is the raw material of trading. PTC's managing director describes it directly: there is always a gap between the contracts a utility holds and the quantum it can effectively use, and "those unbalances are the trading opportunities".

The gaps come in three shapes. Diurnal: solar floods the grid from 10 am to 4 pm and then vanishes just as homes switch on lights and air-conditioners, so power is cheap at noon and scarce at 8 pm. Seasonal: Punjab and Haryana peak in the paddy season, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka in summer, the hill states in winter, which lets one state lend power to another and take it back months later (banking). Hydrological: Himalayan rivers, Bhutan's and Nepal's included, produce a surge in the monsoon and a trickle in winter. Each of these creates sellers and buyers at different moments, and each has a route to market.

The generator captures the price of power; the exchange captures a liquidity toll of up to 2 p per side with almost no capital; the trader captures a capped, competed spread for credit, structuring and access, plus a float on discom payment delays.

What does the trader actually provide? Five things, in roughly descending order of value. Credit intermediation: the generator sells to a trader it trusts rather than to a discom it does not, and the trader carries the receivable. At 30-Jun-2026 PTC's largest debtors were Punjab (about ₹860 crore), Uttar Pradesh (about ₹600 crore), Haryana (about ₹500 crore), Rajasthan (about ₹400 crore) and Tamil Nadu (about ₹300 crore), out of ₹4,469 crore in total. Aggregation and structuring: turning lumpy surpluses into the round-the-clock or peak blocks a discom actually wants, or arranging a swap between a summer-peaking and a winter-peaking state. Exchange access: many open-access consumers and small discoms are not exchange members, so a trader bids for them. Regulatory and scheduling services: open-access approvals, renewable-obligation compliance, certificate trading. Government-to-government execution: the cross-border trades with Bhutan, Nepal and Bangladesh that India's guidelines route through nominated entities.

In plain terms

Most electricity is sold like a long apartment lease: one landlord, one tenant, 25 years, no agent. Trading is the market for the spare rooms and the short stays. The agent who arranged a 25-year lease years ago still collects a good fee on it; the agent arranging tonight's stay competes with an online platform that charges a fraction of a paisa.

The table also explains why a single "trading margin" number misleads. PTC's long-term book earns 7.98 paise because those contracts were signed years ago, many on hydro projects, with the margin written into a 25-year agreement. That book cannot be refilled from conventional power: standard bidding documents for long-term thermal procurement do not allow traders to bid on a generator's behalf. The short-term book earns 0.69 paise because most of it is exchange trading on behalf of clients, where a trader competes with roughly 40 other active licensees and with the client's option to become an exchange member itself. The blended 3.35 paise of FY26 is an average of two very different businesses, and the mix between them is the single most important driver of a trader's earnings.

Exchanges sit in a different economic position. They take no credit risk, because trades clear through the exchange with margin money posted in advance; they need almost no capital; and their fee, 2 paise from each side, is earned on every unit regardless of who wins the auction. That is why IEX earns an 84% operating margin and a 39% return on equity, while PTC, carrying ₹4,000–4,500 crore of receivables and the litigation that comes with them, earns about 8% on its standalone equity.

02

Size of the pool: ₹90,000 crore traded, ₹1,500 crore kept

Short-term electricity has grown faster than generation for fifteen years. The fee pool on top of it is about one-sixtieth of its value.

The short-term market (contracts of less than a year, plus DSM) grew from 65.90 BU in FY10 to 238.35 BU in FY25, a compound rate of 8.9% a year against 5.8% for generation. Its share of gross generation rose from 9.6% to 13.03%. Growth was not smooth. The share sat around 9–10% for a decade of thermal surplus and low prices (FY11–FY21), then jumped to 12.5% in FY22 when the coal crisis sent discoms scrambling for short-term power, and kept rising as renewables made balancing more valuable. Volume in FY20 actually fell, and FY23 grew only 4%, when high exchange prices and the ₹12-then-₹10 caps cut how much cleared.

The value of power traded, and the fee on top

CERC measures the market value of power sold through traders and exchanges (excluding direct bilateral and DSM). It rose from ₹17,622 crore in FY10 to ₹1,00,729 crore in FY24, then fell 11% to ₹89,681 crore in FY25 as prices fell (IEX day-ahead averaged ₹4.47 per unit) and as the 2023 network-access rules reclassified bilateral trades. The split moved in the same direction as volume: traders handled 80% of the value in FY10 and 21% in FY25.

On top of that ₹90,000 crore sits the fee pool, and it is small. IEX's FY26 revenue was ₹616 crore. PTC's trading margin was ₹310.5 crore. The other ~40 active traders, at industry margins of 1–2 paise on bilateral trades and well under a paisa on exchange trades, probably earn a few hundred crore between them, and PXIL and HPX together earn well under ₹150 crore (HPX's revenue was ₹32 crore in H1 FY27). Adding these gives roughly ₹1,200–1,500 crore a year of intermediation revenue for the whole industry (INDICATIVE estimate). That is about 1.5% of the value traded and a rounding error on India's roughly ₹10 lakh crore of annual electricity sales. A sub-sector this small in fee terms can still matter to investors, because its leaders earn high returns (IEX) or sit on large balance sheets (PTC), but nobody should mistake the ₹16,000 crore revenue lines for the size of the business.

In plain terms

Imagine a market where ₹100 of goods changes hands and the brokers and the marketplace together keep about ₹1.50. That is power trading. The marketplace (the exchange) keeps most of the ₹1.50; the brokers (traders) split the rest, and the biggest broker keeps about 35 paise of it.

Prices: the scarcity cycle

Price matters to this market in two ways. Higher prices raise the value traded and the revenue line of every trader that books trades as principal; they do not raise the fee per unit, which is fixed in paise. And very high prices suppress volume, because buyers stop bidding above the cap and because CERC intervenes. IEX's day-ahead average went from ₹5.94 per unit in FY23 (coal crisis) to ₹4.47 in FY25 and ₹3.86 in FY26 (surplus, wet year), then back up to ₹5.70 in Q2 FY27 and ₹7.30 in September 2026, with October at ₹6.66, up 149% on a weak October 2025. Bilateral trader prices sit well above exchange prices because bilateral contracts are mostly round-the-clock, delivered to the state boundary rather than the regional boundary, and contracted weeks ahead: in FY25 trader round-the-clock power averaged ₹7.01 per unit against ₹4.47 on IEX's day-ahead market, and only 4% of trader volume cleared below ₹5, against 79% of IEX day-ahead volume.

A second, related trap is double counting: CERC's trader market shares are computed on the volume each licensee transacts bilaterally and on the exchanges. It is the largest single trading member routing volume onto the exchanges, which makes it simultaneously IEX's largest customer and the incumbent most exposed to IEX's success.

03

The exchange ate the bilateral desk Deep dive

In fifteen years traders' share of short-term power fell from 40% to 11%. They survived by becoming the exchanges' best customers, and market coupling is the next test.

The story of Indian power trading since 2008 is one of disintermediation in slow motion. When IEX opened in June 2008 (co-promoted by PTC and the commodity exchange MCX) and PXIL in October 2008, bilateral trading through licensed traders was the short-term market. Traders matched sellers and buyers by phone and fax, negotiated round-the-clock and peak blocks, and earned the 4 paise CERC fixed in 2006, raised to 7 paise for trades above ₹3 per unit in 2010. Exchange volume in FY10 was 7.19 BU, against 26.72 BU through traders.

By FY25 the positions had reversed and then some. Exchanges traded 143.75 BU, twenty times their FY10 volume, while bilateral trades through traders were 26.25 BU, the same as fifteen years earlier. Traders' share of short-term and DSM volume fell from 40.5% to 11.0%. Three things drove the shift.

  1. Price transparency. A day-ahead auction with 96 fifteen-minute blocks publishes a clearing price every day. Once a discom could see that price, it could judge every bilateral quote against it, and the trader's informational edge evaporated.
  2. Counterparty risk solved by clearing. On an exchange, buyers post margin money and the exchange guarantees settlement, so a generator does not need a trader to stand between it and a weak discom. The trader's most valuable service, credit intermediation, became unnecessary for spot trades.
  3. New products fitted to renewables. The real-time market (June 2020), green term-ahead (August 2020) and green day-ahead (October 2021) gave sellers of intermittent solar and wind a place to sell at short notice. Bilateral contracts cannot be negotiated every half hour.

One caveat matters for anyone reading the FY25 number. The General Network Access (GNA) Regulations, effective 1-Oct-2023, changed how bilateral short-term trades are scheduled and classified, and CERC itself warns that bilateral volumes "are not strictly comparable" with earlier years. CERC's monthly bilateral-through-traders volume fell from 4.50 BU in August 2024 to 1.12 BU in September 2024 and stayed at 0.4–1.4 BU a month afterwards. Some of that is a genuine shift to the exchanges' term-ahead contracts, which do the same job as a bilateral deal; some is classification. The direction of travel is not in doubt, but "bilateral trading halved in FY25" overstates it.

Why the traders did not disappear

If exchanges solved price discovery and counterparty risk, why do traders still exist, and why is PTC's volume at a record? Because most market participants do not want to run an exchange desk. A discom's power-purchase cell has a handful of engineers; an industrial open-access consumer has none. They need someone to forecast their requirement, bid 96 blocks a day on the day-ahead market and 48 times a day in the real-time market, post margin money, handle scheduling with load despatch centres and settle the bills. Traders took that job. PTC's FY26 exchange volume was 52,224 MU, up from 29,499 MU in FY23, and the exchange share of its own volume rose from 41.8% to 56.3%. In H1 FY27 it was about 60%.

Inside the exchanges: one giant, two niche players

The exchange market itself is extremely concentrated, but not uniformly. In the collective auctions (day-ahead and real-time) liquidity attracts liquidity: a buyer bids where the sellers are, so the exchange with the most volume discovers the best price and attracts still more. IEX held about 98–99% of day-ahead volume in FY26. In term-ahead and contingency contracts, which are matched continuously like a bilateral deal, the network effect is weaker and the three exchanges compete almost evenly.

Market coupling: the next round

Market coupling means pooling the buy and sell bids from all three exchanges into one auction run by a single operator, discovering one national clearing price, and then letting each exchange settle its own members' trades. Europe has run day-ahead coupling since 2014, with nine exchanges taking turns as operator. India's Power Market Regulations of 2021 created the enabling clause. In a coupled market, liquidity no longer belongs to one exchange, so PXIL and HPX could compete with IEX on fees and service rather than on the depth of their order books. For IEX it is the biggest regulatory risk; for PXIL and HPX it is the only route to scale.

What coupling does to traders is less dramatic than what it does to exchanges. A trader's short-term margin is already below one paisa; coupling will not compress it much further, and cheaper exchange fees (if competition follows) would modestly help traders' clients. The bigger effect for PTC runs through its balance sheet: PTC owns 22.62% of Hindustan Power Exchange (HPX), which it co-promoted with BSE and ICICI Bank. HPX earned ₹18.13 crore of revenue in Q2 FY27 (₹9.89 crore a year earlier) and a profit of roughly ₹4.6 crore in FY26 (INDICATIVE, derived from PTC's share of ₹1.04 crore). Its net worth is only about ₹72 crore. If coupling hands HPX even a few percent of the day-ahead market, the stake becomes visible value. PTC's MD expects "a significant amount of trade to migrate from one dominant exchange to other exchanges", but only once coupling actually happens.

There is a twist. CERC's market rules cap any trading member's shareholding in an exchange at 5%, so PTC cannot trade on HPX while it owns 22.62%. The largest trader in India is barred from routing its 52 BU of exchange volume through the exchange it co-owns. Management says it keeps evaluating whether to sell down to 5% before or after coupling. Selling before would realise little; selling after would let PTC capture the coupling upside in the stake but forgo using HPX in the meantime.

In plain terms

Today a buyer goes to IEX because that is where the sellers are. Coupling would put all the sellers in one room regardless of which exchange's door they came through, so the smaller exchanges could compete on price and service. PTC's interest is as part-owner of one of those small doors, not as a trader, because its own trading fee is already close to the floor.

A broker estimate cited in our exchanges report has IEX's share falling from about 75% around FY28 to 60% by FY30 once coupling is in place, an INDICATIVE scenario that assumes final regulations in FY27 and a phased rollout. The timing has already slipped once, from January 2026 to "dates to be notified". The one live CERC market-design proceeding found this month is different: suo-motu Petition 13/SM/2026, which proposes adding pumped-storage plants to the sellers eligible for the high-price day-ahead market, with a public hearing on 19-Nov-2026.

The deep lesson of this section is that disintermediation has already happened to the part of the trader's book that could be disintermediated. What remains is either too complex to auction (long-term contracts, cross-border deals, banking swaps, round-the-clock renewable bundles) or is already priced at the floor (exchange access). The next threat to traders is not the exchange; it is the slow expiry of the long-term contracts that pay 8 paise, and the absence of any route to sign new ones on the same terms.

04

A seven-paise ceiling nobody reaches

CERC licenses traders, caps their margin and sets the exchanges' rules. The cap has not bound for years; competition has done the work.

Power trading is a creation of the Electricity Act 2003. Before it, electricity moved between state electricity boards under government arrangements and PTC, set up by the government in 1999 to buy surplus power and de-risk private generation, was the only trader of note. The Act made trading a separately licensed activity (Sections 12 and 14), gave the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) the power to license inter-state traders and to fix trading margins (Sections 79(1)(e) and 79(1)(j)), and asked regulators to develop a market (Section 66). Everything since has been built on those clauses.

The rulebook, in five instruments

  • Trading Licence Regulations 2020 (effective 31-Jan-2020, replacing the 2009 rules): licence categories, net worth, liquidity, payment security and the trading margin.
  • Power Market Regulations 2021 (February 2021): how exchanges run, which products they may offer, the enabling clause for market coupling, and the 5% cap on a trading member's shareholding in an exchange.
  • Deviation Settlement Mechanism Regulations 2022 (effective 5-Dec-2022): the price for missing a schedule, linked to the higher of day-ahead and real-time exchange prices and capped at ₹10 per unit.
  • General Network Access Regulations (effective 1-Oct-2023): how transmission access is granted, which reclassified bilateral short-term trades and broke the comparability of CERC's series.
  • Cross Border Trade of Electricity Regulations 2019 with the Ministry of Power's 2018 import/export guidelines: who may trade with Bhutan, Nepal, Bangladesh and Myanmar, and how. An August-2024 amendment allowed a dedicated exporter, in practice Adani's Godda plant built to supply Bangladesh, to sell into India if the importing country stops scheduling or delays payment.

The trading margin: what the rule actually says

The margin rule has changed three times. In 2005 traders charged up to about 10 paise and CERC stepped in. From 26-Jan-2006 the margin was fixed at 4 paise per unit. From 11-Jan-2010 it was 7 paise where the sale price exceeded ₹3 per unit and 4 paise where it was ₹3 or less. Since 31-Jan-2020 it has been a single band: not less than zero and not more than 7 paise per unit, with negative margins banned. That 2010 rule, with its ₹3 threshold, is still sometimes quoted as current; it was superseded six years ago.

The 2020 regulations add two tighter caps that matter to traders' economics. Under Regulation 8(f) the margin "cannot exceed 2 paise per unit for back to back contracts". Under the proviso to Regulation 8(d), where the trader does not give the seller an escrow arrangement or an irrevocable revolving letter of credit, the margin is again capped at 2 paise. The payment-security rule in Regulation 9(10) sizes those letters of credit at 1.1 times the average monthly bill for long-term contracts and 1.05 times contract value for short-term ones. For banking (swap) contracts the cumulative margin must stay within 0–7 paise with no negative leg. Long-term margins are as agreed between the parties, which is how PTC's legacy book earns 7.98 paise. CERC's March-2026 monthly report still cites the 0–7 paise band; no amendment since 2020 was found.

The law lets a trader charge up to 7 paise a unit. Nobody does, except on old long-term contracts, because a buyer can see the exchange price every day and has 40 traders to call. The ceiling is like a speed limit on a road that is always jammed: it exists, but traffic sets the speed.

The exchanges' rules, and the rules that bind traders indirectly

Exchanges have their own caps. CERC directed them to cut the day-ahead and real-time bidding ceiling from ₹20 to ₹10 per unit from 4-Apr-2023 (after a ₹12 cap in 2022). A separate high-price day-ahead market, open since 10-Mar-2023 to sellers using imported gas, naphtha, imported coal or batteries, has a ₹0–20 band from 11-Apr-2023. The exchange transaction fee is capped at 2 paise per unit per side; December-2025 press reports of a possible cut to about 1.5 paise (1.25 paise for term-ahead) have not been followed by a draft or order. Three further rules shape traders' economics without mentioning margins:

  • Standard bidding documents bar traders from long-term thermal procurement. In PTC's words, "traders cannot represent any generator", so no new long-term trading PPA for conventional power is possible. PTC says nothing in its long-term book is likely to expire in the next three years, but nothing new of that kind can be added.
  • The 5% shareholding cap. A trading member may not hold more than 5% of a power exchange, which is why PTC does not trade on HPX.
  • The Late Payment Surcharge Rules 2022 cut discoms off from exchanges and short-term markets if they default, which forced repayment of old dues and shrank traders' surcharge income (section 7).
In plain terms

The regulator controls the trader's business from three directions: it caps what traders can charge, it decides what products exchanges may offer and at what maximum price, and through bidding rules it decides which contracts traders may even bid for. The third matters most now, because it is why PTC's best-paying contracts cannot be replaced.

For investors, the regulatory picture has a clear asymmetry. The downside risks are real but slow: tighter classification of back-to-back trades, an exchange-fee cut (which helps traders' clients more than it hurts traders), and market coupling (which hurts IEX and helps HPX). The upside regulatory possibilities, a rule requiring new plants to keep some merchant capacity, which PTC's MD has publicly wished for (80% long-term, 20% merchant), or a relaxation of the bar on traders in long-term procurement, are wishes rather than policy. No such proposal is on CERC's or the ministry's docket as of November 2026.

Scarcity is back: record peaks, a ₹10 cap and a gas shock

After a cool, wet FY26, India hit 270.8 GW of demand in May 2026. Scarcity raises the value of every traded unit and suppresses how many clear.

Demand is the base of everything in this market. India's energy requirement rose 4.7% a year between FY15 and FY25, from 1,069 BU to 1,694 BU, and peak demand rose from 148 GW to 250 GW. FY26 broke the pattern: a cool, wet year with energy demand up only 0.8% and peak demand of 245.4 GW, the first annual fall in the series. Generation rose 0.6% to 1,840.11 BU, of which renewables were 477.78 BU (26.0%) and solar 174.76 BU.

FY27 snapped back. Peak demand set an all-time record of 270.8 GW on 21-May-2026 and a September record of 269.06 GW on 10-Sep-2026, against 229.15 GW in September 2025. Generation grew 9.38% in Q1 FY27 to 523 BU and 10.64% over H1 FY27, according to PTC. Consumption in Q2 FY27 rose 11.5% to 502 BU, and October 2026 consumption was 159.8 BU, up 21.9% on a weak base. The generation side of this story is covered in our power-generation and integrated-power reports. Management expects 4–6% a year through the cycle, and the draft National Electricity Policy 2026 targets 2,000 kWh per head by 2030 and more than 4,000 kWh by 2047, against 1,395 kWh in FY24.

The West Asia shock

The war in West Asia from 28-Feb-2026, with the Strait of Hormuz closed until a ceasefire on 7-Apr-2026, hit India's power market through gas (our gas-transmission report covers the supply side). Imported LNG became so expensive that, in PTC's MD's words on 3-Nov-2026, gas power cost "almost ₹30 per unit" to run round the clock. About 25 GW of gas capacity sat mostly idle (the Power Secretary, September 2026); gas generation in 1–19 March 2026 was about 1.0 TWh against a target of about 2 TWh; and about 10 GW of coal-plant maintenance was deferred to keep supply up. The high-price day-ahead market, created precisely for expensive imported-fuel plants, shows the result: in March 2026 it cleared 0.41 MU against 2,492.65 MU of sell bids and only 0.71 MU of buy bids. Sellers needed ₹12–20; buyers would not pay it. The same war pushed up raw-material costs and delayed renewable projects; PTC said one supply contract tied to a renewable project due before June 2026 slipped, though contractual safeguards protected it commercially.

In plain terms

When demand outruns cheap supply, the next unit has to come from a plant that burns expensive fuel. In 2026 that plant was a gas turbine running on imported LNG at around ₹30 a unit. With exchange prices capped at ₹10, that power never sells, so the evening shortage shows up as unmet bids rather than as higher prices.

What scarcity does to an exchange and a trader

Scarcity in 2026 has shown up in prices first. IEX's day-ahead price averaged ₹7.30 in September and ₹6.66 in October 2026, and the ₹10 cap binds on most evenings. Volume behaves differently by product. In October 2026 IEX traded 13,092 MU in total, up 16.5%: real-time volume rose 23.7% to 5,670 MU, term-ahead and contingency rose 138.8% to 2,175 MU as buyers locked in supply ahead of time, while day-ahead volume fell 3.8% to 4,508 MU and green-market volume fell 30% to 738 MU. High prices push buyers out of the day-ahead auction and into contracts they can arrange earlier or adjust later.

Solar noon, evening peak: what renewables do to the trade

Intermittent supply moved trading from monthly blocks to half-hourly auctions. Storage will move it again.

Renewables generated 26% of India's electricity in FY26, and solar alone 174.76 BU. Solar's daily shape, zero at night, a flood at midday, a cliff at sunset, is the single biggest change in what the market trades. A decade ago the valuable product was a month-long round-the-clock block for a summer-peaking state. Today the most valuable product is power at 7–10 pm, and the most volatile is power in any given half hour when a cloud bank crosses Rajasthan.

The real-time market is where that volatility is traded. Launched on 1-Jun-2020 with 48 half-hourly auctions a day, it handled 24.2 BU in FY23, 38.9 BU in FY25 and 54.9 BU in FY26, up 41%, the fastest-growing product on any Indian exchange. It set a record of 315 MU in a single day on 26-Sep-2026, and in October 2026 it was 43% of IEX's electricity volume. PTC's CFO describes the intraday price range as running from "less than ₹1 to the regulatory ceiling of ₹10".

Where the trader fits in a renewable grid

Renewables create three kinds of trading business. The first is balancing: a solar developer without a buyer for its evening shortfall, or a discom with too much midday power, needs someone to buy and sell around its schedule. That is mostly exchange access, priced at the floor. The second is aggregation: about 44.8 GW of awarded renewable capacity had no signed power sale agreement in April 2026, because discoms hesitated to sign 25-year contracts for daytime-only power. Turning solar, wind and storage into a firm round-the-clock product a discom will buy is skilled structuring and pays better. The third is green supply to industry under the Green Energy Open Access Rules 2022, which cut the open-access threshold to 100 kW and waived some transmission charges. PTC has 500–600 large commercial and industrial clients, sourced 74 MW of green power for the refiner MRPL in Q2 FY27, supplies 10 MW of green power for GAIL's green-hydrogen unit at Vijaipur, and calls C&I and captive plants "the new growth driver".

The large back-to-back renewable resellers, the "intermediary procurers" SECI, NTPC, NHPC and SJVN that buy under 25-year PPAs and resell to discoms, are traders in substance but are not counted in CERC's short-term trader shares. A 2021 CERC proceeding shows CERC applying the 2-paise back-to-back cap to an NTPC renewable resale, which suggests even the biggest renewable intermediation earns paise, not the 7 paise sometimes assumed. The ministry made SECI the sole implementing agency for future central renewable bids, according to PTC's annual report, which narrows that channel further for others.

In plain terms

Solar turned electricity from a product sold by the month into one sold by the half hour. That helped exchanges, which run 48 auctions a day, and hurt traders, who are slow by comparison. Traders now earn their best money by packaging intermittent power into the steady supply that discoms and factories still want.

Storage: compressing the spread, creating a new trade

Batteries attack the intraday spread directly. A battery charged at ₹2.50 at noon and discharged at ₹9 at 8 pm earns the difference, less losses. As more storage arrives, noon prices rise and evening prices fall, and the spread narrows. India had less than 20 GWh of storage installed in late 2026 against roughly 272 GWh tendered, so the compression is still ahead. PTC expects storage additions over 12–18 months to temper the spikes.

A worked example shows the economics (ILLUSTRATIVE, round numbers). A 1 MWh battery buys 1.18 MWh at noon at ₹2.50 a unit (₹2,950), loses 15% in the round trip, and sells 1 MWh at 8 pm at ₹9 (₹9,000). The gross spread is ₹6,050 per cycle, or about ₹22 lakh a year at one cycle a day. Against an installed cost of roughly ₹1.5–2 crore per MWh of storage (INDICATIVE), that is a payback of seven to nine years before operating costs, which is why viability-gap funding and transmission-charge waivers were needed. If the trader merely arranges the sale, it earns its capped paise on 365 MWh a year, a few thousand rupees; if it contracts the storage capacity and takes the spread risk, it earns the spread. That is the shift from fee to principal that PTC's 500 MW / 2,000 MWh storage expression of interest represents.

Discoms pay on time, and the surcharge disappears

The 2022 Late Payment Surcharge Rules cleared ₹1.4 lakh crore of legacy dues. For a trader, that was good credit news and bad income news.

For two decades, state discoms paid their power bills late, and the whole supply chain financed them. Generators, transmission companies and traders carried months of receivables and charged late-payment surcharge, often at 15–18% a year, which they collected eventually, sometimes years later, after litigation. For a trader with a strong balance sheet this was a business in itself: pay the generator on time, wait for the discom, collect the surcharge, keep the difference between what it charged the discom and what (if anything) it owed the generator.

The Electricity (Late Payment Surcharge and Related Matters) Rules, notified on 3-Jun-2022, changed that. All overdue amounts, including surcharge, as of that date were rescheduled into up to 48 monthly instalments. Surcharge on new delays was set at a base rate of SBI's marginal cost of funds plus 5%, rising 0.5 percentage points for each month of delay. And the enforcement mechanism had teeth: a discom that defaults loses access to the exchanges and short-term markets and can have supply regulated. Within four years the legacy pile was almost gone.

Surcharge and rebate: the two lines that fell

A trader earns two kinds of income from payment timing, and they are often confused. Net rebate arises when PTC pays a generator early and earns the prompt-payment rebate, while the discom does not claim its own rebate from PTC; the difference sits inside the sale and purchase accounts and is part of PTC's "operational income". Net surcharge arises when a discom pays late: PTC earns surcharge from the discom and, where the contract is back-to-back, pays part of it on to the generator. In FY26 PTC reclassified gross surcharge income into "other income" and surcharge paid into "finance costs", which is why screener shows a company with ₹1 crore of debt paying ₹94 crore of "interest".

Both lines fell as discoms found liquidity. Net surcharge was ₹267.69 crore in FY25 (₹561.17 crore earned less ₹293.48 crore paid on) and ₹104.47 crore in FY26 (₹181.03 crore less ₹76.56 crore). In H1 FY27 it was ₹28.96 crore against ₹80.26 crore a year earlier. Net rebate fell from ₹120.83 crore in FY25 to ₹97.48 crore in FY26, and from ₹52.11 crore to ₹46.15 crore in the first half; management said discoms had "sufficient liquidity" to take rebates themselves. PTC's MD welcomes the trend: "you bill surcharge only when a utility is not paying on time." He is right about credit quality and right that it is a lower-quality income stream. It is still income that has gone.

Exchanges: one listed giant, two small rivals

IEX is listed with no promoter (foreign investors 12.0% and domestic institutions 31.8% at September 2026) and handles about 84% of all exchange volume and 98–99% of day-ahead trading. PXIL is promoted by NSE and NCDEX; its financials are not public. Concentration, in short, is high at both levels and rising at the trader level. That would normally mean pricing power. It does not here, because the trader's real competitor is not another trader but the option, available to every discom and large consumer, of bidding on the exchange directly. Concentration has given PTC scale and client relationships; it has not given it price.

Six ways to make money from electricity you do not own

An exchange, a national trader, a captive desk, an exchange-access specialist, a renewable reseller and a storage aggregator earn very different things from the same unit.

Every participant in this market earns its money from someone else's electricity, but the economics differ sharply depending on where in the flow it sits and how much risk it takes. Six archetypes cover the field. The exchange earns the most per unit with the least capital; the national trader earns a blended margin propped up by legacy contracts and carries a large receivables book; captive desks earn little themselves because the profit sits with the parent generator; independents live on volume at sub-paisa margins; renewable resellers earn a regulated sliver on 25-year back-to-back contracts; and a storage aggregator, still emerging, takes price risk for a spread measured in rupees rather than paise.

Archetype 2 has a hidden cost structure worth spelling out. What the pure trading business earns

Remove surcharge and treasury income and the trading business earned roughly ₹452.57 crore of operational income less ₹192 crore of opex less ₹8 crore of interest: about ₹250 crore before tax in FY26 (ILLUSTRATIVE; it assumes all opex belongs to trading). That figure is the anchor for any valuation of PTC's operating business, and it is under pressure from both ends: blended margin is falling with mix, and litigation provisions recur.

Three sensitivities follow from the identity (ILLUSTRATIVE): ±0.1 paise on 92.8 BU is ±₹9.3 crore of pre-tax profit; ±1 BU of volume at 3 paise is only ±₹3 crore; and the ₹680.82 crore special dividend paid in August 2026, at roughly 7% deposit rates, removes about ₹48 crore a year of treasury income. That last number is larger than the profit effect of five BU of extra volume. With H1 FY27 standalone profit at ₹183.90 crore (down 23%) and Q3 seasonally the weakest quarter, a plausible FY27 standalone profit is ₹330–370 crore (INDICATIVE estimate), against ₹397 crore in FY26.

In plain terms

Volume is the number everyone watches and the one that matters least. Ten percent more units at today's mix adds a few crore; a tenth of a paisa on the average margin adds nine crore; and handing back ₹681 crore of cash took away about forty-eight crore of interest a year. Mix and cash drive PTC's earnings, not volume.

The contrast: an exchange's stack

Run the same exercise for IEX in FY26 (figures as in our exchanges report). 141.1 BU at 2 paise from each of two sides is ₹564 crore at the cap; with certificate fees, admission and annual fees, revenue was ₹616 crore. Opex of ₹96 crore leaves ₹520 crore of operating profit, an 84% margin. Other income of ₹151 crore, depreciation of ₹23 crore and tax take it to ₹493 crore of net profit. Two differences stand out. First, IEX keeps 80 paise of every rupee of revenue as profit; PTC's "revenue" is not comparable at all, but even its ₹452.57 crore of operational income converts to only ₹250 crore of pre-tax trading profit. Second, IEX's other income is a quarter of revenue too, so both companies lean on treasury income; the difference is that IEX's cash is the residue of a toll business and PTC's is partly the proceeds of an asset sale it has already started to distribute.

The lesson of the deep dive is that a trader's earnings have to be read as layers. The trading layer (BU × paise by segment, less opex) is small and pressured by mix. The payment layer (rebate and surcharge) is shrinking for structural reasons. The treasury layer depends on what management does with the cash. The next section turns each layer into a KPI an investor can track.

11

Reading a power trader: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Sixteen numbers explain a trader, an exchange and the holding company around them. Each has a formula, a benchmark and a trap.

A power trader's accounts are built to mislead the casual reader. Revenue is almost all pass-through, the profit line mixes trading with surcharge and treasury income, consolidated figures include a lender, and the industry's own statistics use four different denominators. The way through is to rebuild the business from its operating drivers. This section starts with the profit equation, works an example in round numbers, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to compute it, a worked example, what moves it, a good-average-weak benchmark, how PTC and its reference peers compare, and the definition trap. It ends with an illustrative unit P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected and a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

An exchange's equation is shorter: volume × fee per side × 2 sides + other fees + treasury − fixed cost. It has no credit, rebate or surcharge line, which is why its earnings are cleaner.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). Take a trader handling 100 BU a year. Forty BU sit in long-term and cross-border contracts at 7.5 paise, earning ₹30 crore; sixty BU are short-term and exchange trades at 1 paisa, earning ₹6 crore. Trading margin is ₹36 crore, a blended 3.6 paise. Add ₹10 crore of net rebate and ₹5 crore of consultancy: operational income ₹51 crore. Add ₹10 crore of net surcharge and ₹17.5 crore of interest on ₹250 crore of cash at 7%. Subtract ₹22 crore of opex and ₹4 crore of provisions. Pre-tax profit is ₹52.5 crore, net profit about ₹39 crore at a 25% tax rate. Now move ten BU from the long-term book to the short-term book: trading margin falls by ₹6.5 crore (10 BU × 6.5 paise), more than the effect of adding twenty BU of new exchange volume. Mix, not volume, is the lever.

In plain terms

A trader's profit is the sum of a few small streams: a fee on each unit (large on old contracts, tiny on new ones), a discount for paying suppliers early, interest on its cash and late fees from slow payers, minus its staff and legal costs. Management chooses which contracts to chase and what to spend; the regulator and the market set the fee and the float.

1. Trading volume, by route (BU)

What it is. The units a trader buys and sells, in billion units, split by route: long-term, medium-term, cross-border, short-term bilateral and exchange. Formula: Σ MU scheduled across all contracts ÷ 1,000. Worked example: PTC's FY26 volume was 92,802 MU = 92.8 BU, of which 52,224 MU on exchanges, 5,744 MU short-term bilateral and 34,834 MU long- and medium-term including cross-border. What moves it: national demand (generation +10.6% in H1 FY27), the hydro year (monsoon quarters are biggest), client wins, and balance-sheet appetite (PTC cut exchange volume 19% in the high-price FY23). Benchmark (INDICATIVE): good above 15% growth, average 5–15%, weak below 5%. Comparison: PTC grew 12% in FY26, 18% in H1 FY27 and 24% in Q2 FY27; IEX's electricity volume grew 17% in FY26 and 12.7% in Q2 FY27; NVVN, Powerpulse and other traders do not disclose volumes outside CERC's shares. Trap: trader volume includes exchange trades, so PTC's 92.8 BU and IEX's 141.1 BU overlap by about 52 BU; and growth from the exchange book adds almost nothing to profit.

2. Blended trading margin (paise per unit)

What it is. The average fee a trader keeps per unit across all its trades. Formula: trading margin ₹ crore ÷ volume BU = paise per unit (by the identity in section 10). Worked example: ₹310.52 crore ÷ 92.802 BU = 3.35 paise (FY26); ₹93.88 crore ÷ 32.463 BU = 2.89 paise (Q2 FY27). What moves it: mix above all (the share of short-term and exchange trades), cross-border hydrology, and competition on new contracts; seasonality too, because monsoon quarters carry more hydro long-term and cross-border volume. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): good above 3.5 paise, average 2–3.5 paise, weak below 2 paise for a national trader; CERC's industry average on bilateral trades was 1.9 paise in FY25 and 1.3 paise in March 2026. Comparison: PTC 3.37 paise (FY25), 3.35 (FY26), 3.35 (Q1 FY27), 2.89 (Q2 FY27); no other trader discloses the figure. Trap: CERC's industry number covers inter-state bilateral trades only, while PTC's blend includes long-term and cross-border books, so "PTC earns 1.8 times the industry" compares different things.

3. The segment margin ladder

What it is. The margin per unit earned in each contract type. Formula: segment margin ₹ ÷ segment volume. Worked example: PTC's CFO gave Q2 FY27 margins of 0.69 paise (short-term), 3.17 paise (medium-term), 6.98 paise (cross-border) and 7.98 paise (long-term). ICRA's FY26 split, 38% of volume earning 84% of margin, implies about 7.5 paise on the long-, medium-term and cross-border book and 0.86 paise on the short-term book (INDICATIVE). What moves it: the long-term rates are contractual and stable; cross-border rates are set in government-to-government arrangements; medium-term rates are set in auctions on the DEEP portal; short-term rates are set daily by competition with ~40 traders and the exchange itself. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): short-term above 1.5 paise is good, 0.7–1.5 average, below 0.7 weak; long-term above 7 paise is the norm for legacy contracts. Comparison: only PTC discloses segment margins; independent traders' all-in margins are believed to sit in the 0.5–1.5 paise range. Trap: the company does not disclose segment volumes every quarter, so any quarterly split of the ₹93.88 crore by segment is a reconciliation, not a reported figure.

In plain terms

PTC runs two shops under one roof. One sells a few long-term contracts at about 7.5 paise each and makes most of the money. The other handles most of the units at less than a paisa and makes very little. Whenever the second shop grows faster than the first, the average margin falls even if total profit from trading holds steady.

4. Exchange share of the trader's own volume

What it is. The proportion of a trader's units executed on power exchanges for clients. Formula: exchange MU ÷ total MU. Worked example: 52,224 ÷ 92,802 = 56.3% (FY26); about 60% in H1 FY27, by management's statement. What moves it: client preference for exchange access, the price level (high prices push clients toward term contracts), and the trader's appetite for working capital. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): 40–60% is typical for a national trader; above 70% signals margin dilution; for an independent exchange-access trader, 90%+ is normal. Comparison: PTC 51.0% (FY22), 41.8% (FY23), 49.9% (FY24), 51.8% (FY25), 56.3% (FY26); peers do not disclose. Trap: a rising exchange share is usually reported as "volume growth" and celebrated; it should be read as falling average margin unless the long-term book grows in step.

For an outside analyst the exchange share is also the easiest early warning of margin pressure, because it is disclosed (or stated on the call) every quarter while segment margins appear only occasionally. A rule of thumb from FY26: every percentage point of PTC volume that moves from the long-term, medium-term and cross-border book to the exchange book costs about 0.06–0.07 paise of blended margin, roughly ₹6 crore a year at FY26 volumes (ILLUSTRATIVE, using the implied 7.5 paise and 0.86 paise book margins). The jump from 56% to about 60% in H1 FY27 is worth around ₹25 crore a year of margin on that arithmetic, which matches the scale of the decline in trading margin per unit seen in Q2 FY27.

5. Operational income, and operational income per BU

What it is. PTC's own headline: trading margin plus consultancy plus net rebate, excluding surcharge. Formula: TM + consultancy + net rebate; per-BU version ÷ volume. Worked example: FY26 ₹310.52 + ₹44.57 + ₹97.48 = ₹452.57 crore; per BU ₹452.57 ÷ 92.8 = ₹4.88 crore. Q2 FY27: ₹93.88 + ₹9.86 + ₹30.16 = ₹133.90 crore, or ₹4.12 crore per BU. What moves it: everything in KPIs 2–3 plus rebate and consultancy. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): above ₹5 crore per BU good, ₹4–5 crore average, below ₹4 crore weak. Comparison: PTC's eight quarters to Q2 FY27 ran between ₹88.66 crore and ₹136.91 crore, with per-BU values falling from ₹5.34 crore (Q3 FY25) to ₹4.12 crore (Q2 FY27). Trap: the ₹718 crore FY25 "operating income" seen in some commentary includes ₹267.69 crore of net surcharge; on the company definition FY25 was ₹450.37 crore.

6. Net rebate

What it is. The prompt-payment rebate a trader earns by paying generators early, net of rebates it grants to discoms that pay it early. Formula: rebate earned from suppliers − rebate allowed to buyers (embedded in sale and purchase accounts). Worked example: FY26 net rebate was ₹97.48 crore, about 0.6% of ₹15,803.65 crore of purchases; FY25 ₹120.83 crore. What moves it: the trader's cash (to pay early), the generator's rebate terms, and discom liquidity: when discoms are flush they take their own rebate and the trader's net share shrinks. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): above 0.7% of purchases good, 0.4–0.7% average, below 0.4% weak. Comparison: PTC quarterly rebate ranged from ₹15.99 crore (Q1 FY27) to ₹30.16 crore (Q2 FY27); H1 FY27 ₹46.15 crore against ₹52.11 crore. Peers do not disclose. Trap: rebate does not appear as a separate P&L line; it can only be derived from company presentations (operational income − trading margin − consultancy).

7. Net surcharge, and surcharge as a share of profit

What it is. Late-payment surcharge earned from discoms less surcharge paid back-to-back to generators. Formula: gross surcharge (in other income) − surcharge expense (in finance cost); dependence ratio = net surcharge ÷ PBT. Worked example: FY26 ₹181.03 − ₹76.56 = ₹104.47 crore, 19.5% of PBT of ₹534.90 crore. In FY25 ₹267.69 crore was 50% of PBT excluding the PEL gain (₹534.70 crore). H1 FY27 ₹28.96 crore was 11.7% of PBT. What moves it: discom payment behaviour, now disciplined by the LPS Rules; court awards on legacy disputes; and the contract terms on how much surcharge passes to the generator. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): below 10% of PBT is healthy (earnings not dependent on defaults), 10–25% average, above 25% a dependence risk. Comparison: PTC's quarterly net surcharge fell from ₹79.36 crore (Q3 FY25) to ₹3.06 crore (Q1 FY27) before a ₹25.90 crore Q2 FY27. Trap: since FY26 gross surcharge sits in "other income" and surcharge paid in "finance costs", so screener shows ₹94 crore of interest for a company with ₹1 crore of debt; working-capital interest was only about ₹7 crore a quarter.

In plain terms

In FY25, half of PTC's underlying profit came from discoms paying late. By the first half of FY27 that share was about one-ninth. The company's credit quality improved enormously; its earnings lost their biggest prop. Investors who extrapolate FY25 profit are extrapolating a world that no longer exists.

Surcharge income is also the most litigated line in a trader's accounts. Large awards arrive when a court or APTEL settles a change-in-law or tariff dispute, and the same order usually obliges the trader to pass most of it to the generator. FY26's exceptional ₹1,492 crore surcharge income, matched by ₹1,491 crore payable, is the extreme case. The analyst's task is to separate the recurring, contract-driven surcharge on late payments (now small) from these episodic, largely pass-through awards, and to treat any quarter where surcharge drives profit growth as low quality.

8. Receivable days and the ageing profile

What it is. How long buyers take to pay, and how much of the book is old or disputed. Formula: receivables ÷ revenue × 365 (average or year-end); ageing = share of gross receivables by bucket. Worked example: gross receivables ₹4,471.56 crore at 31-Mar-2026 on revenue of ₹16,256 crore gives about 100 days on a year-end gross basis; the company and ICRA, measuring on average balances and PTC's own exposure, report 44 days. Ageing: 72% under six months, ₹733 crore (16%) over three years, ₹1,105 crore disputed. What moves it: discom liquidity, disputes on change-in-law and tariff awards, and the share of back-to-back contracts where PTC pays the generator only after collecting. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): under 45 days good, 45–90 average, above 90 weak (company basis). Comparison: PTC 81 days (FY21), 51 (FY25), 44 (FY26); exchanges carry essentially no receivables. Trap: screener's 98 days (standalone FY26) and the company's 44 days are both "right"; always state the basis.

9. Treasury income and the cash pile

What it is. Interest earned on the trader's own cash and deposits. Formula: other income − gross surcharge; yield = treasury income ÷ average cash. Worked example: FY26 other income ₹363.60 crore less gross surcharge ₹181.03 crore = ₹182.57 crore, including ₹159.46 crore of bank-deposit interest (₹17.84 crore in FY25) and ₹8.08 crore of interest on tax refunds. Cash and bank balances were ₹2,879 crore at 31-Mar-2026, ₹2,246.72 crore on 19-Aug-2026 (after the special dividend, per ICRA) and ₹2,431 crore on 30-Sep-2026. What moves it: the cash balance (special dividends, receivable swings) and deposit rates (RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7-Oct-2026). Benchmark: no good or bad level; the question is whether the cash earns more inside the company than in shareholders' hands. Comparison: IEX's other income was ₹151 crore in FY26, a quarter of its revenue. Trap: FY26 treasury income was inflated by a full year of interest on PEL sale proceeds, ₹680.82 crore of which was distributed in August 2026; FY27 treasury income will be lower by roughly ₹40–50 crore (INDICATIVE).

The cash also has a strategic meaning beyond the interest it earns. Management describes part of it as a "war chest" for "something strategic which adds value to our core business, not big ticket", and says a wind portfolio like PEL "will [not] be created again". Investors therefore track three things each quarter: the closing cash figure, any new investment commitment (the mineral exchange, the NLC joint venture, storage), and any change in dividend policy. After the special dividend and the FY26 final, management said an interim dividend "may have to wait for some time".

10. Opex as a share of trading margin

What it is. How much of the trading fee the cost base absorbs. Formula: (employee + other expenses + business-specific costs) ÷ trading margin. Worked example: FY26 (₹67.39 + ₹118.12 + ₹6.39) ÷ ₹310.52 = 62%. What moves it: headcount (109 permanent employees), litigation provisions (₹40.12 crore in FY26; ₹17.4 crore in Q1 FY27), expected credit losses (₹18.82 crore), CSR and advisers' fees; on the revenue side, mix. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): below 40% good, 40–60% average, above 60% weak. Comparison: IEX's opex was about 16% of revenue in FY26; independents run lean but do not disclose. Trap: about ₹60 crore of PTC's "opex" is provisions and credit losses tied to legacy disputes, which are lumpy; a single year's ratio can swing ten points on one court order.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 of trading fees PTC earned in FY26, about ₹62 went on salaries, systems, lawyers and provisions for disputes. An exchange spends about ₹16 of every ₹100 of revenue. The gap is the cost of carrying other people's credit risk.

11. Market share and concentration (trader basis)

What it is. A licensee's share of all volume handled by trading licensees, and the Herfindahl-Hirschman index (sum of squared shares). Formula: licensee MU ÷ all licensees' MU; HHI = Σ share². Worked example: PTC 31.80% (FY25), 31.16% (March 2026); HHI 0.1721 (FY24), 0.1760 (FY25), 0.1777 (March 2026), "moderately concentrated" (0.15–0.25). What moves it: client wins, generator-owned desks selling their own power, and group strategy (Adani's Powerpulse +4 points). Benchmark (INDICATIVE): above 25% leader, 10–25% challenger, below 10% niche. Comparison: Powerpulse 21.19%, NVVN 15.15%, Manikaran 7.00% (March 2026). Trap: the shares include exchange trades, so they measure client flow; they do not tell you who earns the margin, and a rising share can coexist with falling profit.

12. Short-term share of generation and the exchange share of short-term

14. Exchange clearing prices and the exchange fee yield

What it is. The average price at which day-ahead and real-time auctions clear, and, for an exchange, revenue per unit traded. Formula: volume-weighted market-clearing price; fee yield = exchange revenue ÷ volume. Worked example: IEX day-ahead ₹3.86 (FY26), ₹5.70 (Q2 FY27), ₹6.66 (October 2026); IEX fee yield ₹616 crore ÷ 141.1 BU ≈ 4.4 paise. How to read a power trader's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volume and growth (investor deck, first page): compare with all-India generation growth for the quarter. Faster growth is normal; ask where it came from.
  2. Trading margin in ₹ and paise: divide ₹ crore by BU. A falling paise figure with rising volume means mix shift; check whether the ₹ figure fell too.
  3. Segment margins and mix (call transcript): short-term share of volume and the long-term, medium-term and cross-border margins. Any change in long-term rates is significant.
  4. Operational income (deck): trading margin + consultancy + rebate, and per BU. This is the cleanest operating number the company gives.
  5. Net rebate: a proxy for discom liquidity and PTC's early-payment capacity.
  6. Net surcharge (deck footnote or call): compute it as a share of PBT. Red flag: a quarter where surcharge is the main reason profit rose.
  7. Other income (results statement): subtract gross surcharge to get treasury income; check the period-end cash figure management quotes.
  8. Opex, provisions and exceptional items (notes to results): litigation provisions, expected credit losses, pass-through exceptional items. Red flag: an adjudication ordering PTC to pay a generator before collecting from the discom.
  9. Standalone versus consolidated profit: the gap is PFS. Read PFS's own results (released a week earlier) for impairments or reversals.
  10. Contracts, cross-border and governance: new long-term or medium-term contracts, Bhutan and Nepal flows, the PFS sale, the NTPC promoter process and the HPX stake. Red flags: loss or non-renewal of a long-term PPA, a fresh regulatory or governance action, cash committed to a new venture without disclosed returns.

A trader's quarter can be read accurately in ten minutes once these are in hand, because the business has few moving parts. What takes longer is judging the two slow variables behind them: how long the long-term book lasts and what management does with the cash. Sections 16–18 take those up.

Hydropower diplomacy: the cross-border franchise Deep dive

PTC's government-nominated trade with Bhutan, Nepal and Bangladesh earns seven paise a unit. FY26 was a record; FY27 shows how fragile the volume is.

India's electricity trade with its neighbours began as foreign policy. India financed and built Bhutan's first large hydro station, Chukha (336 MW), in the 1980s, and agreed to buy its surplus; Kurichhu (60 MW), Tala (1,020 MW), Mangdechhu (720 MW) and Punatsangchhu-II (1,020 MW, commissioning in 2025–26) followed under the same model. The power earns Bhutan most of its export income and gives India cheap, clean, monsoon-heavy supply in the months its own demand peaks. PTC was nominated to handle this trade from 2003–04, added Nepal in 2006 and Bangladesh as a client in 2012, with exports to Bangladesh starting in 2013. Today India's 2018 cross-border guidelines, CERC's 2019 regulations and the Central Electricity Authority's designated-authority procedures govern who may trade, through which routes and with which countries. The guidelines also restrict imports from countries that have no power-sector agreement with India, a rule widely read as aimed at Chinese-owned plants.

For PTC the franchise matters out of proportion to its volume. Cross-border trades earned about 6.98 paise per unit in Q2 FY27, ten times the short-term book. In FY26 PTC's cross-border volume was 11,314 MU, about 12% of its total volume, and on a 7 paise margin it would account for roughly ₹79 crore, or a quarter of the trading margin (INDICATIVE). Revenue from customers outside India was ₹1,151.59 crore in FY26 (₹1,014.10 crore in FY25), and foreign-exchange earnings on sales ₹980.24 crore.

FY26: the record year

FY26 was PTC's best cross-border year: 11,314 MU, up 37% from 8,262 MU. Three things drove it. Bhutan trade rose to 8,670 MU from 6,178 MU on a wet hydrological year, helped by the 118 MW Nikachu project (350 MU into Assam). Nepal trade more than doubled to 1,048 MU, made up of 457 MU imported under a medium-term agreement with the Nepal Electricity Authority and 591 MU exported to Nepal in its dry season. And Bangladesh was steady at 1,596 MU under a 200 MW long-term contract. PTC also sold 218 MU of Bhutanese power on IEX on Bhutan's behalf. (PTC's annual report gives a second total, 9,718 MU, in its management discussion; that figure excludes Bangladesh.)

FY27: the reversal

The first half of FY27 went the other way. PTC's cross-border import volume in Q1 FY27 was 497 MU against 1,657 MU a year earlier, a 70% fall, and the Q2 press release reported a "significant dip" in Bhutan power "due to higher domestic requirement in Bhutan". Bangladesh exports held at about 410 MU a quarter with a "regular flow of payments". The lost Bhutan volume was the main reason PTC's blended margin fell to 2.89 paise in Q2 FY27 even as total volume rose 24%.

The deeper change is that Bhutan has become a two-way partner. Its own demand is rising, and in the dry winter months, when its run-of-river stations produce little, it now imports from India. PTC's exports to Bhutan rose from 240 MU in FY22 to 1,939 MU in FY26, and PTC has signed to export up to 1,200 MW to Bhutan in winter on short-term terms. The franchise survives, but its seasonal shape is changing from a one-way monsoon import to a swap: India buys in summer, Bhutan buys back in winter.

The exchanges move in

The second structural change is that neighbours now trade on Indian exchanges directly. Nepal has sold and bought on IEX's day-ahead market since 17-Apr-2021, Bhutan since 1-Jan-2022, and both on the real-time market since October 2023, after the CEA's designated-authority procedure was modified in July 2023. In FY25 Nepal sold 1,606 MU on IEX at an average of ₹4.22 and bought 1,365 MU at ₹3.50; Bhutan bought 1,098 MU and sold 240 MU. In March 2026 the neighbours bought 412 MU on IEX's day-ahead market. Every unit that moves through an exchange is a unit that does not earn a nominated trader's 7 paise; at most it earns PTC an agency fee if PTC places the bid.

In plain terms

For two decades PTC was the government's chosen middleman for buying Bhutan's monsoon hydropower, and that role pays far better than ordinary trading. It still does. But Bhutan now needs more of its own power and buys from India in winter, and Nepal and Bhutan can sell on Indian exchanges without a middleman. The franchise is intact; the volume it carries has become less predictable.

Bangladesh, Godda and the payment question

Bangladesh is India's largest export market and the one with the most political and payment risk. PTC's slice is a 200 MW long-term contract, around 1,600 MU a year, and management reports regular payments. NVVN handles the government-to-government tranches. The Adani Godda plant, which exports its whole output to Bangladesh, prompted an August-2024 amendment to India's cross-border guidelines that allows such a dedicated exporter to sell into India if the importing country stops scheduling or delays payment, and Godda was later approved to connect to the Indian grid. For traders, the precedent matters: cross-border flows can reverse quickly when politics or payments change, and the Indian market is the backstop.

What would grow the franchise

Four things could lift cross-border volume again. Punatsangchhu-II's full commissioning adds Bhutanese hydro capacity; Nepal's run-of-river additions continue; a regional market (the Bangladesh-Bhutan-India-Nepal grid, and studies such as the ADB and GE Vernova Mekong multilateral-trade work PTC cites) could create three-way trades; and India's own winter shortages could make swap arrangements with Bhutan more valuable. Against that, Bhutan's domestic demand is growing structurally, Nepal increasingly sells directly on IEX, and Bangladesh's demand for Indian power depends on its own politics and dollar liquidity. Our base case (INDICATIVE) is that PTC's cross-border volume settles below the FY26 record but above the FY24 trough, with a stable margin near 7 paise, and that it is the most volatile component of PTC's profitable book.

The lesson for the sub-sector is that cross-border trade is the clearest example of a trader's franchise that exchanges cannot easily replicate, because it rests on government nomination, long-term relationships and the ability to structure two-way seasonal swaps. It is also the clearest example of how a franchise can be intact and still deliver falling volume when the underlying physical flows change.

Twenty-five years, five eras

A state-created trader, a golden age, the exchange takeover, a decade of new products and price caps, and now a governance reset in a scarce market.

1999–2008: the state creates a trader, then the exchanges. PTC was incorporated in 1999 by the government as a public-private initiative to buy surplus power and de-risk private generation; it made India's first electricity trade in 2001, began importing Bhutan's hydropower in 2003–04 and listed in 2004. The Electricity Act 2003 made trading a licensed activity, and margins drifted up to about 10 paise in 2005, prompting CERC's 4 paise cap in 2006. IEX, co-promoted by PTC and MCX, opened in June 2008; PXIL followed in October 2008.

2008–2014: the trader's golden age. Shortage years kept bilateral prices high and traders held 30–40% of short-term volume. PTC raised ₹1,700 crore through two qualified institutional placements in 2008–09, created PTC India Financial Services and PTC Energy (wind) in 2008, and listed PFS in March 2011. The 2010 margin rule (7 paise above ₹3, 4 paise at or below) suited a high-price market.

2014–2020: surplus and the exchange takeover. Thermal overcapacity and low plant utilisation pushed day-ahead prices to ₹3–4; exchanges grew, traders' realised margins fell to about 3 paise. PTC launched medium-term and renewable trading in 2017, and the IEX stake was sold the same year (about ₹132.54 crore; reports differ on whether PTC or PFS sold it). PFS ran into stress and lost about ₹100 crore in FY18.

2020–2023: new products, a coal crisis and price caps. The real-time market (June 2020), green term-ahead (August 2020) and green day-ahead (October 2021) arrived, along with the 2020 trading licence rules (0–7 paise) and the 2021 market rules. The 2021–22 coal crisis (see our coal report) sent day-ahead prices to the ₹20 ceiling, and CERC imposed ₹12 and then ₹10 caps, creating the high-price market for imported-fuel plants in March 2023. HPX, co-promoted by PTC, began trading in July 2022. In January 2022 three independent directors of PFS resigned alleging governance lapses, triggering a SEBI investigation. The Late Payment Surcharge Rules of June 2022 began clearing ₹1.4 lakh crore of legacy discom dues.

2023–2026: governance aftermath, asset sale and scarcity. The October-2023 network-access rules broke the bilateral series. SEBI's 12-Jun-2024 order barred PTC's then CMD from director roles for six months, with a ₹10 lakh penalty, over the PFS lapses; he left office that day. The Securities Appellate Tribunal quashed the order on 11-Dec-2024, calling the allegations baseless, and PTC's board resolved on 13-Dec-2024 not to re-induct him. PTC sold its roughly 288 MW wind portfolio (PTC Energy) to ONGC Green in FY25, booking ₹457.39 crore at the standalone profit level. Dr Manoj Kumar Jhawar became CMD on 13-May-2025. Then came the coupling order (July 2025), the cool, wet FY26 with prices down to ₹3.86, the West Asia war (February 2026), the ministry's decision to make NTPC sole promoter (January 2026), a postal ballot that blocked the key article changes (March 2026), record peak demand (May 2026), the ₹23 special dividend (August 2026) and the restart of the PFS sale.

PTC's profit has looked steady for five years, but the engine underneath has been swapped twice: first late fees from struggling discoms did the work, then interest on the cash from selling its wind farms. Both are fading. That is why a flat profit history does not mean a stable business.

Who is winning: platforms, groups and the generators' own desks

The exchange wins on returns, Adani wins on share, and PTC wins on cash. Nobody is winning the trader's margin.

Winning in this sub-sector has three dimensions, and different players lead each. On returns, the platform model wins outright: IEX earns a 39% return on equity and converts four-fifths of revenue into operating profit. On share, the group-backed traders win: Adani's Powerpulse added four points in a year, and generator-owned desks (JSW, Greenko, Shree Cement) grew fastest of all by selling their own output. On balance sheet, PTC wins: ₹2,431 crore of cash, almost no debt and an AA (Stable) rating from ICRA on a standalone basis, which is what lets it carry discom receivables and pay generators early. What nobody is winning is the trader's margin, which has fallen industry-wide from 2.9 paise (FY24) to 1.3 paise (March 2026).

The map is a useful corrective to the "cheap stock" reading of PTC. Below book value looks like a bargain until it is set against an 8% standalone return on equity, which is below most estimates of the cost of equity for a mid-cap Indian company. A business that earns less than its cost of capital on its book should trade below book. The lever that would move PTC up and to the right is not volume; it is the denominator. Returning surplus cash, as the ₹23 special dividend partly did, raises ROE mechanically; so would a PFS sale at or near book followed by a distribution.

Why the platform keeps winning, and where it might stop

IEX's advantages compound: the deepest order book discovers the best price, which attracts more volume, which spreads fixed costs further. Its risks are regulatory, not competitive: market coupling and a possible fee cut. If coupling arrives and the fee falls to 1.5 paise, IEX would still be the largest platform but its per-unit take would fall by a quarter. PXIL and HPX would gain share in the coupled day-ahead market, and HPX's owners, PTC among them, would benefit.

Why group desks are gaining

Adani, JSW, Greenko and Shree Cement trade their own power. They do not need a third party's balance sheet because their parents have their own; they do not need a third party's relationships because their buyers are often their own customers. Their growth in trader share is the clearest evidence that traders' services are optional for any seller large enough to run a desk. Powerpulse's 2,500 MW supply contract with Maharashtra shows the other side: a large private group can also offer discoms firm power backed by its own plants, competing with PTC for exactly the structured contracts PTC wants.

Where PTC still wins

PTC leads in three places: scale of client relationships (more than 800 clients, "almost all utilities" as short-term clients), the government-nominated cross-border franchise, and the legacy long-term book of about 7.5 GW (ICRA counts 9,328 MW of long-term, medium-term and cross-border contracts, plus about 1,200 MW of signed purchase agreements not yet tied to buyers). It also has the balance sheet to take on structured renewable and storage contracts that smaller traders cannot. Those advantages protect the share; they have not protected the margin.

In plain terms

The exchange is winning the money, Adani is winning the market share, and PTC is winning on cash and contracts. The competitive question for PTC is whether it can use that cash and its relationships to win the new kinds of contract, storage and green power for industry, before its old contracts run off.

About 85% of PTC's share price is cash and its stake in PFS. The trading business is valued at roughly two to three years of its pre-tax profit. What would close the gap is not better trading; it is selling PFS near book value and handing the money back, or proving that the cash will earn good returns inside the company. Until then, the discount reflects a history of governance trouble and a government that tried, and failed, to take firmer control.

Technology and disruption: what could change the first engine

  • Exchanges and market coupling. Already took the bilateral short-term pool; coupling would shift competition among exchanges to fees and service. Traders survive as members and aggregators at under a paisa.
  • Battery storage. Compresses the intraday spread that makes peak contracts valuable, while creating the store-and-sell model PTC's storage bid targets.
  • Algorithmic bidding and AI forecasting. Lower the cost of forecasting renewable schedules and placing 48 real-time bids a day, eroding the advantage of a large human desk.
  • Electricity futures and contracts for difference. Futures on MCX and NSE (from July 2025, about ₹37 crore a day on MCX in Q1 FY27) and SECI's contract-for-difference pilot let buyers hedge financially rather than lock in physical medium-term contracts through a trader.
  • Direct procurement portals. DEEP e-bidding for short- and medium-term purchases lets discoms run their own auctions, shrinking the trader's matching role.
  • Smart meters and time-of-day tariffs. Shift demand into solar hours and flatten the evening peak over time.
  • Neighbours trading directly. Nepal and Bhutan on IEX's day-ahead and real-time markets bypass the nominated trader for spot trades.

Each of these pushes the same way: the act of placing a trade becomes cheaper and more automated, and value migrates to whoever owns a physical asset (a battery, a hydro dam, a transmission right) or a regulatory franchise (an exchange licence, a government nomination). PTC's strategy of contracting storage, buying into a platform business and rebuilding Teesta-III reads as a response to exactly that shift. The risk is that it spends shareholders' cash to buy into asset-heavy businesses that earn less than the cash did on deposit.

Coal is a useful precedent. The Ministry of Coal's requirement to route e-auction volumes through coal exchanges led IEX to incorporate an Indian Coal Exchange on 1-Jun-2026, and MCX to plan its own. PTC's mineral exchange is a bet that the same migration will happen in iron ore and other minerals regulated by the Indian Bureau of Mines, displacing the MSTC and mjunction e-auction platforms. If it works, PTC owns a toll booth in a new market; if it does not, the investment is small by management's account.

The global lens: why India caps what others leave free

Europe couples its exchanges and lets traders earn what they can; the US runs central nodal markets. India caps both traders and exchanges and has one dominant platform.

Power markets elsewhere show what India's might become, and what it deliberately is not. In Europe, wholesale trading is dominated by utilities' trading desks and merchant traders (Statkraft, Uniper, RWE Supply & Trading, the power desks of commodity houses such as Vitol and Trafigura) and by exchanges such as EPEX SPOT and Nord Pool. Day-ahead markets across the continent have been coupled since 2014 under the Single Day-Ahead Coupling, with nine nominated market operators taking turns to run the algorithm. More than half of consumption in the Nordic countries and Germany clears through day-ahead exchanges. No regulator caps a trader's margin; competition and transparency do the work.

In the United States, independent system operators (PJM, ERCOT, CAISO and others) run centralised nodal markets in which every node has its own price; "traders" are mostly financial participants and generators' marketing arms, and margins are unregulated. In both regions the trader is a risk-taker, earning spreads by owning positions, not a fee-earning intermediary.

India is unusual on three counts. It caps traders' margins, at 0–7 paise per unit (at ₹94.9 to the dollar, at most about US$0.74 per MWh, a fraction of typical European trading spreads). It caps exchange fees, at 2 paise per side. And it has a single exchange with 98–99% of day-ahead volume. India's proposed coupling design also differs from Europe's: a single operator, Grid-India, rather than rotation among exchanges, which is what CERC's July-2025 order first proposed and the April-2026 draft abandoned.

Bull versus bear

For the industry, the question is whether scarcity and storage revive the value of structured trading. For PTC, it is whether the cash and the PFS stake get unlocked before the old book runs off.

The bull and bear cases below are for the sub-sector as seen through its one listed member. Most of the industry arguments apply to any trader; the valuation arguments are specific to PTC, because PTC's share price is mostly cash and a listed stake rather than a multiple of trading profit.

The bull case

  • Scarcity and renewables raise demand for structured power: record peaks (270.8 GW), a binding ₹10 cap and evening shortages make round-the-clock, peak and banking contracts more valuable than spot access, and PTC is the largest structurer.
  • The sum of the parts is cheap: cash (₹2,431 Cr) and the PFS stake at market (₹1,080 Cr) cover 85% of the ₹4,117 Cr market value; a PFS sale near book (≈₹2,000 Cr) would on its own exceed the residual value of the trading business.
  • Earnings quality has improved: surcharge has fallen to about 12% of pre-tax profit, receivables are 72% under six months, debtor days are 44 and contingent liabilities fell to ₹707 Cr from ₹975 Cr.
  • A recurring 6.1% yield: FY26 DPS of ₹8.50, a 63% standalone payout and a record of returning PEL proceeds.
  • Optionality at little cost: HPX (22.62%) if coupling arrives, storage contracting, a mineral exchange, Teesta-III and the NTPC renewable contract.
  • Governance is more independent than feared: shareholders blocked NTPC control; an independent director chairs the board.

The bear case

  • Margin dilution is structural: the long-term book cannot be refilled from thermal power, new long-term deals are 10–12 years at best, and every year more volume moves to exchanges at 0.69 p; blended margin fell to 2.89 p in Q2 FY27.
  • Profit props are disappearing: net surcharge fell from ₹268 Cr (FY25) to ₹104 Cr (FY26) and ₹29 Cr in H1 FY27; treasury income falls by about ₹48 Cr a year after the special dividend; FY27 standalone profit could be ₹330–370 Cr (INDICATIVE).
  • Cross-border volume is fragile: Bhutan imports fell 70% in Q1 FY27 and Bhutan now buys from India in winter.
  • Competition from groups and generators: Adani's Powerpulse gained four points in a year; generator-owned desks need no third-party trader.
  • Capital allocation and governance: a six-year-old PFS sale with no bidder, a refusal to sell below book, a government that may try again on NTPC control, and new asset-heavy ventures funded from cash.
  • Returns below the cost of equity: standalone ROE of about 8% justifies a below-book valuation without any "discount".

Weighing the two, the bear arguments describe the trading business more accurately, and the bull arguments describe the balance sheet more accurately. The trading business is a slowly eroding franchise whose profitable book is held up by legacy contracts; nothing in the regulatory or competitive outlook suggests its blended margin will rise. The balance sheet, by contrast, is genuinely large relative to the share price, and the valuation already assumes the trading business is worth little. The swing factor is management: a PFS sale at a reasonable price followed by a distribution would crystallise value; using the cash for low-return ventures would destroy it. Investors in this sub-sector are, in effect, buying a capital-allocation decision.

For the industry more broadly, the bull case is that scarcity, renewables and storage create new kinds of trade, structured round-the-clock and green supply, storage-backed peak power, cross-border swaps, that need a trader's balance sheet and relationships. The bear case is that each new product migrates to an exchange or to the generator's own desk once it standardises, as the bilateral short-term market did. The evidence of the last fifteen years favours the bear case for any product that can be auctioned, and the bull case only for products that cannot.

In plain terms

The pessimists are right about the trading business: its fees will keep shrinking. The optimists are right about the balance sheet: the cash and the lender stake are worth most of the share price. Which matters more depends on what management does with the money.
