00

The one-page view: one smelter, one power utility, one label

The listed "ferro alloys" universe in the NSE 750 is two companies that share a furnace technology and almost nothing else. Read them as two different businesses, and the sub-sector makes sense.

Ferro alloys are small-tonnage additives that steelmaking cannot do without. A stainless-steel mill needs ferrochrome to put the chromium into stainless; a carbon-steel mill needs silico-manganese or ferro-manganese to strip out oxygen and harden the steel. Neither is made in large tonnages compared with steel itself, and neither is glamorous, but both are made by forcing an ore and a reductant through an electric arc at 1,600–1,700°C. That last fact defines the industry. A tonne of ferrochrome takes about 2.5 tonnes of chrome ore, about 0.65 tonnes of metallurgical coke and roughly 3,500–4,000 kWh of electricity (an industry norm). Whoever owns the ore and the power owns the margin. Whoever buys both at market prices sells at the same world price and keeps very little.

The two NSE 750 members illustrate the two ends of that logic. Indian Metals & Ferro Alloys (IMFA) is India's largest integrated ferrochrome producer: it mines its own chromite at Sukinda and Mahagiri in Odisha on leases that run to 2049 and 2055 (ICRA), burns its own coal in a 200 MW captive power plant, and exports about 85% of its revenue to stainless mills in China, Japan, Korea and Taiwan. In FY26 it sold 270,124 tonnes and made ₹588 crore of operating profit on ₹2,826 crore of revenue. Nava Limited makes silico-manganese in Telangana and Odisha, but that business earned a segment result of only ₹31 crore in FY26, a 2.6% margin. Nava's real engine is Maamba Energy in Zambia: the country's largest coal mine attached to a 300 MW mine-mouth power plant that sells to the state utility ZESCO. Maamba produced US$151 million of EBITDA in FY26, roughly two-thirds of the group's operating profit, and Nava owns 65% of it.

Put the two together and the "sub-sector" earned ₹7,117 crore of revenue and ₹2,306 crore of operating profit in FY26 (a 32.4% margin). That margin is not a ferro-alloy margin. Three-quarters of the operating profit comes from Nava, and most of Nava's comes from selling electricity in Zambia. Strip out the power and the coal, and the ferro-alloy business of the two members is an indicative ₹4,018 crore of revenue at about a 15.7% EBITDA margin, nearly all of it IMFA's. This report therefore treats the members as what they are: one archetype of the integrated smelter, and one archetype of a power-anchored conglomerate that still owns furnaces.

The five forces that decide how this business performs, and that shape the rest of the report, are these:

  • The ferrochrome price is a contest between stainless demand and South African supply. China and Indonesia make most of the world's stainless and China smelts about half the world's ferrochrome from imported South African ore. When South Africa's utility Eskom pushed its tariff to smelters to about R1.36/kWh, smelting there nearly stopped (reportedly only 4 of 48 smelters ran in January 2026, an indicative snippet figure). A five-year negotiated tariff of R0.62/kWh (62 cents) is now restarting them, which is why prices are rolling over after IMFA's record first quarter.
  • Captive ore and captive power are the only moats. Indian chromite blocks auctioned since 2015 clear at premiums near 90–110% of ore value; IMFA's pre-2015 leases avoid that. Captive coal power at about ₹3–3.5/kWh against a grid tariff of ₹6–7/kWh is worth ₹10,000–15,000 a tonne (indicative).
  • Nava is really a Zambian coal-power IPP. Its value turns on Maamba's plant load factor, ZESCO's payments, the kwacha, the 300 MW Phase II expansion (now due around June–July 2027) and the 35% of Maamba owned by Zambia's state investor ZCCM-IH.
  • Scale-up against the cost curve. IMFA is taking capacity from 384,000 tpa (FY26) to 534,000 tpa (FY28) through the Tata Steel plant it bought (KNR-2) and its own greenfield (KNR-1). Its EBITDA per tonne has held at ₹20,000–25,000 in ordinary years; volume is the growth lever.
  • Trade policy is redrawing the export map. The EU's ferro-alloy safeguard (Regulation 2025/2351) caps Indian silico-manganese but spares ferrochrome; the EU's carbon border tax (CBAM) does the reverse. US tariffs, India's steel safeguard and Indian steel demand (168.4 mt of crude steel in FY26) complete the picture.

In plain terms

The listed ferro-alloy space is small and lopsided. IMFA is a genuine ferrochrome business whose profit rises and falls with a world price that it does not control, cushioned by owning its own ore and power. Nava carries the "ferro alloys" label but makes almost nothing on its alloys; its profits come from a coal power station in Zambia and from merchant power in India. Adding the two together tells you very little; comparing how each one makes money tells you a great deal.

The timing matters too. Both companies report the September quarter (Q2 FY27) in early November 2026: IMFA's board meets on 4 November to approve results and consider an interim dividend, and Nava, whose trading window has been closed since 1 October, typically reports in the second week of November. IMFA has guided to a realisation "more or less" flat on Q1 with "a little bit of correction", and Nava's Maamba plant took its scheduled half-yearly maintenance shutdown in the quarter. The real test for IMFA comes in the second half of FY27, when South African restarts bite on prices just as its new furnaces add tonnes.

01

How an alloy is smelted, and where the money sits

From an ore lease in Odisha to a stainless mill in Taiwan: five steps, and the value collects at the first and the third.

A ferro alloy is iron mixed with a large share of another element: chromium in ferrochrome, manganese and silicon in silico-manganese, manganese in ferro-manganese, silicon in ferro-silicon. Steelmakers add them because pure chromium or pure manganese is expensive to make, whereas an iron-based alloy dissolves easily into molten steel. The product is a lumpy, grey, metallic rock sold by the tonne, priced on its content of the useful element, and shipped in bags or bulk to steel mills. India produces all the main types except ferro-nickel.

The two members make different products for different customers. IMFA makes high-carbon ferrochrome (50–70% chromium, 6–8% carbon), about 90% of which is bought by stainless-steel mills worldwide; it also makes small volumes of refined low-carbon grades. Nava makes silico-manganese (60–65% manganese, 14–18% silicon), the largest-volume ferro alloy, used by carbon-steel mills making long products such as TMT bars and structurals; it has discontinued ferro-silicon and now recovers only a little from residue. The two share a furnace technology and a dependence on electricity. They do not share a customer, an ore, a price index or a cycle.

Value collects at Step 1 (ore rent for whoever holds an old lease) and Step 3 (power cost for whoever owns a cheap plant). Steps 2 and 4 are costs every producer pays.

Step 1, the mine, is where chrome economics are decided. More than 95% of India's chromite lies in the Sukinda valley of Odisha. IMFA's two operating mines, Sukinda and Mahagiri, raised a record 810,612 tonnes in FY26, of which 536,810 tonnes came from Mahagiri, all underground; its third lease, Nuasahi, is closed and a surrender order is pending with the Odisha government. A smelter without a mine must buy ore at monthly e-auctions run by the Odisha Mining Corporation or import South African concentrate, and pays whatever the market charges. Manganese is different: India's manganese ore is limited, MOIL does not smelt at scale, and more than half the ore used by Indian silico-manganese makers is imported from South Africa, Gabon and Australia. Nobody in manganese alloys has an ore advantage worth the name.

Step 3, the smelter, is where power decides the outcome. Furnaces are rated in megavolt-amperes (MVA) of transformer capacity: IMFA's legacy plants at Therubali and Choudwar have 190 MVA, the KNR-2 plant bought from Tata Steel added 99 MVA, and the KNR-1 greenfield takes the total to 355 MVA by FY28. A smelter that generates its own power from linkage coal pays perhaps ₹3–3.5 per kWh; one that buys from the Odisha grid pays ₹6–7. On 3,800 kWh a tonne that difference is ₹10,000–15,000 a tonne (indicative), roughly half a typical year's EBITDA per tonne for IMFA. That is why both members built captive power plants decades ago, and why both ended up with surplus power to sell.

Step 4, selling, differs by product. Ferrochrome goes overwhelmingly to stainless mills. IMFA sends about 80% of its export sales under long-term contracts, mainly to the Far East, and sold 18.1% of its FY26 tonnes in India (48,769 tonnes, up from 8.3% in FY22). Indian duty protection on ferro alloys is "hardly 2%, 3%" according to IMFA's managing director, so the domestic price is export parity. Silico-manganese goes to Indian carbon-steel mills and to export markets. Nava sells about 40% of its alloy output under annual contracts with two Japanese mills, 40–50% under quarterly fixed-base contracts with large Indian private steelmakers, and only 10–15% spot.

In plain terms

A ferro-alloy plant converts two things it may or may not own, ore and electricity, into a product it cannot price. A company that owns both inputs earns the difference between its own cost and the world price; a company that owns neither earns a conversion fee that can turn negative. Everything else in this report follows from that.

Step 5, selling the spare power, sounds like an afterthought, but it is the hinge of Nava's history. Nava built captive power for its Paloncha smelter in Telangana, then built more than the smelter needed, then became a merchant generator in India with 414 MW operating, and finally used that know-how to build a coal mine and a 300 MW power station in Zambia. In FY26 its Indian plants sold 2,274 million units and Maamba sold 2,115 million units. IMFA went the other way: when the Supreme Court cancelled its Utkal-C coal block in 2014, its 200 MW plant lost its captive fuel and it now buys linkage and auction coal for a plant whose surplus capacity ICRA calls "a drag on RoCE" because Odisha's grid pays a "non-remunerative" price for surplus power.

02

Size: two markets hiding under one word

India makes about 9% of the world's ferrochrome and is the largest exporter of manganese alloys. The rupee values involved are modest; the tonnages are not.

India's ferrochrome output was 1.49 million tonnes in FY26, up 10%, made from 3.53 million tonnes of chrome ore, up 12% (figures from the Indian Ferro Alloy Producers' Association as reported by SMM, a secondary source, so indicative). At IMFA's FY26 blended revenue of about ₹1.05 lakh a tonne, the Indian ferrochrome market is worth roughly ₹15,500 crore, about US$1.8 billion (indicative). Around one-third is exported: India's ferrochrome exports were 0.49 million tonnes in calendar 2025, down from 0.58 million tonnes in 2024. The other two-thirds go to Indian stainless mills, led by Jindal Stainless. IMFA is the outlier: it exports about 82% of its tonnes, so the rest of the Indian industry is far more domestic than IMFA is.

The scale of the world chromium trade is set by stainless steel. In plain terms

India matters in ferrochrome because it has ore, and in manganese alloys because it has furnaces. The first is a scarce position; the second is a crowded one. That is why IMFA earns ₹20,000 or more on a tonne in an ordinary year while silico-manganese makers often earn a few thousand rupees, or nothing.

Growth for the next few years will come from supply more than from demand. IMFA plans to lift capacity by 150,000 tonnes by FY28, and Vedanta's FACOR has claimed it will go from 145,000 tonnes to 500,000 tonnes over the same period (a claim raised on IMFA's call, unverified). Jindal Stainless makes captive ferrochrome for its own melt shops. If all of that arrives, Indian ferrochrome supply could rise 40–50% by FY28 (indicative), far faster than Indian stainless demand of roughly 5–6 million tonnes growing at 8–10% a year. That matters for IMFA's stated plan to sell at least 200,000 tonnes a year in India "in times to come": the domestic market will be more contested, and its price will remain export parity.

03

Demand: stainless for chrome, carbon steel for manganese

Two different steel markets pull on the two members. One is set in China and Indonesia; the other is set in India's construction boom.

Ferrochrome demand is stainless demand. About 90% of chromium ends up in stainless steel, and China makes almost two-thirds of the world's stainless. Chinese mills such as Tsingshan and TISCO publish a monthly long-term tender price for high-carbon ferrochrome, and that tender is the reference the rest of the world watches. Indonesia, where Tsingshan and Delong built integrated nickel-pig-iron-to-stainless complexes at Morowali and Weda Bay, is the fastest-growing source of stainless outside China and increasingly smelts its own ferrochrome from imported ore. India makes about 4.5–5 million tonnes of stainless, led by Jindal Stainless, which is expanding its Jajpur plant and has a joint venture melt shop in Indonesia.

The drivers of stainless demand are consumer durables, kitchen and building products, process industries and transport. China's property weakness from 2023 to 2025 slowed the 300-series grades but did not stop growth; the first half of 2026 brought a 5% rise. Two technology shifts reduce the chromium needed per tonne over time. The first is the 200-series grades popular in India and China, which use manganese and less nickel and contain about 13–16% chromium against 18% in 304 grade. The second is scrap: electric-furnace stainless in Europe and the US takes 50–60% of its chromium from scrap, which reduces primary ferrochrome demand there. Neither changes the picture within a year or two.

For Indian ferrochrome specifically, a domestic issue matters. On IMFA's August 2026 call an analyst pointed out that Indian stainless re-rollers import Chinese and Indonesian flat stainless rather than melting domestically, which weakens Indian ferrochrome demand. Stainless is excluded from India's flat-steel safeguard duty. IMFA's managing director would say only that the industry wants a "level playing field". If India were to protect stainless melt shops, domestic ferrochrome demand would rise; until then IMFA's domestic pivot depends on Jindal Stainless and a few other melters.

Manganese-alloy demand is carbon-steel demand. Every tonne of crude steel uses roughly 10–12 kg of manganese alloys: secondary steelmakers using induction furnaces use more silico-manganese per tonne, and integrated basic-oxygen plants making flat steel use more ferro-manganese. India's crude steel output grew from 103.5 million tonnes in FY21 to 168.4 million tonnes in FY26, a 10.7% rise in the last year alone, and capacity reached 220.3 million tonnes (the steel business itself is covered in our iron & steel and steel products reports). The National Steel Policy targets 300 million tonnes of capacity by 2030–31. At 10–12 kg a tonne, each extra 10 million tonnes of steel adds 100,000–120,000 tonnes of manganese-alloy demand (illustrative).

The problem is that supply has grown faster. With about 4 million tonnes of production against domestic demand of 1.7–2.0 million tonnes (illustrative), India must export about half its output, and its main export market, Europe, imposed country quotas from November 2025. Nava's annual report describes the result: EU quotas, US tariffs and the West Asia conflict "pushed export material back into India and compressed domestic prices". Steel demand growth also slowed after the West Asia shock of 28 February 2026: crude steel output in April–May 2026 was up only 2.7%. India's flat-steel safeguard duty (12% to April 2026, then 11.5% and 11% to April 2028, a snippet-sourced and therefore indicative schedule) supports domestic steel output, and with it domestic silico-manganese demand, but it does not cure the surplus.

In India, Nava's merchant power earns exchange prices, which fell about 13% year on year in FY26 as daytime solar flooded the market; management guides an FY27 realisation of about ₹5.50/kWh.

In plain terms

IMFA's customers are stainless mills in Asia, and its demand is about as good as the world's appetite for kitchen sinks, pipes and appliances, which is growing a few percent a year. Nava's alloy customers are Indian and Japanese carbon-steel mills, and its demand is fine but its industry makes twice what India uses. Nava's real customer is a Zambian state utility that needs every megawatt it can get.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes

For an integrated ferrochrome smelter about a third of revenue is ore and coke, a sixth is power, and in a good quarter nearly a third is left as EBITDA.

IMFA publishes a standalone profit and loss account that makes the cost structure of an integrated smelter unusually clear. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) it reported revenue of ₹960.45 crore. Raw materials, which for IMFA means the cost of mining its own chromite (including royalty) plus purchased coke and other inputs, cost ₹350.72 crore; inventory built up by ₹29.1 crore, which reduces the cost charged in the quarter; power and fuel, mainly coal for the captive plant plus grid power at the new Kalinganagar sites, cost ₹157.22 crore; employees ₹82.27 crore; other expenses ₹118.09 crore. 05

Ore, power and the integrated cost curve Deep dive

In an industry that sells at one world price, the only durable advantage is a low position on the cost curve. In Indian ferrochrome that position is bought with a mine lease and a power plant, and both are hard to replicate.

A cost curve ranks every producer of a commodity from cheapest to dearest, plotting cumulative capacity along the bottom and cash cost up the side. The world price settles near the cost of the marginal tonne needed to meet demand. In ferrochrome that marginal tonne is usually a Chinese smelter running on imported South African ore and Chinese grid power. ICRA puts it plainly: Chinese smelting costs are "heavily influenced by the seaborne price of chrome ore … mainly from South Africa". An Indian integrated producer earns the gap between that Chinese marginal cost and its own captive cost. When ore is dear the gap widens, because the Chinese cost rises and the integrated cost barely moves. ICRA's December 2025 view was that the "upward shift in the cost curve" is "unlikely to lead to a mean-reversion" in the near term.

The ore moat, in rupees per tonne

Since the 2015 amendment to the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, new mineral concessions in India are auctioned, and bidders compete on a premium paid as a percentage of the value of the ore they dispatch. When Odisha auctioned the expired merchant mines in 2020, the Sukinda chromite block went to TS Alloys, a Tata Steel company, at a premium of 93.75% with only one rival bid (BigMint, secondary). Recent Odisha iron-ore blocks have cleared at 101–111%. A miner paying a 94% premium hands the state almost as much again as the ore is worth, on top of the standard 15% royalty. IMFA's leases, granted under the earlier allotment regime, pay royalty and levies but no auction premium, and ICRA says they are valid until 2049 and 2055.

Does the ore moat scale?

IMFA raised 810,612 tonnes of ore in FY26 against consumption of roughly 668,000 tonnes (267,301 tonnes of alloy at 2.5 tonnes each, illustrative), and built an ore stock of about 6 lakh tonnes by June 2026 to feed the expansion. Ore raised per tonne of alloy produced rose from 2.27 in FY22 to 3.03 in FY26 and 3.38 in Q1 FY27 (computed), which is stock-building, not lower yield. The plan is to lift raising to 1.0 million tonnes in FY27, hold it there "for a couple of years", then go to 1.2 million tonnes, eventually all underground, for "close to ₹1,000 crore" of investment. The arithmetic gap is modest: 534,000 tonnes of alloy needs about 1.33 million tonnes of ore at 2.5 tonnes per tonne, against a 1.2 million tonne clearance (illustrative). Stock, better yields or small purchases can close a gap of 0.1–0.15 million tonnes. The KNR expansion's ore "will be entirely met from the company's captive chrome ore mines", according to the company.

The underground shift matters for cost as well as volume. Open-cast mining in Sukinda has become harder under stricter environmental rules, partly because of hexavalent-chromium contamination in the Brahmani and Damsal watersheds. Underground mining is more expensive per tonne than open-cast, so IMFA's ore cost will drift up even as its volume rises. That is part of why its "EBITDA cost per tonne" rose through FY26, alongside royalty that is pegged to rising ore prices. The moat remains wide, but it is not free.

The power moat, and why it is narrowing for new tonnes

IMFA's legacy plants at Therubali and Choudwar sit next to a 200 MW captive coal plant plus 4.55 MWp of solar, which generated 1,138 million units in FY26. That is roughly equal to the legacy plants' demand (about 267,000 tonnes at ~3,800 kWh, or about 1,015 million units, illustrative), so the legacy plants are close to fully self-sufficient. The new Kalinganagar plants are not. They run on grid power and contracted renewable energy. IMFA has contracted 135 MW of hybrid renewable capacity: a re-signed agreement with JSW Renew Energy Twelve (the original JSW Green Energy contract was cancelled when the project site moved) and a 29-year captive agreement with EG Urja Strot for 65 MW of contracted demand (81.4 MW solar, 102.6 MW wind and 25 MWh of battery storage; IMFA invests ₹110.18 crore), signed on 25 May 2026. An agreement with AmpIn Energy was terminated on 14 May 2026. The company says this will take it to about 40% non-fossil power by mid-2027.

What an integrated position is worth through the cycle

The proof that integration stabilises margins sits in IMFA's own five-year record. Its revenue per tonne moved in a narrow band around ₹1 lakh (₹98,700 to ₹107,900) from FY22 to FY26, yet the world ferrochrome price fell sharply from its 2022 peak: the USGS US price for ferrochromium fell from US$3.19/lb of contained chromium in 2022 to US$1.60 in 2025. IMFA's EBITDA per tonne was ₹33,400 in FY22 and then held between ₹20,400 and ₹24,450 in each of FY23–FY26 (computed from company EBITDA and tonnes, indicative). A business that sells at a global commodity price and still earns ₹20,000 a tonne or more in every year of a downturn is a business with a real cost advantage. The quarterly record is choppier, from ₹10,950 in Q4 FY25 to ₹35,484 in Q1 FY27, because long-term contracts reset with a lag and the rupee moves.

In plain terms

IMFA's advantage is not that it is cleverer at smelting. It is that it signed its mining leases before India started auctioning them, so it does not pay the premium that a new miner pays, and it owns a power plant, so it does not pay the grid's price. Those two facts are worth more than its whole margin in an ordinary year. The open question is how much of that advantage the new, grid-powered Kalinganagar tonnes will keep.

For Nava there is no equivalent story in alloys. Manganese ore is bought, and the power advantage of a captive plant is worth something only if the alternative use of that power is worse. Since Nava's Odisha unit became an independent power producer selling 120 MW from November 2025, and a 50 MW five-year contract with Tamil Nadu at ₹5.95/kWh began in February 2026, the opportunity cost of feeding a furnace has risen. Management now openly weighs "produce alloys versus export the power". When the electricity is worth more on the grid than inside a tonne of silico-manganese, the furnace becomes the swing load. The only alloy growth path Nava describes is in Côte d'Ivoire, where it is exploring a 340 km² manganese concession and plans a local alloy plant with biomass power; metallurgical reports are due in December 2026.

Who makes India's alloys

Ferrochrome is a concentrated, ore-gated industry of a handful of integrated players. Manganese alloys are a fragmented, power-gated industry of hundreds.

Indian ferrochrome is an oligopoly with a long tail. IMFA made about 18% of India's 1.49 million tonnes in FY26 (computed, indicative), and screener's company description puts it at about 20% of Indian output and 25% of exports. Tata Steel's ferro-alloys and minerals division runs plants at Bamnipal, Athagarh and Gopalpur and holds major Sukinda chromite leases; it sold its Kalinganagar plant (KNR-2) to IMFA for a ₹610 crore base price in a deal signed on 4 November 2025 and completed on 27 February 2026. Vedanta's FACOR operates at Bhadrak with its own Ostapal and Kalarangiatta mines. Jindal Stainless makes captive ferrochrome for its own stainless. Balasore Alloys, with the Kaliapani mine, has a history of financial stress and insolvency proceedings. The tail consists of small converters around Jajpur buying ore from the Odisha Mining Corporation, which also auctions ferrochrome made for it by conversion agents (3,300 tonnes in February 2026, a snippet-sourced indicative figure).

The same furnace can sit inside very different businesses. Which one a company runs decides how it behaves in the cycle far more than which alloy it makes.

Grouping producers by what they own, rather than by what they make, gives five archetypes. The fully integrated ferrochrome producer owns ore and power. The converter owns neither. The manganese smelter with captive power owns only power. The merchant manganese smelter owns nothing but furnaces. And the power-anchored conglomerate, of which Nava is the only listed example, owns power plants that have outgrown the furnaces they were built to feed. The archetype explains behaviour better than the product does. A captive-power manganese smelter (C) earns a power-cost advantage over merchant smelters (D) but no ore rent, so its margin hovers in single digits except in boom years such as FY22, when Nava's alloy segment earned 26% and Maithan 36%. In plain terms

Who owns what tells you how a ferro-alloy company will behave when prices fall. Own the ore and the power, and you stay profitable. Own only the power, and you scrape by. Own neither, and you shut. Own a power station in Zambia, and the price of silico-manganese hardly matters to you at all.

South Africa sets the price: the swing producer and the ferrochrome cycle Deep dive

South Africa mines almost half the world's chromite. Whether it smelts that ore at home or ships it to China depends on one number, the tariff Eskom charges its smelters, and that number moved more in 2025–26 than in the previous decade.

The world ferrochrome market has a peculiar geography. South Africa mines about 23 million tonnes of chromite a year, 45% of the world total (USGS, 2025 estimate). China mines almost none, yet smelts about half the world's ferrochrome. The link between them is the seaborne trade in South African chrome ore, mostly low-grade "UG2" concentrate produced as a by-product of platinum mining. When South African smelters run, they consume a large share of that ore at home and export alloy. When they stop, the ore goes to China instead, where it is smelted with Chinese coal power. South Africa is therefore the world's swing producer, but in a strange sense: its swing changes who smelts the chromium more than how much chromium exists.

How Eskom shut an industry

South African smelting is electricity-intensive in a country where electricity has become expensive and unreliable. Eskom's standard tariffs rose roughly threefold over a decade, and by the end of 2025 the effective rate for ferrochrome smelters had reached about R1.36/kWh, according to IMFA's managing director. At that price, with about 3,500–4,000 kWh needed per tonne, power alone cost more than a smelter could recover. Glencore and Merafe suspended their Wonderkop and Boshoek smelters in 2025, Samancor idled most of its furnaces, and by January 2026 the industry association reported that "only four of South Africa's 48 ferrochrome smelters" were running (a secondary-source snippet, indicative). Merafe, which owns 20.5% of the Glencore-Merafe Chrome Venture, reported attributable ferrochrome output of 28,000 tonnes in the first half of 2026 against 112,000 tonnes a year earlier, a fall of 75%.

"Chrome units": why a South African restart is not a simple price collapse

IMFA's managing director argues that the market should count "chrome units" rather than tonnes of alloy. If South Africa smelts more of its own ore, it exports less ore to China; Chinese smelters then have less feed and their cost floor rises, because they must bid harder for the ore that remains. On this view a South African restart replaces Chinese tonnes rather than adding to world supply, and the price falls less than a simple supply-and-demand chart would suggest. He also pointed out that the expected South African additions are about 700,000–800,000 tonnes, not a return to the full pre-collapse rate. The reasoning is sound as far as it goes: the world's chromium supply is set by ore, and the ore is not increasing quickly.

The long cycle: what the US price series shows

For a longer view, the US Geological Survey's annual ferrochromium price (US$ per pound of contained chromium) traces the cycle the members have lived through. It was US$1.50 in 2021, jumped to US$3.19 in the 2022 commodity boom, then fell to US$2.55 in 2023, US$1.76 in 2024 and US$1.60 in 2025. Chromite ore, by contrast, kept rising from US$199 a tonne in 2021 to US$331 in 2024 before easing to US$290 in 2025. The divergence, alloy falling while ore stays high, is the squeeze that shut converters worldwide and left integrated producers with their margins intact. IMFA's FY22 peak EBITDA of ₹33,400 a tonne (computed) coincided with the 2022 price spike; its FY23–FY26 plateau of ₹20,000–25,000 coincided with alloy prices falling and ore prices rising.

The cycle anatomy that follows from all this is consistent. Ferrochrome cycles run three to five years. Upswings come from South African supply shocks, whether power, logistics or strikes, rather than from demand. Downswings come from Chinese capacity additions and South African restarts. Indian integrated producers earn the gap between the Chinese marginal cost (South African ore delivered to China plus Chinese power) and their own captive cost. That gap widened in 2025–26 because ore was dear and South Africa was down, and it narrows as South Africa returns. For IMFA the second half of FY27 is where the restart bites: the Q2 FY27 numbers due on 4 November should still show strong pricing, with the managing director guiding to "more or less same" realisation and "a little bit of correction".

In plain terms

When South Africa's power bill made smelting impossible there, the world ran short of ferrochrome and IMFA had the best quarter in its history. South Africa has now fixed that bill for five years, its smelters are coming back, and Chinese prices are already easing. The open question is whether the price falls to where it was in 2024, or settles higher because the ore that fed China is now being smelted in South Africa instead.

Leases, royalty and the law

India's mining law turned old leases into gold and new ones into tax collectors. The rules on royalty, mineral-land taxes and power open access decide how much of the ore rent a smelter actually keeps.

The single most important piece of law for Indian ferrochrome is the 2015 amendment to the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957. It made auctions compulsory for new mineral concessions. Section 8A(5) extended existing merchant leases only to 31 March 2020, after which Odisha auctioned them, many at very high premiums. Section 8A(6) treated captive leases differently: they were deemed extended to 31 March 2030 or 50 years from the date of grant, whichever is later. That is why IMFA's leases run to 2049 and 2055 (ICRA) rather than expiring in 2030. The "2030 cliff" that hangs over some Indian captive miners applies to leases granted before about 1980, and does not appear to bite IMFA (an inference from ICRA's dates; the lease grant dates were not found).

The 2021 amendment then abolished the captive/merchant distinction for future auctions and allowed captive mines to sell up to 50% of their annual production in the open market after meeting the needs of their linked plant, on payment of an additional amount. For IMFA this is an option rather than a plan: it is stockpiling ore (about 6 lakh tonnes by June 2026) to feed its own expansion, not to sell. The same amendment allows leases to be transferred, with statutory clearances carried over for two years, which made it easier for groups to buy captive-lease-backed assets.

The Supreme Court's mineral-land tax

On 25 July 2024 a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court held, in Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India, that states may tax mineral rights and mineral-bearing land. On 14 August 2024 it allowed states to levy such taxes retrospectively back to 1 April 2005, payable over 12 years from 1 April 2026, with interest and penalties waived for the period before 25 July 2024. As of ICRA's December 2025 note, Odisha had not decided whether to levy. IMFA's FY25 annual report says the judgement "has financial implications for the mining sector at large", notes that Odisha's earlier ORISED Act of 2004 was struck down by the Orissa High Court in December 2005 and that the state's appeal is pending in the Supreme Court, and states that no demand is pending against IMFA and none is provided for. This is a contingent risk for every Odisha miner. Its size cannot be estimated until Odisha legislates.

Trade walls: the EU safeguard, CBAM and US tariffs

Europe has built two different barriers, and by accident each member faces one but not the other.

The European Union is the largest market for Indian manganese alloys (roughly 40% of India's ferro-alloy exports, according to Argus), and since November 2025 it has two separate trade instruments that bear on ferro alloys. The first is a safeguard. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2351 applies from 18 November 2025 to 17 November 2028 (read via an Official Journal mirror because EUR-Lex was not reachable, so treat the detail as indicative). It covers ferro-manganese (HS 7202 11/19), ferro-silicon (7202 21/29), silico-manganese (7202 30) and ferro-silico-magnesium, and sets country quotas in quarterly periods, with a duty on out-of-quota imports priced below a threshold. Ferrochrome is not covered.

India's country quotas are tight. For silico-manganese, year 1 gives India 31,958.61 tonnes in each of the first three quarters and 30,916.48 tonnes in the fourth, a total of 126,792 tonnes; year 2, which starts on 18 November 2026 inside Nava's Q3 FY27, gives 126,919 tonnes. For ferro-manganese, year 1 gives 69,928 tonnes. The price thresholds are €1,392 a tonne for silico-manganese, €1,316 for ferro-manganese and €2,408 for ferro-silicon. India has no country quota for ferro-silicon and falls into "other countries". Because the quota is quarterly, an unused quarter cannot be carried forward. Argus estimates the measure cuts India's potential EU shipments "by almost half".

The United States and the rest

Ferro-alloy exports carry no export duty and earn small remissions under RoDTEP (rates of about 0.5–1%, unverified). Exporters also face freight: the West Asia war from 28 February 2026 raised freight costs "a little" for IMFA, which neither buys from nor sells into the region, and delayed shipments of critical equipment for Nava's Maamba Phase II.

Maamba: a ferro-alloy company that is really a Zambian power utility Deep dive

Two-thirds of Nava's operating profit comes from one coal mine and one 300 MW power station in southern Zambia, selling in US dollars to one state utility. Understanding Nava means understanding Maamba's plant load, its tariff, its customer's payments, its currency and its partner.

Maamba Energy Ltd (MEL) owns Zambia's largest coal mine and a 300 MW mine-mouth power station (two 150 MW units) in the Southern Province. Nava took control of Maamba Collieries in 2010, began coal production in FY13, synchronised the power units in 2016 and declared commercial operation in August 2017 (CRISIL). The ownership chain matters: Nava Limited owns 100% of Nava Global Pte (formerly Nava Bharat Singapore), which owns 65% of MEL; ZCCM Investments Holdings, the Zambian state's mining investment arm, owns the other 35%. MEL is fully consolidated in Nava's accounts, so 100% of its revenue and EBITDA appear in the group numbers, while 35% of its profit is deducted as non-controlling interest at the bottom of the income statement.

The plant sells its output to ZESCO, Zambia's state utility, under a 20-year power purchase agreement denominated in US dollars, with capacity and energy charges. In FY26 MEL generated 2,354 million units gross and sold 2,115 million units (auxiliary consumption 9.8%), at a plant load factor of 89.6% and availability of 89.9%. It also sold 471,976 tonnes of coal to outside customers. Its FY26 revenue was US$256.0 million, EBITDA US$151.4 million and PAT US$81.4 million; in rupees, ₹2,260.5 crore, ₹1,336.6 crore and ₹719.0 crore. Nava's consolidated FY26 revenue was ₹4,290.9 crore, so MEL was a little more than half of revenue and roughly 70% of company EBITDA.

Phase II adds 300 MW of coal-fired capacity at Maamba for about US$400 million: debt of US$290 million tied up with Zambian banks and financial institutions, of which US$214.6 million had been drawn by 31 March 2026, and US$100 million of sponsors' equity, fully contributed (Nava's 65% share is about US$65 million). At 9.5 US cents and roughly 2,000–2,100 million units a year (300 MW at about 85% load, net of auxiliary consumption, illustrative), revenue would be about US$190–200 million, matching the company's guide of US$180–200 million. The risks are the usual ones for a frontier power project: no tax holiday has yet been granted (one is being sought), the tariff is below Phase I, and all of the output goes to a single state buyer whose finances defaulted six years ago.

The kwacha and the tax line

Nava's tax charge swings with the Zambian currency. MEL's accounts are kept in dollars, but its tax base is partly in kwacha, so when the kwacha rises against the dollar, MEL books deferred tax on unrealised exchange gains. The kwacha rose about 32% against the dollar over FY26 (Nava annual report) and stood near ZMW 18 per dollar at 30 June 2026. MEL booked a US$29.9 million deferred-tax liability in FY26, and a further ₹25.2 crore charge in Q1 FY27 "with further appreciation of Zambian kwacha". The CFO said it "may get even reversed" if the kwacha weakens. On top of that, Phase I's tax holiday has ended and MEL's power division is now taxed at 50% of the normal rate, about 15% effective, from FY26. That is why Nava's FY26 PAT fell 28% while its pre-tax profit fell only 6.6%, and why its Q4 FY26 tax rate was 58%.

Six decades of cycles

Both members were founded as ferro-alloy makers in the 1960s and 1970s. One stayed one; the other became a power company. The cycles that pushed them apart are the same cycles that drive the sector today.

IMFA was founded in 1961 by Dr Bansidhar Panda and Ila Panda, starting at Therubali in southern Odisha. In the mid-1980s it acquired the Kalinga Tubes assets at Choudwar and built a second ferrochrome and power complex there. It built captive mines and captive power over the next two decades and became India's largest ferrochrome exporter. Nava, then Nava Bharat Ferro Alloys, started in 1972 making ferro-silicon at Paloncha in Telangana. It added silico-manganese, sugar and, crucially, power. Both companies learned early that a smelter without its own power plant is at the mercy of state electricity boards.

The 2007–08 commodity super-cycle took the European ferrochrome benchmark to records (around US$2 a pound in 2008, a figure from memory, indicative) and triggered a boom in Indian ferro-alloy capacity, followed by the global financial crisis crash. In 2010 Nava took control of Maamba Collieries in Zambia, the decision that later transformed it. The 2014 cancellation of coal blocks cost IMFA its Utkal-C captive coal, and the years FY15–FY20 were weak: IMFA's PAT was ₹4 crore, −₹44 crore, ₹249 crore, ₹187 crore, −₹1 crore and −₹66 crore. Meanwhile South Africa dominated ferrochrome supply and China built smelters in Inner Mongolia to process South African ore.

Reading a smelter: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Ferro-alloy companies are judged on a handful of per-tonne and per-unit numbers that rarely appear in a standard screen. This section explains each one, shows how the two members compare, and flags where the definitions trap the unwary.

The equation: what makes a smelter's profit

Every ferrochrome or manganese-alloy producer's operating profit can be written as one line: tonnes sold, multiplied by the gap between the price received per tonne and the cash cost per tonne, plus a small amount of other operating income such as power sold to the grid, export incentives or foreign-exchange gains. Each term behaves differently. Tonnes sold depend on furnace capacity, uptime and power availability, which management largely controls. Realisation per tonne is set by the world price, the rupee and contract timing, which management does not control. Cash cost per tonne depends on ore, coke and power, and is controllable only to the extent the company owns its inputs. The spread between realisation and cost is what the market really trades.

A worked example in round numbers shows the gearing. Take an illustrative smelter selling 100,000 tonnes a year at ₹1,00,000 a tonne with a cash cost of ₹80,000. EBITDA is 100,000 × ₹20,000 = ₹200 crore, a 20% margin. If the price rises 10% to ₹1,10,000 and cost rises only ₹2,000 (royalty and coke follow the price a little), EBITDA becomes 100,000 × ₹28,000 = ₹280 crore: a 10% price rise has produced a 40% profit rise. If the price falls 10% instead, to ₹90,000, and cost falls ₹1,000, EBITDA drops to ₹110 crore, down 45%. That asymmetry is why ferro-alloy shares trade on expectations about the price, not on last quarter's profit.

For silico-manganese the equation is the same, but the spread is smaller and more volatile. An illustrative calculation on Nava's FY26 tonnes: 134,410 tonnes × (₹82,000 price − ₹78,500 cost) ≈ ₹47 crore, close to the segment's indicative FY26 EBITDA of about ₹41 crore. For a mine-mouth power station like Maamba, the equation becomes units sold times tariff, plus coal sales, less fuel and operating cost: 2,115 million units at about 11 US cents, plus about US$21–24 million of coal, roughly reproduces MEL's US$256 million of FY26 revenue (illustrative).

In plain terms

A smelter's profit is the number of tonnes it sells times the gap between what a tonne fetches and what it costs to make. The company controls the tonnes and some of the cost; the world controls the price. Because the gap is small relative to the price, a modest move in the price produces a large move in profit, up or down.

1. Tonnes produced and tonnes sold

What it is. The physical output of the furnaces and the quantity delivered to customers in the period. Formula. Production is the tapped metal in tonnes; sales is invoiced tonnes; the difference is the change in finished-goods inventory. Worked example. IMFA produced 80,690 tonnes in Q1 FY27 and sold 79,268, so it added about 1,400 tonnes to stock; in FY26 it produced 267,301 and sold 270,124, drawing stock down by about 2,800 tonnes. Drivers. Installed furnace capacity (MVA), furnace uptime, power availability, planned shutdowns and ore and coke availability. Nava's Q1 FY27 silico-manganese production fell 13% year on year to 24,462 tonnes because its Odisha furnaces were shut for planned work until 1 August 2026, while sales held up at 29,133 tonnes out of stock. Benchmark. A good producer grows volume without a fall in per-tonne margin; average holds volume flat; weak loses tonnes to outages. How the members compare. IMFA's tonnes sold were 248,280 (FY22), 247,945, 265,800, 259,867 and 270,124 (FY26); FY27 guidance is about 380,000 produced. Nava sold 142,449 tonnes of alloy in FY26 (134,410 silico-manganese plus 8,039 ferro-silicon) and guides about 130,000 tonnes of silico-manganese for FY27, with ferro-silicon discontinued. Definition trap. Production and sales can differ by several thousand tonnes in a quarter, and per-tonne ratios computed on the wrong one mislead. Use tonnes sold for revenue and EBITDA per tonne, tonnes produced for utilisation and ore and power intensity.

2. Realisation per tonne

What it is. The average price received for each tonne sold, net of discounts. Formula. Net sales of alloy ÷ tonnes sold. Worked example. IMFA's company-defined realisation was ₹95,165 a tonne in Q1 FY26, ₹1,09,352 in Q4 FY26 and ₹1,19,888 in Q1 FY27 ("just shy of ₹120,000, blended"). A simple revenue ÷ tonnes calculation gives about ₹1,21,200 for Q1 FY27, roughly ₹1,200 higher, because total revenue includes power, ore by-products and export incentives (computed, indicative). Drivers. The Chinese tender price, the rupee-dollar rate (85% of IMFA's revenue is exported), the share sold under lagged long-term contracts, product grade (chromium content) and freight. Benchmark. Realisation is a price, not a performance measure, so there is no good or bad level; what matters is how it compares with Chinese parity and how fast it follows the market. How the members compare. IMFA's realisation rose 26% year on year in Q1 FY27. Nava's implied alloy realisation was about ₹84,300 a tonne in FY26 (segment revenue ÷ tonnes, computed), and rose ₹3,200 a tonne quarter on quarter in Q1 FY27 on more export sales. Definition trap. Ferrochrome prices are quoted on three incompatible bases: the European benchmark in US cents per pound of contained chromium, the Chinese tender in renminbi per tonne on a 50% chromium basis including VAT, and Indian realisations in rupees per tonne of alloy at 60–70% chromium. Never compare them without converting for content, tax, freight and currency.

4. EBITDA per tonne

What it is. The operating profit earned on each tonne sold, the single best measure of a smelter's earning power through the cycle. Formula. EBITDA ÷ tonnes sold. Worked example. Benchmark (indicative). Good: above ₹30,000; average: ₹18,000–25,000; weak: below ₹12,000. How the members compare. IMFA earned about ₹33,400 a tonne in FY22, then ₹20,750, ₹24,450, ₹20,400 and ₹21,740 in FY23–FY26, with a trough of about ₹10,950 in Q4 FY25 and a peak of ₹35,484 in Q1 FY27 (all computed). Management's "through-cycle ~₹20–22k" framing, an analyst's paraphrase that the managing director declined to endorse as guidance, fits FY23–FY26. Nava's alloy segment earned about ₹2,200 a tonne of segment result in FY26 (computed). Definition trap. Always divide by tonnes sold, not produced, and use company EBITDA consistently; screener's operating profit for IMFA (₹588 crore) and company EBITDA (₹587.23 crore) are almost identical, but for Nava they differ by ₹187 crore.

5. Captive ore cover

What it is. The share of a smelter's ore needs met from its own mines. Formula. Own ore raised ÷ ore consumed (consumption ≈ alloy produced × ore per tonne of alloy). Worked example. Drivers. Lease capacity and environmental clearance, the shift from open-cast to underground mining, ore grade and the expansion of furnace capacity. Benchmark (indicative). Good: about 100%; average: 50–80%; weak: below 30%, which means buying most ore at auction prices. How the members compare. IMFA is close to 100% today. At 534,000 tonnes of alloy in FY28 it would need about 1.33 million tonnes of ore against a 1.2 million tonne clearance (illustrative), a gap stock and yield gains can cover. Nava has no captive manganese ore in India and buys from MOIL and imports; its Côte d'Ivoire exploration is the only route to ore ownership. Definition trap. "Ore raised per tonne produced" (2.27 in FY22 rising to 3.03 in FY26 and 3.38 in Q1 FY27, computed) rises when a company is stockpiling, not because yields are worse; and ore cover says nothing about cost if the mine is underground and more expensive.

6. Captive power share and specific power consumption

What it is. Two linked measures: the share of a smelter's electricity that comes from its own plants, and the electricity used per tonne of alloy (kWh/t). Formula. Captive share = own MU consumed ÷ total MU consumed; specific consumption = MU consumed ÷ tonnes produced. Worked example. IMFA generated 1,138 million units in FY26 and produced 267,301 tonnes, an upper-bound 4,257 kWh a tonne (computed; it includes mines, auxiliaries and the township). At an industry-norm 3,800 kWh a tonne the legacy plants needed about 1,015 million units, so captive power covered roughly all of their demand (illustrative). Drivers. Furnace design (open, semi-closed or closed; pre-heating and pelletising), ore quality, plant load of the captive unit and coal supply. Benchmark (indicative). Captive share good above 80%, average 40–80%, weak below 30%; specific consumption good below 3,300 kWh, average 3,500–3,900, weak above 4,100. How the members compare. IMFA is about 100% captive at its legacy plants but will be about 60% captive coal at 500,000 tonnes, with renewables taking it to about 40% non-fossil and the grid covering the rest. Nava's Telangana smelter runs on its 114 MW captive plant; its Odisha smelter on part of a 150 MW station whose other 120 MW now sells as an IPP. Definition trap. Gross generation per tonne is not consumption per tonne: IMFA's Q1 FY27 ratio fell to 3,582 kWh only because KNR-2's output ran on grid power that is not counted in captive generation.

7. Furnace utilisation

What it is. How fully the installed furnace capacity is used. Formula. Tonnes produced ÷ installed capacity (tpa) for the period. Worked example. IMFA's legacy 284,000 tpa produced 267,301 tonnes in FY26, about 94%. Nava's 180,000 tpa produced 127,486 tonnes of silico-manganese plus 5,966 of ferro-silicon, about 74% (both computed). Drivers. Power availability and cost (Nava now weighs smelting against selling the power), ore and coke supply, maintenance and market demand. Benchmark (indicative). Good above 90%; average 75–90%; weak below 70%. How the members compare. IMFA runs its legacy plants hard; its FY27 utilisation on the new 484,000 tpa capacity will be lower (about 79% on the 380,000 tonne guide) because KNR-1 arrives mid-year and KNR-2 is fixing transformers. Nava's maximum capability is about 160,000 tonnes a year by its own account, so 130,000 tonnes in FY27 is roughly 80% of what it can actually make. Definition trap. Companies quote capacity three ways. IMFA's FY26 earnings release says 434,000 tpa for 289 MVA (counting a furnace still awaiting clearance), the presentation says 384,000 tpa, and the primer said "past half a million". Use the operating figure, and divide by capacity weighted for the months it was available.

In plain terms

Ore cover, power share and utilisation together tell you how much of the smelter's cost is under its own control and how hard it is being run. IMFA scores well on all three today; the question for FY27–FY28 is whether the new plants dilute those scores. Nava's furnaces run well below capacity because its power is often worth more sold to the grid.

How to read a ferro-alloy company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Tonnes first. Pull production and sales tonnes (IMFA: ferrochrome; Nava: silico-manganese). Compare with guidance (IMFA ~380,000 tonnes for FY27, so roughly 95,000 a quarter on average, rising to 120,000–125,000 by Q4). A shortfall of more than 5% is a red flag for the ramp-up.
  2. Realisation per tonne. IMFA publishes it; compare with ₹1,19,888 in Q1 FY27 and with the Chinese tender one to two months earlier. A fall of more than ₹5,000 a tonne takes about ₹40–50 crore off quarterly EBITDA at 95,000 tonnes (illustrative).
  3. Cost per tonne. Compare IMFA's EBITDA cost with ₹85,884. Rising cost with a rising price is normal; rising cost with a falling price is the squeeze to worry about.
  4. Compute EBITDA per tonne. EBITDA ÷ tonnes sold. Above ₹30,000 is peak territory; ₹20,000–25,000 is ordinary; below ₹12,000 is a trough.
  5. Check the rupee and forex lines. A ₹1 move in the dollar is worth about ₹1,050 a tonne for IMFA (illustrative); look for mark-to-market gains or losses in other expenses (Q4 FY26 carried about ₹28–32 crore of loss).
  6. For Nava, go to Maamba. Pull MEL's plant load factor, units sold, US-dollar EBITDA and receivables. Q2 and Q4 are maintenance quarters, so compare with the same quarter last year (Q2 FY26: 80.4% PLF, US$25.6 million EBITDA).
  7. Strip the one-offs. Remove ECL write-backs (now minimal), kwacha deferred tax and Nava Global dividends in standalone figures.
  8. Attributable profit. For Nava, find "profit attributable to owners" and the NCI line; expect NCI near 24% of PAT in a normal quarter.
  9. Capex and debt. Check borrowings and cash against March 2026 (Nava net cash ₹1,242 crore; IMFA net debt ₹489 crore), and Phase II drawdown against US$290 million.
  10. Read the guidance. Note any change to IMFA's FY27/FY28 volume guide, the KNR-1 commissioning date and cost savings, and Nava's Phase II date and tax-holiday status. Red flags: another volume cut, a further Phase II slip, or a rise in ZESCO arrears.
In plain terms

For IMFA, the quarter comes down to tonnes, price per tonne and cost per tonne. For Nava, it comes down to how hard Maamba ran, whether ZESCO paid, what the kwacha did to tax, and how much of the profit belonged to the Zambian partner. Ten minutes on those numbers tells you more than an hour on the headline profit.

The ferro-alloy-only lens (indicative)

Stripping Nava's power and coal out of the aggregate gives a better picture of the members' ferro-alloy business. IMFA's FY26 revenue of ₹2,826 crore and operating profit of ₹588 crore, plus Nava's alloy segment revenue net of inter-segment sales (₹1,192.1 crore) and an indicative alloy EBITDA of about ₹41 crore (segment result of ₹31.1 crore plus about ₹10 crore of depreciation), gives about ₹4,018 crore of revenue and ₹629 crore of EBITDA, a 15.7% margin. That is an indicative estimate, not a sum of reported operating profit lines. On this lens IMFA supplies 70% of revenue and 93% of EBITDA. The members together sold 412,573 tonnes of ferro alloys in FY26, about 6–7% of India's ferro-alloy output (indicative).

The balance sheet and valuation, also on one basis: combined net cash of ₹753 crore (Nava ₹1,242 crore net cash, IMFA ₹489 crore net debt); combined enterprise value of ₹19,550 crore, which is 8.5 times FY26 operating profit and 8.1 times trailing operating profit; 9.4 times if ZCCM-IH's book stake in MEL (₹2,018.8 crore) is added to Nava's enterprise value. Capital work in progress totals ₹2,979 crore (Nava ₹2,185 crore, IMFA ₹794 crore), so both are mid-capex. Combined P/E on FY26 profit to owners is 16.7×, and 15.8× on trailing owners' profit of ₹1,284 crore. FY26 dividends were about ₹308 crore, a 1.5% yield (computed).

The market's weighting is revealing. Nava has 60% of combined revenue, 75% of operating profit, 59% of trailing owners' profit and 71% of market value; IMFA has 40%, 25%, 41% and 29%. Per rupee of trailing owners' profit, the market pays 19.0× for Nava and 11.2× for IMFA. It is paying more for a dollar power contract with a Zambian state utility, sovereign risk and all, than for Indian ferrochrome at what it judges to be a price peak. Ownership is similar: promoters hold 50.03% of Nava and 58.69% of IMFA; foreign institutions 9.73% and 2.88%; domestic institutions only 0.47% and 0.84%, so both are almost absent from Indian mutual funds.

In plain terms

Add the two companies together and you get a ₹7,000 crore, 32%-margin business valued at about 8.5 times operating profit with cash in the bank. Strip out the Zambian power and you get a ₹4,000 crore ferro-alloy business at about a 16% margin, almost all of it IMFA. The second number is the one that tells you about ferro alloys; the first tells you about Zambia.

The global lens

IMFA competes with South African and Kazakh integrated giants and Chinese converters. Nava's real peers are African power producers.

World ferrochrome is dominated by a handful of integrated groups and a mass of Chinese converters. The Glencore-Merafe Chrome Venture in South Africa (Glencore 79.5%, Merafe 20.5%) has historically been the world's largest producer; Samancor Chrome, privately owned, is the other South African major. Both own their ore and both now run on the five-year 62 cents/kWh Eskom tariff. Eurasian Resources Group's Kazchrome in Kazakhstan, integrated with the Donskoy mine and cheap Kazakh power, is widely regarded as the low-cost leader. Outokumpu's Tornio ferrochrome in Finland is integrated with its Kemi mine and its own stainless melt shop and is the lowest-carbon ferrochrome in Europe. Turkey, at 9.0 million tonnes the world's second-largest chromite miner (USGS), hosts Yildirim's Eti Krom, which also owns smelters in Sweden. China's smelters in Inner Mongolia, Guangxi and Sichuan, and Tsingshan's own smelting in Indonesia, buy ore and set the marginal cost.

India's position in this map is distinctive. It is the third-largest supplier of ferrochrome to world markets and holds 79 million tonnes of chromite reserves (USGS), mostly in Odisha, but it restricts ore exports, so its chromium leaves the country only as alloy. IMFA describes itself as "among top 6 global producers", and ICRA expects it to be about the sixth largest globally after its expansion. That is a respectable but not dominant position: at 534,000 tonnes of capacity it would still be a fraction of the South African majors. Its edge over them is not scale but proximity to Asian stainless buyers and a cost position that does not depend on Eskom.

For manganese, the price setters are the ore miners: South32 (GEMCO in Australia and Hotazel in South Africa), Eramet (Comilog in Gabon) and Assmang. South Africa mined 7.6 million tonnes of manganese content in 2025 and Gabon 5.0 million, against India's 0.79 million (USGS). Australia's GEMCO resumed in May 2025 after the 2024 cyclone that removed about a tenth of seaborne manganese ore for a year. China's 44% manganese-ore spot price averaged 22% less in January–October 2025 than in 2024 (USGS), which helped silico-manganese costs but also lowered alloy prices. South Africa's manganese and silicon smelters were left out of the 62 cent deal and are lobbying for similar terms; if they win, Indian silico-manganese exporters would face cheaper South African competition too.

Nava's true global peers are African independent power producers rather than smelters (the Indian side of that business is covered in our power generation report): companies such as Globeleq, Azura-Edo, Lekela and Copperbelt Energy Corporation, which is listed in Lusaka. Few are listed. Contracted-power equity in frontier markets typically trades at around 6–9 times EV/EBITDA (an indicative range, not sourced to specific comparables), which is close to Nava's 7.7× on FY26 operating profit, or 8.8× including the minority. The Zambian peers that matter most to Maamba are its competitors for ZESCO's money and dispatch: the state's own hydro at Kariba and Kafue, and the solar plants now being added, including Chisamba's 200 MW in July 2026 and Nava's own 100 MW.

In plain terms

Globally, IMFA is a mid-sized integrated producer competing with South African giants that have just had their power bills halved, and with Chinese smelters that buy South African ore. Nava, seen from abroad, is an African power company with a good plant and a state customer, and should be compared with other African power producers rather than with smelters.

Technology and disruption

The furnaces have changed little in decades. What is changing is the carbon attached to each tonne, and who will buy it.

The biggest technological pressure on ferro alloys is decarbonisation. A tonne of ferrochrome made with coal power can carry 5–8 tonnes of CO₂ on a scope 1 plus 2 basis (illustrative), because both the reductant (coke) and the electricity are fossil. The EU's carbon border tax now prices that carbon for imports into Europe, and the big stainless buyers in Europe, Japan and Korea have their own scope 3 targets for purchased inputs. Producers with hydro or renewable power, such as Finland's Outokumpu and, partly, Kazakhstan's, gain. IMFA's 135 MW of contracted hybrid renewable power, taking it to about 40% non-fossil by mid-2027, is the right direction; Nava's Indian silico-manganese on coal power and Maamba as a coal station carry environmental and financing risk, which shows up in the valuation discount investors apply to coal assets.

Furnace technology itself offers efficiency gains. Closed submerged-arc furnaces fed with sintered pellets, the Outotec route, cut power use to about 3,100–3,300 kWh a tonne; Glencore's Premus process reaches about 2,500–2,800 kWh; older Indian open and semi-closed furnaces run at about 3,800–4,200 kWh (all industry norms, indicative). Indian chromite is mostly friable fines that must be briquetted or pelletised before smelting, which favours integrated producers that can afford agglomeration plants. IMFA's digital and continuous-improvement programmes are incremental; the larger saving it guides, ₹1,500–4,000 a tonne at Kalinganagar, comes from logistics rather than from furnace technology.

On the demand side, two shifts reduce chromium needed per tonne of stainless over time. The 200-series grades popular in India and China contain about 13–16% chromium against 18% for 304 grade. Electric-furnace stainless in Europe and the US takes 50–60% of its chromium from scrap. China and Indonesia, where most growth is, run more primary units, so neither shift is a near-term threat. Indonesia's integration of nickel pig iron, stainless and local ferrochrome smelting is a longer-term risk to Indian ferrochrome exports to China. Batteries are not a chromium story; manganese's battery demand (high-purity manganese sulphate for LMFP and similar chemistries) uses manganese ore or electrolytic manganese, not silico-manganese.

For Nava the technology question is about Zambia's grid. Solar plus storage, including Nava's own 100 MW and the government's Chisamba plant, reduces Zambia's dependence on drought-prone hydro in the short run, which helps coal baseload stay dispatched. In the long run, cheap solar and batteries reduce the need for coal baseload altogether, which would matter when Maamba's Phase I contract comes up for renewal in the late 2030s. Nava says it is studying renewables, storage and small modular reactors, "location agnostic". In India, daytime solar has already compressed merchant power prices, by about 13% year on year in FY26 on the exchange, which is a direct hit to Nava's Indian IPP margins.

In plain terms

The smelting technology is mature; the change is in how clean the power is. Buyers and regulators are starting to charge for the carbon in each tonne, which favours IMFA's shift to renewables and penalises coal-based production, including Nava's Maamba station in the long run.

Bull vs bear

The case for and against the sub-sector, which here means the case for and against each member's very different engine.

The bull case

  • An ore moat written into law. IMFA's leases run to 2049 and 2055 without auction premiums that cost newer miners ₹23,000–35,000 a tonne (illustrative); EBITDA per tonne held at ₹20,000–25,000 through a falling market.
  • Volume nearly doubles. Capacity rises from 384,000 to 534,000 tpa by FY28; on ordinary-year margins that roughly doubles IMFA's EBITDA (illustrative).
  • "Chrome units" support prices. A South African restart diverts ore from China, raising the Chinese cost floor; demand grew 5% in H1 2026.
  • Nava's arrears problem is solved. ZESCO's award is down to US$13.4 Mn; Nava holds ₹1,242 Cr of net cash; Phase II doubles Maamba's capacity on a dollar contract.
  • Cheap on the numbers. Combined 8.5× EV/EBITDA with net cash; IMFA at 11.2× trailing earnings near its 52-week low; a weak rupee (₹94.9/$) helps exporters.

The bear case

  • The price peak is behind. Eskom's 62 c/kWh brings back 700,000–800,000 t of South African supply; the October tender was cut; IMFA's Q1 FY27 ₹35,484 a tonne may be the high-water mark.
  • Ramp-up risk. The FY27 guide is already cut 5%; new tonnes run on grid and renewable power and dilute the integrated cost advantage.
  • Nava's earnings are noisy and shared. ECL write-backs are over, kwacha deferred tax swings the tax line, 24% of profit goes to ZCCM-IH, and Phase II has slipped three times to mid-2027 without a tax holiday.
  • Silico-manganese is oversupplied. India makes about twice what it needs, the EU caps Indian exports, and Nava's alloy margin is 2.6%.
  • Contingent and ESG risks. The ₹328.5 Cr Utkal-C claim, Odisha's possible mineral-land tax, CBAM, single-buyer concentration at ZESCO (with one other customer, 56.9% of Nava's revenue) and the coal discount.

The bull and bear cases for IMFA are mostly about timing. Both agree that integration makes it a low-cost producer and that its volume will rise sharply; they disagree about the price at which the new tonnes will sell. If the "chrome units" argument holds and the Chinese cost floor rises as South African ore stays home, EBITDA per tonne could settle in the mid-to-high twenties of thousands, and FY28 EBITDA could be well above ₹1,000 crore. If South African and Chinese capacity both run, prices could revisit 2024 levels, EBITDA per tonne could fall back towards ₹15,000–20,000, and the doubled volume would deliver only a modest rise in profit. The stock's position near its 52-week low suggests the market currently leans towards the second.

For Nava the debate is about quality rather than direction. The bull sees a de-risked dollar annuity whose capacity doubles in 2027, a net-cash balance sheet and a management that has returned cash through buybacks and dividends. The bear sees a coal plant in a frontier economy whose new contracts are priced lower than the old, whose minority partner takes a quarter of the profit, whose reported earnings are clouded by currency-driven tax, and whose management is spending heavily on Zambian sugar, avocado and solar through related-party contractors. Neither case depends much on the price of silico-manganese, which is the point to remember about Nava.

In plain terms

For IMFA, the argument is about where ferrochrome prices settle once South Africa is back: high enough, and doubled volume doubles profit; too low, and the extra tonnes barely help. For Nava, the argument is about how much a Zambian dollar power contract is worth once the arrears are gone, a quarter of it belongs to someone else and the next plant is a year late.
