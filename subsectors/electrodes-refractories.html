Electrodes & refractories: a spread, a toll and a cash pile

A complete guide to the three NSE 750 companies that sell steelmakers the things they burn through: Graphite India and HEG, India's two graphite electrode makers and the low-cost survivors of a global oligopoly, and RHI Magnesita India, the country's largest refractory maker. Why electrode profit is a spread set in China and the West rather than in India, why India's steel boom helps refractories far more than electrodes, how a US trade case now names India, why most of the electrode makers' recent profit was treasury income, what the HEG demerger changed, and what the battery-anode bets are worth.

00

The one-page view: two industries that share one customer

Electrodes are a global spread business priced in dollars. Refractories are a domestic toll on every tonne of Indian steel. The screener label puts them together; the economics keep them apart.

Every electric arc furnace in the world melts its scrap and sponge iron with an arc struck from the tips of three graphite electrodes, each a machined cylinder of synthetic graphite up to 800 millimetres across and nearly three metres long. The electrodes burn away as they work, at roughly 1.2 to 2.0 kilograms per tonne of steel, so a mill buys them the way a car buys fuel. Every steel plant, electric or blast-furnace, also lines its furnaces, ladles, converters and casting machines with refractories: bricks and castables made from magnesia, alumina and graphite that hold molten metal at 1,600°C and wear out within weeks or months. A steelmaker cannot run a single heat without either product, and that shared customer is the only thing that joins the two halves of this sub-sector.

The three listed companies split cleanly. Graphite India (80,000 tonnes a year of electrode capacity at Durgapur and Nashik) and HEG (100,000 tonnes at a single site in Mandideep, Madhya Pradesh) are India's only graphite electrode makers. Between them they hold about 180 kilotonnes (kt) of operating capacity, which on our reading of company and industry disclosures is roughly 35–45% of all ultra-high-power electrode supply outside China (an indicative estimate). They sell into a global market, export a large part of their output and earn a spread: the price of an electrode minus the price of the needle coke it is made from. RHI Magnesita India, 56% owned by the world's largest refractory group, makes and distributes refractories for Indian steel and cement plants, earns about 90% of its revenue at home and lives on tonnes of steel produced, kilograms of refractory consumed per tonne, and the price per tonne it can charge.

The year to June 2026 shows how differently they behave. In FY26 the three companies together earned ₹9,440 crore of revenue (+12.6%) and ₹1,044 crore of operating profit (11.1%), but only ₹129 crore of reported profit after tax, because RHI Magnesita India wrote off ₹556 crore of goodwill on its 2023 purchase of Dalmia's refractory business. The electrode makers' ₹601 crore of operating profit was smaller than it looked: ₹421 crore of their combined profit came from other income, mostly treasury returns, equal to 70% of operating profit. Then the cycle turned. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) the two electrode makers' operating profit doubled to ₹295 crore (+98%) on revenue up 19%, with both plants running at more than 90% of capacity, while RHI Magnesita India lifted its EBITDA margin to 14.5% even though its shipments fell 5%.

Five forces define this sub-sector, and the report is built around them:

  • The electrode spread. Indian electrode profit is tonnes (capped near 180 kt) times the gap between the electrode price and the needle coke cost, minus conversion. The price is set by Chinese oversupply on one side and Western discipline on the other. In 2026 the West blinked: GrafTech raised prices by $600–1,200 a tonne in March, by at least 30% in September, and is closing its Monterrey plant. ICRA's warning line for Graphite India is a spread below $1,600 a tonne (sections 06 and 07).
  • Electric steel outside China, against falling consumption per tonne. About 60 Mt of new electric arc furnace capacity is due worldwide in 2026–28, but each tonne of steel needs a little less electrode every year. India's own growth is blast-furnace and induction-furnace led: in April–December 2025, basic oxygen furnaces made 42.5% of Indian crude steel, induction furnaces 37.7% and electric arc furnaces only 19.7%, and induction furnaces use no graphite electrodes (section 03).
  • Trade policy that cuts both ways. The US anti-dumping and countervailing duty case on large electrodes names India as well as China. Preliminary deposits are 13.56% for Graphite India and 8.14% for HEG, against roughly 199% for China. Europe keeps Chinese electrodes out; India has had no duty on them since 2018 (deep dive, section 08).
  • Treasury books and capital allocation. Graphite India holds ₹3,939 crore of net cash, about 29% of its market value. Both electrode makers are now turning cash into battery-anode plants, and HEG has legally split its anode and power business from its electrodes (section 09 and deep dives 10 and 11).
  • Refractories as a toll on Indian steel tonnes. RHI Magnesita India rides blast-furnace-heavy Indian capex, but its margin is capped by Chinese magnesia prices and new competitors, its growth in Q1 FY27 came from price rather than volume, and its accounts carry about ₹1,500 crore of write-downs on acquisitions over FY23–FY26 (section 12).

In plain terms

The electrode makers sell a consumable that every electric steel mill needs, and they make money only when electrodes are scarce. In good years (2018) they earned more than half their revenue as operating profit; in bad years (2021, 2024) they lost money on every tonne and lived off the cash they had banked. In 2026 the supply side tightened again because Western producers shut plants and raised prices, and Indian plants are running full. Whether that becomes a real upcycle depends on China, where most of the world's electrode capacity sits idle.

In plain terms

The refractory maker is a different animal. It earns a small, steady toll on every tonne of steel and cement India produces, and India produces more of both every year. The catch is that its raw materials mostly come from China, its customers are a handful of very large steel groups who negotiate hard, and its parent supplies a tenth of what it sells. Its profit swings far less than the electrode makers', but its reported earnings have been distorted by write-downs on acquisitions.

01

From needle coke to furnace lining: how each half makes money

An electrode takes two to six months and four furnaces to make, and almost all of its value is decided by two numbers: the price of needle coke going in and the price of graphite coming out. A refractory is cheaper, faster and sold as a service.

A graphite electrode starts as calcined needle coke, a highly ordered form of petroleum or coal-tar coke whose long, needle-like crystals let the finished graphite carry enormous current without cracking. Roughly 0.8 to 1.0 tonne of needle coke and 0.25 to 0.35 tonne of coal-tar pitch go into each tonne of finished electrode (industry norms; indicative). The coke is crushed, sized, mixed with pitch and extruded into "green" cylinders. These are baked at 800–1,000°C for two to four weeks, impregnated with more pitch, baked again (sometimes three times), and finally graphitised at 2,800–3,000°C in Acheson or lengthwise furnaces, which consumes four to six megawatt-hours of electricity per tonne. The graphite is then machined and threaded and fitted with a pin so that electrodes can be screwed together into a column.

India makes no needle coke commercially. Indian Oil approved a 56,000-tonne needle coker at Paradip in September 2020 at a cost of ₹1,268 crore, but no construction or commissioning has been reported since, so the country imports all of the 150,000-odd tonnes a year its electrode plants need (an indicative estimate at today's output). Suppliers include Phillips 66 in the US and UK, GrafTech's own Seadrift Coke in Texas, Japanese and Korean coal-tar coke makers and a growing number of Chinese producers. Graphite India and HEG buy all of it, usually on annual quantity contracts, while GrafTech and, partly, Resonac own coke sources. That difference matters most in a shortage: in 2018 needle coke prices quadrupled and part of the electrode windfall flowed upstream.

The long cycle has an accounting consequence that dominates quarterly results. Graphite India says a batch takes about two months; HEG carries about six months of work in progress. FY26 inventory days were 321 at Graphite India and 389 at HEG. When electrode prices fall, coke bought at last year's price is still sitting in the furnaces, and the company must either sell below cost or write the inventory down to net realisable value. When prices rise, the opposite happens and old, cheap coke flatters margins. HEG adds a further lag: its order book is full three to four months ahead, so a price increase agreed in July reaches the profit and loss account from October.

Refractories run on a different clock. RHI Magnesita India buys dead-burned and fused magnesia, calcined bauxite and fused alumina, flake graphite, zirconia and silicon carbide, mostly from China or from its parent's own raw-material operations, and mines quartzite in Chhattisgarh and Odisha. These are crushed, graded, mixed with binders and either pressed and fired into bricks (shaped refractories) or blended into castables and gunning mixes (monolithics). Flow-control parts such as nozzles, stoppers and slide-gate plates, which steer molten steel from ladle to tundish to mould, are the high-technology, high-margin end. Increasingly the company sells not tonnes but outcomes: under its "4PRO" model it supplies, installs, monitors and relines, often billed per tonne of steel the customer makes.

In plain terms

An electrode maker is a converter. It buys one carbon material and sells a better one, and its profit is the gap between the two prices less the cost of a lot of electricity and time. A refractory maker is closer to a maintenance contractor. Its customer barely notices the bill but cares enormously about reliability, which lets the best suppliers charge for know-how and service on top of the bricks.

The products also differ in who captures value at each stage of the cycle. In electrode shortages the electrode maker wins first and the needle coke maker second; in gluts the steel mill wins. Integrated producers such as GrafTech, with their own coke, have a hedge in theory but also higher fixed costs and debt. In refractories, value sits with whoever owns the technology and the service relationship: commodity bricks, especially alumina bricks for cement kilns, are a price fight, while flow control, blast-furnace runner management and hearth repairs are sticky. RHI Magnesita India's own disclosures show the split: in Q1 FY27 its growth came from flow control and legacy steel accounts, while the cement brick business lost share to cheaper competitors during FY26. The rest of this report follows those two different engines.

02

Size: a ₹9,440 crore group inside a global market

India is the largest electrode-making country outside China and the fastest-growing major refractory market. Neither fact makes the listed companies large, and both businesses are smaller today than at their peak.

The global graphite electrode market is about 1.1–1.3 million tonnes a year, of which China consumes perhaps 0.4–0.5 Mt and the rest of the world 0.7–0.8 Mt (indicative estimates built from roughly 520–560 Mt of electric arc furnace steel at 1.6–1.8 kg a tonne plus ladle-furnace use). At 2025–26 prices of $2,200–4,000 a tonne that is a $4–6 billion market, against more than $15 billion at the 2018 peak (indicative). Global capacity is far larger than demand: perhaps 1.8–2.2 Mt in total, with 1.2–1.5 Mt in China alone after the 2018–23 build-out. The ultra-high-power (UHP) segment that matters for large furnaces outside China is about 400–500 kt on HEG's framing, and HEG argues that Chinese producers supply far less of it than the headline capacity suggests.

India's two producers are a big share of that non-China UHP market. HEG's Mandideep plant is the largest single-site electrode plant in the world at 100 kt; the next largest single plants are about 70 kt each. Graphite India runs 80 kt across two Indian plants. Graphite India used to quote 98 kt, but the extra 18 kt was a German plant that stopped producing electrodes in FY2022-23 and is in liquidation, so that figure should no longer be used even as nameplate. GrafTech, the Western price-setter, is cutting to about 127 kt by closing Monterrey, and Tokai Carbon has cut its Japanese and European capacity from 56 kt to 32 kt. On these numbers the two Indian companies hold roughly 35–45% of non-China UHP capacity (indicative), well above the quarter often quoted.

Refractories are a bigger and steadier market. The global market is about US$38–48 billion and roughly 57 Mt a year (2025), with China making perhaps 60–65% of the volume, according to industry estimates quoted in RHI Magnesita India's FY26 annual report. The same report puts India at about US$2.6 billion in 2025 (roughly ₹22,000–23,000 crore at ₹85–88 to the dollar), rising to about US$3.5 billion by 2034, and calls India the highest-growth major refractory market, with 6–8% annual growth forecast. On that whole-market denominator RHI Magnesita India's FY26 revenue of ₹4,020 crore is a share of about 18% (an indicative estimate); the company's own "30%+" claim refers to the steel and cement segments it addresses. The listed company's revenue has doubled since FY22, mostly by acquisition: the Indian RHI entities were merged in 2022 and Dalmia Bharat's refractory business was bought in 2023.

In volume terms India consumes perhaps 1.6–2.2 Mt of refractories a year (indicative: about 168 Mt of crude steel at 8–10 kg a tonne, plus cement, glass and non-ferrous). RHI Magnesita India shipped 523,396 tonnes in FY26, up 4.7%; the "+9.4%" sometimes quoted is its revenue growth, not volume. In plain terms

India matters far more to the world's electrode industry than electrodes matter to India. The two Indian companies make a large share of the world's high-grade electrodes outside China, but their combined revenue is about the size of one mid-cap industrial company. Refractories are the reverse: a large and growing domestic market, of which one listed company holds a minority share once you count every small supplier and importer.

03

Arcs abroad, blast furnaces at home: where demand comes from

Electrode demand follows electric arc furnace tonnes, most of which are outside India. Refractory demand follows all steel tonnes, and India's new tonnes come mostly from blast furnaces, which eat more refractory per tonne than any other route.

Electrode demand has a simple equation: tonnes of electric arc furnace (EAF) steel multiplied by specific consumption in kilograms per tonne, plus a smaller amount for ladle furnaces at blast-furnace plants. The first term is growing outside China. About 28–30% of the world's crude steel now comes from electric furnaces; the share is about 70% in the US, mid-40s in the European Union, about 70% in Turkey and 85–90% across the gas-rich Middle East, where direct-reduced iron feeds arc furnaces. Decarbonisation pushes the same way: a blast furnace and converter emit about 2.0–2.3 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of steel, a scrap-fed arc furnace 0.4–0.7 tonnes. Europe's carbon border adjustment mechanism moved into its definitive phase on 1 January 2026, and its new steel import regime from 1 July 2026 cut import quotas by 47% and set a 50% out-of-quota duty, which supports European mill utilisation. US steel output was up 6% in the first eight months of 2026 behind 50% Section 232 tariffs.

Management at HEG cites roughly 60 Mt of new EAF capacity worldwide in 2026–28 and an OECD figure of about 71 Mt by the end of 2028; HEG's annual report talks of more than 100 Mt of new EAF capacity outside China by 2030, with 20 Mt operating and 60 Mt under construction. At 1.5–2.0 kg a tonne that is 150–200 kt of extra electrode demand, if it all runs. The second term works against it. Specific consumption has fallen from 6–7 kg a tonne in the 1980s to 1.2–2.0 kg on modern UHP furnaces, through better graphite, computer-regulated arcs, DC furnaces and foamy-slag practice, and keeps falling by perhaps 1–2% a year. Net of that, electrode demand outside China grows at an indicative 1–3% a year: real, but slow.

India is the exception that primers tend to gloss over. Indian crude steel output rose 10.7% to 168.4 Mt in FY26, finished steel consumption rose 7.6% to 163.7 Mt, and capacity is about 220 Mt against a National Steel Policy target of 300 Mt by 2030–31. But the growth is coming from blast furnaces. Ministry of Steel provisional data for April–December 2025 show basic oxygen furnaces making 52.96 Mt (42.5%), induction furnaces 47.01 Mt (37.7%) and electric arc furnaces 24.59 Mt (19.7%), against 21% for EAFs in FY25. Induction furnaces melt scrap and sponge iron with electromagnetic fields and use no graphite electrodes. On that basis Indian EAF output is about 33 Mt a year and domestic electrode demand about 50–60 kt (indicative), against 180 kt of Indian capacity. Most of the big projects under way, at JSW, Tata Steel Kalinganagar, AM/NS Hazira, SAIL and JSPL Angul, are blast-furnace routes, so India's EAF share may fall further before it rises.

For refractories, India's mix is good news. A blast-furnace plant lines its blast furnace hearth, torpedo ladles, converters and RH degassers as well as its ladles and casting machines, so it consumes more refractory per tonne than a mini-mill. India's 12% safeguard duty on flat steel imports (from 21 April 2025, stepping down to 11.5% and then 11% until 20 April 2028) supports blast-furnace utilisation at home. RHI Magnesita India's demand equation is crude steel tonnes times kilograms per tonne (about 8–12 kg for modern Indian plants, indicative) times its price per kilogram times its share, plus cement, glass, non-ferrous and DRI-pellet niches. Cement is perhaps 10–15% of its sales (indicative; the company does not disclose an end-market split), with kiln relines mostly annual. In Q1 FY27 Indian crude steel output rose 3.0% to 42.1 Mt, yet RHI Magnesita India's shipments fell 5%, which says the demand is there but the share and the pricing are contested.

In plain terms

India's steel boom is a refractory boom far more than an electrode boom. Nearly four-fifths of the country's new steel comes from blast furnaces and induction furnaces, neither of which burns graphite electrodes. The Indian electrode makers therefore depend on arc furnaces in the Gulf, Europe, the US and Turkey, and on what China does with its idle plants. The refractory maker, by contrast, sells more into every new blast furnace built in Odisha or Jharkhand.

04

Where ₹100 goes: coke and power on one side, magnesia on the other

An electrode maker spends more than half its cash cost on one imported input and another sixth on electricity. A refractory maker spends close to 60% on minerals. Neither controls the price of what it buys.

HEG's standalone accounts, which are the closest public proxy for the new electrode company HEG Limited, show the cost structure plainly. In FY26 it earned ₹2,568.5 crore of revenue and spent ₹1,063.4 crore (41.4%) on raw materials including inventory changes, ₹369.5 crore (14.4%) on power and fuel, ₹123.5 crore (4.8%) on employees and ₹607.2 crore (23.6%) on other expenses, leaving core EBITDA of about ₹405 crore (15.8%) before any other income. But the "other expenses" line hides a non-operating item: about ₹220–230 crore of fair-value loss on HEG's 9.98% stake in GrafTech, booked in the March 2026 quarter (an estimate from the jump in that quarter's other expenses to ₹361 crore from a run-rate near ₹130 crore). Strip that out and FY26 core EBITDA was nearer ₹630 crore, about 24% (indicative). In Q1 FY27 the same structure produced a cleaner 23.7% core margin: raw materials 36.9%, power 14.7%, employees 4.8% and other expenses 20.0% of revenue.

Graphite India does not publish a cost split in the same way, and its profit is complicated by a steel-bar business (Powmex), a treasury subsidiary and, until recently, German specialities units. A composite built from its screener cost structure suggests raw materials of roughly 45–50% of revenue, power and fuel 12–15%, employees 7–8% and other costs in the low-to-mid 20s, leaving 7.1% operating profit in FY26 (an indicative composite, not a company disclosure). Graphite India is mostly a grid-power buyer: its own hydro, wind and solar generated 59.83 million units in FY26, a small fraction of the 350–450 gigawatt-hours an 80 kt graphitising plant needs (indicative). HEG, by contrast, runs a 66 MW captive thermal plant and 13.5 MW of hydro at Mandideep, which both stay with the electrode company after the demerger.

Both businesses are price-takers on their main input. 05

Who makes it: a global oligopoly, an Indian duopoly and a fragmented toll market

Outside China, six companies make almost all the world's large electrodes and two of them are Indian. Refractories are the opposite: one multinational subsidiary with a large but contested share, several other multinational and Indian names, and a long tail of small makers and Chinese importers.

ICRA describes the electrode industry as concentrated, with capacity in the hands of "five to six major players globally". Outside China the top six (Resonac, GrafTech, HEG, Graphite India, Tokai Carbon and a group of smaller makers including Russia's EPM and Japan's Nippon Carbon) hold an estimated 85–90% of capacity. India itself is a duopoly: ICRA calls HEG and Graphite India "among the only two graphite electrode manufacturers in the country", and there are no unlisted Indian rivals of any size, only small carbon-electrode makers serving ferro-alloy furnaces. China is the fringe that sets the floor: dozens of producers led by Fangda Carbon, Kaiyuan Oriental, Jilin Carbon and others, with perhaps 1.2–1.5 Mt of capacity (an unverified industry estimate) running at 50–60%. China is the marginal exporter and the price-setter in high-power and smaller UHP grades; it shipped 25,344 tonnes of UHP electrodes in April 2026, down 30.8% year on year as the Gulf war disrupted its largest export market.

The Western survivors are in poor health. GrafTech, the former UCAR and the integrated owner of Seadrift needle coke, had gross debt of $1,225 million and liquidity of $242 million at September 2026, made a net loss of $46.8 million in Q3 2026 and is closing its Monterrey plant. Resonac, formed from Showa Denko and the electrode business it bought from SGL in 2017, has plants in Japan, the US, Germany, Spain, Austria and Malaysia and has been reviewing the business. Tokai Carbon cut its Japanese and European capacity from 56 kt to 32 kt by July 2025. HEG's management says that apart from its own 15 kt expansion, no additional electrode capacity has been announced "in the western world". That is a structurally tighter supply side than in 2019–25, and it is why the US companies are using trade law to defend what is left.

06

The electrode spread: set in China and the West, earned in India

Three years of falling prices ended in 2026 when Western producers shut plants and raised prices. The question for FY27 is whether the increase sticks after needle coke catches up.

The single most useful number for an electrode maker is the spread: the realised price of a tonne of electrode minus the cost of the needle coke that goes into it. It is not a margin. Conversion costs of roughly $1,000–1,500 a tonne (power, pitch, labour, depreciation; indicative) still have to come out of it. ICRA's rating of Graphite India makes the point precisely: its downgrade trigger is "an industry spread between graphite electrode and needle coke prices below $1,600 per tonne on a sustained basis". On indicative 2025–26 numbers (realisations of $3,200–3,800 and coke of $1,000–1,500 per tonne of electrode) the spread was about $1,800–2,400. That is above the trigger but nowhere near the $6,000–10,000 of 2018 (an indicative range, depending on spot or contract pricing), and it is thin once conversion is paid.

Two price series coexist and do not match. Chinese domestic UHP electrodes sold at RMB 16,200–17,756 a tonne including 13% VAT in May–June 2026, roughly $2,200–2,450, and BigMint/Mysteel expected softer prices into June. Western and Indian export prices are far higher: GrafTech realised about $3,900 a tonne in Q3 2026, down 7% year on year and flat on the quarter. Indian realisations are not disclosed, but HEG's FY26 revenue on roughly 90 kt implies about ₹2.85 lakh a tonne (~$3,280 at FY26's ~₹87 per dollar) and Graphite India's electrode revenue on 72–76 kt implies ₹3.1–3.2 lakh (~$3,600–3,700). All of these Indian per-tonne figures are illustrative estimates. The gap between China and the rest is held open by grade (China's exports are mostly smaller-diameter and high-power grades), by qualification at large furnaces and by trade walls.

07

Same plant, ten times the profit: the 2018 super-cycle, dissected Deep dive

In FY19 Graphite India and HEG earned ₹9,682 crore of operating profit between them. In FY26 they earned ₹601 crore from much the same plants. Understanding why is the key to judging whether 2026 is the start of another cycle.

The two numbers are almost unbelievable side by side. In FY19 Graphite India reported revenue of ₹7,858 crore, operating profit of ₹5,020 crore (a 64% margin) and profit after tax of ₹3,396 crore, with a return on capital employed of 119%. HEG reported revenue of ₹6,591 crore, operating profit of ₹4,662 crore (71%) and profit of ₹3,026 crore, a return on capital of 139%. Seven years later the same companies, with HEG's plant larger (100 kt against 80 kt then) and Graphite India's German plant shut, earned ₹202 crore and ₹399 crore of operating profit. The combined operating profit fell sixteen-fold and profit after tax by more than twelve times. Nothing physical changed enough to explain it. The price of graphite did.

The super-cycle had four causes. First, China closed about 100 Mt of low-grade induction-furnace capacity in 2016–17 and replaced part of it with electric arc furnaces, adding demand for electrodes at the very moment, second, environmental inspections shut Chinese electrode and needle-coke plants. Third, synthetic graphite for lithium-ion battery anodes started competing for the same premium needle coke. Fourth, the West had spent 2015–16 closing plants after a long slump in which electrode prices sat around $2,000–2,500 a tonne: SGL sold its business to Showa Denko and Brookfield bought GrafTech. Spot prices for 600 mm UHP electrodes went from about $2,500 to $10,000–15,000 a tonne, and contract prices settled near $8,000–10,000 (approximate). GrafTech floated in April 2018 on the back of three-to-five-year take-or-pay contracts at about $9,700 a tonne.

The 2018 boom happened because China suddenly needed electrodes and could not make them, while the West had just shut plants. 08

Trade walls cut both ways: the US case, Europe, China and India's open door Deep dive

For a decade trade remedies protected Western electrode prices from China, and India sat outside them. In 2026 the US case named India too. The duties are modest for India and enormous for China, which is why the net effect may still be positive.

Trade policy matters to this sub-sector because the world's electrode prices are held apart by duties. Europe has imposed anti-dumping duties on Chinese graphite electrode systems since 2017 and extended them in 2022 (Implementing Regulation 2022/558) after an expiry review. The US has had an anti-dumping order on small-diameter Chinese electrodes since 2009. India itself once protected its two makers: the Directorate General of Trade Remedies opened a case on applications by HEG and Graphite India in May 2013, a duty on Chinese electrodes of all diameters was imposed on 13 February 2015, and it was revoked on 6 September 2018, at the height of the boom, after a mid-term review. No Indian duty applies today and no new case has been found, so Chinese electrodes can enter India freely. India's small EAF market is mostly served by the two local makers, but a determined Chinese push into it would meet no wall.

The big change is the US investigation into large-diameter electrodes (over 425 mm, plus pins), filed by Resonac Graphite America and Tokai Carbon GE as the "LDGE Fair Trade Coalition". The International Trade Commission instituted the case on 27 February 2026, and Commerce opened anti-dumping (AD) and countervailing (anti-subsidy, CVD) investigations into both China and India on 20 March 2026. The petition alleged dumping margins of 42.59–73.40% for India. The preliminary findings were far lower. In the CVD case, published on 30 July 2026, Commerce set 3.68% for Graphite India, 6.99% for HEG and 5.87% for all other Indian exporters, against 103.49% for China on adverse facts available. In the AD case, published on 30 September 2026, it set a margin of 12.22% for Graphite India (a cash deposit of 9.88% after offsetting export subsidies), 4.97% for HEG (deposit 1.15%) and 6.95% for others (3.53%); China's country-wide rate was 98.79% (deposit 95.61%).

How much does it cost? On a $3,900 tonne, Graphite India's 13.56% combined deposit is roughly $530 and HEG's 8.14% about $320 (an illustrative calculation). Since 24 July 2026 Indian goods have also faced a 10% US tariff under Section 301 on top of most-favoured-nation duties, a measure under legal challenge. The exposure is limited: HEG says the US is "less than about 10%" of its business and that it "will retain America" because US prices are higher than elsewhere; Graphite India says the impact on its performance "remains limited" given its diversified export portfolio. Official US import data showed only 792 tonnes worth $2.25 million under the main tariff line from India in 2025, but that understates the trade, because the case also covers other lines and origin is set by where the electrode was graphitised. A third front opened in 2026: Graphite India's annual report says Brazil has also initiated an anti-dumping investigation on graphite electrodes, and the company is participating.

In plain terms

The US case is a tax on Indian electrodes entering America, but a far heavier tax on Chinese ones. An Indian electrode now pays a deposit of 8–14% at the US border; a Chinese electrode pays about 199%. For a US mill, that makes Indian supply the cheapest legal alternative to US and Japanese-owned plants. The risk is less the US itself, which is under a tenth of HEG's sales, than the precedent: once one market has found Indian electrodes subsidised or dumped, others (Brazil has already started) may follow.

The policy picture on the refractory side is quieter but not empty. China has required export licences for graphite since December 2023 and already licenses fused magnesia exports (HS 25199010) through its commerce ministry, so the raw materials for magnesia-carbon bricks can be squeezed by Beijing at short notice; environmental curbs on magnesia in Liaoning province have done the same in the past. New Chinese supply keeps coming, such as Donghe New Materials' 200 kt fused magnesia project in Inner Mongolia announced in July 2026. Basic customs duty on refractory bricks is about 7.5–10% (approximate), and the anti-dumping duties India imposed on Chinese magnesia-carbon bricks in the late 2010s have lapsed (unverified). India's steel safeguard duty (Notification 02/2025-Customs (SG), 12% from 21 April 2025, then 11.5% and 11% to April 2028, with floor prices exempting high-value imports) protects domestic flat-steel output and therefore refractory demand.

Two other rules shape the long run. India's Green Steel Taxonomy (12 December 2024) defines green steel as below 2.2 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent per tonne of finished steel, with star bands below 1.6, 1.6–2.0 and 2.0–2.2 tonnes, and the carbon credit trading scheme is setting intensity targets for steel. Both favour scrap-based EAFs and gas-based direct reduction over coal-based DRI and induction furnaces, which in time would add electrode demand at home. And for multinational subsidiaries such as RHI Magnesita India, royalty payments to a parent are freely permitted under foreign-exchange rules but are related-party transactions under SEBI's listing regulations, which require shareholder approval for brand and royalty payments above 5% of turnover. RHI Magnesita India's royalty, at 0.9% of revenue, is well below that line; section 12 looks at the larger flows.

Treasury books: when other income outearns the furnace

The electrode makers banked the 2018 windfall. In the lean years since, the return on that cash has been a large share of their profit, and the headline "EBITDA" they report includes it.

The single most important definitional point in this sub-sector is that both electrode makers' own "EBITDA" includes other income. Graphite India's Q1 FY27 presentation carries a footnote stating that operating profit "is including Other Income". So its reported FY26 EBITDA of ₹375 crore (13.1%) is screener's operating profit of ₹202 crore plus ₹174 crore of other income, and its Q1 FY27 EBITDA of ₹241 crore (28.6%) is ₹144 crore of operating profit plus ₹97 crore of other income. Why does this matter for investors? Three reasons. First, valuation: Graphite India's 61× trailing price-earnings multiple is on a profit that is mostly treasury income, so it says little about the electrode business. An investor should value the cash separately and then ask what the electrode plant is worth. Second, sensitivity to rates and markets: fair-value gains on mutual funds and bonds fall when yields rise, as they did after the Reserve Bank raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, and HEG's March 2026 quarter showed how a foreign equity stake can swing reported operating profit by more than ₹200 crore. Third, capital allocation: the cash is finally being spent. Graphite India's capex rose to ₹252 crore in FY26 from ₹166 crore, it is adding electrode capacity, it has committed to a ₹4,330 crore phased anode project and it has raised its stake in the battery start-up Godi India to 52.68%. In plain terms

For most of the last five years, Graphite India and HEG were partly electrode makers and partly investment funds. When electrode prices were poor, interest and gains on their savings paid most of the bills and the dividend. That made their reported profits look steadier than the furnaces were. Any reader comparing their margins with a rival's, or valuing them on earnings, has to take the treasury income out first.

In plain terms

Cash that sits in a fund earns perhaps 6–8% a year. Cash put into a new electrode line or an anode plant could earn far more, or far less. Both companies have decided to stop being savers. Whether that improves returns depends on execution and on Chinese prices, and the decisions are being taken at a time when the electrode cycle has only just turned.

The anode bet: turning electrode cash into battery graphite Deep dive

Both electrode makers are building synthetic graphite anode plants for lithium-ion batteries. The chemistry is adjacent to what they know; the market is dominated by China, priced by China and gated by long customer qualification.

A lithium-ion battery's anode is mostly graphite: either natural flake that has been mined, spheroidised and coated, or synthetic graphite made from petroleum or needle-type coke and graphitised at about 3,000°C, much as an electrode is. Synthetic anode material demands the same competences the electrode makers already have: sourcing premium coke, baking and graphitising at scale, and controlling purity. It also competes for the same feedstock. In 2017–18 battery demand for premium coke was one reason needle coke prices quadrupled, and China's anode market has been tilting towards synthetic graphite since. An Indian anode plant is therefore both a diversification and, at the margin, a competitor for its parent's input.

HEG moved first. Its subsidiary TACC (now under HEG Advanced Materials) is building a 20 kt anode plant for ₹2,200 crore, about ₹110 crore per kilotonne (an indicative ratio). Forty per cent of the capex had been spent by June 2026, 90% will be by the end of FY27 and the rest in Q1 FY28, when production is due to start at 40–50% utilisation. Expanding to 30 kt would take total spending to about ₹3,100 crore, 70% debt-funded (an SBI facility of ₹1,240 crore is in place), with a further 10 kt for about ₹800 crore by 2029 and a goal of 60 kt by FY32. Management targets anode revenue of ₹600–700 crore in the first year, more than ₹1,200 crore in the second and ₹1,500–1,600 crore in the third, at about 35% EBITDA. The NDR presentation lists offtake memoranda for more than 40 kt with a European carmaker and more than 70 kt with a North American automaker, all non-binding. TACC's managing director said about 70% of capacity would go on three-to-five-year contracts with tier-one buyers.

There is a strategic logic beyond diversification. Electrode demand grows slowly and the electrode makers cannot add much capacity without depressing prices; HEG says it has "no more headroom beyond 94% or 95%" utilisation until its expansion. Battery-grade graphite is one of the few adjacent markets where India could plausibly build a supplier outside China for Western and Indian cell makers. In plain terms

Battery anodes are made in a similar way to electrodes, from similar coke, in similar furnaces, so the Indian electrode makers have a real head start over a new entrant. But China makes almost all the world's anodes and sells them cheaply, and carmakers take a long time to approve a new supplier. HEG's plan only works if Western buyers pay more for anodes that do not come from China. Graphite India has been slower and is still largely funding its plans from the cash pile.

In plain terms

Per tonne of capacity, an anode plant costs two to four times as much as an electrode expansion. That means a small miss on price or volume can turn a good-looking project into a poor one. Investors should watch three things: whether TACC starts on time in Q1 FY28, whether the non-binding offtake agreements become signed contracts, and what Chinese anode prices do in the meantime.

Refractories: a toll on every Indian tonne, with a ceiling on the rate

RHI Magnesita India rides India's blast-furnace build-out with a services-led model. Its recent growth came from price and mix rather than volume, a third of what it ships is not made in its own plants, and three years of write-downs have obscured steady cash generation.

RHI Magnesita India's revenue equation is easy to write and hard to forecast: tonnes of Indian steel (and cement, glass, non-ferrous metal) produced, times kilograms of refractory consumed per tonne, times the price per kilogram, times the company's share, plus service contracts billed per tonne of customer output. Each term moves differently. Steel tonnes grow at 7–10% a year. Kilograms per tonne fall slowly as linings last longer and monolithics replace bricks. Price follows Chinese raw materials with a lag and is contested in every tender. Share depends on technology, service and relationships at a handful of large steel groups. In FY26 the company shipped 523,396 tonnes (+4.7%) at an average ₹76,805 a tonne (+4.5%), giving revenue of ₹4,019.95 crore (+9.4%). Its shipments grew about six points more slowly than Indian crude steel output (+10.7%), which says the company lost share on a volume basis, most visibly in cement, where its FY26 presentation reported lost share in alumina bricks and mixes "due to commoditization and competition behavior".

Q1 FY27 sharpened the pattern. Revenue rose 6% to ₹1,014 crore, operating EBITDA rose 42% to ₹147 crore and the margin widened 3.7 points to 14.5%, but shipments fell 5% to 122 kt (the "+5%" on the press-release cover is quarter on quarter). Revenue per tonne shipped jumped about 11.7% to roughly ₹83,100 (an estimate from revenue and tonnes). The finance director was candid that "most of the price increases were nothing but a war surcharge" and that the improvement was "primarily driven by the product mix". Growth came from the legacy "IN" business, especially flow control at the Bhiwadi and Jamshedpur plants, where share at one large steel group "has gone up almost double in the last six months". The ex-Dalmia "IR" business was "mostly flat". Material cost fell to 58.2% of revenue from 62.5%, helped by the price increases and softer magnesia earlier in the year, but magnesite prices then rose 6–8% in the two months to mid-August.

Five decades, four cycles: how the industry got here

The Indian electrode makers were built with Western licences in the 1970s, became exporters in the 1990s and 2000s, and have lived through a slump, a boom, a bust and a long grind since 2015. The refractory company is a 2010s roll-up.

Graphite India was incorporated in 1974 by the Bangur group with the US firm Great Lakes Carbon; HEG's Mandideep plant started in 1977, which its own description calls the most recent global entrant. Both relied on Western technology, grew into exporters as Indian steel was too small to absorb their output, and survived the consolidation that turned UCAR into GrafTech and folded SGL's electrode business into Showa Denko (now Resonac). Graphite India bought a German electrode plant along the way; HEG built the largest single-site plant in the world and a captive power station to feed it.

The modern era has had four cycles. The first was the slump of 2015–16, when China's steel glut pushed electrode prices to about $2,000–2,500 a tonne and Western producers closed plants. India responded with an anti-dumping duty on Chinese electrodes in February 2015. The second was the 2017–19 super-cycle described in section 07, which produced FY19 profits of ₹3,396 crore at Graphite India and ₹3,026 crore at HEG and ended with India revoking its duty in September 2018. The third was the 2019–21 bust, with operating losses in FY21 at both companies. The fourth was the long grind of 2022–25, a restocking burst followed by three years of Chinese oversupply, inventory write-downs and treasury-supported profits, which may have ended with the 2026 price turn. Over the same period RHI Magnesita India was assembled through mergers and acquisitions and then spent three years writing down what it had bought.

The electrode business has made almost all its lifetime profit in a handful of boom years, and spent the rest surviving. Three lessons carry forward. First, booms are short and caused by supply shocks, usually in China; demand alone has never produced one. Second, the companies that saved the windfall (Graphite India, HEG) outlasted the one that levered up (GrafTech), and cash has been their competitive advantage in every trough since. Reading the sector: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Electrode makers do not disclose the numbers that matter most (tonnes and price per tonne), and both report an "EBITDA" that includes treasury income. The refractory maker discloses tonnes but ships far more than it makes. This section shows how to rebuild the economics from what is published, and where each number misleads.

The equation, and who controls each term

Every electrode maker's profit can be written as one line. Capacity in tonnes, times utilisation, gives tonnes sold. Each tonne earns its realisation minus the needle coke in it minus conversion cost (power, pitch, labour, maintenance, freight and overheads). That is core EBITDA. Add treasury income, subtract depreciation, interest and tax, and you reach reported profit. Management controls utilisation (within a narrow band, because the plants already run at 90–100%), conversion cost (through power sourcing, yields and labour productivity) and the size of the treasury book. It does not control the realisation, which is set by Chinese export volumes and Western price discipline, or the needle coke price, which is set by a small group of coke makers and by competing battery-anode demand. Capacity changes only every few years.

In plain terms

For an electrode maker, almost everything that matters happens in one term: the gap between what an electrode sells for and what its coke costs. The plant is already full, so volume cannot rescue a bad price. For a refractory maker, four smaller terms each move a little, and the company can influence two of them (price and share) through technology and service.

Capacity and utilisation

Utilisation is production divided by nameplate capacity. It is the first number to check because it tells you whether volume can grow. Graphite India's standalone utilisation was 82% in Q1 FY26, 104% in Q4 FY26 and 97% in Q1 FY27, all on its 80 kt Indian base; HEG ran at 90% in FY26 and "more than 90%" in Q1 FY27, and guides 90–95% for FY27 with "no more headroom beyond 94% or 95%". GrafTech ran at 64% in Q3 2026. Worked example: a 100 kt plant moving from 85% to 95% sells 10,000 more tonnes; at a contribution of about ₹1.2 lakh a tonne (price less coke, materials, power and freight; illustrative) that adds ₹120 crore of EBITDA without any price change. What drives it up: tight supply, Chinese export disruptions, long-term contracts. What drives it down: price wars, customer destocking (FY20–21), outages. Benchmarks: above 90% is good, 75–90% average, below 70% weak.

The definition trap is the denominator. Graphite India used to quote 98 kt, which included an 18 kt German plant that stopped producing electrodes in FY2022-23 and is in liquidation; on that base its utilisation would look about 18% lower. The company now says 80,000 tonnes across Durgapur and Nashik, and that is the right base. Graphite India's annual report also gives production of 92,889 tonnes in FY26, but that figure covers "graphite electrodes and other miscellaneous carbon and graphite products", so it is not electrode tonnage. For RHI Magnesita India, utilisation is about 66% (339.7 kt produced against 512 kt installed), but that is partly by design, because a third of what it sells is bought in; low own-plant utilisation is a feature of its sourcing model, not only a sign of weak demand.

Realisation per tonne

Realisation is electrode revenue divided by tonnes sold, in rupees or dollars. Neither Indian company discloses it; HEG declined on its call ("we should not be divulging these kinds of numbers"). It can be estimated. HEG's FY26 revenue of ₹2,569 crore on roughly 90 kt (volume up 20% at 90% utilisation) implies about ₹2.85 lakh a tonne, about $3,280 at FY26's ~₹87 per dollar; FY25's ₹2,153 crore on about 75 kt implies ₹2.87 lakh, so the price was flat in rupees and down 3–4% in dollars. Graphite India's Graphite & Carbon segment revenue of ₹2,593 crore on 72–76 kt implies up to ₹3.5 lakh a tonne, but after removing an estimated ₹250–300 crore of non-electrode carbon products the electrode realisation is nearer ₹3.1–3.2 lakh (about $3,600–3,700). All of these are illustrative. GrafTech, which does disclose, realised about $3,900 in Q3 2026.

What drives realisation: the global supply balance, the grade mix (larger UHP electrodes command more), the export mix (HEG's heavier Middle East exposure explains part of its lower figure), and the rupee, since export prices are in dollars. Benchmarks: above $5,000 a tonne is good (it last happened in 2018–19), $3,500–5,000 average, below $3,500 weak. The trap is the "price increase" headline: an announced 30% increase from a Western producer is not a realised increase at an Indian one, and HEG's order book means its realisation lags announcements by three to four months.

Needle coke cost and the spread

The spread is realisation minus the needle coke cost per tonne of electrode. Formula: spread = realisation − (coke price × tonnes of coke per tonne of electrode). Worked example: a $3,500 electrode made with 0.95 tonne of $1,450 coke has a spread of $3,500 − $1,378 = about $2,120. Conversion of $1,000–1,500 still has to come out of that, so the spread is not a margin. ICRA's downgrade trigger for Graphite India is a spread below $1,600 a tonne on a sustained basis, which at typical conversion costs is close to breakeven. Benchmarks (indicative): above $3,000 good, $1,600–3,000 average, below $1,600 weak. On indicative 2025–26 numbers the industry spread was about $1,800–2,400.

What drives it up: Western closures and price discipline, Chinese export disruptions, trade duties that keep Chinese supply out, and falling coke prices. What drives it down: Chinese export surges, battery-anode demand bidding up coke, and oil-price spikes (petroleum needle coke is made from refinery decant oil). In 2026 both sides rose: electrode prices turned up and needle coke rose $200–300 a tonne since the war, lifting HEG's total cost of making an electrode by 10–15%. The definition traps: there is no free published needle coke price series (the Chinese daily series are paywalled), the companies buy on annual quantity contracts so their cost lags spot, and Chinese and Western electrode prices differ by more than $1,000 a tonne, so "the spread" depends on which electrode price you use.

In plain terms

The spread is the electrode maker's raw material margin. If it falls below about $1,600 a tonne, a typical Indian plant earns little or nothing after paying for power and labour; above $3,000 it earns very well. The industry has spent three years in the lower half of that range. The 2026 price increases would lift it, but only if they stick and only by as much as they exceed the rise in coke costs.

EBITDA per tonne

EBITDA per tonne is core EBITDA divided by tonnes sold. It combines price, cost and efficiency into one number that can be compared across companies and over time. Worked example: HEG's standalone core EBITDA of about ₹161 crore in Q1 FY27 on about 23 kt is roughly ₹70,000 a tonne (about $740); in FY26, about ₹405 crore on 90 kt was ₹45,000 a tonne (about $520), or nearer ₹70,000 excluding the GrafTech fair-value loss. Graphite India's FY26 operating profit of ₹202 crore on about 74 kt was roughly ₹27,000 a tonne (about $310); its Q1 FY27 ₹144 crore on about 19.4 kt was roughly ₹74,000, flattered by inventory provision releases and by profit from its steel and other segments. GrafTech earned about $300 a tonne in Q3 2026. All Indian figures are illustrative.

Benchmarks: above $1,500 a tonne is good, $600–1,500 average, below $600 weak. In FY19 the Indian makers earned perhaps $8,000 a tonne. The definition trap is the numerator: using company "EBITDA" that includes treasury income would add ₹20,000–50,000 a tonne of interest and investment gains to the electrode economics. Always use core EBITDA, and remove one-offs such as the GrafTech fair-value swing.

Core margin against company "EBITDA"

Core EBITDA margin is operating profit before other income divided by revenue. For screener data it is the "OPM" line; for company presentations you must subtract other income from their "EBITDA". The trap is in both directions. Including treasury income overstates the furnace; but screener's operating profit can also understate it when non-operating items sit in expenses, as HEG's GrafTech loss did in Q4 FY26. The right approach is to start from screener's operating profit, add back clearly non-operating items in expenses, and never add other income.

In plain terms

When an Indian electrode maker says "29% EBITDA margin", roughly five to twelve percentage points of it is interest and investment gains on its savings. The furnace itself earned about 17–24% in the June 2026 quarter. That is still well above the 6% GrafTech earned, so the Indian cost advantage is real, but it is smaller than the headline suggests.

Export share

Export share is revenue from customers outside India divided by total revenue. It tells you how exposed a company is to the global price, to freight, to the rupee and to foreign trade cases. HEG exported 67.0% of FY26 revenue (₹1,722 crore of ₹2,569 crore) to about 35 countries; Graphite India 42.6% of consolidated revenue by customer location (41.4% of standalone turnover on its sustainability report); RHI Magnesita India 9.8% (₹389 crore), mostly through its parent. Worked example: for a company with ₹2,500 crore of revenue and 67% exports, a ₹1 move in the rupee against the dollar changes revenue by roughly ₹18 crore (₹1,675 crore ÷ ₹94.9), partly offset because needle coke is also bought in dollars.

Reported ROCE for cash-rich companies mixes a low-return treasury with the operating business. Inventory days and the write-down trap

Inventory days are inventory divided by cost of goods (or revenue) times 365. Electrode makers carry very high inventory because of the two-to-six-month production cycle and coke purchases ahead of need: 321 days at Graphite India and 389 at HEG in FY26, against 163 at RHI Magnesita India. Worked example: a company with ₹2,000 crore of annual cost and 330 days of inventory carries about ₹1,800 crore of stock; a 10% fall in electrode prices that pushes realisable value below cost can force a write-down of ₹100–180 crore. Benchmarks for electrodes: below 250 days good, 250–400 average, above 400 weak. The trap: write-downs and reversals sit in operating costs. Graphite India's net-realisable-value provision fell from ₹77 crore (June 2025) to ₹47 crore (March 2026) and ₹24 crore (June 2026); the ₹23 crore release flattered Q1 FY27. When prices rise, old cheap coke flatters margins for a quarter or two; when they fall, the reverse.

Refractory shipments, and shipments against steel growth

Shipments are tonnes of refractory delivered, the volume measure RHI Magnesita India publishes each quarter. The most useful derived number is shipment growth minus Indian crude steel growth, which measures share gain or loss on a volume basis. Worked example: FY26 shipments rose 4.7% while crude steel rose 10.7%, a gap of about −6 points; in Q1 FY27 shipments fell 5% while steel rose 3%, a gap of −8 points. Benchmarks: more than +2 points good, 0 to +2 average, negative weak. What drives it: tender wins and losses at large steel groups, cement share, project timing (coke ovens, new blast furnaces), and exports. The traps: the press-release "+5%" in Q1 FY27 was quarter on quarter, not year on year; the FY26 "+9.4%" was revenue, not volume; and cement and project tonnes make the comparison noisy from quarter to quarter.

Kilograms of refractory per tonne of steel

Specific refractory consumption is tonnes of refractory used divided by tonnes of steel produced, in kilograms per tonne. It falls over time as linings last longer, monolithics replace bricks and furnaces are better controlled. Indicative ranges: global best practice about 6–8 kg a tonne, modern Indian integrated plants about 8–10, older Indian plants and the wider industry 10–15 (the figure our primer used). RHI Magnesita India's own FY26 shipments equal about 3.1 kg per tonne of Indian crude steel, which with cement and other industries in its mix is consistent with a share in the high teens to low twenties. Production against shipments, and payments to the parent

Two governance KPIs matter for a multinational subsidiary. The first is own production divided by shipments: 339.7 kt of 523.4 kt in FY26, about 65%. A low ratio means the company earns a trading margin on a third of its tonnes, often on product bought from its parent. The second is related-party charges as a share of revenue: royalty (₹35.08 crore), IT charges (₹50.98 crore), sales commission (₹25.38 crore) and interest (₹12.22 crore) total about ₹124 crore, 3.1% of FY26 revenue and about 26% of adjusted EBITDA. Add purchases from the group of ₹404 crore (10.1% of revenue) and sales to the group of ₹352 crore (8.8%). Benchmarks for service charges (indicative): below 2% of revenue good, 2–4% average, above 4% weak. The trap: royalty alone (0.9%) looks small, but the IT charge rose 72% in a year and the trade flows are larger than all the service charges combined; the transfer prices on them are not disclosed.

In plain terms

RHI Magnesita India makes about two-thirds of what it sells and buys the rest, much of it from its parent, which also charges it for technology, IT and selling through the group's network. None of this is unusual for a multinational subsidiary, and some of it brings products India cannot yet make. But it means part of the profit from Indian customers is earned upstream, at prices minority shareholders cannot check.

Definition traps, collected

  • Company "EBITDA" includes other income at both electrode makers. Graphite India's ₹375 Cr FY26 = ₹202 Cr operating profit + ₹174 Cr other income; Q1 FY27's 28.6% is 17.1% core. HEG's "29%" is about 23.6% core.

  • Graphite India's capacity is 80 kt, not 98 kt; the extra 18 kt was a German plant shut in FY2022-23. Its 92,889 t of FY26 production includes non-electrode carbon products.
  • HEG's Q4 FY26 operating loss was a GrafTech fair-value loss booked in other expenses, not a furnace loss. HEG Limited keeps that stake, so the swing will recur.
  • Graphite India's FY24 profit was a land sale (₹954 Cr exceptional gain on Bengaluru land), in a year of operating losses.
  • The spread is not a margin: $1,000–1,500 of conversion cost comes out of it. ICRA's $1,600 trigger is near breakeven.
  • Two electrode price series: Chinese domestic UHP (~$2,200–2,450 incl. VAT) and Western/Indian export (~$3,300–3,900) differ by more than $1,000.
  • "70% on three-to-five-year contracts" refers to TACC's battery anodes, not HEG's electrodes.
  • Export shares: HEG 67%, Graphite India ~42%, not "60–70%" for both.
  • RHI Magnesita India ships more than it makes: 523 kt shipped, 340 kt produced. FY26 shipments rose 4.7%; the 9.4% was revenue.
  • RHI Magnesita India's market share is "30%+" of addressed segments but ~18% of the whole US$2.6 bn market (indicative).
  • RHI Magnesita India's write-downs sit in screener's "other income" (FY23 −₹646 Cr, FY24 −₹322 Cr, FY26 −₹529 Cr), and screener's P/E of 38.7 uses a pre-exceptional basis.

How to read an electrode or refractory company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and utilisation (1 minute). Note revenue growth and the stated utilisation. For electrodes, divide revenue by (utilisation × capacity ÷ 4) to estimate realisation per tonne; compare with last quarter. If revenue rose with utilisation flat, price rose.
  2. Strip out other income (1 minute). Take screener operating profit, or company "EBITDA" minus other income. Compute the core margin. Never compare a company's inclusive "EBITDA" with a rival's core figure.
  3. Hunt the one-offs in expenses (1 minute). Look for fair-value changes on strategic stakes (HEG Limited's GrafTech shares), inventory write-downs or reversals (Graphite India's NRV footnote), and exceptional items. A jump in "other expenses" is the tell.
  4. Price against coke (1 minute). Read the management commentary on electrode prices, needle coke prices and the order book. Remember the lag: HEG's book is three to four months forward; coke is on annual contracts.
  5. Segment split (1 minute). For Graphite India, check how much profit came from Graphite & Carbon against Steel and the treasury subsidiary ("Others").
  6. Cash and treasury (1 minute). Note net cash and its change. A falling cash pile with rising working capital is a warning; capex and anode spend should be visible.
  7. Trade exposure (30 seconds). Any update on US deposits, the February 2027 finals or the Brazil case; US share of sales.
  8. For refractories, volume and price (1.5 minutes). Shipments year on year against Indian crude steel growth; production against shipments; revenue per tonne; material cost percentage against last quarter and last year.
  9. For refractories, guidance and the parent (1 minute). Margin against the ~13% guidance; volume against 7–8%; any exceptional items; any change in group purchases or charges.
  10. Red flags (1 minute). Core margin falling while company "EBITDA" holds (treasury masking the furnace); inventory days rising sharply; price increases announced but realisation flat; refractory shipments falling while steel rises for a second quarter; new write-downs after management said there would be none.
In plain terms

The quickest way to misread these companies is to take their headline profit at face value. For the electrode makers, take out the treasury income and any swing in investment values, then ask whether the price per tonne went up faster than the coke. For the refractory maker, ask whether it sold more tonnes than last year, not just more rupees, and whether the margin held after raw materials moved.

Who is winning: cost, treasury and momentum

On the plant, HEG's single giant site and captive power make it the lowest-cost electrode maker in the group. Graphite India has the strongest balance sheet and the most momentum. RHI Magnesita India has the steadiest business but is losing volume share.

The first map plots utilisation against core margin. Both Indian electrode makers sit in the top-right corner, full and profitable in Q1 FY27, while GrafTech sits bottom-left at 64% utilisation and about 6% margin. HEG's higher core margin despite a slightly lower utilisation reflects its scale (one 100 kt site rather than two smaller plants), its captive thermal and hydro power, and its leaner employee cost (about 5% of revenue against about 7% at Graphite India). Technology and disruption: thinner electrodes, smarter linings, new furnaces

Better furnaces use less electrode and less refractory per tonne every year. The bigger long-run risks are new ironmaking routes that use different electrodes, and the battery industry's appetite for the same coke.

The slowest but surest threat to electrode demand is efficiency. Specific consumption has fallen from 6–7 kg per tonne of steel in the 1980s to 1.2–2.0 kg on modern UHP furnaces, through better graphite and joints, computer-regulated arcs, direct-current furnaces, oxygen and carbon injection and foamy-slag practice that shields the electrode tips. It keeps falling by perhaps 1–2% a year. That means new EAF capacity has to grow by more than 1–2% a year just to keep electrode tonnes flat. The new arc furnaces being built in the US, Europe and the Middle East are among the most efficient ever, which helps steelmakers and hurts electrode volume per tonne.

The more radical change is in ironmaking. Hydrogen-based direct reduction followed by an arc furnace is good for electrodes, since it adds EAF tonnes. But a different route is emerging for low-grade iron ore: the electric smelting furnace or open-slag-bath furnace, now being piloted by several steel and mining groups, which melts direct-reduced iron in a submerged-arc furnace using Söderberg or prebaked carbon electrodes rather than UHP graphite. If these furnaces scale in the 2030s, part of the decarbonisation-driven growth in electric steelmaking could bypass graphite electrodes altogether. In India, a policy push to move induction-furnace producers to arc furnaces (for quality and emissions) would be the largest single source of new domestic electrode demand; the Green Steel Taxonomy and carbon-trading scheme point that way but have no deadline.

Battery materials cut both ways. Synthetic graphite anodes compete for the same premium coke as electrodes, which squeezed electrode makers in 2018 and could again as anode demand grows outside China. India still has no needle coke production: Indian Oil's 56 kt Paradip project, approved in 2020, has not been built, so all of the country's roughly 150 kt a year of coke needs are imported (an indicative estimate). At the same time, the anode plants at HEGAM and Graphite India are a hedge against a world in which batteries, rather than steel, become the main user of high-grade graphite. Graphite India is also developing isostatic graphite (it is validating a 10-micron grade) for aerospace and defence, a small but high-value niche.

Refractories are changing more quietly. Linings last longer, monolithic castables replace bricks, digital monitoring lets suppliers reline only what is worn, and robots are taking over gunning and installation (RHI Magnesita India operates two at India's largest integrated steel plant). Each of these lowers kilograms of refractory per tonne of steel, which is why the company is shifting towards per-tonne service contracts that let it capture part of the saving. Recycling of spent refractories (the MINPRO joint venture) cuts raw-material cost and carbon emissions. Low-carbon steel routes will change the lining mix, with less refractory for blast-furnace hearths and torpedo ladles and more for arc furnaces and DRI plants. Finally, the electrode makers face their own carbon question: graphitisation uses 4–6 MWh of mostly coal-based Indian power per tonne, and European buyers facing carbon border costs may start to ask for lower-carbon electrodes. Graphite India has cut water use at Durgapur by half; HEG's captive power is coal and hydro.

In plain terms

Technology is slowly reducing how much electrode and refractory each tonne of steel needs, and that will not stop. The industry grows only if steel output, and especially electric steel, grows faster. The one genuine threat of substitution is a new kind of furnace for low-grade ore that uses cheaper carbon electrodes instead of graphite, but that is a 2030s question, not a 2027 one.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • AD / CVD: anti-dumping duty (against sales below fair value) and countervailing duty (against subsidies), imposed after a trade investigation; "cash deposit" is the provisional amount importers pay.
  • Acheson / lengthwise graphitisation (LWG): the two furnace types that heat baked carbon to 2,800–3,000°C to turn it into graphite.
  • BF-BOF: blast furnace–basic oxygen furnace, the integrated route that makes steel from iron ore and coking coal; uses lots of refractory and few electrodes.
  • CBAM: the EU's carbon border adjustment mechanism, charging importers for embedded emissions from 2026.
  • Core EBITDA / core margin: operating profit before other income; screener's "operating profit".
  • Critical circumstances: a US finding that imports surged ahead of duties, allowing deposits to apply retroactively (here from 2 Jul 2026).
  • DRI: direct-reduced (sponge) iron, made by reducing ore with gas or coal below melting point; fed to arc or induction furnaces.
  • EAF: electric arc furnace; melts scrap and DRI with an arc from graphite electrodes.
  • ESF / OSBF: electric smelting furnace / open-slag-bath furnace, emerging routes for low-grade ore that use carbon (not graphite) electrodes.
  • Flow control: refractory parts (nozzles, stoppers, slide gates, shrouds) that steer molten steel during casting.
  • 4PRO: RHI Magnesita's supply-install-monitor-optimise refractory service model.
  • Induction furnace (IF): melts scrap and DRI by electromagnetic induction; uses no graphite electrodes; 37.7% of Indian steel.
  • kg per tonne (specific consumption): electrode or refractory used per tonne of steel produced.
  • Magnesia (dead-burned, fused): magnesium oxide, the main raw material for steel-plant bricks; largely from China.
  • Monolithics: unshaped refractories (castables, gunning and ramming mixes) installed in place, as opposed to bricks.
  • Needle coke (calcined): a highly ordered petroleum or coal-tar coke, the main raw material for electrodes and a feedstock for synthetic anodes.
  • NRV write-down: reduction of inventory to net realisable value when selling prices fall below cost.
  • Other income: interest, dividends and investment gains; in this sub-sector mostly treasury income (and, on screener, RHIM's impairments).
  • Realisation: revenue per tonne sold.
  • SGAM / anode material: spheroidised, coated synthetic graphite powder used in lithium-ion battery anodes.
  • Shipments vs production: tonnes delivered to customers vs tonnes made in the company's own plants.
  • Spread: electrode realisation minus needle coke cost per tonne of electrode; not a margin.
  • UHP / HP: ultra-high-power and high-power electrodes; UHP grades (often 450–800 mm) feed large modern arc furnaces.
  • Utilisation: production as a share of installed capacity.
