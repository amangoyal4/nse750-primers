00

The one-page view: a commodity mill whose margin is set three borders away

India is the only large steel market still growing 7–9% a year. That makes it the world's favourite export destination, and it means a mill's profit depends less on its own efficiency than on prices it does not control.

India made 170 million tonnes of crude steel in FY26, second only to China, and its use of finished steel rose 8% to 164 million tonnes. Almost every other big steel market is shrinking or flat. China's own consumption is falling with its property market, and in 2025 it exported a record 119 million tonnes, equal to about seven-tenths of everything India consumed. That single fact explains most of what follows. An Indian steel company sells a product whose price is anchored to what a tonne of Chinese, Korean or Japanese hot-rolled coil would cost delivered to an Indian port. It then buys most of its coking coal on the seaborne market, priced in dollars. Its profit is the gap between those two world prices, translated through the rupee, plus whatever it saves by owning iron ore, generating its own power and selling higher-grade products.

This report is built around five forces that between them explain why the 13 listed companies earned what they did in FY26 and what will change their earnings next.

  • The spread. For an integrated blast-furnace mill, about a third of revenue goes on imported coking coal and another eighth to a sixth on iron ore. The gap between the hot-rolled coil (HRC) price and those two inputs, which we call the raw-material spread, moved from about ₹18,430 a tonne in December 2025 to about ₹30,400 a tonne in October 2026 (both illustrative, built from published prices). That swing of roughly ₹12,000 a tonne is larger than any cost programme a mill can run.
  • China's surplus against India's trade wall. A three-year safeguard duty on flat steel (12% to April 2026, 11.5% to April 2027, 11% to April 2028), anti-dumping duties and quality-control orders keep Indian prices a little above the Chinese offer. The wall is porous: free-trade agreements with Korea, Japan and ASEAN, and a price floor above which the duty does not apply, let imports back in whenever world prices rise. India was a net importer again in the first half of FY27.
  • Domestic demand led by the state. Construction and infrastructure take about 60% of Indian steel. The Union government budgeted ₹12.2 lakh crore of capital spending for FY27. Demand growth of 7–9% a year is the whole investment case for the industry's expansion.
  • Raw-material security and the mining regime. Since 2015 new iron-ore leases are auctioned, and winning bids commonly pay the state 90–150% of the ore's value on top of a 15% royalty. Ore from an auctioned "captive" mine is therefore rarely cheaper than ore bought in the market. The real advantage belongs to the few holders of old, non-auctioned leases, and Tata Steel's run out in March 2030.
  • Decarbonisation costs arriving before the technology. India's mills emit about 2.54 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of crude steel against a world average of 1.92. The EU's carbon border charge (CBAM) became definitive in January 2026 and its new import quota cut duty-free volumes by 47% from July 2026. India's own carbon-market targets start in FY27, and about 64% of the capacity being planned is still coal-based blast furnace.

The 13 companies are not one business. Five are integrated producers that run blast furnaces and sell flat and long steel at national scale: JSW Steel, Tata Steel, Steel Authority of India (SAIL), Jindal Steel and NMDC Steel. Lloyds Metals is above all an iron-ore miner moving downstream into pellets, sponge iron and soon steel. Jindal Stainless makes stainless steel from scrap, nickel and chrome, and has almost no exposure to iron ore or coking coal. The remaining six (Shyam Metalics, Sarda Energy, Godawari Power & Ispat, Gallantt Ispat, Jayaswal Neco and Jai Balaji) are "secondary" producers, mostly in Chhattisgarh, Odisha, West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh, that make sponge iron in coal-fired kilns and melt it in induction or electric furnaces into billets, bars and rods. Their economics depend on regional long-product prices, thermal coal, captive power and, for a few, captive ore.

The market already pays differently for these models. The integrated five produce 82% of the group's operating profit but 73% of its value; Lloyds and Jindal Stainless earn 11% of the profit and command 17% of the value, because ore from a low-cost legacy lease and stainless steel's steadier margins are scarcer than blast-furnace tonnes. Combined, the 13 trade at about 17× FY26 earnings, but that figure is flattered by JSW Steel's one-off ₹18,051 crore gain on selling its Bhushan Power & Steel plant into a joint venture with Japan's JFE. Strip that out and the group is on about 25× earnings, a premium to global steel peers that reflects India's growth and the fact that FY26 was a year of depressed spreads for most of its twelve months.

In plain terms

An Indian steel mill is a converter sitting between two world markets. It buys coal from Australia, Russia and the United States, digs or buys iron ore at home, and sells steel at a price that cannot drift far above what an importer would pay. When Chinese steel is cheap and coal is dear, as in late 2023, the mill earns little whatever it does. When a safeguard duty lifts the floor, the rupee weakens and Chinese exports slow, as in 2026, it earns a lot. The mills that win across cycles are the ones that own cheap ore, run full, sell more high-grade product and borrow modestly, because those are the only levers that stay in their hands.

Sections 07 to 12 cover industry structure, the four business models, the mining regime, the capacity race, regulation and the cycle. Section 13 is a full masterclass in the numbers a steel analyst reads, and sections 14 to 16 compare and profile all 13 companies. The closing sections deal with carbon, the global market, technology, the bull and bear cases and the indicators to watch, with thresholds.

01

How a tonne earns: two routes from rock to rebar

Integrated mills turn ore and imported coal into coil in blast furnaces; secondary mills turn ore and thermal coal into sponge iron and melt it with electricity. Who captures the value depends on what each one owns.

Steel is iron with most of its carbon and impurities removed, and the industry's whole cost structure follows from how that iron is extracted from ore. There are two main routes in India, and they run on different fuels, cost different amounts to build and earn margins in different ways.

A third route matters for a few plants. Gas-based direct reduction, used at AM/NS India's Hazira plant and part of JSW's Dolvi plant, reduces ore with natural gas, and Jindal Steel's Angul plant uses gas made from coal (syngas). Stainless steel, Jindal Stainless's business, is made from scrap, nickel pig iron and ferro-chrome in electric arc furnaces. In 2025, 42.3% of India's crude steel came from basic oxygen furnaces and 57.7% from electric furnaces of all kinds, almost the reverse of China's 89% blast-furnace share. India is the world's largest producer of sponge iron, at 58.9 million tonnes in 2025, and about 80% of it is made in coal-fired kilns.

Where the value is captured

Five levers decide how much of the selling price a producer keeps. Ownership of raw materials is the biggest. Iron ore is about a fifth to three-tenths of an integrated mill's cash cost, and a mill with an old captive lease avoids the margin a merchant miner such as NMDC earns, worth roughly ₹2,500–4,000 per tonne of steel. Coking coal cannot be owned in any meaningful way in India, because domestic coking coal is high in ash; that value leaves the country and is the largest single swing in blast-furnace profits. Downstream processing is the third lever: in September 2026 cold-rolled coil sold in Mumbai for ₹12,770 a tonne more than hot-rolled coil. Coastal location is the fourth, because a plant next to a port saves rail freight on imported coal and on exports. For secondary producers, captive power from waste heat and coal fines is the fifth, since electricity is 15–20% of the cost of melting sponge iron.

The secondary route's arithmetic shows why integration matters so much in that half of the industry. In Raipur on 1 September 2026, sponge iron sold for ₹29,400–30,000 a tonne against a cash cost of about ₹21,500 using bought pellets and coal, a margin of about ₹8,000. A billet sold for ₹42,400, but making it from bought sponge iron cost about ₹42,700 once power, scrap and alloys were added. In other words, the melt shop earned nothing on its own that week; the profit sat entirely in the kiln. A producer that makes its own sponge iron keeps the ₹7,500–8,500 integrated margin, and one that also generates its own power at ₹2–3 a unit saves another ₹1,500–2,000. That is why every listed secondary producer has spent the last decade adding pellet plants, kilns, waste-heat boilers and, where it can, mines.

In plain terms

Steelmaking is a chain of conversions, and each link has its own market price. If a company buys an input at the market price and sells the output at the market price, that link earns only what the market leaves it, which is often nothing. Profit comes from owning a link whose input is cheaper than the market: an old mine, a coal block, a power plant fed by waste heat. The more links a company owns, the more of the chain's margin it keeps and the less it depends on any single price.

The two routes also differ in capital and time. A brownfield blast-furnace expansion costs ₹3,500–5,500 crore per million tonnes of annual capacity and takes three to four years. A coal-DRI and induction-furnace unit costs ₹1,500–2,500 crore per million tonnes and can be built in about 18 months. That gap explains why secondary capacity can flood the long-products market quickly after a good year, and why TMT prices are more volatile than coil prices. It also explains why the integrated mills dominate flat products, where quality and scale requirements keep small players out.

02

Size, growth and history: from 77 to 164 million tonnes in eleven years

Indian steel use has roughly doubled since FY15 and grown 7.5% a year since FY19, faster than the economy. Capacity has kept pace, so utilisation has hovered near three-quarters.

India consumed 77 million tonnes of finished steel in FY15. By FY26 it consumed 164.4 million tonnes on the Ministry of Steel's revised figures, up 8.0% on the year. Over the seven years from FY19 to FY26 consumption compounded at 7.5% a year, and over the three years from FY23 at 10.9%, helped by a low post-COVID base. Crude-steel production grew 6.2% a year over FY19–FY26 to 170.2 million tonnes. Consumption has been growing at roughly 1.1–1.4 times real GDP, which is the pattern of an economy still building roads, railways, homes and factories rather than replacing them.

Capacity has kept up with demand, which is why the industry has never run hot for long. Installed crude-steel capacity rose from 122 million tonnes a year (MTPA) in FY16 to 220.4 MTPA in FY26 and about 222 MTPA by June 2026. Utilisation, crude production divided by capacity, has stayed between 72% and 81% throughout. The peak of 81% came in FY24, just before a wave of new plants arrived. The figure needs care: the official capacity number counts the nameplate of every small induction-furnace unit, many of which run at 50–65%, while the large integrated mills run at 90–95%. JSW's Indian plants, for example, ran at 94% in the June 2026 quarter.

The history of the industry is a history of policy. Licensing was abolished in 1991 and prices were decontrolled on 16 January 1992, which let private blast-furnace capacity (JSW, Essar, Ispat) be built for the first time. The 2002–2008 super-cycle, driven by China's urbanisation, produced Tata Steel's US$12 billion purchase of Corus in April 2007, at the top. Court-ordered mining bans in Karnataka (2011) and Goa (2012) and the Supreme Court's cancellation of 204 coal blocks in September 2014 hurt the companies that depended on them. When China exported about 112 million tonnes in 2015 the Indian price collapsed, and the bank loans taken for the previous expansion became the steel component of the bad-loan crisis. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016 then handed Bhushan Steel to Tata (2018), Essar Steel to ArcelorMittal and Nippon Steel (2019) and Bhushan Power & Steel to JSW (2021), consolidating the flat-steel market into today's oligopoly.

In plain terms

Indian steel demand has doubled in a decade and is still growing faster than the economy, because India is at the stage of development where countries pour concrete reinforced with steel. But every good year brings new plants, so the industry rarely runs short. Prices and profits are therefore set at the margin by imports and by whoever has just added capacity, not by a shortage that lets everyone raise prices together.

The latest data show demand still running ahead of supply. In the first half of FY27 consumption rose 7.5% while crude production rose only 2.9%, because mills lost output to the March–April 2026 gas and LPG shortages and to planned maintenance. The gap of about 3.1 million tonnes was filled by drawing down inventories by 2.56 million tonnes and by net imports of 0.59 million tonnes. Per person, India used 115.7 kilograms of finished steel in FY26 on the JPC's fiscal-year measure and 109.2 kilograms in calendar 2025 on worldsteel's, against a world average of 209 kilograms. The National Steel Policy target is 158 kilograms by FY31.

03

Who buys the steel: concrete, rail and the Union budget

Six-tenths of Indian steel goes into buildings and infrastructure, so the demand cycle follows government capital spending and housing more than cars or factories.

The best available split of Indian steel demand, from Infomerics' July 2025 industry report, puts construction (housing, commercial and real estate) at 32% and infrastructure (roads, bridges, metro rail, ports, power and pipelines) at 28%. Together they take about 60% of demand, against roughly half in China at its peak building years. General engineering and fabrication take 11%, automobiles 11%, capital goods 9%, consumer durables 5% and railways 3%. Long products such as TMT reinforcing bars, wire rod and structural sections make up about 50–52% of consumption and flat products about 48–50%.

Automobiles take a smaller share of tonnes but a larger share of profit for the flat-steel mills, because cars and two-wheelers use cold-rolled, galvanised and advanced high-strength grades that sell well above the HRC benchmark, on contracts reset quarterly or half-yearly. Passenger-vehicle output of about 5 million units a year anchors that business. Our auto components report covers the supply chain that buys this steel. Housing demand runs through the PMAY-Urban 2.0 and PMAY-Gramin schemes, which in 2024 set a target of three crore additional houses, and through private real estate. Because TMT bars go into concrete, demand for long products dips every monsoon: Mumbai TMT prices fall 5–7% a month between June and August in most years.

The energy transition is a smaller but growing source of demand. Solar mounting structures, transmission towers, wind towers, pipelines and electrical steel (cold-rolled grain-oriented and non-oriented grades, most of which India still imports) could add 3–5 million tonnes a year of demand by 2030 on the industry dossier's estimate. Exports, at 6.6 million tonnes in FY26 or about 4% of production, are a safety valve rather than a driver, and that valve is narrowing as the EU, the United States and others raise barriers.

The disagreement matters because the whole capacity plan rests on the higher numbers. At 7–8% growth, demand adds about 13 million tonnes a year, which absorbs about 16 MTPA of new capacity at 80% utilisation. If demand slows to CRISIL's 5–7%, the 95–115 MTPA of additions announced to FY31 would arrive into a market growing too slowly to use them, and utilisation would fall. The demand number to track is therefore not the headline growth rate but the gap between consumption growth and capacity growth, which section 10 examines in detail.

In plain terms

India's steel demand is mostly the government's and the home-builder's demand. When the Union budget raises spending on railways, roads and housing, steel mills feel it within months. That makes Indian steel less exposed than Chinese or European steel to a slump in cars or factories, but more exposed to a slowdown in public works, a weak monsoon year for construction, or a budget that shifts money away from capital spending.

04

How the price is set: import parity, a safeguard duty and a $675 floor Deep dive

An Indian mill can charge roughly what a tonne of imported coil would cost delivered to an Indian buyer. The trade-remedy regime raises that ceiling, but only when world prices are low, and only for some origins.

Steel is a commodity that ships easily, and the Indian market is open enough that buyers compare domestic offers with import offers every week. A large buyer of hot-rolled coil, whether a service centre, a pipe maker or an automotive supplier, can book Chinese, Korean or Japanese coil through a trader. The Indian mill's price is therefore capped at roughly the landed cost of that import, adjusted for the convenience of local supply. Analysts call this import parity. In February 2026 ICRA noted that domestic HRC was trading at import parity "with slight premium (~US$3/t)", which means Indian mills had almost no room to raise prices on their own. Everything in the trade-remedy regime is an attempt to push that parity price up.

Through 2025 the parity price was low. Chinese HRC was offered for export at about US$450–500 a tonne free on board, and with US$20–30 of freight it landed in India at roughly ₹44,000–47,000 a tonne, while Indian mills were selling at ₹46,000–52,000 ex-works. China exported a record 119 million tonnes in 2025, at an average price of US$694 a tonne across all products, as its domestic consumption fell 5.4% in the first eleven months of the year. Indian imports had reached 9.55 million tonnes in FY25, and the country was a net importer for the second year running.

The safeguard duty, step by step

The Indian Steel Association applied for protection and the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) opened a safeguard investigation on 19 December 2024. A safeguard duty differs from an anti-dumping duty: it protects an industry against a surge in imports from all sources, not against unfair pricing by a particular country, and it must taper over its life. A provisional duty of 12% ad valorem applied for 200 days from 21 April 2025. The DGTR's final findings in August 2025 recommended three years, and the Finance Ministry's definitive notification came on 30 December 2025. The duty is 12% from 21 April 2025 to 20 April 2026, 11.5% from 21 April 2026 to 20 April 2027 and 11% from 21 April 2027 to 20 April 2028. (One widely copied transcription of the notification prints "5%" for the second year; the DGTR recommendation and every other source say 11.5%.) Between the lapse of the provisional duty around 7 November 2025 and the final notification there was no duty for about seven weeks, and that gap coincided with the price trough of December 2025.

The duty covers hot-rolled coil, sheets and plates, plate-mill plates, cold-rolled coil, metallic-coated and colour-coated flat steel (customs headings 7208–7212, 7225 and 7226). It excludes electrical steels (CRGO and CRNO), tinplate, stainless steel, electro-galvanised steel and a long list of speciality products. Two features matter more than the rate. First, the duty does not apply to any consignment whose CIF value (cost, insurance and freight to the Indian port) is at or above a floor: US$675 a tonne for HRC, sheets and plates, US$695 for plate-mill plates, US$824 for cold-rolled coil, US$861 for metallic-coated and US$964 for colour-coated steel. Second, imports from developing countries are exempt, except China (all five product groups), Vietnam (hot-rolled and metallic-coated) and Nepal (cold-rolled, metallic-coated and colour-coated). Korea and Japan count as developed countries and are covered unless their CIF price is above the floor.

Why the wall leaks

The regime has four gaps, and each one showed up in the data within a year. The first is free-trade agreements. Korea, Japan and the ASEAN countries pay no basic customs duty on most flat steel, so their coil lands cheaper than Chinese coil at the same offer price. Korea was India's largest source of finished-steel imports in FY26, at 35.4% of the total, ahead of China at 23.5% and Japan at 20.2%. The second is the price floor. When world prices rise, Chinese and Korean offers climb above US$675 CIF and the safeguard stops applying at all. By October 2026 Indian HRC itself was being offered to Europe at about US$680 a tonne FOB, which tells you where Asian prices had gone. The third is product scope: excluded grades and non-covered products enter freely. The fourth is timing, as the seven-week gap of late 2025 showed.

The result is visible in the trade numbers. Imports fell 31.7% in FY26 to 6.52 million tonnes, exports rose 35.9% to 6.60 million tonnes, and India was a tiny net exporter for the year. Then, in the first half of FY27, imports rose 24% to 4.14 million tonnes, China's share of them jumped back to 31.8%, and India became a net importer again by 0.59 million tonnes. Chinese HRC was arriving at or above the floor, legally duty-free, or as grades the duty does not cover.

In plain terms

The safeguard is a fence with a gate that opens automatically when steel gets expensive. When Chinese coil is cheap, the 11.5% duty applies and Indian mills can charge more. When world prices rise above about US$675 a tonne, the duty switches off and the fence disappears, which is exactly when Indian mills would like to raise prices further. And it was never a fence against Korea or Japan's basic duty, because India's trade agreements with them already set that at zero.

Anti-dumping duties and the quieter barriers

Anti-dumping duties target particular exporters judged to be selling below their home price. India imposed a definitive duty of about US$121.55 a tonne on hot-rolled flat steel from Vietnam in late 2025 for five years, with Hoa Phat exempt. A new investigation into hot-rolled flat products from China, Japan and Russia, covering calendar 2025, was opened in 2026; its initiation date and any preliminary findings could not be verified. For stainless steel, the DGTR's probe into cold-rolled flat products from China, Indonesia and Vietnam had its deadline extended by six months to 28 March 2027, which matters to Jindal Stainless. Behind these sit two non-tariff tools. Quality Control Orders require imports to carry Bureau of Indian Standards licences: 151 steel standards were notified, though 55 were suspended on 20 November 2025. The Steel Import Monitoring System, relaunched as SARAL SIMS on 21 November 2025, requires advance registration of every steel import.

The deeper point is that the 2026 price rally was not the safeguard's doing. Mumbai HRC rose 40% from ₹54,760 a tonne at the end of December 2025 to ₹76,900 at the end of September 2026 (retail, including GST). The definitive duty helped in January, but the bigger drivers were the West-Asia energy shock of March and April 2026, which raised freight and therefore the landed cost of every import; the rupee's fall to about ₹95–96 per dollar; a coking-coal spike to US$278 a tonne in September; and China's new export-licence regime. These lifted import parity itself. Once world prices are above the floor, the safeguard adds nothing, and Indian prices move with Asian prices. The policy protects the bottom of the cycle, not the top.

In plain terms

To forecast an Indian steel price, start with the Chinese export price, add freight, convert at the current rupee, add whatever duty applies to the cheapest route, and you will be within a few thousand rupees of the Indian mill price. The safeguard lifts that number by about ₹5,000–6,000 a tonne in a weak market and by nothing in a strong one. It expires on 20 April 2028, and the industry's bargaining over what replaces it will be one of the biggest events for these stocks.

For investors, three implications follow. Earnings of the flat-steel majors, JSW, Tata, SAIL, Jindal Steel and NMDC Steel, are tied to Asian prices with a lag of a quarter or so, regardless of how strong Indian demand is. The safeguard step-downs (to 11% from 21 April 2027 and to zero after 20 April 2028) each shave the protection a little. And long products, TMT bars and wire rod, are largely outside the regime because they are dominated by domestic secondary producers and barely traded; their prices follow Indian construction demand and the cost of sponge iron, which is why TMT and HRC prices diverged sharply in mid-2026, with TMT falling ₹7,000–8,000 a tonne between March and June while coil held.

Three prices and a currency: HRC, coking coal, iron ore and the rupee

Between 2023 and 2026 the integrated spread ranged from about ₹12,400 to ₹31,100 a tonne. Every turn can be traced to these four series.

If an analyst could follow only four numbers to forecast the earnings of an Indian integrated steelmaker, they would be the domestic HRC price, the Australian premium hard coking coal price, the NMDC iron-ore price and the rupee-dollar rate. This section sets out what each did between 2023 and October 2026, and then combines them into a single quarterly series of the raw-material spread.

Steel: a 40% rally from the December 2025 trough

The Ministry of Steel publishes month-end retail prices in Mumbai, which include 18% GST and a trader's margin; mill prices ex-works and ex-GST are about 15–20% lower. On that series HRC fell from ₹60,920 a tonne in March 2025, just before the provisional safeguard, to ₹54,760 in December 2025, then rose to ₹76,900 by the end of September 2026. Cold-rolled coil reached ₹89,670 and TMT ₹65,750. The gap between cold-rolled and hot-rolled coil, a rough measure of the reward for downstream processing, widened to ₹12,770 in September 2026 from ₹7,430 eighteen months earlier.

Coking coal: the cost that swings hardest

The rupee: it cuts both ways

The rupee averaged about ₹82.8 to the dollar in FY24, ₹84.6 in FY25 and ₹87–89 in FY26, crossing 90 in December 2025, and traded at about ₹95–96 in September and October 2026. A weaker rupee raises the rupee cost of imported coal, but it raises the rupee price of every competing import of steel by the same proportion. Because steel revenue per tonne is nearly three times the coal bill per tonne, the net effect for an integrated mill is positive: on the dossier's sensitivities a ₹1 fall in the rupee adds about ₹650 a tonne to import parity and about ₹220 to coal cost, a net gain of roughly ₹400 a tonne. The gain is largest for producers whose ore is mined at home and priced in rupees, and for exporters. It is a loss for companies with large dollar debt: JSW Steel says its FY26 hit from rupee depreciation was about ₹5,600 crore, with 64% of its debt in foreign currency.

Structure and concentration: an oligopoly in coil, a crowd in rebar

Five groups make about 60% of India's crude steel and 85–90% of its hot-rolled coil. Long products are split among a thousand-odd smaller units, which is why TMT prices are far more volatile.

India's steel industry has two very different halves. The flat-products half, which makes coil, sheet and plate for autos, appliances, pipes and infrastructure, is an oligopoly of five groups: JSW Steel, Tata Steel, AM/NS India (the unlisted ArcelorMittal–Nippon Steel joint venture that bought Essar Steel), SAIL and Jindal Steel. Together they supply about 85–90% of India's HRC. The long-products half, which makes TMT bars, wire rod and structural sections for construction, is fragmented: secondary producers supply about 55–60% of TMT, from roughly 900 induction-furnace units, about 45 electric-arc furnaces and about 300 sponge-iron plants, alongside the integrated mills' long-product lines. Across both halves the top five make about 58–62% of India's crude steel.

Concentration has a direct effect on prices. In flat steel the major mills announce monthly list prices, SAIL raised its October 2026 HRC and CRC list prices, for example, and the others tend to move within days. That discipline does not set the level, which import parity does, but it does mean the majors pass on cost changes quickly and rarely start price wars at home. Long products behave differently. Hundreds of induction-furnace units in Raipur, Durgapur, Mandi Gobindgarh and elsewhere sell TMT on spot terms, cut output when margins vanish and restart when they return. In June–August each year, when construction slows in the monsoon, TMT prices fall 5–7% a month. The listed secondary producers try to escape that volatility by building brands (Gallantt claims a 25% share of its serviceable market in Uttar Pradesh and a 2–3% price premium) and by selling pellets, ferro-alloys and other non-steel products.

In plain terms

If you sell coil, you compete with four or five big Indian mills and with importers; prices are disciplined but capped by the import price. If you sell rebar, you compete with hundreds of small local mills that switch on and off with the margin; prices are uncapped by imports but much more volatile. The listed companies at the two ends of this market are therefore exposed to different risks, even when both are described simply as "steel".

Four business models wearing one label

Integrated mills, a miner, a stainless producer and six secondary producers share a sector code but earn money in different ways, on different denominators and with different capital needs.

The sector code puts all 13 companies under "Iron & Steel", but an investor who treats them as one group will misread almost every comparison. They differ in what they sell, which input price drives their margin, how cyclical they are, what protects them from competitors and how much capital a tonne of new capacity costs. The third difference is carbon. Coal-based sponge iron melted in an induction furnace is the most carbon-intensive route in India, at about 3–3.5 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of steel. The fourth difference is the meaning of "captive". A captive mine is a moat only if its lease predates the 2015 auction regime. In plain terms

A big integrated mill is a bet on a world price gap and needs huge sums to grow. A secondary producer is a bet on a regional price gap and can grow cheaply, but so can its rivals. A miner with an old lease is a bet on the ore price and on the state not changing the rules. A stainless producer is a bet on Indian demand for a premium material and on keeping Chinese and Indonesian imports out. Ranking them on a single metric such as EBITDA per tonne is meaningless unless you first ask "per tonne of what?"

Ore, coal and the mining regime: why "captive" is not what it used to be Deep dive

Auctions since 2015 have handed most of the value of new iron-ore leases to the states. The real cost edge belongs to a shrinking set of old leases, and coking coal is still almost entirely imported.

Iron ore is the input India has in abundance and coking coal the one it lacks. The rules governing who may mine ore, and what they pay the state for doing so, have changed three times in eleven years and were the subject of a Supreme Court ruling in 2024 and a new law in August 2026. Those rules decide which companies have a durable cost advantage, and they contain a dated cliff for one of the largest producers.

Iron ore: plentiful, but increasingly priced for the state

India produced about 282 million tonnes of iron ore in 2024 on worldsteel's count, roughly 290–300 million tonnes in FY26, and is the world's fourth-largest producer after Australia, Brazil and China. Odisha produces about 55%, Chhattisgarh about 17%, Karnataka about 13% and Jharkhand about 10%. A blast-furnace mill needs about 1.5–1.6 tonnes of ore, as sinter, pellets and lump, per tonne of crude steel; the sponge-iron route needs about 1.7–1.8 tonnes. NMDC, the state-owned miner, produces about 45 million tonnes a year and sets the reference price; its output rose 22.8% to 27.27 million tonnes in the first half of FY27. India's pellet capacity of more than 130 MTPA reflects a shift towards beneficiating lower-grade ore, since only about 45% of reserves are high-grade.

The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment of January 2015 changed the economics. All new leases would be allocated only by auction. Leases held by "captive" users such as steel mills were extended to 31 March 2030; "merchant" leases selling to the market lapsed on 31 March 2020; state-owned miners such as NMDC, OMC and SAIL received 20-year extensions on payment of an additional amount. Royalty on iron ore is 15% of the Indian Bureau of Mines' published average sale price. Leases granted before 2015 pay 30% of royalty to the district mineral foundation, auctioned ones 10%, and all pay 2% of royalty to the national exploration trust.

In plain terms

When the government auctions a mine, bidders compete away most of the profit by promising the state a share of the ore's value. A steel mill that wins a mine at a 100% premium has really agreed to pay about twice the market value of the ore, spread over time, in exchange for security of supply. That security is worth something, but it is not cheap ore. Cheap ore belongs to whoever got their lease before 2015 and still holds it.

The 2030 cliff

The legacy captive leases extended in 2015 expire on 31 March 2030. The most important holder is Tata Steel, whose Indian operations run on 100% captive ore from mines in Jharkhand and Odisha. When those leases lapse, the mines will be auctioned, and Tata will have to bid for them at whatever premium the market sets or buy its ore elsewhere. The industry dossier puts the value of legacy captive ore at about ₹2,500–4,000 per tonne of steel. On Tata India's FY26 deliveries of 22.53 million tonnes, that is about ₹5,600–9,000 crore a year of EBITDA, or 16–26% of the ₹34,272 crore Tata India earned in FY26. That range is ILLUSTRATIVE: it assumes the whole advantage disappears and none of the leases is retained on better terms, and Tata will have three and a half years to prepare. But it is the largest dated cost risk in the sector, and it falls inside the payback period of every project Tata is building now.

The holders of the other true moats are SAIL, whose leases are state-owned and were extended for 20 years, NMDC (which supplies NMDC Steel at arm's length, so NMDC Steel itself owns none), Lloyds Metals, whose Surjagarh lease in Gadchiroli is a 2007 allotment valid to 2057 with no auction premium, and Jayaswal Neco, which meets 100% of its needs from its own mines (the sources used here do not give the lease vintage). GPIL's Ari Dongri and Boria Tibu leases were, on the industry dossier's unverified reading, obtained at low premiums in a less contested state; both GPIL and Sarda still buy part of their ore. Gallantt has won three blocks at auction in Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh, with production due from FY28; its guided EBITDA uplift of about ₹2,000 a tonne will depend on the premiums it bid.

The state-tax overhang, opened in 2024 and closed in 2026

On 25 July 2024 a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court ruled 8:1 in Mineral Area Development Authority v. SAIL that royalty is not a tax and that states have the power to tax mineral rights and mineral-bearing land. A follow-up order on 14 August 2024 allowed states to recover arrears from 1 April 2005, in 12 annual instalments starting 1 April 2026, without interest or penalty for the period up to the judgment. Estimates of the arrears ran to ₹1.5–2 lakh crore nationally, with Odisha's potential claim alone above ₹1 lakh crore. Jharkhand passed a mineral-bearing land cess in 2024. For miners and integrated mills with large lease holdings this was a contingent liability of many thousands of crores.

Parliament's answer was the MMDR (Amendment) Act 2026, introduced on 7 August and passed by the Lok Sabha on 12 August and the Rajya Sabha on 13 August 2026. Its new Section 9D bars any state tax, cess or levy on mineral rights or mineral-bearing land except under conditions set by the Centre, and declares any such levy not already paid "deemed to be invalid at all material times". Amounts already paid are not refundable. In effect, the window for 2005–2024 arrears that the Supreme Court opened is closed for unpaid amounts. Two caveats apply: the Act's commencement by gazette notification could not be verified as of 13 October 2026, and Odisha and Jharkhand are expected to challenge it on federalism grounds. State mineral receipts had already risen from ₹13,258 crore in 2014-15 to ₹71,035 crore in 2024-25.

Coking coal: the dependence that cannot be auctioned away

India's domestic coking coal is high in ash, and only about 6–8 million tonnes a year of washed domestic coal is usable in blast furnaces. Imports cover 85–90% of needs. The Ministry of Coal's Mission Coking Coal, launched in 2021, targets about 140 million tonnes of domestic raw coking coal by 2030 and more washeries; on present evidence it will reduce the dependence at the margin, not remove it.

JSW targets about 50% self-sufficiency in hard coking coal by calendar 2028, with about 20% from a 7 million-tonne Mozambique mine due to start in mid-2028 and about 10% from Australia, plus 3 million tonnes of domestic coal by FY29–30 and a linkage of more than 7.5 million tonnes from BCCL. NMDC Steel imports almost all its coal. In plain terms

India can dig its own iron ore but not its own coking coal, so every blast-furnace mill is permanently short of coal and long of world coal prices. Companies can buy foreign mines, but that swaps one price risk for project risk in Mozambique or Australia, as Jindal Steel's ₹830 crore write-down shows. The practical protection is a strong balance sheet that can ride out a coal spike, and a product mix whose price rises with coal.

Put together, the raw-material picture produces a clear ranking. The strongest positions belong to companies with legacy ore and low exposure to imported coking coal: Lloyds (100% legacy ore, no blast furnace), SAIL (100% captive ore, but 85% imported coal), Tata India (100% captive ore until 2030, 20–25% captive coal) and Jayaswal Neco (100% captive ore, imported coal). The weakest are companies that buy both ore and coal on the market, which describes NMDC Steel, Shyam Metalics, Gallantt and Jai Balaji. JSW sits in between, with about 30% captive ore and large but expensive auctioned mines, and is spending to change that. Over the five years to FY31, as Tata's leases lapse and auction premiums spread, the gap between "captive" and "merchant" ore costs is likely to narrow for everyone except the few holders of leases that run past 2030.

The race to 300 MTPA: who adds, at what cost, and the FY28–FY30 question Deep dive

About 95–115 MTPA of new capacity is announced to FY31, costing several lakh crore rupees. Demand of 7–8% can absorb most of it; if growth slows, utilisation and margins will fall together.

The National Steel Policy of 2017 set a target of 300 MTPA of crude-steel capacity and 255 million tonnes of production by FY31, with consumption of 158 kilograms per person. In FY26 the country had 220.4 MTPA, produced 170 million tonnes and consumed 115.7 kilograms per person. Reaching 300 MTPA requires about 16 MTPA of new capacity a year, a compound rate of 7.8%; reaching 255 million tonnes of output requires 8.4% a year. On announcements, the capacity target will be met and exceeded. The question for investors is whether demand will keep up, and what happens to prices if it does not.

What the expansion costs, and who can afford it

At ₹4,000–8,000 crore per MTPA, 95–115 MTPA of new capacity implies ₹4–7 lakh crore of capital spending over five years. The cost per tonne varies widely by project, and the cheapest additions are brownfield expansions at existing sites. JSW's Dolvi Phase 3, adding 5 MTPA by September 2027, is guided at under ₹5,000 crore per million tonnes even after a ₹2,000 crore overrun. Tata's Ludhiana electric furnace cost about ₹3,200 crore for 0.75 MTPA. At the other end, Tata's NINL expansion of 4.8 MTPA was approved in July 2026 at ₹33,873 crore, about ₹70,600 a tonne, and AM/NS's 6 MTPA Hazira phase is budgeted at ₹60,000 crore including downstream lines. SAIL's programme of more than ₹1 lakh crore for about 14 MTPA works out to roughly ₹71,000 a tonne. Section 13 compares these per tonne.

The balance sheets are in better shape than in the last capacity boom. ICRA puts bank debt at US$184 per tonne of installed capacity in December 2025, against a peak of US$450 a tonne during the FY14–FY18 bad-loan cycle. JSW's net debt fell from about ₹80,000 crore to ₹46,157 crore over the 15 months to June 2026, helped by the BPSL sale; its net debt is 1.46 times EBITDA, with a ceiling of 2.5 times and a hard cap of 3 times. Tata's is 2.3 times, inside its 2.5–3.0 times through-cycle range. SAIL generated ₹19,039 crore of operating cash flow in FY26 and cut debt by ₹8,148 crore before its spending ramps up. Jindal Steel's net debt rose to ₹16,019 crore in FY26 as it doubled Angul, but at about 1.7 times EBITDA at the peak of a 60% capacity addition it stayed far below the levels that broke the company a decade ago.

In plain terms

India's steelmakers have announced more new capacity than the market is likely to need at full speed, which is normal in a growing industry because some plans always slip. The danger is a cluster of big plants starting up in the same two years, FY28–FY30 on current schedules, just as demand cools or Chinese exports surge. Unlike in 2015, the companies would face that with modest debt, so a downturn would cut profits but is unlikely to bankrupt the leaders.

The secondary producers are expanding too, and some are changing category. Shyam Metalics signed a non-binding memorandum on 2 October 2026 with the Maharashtra government for a 9 MTPA integrated complex at Chandrapur costing about ₹50,000 crore, 1.6 times its market value, with a blast furnace and sponge-iron route. Lloyds Metals expects its first 1.2 MTPA long-products mill by March 2027, with a larger integrated plant under study. GPIL, by contrast, has put its planned 1 MTPA blast-furnace plant "in abeyance" and is building a 20 GWh battery-storage factory instead. Gallantt is lifting steel capacity 23% to 1.23 million tonnes in the second half of FY27. Where secondary producers add blast furnaces, they move closer to the integrated model's economics and capital needs.

The signals that the race is overheating would be a fall in utilisation at the large mills below about 85%, a widening discount of Indian HRC to import parity, rising working-capital days as producers push tonnes into the channel, and net debt per tonne of capacity rising back towards US$300. None was visible in October 2026: JSW's Indian plants ran at 88–94%, Indian HRC was at a slight premium to parity and leverage was falling. The test will come in FY28, when Dolvi Phase 3, Angul Phase II, AM/NS Hazira and Tata's NINL start to arrive together.

From price decontrol in 1992 to the carbon market in 2027, policy has been the most frequent swing factor in Indian steel, often more powerful than demand.

Three patterns stand out. The first is that trade policy works through the import-parity mechanism described in section 04, and the government has shown it will use it in both directions. Four decades of cycles: booms, gluts, bans and duties

Indian steel turns every three to five years, usually when China floods the world with steel or coking coal spikes. Policy has amplified or cushioned almost every turn.

Indian steel cycles run about three to five years from trough to trough. Three forces have driven most turns. Chinese export surges (2015, 2024–25) push down import parity and therefore Indian prices. Coking-coal spikes (2016–17, 2021–22, 2023) squeeze the spread from the cost side. And policy, from mining bans to export duties to the safeguard, has frequently been the swing factor that turned a soft patch into a slump or a recovery into a boom. The timeline below records the events that set the industry's current shape.

The comparison says something important about cycle risk. At the FY22 peak, Tata India earned about ₹26,000 a tonne and JSW about ₹19,000–20,000. Two features of the current cycle differ from the last. Leverage is far lower, so a downturn is likely to mean lower profits rather than defaults. In plain terms

Steel profits rise and fall on a three-to-five-year rhythm set mostly outside India. The upswing of 2026 came from a weak rupee, costlier freight and a safeguard duty, not from a sudden improvement in how Indian mills run. The useful question at any point in the cycle is not "is demand strong?" but "where is the spread relative to its range, and how much debt will the company be carrying when it turns?"

Reading a steelmaker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every steel company's profit can be written as tonnes times margin per tonne. The skill is knowing which tonne, which margin and which definition each company is using.

Steel companies publish more operating data than most industrial businesses: production, sales, realisations, EBITDA per tonne, sometimes coke rates and emissions. That abundance is a trap as much as a help, because almost every number is defined slightly differently from one company to the next. This section starts with the equation that links the numbers, works through each key performance indicator with a definition, a formula, a worked example in rupees, the drivers, a benchmark range and a comparison of the 13 companies, and closes with a typical tonne's profit and loss, a scorecard, the definition traps and a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

The equation: tonnes times what each tonne keeps

The profit of any steel business can be written as one line: EBITDA equals sales volume multiplied by the difference between the realisation per tonne and the cost per tonne. Cost per tonne splits naturally into raw materials (coal and ore, which follow world and NMDC prices) and conversion (everything else: fluxes, alloys, power, people, stores, repairs, freight and overheads, net of by-product credits). Raw materials are where the cycle lives; conversion is where management lives.

A worked example using October 2026 spot conditions shows the scale of the numbers. Take HRC at ₹63,000 a tonne ex-works and add a mix premium of ₹3,500 for cold-rolled, coated and long products, for a realisation of ₹66,500. The equation also shows where the leverage sits. On the same 27 million tonnes, every ₹1,000 a tonne of margin is worth ₹2,700 crore of EBITDA, about 6–7% of a ₹40,500 crore base at a more normal ₹15,000 a tonne. A 10% rise in volume adds about as much as a ₹1,500 a tonne rise in margin. Volume growth is slow and costly to buy; margin swings are fast and free. That is why steel shares trade on the spread and the price outlook far more than on volume guidance, even though volume is the part management controls.

In plain terms

Every steel result is three numbers multiplied together: how many tonnes were sold, what each tonne fetched, and what each tonne cost. The price and the raw-material cost move with the world; the tonnes and the conversion cost move with the company. When you read a result, separate what the world gave the company from what the company did itself.

Capacity and utilisation

Definition. Capacity is the nameplate amount of crude (liquid) steel a plant can make in a year, in million tonnes per annum (MTPA). Utilisation is crude production divided by average capacity over the period. Formula: utilisation = crude steel produced ÷ average crude capacity. Worked example: Tata Steel India made 23.43 million tonnes of crude steel in FY26 on about 27.4 MTPA of capacity, a utilisation of about 86% on year-end capacity, higher if capacity is averaged over a year in which Kalinganagar was still ramping up. Jindal Steel made 2.40 million tonnes in Q1 FY27; annualised that is 9.6 million tonnes on 15.6 MTPA, about 62%, because its newly doubled Angul plant was still ramping and had planned shutdowns.

Drivers. Ramp-ups of new furnaces, planned relines and capital repairs (SAIL ran capital repairs at IISCO, Durgapur and Bokaro in Q1 FY27; JSW's Vijayanagar blast furnace 3 was upgraded and relit at the end of June 2026), input shortages (the gas and LPG cuts of March–April 2026) and demand. Benchmarks. For integrated mills, above 90% is good, 80–90% average and below 80% weak; for secondary producers, above 80% is good, 65–80% average and below 65% weak. The national average was about 77% in FY26.

The raw-material spread

Definition. The HRC price minus the landed cost of the iron ore and coking coal needed to make a tonne of steel. Formula: HRC ex-works − (1.6 × landed ore price + 0.8 × landed coking-coal price). Worked example: in October 2026, ₹63,000 − (1.6 × ₹6,200 + 0.8 × ₹28,350) = ₹63,000 − ₹9,920 − ₹22,680 = ₹30,400. At the December 2025 trough, ₹46,000 − (1.6 × ₹6,300 + 0.8 × ₹21,860) = ₹46,000 − ₹10,080 − ₹17,490 = ₹18,430. Both ILLUSTRATIVE. Benchmarks. Above ₹28,000 is good, ₹20,000–28,000 average, below ₹20,000 weak.

The spread is an industry measure, not a company one. What matters is how much of it each company converts into EBITDA, which depends on its conversion cost, its ore source and its product mix. Comparing Q1 FY27 reported EBITDA per tonne with the quarter's illustrative spread of about ₹25,970 makes the differences vivid.

The definition trap. The spread is computed from spot prices and ignores the quarter's lag between buying coal and selling steel, so in a rising coal market the reported margin trails the spread, and in a falling one it beats it. It uses the HRC price, so it misses the mix premium of companies with large cold-rolled, coated or long-product sales. And it assumes merchant ore, so it understates the true spread for SAIL, Tata and other holders of cheap captive ore. Use it to judge direction and the industry's position in the cycle, not to forecast a single company's margin.

In plain terms

The spread tells you how good the weather is for steelmakers. EBITDA per tonne tells you how well each one is sailing in it. In the June 2026 quarter the weather was good, and Tata India kept about three-quarters of it while SAIL kept about two-fifths.

In plain terms

EBITDA per tonne is the single most quoted number in steel and the easiest to abuse. Net debt to EBITDA

Definition. Borrowings minus cash and liquid investments, divided by trailing twelve-month EBITDA: the number of years of operating profit needed to repay net debt. Capex per tonne

Definition. The capital cost of a project divided by the annual capacity it adds. Formula: project cost ÷ added MTPA, usually quoted in ₹ crore per MTPA or ₹ (or US$) per tonne. Worked example: Tata Steel's NINL expansion costs ₹33,873 crore for 4.8 MTPA: ₹33,873 crore ÷ 4.8 million tonnes = about ₹70,600 per tonne, or about US$735 at ₹96. JSW's Dolvi Phase 3, at under ₹5,000 crore per million tonnes, is under ₹50,000 per tonne. Benchmarks. For brownfield blast-furnace capacity below US$600 a tonne (about ₹57,600) is good, US$600–900 average and above US$1,000 weak; greenfield integrated plants typically cost US$900–1,100.

In plain terms

Capex per tonne is what a company pays today for the right to earn EBITDA per tonne for the next 25 years. At ₹70,000 a tonne of capacity and a mid-cycle EBITDA of ₹12,000–15,000 a tonne at 85% utilisation, a new plant earns back its cost in roughly five and a half to seven years before tax and interest. At ₹50,000 a tonne it takes four to five. That difference is why brownfield expansions at existing sites are the most valuable projects in the industry.

Captive raw-material share

Definition. The share of a company's iron-ore (or coal) needs met from its own mines. Formula: captive ore consumed ÷ total ore consumed. Worked example: GPIL mined 2.75 million tonnes in FY26 against 2.86 million tonnes of pellet output, roughly 85–95% captive at about 1.0–1.1 tonnes of ore per tonne of pellet; in Q1 FY27 it mined 0.54 million tonnes against 0.90 million tonnes of pellets, about 60%, because overburden-dump limits and delayed tree-felling permission cut its own output and forced market purchases at about ₹6,200 a tonne against ₹2,900 for its own ore. Its margin fell from 27.3% to 19.1% in a quarter. Benchmarks. Above 80% from a legacy lease is good, 40–80% average, below 40% weak; an auctioned mine at a high premium counts for little.

Working capital

Definition. Inventory plus receivables minus payables, expressed in days of revenue. Formula: (inventory + receivables − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: a producer with ₹20,000 crore of revenue, ₹3,000 crore of inventory, ₹1,500 crore of receivables and ₹1,800 crore of payables carries ₹2,700 crore of working capital, or about 49 days (ILLUSTRATIVE). Benchmarks. Below 45 days is good, 45–75 average, above 75 weak. Steel inventories are large because a blast-furnace mill holds one to one and a half months of coal plus a month in transit, and finished-goods stocks swing with demand.

The sources used for this report do not give comparable working-capital days for the 13 companies, so no chart is shown; the signals that matter are in the disclosures. SAIL added about 0.2 million tonnes of inventory in Q1 FY27 as sales fell 7–8%. CRISIL downgraded Jai Balaji to BBB/Stable on 21 September 2026 partly because it was relying more on short-term debt to fund working capital and capex, and its working-capital borrowings of ₹375–400 crore are now larger than its ₹188 crore of term debt. Gallantt's ₹548 crore of borrowings are all working capital. The definition trap. Year-end working capital can be dressed by delaying payments or pushing stock to distributors; watch the quarterly change in inventory tonnes and receivable days, not the March balance.

Definition traps, collected

  • Crude, liquid, hot metal, HRC or finished? NMDC Steel's FY26 output was 3.07 Mt of hot metal, 2.43 Mt of liquid steel and 2.33 Mt of HRC; its primer called the last figure "crude steel".
  • India or group? Tata's ₹15,213 a tonne is India; ₹10,900 is the group including a Netherlands business with a going-concern uncertainty and a loss-making UK business.
  • Reported, adjusted or screener EBITDA? JSW ₹29,821 / ₹32,048 / ₹29,464 crore; Tata ₹34,848 / ₹34,640 / ₹34,352 crore; SAIL ₹12,000 standalone / ₹12,119 screener.
  • Including other income? Sarda's and Gallantt's "EBITDA" include other income; screener's operating profit does not.
  • Per tonne of what? Sold, produced, billet, pellet, rolled product or all products; this alone can move a figure threefold.
  • Consolidated numerator, standalone denominator. Jindal Stainless's ₹21,668 a tonne uses consolidated EBITDA over standalone tonnes; ₹16,843 is the like-for-like figure.
  • Restated perimeters. JSW's numbers exclude BPSL from March 2026 and restate history; Lloyds' FY26 consolidates Thriveni from July 2025, so its 155% revenue growth is not organic.
  • One-offs in PAT. JSW's FY26 PAT of ₹25,508 crore includes an ₹18,051 crore gain (normalised ₹8,698 crore); Sarda's Q1 FY27 PAT includes a ₹110 crore hydro tariff true-up.
  • Cash that is not counted. Screener's cash excludes liquid investments, overstating net debt at Shyam, Sarda and GPIL.
  • "Captive" is not "cheap". Auctioned mines can cost more than market ore; ask about lease vintage and premiums.
  • Stale consolidated pages. Jai Balaji's consolidated data stop at September 2025, so its consolidated P/E of 20.4× is wrong; the standalone 40.8× is current.

How to read a steel company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the perimeter first. Any acquisition, demerger or joint venture (BPSL, Thriveni, Rathi) changes what "growth" means. Find the restated comparison.
  2. Read volumes against capacity. Production, sales and the gap between them; any inventory build; utilisation against the 90% (integrated) or 80% (secondary) benchmark.
  3. Read realisation against the benchmark. Compare the quarter's realisation per tonne with the average ex-works HRC or TMT price; a widening premium means a better mix.
  4. Separate the spread from the company. Look up the quarter's coking-coal average (and the previous quarter's, for the lag) and the HRC average; estimate how much of the EBITDA change the spread explains.
  5. Find conversion cost. Raw-material cost per tonne, then everything else per tonne; for SAIL, check employee cost; for new plants, check start-up costs.
  6. Compute EBITDA per tonne yourself. On reported EBITDA and tonnes sold, then compare with the company's figure and note the basis.
  7. Strip one-offs. Exceptional gains, FX on intercompany loans, tariff true-ups and write-downs, before and below EBITDA.
  8. Check net debt and its direction. Net debt in rupees and as a multiple of TTM EBITDA; capex in the quarter; cash definitions.
  9. Read the guidance in units. Next quarter's coal cost change (US$/t), price outlook (₹/t), volume guidance (Mt) and capex (₹ crore). Multiply out what each implies for EBITDA.
  10. Note the policy items. Safeguard or anti-dumping status, export restrictions, mining-levy changes, CBAM or carbon-market costs and any plant permits at risk.
In plain terms

A good steel analyst spends the first five minutes of a results day on two questions: what happened to the spread this quarter, and how much of the company's change in EBITDA per tonne does that explain? Whatever is left over, positive or negative, is the company's own performance: its mix, its costs, its volumes. The remaining five minutes go on debt, capex and anything that changes the perimeter.

How to read the table

Start with the combined row, because it frames the group. Together the 13 companies had FY26 revenue of ₹7,02,262 crore, EBITDA of ₹1,06,623 crore (a 15.2% margin) and reported profit of ₹54,249 crore, and are valued at ₹9,45,961 crore. The headline group P/E of 17.4× is misleading because a third of the group's reported profit is JSW's one-off gain; on normalised profit of ₹37,439 crore the group trades at about 25×. Combined net debt of about ₹2,08,370 crore, mixing March and June dates, is roughly 1.95 times combined EBITDA, comfortably below ICRA's 3.0–3.1 times industry average.

Next, read across the integrated rows. Tata Steel has the largest revenue, ₹2,32,140 crore, but much of it is European steel that earns little; its India business is the most profitable large operation in the country, at ₹15,213 EBITDA a tonne in FY26 and ₹19,162 in Q1 FY27. JSW has nearly the same Indian volume, a lower margin per tonne and the highest market value in the group, ₹2,88,076 crore, because investors are paying for its growth pipeline and its rebuilt balance sheet. SAIL's 10.9% margin and ₹6,018 a tonne show the cost of its workforce, but its valuation, 14.8 times earnings and 1.18 times book, is the lowest of the large mills. Jindal Steel's 18.2% margin is the best of the integrated group, but its 33.8× P/E reflects trailing profit depressed by ramp-up costs and an ₹830 crore write-down. NMDC Steel's 141× P/E is mathematically correct and economically meaningless: it made ₹59 crore of profit in its first profitable year.

Then compare the margin column with the EBITDA-per-tonne column, and resist comparing the second across archetypes. Lloyds' 36.3% margin and Sarda's 31.4% are the highest in the table because one sells iron ore from a cheap lease and the other sells electricity under long-term contracts; their per-tonne figures are on ore and are not disclosed, respectively. Jayaswal Neco's ₹19,490 a tonne and GPIL's ₹4,387 a tonne look worlds apart, but the first divides all of the company's EBITDA by rolled-steel tonnes only, and the second divides it by pellets. Jai Balaji's 6.1% margin, the lowest in the group, is the clearest signal in the table: a producer with no captive ore or coal, a stalled pipe business and a credit downgrade.

Finally, look at net debt and valuation together. Five companies (Shyam, GPIL, Sarda, Gallantt and Jindal Stainless) are net-cash or close to it, and all five trade at 14.8–32.3 times earnings. The highest multiples sit with Jai Balaji (40.8×), Jindal Steel (33.8×), Gallantt (32.3×) and Shyam (27.3×), each for a different reason: trough earnings, ramp-up costs, a mine story ahead of any ore, and a volume-growth record. The lowest sit with SAIL (14.8×), Sarda (14.8×), Jayaswal Neco (16.3×) and GPIL (17.0×). Return on capital runs from 27.3% at Lloyds to 3.1% at NMDC Steel, with the four large integrated mills clustered at 7.9–12.5%, below their cost of capital in most years. The positioning maps in the next section put these comparisons on two axes at a time.

In plain terms

The table shows three groups of investments. The integrated mills are large, cyclical and moderately geared, with modest returns on capital and valuations that rise and fall with the spread. The miner and the stainless producer earn the highest returns and carry premium valuations. The six secondary producers are small, mostly debt-free and range from deeply cheap to expensive depending on whether investors believe in their next project. Who is winning: balance sheets, returns, raw materials and ambition

Four maps and a scorecard. The winners combine cheap raw materials with low debt; the market already pays for most of them, and the cheapest stocks are cheap for visible reasons.

No single metric identifies the strongest steel company, because the archetypes earn money differently. The four maps below each put the group on two axes chosen to answer one question: who has the balance sheet to survive the next downturn while earning a decent return; which returns are cheap or expensive; whether owning ore actually translates into margin; and which integrated mills are betting most heavily on the capacity race.

The first map shows why the secondary producers have re-rated over the last five years. GPIL, Gallantt and Sarda earn 17–21% on capital with little or no net debt, Jayaswal Neco earns 21% at 1.4 times, and Jindal Stainless earns 19% at about half a turn of leverage. All five integrated mills sit in the bottom half, with returns of 3–12.5% and net debt of 1.6–2.6 times EBITDA on the table's basis. That is the structural cost of the blast-furnace model: very large, long-lived assets that earn their cost of capital only at good points in the cycle. Lloyds is the outlier in the top right, combining a 27% return with leverage of about three times, almost all of it from acquisitions outside its core mine.

The second map tests whether valuations follow returns. Broadly they do not. Jayaswal Neco (21% ROCE, 7.9× EBITDA) and GPIL (21%, 10.6×) are the cheapest high-return companies, held back respectively by a restructuring history and a quarter of market-ore purchases plus a pivot to batteries. Gallantt (18%, about 21×) and Jai Balaji (10%, about 17×) are the most expensive for what they earn, Gallantt because investors are paying in advance for mines that will not produce until FY28 and Jai Balaji because trough earnings inflate every multiple. Lloyds' 14.9× is high in absolute terms but reasonable for a 27% return. Among the integrated mills, the market pays more for JSW and Jindal Steel's growth than for Tata and SAIL's existing profits.

The third map is a warning against reading "captive ore" as a guarantee of profit. The companies that own all their ore split into two groups: the ones that sell ore or ore products (Lloyds, and GPIL through pellets) with margins of 23–36%, and the blast-furnace mills (Tata, SAIL, Jayaswal Neco) whose ore advantage is real but buried under the cost of imported coking coal and conversion, leaving margins of 11–19%. On the left, the companies with no ore of their own (NMDC Steel, Shyam, Gallantt, Jai Balaji) earn 6–16%. Sarda sits high on the left for a reason unrelated to ore: about 70% of its EBITDA is electricity.

The fourth map is about ambition. The four large listed mills all plan to grow capacity by 45–70% over four to five years, which is why their capex budgets dominate the sector's cash flows and why their returns on capital are likely to stay subdued until the new plants ramp up. AM/NS India, the unlisted competitor, plans to more than double. The listed mills are at least as exposed to the FY28–FY30 commissioning cluster as to the spread, and the one with the most to gain from discipline, JSW, is also the one with the largest plan.

In plain terms

The winners in Indian steel are not simply the biggest. Carbon and the EU door: decarbonisation costs arriving before the technology Deep dive

Indian mills emit a third more CO₂ per tonne than the world average. Europe has started charging for it, India's own carbon market starts in FY27, and most new capacity is still coal-based.

Steel is responsible for a large share of industrial carbon emissions everywhere, and India's steel is more carbon-intensive than most. The Indian sector averaged about 2.54 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of crude steel in FY24, against a world average of 1.92 tonnes on worldsteel's basis. The gap is structural: India makes most of its steel from coal, either in blast furnaces or in coal-fired sponge-iron kilns, because it has little scrap and expensive gas. That has not mattered commercially until now. From 2026 it matters in two places at once: at the EU border and, from FY27, inside India.

The EU's carbon border charge

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) ran in a reporting-only transitional phase from October 2023 to December 2025 and became definitive on 1 January 2026. EU importers of steel, iron ore pellets, ferro-alloys and other covered goods must buy certificates for the emissions embedded in what they import, net of the free allocation that EU producers still receive. Certificate sales start on 1 February 2027, and the first surrender in 2027 covers 2026 imports. The share of embedded emissions that must be paid for rises as free allocation is phased out, reaching 48.5% by 2030. Importers who cannot provide verified plant data must use default values, and those carry mark-ups of 10% in 2026, 20% in 2027 and 30% from 2028. India's default value for flat steel is reported at about 4.28 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per tonne, against verified plant figures of about 2.2–2.6, though that default figure comes from consultancy summaries and could not be checked against the Commission's implementing regulation.

The difference between verified and default data is where the money is. On ILLUSTRATIVE assumptions, an EU carbon price of €80 a tonne (the midpoint of the €70–90 range in the dossier's watch list) and the 2030 payable share of 48.5%, a tonne of Indian coil with verified emissions of 2.4 tonnes would carry a CBAM cost of about €93; the same tonne on the default value with the 30% mark-up (5.56 tonnes) would carry about €216. Good measurement, reporting and verification is worth roughly €120 a tonne, consistent with the dossier's estimate of "€100+ per tonne". GTRI estimates that Indian exporters may need to cut prices by 15–22% to stay competitive in the EU, and ICRIER projects a 24% fall in India's steel exports to the EU.

The EU's import quota, which matters more for now

A second EU measure has a larger near-term effect. Regulation (EU) 2026/1384, which replaced the EU's steel safeguard on 1 July 2026, cut duty-free import quotas by about 47% to 18.3 million tonnes a year across 30 product categories, imposed a 50% duty on volumes above quota, introduced a "melt and pour" origin rule and reserved half the quota for countries with free-trade agreements. India, one of the EU's two largest steel suppliers in 2024 with Türkiye, has no trade agreement in force with the EU; whether the India–EU negotiations have concluded, and what quota India has been allocated, could not be verified. Jindal Stainless's management says the quota cut, not CBAM, is what is hitting its EU volumes.

The trade data already show the shift. In FY26 Italy (16.2%), Belgium (10.9%) and Spain (7.3%) were among India's largest export destinations, and roughly 35–40% of Indian steel exports went to the EU. In the first half of FY27 the UAE (14.2%) and Vietnam (13.4%) led, with Italy at 9.0% and Belgium at 7.8%, consistent with EU buyers turning cautious from January 2026. EU-bound shipments had risen 45% to 2.46 million tonnes in April–November 2025 as buyers restocked before the charge began.

In plain terms

Europe now charges importers for the carbon in their steel, and it charges extra if they cannot prove how much carbon there is. For an Indian mill the bill could be €90–220 a tonne by 2030, depending mainly on whether its emissions data are verified. Today only a few percent of any Indian company's tonnes go to Europe, so the direct hit is small. The larger risk is indirect: steel that can no longer go to Europe from China, Korea or India itself ends up competing for Indian customers.

India's own carbon price

Inside India the instrument is the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme. Its second draft of greenhouse-gas intensity targets for iron and steel, published around 26 June 2026 (G.S.R. 517(E)), covers 255 obligated plants with a combined baseline of 358.6 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent and sets compliance from FY27, skipping FY26. Required cuts in emission intensity range from 2.1% to 9.3%, with a median of about 5.5%; AM/NS's Hazira plant, for example, must go from 2.2701 to 2.1695 tonnes per tonne. Plants that beat their target earn tradable credits and plants that miss buy them. Targets for other sectors were notified in January 2026, but the steel targets were still in draft in October 2026, and per-company targets for JSW, Tata, SAIL and Jindal Steel could not be found. The Ministry of Steel's goal is 2.2 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of crude steel by 2030.

The Green Steel Taxonomy of December 2024 adds a labelling system that public procurement may eventually reward: green steel is below 2.2 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per tonne of finished steel, three-star 2.0–2.2, four-star 1.6–2.0 and five-star below 1.6, with annual certificates. By July 2026, 97 producers in 15 states held certificates, and AM/NS aims for 70% of its output to meet the taxonomy by 2027. A draft green-steel procurement policy and a National Mission on Green Steel are in preparation.

Who is most exposed? In the long run, the coal-DRI and induction-furnace producers, because their route is the most carbon-intensive in India at 3–3.5 tonnes per tonne and because their customers in construction will be the first targets of green public procurement. GPIL's disclosure shows the measurement problem: 2.485 tonnes per tonne on the worldsteel method, which counts fixed carbon, but 3.18 on the CBAM method, which counts total carbon. Blast-furnace mills sit near 2.3–2.5 and have more tools (pulverised coal injection, top-gas recycling, hydrogen injection, waste-heat recovery) to cut a few percent a year. Jindal Steel's coal-gasification DRI at Angul and the electric furnaces at Tata Ludhiana, Tata's UK project and Jindal Stainless sit at the lower end. In the short run the costs are modest: CCTS cuts of 2–9% and a CBAM bill that applies to a few percent of tonnes. The problem is that about 64% of the capacity being planned for FY31 is coal-based blast furnace, and those plants will operate into the 2050s, when carbon prices are likely to be far higher.

In plain terms

India is building most of its new steel capacity on the cheapest technology available today, coal-fired blast furnaces, while the rules that will price carbon are only beginning. That is a rational bet on costs now and a risk for later: a plant commissioned in FY29 will still be running when the EU charges the full carbon price and India's own carbon market has tightened. The companies with verified emissions data, electric furnaces or gas-based routes are buying an option on that future cheaply.

World steel output fell 2% in 2025 and China's fell 4.4%. India grew 10.4%. Every other big market is putting up walls, so surplus steel looks for the open ones.

Technology and disruption: pipelines now, hydrogen later

The changes that will move costs in the next five years are unglamorous: slurry pipelines, beneficiation, waste-heat power and richer product mix. Hydrogen steel needs a hydrogen price India does not have yet.

The technology that will change Indian steel economics fastest is not the one that gets the most attention. Hydrogen-based direct reduction may eventually replace the blast furnace, but green hydrogen in India costs about ₹200–250 a kilogram, against the less than ₹150 that would make it viable for steel on the National Green Hydrogen Mission's estimates. Until that gap closes, the route shift will be slow: about 64% of planned additions are still blast furnaces, because India has only about 30 million tonnes a year of scrap available and little cheap gas. The near-term gains come from moving raw materials more cheaply, using lower-grade ore, generating power from waste heat and selling more high-value products.

Slurry pipelines and beneficiation

Rail freight alone adds about ₹800 a tonne to the plant-gate cost of ore in the industry dossier's worked example, and trucking from remote mines costs more. Pumping ore as a slurry through a pipeline is much cheaper, and several of the group's companies are building them. Lloyds commissioned an 85 km, 10 MTPA line from Hedri to Konsari in 16 months and plans a 195 km, 16 MTPA line to Ghugus that it says will save ₹800–1,000 a tonne, plus ₹250 from a Chandrapur stockyard. Jindal Steel's 192 km Barbil–Angul line, commissioning from August 2026, should save ₹750–1,000 per tonne of finished steel. SAIL is planning a 258 km line from Jamda to Bokaro for 8.3 million tonnes a year, and NMDC, JSW and AM/NS have their own. Beneficiation, which upgrades low-grade ore and banded haematite quartzite (BHQ), widens the usable resource: Lloyds is building a 30 MTPA BHQ plant whose pilot yield is above 38%, and GPIL's 5.4 MTPA crushing and beneficiation plant is due in Q3 FY27.

Cleaner ironmaking, slowly

Each large producer is testing the technologies that could cut emissions. Tata has run hydrogen injection in a Jamshedpur blast furnace since 2023, operates a 1 MTPA HIsarna demonstration plant, has commissioned a 0.75 MTPA scrap-based electric furnace at Ludhiana with emissions below 0.3 tonnes a tonne, and is building a 3 MTPA electric furnace at Port Talbot in the UK. JSW runs a gas-based DRI unit at Salav and green-hydrogen pilots, and reports about 5 million tonnes of cumulative CO₂ reduction since FY22 and a 16% rise in scrap use. Jindal Steel's Angul plant runs the world's largest coal-gasification DRI and has plans for green-hydrogen DRI. AM/NS has more than 1 GW of renewable power at Hazira. Jindal Stainless is commissioning a 600 Nm³ an hour green-hydrogen unit at Jajpur with Greenzo (about ₹35 crore) and replacing propane with piped gas. The secondary producers rely on waste-heat boilers, solar and electric mining fleets: GPIL runs 15 electric dumpers, 24 electric loaders and 15 electric excavators and is piloting 5 tonnes a day of carbon capture with IIT Bombay.

Value-added products and digital sales

The product frontier is moving faster than the process frontier. India imports most of its cold-rolled grain-oriented electrical steel (CRGO), used in transformers, and the specialty-steel PLI scheme is supporting new capacity: JSW has a CRGO joint venture with JFE, and the PLI's second round has 81 agreements for 8.29 million tonnes. Automotive high-strength grades, tinplate (JSW Tarapur, Tata's 0.4 → 0.7 MTPA expansion), coated and colour-coated sheet for roofing and solar structures, and stainless for railway coaches and metros are all growing faster than commodity steel. Selling is changing too: JSW One, the company's B2B platform, reported gross merchandise value of ₹5,919 crore in Q1 FY27 (up 51%) and is EBITDA-positive, and Tata Steel's e-commerce GMV reached ₹8,495 crore in FY26 (up 137%). Both give the majors direct access to the small buyers that secondary producers have traditionally served.

Two disruptions sit at the edges of the group. GPIL has shelved its planned blast-furnace steel plant and is building a 20 GWh battery energy-storage factory for ₹1,425 crore with cells from EVE Energy, a bet that its future lies in energy rather than steel. For the integrated mills, the biggest technology question of the decade is whether to keep building blast furnaces that will operate for 40 years into a tightening carbon regime, or to accept a higher cost now for gas-based or electric routes. On the evidence of the FY26–FY31 pipeline, they have mostly chosen the furnace.

In plain terms

The cost advantages of the next five years will come from plumbing, not chemistry: pipelines that move ore cheaply, plants that turn waste heat into electricity, and finishing lines that turn commodity coil into higher-priced sheet. Hydrogen steel is real but expensive, and most Indian mills will wait for cheaper hydrogen before betting on it at scale.

Bull vs bear: the spread, the wall and the build

The optimist sees a growing, protected, consolidated market with healthy balance sheets. The pessimist sees a peak spread, a leaking wall and too many furnaces arriving together.

The bull case

  • Demand that keeps growing: consumption up 7.5% a year since FY19 and 7.5% in H1 FY27, ₹12.2 lakh crore of Union capex, and per-person use of 109 kg against a world average of 209.
  • A higher floor under prices: a safeguard to April 2028, anti-dumping duties and quality-control orders make a 2015-style collapse unlikely.
  • A spread at multi-year highs: about ₹28,500 a tonne in Q2 FY27 and ₹31,100 at the October spot (illustrative), with a weak rupee lifting import parity.
  • Oligopoly in flats: five groups supply 85–90% of HRC after the insolvency wave, and they pass costs on quickly.
  • Balance sheets ready for the build: bank debt of US$184 a tonne against US$450 at the last peak; JSW at 1.46×, Jindal Stainless at 0.53×, five companies net-cash.
  • Scarce assets re-rate: legacy ore (Lloyds, SAIL, Tata until 2030), captive power, coal blocks and stainless franchises earn returns of 17–27%.

The bear case

  • Peak spreads mean-revert: the spread was ₹12,400–13,000 in FY24 and ₹18,400 in December 2025; a fall to ₹20,000 would take ₹8,500–11,000 a tonne off the spread.
  • The wall leaks: the safeguard switches off above US$675 CIF, FTA origins pay no basic duty, and India was a net importer again in H1 FY27 with China's share back at 31.8%.
  • Too many furnaces at once: 95–115 MTPA announced to FY31 against a need of about 16 MTPA a year; a 7% demand path with every project on time implies utilisation near 71%.
  • Coal and rupee risk: coking coal at US$278 in September 2026, 85–90% imported; JSW's FY26 rupee loss was about ₹5,600 crore.
  • Dated cost steps: Tata's legacy leases lapse in March 2030, auction premiums of 90–150% spread, and CCTS and CBAM costs rise from FY27.
  • Company-specific overhangs: Tata Netherlands' going-concern uncertainty, Lloyds' ₹19,000 crore consolidated debt, Shyam's ₹50,000 crore MoU, Jai Balaji's downgrade, NMDC Steel's stalled sale.

The two cases disagree less about facts than about which numbers are temporary. The bull reads the October 2026 spread as the start of a better era: the safeguard has put a floor under prices, China's export licences and output controls will restrain its surplus, Indian demand will grow 8–9% on public capex, and the consolidated flat-steel market will keep prices disciplined while capacity is added in an orderly way. On that view the integrated mills' Q1 FY27 margins, ₹19,162 a tonne at Tata India and ₹14,997 at JSW, are closer to a new normal than a peak, and the valuation premium to global peers is justified by growth.

The bear reads the same numbers as a cyclical high built on temporary props. The 2026 rally came from a weak rupee, war-risk freight and a coking-coal spike as much as from the safeguard, and those can reverse together. The safeguard steps down to 11% in April 2027 and expires in April 2028, just as the FY28–FY30 commissioning cluster arrives. Chinese exports were still running at 10 million tonnes a month in August 2026. On this view the right way to value Indian steel is on mid-cycle margins, which the industry dossier puts at ₹12,000–16,000 a tonne for integrated mills, and on that basis the stocks already discount much of the good news.

In plain terms

Both sides agree that India's steel demand is growing and that the industry is in better financial shape than a decade ago. They disagree about whether 2026's profits are the new normal or the top of a cycle. History favours the second view for the industry as a whole; the companies that own something scarce are the ones most likely to prove it wrong.
