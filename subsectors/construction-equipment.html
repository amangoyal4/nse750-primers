00

The one-page view: two special cases at the edges of a JCB market

India buys about 1.37 lakh construction machines a year. Almost none of them are made by a listed Indian company, and the two that are listed barely look like each other.

India is the world's third-largest market for construction equipment by units, behind China and North America. In FY26 its makers sold 1,36,995 machines, worth roughly ₹85,000–90,000 crore (an indicative value; the industry body counts units, not rupees). Most of those machines are backhoe loaders and excavators that dig roads, canals, building foundations and mines. Most of them are made by unlisted subsidiaries of foreign groups: JCB of Britain alone sells more than 40% of all units and about 80% of backhoe loaders.

The NSE 750 contains only two companies in this sub-sector, and neither is a backhoe or excavator maker of scale. Action Construction Equipment (ACE) is a crane company. It dominates the "pick-and-carry" mobile crane, the yellow Hydra that lifts steel and precast concrete on almost every Indian site, and also makes tower cranes, forklifts, a few backhoe loaders, compactors and tractors. BEML is a government-controlled defence PSU, founded in 1964 as Bharat Earth Movers, which still makes large mining dump trucks and dozers for Coal India but now earns more from military trucks and metro coaches. In FY26 its "mining and construction" business was 41% of revenue and only about 5% of its order book.

That makes this an unusual sub-sector report. The industry is shaped by forces that the two listed members feel very differently. Five matter most:

  • Machine demand follows the acceleration of infrastructure spending, not its size. Union capex grew 25–37% a year in FY22–FY24 and industry units grew about 26% a year. Capex growth slowed to 11% in FY25 and 4.2% in FY26 (revised estimate), and units went +3% and then −2%, with domestic sales down 6.7%. The trough was Q3 FY26; a recovery began in Q4 FY26 and continued in H1 FY27.
  • An emission rule set the shape of FY25 and FY26. The CEV Stage V norm took effect on 1 January 2025 for all power bands. It raised small-machine and Hydra-crane prices by 12–15%, pulled demand into FY25, and left FY26 short of buyers. ACE's crane-and-equipment volumes fell 19%.
  • China competes hard, but only in some boxes. China supplies 71% of India's crane imports by value, about 20–22% of excavators (mostly assembled in India), and an estimated 95% of heavy cranes. It does not make pick-and-carry Hydras. A 2025 anti-dumping recommendation on Chinese truck and crawler cranes was never notified.
  • BEML is three businesses in one PSU. Mining equipment, defence vehicles and rail and metro cars run on three unconnected clocks. Its cash conversion cycle is 421 days and it ends the year with almost no cash.
  • The two members have opposite financial models. ACE runs a negative cash cycle, earns a 32% ROCE and holds ₹1,283 crore of investments. BEML earns a 7.7% ROCE. Yet the market pays 86 times trailing earnings for BEML and 27 times for ACE.

In plain terms

If you want to own the Indian construction-machinery cycle through a listed share, you can only buy two edges of it. ACE owns the crane edge, which is profitable and cash-generative but swings hard with small contractors' sentiment. BEML owns the heavy mining-truck edge, which is shrinking inside a company now driven by metro trains and army trucks. The core of the industry, backhoe loaders and excavators, belongs to JCB, Tata Hitachi, SANY, Komatsu and other unlisted subsidiaries.

The mix effect inside the combined numbers is striking. BEML supplies 57% of the members' revenue and 56% of their market value but only 25% of their profit. ACE supplies 43% of revenue and three-quarters of the profit. Any "sub-sector" average blends a 15% margin crane maker with a 7% margin PSU and means little; this report therefore reads the two separately wherever it can, and uses straight sums only where it says so.

The year ahead is a recovery story with a catch. Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) was strong for both: ACE's revenue rose 20.5% on 17% more crane-and-equipment units, and BEML's revenue rose 29% with its first positive first-quarter EBITDA in a decade. The combined Q1 FY27 revenue was ₹1,606 crore (+24.9%) and PAT ₹92 crore. But steel is up about 20% since the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026, the rupee is near ₹95 to the dollar, ACE has raised prices about 10% in three steps, and the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 for a product where 85–90% of buyers borrow. Neither company has reported Q2 FY27; both should report in the first half of November (the SEBI deadline is 14 November 2026).

The rest of this report explains each force in turn, then builds a KPI masterclass for reading a construction-equipment company, compares the two members in a peer table with reference rows for Ajax Engineering and JCB India, and ends with leading indicators and dates to watch. 01

How a machine earns: steel, engines, a dealer and a loan

Volume equipment is an assembly-and-distribution business financed by someone else's balance sheet. Build-to-order equipment is a contracting business financed by your own.

A construction machine is a welded steel structure carrying a bought-in diesel engine, a hydraulic system, axles, tyres and a cabin. The maker designs it, fabricates the steel, assembles the rest and sells it through dealers to contractors who almost always borrow to pay for it. That sentence describes JCB, Tata Hitachi and ACE. It does not describe most of BEML, which builds machines and coaches to government order, one contract at a time.

The volume model

Start with the volume model, which covers about nine machines in ten sold in India. The inputs are steel plate and sections (the largest single cost; ACE calls it "our biggest input material"), castings and forgings, diesel engines bought from Kirloskar, Escorts Kubota, Cummins, Ashok Leyland, Perkins, Kubota or Yanmar, hydraulic pumps, valves and cylinders (largely imported from Japan, Europe and China or made locally by Bosch Rexroth, Danfoss and Kawasaki), axles and transmissions (Carraro, Dana, ZF), tyres and electronics. The manufacturer cuts and welds the frame and boom, paints, assembles and tests. It ships to a dealer, which sells to a buyer and arranges a loan from an NBFC or bank. ICRA and ACE both put the financed share at 85–90% of machines.

The buyer is usually small. A typical backhoe loader or Hydra crane buyer is a single-machine owner who hires the machine out by the hour or the day to contractors. ACE calls these customers "retail hirers". They pay the dealer with a loan, and the dealer pays the manufacturer quickly, often within days of delivery, because the financier disburses on delivery. That is why ACE's receivables are only 32 days of revenue. The manufacturer then earns a second stream from spare parts and service for the machine's 8–12 year life, and the machine eventually enters the used market.

The build-to-order model

BEML works differently in most of its business. In mining, Coal India's subsidiaries and other public miners issue tenders for dump trucks, dozers or excavators. BEML bids, wins on price and domestic preference, builds the machines at Kolar Gold Fields (KGF) and Mysuru, delivers, and is paid against milestones, acceptance tests and retention money. It then earns maintenance and repair contracts (MARC) and spares for a decade. In defence, the Ministry of Defence orders high-mobility trucks (built on a Tatra design), armoured recovery vehicles, bridging systems and mine-laying vehicles after years of development and trials. In rail and metro, a metro corporation or Indian Railways orders cars, which BEML builds at its Bengaluru complex using bought-in propulsion, brakes, doors and air-conditioning, and is paid as work progresses. Each of these is a project, with its own penalty clauses and payment schedule.

What the industry makes

The Indian Construction Equipment Manufacturers' Association (ICEMA) groups machines into five families. Earthmoving dominates: backhoe loaders (the JCB-type machine with a loader bucket in front and a digging arm behind) were 54% of earthmoving units in FY25 and crawler excavators about 36%. Material handling covers cranes and forklifts, which is ACE's territory. Concrete equipment covers self-loading mixers, transit mixers and pumps, where the listed non-member Ajax Engineering leads. Road equipment covers compactors and pavers; material processing covers crushers and screens.

In plain terms

In volume equipment the maker sells a standard machine, gets paid within weeks because a financier pays on delivery, and earns again on spare parts for a decade. In build-to-order equipment the maker wins a contract, spends a year or more building to it, and waits months after delivery for the final payment. Same factory floor, completely different cash profile.

Where is the value captured? In volume equipment, by brand, dealer network and access to finance: JCB's 80% share of backhoe loaders comes with about a 16% operating margin and no debt. In niches, by dominance: ACE's crane-and-equipment segment earned an 18.6% margin in FY26, while its management estimates that competitors earn 8–9% at segment level, and Escorts Kubota's construction-equipment arm reported a 7.9% EBIT margin. In government markets, preference substitutes for brand but does not deliver margin: BEML's operating margin has ranged from 7% to 13% over five years. Everywhere, spares and service are the steadiest profit pool; BEML's management names "sustenance" (spares and services) as its highest-margin line.

02

1.37 lakh machines a year, and almost none of them listed

The third-largest market in the world by units, roughly a US$10 billion industry, grew 26% a year for two years and then stopped.

The industry's recent history fits in four numbers. ICEMA counted 1,07,779 machines in FY23, 1,35,650 in FY24, 1,40,191 in FY25 and 1,36,995 in FY26. Two years of 26% growth were followed by a 3% rise and then a 2% fall. ICEMA's own value estimate for FY25 was about US$10 billion; CRISIL put it at roughly ₹83,000 crore. A reasonable FY26 value is ₹85,000–90,000 crore, since units fell 2% while Stage V raised prices 5–8% on average (an indicative estimate).

How big the listed members are

The members' combined FY26 revenue was ₹7,631 crore, up 3.8% from ₹7,349 crore. That number mixes very different trajectories. In plain terms

India's construction-equipment industry is roughly the size of a large listed cement company's revenue, and nearly all of it is earned by foreign-owned subsidiaries that publish little. The two listed members together are about one-twentieth of the market by value. What they show you is not the industry average; it is two specific ways of making money at its edges.

Globally, the top 50 equipment makers sold US$246.6 billion in 2025 (up 3.8%), according to the KHL Yellow Table. India is roughly 4% of that by value but a much larger share of units, because its machines are smaller and cheaper: backhoe loaders and Hydras rather than 200-tonne haul trucks. The global context matters later when we compare margins, but the key point here is that India's market is large in units, small in value, and almost entirely owned by companies whose shares Indian investors cannot buy.

03

Acceleration, not level: why a record capex budget sold fewer machines Deep dive

Contractors buy machines to add capacity. Capacity is needed when spending grows, not when it stays high. That one idea explains FY23–FY26.

In FY26 the Union government spent about ₹10.96 lakh crore on capital assets, close to a record and almost three times FY20's ₹3.4 lakh crore. In the same year, domestic sales of construction machines fell about 7%. The two facts look contradictory until one asks what a machine is for. A contractor buys an excavator to do more work than its current fleet can handle. If next year's work is the same as this year's, the existing fleet does it; only worn-out machines are replaced. New buying depends on the increase in work, so machine demand tracks the growth rate of spending rather than its level.

In plain terms

A road contractor with ten excavators can keep building the same number of kilometres every year with those ten machines, buying only a replacement or two. He needs an eleventh and twelfth only when the work grows. So when government spending goes from "growing fast" to "high but flat", machine orders fall even though the country is still spending record sums. Watching whether capex is accelerating tells you more about machine sales than watching how big the budget is.

Roads: the swing end-market

Roads are the largest single end-market, probably 35–40% of earthmoving demand (an indicative estimate), and FY26 was their trough. National highway construction slipped to about 25.7 km a day (roughly 9,380 km), an eight-year low, from 33.8 km a day in FY24. The National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) awarded only 3,124 km in FY26, against 4,008 km in FY25 and a target of about 4,500. Awards lead machine demand by roughly 6–12 months, because a contractor buys equipment after winning a project and mobilising, so the FY26 award trough fed the weak first half of FY27 in road machinery. Behind the slowdown sit three policy shifts: the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways is moving from the Bharatmala programme to fewer, larger access-controlled corridors; NHAI is cutting its debt by selling toll rights (1,692 km identified for FY27); and its budget support is flat at ₹1.87 lakh crore.

Credit: the second-order lever

Because 85–90% of machines are financed, the cost and availability of credit amplifies the capex signal. A hirer who buys a ₹30 lakh machine with a ₹27 lakh loan at 12–14% over four years pays about ₹71,000–74,000 a month (an ILLUSTRATIVE standard annuity). A one-point rise in the loan rate adds roughly ₹1,200–1,300 a month, small in itself but meaningful for a hirer whose machine works 150–200 hours a month at a few hundred to a few thousand rupees an hour. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026. If NBFC lending rates follow, H2 FY27 demand from small hirers will be tested at exactly the moment prices are rising for steel. Large contractors and miners are less sensitive; they buy when they have work.

In plain terms

The best single forecast of next year's machine sales is not the size of the budget but how much faster it is growing, adjusted for whether roads are actually being awarded and whether small buyers can get loans cheaply. FY26 failed all three tests; FY27 so far passes the first, half-passes the second and is being tested on the third.

What does this mean for the two members? For ACE the capex link is direct but filtered through industrial and real-estate demand: ACE estimates its FY27 end-markets as manufacturing and logistics 40–45%, infrastructure and construction 40–45% (including real estate 10–12%), and agriculture 6–7%. Its cranes follow factory and warehouse building as much as roads. For BEML the link is indirect. Its mining machines follow Coal India's production plans, its metro cars follow city rail projects that run on five- to ten-year cycles, and its defence vehicles follow the Ministry of Defence's procurement calendar. Union capex matters to BEML mainly through the railway budget, which is the subject of the next section.

04

Roads, rails, coal and armies: four end-markets on four clocks

ACE sells into the broad construction and industrial cycle. BEML sells into three government programmes that rarely move together.

Construction equipment is a derived-demand business: nobody wants an excavator, they want a road, a mine, a metro line or a factory shed. Each end-market has its own budget, its own procurement rules and its own timing. Reading the sub-sector means knowing which clock each company is wired to.

Rail and metro: BEML's largest forward driver

The railway budget for FY27 is ₹2.93 lakh crore of capital spending, of which ₹65,497 crore is for rolling stock (coaches, wagons and locomotives). Metro rail adds a second, city-funded stream: India has about 1,000 km of metro in operation and about 900 km under construction across roughly two dozen cities, and BEML's management sees a market of more than 15,000 metro cars over five years. BEML builds stainless-steel and aluminium metro cars at its Bengaluru complex for the Bengaluru, Mumbai and Delhi systems, has built two 16-car Vande Bharat sleeper trains (commissioned by South Western Railway in August 2026, after the first sleeper service was flagged off on 17 January 2026), holds a 600-coach LHB order with dispatches from June 2026, and is building a prototype high-speed train designed for 280 km/h. Indian Railways plans about 260 Vande Bharat sleeper trainsets across BEML, the Integral Coach Factory (ICF), the Kinet consortium and Titagarh–BHEL.

The rail and metro clock is slow and lumpy. A metro contract is tendered, awarded, designed, prototyped, type-tested and then delivered over three to five years. BEML's rail and metro revenue was ₹1,551 crore in FY24, ₹760 crore in FY25 and ₹1,040 crore in FY26, and then grew 178% in Q1 FY27 as Bengaluru Phase 2 deliveries ramped. Rail and metro is about 71% of BEML's order book at March 2026 (₹11,320 crore). Our railway wagons report covers the rolling-stock budget in more detail.

Coal and mining: the clock BEML was built for

Mining is where BEML's construction-equipment identity survives. Two shifts are working against BEML. Steel and the price lag

Steel is the largest single input for a crane or backhoe loader, about 30% of the selling price (an indicative estimate). By July 2026 steel prices were up about 20%, and ACE's management described input inflation of 11–12% across "every possible commodity", including tyres, rubber and plastics. ACE raised prices about 1–1.5% in January 2026, 3–4% in March–May and 5–6% in June, about 10% in all, and said another 2% may follow. Its Q1 FY27 gross margin was still about 140 basis points lower than a year earlier, the visible lag.

A simple ILLUSTRATIVE calculation shows why ACE needed roughly 8–10% of price to hold its margin. Per ₹100 of FY26 revenue, materials cost 67.5, of which steel about 30. A 20% steel rise adds 6.0 of cost. Employees and other expenses stay at 17.2. If ACE raises prices 10% and the whole rise sticks, revenue becomes 110 and costs 90.7, so operating profit is 19.3, a 17.5% margin. At a 5% realised rise, profit is 14.3 on 105, a 13.6% margin. With no price rise at all the margin would fall to about 9.3%. If all materials (not just steel) rise 11.5%, the break-even price rise to hold 15.4% is about 9%.

Engines and Stage V

Engines are the second big cost and the one regulation moved most. The CEV Stage V rule required electronically controlled common-rail engines with exhaust after-treatment (diesel oxidation catalysts, particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction) on machines that previously used simpler mechanical engines. ICRA estimated the price effect at 12–15% for machines jumping from Stage III to Stage V (most small machines, including Hydra cranes) and 4–6% for machines moving from Stage IV. Escorts Kubota reported 6–7% and 3.5–4.5% for its own range. GST 2.0 in September 2025 cut the GST on diesel engines (HS 8408), engine parts and tyres from 28% to 18%; for a manufacturer that claims input-tax credit this helps working capital rather than cost, so its effect on machine prices is small.

JCB's industry, everyone else's niche

One unlisted British-owned company sells more than 40% of India's machines. The rest of the industry is a set of niches, each with its own leader.

Concentration in Indian construction equipment varies enormously by product. Backhoe loaders are close to a monopoly; excavators are a four- or five-player oligopoly with a rising Chinese share; pick-and-carry cranes are a two-company market; heavy cranes are Chinese-dominated; concrete equipment has its own leaders. Who wins depends on which box you are in.

JCB entered India in 1979 through a joint venture with Escorts and has been wholly owned by the British JCB group for years. CRISIL credits it with more than 40% of all construction-equipment units, about 80% of backhoe loaders and more than 15% of excavators. In FY24 it sold 63,355 machines, roughly 47% of ICEMA's total. Its operating income was ₹20,201 crore in FY25 and a provisional ₹19,710 crore in FY26 (−2.4%), with a CRISIL-adjusted profit of ₹2,157 crore, an operating margin of about 16%, no debt, and capacity sufficient for three to four years. India is JCB's largest market, and its Indian plants export to about 140 countries.

In plain terms

In this industry you make money either by being huge (JCB, with a dealer in every district and 80% of one product) or by owning a niche nobody else wants to fight for (ACE in Hydra cranes, Ajax in self-loading mixers). Being medium-sized in a contested product, which describes BEML's mining machines against Komatsu and Caterpillar, earns a thin margin.

Global groups dominate value. In the 2026 KHL Yellow Table, Caterpillar ranks first, Komatsu second and XCMG third (US$14.2 billion, the only Chinese company in the top five); the top ten also include Deere, SANY, Volvo CE, Hitachi Construction Machinery, JCB and Sandvik, and 13 Chinese makers feature in the top 50. The listed route into this industry, apart from the two members, is through Escorts Kubota (whose construction arm is about 15% of its revenue), Ajax Engineering (concrete), L&T (through its machinery arm) and the crane-rental company Sanghvi Movers, none of which is in this sub-sector.

BEML's controlling shareholder is the Government of India (54.03%, unchanged every quarter since at least December 2023), and ACE's is the Agarwal family (65.42%), with founder Vijay Agarwal holding about 28.75% directly according to our primer.

The anti-dumping duty that never came

India's Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) investigated Chinese crane exports and, in final findings dated 19 September 2025, recommended anti-dumping duties of 24% to 52% of the landed (CIF) value for five years on Chinese crawler cranes of 40–260 tonnes and truck cranes of 25–160 tonnes, excluding rough-terrain and all-terrain cranes of 160 tonnes and above. Under Indian practice the Finance Ministry must notify such a recommendation within about 90 days for it to take effect. It did not. By May 2026 ACE's management said the recommendation "seems... time-barred"; in July it said there were "no leads so far... still a ray of hope", and attributed the inaction to geopolitics, including US tariff pressure and a thaw in India–China relations. A similar pattern, where DGTR recommends and the Finance Ministry declines to notify, has appeared in other industries.

Industry sources say Chinese makers hold nearly 95% of India's heavy-crane market, and that five domestic crane makers, TIL, Kobelco India, Tadano Escorts India, ABG Cranes and Tata Hitachi, have shut crane manufacturing. The European Union opened its own anti-dumping investigation into Chinese mobile cranes in December 2025, a sign that Chinese capacity is looking for export outlets worldwide. Basic customs duty on cranes is 7.5%, and ACE argues Chinese crane prices "need to be at least 25–30% higher for them to make money" after duty and freight, which is its explanation for why Chinese makers have started assembling locally (at an extra cost of 8–10%, by ACE's estimate).

In plain terms

The government's own trade investigators decided Chinese truck and crawler cranes were being sold in India below fair value and asked for a duty of up to 52%. The Finance Ministry let the recommendation lapse. For ACE that matters less than it sounds, because its core Hydra cranes are a product Chinese makers do not build. It matters more for ACE's plan to grow in heavy cranes through its Japanese joint venture.

Why the Hydra is safe and the excavator is not

Chinese makers compete on price, financing and credit terms; ACE's management describes their pricing as "subsidised". They have won share where the product is global and standard: excavators (20–22% brand share, up from under 10% five years earlier, per Tata Hitachi's MD), heavy cranes, piling rigs and forklifts. They have not cracked two products. The backhoe loader is a JCB-defined machine with a dealer and service network that would take a decade to replicate. The pick-and-carry Hydra crane, a simple, rugged, articulated mobile crane that travels with its load, is an Indian-market product below 30–35 tonnes; ACE says Chinese makers "are really not doing pick-and-carry cranes". That is the single most important fact about ACE's competitive position, and it explains why its segment margin is more than twice what its management estimates competitors earn.

Other policy levers lean against China. Press Note 3 (2020) requires government approval for investment from countries sharing a land border with India, which slows new Chinese plants. Public procurement rules give preference to "Class-I local suppliers" with at least 50% local content, which helps both members in government tenders. BEML's annual report names "dumping price strategy" by international players in mining and metro as a threat, and global groups (Caterpillar, Komatsu, Alstom, Siemens) "operating directly in India rather than licensing" as another.

In plain terms

China is a price-setter in the products it makes well and ships cheaply: big cranes, forklifts and, through local factories, excavators. It is nearly absent in the two most Indian machines, the backhoe loader and the Hydra. A listed investor in this sub-sector is, in effect, betting on how long those two moats hold.

The same sub-sector contains a company that is paid before it pays its suppliers and one that waits more than a year to turn a rupee of cost into a rupee of cash.

Five business models make money in Indian construction equipment. The two listed members represent the two most different of them, and the difference shows up less in margins than in cash: how long money sits in receivables and inventory before it comes back. That single difference explains most of the gap in returns on capital between them.

Why ACE's cash cycle is negative

ACE sells a standard machine through dealers to buyers who are financed by NBFCs and banks. The financier disburses on delivery, so ACE's trade receivables were only 32 days of revenue in FY26. Its inventory, mostly steel, engines and finished cranes awaiting dispatch, was 100 days of cost. Its suppliers, meanwhile, gave it 151 days of credit. Receivable days plus inventory days minus payable days gives a cash conversion cycle of −20 days: ACE's suppliers fund its working capital, and every extra rupee of sales releases cash rather than consuming it. Screener's "working-capital days", which uses all current assets and liabilities, was −26.

The result is that ACE turns its profit into cash almost one for one. In FY26 its cash from operations was ₹417 crore, about equal to its ₹415 crore profit after tax and 83% of its ₹504 crore operating profit (our calculation), even after paying tax and funding a small rise in inventory; its free cash flow was ₹330 crore, about 80% of its profit after tax. BEML's cash from operations was ₹118 crore, 39% of its operating profit. It holds ₹1,283 crore of investments (₹723 crore non-current, ₹560 crore current), ₹77 crore of cash and bank balances and essentially no debt; its net debt-to-equity ratio was −0.66 at March 2026.

Why BEML's is more than a year

BEML builds to government order. A metro car is recognised as revenue as work progresses, but billed and paid only at milestones. A mining dump truck is paid after delivery and acceptance, with retention money held back. Defence vehicles go through trials before acceptance. So at March 2026 BEML had ₹2,274 crore of trade receivables and another ₹716 crore of contract assets (work done but not yet billable), together about ₹2,990 crore or roughly 251 days of revenue. Screener's debtor days, which exclude contract assets, were 191; CareEdge's, which include them, were 224. Inventory was 394 days of cost on screener's measure (BEML holds long-lead components and work-in-progress for several programmes at once). Suppliers gave it 164 days. The cash conversion cycle was 421 days in FY26, after 487 in FY25, and has ranged between 358 and 673 days since FY15.

BEML funds this gap with customer advances (other liabilities of ₹3,892 crore at March 2026, mostly advances and payables), supplier credit and short-term borrowing (₹292 crore standalone, with no long-term debt). It ended FY26 with ₹6.7 crore of cash and ₹1.3 crore of other bank balances. Its free cash flow was −₹208 crore in FY26 and −₹92 crore in FY25. In the same year it paid a dividend of ₹17.087 per share, about 100% of its profit (roughly ₹142 crore), a choice that reflects its majority owner's needs as much as its own.

Rules that move volumes: Stage V, GST and a trade remedy left unsigned

An emission rule produced the industry's FY25 pre-buy and FY26 slump. A tax change widely reported as a machine-price cut was nothing of the sort.

Regulation shapes construction-equipment demand more than in most capital-goods businesses, because the product is a diesel vehicle (so emission and safety rules apply), it is bought by price-sensitive small owners (so taxes and credit matter), and its main buyer is the state (so procurement rules decide who wins). Four sets of rules matter now.

CEV Stage V: one date, all power bands

Construction equipment vehicles (CEVs) are regulated under Rule 115A of the Central Motor Vehicles Rules. The Stage V norm, aligned with the European Union's Stage V, requires electronic engines and exhaust after-treatment. It was originally due on 1 April 2024; notification GSR 163(E) of 7 March 2024 deferred it to 1 January 2025, and it applied to all power bands from that single date. Notification GSR 542(E) of 30 June 2026 (the CMV Eleventh Amendment Rules, in force from 1 July 2026) restated the CEV schedule unchanged. Two later dates in press reports refer to other things: ICRA's mention of "1 July 2025" most likely marks the end of a six-month registration grace for pre-2025 stock (an analyst inference), and the 1 October 2026 date is the start of TREM Stage V for most tractor bands and the trigger for in-service emission monitoring of CEVs made after that date. One newspaper report that machines "above 56 kW" must comply from October 2026 conflates these.

The demand effect was textbook. A known price rise on a known date pulled purchases into FY25, especially by small hirers who could not easily absorb 12–15% more. FY26 then lacked those buyers and faced a new product many did not trust: the "old-generation" Hydra crane went from a mechanical Tier 3-equivalent engine "straight to Tier 5", as ACE's management put it. ACE says the scepticism "settled in the last 7–8 months" before July 2026. Section 11 follows the effect through ACE's numbers.

In plain terms

Stage V did to construction machinery what a tax increase with a fixed start date does to any big-ticket purchase: everyone who could buy early did, and the months after the deadline were empty. The industry's weak FY26 was partly a hangover from its own FY25 rush, not only a sign of weak building activity.

GST: what GST 2.0 did and did not change

Much commentary in 2025 said that GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, cut the tax on construction machinery from 28% to 18% and so offset the Stage V price rise. That is wrong. Bulldozers, excavators, loaders, road rollers, earth-moving machinery and "fork lifts, lifting and handling equipment", which are HS headings 8426, 8427, 8429 and 8430, moved from 28% to 18% on 15 November 2017, in the 178-item rate cut of that year. They were at 18% before GST 2.0 and stayed at 18% after it; the 56th GST Council's list of 28%-to-18% changes contains none of these headings. What GST 2.0 did cut, from 28% to 18%, were goods vehicles under HS 8704 (which include dumpers and rear-dump trucks), diesel engines (8408), engine parts (8409), vehicle parts (8708), tyres (4011) and cement; tractors and agricultural machinery fell from 12% to 5%.

PSU governance: the disinvestment that went quiet

BEML's ownership is itself a policy variable. The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs gave in-principle approval in October 2016 for the strategic sale of 26% of BEML with management control. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs approved that scheme by an order dated 28 July 2022, with an appointed date of 25 August 2022; BLAL was later listed separately and is not part of BEML's numbers. BEML now leases back about 300 acres in Mysuru and 36 acres in Bengaluru from BLAL on 99-year leases. No financial bids have ever been invited, and CareEdge's July 2026 rationale records "currently no progress". The government's 54.03% stake has not changed. The option of a strategic sale remains in the share price as a possibility, but nothing in 2026 suggests it is moving.

In plain terms

Three regulatory facts a reader must get right: the emission rule took effect for every machine on 1 January 2025; machine GST has been 18% since 2017, so the 2025 tax reform did not make cranes or excavators cheaper; and the crane anti-dumping duty was recommended but never imposed. Many market commentaries in 2025–26 got at least one of these wrong.

Booms, busts and a timeline: six decades in two companies

The industry's cycles follow infrastructure policy. ACE's ROCE went from 3% to 42% and back to 32%; BEML's never left single digits until FY24.

Indian construction equipment has had three modern cycles. The first ended in the 2012–2014 bust, when stalled road projects under the build-operate-transfer (BOT) model left contractors with idle fleets. The second began with the Bharatmala road programme in 2017 and was interrupted by COVID. The third, the public-capex boom of FY22–FY24, peaked in FY25 and troughed in Q3 FY26.

The 2012–2014 bust is the reminder that a crane dominator can still earn almost nothing in a capex collapse. ACE's ROCE fell to 3% in FY15. The NHAI's BOT boom of the late 2000s had attracted private developers who over-bid, then could not finance or complete projects as land acquisition and clearances stalled; contractors stopped buying machines and many defaulted on equipment loans. Industry volumes fell sharply (by about 30% or more from FY12 to FY14, an approximate figure we could not verify). Recovery came through the hybrid annuity model (HAM) introduced in 2016 and the Bharatmala programme of 2017, which shifted risk back to the government and restarted awards.

The FY22–FY24 boom was the biggest in the industry's history. Union capex grew 25–37% a year as the government used its balance sheet to lift investment after COVID. ICEMA units grew about 26% a year in FY23 and FY24 to a record 1,35,650. ACE's revenue roughly doubled from ₹1,630 crore to ₹2,914 crore and its operating margin rose from 9% to 14%. BEML's share price re-rated on defence indigenisation, metro orders and the disinvestment option, even though its revenue did not grow.

FY25 was a pause disguised as a record. The general election froze road awards for months, so underlying demand slowed, but the Stage V pre-buy lifted volumes to 1,40,191. FY26 delivered both hangovers at once: no pre-buy, the weakest capex growth since FY20, an eight-year low in highway construction, slow Jal Jeevan Mission payments, a long monsoon, and then, from 28 February 2026, the West Asia war's shock to steel, crude, freight and the rupee.

Hydra, Stage V and China: the crane market that makes ACE Deep dive

A rugged Indian crane that Chinese makers do not build earns ACE twice its rivals' margin. An emission rule nearly broke its market for a year, and a Japanese joint venture is its answer to China in heavy cranes.

Walk onto any Indian building site, refinery or metro depot and you will probably see a yellow articulated crane driving around with a load hanging from its boom. That is a pick-and-carry (P&C) crane, known across India as a "Hydra" after an early model. It is cheap (roughly ₹25–30 lakh), simple to operate, and can move with its load, which fixed cranes cannot. Single-machine owners buy it on credit and hire it out by the day. It is the single most important product in ACE's business and the key to its margin.

The two kinds of pick-and-carry crane

The market has two generations. The "old-generation" Hydra uses a truck-like chassis with a rear-mounted boom and a mechanical engine; until January 2025 most used Tier 3-equivalent engines. The "new-generation" (NG) P&C crane is a purpose-built articulated machine with a modern cab, safety electronics such as load-moment indicators, and higher capacity, costing 30–35% more than a comparable Hydra. Large industrial users have been moving to NG cranes for safety reasons. ACE's management estimates the market split as follows: in FY25 Hydras were 60–65% of P&C crane volumes and NG cranes 30–35%; in FY26 Hydras fell to "a little less than 60%" and NG rose above 40%, because Stage V hit Hydra buyers harder; in FY27 the split should revert to about 60/40 as Hydra buying normalises; in two to three years it should reach about 50/50. ACE's own mix is about 60/40 Hydra to NG, and it says it has led the NG segment for two years.

Where China fits: heavy cranes, not Hydras

The crane market divides by size. Below about 30–35 tonnes, pick-and-carry cranes (Hydra and NG) dominate and are made in India by ACE and Escorts; ACE says Chinese makers "are really not doing pick-and-carry cranes". The KATO joint venture: ACE's answer in heavy cranes

Rather than wait for the anti-dumping duty, ACE formed a joint venture with KATO Works of Japan, a maker of truck, crawler and rough-terrain cranes. ACE KATO Pvt Ltd was incorporated on 11 March 2026; ACE infused initial capital on 19 April 2026; the JV was not consolidated in FY26 and its shareholding is to be restructured to 50:50. Total investment is about ₹200 crore: KATO contributes ₹100 crore in cash, ACE ₹100 crore in kind (models, technology and infrastructure). ACE's existing truck, crawler and rough-terrain crane business transfers into the JV. There is no royalty on Indian models; KATO-design models made for export, priced at roughly twice Indian models, carry a royalty of about 3% of net selling price. Upgraded Indian models to KATO standards are planned from Q4 FY27 and dedicated KATO-design models in about a year, with a 50–60% localisation target. KATO will also source components from India through ACE.

Management expects JV revenue from Q3 FY27 and meaningful contributions from FY28: more than ₹300 crore within three to four years without the anti-dumping duty, and ₹700–800 crore if the duty were notified (ICICI Direct notes a ₹250–300 crore target). Two accounting points follow. If, as seems likely, the JV is equity-accounted, ACE's reported revenue loses the heavy-crane sales that move into it, and only ACE's share of JV profit appears below operating profit, so reported revenue growth could dip slightly while value is preserved. That needs confirming in the Q2 or Q3 FY27 notes.

In plain terms

BEML's future is being built in Bhopal and Bengaluru, not at the Kolar mining-equipment plant. An ILLUSTRATIVE metro car

One car shows why delays are so costly. At ₹7.74 crore a car, bought-out systems (propulsion, brakes, air-conditioning, doors, signalling interfaces) take perhaps 55–65% of the price. If BEML's contribution after materials and direct labour is 15–20% of the price, about ₹1.2–1.5 crore a car, and its rail fixed costs need about 250 cars a year to be absorbed (all analyst estimates), a three- to six-month slip in deliveries moves a large block of contribution from one quarter to the next while fixed costs keep running. That is the arithmetic behind BEML's loss-making first and third quarters, and behind the large penalty and provision lines when a fixed-price contract runs late in a falling rupee.

In plain terms

BEML can grow only by investing cash it does not have in factories and work-in-progress that its government customers will pay for months later. It is managing that by borrowing short-term and asking for faster billing. If execution improves, the order book turns into profit and cash; if it slips again, more penalties and more debt follow. The 421-day cash cycle is the number to watch.

Reading a construction-equipment company: the KPIs that matter Deep dive

Two equations, twelve numbers and a ten-minute routine. Most of the mistakes investors make with these two companies come from reading the right number on the wrong basis.

A construction-equipment company can be read through one of two equations, depending on whether it sells standard machines from stock or builds to order. ACE is the first kind and BEML mostly the second. The KPIs that follow are the terms of those equations, plus the working-capital and return measures that decide whether reported profit becomes cash. Each KPI below gives the definition, the formula, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, a benchmark, how the two members compare, and the trap that catches readers.

The two equations

For a volume or niche maker such as ACE, revenue is units sold multiplied by realisation per unit, summed across product lines. Operating profit is revenue minus materials, employees and other expenses, and materials move with steel and engine prices with a lag before price increases catch up. Management controls the price list, the product mix, cost efficiency and dealer terms; it does not control the industry's unit demand, the cost of the buyer's loan, steel prices or the timing of emission rules.

A worked example in round numbers. Take ACE in Q1 FY27. It sold 2,740 crane-and-equipment units at about ₹27 lakh each, roughly ₹740 crore of segment revenue (the company reports ₹738 crore). Add about ₹43 crore of agri revenue and a few crore of other sales to reach ₹786 crore of consolidated revenue. Materials took about two-thirds; staff and other expenses about a sixth; operating profit was ₹118 crore, 15.0%. Now BEML for FY26 in round numbers: it began the year with about ₹14,600 crore of orders and booked about ₹5,600 crore of new ones. In plain terms

For ACE, ask "how many machines, at what price, against what steel cost?" For BEML, ask "how big is the order book, how fast is it being delivered, and did any contract go wrong?" The two questions need different numbers, and an investor who applies one company's checklist to the other will misread both.

KPI 1 · Units sold and volume growth

What it is. The number of machines invoiced in a period. ICEMA's industry count is manufacturers' dispatches, including exports. Formula. Volume growth = this period's units ÷ the same period last year's units − 1; relative performance = company growth − industry growth. Worked example. ACE sold 2,740 CMCE units in Q1 FY27 against 2,337 a year earlier: 2,740 ÷ 2,337 − 1 = +17.25%. ICEMA's FY26 total fell 2.3% while ACE's CMCE volumes fell 19%, a relative under-performance of about 17 points in FY26, which reversed in Q1 FY27.

What moves it. Industry demand (the capex-acceleration link, Section 3), credit availability, the emission-rule calendar, the company's dealer reach and share, and its product range. A pre-buy year flatters volumes; the year after punishes them. Benchmark. Good: growing at least 5 points faster than the relevant ICEMA category over a full cycle. Average: within ±5 points. Weak: lagging by more than 5 points for two or more years. How the members compare. ACE discloses quarterly CMCE units; BEML does not disclose units at all, which is reasonable for a company whose products range from a ₹30 lakh grader to a multi-crore dump truck and a ₹7–11 crore metro car, but leaves volume analysis impossible.

KPI 2 · Realisation per unit

What it is. Average revenue per machine sold. Formula. Segment revenue ÷ segment units. Worked example. ACE's CMCE revenue of ₹738.37 crore in Q1 FY27 ÷ 2,740 units = ₹26.9 lakh per unit, against ₹25.9 lakh a year earlier (+4%). ICICI Direct, using a slightly broader revenue line (₹742.4 crore), puts it at ₹27.1 lakh (+4.6%). For FY26, ₹2,953 crore ÷ 10,853 units ≈ ₹27.2 lakh.

What moves it. List-price increases (ACE took about 10% in January–June 2026), mix (heavier Hydras and NG cranes at a 30–35% premium raise it; forklifts and compactors lower it), discounts and dealer incentives, and regulatory cost pass-through such as Stage V. Benchmark. Good: realisation growing at least 3 points faster than input inflation without loss of share. Average: in line with inflation. Weak: below inflation, or rising only because of mix while like-for-like prices fall. How the members compare. ACE: about +18% in FY26 (estimated, as FY25 units are implied) and +4–4.6% in Q1 FY27 (chart in Section 11). BEML: not meaningful, because its products are not comparable units.

The definition trap. A rising realisation can mean pricing power or a mix shift; the two have very different implications. In FY26 ACE's realisation rose partly because Stage V machines cost 12–15% more, which is a cost pass-through, not a margin gain. The test is whether the segment margin rose too: it did, slightly (18.3% to 18.6%), so ACE passed through all of the cost and a little more.

In plain terms

Units times price is revenue. In a bad year ACE lost a fifth of its units and made it up on price; in a good year both rise together. If you see units up and realisation falling, a price war or a cheaper mix is under way; if you see realisation up and margins flat, it is only passing on costs.

KPI 3 · Market share

What it is. The company's units as a share of a defined market. Formula. Company units in category ÷ category units (the basis must be stated). Worked example. If ACE sold, say, 6,000 pick-and-carry cranes in a market of 9,500, its share would be 63%. (These unit figures are ILLUSTRATIVE; ACE does not publish crane-only units.)

What moves it. Product range and price, dealer coverage, service and spares availability, financing tie-ups, and the arrival or exit of competitors (five Indian crane makers have exited; Chinese makers sit out the P&C segment). Benchmark. In a niche, above 40% is dominant, 15–40% is a contender, below 15% is marginal. How the members compare. ACE claims more than 63% of mobile cranes and about 60% of tower cranes; Escorts Kubota claims 41.4% of pick-and-carry cranes on an ICEMA basis. BEML says it has a "major market share in high-capacity dozers and metro cars" and a "relatively small market share" in construction equipment, without numbers. The comparison chart is in Section 11.

The definition trap. Companies choose their own denominator. ACE's "mobile cranes" and Escorts' "pick-and-carry cranes (ICEMA)" are different markets; both companies call themselves number one. Treat any share claim as INDICATIVE unless it is computed from one industry dataset, and never average two companies' claims.

KPI 4 · Order book and book-to-bill

What it is. The value of signed but unexecuted contracts (order book) and the ratio of new orders to revenue (book-to-bill). Formula. Order book cover = order book ÷ trailing revenue. Implied inflow = closing book − opening book + revenue. Book-to-bill = inflow ÷ revenue. Worked example. BEML FY26: ₹15,896 crore − ₹14,610 crore + ₹4,351 crore = ₹5,637 crore of inflow; ÷ ₹4,351 crore of revenue = 1.30. Order book cover = ₹15,896 crore ÷ ₹4,351 crore = 3.7 years of revenue.

What moves it. Government tender timing, win rates, contract sizes (one metro or high-speed rail award can add a year's revenue), cancellations, price-variation adjustments and currency on export orders. Benchmark. For build-to-order engineers, a book of 2.5–4 times revenue with book-to-bill above 1.1 is good; 1.5–2.5 times average; below 1.5 times or book-to-bill below 0.9 weak. A very high cover can also signal slow execution. How the members compare. BEML: 3.7 times with book-to-bill of 1.30 in FY26 and 1.44 in Q1 FY27. ACE: not meaningful; it is a short-cycle business that discloses only a ₹575 crore defence order book.

KPI 5 · Revenue mix and segment result

What it is. The share of revenue (and, where disclosed, profit) from each business line. Formula. Segment revenue ÷ total revenue; segment margin = segment result ÷ segment revenue. Worked example. ACE FY26: CMCE ₹2,953 crore with a result of ₹550 crore (18.6%); agri ₹251 crore external revenue with a result of ₹2.5 crore (about 1%); others ₹77 crore with a result of ₹20 crore. CMCE is about 90% of revenue and about 96% of segment profit. BEML FY26: mining and construction ₹1,789 crore (41%), defence ₹1,522 crore (35%), rail and metro ₹1,040 crore (24%).

What moves it. Relative growth of each line and, for BEML, the timing of deliveries in each vertical. Benchmark. A good mix has more than a quarter of revenue in less cyclical lines (defence, spares, exports) and a core segment margin above 18%. How the members compare. ACE's agri arm is a drag: materials alone are 87% of agri revenue. BEML is exempt from Ind AS 108 segment reporting as a defence producer, so it will never disclose segment margins; management says spares and services are the most profitable and exports second.

The definition trap. ACE's annual report shows CMCE at ₹2,953 crore, its MD&A table rounds it to ₹2,946 crore, and a broker's note shows another number for Q1 that includes inter-segment lines. BEML's "mining and construction" vertical includes spares, servicing and some exports, so its decline (−18%) understates the 35% fall in machine sales. Read the product note, not just the vertical.

KPI 7 · Receivable days and contract assets

What it is. How long customers take to pay. Formula. Trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365; for project businesses, add contract assets (work done, not yet billable). Worked example. BEML: ₹2,274 crore ÷ ₹4,351 crore × 365 = 191 days; including ₹716 crore of contract assets, ₹2,990 crore ÷ ₹4,351 crore × 365 ≈ 251 days. ACE: about ₹284 crore ÷ ₹3,280 crore × 365 ≈ 32 days.

What moves it. Customer type (financier-paid dealer sales versus government milestones), billing timing (BEML bills about 40% of the year's revenue in Q4, so year-end receivables are high), retention money and disputes. Benchmark. Below 45 days good; 45–120 average; above 120 weak. How the members compare. BEML 191 days (screener; 224 on CareEdge's measure including contract assets), up from 116 in FY23; ACE 32 days, 29 in FY25. Together, revenue-weighted, about 123 days, but the average is meaningless: 89% of the members' combined receivables are BEML's.

The definition trap. Screener's debtor days exclude contract assets, which for a company recognising metro revenue over time can be a third of what customers owe. Year-end figures for a Q4-heavy business overstate the average balance through the year, but the year-end balance is what must be financed on 31 March.

In plain terms

Each Hydra crane earns ACE about ₹4 lakh of operating profit and about ₹3 lakh after tax, and the cash arrives before ACE pays its own suppliers. Each crane costs its buyer about ₹75,000 a month in loan payments, which he can afford only if the sites around him are busy. That is the whole industry in one machine.

Definition traps, collected

  • Units are not rupees. ICEMA counts a backhoe and a 100-tonne dump truck as one unit each. Never compare BEML's revenue with ICEMA unit growth.
  • ICEMA's "domestic" was restated. The FY25 release's 1,26,961 included non-OEM exports; the FY26 release restated FY25 domestic to 1,21,301. Use like-for-like series.
  • Margins with and without other income. ACE's "18.3%" includes treasury income on total income; its operating margin is 15.4%.
  • Quarter versus year. BEML's "25.6%" and "15%" are Q4 margins; full-year margins were 12.6% (FY25) and 6.9% (FY26).
  • Three bases for BEML's margin. Screener 6.9%, company 7.54%, CareEdge 4.52%; quote the basis every time.
  • Consolidated versus standalone. BEML's PAT is ₹141 crore consolidated and ₹147.5 crore standalone; ACE's ₹415 crore consolidated and ₹425 crore standalone.
  • Debtor days without contract assets. Screener's 191 days understate what BEML is owed; 251 days including contract assets is the complete figure.
  • Share claims on private denominators. ACE's "63% of mobile cranes" and Escorts' "41.4% of P&C cranes" cannot both be shares of one market.
  • Order book is not guidance. BEML's ₹20,000 crore inflow target carries a 30–40% probability by its own CMD's estimate.
  • R&D includes prototypes. About ₹100 crore of BEML's ₹251 crore was paid prototype cost of sales.
  • Trailing multiples on a seasonal company. BEML earns most of its profit in Q4; a trailing P/E taken mid-year sits on a seasonal trough.
  • Screener's dividend payout for ACE is wrong. 137% is a data error; the true payout is about 5.7%.
  • GST did not cut machine prices in 2025. Machines have been at 18% since November 2017.
  • Joint ventures leave revenue. If ACE's KATO JV is equity-accounted, the heavy-crane revenue moving into it will disappear from ACE's top line.

How to read a construction-equipment company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its growth, by segment (1 minute). ACE: CMCE and agri revenue. BEML: growth by vertical (rail and metro, defence, mining), which management gives on the call. Red flag: total growth carried by one lumpy vertical.
  2. Volumes and realisation (1 minute, ACE). CMCE units and revenue per unit against the same quarter last year and against Escorts Kubota's monthly CE volumes. Red flag: units up but realisation down (discounting), or the reverse with a falling segment margin.
  3. Operating margin on the screener basis (1 minute). Revenue minus operating expenses, excluding other income. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter, because both companies are seasonal. Red flag: a margin that is "up" only because other income is included.
  4. Gross margin and price-cost (1 minute, ACE). Materials as a share of revenue against steel prices; management's commentary on price increases. Red flag: materials ratio rising for two quarters while prices are "under review".
  5. Provisions and exceptional items (1 minute, BEML). Onerous-contract provisions, LDs, write-backs in other operating revenue, gratuity or wage charges. Red flag: new provisions on contracts management called "one-time" earlier.
  6. Order inflow and order book (1 minute, BEML). Inflow by vertical, book-to-bill, executable book for the year. Red flag: book-to-bill below 1 for two quarters, or a target described as "stretch".
  7. Working capital (1 minute). Half-yearly balance sheets (September and March): receivables, contract assets, inventory, customer advances and short-term debt. Red flag at BEML: debtor days above 200 or borrowings rising faster than revenue. Red flag at ACE: inventory days above 110.
  8. Cash and treasury (1 minute). ACE: other income as a share of pre-tax profit (a third in Q1 FY27); size of investments. BEML: cash and short-term borrowings. Red flag: profit growth that comes from other income.
  9. Guidance against delivery (1 minute). BEML: revenue growth "high 20s", EBITDA "at least 13%". ACE: margin "a little over 15%", revenue guidance still pending. Red flag: a second year of missed guidance at BEML; no revenue guidance at ACE by Q2.
  10. Policy and industry checks (1 minute). Any change to the crane anti-dumping duty, the Stage V exemption request, NHAI awards, Coal India tenders, metro and Vande Bharat awards, and the RBI's rate path. Red flag: a rate rise and an award slowdown in the same quarter.

Run in that order, the routine separates what the company controls (price, cost, execution, cash) from what it does not (demand, credit, policy), and keeps the two companies' different equations in view. The upcoming result windows are listed in Section 21.
