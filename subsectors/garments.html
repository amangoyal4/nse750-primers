00

The one-page view: an export trade and a brand trade under one name

Five listed companies, two very different businesses, and one market value that is mostly a single innerwear licence.

The label "Garments & Apparels" covers two businesses that share sewing machines and little else. The first is contract exporting: an Indian factory cuts and sews garments to a foreign brand's design and ships them, priced free on board (FOB) at an Indian, Bangladeshi or Vietnamese port, to be sold under the buyer's label in the US, the UK or Europe. Pearl Global, Gokaldas Exports and Kitex Garments are pure exporters, and Raymond Lifestyle has an export garmenting arm. The second is domestic branding: a company makes or sources garments and sells them under its own (or a licensed) name to Indian shoppers through shops, distributors and websites. Page Industries, the exclusive Jockey licensee, is the purest example; Raymond Lifestyle's suiting fabric and Park Avenue or ColorPlus menswear are the other.

The two businesses respond to different forces. An exporter lives on the landed cost of its garment in New York or London, which is set by its FOB price plus freight plus whatever import duty the destination charges. When the US put a 50% additional tariff on Indian goods in August 2025, Indian exporters lost orders within weeks; when the US Supreme Court struck those tariffs down on 20 February 2026 and India ended up paying a 10% Section 301 tariff while China and Vietnam paid 12.5%, the orders started coming back. A domestic brand lives on the Indian shopper's wallet, its distribution reach and the GST rate on a ₹1,500 vest or a ₹6,000 blazer. Tariffs barely touch it. Cotton and wool prices do.

The group's FY26 numbers show how unequal the two halves are. Combined revenue was ₹21,815 crore, up 6.6%. Combined operating profit (EBITDA before other income) was ₹2,702 crore, a 12.4% margin. Attributable reported profit after tax was ₹1,194 crore. And on 21 October 2026 the five were worth ₹60,869 crore together. Page Industries alone was 24% of the revenue but 64% of the profit and 64% of the market value. Raymond Lifestyle was the largest by revenue (₹6,888 crore) yet earned just ₹46 crore of reported profit, 3.9% of the group's.

Five forces shape everything in this report, and each gets its own treatment below.

  • The US tariff roller-coaster. From 10% in April 2025 to 50% in August 2025 (including a 25% "Russia-oil" penalty), down to 25% when the penalty was removed on 7 February 2026, to zero when the Supreme Court voided the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February, then 10% under Section 122 until 24 July 2026 and 10% under Section 301 since. For an exporter earning a 9–11% margin on its invoice, a 10-point tariff swing is larger than its entire profit.
  • The competing-geography map. China has been losing US share for a decade, but the "+1" orders went mainly to Vietnam and Bangladesh. India's apparel exports were $16.7 billion in FY18 and $15.77 billion in FY26. Pearl Global sidesteps the question by sewing three-quarters of its garments outside India.
  • Trade agreements. The India–UK trade agreement (CETA) came into force on 15 July 2026 and removed UK duties of up to 12% on Indian clothing. The India–EU agreement was concluded in January 2026 and its signing is reported for 16 December 2026. AGOA, which lets Gokaldas's Kenyan factories ship duty-free to the US, was extended to 2028.
  • Cotton to closet. Indian cotton was ₹68,300 per candy on 21 October 2026, about 25% higher than a year earlier, even after a record 62 lakh bales of imports. Wool roughly doubled in a year (Raymond's CFO). Polyester and freight jumped with the West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026.
  • The branded domestic engine. Page sells about 228 million pieces a year at roughly ₹230 each through 1.15 lakh multi-brand outlets, earns a 22% margin and a 64% return on capital, and pays Jockey International a 4.84% royalty. GST 2.0 cut the tax on garments up to ₹2,500 to 5% and raised it to 18% above that line, which helps Page and hurts premium suits.

The most recent quarter shows the two halves pulling apart. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), the latest quarter all five have reported, Pearl and Gokaldas together grew revenue 22.8% and operating profit 32.1% as the tariff gap closed and UK and African orders picked up. Page and Raymond Lifestyle grew revenue 6.9% with operating profit up just 1.9%, squeezed by cotton, wool and packaging costs. Kitex's revenue fell 19.8% and it lost money. None of the five has reported Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) as of 22 October; Raymond Lifestyle's board meets on 28 October and the others have not yet announced dates.

In plain terms

This is really two sub-sectors. Three companies sew clothes for foreign brands and earn about ₹9–11 on every ₹100 of sales; their fortunes swing with US tariffs and the rupee. One company sells its own (licensed) Jockey underwear to Indians and earns ₹22 on every ₹100; it barely notices tariffs. The fifth, Raymond Lifestyle, does both and earns little from either. Any "sector multiple" is mostly Page's multiple, so read the halves separately.

The valuation map follows the same split. On 21 October 2026 the market paid 51 times FY26 reported profit for Page, 40 times for Pearl, 50 times for a depressed Gokaldas, 73 times for Raymond Lifestyle's thin profit (but only 0.35 times its book value), and nothing meaningful for Kitex, which made ₹6 crore of attributable profit only because a ₹17.5 crore provision was written back. The rest of this report explains why those numbers look the way they do and what would have to change for them to move.

01

From cotton boll to closet: who earns what along the chain

A garment passes through six or seven hands before it is worn. The exporter keeps a sliver; the brand keeps most of the price.

Every garment starts as fibre. Cotton is grown by about six million Indian farmers and ginned into lint, which spinners turn into yarn. Polyester comes from crude oil via PTA and MEG; viscose comes from wood pulp. Yarn is knitted (for T-shirts, innerwear and babywear, customs chapter HS 61) or woven (for shirts, trousers, suits and jackets, HS 62) into fabric, which is then dyed, printed and finished. Only then does it reach the garment factory, where it is cut, sewn, finished, checked and packed. The finished piece goes by container ship to the buyer's warehouse and on to a store or a website.

The exporter owns Step 3 and sometimes Step 2. A domestic brand like Page owns Steps 2, 3 and 5 for the Indian market, which is why it keeps a far larger share of the final price.

The value is unevenly split. Garment trade rules of thumb put the FOB price paid to an exporter at roughly 20–25% of the retail price for mass-market clothing. The rest goes to the brand or retailer (rent, marketing, markdowns and profit), to the shipping line and to the customs authority of the importing country. Inside the FOB price, fabric and trims take nearly half, sewing labour about a fifth, and the exporter's own operating profit is a single-digit share of the invoice.

The illustration makes one point that runs through the whole report. The exporter's operating profit on a $30 shirt is around 50–90 US cents, about 2–3% of what the shopper pays and 8–12% of the exporter's own invoice. A tariff is charged on the customs value, which for US imports is close to the FOB price. So a 10-percentage-point tariff equals about 10% of the exporter's invoice, which is roughly its entire operating margin. The US retailer that pays the duty therefore has every reason to ask the exporter to share it, and in 2025 they did: Gokaldas booked ₹97 crore of "US tariff impact" in FY26, and Pearl told investors that India-made goods carried double-digit percentage discounts while its other origins gave 2–3%.

In plain terms

An Indian factory that sells a shirt to Gap for $7 keeps maybe 70 cents of profit. If the US adds a 10% tariff, that is another 70 cents on the shirt, and Gap wants someone else to pay it. Either the factory gives a discount and its profit vanishes, or Gap moves the order to a country with a lower tariff. That is why tariff gaps, not factory skill, decide where the world's clothes are sewn.

The domestic route is a different chain

A domestic brand runs a shorter chain and keeps far more of it. Page Industries buys yarn and fabric, knits and sews in 16 of its own factories (two of them, in Odisha and at K.R. Pet in Karnataka, opened in FY26) plus outsourced units, and sells through about 3,989 distributor accounts to 1.15 lakh multi-brand outlets (MBOs), around 1,600 exclusive brand outlets (EBOs), 930 large-format-store doors and its own and third-party websites. It charges every channel the same consumer price and runs no markdowns. On FY26 revenue of ₹5,247 crore it earned ₹1,153 crore of operating profit (22%) after paying Jockey International a royalty of ₹254 crore (4.84% of revenue, per its FY26 annual report).

Raymond Lifestyle is a vertically integrated version: it spins and weaves worsted wool and wool-blend suiting fabric at Vapi, Chhindwara and Jalgaon (43 million metres a year of capacity), sells it through about 1,112 franchised "The Raymond Shop" (TRS) stores and 20,000+ trade points for tailoring, makes ready-to-wear menswear under Raymond, Park Avenue, ColorPlus and Parx, and runs a B2B garmenting arm that sews suits, jackets and trousers for global labels. Owning more of the chain lifts the margin only if every link is efficient, and at Raymond in FY26 the fabric link earned all the profit (segment result ₹450 crore) while branded apparel lost ₹124 crore.

Page controls price, distribution and product; it does not own the name. The licence is the single largest structural risk in the group's most valuable company.

The cleanest way to see why the market values Page at 51 times earnings and the exporters at 40–50 times depressed earnings is to put the two chains side by side. Page captures the brand's share of the retail price and the factory's share, minus the royalty. An exporter captures only the factory's share and must re-win its orders every season from a handful of large buyers who can shift them across countries. The exporter's advantage is scale of the addressable market: US apparel imports alone were about $77.6 billion in 2025, against Page's ₹5,247 crore of sales into an Indian innerwear market of perhaps ₹85,000–1,00,000 crore (an INDICATIVE composite of market estimates that disagree with each other).

In plain terms

The further along the chain a company sits, towards the shopper, the more of the price it keeps and the less capital it needs per rupee of profit. Exporters sit in the middle; Page sits at both ends; Raymond sits everywhere but has not yet made the shop end pay.

02

Ten flat years: India's apparel exports went nowhere while rivals doubled

The single most important fact about Indian garment exporting is that it has not grown in dollars for a decade.

India's ready-made garment (RMG) exports were about $16.8 billion in FY15 (from memory of Commerce Ministry series) and $15.77 billion in FY26, down 1.36% from FY25's $15.99 billion (CITI analysis of Commerce Ministry quick estimates; Gokaldas's FY26 annual report also describes the year as "broadly flat, declining about 1.4%"). Over the same years the world's apparel exports rose from about $470 billion (2014) to $546 billion (2025) on ITC Trademap data. India's share of world apparel exports slipped from about 4% to about 3%. Bangladesh's rose from 6% to 10% and Vietnam's from 4% to 8%.

Several things explain the stagnation, and none of them is a lack of cotton. India grows more cotton than almost anyone and has a large spinning industry. The weaknesses lie further down the chain. Indian garment factories are small and fragmented compared with the 5,000–10,000-worker plants common in Bangladesh and Vietnam; fabric processing (dyeing, printing, finishing) is a bottleneck constrained by effluent rules; India is weak in man-made fibre garments, which are over 60% of world apparel, partly because quality-control orders on polyester and its feedstocks kept imported fibre expensive until they were rescinded on 12 November 2025; and until 2026 India paid 9.6–12% duty in the EU and UK while Bangladesh paid nothing as a least-developed country. Labour law, now codified in four Labour Codes in force since 21 November 2025, made it hard to run very large plants flexibly.

The year in progress: a hole, then a rebound

FY27 started badly. With the 50% US tariff still on Indian goods in the order books placed in late 2025, India's apparel exports fell about 12% year on year in Q1 FY27 (Commerce Ministry data cited by Gokaldas) and 11.7% in April alone. The decline narrowed to about 4% in July and 3% in August (PL Capital). September 2026 was the first clear rebound: exports of $1,133.9 million, up 13.7%. For April–September 2026 the total was $7,282.9 million, down 6.2%. The Sep rebound coincides with the first shipments priced after the Section 301 regime fixed India's tariff at 10% against 12.5% for China and Vietnam.

In plain terms

For ten years India's garment exports have hovered around $16 billion while the world market grew and Bangladesh and Vietnam doubled their share. The 2025 tariff shock knocked India down further. September 2026's +13.7% is the first sign of recovery, and one month is not a trend; the next two Commerce Ministry prints matter.

The domestic market: ten times larger than the export trade

The Indian apparel market is much larger than India's apparel exports. CareEdge Ratings puts apparel retail at ₹9.30 lakh crore in FY25, growing about 7% a year over FY18–FY25 and forecast to reach roughly ₹16 lakh crore by FY30 (an 11–12% annual rate). Wazir's 2026 estimate, cited in Gokaldas's annual report, is $115 billion for FY26, about ₹10 lakh crore at FY26's average exchange rate. The two agree within about 8%. Organised retail is about 41% of the market, and CMAI–1Lattice research finds that about 58% of apparel consumption is rural.

03

Who buys: American retailers, European chains and the Indian wardrobe

The US is the swing market for exporters; the Indian consumer, the weddings calendar and GST decide the brands' year.

For Indian exporters the United States is the largest and most volatile customer. The US took about a third of India's garment exports in 2024 (AEPC). US apparel imports were about $77.6 billion in 2025, and India supplied about $4.9 billion of that, roughly 6.4% and fourth among suppliers. The 2026 data show what the tariff did. In January–August 2026 US apparel imports fell 7.4% to about $49 billion, while imports from India fell 26.4%. Vietnam held steady at $10.98 billion (−0.8%), China fell 29.4% to $5.63 billion and Bangladesh slipped 4.4% to $5.39 billion (OTEXA data via Prothom Alo, a secondary source).

End demand in the US was not the problem. Gokaldas, citing US Census and OTEXA data, noted that US clothing-store sales grew in the first half of 2026 while US apparel imports fell about 9% in January–May, because retailers ran leaner inventories and because tariffs reshuffled sourcing. Retailers are also ordering closer to the season (Pearl), which shortens lead times and favours suppliers who can turn orders fast. Tiruppur exporters reported holiday-2026 order books to the US down 25–30% in October, the hangover of orders placed when India's tariff was 50%.

The US-exposure ranking was upside down

The companies' own FY26 disclosures, read for this report, overturn the usual assumptions about who depends on the US. Gokaldas, often described as about half US, sold 77.9% of FY26 revenue to North America (its annual report's "global footprint" table; 84–85% in FY22–FY23). Kitex, long described as 80–92% US, sold 63.4% of FY26 product sales to the US, down from 76.1% in FY25, because US sales collapsed 43% while Europe and Australia grew. Pearl Global's managing director put the US at about 48–52% of revenue at its 24 September 2026 Investor Day, with a target of 40% by FY30 and Europe already at 15–17%. Raymond Lifestyle's garmenting arm sells 59–60% to the US, 12% to the UK and 7–8% to Europe, but garmenting is only about 15% of Raymond's revenue, so the US is roughly 9% of the group (an INDICATIVE calculation). Page sells almost nothing outside its South Asian and Gulf licence territories.

Europe, the UK and Japan

Europe is the second front. EU-27 apparel imports fell about 10% in January–May 2026 after rising 2% in 2025, and UK imports fell about 3% after rising 6% (Eurostat and ONS data cited by Gokaldas). The interest for Indian exporters is not current volume but access: the UK removed its duties on Indian clothing on 15 July 2026, and the EU will do the same once the India–EU agreement is in force, expected in 2027. Raymond's garmenting arm has already won UK shirting business from Next and T.M. Lewin, which its CEO attributed to the UK agreement, along with El Corte Inglés in Spain, OVS in Italy and Carl Gross in Germany. Gokaldas's UK share is only about 4–4.5% of revenue, and it is onboarding a new UK customer. Japan is a quieter opportunity: China's share of Japan's clothing imports fell from about 65% to 49–50%, the first such shift in 31 years, according to Pearl, whose top-five customers include MUJI.

In plain terms

Gokaldas sells almost four-fifths of its output to North American buyers, more than any other member. Kitex has already shrunk its US share to 63% the painful way, by losing US orders. Pearl sits near half and plans to fall to 40%. For each exporter, the question is how quickly Europe and the UK can replace US dependence now that trade deals are opening those markets.

Domestic demand: replacement, weddings and the ₹2,500 line

Domestic garment demand behaves differently. Innerwear is a high-frequency replacement purchase with very low markdown risk; customers buy the same size and style repeatedly, and brands rarely discount. Its growth comes from formalisation (shoppers trading up from unbranded or economy brands such as Rupa, Dollar and Lux to Jockey or Van Heusen) and from new categories such as women's innerwear, athleisure and socks. Suiting and wedding wear follow the Hindu wedding calendar: the auspicious windows of October–December and January–February drive Raymond's fabric sales, and in 2026 an extra lunar month (Adhik Maas) pushed festival and wedding dates back by 20–25 days, which Raymond expects to make H2 FY27 "very, very strong".

GST 2.0, in force from 22 September 2025, split the market at ₹2,500 a piece: garments up to that price now pay 5% GST (the threshold was ₹1,000 before), and garments above it pay 18% (up from 12%). Almost all Jockey products sit under the line, so Page benefited; pieces Page previously sold above ₹1,000 MRP, such as premium bras and athleisure, moved from 12% to 5%. Ready-made suits, blazers and premium ethnic sets above ₹2,500 now pay 18%, which hurt Raymond's ready-to-wear and Ethnix; suiting fabric stays at 5% as a textile, which nudges customers back towards buying fabric and getting a suit tailored.

Channel inventory is the other domestic swing factor. After COVID, Page's distributors and retailers over-stocked; Page's revenue fell 3% in FY24 as the channel destocked and the company moved its distributors to an auto-replenishment system (ARS). The recovery since has been gradual: revenue up 8% in FY25 and 6.3% in FY26, volume up 3.9% in FY26 and 5.7% in Q1 FY27. Online is the fastest-growing channel: Page's online sales grew about 60% in FY26 (an analyst's figure management accepted on the Q1 FY27 call), and quick-commerce platforms buy outright rather than on marketplace terms, which is shifting Page's online model.

In plain terms

Indians replace underwear regularly and rarely wait for a sale, which makes Page's demand steady. Suits are bought for weddings, which makes Raymond's demand lumpy and seasonal. A tax change in September 2025 made cheaper clothes cheaper and expensive clothes dearer, which suited Page better than Raymond.

The tariff roller-coaster: 10% to 50% to zero to 10% in sixteen months Deep dive

For a business that earns about ₹10 on every ₹100 of sales, the US tariff on India moved by more than its whole margin, twice.

No event in the last two decades has moved Indian garment exporters as much as the US tariff cycle that began in April 2025. To read any exporter's numbers for FY26 and FY27 you have to know which tariff stack applied to the goods it shipped in that quarter, and which stack applied to its competitors. The phrase "the tariff on India" meant five different things on five different dates.

The sequence, date by date

  • Until Apr 2025
    Only the US "most favoured nation" (MFN) duty applies: 0–32% depending on the garment, averaging roughly 16% for Indian clothing. Same rate for every supplier, except China, which also carries legacy 2018–19 Section 301 duties on many lines.
  • 5 Apr 2025
    IEEPA "baseline" reciprocal tariff of 10% on almost every country, India included. China escalates to 125–145% in April, then falls to about 30% after the 12 May Geneva truce.
  • 7 Aug 2025

  • 27 Aug 2025
    A further 25% "penalty" on India for buying Russian oil takes India's additional tariff to 50% on top of MFN, against about 20% for Bangladesh and Vietnam. A 30-point landed-cost gap opens.
  • 3 Feb 2026
    India–US interim framework announced: reciprocal rate to be cut to 18%. Never implemented as such, because the court ruling overtook it.
  • 7 Feb 2026
    The 25% Russia penalty is removed (dated in Pearl Global's Investor Day deck). India's additional tariff falls to 25%.
  • 20 Feb 2026
    The US Supreme Court rules that IEEPA cannot be used to impose tariffs. All IEEPA tariffs are void; US importers become eligible for refunds (about $166 billion to ~330,000 importers, processed through CBP's system from 20 April 2026).

  • 14 Jul 2026

  • 24 Jul 2026

Why a 10-point tariff is an existential number

The arithmetic is brutal and simple. A US tariff is charged on the customs value of the garment, which is close to the exporter's FOB price. An Indian exporter earns an operating margin of roughly 9–13% of that FOB price. A tariff gap of 10 points against a rival country is therefore roughly the size of the exporter's whole profit, and the 30-point gap of August 2025 to February 2026 was about three times it. The US buyer, who pays the duty, sees the landed cost: FOB plus freight plus duty. If an Indian shirt lands 20–30% dearer than an identical Bangladeshi or Vietnamese one, the buyer moves the order unless the Indian exporter cuts its price.

The reversal and what it is worth

The ruling of 20 February 2026 and the two 10% regimes that followed reversed the gap. Gokaldas's India business grew 16% in Q1 FY27, "benefiting from a shift to the lower tariff regime under Section 122", against a 12% fall in India's apparel exports. Pearl's Indian operations grew 27.4%, and its managing director said customers "are not asking for discounts" to cover the 10%. Raymond's garmenting revenue rose 50% in Q1 FY27 with EBITDA back to 7.3%, and its capacity is booked until December 2026. For the scaled exporters as a group (Pearl and Gokaldas), Q1 FY27 revenue was up 22.8% and operating profit 32.1%.

The most useful reading of the new regime comes from Gokaldas's managing director on the 12 August call. India's 2.5-point edge over China and Vietnam is "not remarkable", he said, because suppliers there can discount it away. The real gain is that India no longer has to discount: the double-digit concessions of FY26 are gone, and that alone explains most of the margin recovery at Pearl India and Gokaldas India. US buyers are "rebalancing towards India", Europeans are sending enquiries, and India is being considered even for basic shirts and bottoms usually sourced from Bangladesh, made in lower-cost Indian states.

In plain terms

The win from the new tariffs is not the small 2.5-point advantage over Vietnam. It is that Indian factories stopped handing back 5–10% of their invoice to American buyers. That is pure margin, and it shows up in Q1 FY27.

Unfinished business

  • Refunds go to importers, not exporters. IEEPA refunds of about $166 billion are being paid to US importers of record. Pearl says "very few" customers have shared them. Exporters that gave discounts in FY26 will not get that money back.
  • Section 301 is under legal challenge. If courts strike it down, India's 10% falls to MFN only, but so do China's and Vietnam's 12.5%, removing India's edge. If the US replaces it with a new instrument, the rates could change again.
  • The Russia-oil bill. The Graham "Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026" would allow up to 100% tariffs on buyers of Russian oil (India takes about 36% of Russian crude exports). Pearl calls it "not an immediate threat", but customers are deferring long-term India commitments because of it.
  • No bilateral deal. The India–US trade agreement's first tranche was finalised on 3 February 2026, but India deferred signing in March until the US settled its new tariff structure. A signed deal could fix a bilateral rate; none has been signed. Raymond Lifestyle's primer describes garmenting as recovering "on the India–US trade deal"; that deal was never signed, and the relief came from the court ruling and the 10% regimes.
  • The quota gap with Bangladesh. Under Section 301, garments from Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia and Malaysia made with US cotton enter free of the 10% up to a quota for three years. Pearl's managing director suggested the allowance applies to any country using US cotton; the memorandum, as summarised by KPMG, names only those four. India is not on the list.
In plain terms

India's tariff position is the best it has been since early 2025, but it rests on a US trade instrument that is being challenged in court, a Russia-sanctions threat that has not gone away, and no signed trade deal. The exporters' Q2 and Q3 FY27 results, the first full quarters under the 10% regime, will show how much of the recovery sticks.

One more effect is easy to miss. The rupee fell from about ₹84 a dollar in FY25 to ₹94.9 on 19 October 2026, near a record low of about ₹96.8. For an exporter whose costs are mostly in rupees, a weaker rupee lifts margins: as an ILLUSTRATIVE rule, each ₹1 move on a $9.18 garment adds about ₹9 of revenue, and if half the cost (imported fabric) is dollar-linked, about ₹4.6 of profit, or roughly half a point of margin. In FY26 most of that cushion was spent funding tariff discounts. In FY27 it is still to come through: Gokaldas had forward cover at about ₹89 a dollar on 70% of its Q1 dollar flows, so today's ₹95 spot will reach the P&L as the hedges roll off. Rupee weakness also flatters exporters' growth in rupees: a flat dollar export number is up 10–13% in rupee terms between FY25 and October 2026.

Bangladesh, Vietnam and the China+1 map Deep dive

China has been shedding garment share for a decade. The orders went to Dhaka and Ho Chi Minh City first, and India is only now getting a real look.

"China+1" is the sourcing strategy of keeping China for some production while adding at least one other country to reduce risk. For apparel the shift began well before 2018: rising Chinese wages pushed basic garments out first, then US–China tariffs from 2018 and forced-labour rules on Xinjiang cotton (the US UFLPA) accelerated it. China's share of US apparel imports fell from about 37% in 2014 to about 15% in 2025 on ITC data, and to roughly 11% in January–July 2026. The question for Indian investors is where that share went, and why so little of it came to India.

Bangladesh: still the giant, carrying three risks

Bangladesh is the world's second-largest apparel exporter after China, with about 6.9% of world exports in 2024 (WTO) and roughly 10% in 2025 (ITC). Its ready-made garment exports were $10.58 billion in July–September 2026, up 6.1% (Export Promotion Bureau data via UNB), with knitwear up 6.9% and woven up 5.1%. In the US it was briefly the second-largest supplier in January–July 2026 before China retook second place in August by about $240 million. Its strengths are scale (very large factories doing long runs of basics), the lowest garment wages among major suppliers (a statutory minimum of Tk 12,500 a month since December 2023, about $100–115), and duty-free access to the EU and UK.

It also carries three risks that Indian exporters watch closely:

  • Politics. The August 2024 uprising ended Sheikh Hasina's government on 5 August 2024, and some orders moved to India during the turmoil. A BNP government under Tarique Rahman was sworn in on 17 February 2026.
  • LDC graduation. Bangladesh is scheduled to graduate from least-developed-country status on 24 November 2026. It asked on 18 February 2026 for a three-year deferral; the UN Committee for Development Policy was reported as positive, but no UN General Assembly decision had been found as of 22 October. After graduation the EU's EBA access continues for a three-year transition (to about 2029), after which Bangladesh faces standard GSP rules unless it qualifies for GSP+.
  • Wages. Unions want Tk 23,000–25,000 a month; no new wage board award had been announced in 2026.

India added a fourth irritant of its own: in May 2025 it barred Bangladeshi ready-made garment imports through land ports, allowing them only through Kolkata and Nhava Sheva by sea. Imports of Bangladeshi garments into India fell 27% in April–September after the restriction.

India's own cost reset: wages moved in April 2026

India's labour-cost advantage over Vietnam is real but narrowing at the margin. Africa: Gokaldas's AGOA bet

Gokaldas built its own "+1" by buying Atraco (Dubai-headquartered, factories in Kenya and Ethiopia, about $55 million, closed in early 2024). In plain terms

Kenya is the one place an Indian-owned factory can ship to the US with no tariff at all. Gokaldas paid to get there and borrowed to run it. With AGOA now extended to 2028, the bet looks better than it did a year ago, but Africa still earns a thinner margin than India and holds most of the company's debt.

The trade-deal dividend: the UK is open, the EU is next

For a decade India paid 9.6–12% duty in Europe while Bangladesh paid nothing. That handicap is being dismantled one agreement at a time.

Europe matters to Indian exporters in a way the tariff headlines miss. The EU and the UK together import more apparel than the US, and until July 2026 India entered both at a disadvantage: the EU charged Indian garments its standard (MFN) duty of about 12%, or about 9.6% under the GSP preference scheme, while Bangladesh, as a least-developed country, paid zero under EBA, and Vietnam's duty was phasing to zero under its own EU agreement. In the UK the picture was the same. For a business earning 9–12% margins, a 9.6–12-point duty gap was the difference between winning and losing a tender.

The EU: the bigger prize, still a year away

The EU deal matters more because the EU market is much larger and Bangladesh's EBA advantage there is larger still (about 22% of EU apparel imports). The agreement was concluded on 27 January 2026 with apparel at zero duty from day one. The European Commission sent its signature and conclusion proposals to the Council on 11–14 September 2026, and the signing is reported for 16 December 2026 in Brussels; the European Parliament must then consent, and entry into force is expected in 2027. Company expectations vary: Raymond's CEO said "6–9 months" in August, Pearl said "beginning of 2027" and Gokaldas's managing director "second half of next year". EU bulk orders, Raymond said, will follow entry into force, not signing.

Timing matters because of Bangladesh's graduation. If Bangladesh graduates in November 2026, its EBA access runs for a three-year transition to about 2029. An EU agreement in force in 2027 would therefore put India at zero duty for two years while Bangladesh is also still at zero, and then ahead of it if Bangladesh loses EBA without qualifying for GSP+.

Rules of origin decide who actually benefits

Zero-duty access is not automatic. Each agreement's rules of origin set how much of a garment must be made in India to qualify. Many Indian exporters import synthetic or technical fabric from China, especially for outerwear and activewear. If the rules require fabric to be made in India or the partner country ("double transformation" or "fabric forward"), garments made from Chinese fabric pay the full duty. The UK agreement uses self-certified origin; the detailed EU product rules will matter for every exporter's sourcing. Integrated players with Indian fabric (Kitex's cotton knits, Raymond's own suiting and shirting fabric, Gokaldas's Matrix fabric unit) are best placed.

Cotton, wool, polyester and freight: what goes into a garment's cost

Raw material is about half of an exporter's sales and two-fifths of a brand's. In 2026 almost every input rose at once.

Fabric and trims are the largest cost for every member. For an FOB exporter they are roughly ₹45–52 of every ₹100 of revenue; for an integrated knitter that spins and dyes its own yarn, yarn alone is ₹30–35 plus ₹8–10 of dyes and processing; for Page, materials are about ₹38–42. So the prices of cotton, wool and polyester, and how fast a company can pass them on, move margins within one or two quarters. In 2026 three shocks hit together: Indian cotton rose about 25% in a year, wool roughly doubled, and the West Asia war pushed polyester feedstocks and freight sharply higher.

Innerwear and the branded domestic engine Deep dive

Page sells about 228 million pieces a year at roughly ₹230 each and earns 64% on its capital. Raymond owns its brands outright and earns 3.5%. The difference is the whole lesson.

Page Industries is the group's most valuable company and its least typical. It makes and sells Jockey innerwear, athleisure, loungewear and socks (and Speedo swimwear) in India and a handful of neighbouring markets under an exclusive licence from Jockey International of Kenosha, Wisconsin. It does not export, it does not own the brand, and it does not depend on US tariffs. It is, in effect, a consumer-staples company that happens to sit in the textile sector.

The licence: borrowed name, owned machine

Page signed its Jockey licence on 1 April 1994; it has been extended to 31 December 2040. The territory is India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, the Maldives, the UAE, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and Bahrain (Q1 FY27 press release). Page paid Jockey a royalty of ₹254.1 crore in FY26, 4.84% of revenue, the same percentage as FY25's ₹238.8 crore (FY26 annual report, note 34); Jockey is not a related party. Speedo, licensed from the Pentland Group for India, is about 1% of revenue, and over 35% of Speedo sales are now online. Everything else is Page's own: the factories (16 units, two opened in FY26), the distribution network, the product design for Indian bodies and climates, and the pricing.

In plain terms

Page rents the Jockey name for about 5% of sales and has built everything else itself. The rent is cheap for what it buys, and the lease runs to 2040. The risk is what happens when the lease comes up for renewal; nobody outside the two companies knows the terms.

Volume times price: the slow recovery after the destock

Page's revenue compounded at about 20% a year for most of the 2010s. Then the channel cycle turned. Distributors and retailers over-stocked after COVID, revenue jumped 37% in FY22 and 21% in FY23, and then fell 3% in FY24 as the channel cleared inventory and Page moved distributors onto an auto-replenishment system (ARS) that ships only what stores sell. The recovery since has been real but modest.

Each member is a different archetype. Margin rises with control of the customer; returns depend on how much capital that control needs.

Second, its net working capital is about 43–44 days against Gokaldas's 75–79, partly because it factors receivables. Third, Gokaldas's FY25 acquisitions (Atraco, Matrix) added goodwill and intangibles of about ₹583 crore and a loss-making fabric unit (Matrix's BTPL, operating EBITDA −7.5% to −8% in Q1 FY27) to the capital base before they earned much. Gokaldas's company-basis ROCE, which excludes cash and leases, fell from 27% in FY23 to 12% in FY26.

Why the integrated knitter swings so hard

Kitex's Kerala campus at Kizhakkambalam spins, knits, dyes, prints, cuts and sews cotton infantwear in one place (capacity 4.32 lakh garments a day). In a full year that integration lets it keep margins that pure garmenters cannot: 22% in FY22 and 21% in FY25. But it also means most costs are fixed. When US orders collapsed in FY26 and the new Warangal plant (160.35 million pieces a year, operational 15 September 2025) started with low utilisation, the operating margin fell to 4.5% and interest jumped from ₹15 crore to ₹47 crore as Warangal's debt moved from capital work in progress to the P&L.

In plain terms

A factory that does everything from yarn to finished baby suit earns more per piece when it is full and loses more when it is not. Kitex is the textbook case: a 21% margin in FY25, 4.5% in FY26, and a new plant, larger than the original Kerala campus, still filling up.

The archetypes also explain how each responded to the tariff shock. The multi-country vendor re-routed. The India-centric exporter discounted, then leaned on Africa and Europe. The integrated mono-market exporter lost a third of its revenue. The brands were untouched by tariffs but hit by input costs. That is why averaging their numbers produces nonsense: a combined margin of 12.4% describes none of them.

The listed five are a small slice of an industry dominated by private exporters, MNC buying offices and local brands.

Export refunds: smaller than assumed, and on a short extension

The Rebate of State and Central Taxes and Levies (RoSCTL) refunds the taxes embedded in exported garments that GST does not credit, such as electricity duty, fuel taxes and mandi fees. Pearl's India RoSCTL is about 4% (an analyst figure management did not contest). The schemes were extended on 30 September 2026 to 31 December 2026 at unchanged rates. Because Pearl's reported realisation includes export incentives, any cut would show up directly in revenue per piece.

PM MITRA and the Telangana bet

The PM MITRA scheme funds seven large integrated textile parks (outlay ₹4,445 crore for FY22–FY28). Labour: codified, costlier, more flexible

The four Labour Codes that came into force on 21 November 2025 matter most to garment makers, which employ tens of thousands of mostly women workers (Gokaldas over 54,000, about 75% women; Page 80–85% women). The Code on Wages defines "wages" as at least half of total pay, which raises the base for provident fund and gratuity; companies booked one-off past-service charges in Q3 FY26 (Page ₹35.0 crore: gratuity ₹24.0 crore, leave ₹10.1 crore, contractor gratuity ₹0.9 crore; Raymond ₹53.9 crore). The Industrial Relations Code raises the threshold for needing government approval for layoffs from 100 to 300 workers and allows fixed-term employment, which helps large factories manage seasonal peaks. Combined with the April 2026 state wage resets, labour has become the swing cost in Indian garmenting, and the response has been to build in Bihar, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh rather than in Haryana or Karnataka. A planned ₹25,000 crore textile skilling programme (October 2026 news) may help supply.

In plain terms

The new labour laws cost companies a one-time catch-up on gratuity and a little more every year, but let large factories hire on fixed terms and run night shifts. Big employers can live with that. The bigger change in 2026 was Haryana raising its minimum wage by over a third, which is why new factories are going east.

Forty years of cycles: quotas, China, COVID, destock, tariffs

Every decade has delivered a shock that rearranged who sews the world's clothes. The listed members are survivors of most of them.

The garment trade has been reshaped by policy more often than by technology. From 1974 to 2004 the Multi-Fibre Arrangement and its successor set national quotas for exports to the US and Europe; Indian exporters grew inside fixed allowances, and Gokaldas (founded in Bengaluru in 1979) and Pearl Global (incorporated in 1987) were quota-era winners. When quotas ended on 1 January 2005, China's share exploded, India grew more slowly, and Bangladesh grew fastest. The boom of 2005–08 brought Gokaldas's IPO (2005), Page's IPO (2007, at ₹360 a share) and Blackstone's purchase of Gokaldas (2007).

Reading a garment maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Pieces, price per piece, margin, market mix, capacity, working capital and return on capital. What each number means, how to compute it and where companies define it differently.

A garment company's results can be decoded with a handful of numbers, but only if you know which definition each company uses. The same word, "EBITDA", means three different things across these five annual reports. "Inventory days" can be 66 or 178 for the same company depending on the denominator. "US share" can mean the share of revenue billed to North America, the share of product sales shipped to the US, or a management estimate. This section builds the revenue and profit equations, then works through each native KPI with a formula, a worked example, the drivers, benchmarks, a comparison of the five and the trap to avoid.

The revenue and profit equations

For an exporter, revenue is simply the number of pieces shipped multiplied by the average FOB realisation per piece. Pieces shipped are installed capacity multiplied by utilisation. Realisation in rupees is the dollar FOB price multiplied by the exchange rate, plus export incentives where the company books them in revenue. Operating profit is revenue minus fabric and trims, labour, power and processing, freight and compliance, overheads and any tariff discounts given to buyers.

KPI 1 · Pieces (volume) growth

What it is. The year-on-year change in the number of finished garments shipped (exporters) or sold (brands). Formula: pieces this period ÷ pieces in the same period last year − 1. Worked example: Pearl shipped 20.8 million pieces in Q1 FY27 against 17.2 million in Q1 FY26: 20.8 ÷ 17.2 − 1 = +20.9%.

Drivers. For exporters: new customers and programmes, the tariff gap with competing countries, buyer inventory cycles and capacity additions. For brands: distribution reach, consumer demand and channel inventory (a destock cuts primary volume even when consumers buy as much). Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): an exporter growing pieces above 12% a year is doing well, 5–12% is average, below 5% weak; for a brand, above 8% is good, 3–8% average, below 3% weak.

When Raymond says its EBITDA was ₹804 crore and a data site says ₹678 crore, both are right: Raymond counts interest income on its treasury, the data site does not. Pick one definition and use it for every company, or the comparison is meaningless.

KPI 4 · Export share and US share

What it is. The share of revenue sold abroad, and the share sold to the US specifically. Formula: US revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Kitex FY26 product sales to the US ₹397 crore; other exports ₹117 crore; India ₹112 crore; total ₹626 crore of product sales; US share = 397 ÷ 626 = 63.4%.

Drivers. Customer base, product category (US mass retailers dominate infantwear and basics), trade access (UK and EU deals, AGOA) and deliberate diversification. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): a diversified exporter has the US below 40% and no single market above 50%; 40–60% is average; above 60% is concentrated. On this test Gokaldas (77.9% North America) and Kitex (63.4%) are concentrated, Pearl (~48–52%) average and moving lower, and Raymond's garmenting arm (59–60%) close to the line. Page is domestic (export share ~1–2% INDICATIVE, from its Gulf and South Asian territories).

Definition trap. Three different bases. Gokaldas reports "North America" by customer location, including Canada, and separately shows 16% of revenue billed to customers located in India (buying offices of global brands, whose goods mostly go to the US). Kitex reports US share of product sales by destination. Pearl's ~48–52% is a management estimate, and its "export share" is complicated by the fact that 78% of its revenue is made outside India; for Pearl, "US share" and "Indian export share" mean different things. And the old ranges (Gokaldas "~50%", Kitex "80–92%") in earlier reports were wrong for FY26.

KPI 5 · Customer concentration

What it is. The share of revenue from the largest customer, or the top three or five. Formula: revenue from top-N customers ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Gokaldas's three largest customers bought ₹1,921 crore in FY26; ₹1,921 ÷ ₹3,988 = 48.2%.

Drivers. Exporters grow by deepening "programmes" with large brands, which naturally concentrates revenue; diversification requires onboarding new customers, which takes two to three seasons of sampling and compliance audits. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): top customer below 20% and top three below 40% is good; top three at 40–55% is average; a single customer above 50% is weak. Kitex (top customer 53.6%) is weak; Gokaldas (48.2%, up from 38.9%) average and worsening; Pearl (24 customers for 90%) better; RLL and Page have no customer above 10%.

Definition trap. Companies disclose concentration in different ways (top one, top three, number of customers for 90%, "customers above 10% of revenue"), and Indian accounting standards require disclosure only of customers above 10%. Gokaldas's "relationships of 10+ years supply 81% of sales" is a stickiness measure, not a concentration measure. Long relationships reduce the risk of losing a customer but not the damage if one is lost.

In plain terms

A garment exporter with one huge customer is really a supplier to that customer, and the customer sets the price. Kitex earns more than half its revenue from one buyer; Pearl needs 24 buyers to reach 90%. The market pays more for the second kind of business.

KPI 12 · Tariff gap and currency: the exporter's two external dials

What it is. Two numbers no exporter controls but every exporter's margin depends on: the difference between the US tariff on its country of origin and the lowest tariff a competing country pays (the tariff gap), and the rupee–dollar rate. Formula: tariff gap = India's additional US tariff − best competing country's additional tariff; FX effect on margin ≈ % rupee depreciation × (1 − share of costs that are dollar-linked). Worked example: since 24 July 2026 India pays 10% and Vietnam 12.5%, so the gap against Vietnam is −2.5 points (India better); against Bangladesh outside the US-cotton quota it is zero, and on quota volumes +10 points (Bangladesh better). On currency, an ILLUSTRATIVE garment with a $9.18 FOB price earns ₹9.2 more per piece for each ₹1 fall in the rupee; if half the cost (imported fabric and trims) is dollar-linked, about ₹4.6 of that reaches operating profit, roughly half a point of margin per rupee.

Drivers. The tariff gap is set in Washington (and, for Europe, by trade agreements). The rupee is set by India's balance of payments, oil prices and the RBI (which raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 with the rupee near ₹95). Companies blunt both: Pearl by moving production between countries, Gokaldas by hedging (forward cover at about ₹89 on 70% of Q1 FY27 dollar flows) and by its Kenyan plants, which pay no Section 301 tariff. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): a tariff gap at or below zero is good, 0–5 points average, above 5 points is order-losing territory. For currency there is no "good" level; what matters is the speed of change, because buyers renegotiate FOB prices when the rupee weakens and the gain is competed away within a few seasons.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA has three meanings. Screener OP (before other income), company EBITDA (often including other income: Gokaldas, RLL) and "adjusted" EBITDA (Gokaldas ex-tariff ₹530 crore; Pearl ex-ESOP and start-up losses). The peer table uses screener OP.
  • PAT: group vs attributable. At Pearl and Kitex minorities absorb start-up losses, so attributable PAT exceeds group PAT (Pearl ₹278 vs ₹270 crore; Kitex +₹6 vs −₹13 crore in FY26). This report uses attributable reported PAT everywhere.
  • Exceptional items differ by source. Page −₹35 crore pre-tax (Labour Codes); RLL −₹128.5 crore pre-tax (Labour Codes, apparel write-downs, stamp duty); Kitex +₹17.5 crore pre-tax (credit-loss write-back on dues from Kitex USA LLC); Gokaldas only ₹6 crore of exceptional income per the company, against ₹35 crore in screener's classification.
  • Revenue vs total income. Gokaldas's "₹4,065 crore" is total income; revenue from operations is ₹3,988 crore.
  • Pieces are not comparable across products. An outerwear jacket takes up to ten times the sewing minutes of a shirt.
  • Per-share figures after Pearl's bonus. Pearl completed a 1:1 bonus (record date 11 September 2026). Its Q1 FY27 deck EPS of ₹21.77 is pre-bonus (about ₹10.9 post-bonus); screener's FY26 EPS of ₹30.08 is bonus-adjusted. Do not mix bases.
  • Raymond's history starts in FY25. FY24 is a pre-demerger carve-out year with a ₹2,159 crore exceptional gain (reported PAT ₹2,645 crore); exclude it.
  • Calendar mismatches. Kitex's US customers buy for back-to-school and holiday seasons that straddle Indian fiscal quarters; compare Kitex on half-years or trailing twelve months. Raymond's weddings move between Q3 and Q4. OTEXA and Eurostat data are calendar years; AEPC data fiscal years.
  • FOB vs CMT vs landed. All members' revenue is FOB or full-package; a CMT (cut-make-trim) contract earns only a conversion fee of about 25–35% of FOB; tariffs are charged on customs value, close to FOB.
  • "Tariff on India" depends on the date. Always state the stack: MFN + IEEPA (Apr 2025–Feb 2026), MFN + Section 122 (Feb–Jul 2026), MFN + Section 301 (since 24 Jul 2026).
  • Cotton units. Prices are per candy (355.6 kg of lint), crops in bales of 170 kg, MSP per quintal of seed cotton. Never compare across units.
  • Inventory days. Screener's 178 days for Page vs the company's 66; use company figures for prose.
In plain terms

Most of the apparent contradictions in garment-company numbers come from definitions, not mistakes: which EBITDA, which PAT, which share count, which inventory measure, which tariff date. Settle the definition first and the numbers usually agree.

How to read a garment company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth and its split (1 minute). Pull revenue YoY, then pieces YoY and realisation per piece. Exporters: is growth volume or price/mix? Brands: is value ahead of volume (pricing) or behind (mix dilution)? Red flag: revenue up only on currency.
  2. The margin, on one definition (1 minute). Compute OP ÷ revenue yourself (excluding other income) and compare with the company's EBITDA. Red flag: company margin flat while OP margin falls, meaning other income is propping it up.
  3. Gross margin and raw-material commentary (1 minute). Cotton, wool, polyester and packaging moves; has a price increase been taken and when does it land? Red flag: costs up and no price action announced.
  4. Geography and tariff exposure (1 minute). For exporters, the US share and any discounts given; India vs overseas (Pearl), India vs Africa (Gokaldas). Red flag: rising US share just as a new tariff action looms.
  5. Customer and order book (1 minute). New customers onboarded, order visibility (Raymond's garmenting "full to December"; Gokaldas's "Q2 like Q1"). Red flag: the top customer's share rising.
  6. Utilisation and capacity (1 minute). New plants commissioned and their ramp; labour availability. Red flag: capacity added faster than orders.
  7. Working capital (1 minute). NWC days, inventory and receivables. Red flag: NWC up 10+ days without a stated reason; growing receivables from a related party.
  8. Below EBITDA (1 minute). Depreciation and interest jumps from new plants; capitalised interest; tax rate (Gokaldas's 42% in FY26). Red flag: interest falling while debt rises (possible capitalisation).
  9. PAT: group, attributable, exceptional (1 minute). Note minority shares and one-offs. Red flag: profit positive only because of a write-back (Kitex FY26).

Its EV/OP of 16.4 times is the cheapest of the profitable exporters. The bull case needs three things to happen together: company-basis margins back to about 13% with no tariff discounts, Africa turning double-digit and Matrix's fabric unit breaking even (PBT-positive in Q4 FY27 is the target), and tax normalising to the guided 20–22%. Foreign investors halved their stake in a year, and the 96.3% promoter pledge is a real overhang.

Raymond Lifestyle: the market's verdict on capital allocation

Raymond trades at 0.35 times a book value of ₹9,636 crore, much of it demerger-era reserves. Its EV/OP of 6.7 times (about 4.7 times on its own net-cash figure) is a fraction of the group's. The market is pricing a business whose textile segment earns a ₹450 crore result but whose branded apparel loses money, whose stores are being cut, whose lease-heavy cost base (₹248 crore of interest, ₹371 crore of depreciation) consumes its operating profit, and whose promoter has just pledged shares. If management delivers its aim of mid-teens margins and returns, the multiple has room to move; if branded apparel keeps losing ₹100+ crore a year, it does not.

Kitex: dilution before valuation

For Kitex, valuation multiples matter less than share count. A pending scheme would issue about 9.22 crore new shares (+46% on about 19.95 crore) to the promoter company's shareholders in exchange for its textile business (about ₹425 crore of revenue in its April 2025 deck); shareholders and creditors approved it at NCLT-convened meetings on 24 July 2026 and it awaits NCLT sanction. A ₹3,000 crore QIP, conditional on that scheme, would be larger than the ₹2,288 crore market cap. Minority shareholders face dilution from both, plus a qualified standalone audit opinion on a promoter-linked associate.

The global lens: sub-scale exporters on rich multiples

India's listed exporters are a fraction of the size of the Asian leaders and earn thinner margins, yet trade at higher multiples.

The world's leading garment manufacturers are larger, more integrated and more profitable than India's listed exporters. Shenzhou International, which makes knitwear for Nike, Uniqlo and Adidas mainly outside China (Vietnam, Cambodia), has revenue of roughly RMB 30 billion (about $4 billion) and operating margins of about 25–28%, because it makes its own fabric and runs very large, automated plants. Technology and disruption: automation, traceability, synthetics and the app

Sewing is still done by hand. What is changing is how orders are won, how inputs are traced and how domestic shoppers buy.

Garment making has resisted automation more than almost any manufacturing process, because fabric is soft and irregular and styles change every season. "Sewbots" handle simple items such as T-shirts in a few pilot plants, but the core of the industry still runs on one worker per machine. The technology that matters today sits around the sewing line: 3D design and digital sampling (Gokaldas runs a 3D design studio), automated cutting and printing, laser finishing for denim, and zero-liquid-discharge laundries required by effluent rules. These reduce sampling time and waste, and they help win orders from brands that want faster development cycles.

The bigger shifts are in compliance and sourcing rules. The US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act already bars goods containing Xinjiang cotton unless importers can prove otherwise, and the 2026 Section 301 tariffs are framed around forced-labour import bans. The EU is adding corporate sustainability due-diligence rules, an ecodesign regulation with a digital product passport for textiles, and extended producer responsibility for textile waste. Each raises the cost of traceability for exporters to Europe and the US, and favours integrated suppliers that can document their chain from fibre to garment. India's cotton industry is moving towards traceable bales (press marks and running numbers, a CAI proposal), which would help Indian exporters prove origin.

Synthetics are the structural gap. Over 60% of world apparel uses man-made fibres, while Indian garmenting is cotton-heavy (Kitex is entirely cotton; Gokaldas's synthetics are up to about 30%). The PLI scheme targets MMF garments, and the rescinding of the polyester QCOs in November 2025 lets garment makers import competitive synthetic fibre and yarn. Whether India builds an MMF garment base matters for the long-run share question: activewear and outerwear, the fastest-growing categories, are mostly synthetic.

Nearshoring is a slower threat. Countries in the USMCA and CAFTA-DR agreements supplied about 13.7% of US apparel imports in April 2026, and Mexico about 2.8%, with around 94% of Mexican apparel claiming USMCA duty-free entry (Shenglu Fashion). Speed to market favours them; cost and fabric availability do not. Pearl's Guatemala unit is a small hedge (loss-making, targeting break-even in FY27).

At home, the disruption is in distribution. Direct-to-consumer brands such as XYXX (+46% in FY25), DaMENSCH and Bummer sell online to younger buyers, and quick-commerce apps deliver innerwear in minutes. Page's online sales grew about 60% in FY26; it now sells outright to quick-commerce platforms, runs two fulfilment centres, is putting every distributor on a distributor management system by FY27-end or Q1 FY28, and went live with a consumer data platform in August 2026. Raymond's e-commerce is growing at "high double digits". The risk for both is that app-led channels weaken the multi-brand-outlet and franchise networks that took decades to build; the opportunity is that they reach towns and customers those networks never did. Online apparel platforms are covered in our e-retail report.

In plain terms

Robots are not about to sew India's shirts. The real changes are paperwork (proving where cotton came from), fibre (the world wears more polyester than India makes into garments) and the phone (shoppers buying underwear on quick-commerce apps rather than in the corner shop). Each favours companies that are integrated, traceable and digital.

Bull vs bear for Indian garments

The case for and against the sub-sector, as of October 2026.

The bull and bear cases differ by half. For exporters, the debate is whether 2026's tariff and trade-deal relief is the start of a durable shift of orders to India or a temporary reprieve in a policy cycle that can turn again. For the brands, it is whether domestic consumption, formalisation and GST 2.0 can restart double-digit growth while input costs rise and new channels erode old moats.

The bull case

  • India now has the best US tariff position since early 2025: 10% against 12.5% for China and Vietnam, and no discounts demanded. September 2026 exports rose 13.7%.
  • Europe is opening: UK duties gone since 15 July 2026; the EU agreement's signing reported for 16 December 2026 with zero duty from entry into force, while Bangladesh faces LDC graduation.
  • Proven exporter momentum: Pearl and Gokaldas grew Q1 FY27 revenue 22.8% and operating profit 32.1%; Raymond's garmenting is full to December with new UK and EU clients.
  • Credible plans: Pearl's FY30 guidance (₹9,000–10,000 Cr revenue, 12–14% EBITDA) from a near-net-cash base; Gokaldas's Africa run-rate at capacity with AGOA to 2028.
  • A weak rupee near ₹95 still to flow through hedged exporters' P&Ls.
  • Page's quality: 64% ROCE, a 2040 licence, low penetration in women's innerwear, socks and athleisure, and a GST 2.0 tailwind.
  • Cotton may ease: record stocks and a falling ICE curve for late 2027.

The bear case

  • US policy risk is unresolved: Section 301 is under legal challenge; a Russia-oil sanctions bill threatens up to 100% tariffs; there is no signed India–US deal.
  • A decade of no growth: India's apparel exports were $16.7 bn in FY18 and $15.77 bn in FY26; structural handicaps (scale, processing, MMF) remain.
  • Concentration: Gokaldas 77.9% North America and 48.2% from three customers; Kitex 53.6% from one.
  • Costs are rising: Haryana wages +35–38%, Labour Codes, wool +100%, cotton +25%, freight +146%.
  • Balance-sheet and governance flags: Gokaldas's 96.3% promoter pledge and rising debt; Kitex's qualified audit, related-party web, +46% scheme dilution and conditional QIP; a new RLL promoter pledge.
  • Brand growth has slowed: Page's volume grew 3.9% in FY26 and 5.7% in Q1 FY27 against a double-digit target; Raymond's apparel loses money and its store count is shrinking.
  • Valuations: Page at 51× and the exporters at 40–50× FY26 profit price in recovery.

Weighing the two, the exporters' operating backdrop in October 2026 is clearly better than at any point in the previous eighteen months, and the trade-deal pipeline gives that improvement some permanence in Europe. But the US, still the largest single market, is governed by instruments that can change by executive action or court ruling, and India's share has not risen for a decade. On the domestic side, Page's quality is not in question, but its growth is; Raymond's assets are cheap, but its earnings are not yet there.

In plain terms

The optimist sees India finally winning garment orders after a decade of losing them, with Europe opening and America treating India better than China. The pessimist sees a business that has not grown in ten years, depends on a few American buyers and a US tariff regime that a court could overturn, and trades at prices that assume the good news lasts.
