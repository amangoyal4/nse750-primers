Consumer electronics & EMS: India's policy-built factory, plus two brands

A complete guide to the six NSE 750 companies filed under consumer electronics: four contract manufacturers (Dixon, Amber, PG Electroplast, Optiemus) that keep 3 to 8 paise of operating profit from each rupee they bill, and two brands (Havells, CP PLUS) that keep 10 to 15. How tariffs and incentives built the factory floor, why 2026 rewrote its economics (an expired PLI, a memory-chip shock, Chinese joint ventures approved), how to read results when most revenue is pass-through, and why the market pays 56–72× earnings for the group.

00

The one-page view: three businesses sharing one label

Four factories that bill the whole bill of materials, and two brands that bill a premium. Read them together and every average misleads; read them apart and the sub-sector makes sense.

The NSE 750 files six companies under "consumer electronics". They do not do the same thing. Dixon Technologies, Amber Enterprises, PG Electroplast (PGEL) and Optiemus Infracom are contract manufacturers: they assemble phones, televisions, air-conditioners, washing machines, routers and wearables for other people's brands. Industry shorthand calls them EMS (electronics manufacturing services) or, where they own the design, ODM (original design manufacturers). Havells India and Aditya Infotech, which sells under the CP PLUS name, are brands: they design, market and distribute products that carry their own name, to dealers, electricians, system integrators and consumers.

The difference shows up in one number. In FY26 the four manufacturers earned a combined operating profit (EBITDA, excluding other income) of ₹3,223 crore on ₹68,116 crore of revenue, a margin of 4.7%. The two brands earned ₹2,807 crore on ₹26,749 crore, a margin of 10.5%. The manufacturers book the full cost of every chip, screen and compressor they buy as revenue, then pass it on to the brand with a thin conversion fee on top. The brands book only the price their channel pays them, and keep the gap between that and what the product cost to make. Averaging the two gives a group margin of 6.4% that describes neither.

What ties the six together is a single national project. India's electronics production rose from ₹1.9 lakh crore in FY15 to ₹13.11 lakh crore in FY26, roughly seven-fold, on the back of rising import duties on finished phones and parts, production-linked incentives (PLI) paid on incremental output, and since 2025 a component scheme (ECMS) that subsidises the parts inside the box. Dixon is the largest home-grown beneficiary: its revenue rose 7.6× in five years, from ₹6,448 crore in FY21 to ₹48,873 crore in FY26. Amber and PGEL rode the same policy wave in air-conditioners. CP PLUS rode a different policy wave: security certification rules that, from April 2025, shut cameras built on Chinese chipsets out of new Indian sales. Havells sits in the bucket by classification more than by business model; its biggest segment is cables and wires, and its consumer-durables arm, Lloyd, is a brand that competes with Voltas, LG and Daikin rather than a contract maker.

Why 2026 is the hinge year

Five things changed in the twelve months to October 2026, and each one hits a different member. First, the mobile PLI scheme that built the phone-assembly industry expired on 31 March 2026, after paying ₹19,091 crore of incentives across all electronics beneficiaries. Its successor, the Mobile Phone Manufacturing Scheme (MPMS, often called "PLI 2.0", ₹62,500 crore over FY27–31), was approved by the Cabinet in mid-July but its guidelines are still awaited. Dixon's Q1 FY27 EBITDA margin fell to 2.98%, about 80 basis points lower than a year earlier, largely because the old incentive stopped.

Second, memory chips went through the steepest price rise in their history. DRAM prices rose more than 50% and NAND flash about 90% year on year in Q1 CY26, and by Q2 cumulative memory costs were roughly four times their pre-shock level, because AI data centres absorbed the supply. Memory is now 30–40% of the bill of materials of a mass-market phone, so phone prices rose 16–20% or more and Indian smartphone shipments fell 12% in Q1 CY26; Counterpoint expects about −10% for the year. Third, the government began approving Chinese joint ventures under Press Note 3: Dixon's 51:49 manufacturing venture with Vivo was cleared on 8 July 2026, after its display venture with HKC and its design venture with Longcheer. Fourth, the component scheme moved from approvals to factories, with 106 projects worth ₹69,548 crore approved by mid-August. Fifth, the air-conditioner season swung from a cool 2025 (industry volume down 10–15% in FY26) to a hot 2026 (up 20–22% in Q1 FY27).

The market values the two halves almost equally: ₹1.18 lakh crore for the four manufacturers and ₹1.18 lakh crore for the two brands. But the manufacturers produce 72% of the revenue and only 44% of the adjusted profit. On adjusted earnings the EMS four trade at about 82× and the branded two at about 64×. The market is paying the manufacturers for growth and for the chance that they climb from assembly into components, displays, design and exports, where margins are higher. It is paying the brands for margin and pricing power, with CP PLUS carrying a growth premium of its own after the Chinese brands were shut out.

In plain terms

If you buy a ₹15,000 phone made by Dixon, Dixon's books show about ₹10,000 of revenue but only about ₹300 of operating profit; almost everything else is chips, screens and memory it bought and passed on. If you buy a ₹15,000 CP PLUS camera kit, CP PLUS books about ₹10,000 and keeps around ₹1,500. Same ticket, five times the profit. The catch is that CP PLUS needs about four times as much capital to do it. Both numbers are illustrative, built in section 15.

Three things to carry through the report

First, margin percentage is the wrong scoreboard for a contract manufacturer. When memory prices double, Dixon's revenue rises and its margin falls even though it earns the same rupees per phone. Read EMS companies on rupees of EBITDA per unit, on return on capital and on cash conversion. Dixon earns a 29.2% return on capital employed (ROCE) on a 3.8% margin because it turns its fixed assets 11.7 times a year and its customers and suppliers fund its working capital. Amber, PGEL and Optiemus earn about 10% on higher margins because their factories, component plants and inventories tie up far more capital.

Second, reported profit is not always operating profit here. Dixon owns 73,05,805 shares of Aditya Infotech, received in July 2024 when it swapped its half of a CCTV joint venture. Every move in the CP PLUS share price flows through Dixon's other income: ₹528 crore of fair-value gain in Q1 FY27 alone. Dixon's FY26 reported profit was ₹1,644 crore; adjusted for these gains it was ₹845 crore. On reported trailing earnings Dixon trades at 39.5×; on FY26 adjusted earnings, at about 88×. Section 11 explains the mechanics.

Third, policy is the demand curve. Tariffs moved assembly onshore, PLI paid for the scale-up, Press Note 3 decides which Chinese partners can enter, ECMS pays for components and the CCTV certification rules decide which cameras can be sold. The six companies' fortunes in FY27–FY31 depend heavily on how MPMS guidelines weight exports versus components, on how quickly component plants convert approvals into output, and on whether the security rules stay as strict as they are now.

01

From silicon to shelf: where a rupee of electronics is earned

Most of the value in an Indian-made phone is still imported silicon, memory and glass. The assembler keeps a sliver; the brand and the channel keep more; the component maker is where the next margin pool sits.

Every product in this sub-sector passes through the same six links. At the top sit the chip companies: system-on-chip (SoC) designers such as Qualcomm, MediaTek and UNISOC, and memory makers Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron. India imports almost all of this, along with most display panels and about 90% of its bare printed circuit boards. Next come the modules: display, camera, battery, printed circuit board (PCB), mechanicals and enclosures. Most still come from China and Korea, and localising them is the whole point of the component scheme. Then assembly: surface-mount technology (SMT) lines place components onto boards (PCBA), the boards go into the box, and the unit is tested and packed. This is where the EMS four sit today. Above assembly is design: owning the product's schematics and intellectual property, which turns an EMS into an ODM that can sell a ready design to several brands. Then the brand, its distribution and its after-sales service. Finally the retailer and the end user: a consumer, a small business, a government safe-city project or a utility laying cable.

Where the value is captured

Follow the money on a ₹15,000 smartphone, using round illustrative numbers calibrated to Dixon's reported cost structure. GST at 18% takes about ₹2,290. The retailer and distributor keep roughly ₹1,270. The brand nets about ₹11,440 and pays the EMS about ₹10,000 ex-factory, keeping roughly ₹1,440 to fund advertising, warranty, royalties and overheads. Of the EMS's ₹10,000, about ₹9,420 goes straight out again on components: memory, chipset, display, cameras, battery, boards and mechanicals. That leaves a value-add of about ₹580, from which the assembler pays its workers and power bill and keeps about ₹300 of EBITDA. Most of the ₹9,420 flows to foreign silicon, memory and display vendors.

On a room air-conditioner the split is less extreme. An appliance ODM such as Amber or PGEL sells a 1.5-tonne inverter split unit to the brand for about ₹22,500 and keeps roughly ₹1,700 of EBITDA (7–8%), because it makes more of the unit itself: heat exchangers, motors, sheet metal, controller boards. In switchgear and wiring devices Havells earns 21–23% segment EBIT, the highest in the group, because a buyer choosing a circuit breaker pays for trust, not for the cheapest price.

In plain terms

An EMS company is paid to turn a box of parts into a working product. It is not paid for the parts, even though they pass through its books. So the size of its revenue says more about the price of memory chips than about how well it is doing. The question to ask is how many rupees it keeps for every unit it makes, and how little capital it needs to make them.

Domestic value addition, the share of a phone's ex-factory value that is actually made in India, was historically only 15–20%. Every policy in this report is an attempt to move that number up: ECMS pays for local components, MPMS (per Dixon management's description) adds a component-linked incentive, and Press Note 3 approvals let Chinese module makers bring their know-how in under Indian majority control. For the EMS four, moving up the chain is the only route to a structurally higher margin. Section 8 tests how far that climb can go.

02

A factory floor built in a decade: size, growth and the export turn

Electronics output grew seven-fold in eleven years, phones became India's largest export, and the six listed members grew faster than the industry they serve.

India's electronics production was ₹1.9 lakh crore in FY15. By FY21 it was ₹5.54 lakh crore, by FY24 ₹9.52 lakh crore, and in FY26 it reached ₹13.11 lakh crore, up 15.8% on the year. That is a compound growth rate of about 19% a year for eleven years, sustained through the pandemic, a chip shortage and the US-China trade war. The single biggest component is mobile phones: production rose from ₹2.14 lakh crore in FY20 to ₹5.5 lakh crore in FY25 on government data. Press summaries put FY26 mobile output at about ₹6.27 lakh crore, though the release we could verify gives only the FY25 figure.

The second transformation is exports. Smartphone exports rose from about ₹27,000 crore in FY20 to ₹2 lakh crore in FY25, more than seven-fold, and in calendar 2025 smartphones became India's single largest export item at about ₹2.62 lakh crore. There is an important caveat for investors in the listed members. The export boom is overwhelmingly Apple's, and Apple's vendors in India, Foxconn and Tata Electronics, are not listed here. Together they account for about three-quarters of smartphone exports. Dixon exported about ₹5,375 crore in FY26, a small share of its ₹48,873 crore revenue; most of its phones are sold in India by Motorola, Xiaomi, Samsung and others. Management sees exports of ₹18,000–20,000 crore within two to three years if the new mobile scheme rewards exports as expected. Optiemus has a small export-oriented venture with Nothing/CMF. For now, the listed EMS companies are mainly domestic manufacturers riding import substitution, not exporters riding China+1.

How big is the EMS market?

There is no authoritative rupee figure for the Indian EMS market on a consistent definition, and the estimates in circulation range from about US$25 billion to US$100 billion depending on what is counted. New Venture Research, which counts only revenue earned by contract manufacturers, put India EMS at US$24.9 billion in 2023, rising to US$37.2 billion by 2029. KPMG's 2025 estimate of US$40–45 billion includes captive and brand-owned assembly. The "US$100 billion" sometimes quoted confuses EMS with total electronics production. Consumer EMS (phones, TVs, appliances, wearables) is about 60–65% of the narrower pool, or roughly US$18–20 billion. The EMS four's combined FY26 revenue of ₹68,116 crore, about US$7.5 billion at the FY26 average rupee, would be roughly 35–40% of that. All of these market-size figures are INDICATIVE.

In plain terms

The listed companies are not the biggest electronics makers in India. Foxconn, Tata Electronics and Samsung's own Noida plant are larger, and none of them is in this report. What the listed four offer investors is the only listed way to own the Android, TV and air-conditioner side of the factory floor.

FY26 itself was a year of divergence. Dixon grew 25.8% on smartphone volumes and new categories. Amber grew 22.2%, with its electronics division up 49% even as RAC growth slowed. 03

Six engines of demand, and why they rarely fire together

Import substitution, exports, household consumption, the weather, security rules and electrification each drive a different member. In Q1 FY27 they produced growth rates from +13% to +103%.

Demand for this sub-sector does not come from one place. It is better understood as six separate engines, each with its own fuel and its own cycle. Knowing which engine drives which company explains most of the quarter-to-quarter noise in the results.

1. Import substitution, forced by tariffs and paid for by PLI

The basic customs duty on finished phones (20%) and on printed circuit board assemblies and parts (10–15%) made it cheaper to assemble in India than to import. The PLI scheme for large-scale electronics manufacturing (PLI-LSEM, 2020) then paid an incentive of roughly 6% falling to 4% on incremental sales above an FY20 base. Together they moved phone assembly onshore: mobile production rose 2.6× between FY20 and FY25. By June 2026 the scheme had paid ₹19,091 crore against ₹20,580 crore of investment by beneficiaries. This engine built Dixon's phone business and Optiemus's subsidiary Optiemus Electronics (OEL). It is now running on the new scheme, MPMS, whose rules are not yet published.

2. Exports and China+1

Western buyers want supply chains that do not run entirely through China. India's smartphone exports to the US almost doubled in FY26, helped by the fact that smartphones were on the exclusion list of the US reciprocal tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which the US Supreme Court struck down on 20 February 2026. Since 24 July 2026 a 10% Section 301 duty has applied on top of most-favoured-nation rates (12.5% for China and Vietnam), and it is under legal challenge. Whether it covers Indian smartphones is unconfirmed; the MFN duty on smartphones is zero. The listed members' direct US exposure is small, and this engine matters mainly through MPMS, which is expected to pay more for exported phones.

3. Household consumption

This engine is currently misfiring for phones. Memory-chip inflation pushed average smartphone prices up 16–20% or more in H1 CY26; shipments fell 12% in Q1 CY26 to 28 million, the weakest first quarter in five years, and the sub-₹10,000 segment fell 65%. Dixon still guides 32–33 million phones in FY27 excluding Vivo, by taking share. Televisions are premiumising (50-inch and larger, QLED, mini-LED), which raises revenue per unit. Washing machines and refrigerators are growing well: Dixon's home-appliance revenue rose 22% and PGEL's washing machines 67% in Q1 FY27. The GST cut on ACs and large TVs from 28% to 18% in September 2025 helped too.

4. The weather

Room air-conditioners are a seasonal, weather-driven product: about half the year's volume sells in March to June. FY25 was a record year (12.5–13.0 million units); the cool, wet summer of 2025 cut FY26 volume to an estimated 11.0–11.5 million (−10–15%, ICRA), and the hot summer of 2026 lifted Q1 FY27 volume 20–22%. This engine drives Amber's consumer-durables division, PGEL and Havells' Lloyd. Section 12 shows how differently the same summers landed on an ODM and on a brand.

5. Security regulation

From 9 April 2025, every CCTV camera model sold in India needs security testing by STQC (the government's Standardisation Testing and Quality Certification directorate) under the Essential Requirements of the Compulsory Registration Order, and designs using Chinese chipsets are refused certification. Full sell-through enforcement followed from April 2026, according to the company. The effect on CP PLUS was immediate: revenue rose 35.6% in FY26 and 89.5% in Q1 FY27, while the Dahua distribution business it used to run shrank by about 80%. This engine is pure policy; Section 13 examines how durable it is.

6. Electrification and infrastructure

Havells' largest segment, cables and wires (₹8,677 crore, 39% of FY26 revenue), grows with construction, power distribution, renewables and data centres. In Q1 FY27 cable revenue rose 27% on flat volumes, almost all of it copper-price inflation. Our Cables & Wires report covers this engine, copper pass-through and the entry of UltraTech and Adani into cables in detail; this report treats Havells' cables mainly as the stable earnings base that funds Lloyd.

Two smaller engines inside Dixon and Amber

IT hardware and telecom are becoming material for Dixon. The one-point problem

The most important arithmetic in this sub-sector is the effect of a small cost slip. At a mobile EMS with ₹94 of materials and ₹3 of EBITDA per ₹100, a one-percentage-point increase in the bill of materials that cannot be passed through to the customer wipes out a third of EBITDA (one point out of three). At an appliance ODM earning ₹7.5, the same slip costs about 13%. At a brand earning ₹13, about 8%. This is why Dixon writes contracts in which the brand bears the component price risk and Dixon earns a fixed conversion fee, and why memory inflation shows up as "optical" margin dilution rather than a loss. It is also why a single mispriced contract, a currency move on unhedged imports or a quarter of idle capacity can wreck an EMS quarter. PGEL's FY26 included a foreign-exchange loss of about ₹39 crore, against an ₹18 crore gain in FY25, on a base of ₹387 crore of EBITDA.

In plain terms

A contract manufacturer is a high-volume, low-margin business that survives on contract terms. As long as the brand agrees to pay for whatever the parts cost, the manufacturer is safe. The danger is the small print: who pays for currency swings, for parts that were bought before a price fell, for a factory that sat idle because the brand's sales slowed.

The branded two have a different exposure. Their gross margins are wide enough to absorb cost increases for a quarter or two, but they must then raise prices and hope volumes hold. Who makes India's electronics: the listed four are not the biggest

Foxconn, Tata and Samsung's own plant dominate phones; brands still make two-thirds of their own ACs; and CCTV went from Chinese-led to CP PLUS-led in eighteen months.

Industry structure differs sharply across the three product markets that matter here: smartphones, room air-conditioners and video surveillance. In each, the listed members compete with large unlisted or foreign players, and in each the structure was reshaped by policy in the last six years.

Smartphones: Dixon leads the Android side

Counterpoint estimates that Dixon made about 19% of India's smartphones in calendar 2025, up from about 11% in 2024, making it the largest producer ahead of Samsung's captive plant in Noida. This is a share of production, not of sales; Dixon sells no phones under its own name. Indian-owned EMS firms together make roughly one phone in four. The Apple ecosystem (Foxconn and Tata Electronics, which bought the Wistron and Pegatron India plants) dominates exports with about 75% of them. Other assemblers include Bhagwati (Micromax), Lava, Karbonn/Jaina and Optiemus. One point of confusion in some sources is worth correcting: Padget Electronics, often listed as a separate Tier-1 assembler, is Dixon's own wholly owned subsidiary, not a partnership and not an Optiemus company. Amber will enter the market from Q1 FY28 with an Oppo/OnePlus/Realme programme of about 8 million units in its first year, rising to 15–16 million.

Room air-conditioners: brands still make most of their own

India has capacity to make 24–26 million RACs a year against demand of 11–13 million. Brands (Voltas, Blue Star, Daikin, LG, Lloyd, Panasonic, Haier) make 65–70% of their units in their own plants; contract manufacturers hold 30–35% of capacity (ICRA). Among the contract makers, Amber is the largest: about 29% of outsourced RAC production on its own primer's basis, or "25%+ of the RAC industry" on ICICI's broader basis. The often-repeated claim that 29% of "top AC brands" are Amber customers misreads this figure; it is a share of outsourced production. Epack Durable (listed, not in this bucket) holds about 24% of outsourced production; PGEL's RAC revenue was ₹1,401 crore in Q1 FY27 alone. Dixon is small in RACs, making mainly control boards. Return on capital is margin times turns

Return on capital employed can be split into two parts: the operating margin (EBIT ÷ revenue) and the capital turn (revenue ÷ capital employed). Multiply them and you get ROCE. Dixon's EBIT margin is about 3.0% and its capital turns roughly 8–10 times a year, giving 25–30%. In plain terms

There are two ways to earn 25–30% on your money in this industry. Either sell at a fat margin and accept that your money sits in stock and receivables for a while (the brands), or sell at a thin margin but make the same rupee go round eight or ten times a year (Dixon). Why the branded two are not interchangeable either

Havells and CP PLUS share an archetype but little else. CP PLUS is a single-category brand that has just been handed a market by regulation, is growing at 35–90%, and has poor cash conversion: ₹13 crore of operating cash flow in FY26 against ₹566 crore of EBITDA. The market prices Havells at about 46× adjusted FY26 earnings and CP PLUS at about 133×.

Equally, the EMS four are four different bets. Dixon is a scaled, cash-generative platform with a portfolio of Chinese and Taiwanese technology partners. Amber is a capital-heavy integrator using air-conditioner cash flows to build PCBs, railways and now phones. PGEL is a fast-growing appliance designer betting on compressors and fridges. Optiemus is a former phone distributor whose revenue depends on one customer. Section 17 places them on maps; the profiles in Section 18 give each its own reading.

PLI 1.0: what it paid and what its end cost

The PLI scheme for large-scale electronics manufacturing (PLI-LSEM), notified in 2020, paid an incentive on incremental sales of mobile phones and specified components above an FY20 base, at rates tapering from about 6% in year one to 4% in year five. Its allocation for mobiles was ₹34,193 crore over FY21–FY25, extended by a year to FY26. By June 2026 it had disbursed ₹19,091 crore against ₹20,580 crore of investment by beneficiaries; the final payout is expected to be about ₹19,908 crore, or 58% of the allocation, because several applicants missed their thresholds. Dixon (through its subsidiary Padget and its Dixon-led programmes) and Optiemus (through its wholly owned OEL) were beneficiaries alongside Foxconn, Tata and Samsung.

The scheme ended on 31 March 2026. Its effect on Dixon showed up immediately: the Mobile & EMS segment's operating margin fell from 3.42% to 2.63% year on year in Q1 FY27, and the company's EBITDA margin fell 78 basis points to 2.98%. Dixon has never disclosed how much PLI income it booked. A rough upper bound: the 79-basis-point margin fall on ₹14,179 crore of quarterly segment revenue is about ₹112 crore a quarter, or about ₹450 crore a year, equal to about 24% of FY26 EBITDA of ₹1,873 crore. Part of the fall reflects memory pass-through rather than lost incentive, so 15–20% of EBITDA is a more likely figure. Both numbers are INDICATIVE estimates, not disclosures.

MPMS: a different kind of reward

The Mobile Phone Manufacturing Scheme, approved by the Cabinet in mid-July 2026 with an outlay of ₹62,500 crore over FY27–FY31, is not a simple extension. Its stated aims are design capability, Indian brands, intellectual property and exports, with total production over the scheme period of about ₹39 lakh crore. Its detailed guidelines, which will set the rates, the eligibility of contract manufacturers versus brands and the base year, have not been published. The only rates in circulation come from Dixon management on its Q1 FY27 call: roughly 2.5–5% of value on exported phones and about 1.5% linked to locally made components. These are INDICATIVE until the guidelines appear.

The shift matters because it changes who earns the subsidy. Under PLI 1.0, a domestic assembler earned an incentive on any incremental phone. Under MPMS as described, a phone made for the Indian market with imported modules may earn little; a phone exported, or built with a locally made display and camera, earns more. On the illustrative ₹10,000 ex-factory phone in Section 15, a 2.5–5% export incentive is worth ₹250–500, between one and two times the assembler's entire base EBITDA of about ₹300. That is why Dixon is courting export programmes from its existing customers (15–20 million phones) and why Optiemus's Nothing/CMF export venture and Amber's Oppo programme from FY28 are being positioned for MPMS.

In plain terms

The first mobile scheme paid companies for making more phones in India. The new one, as far as anyone can tell before the rules are out, will pay them for exporting phones and for making the parts inside. A company that only assembles phones for Indian shops could find its subsidy shrinking even as the total pot grows.

The policy shift reshuffles winners. Dixon loses the old incentive first and gains the new one only once guidelines are out, so FY27 is a gap year for its margin; management says improvement comes from FY28, when MPMS, the Vivo venture and the display and camera modules should all contribute. Amber gains most from ECMS, but the incentives arrive only as plants produce, and meanwhile the capital is spent. PGEL guides an operating margin of about 8% excluding incentives, with white-goods PLI on top. Optiemus holds an approval under the old scheme (amount undisclosed) and must win export programmes to benefit from the new one. The branded two are largely outside the incentive game; their policy exposure runs through certification (CP PLUS) and through BEE energy-label norms and import duties (Lloyd).

The deeper risk is that profits built on policy can be removed by policy. PLI 1.0 paid only 58% of its allocation because targets were hard; MPMS may be stricter still. A business whose incentive income is 15–20% of EBITDA and whose base margin is 3% has little cushion if a scheme is delayed, redesigned or underfunded. Investors should treat incentive income as a separate line, ask companies to disclose it, and value it as finite.

Climbing out of 3%: the value-add ladder Deep dive

Every EMS in the bucket is trying to make the parts it now buys. Success will look like more rupees per unit and higher returns, not a Kaynes-style margin, and the return on capital will dip before it rises.

The central strategic question for the EMS four is whether they can move from assembly, where they keep 3–8% of revenue, into components, displays, design and exports, where margins are higher. Each has a ladder it is climbing. This section sets out the ladders, the evidence so far, and the test investors should apply. Our Industrial Products report covers the same question at the other end of the EMS industry, for low-volume, high-mix makers such as Kaynes and Syrma that earn 34–41% gross margins on industrial, automotive and defence boards. Consumer EMS is the high-volume end, with gross margins of 6–12%, and its ladder looks different.

Dixon's ladder: seven rungs, most of them joint ventures

Dixon's climb runs through partnerships. The bottom rung is assembly, where its Mobile & EMS segment earned a 2.6% operating margin in Q1 FY27. The next rungs are camera modules, through Q Tech India (Dixon 51%), with capacity planned to rise from 70 million to 180–190 million modules a year over 15–18 months; and display modules, through a 74:26 venture with China's HKC, with trial production in Q3 FY27 and commercial output from the end of Q3 or early Q4. The test: what success should look like

Investors should not expect the EMS four to look like Kaynes, with double-digit margins. Pass-through revenue grows at least as fast as value-add, so margin percentages barely move even when the climb succeeds. ICICI's own estimates for Dixon, from a broker with a buy rating, show an EBITDA margin of 3.5% in FY27 and 3.8% in FY28, the same as FY26, on revenue that more than doubles to ₹98,990 crore. Success means three things instead: rupees of EBITDA per unit rising (₹300 towards ₹500 per phone; ₹1,700 towards ₹2,400–2,600 per AC), value-add (gross margin) rising a point or two, and ROCE dipping during the build and then recovering above 25%. A reasonable target for a successful consumer EMS is 4–5% EBITDA on a revenue base two to three times today's, with 25%+ ROCE.

In plain terms

Making your own screens and cameras does not turn a phone assembler into a high-margin company, because the screens and cameras also pass through its revenue. What it does is add a few hundred rupees of profit to every phone. Multiply that by 50 million phones a year and it matters a great deal; divide it by revenue and it barely shows.

The warning signs are the reverse: component plants that run below capacity for years (turns stay low, ROCE stays near 10%), margin gains that come only from incentives, and debt that rises faster than EBITDA. Amber is the member closest to that line today, Dixon the furthest from it. The next 18 months, as HKC, Q Tech, Jewar and Supa move from approval to output, will show which climbs are working.

AI data centres bought up the world's memory in 2026. Phone prices rose a fifth, Indian shipments fell, and the six companies' revenue grew twice as fast as their operating profit.

Demand for high-bandwidth memory from AI data centres pulled DRAM and NAND flash production capacity away from phones, PCs and consumer devices. By mid-2026 cumulative memory costs were roughly four times pre-shock levels, and one source expects them to stay elevated into 2028.

What it did to the Indian phone market

Memory is now 30–40% of the bill of materials of a mass-market smartphone, up from a much smaller share before 2026. When the most expensive component doubles or quadruples, a phone that sold for ₹10,000 cannot stay at ₹10,000. Average Indian smartphone prices rose 16–20% or more in the first half of CY26. Shipments fell 12% in Q1 CY26 to 28 million, the weakest first quarter in five years, and the sub-₹10,000 segment, the heart of mass-market Android and of Dixon's customer base, fell 65%. Counterpoint expects shipments to fall about 10% for the whole of 2026.

For Dixon this is a double effect. Its revenue per phone rises because it passes the memory cost through to the brand, but the number of phones its customers want falls. Dixon made 7.5 million phones in Q1 FY27, roughly flat on a year earlier, and still guides 9.0–9.2 million in Q2 and 32–33 million for FY27 excluding Vivo, which assumes it gains share in a market falling 10–12%. Feature phones, where Dixon makes about two-thirds of India's output, are less exposed to memory but are a shrinking category.

The mechanics: optical dilution

Under a pure pass-through contract the EMS earns a conversion fee per phone. Who else it hits

Optiemus faces the same pass-through effect on its AI+ phone programme, with less bargaining power; its Q1 FY27 operating margin was 3.4% on doubled revenue. CP PLUS is hit differently. Its video recorders and IP cameras use DRAM and flash, and DRAM rose 45–50% quarter on quarter in the June quarter. As a brand it cannot pass costs through by contract; it must raise prices, and it planned increases of 6–8% a month. So far its margin has held (14.6% in Q1 FY27, down from 18% in Q4 FY26 but up six points year on year), helped by the absence of Chinese competition. Havells, Amber and PGEL are largely outside the memory shock; their exposure is copper.

In plain terms

A shortage of memory chips made phones dearer, so fewer people bought them. The companies that assemble phones did not lose money on each phone, because the brands paid for the dearer chips, but they made fewer phones and had to tie up more cash in chip stocks. How long it lasts, and what reverses it

Memory is a famously cyclical commodity. Past booms (2017–18, 2021) ended when new fabs came on and demand cooled, and prices then fell faster than they had risen. The 2026 cycle is different in one respect: the demand driver is AI infrastructure spending, which has so far proved more persistent than PC or phone demand. If prices stay high into 2028 as one source expects, Indian smartphone volumes may not recover until CY27–28, and Dixon's growth will depend on share gains, the Vivo venture, exports and non-phone categories. If memory prices fall sharply, the reverse happens: revenue per phone falls, margins rise "optically", volumes recover, and the working capital tied up in memory is released, though any inventory bought at the peak would need to be passed through or written down. The quarterly DRAM contract price published by Counterpoint and TrendForce is the leading indicator to watch.

Chinese JVs under Press Note 3: partners, customers and future rivals

India now lets Chinese electronics know-how in under Indian majority control. Dixon has the deepest portfolio of approved ventures; the same route is open to everyone else.

In April 2020 the government issued Press Note 3, which requires prior approval for any foreign direct investment from a country sharing a land border with India. In practice it froze Chinese investment. Each Chinese joint venture now needs case-by-case clearance by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology and an inter-ministerial committee. For four years the effect was mainly negative: Chinese phone brands (Xiaomi, Vivo, Oppo, Transsion) could not easily expand their own Indian plants, so they outsourced more volume to Indian EMS firms such as Dixon. From mid-2025 approvals began to arrive, and the policy turned into a channel for Chinese technology to enter on India's terms.

Why it cuts both ways

For Dixon the ventures do three things. They convert customers (Vivo) and suppliers (HKC, Q Tech, Longcheer) into partners with equity at stake, which makes them stickier than a purchase order. For the rest of the bucket the thaw is a competitive threat as much as an opportunity. In plain terms

India decided to let Chinese electronics companies in, but only as junior partners to Indian firms and only one approval at a time. Havells' FY26 profit of ₹1,689 crore includes a fair-value gain of about ₹283 crore on its investment in Goldi Solar; PL Capital's adjusted figure is ₹1,483 crore, up just 0.7%. Amber's adjustment runs the other way: impairments and joint-venture losses of about ₹112 crore depressed reported profit (₹226 crore) relative to the company's adjusted ₹338 crore. In Q1 FY27 Amber also booked an exceptional loss of ₹122 crore that ICICI describes as "related to subsidies" and an aggregator links to Ascent; the company's explanation is unclear, and this report treats it as unexplained.

For these reasons every aggregate in this report shows both reported and adjusted profit. On reported FY26 profit the six trade at 56× combined; on adjusted profit, 72×. The gap is almost entirely Dixon. Investors who read the bucket on reported earnings will think it is cheaper than it is.

Capacity is the ODMs' constraint

The ODMs' own risk is the industry's excess capacity. With 24–26 million units of capacity against 11–13 million of demand, brands can always find a contract maker, so pricing is set by the cheapest credible supplier. In plain terms

CCTV: the trusted-source windfall and its two hidden costs

Security rules removed Hikvision and Dahua from new Indian sales. CP PLUS doubled its revenue and margin in two years; it has not yet turned that profit into cash.

India's video-surveillance market was, until 2024, dominated by Chinese brands. Hikvision (selling through Prama India) and Dahua, which Aditya Infotech itself distributed, together with CP PLUS held roughly 70–75% of the market. Concerns about data security in cameras installed in government buildings, police networks and critical infrastructure led the Ministry of Electronics and IT to bring CCTV under the Essential Requirements of its Compulsory Registration Order. The compliance deadline for new models, after extensions, was 9 April 2025; the company says full enforcement of sell-through followed from 1 April 2026. Both dates can be true (new certifications from 2025, all sales from 2026), and this report uses them in that sense.

The windfall

CP PLUS had already moved its camera system-on-chips to Taiwan's Novatek and built a large plant at Kadapa in Andhra Pradesh (capacity about 2.2–2.5 million units a month on 12 SMT lines). When rivals' uncertified products were shut out, it was the obvious substitute. Revenue rose 35.6% in FY26 to ₹4,221 crore and 89.5% year on year in Q1 FY27 to ₹1,402 crore. Three decades, six shocks: how the bucket was built

Traders and assemblers became a policy-built industry in ten years. Each phase was set off by a government decision or an external shock, not by a gradual market shift.

The six companies have very different origins. Dixon was founded in Noida in 1993 by Sunil Vachani to assemble televisions, then DVD players, lighting, washing machines and set-top boxes. Aditya Infotech was founded by the Khemka family in 1994 as a distributor and launched the CP PLUS brand in 2007 while distributing Dahua. Optiemus began as a mobile-phone distributor for Nokia and Samsung. Amber built air-conditioner manufacturing capability for brands and listed in January 2018. PGEL began as a plastic moulder and moved into appliance assembly. Havells built switchgear, cables and fans, bought Sylvania in 2007 (later exited) and bought Lloyd's consumer-durables business in February 2017 at an enterprise value of about ₹1,600 crore.

Phase 1, 2017–2020: Make in India through tariffs

The phased manufacturing programme for phones and successive customs-duty increases made local phone assembly viable for the first time. Dixon listed in September 2017. Optiemus formed Optiemus Electronics (OEL) with Wistron in 2016, then bought Wistron's 19.91% stake in 2020–21; OEL has been wholly owned since, which an October 2026 surety filing for its "wholly owned subsidiary" confirms. Amber bought Sidwal, a railway HVAC maker, in 2019.

Phase 2, 2020–2025: the PLI boom

Two decisions in April 2020 shaped the next five years. The PLI scheme for mobile phones paid incentives on incremental output, and Press Note 3 froze Chinese FDI. Chinese phone brands that could not easily expand their own plants outsourced to Indian EMS firms, and Dixon's revenue rose from ₹6,448 crore in FY21 to ₹38,860 crore in FY25. Amber bought ILJIN and Ever (EMS), Ascent (bare PCBs), Power-One and Unitronics (power electronics) and Shogini (PCBs). PGEL's RAC revenue rose 128% in FY25, and it raised equity through a QIP in 2024, diluting its promoters to today's 43.2%.

Phase 3, 2024–2026: the CCTV reset and the policy turn

In 2024 the government brought CCTV under security certification, Dixon swapped its CCTV venture stake for CP PLUS shares, and CP PLUS listed in August 2025. In FY26 the cool summer cut RAC volumes, Lloyd swung to a loss and PGEL's profit fell by a third; from Q4 FY26 memory costs squeezed Dixon; and on 31 March 2026 the mobile PLI ended. FY27 so far has brought a hot summer, approval of the Vivo venture and MPMS, ECMS at 106 projects, CP PLUS's ₹1,500 crore QIP, Dixon's Gemtek venture and Amber's buy-out of minority holders in two subsidiaries.

Reading an EMS and a brand: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, two equations and three worked examples. The aim: to read any of the six companies' results and know within ten minutes whether the quarter was good, and why.

The six companies in this bucket report in different formats, use different definitions of EBITDA and segment margin, and run two fundamentally different business models. This section builds a toolkit that works across all of them. It opens with the profit equation for each archetype, works through three examples in rupees, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to compute it, a worked example, what moves it, what good looks like, how the six compare, and the trap in its definition. It closes with an anatomy of one air-conditioner unit, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps collected in one place, and a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

The two profit equations

For a contract manufacturer, revenue is the number of units multiplied by the price per unit, and that price has two parts: the bill of materials (BOM), which is passed through at cost, and a conversion fee, which is the manufacturer's value-add. EBITDA is the value-add per unit minus the cost of converting (labour, power, overheads), multiplied by units, plus any incentives and any margin earned by the manufacturer's own component ventures. Management controls conversion cost and, through negotiation, the fee; it does not control units (the brand's orders), BOM prices (memory, copper) or incentive rates (policy).

PGEL's "₹442 crore (8.3%)" is ₹387 crore (7.3%) on an operating basis. Amber's EBITDA is ₹865 crore on screener's basis, ₹952 crore on ICICI's and ₹337 crore "operating EBITDA" for Q1 FY27 against screener's ₹312 crore. Always state which basis you are using; this report uses operating profit excluding other income throughout.

KPI 2 · Value-add % (gross margin)

What it is. The share of revenue that is not purchased materials: what the company actually adds. Formula. (Revenue − cost of materials) ÷ revenue. Worked example. On the illustrative phone, ₹580 ÷ ₹10,000 = 5.8%, matching Dixon's Q1 FY27 gross margin. On the illustrative RAC in this section, ₹4,050 ÷ ₹22,500 = 18%. What moves it. Making components in-house raises it; BOM inflation passed through lowers it; new low-value-add programmes (phones at Amber, AI+ at Optiemus) lower it. Benchmarks. EMS: good ≥8%, average 5–8%; appliance ODM: good ≥22%, average 18–22%; brand: good ≥35%, average 28–35%. How the six compare. Dixon 5.8% (Q1 FY27, down 174 basis points); Amber about 22% (materials about 78% of Q1 FY27 revenue); Havells 31.3% (Q1 FY27, down 210 basis points); CP PLUS about 35% (INDICATIVE); PGEL and Optiemus do not disclose comparable figures.

Definition trap. Gross margin depends on what is classified as materials. Some companies include freight, power or job-work charges; some book consigned components (supplied free by the brand) outside revenue altogether, which raises their apparent value-add percentage without changing rupee profit. Dixon's revenue is mostly full-BOM but some programmes are consigned. Compare value-add trends within a company, and compare across companies only on rupees per unit.

In plain terms

Value-add tells you how much of the product the company actually makes. A phone assembler adding 6% is doing the last step; an AC maker adding 22% is making most of the box. Rising value-add is the clearest sign that the climb up the ladder in Section 8 is working.

KPI 3 · EBITDA per unit

What it is. Rupees of operating profit earned on each phone, AC or camera. It is the EMS scoreboard that survives BOM inflation. Formula. Segment EBITDA ÷ units, or the per-unit P&L built above. Worked example. Dixon's Q1 FY27 Mobile & EMS revenue was ₹14,179 crore. Stripping out telecom (about ₹2,100 crore) and IT hardware (about ₹1,350 crore) leaves about ₹10,700 crore of phone-related revenue; divided by 7.5 million phones that is about ₹14,300 per phone, an upper bound because it includes feature-phone and wearable revenue. At the segment's 2.6% operating margin, that is roughly ₹370 of operating profit per phone-equivalent (INDICATIVE, derived). What moves it. Conversion-fee negotiation, utilisation, in-house components, incentives. Benchmarks. Phones: good ≥₹450, average ₹250–450, weak <₹250. RAC ODM: good ≥₹2,000, average ₹1,400–2,000, weak <₹1,400.

KPI 4 · Revenue per unit

What it is. Average revenue earned on each unit shipped. For an EMS it mainly tracks the BOM; for a brand it tracks price and mix. Formula. Segment revenue ÷ units. Worked example. Dixon's derived ₹14,300 per phone-equivalent in Q1 FY27 (above). If memory doubles on a ₹10,000 phone, revenue per phone rises to ₹13,000 while EBITDA per phone stays near ₹300. What moves it. Component prices (memory above all in 2026), product mix (premium phones and 55-inch TVs raise it; feature phones lower it) and consigned versus full-BOM contracts. Benchmarks. There is no "good" level; what matters is the direction and the reason. A rising figure with flat EBITDA per unit means pass-through; a rising figure with rising EBITDA per unit means genuine mix improvement. How the six compare. Only Dixon gives enough data to estimate it, and only for phones. Definition trap. Revenue per unit computed from segment revenue that also contains telecom, IT hardware or PCBA-only work will overstate the per-phone figure; always strip out the non-phone lines first, and say the result is an upper bound.

In plain terms

If revenue per phone rises 30% but profit per phone does not move, nothing good has happened; the chips just got dearer. Read revenue per unit only alongside profit per unit.

KPI 5 · Asset turn and capital per ₹100 of revenue

What it is. How much revenue each rupee of fixed assets generates, and its inverse, how much capital (fixed plus working) each ₹100 of revenue needs. Formula. Asset turn = revenue ÷ net fixed assets. Capital intensity = (net fixed assets + net working capital) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Dixon: ₹48,873 crore ÷ ₹4,172 crore of net fixed assets = 11.7×. Amber: ₹12,186 crore ÷ ₹5,633 crore = 2.2×. On the illustrative units, the EMS phone needs about ₹10.6 of capital per ₹100 of revenue, the ODM air-conditioner about ₹45 and the branded CCTV kit about ₹39. What moves it. New plants lower it until they fill; component fabs (PCBs, displays) turn at less than 1× in their early years; acquisitions add goodwill; utilisation raises it. Benchmarks. EMS: good ≥8×, average 4–8×, weak <4×. ODM: good ≥4×, average 2.5–4×, weak <2.5×. Brand: good ≥5×, average 3–5×, weak <3×.

KPI 7 · Working-capital days

What it is. How many days of revenue are tied up in receivables and inventory, net of what suppliers fund. Formula. Cash conversion cycle (CCC) = debtor days + inventory days − payable days; screener's working-capital days use a broader balance-sheet definition. Worked example. Dixon: CCC −7 days; working-capital days −3, so its suppliers and customers fund its operations. What moves it. Channel credit (brands extend more), memory stocking (Dixon in Q1 FY27), seasonality (ODMs build inventory before summer), government receivables (CP PLUS's safe-city projects). Benchmarks. EMS: good ≤10 days, average 10–45, weak >45. Brand: good ≤30, average 30–60, weak >60.

KPI 10 · Incentive share of EBITDA

What it is. The share of operating profit that comes from government incentives (PLI, MPMS, ECMS, white-goods PLI) rather than from operations. Formula. Incentive income booked ÷ EBITDA. Worked example. If Dixon's PLI was worth ₹100–200 per phone on a base EBITDA of ₹300, incentives were a quarter to two-fifths of phone EBITDA. At company level, the INDICATIVE estimate in Section 7 is 15–20% of FY26 EBITDA, with an upper bound of about 24%. What moves it. Scheme design, the base year, thresholds met or missed, the timing of approvals and payments. Benchmarks. Good <10%, average 10–25%, weak >25%. How the six compare. None of the six discloses the figure. PGEL is the most transparent: it guides an operating margin of about 8% "excluding incentives", so incentives come on top. Amber receives white-goods PLI and will receive ECMS support; its Q1 FY27 exceptional loss of ₹122 crore was described by a broker as "related to subsidies", but the company's explanation is unclear. Optiemus holds a mobile PLI approval with undisclosed amounts. Havells and CP PLUS have low incentive exposure. Definition trap. Incentives may be booked as revenue, as other operating income or netted against cost; some are accrued before cash is received. A change in accounting treatment can move EBITDA without any change in cash.

KPI 11 · Capacity, utilisation and the capex pipeline

What it is. How much a company can make, how much of that it is using, and how much new capacity it has committed to. Formula. Utilisation = units produced ÷ installed capacity; pipeline = announced capex and approvals. Worked example. The RAC industry has 24–26 million units of capacity and sold 11–13 million, so average utilisation is about 45–50%. CP PLUS's Kadapa plant can make 2.2–2.5 million units a month; at FY27 guidance of ₹6,000–6,500 crore and an assumed realisation of ₹2,500 per unit (ILLUSTRATIVE), it would need about 2.1 million units a month, near capacity. What moves it. Demand, seasonality, new lines. Benchmarks. Utilisation good ≥75%, average 55–75%, weak <55%.

KPI 12 · Market share and domestic value addition: always ask "of what?"

What it is. Share of a market or of production, and the share of a product's value made locally. Formula. Company volume or revenue ÷ market volume or revenue; domestic value addition (DVA) = local content ÷ ex-factory value. Worked example. Dixon's "~19% of India's smartphones" (Counterpoint, CY25) is a share of production; it sells no phones itself. Amber's "~29%" is its share of outsourced RAC production; ICICI's "25%+ of the RAC industry" is a broader base. CP PLUS's 20.8% (FY25) is a revenue share of the whole surveillance market; its 39–45% claims for FY26 have an unclear basis and are INDICATIVE. Lloyd's 6–12% comes from aggregators (INDICATIVE). Voltas's 17.3% is its own secondary-sales figure. Phone DVA has historically been only 15–20%; CP PLUS's "85% → 98% Make in India content" uses its own definition. Benchmarks. DVA good ≥35%, average 20–35%, weak <20%. Definition trap. Share of production, of outsourced production, of sales value and of sales volume are four different numbers; a company can quote whichever is largest.

KPI 13 · P/E reported vs adjusted, and segment margins by basis

What it is. The valuation multiple on profit with and without one-off and non-operating items. Formula. Market cap ÷ PAT, on both reported and adjusted PAT. Worked example. Dixon: ₹74,084 crore ÷ ₹1,644 crore = 45.1× FY26 reported; ÷ ₹845 crore = 87.7× adjusted. Havells: 40.7× reported, 46.4× adjusted for the Goldi Solar gain. Amber: 101.5× reported, 67.9× on the company's adjusted ₹338 crore. Benchmarks. No absolute benchmark; the gap between the two is the signal. Definition trap. Havells' segment margins come in three bases: "contribution margin" in press releases (Lloyd 9.2% in Q1 FY27), "segment EBIT" in broker notes (Lloyd −3.9%) and an older contribution basis quoting switchgear at ~35%. Havells also restated its segments in Q1 FY27, so FY26 figures in old and new formats do not match line by line (Lloyd ₹3,948 crore and a ₹203 crore loss in the old format; ₹3,974 crore and −₹214 crore EBIT in the new). Never compare a contribution margin with an EBIT margin.

In plain terms

Two numbers can both be called "Lloyd's margin" and differ by thirteen percentage points: one before Lloyd's share of advertising and overheads, the other after. The second is the one that tells you whether Lloyd makes money. It does not.

KPI 14 · Net debt, and why each source gives a different answer

What it is. Borrowings less cash and liquid investments; the balance-sheet cushion or burden behind the business. Formula. Gross debt − cash and bank − liquid investments. Worked example. Amber's screener balance sheet shows ₹2,702 crore of borrowings at March 2026, but the company's own net debt was about ₹510 crore at March and about ₹1,225 crore at June 2026 after a quarter of capex and working-capital build. Dixon's borrowings of ₹994 crore less ₹1,007 crore of investments give screener net cash of ₹13 crore, but those investments are largely the strategic CP PLUS stake, which Dixon cannot sell without losing its fair-value gains and which is not operating cash. CP PLUS had ₹255 crore of net debt at March 2026 and, after its ₹1,500 crore QIP, an estimated pro-forma net cash of about ₹1,245 crore (INDICATIVE). What moves it. Capex, working capital (seasonal for the ODMs), equity raises and acquisitions. Benchmarks. For an EMS, net debt below one year's EBITDA is comfortable; above two years is a warning, because a 3–8% margin business has little room to service debt in a bad year. How the six compare. Dixon roughly neutral; CP PLUS net cash after the QIP; Optiemus ₹286 crore net debt on ₹98 crore of EBITDA (about 2.9×); Amber ₹1,225 crore at June on ₹865 crore of FY26 EBITDA (about 1.4×); PGEL ₹586 crore on a gross basis although the company describes itself as net cash, a gap we could not reconcile; Havells' net cash is not captured reliably by our data source, so this report does not quote a figure. Definition trap. Data aggregators often miss bank balances held as fixed deposits, count strategic stakes as liquid investments, and ignore lease liabilities. Use the company's own net-debt statement where it gives one, and say so.

  • Margin percentage under pass-through. BOM inflation lowers the percentage with no change in rupee profit; read per-unit figures.
  • Segment margin bases. Havells' contribution margin vs segment EBIT, and its Q1 FY27 restatement.
  • Market-share bases. Production vs sales, outsourced vs total, revenue vs volume, company-cited vs third-party.
  • Net debt. Screener's borrowings-less-investments can miss bank balances (Havells, PGEL) or include strategic stakes (Dixon's CP PLUS shares); use company net-debt figures where given (Amber ₹510 Cr at March, ₹1,225 Cr at June 2026).
  • Joint-venture consolidation. A 51:49 venture adds 100% of revenue but only 51% of profit to Dixon's shareholders.
  • Incentive accounting. Booked as revenue, other operating income or netted against cost; accrued before receipt.
  • Capacity and approvals. Nameplate vs effective; approved investment vs money spent.

How to read a consumer-electronics company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Identify the archetype first. EMS/ODM or brand. Every benchmark that follows depends on it.
  2. Revenue growth, split into volume and price. Look for units (Dixon's phones, PGEL's RAC growth, Lloyd's volume). If revenue grew much faster than units, the difference is mostly BOM or copper inflation.
  3. EBITDA in rupees, excluding other income. Recompute it yourself from the P&L; ignore headline figures that include other income.
  4. EBITDA per unit and gross margin. For an EMS, is rupee profit per unit flat, up or down? For a brand, did gross margin hold despite input inflation? A brand whose gross margin fell more than 150 basis points did not pass costs on.
  5. Other income and exceptional items. At Dixon, find the CP PLUS fair-value line and subtract it. At Amber, read the exceptional items note. At Havells, look for investment fair-value gains. Rebuild adjusted PAT.
  6. Segment table. Dixon: Mobile & EMS margin (2.6% in Q1 FY27), consumer electronics, home appliances. Havells: Lloyd EBIT and cables EBIT. Amber: electronics margin (10.8%) and railway margin.
  7. Working capital and cash flow. Half-yearly balance sheets (September and March) show receivable and inventory days. Red flag: inventory or receivables rising faster than revenue for two periods running.
  8. Net debt in rupees. Red flag: net debt rising faster than EBITDA, as Amber's did from ₹510 Cr to ₹1,225 Cr in one quarter.
  9. Policy updates. MPMS guidelines, Press Note 3 approvals, ECMS approvals, CCTV rule changes, PLI receipts.
  10. Guidance against delivery. Dixon's 32–33 million phone guide, CP PLUS's ₹6,000–6,500 Cr, Amber's 13–15% CD growth, PGEL's ~8% margin ex-incentives, Optiemus's ₹3,600 Cr. Two consecutive misses matter more than one large one.

In plain terms

Indian contract manufacturers make about the same margin as Foxconn but are valued at four to eight times Foxconn's multiple. Investors are not paying for today's business; they are paying for five years of fast growth that government policy is designed to deliver. If the policy delivers and the companies climb into components, the price can be justified. If either falters, the gap to global multiples is very large.

One structural difference supports part of the premium: Indian EMS companies are growing in a domestic market that is still under-penetrated in appliances and in which the government actively steers demand to local producers. Global EMS firms mostly serve mature markets and global brands that can move production at will. Against that, Indian EMS margins are no higher than global ones, their customers are mostly the same global brands, and the policies that protect them are explicitly time-limited.

Technology and disruption: seven ways the model could change

Brands can insource, Chinese partners can become competitors, memory can stay dear, and component plants can sit half-empty. Not every threat is technological; most are commercial or political.

Disruption in this bucket rarely comes from a new invention. It comes from changes in who controls the factory, the parts and the rules. Seven risks stand out.

1. Brands insource or switch

A brand that outsources can take production back or move it. Motorola, Xiaomi or Samsung could shift volume to Samsung's Noida plant, to Bhagwati, or to a newly approved Chinese-partnered EMS. AC brands can build captive capacity; the Voltas–Atomberg compressor venture shows brands investing in components themselves. For Dixon, with a top-three customer share of about 80% in FY25, losing one programme would be material. For Optiemus, with about 57% from one customer, it would be existential.

2. Chinese partners become competitors

Press Note 3 approvals give Dixon technology and volume, but the same route lets Chinese brands co-own Indian capacity with other partners, as Oppo's arrangement with Amber shows. Over time, a Chinese brand with an approved Indian venture may need a standalone EMS less. The approvals also make Indian firms dependent on Chinese process know-how in displays, cameras and precision parts.

3. The memory super-cycle lasts

If AI data-centre demand keeps memory prices near four times pre-shock levels into 2028, mass-market smartphone volumes may stay depressed, sub-₹10,000 phones may not recover, and working capital stays elevated. CP PLUS's recorders and cameras face the same cost pressure without pass-through contracts.

4. Component plants turn slowly

Displays, camera modules, bare PCBs and copper-clad laminate turn their assets at less than once a year in their early years. Industrial EMS shows the pattern: Kaynes' ROCE fell from 22% in FY23 to 13% in FY26 during its build. Amber's ₹4,700 crore of PCB approvals, PGEL's compressor and fridge plants and Dixon's display and camera ventures will all dilute returns before they lift them, and some may never fill.

5. Design and AI shift value

MPMS is meant to reward design and intellectual property, which favours ODMs (Dixon's Dixtel, PGEL's in-house AC and washing-machine designs) over pure assemblers. In TVs, mini-LED and OLED raise the value of panels that India does not make. In surveillance, AI analytics and video-surveillance-as-a-service shift value from cameras to software, where CP PLUS's OnVigil monitoring is small. In ACs, efficiency norms push inverter and BLDC technology, which raises the value of compressors and controllers.

6. Policy reverses or is redesigned

MPMS guidelines may weight domestic and export production differently from what Dixon expects; ECMS incentives could be slower than planned; CCTV rules could be relaxed; and US trade policy remains uncertain. The IEEPA tariffs were struck down on 20 February 2026 and a 10% Section 301 duty applies from 24 July 2026 (under legal challenge). Only the earlier exclusion of smartphones from the IEEPA reciprocal tariffs is verified; how Indian smartphones are treated now is unconfirmed, as is any Section 232 action on semiconductors that might cover phones and laptops.

7. Climate makes the AC cycle more volatile

Hotter summers raise long-run AC demand, but the 2025–26 swing (−10–15% then +20–22%) shows that year-to-year volatility is rising too. With industry capacity at twice demand, a cool year hits ODM utilisation and brand inventories at once.

Bull vs bear: policy-built growth against pass-through economics

Both cases are strong. Which wins depends on MPMS, memory, the Chinese ventures and whether component plants fill.

The bull and bear cases for the sub-sector rest on the same facts read differently. Bulls see a policy-backed industry in its first decade, with scale players learning to make components and design. Bears see thin-margin assemblers priced like software companies, whose profits depend on subsidies, a share price, and customers who could leave.

The bull case

  • Policy continuity. MPMS (₹62,500 Cr), ECMS (106 projects, ₹69,548 Cr) and PLI IT Hardware 2.0 extend support to FY31, now aimed at exports and components where margins are higher.
  • Proven returns. Dixon earns 29% ROCE on a 3.8% margin with 95% cash conversion; CP PLUS and Havells earn 25–29%.
  • The ladder is visible. Amber's electronics margin rose from 6.4% to 10.8%; Dixon's camera and display ventures start in FY27; PGEL's compressors from Dec-26/Jan-27.
  • Chinese partners on Indian terms. Vivo (20–22 mn phones a year), HKC and Longcheer bring volume and technology under Indian control.
  • Demand runway. Low appliance penetration, hotter summers, premium TVs, telecom and IT hardware; CCTV substitution continuing.
  • Memory normalises. When memory prices fall, phone volumes recover and EMS margins rise optically.

The bear case

  • Valuation. 72× adjusted FY26 earnings for the six, 82× for the EMS four, against 10–20× for global EMS on similar margins (indicative).
  • Earnings quality. Half of Dixon's FY26 reported profit came from CP PLUS fair-value gains; adjusted P/E ~88×.
  • The incentive gap. PLI 1.0 ended, cost Dixon ~80 bps of margin, and MPMS guidelines are not out.
  • Pass-through squeeze. Q1 FY27 revenue +24%, operating profit +11%; smartphone volumes −10% in CY26.
  • Capital and cash. Amber's net debt more than doubled in a quarter; CP PLUS converted 2% of EBITDA to cash; Optiemus' CFO was negative.
  • Concentration and insourcing. Dixon top-3 ~80% (FY25); Optiemus one customer ~57%; brands can switch to Chinese-partnered rivals.
  • Lloyd. Havells' AC arm lost money in a hot season while losing share.

Weighing the two

The bull case is strongest for the companies whose returns already prove the model (Dixon, Havells) and weakest for those whose returns depend on capital still being spent (Amber, PGEL, Optiemus). CP PLUS sits in between: excellent margins and returns on reported profit, but cash conversion that has not yet caught up. The bear case is strongest on price: even if every bullish operating claim proves right, the bucket's adjusted multiples leave little cushion for a delayed scheme, a lost customer or a second year of weak phone demand.

For a reader deciding which side to take, four questions matter most. Will MPMS guidelines pay EMS firms for exports and local components at the rates Dixon expects? Will memory prices start falling within 12 months? Will the display, camera and PCB plants reach commercial output on schedule in FY27–28? And will CP PLUS's cash flow converge with its profit? Three yeses would support the bull case; two or more noes would favour the bear.

In plain terms

The optimists are betting that government support plus Indian factories climbing into parts will make these companies far bigger and somewhat more profitable. The pessimists point out that today's profits are thin, partly subsidised and partly a paper gain on another company's shares, while the share prices already assume the optimists are right.
