Rice, agri commodities & seeds: four businesses, one harvest gamble

A complete guide to the five NSE-listed companies filed under "Other Agricultural Products": two branded basmati houses, a maker of chocolate fats from forest seeds, India's largest maize wet-miller and a hybrid-seed breeder. How each turns a seasonal Indian crop into profit, why government policy and a single monsoon can reset that profit overnight, what the 2026 war in West Asia, the cocoa crash and the maize glut did to each of them, and how to read their numbers like an analyst.

00

The one-page view: five companies, four businesses, one calendar

The NSE label lumps together rice, chocolate fat, corn starch and seed. What really unites them is a crop bought in a hurry, held for months and sold at a price that policy can move overnight.

The National Stock Exchange's "Other Agricultural Products" bucket holds five companies that do not compete with one another and in most cases never meet in a market. LT Foods and KRBL buy basmati paddy, age it and sell branded rice under Daawat, Royal and India Gate. Manorama Industries turns sal and shea seeds into fats that chocolate makers use in place of cocoa butter. Gujarat Ambuja Exports (GAEL) grinds maize into starch, glucose and sorbitol and crushes soybean into meal and oil. Kaveri Seed breeds hybrid cotton, maize, rice and vegetable seed and sells it once a year to farmers. Adding up their margins or averaging their price-to-earnings ratios would tell you nothing. This report therefore treats them as four archetypes and compares like with like throughout.

What they do share is a shape. Each buys an Indian farm product in a short seasonal window: basmati paddy from October to January, sal seed in May to July, maize in the kharif and rabi harvests, hybrid seed grown on contract a season ahead. Each then carries that crop on its balance sheet for months, sometimes for two years. And each sells into a market where a government notification can change the price faster than any harvest: an export ban or a minimum export price on rice, a United States tariff, a European pesticide limit, a cap on the retail price of cotton seed, the price at which the Food Corporation of India sells rice to ethanol distillers. Whoever finances the carry cheaply and reads the crop and the policy correctly earns the margin. Whoever gets either wrong writes down inventory.

Five forces that decide the numbers

  • The basmati paddy cycle and the cost of carrying it. Basmati millers buy a year's paddy in three months and age it for 12–24 months. Paddy that opened at ₹2,500–3,200 a quintal in October 2025 after the Punjab floods was fetching ₹4,300–4,700 by February 2026, and 1509 paddy crossed ₹4,800 on 19 October 2026 with 1121 at a record. Cheap stock sold into a dear market gave KRBL a 36.3% gross margin in Q1 FY27 against 25.7% a year earlier. The new crop is now expensive, and the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, so the next carry costs more.
  • Policy whiplash. India banned broken-rice exports in September 2022 and white-rice exports in July 2023, put a US$1,200 a tonne floor on basmati in August 2023, then unwound all of it between September 2024 and March 2025. Government rice stocks now sit at about three times the buffer norm. Abroad, US tariffs on India went to 50% in August 2025, were struck down in February 2026 and replaced by 10%, a big deal for LT Foods, which earns half its revenue in North America.
  • West Asia. The Middle East takes about 71% of India's basmati exports by volume. Iranian payments froze in January 2026, and the war that began on 28 February 2026 closed Hormuz and multiplied container freight to the Gulf up to tenfold. KRBL's exports halved in Q1 FY27.
  • Commodity prices in a specialty wrapper. Manorama's demand rides the cocoa price, which rose to about US$12,900 a tonne in December 2024 and then collapsed. GAEL's margin rides the maize price, which in turn rides the ethanol programme: maize supplied 41.8% of the ethanol delivered in the 2025-26 supply year.
  • The monsoon. Kaveri earns its whole year in one quarter. The 2026 monsoon ended 12.6% below normal, the weakest since 2015, and Kaveri's Q1 FY27 revenue fell 14%, with maize seed down 43%.

The latest reported quarter for all five is Q1 FY27, the three months to June 2026. It was strong for four of them. Combined revenue rose 13.9%, operating profit 33.8% and net profit 29.3%. The crop processors (LT Foods, KRBL and GAEL) grew revenue 17% and operating profit 61%. That is the signature of a spread quarter: KRBL sold rice it had bought cheaply into a market where paddy had become dear, and GAEL bought maize after the price had fallen from the ethanol-driven highs of FY26. Kaveri was the exception, hurt by a monsoon that was 36% short in June. Both basmati houses have told analysts not to extrapolate Q1: KRBL guides to a gross margin of about 30% for FY27, not 36%. The September quarter results, Q2 FY27, start arriving now: GAEL's board met on 24 October 2026 (numbers not yet published at the time of writing), LT Foods reports on 27 October and Kaveri on 28 October; KRBL and Manorama usually report by mid-November.

In plain terms

These five companies are less alike than any five banks or five cement makers. What links them is timing risk: each buys a crop when it ripens, holds it, and hopes the selling price has not been knocked down by a ban, a tariff, a war or a weak monsoon by the time it sells. In the June 2026 quarter most of them caught the timing right. The question for the rest of FY27 is whether they can repeat it now that paddy and maize are expensive again.

Valuations already price four very different stories. On trailing earnings KRBL trades at 10.6 times with over ₹1,800 crore of cash and investments; LT Foods at 20.6 times; GAEL at 16.8 times; Kaveri at 15.0 times, near its 52-week low; and Manorama at 43.2 times, or roughly 9 times its post-QIP book (an estimate). The rest of the report explains why the market draws those lines, where it may be wrong, and which numbers will tell you first.

01

Four ways to turn a crop into cash

A basmati house earns on time and brand, a fat refiner on chemistry and cheap forest seed, a wet-miller on the gap between grain and starch, and a seed breeder on genetics. Each value chain puts the profit in a different place.

The fastest way to understand this group is to follow a rupee through each of the four value chains. They start in very different fields, Punjab paddy, the sal forests of central India, the maize belts of Bihar and Karnataka, and contract seed farms in Telangana, but each has a single step where most of the margin is made. Knowing which step that is tells you which number to watch.

Branded basmati. Basmati is a geographical-indication rice grown only in the Indo-Gangetic plain. The varieties that matter commercially are Pusa 1121 (released in 2003, the extra-long grain that changed the trade), 1509, 1718, 1401 and the newer disease-resistant 1885 and 1886. Unlike common rice, basmati has no minimum support price and no government procurement, so millers buy everything from private traders in a three-month window. They then hold it as paddy or milled rice for a year or more, because aged basmati commands a premium. KRBL held ₹2,944 crore of inventory at 30 June 2026 (71,000 tonnes of paddy and 3,89,000 tonnes of rice). LT Foods held 3,46,000 tonnes of rice at an average ₹56 a kilo and 1,64,000 tonnes of paddy at ₹38 a kilo. The milling itself is a utility step. The money is made by owning the right stock at the right price and by selling it under a brand at a premium to loose rice.

Specialty fats. Cocoa butter gives chocolate its snap and its melt in the mouth because of a particular arrangement of fatty acids. A handful of tropical fats (sal, shea, illipe, kokum, mango kernel, and some palm fractions) contain the same building blocks. Fractionate them and blend them correctly and you get a cocoa butter equivalent, or CBE, which European and Indian rules allow at up to 5% of a chocolate bar and which compound coatings and fillings use in far larger quantities. Manorama's edge is at both ends of the chain: it buys a seed that tribal collectors gather from the forest floor at a low price, and it has built the fractionation and recipe know-how that big confectioners take three to four years to qualify. "Stearin and CBE" made up 71.4% of its FY26 revenue.

Agro-processing. GAEL is a converter. It buys maize and turns it into a menu of products whose prices move more slowly than the grain's. When maize falls, the spread widens and margins jump; when maize spikes, as it did through most of FY26 while distilleries competed for grain, the spread narrows. The company's strategy is to climb the ladder from commodity starch (₹32–36 a kilo, an indicative industry range) to pharma-grade dextrose and sorbitol and to sodium gluconate, a concrete admixture made by fermenting glucose. Its second leg, oilseed crushing, earns the gap between the price of soybean and the combined value of meal and oil, a thin and volatile margin of around 6%.

Hybrid seed. A hybrid seed loses its yield advantage if the farmer saves and replants it, so farmers buy fresh seed every season. That makes a seed company an intellectual-property business with a manufacturing tail. Kaveri breeds parent lines, pays contract farmers to grow the hybrid seed, conditions and packs it, and sells it through dealers in a few weeks before the monsoon sowing. Cotton seed carrying the Bollgard-II trait is sold at a maximum retail price set by the government (₹901 a packet of 475 grams in 2025-26). Maize, rice and vegetable seed are priced freely, which is why Kaveri has spent a decade moving away from cotton: non-cotton crops made up 81% of its FY26 standalone revenue.

In plain terms

A basmati house is a warehouse with a brand on the door: it profits when the rice it bought last year is worth more this year and when shoppers pay extra for the name on the bag. Manorama is a chemistry shop that buys forest seed cheaply and sells chocolate makers a fat that saves them money on cocoa butter. GAEL is a grain refinery that wins when maize is cheap relative to starch and glucose. Kaveri sells genetics in a packet once a year.

02

How big each market is

There is no single market size for a mixed bucket. Rice, chocolate fat, maize and seed are sized separately, on different bases, and each number carries a caveat.

Start with rice, because it is two-thirds of the group's revenue. India grew 154.02 million tonnes of rice in 2025-26 according to the agriculture ministry's third advance estimate, up from 150.18 million tonnes the year before and about 30% more than in 2019-20. It is the world's largest rice exporter, with roughly 40% of global trade. The United States Department of Agriculture, which counts on a different crop year, put India's 2026-27 output at about 150 million tonnes and world output at about 537 million tonnes in August 2026. Quote the agency and the year whenever you use a production figure: agencies differ by several million tonnes.

Basmati is a small slice of that crop, roughly 13.5 million tonnes produced in 2025-26 by KRBL's estimate, but a large slice of the money. At home, about 2–2.5 million tonnes of basmati is eaten each year, and the packaged branded segment is worth an estimated ₹12,000–15,000 crore (an INDICATIVE figure; no audited series exists). India Gate and Daawat together hold roughly 60% of it. LT Foods' investor deck projects packaged basmati growing 10–12% a year in India and North America, 9–10% in the Middle East and 6–8% in Europe; those are company projections, not market data.

Specialty fats. Manorama's annual report cites a global specialty fats and oils market of US$18.17 billion in 2026, growing 7.6% a year to US$32.5 billion by 2034. That is the broad category: bakery fats, fillings, confectionery fats, cocoa butter substitutes and equivalents. Cocoa butter equivalents alone are a much smaller pool, estimated at US$1.5–2.0 billion (an INDICATIVE estimate; no fresh source). On that estimate Manorama's FY26 revenue of about US$155 million would be roughly 2% of global CBE supply, a figure its own primer has used. The market leaders are much larger: AAK of Sweden, Bunge Loders Croklaan, Fuji Oil, Wilmar, Cargill and Olam.

Maize, starch and oilseed. This is where the numbers moved most. India produced 55.09 million tonnes of maize in 2025-26 (third advance estimate: kharif 30.51, rabi 20.03, summer 4.56), up 27% from 43.41 million tonnes the year before and nearly double 2019-20. The ethanol programme drove it: distilleries now take an estimated 10–11 million tonnes of maize a year. India's wet-milling (starch) capacity is roughly 20,000–25,000 tonnes a day (an INDICATIVE industry estimate), of which GAEL's 5,600 tonnes a day is about a quarter. Soybean went the other way: the trade body SOPA put the 2025-26 crop at 105.36 lakh tonnes against 125.82 lakh tonnes the year before, while the ministry's estimate was 125.96 lakh tonnes. For crushers like GAEL, SOPA's number is the one that drives margins.

In plain terms

The group sits on top of very large markets, but its share of each is small except in branded basmati, where the two listed houses own most of the packaged shelf. 03

Who buys, and why

Gulf kitchens, Indian supermarkets, American jasmine lovers, chocolate factories, ethanol distilleries and cotton farmers: six very different demand engines feed the five companies.

The Gulf table. The Middle East takes about 71% of India's basmati exports by volume (KRBL's FY26 reading of APEDA data, and "close to three quarters" in its chairman's words). Saudi Arabia is the anchor buyer at roughly 1.1–1.2 million tonnes a year, followed by Iraq, Iran (volatile, 0.6–1.0 million tonnes), the UAE and Yemen; these country figures are INDICATIVE orders of magnitude from FY24–25 data. Demand is price-inelastic because basmati is the everyday rice of Gulf households. In plain terms

Each company's customer behaves differently. The Gulf family will buy basmati whatever it costs, if the ship arrives and the bank pays. The Indian shopper is slowly trading up from loose rice to a brand. The American shopper buys Royal and Golden Star in a supermarket and pays whatever tariff is passed on. The chocolate maker buys CBE to save money on cocoa butter. The distillery competes with GAEL for maize. And the farmer buys seed only if the rain comes on time.

The demand engines also explain why the group's earnings rarely move together. In Q1 FY27 Gulf logistics hurt KRBL, US tariff relief helped LT Foods, cocoa normalisation had not yet reached Manorama's volumes, a maize glut helped GAEL, and a failed June monsoon hurt Kaveri. Over a full cycle that diversity is an advantage for anyone holding all five, but it makes any sector-wide forecast close to meaningless.

Inputs, carry and where ₹100 of revenue goes

Raw material is 50–78% of revenue for every member. The real cost differences lie in how long each company must hold that raw material and who pays to finance it.

All five companies are, at bottom, buyers of a single farm product, and that product dominates their cost base. For a basmati house, paddy and packaging are about 72% of revenue. For GAEL, maize, soybean and traded goods are about three-quarters. Even Manorama, whose seed is cheap, spends over half its revenue on raw material, and Kaveri's grower payments and processing come to roughly half. Everything else (staff, power, freight, advertising) is small by comparison. So the first question for any of them is not "what are the overheads?" but "what did the crop cost, and when was it bought?"

In plain terms

In this sector the biggest decisions are made in the buying season, not the selling season. A miller who buys a year's paddy at the bottom of the market has locked in a good year before a single bag is sold; one who buys at the top has locked in a weak one. The balance sheet, not the factory, is the main asset.

Capacity is less of a constraint for the basmati houses, which mill far less than their plants could handle, than for the processors. KRBL can mill 207 tonnes an hour and runs 150.7 MW of wind, solar and biomass power. Manorama's fractionation capacity (47,500 tonnes a year, rising to 52,000 in Q3 FY27) is the gate on its growth: it ran at about 80% in Q1 FY27, and the next big step, a 75,000-tonne plant, arrives around Q3 FY28. GAEL grinds 5,600 tonnes of maize a day, rising to 6,500 when its 900-tonne Himmatnagar plant starts in FY27, and reported 83% capacity availability in FY26. Kaveri's constraint is not plant but land and growers: it decided in August 2026 to cut next year's seed production after building ₹200 crore of extra inventory for a season that did not come.

Who else is in the field

Packaged basmati is a two-brand market with a long tail; exports are fragmented; specialty fats are a global oligopoly whose members are also Manorama's customers; maize starch and seeds are crowded with multinationals.

Each archetype faces a different competitive landscape, and none of the five members competes with more than one of the others. The listed companies also tell only part of the story: in every one of the four markets, large unlisted players, multinationals and conglomerates shape prices.

Packaged basmati: a duopoly with a long tail

India Gate and Daawat dominate the branded shelf. KRBL's Q1 FY27 deck, using Nielsen retail data for the twelve months to June 2026, gives India Gate 37% of packaged basmati in general trade (kirana stores), 38% in modern trade (supermarkets) and an internal estimate of 41% in e-commerce. LT Foods' deck gives Daawat 23.1% for the same period, down from 23.7% in FY26; management blames a reset in Nielsen's methodology. The two series are not the same basis (Daawat's is an all-India figure, India Gate's is by channel), but together they suggest the two brands hold roughly 60% of packaged basmati. Older industry commentary quoting 42% and 30% used a different series and should not be compared with these.

Basmati exports: hundreds of exporters

The export trade is far less concentrated. India has an estimated 400–500 registered basmati exporters, and the top ten handle perhaps 45–55% of volume (INDICATIVE). Large unlisted players include Amir Chand Jagdish Kumar, Sarveshwar, Shri Lal Mahal, Galaxy and Pari Foods; small listed exporters include Chaman Lal Setia and GRM Overseas. Pakistan is the only other basmati origin, exporting roughly 0.7–1.0 million tonnes; in 2026 its basmati traded about US$100 a tonne above India's, and India holds about 85% of world basmati trade. India's application for an EU geographical indication for basmati, filed in 2018, is still contested by Pakistan.

Specialty fats: the customers are also the competitors

The global CBE and specialty-fats business is an oligopoly: AAK of Sweden (the largest), Bunge Loders Croklaan, Fuji Oil (which owns Blommer), Wilmar and IOI, Cargill and Olam. In India, 3F Industries and AAK's local arm compete with Manorama. The twist is that Cargill, Olam and Loders Croklaan also appear on Manorama's customer list alongside Ferrero, Mondelez, Mars, Hershey, Nestlé and Barry Callebaut. The majors buy sal and shea stearin from Manorama as an ingredient for their own blends. That makes them "frenemies": dependable volume while they lack their own sal supply, but potential rivals if they integrate backward. It also sharpens the concentration risk disclosed in Manorama's FY26 annual report: two unnamed customers took 40.5% of standalone revenue (21.05% and 19.49%).

Maize starch, oilseed and seeds

GAEL claims the largest maize wet-milling plant in India (Sitarganj, 1,950 tonnes a day) and is the largest grinder overall. Its main specialty rival is Roquette of France, which bought the starch business of Riddhi Siddhi Gluco Biols in 2017–18. Sukhjit Starch, Sanstar (listed 2024) and Gulshan Polyols are listed peers; Tirupati Starch, Universal Starch and Bharat Starch are unlisted; Cargill also grinds in India. In oilseed crushing GAEL is a mid-sized player in a market led by AWL Agri, Patanjali Foods, Cargill, Bunge and Emami Agrotech.

Seeds are the most crowded market. In hybrid cotton the leaders are unlisted Rasi Seeds (roughly a quarter of the market, INDICATIVE) and Nuziveedu, with Kaveri, Ankur, Ajeet and Tulsi following. In maize, Kaveri competes with Bayer (Dekalb), Corteva (Pioneer), Syngenta, Advanta (UPL) and Shriram Bioseed; analysts on Kaveri's Q1 FY27 call noted that Bayer and Advanta grew maize seed about 20% while Kaveri fell about 40%. Hybrid rice pits Kaveri against Bayer (Arize), Corteva, Savannah (Sumitomo) and Syngenta, and vegetables are dominated by Syngenta, Seminis (Bayer), East-West, Namdhari and Sakata.

07

Policy whiplash, 2022–26

India's own export bans and floor prices, then the unwinding, then a US tariff saga: in four years policy moved rice prices more than any harvest did. Every member is, in part, a policy-arbitrage business.

The single most important lever on this sector's prices is not the weather or the consumer. It is a notification from the Directorate General of Foreign Trade, the US Trade Representative, the European Commission or India's agriculture ministry. Between September 2022 and March 2025 India restricted, then freed, almost every kind of rice export. Between August 2025 and July 2026 the United States raised, lost in court and replaced its tariffs on Indian goods. Cotton seed has had a government price cap since 2016, edible-oil duties are changed by notification, and the price at which the Food Corporation of India sells rice to distilleries decides how much maize GAEL must compete for.

What the ban cycle did. Non-basmati exports fell from about 17.8 million tonnes in FY23 to about 11 million in FY24, then recovered to roughly 14–15 million in FY25 and 15.01 million in FY26 (the FY23–FY25 figures are approximate). World prices spiked in 2023 and fell back as India returned. The basmati floor price was aimed at fraud, not at basmati, but it cost Indian exporters Gulf business to Pakistan for a year. A useful rule: India's rice export policy is the world rice price. For the two listed basmati houses the direct effect was mixed (KRBL's realisations held up; LT Foods' North American business was unaffected), but the episode taught Gulf buyers that Indian supply can be switched off, and it is one reason Gulf importers hold more stock than before.

The American tariff saga. For LT Foods, which earns half its revenue in North America, US trade policy is now the largest single policy exposure. Paddy, ageing and the Gulf: the basmati cycle in 2025–26

A flood made paddy cheap, a shortage made it dear, and a war cut the shipping lane to three-quarters of the export market. The same twelve months produced a record quarter for one basmati house and a halving of its exports.

Basmati economics run on a simple clock. Paddy is harvested from September to November and bought by millers from October to January. It is then aged, milled and sold over the following 12–24 months. A miller's gross margin in any quarter is therefore the gap between today's selling price and the price of paddy bought one or two seasons ago, less the cost of holding it. When paddy prices rise between the buying season and the selling season, the miller earns a windfall on old stock. When they fall, it sells at a loss against what it paid. The cycle in 2025–26 shows both forces at full strength.

From flood to shortage

Late August and early September 2025 brought severe floods to Punjab. KRBL's chairman told analysts that roughly 2 lakh hectares of crop were destroyed, including about 60,000 acres of basmati, and that Punjab's basmati output fell 20–25% with uneven quality. Counter-intuitively, prices opened weak: 1509 paddy traded at ₹2,500–3,200 a quintal in Punjab in October 2025, because exporters were struggling with Iranian payments and buyers held back. As the shortfall became clear, prices climbed: 1121 paddy reached ₹4,300–4,700 a quintal in Haryana mandis by February 2026, and old-crop paddy had run out by May. The 2026-27 season opened higher still. Reports dated 19 October 2026 put 1509 paddy above ₹4,800 a quintal and 1121 at a record. Management of both houses expects "firm, not soft" prices: about 20% of the basmati area faces groundwater stress, the 2026 monsoon was weak, and KRBL's chairman does not expect this year's crop to exceed last year's.

The company controls the timing and size of its buying and the cost of its money. It does not control the paddy price a year later. That is the bet embedded in every basmati balance sheet.

This is exactly what showed up in KRBL's Q1 FY27 results. Revenue fell 6% to ₹1,496 crore, yet gross profit rose from ₹415 crore to ₹566 crore, a 36.3% margin against 25.7%. The Gulf: payments first, then the war

The second force was West Asia. In January 2026 unrest in Iran and a banking and internet blackout left roughly ₹2,000 crore of Indian rice export payments stuck (a single-source figure, INDICATIVE). Then on 28 February 2026 war broke out in the region and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was restricted. Industry estimates put 4–5 lakh tonnes of Indian basmati stranded at the peak. Container rates from India's west coast to the Gulf rose from about US$500 to as much as US$5,000 (KRBL) and from US$200 to US$4,000 on LT Foods' lanes. Cargo was rerouted through Khor Fakkan, Jeddah and Salalah, adding 7–14 days. The rice exporters' federation advised members to switch Iranian and Gulf contracts from CIF (seller pays freight and insurance) to FOB (buyer pays), and Punjab millers asked the government for a rice-for-crude barter with Iran.

In plain terms

In 2025–26 basmati rice did two opposite things at once. The crop got scarcer, so the rice already in warehouses became more valuable, and KRBL made a record profit by selling it. At the same time the main export route was blocked by war, so less rice could be shipped to the Gulf, and KRBL's export sales halved. Higher prices paid for lower volumes, for now.

The cost of money is the last piece. The new crop will cost more per quintal and the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, so the working capital needed to buy the FY27 crop is larger and dearer. KRBL, with ₹1,841 crore of cash and investments and ICRA AA working-capital lines of ₹1,540 crore, can buy aggressively; its joint managing director said he will buy more paddy this season. LT Foods, already using supplier finance and with only "4–5 days" of further room on it according to its CFO, has less slack. The quarter to watch for the next swing in gross margin is Q4 FY27 and Q1 FY28, when rice milled from paddy bought at ₹4,800 a quintal starts to reach the shelf.

Two basmati houses, two maps Deep dive

LT Foods and KRBL buy the same paddy from the same mandis. One has built a levered global brand house earning half its money in America; the other is a cash-rich Indian brand leader selling to the Gulf. In 2026 their results went in opposite directions for exactly that reason.

Put LT Foods and KRBL side by side and the similarities end at the mill gate. Both started as family rice traders in north India (KRBL's origins go back to 1889; LT Overseas was incorporated around 1990), both built national rice brands (India Gate was launched in 1993), and both buy basmati paddy in the October–January season and age it. From there their strategies diverge on almost every axis that matters: geography, leverage, product range, growth rate and margin volatility. The 2026 shocks, US tariffs on one side and the Gulf war on the other, hit them in mirror image.

LT Foods: the global brand house

LT Foods has turned an Indian basmati miller into a branded rice company that happens to be headquartered in India. Royal holds more than 60% of North American basmati imports; Golden Star, the leading US jasmine brand, became a wholly owned subsidiary in May 2025 when LT Foods paid US$15 million (₹128.5 crore) for the 49% it did not already own, having bought 51% in June 2022 for US$8.16 million. In FY26 Golden Star contributed ₹494.7 crore of revenue and ₹62.3 crore of profit, about a tenth of group profit, which makes the buy-out look cheap. Daawat is the number-two brand in India and is pushing into the UK (a £100 million revenue target over four years, served from a fully operational plant at Harlow). An organic business, Nature Bio-Foods, supplies European food makers and is now moving from wholesale to supermarket brands through a new plant in Rotterdam. A ready-to-heat range (₹187 crore in FY26) adds a US facility from Q2 FY27. Saudi Arabia's SALIC owns 9.22% and has a board seat, which gives LT Foods a partner in the Gulf's largest market (₹17 crore of Saudi revenue in Q1 FY27, from a small base).

The cost of that breadth is margin. LT Foods' operating margin has sat at 10–12% for five years. Q1 FY27 revenue rose 28% (19% "normalised" for Golden Star and the pass-through of US tariffs), but EBITDA margin slipped from 12.1% to 11.5% and PAT grew only 9% to ₹183 crore. Three things dragged: organic EBITDA fell from 10% to 4% as the business switched from bulk sales to retail packs (management guides to 7–8% by the end of FY27 and double digits in FY28); the ready-to-heat range still loses money (break-even expected at about ₹400 crore of revenue); and freight to the Middle East rose twentyfold on some lanes without being passed on. Interest plus depreciation rose from ₹239 crore in FY24 to ₹376 crore in FY26 as the company leased plants abroad, consolidated Golden Star's intangibles and used supplier finance to fund paddy. The basmati segment itself still earns about 13–14% EBITDA, and management says paddy inflation moves that by about a point either way.

KRBL: the domestic fortress with a Gulf window

KRBL owns India's leading rice brand and the world's largest basmati milling complex at Dhuri in Punjab (207 tonnes an hour in total across its units), plus 150.7 MW of wind, solar and husk-fired power. India Gate leads in kirana stores, supermarkets and e-commerce. KRBL has stayed narrow (basmati, regional rice of about ₹270 crore, a small masala range) and kept a net cash balance sheet: net bank cash of ₹789 crore at March 2026 and cash plus investments of ₹1,841 crore at June 2026. That makes its earnings a direct, unhedged play on the paddy cycle. Its operating margin has ranged from 12% to 21% in the last eight quarters while revenue barely moved from ₹1,480–1,580 crore a quarter.

KRBL's weaknesses are equally clear. It is losing a little share in general trade (down about two points year on year) and admits that modern trade "needs improvement". Its export business depends on the Gulf and on Saudi distribution, where it says choosing "a wrong partner" set it back about eighteen months. And it carries a legal overhang that is widely misunderstood. The 6.26% of KRBL shown as "Government" in the shareholding pattern is not a government investment. It is 1,43,33,221 shares owned by the Balsharaf Group, a KRBL customer in the Gulf, which the Enforcement Directorate attached under a money-laundering complaint filed in 2021 in connection with the AgustaWestland helicopter case. The complaint names KRBL, its Dubai subsidiary and a joint managing director, and alleges that US$24.62 million of proceeds reached KRBL through Gulf trading firms; Balsharaf says by affidavit that the money was its own. A separate attachment covers land and buildings at Dhuri worth ₹15.32 crore. Hearings took place in September 2026 and management declines to comment. The block is a free-float overhang, not a sign of state support.

What the valuation gap asks

The market pays about 20.6 times trailing earnings for LT Foods and 10.6 times for KRBL. On EV to FY26 operating profit, using screener's borrowings-less-investments net debt for both, the gap is 12.7 times against 8.1 times; on company net debt and cash it would be wider still. The market is paying for LT Foods' growth and its global brand footprint, and discounting KRBL for its legal overhang, its Gulf concentration and the volatility of its margin. Two questions decide whether that gap is right. First, can LT Foods turn its scale into margin as organic and ready-to-heat mature, or will tariffs, freight and leases keep it at 11%? Second, can KRBL hold a 17–18% EBITDA margin through a full paddy cycle and put its cash to use, or is Q1 FY27 a one-off peak? KRBL's own guidance for FY27, a gross margin of about 30% and EBITDA of 17–18%, implies the remaining three quarters will run at roughly 15–16%, closer to FY26 than to Q1.

In plain terms

The market treats LT Foods as a growing consumer brand and KRBL as a cyclical commodity miller with legal baggage, even though KRBL earns slightly more profit. Which view is right depends on whether KRBL's margins hold up when it has to buy this year's expensive paddy, and whether LT Foods can make its global expansion pay better than 11 paise in the rupee.

Manorama and the cocoa cycle Deep dive

A forest-seed fat maker grew revenue fivefold in three years as cocoa tripled. Cocoa has since crashed and partly rebounded, but Manorama keeps growing. The question is how much of its 43× multiple rests on a price that has already turned.

Manorama Industries makes fat for chocolate from seeds that fall on the floor of central India's sal forests and from shea nuts gathered in West Africa. It was incorporated in 2005, listed on the BSE SME platform in 2018 and later moved to the main board and to the NSE. For most of its life it was a ₹250–450 crore niche business. Then cocoa prices exploded. Revenue went from ₹457 crore in FY24 to ₹771 crore in FY25 and ₹1,367 crore in FY26 (consolidated; ₹1,358 crore standalone), with an operating margin of 26%. Its market value of ₹11,145 crore is now larger than KRBL's, which earns nearly three times its profit.

How sal becomes chocolate fat

Cocoa butter is solid at room temperature and melts at body temperature because most of its fat molecules carry a particular arrangement of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids (known in the trade as POP, POS and SOS). Sal, shea, illipe, kokum and mango kernel fats contain the same molecules, but mixed with others. Manorama extracts the crude butter from the seed (90,000 tonnes a year of seed milling and solvent extraction), refines it (45,000 tonnes), and then fractionates it (47,500 tonnes a year) to separate the hard stearin, which behaves like cocoa butter, from the liquid olein. Stearin is blended to each customer's recipe into a cocoa butter equivalent or a cocoa butter improver. European and Indian rules allow up to 5% of a chocolate bar to be such vegetable fat; compound coatings, fillings and tropical chocolates use much more. Manorama is now also building an enzymatic CBE plant that will use interesterification to turn its surplus olein into hard fat.

The economics rest on two advantages. The raw material is cheap: wild sal seed is collected by tribal households through a network of some 18,000 villages and bought at prices tied to state minor-forest-produce support levels, and only about 5% of the available sal is thought to be collected. And the product is sticky: a chocolate maker takes three to four years to qualify a new CBE supplier and rarely changes a working recipe. That is why Manorama's gross margin stayed between 43% and 47% across the cycle, and why its EBITDA per kilo, at an estimated ₹84 against about ₹19–20 for AAK on its EBIT basis (both INDICATIVE), is roughly four times the global leader's.

The ethanol programme cuts both ways

Ethanol made maize scarce and then made it abundant. India's 20% blending target, reached in 2025, required more grain than sugar could supply, and the government steered distillers to maize and to surplus FCI rice. In the 2025-26 supply year, maize supplied 413 crore litres of the 987 crore litres delivered by end-September (41.8%) against an allocation of 614 crore litres; FCI rice supplied 222 crore litres. At roughly 380–400 litres a tonne, that is about 10–11 million tonnes of maize (an estimate), roughly a fifth of the crop. Farmers responded by planting much more: output jumped 27% to 55.09 million tonnes. That supply response is what brought GAEL's input cost down.

The danger is that the swing is reversing. Kaveri's management said maize rose from about ₹16–17 a kilo at the start of the 2026 season to ₹27–28 by mid-August, as a weak monsoon cut kharif sowing (maize area 89.51 lakh hectares at 21 August against 93.30 a year earlier) and El Niño threatened yields. The FAO maize price index rose 5.6% in September 2026, partly because the Hormuz disruption raised the value of biofuel feedstocks. If maize stays near those levels, the spread GAEL earned in Q1 FY27 will narrow in H2 FY27. The government's release valve is FCI rice: distillers can buy it at ₹2,320 a quintal (₹2,390 from 1 November 2026), far below its ₹4,100 economic cost, and every litre made from rice is a litre not made from maize. A policy shift that pushed distillers back to maize, or a fall in FCI rice sales, would tighten the market for GAEL. A US trade deal that allowed imports of non-GM American maize or distillers' grains would loosen it.

In plain terms

GAEL buys corn and sells what comes out of it. The company is trying to escape the swing by turning more of its corn into specialty products whose prices do not follow corn, but that will take two or three more years.

It also lost share in Madhya Pradesh, where maize area rose but Kaveri "did not have a product", and in Maharashtra and Bihar. Analysts on the call pointed out that Bayer and Advanta grew maize seed about 20% while Kaveri's fell about 40%; management attributed the gap to geography. Illegal herbicide-tolerant cotton rose again because a delayed monsoon leads farmers to "compromise on the seed", with Gujarat worst hit.

The cash machine has slowed

Kaveri used to be described as a cash machine, and its record of buybacks supports that: ₹125.65 crore in 2022 and ₹325 crore in 2024 (a tender at ₹725 a share). FY26 broke the pattern. Operating cash flow was −₹6 crore against ₹197 crore in FY25; working-capital days rose from 99 to 171; investments fell from ₹613 crore to ₹401 crore (consolidated; ₹454 crore standalone). The reason became clear on the Q1 FY27 call: Kaveri produced seed for a good season, so inventory reached about ₹1,200 crore, some ₹200 crore higher than a year earlier, and it will cut production next year. Cash on the books was ₹267 crore at June 2026, guided to about ₹300 crore by September. Contingent liabilities also need watching: "legal claims" jumped fivefold to ₹96.6 crore in FY26 without explanation in the notes, while the tax tribunal has repeatedly upheld Kaveri's agricultural-income exemption, setting aside about ₹197 crore of demands across three assessment years.

In plain terms

Kaveri sells almost all its seed in about ten weeks before the monsoon. If the rain is late, farmers sow less or switch crops and the year is lost. In 2026 the rain was late and Kaveri's maize seed sales fell by more than 40% in the quarter. It has also built up a large pile of unsold seed, so less cash came in than usual. The company is cheap at 15 times earnings and near its 52-week low, but it has not grown profit in a decade.

Two features make Kaveri's results easy to misread. Consolidated revenue in the season quarter is lower than standalone (₹742 crore against ₹815 crore in Q1 FY27), because subsidiaries that sell their own cotton hybrids (10–12 lakh packets) buy seed from the parent and those sales are eliminated. And the share of PAT in Q1 is so high that any annual estimate is mostly a forecast of one quarter. The Q2 FY27 result (board 28 October, with an interim dividend; call 29 October) should be small; the useful information will be in the commentary on rabi demand, inventory and any buyback.

Four decades of shocks

From the first branded basmati in the 1990s to the 2026 war in West Asia, the sector's history is a sequence of policy and price shocks. Each one rewarded the balance sheets that could wait and punished those that could not.

The history of this group is short in corporate terms (four of the five were incorporated between 1986 and 2005) but long in shocks. Reading it as a sequence helps calibrate how often the bad years arrive and how long they last. Basmati has had a major price or policy shock roughly every three to four years; cocoa had the most extreme cycle in its traded history in 2024–26; maize was transformed by the ethanol programme in five years; and Bt cotton seed has been under price control for a decade.

Three patterns stand out. First, policy shocks cluster around India's own food-security scares: the 2008, 2022 and 2023 export restrictions all followed weak monsoons or global price spikes, and all were unwound within one to two years once stocks rebuilt. With government rice stocks about three times the norm in 2026, that trigger is absent for now. Second, the Gulf payment problem recurs: Iranian payments froze in 2012–13, after 2019 and again in 2026. Each time, Indian millers who had extended credit to Iranian buyers took losses, and the industry learned to shift to FOB terms and letters of credit. Third, every upswing in a member's commodity eventually brought new supply: the 1121 variety in the 2000s, the maize planting boom of 2025-26, the recovery in West African cocoa output. The companies that came through best were those with balance sheets that could hold inventory through a down-cycle without forced selling.

For investors the history also shows how slowly the market's view of each company changes. KRBL was valued as a cyclical miller through the 2014–17 crash and has never fully shed that label, even after the 2023 and 2026 profit peaks. LT Foods earned a consumer-brand multiple only after its US business proved itself over a decade. Manorama went from an SME listing to a large-cap valuation in under eight years on the back of one commodity cycle. GAEL's valuation has followed its margin up and down with a lag of about a year. Kaveri's rating fell as cotton shrank, and it has not recovered despite the mix shift.

In plain terms

Every few years something knocks these businesses off course: a ban, a sanction, a flood, a war, a crop failure, a price spike that turns into a crash. The ones that survive best are the ones that do not need to sell in a hurry, because they have cash or cheap credit. That is why the balance sheet matters as much as the brand in this sector.

Twelve measures, defined, worked through in rupees, benchmarked and compared across the five. Most of them mean something different in each archetype, and several are reported on two or three bases by the same company. This is the section to keep open while reading a results release.

In plain terms

A crop processor makes money in three places: selling more tonnes, getting a better price per kilo than the crop cost, and not paying too much to hold the crop while it waits. The first two show up in the income statement; the third hides in the balance sheet and the interest line.

1. Volume: tonnes sold

What it is. The quantity of product sold in a period, in tonnes (rice, fat, starch) or in packets and kilos (seed). Formula. Tonnes this period ÷ tonnes in the same period last year − 1. Worked example. LT Foods sold 2.26 lakh tonnes in Q1 FY27, 1.09 lakh in India and 1.17 lakh abroad; its basmati volume grew 11% while basmati revenue grew 34%, so roughly two-thirds of the revenue growth came from price, mix and tariff pass-through rather than tonnes. What moves it. Distribution reach, brand pull, access to export lanes (the Gulf war cut India's Q1 FY27 basmati exports from about 1.7 to 1.5 million tonnes), the size of last season's purchases and, for Manorama and GAEL, plant capacity. Benchmarks. For a branded basmati house, volume growth above 10% a year is good, 5–10% average, below 5% weak; KRBL has committed to about 10% domestic volume growth a year for two to three years.

How the five compare. Disclosure is patchy. LT Foods gives tonnes and growth. KRBL gives realisation growth but not tonnes (its Q1 FY27 domestic volume was about +3%, inferred from +14% revenue and +11% realisation). Manorama gives utilisation (about 80% of 47,500 tonnes a year) but not tonnes sold, and says about 85% of its growth is volume. GAEL gives segment revenue but neither tonnes nor utilisation (only "83% capacity availability"). Kaveri gives crop revenue and occasional volumes (maize seed volume −39% in Q1 FY27; Karnataka maize seed about 1,500 tonnes against 2,600). Definition trap. "Volume growth" sometimes means branded volume only (LT Foods' +10%) and sometimes all basmati (+11%); and when a company acquires a business, as LT Foods did with Golden Star, reported volume growth includes the acquisition unless it says "like-for-like".

2. Realisation per kilo

What it is. Average net revenue per unit sold. Formula. Segment revenue ÷ tonnes sold. Worked example. LT Foods' basmati and specialty rice revenue of ₹2,845 crore in Q1 FY27 divided by 2.26 lakh tonnes gives about ₹126 a kilo, assuming the tonnage refers to the rice segment (INDICATIVE). Manorama's FY26 revenue of ₹1,367 crore over an estimated 42,000–45,000 tonnes gives roughly ₹300–330 a kilo (INDICATIVE). What moves it. The crop price (a dearer paddy market lifts the whole shelf), brand premium, channel and geography mix (US retail realises more than Indian kirana), the rupee (a move from ₹83 to ₹95 to the dollar adds about 14% to export realisations at unchanged dollar prices), tariffs passed through, and for CBE the cocoa price. India's basmati exports realised about ₹76,900 a tonne in FY26, 7–8% less than FY25, then about 20% more year on year in Q1 FY27.

3. Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after the cost of goods sold (the crop, packaging and direct processing). Formula. (Revenue − cost of goods sold) ÷ revenue. Worked example. KRBL's Q1 FY27: revenue ₹1,496 crore, cost of goods ₹995 crore, gross profit ₹566 crore including other income in the company's presentation, a 36.3% margin; on revenue alone, excluding other income, the CFO gave 33.51% against 24.15% a year earlier. What moves it. For basmati, the gap between the price of the paddy in stock and today's selling price (the carry); for CBE, raw-material cost and by-product realisations; for seeds, production cost per kilo against price. Benchmarks. Basmati above 32% is good, 25–32% average, below 25% weak. CBE above 42% good, 35–42% average. Seeds roughly 50–55% (INDICATIVE; Kaveri does not disclose).

How the five compare. KRBL 28.3% for FY26 and 36.3% in Q1 FY27, guided back to about 30% for FY27. LT Foods 32.6% in Q1 FY27 against 34.7%, a fall management attributes to a change in shipping terms with its US subsidiary and to organic. Manorama 46.3% for FY26 and 43.6% in Q1 FY27, below its 45–50% guide. GAEL and Kaveri do not report gross margin; GAEL's maize segment result (7.0% FY26, 17.6% Q1 FY27) is the nearest substitute. Definition trap. KRBL's headline gross margin includes other income, which in Q1 FY27 was ₹64 crore, including investment gains and foreign exchange. Always strip other income. And LT Foods' switch from CIF to C&I terms with its own US arm moved freight between "cost of goods" and "other expenses", lowering gross margin without changing EBITDA.

In plain terms

Gross margin in rice is mostly a measure of luck and timing in last year's buying, not of efficiency. A ten-point jump in a quarter means the company sold cheap stock into a dear market. It will not repeat when the stock has to be replaced at today's prices.

4. The paddy carry and inventory days

What it is. How long the company holds its stock, and what it costs. Formula. Inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365 (screener's basis), or inventory ÷ revenue × 365 (the basis LT Foods and KRBL use in their decks). Carry cost = average inventory × interest rate × share debt-funded, plus storage and shrinkage. Worked example. LT Foods' company figure is 187 days at June 2026 (221 a year earlier) on revenue; screener's FY26 figure on cost of goods is 256 days. KRBL's deck shows about 188 days on revenue; screener shows 329. For carry: ₹700 crore of rice inventory, 60% funded by debt at 9%, costs about ₹38 crore a year, before storage. What moves it. How much paddy the company chooses to buy in the season, how long it ages it, export disruptions that leave stock unsold, and in seeds, production planned for a season that does not come. Benchmarks. Basmati on the company basis: below 190 days good, 190–260 average, above 260 weak. Seed below 450 days good, above 550 weak.

6. Export share and destination mix

Shipping lanes and payments (Hormuz, Iran), tariffs (US Section 301), residue rules (EU), the rupee, and the company's own distribution build-out. Benchmarks. No single good level; the test is concentration. A destination taking more than half of exports is a risk unless it is a stable, high-income market.

Definition trap. Nielsen's universe and channel definitions change, and the two companies quote different cuts (by channel vs all-India, Nielsen vs internal e-commerce data). In plain terms

Market share numbers in Indian rice come from shop audits that change their methods. Use them to see which way a brand is moving, not to argue over a point or two between companies.

8. The CBE spread against cocoa butter

What it is. The economic incentive for a chocolate maker to use a cocoa butter equivalent, and the margin room it gives the CBE maker. Formula. CBE spread = cocoa butter price − CBE price; Manorama's margin = CBE price − (seed cost ÷ fat yield) − processing. Worked example. At US$5,500 a tonne for beans and a butter ratio of about 2.2, cocoa butter is about US$12,000 a tonne, roughly ₹1,140 a kilo; CBE at a 30–50% discount is ₹570–800 a kilo (ILLUSTRATIVE). At US$3,000 beans the saving per kilo narrows by roughly ₹250–350. What moves it. Cocoa prices and the butter ratio; sal and shea crop and collection costs; competition from palm-based CBEs; customer qualification. Benchmarks. CBE EBITDA margin above 22% good, 15–22% average. Gross margin above 42% good. Manorama: 26.4% and 46.3% in FY26.

How to track it without disclosure. Manorama does not publish realisation per tonne. The practical proxies are its gross margin against the 45–50% guide, revenue per tonne of fractionation capacity (which rose 64% from FY22 to FY24, before new capacity, as cocoa lifted prices), and commentary on contract renewals, which run 9–12 months on a rolling basis. Definition trap. "Stearin and CBE" (71.4% of FY26 revenue) is a product bucket, not CBE alone: the company's investor-relations head said CBE was "30%", without clarifying whether of revenue or of the bucket.

9. Maize segment margin and grind utilisation

What it is. The profit GAEL earns on each rupee of maize-derived sales, and how fully its mills run. Formula. Segment result ÷ segment revenue; utilisation = tonnes ground ÷ (capacity in tonnes a day × operating days). Worked example. Q1 FY27: ₹190.41 crore ÷ ₹1,084.75 crore = 17.6%. A 5,600-tonne-a-day mill running 90 days at 85% grinds about 4.3 lakh tonnes a quarter (ILLUSTRATIVE). What moves it. The maize price against product prices (Section 11), the mix of value-added products, plant availability (83% in FY26) and start-up costs of new lines. Benchmarks. Maize segment margin above 12% good, 7–12% average, below 7% weak; utilisation above 85% good. Definition trap. Segment result is before unallocated income and costs and tax, so the segment margins of GAEL cannot be compared with another company's EBITDA margin. GAEL does not disclose utilisation, only "capacity availability", which measures uptime, not load.

How to use it. Because GAEL holds no earnings call, the segment table in its quarterly deck is the only regular window on the spread. Read the maize margin against three reference points: the FY26 average of 7.0%, the Q1 FY27 peak of 17.6%, and the direction of maize prices in the quarter (they rose from about ₹16–17 to ₹27–28 a kilo between the start of the 2026 season and mid-August, by Kaveri's account). A margin that holds above 12% while maize rises would be the first evidence that the value-added mix, not just cheap grain, is doing the work. A fall back towards 7–8% would say the opposite. Track maize revenue growth as a rough volume proxy too: it rose 36% in Q1 FY27, helped by new capacity at Sitarganj and Hubli.

10. Seed mix: cotton versus non-cotton, and new-hybrid share

What it is. The share of seed revenue from price-capped cotton versus freely priced crops, and the share from recently launched hybrids. Formula. Non-cotton revenue ÷ total seed revenue; new-product share = revenue from hybrids launched in the last three years ÷ crop revenue. Worked example. Kaveri FY26 standalone: non-cotton ₹1,060 crore ÷ ₹1,304 crore = 81.3%. New cotton hybrids were 30% of FY26 cotton sales and 37% in Q1 FY27. What moves it. Breeding success, the cotton price cap and illegal seed, farmers' crop choices after a bad season, and launches. Benchmarks. Non-cotton above 70% good; new-product share above 35% good, 20–35% average. Definition trap. Kaveri's "new product" means launched in the last three years, and the share is quoted per crop, not for the company; the 62% figure for new hybrid-rice launches is their share of the new-product bucket, not of hybrid rice, where new products are under 20–25%. Also, crop detail is standalone only; consolidated revenue is lower in the season quarter.

How to use it. Mix tells you about pricing power; new-product share tells you whether the pipeline can defend it. In FY26 Kaveri's non-cotton revenue grew about 23% (₹861 crore to ₹1,060 crore) while cotton fell 6.6%, and maize alone grew 40% on 19% more volume, so price and mix contributed as much as tonnes. In Q1 FY27 the mix fell back to 74% non-cotton because the monsoon hit maize hardest. The warning sign would be a non-cotton share that rises only because cotton shrinks, with non-cotton revenue itself flat. R&D spending (7.18% of turnover) is the lead indicator for new-product share two to four years out.

11. Net debt and the funding of the carry

What it is. Borrowings less cash and liquid investments, and its size relative to EBITDA. Formula. (Borrowings − cash − liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example. LT Foods at March 2026 on its auditor's note: borrowings including leases ₹1,610 crore less cash ₹233 crore = ₹1,377 crore; the company's own KPI, excluding leases and netting other liquid assets, was 0.6× EBITDA (0.48× by June 2026). KRBL: net bank cash ₹789 crore at March 2026; cash and investments ₹1,841 crore at June. What moves it. The size and price of the crop bought, capex, acquisitions and equity raises. Benchmarks. For basmati, below 0.5× or net cash good, 0.5–1.5× average, above 2× weak. Definition trap. Screener's net debt (borrowings − investments) ignores bank cash, so it understates cash-rich companies: on that basis KRBL shows only ₹690 crore of net cash. Manorama's ₹356 crore of March 2026 debt predates its ₹500 crore QIP. State the basis every time.

How the five compare. On screener's basis (March 2026 borrowings less investments) the five show net debt of ₹1,562 crore at LT Foods and ₹356 crore at Manorama, and net cash of ₹690 crore at KRBL, ₹620 crore at GAEL and ₹401 crore at Kaveri, a combined net debt of only ₹207 crore. On company and adjusted bases the group is comfortably net cash: KRBL alone has ₹1,841 crore of cash and investments, Manorama's QIP more than covers its March debt, and GAEL's annual report shows ₹1,255.5 crore of financial investments against ₹433.6 crore of borrowings. Only LT Foods uses real leverage, and it uses it to fund the carry for a business twice KRBL's size. The rising repo rate therefore matters most to LT Foods and, once its plants need working capital, to Manorama.

12. Returns: ROCE and ROE

What it is. Operating profit earned on all the capital in the business (ROCE) and net profit on shareholders' equity (ROE). Formula. EBIT ÷ (equity + net debt) for ROCE; PAT ÷ average equity for ROE. Worked example. Manorama's FY26: screener ROCE 34.9% and ROE 39.3%; the company's own ROCE is 46.2%. KRBL: ROCE 15.2%, ROE 11.7%, because about a third of its equity sits in cash and investments earning treasury returns. What moves it. Margin, asset turnover (Manorama claims 7.4 times fixed assets), working-capital intensity and idle cash. Benchmarks. Above 20% good, 12–20% average, below 12% weak.

How to read a crop processor's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue, split by stream (1 minute). Domestic vs export (KRBL), segment and geography (LT Foods), maize vs agro (GAEL), cotton vs non-cotton and crop by crop (Kaveri). Ask whether growth came from tonnes or price. Red flag: revenue growth driven entirely by price in a year when the crop got dearer.
  2. Volume or utilisation (1 minute). LT Foods' tonnes and basmati volume growth; Manorama's utilisation against 80% of capacity; Kaveri's crop volumes. Red flag: volume falling while the company talks about share gains.
  3. Gross margin, ex other income (1 minute). Compare with the same quarter last year and with guidance (KRBL ~30% for FY27; Manorama 45–50%). Red flag: a large jump that management itself says is "not sustainable".
  4. Other income (30 seconds). Strip out forex, investment gains and one-offs (KRBL ₹64 crore and Manorama ₹16 crore in Q1 FY27). Watch for LT Foods booking the US tariff refund.
  5. EBITDA on one basis (1 minute). Use revenue from operations and exclude other income, so the five are comparable.
  6. Inventory, in tonnes and rupees (1 minute). For the rice houses: tonnes held and the implied ₹/kg against today's paddy price. For Kaveri: inventory against last year. Red flag: rising inventory with falling sales.
  7. Debt, cash and interest (1 minute). Net debt on a stated basis; interest cost per quarter; any new supplier-finance arrangement. Red flag: interest rising faster than revenue.
  8. Segment and geography detail (1 minute). LT Foods' organic and ready-to-heat margins; GAEL's maize segment margin; Manorama's export share; KRBL's exports outside the Middle East.
  9. Policy and macro in the commentary (1 minute). Tariff rate paid, freight on Gulf lanes, paddy and maize prices, monsoon, cocoa. Red flag: guidance that assumes last quarter's commodity price will hold.
  10. Guidance and one-offs (1 minute). Margin guidance (KRBL 17–18% EBITDA; LT Foods organic 7–8% by FY27 exit), capex (Manorama ₹225–250 crore), capacity dates, and items like Manorama's ₹20.64 crore customs payment in Q2 FY27. Note whether guidance was raised, held or cut.

Run through those ten steps for each archetype and the quarter usually explains itself. The biggest single misreading in this group is to treat a carry-driven or spread-driven quarter as a new run-rate: KRBL's 36% gross margin and GAEL's 17.6% maize margin in Q1 FY27 are both, by management's own account, above what the rest of the year will deliver.

The second most common error is comparing numbers across archetypes as if they were the same thing. A 600-day inventory is normal for a seed company and alarming for a starch maker; a 26% margin is excellent for a fat refiner and ordinary for a seed breeder in its season quarter; a 10% US duty is a headline for LT Foods and irrelevant to Kaveri. Read each company against its own history first, against its archetype second, and against the group only on return on capital and cash conversion.

Where India earns more. Manorama's margin is roughly two and a half times AAK's, and its EBITDA per kilo about four times (INDICATIVE). The gap comes from raw material: wild sal and West African shea bought through its own collection network, against the palm and shea that AAK buys on world markets, plus the cocoa windfall of 2024–26. Kaveri's 24% beats Corteva and KWS because Indian seed income from agricultural operations is largely tax-exempt and its overheads are lower, but its scale is tiny and its research budget a third of KWS's as a share of sales. Where India earns less. GAEL's FY26 margin of 8% is less than half Ingredion's, because its mix is weighted to native starch and crushing and because Indian maize was expensive relative to products for most of the year; its Q1 FY27 margin of 15% shows how much of the gap is cyclical. Where India is level. The basmati houses earn margins close to Ebro's, which owns Tilda, and several times what grain traders earn, which is the value of their brands.

Valuation. Manorama trades at about 43 times earnings against roughly 22–26 times for AAK, a premium for faster growth and higher margins but on a far narrower base and with heavier customer concentration. LT Foods' 20.6 times is above Ebro's 13–15 times, reflecting faster growth. KRBL's 10.6 times is close to where global grain traders trade, despite its brand leadership: the market is treating it as a cyclical commodity business with a legal overhang. GAEL's 16.8 times trailing is a little above Ingredion's 13 times, on the hope that fermentation lifts its mix. Kaveri's 15 times is in line with KWS.

The rupee. At about ₹94.9 to the dollar in October 2026 (a record low near ₹96.8 earlier), the rupee has added roughly 14% to the rupee value of dollar sales since FY24 at unchanged dollar prices. That helps the exporters, LT Foods (71% international) and Manorama (about 60% exports), and inflates their reported growth. It also raises the rupee cost of imported shea, palm fractions and freight. A reversal would work the other way.

By world standards, Manorama and Kaveri are unusually profitable, GAEL is unusually unprofitable outside good quarters, and the two rice brands earn about what a good rice company earns anywhere. The market prices Manorama richly, LT Foods like a growing brand, and KRBL like a commodity trader.

Technology and disruption

Lab-grown cocoa fat, gene-edited rice, herbicide-tolerant cotton, quick commerce and new ethanol feedstocks: the changes that could reset each archetype's economics over the next five to ten years.

None of the five is a technology company, but each faces a technological shift that could change its margin structure. Most of these are slow-moving; one or two could arrive suddenly through a regulatory decision.

Cocoa-free and cultured cocoa fat. Start-ups such as California Cultured and Celleste Bio are growing cocoa cells to produce cocoa butter, and others such as Planet A Foods (ChoViva), WNWN and Voyage Foods make "cocoa-free" chocolate from oats, sunflower seeds or other inputs. Their scale today is tiny. But they target exactly the cost-inflation demand that Manorama rides, and if cocoa prices stay high for years they will attract capital. A second, nearer competitor is enzymatic interesterification: AAK, Bunge and others can restructure palm and shea fats into cocoa-butter-like fats at scale. Manorama's own enzymatic CBE plant is a defensive move in the same direction.

Gene editing and new traits in seeds. India exempted simple genome edits (SDN-1 and SDN-2) from its genetically-modified rules in March 2022, and in May 2025 ICAR released two gene-edited rice varieties (DRR Dhan 100 Kamala and Pusa DST Rice 1). Public-sector gene-edited varieties could compete with private hybrids in rice. In cotton, the approval of herbicide-tolerant Bt (HTBt) would reset the market overnight: legal seed with the trait would command a premium and displace the illegal seed that now takes an estimated 15–25% of the market in some states (INDICATIVE). The environmental release of GM mustard (DMH-11) has been before the Supreme Court since 2022. Kaveri's R&D spend of 7.18% of turnover is its hedge.

New basmati varieties and direct seeding. IARI's short-duration and disease-resistant varieties (1509, 1692, 1847, 1885, 1886) change the paddy supply curve and reduce pesticide residues, which matters for the EU. Direct-seeded rice cuts water use. Both could raise supply in good years and make the paddy cycle shallower.

Quick commerce and direct-to-consumer. India Gate's e-commerce share of about 41% and LT Foods' 40%-plus show that the leading brands win online. Platforms take high trade margins, but they also let brands reach households faster and sell premium and regional variants (KRBL says quick-commerce platforms ask it for regional rice). The risk is private label: platform-owned rice brands could undercut on price.

Ethanol feedstocks. Second-generation ethanol from crop residues and a shift in the mix between cane and grain would change maize demand. More FCI rice to distillers or a return to cane-heavy ethanol would loosen maize; a higher blending target (E27 or E30 has been discussed) would tighten it.

Bull vs bear

The case for and against the group, archetype by archetype. Most of the bull case is already visible in Q1 FY27; most of the bear case is in what management guided for the rest of the year.

Because the five companies have different drivers, the bull and bear cases are best read as a set of arguments that apply to some members more than others. The columns below list the strongest points on each side, followed by a short note on which members each point touches.

The bull case

  • Cheap stock, dear market. Both basmati houses hold rice and paddy bought well below replacement cost (LT Foods ₹56/kg rice; KRBL ~₹64/kg), so FY27 gross margins should stay above FY26's even as they normalise from Q1's peak.
  • Packaged basmati headroom. Only 20.4% of urban households buy packaged basmati against 43.4% who eat basmati; the two leaders hold about 60% of the packaged shelf and win online.
  • Policy has stabilised at home. With government rice stocks about three times the norm, a return of export bans is unlikely, and US tariffs are back to 10% with refunds owed.
  • Gulf demand is intact. The 2026 shock was logistics, not demand; Hormuz transit resumed by August and Gulf shelf prices were reset upward.
  • Manorama's moat. Cheap forest seed, sticky qualified recipes and a doubling of capacity by FY28, funded by equity.
  • GAEL's spread and fermentation. A 55 mt maize crop, cheap FCI rice for distillers, and a fourfold sodium-gluconate expansion.
  • Balance sheets. Three members are net cash and a fourth is close; only LT Foods uses real leverage.
  • Valuation outside Manorama. The other four trade at about 15.5 times trailing earnings.

The bear case

  • Q1 was a peak. KRBL guides its gross margin down to ~30% from 36%; GAEL's maize spread was struck on cheap grain that has since risen to ₹27–28/kg.
  • Expensive new crop, dearer money. 1509 paddy above ₹4,800/qtl and a 5.50% repo raise the cost of the FY27 buy and squeeze FY28 margins.
  • US policy risk. The 10% Section 301 duty is in court and a rice-specific investigation has been requested; half of LT Foods' revenue is in North America.
  • Cocoa has turned. Manorama's gross margin is already below guide, two customers are 40.5% of revenue, and its 43× multiple assumes FY29 capacity is filled at today's prices.
  • Gulf concentration. 71% of basmati exports go to one region with recurring payment and shipping crises.
  • Kaveri's stalled engine. Profit flat for a decade, negative operating cash flow in FY26, ₹1,200 Cr of inventory and illegal cotton seed spreading.
  • Governance and legal overhangs. The ED case naming KRBL; family remuneration and related-party purchases at LT Foods; rising contingent liabilities at Kaveri.
  • Crush and seed policy. Lower edible-oil duty, a shrinking soy crop and a frozen Bt cotton price.

Who each argument touches. The inventory-carry bull point and the expensive-new-crop bear point are two sides of the same coin for LT Foods and KRBL: FY27 benefits, FY28 pays. The packaged-headroom argument favours KRBL (the leader) and LT Foods (the fastest online grower) about equally. US policy is almost entirely an LT Foods question, Gulf concentration a KRBL one. Manorama's case is binary on the cocoa cycle and execution: its moat is real, but its valuation leaves little to spare if growth slows. GAEL's case hinges on the maize price over the next two quarters, Kaveri's on the 2027 monsoon and on whether it can show growth rather than just mix change.

What would change the balance. For the bulls, the most important near-term confirmation would be KRBL holding an EBITDA margin near its 17–18% guide through Q2–Q4 FY27 while exports recover, and GAEL holding a maize margin above 12% as maize rises. For the bears, the clearest warning would be Manorama's gross margin falling further below 43% with slower volume growth, or LT Foods' margin failing to recover as organic remodelling ends. The Q2 FY27 results arriving between 24 October and mid-November will provide the first test of each.

In plain terms

The good news for this group is already in the numbers: cheap stock sold at high prices, cheap corn, a weaker rupee and a chocolate-fat boom. The bad news is what comes next: dearer paddy and corn, a cocoa price that has halved from its peak, a tariff case in American courts and a failed monsoon. The next three quarters will show which side is stronger.
