Indian travel services: a toll, two marketplaces, a forex desk

00

The one-page view: four businesses that share a label

A state-granted toll, a global wholesale marketplace, a mass-market booking app and a currency-and-resorts group are filed together as "travel services". Almost nothing else about them is shared.

The four companies in this report all make money when Indians travel, and that is close to where the resemblance ends. Indian Railway Catering and Tourism Corporation (IRCTC) is the only platform allowed to sell Indian Railways' reserved tickets online, and it also runs on-board catering, the Rail Neer bottled-water business and rail tourism. TBO Tek is a business-to-business marketplace that sells hotel rooms and airline seats to about 33,700 travel agents a month in more than 100 countries, and since October 2025 it also owns a US luxury wholesaler, Classic Vacations. Le Travenues Technology, which trades as ixigo, is a consumer booking app that grew out of trains and buses into flights and now hotels. Thomas Cook (India), 64.77% owned by Fairfax, sells outbound holidays and corporate travel, runs India's largest non-bank foreign-exchange business, and owns Sterling Holiday Resorts and a souvenir-photography business in the Gulf and Asia.

Together they reported ₹17,518 crore of revenue in FY26, up 13.3% from ₹15,466 crore, ₹2,534 crore of operating profit (a 14.5% margin) and ₹1,928 crore of reported profit after tax, up just 3.5% from ₹1,863 crore. At the close on 21 October 2026 the four were worth ₹63,897 crore, or 33.1 times FY26 profit. Those totals hide a lopsided shape. IRCTC is under a third of the revenue but 72% of the profit and 57% of the market value. Thomas Cook is nearly half the revenue but only 7% of the value, because it books holiday packages at their full price and keeps about five rupees of operating profit per hundred. The two platforms, TBO and ixigo, together handled ₹55,502 crore of bookings (gross transaction value, or GTV) in FY26 but kept only ₹3,905 crore of it as revenue.

Five forces define the group in 2026, and this report is built around them. The first is IRCTC's convenience-fee toll and the Railways' pen. IRCTC sells about 14.58 lakh tickets a day and its internet-ticketing segment converted 82.6% of its FY26 revenue into segment profit, which made it 74.5% of the company's segment profit. But ticket counts have drifted down for four quarters, ticketing revenue grew only 0.6% in Q1 FY27 (the June 2026 quarter), and almost all of IRCTC's growth now comes from catering at a margin near 10%. Every lever that matters, from the fee to Tatkal booking rules to the Railways' own RailOne app, sits with the owner, which holds 62.40% of the shares.

The second is the outbound wave meeting a war and a weak rupee. Indians spent US$16.44 billion on travel abroad under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme in FY26, and the Budget cut the tax collected at source on overseas packages to a flat 2% from April 2026. Then the war in West Asia that began on 28 February 2026 closed Gulf airspace, and the rupee slid to about ₹94.9 to the dollar (19 October 2026), near record lows. Thomas Cook's long-haul holidays fell 28% in Q1 FY27 and its Gulf subsidiaries dragged pre-tax profit down 21%; TBO lost margin in its Middle East market. The third is air capacity and fares: IndiGo's December 2025 crew-rostering collapse, groundings and war rerouting left domestic passenger numbers 6.34% lower in August 2026 than a year earlier, which pushes fares up and volumes down. The fourth is platform economics, the take rates, product mix and revenue-recognition choices that decide how much of a booking a TBO or an ixigo keeps. The fifth is Thomas Cook as a forex-and-resorts business: its foreign-exchange and Sterling segments earned about 54% of its FY26 segment profit on about 10% of its revenue, and a scheme now before the courts will hand part of Sterling directly to shareholders.

In plain terms

Only one of these four companies earns a fat margin on every trip, and that is IRCTC, because the government lets it alone sell train tickets online. The other three are middlemen who keep between ₹2 and ₹25 of every ₹100 a traveller spends, depending on whether they sell flights, hotels or packages. In 2026 the middlemen were hit by a war, a falling rupee and expensive flights, while IRCTC's problem was quieter: it has stopped selling more tickets and its owner, the Railways, is building a rival app.

Two traps run through everything that follows. First, a booking is not revenue. TBO's ₹36,809 crore of FY26 GTV became ₹2,677 crore of revenue, and IRCTC does not report a GTV at all: the train fare passes straight to Indian Railways and only the fee and related income stay with IRCTC. Second, the four companies recognise revenue in different ways. Thomas Cook books holiday packages gross (the full price is revenue and the hotel and airline cost is an expense) but books forex net (only the spread is revenue). TBO books air net and, through Classic, a slice of US wholesale close to gross. Adding or comparing their revenue lines without adjusting for this produces nonsense, which is why the report leans on profit, gross profit as a share of bookings and cash conversion rather than on revenue growth.

01

How a trip becomes revenue: suppliers, middlemen and the fee in between

Every journey runs from a supplier that owns the seat or the room, through one or more intermediaries, to a traveller. Where a company sits in that chain decides how much of the fare it keeps.

A travel intermediary does not own the train, the aircraft or the hotel. It earns a fee or a markup for matching a traveller to inventory that someone else controls, and the size of that fee depends on how fragmented and opaque the supply is. Airlines are few, their fares are public and they would rather sell direct, so they pay intermediaries very little. Hotels are many, their prices are hard to compare and they need distribution, so they pay much more. A railway ticket sold by a state monopoly pays a fixed fee set by the state. The four listed companies sit at different points on this spectrum, which is why their margins range from about 3% to more than 80%.

But IRCTC does not set the fee, the fare, the quota or the booking window, and it does not decide how many trains run. Its volume is the Railways' capacity, and its price is the Railways' decision.

TBO keeps 2.3% of an air booking but 10.6% of a hotel booking (Q1 FY27, consolidated); in gross-profit terms the gap is 1.25% versus 6.21% of GTV. Fragmented supply pays the intermediary roughly five times more.

The hotel and flight chain is longer and more contested. An airline seat can reach a traveller through a global distribution system, a consolidator, a travel agent and a consumer app, and each layer competes for a share of a commission that airlines have spent twenty years shrinking. TBO earned a take rate of only 2.3% on air in Q1 FY27 and a gross profit of 1.25% of air GTV; for most platforms air is the habit that brings customers back, not the profit. Hotels are the profit pool. India's hotel market, by a company-cited estimate from the research firm VIDEC, was about ₹1,57,100 crore in FY26, with independents holding 73% of rooms and about 60% of room-nights selling below ₹2,000 a night (a single-source, indicative figure). That fragmentation is exactly what pays an intermediary: TBO's hotels and ancillaries (H+A) line produced about 85% of its gross profit in Q1 FY27 on 68% of its bookings.

The money layer is the one most investors overlook. When a traveller buys foreign currency, loads a prepaid card or sends money abroad for a holiday or tuition, someone earns a spread, and someone holds the unspent balance. Thomas Cook's forex business turned over about ₹13,300 crore (₹133 billion) in FY26 and held about ₹1,800 crore of float on prepaid cards in Q1 FY27. IRCTC, similarly, holds customer and Railway money in transit and earned ₹277 crore of other income in FY26, much of it interest. That is why both companies report other income large enough to matter to their profit.

In plain terms

When you book a hotel through an app, the app keeps about one rupee in ten. When you book a flight, it keeps about two rupees in a hundred. When you book a train ticket online, IRCTC keeps a flat ₹10 to ₹30 however long the journey. And when you buy euros for the trip, the forex dealer keeps two or three rupees in a hundred. The rest goes to the hotel, the airline or the Railways.

02

A ₹17,518 crore revenue pool on top of a much larger travel bill

The listed four are a small slice of what Indians spend on travel. Their combined revenue has grown 72% in three years; their profit has grown less than their bookings.

Sizing Indian travel is harder than it looks, because every source draws the boundary differently and none of them matches what the listed companies report. The research firm Phocuswright put India's total travel market at US$41.7 billion in 2025, with online gross bookings of US$24 billion growing only 4%. IMARC used a different scope and reached US$51 billion for online travel alone in 2024. VIDEC, cited by ixigo, puts India's online share of travel bookings at about 42.5% in FY26. These are indicative numbers drawn from different definitions, and they should not be mixed or compared with company revenue. What they agree on is that India is a large market with low spending per head, and that online penetration has room to rise in hotels and buses even if it is already high in flights and rail.

The history of this revenue pool has two halves. The first is a recovery: Thomas Cook's revenue fell from ₹6,833 crore in FY20 to ₹795 crore in FY21 when COVID shut travel, and it took until FY24 to exceed the pre-pandemic level. IRCTC's revenue fell too, but its ticketing toll recovered as soon as trains ran, which is why its operating margin touched 47% in FY22, the year catering and tourism were still shut. The second half, from FY24 on, is a slowdown in quality. Combined operating profit rose only 6.2% in FY26 while revenue rose 13.3%, because three mix effects pulled the margin down at once: Classic Vacations brought in revenue booked near gross at a low margin, IRCTC's growth shifted to catering, and Thomas Cook's costs grew faster than its revenue. In plain terms

IRCTC is two businesses in one cost stack. About ₹48 of every ₹100 goes on food, Rail Neer inputs and the licence fees and profit shares it pays the Railways for catering; staff take about ₹6 and other costs about ₹14, leaving an operating profit of about ₹32 (screener's 31.9% margin). Add ₹5.3 of other income, take off a rupee of depreciation, a fraction of interest and about ₹9 of tax, and ₹26.7 is left as profit (₹1,393 crore on ₹5,215 crore). The ticketing segment alone keeps about ₹83 of every ₹100 it earns; catering keeps about ₹10. As catering grows faster, the blended margin falls, which is what has happened every year since FY22.

TBO's organic business in Q1 FY27 shows the economics of a wholesale marketplace. Its ₹595 crore of organic revenue sat on ₹9,918 crore of bookings, a 6.0% take rate. About ₹35 of each ₹100 of revenue went to suppliers and other direct costs inside revenue, leaving gross profit of ₹64.7. Selling, general and administrative costs excluding share-based pay took ₹47, leaving adjusted EBITDA of about ₹17.8. Then depreciation (about ₹5), interest (₹2.5) and tax take it down to profit of about ₹8–9. The point of a marketplace is that the ₹47 should grow more slowly than gross profit: in Q1 FY27 organic SG&A grew 12% against gross profit growth of 16%, and only 4% in constant currency.

ixigo spends most of its revenue acquiring and serving customers. In FY26, ₹61 of every ₹100 went on performance marketing, discounts, payment-gateway charges and customer support, leaving a contribution margin of ₹38.6. Technology, people and administration (before share-based pay) took another ₹29, leaving adjusted EBITDA of about ₹9.8. Share-based pay, depreciation, tax and losses at the Freshbus associate, partly offset by ₹3.7 of other income, brought profit to about ₹5.8 (₹71.5 crore). That is the economics of a consumer app competing with MakeMyTrip and Google for the same searches.

Thomas Cook looks the weakest on this measure only because it books packages gross. About ₹72 of every ₹100 of revenue is the cost of the flights, hotels, ground services and currency it resells; staff take about ₹14 and other costs about ₹9; operating profit is about ₹5.1 (₹428 crore). Other income adds ₹1.9, interest and depreciation take ₹3, tax takes ₹1.3, and about ₹2.6 is left. Measured instead against the profit it makes on each segment, Thomas Cook is a decent business: its forex segment keeps ₹46 of every ₹100 of net revenue as EBIT, and Sterling keeps ₹24.

04

Who owns the traveller: a monopoly, an oligopoly and a crowd

Each member competes in a differently shaped market, and each has a dominant owner whose interests are not quite those of a minority shareholder.

The structure of Indian travel distribution runs from a legal monopoly to a fragmented bazaar. Rail e-ticketing is a monopoly by statute: only IRCTC can issue a reserved e-ticket, and about 89% of reserved tickets are now booked online. Consumer online travel for flights and hotels is an oligopoly led by MakeMyTrip, which reported US$10.39 billion of gross bookings in its year to March 2026 and, by one trade estimate, holds more than half the Indian online travel market (an indicative, single-source share). Online bus ticketing is close to a duopoly between MakeMyTrip's redBus and ixigo's AbhiBus. B2B distribution, outbound tour operating and retail forex are crowded, with a handful of large brands and thousands of small agents and money changers.

Four business models: toll, wholesaler, retailer, money-changer

The members are not four versions of the same business. Each is a different archetype with its own revenue engine, capital need and failure mode.

It helps to name the four archetypes plainly. IRCTC is a state-granted toll with mandated services attached: a fixed fee on a fixed flow of tickets, plus catering and water businesses it runs because the Railways asks it to. TBO is a B2B wholesale marketplace: it aggregates inventory from suppliers and sells it to agents who own the customer. ixigo is a B2C retail marketplace: it owns the customer and pays to acquire them. Thomas Cook is an integrated tour operator with a money business and a hotel business: it takes inventory risk on packages, earns a spread on currency and runs resorts. The toll with a landlord: IRCTC's ticket engine stalls and catering takes over Deep dive

IRCTC earns three-quarters of its segment profit from a fee it does not set, on tickets whose number it does not control. That fee has stopped growing, and the growth that replaced it earns a tenth of the margin.

IRCTC's internet-ticketing segment is one of the best businesses on the Indian market. In FY26 it earned ₹1,535.5 crore of revenue and a ₹1,267.9 crore segment result, an 82.6% margin, and produced 74.5% of IRCTC's segment profit on 29.3% of its segment revenue. Its cost base is a website, an app, a payment stack and a maintenance contract with CRIS. But it has two weaknesses that 2026 exposed. Its volume has peaked for now: IRCTC sold about 13.6 crore tickets in Q2 FY26, then 13.5, 13.4 and 13.3 crore in the next three quarters, or 14.58 lakh a day in Q1 FY27. And its price is fixed by its majority owner. The rest of IRCTC, mainly catering, is growing fast, but at a margin of about 10%. The result was the Q1 FY27 quarter: revenue up 18.1% to ₹1,370 crore, profit after tax flat at ₹330 crore.

Why the fee toll has stalled

Three things cap the ticketing segment. The first is volume. IRCTC's ticket count is the number of reserved berths and seats the Railways offers, multiplied by the share booked online, and both are near their limits for now. E-booking penetration was 89.2% in Q2 FY26 and has sat at 89.0% for three quarters; the remaining tenth of tickets are counter bookings that are slow to move. New trains such as the Vande Bharat chair-car and sleeper services add catering revenue more than they add ticket volume. Shortening the advance-reservation period from 120 to 60 days from 1 November 2024 also compressed the window in which tickets are booked and cancelled, which reduced re-bookings.

The second is the price. The fee is ₹15 for a non-AC ticket and ₹30 for an AC ticket plus GST, restored on 1 September 2019 after a three-year waiver, but a 2019 government mandate set it at ₹10 and ₹20 when the passenger pays by UPI or BHIM. That two-tier structure is why the realised fee per ticket is about ₹18.6 (₹248 crore across about 13.3 crore tickets in Q1 FY27), and why it drifts down: UPI's share of IRCTC's transactions rose from 48.7% to 51.2% in a year. Each percentage point of UPI share costs roughly ₹1 crore of fee revenue a quarter (an indicative estimate: 1% of 13.3 crore tickets at an average UPI discount of about ₹7.5). The Railways has not raised the base fee since 2019, and with UPI being promoted as public infrastructure, it is hard to see it doing so.

Tatkal rules deserve a paragraph of their own because they move volume between IRCTC's channels. The catering pivot: growth at a tenth of the margin

Catering is where IRCTC's growth now lives. Segment revenue rose 33.9% to ₹732.3 crore in Q1 FY27, and in FY26 it was ₹2,398.8 crore, 45.8% of segment revenue. Three engines drive it. Pre-paid catering on premium trains (Rajdhani, Shatabdi, Duronto and especially Vande Bharat) earned about ₹413 crore in Q1 FY27, with the meal price bundled into the fare and an opt-out rate of 25–30%. Licence fees from mobile and static catering contractors earned about ₹257 crore. One-off election special trains added about ₹41 crore. Mobile catering ran on 1,318 trains in Q2 FY26, and 40 more were added in Q3 FY26, 19 of them Vande Bharat. E-catering, where passengers order restaurant food to their seat, grew from about 1.25 lakh to more than 1.6 lakh meals a day.

The margin is the problem. Catering's segment margin was 13.1% in Q1 FY26, 12.9% in Q2, 10.4% in Q3, 6.2% in Q4 and 9.3% in Q1 FY27. Some of the fall is one-off: about ₹20 crore of gratuity and HR cost and ₹4 crore of losses on proof-of-concept trains that IRCTC runs itself, which management says will be phased out by November 2026. Some is structural. Meal tariffs are set by the Railways and revised rarely, while food, labour and gas costs rise every year; the West Asia war added a commercial-LPG shortage that forced on-board vendors to cook with electricity. And the Railways takes its own cut: IRCTC's related-party disclosures show ₹227.5 crore of "Railway share on catering services" in H2 FY26 alone, plus a share of Rail Neer profit. The convenience fee is the one stream the Railways does not share.

Who writes the rules: the Railways, the taxman, DGCA and the ED

Few consumer businesses have so much of their economics set directly by the state. In travel, the government prices the rail fee, taxes the outbound trip at source and polices the forex that pays for it.

For IRCTC, regulation is not an external force; it is the business model. The Railways fixes the convenience fee, the catering tariff, the Rail Neer price, the Tatkal rules and the advance-reservation window, and it owns 62.40% of the company. For Thomas Cook and TBO, the most important policy instrument is tax collected at source (TCS) on money sent abroad, together with the foreign-exchange rules under FEMA. For ixigo and TBO's India business, aviation rules on pilot rest and airspace decide how many seats there are to sell. And GST 2.0, which took effect on 22 September 2025, changed the tax on hotel rooms and premium air tickets. The table collects the levers that moved in the last two years.

The tax on going abroad

Tax collected at source is not a final tax: the traveller can set it against their income-tax bill. But it is collected upfront, at the moment of payment, and for a family that does not file a large return it can feel like a surcharge. From 1 October 2023 TCS on overseas tour packages was 5% up to ₹7 lakh a year and 20% above; from 1 April 2025 the threshold rose to ₹10 lakh. The Budget of 1 February 2026 then cut the rate on overseas tour packages to a flat 2% from the first rupee, effective 1 April 2026 (now under section 394(1) of the Income-tax Act 2025), and cut the rate on education and medical remittances to 2%. Other LRS remittances still attract 20% above ₹10 lakh. EaseMyTrip's co-founder called it one of the most significant measures for the industry. For the organised tour operators, it removes a reason for customers to book land arrangements abroad directly and pay for flights separately.

GST 2.0 and the hotel credit fight

Under GST 2.0, hotel rooms priced up to ₹7,500 a night moved from 12% with input tax credit to 5% without it. The consumer pays less tax, but the hotel can no longer offset GST paid on its own inputs, so its costs rise; the hotel association FHRAI has argued this hurts small hotels. For platforms the effect is mixed. A lower headline price helps conversion in the budget segment where ixigo wants to grow, but hotels that lose margin push back on commissions. Economy air tickets stay at 5% without credit; premium economy, business and first class moved from 12% to 18% with credit. GST on tour operators' packages is understood to remain at 5% without credit, though that was not confirmed for this report. Commissions and convenience fees earned by OTAs carry 18%.

Enforcement: the ED order on TBO and the GSTAT order on IRCTC

Two enforcement actions landed in October 2026. The Special Director (Adjudication) of the Enforcement Directorate issued an order dated 16 October 2026, received by TBO on 21 October, imposing a penalty of ₹22.22 crore on TBO Tek and ₹2.47 crore on each of its two Joint Managing Directors for contravening FEMA section 3(c) read with section 42(1). Section 3(c) bars receiving payment on behalf of a person resident outside India other than through an authorised person; section 42 makes officers in charge liable. TBO's auditors had already flagged the proceeding as an emphasis of matter in the FY26 report, and in 2025 the company sought post-facto approval for transactions of about ₹71.23 crore through its authorised dealer bank. TBO says it is examining the order and evaluating its options with counsel; no appeal had been filed by 22 October. The total of ₹27.16 crore is about 11% of FY26 profit if the company bears it all. On IRCTC, the GSTAT anti-profiteering order of 14 October 2026 (₹50.41 crore plus 18% interest from 28 June 2019, an indicative ₹117 crore with interest) is under appeal.

The outbound wave meets a war and a weak rupee

India's outbound travel boom was the growth story for Thomas Cook and part of TBO's. In 2026 it collided with closed Gulf airspace, a rupee near record lows and tighter student visas.

Indians have been travelling abroad in growing numbers since the pandemic. The Ministry of Tourism's 2025 snapshot, seen only in summaries for this report, counts 3.28 crore outbound departures in calendar 2025, up 6.3% (a single-source, indicative figure). The same war produced four different outcomes. IRCTC felt it in its kitchens, TBO in its margins, ixigo in its fares and Thomas Cook in its Gulf subsidiaries.

Seats, fares and capacity: why expensive flights are a mixed blessing

Domestic air traffic was flat for the first eight months of 2026 and fell 6.34% in August. Higher fares lift bookings by value and cut them by number, and platforms paid per ticket lose.

India is the world's third-largest domestic aviation market, and for most of the past decade it has grown faster than seats could be added. That relationship broke in 2025–26. IndiGo, which carried 65.0% of domestic passengers in August 2026, cancelled about 2,507 flights between 3 and 5 December 2025 when new pilot duty-time rules collided with its rosters; DGCA's penalty, as reported in the press, was ₹22.20 crore (₹1.80 crore for systemic failures and ₹20.40 crore for 68 days at ₹30 lakh a day) plus a ₹50 crore bank guarantee (secondary reports, indicative). Pratt & Whitney engine problems had already grounded part of the A320neo fleet, and Air India was still under a safety review after the June 2025 AI171 crash. Then the war reduced capacity and raised fuel costs again. ixigo's Q2 FY27 letter counted about 130 of more than 840 Indian-carrier aircraft grounded in August 2026, with roughly 2,000 on order.

Twenty-five years of shocks: demonetisation, COVID, a fee fight and a war

Travel volumes fall fast in a shock and come back. Policy changes, by contrast, tend to stay. The history of this group is a sequence of both.

The members' histories are short in stock-market terms (two of them listed in 2024) but long in business terms. Thomas Cook has operated in India since 1881. IRCTC was incorporated in 1999 and launched internet ticketing in 2002. TBO was founded in 2006 and ixigo in 2007. Between them they have lived through every kind of shock the industry produces: a policy shock in 2016, when the convenience fee was waived after demonetisation; a collapse of a rival in 2019, when Cox & Kings failed; a near-total halt in 2020–21; a fee-sharing order in 2021; a period of "revenge travel" in 2022–24; and in 2025–26 a sequence of aviation, tax and geopolitical shocks.

Reading a travel company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Travel companies report bookings, take rates, users, tickets, turnover, float and room rates, each defined slightly differently. This section explains every one, shows how the four members compare, and flags where the definitions mislead.

Every travel intermediary, whatever its archetype, runs on the same basic equation. A number of trips at an average price produces a booking value. The intermediary keeps a share of that booking value as revenue. From revenue it pays the variable costs of winning and serving the booking, which leaves gross profit or contribution. From that it pays the fixed costs of people and technology, which leaves operating profit. The KPIs that travel companies publish are the terms of this equation, and the art of reading them is knowing which terms management controls and which it does not.

The equation, worked through in rupees

Take ₹1,00,000 of bookings on TBO in Q1 FY27, split as TBO's consolidated business was: about ₹32,100 of air and ₹67,900 of hotels and ancillaries. After selling, general and administrative costs, adjusted EBITDA is about ₹1,344, or 1.34% of GTV. Strip Classic out and the organic business turns ₹1,00,000 of bookings into ₹6,000 of revenue, ₹3,880 of gross profit and ₹1,070 of adjusted EBITDA.

The same equation for IRCTC's ticketing segment is simpler. In Q1 FY27 it sold about 13.3 crore tickets (14.58 lakh a day across 91 days). Convenience fees of ₹248 crore came to about ₹18.6 a ticket and non-fee income of ₹113 crore to about ₹8.5 a ticket, so segment revenue was ₹361 crore, about ₹27 a ticket. The segment result was ₹289.6 crore, an 80.2% margin, or about ₹21.8 of profit per ticket. Annualised, that is roughly ₹1,445 crore of revenue and ₹1,160 crore of segment profit, a little under two-thirds of IRCTC's FY26 profit before tax. These per-ticket figures are indicative arithmetic on company-stated totals.

Thomas Cook needs a longer version of the equation because it runs four engines. Its profit is roughly the number of package travellers × the average package price × the package margin (about 3% at EBIT in FY26), plus forex turnover × the spread (₹13,300 crore × about 2.45% of revenue, of which about 46% became EBIT), plus Sterling's room-nights × average room rate less resort costs, plus DEI's photo sales at attractions. In FY26 those four produced segment EBIT of ₹221.7 crore, ₹149.3 crore, ₹129.2 crore and ₹11.0 crore. The equation shows where management has leverage: it cannot set airfares or the rupee, but it can price packages, choose which forex products to push, and decide how many resorts to add on a managed rather than owned basis. It also shows why the war hurt: it cut the number of long-haul package travellers and DEI's visitors at the same time.

In plain terms

Every travel business is the same sum: how much is booked, times the share the company keeps, minus what it spends to get and serve the booking. IRCTC keeps about ₹27 per ticket and spends about ₹5. TBO keeps about ₹8 per ₹100 booked and ends up with about ₹1.30. The rest of this section explains each part of that sum.

Tickets per day and convenience fee per ticket (IRCTC)

What it is. The average number of reserved e-tickets IRCTC books each day, and the average convenience fee it earns on each. Formula. Tickets per day = tickets in the period ÷ days; fee per ticket = convenience-fee revenue ÷ tickets. Worked example. Q1 FY27: about 13.3 crore tickets ÷ 91 days = 14.58 lakh a day; ₹248 crore ÷ 13.3 crore = ₹18.6 a ticket. The fee schedule is ₹15 non-AC and ₹30 AC plus GST, or ₹10 and ₹20 via UPI or BHIM; with UPI at 51.2% of transactions and AC tickets about half of the total, the blended fee works out at about ₹18.7, which matches.

What drives it. Tickets per day depends on the Railways' seat supply, the share booked online and seasonality (Q3 and Q4 carry festivals and holidays). Fee per ticket depends on the AC share (higher with more AC and Vande Bharat seats) and the UPI share (lower as UPI rises). Benchmarks. Volume growth above 5% a year is good for a mature monopoly, 0–5% average, a decline weak; IRCTC's quarterly ticket count fell about 2% between Q2 FY26 and Q1 FY27. Fee per ticket stable or rising is good; it has been flat at ₹18.4–18.6 for four quarters. The trap. Tickets are not passengers: one PNR can carry up to six passengers, so ixigo's "passenger segments" and IRCTC's "tickets" are different units. And ticketing revenue is not convenience fee: about a third of segment revenue is non-fee income (₹546 crore in FY26, indicative), which fell 5.5% in Q1 FY27.

Above 90% is mature; 80–90% is where IRCTC has been since COVID; below 80% would leave room for volume growth. IRCTC reported 89.2% in Q2 FY26 and 89.0% in the next three quarters; the Rail Minister cited "about 87%" in August 2025. How members compare. Only IRCTC reports it; for ixigo the related figure is its claimed 64% share of the OTA rail market. The trap. Some summaries still quote "about 80%", which is out of date. At 89% the online share has little room left, so future ticket growth has to come from the Railways adding seats.

Segment margin: ticketing versus catering

What it is. Segment result as a share of segment revenue. For IRCTC, the segment result is profit before unallocable items and tax, which is close to EBIT but not EBITDA. Thomas Cook reports segment EBIT the same way. Formula. Segment result ÷ segment revenue. Worked example. IRCTC FY26: ticketing ₹1,267.9 crore ÷ ₹1,535.5 crore = 82.6%; catering ₹249.9 crore ÷ ₹2,398.8 crore = 10.4%. Move ₹100 crore of revenue growth from ticketing to catering and segment profit grows by about ₹10 crore instead of ₹83 crore. What drives it. Ticketing margin depends on platform costs (one-off upgrades cost about ₹10 crore in Q1 FY27); catering margin on food and labour inflation against tariffs the Railways sets, on the mix of pre-paid catering and on one-off HR charges. Benchmarks. Ticketing above 80% is good, 70–80% average, below 70% weak. Catering above 14% good, 9–14% average, below 9% weak. Forex above 40% good; resorts above 30% good. The trap. Segment margins are pre-unallocable items: IRCTC's FY26 total segment margin was 32.5%, but its operating margin on screener was 31.9% and its PBT includes ₹174 crore of unallocable net income, mostly interest.

GTV and gross bookings

What it is. Gross transaction value (also called gross bookings or GBV) is the total price paid by travellers for bookings made through the platform, including taxes and the supplier's share. Formula. Sum of booking values, usually net of cancellations, often at booking date (Classic Vacations reports on check-in date). Worked example. ixigo Q2 FY27: ₹5,304.47 crore of GTV, of which buses were ₹753 crore and hotels ₹111.34 crore; trains and flights together made up the remaining ₹4,440 crore, but ixigo does not disclose the split in rupees. What drives it. Volumes, average fares and room rates (a 27% rise in ixigo's average flight ticket lifted GTV even as segments grew 8%), FX for international bookings, and acquisitions. Benchmarks. For an Indian B2C platform, GTV growth above 25% is good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak; ixigo is at 22%. For TBO, organic growth in constant currency matters most: 15% in Q1 FY27. How members compare. TBO ₹36,809 crore and ixigo ₹18,693 crore in FY26; IRCTC and Thomas Cook do not report GTV. The trap. GTV inflates with fares and a falling rupee, and it adds Classic's ₹1,300 crore a quarter of acquired bookings. Never compare GTV with another company's revenue, and never add IRCTC's ticket value to ixigo's: every ixigo train ticket is also an IRCTC ticket.

Take rate, and gross versus net recognition

What it is. The share of bookings that a platform records as revenue. Formula. Revenue ÷ GTV. Worked example. ixigo Q2 FY27: ₹320.96 crore ÷ ₹5,304.47 crore = 6.05%, against 6.54% a year earlier (₹284.23 crore ÷ ₹4,347.50 crore). TBO Q1 FY27: ₹925.8 crore ÷ ₹11,154.3 crore = 8.3%; organic 6.0%. What drives it. Product mix (hotels pay several times what airlines do), source-market mix (TBO's Indian business takes 2.8%, its international business 11.4%), fee design (fixed per-ticket fees shrink as a share of rising fares), discounts netted from revenue, and accounting. Benchmarks. For a B2B blend, above 7% good, 5.5–7% average, below 5.5% weak; TBO organic 6.0% is average. For a B2C blend, above 8% good, 6–8% average, below 6% weak; ixigo is on the line.

Gross versus net. A company that acts as agent records only its commission or fee as revenue (net). A company that takes on the inventory risk or acts as principal records the full price as revenue and the supplier cost as an expense (gross). Thomas Cook books packages gross and forex net. TBO books air net. Classic Vacations books much closer to gross: its 25.4% take rate in Q1 FY27 includes a service-fee component of about 12.4% of GTV that is passed on to US travel advisors, which is why its gross profit (12.9% of GTV) is about half its take rate. ixigo nets discounts against revenue, so its hotel "gross take" above 12% becomes about 7.3% as reported revenue (₹8.1 crore on ₹111 crore). The trap. A rising take rate is not necessarily good news. TBO's rose from 6.3% to 8.3% in a year almost entirely because it bought a gross-booking business; its organic take rate fell. Use gross profit ÷ GTV to compare economics.

Gross profit ÷ GTV and adjusted EBITDA ÷ GTV

What it is. The cleanest economic measures for a platform: how much of each rupee booked is left after the direct cost of the booking, and after all operating costs. Formula. Gross profit (TBO) or contribution margin (ixigo) ÷ GTV; adjusted EBITDA ÷ GTV. Worked example. TBO organic Q1 FY27: ₹385.1 crore ÷ ₹9,918 crore = 3.9% gross; ₹106.0 crore ÷ ₹9,918 crore = 1.07% adjusted EBITDA. ixigo Q2 FY27: contribution ₹124.86 crore ÷ ₹5,304 crore = 2.35%; adjusted EBITDA ₹23.58 crore ÷ ₹5,304 crore = 0.44%. What drives it. Mix and pricing drive the gross line; operating leverage drives the gap between the two. TBO's organic SG&A growth slowed from 29% to 13% over six quarters, so adjusted EBITDA rose from 23.5% of gross profit in Q4 FY26 to 27.5% in Q1 FY27. ixigo's moved the other way as it invested in hotels. Benchmarks. Gross profit above 5% of GTV good for B2B, 3.5–5% average; adjusted EBITDA above 1.5% of GTV good, 0.8–1.5% average, below 0.8% weak. MakeMyTrip is often cited at roughly 1.5–1.8% of gross bookings (indicative). The trap. TBO's "gross profit" and ixigo's "contribution margin" are not identical: contribution deducts marketing and discounts, while TBO's selling costs sit below gross profit. And the two companies' adjusted EBITDA definitions differ: TBO adds back share-based pay and deal costs; ixigo adds back share-based pay and also removes other income.

Transacting users and monthly transacting buyers

What it is. The number of customers who actually book in a period. TBO counts monthly transacting buyers (MTBs), the agents and travel businesses that make at least one booking a month; ixigo counts annual active users (AAU), people who used its apps in the year. Formula. Unique transacting accounts in the period. Worked example. TBO Q1 FY27: 33,736 MTBs, of which 18,322 in India and 15,414 international (+39% YoY); GTV per MTB is about ₹33 lakh a quarter (₹11,154 crore ÷ 33,736), or about ₹31 lakh on organic GTV and 31,540 organic MTBs (indicative). ixigo FY26: 57 crore AAU. What drives it. Sales coverage and product breadth for B2B; brand, app-store ranking and marketing for B2C. Benchmarks. MTB growth above 15% good, 8–15% average, below 8% weak; TBO's 14% is average, with the international number doing all the work. The trap. AAU counts people who opened an app, not people who booked, and ixigo's 57 crore is far larger than the number of Indians who buy travel online; it should not be compared with TBO's MTBs or used to compute revenue per user. A falling Indian MTB count (18,486 to 18,322 in a year) matters more for TBO than the headline total.

Customer acquisition cost and marketing as a share of revenue

What it is. What a consumer platform spends to win and keep each booking: performance marketing on Google and Meta, app installs, discounts and cashback, plus payment-gateway and customer-support costs. ixigo reports these as the gap between revenue and contribution margin. Formula. Direct costs ÷ revenue = 1 − contribution margin %. Worked example. ixigo FY26: revenue ₹1,228 crore, contribution margin ₹474.3 crore (38.6%), so direct costs were about ₹754 crore, or 61% of revenue. Q2 FY27: contribution margin 38.9%, so 61.1%. By segment, trains spent about 68% of their revenue on direct costs (31.7% contribution), buses about 47% (53.2%). What drives it. Competition (MakeMyTrip, Cleartrip, EaseMyTrip and Google bid on the same keywords), the share of repeat and organic traffic, and how much discount management chooses to fund; in Q2 FY27 ixigo "pushed the pedal on growth" in trains, raising direct costs 11% quarter on quarter for 1% revenue growth. Benchmarks. Contribution above 40% of revenue good for a B2C blend, 30–40% average, below 30% weak. How members compare. Only ixigo reports it; TBO's equivalent cost is its sales team inside SG&A, and agents bear the consumer acquisition cost. The trap. Contribution margin is not a profit. ixigo's ₹124.9 crore Q2 contribution became ₹23.6 crore of adjusted EBITDA after technology, people and the hotel build-out.

In plain terms

A consumer travel app spends about sixty rupees of every hundred it earns on ads, discounts and payment fees before it pays a single engineer. A business-to-business platform like TBO lets its travel-agent customers do that spending, which is why it keeps more of each booking.

Forex turnover and forex margin

What it is. Forex turnover is the gross value of currency bought and sold; forex revenue is the spread and fees earned on it; forex margin can mean revenue ÷ turnover or EBIT ÷ revenue. Formula. Revenue ÷ turnover (the spread) and segment EBIT ÷ segment revenue (the operating margin). Worked example. Thomas Cook FY26: ₹326.2 crore of net revenue on ₹13,300 crore of turnover = 2.45%; EBIT ₹149.3 crore ÷ ₹326.2 crore = 45.8%, or about 1.1% of turnover (indicative). What drives it. The retail–wholesale mix (retail earns a far wider spread), product (cash notes and remittances earn more than card loads), competition from banks and zero-markup cards, and the float income on card balances. Benchmarks. EBIT margin above 40% good, 30–40% average, below 30% weak. Thomas Cook moved from 29% in FY23 to 46% in FY25 and FY26 as volumes recovered over a fixed branch network. The trap. Turnover growth flatters: in FY26 turnover rose about 8% but card loads fell 11% and active card users fell, because the growth came from lower-spread wholesale and education. A "financial services revenue" figure of about ₹485 crore that appears in some summaries is a residual of total income, not the segment figure of ₹326.2 crore.

Float

What it is. Money a travel company holds that belongs to customers or suppliers: unspent balances on prepaid forex cards, advance payments for holidays, fares collected and not yet passed on. Formula. The relevant balance-sheet liability, and the interest it earns (other income). Worked example. Thomas Cook's card float was ₹1,600 crore at FY26 and ₹1,800 crore in Q1 FY27; at an illustrative 6% yield, ₹1,800 crore earns about ₹108 crore a year before tax, against Thomas Cook's FY26 other income of ₹156 crore. IRCTC holds ₹863.8 crore of rolling deposits with the Ministry and large payables to the Railways; its FY26 other income was ₹277 crore. What drives it. Card adoption, booking lead times (longer lead times mean more advance money), and interest rates; the RBI's hike to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 raises float income. Benchmarks. Float growing faster than revenue is good; float shrinking with card users is weak. The trap. Float is not free cash. Thomas Cook's ₹2,648.8 crore of cash and short-term investments (June 2026) includes it, so net cash after its ₹268.8 crore of debt (about ₹2,380 crore, indicative) overstates what shareholders own. The same applies to IRCTC's ₹2,841.6 crore.

Occupancy and ARR (Sterling)

What it is. Occupancy is rooms sold ÷ rooms available; ARR (average room rate) is room revenue ÷ rooms sold; RevPAR (revenue per available room) is occupancy × ARR. Formula. RevPAR = occupancy × ARR. Worked example. Sterling FY26: 59% × ₹6,176 = about ₹3,644 RevPAR; Q1 FY27: 67% × ₹7,809 = about ₹5,232 (indicative). Branded Indian hotels ran at 63–65% occupancy and ₹8,500–8,700 ARR in calendar 2025, a RevPAR of roughly ₹5,400–5,600 (HVS-Anarock, indicative). What drives it. Location, season (Q1 is the summer peak for hill and beach resorts), the share of member versus paying guests (the guest ratio was 78% in Q1 FY27 against 74%), and portfolio mix: new managed resorts in smaller towns lower the average rate. Benchmarks. For a leisure resort chain, occupancy above 65% good, 55–65% average, below 55% weak; resort EBIT margin above 30% good, 20–30% average. Sterling's FY26 margin of 24.2% is average; its Q1 32.4% is seasonal. The trap. A falling ARR is not necessarily bad if it comes from adding managed, capital-light resorts; Sterling's ARR fell from ₹6,405 in FY24 to ₹6,176 in FY26 as resorts went from 48 to 78. Compare like-for-like resorts where possible. For the listed hotel chains, see the Indian hotels report.

Working capital and cash conversion (OCF ÷ EBITDA)

What it is. How much of operating profit turns into operating cash flow, which in travel depends mostly on who pays whom first. Formula. Cash from operations ÷ EBITDA (here screener operating profit). Worked example. FY26: ixigo ₹196 crore ÷ ₹62 crore = 316% (162% on its adjusted EBITDA); Thomas Cook ₹643 crore ÷ ₹428 crore = 150%; IRCTC ₹1,273 crore ÷ ₹1,666 crore = 76%; TBO −₹21 crore ÷ ₹378 crore = −6%. What drives it. Consumer platforms collect from travellers at booking and pay suppliers later; wholesalers extend credit to agents and often prepay hotels; tour operators collect advances; IRCTC's receivables from the Ministry (₹1,883.2 crore of trade receivables, with ₹138.3 crore provided for) slow its conversion. Growth and seasonality swing the figure: TBO generated ₹392 crore of cash in Q1 FY27 alone, which management said was partly timing. Benchmarks. Above 100% good for asset-light travel, 70–100% average, below 70% weak; a multi-year average matters more than one year. The trap. Very high conversion at a consumer platform can reverse if growth slows, because the negative working capital unwinds. Very low conversion at a wholesaler can mean credit risk building in agent receivables; screener's 724 debtor days for TBO is inflated by its net revenue base and is not meaningful on its own.

In plain terms

A shop that is paid by customers before it pays its suppliers can show more cash than profit. A wholesaler that gives its customers credit shows less. Neither is good or bad on its own; what matters is whether the pattern is steady, and whether the money owed to the company is actually collected.

Definition traps, collected

  • GTV is not revenue. TBO's ₹36,809 crore of FY26 bookings became ₹2,677 crore of revenue. IRCTC has no GTV: the fare is the Railways' money.
  • Take rate is not a margin, and it rises when a company buys a gross-booking business. TBO's 6.3% to 8.3% move was Classic; its organic rate fell to 6.0%.
  • Gross and net recognition differ by product and company. Thomas Cook packages gross and forex net; TBO air net; Classic near gross with about 12.4% of GTV passed to advisors; ixigo nets discounts.
  • "Adjusted EBITDA" has three definitions here. TBO adds back share-based pay and deal costs (₹399.5 crore FY26; ₹414.1 crore before ₹14.5 crore of acquisition costs). ixigo adds back share-based pay and removes other income (₹120.9 crore). Screener's operating profit deducts share-based pay (TBO ₹378 crore, ixigo ₹62 crore).
  • Convenience fee is not ticketing revenue. About a third of IRCTC's ticketing segment revenue is non-fee income from gateways, advertising and loyalty.
  • Segment results are not EBITDA. IRCTC's and Thomas Cook's segment figures are before unallocable items and tax, and after depreciation.
  • Total income is not revenue. Thomas Cook's ₹8,558 crore includes ₹156 crore of other income; revenue from operations is ₹8,398 crore.
  • Forex turnover is not forex revenue. ₹13,300 crore of turnover produced ₹326.2 crore of revenue.
  • Reported PAT can include one-offs. ixigo's record ₹63.42 crore in Q2 FY27 includes a ₹40.66 crore Freshbus gain; IRCTC's FY26 includes a small exceptional gain (about ₹16.8 crore).
  • Cash is not all free cash. Thomas Cook's and IRCTC's cash balances include customer float and money owed to the Railways; screener's borrowings include leases (Thomas Cook ₹539 crore against company-reported debt of ₹268.8 crore).
  • Reported growth can be FX and acquisitions. TBO's +37% GTV was +15% organic in constant currency.
  • Users are not buyers. ixigo's 57 crore annual active users and TBO's 33,736 monthly transacting buyers measure different things.
In plain terms

Before comparing any two numbers from these companies, check three things: is it bookings or sales, is it before or after the company's own "adjustments", and does it include a business bought during the year. Most of the misleading comparisons in travel come from getting one of those wrong.

How to read a travel company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find the volume line first (tickets per day for IRCTC; GTV for TBO and ixigo; segment revenue and forex turnover for Thomas Cook). Ask how much of its growth is price (fares, room rates), FX and acquisitions. For TBO, read the organic and constant-currency figures; for ixigo, compare GTV growth with segment counts.
  2. Compute the take rate (revenue ÷ GTV) and compare it with a year ago. If it moved, find out whether mix, fees, discounts or accounting moved it. A falling take rate with rising GTV usually means higher fares or more discounting.
  3. Go to gross profit or contribution as a share of GTV. This is the real economic yield. For IRCTC, read segment margins: ticketing should be 80–85%, catering 9–14%.
  4. Check operating leverage. Compare growth in SG&A or indirect costs with growth in gross profit. TBO's should be lower; if ixigo's is higher, find out how much is the hotel investment.
  5. Reconcile adjusted EBITDA to reported EBITDA using the company's own table, and note share-based pay, deal costs and other income.
  6. Strip out one-offs from PAT: exceptional gains (Freshbus), gratuity and HR charges (IRCTC), FX gains or losses (TBO's ₹5.5 crore loss), associate losses.
  7. Read cash and working capital. Cash from operations against EBITDA; for TBO, receivables and the cash balance (₹1,984 crore at June 2026); for Thomas Cook and IRCTC, remember the float.
  8. Read the segment or regional table for the quarter's story: catering versus ticketing at IRCTC; Middle East versus Asia-Pacific at TBO; trains, flights, buses and hotels at ixigo; travel, forex, Sterling and DEI at Thomas Cook.
  9. Note the policy and legal items: fee or Tatkal changes, TCS, GST, the GSTAT order on IRCTC, the ED order on TBO, the NCLT step on Thomas Cook's scheme.
  10. Read management's guidance language for changes in tone ("cautiously optimistic", "not guidance", "should not be considered a steady state") and red flags: a rising take rate with falling gross profit per booking, receivables growing faster than GTV, a cash balance that rose only because payables did, or reported profit that is mostly other income.
In plain terms

In ten minutes you can answer four questions: did the company sell more travel, did it keep more or less of each booking, did its costs grow slower than what it kept, and is the profit real cash that is not a one-off. If all four answers are yes, it was a good quarter.

Valuation: what the gap is paying for

The Indian platforms trade at two to four times the earnings multiples of the global leaders. The cleanest way to see what that buys is to compare TBO with Hotelbeds, the business it most resembles. Hotelbeds listed in February 2025 at an enterprise value of about €3 billion on €8–9 billion of transaction value and €300–350 million of adjusted EBITDA: roughly 0.35 times bookings and 9–10 times EBITDA (indicative). The market is paying for TBO's margin per booking to rise towards the bed-bank level as hotels grow and costs stay flat, which is exactly the operating leverage TBO's letters describe.

ixigo's market cap of ₹6,233 crore is about 0.33 times its FY26 GTV, and its multiple sits near MakeMyTrip's. Investors are pricing it as a smaller version of the market leader whose profit per booking should rise as it adds hotels. Three forces support Indian multiples in general. The second is scarcity: domestic funds that want listed travel-platform exposure have only TBO and ixigo, because MakeMyTrip is listed in New York. The third is the rupee. IRCTC has no listed global analogue. The nearest equivalents, the ticketing arms of state railways such as SNCF Connect in France or Renfe in Spain, are not listed, and none combines a 46% return on capital with a state-set fee. IRCTC's 26 times trailing earnings is in the same range as Booking's, for a business with a stronger return on capital but no growth in its core toll and a single customer that is also its owner and regulator. On an enterprise-value basis, using gross cash, it trades at about 20 times FY26 operating profit. The market is pricing a bond-like toll with policy risk, not a growth platform.

Thomas Cook's closest global comparison is TUI, the integrated European tour operator, which earns an EBIT margin of roughly 2–4% (indicative). Thomas Cook's travel segment earned 3.3% in FY26, in line with that, and its group operating margin of 5.1% is higher only because of forex and Sterling. Global tour operators trade at low multiples because package margins are thin and volatile. Thomas Cook's 20 times earnings is above that level because its forex franchise, with a 45.8% EBIT margin on ₹13,300 crore of turnover, has no equivalent at TUI. On the global yardstick, the market gives Thomas Cook little credit for that franchise.

In forex itself, the global and Indian comparisons point the same way. In India the banks handle the bulk of Liberalised Remittance Scheme volume. Thomas Cook is the largest non-bank dealer, competing with BookMyForex, Orient Exchange and a weakened EbixCash. Its edge is physical reach and the float on 1.1 million prepaid cards, not price. The big global travel companies keep more of every booking than India's platforms do, because they sell mostly hotel rooms, and hotels pay much bigger commissions than airlines. Yet investors pay two to four times as much for each rupee of TBO's or ixigo's profit. They are betting that India's travel market will keep growing faster, that the Indian platforms will sell more hotels and earn more per booking, and that there are few other ways to own this growth on an Indian exchange. If the profit per booking does not rise, those multiples have nothing to rest on.

Technology and disruption: AI agents, direct distribution and the Railways' own app

Every intermediary in this report sits between a supplier and a traveller. The technologies that matter are the ones that let either side skip the middleman, and the ones that let the middleman serve more people for less.

Disruption in travel has a simple test: does a new technology let the supplier reach the traveller directly, or let someone else own the traveller's first question? The four members face different versions of that test. IRCTC's threat comes from its own owner. ixigo's comes from search engines, AI assistants and IRCTC itself. TBO's comes more slowly, from airlines and hotel chains pushing direct distribution. Thomas Cook's comes from fintech forex cards and zero-markup credit cards. Each company also uses technology defensively, and the evidence on how well is mixed.

AI agents and LLM trip planning

The most discussed risk is that travellers will plan and book trips through general-purpose AI assistants and search engines (Google's AI Mode, and OpenAI and Perplexity, which have integrated Booking and Expedia inventory) rather than through travel apps. If the assistant owns the conversation, the travel app becomes a supplier of inventory and loses its pricing power and its advertising income. The B2C platform, ixigo, is most exposed. Its response has two parts. It has put its own apps inside ChatGPT as native apps and is building ixigo NEXT on its own small language models. It also leans on products that general assistants handle badly: trains and buses together produce more than 70% of its contribution margin, and 94% of its transactions involve tier-2 and tier-3 travellers who book Tatkal train tickets, waitlisted berths and regional buses rather than international hotels.

ixigo is also using AI on its own costs. Its TARA assistant resolved 94% of chats and 82% of calls end to end in Q2 FY27 and handled 43 lakh queries. Management is clear that this shows up as capacity rather than lower cost: technology spending was ₹14.3 crore in the quarter, about 0.27% of GTV, roughly where it has always been. TBO is less exposed because its customers are travel agents, not travellers, and an AI agent still needs hotel inventory, rates and contracts, which TBO aggregates. It has an AI centre of excellence and is building "Voya" tools for the US advisors Classic Vacations serves, and its hosting costs fell 13.7% in Q1 FY27. The risk for TBO is second-order: if AI assistants cut the number of small travel agents, its customer base shrinks.

Airline NDC and direct distribution

Airlines have spent a decade pushing bookings onto their own websites and onto NDC, the industry's direct-distribution standard, which lets them bypass the global distribution systems (Amadeus, Sabre, Travelport) that consolidators have used for decades. IndiGo and Air India sell heavily through their own apps. For TBO and ixigo the direct financial damage is limited because air already pays so little: TBO's air take rate is 2.3% and its air gross profit 1.25% of GTV, so air is a traffic product, not a profit product. The real risk is to ixigo's flight business, whose gross take fell from 9.4% to 7.0% in Q2 FY27. Part of that was high fares, because ixigo's fees are per ticket, but every point of direct share an airline wins also reduces the volume on which those fees are earned.

RailOne, UPI and IRCTC's own platform

The Railways' own passenger app, RailOne, built with the Railways' IT arm CRIS, is the disruption that matters most for the rail-linked members. It books reserved tickets through the same back end as IRCTC, and IRCTC's management named diversion to RailOne, together with agent restrictions and an ad-free new interface, as reasons non-fee ticketing income fell 5.5% to ₹113 crore in Q1 FY27. About 28% of IRCTC's tickets come through channel partners and online agents, and ixigo claims about 64% of the online-agent rail market, so roughly 18% of IRCTC's e-tickets pass through ixigo and ConfirmTkt (indicative). The Railways is therefore competing with both its listed toll collector and that collector's largest reseller. The Tatkal Aadhaar and OTP rules of July 2025 already showed how quickly a rule change can move volume away from agents.

Payments are a slower squeeze. A 2019 mandate charges ₹10 or ₹20 for UPI and BHIM bookings against ₹15 or ₹30 for other methods, and UPI's share of IRCTC transactions has crept from 48.7% to 51.2% in a year. Each extra point of UPI share costs IRCTC roughly ₹1 crore of fee revenue a quarter (illustrative). IRCTC is responding with a planned unified tourism-and-ticketing portal and a payment-aggregator subsidiary, IRCTC Payments, for which it expects RBI approval in FY27. Its own platform also carries outage risk during the Tatkal windows and cost: about ₹10 crore of platform-upgrade maintenance hit the Q1 FY27 ticketing margin.

Google, hotel chains and bed-bank disintermediation

Hotels are where intermediaries earn the most and where disintermediation is most contested. Branded chains push loyalty members to book direct, and Google's hotel and flight search sits above every travel app, charging for the click. India's hotel market is unusually fragmented, which protects the middlemen: independents hold about 73% of rooms and about 60% of room-nights sell below ₹2,000 a night (company-cited industry figures). Those hotels cannot afford their own distribution, which is why ixigo sees budget and mid-market hotels as its next growth line and why TBO's hotel take rate is 10.6%. The threat for TBO's international bed-bank business is the same one Hotelbeds and WebBeds face: hotel chains connecting directly to large agencies and to Expedia's B2B arm.

Forex digitisation and cards

Thomas Cook's retail forex business faces fintech forex cards (Niyo, Scapia, BookMyForex) and zero-markup credit cards that let travellers spend abroad without buying currency in advance. The numbers suggest some share loss in holiday cards: active prepaid card users fell from 1.3 million in FY25 to 1.2 million in FY26 and 1.1 million in Q1 FY27, and card loads fell from US$860 million to US$764 million in FY26, even as total forex turnover rose to ₹13,300 crore on wholesale and education business. Thomas Cook's response is to digitise its own product: a "Zero Forex Card", cards in 28 currencies, delivery through Blinkit, airport counters at Delhi, a Muthoot Forex partnership and 23.5% digital adoption. Its forex EBIT margin held at 45.3% in Q1 FY27, so the pressure is on volume, not yet on margin.

Bull vs bear: the same five forces, read both ways

The case for the sub-sector rests on a growing, under-penetrated travel market and a toll that throws off cash. The case against rests on a state that sets the rules, thin margins per booking and multiples that assume both will improve.

Each of the forces in this report cuts both ways. The outbound wave is a tailwind that a war and a weak rupee interrupted. IRCTC's toll is a cash machine whose owner is building a rival. The platforms' growth is real, but so far it has shown up in bookings rather than profit. The two columns below set out the case for the group as a whole; the table after them gives each company's bull and bear case and the number that would settle the argument.

The bull case

  • A long runway: US$16.44 billion of LRS travel spending in FY26, online channels at only about 42.5% of travel spending, and a 2% TCS on packages from April 2026 that has not yet shown up in volumes.
  • Operating leverage at the platforms: TBO's organic SG&A growth slowed for six quarters and adjusted EBITDA rose 77% in Q1 FY27; ixigo's contribution margins sit above its own bands.
  • Mix moving to hotels: TBO's hotels and ancillaries reached 67.9% of GTV, and ixigo's hotel GTV grew 159%.
  • Cash and balance sheets: IRCTC has ₹2,841.6 crore of cash and no borrowings; ixigo holds the Prosus money; Thomas Cook has company-basis net cash.
  • Hidden engines: Thomas Cook's forex and resorts earn about 54% of segment EBIT, and the scheme will put part of Sterling directly in shareholders' hands.

The bear case

  • The state sets the economics: the convenience fee, Tatkal and agent rules, RailOne, TCS, GST and FEMA enforcement all sit with the government.
  • A stalled toll: IRCTC's ticket counts have slipped for four quarters and its growth now comes from catering at a margin near 10%.
  • Thin and falling take rates: TBO's organic 6.0% and ixigo's 6.05% are under pressure from airlines, Google and AI assistants.
  • Profit lagging bookings: the platforms' FY26 revenue grew 47% and their PAT about 9%.
  • Macro exposure: the West Asia war, a rupee near record lows (record close about ₹95.7 in May 2026), a repo rate raised to 5.50% and domestic air traffic down 6.34% in August.

What to watch: leading indicators and the event calendar

The numbers that move first, the levels at which they change the story, and the dated events between now and the FY27 results.

If all three improve, the 2026 slump was a war-year blip. If they do not, the problems are structural.

Glossary: the travel-services vocabulary in plain English

The terms used in this report and in the four companies' filings.

TermMeaning
GTV (gross transaction value)The full value of tickets, rooms and packages booked through a platform. Not revenue: the company keeps only part of it.

Gross vs net recognitionGross: the full selling price is revenue and the supplier's cost is an expense (Thomas Cook packages, IRCTC catering). Net: only the fee or markup is revenue (IRCTC tickets, forex, most platform sales).
