Indian travel services: a toll, two marketplaces, a forex desk

A complete guide to the four listed companies that sell India its journeys: how IRCTC turns a railway ticket into an 83% segment margin and why that toll has stopped growing, how TBO Tek and ixigo earn a sliver of every booking from opposite ends of the market, why Thomas Cook makes most of its profit from currency and resorts rather than holidays, and what a war in West Asia, a weak rupee and a state that sets the rules did to all four in 2026.

00

The one-page view: four businesses that share a label

A state-granted toll, a global wholesale marketplace, a mass-market booking app and a currency-and-resorts group are filed together as "travel services". Almost nothing else about them is shared.

The four companies in this report all make money when Indians travel, and that is close to where the resemblance ends. Indian Railway Catering and Tourism Corporation (IRCTC) is the only platform allowed to sell Indian Railways' reserved tickets online, and it also runs on-board catering, the Rail Neer bottled-water business and rail tourism. TBO Tek is a business-to-business marketplace that sells hotel rooms and airline seats to about 33,700 travel agents a month in more than 100 countries, and since October 2025 it also owns a US luxury wholesaler, Classic Vacations. Le Travenues Technology, which trades as ixigo, is a consumer booking app that grew out of trains and buses into flights and now hotels. Thomas Cook (India), 64.77% owned by Fairfax, sells outbound holidays and corporate travel, runs India's largest non-bank foreign-exchange business, and owns Sterling Holiday Resorts and a souvenir-photography business in the Gulf and Asia.

Together they reported ₹17,518 crore of revenue in FY26, up 13.3% from ₹15,466 crore, ₹2,534 crore of operating profit (a 14.5% margin) and ₹1,928 crore of reported profit after tax, up just 3.5% from ₹1,863 crore. At the close on 21 October 2026 the four were worth ₹63,897 crore, or 33.1 times FY26 profit. Those totals hide a lopsided shape. IRCTC is under a third of the revenue but 72% of the profit and 57% of the market value. Thomas Cook is nearly half the revenue but only 7% of the value, because it books holiday packages at their full price and keeps about five rupees of operating profit per hundred. The two platforms, TBO and ixigo, together handled ₹55,502 crore of bookings (gross transaction value, or GTV) in FY26 but kept only ₹3,905 crore of it as revenue.

Five forces define the group in 2026, and this report is built around them. The first is IRCTC's convenience-fee toll and the Railways' pen. IRCTC sells about 14.58 lakh tickets a day and its internet-ticketing segment converted 82.6% of its FY26 revenue into segment profit, which made it 74.5% of the company's segment profit. But ticket counts have drifted down for four quarters, ticketing revenue grew only 0.6% in Q1 FY27 (the June 2026 quarter), and almost all of IRCTC's growth now comes from catering at a margin near 10%. Every lever that matters, from the fee to Tatkal booking rules to the Railways' own RailOne app, sits with the owner, which holds 62.40% of the shares.

The second is the outbound wave meeting a war and a weak rupee. Indians spent US$16.44 billion on travel abroad under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme in FY26, and the Budget cut the tax collected at source on overseas packages to a flat 2% from April 2026. Then the war in West Asia that began on 28 February 2026 closed Gulf airspace, and the rupee slid to about ₹94.9 to the dollar (19 October 2026), near record lows. Thomas Cook's long-haul holidays fell 28% in Q1 FY27 and its Gulf subsidiaries dragged pre-tax profit down 21%; TBO lost margin in its Middle East market. The third is air capacity and fares: IndiGo's December 2025 crew-rostering collapse, groundings and war rerouting left domestic passenger numbers 6.34% lower in August 2026 than a year earlier, which pushes fares up and volumes down. The fourth is platform economics, the take rates, product mix and revenue-recognition choices that decide how much of a booking a TBO or an ixigo keeps. The fifth is Thomas Cook as a forex-and-resorts business: its foreign-exchange and Sterling segments earned about 54% of its FY26 segment profit on about 10% of its revenue, and a scheme now before the courts will hand part of Sterling directly to shareholders.

In plain terms

Only one of these four companies earns a fat margin on every trip, and that is IRCTC, because the government lets it alone sell train tickets online. The other three are middlemen who keep between ₹2 and ₹25 of every ₹100 a traveller spends, depending on whether they sell flights, hotels or packages. In 2026 the middlemen were hit by a war, a falling rupee and expensive flights, while IRCTC's problem was quieter: it has stopped selling more tickets and its owner, the Railways, is building a rival app.

Two traps run through everything that follows. First, a booking is not revenue. TBO's ₹36,809 crore of FY26 GTV became ₹2,677 crore of revenue, and IRCTC does not report a GTV at all: the train fare passes straight to Indian Railways and only the fee and related income stay with IRCTC. Second, the four companies recognise revenue in different ways. Thomas Cook books holiday packages gross (the full price is revenue and the hotel and airline cost is an expense) but books forex net (only the spread is revenue). TBO books air net and, through Classic, a slice of US wholesale close to gross. Adding or comparing their revenue lines without adjusting for this produces nonsense, which is why the report leans on profit, gross profit as a share of bookings and cash conversion rather than on revenue growth.

01

How a trip becomes revenue: suppliers, middlemen and the fee in between

Every journey runs from a supplier that owns the seat or the room, through one or more intermediaries, to a traveller. Where a company sits in that chain decides how much of the fare it keeps.

A travel intermediary does not own the train, the aircraft or the hotel. It earns a fee or a markup for matching a traveller to inventory that someone else controls, and the size of that fee depends on how fragmented and opaque the supply is. Airlines are few, their fares are public and they would rather sell direct, so they pay intermediaries very little. Hotels are many, their prices are hard to compare and they need distribution, so they pay much more. A railway ticket sold by a state monopoly pays a fixed fee set by the state. The four listed companies sit at different points on this spectrum, which is why their margins range from about 3% to more than 80%.

The rail chain explains both IRCTC's strength and its limit. Because it is the only platform that can issue a reserved e-ticket, IRCTC collects its fee whether the passenger books on irctc.co.in or through an agent such as ixigo. In FY26 that fee came to roughly ₹989 crore (an indicative estimate built from the quarterly figures management gave on its calls: ₹252 crore, ₹251 crore and ₹247 crore in the last three quarters), with another ₹546 crore or so of non-fee income from payment gateways, advertising, loyalty programmes and co-branded cards. But IRCTC does not set the fee, the fare, the quota or the booking window, and it does not decide how many trains run. Its volume is the Railways' capacity, and its price is the Railways' decision.

TBO keeps 2.3% of an air booking but 10.6% of a hotel booking (Q1 FY27, consolidated); in gross-profit terms the gap is 1.25% versus 6.21% of GTV. Fragmented supply pays the intermediary roughly five times more.

The hotel and flight chain is longer and more contested. An airline seat can reach a traveller through a global distribution system, a consolidator, a travel agent and a consumer app, and each layer competes for a share of a commission that airlines have spent twenty years shrinking. TBO earned a take rate of only 2.3% on air in Q1 FY27 and a gross profit of 1.25% of air GTV; for most platforms air is the habit that brings customers back, not the profit. Hotels are the profit pool. India's hotel market, by a company-cited estimate from the research firm VIDEC, was about ₹1,57,100 crore in FY26, with independents holding 73% of rooms and about 60% of room-nights selling below ₹2,000 a night (a single-source, indicative figure). That fragmentation is exactly what pays an intermediary: TBO's hotels and ancillaries (H+A) line produced about 85% of its gross profit in Q1 FY27 on 68% of its bookings.

The money layer is the one most investors overlook. When a traveller buys foreign currency, loads a prepaid card or sends money abroad for a holiday or tuition, someone earns a spread, and someone holds the unspent balance. Thomas Cook's forex business turned over about ₹13,300 crore (₹133 billion) in FY26 and held about ₹1,800 crore of float on prepaid cards in Q1 FY27. IRCTC, similarly, holds customer and Railway money in transit and earned ₹277 crore of other income in FY26, much of it interest. That is why both companies report other income large enough to matter to their profit.

In plain terms

When you book a hotel through an app, the app keeps about one rupee in ten. When you book a flight, it keeps about two rupees in a hundred. When you book a train ticket online, IRCTC keeps a flat ₹10 to ₹30 however long the journey. And when you buy euros for the trip, the forex dealer keeps two or three rupees in a hundred. The rest goes to the hotel, the airline or the Railways.

02

A ₹17,518 crore revenue pool on top of a much larger travel bill

The listed four are a small slice of what Indians spend on travel. Their combined revenue has grown 72% in three years; their profit has grown less than their bookings.

Sizing Indian travel is harder than it looks, because every source draws the boundary differently and none of them matches what the listed companies report. The research firm Phocuswright put India's total travel market at US$41.7 billion in 2025, with online gross bookings of US$24 billion growing only 4%. IMARC used a different scope and reached US$51 billion for online travel alone in 2024. VIDEC, cited by ixigo, puts India's online share of travel bookings at about 42.5% in FY26. These are indicative numbers drawn from different definitions, and they should not be mixed or compared with company revenue. What they agree on is that India is a large market with low spending per head, and that online penetration has room to rise in hotels and buses even if it is already high in flights and rail.

The listed four are a thin layer over that spending. Their ₹17,518 crore of FY26 revenue mixes three very different things: IRCTC's ₹5,215 crore, which is mostly catering, water and tourism sold gross plus a ticketing fee; Thomas Cook's ₹8,398 crore, which is mostly holiday packages sold gross; and the two platforms' ₹3,905 crore, which is a slice of bookings. (Thomas Cook's ₹8,558 crore "total income" figure includes ₹156 crore of other income; revenue from operations is ₹8,398 crore, and that is the number used here.) The fastest growth came from the platforms. TBO's revenue rose 54.1% in FY26, but that was mostly the consolidation of Classic Vacations from 1 October 2025, which added about ₹534 crore in six months (an indicative figure built by adding up quarterly disclosures); TBO's own bookings grew about 19%. ixigo's revenue rose 34.4% on bookings up 25%. IRCTC grew 11.6% and Thomas Cook 3.2%.

The history of this revenue pool has two halves. The first is a recovery: Thomas Cook's revenue fell from ₹6,833 crore in FY20 to ₹795 crore in FY21 when COVID shut travel, and it took until FY24 to exceed the pre-pandemic level. IRCTC's revenue fell too, but its ticketing toll recovered as soon as trains ran, which is why its operating margin touched 47% in FY22, the year catering and tourism were still shut. The second half, from FY24 on, is a slowdown in quality. Combined operating profit rose only 6.2% in FY26 while revenue rose 13.3%, because three mix effects pulled the margin down at once: Classic Vacations brought in revenue booked near gross at a low margin, IRCTC's growth shifted to catering, and Thomas Cook's costs grew faster than its revenue. Combined profit after tax grew 3.5%.

The latest quarter shows how uneven growth has become. In Q1 FY27 (the June 2026 quarter) the four together grew revenue 8.0% to ₹4,745 crore, operating profit 2.7% to ₹638 crore and profit after tax 4.9% to ₹511 crore. Strip out TBO, whose Classic acquisition was not in the base quarter, and the other three shrank: revenue fell 1.7% to ₹3,819 crore and profit rose 0.9%. IRCTC grew revenue 18% but not profit; Thomas Cook's revenue fell 13%; ixigo's growth slowed to 13% in Q2 FY27 even as its bookings grew 22%. A sector-wide Q2 picture will not be possible until mid-November, when IRCTC and TBO are expected to report.

In plain terms

The four companies have sold a lot more travel since COVID, but each extra rupee of sales now brings in less profit than it used to. Part of that is accounting (TBO bought a business that records sales at a higher gross value), and part is real: IRCTC is growing through low-margin food, and Thomas Cook's holidays were hurt by the war.

03

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: four very different cost stacks

IRCTC keeps ₹27 of every ₹100 of revenue as profit; Thomas Cook keeps under ₹3. Most of that gap is how revenue is booked, not how well each company is run.

Because the four record revenue so differently, the most useful way to compare their cost structures is to take ₹100 of each company's revenue and follow it down to profit. IRCTC is two businesses in one cost stack. About ₹48 of every ₹100 goes on food, Rail Neer inputs and the licence fees and profit shares it pays the Railways for catering; staff take about ₹6 and other costs about ₹14, leaving an operating profit of about ₹32 (screener's 31.9% margin). Add ₹5.3 of other income, take off a rupee of depreciation, a fraction of interest and about ₹9 of tax, and ₹26.7 is left as profit (₹1,393 crore on ₹5,215 crore). The ticketing segment alone keeps about ₹83 of every ₹100 it earns; catering keeps about ₹10. As catering grows faster, the blended margin falls, which is what has happened every year since FY22.

TBO's organic business in Q1 FY27 shows the economics of a wholesale marketplace. Its ₹595 crore of organic revenue sat on ₹9,918 crore of bookings, a 6.0% take rate. About ₹35 of each ₹100 of revenue went to suppliers and other direct costs inside revenue, leaving gross profit of ₹64.7. Selling, general and administrative costs excluding share-based pay took ₹47, leaving adjusted EBITDA of about ₹17.8. Then depreciation (about ₹5), interest (₹2.5) and tax take it down to profit of about ₹8–9. The point of a marketplace is that the ₹47 should grow more slowly than gross profit: in Q1 FY27 organic SG&A grew 12% against gross profit growth of 16%, and only 4% in constant currency.

ixigo spends most of its revenue acquiring and serving customers. In FY26, ₹61 of every ₹100 went on performance marketing, discounts, payment-gateway charges and customer support, leaving a contribution margin of ₹38.6. Technology, people and administration (before share-based pay) took another ₹29, leaving adjusted EBITDA of about ₹9.8. Share-based pay, depreciation, tax and losses at the Freshbus associate, partly offset by ₹3.7 of other income, brought profit to about ₹5.8 (₹71.5 crore). That is the economics of a consumer app competing with MakeMyTrip and Google for the same searches.

Thomas Cook looks the weakest on this measure only because it books packages gross. About ₹72 of every ₹100 of revenue is the cost of the flights, hotels, ground services and currency it resells; staff take about ₹14 and other costs about ₹9; operating profit is about ₹5.1 (₹428 crore). Other income adds ₹1.9, interest and depreciation take ₹3, tax takes ₹1.3, and about ₹2.6 is left. Measured instead against the profit it makes on each segment, Thomas Cook is a decent business: its forex segment keeps ₹46 of every ₹100 of net revenue as EBIT, and Sterling keeps ₹24.

IRCTC's costs are mostly food: the ticket business costs almost nothing to run.
