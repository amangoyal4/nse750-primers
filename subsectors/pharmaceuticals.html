00

The one-page view: two industries that share a name

Indian pharma is a branded consumer business at home and a commodity manufacturing business abroad. Almost every number in this report follows from a company's mix between the two.

The 45 listed pharmaceutical companies in this report (47 names in the NSE 750, less one research lab with a one-off year and one that has just merged away) earned ₹4.28 lakh crore of revenue in FY26, grew it by about 11%, turned 24.4% of it into EBITDA and kept ₹61,480 crore as net profit. On 5 October 2026 the stock market valued them at ₹24.8 lakh crore, about 40 times those earnings. That multiple is high for a manufacturing industry and roughly double what global generic-drug makers fetch. Explaining why, and whether it holds, is the job of this report.

The starting point is that "Indian pharma" describes two businesses with opposite economics. The first is the domestic branded-generics market, worth ₹2.59 lakh crore at retail prices over the twelve months to August 2026. Nearly every drug sold in it is off-patent, yet it behaves like a consumer franchise: a doctor writes a brand name on the prescription, the chemist dispenses that brand, and patients rarely switch. Companies are allowed to raise prices on most products by up to 10% a year. A good Indian franchise earns 25–35% EBITDA margins and compounds at about 10% a year with little capital.

The second is the export business, about $31 billion in FY26, of which the United States is the largest single destination. In the US a generic is a commodity. The pharmacist dispenses the cheapest approved equivalent, three buying groups purchase about 90% of the country's generic volume, and once four or more makers are approved for a molecule its price collapses towards the cost of making it. Indian companies supply 47% of all US generic prescriptions, but they do so on thin, eroding margins, punctuated by occasional windfalls when they are among the first to launch a copy of a big drug.

Between these two poles sit the businesses that have tried to escape commodity economics: specialty and complex products (Sun's dermatology and oncology brands in the US, injectables, inhalers, biosimilars), contract research and manufacturing for global innovators (CDMO/CRDMO, led by Divi's, Laurus, Sai and Neuland) and chronic-therapy franchises in India (Torrent, Abbott, Eris). FY26 and FY27 form a hinge for four reasons. First, the gRevlimid windfall ended: a court settlement had allowed a handful of companies to sell limited volumes of generic lenalidomide, a blood-cancer drug, at high prices from March 2022, and unlimited generic entry from 31 January 2026 wiped that out. Dr Reddy's, Cipla, Zydus and Natco are the visible casualties. Second, the US Section 232 proclamation imposed a 100% tariff on patented drugs but explicitly exempted generics and biosimilars, removing the biggest tail risk for Indian exporters, at least until a Commerce Department review due within a year. Third, semaglutide went generic in India on 21 March 2026, starting a price war in the fastest-growing drug class in the world. Fourth, the US BIOSECURE Act became law on 18 December 2025, nudging Western drug developers away from Chinese contract manufacturers.

In plain terms

An Indian pharma company is worth a lot when Indian doctors keep prescribing its brands or when a global innovator cannot easily stop buying from it. It is worth much less when it sells interchangeable pills to American wholesalers who can switch supplier at the next tender. The industry's 40× price tag is really a blend: roughly 30× for the franchises and the innovator suppliers, nearer 15× for the generic exporters, and a premium on top for India's scarcity of large, cash-rich healthcare companies.

The five forces that define this sub-sector

  • The India annuity versus the US treadmill. Domestic branded sales grow about 10% a year at 25–35% margins because doctors choose brands and prices index upward. US generics erode every year, and each windfall (a first-to-file launch, a shortage, gRevlimid) fades to nothing. Mix sets the multiple.
  • Escaping commodity. The winners of 2020–26 made products that are hard to copy or sold services a customer cannot easily switch away from: specialty brands, injectables, inhalers, biosimilars, CDMO. GLP-1 peptides and BIOSECURE are the next leg.
  • Compliance as moat and landmine. A USFDA warning letter or import alert can freeze a company's US growth for two to three years. At home, the revised Schedule M manufacturing rules and the 2024 marketing code raise the floor and push out small players.
  • The supply-chain triangle. The US needs India's cheap generics (hence the tariff exemption). India needs China's ingredients: 73.7% of its API and starting-material imports. Tariffs, BIOSECURE and the production-linked incentive (PLI) schemes all act on this triangle.
  • Price control at home. The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority caps prices of about 384 essential medicines and limits other increases to 10% a year. So far the price lever has stayed positive (+6% in the quarter to August 2026), but it is the main policy risk to the India annuity.

01

From molecule to money: the value chain and where profit pools

A pill passes through six or seven hands between a Chinese chemical plant and a patient. The profit does not spread evenly; it collects where someone has to make a choice that is hard to reverse.

Pharmaceutical products come in a few forms. Formulations, or finished dosage forms, are what patients take: tablets, capsules, injectables, inhalers, creams, eye drops, patches. They are sold either as branded generics (in India and other emerging markets, where the maker's brand name matters), as unbranded generics (in the US and in European tenders, where it does not), or, rarely for an Indian company, as specialty brands still protected by patents. Upstream sit active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs, also called bulk drugs), the chemically active part of the pill, and the key starting materials (KSMs) and intermediates from which APIs are made. Alongside these are biologics and biosimilars (protein drugs made in living cells, where Biocon leads), vaccines (dominated by unlisted Serum Institute and Bharat Biotech) and peptides such as the GLP-1 drugs. Finally there are services: contract research (CRO), contract development and manufacturing for innovators (CDMO, or CRDMO when it includes discovery research) and contract manufacturing for Indian brand owners (Akums's business).

The money collects at Step 6 in India (the doctor's choice of brand) and at Step 4 abroad (an approval few others hold, or a filing a client cannot easily move). Steps 1–3 earn a manufacturing spread.

The two main selling channels look nothing alike. In India, a company ships to a carrying-and-forwarding (C&F) agent in each state, who supplies thousands of stockists, who supply about a million chemists. By trade convention the stockist keeps about 10% of the retail price and the retailer about 20%; trade margins on non-price-controlled drugs, especially unbranded "trade generics", are often much higher. Demand is created by medical representatives (MRs) who visit doctors, and the prescription is the moment of sale. In the US, the Indian maker's American subsidiary sells to one of three buying consortia (Red Oak, formed by Cardinal and CVS; WBAD, formed by Walgreens and Cencora; and ClarusONE, formed by McKesson and Walmart), which supply wholesalers and pharmacies, whose reimbursement is set by pharmacy-benefit managers and insurers. There is no doctor to persuade. The pharmacy simply dispenses whichever "AB-rated" equivalent is cheapest that month.

Read the chart from the bottom up and a pattern appears. The further a product is from the commodity chemistry, and the harder it is for a buyer to switch, the more of the value its maker keeps. A KSM plant competes with Chinese giants on cost and earns 10–18%. A company that owns the prescription decision in India earns 25–35% for decades. A company whose API is written into an innovator's US drug approval (a CDMO) earns similar margins because changing supplier would require the client to file with the FDA again. And a company with one of only two or three approvals for a US generic can earn 30–50% until the next competitor arrives, which is usually within a year or two.

That gives three durable sources of profit and one temporary one. The durable ones are the prescription decision (Indian brands and US specialty brands), regulatory lock-in (CDMO contracts and APIs referenced in customers' filings) and hard-to-copy technology (inhalers, sterile injectables, biologics, fermentation). The temporary one is exclusivity: a first-to-file 180-day exclusivity period or a limited-competition launch. The commodity middle (plain oral tablets for the US, bulk APIs) earns about its cost of capital in a normal year.

In plain terms

Profit in pharma goes to whoever controls a decision that is hard to reverse: the Indian doctor who writes a brand name, the global innovator whose approved drug names a particular factory, or the US regulator who has approved only three makers of a molecule. Everyone else in the chain is paid roughly for their manufacturing effort, and Chinese plants set the price of that effort.

Two consequences follow for investors. First, most Indian large caps are hybrids that own several links at once. Sun is an Indian franchise, a US specialty company and a US generics maker; Cipla is an Indian franchise plus a US respiratory business plus South Africa; Aurobindo is a US and European generics house with an injectables arm and a biosimilars start-up. Their blended margins hide very different businesses inside, so segment data matter more than group totals. Second, backward integration into APIs protects a formulation maker's supply and cost, but it does not create pricing power on its own. Aurobindo makes most of its own ingredients and still earns about 20%; Abbott India makes almost nothing and earns 27%.

02

A ₹4.7 lakh crore industry, split down the middle

Third in the world by volume, eleventh by value. Half of what India makes is consumed at home under brand names; the other half is shipped abroad, mostly as cheap generics.

The Department of Pharmaceuticals puts the industry's total turnover at ₹4,71,898 crore in FY25, growing at about 9.5% a year since FY21. India ranks third in the world by volume of medicines produced and eleventh by value, and supplies roughly 20% of the world's generic medicines by volume. Those three facts together describe the industry: enormous output, low unit prices.

Roughly half of that turnover is exported. FY25 exports were ₹2,45,962 crore (about $30.5 billion), against imports of ₹63,573 crore, a large trade surplus. In FY26 exports reached a record $31.1 billion, but grew only 2.3% in dollar terms, the slowest in years, and March 2026 alone was 23% below the previous March. In rupees that is roughly ₹2.7–2.9 lakh crore; sources differ on the conversion, so this report uses "about ₹2.7 lakh crore". Formulations and biologicals made up 79% of FY26 exports, with APIs and intermediates most of the rest. In August 2026 the export council, Pharmexcil, cut its 2030 target from $130 billion to $80–90 billion, citing geopolitics.

The domestic half is measured differently. The Indian Pharmaceutical Market (IPM) is the value of formulations sold through retail chemists, as tracked by two data vendors from stockist and chemist panels. On the PharmaTrac series used in this report, the IPM was ₹2,58,977 crore on a moving annual total (MAT) basis to August 2026, up 10.4% in value but only 1.0% in units. (MAT means the sum of the latest twelve months, recalculated every month; a "MAT growth" figure compares that rolling year with the twelve months before it.) The other vendor, IQVIA, publishes figures 5–10% different because its panel is different, so analysts must pick one series and stay with it. Over FY15–25 the IPM grew about 9–10% a year in value: roughly 2–3 points from volume, 4–5 from price and 2–3 from new launches (an indicative decomposition).

In plain terms

Do not add the domestic market and exports and call the total "Indian pharma". The domestic number is measured at the price a patient pays at the chemist, which includes 30% or more of trade margin; the export number is measured at the factory gate. The listed companies' revenue is different again, because it includes what their US and European subsidiaries sell, at US and European prices, from Indian factories.

The listed universe is large relative to the market. The 45 companies' combined revenue of ₹4.28 lakh crore is close to the DoP's whole-industry turnover, but that is partly because their revenue includes sales made abroad by their subsidiaries at higher overseas prices, and partly because it includes non-pharma lines (Zydus Wellness's consumer foods, Akums's contract manufacturing for other brand owners, Jubilant's US radiopharmacies). The domestic market itself is highly fragmented: the ten largest companies hold only about half of it, as Section 5 shows, and several of the biggest domestic players (Intas, Macleods, Micro Labs, USV) are not listed at all.

Growth is now running ahead of the long-run average at home and behind it abroad. The IPM grew 10–11% over the year to August 2026, faster than the 7.8–8.1% the data vendor itself forecast for calendar 2026, while exports barely grew. The listed companies' combined revenue rose 11.2% in FY26, but that blends Lupin's +23%, Torrent's acquisition-boosted +21% and the declines at Natco (−8%), Cohance (−13%) and Aarti Pharmalabs (−14%). Averages hide almost everything in this industry; the business model tells you more.

03

What drives demand: price at home, patent cliffs abroad

India's market grows mostly through price and new launches, not volume. America's generic market grows when big brands lose their patents and shrinks when too many copies arrive.

The single most useful chart for the India half is the decomposition of IPM growth. In the three months to August 2026 the market grew 11.9%: 6.0 points from price, 3.6 points from new products and only 2.3 points from volume. Indians are not consuming dramatically more medicine each year; they are paying more for the same medicines and switching to newer, pricier combinations and brands.

The price component is high for a structural reason. Drugs on the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM) are price-controlled and their ceiling prices move each April with the wholesale price index; in April 2026 that meant an increase of only 0.65%. Everything else, the large majority of the market by value, can be repriced by up to 10% a year. Companies use most of that allowance on their stronger brands. New products contribute because Indian companies launch hundreds of new brands and combinations every year, many of them line extensions of existing molecules. Volume is weak partly because the acute categories are shrinking: anti-infectives and respiratory drugs lost more than 5% in units in August 2026, a weak-season effect layered on top of a long-term decline in antibiotic use.

In plain terms

India's drug market grows about 10% a year, but only about two points of that is people taking more pills. The rest is companies raising prices by a few percent and persuading doctors to switch patients to newer, more expensive brands. That works as long as the government lets prices rise and doctors keep trusting brands, which is why price control and prescribing rules are the main policy risks to the domestic half.

Export demand: the patent cliff, shortages and emerging markets

American generic demand depends on a different calendar. When a branded drug loses patent protection (its "loss of exclusivity", or LOE), generic makers file to copy it, and the first few approved can take most of the volume at a price still well above cost. The size of the LOE calendar in a given year sets the opportunity for the whole industry. Demand also jumps during drug shortages: when a supplier exits or fails an inspection, the remaining makers can raise prices, as happened with cancer injectables such as cisplatin and carboplatin in 2023. Conversely, a wave of new approvals for the same molecule depresses prices for everyone.

Outside the US, demand is more like India's. In emerging markets (Africa, Latin America, Russia and the CIS, South-East Asia) Indian companies sell branded generics through their own field forces, with India-like margins but currency and payment risk; Ajanta, Caplin Point, Glenmark and Dr Reddy's (in Russia) are the main exposures. In Europe, generics are bought through tenders, most famously Germany's AOK insurance tenders, and price is everything; Aurobindo is the largest Indian player, and crossed €1 billion of European sales in FY26. Finally, CDMO demand comes from innovator drug companies outsourcing development and manufacturing, and depends on their pipelines, on biotech funding cycles, on the move away from Chinese suppliers, and on new modalities such as peptides, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and oligonucleotides.

The overall picture for 2026–27: domestic demand is steady and price-led, US generic demand is past a windfall peak and facing renewed erosion, emerging markets are compensating for the US, and CDMO demand is in a strong upswing driven by GLP-1 capacity needs and BIOSECURE. Companies' fortunes in FY27 will track which of these four pools they draw from.

04

Where ₹100 of revenue goes, and why China sits inside it

Three cost structures for three kinds of company. The biggest line is usually materials, and three-quarters of the imported ones come from China.

A pharma company's cost structure depends on what it sells and to whom. An Indian branded franchise spends little on materials, because a brand sells at many times its chemical cost, but a lot on people, because demand is created by thousands of medical representatives. A US generics exporter spends more on materials and on research (each new product needs a filing) and has almost no field force. A CDMO or API maker spends on materials, utilities and scientists, and passes much of its R&D cost to clients.

The sector's actual blended EBITDA margin in Q4 FY26 was an estimated 24.6%, down 117 basis points year on year as gRevlimid ended, on revenue of ₹88,400 crore for the quarter (B&K Securities).

The labels hide the economics of each line. "Materials" for a branded franchise is mostly the API and packaging for a pill sold at 3–5 times its cost; for an exporter it is closer to half the price, because the selling price has been competed down. "Staff" for a franchise means the field force: Mankind employs more than 18,500 MRs and Alkem about 14,500, and a fully loaded rep costs about ₹7–8 lakh a year (indicative). For a CDMO it means PhD chemists and quality-assurance staff. The scarce input across the whole industry is quality-assurance talent able to run a US-compliant plant, which is why inspection failures cluster at fast-growing companies.

The China inside the pill

India is a net exporter of medicines but a net importer of the chemistry behind them. China's share of India's imports of APIs, KSMs and intermediates rose from 68.2% in FY21 to 73.7% in FY25, and the value from ₹18,646 crore to ₹27,032 crore, despite five years of policy aimed at reducing it (DGCI&S data via IPA Newspack). The commonly quoted "70–80% of bulk drugs come from China" mixes value and volume; the 73.7% share of imports by value is the cleaner number. Dependence is deepest in fermentation-based antibiotics (penicillin-G, 7-ACA, clavulanic acid), vitamins and certain starting materials.

Policy has tried to close the gap. The production-linked incentive (PLI) scheme for bulk drugs, with an outlay of ₹6,940 crore from 2020, has attracted ₹5,211 crore of investment against ₹4,330 crore committed; 39 projects have been approved and 28 APIs commissioned, including penicillin-G, clavulanic acid and rifampicin. In plain terms

India sells the world its finished medicines but buys about three-quarters of its imported ingredients from China. When Chinese chemical prices rise, Indian pill makers' margins shrink; when Chinese factories overproduce, Indian ingredient makers suffer. Government subsidies have built some domestic capacity, but China's share of the import bill has gone up, not down.

Capacity and utilisation

There is no official utilisation series. Industry convention holds that US-focused oral-solid capacity at Indian generics makers runs at an indicative 60–70%, the legacy of a decade of price erosion and of plants built in the 2010s for a market that then shrank. Sterile injectable and inhaler capacity is tighter, because those plants are harder to build and to get approved. CDMO capacity is in an expansion cycle: Divi's third unit at Kakinada (peptides, contrast media, backward integration), Sai's Bidar campus (about 700 kilolitres of reactor volume, ~65% utilised in Q1 FY27), Neuland's commercial peptide plant (7,000 litres, commissioned mid-2026) and OneSource's GLP-1 pen lines (about $100 million). The risk with build-ahead capacity is the same as in any manufacturing industry: depreciation and staff costs arrive before the orders do, which is why Sai, Alembic and Aarti Pharmalabs show low current returns.

The hybrids

Most of the large companies combine three or four archetypes, and their valuations reflect the blend. Sun is a US specialty company (about 21% of sales), India's largest branded franchise (₹19,290 crore, +14%) and a generics maker in the US and emerging markets. The rulebook: price caps at home, inspectors and tariffs abroad

Four regulators in India and one in Maryland can change a pharma company's earnings more than any competitor. The rules cover price, quality, marketing and, since 2025, trade.

In India, the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO), headed by the Drugs Controller General of India (DCGI), approves new drugs and sets manufacturing standards under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act of 1940 and the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules of 2019; state drug controllers license individual factories. The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) sets prices under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order 2013. The Department of Pharmaceuticals runs industrial policy (the PLI schemes) and the marketing code. Abroad, the US Food and Drug Administration (USFDA), Europe's EMA and the UK's MHRA, and the WHO's prequalification programme decide which Indian plants can supply their markets.

Price control

The current essential-medicines list, NLEM 2022, covers 384 medicines (about 870 scheduled formulations). For each, NPPA sets a ceiling price equal to the simple average price of all brands with at least 1% market share, plus a 16% retailer margin. Ceilings move each April with the wholesale price index: +0.65% in April 2026, on about 767 formulations. All other drugs are "non-scheduled", and their prices may rise by up to 10% a year. The share of the IPM under direct price control is an indicative 15–18%. A 2019 order capped trade margins at 30% on 42 cancer drugs; a broader trade-margin rationalisation has been debated for years but not enacted.

The structure explains the industry's behaviour. Because ceiling prices are set at the average of brands above 1% share, a company whose brand is priced above average loses most when a molecule enters the list, and companies with big brands in essential molecules (cardiac, diabetes, anti-infectives) carry the most exposure. Companies respond by launching combinations and new strengths that sit outside the list, which is one reason "new products" contribute 3–4 points of IPM growth a year. The Jan Aushadhi scheme, with more than 15,000 government-backed outlets selling unbranded generics at 50–90% below branded prices, is about 1% of the market but a political signal against brand premiums.

Quality: the revised Schedule M

The revised Schedule M, notified on 28 December 2023, raised Indian manufacturing standards towards WHO and international norms. Large manufacturers (turnover above ₹250 crore) had to comply in 2024; MSMEs that submitted upgrade plans got until 31 December 2025. By then CDSCO had inspected more than 800 units and 252 testing labs, and after the deadline lapsed it told the states to begin enforcement. The trigger was a series of scandals: contaminated cough syrups made by Maiden Pharma linked to child deaths in The Gambia (October 2022) and by Marion Biotech in Uzbekistan (December 2022), and in October 2025 the Coldrif syrup made by Sresan Pharma, linked to more than 20 child deaths in Madhya Pradesh, after which its licence was cancelled and testing for diethylene glycol tightened. The effect on listed companies is mostly positive: the weakest small competitors either invest or close, and their volumes move to large brands and organised contract makers.

Marketing: the 2024 code

The Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical Marketing Practices (UCPMP 2024), notified in March 2024, bans gifts, travel and hospitality for doctors and their families, restricts free samples and requires disclosure of spending on medical education events. Companies file annual self-declarations, published by the Indian Pharmaceutical Alliance. Enforcement runs through industry-association ethics committees, which is weaker than a statute, but the Supreme Court's 2022 Apex Laboratories ruling already made freebies to doctors non-deductible for tax. The code favours companies whose brands win on science and field-force quality rather than on inducements.

In plain terms

At home, the government caps the price of about one-sixth of the market and limits price increases on the rest to 10% a year. It is also forcing small, poorly run factories to upgrade or shut and has banned the gifts that used to win doctors' loyalty. All three rules squeeze the weakest players and leave the large, well-run brand owners relatively stronger.

The USFDA: inspections as a business risk

For any company selling to the US, the FDA's inspection outcome is a direct earnings variable. After an inspection, the investigator issues a Form 483 listing observations. The FDA then classifies the inspection as NAI (no action indicated), VAI (voluntary action indicated: fix it yourself) or OAI (official action indicated). OAI can escalate to a warning letter and, in the worst cases, an import alert (number 66-40) that stops the plant's products at the US border. A site under OAI or a warning letter gets no new product approvals until it is cleared, typically after 18–36 months.

Trade: Section 232 and BIOSECURE

The US opened a Section 232 national-security investigation into pharmaceutical imports in April 2025. In August 2025, when India was hit with a combined 50% reciprocal and penalty tariff on most goods, pharmaceuticals were excluded. The proclamation that followed (White House fact sheet, 2 April 2026) set a 100% tariff on patented pharmaceutical products and their ingredients, effective 31 July 2026 for named companies and 29 September 2026 for everyone else, but explicitly excluded generic drugs, their ingredients and biosimilars. The Commerce Department must report within one year on whether to tariff generics. India is one of 20 partner jurisdictions eligible for zero duty on specified specialty medicines (rare-disease, fertility, cell and gene therapies, ADCs, veterinary). Sun's US innovative portfolio is the main Indian exposure to the patented-drug tariff.

The BIOSECURE Act, signed on 18 December 2025 as part of the FY2026 defence authorisation, restricts US federal contracting and funding links with "biotechnology companies of concern", identified through a designation process, with long phase-in periods for existing contracts (to about 2032). It does not ban American companies from using Chinese suppliers outright, but it makes them nervous enough to qualify alternatives, and Indian CDMOs are the obvious candidates. Fifty-five years in seven eras

A patent law of 1970 created the industry; an American act of 1984 gave it a market; inspectors, price wars and a pandemic then reshaped it three times.

Indian pharma's history explains its present shape. Each era left behind a type of company and a lesson that still shows in valuations.

  • 1970–2005, the process-patent era. The Patents Act of 1970 recognised only process patents for medicines, so Indian chemists could legally make any drug by a new route. Price controls under the 1970 and 1979 drug price orders kept domestic prices low, and the multinationals' share of the Indian market fell from about 70% to 20–25%. Ranbaxy, Cipla, Dr Reddy's, Sun, Lupin, Cadila (now Zydus) and Wockhardt were built in this window.
  • 1995–2005, the TRIPS transition. India joined the WTO and accepted a ten-year move to product patents, which began on 1 January 2005 with Section 3(d), a clause that blocks patents on minor variations of known drugs. In 2001 Cipla offered a triple-drug AIDS regimen for about $350 a patient a year, under a dollar a day, and changed global HIV treatment.
  • 2005–2013, the US generics gold rush. Indian companies filed patent challenges for 180-day exclusivity, bought US companies, and built plants for the American market. Daiichi Sankyo paid about $4.6 billion for control of Ranbaxy in 2008.
  • 2013–2016, the FDA crackdown. Ranbaxy pleaded guilty to felony charges and paid $500 million in 2013; warning letters and import alerts hit Wockhardt, Sun, Dr Reddy's and Ipca. Approvals stalled.
  • 2015–2020, the price-erosion bear market. US buyers consolidated into three groups, the FDA cleared its approval backlog, and price erosion reached 10–12% a year. The Nifty Pharma index peaked in April 2015 and fell an estimated 40–50% to its 2019–20 low; return ratios fell from above 20% to the low teens.
  • 2020–21, COVID. A Chinese supply shock, Indian export restrictions on 26 APIs (March 2020), the PLI schemes, a sharp re-rating and an IPO wave.
  • 2022–2026, recovery and re-rating. Easing US erosion, the gRevlimid windfall, consolidation of Indian brands, CDMO demand from China+1, and the GLP-1 boom took the Nifty Pharma price-earnings ratio to 40.6 in September 2026.

The Ranbaxy lesson

Ranbaxy's story is the industry's cautionary tale. Daiichi Sankyo paid about ₹19,800 crore to the promoters, at ₹737 a share, in June 2008; within three months the FDA had placed import alerts on two plants. Ranbaxy pleaded guilty in 2013, more plants were barred in 2013–14, and Sun Pharma bought the whole company in an all-stock deal worth about $4 billion including debt, announced in April 2014 and closed in March 2015. That deal made Sun the largest company in the Indian market, a position it still holds. A Singapore arbitration later awarded Daiichi about ₹3,500 crore against the former promoters, starting years of litigation. The lesson investors took away, that data integrity at a plant can destroy a franchise worth billions, is why FDA status is priced so heavily today.

After the gRevlimid cliff: the US generics reset Deep dive

For four years one cancer drug, sold in limited volumes at high prices, inflated the American profits of half a dozen Indian companies. It ended on 31 January 2026. What remains is the treadmill underneath.

To understand Indian pharma's US business, start with the law that created it. The Hatch-Waxman Act of 1984 lets a generic maker win approval with an abbreviated new drug application (ANDA), proving only that its copy behaves the same way in the body as the original (bioequivalence), without repeating clinical trials. A company that challenges a brand's patents first (a "Paragraph IV" filing) and wins or settles can earn 180 days of exclusivity as the only generic. Those two features made it possible for an Indian chemistry company with good lawyers to make a fortune from a single product.

The economics follow from the number of competitors. With one to three generic makers, prices typically hold at 40–70% of the brand's price. With six or more, they fall to 10–20%, close to the cost of goods plus freight. And the buyers are concentrated: since the 2013–16 consolidation, three purchasing groups (Red Oak, WBAD and ClarusONE) buy about 90% of US generic volume, so every contract is a negotiation with a customer that can move its entire order to a rival.

The price-erosion cycle

"Price erosion" is the annual decline in prices on a company's existing US products, measured like for like. It has swung widely over two decades. In 2010–14, a wave of patent expiries on blockbusters (Lipitor, Plavix, Singulair, Diovan) kept erosion in low-to-mid single digits. In 2015–19 it reached high single to low double digits, worst in 2016–17, as buyers consolidated, the FDA cleared a backlog of approvals, and price-fixing investigations made companies wary of raising prices. In 2023 it fell to about 1%, helped by drug shortages and the exit of weaker competitors (Akorn's bankruptcy in February 2023, Lannett, Teva's rationalisation). In 2026 it is back to mid-to-high single digits in crowded categories.

What gRevlimid was

Revlimid (lenalidomide) is Bristol Myers Squibb's multiple-myeloma drug, with US sales of about $8–9 billion a year before generics. Rather than fight every challenger to the end, BMS settled. The settlements allowed named generic makers to sell limited volumes from March 2022, with the permitted percentage rising each year, and unlimited quantities from 31 January 2026. Because volumes were capped, prices stayed close to branded levels: the few companies with a quota sold a cancer drug at near-brand prices and almost pure margin. Natco (the first filer, partnered with Teva), Dr Reddy's, Cipla, Sun, Zydus and Aurobindo all took part. Indian participants are estimated to have earned $150–400 million a year each at peak (dossier estimate; company-level figures were never disclosed).

When unlimited entry arrived, the price collapsed as in any commodity. The effect shows up clearly in the results. Cipla's North American sales fell from about $934 million to about $780 million in FY26 (−16.5%) and another 21% in Q1 FY27, when group profit fell 39%. Dr Reddy's North America fell about 22% in FY26 (51% in the fourth quarter), and Q1 FY27 profit fell 69%. Zydus lost both gRevlimid and gAsacol HD exclusivity; the latter alone takes out ₹400–500 crore of annualised high-margin revenue, and its Q1 FY27 margin fell to 24% with attributable profit down 36%. Natco, the most concentrated, saw revenue fall 8% in FY26 and 43–45% in Q1 FY27; management now calls Revlimid revenue "negligible" and guides FY27 profit to ₹700–750 crore, about half of FY26's ₹1,418 crore.

In plain terms

For four years a court settlement let a handful of companies sell a costly cancer drug in America at close to the branded price, because only a limited quantity of copies was allowed. What "normal" looks like after the windfall

Strip out the windfalls and a large Indian US-generics business looks like this: a few hundred approved products, 4–8% annual price erosion on the base, 10–40 launches a year, and a margin of 18–25% (indicative). Aurobindo is the purest example: 728 final approvals out of 888 filings, the largest ANDA book of any Indian company, and an EBITDA margin that has sat near 20% for years. Growth in that model comes from three places. The first is volume and launches: Zydus guides 30–40 US launches in FY27; Alembic grew US sales 49% in Q1 FY27 on seven launches after years of building plants. The second is complexity: products with fewer competitors because they are hard to make, such as injectables (Gland, Eugia, Caplin), inhalers (Lupin, Cipla, Glenmark's first generic Flovent HFA with 180-day exclusivity), long-acting and modified-release forms (Rubicon), and biosimilars (Dr Reddy's abatacept, targeted for US launch around end-2026). The third is leaving generics for brands.

Sun is the furthest down that third road. Its global innovative medicines (psoriasis drug Ilumya, alopecia drug Leqselvi, Cequa, Odomzo, Winlevi and the skin-cancer drug Unloxcyt) sold $1.42 billion in FY26, up 16.8%, about 21% of group revenue, and US specialty sales now exceed US generics. In April 2026 Sun agreed to buy Organon for $11.75 billion in cash, roughly doubling revenue to about $12.4 billion and turning a net-cash balance sheet into about 2.3× net debt to EBITDA. It is the boldest bet any Indian company has made on leaving commodity generics, and it carries integration and leverage risk alongside the Halol import alert.

In plain terms

A US generics business is a treadmill: prices on old products fall every year, so a company has to keep launching new ones to stand still. Occasional windfalls make some years look spectacular, but they always fade. The companies that have escaped the treadmill did so by making things that are hard to copy, such as injectables, inhalers and biosimilars, or by owning branded drugs outright, as Sun does.

Two policy questions hang over the reset. The US Commerce Department's review of whether to tariff generics is due by about April 2027; a tariff would hit the 47% of US generic prescriptions that Indian companies supply, though the IQVIA Institute's estimate that Indian medicines saved US healthcare $219 billion in 2022 alone is the industry's best argument against one. And the FDA's move to unannounced foreign inspections raises the probability of more warning letters, each of which freezes a site's launches. Investors should expect US earnings for the gRevlimid-exposed companies to trough in FY27 and to recover only as complex launches, biosimilars and GLP-1 exports replace what was lost.

India's branded-generics machine Deep dive

How a company turns an off-patent molecule into a brand that compounds for decades: field forces, chronic patients, annual price rises and serial acquisitions. And the four policy threats to the machine.

India is unusual among large drug markets. Almost everything sold is off-patent, yet the market is branded: the same molecule may be sold under dozens of brand names at different prices, and the brand on the prescription decides which one the patient buys. There are no pharmacy-benefit managers, little insurance coverage of outpatient medicines, and patients mostly pay out of pocket. So the decision-maker is the doctor, and the company's job is to win and keep the doctor's habit.

The machine that does that is the medical representative. An MR visits 8–12 doctors a day in an assigned territory, details the company's brands, leaves samples within the new code's limits, and checks that local chemists stock the product. A large company may run 15 or more "divisions", each a separate team of MRs focused on one specialty (cardiology, diabetology, gynaecology, dermatology) or one tier of town. Mankind has more than 18,500 MRs covering over five lakh doctors; Alkem about 14,500; Torrent about 7,000; Eris about 3,400 across 14 divisions; Corona about 2,671. The economics of the machine come down to how much each MR sells, the subject of the KPI masterclass in Section 13.

Why chronic beats acute

A course of antibiotics lasts a week; a diabetes or blood-pressure prescription can last for the rest of the patient's life. A company that wins a diabetologist's preference for a gliptin combination gets a repeat sale every month from every patient that doctor manages. Chronic therapies also grow faster (cardiac and anti-diabetic sales rose about 15% in August 2026 against 2–3% for anti-infectives and respiratory) and are less seasonal. The result is that chronic share has become the most watched quality marker in the domestic business.

Buy and build: the consolidation premium

Because brands are sticky, the fastest way to grow a domestic franchise is to buy one. The industry's reference price was set in 2010, when Abbott paid $3.72 billion, about nine times sales, for Piramal's domestic business. Since then the buyers have been mostly Indian. Torrent built its franchise through Elder's brands, Unichem's India business (₹3,600 crore, 2017), Curatio (2022) and finally J. B. Chemicals: KKR's 46.39% bought at ₹1,600 a share in January 2026, an open offer at ₹1,639, and a merger effective 8 July 2026 on a swap of 51 Torrent shares for every 100 J. B. shares. The deal valued J. B. at a fully diluted ₹25,689 crore, about 6.2 times its FY26 revenue of ₹4,148 crore and about 23 times its EBITDA, and Torrent funded the cash part with about ₹14,000 crore of new debt. Mankind paid an enterprise value of ₹13,630 crore for Bharat Serums & Vaccines in 2024. Eris assembled a chronic franchise from Oaknet (dermatology), Biocon's Indian brands (insulins, oncology, critical care), Swiss Parenterals (injectables) and MJ Biopharm (insulin devices).

The logic is that a brand's growth and margin improve in a larger company's hands: more MRs detail it, it reaches more doctors, and overheads are shared. J. B. itself is the case study. KKR bought control in 2020, added Novartis's ophthalmology brands (about ₹964 crore of sales), Sanzyme, Glenmark's Razel and Dr Reddy's paediatric brands, and turned a family company into a 27–28%-margin chronic franchise with brands such as Cilacar, Rantac, Metrogyl and Nicardia. Torrent's Q1 FY27 shows J. B. contributing ₹1,201 crore of revenue (+10%) and ₹424 crore of EBITDA (35%). The price of this strategy is leverage: Torrent's net debt went from almost nothing to about ₹12,900 crore, about 2.5× EBITDA at the FY27 peak, and its trailing P/E of 81 is depressed by deal-related charges.

Four threats to the machine

  • Price control. An expansion of the NLEM, or a general trade-margin cap, would hit the most profitable brands in essential molecules. Abbott (with concentrated mega-brands such as Thyronorm and Duphaston) and the cardiac and diabetes franchises carry most exposure.
  • Prescribing rules. The National Medical Commission's August 2023 regulation requiring doctors to prescribe by generic name was put in abeyance after protests, but it shows the direction of policy. In August 2024 the government banned 156 fixed-dose combinations.
  • Doctors moving into hospitals. As more doctors work for hospital chains, purchasing decisions move from the individual prescriber to the hospital formulary, which negotiates on price.
  • New channels. E-pharmacies (Tata 1mg, PharmEasy) and quick-commerce platforms shift bargaining power from MRs and chemists towards platforms that can steer patients to cheaper or private-label alternatives (an indicative risk; no market-share data yet).

None of these has yet dented the machine: price contributed +6 points to IPM growth in the quarter to August 2026, and the large franchises are growing faster than ever. But they explain why the market still values the best India franchise at about 30× EBITDA rather than the 50× it pays for the best CDMO.

In plain terms

Indian drug companies grow their home business by hiring more sales staff, pushing into chronic diseases and buying other companies' brands. Torrent's purchase of J. B. Chemicals for about ₹25,700 crore is the biggest example. The risks are political: the government could cap more prices, force generic-name prescribing or squeeze trade margins, and doctors are increasingly employed by hospitals that bargain harder than individual prescribers.

CDMO, China+1 and BIOSECURE: can India become the West's chemist? Deep dive

The market's favourite pharma theme pays 50× EBITDA for companies that make other people's drugs. The case rests on regulatory lock-in, a US law aimed at China, and the GLP-1 capacity scramble. The risk is that revenue is lumpy and order books are invisible.

A contract development and manufacturing organisation (CDMO) works for drug innovators. It develops the chemical process for a new molecule, makes material for clinical trials, and, if the drug is approved, manufactures the API (and sometimes the finished dose) commercially for years. A CRDMO also does discovery research for the client. The model's appeal is that once a CDMO's plant is named in an approved drug's filing, the client must file again with regulators and run validation batches to switch supplier. Most clients will not do that for a few percent of price, so a commercial molecule tends to stay with its CDMO for the life of its patent.

The signals from management are strong. Sai Life described "unprecedented demand" and a "significant increase in strategic conversations" in Q3 FY26. The numbers support it: Laurus's CDMO grew 67% in Q1 FY27, Neuland's revenue 116% and Divi's revenue about 32% at a 41% operating margin.

Funnels are the best available visibility. Sai has 34 commercial molecules, 11 in Phase III or pre-registration and about 155 in early stages, and serves 19 of the top 25 drug companies. Cohance has 10 Phase III molecules and a niche in payloads for antibody-drug conjugates. Aarti Pharmalabs has 57 CDMO projects with 22 customers. Because roughly 10% of Phase III molecules a year reach commercial supply (an indicative broker convention), a Phase III count is the nearest thing to a forward order book.

In plain terms

A CDMO is a specialist chemical factory hired by global drug companies to make their new medicines. Once a drug is approved with a particular factory named in its paperwork, swapping factories means redoing regulatory work, so the customer usually stays for years. A new American law that discourages reliance on Chinese suppliers, plus a global shortage of capacity for weight-loss drugs, has pushed more of this work towards India.

The risks the multiple ignores

The first risk is lumpiness. Because a few molecules carry each CDMO, years swing sharply: Neuland's revenue fell in FY25, rose 37% in FY26 and 116% in Q1 FY27. Piramal's CDMO revenue fell 10% in FY26 on client destocking before rising 19% in Q1 FY27. Cohance's revenue fell 13% in FY26 and 23% in Q1 FY27, when it made a loss, because two large molecules were destocked. Sai's CDMO grew only 6% in Q1 FY27. The second is concentration: Sai's top client is about 12% of revenue and its top ten 54%. The third is the biotech funding cycle: the 2022–23 drought in US small-biotech funding hurt Piramal and Syngene, and the conversion of requests for proposals (RFPs) into revenue takes 2–4 years. The fourth is invisibility: not one Indian CDMO in this report discloses an order book in rupees. Investors are pricing a pipeline they cannot see, using proxies such as molecule counts and management tone.

There is also a paradox at the heart of "China+1". India sells itself as the alternative to Chinese suppliers while buying 73.7% of its own imported APIs and intermediates from China. A CDMO making a complex intermediate in Hyderabad may still rely on a Chinese starting material. Western clients increasingly ask about the full supply chain, which rewards the backward-integrated (Divi's has invested about $2.5 billion in backward integration since 2018) and penalises those who are not.

How the market prices it

The CDMO group trades at about 51× EV/EBITDA on FY26 numbers, against roughly 25–30× for Lonza, 40× or more for Samsung Biologics and 10–15× for WuXi AppTec, which is discounted because of BIOSECURE (global multiples approximate). Divi's trades at about 70× EBITDA and 82× trailing earnings; Laurus at 61× and 97×; Sai at 51× and 87×. Those prices assume several years of 20%+ growth at stable or rising margins. Some of them will deliver: FY26 showed operating leverage at Divi's, Laurus and Neuland. But the history of contract manufacturing worldwide says that capacity follows demand, pricing tightens, and a cyclical downturn eventually tests the most richly valued names.

In plain terms

Investors are paying 50 to 70 times operating profit for India's best contract manufacturers because they expect years of fast growth from Western drug companies moving work out of China. The weak point is that none of these companies publishes an order book, revenues jump around with a handful of products, and a slowdown in biotech funding or a single lost customer can halve a year's growth.

The GLP-1 land-grab Deep dive

Semaglutide lost patent protection in India in March 2026 and in a handful of other countries this year. Indian companies are racing to sell it cheaply at home, export it to Canada and Brazil, and make its ingredients and pens for everyone else.

GLP-1 receptor agonists mimic a gut hormone that lowers blood sugar and appetite. Novo Nordisk's semaglutide (sold as Ozempic and Rybelsus for diabetes and Wegovy for obesity) and Eli Lilly's tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) have become the best-selling drug class in the world; semaglutide alone sells more than $30 billion a year. They are peptides, chains of amino acids made by solid- or liquid-phase synthesis, and are usually injected with a pen device, which makes them harder to copy than a tablet.

India's launch day

Novo's core Indian patent on semaglutide expired on 20 March 2026. On 21 March generics launched. Dr Reddy's had the first DCGI approval and launched Obeda, a pre-filled pen at about ₹4,200 a month. Zydus launched Mashema, co-marketed by Lupin under its own brands. Sun launched Noveltreat for obesity and Sematrinity for diabetes. Glenmark, Alkem (which took an early unit share of about 11%), Eris, Natco and others followed. More than five companies launched on the first day, at 70–90% below the innovator's price, and more than 50 brands were reported to be in the pipeline. The innovators had prepared: Novo partnered Emcure for a second Wegovy brand (Poviztra) in 2025, and Lilly partnered Cipla to co-market tirzepatide as Yurpeak.

Reading a pharma company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Two revenue equations, fifteen native measures, one US product launch and one Indian brand taken apart, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps, and a ten-minute routine for any quarterly result.

Because Indian pharma is several businesses under one name, there is no single equation for it. This section uses two: one for a US generics exporter, where revenue is the sum of hundreds of products sold at falling prices, and one for an Indian branded franchise, where revenue is what a field force can persuade doctors to prescribe. A short third equation covers CDMOs. Each KPI that follows is a term in one of these equations or a test of how well the equation converts into cash.

A note on the CDMO equation

A CDMO's revenue is the sum over projects of development fees plus commercial volumes times the contract price, and it is driven by how many client molecules move from one clinical phase to the next and how much demand each approved drug has. An ILLUSTRATIVE mid-sized CDMO with 40 development projects at ₹8 crore each (₹320 crore) and six commercial molecules at ₹180 crore each (₹1,080 crore) has revenue of ₹1,400 crore and, at a 30% margin, EBITDA of ₹420 crore. If it needs ₹2,000 crore of plant to do that, its asset turnover is only 0.7×, so return on capital sits at 15–18% while new capacity fills and rises above 25% once the lines are full. Investors in CDMOs are mostly betting on that fill.

In plain terms

A US generics business grows by launching new products faster than old ones get cheaper. An Indian franchise grows by adding salespeople, making each one more productive, and raising prices a little every year. A CDMO grows when its customers' experimental drugs succeed. Every useful pharma KPI measures one of those three engines, or how much of the resulting profit turns into cash.

3. US price erosion

What it is: the year-on-year decline in prices on a company's existing US products, excluding new launches. Formula: (this year's price on last year's portfolio ÷ last year's price) − 1, usually disclosed only as a management comment. Worked example: a ₹2,000 crore US base business with 6% erosion loses ₹120 crore of revenue before any launches; at a 55% gross margin that is about ₹66 crore of gross profit gone. What drives it: the number of competitors per molecule, buyer re-tenders, new FDA approvals for rivals, and shortages (which reverse it). Benchmarks (indicative): good is 3–4% or less; average 5–8%; weak 10% or more. How they compare: most companies describe erosion qualitatively. 9. Return on capital employed (ROCE)

What it is: operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + net debt), ideally excluding acquisition goodwill. Worked example: a company with ₹1,000 crore of EBIT on ₹4,000 crore of equity and ₹1,000 crore of net debt earns 20%. 11. Specialty share and top-product concentration

What they are: the share of US sales from specialty or limited-competition products, and the share of sales or profit from the single largest product. Formulas: specialty and limited-competition revenue ÷ US revenue; largest product ÷ total (or US) sales. Worked example: Sun's innovative medicines of $1.42 billion against total US sales of about $1.9 billion, much of which is specialty, put its US specialty share above 50%. Benchmarks (indicative): specialty share good above 40%, average 15–35%, weak below 10%; top-product concentration good below 10%, average 10–20%, weak above 25%. How they compare: Sun is the only company with a majority-specialty US business. Concentration is high at Lupin (generic tolvaptan was about a quarter of FY26 earnings), Natco (gRevlimid-led exports were an estimated 78% of FY24 revenue), Strides (top-three positions in about 37 products make 70% of US sales), Sai (top ten customers 54%), Supriya (top ten customers 49%) and Corona (27 "engine brands" make 70% of sales). Definition trap: companies rarely name their top product's sales, so concentration must be inferred from guidance changes, the size of an exclusivity loss, or analyst estimates; and "specialty" is defined loosely (some include any injectable).

In plain terms

A pharma company's profits are often far more concentrated than its long product list suggests. One drug can be a quarter of a year's earnings, and when its exclusivity ends, the company has to replace it from scratch. Look for the product the guidance revolves around, and ask what happens when it erodes.

12. CDMO order book and project funnel

Definition traps, collected

  • Screener EBITDA versus company EBITDA. Gaps of one to four points are common (Dr Reddy's 19.2% versus ~23%; Piramal ₹922 crore versus ₹1,135 crore). Pick a basis and stick to it.
  • One-offs inside revenue. Glenmark's FY26 revenue includes AbbVie licensing income; SPARC's is almost entirely a $195 million priority review voucher sale; Divi's FY26 included a ₹211 crore forex gain. Exclude them before comparing growth or margin.
  • One-offs inside profit. Eris's FY26 PAT includes a ~₹150 crore deferred-tax gain; Strides's FY25 PAT included a ~₹3,300 crore demerger gain; OneSource's FY26 is a reported loss of ₹74 crore whatever "adjusted" figure is quoted; Glenmark's PAT is after ₹2,266 crore of exceptional charges.
  • Gross margin proxies. 100 minus "material cost %" overstates gross margin and breaks for traded-goods models (Abbott) and licensing income (Glenmark).
  • Net cash. Strategic stakes counted as investments (Sun, Natco), deposits filed under other assets (Divi's, Dr Reddy's, Lupin, Alkem), leases counted as debt (Marksans) and customer advances counted as cash (Akums) all distort calculated figures.
  • Consolidated versus standalone. Abbott India, Pfizer, Alivus and Supriya are reported here on a standalone basis because their consolidated pages are empty or stale; Sanofi Consumer Healthcare has a December year-end.
  • Growth after acquisitions. Torrent's +21% includes about two months of J. B. Chemicals; Mankind's includes a full year of Bharat Serums; Emcure's domestic +10% is 6–7% organically.
  • IPM growth. PharmaTrac and IQVIA differ by 5–10% in level; IPM figures are MAT (moving annual total) unless stated; secondary and primary growth differ; quarterly comparisons are seasonal.
  • PCPM and chronic share. Self-defined numerators and denominators; compare each company only with its own history.
  • ANDA counts. Filed, approved, tentatively approved, commercialised and "products" are all different numbers.
  • US sales. Some companies report in dollars, some in rupees (currency moves alone can change growth by 3–4 points), some report "North America", and Sun's "US" includes specialty and generics together; do not add them twice.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in pharma results has a trap: a one-off licence fee in revenue, a tax gain in profit, a strategic stake counted as cash, a "productivity" figure defined to flatter. The defence is simple: read the notes, use the same definition for every company you compare, and treat any number a company invents itself as a trend line for that company only.

How to read a pharma company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue split by business (one minute). Pull India, US (in dollars), Europe, emerging markets, API and CDMO separately. The group total hides which engine moved. Red flag: the company stops disclosing a segment that used to be shown.
  2. India growth against the market (one minute). Compare domestic growth with PharmaTrac or IQVIA for the same quarter, and strip out acquisitions. Red flag: primary growth far above secondary growth (channel loading).
  3. US sales in dollars, and why (one minute). Look for named launches, exclusivity losses and erosion commentary. Red flag: a big jump with no named product (it is probably a one-off).
  4. Gross margin, against last quarter and last year (one minute). A move of more than 150 basis points needs an explanation: mix, a provision, a windfall ending. Red flag: margin up while India and specialty shares are flat.
  5. EBITDA margin and one-offs (one minute). Reconcile reported with adjusted EBITDA. Red flag: forex, licensing income or "other operating revenue" doing the work.
  6. R&D spend (thirty seconds). In rupees and as a share of sales. Red flag: a sharp cut in R&D that lifts margin (it borrows from future launches).
  7. FDA and regulatory updates (one minute). Any new 483, OAI, warning letter, import alert or EIR; for India, any NLEM, NPPA or licence action. Red flag: a key US site moving from VAI to OAI.
  8. Pipeline and launches (one minute). Filings, approvals and launches this quarter; for CDMOs, commercial molecules, Phase III count and capacity utilisation. Red flag: approvals rising but launches not.
  9. Cash and balance sheet (one minute). Operating cash flow against EBITDA, inventory and receivable days, net debt. Red flag: receivables growing much faster than sales, or net cash shrinking with no capex to explain it.
  10. Guidance and tone (one minute). Margin and growth guidance against what the quarter implies; what management stopped talking about. Red flag: guidance kept unchanged after a weak first half, with the gap pushed to "H2".
In plain terms

In ten minutes you can learn more from the segment table, the dollar US sales, the gross margin and the FDA paragraph than from the headline profit. Profit in pharma is often moved by one product, one provision or one licensing fee; the four engines underneath tell you whether the next year will be better or worse.

How to read the table

Start with the business model, not the size. The small grey line under each name gives the company's primary model. A 20% margin is excellent for a contract manufacturer (Akums earns 12%), ordinary for a US generics house and weak for an Indian franchise. Compare within models first.

Then check the margin against the basis. The EBITDA column is screener's operating profit, which excludes other income and is struck after some one-offs. Where a company's own number differs materially (Sun 30.3% against 28.2%; Dr Reddy's about 23% against 19.2%; Piramal 12.8% against 10.4%) the gap is noted in its profile. Glenmark's figures carry the ᵍ mark because its FY26 includes one-off licensing income; use its FY27 guidance of 21–22% instead.

Read growth and profit together. FY26 growth ranged from Rubicon's +36.6% and Neuland's +37.0% to Aarti Pharmalabs's −14.0% and Cohance's −13.0%. Profit tells a different story in places: Biocon earned only ₹369 crore on ₹16,927 crore of revenue because interest and amortisation from its Viatris deal absorb most of its EBITDA; Natco earned ₹1,418 crore but guides to roughly half that in FY27; Eris's profit includes a tax gain.

The balance sheets are strong. Twenty-four of the 45 companies are net cash and Akums is near debt-free; together the 45 hold about ₹20,000 crore more cash than debt. The levered minority is listed in the KPI section: OneSource, Piramal, Torrent (after J. B.), Biocon, Wockhardt and Eris are above 2× net debt to EBITDA, and Sun will join them after Organon.

The multiples span a factor of twenty. On EV/EBITDA the cheapest are Natco (8.3×, on a profit base about to halve), Lupin (10.7×, on a partly windfall year), Strides (12.4×), Aurobindo (13.6×), Aarti Drugs (13.8×) and Jubilant (14.1×). The most expensive are Divi's (69.7×), Rubicon (66.3×), Laurus (60.7×), OneSource (59.9×), Wockhardt (53.8×) and Sai (50.9×). On trailing P/E, Cohance (159×) and Laurus (97×) lead, while Lupin (15.4×) and Natco (14.1×) sit at the bottom. The extremes are not mistakes: they are the market's view of which earnings are about to grow and which are about to shrink.

The native columns explain the multiples. The key-KPI column picks the one operating number that matters most for each company (India growth against the market, US sales, a CDMO funnel, an ANDA count) and the USFDA column shows the regulatory overhang. Companies with live warning letters or import alerts at important sites (Sun's Halol, Zydus's Baddi, Granules's Gagillapur, Glenmark's Indore) trade at a discount to peers with similar growth, while those with no live action and a growing CDMO or India business trade at the top of the range.

Who is winning, and on which axis

Three maps, one per question: which large companies combine growth and margin, whether India exposure earns a higher multiple, and how much the market pays for a CDMO pipeline. Then a scorecard of the twenty largest.

With 45 comparable companies a single chart would be unreadable, so the maps below each answer one question for one slice of the universe. Labels are kept to the companies that matter for that question; the peer table has the rest.

Among the twenty largest companies, the top-right quadrant (growth above 12% and EBITDA margin above 25%) holds Torrent, Lupin, Zydus, Laurus, Ajanta, Mankind and Gland, with Divi's just inside on growth (Glenmark plots there too, but only because of one-off licensing income). Each got there differently: Torrent by acquisition, Lupin by limited-competition US launches, Zydus by India growth and US injectables, Laurus by CDMO scale-up, Ajanta by US and India together, Mankind by Bharat Serums, Gland by the recovery of its French CDMO, Cenexi. Sun, Abbott and GSK sit in the top-left: very profitable, growing at or below 11%. The bottom-left holds the two companies hit hardest by the end of gRevlimid (Cipla and Dr Reddy's) along with Aurobindo, Ipca, Biocon and Wockhardt. Several will move in FY27: Lupin's margin is guided down to about 25% as tolvaptan erodes; Zydus's fell to 24% in Q1 FY27; Divi's rose to 41% in the same quarter.

The second map tests the report's central claim: that the market pays more for Indian brands than for exported generics. The pattern holds broadly. Torrent, Corona, Mankind, GSK and Abbott, all majority-India, sit at 28–43× EV/EBITDA, while the US-heavy Lupin, Dr Reddy's, Zydus, Aurobindo, Natco and Strides sit at 8–15×. But the exceptions are instructive. Rubicon (almost entirely US) trades at 66× because its products are complex, its approvals convert at 88% and it grew 37%; the market does not treat it as a commodity exporter. Pfizer and Eris, both nearly 100% India, trade at about 16×, Pfizer because its five-year sales are flat and its pipeline is set by its parent, Eris because of acquisition debt and a 2026 de-rating. India exposure earns a premium only when it comes with growth.

The third map shows the CDMO premium at work. Divi's, Laurus, Sai and Neuland, all growing 13–37% with visible capacity additions, trade at 48–70× EBITDA. OneSource and Cohance shrank in FY26 but trade at 60× and 40× because investors are looking through to GLP-1 pens and ADC payloads. Concord (−12% revenue) holds 37× on the strength of its fermentation niche. At the other end, the commodity API makers (Aarti Drugs, Viyash, Alivus, Supriya) and Jubilant, whose Montreal plant problem has dented profits, trade at 14–24×, and the market effectively treats them as industrial chemistry companies. Shilpa (48×) and Wockhardt (54×) are priced on future approvals: Shilpa's European films, patches and biologics; Wockhardt's Zaynich antibiotic, approved by the FDA in May 2026.

In plain terms

The winners of FY26 were the companies that combined growth with high margins: Torrent, Lupin, Zydus, Divi's, Laurus and Ajanta among the large names. The market rewards Indian brand exposure and contract manufacturing with high multiples, but only when they come with growth; an India-only company growing slowly, such as Pfizer, is valued like a generics exporter.

The sector's multiple is a blend of very different prices. Taken apart, it says what investors believe about each business model, and what has to go right for today's buyers to earn a decent return.

Five reasons for the premium

  • The India annuity. A domestic branded franchise grows about 10% a year, with pricing power, little capital and almost no exposure to US regulators. Investors value it like a consumer-staples business, and every large company owns some of it.
  • Net cash. Twenty-four of the 45 companies are net cash and the group as a whole holds about ₹20,000 crore more cash than debt. Global generics peers such as Teva and Viatris still carry debt from 2015–16 acquisitions.
  • Returns. The best franchises and multinationals earn 30–60% ROCE, and the sector as a whole earns mid-to-high teens, above most global generics companies.
  • Scarcity. Indian investors have few large, cash-generative healthcare companies to choose from, and steady domestic fund inflows chase them. That premium is a feature of India's market, not of pharma economics.
  • Growth options. CDMO, GLP-1, biosimilars and specialty drugs give investors something to pay for beyond current earnings; the market capitalises those options generously, as the CDMO multiples show.

Buying Indian pharma at 40 times earnings works out well only if profits grow fast for a long time. If they grow 10% a year, roughly the Indian market's pace, an investor who pays today's price and later sells at a more normal 25 times would earn about 5% a year. The premium is justified for the companies with durable franchises and real growth engines, and harder to defend for those whose recent profits came from a windfall.

Valuation within the sector is also unusually dispersed, which creates both risk and opportunity. The market is paying 60–70× EBITDA for Divi's, Laurus and Rubicon and 8–15× for Natco, Lupin, Strides and Aurobindo. Both ends embed strong views: that CDMO growth will compound for years, and that US generics profits will shrink. History suggests both views are usually right in direction and often too extreme in degree, which is why the gap between the most and least expensive names tends to narrow in the year after an earnings disappointment at the top or a positive surprise at the bottom.

The global lens: India against Teva, Sandoz and Lonza

Indian companies supply almost half of America's generic prescriptions, yet the world's largest generics makers are American, Israeli and Swiss. Indian ones trade at two to three times their multiples.

The global pharmaceutical market is worth about $1.6–1.7 trillion a year (IQVIA). India produces about 20% of the world's generic medicines by volume but accounts for only about 2% of global value, which is the volume-versus-value gap seen at home writ large. In the US, Indian companies supplied 47% of all generic prescriptions in 2022 and about four in ten of all prescriptions, saving American healthcare an estimated $219 billion that year and $1.3 trillion over 2013–22 (IQVIA Institute). They supply the majority of US prescriptions for blood-pressure, mental-health, cholesterol and anti-ulcer drugs, and about 15% of US biosimilar volume. US generics are about 90% of prescriptions but only about 13–20% of spending (the Association for Accessible Medicines puts it near 13%).

The reasons for the gap are the same five as in the previous section, but the comparison sharpens them. Global generics companies are mostly US and European commodity businesses with legacy debt; Indian companies blend commodity exports with a domestic branded franchise that has no Western equivalent, carry net cash, and earn higher returns on a lower cost base. The contract manufacturers trade even above their Swiss and Korean rivals, which assumes they keep winning business moved out of China.

Technology and disruption

The threats to Indian pharma come less from new science than from new rules: American tariffs and onshoring, Chinese competitors moving up the value chain, and Indian prescribing reforms. The opportunities come from new molecules: peptides, conjugates and biosimilars.

Pharma is a technology business in which most Indian companies are fast followers. They do not need to invent molecules to prosper; they need to copy, scale and supply them earlier and more cheaply than anyone else. Disruption therefore matters in two ways: changes that make copying harder or less profitable, and new kinds of molecules that create fresh copying and contract opportunities.

The most consequential is the US policy turn. The 2026 tariff exempted generics, but the Commerce Department's review is due within a year, and other measures push in the same direction: a May 2025 executive order on "most favoured nation" pricing for branded drugs, direct-to-consumer pricing deals, the FDA's PreCheck programme to speed approvals of new US plants, and an August 2025 order to build a Strategic API Reserve. None directly hits Indian generics today. Together they signal that Washington wants more medicines made in America, which over a decade could erode India's share of US supply.

China is the second force. Chinese companies already set the price of most APIs and starting materials; they are now moving into biosimilars, peptides and complex injectables, often at lower prices than Indian rivals. BIOSECURE slows Chinese CDMOs in the US market, but it does not stop Chinese generic exporters, and Chinese biosimilar and peptide makers are targeting the same emerging markets as Indian companies.

On the opportunity side, three technologies matter most. Peptides (GLP-1s and their successors) need solid- and liquid-phase synthesis capacity that India is building quickly at Divi's, Neuland, Granules and Laurus. Antibody-drug conjugates need high-potency handling and conjugation chemistry, where Cohance, Piramal and SPARC have niches. Biosimilars are becoming easier to sell in the US as the FDA drops switching-study requirements for interchangeability, favouring scale players such as Biocon. Continuous manufacturing (Alkem's Enzene) and radiopharmaceuticals (Jubilant) are smaller but real niches.

Finally, AI-assisted drug discovery cuts both ways. It should increase the number of molecules innovators push into development, which is good for CRDMOs with deep chemistry benches, but it may compress the price of routine discovery services. And at home, the gradual move of doctors into hospital chains and of patients to e-pharmacies and quick-commerce apps shifts some bargaining power away from medical reps, which matters most for mass-market and trade-generics brands.

In plain terms

The biggest threats to Indian drug makers are political rather than scientific: America wants more medicines made at home, China is copying the same products more cheaply, and Delhi can cap prices or change how doctors prescribe. The biggest opportunities are new kinds of drugs (weight-loss peptides, cancer conjugates, biosimilars) that need exactly the large-scale, regulated chemistry Indian companies are good at.

Bull versus bear

The case for and against the sub-sector as a whole, at 40× earnings, after the end of gRevlimid.

The bull case

  • A compounding home market: the IPM grows about 10% a year on price and launches, chronic disease is rising (101 million diabetics), and the best franchises grow 13–20%.
  • Escape routes that work: Sun's specialty portfolio, Lupin's complex launches, Gland's injectables and Divi's custom synthesis show Indian companies can leave commodity economics.
  • A structural CDMO tailwind: BIOSECURE, China+1 and the peptide capacity shortage give Indian CDMOs a decade-long demand runway, already visible in Divi's 41% margin and Laurus's 67% CDMO growth.
  • GLP-1s: India, Canada, Brazil and other markets open in 2026–27; the US and EU follow in 2031–32.
  • Strong balance sheets: most companies hold net cash, funding acquisitions and capex without dilution.
  • The tariff exemption: the US explicitly spared generics and biosimilars, recognising its dependence on Indian supply.

The bear case

  • Valuation: 40× earnings needs about 18% annual profit growth for a decade to earn a normal return, while revenue grew 11% in FY26.
  • The US reset is not over: gRevlimid is gone, Lupin's tolvaptan windfall fades from late 2026, and price erosion is back to mid-to-high single digits.
  • Regulatory landmines: live FDA actions at Sun, Zydus, Cipla, Aurobindo, Glenmark, Granules and Cohance; unannounced inspections; a generics-tariff review due by 2027.
  • CDMO cyclicality: no order books are disclosed, revenues swing with a few molecules (Cohance and Piramal fell in FY26), and multiples of 50–70× EBITDA leave the group exposed to any slowdown.
  • China dependence: 73.7% of imported APIs and intermediates come from China, and Chinese rivals are moving into India's growth niches.
  • Domestic policy: price-control expansion, trade-margin caps or generic-name prescribing would hit the most valued part of the industry.

Weighing the two, the bull case is strongest for companies whose growth does not depend on the US generics market: the India franchises growing well ahead of the IPM, the CDMOs with visible capacity and molecules moving into commercial supply, and the specialty businesses with products few others can make. The bear case is strongest for companies whose recent profits came from a single exclusivity, whose key plants are under FDA action, or whose valuations already assume a decade of best-case growth. Most investors' mistakes in this sector have come from treating a windfall year as a new base or a regulatory problem as temporary.

In plain terms

The optimists see a fast-growing home market, a decade of Western drug companies moving work to India, and a new class of blockbuster drugs going generic. The pessimists see a sector already priced for all of that, an American business still shrinking after a windfall, and regulators in two countries who can change the economics overnight. Both are right about different companies, which is why picking within the sector matters more than the sector call.

US exports and price erosion. Pharmexcil's monthly data shows US shipments before companies report: about $9.5 bn in FY26, down from $10.5 bn (indicative). Price erosion is the yearly fall in the price of products already on sale. In the report's illustrative US model, 7% erosion takes ₹122 crore off a ₹1,742 crore base, close to the ₹158 crore that twelve new launches add. Cohance and Zydus both received warning letters in 2026.

Biotech funding sits one step further back, because small biotechs pay for development from venture money; the 2022–23 drought hit Piramal Pharma and Syngene. Funding rose about 75% in H2 FY26 (Piramal). With Indian CDMOs at roughly 35–60× earnings, a miss here moves Divi's, Sai Life, Neuland, Piramal Pharma, OneSource and Cohance sharply.

Nifty Pharma trades at 40.6× trailing earnings against a five-year median of 33.5×; above 45× without earnings upgrades, prices would be running ahead of profits.

The first is the price component of IPM growth: a fall below three points would be the earliest sign that price control or channel pressure is biting the India annuity. The second is FDA actions at the largest companies: one import alert at a key site of a top-15 company can change that company's next three years and the sector's mood.
