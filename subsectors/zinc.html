00

The one-page view: one listed company, a global market and three engines

On the NSE, "zinc" means Hindustan Zinc. But the price, the costs and the risks are set in London, Beijing, Jaipur and Delhi.

This sub-sector has a single listed member, Hindustan Zinc Limited (HZL), and that fact can mislead. HZL is not a company in a small niche. It is the world's largest integrated zinc producer, India's only primary producer of zinc and lead of any scale, and India's only primary silver producer. Its revenue moves with the London Metal Exchange (LME) price of zinc and lead, the London (LBMA) price of silver and the rupee, none of which it controls. Its profit depends on a set of forces that shape the whole global industry: a shortage of mined concentrate, a silver boom that has already turned, a cost curve on which HZL sits near the very bottom, and a state that takes more out of the business than any shareholder does. This report explains that whole industry, with HZL as the lens.

Start with the scale. In FY26 HZL mined 1,114 thousand tonnes (kt) of zinc and lead metal contained in ore, refined 851 kt of zinc and 197 kt of lead, and produced 627 tonnes of silver. Revenue was ₹40,844 crore, EBITDA ₹22,162 crore (54%) and net profit ₹13,832 crore. In the half year since, prices exploded and then turned: Q2 FY27 (Sep-26) brought revenue of ₹15,093 crore, up 77% year on year, EBITDA of ₹9,285 crore at a 62% margin, and profit of ₹6,412 crore, all records. The trailing twelve months to September show ₹53,364 crore of revenue and ₹20,830 crore of profit. At ₹555 a share on 13 November 2026 the company was worth ₹2,34,442 crore, 11.3 times trailing earnings and about 7.2 times trailing EBITDA after netting off ₹11,268 crore of cash.

The revenue mix corrects a common misreading. HZL is often described as a zinc company with a silver sideline; our own primer once put zinc at about 85% of revenue. Eight forces explain almost everything about this sub-sector, and the report is organised around them:

  • The concentrate squeeze. Mines have not kept up with smelters, so the treatment charge (TC) that miners pay smelters fell to $80 per dry tonne in the 2025 annual benchmark, the lowest in more than 50 years, and to below zero on the spot market (−$115 to −$150 in October 2026). An integrated miner-smelter like HZL pays no TC and keeps both halves of the margin (section 07).
  • Silver as a second engine, now reversing. LBMA silver rose from $30 an ounce (FY25 average) to $84 (Q4 FY26 average) and $118 at its peak, then fell to about $49 by early November 2026 (section 08).
  • A first-decile cost moat. HZL's zinc cost of production excluding royalty fell to $703 a tonne in Q2 FY27, but its own FY27 guidance of $825–850 implies the second half will run at roughly $875–925 (section 09).
  • The ownership triangle. Vedanta owns 60.71%, the Government of India (GoI) 27.92% and the public 11.37%. Dividends, a 3% brand fee and a pending vote to double inter-corporate lending limits decide where the cash goes (section 16).
  • India's galvanising boom against a global surplus. Indian zinc demand grows 6–8% a year; the world's will shift to a small surplus in 2027 (section 03).
  • Growth against reserves and leases. A plan to double refined capacity runs into reserves of roughly 12.5 years of output and mining leases that expire around 2030 (section 17).
  • The state's take. Royalty is HZL's largest single cost line (₹4,948 crore in FY26), and a 2026 law has just capped state mineral taxes (section 10).
  • Macro. The rupee, coal and energy prices after the 2026 West Asia war, and US tariffs (section 20).

In plain terms

Hindustan Zinc digs unusually rich ore out of Rajasthan, turns it into metal in its own smelters, and sells it at world prices. It spends about $700–950 to make a tonne of zinc that sells for $3,000–4,000, pays the state roughly another $500 a tonne in royalty, and gets silver almost free as a by-product. That gap makes it one of the most profitable metal producers on earth. The catch is that nearly every number that matters, from the zinc and silver price to the treatment charge to the rupee, is set by someone else.

Two warnings run through every section. First, Q2 FY27 was very likely a peak print. Zinc made its high in September 2026, after the quarter's close; silver averaged $57 in October and was nearer $49 by 9 November; and the company's own cost guidance implies a higher second-half cost. Second, the definitions are slippery. Mined metal is not refined metal, cost of production excludes both royalty and silver, the "realised" silver price is not the LBMA price, and the precious-metal profit share is on EBIT, not revenue. The KPI section collects these traps in one place.

01

Ore to ingot: how the metal earns, and who keeps the rent

The money in zinc is made at the mine face. Everything downstream earns a processing fee.

Zinc rarely occurs alone. In Rajasthan's sedimentary-hosted deposits it sits in sulphide ore with lead and silver, at combined zinc-plus-lead grades of around 7–8%, which is high by world standards. Turning that rock into saleable metal takes five steps, and each step is a separate business somewhere in the world. Understanding which step captures the value explains why integrated producers have done so well in 2025–26 while standalone smelters have closed.

Step by step

Mining. All five HZL mines are now underground. Rampura Agucha, discovered in 1977 and once the world's largest open-pit zinc mine, completed its move underground around 2018 and still produced a record 576 kt of metal in FY26. Sindesar Khurd is the silver-rich mine. Zawar, worked since antiquity, produced a record 214 kt; Rajpura Dariba 55.9 kt; and Kayad and Bamnia Kalan are smaller or in development. Mining cost is driven by development metres, explosives, diesel, ground support and contractor rates. HZL's ore grade, around 7.6% zinc plus lead in Q2 FY27 and about 7% across its reserves and resources, compares with a world average reserve grade that the company's Woodmac-sourced deck puts at 2.8%, down from 6.9% in the 1990s.

Concentration. Ore is crushed and ground to a powder and floated in tanks of reagents. Zinc and lead sulphides attach to air bubbles and are skimmed off as separate concentrates. Recovery, the share of metal in ore that ends up in concentrate, typically runs in the high 80s to low 90s per cent. HZL does not routinely sell concentrate; it feeds its own smelters. The exception since FY26 has been off-spec lead concentrate built up while a new mill at Rajpura Dariba stabilised: 24 kt sold in FY26 and 20 kt in H1 FY27.

Smelting and refining. Most zinc is made by the hydrometallurgical route: concentrate is roasted to drive off sulphur (which becomes sulphuric acid), the calcine is leached in acid, purified, and the zinc plated out by electrolysis in cell houses that consume enormous amounts of power. HZL also runs a pyrometallurgical smelter at Chanderiya (a record 164 kt in FY26) and lead smelters at Dariba and Chanderiya, where silver is separated from lead bullion. Refined capacity is 1,129 ktpa: zinc 919 and lead 210.

By-products. Silver is the big one. Sulphuric acid matters more than it looks: HZL sold about ₹1,000–1,200 crore of it in FY26, and acid prices tripled in 2026 after the Strait of Hormuz closure and a Chinese export ban. Because HZL reports its zinc cost net of acid and other non-silver by-products, an acid windfall shows up as a lower cost of production.

Selling. HZL sells to continuous galvanising lines at the steel majors, to thousands of hot-dip galvanisers, to die-casters, brass makers and battery makers, and to silver traders and fabricators. Prices are linked to the LME (zinc, lead) and to the international silver price, plus a domestic premium that reflects what an importer would pay including the 5% basic customs duty on zinc. On the company's numbers, implied zinc realisations have run a steady 5–8% above LME. About 30% of refined zinc (251 kt of 851 kt in FY26) and 23% of lead were exported; silver is sold almost entirely in India.

Who keeps the rent

Economists call the excess of price over the full cost of production a "rent", and in zinc the rent belongs to whoever owns a low-cost orebody. In Q2 FY27 each tonne of zinc sold for about $3,825 on the LME. HZL spent $703 producing it, the state took $525, and the remaining $2,600 or so was margin. A custom smelter that processes someone else's concentrate earns only the treatment charge, the "free metal" it recovers above the share it pays for, and its by-products. At the 2026 benchmark TC of $85 per dry tonne of concentrate, that processing revenue works out to only about $155 per tonne of zinc, before the smelter has paid for its own power, labour and reagents. At October 2026's negative spot TCs, the processing fee is below zero.

Silver is even closer to pure rent. It rides in ore that HZL mines for zinc and lead, so almost all of its cost is already counted in the zinc and lead accounts. After a royalty of 7% of the international price (plus District Mineral Foundation and exploration levies on top of that royalty) and the cost of refining, nearly every rupee of silver revenue becomes EBITDA. In H1 FY27 silver revenue was ₹7,950 crore and precious metals' contribution, at about 45% of a ₹17,359 crore EBITDA, was roughly ₹7,800 crore.

The state also captures a large share. HZL's contribution to the exchequer, including royalty, the District Mineral Foundation (DMF), income tax, GST and other levies, was about ₹19,000 crore in FY26, more than its ₹13,832 crore net profit; more than ₹6,000 crore of that went to Rajasthan. Add the Government of India's 27.92% share of dividends and the Indian state takes more out of HZL each year than any single shareholder does.

In plain terms

Owning the mine is what matters. When concentrate is scarce, a smelter without its own mine has to accept whatever terms the miners offer, and right now the miners are offering almost nothing. HZL owns both the mines and the smelters, so it simply moves ore from one to the other and keeps the whole margin. The government, through royalty and taxes, takes a slice before anyone else.

Three measures of volume run through every HZL disclosure and should never be mixed. Mined metal is the zinc and lead contained in ore at the mines (1,114 kt in FY26). Refined metal is zinc and lead ingot out of the smelters (1,048 kt = 851 zinc + 197 lead). Silver is reported separately as refined saleable silver in tonnes (627 t). None includes the others, and the difference between mined and refined metal is mostly smelter recovery losses, stock movements and, since FY26, lead concentrate sold rather than smelted.

02

A 14 Mt world, a 1 Mt India: size, growth and price

Zinc is the fourth most-used metal on earth. India uses only a small share of it, but that share is growing fast.

The world consumed about 14.0 million tonnes (Mt) of refined zinc in 2026 on the International Lead and Zinc Study Group's October 2026 estimate: demand of 14.02 Mt against refined output of 13.93 Mt and mine output of 12.40 Mt. At the 2026 average LME price of roughly $3,500–3,700 a tonne, that is a market worth about $50 billion, or close to ₹4.9 lakh crore a year (indicative). Refined lead is a similar-sized market by tonnage, about 13.8 Mt, but at roughly half the price; more than 60% of it is recycled from old batteries. Silver is tiny by tonnage, around 26,000 tonnes (about 830 million ounces) a year, but at $50–60 an ounce it is worth a comparable sum, and about 70% of mined silver comes as a by-product of lead-zinc, copper and gold mines.

The single most important line in that table is the gap between mine output (12.4 Mt) and refined output (13.9 Mt). Smelters can make more metal than miners dig only by drawing on secondary zinc and concentrate stockpiles. That 1.5 Mt gap is the structural reason treatment charges collapsed: smelters are competing for too little feed. It also explains why ILZSG's 2027 forecast matters: mine output is expected to grow faster (+3%) than refined output (+2%) next year, which would start to ease the squeeze and nudge the balance into a small surplus.

Price: a decade in two charts

Zinc has traded between about $1,900 and $4,000 a tonne (annual averages) over the past decade. On HZL's April–March fiscal years, using World Bank LME-based data for the years before FY25 and the company's own figures after, the average went from $2,746 in FY19 down to $2,404 in FY20, up to $3,248 in FY22 and $3,334 in FY23 (the European energy crisis), down to a trough of $2,480 in FY24, and back to $2,875 in FY25 and $2,970 in FY26. H1 FY27 averaged $3,651 and the monthly average peaked at $4,021 in September 2026. One correction matters here: FY23 averaged $3,334, higher than FY22, not lower as some earlier tables show.

India: a 1 Mt market growing 6–8% a year

India consumes about 1.0–1.15 Mt of zinc a year once secondary (recycled) metal is counted, on estimates from BigMint (about 1.0 Mt in 2025) and the International Zinc Association (about 1.1 Mt). On a narrower primary-metal basis, HZL's domestic sales plus imported ingot, apparent consumption was about 795 kt in FY26 (indicative), up from 595 kt in FY22 on Indian Bureau of Mines data. That is roughly 7.5% a year. India is about 7–8% of world zinc demand; HZL's refined zinc is about 6% of world refined output and its mined metal about 9% of world zinc-plus-lead mine output (indicative).

Lead consumption in India is roughly 1.3–1.6 Mt a year (indicative), most of it secondary, recycled from used batteries by firms such as Gravita and Pondy Oxides and by battery makers' captive plants. HZL's 197 kt of refined lead is a minority of the total. Silver is the other way round: India is one of the world's largest silver importers, at roughly 4,000–7,000 tonnes a year depending on prices and duty, so HZL's 627 t meets perhaps 10–15% of domestic demand (indicative).

In plain terms

Zinc is a mature global market that grows about 1–1.5% a year and swings with China. India is a small but fast-growing corner of it, adding demand at 6–8% a year as it builds roads, towers, roofs and railways. Hindustan Zinc's revenue tracks the world price, not Indian demand; Indian demand decides how much it can sell at home at a premium instead of exporting.

For a single-company sub-sector, the "sector" totals are simply HZL's: FY26 revenue ₹40,844 crore, EBITDA ₹22,162 crore, PAT ₹13,832 crore and market capitalisation ₹2,34,442 crore. In a broader sense, the Indian zinc economy also includes about ₹7,000–8,000 crore a year of imported zinc ingot (about 0.2 Mt), the secondary zinc and lead recyclers, and the galvanisers who consume most of the metal; the market value of all zinc used in India is roughly ₹38,000 crore a year at current prices (indicative).

03

Galvanising India against a surplus world: the demand story

Indian demand decides how much HZL can sell at a premium at home. It does not decide the LME price.

Most zinc is used to stop steel rusting. Hot-dip and continuous galvanising coat steel with a thin layer of zinc that corrodes in its place; it is the cheapest durable protection for structures exposed to weather. Globally, galvanising takes about 50–60% of zinc, die-casting alloys about 15%, brass about 15%, chemicals and zinc oxide (for tyres, rubber, ceramics and feed) 6–8%, with rolled zinc and batteries making up the rest. In India the galvanising share is higher, about 72% on BigMint's 2025 estimate, because the country's die-casting, brass and chemical industries are smaller. The Indian Bureau of Mines, which counts coatings separately, put galvanising at 57% plus coatings at 16% in its last full yearbook.

Driver 1: steel and the infrastructure build-out

India's crude steel output was about 152 Mt in FY25 and roughly 168 Mt in FY26, with the National Steel Policy targeting 300 Mtpa of capacity by 2030. HZL's deck, citing CMIE, maps a path of 182 Mt in FY27 and 195 Mt in FY28 (an indicative mapping). Galvanised and coated flat products, including galvanised plain and corrugated sheet, colour-coated roofing, galvalume, and structural sections for transmission towers, solar module mounts, highway crash barriers, metro and railway electrification and pre-engineered buildings, are the fastest-growing grades. Government capital spending has risen about 2.6 times in six years, and effective central capex for FY27 is budgeted at about 4.4% of GDP. A rule of thumb (indicative): hot-dip galvanised structural steel uses about 30–40 kg of zinc per tonne, continuous galvanised sheet about 8–10 kg.

Driver 2: autos, renewables and batteries

Only about 20–25% of Indian vehicles use galvanised body steel, against 90–95% globally, according to the International Zinc Association. Export-grade platforms and longer corrosion warranties are pushing that share up, and each step adds zinc demand. Solar mounting structures and wind towers are galvanised; transmission lines for renewable power need galvanised towers. Lead goes overwhelmingly (80–85%) into lead-acid batteries for vehicle starting, inverters, telecom towers and e-rickshaws. Silver's demand has shifted toward industry: industrial uses were 58% of global silver demand in 2025, up from 46% in 2022, with photovoltaics alone about 17%, according to the World Silver Survey 2026 as cited by HZL.

Driver 3: per-capita catch-up

On one consistent refined-metal basis, world zinc use is about 1.7 kg per person (14.0 Mt across about 8.2 billion people) and India's about 0.7 kg (about 1.0 Mt across 1.45 billion); on primary metal alone India is nearer 0.55 kg (indicative). Figures of 3–4 kg sometimes quoted are developed-economy levels, not the world average. If India merely reached the world average, its demand would more than double, which is consistent with the International Zinc Association's view that Indian demand could exceed 2 Mt within about ten years.

The catch: India's demand does not set the price

The tension in this force is simple. Indian zinc demand is among the fastest-growing anywhere, but India is only 7–8% of the world market, and price is set on the LME by the global balance, in which China accounts for about half of both demand and refined output. Chinese property construction is the largest single swing factor in world zinc demand. When the LME rallied in 2026, China flipped to exporting refined zinc, which capped the squeeze. ILZSG now expects a small 25 kt surplus in 2027, as mine supply recovers. India's growth therefore supports HZL's volumes, its domestic premium of 5–8% over LME and its ability to sell more at home rather than export, but not the LME price itself.

There is a quieter consequence. HZL exports about 30% of its zinc because domestic demand is still smaller than its output. India also imported about 195–207 kt of zinc ingot a year in FY25–FY26, around 70% of it from Korea under the India–Korea trade agreement, plus about 82 kt of zinc scrap in FY25. As Debari adds 250 ktpa from Q2 FY29, HZL will need either faster Indian growth or more exports. At 7.5% a year, domestic primary demand reaches about 1.06 Mt by FY30 (illustrative), enough to absorb Debari and displace most imports. Slower growth would push more metal into export markets, where it earns the LME price without the Indian premium.

In plain terms

Every new roof, tower, highway barrier and solar frame in India needs zinc, and India is building a lot of them. That lets HZL sell most of its zinc at home at a little above the world price. But the world price itself is decided by China and by how much new mine supply arrives elsewhere, and the forecasters expect the world market to slip into a small surplus in 2027.

Lead and silver demand follow their own logic. Lead faces slow substitution by lithium-ion batteries in two- and three-wheelers, telecom backup and storage, and the world lead market is in surplus (ILZSG sees 163 kt in 2027), so the lead price has been flat at about $1,850–2,000 a tonne. Silver demand is torn between a strong industrial thesis, solar panels and electronics in a sixth straight annual market deficit (about 1.44 kt in 2026 on the figure cited by HZL), and the risk that the 2025–26 price spike accelerates "thrifting", the solar industry's steady reduction of silver per watt. Section 08 deals with silver in full.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: royalty first, then power, then the rest

The largest single cost in Indian zinc is not coal or labour. It is the state's royalty on every tonne of metal in the ground.

A mining company's cost structure tells you what it can control. For HZL, most of the cost of producing zinc is in rupees: underground mining, milling, smelting consumables, power from captive coal plants and renewables, and wages. Royalty and its add-ons, which are calculated as a percentage of the international metal price, sit outside the headline "cost of production" but inside the P&L. Because revenue moves with prices while most costs move with Indian inflation and the rupee, margins swing far more than revenue does.

Imports and China

India imports roughly 0.2 Mt of refined zinc a year, about 70% from Korea under the India–Korea Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement and the Asia-Pacific Trade Agreement, which carry concessional duties. China matters three ways: its concentrate imports set the tone for global TCs; its refined-zinc exports, when the LME-Shanghai arbitrage opens, cap international price spikes, as they did in 2026; and its 2026 ban on sulphuric-acid exports lifted global acid prices, which helped integrated smelters' by-product credits.

In plain terms

At home, HZL faces almost no competition from other producers; its only real rival is imported Korean metal. Abroad, it is one of the three biggest zinc miners and one of the five biggest smelters, and unlike most rivals it owns both stages. Five ways to earn from zinc, and why only one is winning now

Integrated miners, concentrate sellers, custom smelters, recyclers and galvanisers sit at different points of the same chain and profit from opposite conditions.

Every company in the zinc chain earns money in one of five ways, and the right way to analyse each is different. The key distinction is exposure to the treatment charge: low TCs transfer value from smelters to miners, so the same market condition can be a windfall for one model and a disaster for another. Understanding the archetypes also explains why HZL's margins look so different from those of the global companies that appear in the peer table.

The custom smelter owns no mine. The recycler buys scrap (used batteries, dross, ash, brass) at a discount to the LME, refines it and sells at an LME-linked price. Why the smelters are dying and the miner is minting Deep dive

Treatment charges hit a 50-year low in 2025 and went negative in 2026. That one number explains most of HZL's record margin and most of the industry's distress.

The treatment charge is the price a smelter charges a miner to turn concentrate into metal. It is set each year in a "benchmark" negotiation, traditionally between Teck and Korea Zinc or a European smelter, and traded day to day on a spot market for concentrate delivered to China. When concentrate is plentiful, smelters can charge $200–300 per dry metric tonne (dmt) of concentrate. When concentrate is scarce, miners can push the TC down. In 2025 the annual benchmark fell to $80/dmt, the lowest in more than 50 years. In 2026 it was settled at $85. On the spot market, charges went negative: by 9 October 2026, Fastmarkets reported spot TCs for concentrate into China at a record −$115 to −$150 per tonne. Smelters were paying miners to take their ore.

How the treatment charge works

A custom smelter buys zinc concentrate, typically 50–55% zinc, and pays for a "payable" share of the zinc content, usually about 85%, at the LME price. From that payment it deducts the TC. It then recovers more than 85% of the zinc, perhaps 95–97%, and keeps the difference, the "free metal". It also keeps the by-products: sulphuric acid, and sometimes silver, germanium or indium (miners are increasingly charging for these too; in 2026 Teck added a germanium payable). And it sells its refined metal at LME plus a regional premium. The smelter's gross processing revenue is therefore the TC plus the free metal plus by-products plus the premium, and from that it must pay for power, reagents, labour and capital.

ILZSG estimates world zinc mine output fell 2.6% to 12.40 Mt in 2026, after rising 6.3% in 2025, because of problems at Antamina in Peru, seismic disruption at Boliden's Garpenberg, declining grades at Red Dog and the closure of Lady Loretta in Australia in December 2025. Glencore's own-sourced zinc is down about a quarter. Meanwhile refined output rose 0.9% to 13.93 Mt, driven by China (+3.7%). Smelters outside China cut output by about 2% because they could not get feed on viable terms.

What HZL gained, and the concentrate it sold

HZL pays no treatment charge because it smelts its own concentrate. It therefore captures the full value of the metal, the combined margin of a miner and a smelter. The opportunity was visible in an unusual line of revenue. While its new 1 Mtpa mill at Rajpura Dariba stabilised, HZL built up off-spec lead concentrate that its smelters could not easily take. Rather than derate the smelters, it sold the material: 24 kt in FY26 and 20 kt in H1 FY27. Because high-silver lead concentrate traded at deeply negative TCs, buyers effectively paid for the silver. The economics were rich: EBITDA of about ₹250 crore in Q3 FY26 and about ₹330 crore in Q4 FY26 (on roughly ₹500 crore of revenue), revenue of ₹315 crore in Q1 FY27 and ₹300 crore of revenue for ₹155 crore of profit in Q2 FY27. Management is clear that this is not a change of policy ("We still don't sell concentrate") and that the stock is nearly exhausted, so this line should fade in the second half of FY27.

In plain terms

Miners and smelters argue each year over the processing fee. Because the world is short of ore, the miners have won so completely that smelters are now paying miners to take their ore. Smelters that own no mines are closing or losing money. HZL owns its mines, so it never pays the fee, and it even made a few hundred crore a quarter selling surplus lead ore to desperate buyers.

When the cycle turns

Treatment charges have always mean-reverted, because low TCs eventually close smelters (reducing demand for concentrate) and high metal prices encourage new mines. ILZSG's forecast of 3% mine growth in 2027 against 2% refined growth suggests the squeeze could ease next year; the 2027 benchmark negotiation, normally concluded in the first quarter, is the first test. When TCs rise, value will shift back from miners to smelters. For HZL the effect is mostly an internal reallocation, so its total margin should hold up better than either a pure miner's or a pure smelter's. The bigger risk for HZL from a normalising concentrate market is indirect: if mine supply recovers enough to push the zinc market into surplus, as ILZSG expects for 2027, the LME price itself would soften.

There is also a strategic lesson for India. HZL's leadership has argued for years that integration is its moat, and 2025–26 has proved it more decisively than any previous cycle. That shapes the growth plan: Debari's 250 ktpa expansion comes with its own mine expansion (mined metal capacity rising from 1,180 to 1,510 ktpa), so that new smelting capacity is fed from HZL's own ore, not bought-in concentrate. A smelter-only expansion would have been a bet on TCs recovering; an integrated one is not.

In plain terms

Fees for processing ore go up and down over many years. When they recover, smelters will earn more and miners less. Because HZL is both, it mostly moves profit from one pocket to the other. The real danger is not the fee but the zinc price, which could fall if new mines bring more ore than the world needs.

A silver miner disguised as a zinc company Deep dive

Silver is a quarter of revenue and close to half of profit. It came almost free with the ore, it quadrupled in price, and it has since halved.

HZL's silver comes out of lead concentrate, above all from Sindesar Khurd, whose ore is unusually rich in silver. It is recovered in the lead smelters at Dariba and Chanderiya and sold as refined silver bars, almost entirely in India. Because the ore is mined for its zinc and lead anyway, silver carries almost no extra mining cost; after a royalty of 7% of the international price plus district and exploration levies, and the cost of refining, nearly all of the revenue drops to profit. When silver was $20 an ounce, that made a pleasant bonus. At $60–80 it made silver the second engine of the business.

From 24 tonnes to a top-ten producer

HZL made just 24 tonnes of silver in FY06. Sindesar Khurd's ramp-up took output to 207 t by FY12, and the company climbed from 23rd among global silver producers to the top ten. Output then rose to 647 t in FY22, 714 t in FY23 and a peak of 746 t in FY24, before falling to 687 t in FY25 and 627 t in FY26. The FY27 guidance is 680 ±10 t; management "can hope" to reach 730–750 t in FY28 through debottlenecking; and the long-term vision is 1,500 tonnes a year. H1 FY27 produced 321 t of refined silver, plus about 19–20 t contained in the lead concentrate sold, which management rounds to "340, you can assume".

The industrial thesis, and its limit

The case for silver staying high rests on industry. Industrial demand rose from 46% of the global total in 2022 to 58% in 2025; photovoltaics are about 17% of demand, and solar installations have grown roughly ninefold since 2016. The market is in its sixth straight annual deficit, about 1.44 kt in 2026, and about 70% of mined silver comes as a by-product, so higher prices do not quickly bring more supply. Against that, the solar industry cuts silver use per watt every year, and copper metallisation and silver-coated copper are in trials; a price spike like 2025–26 accelerates that thrifting. Investment demand, the most volatile component, also drove much of the rise and much of the fall. HZL's decks lean on the bullish half of this argument; investors should hold both.

Should HZL trade like a precious-metals company?

Part of the logic of HZL's 2023 proposal to demerge into separate zinc-lead, silver and recycling companies was that a pure silver business might earn a precious-metals multiple. The Mines Ministry rejected the plan in March 2024, HZL trimmed it to two verticals, and it has been dormant since; no scheme was ever filed. The market has instead treated silver as a cyclical bonus: when silver peaked, HZL's market value reached about ₹3.1 lakh crore; at ₹2.34 lakh crore on 13 November it was 24% below its 52-week high share price of ₹733. The reversal in silver accounts for much of that.

In plain terms

For two years, HZL was really a silver company in zinc clothing: silver made almost half its profit. That was wonderful while silver went from $30 to over $100, but every dollar the silver price falls takes about ₹200 crore a year off profit. Silver has already halved from its peak, so the coming quarters will show how much of the record profit belonged to zinc and how much to a silver boom.

The first-decile cost moat, and how much of it is luck

HZL's zinc cost fell 33% in two years. Part of that is better mining. Part is acid prices, the rupee and grade, which can reverse.

HZL describes its position, citing Woodmac, as "first decile" on the global zinc cost curve: among the lowest-cost tenth of the world's production. The numbers support it. Its zinc cost of production excluding royalty, which is net of sulphuric acid and other non-silver by-products and includes smelting, was $1,122 a tonne in FY22, rose to $1,257 in FY23 on European-crisis coal prices, then fell to $1,117 in FY24, $1,052 in FY25 and $959 in FY26. In H1 FY27 it was $776, with Q2 at $703, the lowest since the underground transition. The FY27 guidance was cut in October from $975–1,000 to $825–850.

Why the cost fell: the structural part

Some of the decline is durable. Mined metal rose to a record 1,114 kt in FY26 and 271 kt in Q2 FY27 (a record second quarter), spreading fixed costs over more tonnes. Ore grade improved to about 7.6% in Q2 FY27. Rampura Agucha and Zawar set production records; Balaria had its first million-tonne ore year; the Sindesar Khurd crushing circuit was debottlenecked from 460 to 520 tonnes per hour; and cell-house current was raised from 200 to 212 kA at Chanderiya and Dariba, adding about 21 ktpa of capacity. Renewable power rose from 18% to 27% of consumption, and HZL invests in automation: tele-remote raise-boring at Sindesar Khurd (a world first), battery-electric vehicles underground, AI-assisted froth flotation and digital twins.

Why the cost fell: the cyclical part

Three tailwinds can reverse. The first is sulphuric acid. HZL nets acid revenue against its zinc cost, and acid prices rose about 200% year on year in 2026 after the Hormuz closure and China's export ban. The CFO said on the Q2 call that the acid windfall was a "positive surprise" that offset about $50 a tonne of geopolitical cost inflation. The second is the rupee. Roughly 80% of HZL's costs are in rupees (indicative), so a weaker rupee mechanically lowers the dollar cost: in rupee terms the zinc cost fell from ₹86,800 a tonne in Q2 FY26 to ₹67,141 in Q2 FY27, down 23%, against a 29% fall in dollars. The quarterly average rate moved from ₹87.31 to ₹95.57. The third is grade, which varies quarter to quarter with mine sequencing.

In plain terms

HZL's cost per tonne of zinc fell from about $1,050 to $700 in eighteen months. About a third of that is because the rupee weakened, which makes rupee costs look smaller in dollars, and a good chunk is because the acid HZL sells as a by-product suddenly became very valuable. Those two help today but can reverse. The rest, more ore, better grades, cheaper power and automation, is the part that should last.

The second-half warning

The revised FY27 guidance of $825–850 contains a message the headline missed. With H1 at $776, the full-year range mathematically requires H2 to run at roughly $875–925 a tonne, about 25–30% above Q2's $703 (indicative arithmetic). Management expects H2 to be "broadly in the same range" as the guidance. Reasons include lower domestic coal (32% of plant coal in Q2), imported coal prices, higher mine development, and the likelihood that acid prices ease from their peaks. Investors anchored on $703 will be surprised.

Where HZL sits against the rest of the curve

Global comparisons are hard because companies publish different cost measures. HZL's ex-royalty cost includes smelting and credits acid but not silver. In plain terms

The state's share: royalty, district funds, disputes and a new mineral-tax law

The Indian state takes more out of HZL than any shareholder. A 2026 law has capped one risk; a ₹2,560 crore royalty dispute and the 2030 leases remain.

Mining in India is a partnership with the state whether a company likes it or not. Minerals belong to the state; leases are granted for fixed terms; royalties are charged on what is extracted; and a share of royalty goes to District Mineral Foundations for the communities around the mines. On top come corporate tax, GST, dividends on the government's stake, and, until August 2026, the risk of new state taxes on mineral rights. For HZL the result is that the state's take exceeds the company's profit.

Royalty: how it is charged

Royalty rates for major minerals are set in the Second Schedule of the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act 1957 (MMDR), with the current rates effective from 1 September 2014. For zinc the rate is 9.5% of the LME zinc price, charged on zinc contained in ore produced (10% if levied on concentrate). For lead it is 8.5% on lead in ore (14.5% on concentrate). By-product silver pays 7% of the international silver price on silver actually produced (primary silver mines pay 5%). On top, the District Mineral Foundation takes 30% of royalty for leases granted before 12 January 2015 (10% for later ones) and the National Mineral Exploration Trust 2% of royalty. A 2013 Supreme Court judgment in a dispute between Rajasthan and HZL confirmed that royalty applies to metal in ore produced, not to tailings. There is no royalty on sulphuric acid, so higher acid revenue lowers royalty as a share of total revenue.

The mineral-tax scare, and the law that ended it

On 25 July 2024 a nine-judge Supreme Court bench ruled 8:1, in Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India, that royalty is not a tax and that states have the power to tax mineral rights and mineral-bearing land. A follow-up order on 14 August 2024 allowed states to recover arrears back to 1 April 2005, in instalments over twelve years starting 1 April 2026, with interest and penalties waived. For a miner in a mineral-rich state, that opened an open-ended liability. No Rajasthan levy aimed at HZL was found, but the risk was real: a cess of 1% of value on HZL's FY26 revenue would have cost about ₹400 crore a year, and 5% about ₹2,000 crore (illustrative).

Parliament then closed the door. The MMDR (Amendment) Bill 2026 passed the Lok Sabha on 12 August 2026 and the Rajya Sabha on 13 August. Its new Section 9D bars states from levying any tax, cess or levy on mineral rights or mineral-bearing land, whether based on quantity, value, royalty or otherwise, except under conditions the Centre prescribes, and it retrospectively invalidates levies not yet deposited or recovered (without refunds of amounts already paid). It takes effect on notification; presidential assent and notification had not been confirmed in the research for this report, and mineral-rich states are expected to challenge it. HZL's own exposure under the old regime was small: a provision of ₹56 crore for Rajasthan's Environment and Health Cess (₹150 a tonne of ore under 2008 rules, rescinded in 2017), booked in FY25, was reversed in FY26 on legal advice and is now a contingent liability. Our coal and iron & steel reports discuss the same law from the miners' side.

The disputes that remain

The live state-level exposure is now a dispute over royalty methodology, not a new tax. A separate ₹311 crore demand went against HZL at the Revisionary Authority on 25 July 2024 and is stayed by the High Court with ₹100 crore deposited. Leases, compliance and the other rules

HZL's legacy mining leases were extended to 2030 under the 2015 amendment; after that, new leases are auctioned. Indian Bureau of Mines records show Rampura Agucha expiring on 12 March 2030. Management said on the Q4 FY26 call that it is "comfortable because we have the first right of refusal… it's only a matter of how much premium we are ready to pay." The statutory basis of that right was not verified, but the sensible framing is that 2030 is a question of the price of renewal, an auction premium on top of royalty, rather than of losing the mines. Section 17 returns to it. There are also compliance frictions: in October 2025 the Bureau issued a show-cause notice on the Kayad lease for generating about twice the approved waste and deviating from the production plan; the notice formally lists lease termination among possible sanctions, though the issue appears procedural.

Three other rule sets matter. SEBI's listing rules require shareholder approval, with related parties abstaining, for related-party transactions above ₹1,000 crore or 10% of turnover, and specifically for brand or royalty payments above 5% of turnover; HZL's 3% fee sits below that threshold. New extended-producer-responsibility rules for aluminium, copper and zinc scrap took effect on 1 April 2026, with recycling targets of 10% in 2026-27 rising to 75% by 2032-33, which will gradually formalise the secondary zinc market. And trade rules, from the 5% customs duty on zinc ingot to US tariffs and the EU's carbon border adjustment, shape where HZL can sell (section 20).

In plain terms

The state takes about ₹12 of every ₹100 HZL earns as royalty, plus taxes and its share of dividends, adding up to more than the company's own profit. A 2024 court ruling threatened to let states add new mineral taxes on top; Parliament blocked that in August 2026. What is left is a ₹2,560 crore argument with Rajasthan about how royalty was calculated, and the question of what HZL will have to pay to keep its mines after 2030.

Six decades of cycles: from a state miner to a 62% margin

Every boom in zinc has been followed by a bust. What has changed is the cost base, the silver and the ownership.

HZL's history explains its present. It was a state company for 36 years, privatised in two steps in 2002–03, ran through a China-driven super-cycle with a growing cash pile, spent a decade moving its biggest mine underground while paying out huge dividends, rode an energy-crisis spike, slumped in FY24, and then caught the twin booms of collapsing TCs and soaring silver in 2025–26. The zinc price has swung between about $1,450 and $4,900 a tonne across that period. The lesson of the history is that HZL's profit is mostly a function of where in the price cycle it stands, and that each peak has been followed by a period of returning cash rather than building.

The state era (1966–2002)

Hindustan Zinc was incorporated on 10 January 1966 as a public-sector company, taking over the assets of Metal Corporation of India, including Zawar (mined since antiquity, with modern operations from the 1940s). The Debari smelter came in 1968. The transformative event was the discovery of Rampura Agucha in 1977, one of the world's richest zinc orebodies, commissioned around 1991 alongside the Chanderiya smelter (1991–92). For most of this era the company was a modest domestic supplier behind tariff walls.

Privatisation and the super-cycle (2002–2011)

In April 2002 the government sold 26% to Sterlite, part of the Vedanta group, for about ₹445 crore, with management control; an open offer for 20% followed. In November 2003 Sterlite exercised a call option for a further 18.92%, taking it to 64.92%, a stake it held unchanged for more than twenty years, until June 2024. The government kept about 29.5%. The timing was fortunate: the China-led commodity super-cycle took zinc to about $4,500 a tonne in November 2006, and HZL expanded smelting at Chanderiya and Dariba while its cash pile grew. In November 2021 the Supreme Court allowed the government to sell its residual stake through a transparent process and ordered a CBI inquiry into the 2002 sale.

Treasury era and the underground move (2012–2020)

By FY15 HZL had zero borrowings and ₹27,254 crore of investments. Sindesar Khurd became a world-class silver mine. Rampura Agucha moved from open pit to underground, with the pit closing around 2018. Cash was returned through very large dividends (payouts of 144–149% in FY16–FY17 and 113% in FY21). The cycle was violent: zinc fell to about $1,450 in January 2016 after Glencore's 500 kt supply cut and the closure of Century and Lisheen, then rallied to $3,500 and above in early 2018 as those closures tightened the market, with TCs falling to a 12-year low.

Reading a zinc miner: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Sixteen numbers explain an integrated zinc-lead-silver producer. Each has a formula, a benchmark and at least one trap.

An integrated zinc producer's profit can be written as one equation, and almost every disclosure the company makes is a term in it. The equation has three revenue engines (zinc, silver, lead), a cost that the company controls, a royalty that the state sets as a share of price, and an exchange rate that converts dollar prices into rupee profit. Management controls volume, grade, recovery, cost and the ore mix. It does not control the LME or LBMA price, the treatment-charge market, the royalty rate or the rupee. Reading HZL well means knowing which term moved each quarter, and whether the move was earned or given.

1. Mined metal and mine utilisation

What it is. The tonnes of zinc and lead contained in ore hoisted from the mines in a period. Formula: ore mined × grade (zinc + lead). Utilisation = mined metal ÷ mined-metal capacity. Worked example (illustrative): about 14.6 Mt of ore at 7.6% contains about 1,110 kt of metal; HZL's FY26 output of 1,114 kt against roughly 1,180 ktpa of capacity is about 94% utilisation. What drives it: development metres (how much new ground is opened), hoisting and crushing capacity, mine sequencing, ground conditions and safety stoppages; Q4 is usually the seasonal peak. Benchmark: utilisation above 90% is good, 75–90% average, below 70% weak. How HZL compares: mined metal rose every year from 1,017 kt in FY22 to 1,114 kt in FY26, a record; Q2 FY27's 271 kt was a record second quarter. FY27 guidance is 1,150 ±10 kt, which requires about 611 kt in H2 after 539 kt in H1. Peers report differently (Glencore and Teck report zinc in concentrate), so like-for-like comparison is approximate.

2. Ore grade and recovery

What it is. Grade is the percentage of zinc plus lead in ore; recovery is the share of that metal captured in concentrate and then in refined metal. Worked example (illustrative): treating 1 million tonnes of ore at 7.6% grade with 90% mill recovery yields about 68,400 tonnes of metal in concentrate; at 6.0% grade it yields 54,000 tonnes, a 21% drop for the same mining cost. Benchmark: above 8% is good, 4–8% average, below 4% weak. How HZL compares: HZL's Q2 FY27 grade was about 7.6%, and its reserves and resources average about 7%, against a world-average reserve grade of about 2.8% (down from 6.9% in the 1990s), on the Woodmac figures in HZL's deck. 6. Cost including royalty, and the royalty rate

What it is. The headline cost plus royalty, DMF and NMET per tonne of zinc. Formula: CoP ex-royalty + (royalty + DMF + NMET) ÷ zinc tonnes; royalty intensity = royalty ÷ LME price. Worked example (illustrative): Q2 FY27: $703 + $525 = $1,228; $525 ÷ $3,825 = 13.7% of LME. At $2,800 zinc, the same structure would give royalty of roughly $385 (illustrative), so cost with royalty would fall even if operations did not change. What drives it: the LME price (mechanically), the metal-in-ore base, and any change in rates or state levies. Benchmark: below $1,400 good, $1,400–1,900 average, above $2,000 weak. How HZL compares: $1,440 in FY25, $1,370 in FY26, $1,330 in Q1 FY27 and $1,228 in Q2 FY27. Annual royalty was ₹4,948 crore in FY26, 12.2% of revenue, the largest single cost line. Peers in other jurisdictions pay different royalty regimes, often on profits rather than value, so this line is rarely comparable. The trap: because royalty rises with price, cost with royalty can rise in a good quarter even as operating efficiency improves; separate the two.

7. LME margin per tonne

What it is. The spread between the zinc price and the cost of producing it. Formula: LME zinc − CoP ex-royalty (operating margin) or LME − CoP with royalty (after state levies). Add the domestic premium for realised margin. Worked example (illustrative): Q2 FY27: $3,825 − $703 = $3,122 before royalty; $3,825 − $1,228 = $2,597 after; adding a 5% premium lifts the realised spread to about $2,790. Multiplied by about 214 kt and ₹95.57, that is the ₹5,712 crore zinc margin in the worked example above. What drives it: price above all; cost second. Benchmark: after royalty, above $1,500 is good, $800–1,500 average, below $500 weak. How HZL compares: LME minus ex-royalty cost was about $2,126 in FY22, $2,077 in FY23, $1,363 in FY24, $1,823 in FY25 and $2,011 in FY26 before jumping to about $3,122 in Q2 FY27 (indicative arithmetic).

9. Silver realisation versus LBMA

10. Silver's share of revenue and of EBIT

What it is. How dependent the company is on silver. Formula: silver revenue ÷ revenue from operations; precious-metal EBIT ÷ total EBIT (company-disclosed). Worked example (illustrative): FY26: ₹9,841 crore ÷ ₹40,844 crore = 24% of revenue; the company puts precious metals at 45% of EBIT. In H1 FY27, silver was ₹7,950 crore of ₹28,840 crore (28%) and about 45% of EBIT. What drives it: the silver price relative to zinc, silver volume and the ore mix. Benchmark: for a diversified base-metal producer, 15–35% of profit from precious by-products is a healthy diversification; above 40% is a concentration risk, because a precious-metal price fall then dominates results. How HZL compares: revenue share 14% (FY22) → 24% (FY26) → 28% (H1 FY27); EBIT share about 27% (FY22) → 45% (FY26). No peer discloses an equivalent figure (n/a). The trap: the company's profit share is on EBIT (after depreciation, which is borne mostly by zinc), not on EBITDA or revenue. Earlier commentary, including our own primer, called it "45% of profitability", which overstates its precision.

In plain terms

Silver brings in about a quarter of HZL's sales but nearly half of its operating profit, because the silver comes out of ore the company is mining for zinc anyway. That makes the company more profitable when silver is high and more fragile when it falls. Above about 40%, investors should treat HZL as partly a silver bet.

11. The treatment charge

What it is. The fee, in dollars per dry metric tonne of concentrate, that a smelter deducts from what it pays a miner. Formula: miner's receipt per dmt = (zinc content × payable % × LME) − TC. Worked example (illustrative): a 55%-grade concentrate at 85% payable and $3,000 LME is worth $1,402.50 of payable metal per dmt; at an $85 TC the miner receives $1,317.50, at a $274 TC only $1,128.50. Per tonne of zinc, the $85 TC is about $155. What drives it: the balance between mine supply and smelter capacity, Chinese concentrate demand, mine disruptions and smelter closures. Benchmark: for a custom smelter, benchmark TCs above $200 are good, $150–200 average, below $100 weak; for a concentrate seller, the reverse. How HZL compares: not applicable to its own feed (it pays itself), but it shaped HZL's lead-concentrate sales, which earned ₹155–330 crore of EBITDA a quarter at deeply negative TCs. The trap: the annual benchmark ($85 in 2026) and the spot TC (−$115 to −$150 in October) can be far apart; quote both, and remember that a TC per dmt of concentrate is not a TC per tonne of zinc.

12. By-product credits: sulphuric acid

What it is. Revenue from sulphuric acid (and smaller by-products like cadmium) that HZL nets against its zinc cost. Formula: acid tonnes × acid price ÷ zinc tonnes = credit per tonne of zinc. Worked example (illustrative): roasting produces about 1.8–2 tonnes of acid per tonne of zinc, so 851 kt of zinc yields roughly 1.6 Mt of acid. FY26 acid revenue of about ₹1,100 crore over 1.6 Mt is about ₹6,800 a tonne of acid, or about ₹12,900 per tonne of zinc, about $146 at ₹88.36: roughly 15% of the FY26 CoP of $959. If acid prices doubled, the credit would add about another $150 a tonne, enough to explain a large part of the 2026 cost fall. What drives it: fertiliser demand (phosphoric acid and DAP), Chinese export policy, and Middle East sulphur supply. Benchmark: none standard; track it as a share of CoP. How HZL compares: FY26 acid revenue "around ₹1,000–1,200 crore"; FY27 not disclosed ("we sell everything on auction basis"), but analysts cited prices up about 200% year on year. The trap: because the credit is buried in CoP, an acid windfall looks like cost efficiency; and because acid carries no royalty, it also lowers royalty as a share of revenue.

13. Reserves and resources, and reserve life

What it is. Reserves are the economically mineable part of a deposit, with a mine plan; resources are the broader identified mineralisation. Formula: reserve life = metal reserves ÷ annual mined metal; R&R life = metal R&R ÷ annual mined metal. Worked example (illustrative): HZL's FY26 metal reserves of 13.9 Mt ÷ 1.114 Mt mined = about 12.5 years (ILLUSTRATIVE); total metal R&R of 29.2 Mt ÷ 1.114 = about 26 years. What drives it: exploration drilling, conversion of resources to reserves (which needs drilling density and a mine plan), price assumptions and depletion. Benchmark: reserve life above 15 years is good, 8–15 average, below 8 weak; R&R life above 25 years good. How HZL compares: ore R&R of 468.6 Mt (FY20 403.0 Mt, minus 98.0 Mt produced, plus 163.6 Mt added); metal R&R 29.2 Mt; metal reserves 13.9 Mt; silver R&R 24.2 kt and silver reserves 10.9 kt. The targets are 30 Mt of metal reserves and about 50 Mt of metal R&R. HZL claims the world's second-largest zinc-lead R&R (Woodmac).

14. Capex intensity

What it is. The capital cost of adding a tonne of annual capacity, and the sustaining capex needed to stand still. Formula: project capex ÷ annual capacity added; sustaining capex ÷ tonnes produced. Worked example (illustrative): Debari's c.₹12,000 crore for 250 ktpa of refined zinc (with matching mine expansion) is ₹48 crore per kt, or about ₹4.8 lakh per tonne of annual capacity, about $5,050 at ₹95. The second-stage smelters, about 650 kt of capacity including mines for ₹24,000–25,000 crore, work out to about ₹37–38 crore per kt. The tailings plant, ₹3,823 crore for 30–35 kt of zinc a year, is ₹109–127 crore per kt, expensive per tonne but recovering metal from waste without new mining. What drives it: brownfield versus greenfield, inclusion of mines and power, equipment prices and the rupee. Benchmark: brownfield integrated zinc capacity below about ₹50 crore per kt (indicative) is efficient. How HZL compares: FY26 total capex was about ₹3,600 crore, of which growth ₹2,000 crore and sustaining ₹1,600 crore; FY27 growth capex guidance is $500–600 million, about ₹5,000 crore, back-end loaded. The trap: headline project capex often excludes mine development, power and working capital; check what is in the number.

15. Net cash, payout and ROCE

What it is. The balance-sheet and capital-return measures. Formulas: net cash = cash and investments − borrowings; payout = dividends ÷ PAT; ROCE = EBIT ÷ average capital employed. Worked example (illustrative): at 30 September 2026, gross cash and investments of ₹17,439 crore less borrowings of ₹6,171 crore gave net cash of ₹11,268 crore, up from ₹5,594 crore in March and ₹5,572 crore in June (after the ₹4,648 crore interim dividend). FY26 dividends of ₹10 a share (₹4,225 crore) on PAT of ₹13,832 crore were a 31% payout. What drives it: free cash flow, dividend decisions, capex and the parent's needs. Benchmarks: net cash or below 0.5× EBITDA is good; payout of 40–70% is sustainable; ROCE above 25% is good. How HZL compares: payouts have swung from 303% (FY23) to 71% (FY24), 118% (FY25) and 31% (FY26); company ROCE was about 67% and ROE about 77% in FY26 (screener: 70% and 76.8%). Gravita's ROCE is 16.2% and Pondy Oxides' 24.0%. The traps: the FY23 special dividend shrank equity from about ₹34,000 crore to ₹13,000 crore, flattering ROCE and ROE ever since; and screener shows HZL's FY26 consolidated payout as 0%, an artefact (standalone shows 31%).

In plain terms

HZL returns most of its cash to shareholders in good years, but how much depends heavily on what its parent needs. Its very high return on capital is partly real (rich orebodies) and partly arithmetic: paying out a huge special dividend in FY23 made its equity small, so every rupee of profit looks like a bigger return.

16. Price and currency sensitivities

What it is. The change in annual EBITDA for a given change in each external driver, published by the company. The FY27 numbers: ₹725–750 crore per $100/t of zinc; ₹200–225 crore per $1/oz of silver; ₹325–350 crore per ₹1 move in the rupee; ₹225–250 crore per $25/t change in CoP; ₹150–175 crore per $100/t of lead. Worked example (illustrative): a 10% move in each driver from November 2026 levels: zinc ($370 on $3,700) ≈ ₹2,700 crore; silver ($5 on $50) ≈ ₹1,060 crore; the rupee (₹9.5 on ₹95) ≈ ₹3,200 crore. On equal percentage moves, the rupee and zinc each matter more than silver at today's price. Benchmark: none; use them to translate price moves into earnings. How HZL compares: no peer publishes an equivalent rupee sensitivity. The trap: sensitivities are linear approximations around a base case and ignore second-order effects: royalty rises with price, hedges cap part of the move, and a weak rupee raises imported coal costs.

Definition traps, collected

  • Mined metal ≠ refined metal ≠ "1.1 Mt". FY26: 1,114 kt mined, 1,048 kt refined (851 zinc + 197 lead), 627 t silver. Refined capacity is 1,129 ktpa. Vision 2.0 is 2.0 Mtpa of refined capacity plus 1,500 tpa of silver by FY30.
  • CoP excludes royalty and silver but includes acid credits. Add about $500 for royalty; remember acid windfalls and the rupee flatter it.
  • "Realised" is not LBMA. The zinc, lead and silver prices in company tables are LME/LBMA averages, not realisations. Implied silver realisation ran 16–24% above LBMA in Q1–Q2 FY27, for undisclosed reasons.
  • Precious-metal share is of EBIT. c.45% of EBIT, not of revenue (24% in FY26) and not "of profitability" in general.
  • Lead concentrate sits outside refined volumes. 24 kt in FY26 and 20 kt in H1 FY27, carrying silver equivalents; management's "340 t" H1 silver includes it.
  • Market share is of primary metal. c.74% of India's primary zinc and ~92% of primary lead (64% in the FY24 annual report); recycled metal is excluded.
  • ROCE has three versions. Company ~67%, screener 70% for FY26 and 69.5% TTM; all flattered by the FY23 special dividend.
  • Consolidated and standalone are nearly identical. But screener's 0% FY26 consolidated payout is wrong; standalone shows 31%.
  • Reserve life vs mine life. "25+ years" is on R&R; reserves alone are about 12.5 years of output.
  • The brand fee is prepaid annually. The ₹1,060 crore reported in Q1 FY26 was the year's prepayment, not a quarterly run-rate; the audited FY26 expense was ₹1,171 crore.
  • Calendar vs fiscal years. ILZSG, LME and TC benchmarks are calendar-year; HZL reports April–March.
In plain terms

Most mistakes about HZL come from mixing up similar-sounding numbers: mined versus refined tonnes, cost with and without royalty, the market silver price versus what HZL received, and silver's share of sales versus its share of profit. Pin down which one a headline uses before reacting to it.

How to read a zinc miner's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volumes first (1 minute). Mined metal, refined zinc, refined lead and silver against the same quarter last year and against full-year guidance (FY27: 1,150 / 1,100 kt and 680 t). A miss in mined metal flows into future quarters.
  2. Prices next (1 minute). Note the quarter's LME zinc, LME lead and LBMA silver averages and the ₹/$ rate, and compare them with the current spot. This tells you how much of the result is already stale.
  3. Cost of production (1 minute). CoP ex-royalty in dollars and rupees, and CoP with royalty. Ask how much of any change came from the rupee and from acid credits (check the call).
  4. Revenue mix (1 minute). Zinc, lead, silver and others in ₹ crore. Divide silver revenue by ounces and FX to get the implied realisation; flag premiums above 8% over LBMA.
  5. EBITDA and the bridge (1 minute). Use the company's bridge to split the change into prices, FX, volume and cost. Price and FX are not earned.
  6. One-offs (1 minute). Hedge gains or losses ("delta vs market"), concentrate sales, inventory movements and any provision reversals.
  7. Below EBITDA (1 minute). Depreciation, finance cost, treasury income and tax rate; check PAT against EBITDA.
  8. Cash and balance sheet (1 minute). Free cash flow before growth capex, growth capex against guidance, net cash, and any dividend declared.
  9. Guidance changes (1 minute). Cost, volume and capex guidance revisions, and what they imply for the rest of the year (for example, H2 FY27 CoP of ~$875–925 implied by the full-year range).
  10. Governance and projects (1 minute). Related-party items (brand fee, power agreements), project milestones (Debari, tailings, fertiliser), and anything new on the GoI stake, Section 186 or leases.

Red flags: mined metal falling while refined metal holds (drawing down stock); cost falling only in dollars, not rupees; a silver realisation far above LBMA with no explanation; payouts above 100% while growth capex rises; project dates slipping without comment; and related-party lines growing faster than revenue.

In plain terms

The fastest way to read HZL's results is to ask three questions: did it produce more, did prices and the rupee help, and did its own costs really fall? Most quarters, the answer to the second question explains most of the change in profit. The other seven checks tell you whether what is left is good management or good luck.

Section 186: a vote to double the lending limit

On 30 October 2026 HZL issued a postal-ballot notice (filed on 3 November) seeking to raise its limit for loans, guarantees, securities and investments under Section 186 of the Companies Act from ₹15,000 crore, approved on 5 September 2024, to ₹30,000 crore. E-voting runs from 4 November to 3 December 2026. The resolution is a special resolution, so it needs 75% of votes cast. That makes the government decisive (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic): if it voted against, Vedanta's 60.71% would be only 68.5% of the combined Vedanta-plus-government vote (60.71 ÷ 88.63), short of 75% even with every public vote in favour. Given the 2023 Zinc International episode, investors will read the result as a test of how far the government will let HZL's balance sheet be used beyond its own operations.

The five-year cash arithmetic

The FY26 annual report's CFO letter gives a planning frame: ₹55,000–65,000 crore of pre-growth free cash flow over five years against ₹40,000–50,000 crore of growth capex for Vision 2.0. Sustained high payouts therefore require prices to stay high or the company to borrow, which is the context for the Section 186 headroom and for the Q4 FY26 remark that a loan "is much cheaper". In plain terms

Vedanta controls HZL and needs its cash, through dividends and a 3% fee on sales, to support a parent group that carries debt. The government owns a big enough slice to block major decisions and has done so before; it is also likely to sell more shares at some point. Public shareholders own only 11%. In plain terms

The vote closing on 3 December is more important than it looks. It asks shareholders to double how much HZL can lend or invest outside its own subsidiaries. Recyclers vs the miner: India's secondary lead and zinc loop

Two different businesses sell the same metal. One earns the rent in the ore; the other earns a spread on scrap.

Most of India's lead, and a growing share of its zinc, does not come from mines at all. Used lead-acid batteries are collected, broken, smelted and refined back into lead, which goes back into new batteries. Zinc comes back from galvanisers' dross and ash, brass scrap and die-cast scrap. The secondary route is a different business from HZL's, and the comparison shows what makes the miner special. HZL, by comparison, earns more EBITDA in about five days of Q2 FY27 (₹9,285 crore ÷ 92 days × 5 ≈ ₹505 crore) than Gravita earns in a year (illustrative arithmetic).

The economics differ in kind, not just degree. Because a recycler buys scrap priced off the LME and sells metal priced off the LME, a $100 move in lead barely changes its EBITDA; the spread between scrap and metal does, along with volume, scrap availability and working capital. That stability is why the market pays 22–28 times earnings for recyclers against HZL's 11 times. HZL's margin is a lever on price; a recycler's is a toll.

Regulation is pushing the loop into the formal sector. New extended-producer-responsibility rules for aluminium, copper and zinc scrap took effect on 1 April 2026, with recycling targets rising from 10% in 2026-27 to 75% by 2032-33, on top of existing battery-waste rules for lead. Over time that should move volume from informal smelters to licensed recyclers, and increase the supply of secondary zinc that competes with HZL's primary metal at the margin. For HZL the threat is small: secondary zinc is a fraction of Indian supply and galvanisers want high-grade metal. For lead, where HZL's 197 kt is a minority of a recycling-dominated market, it is already the norm. See the Gravita primer for the recycler's side.

In plain terms

Recyclers make money by turning old batteries and scrap back into metal and earning a small, steady margin, whatever the metal price. HZL makes money by digging metal out of the ground for much less than it sells for, which is far more profitable but swings with prices. Investors pay more per rupee of profit for the steady business than for the swinging one.

Macro, technology and disruption

The rupee, coal, tariffs and carbon rules move HZL's numbers quarter to quarter; substitutes and thrifting move them decade to decade.

Four macro forces bear on the sub-sector in late 2026. The rupee is the most powerful: it traded around ₹94.9 to the dollar on 19 October 2026, near a record low of about ₹96.8, and HZL's Q2 FY27 average was ₹95.57 against ₹87.31 a year earlier. With most costs in rupees and all prices in dollars, every ₹1 move is worth ₹325–350 crore of annual EBITDA. The Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 to steady the currency; a stronger rupee would be a headwind. Energy is the second: the West Asia war from 28 February 2026 closed the Strait of Hormuz for weeks and raised crude, coal, LPG and freight prices, adding about $50 a tonne to HZL's costs, but it also tripled sulphuric-acid prices, which HZL counts as a credit. Tariffs are the third. The US Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026; since 24 July a 10% Section 301 tariff applies on top of normal duties for Indian goods, and Section 232 tariffs of 50% hit steel, aluminium and copper derivatives. Refined zinc is not, to our knowledge, a Section 232 product, but Indian galvanised-steel exports to the US are hit by the steel tariff, which can slow domestic galvanising. HZL's own exports go mainly to Asia and the Middle East. Carbon rules are the fourth: the EU's carbon border adjustment, in its definitive phase from January 2026, covers steel and aluminium, not zinc ingot, but steel exporters need low-carbon inputs, which supports EcoZen. Our steel products and aluminium reports cover those industries.

Technology and disruption

Substitution in coatings. Galvalume, an aluminium-zinc coating of about 55% aluminium, uses less zinc per square metre than pure galvanising and is gaining share in roofing. Paint systems, stainless and weathering steel substitute in some infrastructure. Lithium-ion versus lead-acid. Lithium batteries are displacing lead in e-rickshaws, two-wheelers, telecom backup and energy storage, a slow-burn risk for HZL's lead and for recyclers; the world lead market is already in surplus. Silver thrifting. Solar-cell makers cut silver per watt every year and are trialling copper metallisation; the 2025–26 price spike accelerates that. India's large silver jewellery and investment demand, covered in our jewellery report, absorbs HZL's output domestically regardless. Zinc batteries. Zinc-air, zinc-bromine and zinc-ion chemistries for long-duration storage are a possible upside that HZL promotes, but not yet material.

Mining technology is a cost lever: tele-remote raise-boring, battery-electric underground fleets, AI-assisted flotation, digital twins, and research partnerships on fine-particle flotation and silver recovery. Decarbonisation and water are constraints: scope 1 and 2 emissions are about 4.6 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per tonne of metal, dominated by coal-fired captive power; the net-zero target is 2050; and HZL withdrew about 18 million cubic metres of freshwater in FY26 (plus about 9.5 million of treated sewage water) in water-stressed Rajasthan. HZL ranks first in metals and mining in S&P Global's corporate sustainability assessment for three years and was the first Indian member of the ICMM; safety shows zero fatalities in FY26 and H1 FY27 and an injury rate of 1.2–1.3. Depletion is the deepest structural risk: reserves of about 12.5 years mean the business must keep finding and proving ore to stand still.

In plain terms

In the short run, a weak rupee and expensive acid have been HZL's friends, while dearer coal and US tariffs on steel have been mild enemies. In the long run, the threats are slower: coatings that use less zinc, batteries that use no lead, solar panels that use less silver, and the simple fact that every tonne mined must be replaced by a tonne discovered.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

Both cases agree on the quality of the assets. They disagree on prices, silver and who gets the cash.

The bull and bear cases for Indian zinc are, in practice, cases for and against Hindustan Zinc, and both start from the same facts: a world-class orebody, an integrated chain, a first-decile cost and a record quarter. The disagreement is about what happens next to prices, how much of the record was luck, and whether the ownership structure lets minority holders share in the cash.

The bull case

  • Structural concentrate shortage. Mine output (12.4 Mt) sits 1.5 Mt below refined output; Western miners are shrinking; integration means HZL keeps both halves of the margin whatever the TC.
  • Bottom of the cost curve. A cost of $700–900 a tonne including smelting, negative once silver is credited, so HZL earns money at any plausible zinc price.
  • Silver optionality. A sixth straight market deficit and rising industrial demand; HZL can tilt its ore mix toward silver when zinc weakens.
  • Indian demand. 6–8% annual growth from galvanising, infrastructure and autos supports a 5–8% domestic premium and absorbs Debari's extra metal.
  • Growth with funding. +22% refined capacity by Q2 FY29, fully funded from cash; tailings and exploration extend life.
  • Valuation. About 7.5 times EBITDA at spot prices, in line with global peers that earn a fraction of its margin; net cash ₹11,268 crore.
  • State risk capped. The 2026 MMDR amendment removed the mineral-tax tail risk.

The bear case

  • Peak earnings. Q2 FY27 combined $3,825 zinc, $62.6 silver with an unexplained 24% realisation premium, a ₹95.6 rupee and an acid windfall; mid-cycle EBITDA is about a third lower (illustrative).
  • Silver reversal. Silver is down from $118 to about $49; every $1 costs ₹200–225 crore a year, and PV thrifting is accelerating.
  • Surplus ahead. ILZSG sees a 2027 zinc surplus and a widening lead surplus; recovering mine supply would raise TCs and soften LME prices together.
  • Costs rising in H2. Guidance implies ~$875–925 a tonne in H2 FY27, against Q2's $703; domestic coal is down to 32%.
  • Governance. Payouts swing with Vedanta's needs; a 3% brand fee rising with turnover; a vote to double inter-corporate lending; SEBI warning and related-party audit matter.
  • Supply overhang. A government OFS of 1.5–2% has been mooted, and Vedanta has sold down three times since 2024.
  • Reserves and leases. About 12.5 years of reserves and leases expiring around 2030, with renewal premiums unknown.
