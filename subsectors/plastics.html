00

The one-page view: seven companies, one raw material, five economic engines

The label "plastic products" hides five different businesses. What they share is a resin bill; what separates them is everything else.

The seven companies in this report all buy petrochemical polymers (PVC, CPVC, polyethylene, polypropylene, PET, polyurethane) and convert them into something a customer will pay more for. That is where the similarity ends. Supreme Industries, Astral and Finolex Industries make pipes and fittings for farms, homes and city water systems. Garware Hi-Tech Films sells branded window and paint-protection film to car owners in the United States and elsewhere. Shaily Engineering Plastics moulds furniture parts for IKEA and, increasingly, the pen injectors that deliver generic GLP-1 diabetes and weight-loss drugs. Time Technoplast makes most of India's large industrial plastic drums and a growing number of composite gas cylinders. EPL makes about eight billion laminated tubes a year, mostly for toothpaste and cosmetics, in plants across a dozen countries.

Put together, the seven earned ₹35,879 crore of revenue in FY26, up 8.8%, and ₹5,866 crore of operating profit (a 16.3% margin, measured before other income). Their combined net profit was ₹3,467 crore and their combined market value on 19 October 2026 was ₹1.41 lakh crore. But the group is lopsided. The three pipe makers account for 61% of the revenue and 67% of the market value; Supreme alone is about a third of the market value. Any "sector view" that averages the seven is mostly a view on PVC pipes, with four smaller and very different businesses attached.

The single most useful fact about this group is that its stock-market values are set by two opposite forces. The pipe makers are valued as consumer brands that happen to sell a commodity: Supreme at about 29 times FY26 operating profit (EV/EBITDA) and Astral at about 36.5 times. Garware (35.5 times) and Shaily (52 times) are valued as growth stories built on a change in product mix. Finolex, Time Technoplast and EPL trade at 8.5 to 10 times, the multiples of industrial companies. Inside a single "sub-sector", the price of a rupee of operating profit varies six-fold.

The four things that defined 2026

  • A PVC whipsaw that cut volumes and lifted margins. The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 pushed Indian polymer prices up about 43% by mid-March; PVC rose roughly ₹32/kg and then fell roughly ₹34/kg in early April after the ceasefire. Dealers stopped buying. Supreme's April pipe volumes fell by more than half, and Q1 FY27 volumes were down 14.3% at Supreme and 26.5% at Finolex. Yet margins rose, because realisations held and the companies had run down their own stocks before the fall (section 07).
  • Policy replaced the market as the PVC price-setter. An anti-dumping duty recommended in August 2025 was never notified, a quality-control order on PVC was withdrawn in November 2025, and in July 2026 the government chose a floor price instead: a minimum import price of $766 per tonne for six months (section 08).
  • Mix shift outside pipes. Shaily's healthcare business went from 21% of revenue in FY25 to 51% in Q1 FY27 on GLP-1 pens; Garware's revenue rose 28% and its profit 60% in Q1 FY27 on paint-protection film (sections 10 and 11).
  • A run of deals. Supreme bought Wavin's India pipe business; Indorama is merging its packaging arm into EPL and will own about 51.8% after Blackstone sold out on 1 September 2026; Astral withdrew a planned demerger; Time is absorbing its listed subsidiary TPL Plastech (section 12).

Two of the seven barely grew their revenue in FY26 and both stories are misleading on the surface. Finolex's revenue fell 0.7% because PVC prices were low for most of the year, not because it sold fewer pipes. Garware's rose only 0.5%, yet its paint-protection film business was scaling fast: the growth was hidden by weak commodity polyester film and by a 50% US tariff on Indian goods for part of the year. In plain terms

These seven companies all buy plastic resin and sell something made from it, but they are not one industry. Three make pipes, and their profits move with the price of PVC and with government trade policy. The other four make film, medical pens, drums and tubes, and their profits move with how fast they can sell more valuable products from the same machines. Investors pay very high prices for the pipe brands and the two mix-shift stories, and low prices for the rest.

The report is organised around those forces. Sections 01 to 06 explain how the seven businesses earn money, how big the markets are, where costs go and who competes. Four deep dives follow: the April 2026 PVC crash (07), the shift from duties to a price floor (08), Finolex's resin integration (09) and Shaily's GLP-1 pen ramp (10). Section 14 is a masterclass in the numbers that matter, from EBITDA per kilogram to inventory days, and sections 15 to 21 compare the companies, explain the valuation split and list what to watch, with thresholds.

01

From cracker to customer: where the value is added

Every product in this report starts as crude oil, gas or coal. The money is made in the last two steps: conversion and the brand or qualification that sits on top of it.

The plastics value chain runs in a straight line. Crude oil, natural gas or (in China) coal is cracked into ethylene and propylene; chlorine from salt is added to make ethylene dichloride (EDC), which becomes vinyl chloride monomer (VCM), which is polymerised into PVC resin, a white powder. Polyethylene, polypropylene and PET come out of parallel chains. Resin makers in India include Reliance Industries (about half of domestic PVC capacity), Chemplast Sanmar, DCM Shriram, DCW and Finolex; the rest of India's PVC comes from China, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, the United States and South-East Asia. The resin is then compounded with stabilisers, fillers and impact modifiers. CPVC, the chlorinated PVC used for hot-water plumbing, is a special case: Indian pipe makers buy the resin or compound from a handful of global licensors (Lubrizol, Sekisui, Kaneka).

The seven listed companies all sit at Step 3, conversion, and they all try to build something at Step 4 that a rival cannot copy quickly. For a pipe maker that is a brand plumbers trust, thousands of dealers and a range of 15,000 or more products so that a builder can buy every pipe and fitting from one supplier. For Garware it is the coating, metallising and paint-protection film technology and a network of installers who stand behind a warranty. For Shaily it is a pen design that a drug company has written into its regulatory filing; changing the pen means changing the filing. For EPL it is the audits that Colgate-type customers run before approving a tube plant. For Time Technoplast it is plants next to India's chemical clusters, because empty drums are mostly air and expensive to truck far, plus PESO certification for gas cylinders.

Where value is captured shows up directly in the margins. Shaily, whose device business depends on intellectual property and regulatory qualification, earned a 28.3% operating margin in FY26 and a 29.3% return on capital employed. In plain terms

Anyone can buy PVC powder and an extruder. What is hard to copy is a plumber who asks for your pipe by name, a drug company that has written your pen into its approval, or a toothpaste maker that has audited your tube plant. The seven companies are valued by how much of that hard-to-copy layer they own, not by how much plastic they melt.

One more feature of the chain matters for every company here: resin is priced globally in dollars, mostly in Asia, and reaches India through imports or import-parity pricing. Two-thirds of the resin used by pipe makers is imported, according to CRISIL Ratings. So an Indian pipe company's single largest cost is set by Chinese coal-based PVC plants, freight rates, the rupee and Indian trade policy, none of which it controls. What it controls is how quickly it passes the cost on, how much inventory it carries when prices move, and how much of its sales come from products where the brand, not the resin, sets the price.

02

Size, growth and seasons: an estimated ₹50,000–60,000-crore pipe market and four niches

The pipe market is large and only partly organised; the other four businesses are niches where one Indian company is a national or global leader.

No official series measures the Indian plastic pipe market, so its size has to be estimated. The firmest anchor comes from CRISIL Ratings, which said in June 2026 that the five leading PVC pipe makers had about ₹20,000 crore of revenue and held about 60% of the organised segment. That implies an organised PVC pipe market of roughly ₹33,000 crore in FY26 (an estimate derived from CRISIL's figures, not a reported number). Adding unorganised makers, polyethylene and HDPE pipe, the industry estimates companies and brokers commonly cite put the total plastic pipe market at about ₹50,000–60,000 crore; that range is indicative and has not been verified against a primary source. The three listed pipe makers had ₹21,900 crore of FY26 revenue between them, but that includes Supreme's non-pipe businesses and Astral's adhesives and paints. Their pipe revenue alone is closer to ₹16,500 crore (Supreme piping ₹7,776 crore, Astral plumbing ₹4,679 crore and roughly ₹4,000 crore at Finolex, which includes some external resin sales).

On volume, India consumed roughly 4.0–4.6 million tonnes of PVC resin in 2025–FY26, with forecasts of about 6 million tonnes by FY30, and pipes take about 70% of it by common industry convention. Domestic resin capacity is only about 1.6–2.0 million tonnes, which is why India imported 3.15 million tonnes in calendar 2025. CPVC is a small but rich slice: about 7–8% of pipe industry volume, according to Finolex's Q1 FY27 commentary, but a much larger share of value because CPVC pipe sells for well above PVC per kilogram.

The four non-pipe businesses are best measured against global markets, because three of the four companies earn most of their revenue abroad. EPL is the world's largest maker of laminated tubes at about eight billion a year, with France's privately owned Albéa its main premium rival. Garware sells into a global paint-protection film market of roughly $0.5–0.7 billion led by XPEL, 3M and Eastman (an indicative figure, not re-verified). Shaily is a small supplier in a global pen-injector market measured in billions of units, dominated by drug companies' in-house devices and by device makers such as Ypsomed and SHL Medical. Time Technoplast is the world's largest maker of large plastic drums and holds about 55% of India's industrial drum market.

Growth: the listed group grew faster than the pipe market

The seven grew combined revenue from ₹31,321 crore in FY24 to ₹32,986 crore in FY25 and ₹35,879 crore in FY26. The pipe trio grew 7.2% in FY26 and the four non-pipe companies 11.2%. In Q1 FY27 the gap widened sharply: pipes grew revenue only 3.3% (volumes fell after the April crash) while the other four grew 24.7%, led by Garware (+27.9%), EPL (+25.3%) and Time (+25.1%). CRISIL noted that pipe industry revenue had been "flattish" over FY25 and FY26 despite higher volumes, because resin prices fell. It expects FY27 to be the mirror image: volumes down 3–5% and revenue up 10–15% on 12–15% higher realisations.

Seasons: pipes live by the monsoon calendar

Pipe demand peaks in the January–March quarter, when construction is at full pace and farmers prepare for the sowing season, and in April–June ahead of the monsoon. It troughs in July–September, when rain stops construction and fields are wet. Supreme's revenue in Q4 FY26 was ₹3,528 crore against ₹2,394 crore in Q2 FY26, 32% lower; Finolex's was ₹1,314 crore against ₹859 crore. Finolex has a second seasonal constraint: its Ratnagiri jetty cannot take VCM imports in the monsoon, so it builds feedstock stocks beforehand, which helps explain its high inventory days. Comparisons should always be made year on year, never quarter on quarter.

In plain terms

India's plastic pipe market is worth something like ₹50,000–60,000 crore a year, of which the big brands own perhaps two-thirds. The three listed pipe makers sell roughly ₹16,500 crore of pipe between them. The other four companies are leaders in small markets: drums in India, tubes globally, special films and medical pens for export. In a normal year pipes grow with the economy; in 2026 they shrank while everything else grew 25%.

The Q2 FY27 results (July–September 2026) were not out for any of the seven on 19 October 2026; Garware's board meets on 22 October. 03

Who buys the plastic, and why: water, homes, chemicals, cars and diabetes

Ten demand drivers, each moving a different company. Pipes depend on India's water and housing spending; the rest depend on foreign consumers and drug launches.

For the pipe makers, demand splits roughly in half. CRISIL estimates that irrigation and agriculture account for about 45% of PVC pipe demand and plumbing and water supply for about 55%. The agricultural half depends on the monsoon, borewells, the shift from open canals to piped irrigation and farm incomes. The plumbing half depends on housing construction, government water schemes and the replacement of old metal and cement pipes. The two halves behave differently: farm demand swings with rainfall and crop prices, while plumbing demand follows the property cycle (see our realty and cement reports for the construction side).

Government water spending is the single largest policy lever. The Jal Jeevan Mission, which aims to give every rural household a tap connection, has a second edition budgeted at ₹67,670 crore, roughly three times the previous edition, according to CRISIL. It drives demand for PVC and HDPE pipes, column pipes and fittings, and is lumpy: disbursement depends on state execution and central releases. Urban schemes such as AMRUT (sewerage and water) and the build-out of city gas distribution networks add demand for larger-diameter and specialty pipes; Supreme expects about ₹600 crore of revenue from gas piping in FY27.

Organised brands also gain share from unorganised makers in every resin price crash. Small local extruders typically buy resin on credit, carry thin capital and cannot absorb a sudden inventory loss. When PVC falls fast, they either sell at a loss or stop buying, and the brands, with cash and dealer networks, pick up their customers. This happened in FY23, in FY25 and again in Q1 FY27. It is one reason why the brands' long-term volume growth runs ahead of the industry's.

In plain terms

Pipe demand comes from two places: farmers watering fields and builders plumbing homes, with the government's rural water programme on top. The other four companies sell mostly to foreign customers: American car owners, global toothpaste brands, generic drug makers and IKEA. So a bad monsoon hurts Finolex, while a US tariff or a delayed drug approval hurts Garware or Shaily. Few single events hit all seven at once; a resin price spike is the main exception.

04

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: resin is most of the business

In a pipe company two-thirds of every rupee buys raw material. In films, pens and tubes the share is under half, which is why their margins are higher and their swings smaller.

The cost structure explains most of what follows in this report. For a branded PVC pipe maker, resin and additives absorb about two-thirds of revenue. CRISIL estimates that resin alone is 75–80% of a pipe maker's total costs. What is left, about ₹33 of every ₹100, pays for the factory, the people, the freight, the dealer schemes and the advertising, and leaves about ₹14 of operating profit. A small change in the resin price therefore moves profit by a large proportion. If resin falls 10% and the pipe price falls 10% at the same moment, the margin is unaffected; if the pipe price falls first, because dealers expect cheaper pipe and stop buying, the company sells stock bought at the old price into a falling market and books an inventory loss.

Six business models, one raw material

Each archetype has its own revenue engine, margin engine and swing factor. The market prices them very differently, and mostly for good reasons.

The seven companies sort into six archetypes. Two pipe converters, Supreme and Astral, buy resin and sell branded pipe; their profit is tonnes multiplied by the gap between the selling price and resin, minus conversion costs. One integrated pipe maker, Finolex, also makes its own PVC, so it earns the resin spread as well as the conversion margin. The other four each form an archetype of one: a specialty film exporter (Garware), a precision and contract component maker (Shaily), an industrial packaging and composites maker (Time Technoplast) and a packaging tube maker (EPL).

The April crash: anatomy of a PVC whipsaw Deep dive

In six weeks PVC rose about ₹32/kg and fell about ₹34/kg. Pipe volumes collapsed, yet margins rose. Understanding why is the key to reading any pipe company.

The 2026 shock began outside the industry. On 28 February 2026 war broke out in West Asia and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz stopped. Brent crude rose to about $118 a barrel. The Middle East supplies around 15% of the world's polyethylene capacity and about 9% of its polypropylene, and the region's naphtha and other feedstocks flow through Hormuz to Asian crackers. Naphtha delivered to East Asia jumped from about $620 a tonne to $1,077–1,079 in two weeks to 17 March. S&P Global reported that Indian polymer prices were up about 43% by 19 March. PVC followed: according to Supreme's management, domestic PVC rose about ₹32/kg into March. (The secondary market report of a domestic peak near ₹114/kg could not be confirmed against a primary price circular and should be treated with caution.)

Pipe dealers did what dealers always do when prices fall fast: they stopped buying, sold down their own stocks and waited for cheaper pipe. A dealer who buys a lorry-load of pipe at the old price and sees the list price cut by 15% the next week takes the loss himself, so he would rather run his shelves empty for a few weeks. Supreme said its April pipe volumes fell by more than half.

From duty to price floor: how policy came to set the PVC price Deep dive

In 18 months India tried an anti-dumping duty, a quality-control order, a duty exemption and a minimum import price. Each step moved domestic PVC by more than a year's margin gain.

Because India imports most of its PVC, the government's trade decisions decide where the domestic price settles relative to the Asian market. Domestic resin makers (Reliance, Chemplast Sanmar, DCM Shriram, DCW and Finolex) want protection from cheap imports, especially Chinese coal-based PVC. Pipe converters want cheap resin but also want stable prices, because volatility is what causes inventory losses and dealer destocking. Policy has swung between these interests, and the swings have been large.

The old duty and what happened when it lapsed

India first imposed anti-dumping duty on suspension PVC (the grade used for pipes) in January 2008. It ran, with reviews, until February 2022, when it lapsed without extension. Imports rose 65% in FY23. That coincided with the end of the post-pandemic PVC super-cycle, and prices crashed. Finolex, which had earned a 28.6% operating margin in FY21 when PVC was scarce, earned 6.7% in FY23. Converters with high inventory booked losses; unorganised makers were squeezed; and the brands took share.

The new case that was never notified

In March–April 2024 the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) began a fresh anti-dumping investigation into suspension PVC from China, Indonesia, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Thailand and the United States, on a petition from Chemplast Cuddalore Vinyls, DCM Shriram and DCW. Proposed duties ranged from $25 to $339 a tonne depending on the exporter. In August 2025 DGTR issued final findings recommending a five-year duty. But anti-dumping duties take effect only when the Finance Ministry notifies them, and our research found no notification. The usual three-month window for the ministry to act lapsed around November 2025, so the duty should be treated as not imposed. That conclusion is an inference from the absence of a notification, not a confirmed decision; one trade publication's claim that the duty was "effective since 14 August 2025" conflicts with it and appears to be wrong.

The quality-control order that was withdrawn

In parallel, a Bureau of Indian Standards quality-control order (QCO) required imported PVC homopolymer to carry BIS certification, which restricted some overseas suppliers. Around 12 November 2025 the government rescinded it along with 13 other polymer QCOs (covering polyethylene, polypropylene, ABS, EVA, polyurethane, polycarbonate and others) as part of a wider easing of non-tariff barriers on industrial inputs. For converters this was a cost win; for domestic resin makers it removed a shield.

The floor price that took over

In July 2026, with the April crash fresh, the government changed tools. A customs duty exemption on PVC imports was withdrawn effective 16 July 2026 (the specific notification was not traced in our research). On 24 July the Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) made imports of suspension PVC (HS 39041020) "restricted" if the landed CIF price is at or below $766 a tonne, for six months to about 23 January 2027. Export-oriented units, SEZ units and advance-authorisation holders are exempt if the resin is not sold into the domestic market. At ₹95.5 to the dollar, the rate used for current conversions in this report, $766 a tonne is about ₹73 per kg before duties and freight inland. (The research dossier converted it at ₹89.5, giving ₹68.6/kg; we use the current rate.) Domestic PVC rose ₹9–13/kg and channels restocked in July.

A floor price works differently from a duty. A duty adds a fixed amount to every import, whatever the price; a floor does nothing while the market is above it and blocks the cheapest cargoes when the market falls below it. At the time it was introduced, Asian PVC was above the floor after the war spike, so its immediate effect was mostly psychological; freight and war risk dominated landed prices. Its real effect is insurance: it caps how far domestic PVC can fall if Chinese oversupply reasserts itself, which is precisely the scenario that produced the FY23 and FY25 inventory losses. Note that the floor, at about $766, sits above the FY26 average of about $720, so had it existed last year it would have lifted domestic prices for much of the year.

Who wins and who loses

Domestic resin makers gain most from a floor: their selling price is set by import parity, so a higher floor lifts their realisations. Finolex, as a resin maker, gains on its 2.72 lakh tonnes of PVC. Pipe converters see their main cost rise, but in the short run they pass it on; what they gain is stability and an end to the deepest troughs. Small unorganised converters are hurt most: they cannot raise prices as easily and have less working capital to absorb a cost jump. For Supreme and Astral, a protected, more stable PVC price is arguably better than cheap but volatile resin, because the brands earn their premium on conversion and distribution, not on buying resin cheaply. The losers in a simple sense are end users: farmers and home builders pay more for pipe.

Finolex's double-edged resin: integration as a cycle amplifier Deep dive

India's only listed resin-to-pipe maker earns two margins in a good year and loses one in a bad year. Its ₹2,640 crore of idle cash and a family cross-holding complicate the picture.

Finolex Industries is two businesses joined by a pipeline. At Ratnagiri on the Maharashtra coast it runs a PVC plant with 2.72 lakh tonnes of annual capacity, about 56% made through the EDC route and the rest through the ethylene route, with 43 MW of captive power and India's first petrochemical cryogenic jetty for unloading feedstock. Downstream it sells about 3.3 lakh tonnes a year of PVC and CPVC pipes and fittings through about 15,000 outlets, mostly in western and southern India, with agriculture taking 63% of FY26 volume and 69% in Q1 FY27. Because it sells more pipe than it makes resin, it also buys some PVC; because of the timing of production, it sometimes sells resin outside.

The spread decides the year

Finolex's profit has two legs. The resin leg earns the "spread": the PVC price minus the cost of the feedstock (EDC or VCM, plus ethylene), minus the cost of running the plant. The pipe leg earns a conversion margin, like Supreme or Astral but with a smaller brand premium. Finolex discloses its spreads. In Q1 FY27 PVC averaged $873 a tonne against $707 a year earlier; the PVC–EDC spread was $501 against $522; the PVC–VCM spread was $122 against $163. In other words, even though PVC prices rose, the margin for making PVC narrowed slightly, because the war pushed feedstock up as much as product.

The cost of integration

Integration makes Finolex's earnings far more volatile than a converter's: its operating margin has ranged from 6.7% to 28.6% in five years, while Supreme's has moved within about two points. It also ties up capital. Finolex carries 159 days of inventory, twice Supreme's, because it holds feedstock, resin and pipe and must stock VCM before the monsoon closes its jetty. Its cash conversion is poor: FY26 operating cash flow was ₹192 crore on ₹679 crore of operating profit, about 28%. And its return on capital employed is only 11.8%, diluted by about ₹5,000 crore of investments, which include treasury cash and its fair-valued stake in Finolex Cables.

Feedstock supply is a further risk. The 2026 war disrupted VCM supplies from the Middle East, and management said VCM availability was constrained. Unlike a converter, which can switch resin suppliers, an integrated plant that cannot get feedstock cannot run. The coming wave of domestic PVC capacity, from Reliance (toward ~1.5 mtpa by 2027) and Adani (1 mtpa at Mundra, FY28), cuts the other way: it should reduce India's import dependence and the volatility of domestic prices, but it also ends the scarcity that has given a western-India resin plant pricing power.

From furniture parts to GLP-1 pens: Shaily's re-invention Deep dive

The same moulding floor in Gujarat makes IKEA parts and the injector pens for generic semaglutide. The pens now earn half the revenue and set the valuation.

Shaily Engineering Plastics was, until recently, a precision moulder best known as a supplier to IKEA, making plastic components for home furnishings in about seven plants around Vadodara with more than 200 injection-moulding machines. Over the past few years it developed its own pen-injector platforms (ShailyPen Neo and Axiom, plus Toby and Tristan auto-injectors) and positioned them for the moment patents on GLP-1 drugs, the diabetes and weight-loss class led by semaglutide, began to expire. That moment arrived in 2026, when semaglutide patents expired in India (March), Canada, Brazil and China. In the US, the key patents run to about 2031–32.

The economics of a generic injectable drug explain why the pen matters. A generic maker needs not only the drug but a device to deliver it. Building a pen in-house takes years and engineering skills most generic makers lack, so they license or buy a platform. The chosen pen is written into the drug's regulatory filing. Switching to another pen later means re-filing, new stability and usability studies and months of delay. Once a partner's product is approved with Shaily's pen, Shaily is very hard to displace for the life of that product. One clarification matters here: in the US, the tentative approval reported in connection with the ShailyPen was granted to the partner's (Orbicular's) generic semaglutide drug-device combination filing, not to the pen itself, since devices are approved as part of the drug product.

Garware: film sold by the square foot, and a tariff that came and went

A 90-year-old polyester film maker turned branded exporter of paint-protection and window film. FY26 looked flat; the next quarter showed what the doubled capacity can do.

Garware Hi-Tech Films sells two kinds of product. Its Consumer Products Division, about 71% of FY26 revenue, sells branded sun-control window film for cars and buildings and paint-protection film (PPF), a thick, clear, self-healing polyurethane film applied over a car's paint to protect it from stone chips and scratches. Its Industrial Products Division, about 29%, sells polyester (BOPET) film for packaging and industrial uses, a commodity business. The company counts 87% of sales as "value-added", which includes the whole consumer division plus specialised industrial grades. About three-quarters of revenue is exported, and the United States is the largest market; one undated source puts the US at about half of revenue, which we could not verify.

The economics differ sharply by division. PPF sells by the square foot, through distributors and certified installers who cut and apply the film and stand behind a multi-year warranty. A car owner pays mostly for the installation and the brand promise; the film itself is a fraction of the bill. That is why a branded PPF maker can earn margins several times those of a commodity film maker using similar polymer inputs. Garware's global rivals are well-known names: 3M, Eastman (with LLumar and SunTek), XPEL, Avery Dennison and Saint-Gobain's Solar Gard. Its edge is that it makes the base film, coats and metallises it in-house, and is integrating backward into the polyurethane (TPU) film that PPF is made from.

A flat year that hid a fast-growing business

Garware's FY26 revenue was ₹2,120 crore, up only 0.5%, and net profit rose about 2% to ₹338 crore (from ₹331 crore). The primer's description of FY26 profit as having "jumped" is misleading: the jump came later. Two things held the year back. The industrial division suffered from weak polyester film prices. And from 27 August 2025 the United States imposed a 50% tariff on Indian goods (a 25% "reciprocal" tariff plus a 25% penalty linked to Russian oil purchases), which hit just as Garware doubled its PPF capacity from 30 million to 60 million square feet in Q2 FY26. Garware still exported 74% of its sales in Q3 FY26 under the 50% tariff, but that quarter was its weakest: revenue ₹459 crore and an operating margin of 15%.

Inventory is high at 149 days because of the export pipeline, and FY26 free cash flow was ₹114 crore against ₹338 crore of profit, as capacity spending continued. The balance sheet is strong, with no borrowings and about ₹774 crore of liquidity, and the family owns 60.73%, with institutions holding only about 10.6%.

Four decades of cycles: formalisation, super-cycle, grind and war

PVC replaced metal and cement pipe, brands consolidated the trade, and every few years a resin boom or bust reset the winners.

The industry's history falls into seven eras. The first, from the 1980s to the 2010s, was formalisation. PVC pipe displaced galvanised iron and asbestos-cement pipe in Indian farms and homes because it does not rust, weighs a fraction as much and is far cheaper to lay. Finolex built India's only integrated resin-to-pipe complex at Ratnagiri. Astral, founded in 1996, launched India's first CPVC plumbing pipes in 1999 under licence from B.F. Goodrich (now Lubrizol). Supreme, whose roots go back to the 1940s and which the Taparia family built into India's largest plastics processor, spread across pipes, packaging, industrial mouldings and furniture. Essel Propack, founded in 1982, globalised the laminated tube. Time Technoplast, incorporated in 1989, became the world's largest maker of large plastic drums.

The second era, from 2013 to 2019, was consolidation by brands and global owners. Aliaxis bought Ashirvad in 2013. Astral bought Resinova and the UK's Seal It (Bond-It) in 2014, starting its adhesives business. Blackstone bought control of Essel Propack in 2019. The third era was the PVC super-cycle of FY21–FY23. Pandemic disruption to Chinese and US supply pushed PVC to record prices; Finolex's operating margin reached 28.6% in FY21. Then the old anti-dumping duty lapsed in February 2022, imports surged 65% in FY23, prices crashed and Finolex's margin fell to 6.7%.

The fourth era, 2022–2025, was diversification into second legs: Astral took 51% of Gem Paints in 2022 and launched Astral Paints; Garware scaled paint-protection film from 2021; Shaily built its pen platforms; Time pushed Type-IV composite cylinders. The fifth was the China-oversupply grind of FY25–FY26. Cheap Chinese PVC held prices near $720 a tonne, Supreme booked a roughly ₹150 crore inventory loss in FY25, and CRISIL noted that pipe industry revenue was flat for two years despite volume growth. The sixth was the deal wave of 2025–26 described in section 12. The seventh, still under way, is the 2026 war shock and the shift to a price floor (sections 07 and 08).

The equation: tonnes, spread, conversion and inventory

Every pipe company's operating profit can be written as one line. It is the number of tonnes sold, multiplied by what the company earns on each kilogram after buying resin and additives and paying to convert them, plus or minus the gain or loss on the inventory it was holding when resin prices moved. The equation matters because each term has a different owner. Management controls conversion cost and, partly, tonnes and inventory. It does not control the resin price, and it controls realisation only to the extent its brand lets it charge a premium over the resin-linked market price.

In plain terms

A pipe company's profit is how many kilograms it sells times what it keeps on each kilogram, plus or minus a gain or loss on the plastic it was holding when prices changed. The first two are the business; the third is luck and timing. Good analysis separates them.

1. Volume growth (tonnes)

What it is: the year-on-year change in tonnes sold. Formula: tonnes this period ÷ tonnes in the same period last year − 1. Worked example: Supreme sold 7.54 lakh tonnes in FY26, 12% more than about 6.73 lakh tonnes in FY25; in Q1 FY27 it sold about 1.58 lakh tonnes, 14.3% less. What drives it: underlying demand (water schemes, housing, farm incomes, monsoon), dealer stocking or destocking around resin price moves, share gains from unorganised makers, new capacity in new regions, and acquisitions such as Wavin. Benchmark: judge against the industry, not in isolation. Good is more than twice industry growth or, in a falling market, flat while the industry falls; average is in line; weak is a decline larger than the industry's. Comparison: in Q1 FY27 Astral's plumbing volume was flat against an industry down about 10% (good), Supreme fell 14.3% (slightly weak) and Finolex fell 26.5% (weak). Definition trap: Supreme reports total volume and piping separately; Astral reports plumbing segment volume, which includes tanks and bathware; Finolex reports pipes and fittings. Never compare one company's total with another's segment figure, and remember that a volume figure says nothing about price.

What it is: how many days of cost of goods the company holds in stock. Formula: inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365. The inventory effect is roughly Δresin price × (inventory days ÷ 365) × annual tonnes × resin share of inventory. Worked example: Supreme holds 80 days. A ₹10/kg resin move gives ₹10 × (80 ÷ 365) × 7.54 lakh tonnes × 0.75 ≈ ₹124 crore, about 8% of its FY26 operating profit (ILLUSTRATIVE). For Finolex, with 159 days on about 3.33 lakh tonnes, the same move is about ₹109 crore, about 16% of its operating profit, although part of its stock is its own feedstock. What drives it: business model (integration, exports, many plants), seasonal stocking (Finolex's VCM before the monsoon), and deliberate decisions to run stocks down before an expected fall. Benchmark: for a converter good is under 70 days, average 70–100, weak above 120; exporters and integrated makers naturally run higher. Comparison: Supreme 80, Astral 107, Time 111, Shaily 129, Garware 149, Finolex 159, EPL 177 (section 04). Definition trap: year-end inventory days can be distorted by a seasonal build or a deliberate run-down; Supreme's Q1 FY27 margin rose precisely because inventory was low when prices fell.

8. The PVC spread (for the integrated maker)

What it is: the gap between the PVC price and the cost of the feedstock used to make it. Formula: PVC price − EDC (or VCM, or ethylene-equivalent) cost per tonne. Worked example: in Q1 FY27 Finolex's disclosed PVC–EDC spread was $501 a tonne, about ₹47.8/kg at ₹95.5/$. After plant operating costs of, say, ₹35/kg (ILLUSTRATIVE), the resin leg earns about ₹13/kg on 2.72 lakh tonnes, roughly ₹350 crore a year. What drives it: the global PVC cycle (Chinese supply), crude and naphtha (feedstock), chlor-alkali economics, freight, and Indian trade policy. Benchmark: above about $500 a tonne is healthy; around $300 (FY23-type, approximate) makes the resin leg loss-making. Comparison: only Finolex among the seven makes PVC; for Supreme and Astral the spread is a cost question, not a profit line. Definition trap: the PVC–EDC and PVC–VCM spreads differ by the cost of converting EDC to VCM; Finolex discloses both ($501 and $122 in Q1 FY27). Use the one that matches the route the plant actually runs, and note that a high PVC price does not mean a high spread: in Q1 FY27 PVC was up 23% but the EDC spread was down 4%.

In plain terms

For a company that makes its own plastic, what matters is not whether PVC is expensive but whether PVC is expensive compared with the chemicals it is made from. In early 2026 both rose, so Finolex's resin plant earned slightly less per tonne even though PVC prices jumped.

9. Revenue and profit per unit outside pipes

What it is: the equivalent of realisation and EBITDA per kg for products sold by the piece or by area. Formula: revenue ÷ units, EBITDA ÷ units. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE; none of these companies discloses full unit data): EPL's ₹4,763 crore on about 8 billion tubes is about ₹5.95 per tube, with EBITDA of about ₹1.21 per tube. If oral care is about 5.5 billion tubes at about ₹3.8 and everything else about 2.5 billion at about ₹10.6, each 1% of tube volume shifted from oral care to beauty adds about ₹55 crore of revenue. Shaily at about ₹120 a pen; Garware's PPF at about ₹200 a square foot; Time's 210-litre drum at roughly ₹1,500–1,800 and a 10-kg composite LPG cylinder at roughly ₹3,000–3,500 (unsourced indicative figures). What drives it: mix within the category (decorated beauty tubes, auto-injectors versus simple pens, PPF versus window film). Benchmark: the trend matters more than the level: rising per-unit EBITDA at constant volume is good. Definition trap: unit counts are rarely disclosed, so most per-unit figures in research (including these) are back-calculated and should be treated as indicative.

10. Agricultural share of pipe volume

What it is: the share of a pipe maker's tonnes sold into irrigation and farm uses. Formula: agri tonnes ÷ total pipe tonnes. Worked example: Finolex's agri share was about 63% in FY26 and 69% in Q1 FY27; of its 67,699 tonnes in Q1 FY27, roughly 46,700 were agricultural. What drives it: distribution footprint, product range and seasonality (agri peaks before the monsoon). Benchmark: below 50% (plumbing-led) is good for stability; 50–65% average; above 65% means the company is hostage to the monsoon and rural dealer behaviour. Finolex targets 50:50. Comparison: Astral is plumbing-led; Supreme does not publish the split. Definition trap: agri pipe is lower value per kg than plumbing pipe, so a rising agri share depresses realisation and per-kg profit even if the business is doing well; it also makes Q1 comparisons seasonal.

11. Export share

What it is: revenue from customers outside India. Formula: export (or overseas) revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Garware exports about 75% of revenue; at 75% of ₹2,120 crore that is about ₹1,590 crore exposed to US demand, tariffs and the dollar. Supreme exports about $26 million (roughly ₹250 crore at ₹95.5/$), about 2%, and targets $150 million in six to seven years. What drives it: product competitiveness abroad, trade agreements (the India–UK deal's zero duty on plastic pipes), tariffs and the rupee. Benchmark: for film and device makers, more than 50% spread across several regions is good; more than 50% concentrated in one market (the US) is a risk. Comparison: section 03 chart. Definition trap: EPL's and Time's foreign revenue comes largely from overseas plants, not exports from India, so it carries currency-translation risk but little tariff risk; Garware's and Shaily's are true exports.

12. Capex intensity and utilisation

What it is: capital spending relative to revenue, read together with how full existing plants are. Formula: capex ÷ revenue; utilisation = output ÷ capacity. Worked example: Supreme plans about ₹1,000 crore of capex in FY27, about 9% of FY26 revenue, adding 1.10 lakh tonnes while running at an estimated 60–65% of installed capacity. Shaily spent about 19% of revenue while guiding to 36 million pens against 75 million capacity. What drives it: growth ambitions, regional expansion (pipes), customer commitments (pens), technology (TPU line). Benchmark: 5–9% of revenue with utilisation above 60% is good; 9–12% average; above 15% with low utilisation is ahead of demand. CRISIL puts industry pipe utilisation at about 70%. Definition trap: installed capacity in pipes is a soft number (it depends on the product mix run on each line), and investing cash flow includes acquisitions and treasury, so "capex" from a cash-flow statement can mislead.

13. Return on capital employed

What it is: operating profit earned on all the capital in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), averaged. Worked example: Shaily's FY26 ROCE was 29.3%; Supreme's 20.7% despite a 13.8% margin, because its plants turn their capital over quickly; Finolex's 11.8% despite a 16.5% margin, because about ₹5,000 crore of investments and cash sit in its capital base. What drives it: margin × asset turnover, and how much idle cash or investments are carried. Benchmark: good above 20%, average 14–20%, weak below 12%. Definition trap: the screener ROCE includes cash and investments in capital employed, which penalises cash-rich companies (Finolex, Garware) and flatters leveraged ones; an "operating ROCE" excluding surplus cash would rank Finolex and Garware higher.

16. Customer concentration

What it is: how much revenue depends on the largest customer or the top few. Formula: revenue from the top customer (or top five) ÷ total revenue. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE, because none of the seven discloses the figure): if a tube maker's largest customer buys 25% of its ₹4,763 crore, that customer controls about ₹1,190 crore of revenue and can press for price cuts at every contract renewal. What drives it: the archetype. Pipe makers sell to thousands of dealers and have almost no concentration. EPL sells to a small number of global oral-care houses, with a Colgate-type customer the largest. Shaily's consumer business depends heavily on IKEA, and its device business on a handful of generic drug partners. Time's composite cylinders depend on oil-company tenders. Benchmark: top customer below 15% of revenue is good, 15–30% average, above 30% weak. Comparison: pipes good; Garware (distributors and installers in many countries) average; EPL and Shaily weak by structure, though the stickiness of qualified tubes and filed pens offsets part of the risk. Definition trap: concentration cuts both ways. A customer that has written your pen into its drug approval or audited your tube line cannot leave quickly, so high concentration in a qualified product is less dangerous than the same share in a commodity. The disclosure is thin, so treat any figure in research as an estimate.

In plain terms

A pipe plant is a modest investment that pays well only when it is busy. At half capacity it barely earns its keep; at 80–90% it returns over 20% a year on the money tied up in it. That is why pipe makers chase volume so hard, why a quarter of dealer destocking hurts, and why Supreme's spare capacity is both an opportunity and a drag until it fills.

Definition traps, collected

  • Revenue growth in a pipe company says almost nothing. Pipe prices are reset off resin every few weeks. Supreme's Q1 FY27 revenue rose 4.2% while volume fell 14.3%; Finolex's revenue fell 15.3% on a 26.5% volume fall. Quote tonnes first.
  • EBITDA basis. Company EBITDA often includes other income; screener operating profit does not. Garware ₹500 crore vs ₹435 crore; Astral ₹1,109 crore vs ₹1,062 crore. Peer tables in this report use screener operating profit.
  • PAT basis. Supreme's consolidated profit includes its ~30% associate Supreme Petrochem: Q1 FY27 ₹281 crore (+39%) consolidated vs about ₹208 crore (+17%) excluding it. Finolex's FY26 profit is ₹599 crore consolidated vs ₹580 crore standalone (the primer uses standalone), and FY25's ₹800 crore included ₹703 crore of other income with a one-off gain. Shaily's Q1 FY27: ₹48.0 crore consolidated vs ₹52.5 crore standalone.
  • "Value-added" is company-defined. Supreme 42%, Time 29% and Garware 87% measure different things.
  • EBITDA per kg is comparable only within an archetype. Finolex's figure includes the resin margin; Astral's segment includes tanks and bathware.
  • Net cash is not what screener calls investments. Supreme's investments are mainly an associate stake; Finolex's include the Finolex Cables cross-holding; Time's borrowings are gross.
  • Share counts changed. Time's equity capital rose from ₹23 crore to ₹49 crore in FY26 (bonus issue plus a share sale to institutions), so per-share figures before FY26 must be adjusted.
  • Ownership lines are in transition. EPL's 26.38% "promoter" holding in June filings is now zero and will become about 51.8% Indorama.
  • Percentage margins move with resin prices. A constant ₹21/kg is 14% of a ₹150 realisation and 12.4% of ₹170.
  • Pass-through inflates growth. About 5 points of EPL's 25.3% Q1 FY27 revenue growth was raw-material pass-through; underlying margin was 19.6% against a reported 18.8%.
In plain terms

Most of the mistakes people make with these companies come from comparing numbers that look alike but are built differently: two "EBITDA" figures, one with interest income and one without; two "value-added" shares that mean different products; a net-cash figure that is really a stake in a sister company. Check the basis before the number.

How to read a plastics company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Pull volume first (minute 1). Tonnes for pipes (total and piping or plumbing), units or square feet where disclosed. Compare year on year, never quarter on quarter, because of the monsoon. Red flag: volume falling faster than the industry estimate the company itself quotes.
  2. Then realisation (minute 2). Segment revenue ÷ tonnes. Ask whether the change is resin (check PVC moves in the quarter) or mix. Red flag: realisation falling faster than resin.
  3. Then per-unit profit (minute 3). EBITDA per kg for pipes; EBITDA margin for others. Compare with the same quarter last year and with the full-year level.
  4. Ask about inventory (minute 4). Did resin rise or fall during the quarter? Did management quantify an inventory gain or loss? Strip it out to find the underlying per-kg profit. Red flag: a margin jump in a quarter of rising resin that management does not explain.
  5. Check the EBITDA and PAT basis (minute 5). Other income in or out? Associate (Supreme Petrochem) in or out? Consolidated or standalone? Pass-through (EPL)?
  6. Read the mix line (minute 6). Value-added share, CPVC share, healthcare share, consumer division share, beauty share. Rising is the re-rating story; falling (Time in Q1 FY27) needs an explanation.
  7. Look at working capital (minute 7). Inventory and receivable days at the half-year; operating cash flow. Red flag: profit rising while cash flow falls, especially at Time and Finolex where conversion is already weak.
  8. Note capacity and capex (minute 8). New lines commissioned, utilisation commentary (Wavin plants, Shaily's pen lines, Garware's TPU line, Astral's CPVC resin plant), capex guidance changes.
  9. Compare against guidance (minute 9). Supreme's +15–17% piping volume and 14–14.5% margin; Garware's >₹2,500 crore and 25% ±2%; Shaily's 36 million pens; EPL's high-teens growth and ~20% underlying margin; Astral's double-digit plumbing volume and 16–18% plumbing margin. Red flag: guidance held while the run-rate implies a miss.
  10. Finish with policy and price (minute 10). PVC floor status, any DGTR or Finance Ministry move, US tariff changes, partner drug approvals (Shaily), and the merger timetable (EPL, Time). These decide next quarter more than this one.
In plain terms

Start with how much was sold, then the price, then the profit on each unit. Before believing a big margin swing, ask whether plastic prices moved during the quarter. Then check what is included in "profit", whether the growth products are gaining share, whether cash is coming in, and whether the company is on track for what it promised.

Why 8× to 52×: the valuation split and the global lens

The market prices runway, returns and ownership quality, and discounts commodity exposure, customer power and complexity. Global peers show how unusual the Indian premiums are.

The six-fold spread in EV/EBITDA, from EPL's 8.5 times to Shaily's 52 times, is not random. It follows a small number of factors that investors weigh consistently. They pay for the length and visibility of growth (Shaily's pen runway; the pipe brands' formalisation and water-scheme demand), for returns on capital through the cycle (Supreme around 20% with no debt; Shaily 29%), and for clean balance sheets and settled ownership. They discount commodity exposure (Finolex's resin), customer power and slow growth (EPL's oral-care base), and a history of leverage or complexity (Time's overseas subsidiaries and listed subsidiary, EPL's ownership transition).

One way to make the multiples concrete is to ask how long a company would have to grow operating profit for its current multiple to fall to an ordinary level, if the share price stayed flat. At 15% annual EBITDA growth, Supreme's 29.3 times would fall to 15 times in about five years, Garware's and Astral's in about six, and Shaily's in about nine. Finolex, Time and EPL already trade below 15 times. This is an ILLUSTRATIVE calculation, not a forecast: it shows how much future growth each price already assumes.

The mix-shift premium

Garware trades above XPEL, the US paint-protection film leader, which has typically traded at 15–20 times EBITDA; Shaily trades above Ypsomed, the Swiss pen and auto-injector maker, which has traded around 30–40 times (both INDICATIVE, from memory). Technology and disruption: resin at home, new materials, new drugs

The changes that could reshape the group over five years: domestic PVC and CPVC capacity, material substitution, TPU film, GLP-1 formulations and recyclability rules.

Domestic PVC capacity. India's import dependence is about to shrink. Reliance is expanding from about 0.75 million tonnes a year toward about 1.5 million by 2027, and Adani is building 1 million tonnes at Mundra for FY28, with a first phase possibly from December 2026 and a further 1 million planned. If both arrive, domestic capacity roughly doubles from about 1.8 million tonnes. For converters this is good news: lower landed cost, less currency risk and fewer price shocks from freight and war. For Finolex it is mixed: lower volatility but the end of the scarcity that has given a western-India resin plant pricing power. Demand is expected to rise to about 6 million tonnes by FY30, so imports would still be needed. The feedstock economics behind these plants (ethylene, chlorine, EDC) are covered in our chemical intermediates report.

CPVC resin self-sufficiency. Astral's 40,000-tonne CPVC resin plant, due in December 2026 with trials in Q4 FY27, would be the first serious Indian challenge to the two or three global licensors that supply CPVC resin and compound to every Indian brand. If it works at scale, Astral gains a cost and supply-security edge in its most profitable category, and rivals may face a competitor that also controls their raw material. If capacity runs ahead of Astral's own needs, it could also push CPVC prices down across the industry.

Material substitution in pipes. PVC competes with HDPE, PE-RT, PEX and PPR in plumbing, and with ductile iron and steel in large-diameter water mains. Supreme and Astral span several materials; Finolex is PVC-centric. Supreme's Wavin licence adds engineered systems for water management where materials and installation know-how matter more than resin price.

Paint-protection film technology. The quality of the TPU base film (clarity, self-healing, resistance to yellowing) decides whether a PPF brand can command premium installer pricing. Low-priced Chinese TPU-based PPF is the main threat; Garware's in-house TPU line, due in Q3 FY27, is its defence, and India's anti-dumping action on Chinese PPF supports the home market.

GLP-1 formulations. Oral semaglutide and newer oral GLP-1 drugs could, at the margin, reduce demand for injector pens, and once-weekly or once-monthly injectables may need new device platforms. Shaily's pens are designed for today's generic semaglutide; its signing of two new intellectual-property-led platform projects suggests it is preparing for the next generation, but the risk of a format shift is real over a five-year view.

Recyclability and producer responsibility. India's Plastic Waste Management Rules impose extended producer responsibility on packaging, and global brand owners have pledged recyclable or recycled-content packaging. That favours EPL's Platina mono-material tubes (Colgate India uses them) and could penalise multi-layer laminates over time. EPL's coming integration with Indorama, which runs PET recycling, may strengthen its offer.

Composite cylinders and hydrogen. Type-IV composite cylinders are lighter and safer than steel, but adoption depends on oil-company tenders and on price relative to steel. Hydrogen demand in India is still pre-commercial. Time's PESO approvals are a barrier to entry; Supreme's entry shows the barrier is not permanent.

Automation and digital printing. In tubes and components, digital printing and automated decoration lower the cost of short runs, which suits beauty brands that launch many variants. That supports EPL's beauty mix shift and gives an edge to plants that invest in it.

In plain terms

Over the next few years India will make much more of its own PVC, Astral will start making its own CPVC, and recycling rules will push packaging toward simpler materials. Those changes mostly help pipe brands and EPL. The risks are Chinese low-cost film for Garware, new drug formats for Shaily, and new competitors in gas cylinders for Time.

Bull vs bear for the sub-sector

The case for and against owning this group, stated plainly.

The bull and bear cases differ by archetype, so they are best read as a set of claims that can be tested against the next four quarters rather than as a single view. The summary below is for the group as a whole, weighted toward the pipe makers that make up two-thirds of its market value.

The bull case

  • Structural pipe demand. Jal Jeevan Mission 2.0 (₹67,670 crore), urban water and sewerage, housing and irrigation, with organised brands gaining share in every resin crash.
  • Margins held through a crash. Q1 FY27 showed the leaders can protect per-kg profit (Supreme ₹25/kg, Astral plumbing 18.9%) even when volumes fall 10–15%.
  • A price floor reduces volatility. The $766/t minimum import price, and domestic PVC capacity from 2027, should make inventory losses smaller and rarer.
  • Mix-shift engines are working. Shaily's healthcare at 51% of revenue, Garware's Q1 revenue +28% and profit +60%, EPL's beauty growth, Supreme's value-added products at 42%.
  • Clean balance sheets. Group net cash of about ₹2,611 crore; four of seven are net cash and none is above 0.8× net debt to EBITDA.
  • Strategic interest. Indorama paid the equivalent of ~12× for EPL; Supreme bought Wavin's Indian business cheaply; consolidation can continue.

The bear case

  • Volume may be shrinking, not pausing. CRISIL expects FY27 pipe volumes to fall 3–5%; Supreme's +15–17% guidance needs ~28% growth over July–March.
  • Policy can reverse. The price floor expires around 23 January 2027; the 2025 duty recommendation was never notified; each policy switch moves PVC by ~₹10/kg.
  • Valuations assume a lot. Shaily at 52×, Astral at 36.5× (profit flat FY24–FY26) and Garware at 35.5× need years of high growth just to reach ordinary multiples.
  • Concentrated exposures. Garware on the US and its tariff regime; Shaily on a few drug partners and IKEA; EPL on a few oral-care houses.
  • Weak cash conversion in places. Time (26%) and Finolex (28%) turn little operating profit into cash; Shaily's free cash flow was ₹41 crore.
  • Governance and transitions. Finolex's family dispute and cross-holdings; EPL with no promoter until ~March 2027 and a polymer-making parent to come.

Weighing the two, the pipe makers' near-term numbers hinge on a question that cannot be answered yet: was Q1 FY27 a one-off destocking quarter, as Supreme argues, or the start of a year of lower volumes, as CRISIL expects? Margins held in Q1, which supports the brands' pricing power; but valuations at 29–36 times operating profit leave the pipe trio dependent on volume growth resuming. Outside pipes the question is execution: whether Shaily's capacity fills with partner launches, whether Garware's growth survives the US cycle and tariff changes, and whether EPL's new owner improves or dilutes its returns.

The most robust part of the bull case is the balance sheets: no member is stretched, so none is forced into a bad decision by a downturn. The most robust part of the bear case is policy: in a market where the government sets the resin price floor every six months and US tariffs have changed four times in a year, the earnings of the two largest archetypes are partly outside management's control.

In plain terms

The optimist sees a growing water and housing market, companies that kept their profits through a price crash, debt-free balance sheets and new businesses taking off. The pessimist sees pipe sales that may fall this year, a government that keeps changing the rules on plastic prices, high share prices for the fastest growers and a few companies that depend on one customer or one country.

Glossary

Terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • Anti-dumping duty (ADD): an extra import duty on goods sold into India below their fair price; recommended by DGTR, imposed only when the Finance Ministry notifies it.
  • Associate: a company in which another holds a significant minority stake (Supreme owns ~30% of Supreme Petrochem); its profit share is added to consolidated profit.
  • BOPET film: biaxially oriented polyester film, the base for packaging, industrial and window films; a commodity in its plain form.
  • CIF: cost, insurance and freight; the landed price of an import at the Indian port, before duties.
  • Composite (Type-IV) cylinder: a gas cylinder with a plastic liner wrapped in carbon or glass fibre; lighter than steel and does not rust.
  • CPVC: chlorinated PVC, which tolerates hot water; used for hot-and-cold plumbing and sold at a premium to PVC.
  • DGFT: Directorate General of Foreign Trade, which set the PVC minimum import price in July 2026.
  • DGTR: Directorate General of Trade Remedies, which investigates dumping and recommends duties.
  • Destocking: dealers selling down their inventory without reordering, typically when they expect prices to fall.
  • EBITDA per kg: operating profit divided by kilograms sold; the core profitability measure for pipe makers.
  • EDC and VCM: ethylene dichloride and vinyl chloride monomer, the feedstocks from which PVC is made.
  • EPR: extended producer responsibility; rules that make packaging producers responsible for collection and recycling.
  • EV/EBITDA: enterprise value (market value plus net debt) divided by operating profit; the main valuation yardstick in this report.
  • Fittings: elbows, tees, couplers and valves that connect pipes; higher value per kg than pipe.
  • HDPE: high-density polyethylene, used for drums, jerry cans, water and gas pipes.
  • IBC: intermediate bulk container, a large plastic tank in a metal cage used to ship liquids (about 1,000 litres).
  • Inventory effect: the one-off gain or loss from holding stock when raw-material prices change.
  • Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM): the government programme to provide piped water to every rural household; its second edition is budgeted at ₹67,670 crore.
  • Laminated tube: a squeeze tube made from layers of plastic (and sometimes aluminium) film; used for toothpaste and cosmetics.
  • Minimum import price (MIP): a floor below which imports are restricted; for PVC, $766 a tonne CIF from 24 July 2026 for six months.
  • Operating profit (screener): revenue minus operating costs, excluding other income; used in this report's tables.
  • PESO: Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation, which approves gas cylinders in India.
  • Platina: EPL's recyclable mono-material tube range.
  • PPF: paint-protection film, a clear polyurethane film applied over a car's paint.
  • Pen injector: a reusable or disposable pen-shaped device that delivers measured doses of an injectable drug such as insulin or semaglutide.
  • PVC: polyvinyl chloride, the plastic used for most pipes; made from salt-derived chlorine and ethylene.
  • QCO: quality-control order requiring BIS certification; the PVC QCO was withdrawn in November 2025.
  • Realisation: average revenue per unit sold (₹ per kg for pipes).
  • Semaglutide: a GLP-1 drug for diabetes and weight loss whose patents expired in India, Canada, Brazil and China in 2026.
  • Spread: for a resin maker, the PVC price minus the cost of its feedstock.
  • SWR: soil, waste and rainwater drainage pipes.
  • TPU: thermoplastic polyurethane, the base material of paint-protection film.
  • Utilisation: output as a share of installed capacity.
  • Value-added products (VAP): a company's own label for its higher-margin lines; not comparable across companies.
