Diversified metals and recycling: one price boom, opposite margins

A complete guide to a small, lopsided corner of the NSE 750: what is left of Vedanta after its five-way break-up (in effect a geared holding company on Hindustan Zinc's zinc and silver), and two scrap recyclers, Gravita India and Jain Resource Recycling, that turn dead batteries and imported copper wire back into metal. The same 2026 metal-price surge doubled the miner's operating profit and squeezed the recyclers' margins. This report explains why, what each number means, and what to watch next.

00

The one-page view: a miner that rides the price, two recyclers that pay for it

Three listed companies, two different species, one metal market. Read every "sector" number as a Vedanta number unless the recyclers are split out.

The NSE 750 files three companies under "Non - Ferrous Metals · Diversified Metals". On paper that sounds like a basket of similar businesses. In practice it holds one primary miner and holding company, Vedanta Limited, and two secondary-metal converters, Gravita India and Jain Resource Recycling. They sell the same metals (zinc, lead, copper, aluminium and silver, all priced off the London Metal Exchange) but they make money in opposite ways. Vedanta digs ore out of the ground at a fixed cost and keeps whatever the market pays above that cost. The recyclers buy scrap at a discount to the metal price, melt and refine it, and keep a thin conversion spread. When metal prices jump, the miner's profit jumps with them. The recyclers' profit barely moves, and the cash tied up in their inventory grows.

That contrast is the whole story of 2026. In Q1 FY27 (April to June 2026) Vedanta's operating profit rose 99% year on year to ₹8,501 crore on screener's basis, a 35% margin. Over the same quarter the two recyclers grew revenue 62% but operating profit only 14%, and their combined operating margin fell from 7.4% to 5.2%. Hindustan Zinc, the 60.71%-owned subsidiary that produces almost all of Vedanta's profit, then reported a record Q2 FY27 on 19 October 2026: EBITDA of ₹9,285 crore at a 62% margin, with silver contributing roughly 45% of profitability. None of the three members has yet reported Q2 FY27. Gravita's board meets on 27 October, Vedanta's on 28 October, and Jain has not announced a date.

The scale gap is extreme. Of the ₹92,245 crore of combined FY26 revenue, Vedanta contributes 85%. Of the ₹24,185 crore of operating profit it contributes 96%, and of the ₹1.08 lakh crore of market value at the 23 October 2026 close it contributes 82%. The recyclers together earned ₹725 crore of net profit on ₹13,808 crore of revenue and are valued at ₹19,894 crore, about 27 times those earnings. Vedanta's ₹88,570 crore market value, by contrast, is worth only about 70% of what its own Hindustan Zinc stake fetches on the market (₹1,27,234 crore). The market is paying a growth multiple for the recyclers and a holding-company discount for the miner.

The six forces that define the sub-sector

  • 1. The price deck versus the scrap spread. Vedanta is a geared bet on zinc, silver and the rupee: the company's own bridge attributes 99% of its Q1 FY27 EBITDA increase to prices and currency. The recyclers hedge their metal on the LME and earn a per-tonne spread between scrap and refined metal. Rising, volatile prices narrow scrap discounts, widen hedge timing gaps and inflate working capital. Same prices, opposite effects.
  • 2. Formalisation by regulation versus a cheap informal sector. The Battery Waste Management Rules 2022 created tradable recycling certificates for lead-acid batteries; the Hazardous Waste amendment of July 2025 (G.S.R. 438(E)) extends extended producer responsibility (EPR) to aluminium, copper and zinc scrap from 1 April 2026, with recycled-content mandates from FY28–FY29. Every rule raises the cost of the backyard smelter and the value of a registered plant. Enforcement is the variable.
  • 3. India is short of ore and long on scrap imports. FY26 imports: 2,028 kt of aluminium scrap, 467 kt of copper scrap (duty-free since February 2025) and 498 kt of spent lead-acid batteries. The Gulf supplies a fifth to a quarter of key streams, so the 2026 Hormuz closure hit the recyclers' feedstock. Exports face a 50% US Section 232 tariff on aluminium and copper derivatives.
  • 4. Vedanta group leverage and cash upstreaming. The unlisted parent, Vedanta Resources (VRL), still carries about US$4.7 billion of net debt. It is serviced by dividends and brand fees (₹2,727 crore paid by the Vedanta group in FY26) from what are now five listed companies. The promoter's VEDL shares sit under a negative lien, the promoter sold 1.66% in June 2026, the Enforcement Directorate searched group premises in June 2026, and the July 2025 Viceroy short report still hangs over the name.
  • 5. The Hindustan Zinc holdco discount. Vedanta's market value is about 70% of its HZL stake alone, which means the market puts a negative value on everything else it owns. Whether that gap closes depends on how much of HZL's cash reaches Vedanta's minority shareholders rather than the parent's lenders.
  • 6. Working capital is the recyclers' binding constraint. Jain's FY26 operating cash flow was −₹591 crore on 48% revenue growth: its IPO money became copper inventory. Gravita's borrowings rose from ₹286 crore to ₹736 crore alongside a ₹559 crore copper acquisition. At US$14,000 a tonne, copper needs about six times the working capital per tonne that lead does.

Three warnings before going further. First, "Vedanta" now means four different things: the pre-demerger group (FY26 revenue ₹1,74,075 crore), the screener-restated continuing company (₹78,437 crore), the residual company as it reports itself from Q1 FY27, and the five-company Vedanta family under Vedanta Resources. Third, recyclers' revenue is mostly metal value passing through: compare them on EBITDA per tonne and on cash conversion, not on margin percentages.

In plain terms

Picture two businesses selling the same kilo of zinc or copper. One owns the mine, so when the world price rises its costs stay put and the extra money is profit. The other buys old metal to melt down, so when the price rises the scrap it buys costs more too, and it has to borrow more to hold the same tonnage in its yard. That is why one company in this group just had the best quarter in its history and the other two saw their margins shrink at the same time.

01

From ore and scrap to metal: two value chains that meet at the LME price

The miner captures value underground, through ore grade and a silver credit. The recycler captures it at the scrap yard gate, through the discount it pays and the speed it turns its stock.

Non-ferrous metal reaches an Indian cable maker, battery factory or galvanising line by one of two routes. The primary route starts with ore: rock containing a few per cent of zinc, lead or copper is mined, crushed, concentrated and smelted, and the refined metal is sold at a price set on the London Metal Exchange plus a local premium. The secondary route starts with scrap: a spent car battery, a coil of old cable, a radiator or a pile of turnings is collected, sorted, melted and refined into metal that goes back into the same products. Both routes end at the same price. Their costs, risks and capital needs could hardly be more different.

What each member actually sells

Vedanta, after the 1 May 2026 demerger, sells refined zinc, primary lead and silver through Hindustan Zinc; mined zinc from Gamsberg and Black Mountain in South Africa and Namibia through Zinc International; copper cathode and rod from its Silvassa refinery (the Tuticorin smelter has been shut since May 2018); ferro-chrome from FACOR; and port services at the Vizag General Cargo Berth. Aluminium, oil and gas, power and iron ore have gone to four separately listed companies. Gravita sells recycled lead and lead alloys (88% of FY26 revenue), aluminium alloy, recycled plastic granules, reclaimed rubber, turnkey recycling plants and, since it bought Rashtriya Metal Industries in March 2026, copper and brass products. Jain sells copper ingots, anodes and, from late 2026, cathode, rod and busbar (67% of Q1 FY27 revenue), recycled lead and alloys, aluminium alloy, tin and plastics, and exports 62% of what it makes.

Where the value is captured

The margins on each link differ by an order of magnitude. A first-decile mine with a silver credit earns 60% or more of its revenue as EBITDA at today's prices. An integrated aluminium smelter with captive power and alumina, the business Vedanta has just spun off, earned close to half its revenue in Q1 FY27. A custom copper refiner without its own mine earns almost nothing: Vedanta's copper business lost ₹48 crore of EBITDA on ₹31,069 crore of revenue in FY26 because treatment and refining charges, the fee a smelter is paid to process someone else's concentrate, collapsed to zero. Recyclers earn 4–10% on revenue, but because their plants are cheap and their stock turns several times a year, their return on capital (16–26%) can rival the miner's consolidated figure.

The informal recycler is the hidden fifth link. Thousands of unregistered smelters melt batteries and cable in small furnaces without paying goods and services tax, investing in pollution control or meeting labour norms. They can outbid organised plants for scrap because their costs are lower, and they sell metal at a discount for cash. The battery and scrap rules described in Section 8 exist to close that arbitrage; the listed recyclers' long-run value rests on how fast it closes.

In plain terms

A mine makes money because the ground is rich and the cost of digging is fixed; a recycler makes money because it buys old metal a little cheaper than new metal and turns it quickly. The mine's margin depends on the world price; the recycler's depends on the gap between two prices and on how much cash it has to tie up while the metal sits in the yard.

02

Size, growth and the price deck: copper and silver boomed, lead did not

The 2026 metal-price surge was uneven. The metals that drive Vedanta (zinc, silver) rose sharply; lead, the recyclers' main metal, was flat.

Every revenue line in this sub-sector is a tonnage multiplied by an LME price and an exchange rate, so the starting point is the price deck. Between 2020 and the first ten months of 2026, the average LME copper price rose 119% to US$13,541 a tonne, aluminium 94% to US$3,305, zinc 56% to US$3,539, and silver more than tripled to an average of US$73.2 an ounce in Q1 FY27. Lead, which accounts for most of Gravita's revenue and about a quarter of Jain's, rose just 5%, to US$1,908. On 17 August 2026 copper set a record of US$14,850; on 23 October 2026 the LME official cash settlement was copper US$14,415, zinc US$3,790, aluminium US$3,043 and lead US$1,886.

Market size in tonnes

India's non-ferrous markets are smaller than its steel market but growing faster. Zinc demand is roughly 1.1–1.2 million tonnes a year (an indicative industry estimate), growing 6–8% a year with steel galvanising; Hindustan Zinc refines about one million tonnes and holds about 74% of the primary market, with imports (195 kt of high-grade zinc in FY26, 70% from Korea) supplying most of the rest. Lead consumption is roughly 1.3–1.6 million tonnes (indicative), 80–85% of it in batteries. India's secondary lead output is about 0.85 million tonnes according to Gravita's FY26 annual report, so scrap already supplies most of the country's lead. Copper demand including scrap was about 15.6 lakh tonnes in FY26, with recycled copper supplying around 39%. Primary aluminium output is about 4.2 million tonnes; India imported 2.03 million tonnes of aluminium scrap in FY26, the first year above 2 million.

03

Same prices, opposite margins: why the boom doubled one profit and squeezed two

Force 1. A miner owns the gap between price and a fixed cost. A recycler owns a spread between two moving prices, and pays for the stock in between.

Vedanta's own EBITDA bridge for Q1 FY27 is the cleanest demonstration of what a price-geared miner is. EBITDA rose from ₹4,267 crore in Q1 FY26 to ₹8,469 crore, an increase of ₹4,202 crore. Higher LME and silver prices contributed ₹3,145 crore and a weaker rupee another ₹1,012 crore: ₹4,157 crore, or 99% of the increase, came from what the company calls "market and regulatory" factors. Higher volume added ₹293 crore and higher costs took away ₹320 crore; marketing and other items added ₹71 crore. Operations, in other words, netted to roughly zero. That is not a criticism of management. It is a description of the business: the residual Vedanta is a leveraged price-taker whose quarterly result is decided mostly in London and on the rupee.

Vedanta's published sensitivities make the same point. Every US$100 a tonne on zinc moves annual EBITDA by ₹1,000–1,050 crore; every US$1 an ounce on silver by ₹200–225 crore; every rupee of depreciation against the dollar by ₹350–375 crore. A US$1,000 move in copper is worth only ₹55–70 crore, because Silvassa is a custom refiner that earns a processing fee rather than the metal price. With zinc up US$367 a tonne between Q1 and Q2 FY27 averages, the zinc sensitivity alone implies an annualised EBITDA gain of roughly ₹3,700–3,850 crore, about ₹900–950 crore a quarter, before silver (indicative arithmetic on company sensitivities).

Why the recyclers lost margin in the same quarter

The recyclers are hedged. When Gravita or Jain buys a tonne of scrap, it sells the equivalent metal forward on the LME, so a rising price neither helps nor hurts the value of its stock. What the recycler earns is the spread: the gap between what it pays for scrap (a discount to the metal content's LME value) and what it sells refined metal for, less conversion cost. Rising, volatile prices hurt that spread in four ways. Scrap holders hold back material waiting for higher prices, so the discount narrows. Hedges are marked at quarter-end while physical sales lag by weeks or months, so reported margins swing between quarters. Percentage margins fall mechanically because revenue rises with the price while the rupee spread per tonne does not. And working capital, the cash tied up in inventory and receivables, rises in proportion to the price, so interest costs and borrowings rise.

All four showed up in Q1 FY27. Jain's copper segment margin fell to 2.48% from 4.70% a year earlier, though its copper EBITDA per tonne (₹31,027) was back near its "normalised" ₹30,000–32,000 after a disastrous Q4 FY26 (about ₹13,000–14,000 a tonne). Gravita's operating margin on screener's basis fell from 9.7% to 7.5% as it consolidated Rashtriya Metal Industries' lower-margin copper and brass business; on the company's adjusted basis it was 9.8%. And the 2026 West Asia war, which closed the Strait of Hormuz for weeks from 28 February, cut off scrap supply from the Gulf: Gravita's lead volumes fell 5% year on year and Jain's 12%.

In plain terms

When metal prices jump, a mine's costs do not, so nearly all of the extra revenue becomes profit: Vedanta's own accounts say 99% of its profit growth last quarter came from prices and the weaker rupee. A recycler has locked in its selling price when it buys scrap, so the price jump does not help it; it just has to hold more expensive stock and finds sellers of scrap driving a harder bargain.

There is a second-order symmetry worth noting. The recyclers' exposure to the price level is not zero but negative over a cycle: higher prices raise the working capital they must finance at 8–9% interest, compress percentage margins that some investors still screen on, and encourage informal competitors who do not hedge to take a punt on rising prices with cheap scrap. Falling prices do the reverse, releasing working capital and widening scrap discounts as holders rush to sell. The miner, conversely, has a strongly positive exposure. A portfolio holding all three members is therefore less exposed to metal prices than Vedanta alone, but because Vedanta is 82% of the combined market value, that diversification is small in practice.

04

Who buys the metal: galvanising lines, battery plants, cable mills and solar paste

Four end-markets set demand. Zinc follows steel, lead follows the installed base of batteries, copper and aluminium follow electrification, and silver follows solar panels and investors.

Demand in this sub-sector is really four separate demand stories stapled together. The members feel each one differently, and the timing differs: zinc demand moves with steel output this year, lead scrap supply moves with batteries sold three to five years ago, and silver moves with global investors as much as with any Indian factory.

Zinc: a steel derivative

Roughly half to three-fifths of zinc goes into galvanising: coating steel sheet, wire, pipes and structures to stop rust. Transmission towers, solar mounting structures, pre-engineered buildings, auto body panels and crash barriers all use galvanised steel. India's crude steel capacity of about 220 million tonnes is planned to reach 315–335 million tonnes by FY31 (see the Iron & Steel report), and galvanised flat products are among the fastest-growing grades. That supports zinc demand growth of roughly 6–8% a year (indicative). The rest of zinc goes into die-cast alloys, brass (copper plus zinc), zinc oxide for tyres and chemicals, and batteries. Hindustan Zinc sells about 74% of India's primary zinc and competes mainly with imports from Korea Zinc and other Asian smelters; the price it charges is the LME price plus a domestic premium that reflects the cost of importing.

Lead: the battery installed base

Lead-acid batteries take 80–85% of lead. They start almost every petrol and diesel vehicle (India makes roughly 25 million vehicles a year, most of them two-wheelers), back up homes and offices through inverters, and keep telecom towers and data centres running through uninterruptible power supplies. Replacement demand is two to three times original-equipment demand. The key point for recyclers is that every battery sold becomes scrap in two to five years, so the scrap pool grows with the installed base of vehicles and inverters, not with this year's sales. A downturn in new car sales does not shrink next year's scrap supply; a decade of lithium-ion substitution eventually would (Section 20). Battery makers such as Exide and Amara Raja are both the recyclers' biggest customers (Gravita's two largest customers took 38.9% of its FY26 revenue) and, through their own captive recycling plants, their competitors.

Copper and aluminium: electrification

Copper goes into wire and cable, transformers, motors and electronics; aluminium into power conductors, vehicles, packaging and construction. Grid investment, renewable energy, data centres and electric vehicles are pushing both. India's copper demand of about 15.6 lakh tonnes is growing at roughly 8–10% a year. At US$14,000 a tonne, copper is expensive enough that cable makers substitute aluminium where they can and buy more recycled copper; the Cables report and the Copper report cover both effects. For Jain, the more relevant demand is abroad: 62% of its FY26 revenue was exported, with China the largest foreign buyer, followed by Singapore and South Korea. Secondary aluminium alloy goes mainly to die-casters making two-wheeler and car parts.

Silver: solar, electronics and investment

Silver is a by-product of Hindustan Zinc's lead smelting, but it has become the single largest swing factor in Vedanta's profit. Industrial demand (silver paste on solar cells, electronics, electric-vehicle components) has grown fast, and investment demand pushed the LBMA price to an average of US$73.2 an ounce in Q1 FY27, against US$33.7 a year earlier. In Q2 FY27 silver sales brought in ₹4,111 crore, 27% of Hindustan Zinc's revenue, but about 45% of its profitability, because silver carries almost no incremental cost once the lead circuit is running.

The holdco: copper is half the revenue and none of the profit

Residual Vedanta's consolidated ₹100 looks different. In Q1 FY27 copper supplied ₹8,538 crore of ₹23,456 crore of revenue, about ₹36 of every ₹100, and earned ₹11 crore of EBITDA: the refinery passes the metal value straight through. Zinc India supplied about ₹55 of revenue and ₹35 of EBITDA; Zinc International, FACOR and the port made up most of the rest. The company quotes a 57% "EBITDA margin excluding copper" for exactly this reason. Below EBITDA, depreciation takes about ₹5, interest about ₹3, other income (including treasury income on ₹19,992 crore of cash) adds back several rupees, and tax takes about ₹8. A large slice of what is left belongs to Hindustan Zinc's minority shareholders (the Government of India's 27.92% and the public), who own 39.29% of the main profit engine.

The recycler: ₹84 of scrap, ₹8 of conversion, ₹8 of EBITDA

For a recycler, the overwhelming cost is the scrap itself: ₹80 for Gravita's lead-heavy mix, ₹88–90 for Jain's copper-heavy mix, about ₹84 blended. Conversion (furnace fuel and power, fluxes, alloying metals, labour, logistics and pollution control) takes ₹7–9. EBITDA is ₹6–10 (Jain 5.9%, Gravita 10.3% in FY26). Depreciation is tiny (₹0.2–0.9) because the plants are cheap; interest takes ₹0.6–1.0 because inventory is financed with short-term bank lines; tax takes ₹1–2. Net profit ends at roughly ₹3.6 for Jain and ₹8.9 for Gravita. Because ₹84 of the ₹100 is metal value that simply passes through, a 1% change in what the recycler pays for scrap, relative to the LME, is worth about ₹0.84, or 10% of its EBITDA. That is why sourcing is the core competence.

Force 3. Scrap imports rose by double digits in FY26 across every major stream. The Gulf supplies a fifth to a quarter of them, and trade rules decide who can buy and sell.

India has rich zinc-lead deposits in Rajasthan and little else in non-ferrous ore. Trade rules on the way out

On the export side, the United States' Section 232 tariff of 50% on aluminium and on copper semi-finished products and derivatives (copper since 1 August 2025; refined cathode exempt, with a possible duty from 2027 undecided) limits what Indian rod, brass and alloy makers can send to America. Since 24 July 2026 Indian goods generally face a 10% Section 301 tariff on top of most-favoured-nation duty, a measure under legal challenge. The European Union's carbon border adjustment mechanism moved to its definitive phase on 1 January 2026 for aluminium and steel; it penalises coal-powered primary aluminium but favours recycled metal, whose embedded emissions are a fraction of primary. China, which banned most solid-waste imports between 2018 and 2021, now again buys high-grade copper and aluminium scrap as "recycled raw material" and competes with India for the same US and Gulf supply, while also being Jain's largest export market.

In plain terms

India's recyclers are, to a large extent, processors of other countries' rubbish: American, British and Gulf scrap metal and dead batteries arrive by ship, are melted down in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu, and leave as metal for Indian battery makers or for buyers in China. That makes them exposed to shipping routes, foreign scrap prices and tariffs, as the 2026 Gulf war showed.

For investors the implication is straightforward: scrap-import data are a leading indicator for the recyclers. Rising imports at stable or wider discounts mean rising volumes and stable EBITDA per tonne; flat imports with a strong LME usually mean a scrap squeeze. The monthly TradeStat release is one of the few public datasets that tells you how the recyclers' quarter is going before they report.

07

A zinc monopoly, two organised recyclers and a long informal tail: structure and business models

Primary zinc is a one-company market. Secondary metals are fragmented, with two listed consolidators, battery makers' captive plants and thousands of unregistered smelters.

Concentration differs completely by metal and by route. At the primary level, Indian zinc is effectively a monopoly: Hindustan Zinc holds about 74% of the market and the rest is imported, with no other Indian zinc miner or smelter of scale. Primary lead is a by-product of the same company. Primary copper is an oligopoly of three refiners (Hindalco's Dahej, Adani's Kutch Copper and Vedanta's Silvassa; see the Copper report). Primary aluminium, now outside this sub-sector, is three companies: Vedanta Aluminium Metal (VAML), Hindalco and NALCO.

Secondary metals are the opposite. In lead, the organised listed players are Gravita, Jain and the smaller Pondy Oxides; battery makers Exide and Amara Raja run captive recycling plants; hundreds of registered small smelters operate below them; and a large informal tail, commonly put at 50–60% of the secondary pool (an indicative figure with no authoritative recent source), melts batteries without paying tax or meeting pollution norms. Gravita calls itself India's largest organised lead recycler. In copper, Jain claims to have been India's largest non-ferrous recycler by revenue in FY22–FY24, competing with Gravita's RMIL, Hindalco and many rod and brass makers in Jamnagar and elsewhere in Gujarat. Secondary aluminium is extremely fragmented, with hundreds of small remelters around Jamnagar, Chennai, Delhi NCR and Pune.

08

Rules that formalise scrap: EPR certificates, recycled-content mandates and a duty-free copper door

Force 2. Regulation is the organised recyclers' tailwind. It raises the cost of the informal smelter and creates a second revenue stream for registered plants.

For most of its history, secondary metals in India were regulated mainly through pollution and hazardous-waste permits, which the informal sector largely ignored. Three instruments changed that between 2022 and 2026, and a fourth, on customs duty, changed the economics of imported copper scrap.

Battery Waste Management Rules 2022 and amendments

Notified in August 2022, the Battery Waste Management Rules impose extended producer responsibility (EPR) on battery makers and importers. A producer must ensure that a set percentage of the batteries it placed on the market in earlier years, keyed to their expected life, is collected and recycled. It can do that through its own recycling plant or by buying EPR certificates from registered recyclers on a Central Pollution Control Board portal. The rules set minimum material-recovery targets for recyclers (automotive and industrial lead-acid batteries 50% rising to 60% by 2026-27; EV and portable batteries 70% rising to 90%). A 2024 amendment fixed a price band for certificates: a ceiling of 100% and a floor of 30% of the Environmental Compensation a producer would pay for non-compliance. That puts a floor under recyclers' certificate income. A second 2024 amendment mandates minimum recycled content in new batteries from FY2027-28. Paying Environmental Compensation does not discharge the obligation.

The Hazardous Waste amendment of 2025: EPR for copper, aluminium and zinc scrap

The more recent and less understood change is the Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Amendment Rules 2025, G.S.R. 438(E) of 1 July 2025, in force from 1 April 2026. It creates a new EPR chapter for scrap of aluminium, copper and zinc. Producers must meet recycling targets that start at 10% in 2026-27 and rise to 75% by 2032-33, with minimum domestic recycled content in new products from FY28–FY29 (a metal-specific schedule: 5% rising to 10%; Jain cites FY28, a legal summary cites FY2028-29 for aluminium products). Producers, recyclers, collection agents and bulk consumers using 1,000 tonnes or more a year register on a CPCB portal. Certificates are valid for two years, and Environmental Compensation is refunded at 85%, 60% or 30% if compliance is achieved within one, two or three years. Jain's management called it positive for both domestic scrap availability and recycled-metal pricing but said quantification was "premature".

The ₹1,500 crore recycling scheme: not for lead or copper scrap

The Cabinet approved a ₹1,500 crore critical-mineral recycling incentive scheme on 3 September 2025, under the ₹16,300 crore National Critical Mineral Mission, with guidelines on 2 October 2025. It runs from FY26 to FY31 and offers a 20% capital subsidy plus an operating subsidy on incremental sales, capped at ₹50 crore per large entity and ₹25 crore per small one, with a third reserved for small and new recyclers. Its targets are at least 270 kt of recycling capacity, about 40 kt of critical minerals and around ₹8,000 crore of investment. The feedstock it covers is e-waste, lithium-ion battery scrap and catalytic converters, not lead-acid batteries or copper and aluminium scrap. Only Gravita's 6,000 tpa lithium-ion plant at Mundra is plausibly eligible among the members, and a ₹50 crore cap is immaterial to valuations.

Mining rules that matter for Vedanta

Hindustan Zinc pays royalty on zinc and lead as a percentage of the LME price, plus District Mineral Foundation contributions (30% of royalty for pre-2015 leases) and 2% to the National Mineral Exploration Trust. The Supreme Court's judgment of 25 July 2024 allowed states to tax mineral rights and mineral-bearing land, with arrears from 2005 payable over twelve years; any Rajasthan levy would fall directly on HZL's costs. HZL's leases were extended to 2030 under the mining law, with auction thereafter. On the governance side, SEBI's listing rules require shareholder approval of material related-party transactions, which includes brand-fee agreements such as HZL's 3% fee to Vedanta.

In plain terms

The government now makes battery makers, and from April 2026 users of copper, aluminium and zinc, prove that a growing share of what they sell is recycled by a registered plant. They do it by buying certificates from recyclers like Gravita and Jain. That gives the registered recyclers an extra income stream and makes it harder for unregistered backyard smelters to sell their metal to big buyers. How fast the rules are enforced decides how much this is worth.

One caveat for analysts: neither Gravita nor Jain discloses EPR-certificate income separately in the documents reviewed. Its value is therefore inferred rather than measured, and claims about the scale of the "formalisation dividend" should be treated as directional until a company reports it.

Booms, busts and break-ups: four decades that built a holdco and two recyclers

Privatisation built Vedanta, debt-funded acquisitions loaded its parent, commodity busts tested it, and a five-way demerger reshaped it. The recyclers grew up in the shadow of the informal sector.

The history of this sub-sector is mostly the history of one group. Anil Agarwal began trading scrap and cable in Mumbai in 1976 and built Sterlite Industries. Its defining moves came during India's privatisation of state metal companies: it bought 51% of Bharat Aluminium (BALCO) in 2001, and in 2002–03 took control of Hindustan Zinc, first buying 26% plus an open offer and then a further 18.92% from the government. Those two assets are still the core of the five listed Vedanta companies. Vedanta Resources listed in London in 2003; Sesa Goa (iron ore) was bought in 2007; Cairn India was acquired in 2011 for about US$8.7 billion, funded largely with debt; and in 2013 Sesa Goa and Sterlite merged into what became Vedanta Ltd.

The commodity bust of 2015–16 showed how geared the structure was. Vedanta's FY16 operating profit was negative ₹18,479 crore after impairments and its net loss ₹17,862 crore. In May 2018 police fired on protesters near the Tuticorin copper smelter, killing 13, and the plant was shut; it has never reopened. The same year VRL delisted from London. An attempt to delist Vedanta Ltd in India failed in October 2020. Then the post-pandemic super-cycle produced record operating profit of ₹44,824 crore in FY22, and the group used it to pay itself: Vedanta's dividend payout ran at 357% of consolidated profit in FY23 and 259% in FY24, funded partly by special dividends from Hindustan Zinc, as VRL refinanced bonds falling due in 2024.

In September 2023 Vedanta's board approved a plan to split the company into six. The base-metals entity was later dropped, leaving copper inside Vedanta. The recyclers' history

Jain's roots go back to 1953, when the family started Jain Metal Rolling Mills in Chennai; the current company converted from a partnership and LLP structure in 2022 and listed on 1 October 2025 after a ₹1,250 crore IPO at ₹232 (₹500 crore fresh, ₹750 crore offer for sale), subscribed 16.76 times. In plain terms

Hindustan Zinc is one of the world's lowest-cost zinc producers and a top-ten silver producer. Its margin has risen for three years straight. Every rupee of Vedanta's value starts here.

Why the cost is so low, and what royalty adds

In plain terms

Hindustan Zinc owns some of the richest zinc mines in the world, and they happen to contain a lot of silver. Digging out a tonne of zinc costs it about US$700, plus about US$500 in royalty to the state, and the metal sells for about US$3,800. The silver comes almost free. When silver prices doubled, almost all of the extra money became profit. That is why Vedanta had a record year even though its copper business earned nothing.

The battery loop: EPR, the informal smelter and a tonne of recycled lead Deep dive

Lead is the most recycled metal in India and the most informally recycled. The economics of one tonne explain why regulation, not demand, decides the organised recyclers' future.

A lead-acid battery is about 60% lead by weight, and almost all of it can be recovered. That makes lead the metal with the highest recycling rate in the world: globally, secondary lead accounts for about two-thirds of refined supply (67.4% according to Gravita's FY26 annual report). India is no exception, with secondary output of roughly 0.85 million tonnes. What makes India different is who does the recycling. A large share of spent batteries still goes to unregistered smelters that break batteries by hand, pour the acid on the ground and melt the lead in open furnaces. The listed recyclers compete with them for every battery.

The informal smelter's cost advantage

The informal sector's edge is not technology; it is what it does not pay. An unregistered smelter that buys batteries for cash and sells lead without invoices avoids 18% GST on the sale, worth about ₹34,000 on a tonne priced at ₹1,90,000 if the transaction goes entirely unrecorded. It skips most pollution control, effluent treatment and slag disposal (about ₹4,000 a tonne at an organised plant) and pays lower wages with fewer safety measures (perhaps ₹1,500–2,000). Against that, it recovers less lead (crude furnaces may lose a tenth of the metal, worth perhaps ₹6,000 a tonne) and cannot sell to large battery makers who need GST invoices and certified alloys. On these indicative numbers, an informal operator can still pay noticeably more for a battery than an organised plant earning ₹22,000 a tonne and stay profitable.

That arithmetic is why the Battery Waste Management Rules matter. A battery maker that must show recycling equal to a rising share of its past sales has to route batteries, or buy certificates, through registered recyclers. Certificates trade within a band set at 30–100% of the Environmental Compensation for non-compliance, so the registered recycler earns something the informal smelter cannot. If a certificate were worth, say, ₹2–3 a kilogram of lead recycled (a purely illustrative figure; neither listed recycler discloses certificate income), it would add 9–14% to the ₹22-a-kilogram EBITDA in the worked example and narrow the informal advantage. The minimum recycled-content mandate for new batteries from FY2027-28 adds a second lever: battery makers will need certified secondary lead in their product, not just paperwork.

In plain terms

A backyard smelter that pays no tax and dumps its waste can afford to pay more for a dead battery than a licensed factory. The new rules try to fix that by making battery companies prove their old batteries were recycled properly, which they can only do by dealing with registered recyclers. Every year the rules are enforced more strictly, more batteries should flow to companies like Gravita and Jain.

Where the lead comes from

About half or more of the organised recyclers' feed is imported. India imported 498 kt of spent lead-acid batteries in FY26 (up 32%, with the United States the largest source at 142 kt) and 205 kt of other lead scrap. At about 60% recovery, those imports carry roughly 500 kt of lead units, compared with India's secondary output of about 0.85 million tonnes (indicative calculation). Domestic sourcing is about 35% of Gravita's Indian plants' feed. Imports are attractive because they come from formal channels abroad, arrive in bulk and avoid the informal sector's competition at home, but they carry freight, currency and hazardous-waste-permit risks, and the 2026 Gulf disruption showed how fragile some routes are.

Captive recyclers, customers and the lithium question

Battery makers complicate the picture. Exide and Amara Raja operate their own recycling plants, which lets them meet part of their EPR obligation in-house and gives them bargaining power over independent recyclers. In the long run, lithium-ion batteries threaten the scrap pool. Electric two-wheelers, telecom towers and home storage are moving to lithium iron phosphate, which shrinks future lead-acid scrap over a decade, though starting batteries in combustion vehicles and UPS systems remain lead-acid for now. Gravita commissioned a 6,000 tpa lithium-ion recycling plant at Mundra in FY26, which it describes as India's first commercial-scale dedicated facility, and is researching black-mass refining. That plant is the members' main route into the ₹1,500 crore recycling incentive scheme, whose ₹50 crore per-entity cap limits the benefit.

In plain terms

When the Gulf war cut off scrap supplies, the listed recyclers processed less lead but earned more on each tonne, because battery makers still needed certified lead and fewer people could supply it. That is a sign of pricing power, but also a warning: if scrap becomes plentiful again, the extra margin may go.

Why margin percentage falls even when EBITDA per tonne rises

A tonne of copper sold for about ₹12.5 lakh in Q1 FY27, against about ₹2.2 lakh for a tonne of lead. Jain earned ₹31,027 of EBITDA on each tonne of copper and ₹18,660 on each tonne of lead. Shifting the mix toward copper therefore raises profit per tonne and cuts the percentage margin at the same time. Gravita's management made the point on its Q1 call: being fully hedged, a higher LME price (about US$13,500 against about US$9,000 a year earlier) dilutes margin percentage but not EBITDA per tonne. Anyone screening recyclers on margin percentage will conclude, wrongly, that the copper pivot is destroying value; anyone looking only at EBITDA per tonne will miss what it does to capital.

Jain's copper business today is mostly ingot and anode sold close to the LME price, much of it abroad: 62% of FY26 revenue was exported, to China (about 27% of revenue), Singapore (about 25%) and South Korea (about 7%). Gravita also plans its own scrap yards in the United States and other developed markets by FY27-end.

In plain terms

Jain mostly melts imported copper scrap into basic copper bars and sells them abroad at roughly the world price, earning about 2.5% on each sale. Gravita bought a company that turns copper and brass into finished products and earns more than twice as much per tonne. Both are betting that more processing means more profit per tonne, but both need much more money tied up in stock to do it.

The working-capital arithmetic

The real cost of copper is cash. A recycler that adds 1,000 tonnes a month of throughput (12,000 tonnes a year) and holds 60 days of working capital needs very different sums for each metal. In lead, at ₹1.9 lakh a tonne, annual revenue is about ₹228 crore and 60 days of working capital about ₹37.5 crore; at ₹18,660 a tonne, EBITDA is about ₹22.4 crore, a 60% return on that working capital before fixed assets. In copper at Jain's realisation of ₹12.5 lakh a tonne, revenue is about ₹1,500 crore and working capital about ₹247 crore; at ₹31,027 a tonne, EBITDA is about ₹37.2 crore, a 15% return. At RMIL-type economics (₹9.9 lakh a tonne revenue, ₹55,151 EBITDA a tonne) the return is about 34%. Copper therefore needs roughly six to seven times the cash per tonne that lead does for less than twice the EBITDA, unless it moves well up the value chain.

Reading the numbers: a KPI masterclass for miners and recyclers Deep dive

Two equations, fourteen indicators, one plant P&L, a scorecard, the traps, and a ten-minute routine for results day. Every indicator is explained for both archetypes where it applies.

Analysing this sub-sector means running two different models side by side. For the miner, the question each quarter is how much of the price deck reached profit, and how much of that profit reached the listed shareholder. For the recyclers, the question is how many tonnes moved, at what spread per tonne, and how much cash they had to tie up to move them. Revenue, the headline number most investors start with, is close to meaningless for both: Vedanta's is inflated by copper that earns nothing, and the recyclers' moves with the LME price of metal they have already hedged.

The miner's equation

Hindustan Zinc, and therefore residual Vedanta, earns money according to a simple formula. Refined tonnes sold, multiplied by the price received (LME plus a local premium, converted at the rupee rate), less the cost of production excluding royalty, less royalty, plus the silver credit, gives EBITDA. Management controls volume and cost; it does not control the LME, the rupee, the silver price or the royalty rate.

The recycler's equation

A recycler's equation runs per tonne. Realisation per tonne (LME-linked, plus any alloy or product premium) less scrap cost per tonne, less conversion cost per tonne, gives EBITDA per tonne; multiply by tonnes sold and add certificate and turnkey income to get EBITDA. Management controls sourcing (and therefore the scrap discount), conversion efficiency, product mix and volume. It does not control the LME, which it hedges away, or the availability of imported scrap.

The worked example makes the copper trap concrete. In plain terms

For the miner, ask: how many tonnes, at what world price, against what cost per tonne, plus how much silver? For the recycler, ask: how many tonnes, and how many rupees did it keep on each one? If you start from revenue or margin percentage instead, both businesses will mislead you.

What it is. KPI 3 · Silver output and price sensitivity

What it is. Tonnes of silver produced and sold, and the profit impact of a change in the silver price. Formula. Silver EBITDA ≈ ounces sold × (realised price − royalty − small refining cost); 1 tonne = 32,150.7 troy ounces. KPI 4 · Segment EBITDA and the copper drag

What it is. EBITDA reported by business segment. Formula. Segment revenue − segment costs (after common-cost allocation). Worked example. In Q1 FY27 Vedanta's EBITDA of ₹8,469 crore split into Zinc India ₹8,096 crore, Zinc International ₹250 crore, FACOR ₹101 crore and copper ₹11 crore. Removing copper's ₹8,538 crore of revenue lifts the margin from 36% to 57%, the "ex-copper" figure management quotes. Drivers. Prices and costs per segment; for copper, treatment and refining charges (zero or negative in 2026), rod premiums and acid credits. Benchmark. For a holdco, a dominant segment above 90% of EBITDA signals concentration risk; a loss-making segment that consumes capital is a red flag. How members compare. Zinc India is 96% of Vedanta's EBITDA. Gravita's FY26 segment results show lead at ₹454 crore of ₹480 crore of total segment profit (95%); copper contributed ₹2.4 crore in its first 19 days. Jain reports copper, lead and aluminium segments. Definition trap. Segment EBITDA depends on how common costs are allocated (Vedanta's copper figure includes about ₹86 crore of allocation); compare trends within a company, not levels across companies.

Segment reporting also explains how Vedanta's consolidated numbers relate to Hindustan Zinc's own. Vedanta's "Zinc India" segment is essentially HZL, but the figures differ slightly because of consolidation adjustments, intra-group eliminations (including the 3% brand fee HZL pays Vedanta, which cancels out on consolidation) and timing. HZL's Q1 FY27 EBITDA was ₹8,074 crore on its own reporting; Vedanta's Zinc India segment showed ₹8,096 crore. When previewing Vedanta's Q2 FY27 result on 28 October, HZL's already-published ₹9,285 crore is therefore the best single input: on Vedanta's Q1 segment mix it would imply group EBITDA of roughly ₹9,600–9,800 crore, depending on Zinc International's ramp-up and copper (an indicative calculation, not a forecast).

KPI 10 · Dividend payout and upstreaming

What it is. The share of profit paid out as dividends and, for a controlled subsidiary or holdco, the share of cash that reaches the parent. Formula. Dividends ÷ net profit; upstream cash = promoter stake × dividends + fees paid to the promoter. Worked example. HZL's ₹4,648 crore FY27 first interim sent ₹2,822 crore to Vedanta and ₹1,826 crore to minorities; Vedanta's FY26 dividend of ₹13,295 crore sent about ₹7,500 crore to VRL at its then 56.38% stake. Drivers. Parent debt service, capex plans, the Government's interest as HZL's second-largest owner. Benchmark. For a miner, 40–70% of profit is sustainable; 70–100% average; above 100% means borrowing or depleting cash to pay. How members compare. Vedanta 76% in FY26 after 113%, 259% and 357% in the three prior years; Gravita 12% (15% historically); Jain 0%. Definition trap. Payout on screener's consolidated profit for Vedanta includes minority profit and, in FY26, discontinued operations; payout of attributable continuing profit would be higher.

For Vedanta, the more revealing measure is upstream cash per year relative to what the parent needs. On the illustrative bridge in Section 11, residual Vedanta could fund about ₹8,800 crore of dividends from HZL's payout and fees, sending about ₹4,800 crore to VRL. Watch two ratios when the Q2 numbers arrive: dividends declared by HZL as a share of its profit (FY26 was unusually low at 31%), and Vedanta's dividends as a share of the cash it actually receives from HZL. If the second ratio exceeds 100% for long, the holdco is borrowing to pay its parent.

KPI 11 · ROCE and ROIC

What it is. Operating profit after depreciation divided by the capital used to earn it. Formula. EBIT ÷ (equity + debt − cash), or for Gravita's ROIC, pre-tax hedging-adjusted EBIT ÷ average invested capital. Worked example. A recycling plant with ₹60 crore of fixed assets and ₹187 crore of working capital earning ₹82 crore of EBIT returns 33% before tax (see the anatomy below). Drivers. Margin per tonne, asset turns, working-capital days, idle capacity and acquisition goodwill. Benchmark. Recyclers above 25% good, 15–25% average, below 12% weak; miners are cyclical and should be judged through the cycle. How members compare. HZL 70% (FY26); Jain 25.7% on screener (21.4% company, June 2026); Gravita 16.2% on screener, about 20% company including copper, with pre-tax ROIC of 27% in FY26 before RMIL; Vedanta 16.1% on screener, about 29% on the company's continuing basis. KPI 12 · EPR certificate economics

What it is. Income from selling extended producer responsibility certificates generated by recycling, under the battery rules and, from April 2026, the non-ferrous scrap rules. Formula. Certificates generated (tonnes recycled × conversion factor) × market price, within a band of 30–100% of the Environmental Compensation. Worked example. In plain terms

Recycling certificates could become a meaningful second income for registered recyclers, but nobody yet reports how much they earn from them. Treat claims about them as a possibility, not a number.

KPI 13 · Value-added share and product premium

What it is. The share of a recycler's revenue that comes from products sold above plain commodity metal: specification alloys for battery makers, copper rod, busbar, profiles, brass and finished plastic granules. Formula. Value-added product revenue ÷ total revenue; premium = realisation per tonne − LME-equivalent value per tonne. Worked example. If a copper recycler sells ingot at roughly LME (Jain realised about US$13,200 a tonne against a US$13,324 LME average in Q1 FY27) and then moves 1,000 tonnes a month into rod and busbar earning an extra ₹20,000 a tonne, annual EBITDA rises by about ₹24 crore with little extra working capital, because the metal was already in stock. Drivers. Downstream capacity, customer approvals (which take months), brand registrations such as Gravita's "GRAVITA M" and Jain's "Jain 9997" lead brands on the LME and MCX, and product quality. Benchmark. Gravita's Vision 2030 targets value-added products at 45–50% of revenue and non-lead businesses at 35–40%; above 50% is good, 30–50% average, below 30% weak for a recycler seeking higher returns. How members compare. Gravita reported 63% value-added share in Q1 FY27 (50% excluding copper); Jain's value-added copper lines start between Q2 and Q3 FY27, so its share is still low. Definition trap. Each company defines "value-added" itself; Gravita counts alloys sold to battery makers, which a stricter definition would treat as standard product.

KPI 14 · Valuation measures that work here, and ones that do not

What it is. The multiples investors use to price these companies. Formula. EV/EBITDA = (market value + net debt) ÷ EBITDA; P/E = market value ÷ net profit; holdco discount = 1 − (holdco market value ÷ market value of its listed stakes); for recyclers, market value per tonne of annual volume. Worked example. Vedanta's market value of ₹88,570 crore against its HZL stake of ₹1,27,234 crore gives a holdco discount of 30%. Adding HZL's minority stake (₹82,342 crore) and Vedanta's ₹8,299 crore of net debt gives a claim-adjusted enterprise value of ₹1,79,211 crore, about 5.3 times annualised Q1 FY27 company EBITDA of ₹33,876 crore. The recyclers trade at about 27 times FY26 earnings and 21 times EV to operating profit in aggregate; Gravita's ₹9,914 crore market value is roughly ₹4.5 lakh per tonne of annualised Q1 FY27 volume (an indicative calculation). Drivers. Metal prices for the miner; growth, return on capital and governance perception for the recyclers. Benchmark. Global diversified miners trade at about 5–6 times EBITDA and global smelters and recyclers at 5–8 times (approximate); Indian recyclers have traded at 20–35 times earnings. How members compare. See Sections 16 and 19. Definition trap. Vedanta's screener P/E (8.05) does not reconcile with its own trailing EPS, and any P/E on profits that include discontinued operations and minorities is not meaningful. Use the look-through and claim-adjusted EV for Vedanta, and P/E or EV/EBITDA on the recycler subgroup only.

In plain terms

Vedanta is valued like a discounted wrapper around Hindustan Zinc, so the useful question is "how big is the discount and why?", not "what is the P/E?". The recyclers are valued like growth companies, so the useful questions are how fast their tonnes and profit per tonne can grow, and how much cash that growth will eat.

The anatomy shows three things that the published accounts obscure. In plain terms

A typical lead recycling plant costs little to build. What costs money is the metal sitting in it: about three months' worth of sales.   • "Vedanta" has four meanings: the pre-demerger group (FY26 revenue ₹1,74,075 crore), the screener-restated continuing company (₹78,437 crore), the residual company on its own reporting basis (Q1 FY27 revenue ₹23,456 crore), and the five-company family. State which one every time.

How to read a diversified-metals or recycler company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Pull the price deck first (one minute). Quarterly average LME zinc, lead, copper and aluminium, LBMA silver and the rupee. For Q2 FY27: zinc US$3,830 (+11% QoQ), copper US$14,121 (+6%), aluminium US$3,227 (−10%), lead US$1,857 (−5%). This tells you most of the miner's result before you open it.
  2. Miner: volumes from the production release (one minute). Mined, refined and silver tonnes; Zinc International; copper cathode. Red flag: lower refined output without a stated maintenance shutdown.
  3. Miner: cost of production ex-royalty and with royalty (one minute). Compare with the prior quarter. Red flag: rising ex-royalty CoP while volumes rise.
  4. Miner: segment EBITDA and the bridge (one minute). How much came from price and forex versus volume and cost? Red flag: an operational contribution that turns sharply negative.
  5. Miner: net debt and the walk (one minute). Did net debt fall? How much went to dividends, and to whom? Red flag: dividends or fees funded by rising holdco debt.
  6. Miner: attributable profit (thirty seconds). Use continuing profit after minorities; ignore P/E on totals that include minorities or discontinued businesses.
  7. Recycler: tonnes by metal (one minute). Lead, copper, aluminium, plastic. Red flag: falling lead tonnes for a second quarter without a scrap-supply explanation.
  8. Recycler: EBITDA per tonne by metal, on the company basis (one minute). Compare with guidance (Gravita copper ₹60,000 by FY27-end; Jain copper ₹30,000–32,000 normalised). Red flag: per-tonne EBITDA held up only by "other income" hedge gains.
  9. Recycler: working capital and borrowings (one minute). Inventory and receivable days, short-term debt, CFO when disclosed. Red flag: borrowings rising faster than EBITDA for two consecutive half-years.
  10. Everyone: the one-off and governance check (one minute). Related-party transactions, fees, guarantees to subsidiaries, accidents, exceptional items, rating actions. Red flags: new guarantees, brand-fee increases, or asset transfers to group companies.
In plain terms

For Vedanta, check world prices, tonnes, cost per tonne, and whether debt went down. For the recyclers, check tonnes, profit per tonne and whether they borrowed more. If those six numbers look right, the rest of the results release is detail.

Who is winning: the miner on price, Gravita on spread, Jain on volume

Each member is winning a different game. The question is which game the next two years will reward.

Ranking three companies with such different economics needs care, so this section compares them on what each is trying to achieve. Vedanta is winning the price game: its operating profit doubled in Q1 FY27, its leverage is at a decade low, its ratings are at their highest in years, and Hindustan Zinc's record Q2 virtually guarantees a strong Q2 for the parent. What it is not winning is the market's trust: its share price fell 27% from listing day, its promoter is selling and pledging, and the holdco discount to its HZL stake has not narrowed.

Gravita is winning the spread game. It earns more per tonne than Jain in every metal they share (lead ₹24,181 against ₹18,660; copper ₹55,151 against ₹31,027; aluminium ₹25,175 against about ₹21,200), it diversifies by metal and by country (14 plants, 24% of capacity overseas), it has the best credit rating among the recyclers (ICRA AA) and the lowest net debt. Its weaknesses are slow volume growth (55,455 tonnes in Q1 FY27, +4%), lead concentration (95% of FY26 segment profit), customer concentration (two customers at 39% of revenue) and a falling cash conversion as working capital rises toward 95 days.

Jain is winning the volume game. Its revenue grew 48% in FY26 and 76% in Q1 FY27; it is India's largest non-ferrous recycler by revenue; and its asset-light, port-side model in Chennai returns about 26% on capital (21% on its own mid-year figure). But its copper earns a thin, volatile spread, its working capital consumed the IPO, its operating cash flow was −₹591 crore in FY26, foreign institutions have almost left (0.79% from 6.42% at listing), and a fatal furnace accident in July 2026 is a reminder of operational risk.

Why Vedanta trades like Glencore, not like Teck's zinc

Hindustan Zinc by itself trades at about 9 times EBITDA and 10 times earnings, in line with or slightly above global zinc miners, which is reasonable for a first-decile, net-cash asset with a large silver credit. If the market came to see Vedanta as a clean conduit for HZL's cash, its multiple would converge toward HZL's; for now it prices in the parent's debt, the fees and the record of capital allocation.

Why Indian recyclers trade at three to four times global multiples

Aurubis, the world's largest copper recycler, earned an operating EBITDA margin of 3.2% and an operating return on capital of 8.8% in FY2024/25. Jain earned a 5.9% margin and about 26% on capital in FY26. The Indian recycler earns about three times Aurubis's return on capital because it is asset-light: Jain's fixed assets are small relative to ₹9,543 crore of revenue, and its plants are cheap furnaces beside the port rather than integrated smelters. Add India's growth (copper demand 8–10% a year, scrap imports rising by a third), the formalisation thesis (EPR, recycled-content mandates) and a scarcity of listed recyclers, and the market pays 20–23 times EBITDA against about 5–8 times for global smelters and recyclers. The premium is defensible only as long as returns stay high while working capital grows; the FY26 cash flows are the first sign that copper growth will test it.

In plain terms

Compared with foreign companies, Vedanta looks cheap and the Indian recyclers look expensive. Vedanta is cheap because of how it is owned and financed, not because its mines are bad. The recyclers are expensive because they earn much higher returns than foreign recyclers and are expected to keep growing quickly; if their cash keeps disappearing into metal stock, that premium will be hard to keep.

Technology and disruption: lithium, black mass, cleaner furnaces and silver thrifting

The slow threats are to lead-acid scrap and to silver demand; the fast-moving opportunities are in battery recycling chemistry, sorting technology and low-carbon metal.

Technology changes this sub-sector slowly but in ways that could eventually reshape both species. Seven shifts matter, ranked roughly by how much they could change the members' economics over a decade.

1. Lithium-ion displacing lead-acid

Electric two-wheelers, telecom tower back-up and home inverters are moving to lithium iron phosphate batteries. Over ten years that shrinks the future pool of lead-acid scrap, the feedstock for Gravita's core business and about a quarter of Jain's revenue. Starting batteries for combustion vehicles and uninterruptible power supplies for data centres remain lead-acid for now, and India's installed base of lead-acid batteries will keep producing scrap well into the 2030s. The hedge is to recycle lithium-ion too: Gravita commissioned a 6,000 tpa lithium-ion recycling plant at Mundra in FY26 and is researching black-mass refining.

2. Black mass and hydrometallurgy

Lithium-ion recycling is a different technology from lead smelting. Batteries are shredded into "black mass" containing lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese and graphite, which is then leached and purified chemically. Venture-backed specialists such as Attero, Lohum and Recyclekaro, plus battery makers' in-house loops, are ahead. The ₹1,500 crore recycling scheme targets exactly this feedstock, but its ₹50 crore per-entity cap limits the subsidy. For Gravita, success would mean a second growth engine; failure would mean a modest write-off within a ₹1,680–1,700 crore capex plan.

3. Pollution technology as a moat

Lead smelting releases sulphur dioxide and lead dust and leaves slag that must be disposed of safely. Each tightening of Central Pollution Control Board norms raises the cost floor for everyone, but costs the organised players little extra because they already comply, while it raises the informal smelter's cost sharply if enforced. Compliance spending of roughly ₹4,000 a tonne in the illustrative plant is therefore partly an investment in market share.

4. Sensor-based sorting and value-added downstream

X-ray fluorescence and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy sorting lets organised recyclers grade mixed scrap automatically and upgrade low-grade material, a scale advantage over small operators. Downstream lines (Jain's cathode, rod and busbar; Gravita's copper products and alloys) shift recyclers from commodity ingot toward specification products with premiums, and require customer approvals that take time to win.

5. Smelter decarbonisation and carbon borders

Hindustan Zinc's captive coal is a cost advantage and a carbon liability. Its renewable share reached 27% of power in Q2 FY27, and it markets lower-carbon zinc. The EU's carbon border mechanism penalises coal-based primary aluminium but favours recycled metal, whose embedded emissions are a fraction of primary; that is a structural tailwind for aluminium and copper recyclers that can document their inputs.

6. Copper substitution

At US$14,000 a tonne for copper against about US$3,000 for aluminium, cable and transformer makers substitute aluminium wherever performance allows. That trims copper demand at the margin and raises demand for aluminium scrap, which Gravita and Jain also process.

7. Silver thrifting

Solar-cell makers are reducing the silver in their paste per watt as prices rise. Over time, that could cap the industrial demand that supports silver prices, a long-run risk to the by-product credit that supplied about 45% of Hindustan Zinc's first-half profitability.

Bull vs bear: a price boom with a governance discount, a policy tailwind with a cash bill

The bull case is prices plus formalisation; the bear case is price reversal plus leakage and working capital.

The sub-sector's two species have separate bull and bear cases, but they share one variable: metal prices. A sustained boom in zinc, silver and copper is the strongest possible tailwind for Vedanta and, through scrap scarcity, a modest headwind for the recyclers; a sharp reversal would cut Vedanta's profit while easing the recyclers' working capital and widening their scrap discounts. The lists below set out the strongest arguments on each side, using only figures already established in this report.

The bull case

  • A first-decile mine at a 30% discount: Vedanta trades at 70% of its HZL stake; HZL's zinc CoP of US$703 a tonne and its silver credit make it one of the most profitable miners anywhere.
  • Deleveraging is real: VRL's net debt has fallen from US$8.9 billion to US$4.7 billion, group leverage from 3.3 times to 1.2 times, and ratings are the highest since 2014. Each step reduces the pressure to extract cash.
  • Growth in the holdco: Gamsberg's doubling to 500 ktpa, HZL's path to 2 mtpa and FACOR's expansion add volume to a price-geared base.
  • Formalisation by law: battery EPR, non-ferrous scrap EPR from April 2026 and recycled-content mandates from FY28–FY29 push scrap and buyers toward registered recyclers.
  • Scarcity pricing: both recyclers lifted lead EBITDA per tonne during the 2026 scrap shortage; Gravita's copper path from ₹55,000 to ₹70,000–75,000 a tonne is guided.
  • High returns on cheap plants: recyclers earn 16–26% on capital against about 9% for Aurubis, with ICRA and CRISIL upgrades in 2026.

The bear case

  • Profit is price: 99% of Vedanta's Q1 FY27 EBITDA increase came from prices and the rupee; a US$10 fall in silver and US$300 in zinc would remove about ₹5,000 crore of annual EBITDA (indicative, on company sensitivities).
  • Leakage and governance: brand fees of ₹2,727 crore, promoter shares fully under a negative lien, a June 2026 block sale, ED searches, the Viceroy report and a new property demerger with possible related-party injections.
  • The copper refinery earns nothing and Zinc International costs more than twice Zinc India per tonne.
  • Working capital eats the recyclers' profit: combined FY26 CFO of −₹422 crore on ₹725 crore of PAT; Jain's IPO money became inventory.
  • Margins are thin and volatile: Jain's copper spread swung from ₹38,500 to about ₹13,750 to ₹31,000 a tonne in three quarters; Gravita's lead volumes fell for the first time.
  • Rich multiples, slow 2026 delivery: 27 times earnings for operating-profit growth of 14% in Q1 FY27; lithium-ion is a long-run threat to the lead scrap pool.

On balance, the evidence supports a split view. Vedanta's operating case is as strong as it has been in years, and the discount is wide enough to absorb some price weakness; its risk is concentrated in governance and in the silver price. The recyclers' long-run thesis is credible, because regulation genuinely favours them, but 2026 has shown that copper growth costs cash and that per-tonne spreads can swing; their valuations require that FY27 shows working capital stabilising and EBITDA per tonne holding as scrap normalises.

In plain terms

If metal prices stay high and Vedanta's parent keeps paying down debt, Vedanta looks cheap. If prices fall or more cash leaks to the parent, the discount is deserved. For the recyclers, the rules are on their side, but they need to prove they can grow without borrowing ever more to hold metal.
