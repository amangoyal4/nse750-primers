00

The one-page view: a royalty annuity waiting for India to pay

Two listed companies own perpetual rights to a large slice of India's favourite songs. The audience is enormous; the money is not, yet.

India's listed music sub-sector is small enough to describe in two names and important enough to command more than ₹20,000 crore of market value. Saregama India, the RP-Sanjiv Goenka group company that grew out of the Gramophone Company's 1902 recordings, owns more than 1,80,000 songs and has built a "music plus" business around them: artiste management, live events, Carvaan players, short-form video and, since January 2026, a stake in Sanjay Leela Bhansali's film company. Tips Music, controlled by the Taurani family, owns more than 38,000 songs, employs fewer people than a mid-sized restaurant chain and does almost nothing except license its catalogue. Between them they reported ₹1,361 crore of revenue and ₹423 crore of profit in FY26 on screener's basis, and the market values them at about 48 times that profit.

The asset both companies own is the same: copyright in Indian sound recordings and, for most film music bought outright before 2012, in the underlying compositions and lyrics as well. A sound recording is protected for 60 years from the year after publication, and the label is paid every time that recording is streamed, played on YouTube, synchronised into an advertisement or performed in a hotel lobby. Old songs cost nothing to keep earning. That is why the industry's best businesses run 60–75% operating margins after paying for all new content, and why a 1990s Hindi film catalogue bought for a few crore can throw off hundreds of crore of cash two decades later.

The catch is that India pays very little per listen. The country has roughly 18% of the world's population but, on management's IFPI-based framing, about 1% of global recorded-music revenue. Only about 14.4 million Indians paid for a music subscription in 2025, roughly 3% of internet users, against 57% in the United States and 67% in Sweden. Most Indian listening happens free, on YouTube or on ad-funded streaming tiers that pay a few paise per play. Indian labels earn an indicative 4–10 paise per stream, Tips says, against 50–90 paise for labels in mature markets. The whole investment case for the sub-sector rests on that gap closing.

Five forces decide how the two companies' numbers move, and this report is built around them.

  • Free-to-paid conversion. Every 10 million extra paying subscribers at ₹100–140 a month adds about ₹1,200–1,700 crore of gross subscription spend, of which rights holders keep roughly half. Labels do not control this lever; platforms do, by deciding how generous their free tiers stay.
  • Content-cost inflation against catalogue yield. Saregama's content investment rose from ₹19.5 crore in FY21 to ₹340.1 crore in FY26; Tips guides ₹90–100 crore for FY27 against ₹59.2 crore in FY26. Film-music prices are rising because more bidders, including film studios tied to labels by equity, want the same hits.
  • Platform power. Resso, Wynk and Hungama shut their music services within 15 months in 2024–25, and FICCI-EY says digital licensing grew only 2% in 2025. The buyers that remain are Google, Spotify, Reliance's Jio Platforms, Meta, Apple and Amazon.
  • Accounting shapes perception. Tips expenses 100% of content cost in the quarter of release; Saregama capitalises it and charges it over about ten years. Tips' 73% margin and Saregama's 41% "adjusted EBITDA" are not on the same basis, and many published comparisons put them side by side.
  • Diversification against purity. Saregama is building a flywheel of artistes, live shows and video around its songs; Tips refuses anything beyond licensing. FY26 shows the cost of the flywheel: non-music businesses absorbed roughly ₹112 crore of Saregama's music EBITDA.

In plain terms

A music label owns songs the way a landlord owns buildings, except the buildings never need repairs and the lease runs 60 years. Old songs keep paying rent for free. The two questions that decide whether these stocks are cheap or expensive are how much more rent India's listeners will pay once they start paying for subscriptions, and whether the labels are overpaying for the new songs they need to keep their libraries current.

Status matters for every number in this report. Tips reported Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) on 15 October 2026: revenue ₹120.1 crore, up 35%, and profit ₹65.5 crore, up 23%. Saregama's latest reported quarter is Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), reported on 4 August 2026; its board meets on 4 November 2026 to approve Q2 FY27, with an analyst call the same afternoon. Where the report compares the two companies over the same period it uses FY26 or Q1 FY27, and it says which.

01

How a song earns: from a film studio's mixing desk to a royalty statement

A label buys or makes a recording once, then rents it to every platform, broadcaster and brand that wants it, for as long as copyright lasts.

Indian popular music is mostly film music. A producer commissions the songs for a Hindi, Tamil, Telugu or Punjabi film: a composer writes them, a lyricist writes the words, singers and session musicians record them. Before the film is released, the producer sells the music rights to a label. For most of the industry's history that meant an outright, perpetual, worldwide assignment of the master recording plus the underlying composition and lyrics, paid as a lump sum or as a minimum guarantee with a royalty on top. Big Hindi albums are reported in the press to change hands for ₹15–40 crore each (indicative; no company discloses album prices). The label then markets the album, distributes it to every digital service, collects money for decades and, where contracts require it, pays royalties back to authors and artists.

Value concentrates with whoever owns the master at Step 2. Platforms keep roughly 30–50% of what they collect (global norm, indicative) and pass the rest to rights owners in proportion to streams.

What the label owns is a bundle of separate rights, and each is sold to a different buyer. The sound recording is the core asset. The underlying musical and literary works (publishing) are a second asset, and since the Copyright (Amendment) Act 2012, authors of those works have an unwaivable right to share royalties for most non-cinema uses. Streaming licences, YouTube, short-form video, synchronisation, public performance and broadcast each have their own counterparties, pricing conventions and contract cycles. The table sets them out.

The two members use this bundle very differently. Tips earned 73% of H1 FY27 revenue from digital licensing (71% in Q2 FY27, 75% in Q1) and the rest from "non-digital" lines such as broadcast, sync and public performance; it has no other business. A platform rupee carries almost no incremental cost once the catalogue is owned; a brand or consumer rupee usually carries an artist fee, hardware cost or venue bill.

How platforms divide their money explains why consumption alone is not revenue. Subscription services such as Spotify, JioSaavn, Apple Music and Amazon Music pool their subscription and advertising income each month, keep their share, and split the rest among rights owners by share of streams. YouTube pays a share of advertising revenue on official channels and on user uploads that Content ID matches to a label's recording. Short-video apps have mostly paid labels a fixed annual fee or minimum guarantee, so a hit reel with a billion views earns the label nothing extra until the contract is renegotiated or moves to an advertising share. Both managements say that migration is coming; neither can say when.

In plain terms

An Indian label is paid like a landlord whose tenants mostly pay with advertising coupons. The flat is the same, the tenants listen just as much, but each play is worth about a tenth of what a Swedish or American play earns. Convert a coupon tenant into a cash tenant and the label's income from the same song jumps several-fold, with no extra cost.

Collection is the unglamorous part of the chain and a growing source of disputes. Labels collect from platforms directly or through distributors; public-performance money from hotels, malls and events goes through Phonographic Performance Ltd (PPL), Novex or the registered sound-recording society RMPL; and authors and publishers collect separately through the Indian Performing Right Society (IPRS). IPRS collected ₹741.6 crore in FY25, up 42%, which is about the size of Saregama's entire music-licensing business. Who has the right to license which part of a song, and at what tariff, is now being settled by the High Courts and the Supreme Court; section 11 explains why that matters for margins.

02

Size of the prize: a ₹5,900 crore industry, measured three ways

Indian music is roughly 2% of the country's media and entertainment spend and about 1% of the world's recorded-music revenue. Which number you quote depends on the definition.

The FICCI-EY 2026 report puts India's music segment at ₹5,900 crore (₹59 billion) in calendar 2025, up 10%, and projects ₹7,500 crore by 2028, a 9% compound growth rate on that gross-of-tax basis. Saregama's presentation cites the same report as forecasting 8% growth, probably on a net-of-tax series. Tips' presentation uses a third cut, net of tax and excluding live performance and artist management: ₹3,979 crore in 2023, ₹3,906 crore in 2024, ₹4,348 crore in 2025, and ₹5,527 crore by 2028 (8.3% a year). All three come from the same consultancy. None is wrong, but they cannot be mixed.

There is also a definitional break that trips up anyone reading older reports. Until its 2024 edition, FICCI-EY measured a narrower music segment, essentially label and publisher licensing, that was about ₹2,200 crore in 2022 and ₹2,400 crore in 2023. Any chart that splices the two series overstates the decade's growth by a factor of about two.

The composition matters more than the total. Digital licensing, 58% of the segment and the line most investors equate with "streaming", grew only about 2% in 2025. The growth came from other licensing, up 23%, and from live and artiste income. That is the footprint of the 2024–25 platform shake-out: three services that paid minimum guarantees shut down, and the survivors did not immediately make up the lost income. It also reconciles an apparent contradiction. Tips grew 21% and the two listed labels' music revenue grew 18.4% in FY26 while industry digital licensing barely moved, which means the listed labels gained share, grew their non-digital lines quickly, or both. Saregama's own licensing line grew only about 12% in FY26 according to CARE Ratings; the rest of its 17% music growth came from artiste management, which nearly doubled.

In plain terms

India's music market grew 10% in 2025, but the streaming part that everyone talks about grew about 2%. The money came from licensing songs to hotels, events, advertisers and film producers, and from labels running concerts and managing artistes. Streaming is the long-term prize; in 2025 it was the slowest-growing part of the business.

Against the world, India is small. IFPI's Global Music Report 2026 put recorded-music revenue at US$31.7 billion in 2025, up 6.4%, with streaming at 69.6% and paid subscriptions alone at 52.4%. There were 837 million paid music-subscription accounts globally, of which India's 14.4 million is under 2%. India ranks about 15th among recorded-music markets on one press report of IFPI data (single source, INDICATIVE); its 2024 growth was under 3% according to IFPI's chief executive. Converting the FICCI-EY figure at ₹94.9 to the dollar gives about US$0.62 billion, around 2% of the IFPI total, but the two series are not like-for-like because FICCI-EY includes publishing, performance and labels' other income while IFPI measures labels' trade revenue. Saregama's "India is about 1% of global" uses the IFPI framing.

The global mix is the clearest picture of where India could go. In mature markets, paid subscription is more than half of label revenue; in India, Tips says subscription is 10–15% of its revenue across platforms, and Saregama says international labels earn 50–70% of revenue from paid subscription. Music is a small slice of a large media economy. FICCI-EY put India's media and entertainment sector at ₹2.78 lakh crore in 2025, up 9%, with digital advertising at ₹94,700 crore (+26%), digital subscriptions at ₹16,300 crore and live events at ₹14,500 crore (+44%). Music, at ₹5,900 crore, is about 2% of the total. For comparison readers can look at the neighbouring reports on TV broadcasting, where advertising is shrinking, and internet platforms, where it is growing fastest. Music labels sit downstream of both: they sell content to broadcasters and to the platforms that are taking their audiences.

The listed members are a large part of this market. On the gross ₹5,900 crore base, Saregama's FY26 music revenue of ₹814 crore is about 13.8% and Tips' ₹376 crore about 6.4% (INDICATIVE: calendar-year industry against fiscal-year companies). On the narrower net ₹4,348 crore base the shares are 18.7% and 8.6%. On digital licensing alone (~₹3,420 crore), Tips' digital revenue of roughly ₹274 crore is about 8%, which is where management's "8% to 10% of the industry" claim fits best. The unlisted T-Series is larger than both; section 7 sizes the field.

03

What makes India listen: phones, films, YouTube and old songs found again

Demand for music is not the constraint. The constraint is how much of that listening turns into money, and through which pipe.

India does not lack listeners. EY's 2026 consumer study with the Indian Music Industry body (IMI), a survey of 15,373 smartphone users, found that 96% listen to music and 80% listen for more than an hour a day. EY estimates about 178 million music-streaming users; IFPI's chief executive used 192 million in 2025. Sixty per cent of respondents listen mainly on audio streaming services and 32% mainly on YouTube. The drivers of listening have been cheap mobile data since Reliance Jio's launch in September 2016, cheaper smartphones, and the migration of Indian entertainment from television to phones. None of these is slowing.

The drivers of revenue are narrower. They are, in rough order of size: the number of paying subscribers and the price they pay; YouTube and ad-funded streaming, which track digital advertising budgets; short-form video, where labels are still mostly on fixed fees; film releases, which create new hits; regional-language growth; the rediscovery of catalogue songs through recreations and reels; public performance; and, newly, brand and AI licensing. Each is discussed below; the paid-subscription driver gets its own deep dive in section 4.

Digital advertising: the money behind free listening

Most Indian listening is paid for by advertisers. YouTube is the country's largest ad-supported video platform, and Tips' presentation cites Dentsu data showing adults spend more than 72 minutes a day on it. Dentsu estimates India's digital ad spend at ₹71,621 crore in 2025, up from ₹49,251 crore in 2024, rising to ₹98,034 crore by 2027, when digital would be 70% of all advertising. FICCI-EY measures digital advertising differently, at ₹94,700 crore for 2025; the two should not be mixed. Either way, the ad pool that funds YouTube and free streaming tiers has roughly sextupled since 2020.

The link from ad spend to label revenue is real but loose. YouTube's revenue share to rights holders has been "currently stable", Tips says, with no increase expected. YouTube reach: big numbers that need care

Both labels report YouTube scale prominently. Saregama had 211 million YouTube subscribers across its channels in Q1 FY27, up from 137 million in Q3 FY25, and reported 333 billion views in that quarter, including Shorts and user-generated content. Tips had 164.5 million subscribers in Q2 FY27 and 57.4 billion views in the quarter; its annual views rose from 59.6 billion in FY22 to 228.3 billion in FY26. These figures show reach and catalogue relevance. They are not revenue: YouTube changed how it counts Shorts views from 31 March 2025, and Saregama's quarterly views jumped from 129 billion to 284 billion on the change alone.

Films and hits: the new-content engine

New film music is the fuel that keeps a catalogue current, and one big album can move a label's year. Saregama's FY26 hit was Dhurandhar: all 11 songs entered Spotify India's top 200, the album was number one on India's weekly album chart and 17th globally, and five months after release it was still generating about 6 million audio streams and 11 million YouTube views a day, according to the Q4 FY26 call. Saregama's FY27 slate includes Bhansali's Love and War (due January 2027), a Rajinikanth film, the Telugu Paradise, Dharma's Naagzilla and a second Bhansali Productions film. Tips buys music for 5–6 films a year, 4–5 of them in Hindi. Films slip, flop or break out; content spend is lumpy because release dates are.

Regional languages and catalogue rediscovery

Growth outside Hindi is a second engine. Saregama released more than 750 tracks across 10+ languages in Q1 FY27 (200+ film, 550+ non-film) and roughly 5,500 a year; it bought the NAV Records Haryanvi catalogue in July 2025. Tips' Punjabi album Ishqnama crossed 100 million YouTube views in Q2 FY27, and Tips bought about 4,000 Gujarati and Kutchi heritage songs from Studio Radha in FY26. Old songs also find new audiences: Tips' catalogue track Tera Naam Doon passed a billion Instagram views, and its recreation Aankhon Se Tune 2.0 drew 66 million YouTube views. Saregama says like-for-like catalogue revenue grows at high single to low double digits a year, and that 60% of its FY26 licensing revenue came from songs released after 2000.

In plain terms

Indians are not short of music; they are short of reasons to pay for it. Every demand driver in this section raises listening. Only a few raise the price per listen: more paying subscribers, ad-share deals on Shorts and Reels, and licensing to businesses that play music in public. Those are the numbers to follow.

Public performance, brands and AI

The smaller lines are growing fastest. Tips' presentation, citing industry data, says public-performance revenue is growing at 40–50% a year and could exceed ₹2,500–3,000 crore within five years; that is a management view, not an independent forecast, and should be read as INDICATIVE. Saregama calls public performance "relatively untapped". Brand licensing is a growing part of Saregama's brands bucket, and in Q4 FY26 it licensed music to OpenAI for a brand campaign. Tips lists "royalty from AI" among its growth drivers. Section 19 covers the AI question, which cuts both ways.

04

The great Indian paywall Deep dive

Fourteen million payers out of nearly two hundred million listeners. Every serious bull case for Indian music is a forecast of how fast that ratio changes, and the labels do not control the answer.

The single most important number for an Indian music label is one that neither listed company reports: how many Indians pay for music each month, and at what price. FICCI-EY counts 14.4 million paid music subscriptions at the end of 2025, up 37% in the year, and EY's separate consumer study with IMI puts the figure at about 14 million. Those 14 million produced roughly ₹1,030 crore of subscription revenue in 2025, according to a trade-press summary of the FICCI-EY report, which works out to an average of about ₹60 a month per subscription once student plans, family plans, telecom bundles and promotional months are averaged in. Every scenario for the sub-sector's next decade starts from those two numbers.

A figure of "about 20 million" also circulates, from a 2025 speech by IFPI's chief executive ("20 million of 192 million"). It probably counts family-plan users or bundled accounts. Two EY products agree on about 14 million, so this report uses 14.4 million.

How many pay, and how fast it is growing

Paid subscriptions have roughly tripled in three years, from about 5 million in 2022 to 14.4 million in 2025, according to successive FICCI-EY reports (the 2022–2024 figures come from press summaries of those reports and are approximate). EY–IMI forecasts 28–30 million by 2028 "if current monetisation initiatives continue". That is fast growth from a tiny base. Even at 30 million, India would have about 4% of the world's 837 million paid music accounts while holding roughly 18% of its population.

Price: the paywall can open by getting cheaper

Price is the second lever, and in 2026 it moved the wrong way for revenue per user. Spotify raised Indian prices at the end of 2025, then on about 14 May 2026 cut its Standard plan from ₹199 to ₹139 a month (−30%), cut the Student plan from ₹99 to ₹69, and discontinued Premium Lite, which had launched only in November 2025 (press reports; INDICATIVE). Apple Music charges about ₹119. Amazon Music launched a three-tier structure that includes non-Prime users. Tips' CFO said the Spotify cut was "not a material impact". The reading is that platforms are choosing volume over price: a cheaper plan may convert more free users, which helps labels if the subscriber count grows faster than the price falls.

Who holds the key

The labels can lobby, license selectively and price their catalogues, but the decision to restrict free listening belongs to the platforms. Spotify, which had 290 million premium subscribers and 751 million monthly users worldwide in 2025 and an operating margin of about 13%, uses India as a user-acquisition market. YouTube gives almost every song away with advertising. JioSaavn sits inside Jio Platforms, which listed on 28 October 2026 at a 21% premium to its issue price; its first results as a listed company may show how Reliance weighs music subscriptions against bundling music into telecom plans. Bharti Airtel shut Wynk in 2024 and moved its users to an Apple Music offer. Telecom bundling can lift reported "paid" users while lowering the price per user, so a rising subscriber count is not automatically a rising revenue pool.

Saregama's management says revenue from free tiers was flat in FY26 and that all of its streaming growth came from paid subscription; it will not split the two ("too competitor-sensitive"). Tips says subscription is 10–15% of its revenue against more than 50% at global labels, and expects the Indian share to rise over three to five years. The labels also point to history: in the 2000s, telecom operators earned around ₹5,000 crore from caller-tune subscriptions at about 8% penetration, according to the Tips chairman, which shows that Indians will pay small, recurring amounts for music when it is packaged conveniently. That business collapsed when smartphones arrived. It is a reminder that the pipe through which India pays for music has changed before, and can change again.

Why the paywall might stay low

  • YouTube is free and comprehensive. As long as almost every Indian song is on YouTube with ads, a streaming paywall pushes some users there rather than to a subscription.
  • Piracy is a real alternative. 39% of EY–IMI respondents said they would turn to piracy or other free sources if free streaming ended.
  • Platforms compete on price. Spotify's 30% cut in May 2026 shows that conversion may come at lower revenue per user; telecom bundles push the effective price lower still.
  • Platforms themselves are fragile. Three music services shut within 15 months; a platform that cannot make money from free users may exit rather than raise the paywall.

For an investor, the practical test is the next two data points: the FICCI-EY 2027 report (expected around March 2027) for the 2026 subscriber count, and any platform announcement restricting free on-demand listening in India. A count above 20 million for 2026 would put the market ahead of the EY–IMI trajectory; a count below 17 million would suggest the 2025 jump was a one-off. Section 21 sets these thresholds alongside the others.

The buyers who shrank: platform power and the 2024–25 shake-out

A label sells to a handful of customers worth trillions of dollars. When three of the smaller ones went bust in 15 months, the industry stopped growing for a year.

Indian labels have an unusual customer list. On one side are some of the largest companies in the world: Google (YouTube and YouTube Music), Meta (Instagram and Facebook), Apple, Amazon, Spotify and Reliance's Jio Platforms. On the other side, until 2024, were smaller services that paid minimum guarantees to secure catalogues: Bytedance's Resso, Bharti Airtel's Wynk Music, Hungama Music and Gaana. Minimum guarantees are valuable to a label because they pay a fixed amount whether or not the service succeeds. When a service shuts, the guarantee goes with it.

In plain terms

A label's customers are among the most powerful companies on earth, and the smaller ones can disappear overnight. Labels survive this because their songs cannot be replaced: a platform without the hits of the 1990s and the latest Bollywood soundtrack loses users. That makes the relationship a tug of war rather than a dictatorship, but the platforms set the rules of the game, including how much free listening there is.

The labels' counterweights

Labels have three defences. The first is repertoire: a platform without the major Indian catalogues is not a viable Indian music service, which is why Tips' chairman says fewer platforms have had "no impact … they also require the content … it's a win-win". The second is the law. The 2019 Bombay High Court ruling in Tips Industries v. Wynk held that streaming services cannot use the statutory licence in s.31D of the Copyright Act, which would have let them play music at a regulator-set rate; the government rescinded its 2016 memorandum supporting the opposite view in August 2024. Rates are therefore negotiated. The third is the willingness to walk away: Tips cancelled a direct contract with Meta earlier because, in its view, Meta was "not doing the valuation of Tips correctly", and now reaches Meta through Warner.

The weakness is that concentration on the buyer side is rising while the number of sellers is falling more slowly. Each platform exit removes a bidder for catalogues; each new short-video feature adds listening without, at first, adding money. The next pricing reset in the industry is the shift from fixed fees to advertising shares on Shorts and Reels, covered in section 19. Who owns India's music: one giant, two listed labels and the global majors

A handful of catalogues own most of the songs Indians actually play. The largest of them answers to no public shareholder.

Indian recorded music is concentrated, but nobody publishes official market shares. What exists is company revenue on different definitions, management claims and arithmetic against a moving industry denominator. With that caveat, the field has four tiers. At the top is T-Series (Super Cassettes Industries Pvt Ltd), founded by Gulshan Kumar in 1983 and run by Bhushan Kumar, which is both India's largest label and a major film producer. Finally, a long tail of regional and specialist labels: Aditya Music and Lahari in the South, Speed Records in Punjabi, Think Music, Times Music, YRF Music (owned by the film studio), Venus, Shemaroo, Ultra and others. These regional owners are the most frequent sellers of catalogues.

Warner matters to this report as Tips' distributor. Universal matters because in January 2026 Universal Music India took a 30% stake in Farhan Akhtar and Ritesh Sidhwani's Excel Entertainment at a reported valuation of about ₹2,400 crore, securing the global distribution of all future Excel soundtracks. A month earlier, Saregama had agreed its ₹325.16 crore investment in Bhansali Productions. Two of India's best-known film houses are now tied to labels by equity rather than by album-by-album auctions. (Adar Poonawalla's 50% stake in Dharma Productions in 2024, for ₹1,000 crore, was the non-label precedent.)

In plain terms

Most Indian hit songs end up with a few owners. The biggest, T-Series, is private, very profitable and sitting on a mountain of cash, which means it can pay whatever it takes for the films it wants. The listed labels and the global majors are responding by buying stakes in film studios, so they get the music of future films at a pre-agreed price instead of bidding for each one.

There are no imports in the conventional sense and no Chinese competition. Foreign capital enters through the majors' subsidiaries and, globally, through catalogue investors such as Blackstone, KKR and Concord, which have bought Western catalogues. Saregama says "institutional capital is now flowing into the space through either JVs or direct catalog purchases". In India that capital has so far been strategic (labels buying catalogues and studio stakes) rather than financial. For a listed label, the risk is that a deep-pocketed financial buyer arrives and bids up the regional catalogues that Saregama has been buying. The opportunity is the same thing in reverse: a public valuation of 40-plus times earnings is a currency for acquisitions if a label chooses to use its shares.

Concentration on the buyer side is higher still. A label with a 10% share of Indian music sells most of its output to perhaps six platforms. That asymmetry, discussed in section 5, is why the industry's real competition is not label against label but labels collectively against the platforms over how much of a listener's time and money reaches the song.

Tips' refusal

Tips' management has been explicit that it does not want artiste management or live events; the chairman called them "a lot of trouble". Tips buys music for five or six films a year, releases a steady flow of non-film tracks (399 new songs in FY26, 146 in H1 FY27), markets them and licenses everything. Kumar Taurani and Ramesh Taurani each own about 20% of Tips Films, which reported turnover of ₹158.3 crore and a loss of ₹15.8 crore in its latest year. ₹60 crore would be about 60% of Tips' FY27 content budget; how much of it is used is worth asking every quarter.

In plain terms

Saregama is betting that owning the artistes, the concerts and the videos makes its songs more valuable, even if those businesses earn little themselves. Tips is betting that the songs are valuable enough on their own and that everything else is a distraction. On FY26 numbers, Tips' bet produced more profit from less revenue; Saregama's bet will be judged by whether its newer songs keep earning for decades.

CARE Ratings' revenue mix for Saregama captures the direction of travel: licence fees plus artiste management rose from 52% of total operating income in FY25 to 75% in FY26, while events fell from 24% to 6% and TV and films from 16% to 9%; Carvaan was 7% in both years. Saregama is becoming more of a music company again, with the flywheel businesses shrinking as a share of revenue but still costing money. The investor's test is whether the music vertical's 20–23% growth guidance, which depends partly on artiste management and brands, holds as the non-music drag falls. If it does, Saregama's group margin should converge towards its music margin over three to five years. If artiste management stalls, the guidance relies on licensing, which grew about 12% in FY26.

Two ways to count a song Deep dive

Tips writes off every rupee of new music the quarter it is released. Saregama spreads it over ten years and reports a headline profit before the charge. Restated on one basis, the gap between them narrows, and a different gap opens.

The most common mistake in comparing India's two listed labels is to put Tips' 73% EBITDA margin next to Saregama's 41% adjusted EBITDA margin and conclude that Tips is the better operator by 32 points. The two numbers measure different things. Tips' margin is after all content cost, because Tips charges the full cost of new music to the profit and loss account in the quarter of release; it calls itself the "only listed player to expense off 100% of content cost in the quarter of release". Saregama's "adjusted EBITDA" is defined by the company as "EBITDA excluding music content charge", that is, before the cost of new music. Any useful comparison starts by putting them on one basis.

How each company books a new album

Tips. When Tips pays for a film's music before release, the payment sits on the balance sheet as an advance (advances for music rights were ₹14.5 crore at March 2026, up from ₹2.0 crore a year earlier). On release, the full amount is charged through a profit and loss line labelled "Amortisation of content – film titles / non-film titles", along with royalty and advertising and promotion. Tips carries only ₹0.14 crore of other intangible assets, no inventory and no subsidiaries. Its balance sheet holds no content asset at all, however valuable its catalogue.

Saregama. Outright purchases of music copyrights are capitalised as intangible assets and amortised on a straight line over one to ten years from release; music rights held in inventory (acquisition or production cost) are amortised the same way, and both are reviewed for impairment against expected revenue. The "content charge" in the investor presentation is wider than accounting amortisation: it includes amortisation of royalty-based deals and one-time fees plus marketing of new content, but excludes royalty paid after a minimum guarantee is recouped. In FY26 the content charge was ₹139.9 crore, while amortisation of music copyrights in the accounts was ₹71.9 crore; the difference is marketing and the amortisation of rights held in inventory.

In plain terms

Tips pays for a new album and takes the whole hit to profit immediately. Saregama pays for an album and spreads the hit over ten years. Neither method changes how much the album earns; it changes when the cost shows up. Comparing their headline margins without adjusting is like comparing two households, one of which counts its mortgage payment as a monthly cost while the other leaves it out.

The quarterly swings the expensing method creates

Because Tips charges content in the release quarter, its margin moves with the film calendar. In Q4 FY25 it spent ₹29.9 crore on content (38% of revenue) and its operating margin fell to 48%; in Q3 FY26 it spent ₹8.9 crore (9%) and the margin rose to 79%. In Q1 FY27 it spent ₹44.6 crore, up 90%, and the margin fell to 50%, before recovering to 71% in Q2 FY27 on ₹26.2 crore of content. The map shows that almost all of the variation in Tips' quarterly margin is explained by content timing.

Saregama's amortisation smooths this away, so its quarterly music net margin moves less (between 37% and 55% over nine quarters), and its swings come from revenue seasonality rather than release timing. The trade-off is that the cost of a weak album shows up slowly at Saregama, through years of amortisation and possibly an impairment, while at Tips it shows up immediately and is then forgotten.

A change of estimate worth knowing

From 1 April 2022 Saregama lengthened the amortisation period for its music rights to ten years, having concluded that they last longer and are worth more. The audited note quantifies the effect: amortisation was lower by ₹10.7 crore in FY23, ₹6.1 crore in FY24, ₹5.0 crore in FY25 and ₹7.6 crore in FY26, and will be ₹7.4 crore lower in FY27; the cumulative ₹36.8 crore is charged in later years instead. The change is disclosed and defensible (global majors amortise catalogue over 10–20 years), but it lifts FY23–FY27 profit before tax by roughly 2–4% a year and moves about ₹37 crore of cost into FY28 and beyond, the same period in which management says content spending will flatten.

What accounting does to returns

The accounting choice also drives the most quoted return ratios. Buying tomorrow's catalogue Deep dive

The songs that will pay royalties in 2046 are being bought now, at prices set by an auction that keeps getting more crowded. This is the capital-allocation test for both labels.

A catalogue decays unless it is refreshed. Listening skews towards new releases, and a label that stops buying gradually becomes a heritage archive with a shrinking share of streams. Both listed labels have therefore raised their spending on new music sharply since streaming took off, into a market where film-music prices are rising because more bidders want the same small number of hit albums. Whether that spending earns its cost of capital is the most important unanswered question in the sub-sector, because the accounting (section 9) hides the answer for years.

Saregama: from ₹20 crore to ₹340 crore a year

Saregama's investment in content (its own KPI, covering the cost of content plus marketing) was ₹19.5 crore in FY21, the year before its ₹750 crore QIP. It then rose to ₹109.5 crore in FY22, ₹194.9 crore in FY24, ₹316.0 crore in FY25 and ₹340.1 crore in FY26: ₹235.4 crore of new music (organic) and ₹104.7 crore of catalogue purchases (inorganic, including the NAV Records Haryanvi catalogue). Management committed ₹1,000 crore over FY25–FY27 and guides ₹300–350 crore for FY27, "most of that … already committed". From FY28, it says, investment should rise only "closer to the rate of inflation", once Saregama holds a 25–30% share of new music, and EBITDA and then profit growth should converge on revenue growth.

The auction for a big Hindi film's music has more and stronger bidders than five years ago. T-Series can outbid anyone and produces films itself. Zee Music, Sony and Universal buy aggressively. Bhansali Productions is accounted as an associate (ownership interest shown as 30.37%); Saregama booked a ₹0.58 crore share of profit in FY26 and a ₹1.2 crore share of loss in Q1 FY27. The risks are concentration and capital. Saregama has tied a large share of its Hindi film pipeline to one director's slate, whose films are expensive and sometimes delayed. It has locked ₹325 crore of cash into an unlisted associate whose profits will be lumpy, at the cost of treasury income (Saregama's other income fell from ₹14.2 crore in Q1 FY26 to ₹4.2 crore in Q1 FY27, which management attributes to the money redeployed "namely" into Bhansali Productions). And by locking up supply, it may push the remaining A-list films towards rivals at even higher prices, raising costs for every other buyer, Tips included.

T-Series, the unlisted bidder

Every listed label's content strategy is shaped by the one competitor it cannot see in detail. T-Series' FY25 accounts show ₹7,151.7 crore of net worth and ₹1,057.4 crore of annual profit, enough to buy every major Hindi film album of a year several times over without borrowing. It also produces its own films, so it keeps their music at cost. Its sales fell 9.7% in FY25 and its FY26 accounts are not yet public, so whether it is bidding more or less aggressively now cannot be checked. What can be said is that the listed labels' responses (Saregama tying up Bhansali, Tips front-loading H1 FY27 spending) look like attempts to secure supply before prices rise further.

Paying for it: Saregama's balance sheet

Saregama's content programme is funded partly from the QIP raised in 2021–22. At 31 March 2026 about ₹101 crore of QIP proceeds remained unspent. In FY26 Saregama redeemed about ₹350 crore of fixed deposits and drew ₹70.4 crore of working-capital borrowings to fund content (free cash flow after content about −₹110 crore), the Bhansali payment and its dividend. Treasury net cash was ₹146.7 crore at March 2026 and ₹181.1 crore at June 2026. It also carries ₹89.9 crore of contracted but unprovided commitments for music-rights acquisitions, plus ₹54.1 crore of advances already paid: roughly ₹144 crore of contracted pipeline. CARE rated it AA-/Stable and A1+ in July 2026, including a new ₹130 crore short-term line. With FY27 content of ₹300–350 crore, Saregama will need operating cash flow well above FY26's level, or more debt. The Q2 FY27 cash-flow statement, due on 4 November 2026, is the first test.

In plain terms

Saregama is spending heavily on new songs and has bought a big stake in a famous director's studio to guarantee a supply of film music. The money mostly came from a share sale five years ago, and most of it is now spent. From here, the new songs have to start paying for themselves, or Saregama will need to borrow or slow down.

The test for investors over FY27–FY29 is straightforward to state. If Saregama's music revenue keeps growing at 20%+ while content investment flattens from FY28 as promised, the payback ratio will fall, free cash flow after content will turn positive and the market's patience will be rewarded. If revenue growth slows towards the ~12% licensing rate while content spend stays at ₹300 crore-plus, the model will look like a treadmill. Tips faces a smaller version of the same test: its FY27 content budget is about 1.5 times FY26's, so its FY28 revenue needs to show the return.

Copyright: the moat and the risk

Indian copyright law gives labels perpetual-looking assets and the right to set their own prices for streaming. Courts and regulators are now deciding how much of that value flows to authors, collecting societies and AI companies.

A label's economics rest on three legal facts. First, sound recordings are protected for 60 years from the beginning of the calendar year after publication (s.27 of the Copyright Act, 1957), and literary and musical works for the life of the author plus 60 years (s.22). Saregama's earliest shellac-era masters have already entered the public domain; most of the commercially relevant Indian catalogue, from the 1960s onwards, is protected well into the second half of this century. Second, labels own most film music outright, including publishing, for contracts signed before 2012. Third, rates for streaming are negotiated, not set by a regulator. The first two are settled. The third was won in court, and the boundaries around all three are being redrawn.

The streaming moat: s.31D and Tips v. Wynk

The 2012 amendment created a statutory licence (s.31D) under which radio and TV broadcasters can use music by paying a rate fixed by the Copyright Board (now the High Courts) instead of negotiating. In 2016 the government said internet broadcasters could use it too, which would have let streaming services pay regulated rates. Tips challenged this when Wynk tried to rely on it, and the Bombay High Court held in April 2019 that streaming is not covered. The government formally withdrew its 2016 view in August 2024. A 2019 draft amendment that would have brought internet broadcasting into s.31D did not become law. As of 2026, Indian labels set their own streaming prices. A reversal, by legislation or rules, would be the single largest regulatory risk to the sub-sector, because it would cap streaming royalties at a government-set rate.

Authors take a bigger slice

The 2012 amendment also gave lyricists and composers an unwaivable right to share royalties equally with the owner for every use other than the screening of a film in a cinema hall. This is a permanent cost on post-2012 deals that legacy catalogue largely avoids, and it is one reason Tips' royalty line (₹5.9 crore) is a tenth of Saregama's (₹63.4 crore). The Indian Performing Right Society, which collects for authors and publishers, has grown into a business the size of a listed label: collections of ₹741.6 crore in FY25, up 42%, and record distributions of ₹608.8 crore, according to press coverage of its annual report.

In plain terms

A song has two owners in law: whoever owns the recording, and the people who wrote the words and music. Since 2012 the writers must always get a share, and in 2026 a court confirmed that businesses need a separate licence from the writers' society. Labels still get paid, but they no longer control the whole fee, and their slice of newer songs is smaller.

Public performance: a live split between two High Courts

Labels collect public-performance money (hotels, restaurants, malls, events) mainly through PPL and Novex, which act as agents for member labels without being registered copyright societies. The registered sound-recording society is RMPL, registered in June 2021; a Delhi High Court single judge set aside its registration in March 2022, but a Division Bench stayed that order, so RMPL operates. In April 2025 a Delhi High Court Division Bench held in Azure Hospitality v. PPL that PPL cannot license without being or joining a registered society, and directed users to pay RMPL's tariff pending trial; the Supreme Court stayed that direction between the parties and the appeal is pending. The Bombay High Court has gone the other way, granting PPL and Novex interim injunctions through late 2025 and early 2026 and saying the Delhi ruling does not bind it. India therefore has a live Delhi–Bombay split on who may license public performance. A final Supreme Court loss would push labels into RMPL's tariffs; a win would strengthen their hand in a stream that Tips' management expects to grow quickly.

AI: the next statutory-licence fight

Generative AI raises the same question as streaming did in 2016: will labels negotiate prices, or will the state set them? Abroad, the majors have chosen negotiation backed by litigation: Universal settled with Udio in October 2025 and plans a licensed AI platform; Warner settled with Udio and Suno in November 2025; Sony and Universal were still litigating against Suno in 2026. In India, IMI, T-Series and Saregama applied in February 2025 to intervene in the news agency ANI's copyright suit against OpenAI. More consequential is DPIIT's working paper of 8 December 2025, whose majority view favours a mandatory blanket licence for AI training on lawfully accessed works, with royalties set by a government committee, payable once a system is commercialised, retroactive to models already trained, and collected by a new Copyright Royalties Collective for AI Training. That would turn a potentially lucrative negotiated licence into a regulated royalty. Comments closed on 7 January 2026; no final policy has been found.

A century in seven cycles: from shellac to the paywall

Every format change has threatened Indian labels, and every time the owner of the master survived. The risk has always been the pipe, not the song.

Indian recorded music has gone through seven distinct cycles, and each one redistributed value between the people who own songs and the people who carry them to listeners. The pattern is consistent: a new format arrives, piracy or a new middleman captures the early value, and then the rights owners recover their share through law, contracts or consolidation. The timeline sets out the events; the paragraphs below draw the lessons.

  • 1902–1985
    The Gramophone Company makes India's first commercial recordings (1902), presses discs at Dum Dum and, as HMV, dominates film music for eight decades. Gramophone Company of India incorporated 1946.
  • 1983–2000
    Cassette revolution: T-Series (1983) and Tips (company founded 1988) break HMV's grip with cheap tapes and cover versions. RPG acquires HMV India from EMI (1985–86); renamed Saregama in 2000.
  • 2000–2015
    Piracy and the lost decade: MP3s and CD piracy flatten revenue; caller tunes become a large but unstable stream for telecom operators.
  • 2016–2019
    Jio's cheap data (Sep 2016) explodes listening; Gaana, JioSaavn, Wynk, Hungama, Spotify (2019) and Resso compete with free tiers and minimum guarantees. Tips v. Wynk (2019) keeps rates negotiated. Saregama launches Carvaan (2017).
  • 2020–2022
    Re-rating of catalogue owners: Tips' film business demerged (appointed date 1 Apr 2021); Saregama raises ₹750 Cr by QIP (FY22) and begins buying new music at scale. Tips' P/E rises from 8.6× (Apr 2020) to about 30×.
  • 2023–2024
    Diversification and peak multiples: Saregama announces Pocket Aces (Sep 2023) and promotes the Diljit Dosanjh tour (FY25); Digidrive demerger (NCLT order Jun 2023); Tips' promoters sell ~11% (75.0% → 63.86% by Mar 2024); Warner–Tips global deal (Mar 2024); Tips' P/E peaks at 77.7× (14 Nov 2024).
  • 2024–2025
    Platform shake-out: Resso, Wynk and Hungama exit; India music −2% in 2024; Tips' Q4 FY25 PAT dips to ₹30.6 Cr on heavy content; both stocks de-rate (Saregama P/E 34.2× in Apr 2026).
  • 2025–2026
    Paywall push and recovery: paid subscriptions +37% to 14.4 mn; Saregama–Bhansali (Dec 2025/Jan 2026), Dhurandhar, film production wound down; Spotify price cut (May 2026); Tips' ₹44.44 Cr buyback and +35% Q2 FY27; Jio Platforms lists (28 Oct 2026).

The monopoly era (1902–1985) built the asset that still underpins Saregama: the masters of Indian film music's golden age. The cassette revolution showed that a new format can let a challenger break an incumbent: T-Series and Tips grew by selling cheap tapes and recording cover versions, and in doing so assembled the 1980s and 1990s film catalogues that are the core of Tips' revenue today. The piracy decade showed the opposite: when the pipe leaks, owning the songs is not enough, and labels' revenues stagnated for fifteen years. The one bright spot, caller tunes, enriched the telecom operators more than the labels and vanished with smartphones.

The data shock after Jio's launch in 2016 was the turning point. Cheap data turned every phone into a jukebox, and platforms paid minimum guarantees to secure catalogues. The 2019 Tips v. Wynk ruling ensured labels could price those licences themselves. The re-rating followed: by 2021–22 investors began valuing catalogue owners as royalty compounders. Tips separated its loss-making films; Saregama raised ₹750 crore and started buying. The diversification and peak-multiple years (2023–24) saw Saregama expand into short video, live and artistes, Tips' promoters sell down, and Tips' valuation peak at nearly 78 times earnings in November 2024.

Saregama's oldest songs have survived shellac, vinyl, cassettes, CDs, downloads and streaming. Each new format first helped pirates or middlemen, then the song owners clawed back their share. Reading a music label: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain almost everything about an Indian label. Each one comes with a trap, because the two listed companies define half of them differently.

A music label's results look simple, a revenue line and a profit line, but the numbers that matter sit underneath: where the revenue comes from, what the company paid for new songs, how it counted that cost, how much cash survived, and how dependent it is on one platform or one distributor. This section builds those numbers from the ground up. It starts with the equation that generates a label's revenue, works through each native KPI with a formula, a worked example, benchmarks and the listed companies' current values, builds an ILLUSTRATIVE profit and loss for one film album, and ends with a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

The revenue equation

Every rupee a label earns from a platform is the product of four terms: how much listening the platform carries, how much money it makes per unit of listening, what share of that money goes to rights holders, and what share of the listening belongs to this label. Add one-off licences (sync), public-performance collections and, for diversified labels, other businesses, and that is total revenue. Profit is revenue minus the cost of new content (expensed or amortised), royalties to authors and artists, and a small overhead.

Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE, round numbers). Suppose Indian streaming services collect ₹2,000 crore a year net of GST, pass 52% (₹1,040 crore) to rights holders, and a label owns 10% of plays: it earns ₹104 crore from streaming. Add 50 billion YouTube views at 1.5 paise of label revenue each (₹75 crore) and ₹40 crore of sync, broadcast and public-performance income, and revenue is ₹219 crore. If the label spends 20% of revenue on new content (₹44 crore), pays ₹5 crore of royalties and ₹25 crore for staff and overheads, EBITDA after content is ₹145 crore, a 66% margin. Tips' FY26 accounts follow the same shape: ₹375.5 crore of revenue, ₹59.2 crore of content cost, about ₹40 crore of staff and other costs, ₹276 crore of operating profit, then ₹18.8 crore of treasury income and about ₹75 crore of tax for ₹216.7 crore of profit after tax.

In plain terms

A label controls only one of the four big terms in its revenue: how many of the songs people play belong to it. The platforms decide how much money each play makes and how much they hand over. That is why labels spend so much on new songs, and why platform decisions about free tiers move label revenue more than anything the labels do themselves.

The equation also shows which terms move fastest. Listening grows 30–35% a year at Tips by its own account, but money per play has barely moved because most growth is on free tiers and fixed-fee short video. The rights-holder share is close to a global norm and changes only at contract renewals. The label's share of plays shifts slowly, through years of buying and marketing. So in any one year, label revenue growth is mostly listening growth discounted by the drift of listening towards lower-paying surfaces. The paywall (section 4) and the move to advertising shares on Shorts and Reels (section 19) are the two events that could change money per play, the term that has been stuck.

An ILLUSTRATIVE attribution test for Tips (15% of FY26 revenue from FY24–26 content costing ₹185.8 crore) suggests a simple payback of about 3.3 years.

Definition trap. The one-year ratio penalises a label that is investing for the long term, because songs earn for decades and incremental revenue includes catalogue growth that has nothing to do with new content. Use it to spot trends, not to judge a single year.

In plain terms

Payback asks a simple question: how many years until the money spent on a new album comes back? Under five years is good for music, because the songs keep earning for decades afterwards. Neither company reports payback album by album, so outsiders can only approximate it from total spending and total revenue growth.

5. EBITDA margin before and after content

What it is. Operating profit as a share of revenue, measured either before the cost of new content (Saregama's "adjusted EBITDA") or after it (Tips' operating EBITDA). Formula. (Revenue − operating costs, with or without content) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Saregama FY26: ₹404.7 crore ÷ ₹984.6 crore = 41.1% before content; after its ₹139.9 crore content charge, ₹264.8 crore = 26.9% (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic). Tips FY26: ₹276 crore ÷ ₹375.5 crore = 73.4% after all content. Drivers. Mix (catalogue versus new, music versus non-music), royalty burden, the timing of content (at Tips) and the amortisation life (at Saregama). Benchmarks. After all content: a pure licensor ≥65% good, 50–65% average, below 50% weak; global majors run 21–23% adjusted EBITDA. Members. Tips 73.4% in FY26, 50% in Q1 FY27 and 71% in Q2 FY27 (screener). 10. Platform and channel concentration

What it is. Dependence on the largest customer or route to market. Formula. Revenue from the largest counterparty ÷ total revenue. Worked example. In plain terms

12. ROCE and ROE

What they are. Return on capital employed (EBIT ÷ debt plus equity) and return on equity (PAT ÷ equity). Worked example. Tips' FY26 PAT of ₹216.7 crore on year-end equity of ₹260 crore gives 83%; screener reports 87.6% on average equity, the company 92%. Margins, payout (cash returned shrinks equity) and, above all, accounting: capitalised content and goodwill enlarge Saregama's capital base; expensing keeps Tips' tiny. Benchmarks. ROCE ≥40% good for a licensor, 20–40% average, below 20% weak. CARE's upgrade trigger for Saregama is ROCE above 30%. Members. ROCE: Tips 118%, Saregama 17.3%. Definition trap. Tips' triple-digit ROCE is partly an artefact of a balance sheet that carries no value for its catalogue. Saregama's is depressed by ₹750 crore of QIP money, goodwill and the Bhansali stake. Compare returns on incremental capital (content spend against extra profit) rather than headline ratios.

13. Net cash and the balance sheet

What it is. Treasury cash and liquid investments minus borrowings. Formula. Cash + bank balances + current investments − borrowings. Worked example. Saregama, June 2026: ₹42.7 crore + ₹117.9 crore + ₹85.0 crore − ₹64.5 crore = ₹181.1 crore. Tips, September 2026: ₹311 crore of cash and investments, debt-free apart from ₹4.1 crore of leases. Drivers. Free cash flow, acquisitions and payouts. Benchmarks. Net cash covering at least one year of content spend is comfortable. Members. Saregama's ₹181 crore covers about seven months of FY27 guided content; Tips' ₹311 crore covers more than three years.

Definition trap. Screener shows Saregama with ₹379 crore of "investments" at March 2026, but about ₹310 crore is non-current, chiefly the Bhansali preference shares. That is a strategic asset, not cash.

15. Valuation: P/E and EV/EBITDA

What it is. Market value relative to profit. Formulas. Market cap ÷ PAT; (market cap − net cash) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example. Saregama: ₹10,578 crore ÷ trailing PAT gives 46.4× on screener's basis; EV of ₹10,397 crore ÷ FY26 screener operating profit of ₹337 crore = 30.9×, or 25.7× on adjusted EBITDA of ₹404.7 crore. Tips: 43.8× trailing earnings; EV ₹9,634 crore ÷ ₹276 crore = 34.9×. Drivers. Growth expectations, the paywall, interest rates (the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026) and scarcity: these are the only two listed pure plays. Benchmarks. Ten-year median P/E: Saregama 38.4×, Tips 34.9×. Members. Both trade above their medians.

Definition trap. EV/EBITDA for Saregama depends on which EBITDA: ~26× on the before-content figure, ~31× on screener's, ~39× on EBITDA after the content charge (ILLUSTRATIVE). Tips' 34.9× is already after content.

A simple way to read a multiple is to ask what growth it needs. At 45 times earnings, an investor earns about a 2.2% earnings yield today. If profits grow 20% a year for ten years and the multiple then falls to the 10-year median of about 35 times, the annual return is roughly 17% before dividends; if growth is 12%, closer to the industry's pace, the return falls to roughly 9% (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic). The multiple therefore embeds the guidance: Saregama's 20–23% music growth and Tips' "exceed 20%". A sustained miss on growth matters more to the share price than any single quarter's margin.

In plain terms

Investors pay about 45 times a year's profit for either company, which means they expect profits to keep growing fast for many years. Saregama looks cheaper on its own preferred profit measure and more expensive on a measure comparable with Tips. Always check which profit a multiple is based on before comparing.

16. Releases and hit rate

What it is. The number of new tracks a label releases and the share that become hits. Formulas. Releases per period; hits (for example, entries in streaming top-200 charts or 100 million YouTube views) ÷ releases. Worked example. Tips released 73 songs in each of Q1 and Q2 FY27 (146 in H1) and 399 in FY26, against 443 in FY25 and 733 in FY24; in Q2 FY27 at least two of them, Ishqnama and Chunnari Chunnari – Let's Go!, crossed 100 million YouTube views. Saregama released more than 750 tracks in Q1 FY27 and about 5,500 a year. Drivers. Film slates, non-film A&R and the regional push. Benchmarks. There is no published industry hit rate; what matters is whether rising releases come with rising revenue from recent content. Members. Tips is releasing fewer songs while spending more per song, consistent with chasing bigger film albums; Saregama is releasing many more, across ten-plus languages. Definition trap. Release counts mix single tracks, albums and re-releases, and neither company discloses revenue per release, so volume says little about return.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA has three meanings. Tips' is after all content; Saregama's adjusted EBITDA is before the content charge; screener's operating profit for Saregama sits in between.
  • "Music" changed definition. Saregama's music segment includes artiste management and retail; licensing alone grew about 12% in FY26 against the 17% headline.
  • Content spend has four numbers at Saregama (investment ₹340.1 crore, cash ₹214–265 crore, charge ₹139.9 crore, acquisitions ₹336.1 crore) and one at Tips.
  • Industry size has three definitions (FICCI-EY gross ₹5,900 crore, net ₹4,348 crore, IFPI trade value), and an older, narrower FICCI-EY series that cannot be spliced on.
  • Calendar against fiscal years. Industry data are calendar years; company data run April–March. Market shares mixing them are approximate.
  • Returns reflect accounting. Tips' ROCE excludes its catalogue's value; Saregama's includes goodwill, Bhansali and QIP cash.
  • Views are not revenue. YouTube's 2025 counting change doubled Saregama's views overnight; Shorts and Reels are mostly on fixed fees.
  • Per-stream rates are management illustrations (4–10 paise in India against 50–90 paise globally), not audited data.
  • Distributors are not customers. Tips' Warner share is a route to several platforms.
  • Different latest quarters. Tips has reported Q2 FY27; Saregama's latest is Q1 FY27 until 4 November 2026.

How to read a music label's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its growth, by segment. For Saregama, pull music (and within it licensing versus artiste management and retail), video and live separately; a video or live swing can hide a music trend. For Tips, revenue growth and the digital share. Red flag: music growth below 15% for two quarters.
  2. Content spend for the quarter. Tips' content cost line; Saregama's investment in content and its content charge. Compare with guidance (Tips ₹90–100 crore for FY27; Saregama ₹300–350 crore). Red flag: spend above guidance without new revenue to show for it.
  3. Margin after content. Tips' operating EBITDA margin, read on a rolling four quarters; Saregama's music net margin. Never compare across the two on headline EBITDA. Red flag: Saregama music EBITDA below the 60% guidance floor.
  4. Royalty and content charge trends. At Saregama, the content charge rose 53% in Q1 FY27; if it keeps rising faster than music revenue, net margin will fall. Red flag: royalty true-ups that reverse a prior quarter's provisions.
  5. Other income. Both companies hold treasury cash; Saregama's other income fell from ₹14.2 crore to ₹4.2 crore after the Bhansali payment. Strip it out to judge operating trends.
  6. Cash flow after content. Saregama presents a management cash-flow table each quarter; compute operating cash flow minus content. Tips' cash flow is in its half-yearly results. Red flag: Saregama drawing more on its ₹130 crore line.
  7. Platform news on the call. Listen for Shorts and Reels ad-share deals, renewals, price changes and the Warner relationship. Red flag: a delayed deal (Tips' YouTube Shorts deal was about four months late at Q2 FY27).
  8. Reach KPIs. YouTube subscribers and views, releases, artistes managed. Check for counting changes before celebrating a jump.
  9. Associates, related parties and exceptional items. Saregama's Bhansali share of profit or loss; Tips' purchases from Tips Films under the ₹60 crore omnibus approval; any impairment.
  10. Guidance changes. Saregama: music growth 20–23%, music EBITDA 60–65%, content ₹300–350 crore. Tips: growth to "exceed" 20%, content ₹90–100 crore. Any cut is the single most important line in the release.

The checklist applied to Tips' Q2 FY27 (Sep-26). Revenue ₹120.1 crore, up 35%, the fastest in eight quarters, with digital at 71%. Content cost ₹26.2 crore, taking H1 to ₹70.8 crore against a ₹90–100 crore full-year guide, so H2 should carry only ₹20–30 crore. Operating EBITDA ₹84.6 crore, a 70.5% margin; on a rolling four quarters (Q3 FY26 to Q2 FY27) revenue was ₹424.8 crore and content ₹93.3 crore, 22% of revenue, in line with policy. Other income ₹4.5 crore; PAT ₹65.5 crore, up 23%. Cash: ₹311 crore of cash and investments after a ₹44.44 crore buyback and a ₹6.50 interim dividend. On the call: the YouTube Shorts deal still unsigned, the Warner channel at 25–30% and rising, guidance to "exceed" 20%, and a CEO search under way. Verdict in ten minutes: strong volume growth, content on budget, no red flags beyond the delayed Shorts deal.

In plain terms

Four numbers tell you most of what you need from a label's quarter: how fast music revenue grew, how much it spent on new songs, what margin was left after paying for those songs, and how much cash came in after that spending. Everything else, including the impressive YouTube statistics, is supporting detail.

How to read the table

Revenue and growth. Saregama's −15.9% is a mix effect: FY25 included ₹285.2 crore of live-events revenue from the Diljit Dosanjh tour, and video shrank as film production was wound down. Its music vertical grew 17%. The combined −8.2% inherits that distortion; on music revenue alone the pair grew 18.4%, from ₹1,004.8 crore to ₹1,189.9 crore.

Margins. The OPM column is screener's, and it is not like-for-like. Tips' 73.4% is after all content; Saregama's 34.2% books part of its content amortisation as depreciation. On a comparable after-content basis Tips earns 73% against 46% for Saregama's music vertical and about 27% for the Saregama group (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic, section 9).

Profit and value. Tips is 27.6% of combined revenue but 51.3% of combined PAT and 48.5% of combined market value. The market pays 26.5 times music revenue for Tips and 13.0 times for Saregama: roughly twice as much per rupee of music revenue for purity, margin and conservative accounting.

Returns and cash. ROCE of 118% against 17.3% overstates the economic gap, because Tips carries no content asset and Saregama carries goodwill, the Bhansali stake and QIP cash. Net cash is the cleaner comparison: ₹311 crore at Tips after buybacks, ₹181 crore at Saregama after ₹336 crore of acquisitions.

Multiples. On FY26 profit the pair trades at 48.5 times; on trailing profit, 45.8 times. Saregama looks more expensive than Tips on FY26 PAT (51.3× against 45.8×) and cheaper on its own adjusted EBITDA (25.7× against 34.9×). The native columns explain why: Saregama reinvested 34.5% of revenue in content in FY26, Tips 15.8%. If Saregama's spending earns what management says it will, today's P/E overstates how expensive it is; if not, the adjusted-EBITDA multiple understates it.

In plain terms

The two companies are worth about the same, but Tips makes the same profit from less than half the revenue and spends far less on new songs. Investors are paying Saregama's price for a bigger, busier company that is still investing heavily, and Tips' price for a smaller one that already turns most of its revenue into cash.

Who is winning: margin, scale and the price of staying current

With only two listed members, the useful comparisons are against reference points: the unlisted market leader, the global majors and the platforms that pay them all.

Three maps place the listed labels against each other and against reference companies. Magenta dots are the two members; gold dots are reference points (T-Series, Universal, Warner, Spotify), which are not members and are not in the peer table. The first map asks who combines growth with margin after content; the second asks whether scale buys margin; the third tracks each member's own path over five years.

The first map makes the sub-sector's core attraction visible. Indian catalogue owners earn margins that no global music company matches: Tips at 73% after all content, Saregama's music vertical at 46%, against 21.5–22.5% at Warner and Universal and about 13% at Spotify. Part of the gap is accounting and part is economics. Western labels pay artist royalties of a kind that India's outright-purchase film contracts largely avoided, and the Indian labels run with very small teams. The map also shows how much Saregama's group figure depends on its mix: the music vertical sits near Tips, the group sits near the bottom-left corner because of FY25's concert base and non-music costs.

The second map makes a less obvious point: scale does not bring margin in music. Universal's revenue is about 140 times Saregama's and 365 times Tips', yet its margin is a third of Tips'. Large labels carry big artist-royalty bills, global overheads and a constant need to sign and develop new talent. Small catalogue owners with cheaply acquired rights can run at very high margins indefinitely. For the listed Indian labels, this is both comfort and warning: there is no need to get bigger to be profitable, but getting bigger by buying expensive new content, as Saregama is doing, can move a company down this chart.

The third map tracks the capital-allocation test over five years. Tips' path has moved towards the top-left: content intensity fell from 24% of revenue in FY22 to 16% in FY26 while its margin rose from 64% to 73%, because revenue grew faster than spending. FY27 will reverse part of that as content spending rises to ₹90–100 crore. Saregama's path has moved right: content investment rose from 19% of revenue to 35%, while its group margin after the content charge stayed between 20% and 30%. The flat margin despite heavier spending is the amortisation at work; the cash figures in section 9 show the cost more clearly.

Why 45× for royalties: what the valuation assumes

Both stocks trade above their ten-year median multiples, after a full cycle from 9× to nearly 78× and back. The price embeds 20%-plus growth for years.

The two labels are valued at ₹20,523 crore together: 48.5 times FY26 profit and 45.8 times trailing profit. On screener's trailing basis Saregama trades at 46.4 times and Tips at 43.8 times, against ten-year medians of 38.4 and 34.9. Tips trades at 30.8 times book value because its balance sheet carries no catalogue; Saregama at 6.3 times. Dividend yields are 0.82% and 1.66%. These are growth-stock multiples on companies whose profits are largely royalty income, and they have moved far more than the underlying earnings.

Three things justify a premium multiple. Durability: catalogue royalties are among the most predictable revenue streams in media; Saregama's songs have earned through six format changes. Growth: both companies guide to 20%-plus, roughly double the industry, and the paywall offers upside that no mature-market label has. Three things argue for caution. Content inflation could permanently lower the conversion of revenue into free cash; Saregama's free cash flow after content was negative in FY26. What do today's prices assume? An ILLUSTRATIVE way to frame it: at 45 times earnings, a buyer who wants a 13% annual return over ten years, with the multiple drifting back to the ten-year median of about 35 times, needs profit to grow at about 16% a year. Both companies' guidance is above that; their history (Tips' PAT grew 37% a year over five years, Saregama's 13%) brackets it. The difference between them is the source of growth. Tips needs volume growth on a cheap old catalogue to continue. Saregama needs its heavy content spending to start converting into cash, and needs the non-music businesses to stop absorbing profit. Saregama's guidance that music net margin will rise 3–5 points over three to five years, and that content investment will rise only with inflation from FY28, is the most important set of promises for its valuation.

In plain terms

At about 45 times profit, investors are assuming these companies keep growing profits at mid-to-high teens or better for a decade. That is plausible if India starts paying for music and the labels do not overpay for new songs. If either assumption slips, history shows the multiple can halve within a year even while profits rise.
