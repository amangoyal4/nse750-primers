Performance chemicals: small dose, big consequence

A complete guide to the five Indian companies that sell chemicals for what they do inside someone else's product: an inhibitor that keeps acrylic acid from setting solid in a tanker, a scent molecule in a detergent, the mineral that makes up most of a tablet, the pigment in a car's paint, the enzyme in animal feed. How they earn 25–37% margins on tiny doses, why they carry five months to a year of inventory to do it, how China sets the ceiling on their prices, and whether an Indian pigment maker can fix the European group it bought out of insolvency.

00

The one-page view: five niches, one economic idea

Each of these companies sells something that is a sliver of the customer's cost and most of the customer's risk. That is where the margin comes from, and also why it has limits.

This report covers five listed companies that look unrelated. Clean Science and Technology makes inhibitors and antioxidants that stop monomers and fats from degrading. Privi Speciality Chemicals makes single scent molecules for the world's fragrance houses. Sudeep Pharma makes mineral salts that fill and bind tablets and fortify food. Sudarshan Chemical Industries makes colour pigments and, since March 2025, owns the global pigment business of Germany's Heubach. Advanced Enzyme Technologies brews enzymes and probiotics for supplements, feed and industrial processing.

What they share is the boundary rule that defines this sub-sector: they sell chemicals for what the chemical does inside the customer's product, not for the tonnes delivered. A drum of the inhibitor MEHQ protects a tanker of acrylic acid worth many times more. A mineral excipient can be about 95% of a tablet's weight and about 5% of its cost. An enzyme costs cents per capsule. When the ingredient is a small line on the customer's bill but decides whether the product works, the customer does not shop for the cheapest kilogram. It qualifies a supplier, writes that supplier into its formula and regulatory file, and stays unless something goes badly wrong.

That gives the four niche specialists their economics. In FY26 Clean Science earned an EBITDA margin of 37.2%, Sudeep 34.7%, Advanced Enzymes 30.8% and Privi 25.4%. Gross margins, where disclosed, run from about 54% to 64%. These are among the richest margins in Indian chemicals, well above the commodity and intermediate makers covered in our chemical intermediates report.

The bill for those margins comes in the balance sheet. To serve global customers with approved, multi-grade product on demand, these companies hold inventory for 157 days (Clean Science) to 356 days (Sudeep). Sudeep's cash conversion cycle is 372 days, more than a year. Inventory is the hidden cost of specialty chemistry, and it is why a 35% margin business can earn a return on capital in the low-to-mid twenties rather than the forties.

Five forces shape this sub-sector, and the report is built around them.

  • Approval lock-in sells function, not tonnes, but only up to a price gap. Customers pay for qualification, consistency and documentation. That lock-in held Privi's margin through 22% growth, but it did not stop Clean Science losing a large 4-MAP customer in FY26 or Advanced Enzymes losing pharma-enzyme sales in Q1 FY27.
  • China sets the marginal price. In inhibitors, aroma chemicals, azo pigments and phosphates, Chinese capacity caps what anyone can charge. Indian winners either have a cost floor below China's or paperwork China lacks.
  • The buyers are few and powerful. Four fragrance houses buy about two-thirds of the world's fragrance chemicals; a dozen acrylic majors buy MEHQ; global coatings and nutrition multinationals buy the rest. None of the five discloses its top-customer share.
  • Growth has to come from new legs, which dilute margins while they ramp. Clean Science's HALS light stabilisers, Sudeep's battery-grade iron phosphate, Advanced Enzymes' industrial lines and Sudarshan's win-back of Heubach customers all start below the core margin.
  • Scale through acquisition abroad is now being tested. Sudarshan paid €127.5 million plus €29.4 million of adjustments for a business with about €700 million of sales. Whether it can lift that business's margin decides the largest single number in this sub-sector.

Overlaying all five in the most recent quarter (April–June 2026) is the 2026 Middle East disruption. It arrived as a cost and logistics shock rather than a demand shock: an LPG shortage cut Sudeep's April output to about a third of normal, a propylene supplier shutdown cost Clean Science about two weeks of production, phosphoric acid rose about 50%, and shipping lead times stretched one to two weeks. Results for July–September 2026 are not yet out for any of the five, so Q1 FY27 is the latest quarter used throughout.

In plain terms

These companies sell the part of a product that the customer cannot afford to get wrong and does not spend much on. That lets them charge well, and customers rarely switch. But they must keep large stocks of approved product, they compete against Chinese plants that set the ceiling on prices, and the few big customers they serve can still walk if the price gap gets wide or supply fails. The best of them earn 30–37% operating margins; the group's largest member, Sudarshan, earns 6% while it repairs a European business it bought cheaply.

Valuations follow quality and growth, not size. On 14 October 2026 the five were worth ₹46,078 crore together. The market paid 65× trailing earnings for Sudeep, about 38× for Privi and Clean Science, and 19× for Advanced Enzymes, whose sales have grown about 8% a year for five years. Sudarshan's 90× trailing P/E reflects depressed profit, not enthusiasm: on management's FY27 EBITDA guidance of about ₹800 crore it trades near 12× EV/EBITDA. Section 18 explains the spread.

01

Function by the gram: what these companies actually sell

Five niches, five chemistries, one transaction: a small dose, priced per kilogram on performance, written into somebody else's formula.

A performance or ingredient chemical is bought for a job: to stabilise, preserve, colour, scent or catalyse. It is dosed in parts per million or a few per cent, and priced per kilogram on what it does. That separates it from the tonnage chemicals in our intermediates report, where buyers compare price per tonne against a published benchmark, and from the contract chemistry in our custom synthesis report, where the customer owns the molecule and the Indian company sells capacity and know-how.

The five members span five different niches. The table below defines what each one sells and what it does in the customer's product. The vocabulary matters because each niche has a different buyer, a different approval system and a different Chinese competitor.

Each niche sits in the middle of a value chain. Raw materials come in from refineries, pulp mills, mines or farms. The five companies convert them through a process that is their real asset: Clean Science's catalytic, effluent-light routes; Privi's purification of crude sulphate turpentine from about 20,000 ppm sulphur to below 1 ppm; Sudeep's precipitation, granulation and encapsulation; Sudarshan's synthesis and finishing; Advanced Enzymes' fermentation and strain library. The product then goes to a formulator who owns the end recipe, and from there to a consumer brand.

Where is value captured? In fragrance, the compounders capture the most: they own the formula and sell to the brands, and Givaudan earns an EBITDA margin of roughly 23–24% on about CHF 7.4 billion of sales (indicative, not fetched). Molecule makers like Privi earn spread multiplied by scale and approvals, which gave Privi 25% in FY26. In inhibitors, value sits with whoever has the lowest process cost at an equal specification; that has allowed Clean Science to earn around 40% in good years. In excipients, the lock is the regulatory file plus engineered grades. In pigments, value sits in formulation-specific shade approvals and in high-performance pigments, while commodity azo and phthalocyanine grades earn little. In enzymes, it sits in the strain library and application know-how; the global leader Novonesis reported a 37.1% adjusted EBITDA margin for 2025.

Two facts in that chart surprise first-time readers. Sudarshan's gross margin is close to the others', yet its EBITDA margin is a fraction of theirs; the gap is a European cost base where employees take about 15% of revenue and other operating costs about 27%. In plain terms

The five buy common raw materials, put them through a process few rivals can run as cheaply or as cleanly, and sell the result to a formulator who has tested and approved it. The approval is the reason the customer pays more than the raw-material cost suggests. The process is the reason the company can still make money when a Chinese competitor undercuts on price.

Customer concentration is the missing number in this sub-sector. None of the five discloses the share of revenue from its top customer or top ten customers in the sources reviewed for this report. What is disclosed is qualitative. Privi supplies all of the top-10 fragrance houses and makes more than 40 molecules exclusively for Givaudan. Sudeep counts 14 Fortune 500 companies and over 40 other blue-chip customers, with about seven years' average tenure among its top customers, and management has mentioned "two large customers" tied to battery-material offtake. Clean Science's FY26 was dented by the loss of one large 4-MAP customer. Advanced Enzymes' US supplement-brand customers carry a disproportionate share of profit. Sudarshan's customers are coatings, plastics and ink majors. Readers should treat concentration as high and undisclosed.

02

Small markets, big shares: how large these niches are

The global markets are measured in single-digit billions of dollars, so an Indian company can hold a meaningful slice of world supply with under ₹1,000 crore of revenue.

Performance-chemical niches are small. The global market for MEHQ, the acrylics inhibitor at the heart of Clean Science, is estimated at roughly US$160–290 million depending on the publisher. HALS light stabilisers are about US$1.5–1.7 billion. Aroma chemicals are US$4.9–7.9 billion, with about US$6 billion a reasonable planning figure. Pharma excipients are about US$11 billion. Industrial enzymes are US$7.7–8.0 billion. Organic pigments range from US$7 billion on a narrow definition to US$16–18 billion on a broad one. All of these are third-party estimates that vary by scope; treat them as INDICATIVE. Conversions in this report use working rates of about ₹88 per US dollar and ₹103 per euro.

Set against those markets, the Indian five are meaningful players in some niches and small in others. Clean Science is estimated to hold about 50–55% of world MEHQ capacity (a third-party estimate quoted in its primer, not a revenue share). Privi's ₹2,564 crore is about 4–5% of a roughly ₹53,000 crore global aroma-chemicals market, and it claims more than 20% of global supply in about ten individual molecules. Sudarshan's group revenue of ₹9,787 crore, including inorganic and effect pigments, is perhaps 7–15% of global pigments depending on the scope used, consistent with trade-press descriptions of Heubach as the world's second-largest pigment company before its insolvency. Sudeep's ₹642 crore is under 1% of global excipients, and Advanced Enzymes' ₹746 crore is about 1% of industrial enzymes. These share figures are calculations on indicative market sizes and should be read as orders of magnitude.

In plain terms

The whole world buys perhaps ₹2,000 crore of MEHQ a year, and Clean Science has capacity for about half of it. That is why its growth stalled: it already dominates its core molecule and has to find new ones. Privi, Sudeep and Advanced Enzymes are small fish in bigger ponds, which gives them room to grow if they can win approvals. Sudarshan, after Heubach, is now one of the largest pigment groups in the world.

India's own markets are less well measured. The Indian flavour-and-fragrance market is estimated at about US$2.7–3.1 billion in 2025, and Indian pharma excipients at about US$400 million; there is no current, reliable India-only size for pigments, aroma chemicals or enzymes. More important, all five companies are export businesses. Privi exports about 75–77% of sales. Sudeep exported 55% in Q1 FY27, up from 49% a year earlier. After Heubach, roughly three-quarters of Sudarshan's revenue comes from the acquired, mainly overseas business. Advanced Enzymes reports exports of 33.6% of FY26 sales, but that appears to be a standalone figure; with about ₹290 crore of revenue booked in overseas subsidiaries, the consolidated overseas share is likely about 55–65% (estimate). Clean Science's export share is commonly put at 65–70% but was not verified for this report.

Growth history: one stall, one surge, three steady climbers

The five companies' records over FY23–FY26 are very different, which matters because the sub-sector is too small for an "industry growth rate" to mean much. Privi grew revenue from ₹1,608 crore to ₹2,564 crore, a 17% three-year compound rate. Sudeep grew from ₹429 crore to ₹642 crore (14%). Advanced Enzymes grew from ₹541 crore to ₹746 crore (11%), though its five-year rate is only 8%. Clean Science barely moved: ₹936 crore in FY23, a 15.5% fall to ₹791 crore in the FY24 destocking year, then ₹967 crore and ₹957 crore; its three-year rate is 1%. Sudarshan's jump from ₹2,302 crore to ₹9,787 crore is inorganic and says nothing about underlying demand.

Two lessons follow. First, there is no single cycle that moves all five together; each niche runs on its own customer demand, its own feedstock and its own Chinese competitor. 03

Who buys function: the end-markets behind the five

Acrylics, fragrance, pills, paint and feed: five demand engines that rarely move in step, and a most recent quarter in which costs, not customers, set the tone.

Each company's demand comes from a different end-market, so the useful question is not "how is the sub-sector doing?" but "what are acrylic-acid makers, fragrance houses, drug makers, paint companies and feed mills doing?" The five are tied to these markets in very different ways.

Acrylics and polymers: Clean Science

MEHQ demand follows acrylic-acid and superabsorbent-polymer output (the absorbent in diapers), plus the coatings and adhesives made from acrylates. That grows roughly with world GDP, and Clean Science already supplies about half the world's capacity, so it cannot grow quickly in its core molecule. Its new demand engine is HALS, the light stabilisers that stop plastics and coatings breaking down under ultraviolet light. HALS was about 22% of Clean Science's Q1 FY27 sales, with exports nearly half of HALS revenue, and management targets ₹250–300 crore of HALS sales in FY27. The global HALS market grows about 5–7% a year, so Clean Science's growth there has to come from taking share from BASF, Songwon and Clariant.

Fragrance and home care: Privi

Aroma-chemical volume follows consumption of detergents, soaps and personal care products, which is led by emerging markets. The fragrance houses that buy Privi's molecules grow about 4–7% a year. Privi grew faster, 22% in FY26 and 19% in Q1 FY27, helped by customers diversifying away from China and by new molecules such as maltol, ethyl maltol, renewable cyclopentanone and furfural.

Pharma and nutrition: Sudeep, Advanced Enzymes and part of Clean Science

Excipient demand follows generic drug volumes, supplement consumption, infant nutrition and food fortification programmes. The global excipient market grows about 6%. Sudeep's Pharma, Food and Nutrition segment grew 30% year on year in Q1 FY27, mostly on volume. Advanced Enzymes' human healthcare segment, 60% of its revenue and its profit centre, fell 7% in the same quarter on lower sales to pharma customers who use enzymes in drug manufacture. Clean Science's guaiacol goes partly into drug and agrochemical intermediates.

Coatings, plastics and inks: Sudarshan

Pigments follow paint, plastics and printing. On the Q1 FY27 call, Sudarshan described soft US and European decorative and automotive coatings, volatile plastics demand as polymer prices spiked, a structural decline in volume printing inks offset by growth in packaging, agricultural and digital inks. The India-led legacy business grew 22.3%; the acquired Heubach business grew 4.9%, mostly by winning back customers lost during insolvency.

Feed and industrial processing: Advanced Enzymes

Feed enzymes (phytase, xylanase) gain when feed costs rise and when phosphorus-discharge rules tighten, because they let animals extract more nutrients. Advanced Enzymes' animal segment fell 3% in Q1 FY27. Its bio-processing segment, which sells enzymes to grain-ethanol, textile and baking plants, rose 30%, and specialised manufacturing rose 41%.

Because the five export so much, three external variables matter more than Indian demand. The first is European and US industrial activity, which drives coatings, plastics and fragrance volumes. The second is currency: a weaker rupee lifts reported revenue (Sudeep cited about three percentage points of FX benefit in Q1 FY27). The third is US trade policy. In plain terms

Nobody buys these chemicals because India is growing. They are bought because a European paint maker, an American supplement brand or a Swiss fragrance house needs them. In the latest quarter, the companies' customers mostly kept buying; what hurt was the cost of raw materials and shipping after the 2026 Middle East disruption.

A final demand engine is still on the drawing board. Sudeep is building a battery-grade iron phosphate business (the precursor for lithium iron phosphate, or LFP, battery cathodes) through a subsidiary, Sudeep Advanced Materials. Phase 1 is due by March–April 2027, with 100,000 tonnes a year of base capacity rising to 200,000 tonnes by FY30–31. Management reports 21 prospective customers at lab validation, 16 at pilot stage and 7 commercially validated, two memoranda of understanding signed in South Korea, and cites Chinese export controls on battery technology as a tailwind for non-Chinese supply. This is a bulk, capital-heavy business outside the excipient moat, and section 16 treats it as a distinct risk.

04

Where ₹100 goes: four lean cost sheets and one European one

Raw materials take ₹36–57 of every ₹100, and the niche specialists keep ₹25–37 as operating profit. Sudarshan keeps about ₹10, because its acquired business carries a European cost base.

The supply side of this sub-sector is mostly about raw materials and energy, because the companies are not labour-intensive and their plants are modest in size compared with commodity chemicals. Each company's key inputs are different. Clean Science buys phenol, anisole, methanol, hydrogen peroxide and propylene-linked materials. Privi buys crude sulphate turpentine (CST), a by-product of kraft pulp mills, and gum turpentine; it buys an estimated 20–25% of the world's supply from more than 60 mills in Canada, the US and Europe, and imports about 70% of its raw materials. Sudeep buys limestone, phosphoric acid, metal salts and LPG for its dryers. Sudarshan buys aromatic amines, naphthols, crude copper phthalocyanine, iron salts and solvents, and pays European gas prices at its German plants. Advanced Enzymes buys sugars, soy and corn steep liquor for its fermentation media, plus energy.

China, the majors and the private specialists: who else makes these molecules

Each Indian company faces a different competitor set: a European chemical major, a Chinese price-setter, and in several niches a family-owned German or American specialist that never lists.

Industry structure in this sub-sector has two layers, and they behave very differently. The customer layer is concentrated: four fragrance houses (Givaudan, dsm-firmenich, IFF and Symrise) account for roughly two-thirds of the fragrance market; about a dozen global majors make acrylic acid; the top ten coatings companies hold about half of global paint sales. The producer layer varies by niche: highly concentrated in MEHQ and enzymes, fragmented in pigments, aroma chemicals and mineral salts. When concentrated buyers face fragmented sellers, the buyers set terms; when a seller dominates a niche, as Clean Science does in MEHQ, the balance tilts the other way.

The price of specialty is paid in inventory Deep dive

The same approvals that protect these margins oblige the companies to hold five months to a year of stock. That inventory is the hidden cost that keeps a 35% margin from becoming a 40% return.

Start with what the customer actually buys. A fragrance house wants each of the dozens of molecules in a formula to arrive on time, to the same odour profile, every time; a missed delivery stops a production line. A drug maker wants the exact grade of calcium phosphate named in its regulatory filing, in the particle size it validated; it cannot substitute another grade without a filing change. A paint maker wants a pigment that matches the approved shade batch after batch. To give customers that certainty, the supplier holds stock: raw materials bought when they are available (Privi buys turpentine seasonally from pulp mills), work in progress across multi-step processes, and finished goods in every approved grade, often in warehouses near the customer.

That is why four of the five carry 150–360 days of inventory, against perhaps 30–90 days for a typical commodity chemical maker (an indicative range). The figures are screener's inventory days for FY26.

What inventory costs, in rupees

A worked example shows why this matters. Take an ILLUSTRATIVE specialist with ₹1,000 crore of revenue and a 60% gross margin, so ₹400 crore of raw-material cost. If it holds 235 days of inventory measured on that cost, it carries 400 × 235 ÷ 365 = about ₹258 crore of stock. Add debtors of 75 days of revenue (about ₹205 crore) and subtract supplier credit of, say, 60 days of cost (about ₹66 crore), and roughly ₹397 crore is tied up in working capital, about 40% of a year's sales. At a 10% cost of capital, carrying that costs about ₹40 crore a year, four points of margin that never appear in the income statement.

Now take the same company with 120 days of inventory. Stock falls to about ₹132 crore and working capital to about ₹271 crore. The ₹126 crore released is cash the company can return or invest, and the carrying cost falls to about ₹27 crore. This is why management guidance on working capital deserves as much attention as guidance on margins. Sudeep, for instance, guides to 160–170 working-capital days at steady state and 150 in the long term, down from levels that left FY26 operating cash flow at only ₹70 crore against EBITDA of ₹223 crore.

In plain terms

A specialty-chemical company that sells ₹1,000 crore a year may need ₹300–400 crore permanently parked in drums, sacks and unpaid invoices. That money earns nothing until the product ships and the customer pays. A high margin on paper can therefore turn into a modest return on the money actually invested, and a fast-growing company can report rising profit while generating little cash.

Cash conversion: where it shows up

The clearest single test is operating cash flow as a share of EBITDA. If inventory and debtors grow faster than sales, cash flow falls behind profit. In FY26, Privi converted 84% of EBITDA into operating cash, Clean Science 77%, Advanced Enzymes 68% and Sudarshan 57%. Sudeep converted 31%: ₹70 crore of cash from ₹223 crore of EBITDA. Part of Sudeep's gap is growth (revenue rose 28%), part is new-grade and battery-venture inventory, and part may be integration of the NSS acquisition; but a year in which less than a third of operating profit became cash is a red flag that the company needs to reverse.

The arithmetic is simple. A 35% EBITDA margin, minus 5–8 points of depreciation, leaves an EBIT margin around 28–30%. If capital employed is roughly equal to a year's sales, as it is for businesses carrying fixed assets plus nearly a year of working capital, the pre-tax return is about 28–30%. If capital employed is 1.2 times sales, the return falls to about 24%. Inventory is the biggest single reason capital employed is so high relative to sales in this sub-sector.

In plain terms

A specialty chemical company with a 35% margin sounds like a money machine. But if it has to keep almost a year of stock to hold its customers, the money tied up in that stock dilutes the return to the low-to-mid twenties. That is still good, but it is what "specialty" really earns once the stock is counted. Watch inventory days: a company that can bring them down without losing customers is creating value that never shows in the margin.

What would good look like? A company reducing inventory days while growing revenue, raising operating cash flow above 70% of EBITDA, and keeping the margin steady. Sudarshan provides an early example in the acquired business: working-capital days fell from 91.5 to 62 and working capital as a share of sales fell from 25.9% to 23.6%, which helped cut net debt from a peak of ₹922 crore to ₹531 crore by June 2026. The opposite pattern, rising inventory alongside rising revenue and falling cash conversion, is what investors should look for in Sudeep's Q2 FY27 results. A company that grows on inventory is borrowing from future cash flow, and the bill comes due if a new grade or new customer fails to absorb that stock.

China sets the marginal price, and 2026 brought a cost shock

In four of the five niches, Chinese capacity caps what anyone can charge. India competes on process cost and paperwork, and in the latest quarter all five were hit by inputs rather than demand.

The most important external force on this sub-sector is not Indian demand or even global demand. It is Chinese supply. China is the marginal producer in MEHQ and BHA outside the two Tier-1 makers, in the citral and terpene chains that compete with many aroma chemicals, in azo reds and yellows (India's strength is phthalocyanine blues and greens), in phosphates and basic mineral salts, and at the low end of enzymes. When Chinese plants run hard and sell abroad, they set a ceiling on prices that no amount of approval lock-in can lift far above.

That ceiling works through the customer's arithmetic. A fragrance house or paint maker will tolerate paying 5–10% more to a qualified Indian supplier to avoid the cost and risk of re-qualifying a Chinese one. It will not tolerate paying 30–40% more for long. Lock-in is a band, not a wall. The width of that band depends on the niche: wider in pharma excipients, where a change means a regulatory filing; narrower in pigments and aroma chemicals, where re-approval is a lab exercise measured in months.

How the cycle played out, FY22 to FY27

FY22 was the boom. COVID restocking, freight disruption and the "China+1" rush to diversify supply chains away from China gave Indian specialty chemical makers their best year, and Clean Science listed in July 2021 at a rich multiple. FY23–FY24 was the bust: global chemical customers ran down the stocks they had built, Chinese overcapacity met weak European demand, and prices fell. Clean Science's revenue fell 15.5% in FY24, from ₹936 crore to ₹791 crore. Privi's margin had already troughed at 12% in FY23 when turpentine feedstock costs spiked. FY25–FY26 brought an uneven recovery. FY27 so far has been defined by a different kind of shock.

Clean Science's HALS business is partly an import-substitution and export play against Chinese and European incumbents. Sudeep's battery pitch rests on Chinese export controls on battery-material technology, which management refers to as "Decree 837". In each case, China+1 opens a door; the Indian company still has to win on cost and quality once inside.

The deeper protection is process. Clean Science's margin record is the test case. Its consolidated margin fell from 43% in FY23 to 37% in FY26, which looks like erosion. But its standalone margin, which reflects the core MEHQ and antioxidant business without the HALS subsidiary, was still about 43% in Q1 FY27. The core process advantage over Chinese producers appears intact; the consolidated decline comes from new plants and HALS ramping at lower margins, plus some pricing pressure. Section 16 works through the arithmetic.

What would break the model? A Chinese producer replicating a catalytic route at scale, which is plausible over time because processes are not legally protected; a sustained period of Chinese overcapacity combined with weak Western demand, as in FY24; or anti-dumping and trade measures that change which supplier is cheapest in a given market. India's trade data for these products by customs code (for example HS 2909 for MEHQ and anisole, 3302 for aroma mixtures, 3204 for organic pigments, 3507 for enzymes) was not analysed for this report, and no recent Indian anti-dumping cases in these niches were checked; both are worth monitoring.

The paper moat: approvals, dossiers and effluent permits

Regulation in this sub-sector rarely sets prices. It sets the cost of entry and the cost of switching, which is where the margin comes from.

Regulation matters to these companies in a particular way. Unlike drug pricing or electricity tariffs, nobody regulates what a performance chemical can sell for. What regulators control is who may sell into a product category and what paperwork they must carry. Every regulatory file a supplier holds is a barrier a new entrant must pay to cross, and every file a customer has written a supplier into is a cost the customer must pay to switch.

The pharmaceutical and food rules create the deepest moat. When a drug company files a generic tablet with a regulator, it names its excipient suppliers. Changing supplier means testing the new material, potentially repeating stability studies and filing a change. For an ingredient that costs perhaps 5% of the tablet, few customers bother unless the incumbent fails. Sudeep's primer cites qualification cycles of five to seven years. That is why Sudeep can sustain a 64% gross margin on what is chemically a simple mineral salt. Our pharma APIs and intermediates report covers the same regulatory machinery from the drug side.

The fragrance rules cut both ways. IFRA standards and the EU's expanded allergen-labelling rules restrict some molecules, which can cost a supplier volume. But re-formulation also creates demand for substitutes, and a supplier with a broad molecule list and strong relationships with the houses that do the re-formulating is well placed to win it. Privi's push from about 75 molecules toward 95–100 is partly a response to that churn.

In plain terms

The real barrier in this business is paperwork. A drug maker that has listed Sudeep's calcium salt in its US filing would have to re-test and re-file to switch, which costs more than any saving on the salt. A fragrance house that has approved Privi's molecule in a detergent scent would need new odour panels. That paperwork is why customers stay, and why new rivals, Chinese or otherwise, struggle to get in quickly.

Environmental regulation is the quieter advantage. Indian specialty chemicals clusters in Gujarat and Maharashtra have faced repeated closures and tighter effluent norms. A process that produces little effluent, like Clean Science's catalytic routes, is cheaper to run and easier to license, and it is what lets Clean Science expand at its existing sites. European customers also increasingly audit suppliers' environmental footprints, which favours cleaner processes over cheaper ones.

Two regulatory risks deserve watching. The first is substitution by rule: BHA is under periodic safety review as a food additive, certain azo pigments face restrictions on trace impurities, and PFAS-style restrictions show how quickly a class of chemistry can be curtailed in Europe. The second is US trade policy. The 2025 escalation of US tariffs on Indian goods matters to companies with large US sales (Advanced Enzymes' supplement business above all), and its status in October 2026 was not verified for this report.

Finally, the GST searches at Clean Science (completed 29 September 2026 with a ₹9.9 crore liability and a ₹2.0 crore net impact) and at Sudarshan (concluded 6 October 2026 with no material impact reported) were company events rather than sector policy. CRISIL reaffirmed Clean Science's AA-/Stable and A1+ ratings on 24 September 2026, during the search.

Six years, four phases: boom, destock, deal, disruption

The sub-sector's recent history is a sequence of shocks that hit the five in different ways, and one transforming acquisition.

The five companies were built over seven decades, but the modern sub-sector took shape after 2020. The founders' era set the ownership patterns that still matter. Sudarshan was founded in Pune in 1951–52 by the Rathi family. Sudeep was founded in 1989 by the Bhayani family in Gujarat; the same year, Advanced Enzymes' origins trace to a different Rathi family in Thane (the two Rathi families appear to be unrelated; not verified). Privi began aroma-chemical manufacturing in 1993. Clean Science was founded in Pune in 2003 by the Boob family with the Sikchis.

The 2000s and 2010s brought capital. Biocon sold its enzymes business to Novozymes in 2007, leaving Advanced Enzymes as the main Indian home-grown enzyme maker; it listed in 2016. Privi took private equity from 2007 to 2011, Fairfax invested in 2016, Privi Organics merged into Fairchem Speciality in 2017, and in 2020 the oleochemicals business was demerged and the listed company was renamed Privi Speciality Chemicals (the precise sequence is as described in our primer and the research notes; worth checking against filings).

Reading a performance-chemicals company: the KPIs Deep dive

Thirteen numbers explain almost everything about these five businesses. Each one has a formula, a benchmark and a trap, and two of them (inventory and cash conversion) are where most investors stop looking too early.

The equation and a worked example

Every company in this sub-sector, whatever its chemistry, earns money through the same equation. Revenue is the number of approved products, times the customers who buy them, times the tonnes they buy, times the price per kilogram. Operating profit is the tonnes sold times the spread between that price and the variable cost of each kilogram, minus the fixed cost of running the plants and the organisation. A worked example with round numbers, ILLUSTRATIVE. A company sells 5,000 tonnes a year at an average ₹1,000 per kilogram, so revenue is 5,000 × 1,000 × 1,000 = ₹500 crore. Raw materials and other variable costs (power, packaging, freight) come to ₹550 per kilogram, leaving a spread of ₹450 per kilogram, or ₹225 crore of contribution. Cut the spread by ₹50 per kilogram instead (a Chinese price cut or a feedstock spike): contribution falls to ₹200 crore and EBITDA to ₹140 crore, down 15%. That asymmetry, where a modest change in spread moves profit more than a modest change in volume, is the first thing to understand about these businesses.

In plain terms

The company makes money on each kilogram it sells, after paying for the raw materials in it, and then has to cover a fixed bill for its plants and people. Sell a bit more and profit rises faster than sales because the fixed bill does not change. Let the gap between price and raw-material cost narrow and profit falls faster than sales. Most surprises in these companies' results come from that gap.

1. Gross margin: pricing power over raw materials

What it is. The share of revenue left after paying for raw materials. Formula: (revenue − raw-material cost) ÷ revenue. Companies sometimes include packing material or changes in inventory; screener's "material cost %" is a usable proxy when the company does not disclose gross margin. Worked example: ₹500 crore of revenue and ₹200 crore of raw materials give a 60% gross margin.

What drives it. Up: mix shift to engineered or high-performance grades, falling feedstock prices before contract prices adjust, new molecules with little competition. Down: feedstock spikes with a pass-through lag, Chinese price cuts, a shift toward bulk products (or toward battery-grade materials, in Sudeep's future). Benchmark: above 55% good; 40–55% average; below 40% weak, unless the model is a high-tonnage converter like Privi's.

How the five compare. Sudeep 64.25% in Q1 FY27; Clean Science about 60% (estimate from a 39.7% raw-material share); Advanced Enzymes about 55%; Sudarshan about 54% in Q1 FY27; Privi about 40%. The comparison is on mixed bases, and gross margin alone misleads, as the next chart shows.

3. Realisation per kilogram and the spread

What it is. Average revenue per kilogram sold, and its difference from raw-material cost per kilogram. Formula: realisation = revenue ÷ tonnes sold; spread = realisation − raw-material cost per kg. Worked example: Clean Science sold about 1,000 tonnes of HALS in Q1 FY27 at about ₹550 per kilogram, roughly ₹59 crore, or 22% of its ₹268 crore revenue. At the same price, its FY27 HALS target of ₹250–300 crore implies 4,500–5,500 tonnes for the year.

What drives it. Product mix (a high-performance pigment can sell for many times a commodity azo), Chinese price competition, contract pass-through clauses for raw materials, and currency. Benchmark: no universal range; the direction and the spread trend matter. How the five compare: only Clean Science's HALS realisation is disclosed (₹550 per kilogram, up from ₹440). For Privi, an ILLUSTRATIVE calculation at an assumed 80% utilisation of 48,000 tonnes gives about 38,400 tonnes and an average realisation of about ₹668 per kilogram, with raw materials at about ₹381 and a spread near ₹287.

4. Utilisation and incremental margin: operating leverage

What it is. Utilisation is output as a share of nameplate capacity. Incremental margin is the change in EBITDA divided by the change in revenue between two periods. Formula: utilisation = tonnes produced ÷ capacity; incremental margin = ΔEBITDA ÷ Δrevenue. Worked example: Privi's revenue rose from ₹1,608 crore in FY23 to ₹2,564 crore in FY26 (+₹956 crore). Its EBITDA rose from about ₹193 crore (12% of FY23 revenue) to ₹651 crore (+₹458 crore). The incremental margin was about 48%, nearly twice the FY26 average margin of 25.4%. That is operating leverage: fixed costs were already paid for, so each extra rupee of spread fell largely to profit.

What drives it. How full the plants are, how much of the cost base is fixed, and whether new capacity has come on stream (which adds fixed cost before it adds volume). Benchmark: utilisation above 80% good; 60–80% average; below 60% weak. Disclosed data is sparse: Sudeep's existing capacity is "nearly fully utilised" (but its April 2026 output fell to about a third of normal during the LPG shortage); Privi's capacity is about 48,000 tonnes with no utilisation disclosed; Sudarshan's acquired sites are under-used, which is why it needs no growth capex.

7. Cash conversion: operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA

What it is. How much of operating profit became cash. Formula: cash from operations (after working capital and tax) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: Sudeep's FY26 cash from operations was ₹70 crore against EBITDA of ₹223 crore: 31%. Privi's was ₹550 crore against ₹651 crore: 84%.

What drives it. Working-capital changes above all, then tax payments. Fast growth almost always lowers it for a year because inventory and debtors grow ahead of collections. Benchmark: above 70% good; 50–70% average; below 50% weak. How the five compare: Privi 84%, Clean Science 77%, Advanced Enzymes 68%, Sudarshan 57%, Sudeep 31% (chart in section 7). Combined, the five converted 68% (₹1,396 crore of ₹2,063 crore); ex-Sudarshan 72%.

The definition trap. One year is noise; three years is signal. A company can show strong conversion by stretching suppliers or factoring receivables. Check whether payable days rose or whether "other current liabilities" jumped. And interest paid is sometimes classified in operating cash flow, sometimes in financing: compare like with like.

In plain terms

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. If a company reports ₹223 crore of operating profit and only ₹70 crore arrives in the bank, the other ₹150 crore is sitting in warehouses and customers' unpaid bills. That can be fine for a year of fast growth. It is a problem if it repeats.

8. ROCE: reported and excluding plants under construction

What it is. Operating profit after depreciation as a return on the money invested in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), usually averaged over the year; the stricter version removes capital-work-in-progress (CWIP) and treasury investments from the denominator. Worked example: EBIT of ₹160 crore on ₹640 crore of capital employed is 25%.

What drives it. Margin, asset turnover and working capital. A plant under construction depresses reported ROCE until it earns; a large treasury does the same. Benchmark: ex-CWIP above 25% good; 15–25% average; below 15% weak. How the five compare (FY26): screener ROCE Sudeep 28.2%, Privi 22.3%, Clean Science 20.7%, Advanced Enzymes 14.0%, Sudarshan 5.5%; ex-CWIP and ex-investments about 26%, 25%, 25%, 18% and 5% for Clean Science, Privi, Sudeep, Advanced Enzymes and Sudarshan respectively (calculation; chart in section 7). Sudarshan's own figure is 10.8% for FY26 and 22.7% annualised for Q1 FY27 on its company basis.

The definition trap. Sudarshan illustrates the problem: three different ROCE figures (5.5%, 10.8%, 22.7%) are all quoted for the same company, depending on whether goodwill, cash and annualised quarters are used. Ask which. And Advanced Enzymes' screener ROCE is held down by ₹584 crore of investments that earn treasury returns; its operating business earns more.

A practical rule for this sub-sector. Because so much capital sits in inventory, a useful cross-check is to compare ROCE with the cash conversion ratio. A company with 25% ROCE and 80% cash conversion (Privi) is earning its return in cash; a company with 28% ROCE and 31% cash conversion (Sudeep in FY26) is earning much of its return as additional stock and receivables, which only become cash if the products sell and customers pay. Over three to five years, the two ratios should converge; if ROCE stays high while cash conversion stays low, the reported return is overstated.

9. Net debt to EBITDA

What it is. How many years of operating profit it would take to repay net borrowings. Formula: (borrowings − cash and liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: Privi's company-reported net debt of ₹887 crore at FY26-end divided by its ₹665 crore company EBITDA is 1.33×, down from 2.25× a year earlier.

What drives it. Capex and acquisitions up; cash generation and equity raising down. Benchmark: net cash good; under 1.5× average; above 2.5× weak. How the five compare: Clean Science and Advanced Enzymes have net cash (at least ₹403 crore and ₹552 crore, using screener investments, since cash balances are not shown); Privi 1.33×; Sudarshan ₹531 crore at June 2026, about 0.9× FY26 EBITDA or 0.66× its FY27 guided ₹800 crore; Sudeep at most 0.7× on gross debt of ₹150 crore.

10. Export share and the currency effect

What it is. The share of revenue sold outside India. Formula: export revenue ÷ total revenue, stating whether standalone or consolidated. Worked example: a company with 60% exports and ₹500 crore of revenue gains about ₹3 crore of revenue for each 1% fall in the rupee, almost all of which drops to profit if costs are rupee-based.

What drives it. Customer base, overseas subsidiaries, acquisitions. Benchmark: context, not a quality score. How the five compare: Privi about 75–77%; Sudarshan about 75% overseas (acquired-group share); Clean Science about 65–70% (unverified); Advanced Enzymes 33.6% reported (likely standalone; consolidated estimated 55–65%); Sudeep 55% in Q1 FY27.

The definition trap. Advanced Enzymes is the case study: its "33.6% exports" excludes sales by its US and German subsidiaries, so the consolidated overseas share is far higher. And for companies importing raw materials (Privi imports about 70% of its inputs), currency moves partly cancel: what matters is net exposure, not gross exports.

11. Customer concentration and approval count

What it is. The share of revenue from the top one or top ten customers; and the number of approved products, grades or regulatory files. Formula: top-10 revenue ÷ total revenue; count of commercial molecules or grades. Worked example: if a ₹1,000 crore company's largest customer is 15% of revenue and buys a product earning 40% EBITDA, losing it removes about ₹60 crore of EBITDA, more than a sixth of a ₹330 crore total (ILLUSTRATIVE).

Benchmark: top-10 under 30% good; 30–50% average; over 50% weak; approval counts should rise. How the five compare: no company discloses concentration in the sources reviewed. Approval-type counts: Privi about 75 molecules heading to 95–100, plus more than 40 in PRIGIV; Advanced Enzymes 68 enzymes and 400+ formulations (company claim); Sudeep 14 Fortune 500 customers and over 40 other blue chips.

The definition trap. Product counts flatter: a new grade of the same molecule counts as a "product". And concentration can hide inside a joint venture: PRIGIV is effectively a single-customer business with Givaudan.

12. R&D intensity

What it is. Research and development spending as a share of revenue. Formula: R&D expense (revenue and capital) ÷ revenue. Worked example: ₹15 crore on ₹500 crore is 3%.

Benchmark: above 3% good; 1.5–3% average; below 1.5% weak; the global enzyme leader Novonesis spends about 10%. How the five compare: not fetched for this report; it requires annual reports. The definition trap: capitalised development cost, and process-engineering staff classified as production rather than R&D, make reported figures hard to compare. For a process-cost leader like Clean Science, much of the real "R&D" is process chemistry inside the plant.

13. Fixed-asset turnover

What it is. Revenue generated per rupee of plant. Formula: revenue ÷ net fixed assets. Worked example: Sudeep guides that its new plants will turn over 2.7–3.0× at maturity, so every ₹100 crore of plant should support ₹270–300 crore of revenue. Benchmark: above 2.0× good; 1.2–2.0× average; below 1.2× weak. How the five compare: not fetched, apart from Sudeep's guidance. The definition trap: revalued or old, heavily depreciated plants flatter the ratio; acquired plants bought out of insolvency (Heubach) carry low book values that make turnover look high.

14. Promoter holding and free float

What it is. The share of equity held by the founding family or controlling group, and its inverse, the share that trades freely. Formula: promoter shares ÷ total shares, from the quarterly shareholding pattern. Worked example: a company worth ₹12,000 crore with 76% promoter holding has only about ₹2,900 crore of shares in other hands, and less still that actually trades once long-term institutions are excluded.

Why it matters here. It is not a profitability KPI, but it shapes valuation and governance in this sub-sector more than in most. A small free float makes a stock scarce for institutions and can support a high multiple; it also means lock-in expiries after an IPO can bring heavy selling. A very low promoter stake changes who controls strategy. Benchmark: no good or bad level; watch the direction and the reason for changes. How the five compare: Sudeep 76.16% (September 2026), Privi 60.60%, Clean Science 51.29% (June 2026; our primer's "~72%" is out of date, as the founding families have sold blocks since the 2021 listing), Advanced Enzymes 43.25%, Sudarshan 8.19%.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA with or without other income. Company presentations often include treasury income; screener does not. Gaps of 3–7 points (Clean Science, Advanced Enzymes).
  • Business versus reported EBITDA. Sudarshan's Q1 FY27 business EBITDA (₹247 crore) excludes an ₹18 crore inventory-overhead credit that the reported ₹266 crore includes.
  • Revenue versus total income. Our Privi primer quoted ₹2,583 crore, which is total income including ₹19 crore of other income; sales were ₹2,564 crore. Sudeep's primer quoted ₹511 crore for FY25 total income; sales were ₹502 crore.
  • Consolidated versus standalone. Clean Science's standalone margin (~43%) and consolidated margin (37%) tell different stories; Advanced Enzymes' 33.6% export share is a standalone figure.
  • Net debt without cash. Screener's data for these five omits cash, so net cash is understated (Clean, Advanced Enzymes) and net debt overstated (Sudeep). Use company figures where available.
  • Three ROCEs for one company. Sudarshan's 5.5% (screener), 10.8% (company FY26) and 22.7% (company Q1 annualised) are all "right" on different bases.
  • "Specialty share" by acquisition. Sudeep's specialty segment includes the lower-margin NSS acquisition; growth there is not the same as mix improvement.
  • Inorganic growth. Sudarshan's +192% FY26 growth and the group's +94.6% are consolidation effects. The like-for-like figure is not available on a pro forma basis.

In plain terms

Almost every headline number in this sub-sector comes in two or three versions. Before comparing two companies, check that the margin counts the same things, that the revenue is sales and not total income, and that net debt includes cash. Most apparent contradictions between our primers, broker notes and screener vanish once the basis is stated.

How to read a performance-chemicals company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, split into volume and price/mix (1 minute). Flat revenue with rising volume means prices fell; ask whether Chinese pricing or raw-material pass-through caused it. Red flag: falling volume in the core while management talks only about new products.
  2. Gross margin or raw-material share versus last year (1 minute). A two- to five-point swing is normal in a cost shock; check whether management expects to recover it with a one- to two-quarter lag. Red flag: gross margin falling while raw-material prices are also falling.
  3. EBITDA margin, on a stated basis, versus guidance (1 minute). Privi's band is 24–26%; Sudeep targets 37–38%; Sudarshan guides about ₹800 crore of EBITDA for FY27. Red flag: a miss explained by "one-offs" two quarters running.
  4. The new leg (1 minute). HALS revenue at Clean Science against ₹250–300 crore; specialty share and margin at Sudeep; industrial lines at Advanced Enzymes; acquired-group margin at Sudarshan. Red flag: the new leg's share rising while its margin falls.
  5. The core (1 minute). Clean Science standalone; Advanced Enzymes human healthcare; Sudarshan legacy India. Red flag: a second consecutive decline in the core.
  6. Working capital (1 minute, half-yearly). Inventory and debtors against revenue growth. Red flag: inventory growing faster than revenue for two half-years.
  7. Cash flow (1 minute, half-yearly). Operating cash flow against EBITDA. Red flag: below 50% for a second period (Sudeep's FY26 was 31%).
  8. Net debt and capex (1 minute). Privi's ₹887 crore against its ₹1,200 crore expansion; Sudarshan's ₹531 crore against its ~₹500 crore FY27 target. Red flag: net debt rising while capex is "maintenance".
  9. Other income and tax (1 minute). Strip treasury income from profit growth (it explains part of Advanced Enzymes' and Sudeep's PAT); check Sudarshan's tax rate. Red flag: PAT growth explained mostly by other income or a lower tax rate.
  10. The call: plants, customers, one-offs (1 minute). Plant timelines (Clean Science's second plant by Q3 FY27; Sudeep's greenfield FDA approval; Privi's capacity), customer wins or losses, restructuring charges (Sudarshan's European charge). Red flag: a timeline that slips twice.

Run these ten steps and you will know, within ten minutes of a release, whether a quarter's surprise came from the spread, the volume, the new leg or the balance sheet, and whether it is likely to persist. For this sub-sector, steps 6 and 7 are the ones most commonly skipped and the ones that most often explain a later disappointment.

In plain terms

Look first at whether sales grew because more was sold or because prices moved, then at whether the gap between price and raw-material cost held, then at the new product line, and last, but most important, at whether the profit arrived as cash. Most bad surprises in this sub-sector were visible in the warehouse and the receivables book a year before they reached the income statement.

The first map confirms the central argument of section 7: in this sub-sector, high margins and heavy inventory go together. The only company that combines a top margin with the lowest stock is Clean Science, whose few large molecules allow longer production runs and fewer grades. The Heubach test: can an Indian buyer fix a European pigment group? Deep dive

Sudarshan bought about €700 million of sales for about €157 million. The price was low enough that the risk lies in running the business, not in overpaying for it, and each point of margin is worth twice last year's profit.

Heubach's story begins with consolidation in Europe's pigment industry. In 2022 the German family company Heubach, backed by the US private-equity firm SK Capital, bought Clariant's pigments business, creating what trade press called the world's second-largest pigment company. The combination struggled: European energy prices spiked, coatings and plastics demand weakened, integration absorbed management, and debt was heavy. In 2024 the Heubach Group entered insolvency proceedings in Germany. Customers, worried about supply, began qualifying other sources. For any acquirer, that meant buying a business whose revenue was falling for reasons of confidence rather than quality.

Sudarshan, India's largest pigment maker with about a third of the domestic market and about US$285 million of revenue, signed on 11 October 2024 to buy Heubach's global pigment business through its Dutch subsidiary, Sudarshan Europe B.V. It paid €127.5 million in cash (about ₹1,180 crore at the signing-date rate) for assets bought from the insolvency administrator plus all of Heubach Holdings S.à r.l. in Luxembourg. The deal completed in early March 2025, with a further €29.4 million of adjustments for inventory, working capital and VAT. Sudarshan funded it with an ₹800 crore QIP at ₹1,070 per share (₹325 crore of which was earmarked for the deal) and debt.

The new-leg problem: growth that dilutes before it pays Deep dive

Clean Science's margin slide from 43% to 37%, Sudeep's specialty segment at 26% and Advanced Enzymes' shift to industrial lines are the same arithmetic: a mature core plus a younger, lower-margin engine.

The core products of most of these companies have run into a ceiling. Clean Science already supplies about half the world's MEHQ capacity; its three-year sales growth is 1%. Advanced Enzymes' five-year sales growth is about 8%, and its human-nutrition core shrank in Q1 FY27. Sudeep's core excipient plants are nearly full. To keep growing, each company has launched a new leg. And because a new product starts with fewer customers, unabsorbed plant cost and introductory pricing, each new leg earns less than the core while it ramps. The result is consolidated margins that fall even when nothing is wrong with the core. Telling dilution from deterioration is the analytical job.

Clean Science: HALS and the 43% to 37% slide

Clean Science's consolidated EBITDA margin fell from 43% in FY23 to 42%, 40% and 37.2% in FY26. In isolation that looks like a franchise losing pricing power. But two things happened at once. First, HALS (hindered amine light stabilisers), made in subsidiary Clean Fino-Chem, grew from nothing to about 22% of revenue by Q1 FY27; it reached EBITDA breakeven only in Q3 FY26 and still earns less than the core. Second, new plants for hydroquinone and catechol added depreciation and fixed costs before they reached full output. Meanwhile, the standalone business, which is mostly the MEHQ and antioxidant core, still earned about 43% in Q1 FY27, the same as the consolidated margin in FY23.

How the fragrance chain divides the money

The fragrance industry has three layers. At the top are consumer brands (Unilever, P&G, Henkel, Reckitt) that sell detergents, soaps and personal care. In the middle are the fragrance houses (Givaudan, dsm-firmenich, IFF, Symrise, then Takasago, MANE, Robertet) that own the formulas: a single fragrance may combine dozens of molecules, and the house sells the compounded fragrance to the brand. At the bottom are aroma-chemical makers like Privi that sell individual molecules, about 3,000 of which are in commercial use. The house captures the most value because it owns the formula and the brand relationship; Givaudan earns roughly 23–24% EBITDA on about CHF 7.4 billion of sales (indicative). A molecule maker earns its spread multiplied by scale and approvals.

For a molecule maker, being designed into many formulas at the top four houses is the moat. A house that has written Privi's molecule into hundreds of fragrances, each sold to brands that have run consumer tests, will not switch for a small saving. But the four houses buy about two-thirds of the world's fragrance, so their buying power is real; they can and do run dual-supplier programmes and push for annual price reductions. Privi's PRIGIV joint venture with Givaudan (51% Privi, 49% Givaudan) turns that power into partnership: Givaudan co-invested, and PRIGIV makes more than 40 molecules exclusively for it.

The spread machine

Privi's main raw material is crude sulphate turpentine, a by-product of kraft pulp mills, plus gum turpentine and petrochemical aromatics. It buys an estimated 20–25% of the world's CST from more than 60 mills, purifies it (removing sulphur from about 20,000 ppm to below 1 ppm) and converts it into terpene-based scent molecules. Its profit is roughly tonnes sold multiplied by the spread between its selling price and its feedstock cost, minus a largely fixed cost of running two plants (Mahad and Jhagadia) and an organisation.

An ILLUSTRATIVE calculation: assume about 38,400 tonnes sold (80% of 48,000 tonnes of capacity). FY26 revenue of ₹2,564 crore then implies about ₹668 per kilogram. With raw materials at about 57% of revenue, feedstock costs about ₹381 per kilogram, leaving a spread of about ₹287 per kilogram, or about ₹1,102 crore of gross profit. Subtract conversion and fixed costs of about ₹451 crore and EBITDA is ₹651 crore, the reported figure.

Those sensitivities explain the three-year swing. In plain terms

Privi buys a by-product of paper mills and turns it into scent molecules. In a bad year, the by-product got expensive while scent prices did not, and Privi barely made a profit. When the gap went back to normal, and Privi sold more, most of the extra money fell straight to the bottom line because the plants and people were already paid for. That is why profit rose fifteen times while sales rose by about half. It also works in reverse.

The next leg up: capacity, new molecules and a merger

Privi's ambition is ₹5,000 crore of revenue and more than ₹1,000 crore of EBITDA by FY29–30, roughly double FY26 revenue. The capacity path to get there is unclear: sources variously describe about 54,000 tonnes "shortly", 66,000 tonnes by September 2027, commercial operations of the expansion in the second half of 2028, and 72,000 tonnes by about 2028 for about ₹1,200 crore of capex. An ILLUSTRATIVE check shows why the revenue target needs more than tonnes. At 72,000 tonnes and the same 80% utilisation, ₹668 per kilogram and ₹287 per kilogram spread, with fixed costs rising in proportion to capacity, Privi would reach about ₹3,850 crore of revenue and about ₹980 crore of EBITDA. Reaching ₹5,000 crore therefore requires higher-value molecules, not just more of the same: maltol, ethyl maltol, renewable cyclopentanone and furfural are early examples, alongside PRIGIV's growth and the move from about 75 molecules to 95–100.

A related step is the proposed amalgamation of Privi Fine Sciences Pvt Ltd (PFSPL) and Privi Biotechnologies Pvt Ltd (PBPL) into the listed company, filed with NCLT Mumbai on 25 June 2026. The NCLT-convened shareholders' meeting is on 27 October 2026. Management says the scheme adds about 6,000 tonnes of capacity and expects completion in FY27. The swap ratio, consideration and whether the two companies are promoter-owned were not found for this report; shareholders should read it as a related-party scheme and check the valuation basis. Separately, a Reg 29(2) takeover-code disclosure was filed on 25 September 2026 and the status of Fairfax's FIH Mauritius holding could not be confirmed.

The risks are the mirror image of the leverage. Q1 FY27 already showed it: material cost up 485 basis points, margin down to 22.9% below the 24–26% band, even as revenue rose 19% and PAT 36% (helped by lower finance cost, down 29% to ₹16.74 crore). Feedstock supply is concentrated in pulp mills whose output depends on paper demand. Chinese aroma-chemical pricing caps realisations. The balance sheet works hard: 235 days of inventory and ₹887 crore of net debt (1.33× EBITDA), with a ₹1,200 crore expansion ahead. Management's long-term margin guide of above 20% is a reminder that 25% is not guaranteed.

Why the multiples differ

Three of the Indian specialists, Clean Science, Privi and Sudeep, trade at 1.5–3 times the EV/EBITDA of most global fragrance and additive majors. Four factors explain the premium. Their margins are higher. Their growth runway is longer, because they start from small shares of large markets. Their free float is small: Sudeep's promoters hold 76% and its listing is recent. And Indian equity investors have few listed "specialty-quality" chemical names to choose from, so domestic money concentrates on them. The premium is not a mispricing in itself, but it means these stocks need sustained growth to justify current prices: at 54× EV/EBITDA, Sudeep would need many years of 20%-plus EBITDA growth before its multiple looks like Novonesis's.

Advanced Enzymes is the opposite case. Its margin (30.8%) is close to Sudeep's and not far from Novonesis's, and it has net cash, yet it trades at 10.7× EV/EBITDA, half Clean Science's multiple and a fifth of Sudeep's. The market is pricing its growth stall (8% five-year sales growth, Q1 FY27 +2%) and its exposure to a few US supplement customers. Management's ₹69.7 crore open-market buyback signals that it, too, thinks the stock is cheap. A recovery in human healthcare would be the trigger for a re-rating; a further decline would confirm the discount.

Sudarshan is priced on recovery, not on current earnings. At about 2.6 times book value and about 12× EV on FY27-guided EBITDA, it trades in line with or below global pigment and additive peers, and below the Indian specialists. Arihant Capital's target of ₹1,474 is based on 27× its FY28 EPS estimate of ₹54.6. The re-rating case requires the acquired group to reach its €90–100 million EBITDA aspiration; if it does, Sudarshan's EBITDA would roughly double from FY27 guidance, and the current valuation would look low.

In plain terms

Indian specialty chemical makers earn margins as good as or better than the world's best, and investors pay up for them, often more than they pay for Givaudan or Novonesis. The exceptions are Advanced Enzymes, which is cheap because it stopped growing, and Sudarshan, which is cheap on next year's profit because it is still fixing what it bought. The expensive ones need to keep growing fast for a long time to earn their prices.

One more global comparison matters: structure. Novonesis, Givaudan and DIC are diversified across products, customers and regions, with large R&D budgets (Novonesis spends about 10% of sales). The Indian five are each concentrated in one niche and, mostly, one or two plants. That concentration lifts margins (no sprawling overhead) but raises risk: a single plant outage, customer loss or regulatory change can move a year's numbers, as the 2026 propylene outage at Clean Science and the loss of a 4-MAP customer showed. Investors in the Indian names are paying a premium multiple for a business that carries more single-point risk than its global comparators.

Technology and disruption: copied processes, bio-routes and batteries

The main technology risks are not that the products become obsolete, but that someone finds a cheaper or cleaner way to make them, or a regulator decides they should not be made at all.

Performance chemicals are not usually displaced by new inventions; customers keep needing inhibitors, scents, excipients, colours and enzymes. The disruption risk lies in how the products are made and in which specific molecules regulators allow. Five threads matter for these companies.

Process copying

Clean Science's advantage is a catalytic, solvent-light, effluent-light process that gives it the lowest cost in its molecules. Processes are protected by know-how and trade secrets, not by enforceable patents in most markets. A well-funded Chinese producer, possibly with state support for capex, can reverse-engineer or independently develop a comparable route. This is the core bear case for Clean Science, and the reason its standalone margin (about 43%) is the single most important number to track: if it starts falling while volumes hold, competitors are closing the cost gap.

Bio-based and green routes

Fragrance houses are investing in biotechnology to make scent molecules by fermentation or from renewable feedstocks: dsm-firmenich and Givaudan both run biotech programmes. Over time, fermentation-derived molecules could displace some terpene chemistry. Privi is partly on the right side of this trend, because its main feedstock is itself a renewable by-product of paper making, and it is launching renewable cyclopentanone and furfural. But a fragrance house that develops an in-house bio-route for a molecule Privi supplies would stop buying it. Green chemistry is also a selling point: customers' sustainability audits increasingly favour low-effluent, renewable-feedstock suppliers, which helps Clean Science and Privi.

Regulatory substitution

Regulators can remove a molecule from use. BHA is under periodic safety review as a food additive. Europe's expanded fragrance-allergen labelling (moving from about 26 to about 80 named allergens, with transition to 2026–28) pushes brands to re-formulate. Azo pigments face limits on trace impurities such as PCBs. PFAS-style restrictions in Europe show how quickly a class of chemistry can be curtailed. Each of these is a threat to an incumbent molecule and an opportunity for a supplier with a ready substitute and the regulatory dossiers to prove it.

Enzyme engineering and precision fermentation

Enzymes are being redesigned with computational and AI-assisted protein engineering, and precision fermentation is broadening what can be produced biologically. The global leader Novonesis spends about 10% of sales on R&D. For Advanced Enzymes, whose R&D spending was not established for this report, the risk is that competitors produce more active or more stable enzymes at lower cost, eroding its position in human nutrition. Its strain library and application know-how remain assets, but the R&D bar is rising.

Battery chemistry and print decline

Sudeep's battery-grade iron phosphate bets on LFP chemistry continuing to gain share in batteries. A shift toward sodium-ion or other chemistries, or continued Chinese price declines in iron phosphate, would hurt the economics of a 100,000–200,000-tonne investment. Separately, Sudarshan faces a structural decline in volume printing inks (offset by growth in packaging, agricultural and digital inks) and the spread of digital colour matching, which can make shade approval faster and therefore switching easier.

Bull vs bear

The case for and against the sub-sector as a whole, and the questions that will decide between them.

The bull and bear cases are not mirror images; they put weight on different numbers. The bull case rests on margins and lock-in, the bear case on inventory, cash, concentration and valuation. Both are argued below as the evidence supports them.

The bull case

  • Real economic moats: approval lock-in and process cost leadership support 25–37% EBITDA margins for the four specialists (29.7% combined), at or above global leaders.
  • Long runways: under 1% of excipients, about 1% of enzymes, 4–5% of aroma chemicals; Privi targets ₹5,000 crore of revenue by FY29–30.
  • New legs with momentum: Clean Science's HALS at 22% of sales and rising in price; PRIGIV profitable; Sudeep's specialty grades and greenfield plant.
  • A cheap, fixable giant: Sudarshan bought Heubach at ~0.2× sales; each margin point is worth ~₹79 crore of EBITDA, and Q1 FY27 was ahead of plan.
  • China+1 and regulation: customers want non-Chinese suppliers with documentation; EU allergen rules and Chinese battery export controls create openings.
  • Strong balance sheets: combined net debt about ₹613 crore on ₹2,063 crore of EBITDA; Clean Science and Advanced Enzymes net cash.

The bear case

  • Inventory eats the returns: 157–356 days of stock keep ROCE in the low-to-mid twenties; Sudeep converted only 31% of EBITDA to cash in FY26.
  • Cores have stalled: Clean Science's three-year sales growth is 1%, Advanced Enzymes' five-year 8%; new legs dilute margins.
  • China caps prices: lock-in protects only against small price gaps, as the FY24 destock and Clean's 4-MAP loss showed.
  • Concentrated, undisclosed customers: four fragrance houses, a dozen acrylic majors, a few US supplement brands; no company discloses top-customer share.
  • Valuations: Sudeep at 65× earnings and 54× EV/EBITDA; Clean Science and Privi at 37–38× earnings despite slowing or volatile profits.
  • Execution and governance: Heubach's restructuring charge is unquantified; Privi's related-party merger terms are unknown; Sudeep's battery venture sits outside its moat.

Three questions will decide which case wins over the next two years. First, do the new legs converge on core margins? If HALS, specialty grades and industrial enzymes climb toward their cores' margins as they scale, consolidated margins recover and the growth premium is justified. If they stay diluted, the sub-sector becomes a set of mature niches with lower-margin add-ons. Second, does working capital improve? A sub-sector whose specialty is paid for in inventory can create a lot of value simply by needing less of it; Sudarshan's acquired business has started, and Sudeep needs to follow. Third, does the Heubach turnaround keep its pace? It is the single largest swing factor in the group's aggregate profit.

In plain terms

Optimists see five companies with genuine advantages, years of growth ahead and one cheap turnaround. Pessimists see businesses that tie up too much cash in stock, depend on a handful of powerful customers, face a price ceiling set in China and, in three cases, cost a lot. Both views are grounded in the numbers. The tie-breakers are whether the new product lines become as profitable as the old ones, whether inventories come down, and whether Sudarshan keeps fixing Heubach.

For individual companies the balance differs. The bull case is strongest where the evidence is already in the numbers: Privi's margin and cash conversion, and Sudarshan's Q1 FY27 progress. The bear case is strongest where the valuation assumes things not yet in the numbers: Sudeep's battery venture and margin recovery at 65× earnings. Clean Science sits between, with a strong core, a promising new leg and a multiple that assumes the new leg works. Advanced Enzymes is the contrarian case: the bear story is fully priced at 10.7× EV/EBITDA, so any recovery in human healthcare would be a surprise to the upside.
