00

The one-page view: a metal converter priced like a consumer brand

Two-thirds of the price of a cable is copper or aluminium that the maker buys and passes on. The business is the thin spread on top of it, and the market pays 43 times earnings for that spread.

An Indian cable company buys copper cathode or aluminium rod, draws it into fine wire, wraps it in plastic insulation, sometimes armours it with steel, tests it and sells it by the coil or the drum. Between 62% and 70% of what the customer pays is the metal itself, and the maker passes that cost on, sometimes within a week. What the company keeps is a spread for conversion, brand, distribution and engineering that works out to roughly ₹9–14 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales. On the face of it this is a commodity-processing business. Yet the six listed makers covered here, Polycab India, APAR Industries, KEI Industries, R R Kabel, Finolex Cables and Diamond Power Infrastructure, are worth a combined ₹2.98 lakh crore on 19 October 2026, which is 42.7 times their trailing earnings and about 33 times their FY26 operating profit after netting cash.

Three things justify the premium, and each is under strain in 2026. The first is a brand-and-distribution moat in house wire: the electrician, not the homeowner, picks the wire, and he picks what he trusts and what is in stock at the counter tomorrow morning. Polycab reaches more than two lakh retail outlets and lifted its share of the organised market from 26–27% in FY25 to 30–31% in FY26. The second is a qualification barrier at the top of the voltage ladder: extra-high-voltage (EHV) cable and premium overhead conductors need type tests, utility approvals and specialised lines that take years to obtain. The third is the broadest alignment of end-demand in two decades: transmission, distribution, renewables, data centres, housing, railways and exports are all growing at once.

The strain comes from four directions. Copper hit a record of $14,850 a tonne on 17 August 2026, so revenue growth looks spectacular while volumes grow at single or low-double digits. Two of India's largest industrial groups have physically arrived: UltraTech's "Ultravolt" plant at Jhagadia began commercial production on 1 September 2026, and Adani's Praneetha Ecocables joint venture began dispatching from Sanand in the same month. Industry capacity will grow 20–22% by the end of FY27, nearly half of it from the newcomers, against utilisation of about 75% in FY26. And a copper spike eats cash at exactly the moment it inflates sales, which separates the makers who fund metal cheaply from those who do not.

In plain terms

A cable maker's sales figure mostly tells you what copper did. When copper rises by half, sales jump by a third without a single extra metre being sold. The questions that matter are how many more metres or tonnes went out of the door, how many rupees the company kept on each kilogram of metal, and whether it had to borrow to buy the dearer copper. This report is organised around those three questions.

The five forces that define this sub-sector

  • Metal is the revenue line; the spread is the business. Copper and aluminium are 62–70% of sales. In 2026 reported growth is mostly a copper story, margin percentages fall mechanically when metal rises even if the rupee spread holds, and inventory gains and losses blur the true run-rate (deep dive, Part 09).
  • Demand is aligned across seven end-markets at once. Grid transmission (₹9.15 lakh crore planned to 2032), distribution reform, renewable evacuation, data centres, housing, railways and defence, and exports. Crisil counts a ₹10–12 lakh crore investment pipeline behind cable demand (Part 03).
  • A capacity wave and two deep-pocketed entrants meet an uneven moat. The moat is strong in branded house wire and at EHV, weak in commodity low-tension aluminium cable and plain conductors. The 2025 de-rating was a cut in multiples, not in earnings (deep dive, Part 10).
  • Mix migration is the margin engine. Up the voltage ladder, into the dealer channel, into exports and into fans, lights and switches that finally make money (deep dives, Parts 11 and 12).
  • Working capital and metal financing separate quality. Operating cash flow ranged from 95% of EBITDA at Polycab to 8% at Finolex in FY26, and letter-of-credit funding of metal hides trade debt that net-cash figures leave out (Part 13).

The rest of the report follows that order. Parts 01 to 08 explain the products, the money flows, the size of the market, demand, costs, industry structure, business models, rules and history. Parts 09 to 13 are the deep dives on copper, the entrants, the voltage ladder, FMEG and cash. Part 14 is the KPI masterclass: every number an analyst uses to read a cable company, with worked examples. Parts 15 to 18 compare the six companies and their valuations, and the closing parts cover the global lens, technology, the bull and bear cases and what to watch. Readers who want the grid context should pair this report with the sub-sector reports on heavy electrical equipment and electrical equipment, which cover the transformers and switchgear that the same transmission build is buying.

01

From cathode to wall socket: how a cable earns its spread

The product ladder runs from a 1.5 sq mm house wire chosen by an electrician to a 400 kV cable that takes a utility years to approve. Value sits at the two ends.

Every product in this industry is a conductor (copper or aluminium) inside an insulating layer, and the differences lie in how much current and voltage it carries, how hostile the environment is and who decides the purchase. At the bottom of the ladder sits the building or house wire: single-core copper, 0.75 to 6 square millimetres, insulated with PVC or a flame-retardant compound, sold in 90-metre coils through hardware and electrical shops. It is a retail product bought on the electrician's recommendation, and wires are roughly 30% of the ₹1.1 lakh crore market. Next come flexible and special cables: solar DC string cable, EV-charging leads, submersible pump cable for farm borewells, fire-survival cable for hospitals and metros, instrumentation, control and LAN cable.

The volume workhorse is the low-tension (LT) power cable, rated up to 1.1 kV, multi-core, insulated with cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) or PVC and usually armoured with galvanised steel wire or strip. Copper versions feed buildings and factories; aluminium versions dominate bulk distribution because aluminium carries the same current at about a quarter of the metal cost. Above that are high-tension (HT) or medium-voltage cables from 3.3 to 33 kV for utilities, industry and the collection networks of solar and wind farms, and then extra-high-voltage (EHV) cables from 66 kV to 400 kV and beyond, which need vertical or catenary continuous-vulcanisation lines, clean rooms and long type tests. Only a handful of Indian makers qualify: KEI up to 400 kV, Diamond Power claims up to 400 kV, and Polycab's own EHV plant is now due in the first quarter of FY28.

Alongside insulated cable sit bare overhead conductors (AAAC, ACSR and high-temperature low-sag, or HTLS, conductors, plus optical-ground wire) that hang from transmission towers. APAR is the world's largest maker of aluminium conductors; Diamond Power, Sterlite Electric and KEC's captive plant are the other domestic names. Communication cables (optical fibre, telephone and LAN) are a smaller pool where Finolex, APAR and now R R Kabel compete. Finally, several makers bolt on fast-moving electrical goods (FMEG) such as fans, lights, switches and switchgear, and a few run EPC (turnkey electrification) or adjacent businesses such as APAR's transformer and specialty oils.

Where is value captured? Three places, and one place it is not. First, brand plus channel in house wire: the leaders earn 13–15% segment EBIT here with light working capital, because the electrician will not risk a callback or a fire for a few rupees saved on a coil, and because the dealer wants the brand that turns over fastest. Polycab's wires and cables segment earned 13.6% EBIT in FY26 and 14.2% in Q2 FY27. Second, qualification at HT, EHV and premium conductors: KEI describes EHV as its highest-margin product, and APAR's conductor EBITDA per tonne rose 22% year on year in Q1 FY27 as premium conductors crossed half of conductor revenue. Third, scale in procurement: the ability to hedge metal, open cheap letters of credit and, for some, cast rod in-house. Value is not captured in the metal itself, which is passed through, nor in commodity aluminium LT cable and plain AAAC or ACSR conductor, where tenders are won on price.

In plain terms

The money is made at the two ends of the ladder. At the bottom, a house wire is a brand business: the shop, the electrician and the speed of delivery decide the sale, and the leaders charge a premium for trust. At the top, an EHV cable or a high-temperature conductor is an engineering business: a utility will not buy it until the product has passed years of tests. The middle of the ladder, ordinary power cable and plain conductor sold by tender, is where competition is fiercest and margins thinnest.

One structural point runs through everything that follows: the same plant can make several rungs, and the same company can sell through several routes. Polycab sells house wire through dealers, power cable through both dealers and institutions, and EHV will go mostly to utilities. KEI started as an institutional supplier and has spent five years pushing its dealer share up, from about 54% of sales in FY26 to 59% in Q1 FY27. APAR is mostly business-to-business and export. Diamond Power is entirely business-to-business. This is why a single industry margin is meaningless: a company's margin is the weighted average of its position on the ladder and its route to market.

02

How big: three market sizes and a growth rate that is mostly copper

The house figure is about ₹1.1 lakh crore. Crisil's numbers imply ₹1.5 lakh crore. Neither tells you how many kilometres were sold.

There is no single authoritative size for the Indian wires and cables market, and the published figures differ by almost half. This report uses about ₹1.1 lakh crore for FY26 as its house number: it is the figure Polycab's management gave in October 2026 and Motilal Oswal repeats, and it squares with Polycab's own share data (30–31% of the organised market and 22–23% of the overall market imply a total near ₹1.1 lakh crore and an organised pool near ₹82,000 crore). Crisil Ratings' June 2026 figures point higher: it puts organised revenue at about ₹1 lakh crore and calls that "nearly two-thirds" of the industry, which implies a total of roughly ₹1.5 lakh crore. That is our upper bound. Older figures in our own primers (₹80,000 crore for FY25 in the Polycab primer, "₹50,000 crore-plus" in the R R Kabel primer) are on earlier or narrower bases and are not used.

Why do the numbers diverge? Mostly because "organised" has no fixed definition. Crisil's sample is the set of rated companies it tracks; Polycab and Motilal Oswal use all branded players; other write-ups put the organised share as high as 78–80%. The unorganised segment, small regional makers selling unbranded or locally branded wire, is put at 30–35% of the market by Polycab. Whatever the base, the direction is clear: organised players are gaining share through GST (since July 2017), Bureau of Indian Standards certification and quality-control orders, rising safety awareness, and the leaders' ability to fund metal and inventory through a price spike that squeezes small firms.

Growth: strong, but read the basis

The six listed members grew revenue from ₹54,248 crore in FY24 to ₹64,777 crore in FY25 (+19.4%) and ₹81,487 crore in FY26 (+25.8%), and their trailing twelve months to June 2026 (September for Polycab) sum to about ₹91,400 crore. Q1 FY27 revenue rose 37.4% year on year and Polycab's Q2 FY27 revenue rose 52.8%. These are value figures, inflated by metal. Crisil described FY26 industry growth as above 20% and volume-led, and forecasts FY27 revenue growth of 28–30% on volume of only about 10%, because high copper prices are delaying some industrial projects. IEEMA's production index for cables, which tracks a narrower set of members and physical output, grew only 4.6%, 7.3% and 5.9% in the first three quarters of FY26. We cannot reconcile Crisil's "volume-led" description with IEEMA's index and flag the gap rather than pick a side.

Exports: a net exporter, led by the US

India is a net exporter of insulated wire and cable. UN Comtrade data for HS code 8544 show exports rising from $1.13 billion in 2019 to $2.01 billion in 2023, up 17% in 2023 alone, against imports of $1.30 billion, 38% of them from China. The United States took 22% of 2023 exports, the UAE 9.5%, the UK 8.3% and Australia 4.7%. Exports matter more to some members than others: R R Kabel earned 26.2% of FY26 revenue abroad and APAR about 30%, while Polycab's international business was 5.4% (target above 10% by FY30), KEI's an estimated 10–12% and Finolex's and Diamond Power's small. No FY26 customs figure for cable exports was available, so the series stops at 2023.

In plain terms

Nobody measures this market precisely. A fair answer is "₹1.1 to ₹1.5 lakh crore, about a third still unbranded, growing 10% a year in physical terms and much faster in rupees while copper is expensive". The six listed companies sell about ₹81,500 crore a year across all their businesses, so they are large relative to the market but far from owning it.

In global terms, the world wire and cable market is commonly put at $200–220 billion (an industry estimate we have not verified), which would make India about 5–7% of it. That share is small relative to India's population and large relative to its current grid spend, and it is the gap the transmission plan is meant to close. The size of the market also frames the entrant debate in Part 10: UltraTech's plant, at full use, would add roughly ₹2,400–3,000 crore of revenue by our estimate, which is 2–3% of the whole market but about a tenth of the house-wire pool where incumbents earn their best margins.

03

Seven demand engines running at once

Transmission, distribution, renewables, data centres, housing, railways and exports are all pulling in the same direction for the first time in two decades.

Cable demand is derived demand: nobody buys cable for itself, only as part of a grid line, a building, a factory, a solar park or a server hall. What makes the current cycle unusual is that almost every one of those end-markets is expanding at once. In earlier cycles, a housing boom might coincide with weak industrial capex, or a transmission push with a stalled real-estate market. In 2026 the seven engines below are all running, and Crisil counts an investment pipeline of ₹10–12 lakh crore behind them.

1. Power transmission and distribution

The National Electricity Plan for transmission (2023–32) envisages more than 1.91 lakh circuit-kilometres of new lines, 1,270 GVA of transformation capacity, 33 GW of HVDC links and over ₹9.15 lakh crore of investment. The Central Electricity Authority's FY27–36 plan adds roughly another ₹7.9 lakh crore, and ICRA expects ₹5–6 lakh crore of transmission capex between FY27 and FY32. The FY27 target for new transformation capacity is 1,58,339 MVA, 40% above what was achieved in FY26, and Power Grid has guided to ₹37,000 crore of capex this year. On the distribution side, the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS, ₹2.83–3.03 lakh crore) is paying for feeder separation, smart meters and loss reduction. This engine buys conductors (APAR, Diamond Power), HT and EHV cables (KEI, and Polycab from FY28) and control cables. The sister reports on heavy electrical equipment and power generation cover the transformers and generation capacity at the other end of these wires.

2. Renewables evacuation and solar

India's target of 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 is unusually cable-intensive. A solar park needs DC string cable from every panel row, LT and HT collection cable to inverters and pooling substations, and EHV or overhead lines to evacuate power to the grid. Polycab's solar business, including traded inverters, roughly doubled in FY26; Finolex's electrical-cable volumes were helped by solar and farm-pump demand. Because renewable sites are remote, evacuation lines are a large part of the transmission plan above.

3. Data centres

Data centres are the newest and fastest engine. Polycab's management puts India's installed data-centre capacity at 1.6–1.7 GW, heading to 8–10 GW and eventually 15 GW; Wood Mackenzie forecasts 2.2 GW in 2025 rising to 12 GW by 2030. A data hall needs dense power cabling, fire-survival and low-smoke cables, and high-voltage feeders from the grid. Diamond Power targets about 20% of revenue from data centres (₹750 crore in FY27 and ₹1,500 crore in FY28); Finolex's communication cables rose 62% in Q1 FY27 on data-centre and AI fibre demand; and APAR holds approvals from contractors building US data centres for Meta, Microsoft and Google.

4. Real estate and housing

House wire, switches, fans and lights follow residential and commercial construction, usually 12–24 months after a project launches, because wiring goes in during the finishing stage. This is the retail engine that drives the channel business of Polycab, R R Kabel and Finolex. In Polycab's Q2 FY27, wires grew at an early double-digit volume rate and channel sales outpaced institutional ones. The realty report covers the housing cycle that feeds this demand.

5. Railways, metros, defence and airports

Signalling, traction and station cabling for railways and metros, and specialty cables for defence platforms, are specification-heavy markets with long approval cycles. APAR's domestic cable sales rose 59.9% in Q1 FY27, led by railways and defence orders.

6. Industrial capex, smart meters, EV charging and BharatNet

Factories need LT and control cable; smart-meter roll-outs need service cable; EV charging needs flexible high-current leads; and the BharatNet rural broadband programme needs optical fibre and the EPC work to lay it. Polycab's EPC order book of ₹9,000–10,000 crore is mostly BharatNet and RDSS work. Crisil's warning for FY27 is that copper at record prices may delay some industrial capex, which is one reason it sees volume growth of only about 10%.

7. Exports

The US is the largest single export market, driven by grid reconductoring, utility upgrades and data centres. It is also the most contested. Since 24 July 2026, Indian goods face a 10% Section 301 duty on top of the normal (most-favoured-nation) tariff, a measure now under legal challenge, after the US Supreme Court struck down the earlier IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026. Copper- and aluminium-intensive products also face the separate 50% Section 232 metals tariff, so APAR's US conductor and cable exports carry far more than 10%; whether finished insulated cable falls fully within Section 232's scope has not been confirmed. Even so, Polycab's exports rose 17% year on year and 85% quarter on quarter in Q2 FY27, and APAR's US cable sales grew 2.5% while its overall cable exports fell 13.7%.

Where ₹100 goes: three-quarters of the bill is bought in

A cable maker's cost sheet is dominated by one line it does not control. Everything a manager can influence fits inside about ₹25.

Start with the bill of materials. In a copper cable, the copper alone is 60–70% of cost; across the sector, Crisil puts copper and aluminium together at about 70% of sales. Add the insulating and sheathing compounds (PVC, XLPE, low-smoke zero-halogen compounds), the galvanised steel wire or strip used for armouring, and the wooden or steel drums the cable ships on, and raw materials reach roughly three-quarters of revenue. Polycab's gross margin of 23.6% in Q2 FY27 means raw materials took ₹76.4 of every ₹100 of its sales in that quarter, up from about ₹73 a year earlier because copper was dearer. What remains pays for power and conversion, people, freight, dealer schemes, advertising and overheads, and leaves an EBITDA margin of 8–14% depending on the company.

How the pass-through actually works

There are three pricing regimes, and a company's mix among them determines how cleanly it passes on a metal move. In the retail channel, makers revise list prices, often weekly or fortnightly during a spike; Polycab raised prices by about 18–19% in Q4 FY26 and Finolex took a hike in May 2026. In institutional contracts with utilities and public-sector buyers, price-variation (PV) clauses adjust the invoice to published indices such as IEEMA's, with a lag. In firm-price contracts, typical of private EPC and some export orders, the maker absorbs the move unless it hedged at the time of the order. Polycab says it hedges copper and aluminium against confirmed orders. The worked example in Part 14 shows what each regime does to margin.

In plain terms

Roughly ₹75 of every ₹100 a cable maker earns goes straight back out to buy copper, aluminium, plastic and steel. The company's skill lies in the remaining ₹25: running the plant efficiently, getting the cable to the shop or the site, persuading electricians and engineers to ask for its brand, and passing on metal price changes quickly enough that it never sells cable for less than the metal inside it cost.

Capacity and utilisation: the supply side is growing fast

Crisil estimates the industry ran at about 75% utilisation in FY26 and that capacity will rise 20–22% by the end of FY27, nearly half of it from new entrants. Incumbents are building too. Polycab is twice the size of the next player and still gaining share. Behind it sit a dozen branded names, a shrinking unorganised tail and India's largest cement and metals groups.

Domestic rules: a floor under quality

The Bureau of Indian Standards framework makes the ISI mark compulsory for house wire, and quality-control orders on copper and aluminium products limit the import of sub-standard metal. Enforcement has historically been uneven, but every tightening raises costs for the small makers who compete on price by using less copper than the stated cross-section or cheaper insulation, and it helps the organised players who already comply. GST had a similar effect from 2017: once every sale had to carry an invoice for the buyer to claim input credit, the unorganised trade's cash-pricing advantage shrank. There is no production-linked incentive scheme for cables, though the solar-module and battery schemes add indirect demand.

Public procurement rules matter for the B2B makers. Utilities keep approved-vendor lists and demand type tests and supply references before a new maker can bid for HT or EHV cable or for transmission conductors. This is the qualification barrier that protects KEI at 400 kV and APAR in premium conductors, and it is the reason Polycab's EHV plant will take several quarters after commissioning to generate meaningful revenue. Price-variation clauses tied to published metal indices make institutional cable a spread business rather than a metal bet, as long as the clause exists; private EPC contracts and some exports are firm-price, which is where metal spikes do damage.

US trade: the wedge on exports

US trade policy has been the most volatile rule in the industry's life over the past eighteen months. A 25% reciprocal tariff in August 2025 prompted R R Kabel to redirect exports to other markets. The US Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026. Since 24 July 2026, Indian goods have faced a 10% Section 301 duty on top of the normal most-favoured-nation rate, compared with 12.5% for China and Vietnam, and that measure is itself under legal challenge. Separately, the Section 232 metals tariff has applied 50% to copper semis and copper-intensive derivatives since 1 August 2025, with aluminium derivatives treated similarly. According to the Global Trade Research Initiative, some equipment was cut to 15% from June 2026 and a 10% rate applies where at least 85% of the metal is US-origin, but core metal articles remain at 50%. India sought WTO consultations in September 2025; the US rejected India's safeguard framing in August 2026.

08

Six decades, five shocks and a sixth under way

Family founders, a slow march from unbranded to branded, a bankruptcy, two copper spikes and an entrant scare. Each shock left the leaders stronger.

Every one of the six listed makers was founded by a family, and most are still run by one. APAR began in 1958 as Power Cables Pvt Ltd under Dharmsinh Desai and added transformer oil a decade later; the Chhabria brothers began trading wires in Pune in 1958 and incorporated Finolex Cables in 1967, building it into India's telephone-cable leader in the 1980s; KEI started in 1968 as Krishna Electrical Industries; Diamond Power was incorporated in 1970; Polycab's roots go back to Thakurdas Jaisinghani's Sind Electric Stores in Mumbai in 1964, with the first factory at Halol in 1983; and R R Kabel, part of the Kabra family's Ram Ratna group, was incorporated in 1995.

The 2000s were a decade of specialisation. APAR took the Eni lubricants licence and bought Uniflex Cables in 2008; Polycab formed a short-lived joint venture with Nexans and entered EPC in 2008–09; KEI moved into EHV cable in 2010. The 2010s brought formalisation. Polycab launched FMEG in 2014; GST arrived in July 2017 and began to squeeze the cash-based unorganised trade; Polycab listed in April 2019 in a ₹1,346 crore IPO that was subscribed more than 50 times.

The five shocks

Shock one, distress at the margin (2016–2022). Diamond Power was admitted to insolvency in August 2018; a plan from GSEC and the Rakesh Shah family was approved in June 2022 and the plant restarted in December 2022. Finolex spent 2016–2024 in a family dispute over control, settled in August 2024 according to our primer. Both showed how a cable maker fails: working capital, governance and debt, not demand.

Shock two, the first copper spike (2020–21). Copper rose from ₹4.51 lakh a tonne in January 2020 to ₹6.85 lakh in March 2021, a 52% jump, according to an IEEMA price letter. It was the first large test of the pass-through mechanism for the modern listed makers, and the branded leaders passed it, raising prices and holding spreads while small makers struggled to fund inventory.

Shock three, governance (December 2023). Income-tax officials searched more than 50 Polycab premises over alleged unaccounted cash sales of about ₹1,000 crore; the stock fell 21% in a day. The business kept growing, but the episode remains part of the governance discount investors apply. In 2026 a second, smaller item was added: the NCLT admitted an insolvency petition filed by an operational creditor, Asier Metals, over a ₹2.79 crore commercial dispute, and the NCLAT stayed that order in its entirety and reserved judgment on 15 October 2026. It is financially immaterial to a company with ₹4,520 crore of net cash, but it is a headline risk until the appeal is decided.

Shock four, the entrant scare (February–March 2025). UltraTech announced a ₹1,800 crore entry into wires and cables in late February 2025 and Adani's Kutch Copper formed its 50:50 joint venture with Praneetha in March. Listed incumbents fell by up to 15–20% in a day. Part 10 analyses what happened next: earnings kept rising, but multiples did not recover.

Shock five, trade (2025–26). The US imposed the Section 232 copper tariff in August 2025 and a 25% reciprocal tariff on India the same month; the latter was struck down in February 2026 and replaced by a 10% Section 301 duty from July 2026.

The sixth, under way: copper and West Asia (2026). Crude went above $100 a barrel in March–April, PVC spiked, Finolex's rod plant ran out of fuel, copper set a record in August, and the entrants switched on their plants in September. Polycab's Q2 FY27 revenue rose 53%. Whether this shock ends like the 2021 one, with the leaders stronger, is the central question of the next four quarters.

Copper 2026: the pass-through, the value–volume gap and the inventory lottery Deep dive

A 50% rise in the rupee price of copper turned single-digit volume growth into 40–55% revenue growth, cut margin percentages while lifting rupee profits, and handed some makers windfalls they will not repeat.

Copper is the single most important number for reading a cable company in 2026, and it is the number most often misread. It does three separate things to the accounts at once. It inflates revenue, because metal is two-thirds of the selling price. It depresses the margin percentage, even when the rupee spread per kilogram is unchanged, because the same profit is now divided by a larger revenue. And it creates inventory gains or losses, because metal bought at one price is sold at another. A reader who does not separate these three effects will misjudge growth, misjudge profitability and misjudge momentum.

The move

LME copper averaged roughly $9,800 a tonne in July–September 2025 (an indicative level), then climbed through 2026 to average about $13,150 up to July, set an all-time high of $14,850 on 17 August and stood near $14,751 on 14 October. At a constant ₹95.5 to the dollar, that is a rise from about ₹9.4 lakh a tonne to about ₹14.1 lakh, or roughly 50%; part of the true rupee move came from rupee weakness, which a constant-rate conversion hides. Aluminium rose too, to about $3,222 a tonne by late August. Crisil estimates that copper and aluminium together rose 22–27% over the past fiscal year and that cable realisations rose 18–20%.

Effect one: value growth is not volume growth

Polycab's Q2 FY27 is the cleanest example. Wires and cables revenue rose 47% year on year, but volume grew only at a "mid-to-high single digit" rate: wires at an early double-digit rate and cables at a mid-single-digit rate. Taking 7.5% as the volume midpoint, the rest must be price. If metal is about 65% of the selling price and copper rose about 47% year on year, metal alone adds roughly 30% to the price of the same cable (0.65 × 47%). Volume of 1.075 times metal of 1.30 gives about 1.40, or 40% growth; the remaining 5% or so of price, compounded, comes from PVC and compound costs passed on, list-price increases beyond metal, and mix (more wires, more channel sales, more exports). In plain terms

When a cable company says sales rose 47%, most of that is copper. Polycab sold perhaps 7–8% more cable in July–September 2026 than a year earlier; the rest of the increase came from copper being half as expensive again. That is not a criticism: passing on the metal cost in full is exactly what a strong brand should do. But it means a 47% sales jump this year could become a flat or falling sales figure next year if copper drops, with no loss of business at all.

Effect two: margin percentages fall while rupee profits rise

If a maker passes on copper exactly, rupee for rupee, its spread per kilometre is unchanged but its revenue per kilometre is higher, so its margin percentage falls. Polycab's gross margin fell 3.5 percentage points year on year to 23.6% in Q2 FY27, and its wires and cables EBIT margin slipped 90 basis points to 14.2%, yet absolute wires and cables EBIT rose 38% to about ₹1,190 crore. Part 14's worked example shows the mechanics on a single kilometre of cable: a 10% copper rise, fully passed through, cuts the EBITDA margin from 13.0% to 12.1% with no change in rupee profit. A 50–90 basis-point dip in a copper spike is therefore not, by itself, evidence that a maker has lost pricing power.

Effect three: the inventory lottery

A cable maker holds two to three months of raw material and finished goods. When metal rises, stock bought earlier at a lower price is sold at the new, higher list price, and the difference flows into gross margin as a one-off gain. When metal falls, the reverse happens. Makers that hedge their metal against confirmed orders (Polycab says it does) see less of this; makers that hold unhedged stock see more. The clearest 2026 example is not copper at all but base oil: APAR's oils business earned EBITDA of ₹25,482 a kilolitre in Q1 FY27 against ₹7,004 a year earlier, on a base-oil price spike, and booked a ~₹93 crore inventory-valuation provision in the same quarter.

The sharp Q1 FY27 margin improvements at several makers probably contain some inventory benefit. Operating margins (screener basis) rose from 10.0% to 12.4% at KEI, from 9.7% to 12.1% at Finolex, from 6.9% to 8.9% at R R Kabel and from 8.9% to 11.5% at APAR, while Polycab's, the most hedged, fell from 14.5% to 13.8%. Ex-Polycab, the five's combined margin jumped from 8.9% to 11.2%. Some of that is genuine operating leverage and mix, but how much is inventory cannot be separated from the outside. The Q2 FY27 results of KEI (21 October) and APAR (29 October) will help: if margins hold with copper flat, the gains were mostly real.

Demand elasticity and substitution

Expensive copper does not destroy demand for cable, because there is no substitute for wiring a building, but it changes the mix and the timing. Crisil warns that high prices may delay some industrial capex, which is why it expects only about 10% volume growth in FY27. Aluminium substitution rises: aluminium carries the same current at roughly a quarter of the metal cost, and in the US the cable market is already about 66% copper and 33% aluminium by value according to APAR. At the low end, copper-clad aluminium appears in unbranded wire, which is a safety problem and an opportunity for branded makers to stress quality.

Supply-chain shocks on top

2026 added three non-copper shocks. PVC prices spiked in March during the West Asia disruption. Finolex's copper-rod plant, normally an integration advantage, was idle in Q1 FY27 because LPG and piped gas ran short, forcing it to buy rod. And export transit times lengthened by 10–12 days, according to Polycab, which ties up more working capital in goods at sea.

The reversal risk

Copper does not stay at records for ever, and the downside is the mirror image of the upside. A $2,000 a tonne fall in a quarter (about 13.5% from $14,751) would force list-price cuts, encourage dealers to run down their stock while waiting for lower prices, and turn inventory gains into losses. A rough sense of the size: the six members' trailing revenue of about ₹91,400 crore implies roughly ₹59,000 crore a year of metal; at about 90 days of holding, some ₹14,500–15,000 crore of metal sits in inventory and pipeline. If half of it were unhedged, a 13.5% fall would cost about ₹1,000 crore, roughly a tenth of the group's trailing EBITDA of ₹10,146 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE; assumes the whole metal pool moves together). That is the kind of air pocket the industry saw when copper fell in FY12 and FY20. Watch Q3 and Q4 FY27.

In plain terms

This year's cable results contain a bonus that will not recur and a risk that has not yet arrived. The bonus is the profit made on copper bought cheaply and sold dear. The risk is the opposite trade when copper eventually falls. Companies that hedge their metal, like Polycab, show smaller bonuses now and should suffer smaller losses later; those whose margins jumped most this year deserve the most scrutiny.

The entrants arrive: what capital can and cannot buy Deep dive

UltraTech and Adani announced in early 2025, knocked up to a fifth off incumbent share prices in a day, and switched on their plants in September 2026. Incumbent earnings rose 29% in between. The cost so far has been in multiples.

Few events have shaped this sub-sector's valuation as much as two announcements in February and March 2025. Late in February, UltraTech Cement, India's largest cement maker and part of the Aditya Birla group, said it would invest ₹1,800 crore over two years to enter wires and cables. In March, Adani Enterprises' wholly owned Kutch Copper formed a 50:50 joint venture, Praneetha Ecocables, with Praneetha Ventures. Polycab, KEI, Havells, R R Kabel and Finolex fell by up to 15–20% in a day. Investors asked a simple question: if two of India's best-capitalised groups want into a business earning 25–30% returns on capital, how long do those returns last?

From announcement to production

Eighteen months later the entrants are physical. UltraTech had spent ₹888 crore of its ₹1,800 crore programme by June 2026 and began commercial production at Jhagadia in Bharuch district, Gujarat, on 1 September 2026, ahead of its December target. The plant can make 10.98 lakh kilometres a year of house wire and light-duty cable. The brand, "Ultravolt", was launched on 3 September by Aryaman Vikram Birla, and the stated aim is to be among the top two players within five years. Its edge is distribution: UltraTech's cement and building-solutions dealers already reach contractors and home-builders across the country. Praneetha began commercial production at a 30-acre greenfield in Sanand in September 2026, dispatching bare copper wire first, with solar cables in October and building and flexible wires due in November 2026. (An earlier plan named a site at Virochannagar near Ahmedabad; the discrepancy is unresolved.) Its edge is metal: Kutch Copper's 0.5 million-tonne smelter, expanding to 1 million tonnes, gives it captive copper.

What the entrants are aiming at

Both newcomers are starting where incumbents earn their best margins: house wire and light-duty cable, the retail pool of roughly ₹33,000 crore. Ultravolt at full capacity would, by our estimate, generate ₹2,400–3,000 crore of revenue a year, about 2–3% of the whole market but close to a tenth of the house-wire pool. Reaching the top two would require revenue several times larger, comparable to the cable businesses of KEI or Havells today. Praneetha's capacity is undisclosed, but its starting product, bare copper wire, and its captive smelter suggest it will compete on metal cost in institutional LT cable as well as in building wire.

What capital can buy, and what it cannot

Capital can buy plants, which are not expensive relative to revenue: a modern wire line costs little compared with the metal it processes. It can buy copper, and in Adani's case it already owns the smelter. It can buy BIS certification, which is a process rather than a moat. It can buy dealer attention, at least for a while, through richer schemes and credit terms. And it can buy a launch campaign.

Capital cannot quickly buy three things. The first is electrician trust: the person who installs the wire bears the reputational risk of a fire or a callback, and he recommends what has worked for him for years. The second is availability: Polycab's two lakh-plus outlets and 9,600-plus products mean the electrician finds the right size, colour and length today, not next week. Building that depth of stock across thousands of counters takes years and a lot of working capital. The third is qualification at the top of the ladder: utility approvals for HT and EHV cable and for premium conductors take years of type tests and supply references. That is why both entrants are starting at the bottom.

In plain terms

Building a wire factory is the easy part. The hard part is persuading thousands of electricians to switch brands and making sure every electrical shop has the right coil in stock every morning. UltraTech's cement dealers give it a head start on the second; nobody has a shortcut to the first. Expect the newcomers to win share first in large institutional orders, where price decides, and last in the retail shop.

How the incumbents responded

Polycab's first response was to gain share. Its organised share rose about four percentage points in FY26, to 30–31%, in the very year the entrants announced. On its Q2 FY27 call it said it "has not changed prices" in response to Ultravolt and that "execution will be key for the new entrant". R R Kabel and KEI have pushed dealer expansion: KEI's dealer sales rose 42% in Q1 FY27 and its active dealer count reached 2,128. None of the six has started a price war, and no incumbent has reported losing a major distributor.

Three ways the entrants could still hurt

  • Institutional LT price competition. Praneetha's captive copper gives it a cost edge in large, price-led LT cable tenders, the least defended part of the market. This would show first in the institutional margins of KEI, Polycab and Diamond Power.
  • A dealer-margin bidding war in house wire. If Ultravolt buys shelf space with richer dealer schemes, incumbents may have to match, eroding the retail premium without any list-price cut. Watch Polycab's and R R Kabel's A&P and scheme costs and their wire-segment margins.
  • A capacity overhang. Crisil expects industry capacity to grow 20–22% by the end of FY27 while volume grows about 10%. If demand slows when copper falls, utilisation could drop from about 75% towards the mid-60s, and every maker would chase volume.

The defence against all three is the same: incumbents growing volume faster than the market, at stable spreads, with dealers loyal. The Q2 and Q3 FY27 results are the first full quarters with Ultravolt in the market, so the next two quarters are when this argument gets data. For the cement side of the story, see our cement report, which covers UltraTech's core business and the dealer network it is leveraging.

In plain terms

So far the newcomers have cost the incumbents nothing in profit and a great deal in share price. Investors are paying about a third less per rupee of Polycab's earnings than they did three months ago. If the incumbents keep growing through the next few quarters with the new plants running, that discount looks excessive; if dealer margins or institutional prices start to slip, it was a warning.

Up the voltage ladder: EHV, premium conductors and the export premium Deep dive

The entrants start at the bottom of the ladder. The listed incumbents are racing to the top, where qualification takes years and margins are highest.

If house wire is the part of the business most exposed to the entrants, the top of the voltage ladder is the part least exposed, and it is where the incumbents are concentrating their capex. Three markets matter: extra-high-voltage cable (66–400 kV), premium overhead conductors (high-temperature low-sag and similar designs), and export and data-centre cable. Each requires approvals that a newcomer cannot buy quickly, and each earns more per tonne of metal than standard products.

EHV cable: import substitution at 220–400 kV

EHV cable carries bulk power underground in cities, into substations and, increasingly, into data-centre campuses that need their own high-voltage feeders. It requires vertical or catenary continuous-vulcanisation (CCV) lines that extrude the insulation in a clean environment, followed by long-duration type tests. Historically, a share of India's 220–400 kV demand was met by imports and multinationals such as LS Cable, Nexans, Brugg and Südkabel; the size of that import pool is not published, but it is the prize. KEI is the established domestic qualifier, approved up to 400 kV. Its domestic EHV revenue rose 66% in FY26; Q1 FY27 EHV sales were ₹186 crore, up 48% from ₹126 crore; and its EHV order book stood at ₹625 crore in March 2026. KEI's new EHV line at Sanand is due by the end of FY27, later than the Q1 FY27 date in our primer. Polycab is building its own EHV plant, now due for commissioning in the first quarter of FY28 (earlier guidance pointed to revenue from around FY27). Diamond Power claims capability up to 400 kV, though this was not confirmed on its Q1 FY27 call, and is adding two corrugation lines for 66/132 kV cable (about ₹17 crore) and a sixth CCV line (about ₹100 crore, due December 2027).

In plain terms

Data centres and the US: the export premium

Export customers, particularly US utilities and data-centre contractors, pay for certification (UL, utility approvals) and reliability. APAR holds cable approvals from contractors building data centres for Meta, Microsoft and Google, and a 144-fibre optical-ground-wire approval from a large US utility; its US cable sales grew 2.5% in Q1 FY27 even as its total cable exports fell 13.7%. Polycab's exports rose 85% quarter on quarter in Q2 FY27 across the US, Europe, Latin America and Asia. R R Kabel earns 26.2% of revenue abroad across 49-plus countries, with the US only about 2.5% of its total revenue. Diamond Power targets ₹500 crore of export orders by the end of 2026. India's HS 8544 exports to the US were $451 million in 2023, 22% of the total.

The tariff wedge

The US is both the richest export market and the most taxed. Indian goods now carry a 10% Section 301 duty on top of the normal tariff, and copper- and aluminium-intensive goods also face the 50% Section 232 metals tariff, so the effective duty on a conductor or copper cable can be far above 10%; the precise Section 232 treatment of finished insulated cable has not been confirmed. The UAE (9.5% of exports) and the UK (8.3%) are the main diversification markets. APAR's plan to set up a UK subsidiary, approved in 2026, fits that pattern.

FMEG finally pays: the low-margin bolt-on that just turned Deep dive

Fans, lights and switches were a decade-long drag on the cable makers' returns. In 2026 Polycab's FMEG arm hit a 10% EBIT margin and R R Kabel's broke even.

Why would a cable maker sell ceiling fans? The logic is the channel. The same electrical shop that stocks house wire also sells switches, MCBs, fans and LED lights; the same electrician who chooses the wire often recommends the switch and installs the fan. A cable maker with thousands of dealers and a strong brand among electricians can, in principle, push more products through the same pipes at low incremental cost. FMEG also diversifies away from copper: a fan's price does not swing with the LME.

In practice the logic took a decade to pay. Polycab launched FMEG in 2014; R R Kabel bought the Luminous home-electricals business and a licence for the Luminous Fans & Lights brand in 2022; Finolex sells a small range of fans, lights, switches and water heaters. All three ran into the same wall: the FMEG shelf is dominated by brands that have spent decades building it, including Havells, Crompton, Bajaj, Orient and V-Guard, and competing with them requires heavy advertising, dealer schemes and a product range wide enough to matter. A sub-scale FMEG business with thin gross margins and large brand spend loses money even as it grows. Polycab's FMEG segment lost 2.3% at EBIT in FY25; R R Kabel's lost about ₹46 crore.

Cash is the tell: working capital, letters of credit and the hidden trade debt

In FY26 Polycab converted 95% of its EBITDA into operating cash; Finolex converted 8%. A copper spike consumes cash just as it inflates sales, and how a maker funds its metal decides who feels it.

A cable maker's profit and its cash flow can diverge sharply, and the divergence is widest when copper rises. Every kilogram of copper in inventory, in transit and in unpaid customer invoices costs more, so the money tied up in working capital rises in line with the metal price even if volumes are flat. A company carrying 60 days of working capital on ₹1,000 crore of revenue ties up about ₹164 crore; if copper pushes revenue up 25%, the same 60 days ties up ₹205 crore, and the extra ₹41 crore has to come from profit, suppliers or lenders. Across the six, the ₹16,710 crore rise in FY26 revenue at a revenue-weighted average of about 46 working-capital days implies roughly ₹2,100 crore of extra working capital (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic), about a quarter of the group's FY26 operating profit.

Who funds the metal?

There are three ways to fund the copper in the pipeline: your own cash, bank borrowings, or supplier credit, usually backed by a letter of credit (LC) or a bank acceptance. Supplier credit sits in "trade payables" on the balance sheet, not in "borrowings", so a company that leans on it shows a short working-capital cycle and little debt even though a bank is effectively financing its inventory. The interest on that credit, however, appears in finance costs. That is why APAR paid ₹474 crore of interest in FY26 on only ₹956 crore of reported borrowings: most of its finance cost is LC and acceptance charges on aluminium, copper and base-oil purchases, and its payables run at 138 days. Polycab's ₹243 crore of interest on ₹236 crore of debt has the same character, and its Q2 FY27 working-capital cycle fell to 11 days, which Motilal Oswal attributes to "higher payable days due to increased use of letters of credit for raw-material procurement"; management guides the cycle back to 45–50 days.

Reading a cable maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number an analyst uses to judge a wires and cables company: what it means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, where the six stand and how it can mislead.

A cable maker's income statement is unusually easy to misread, because its largest line, the metal, is a pass-through that swamps everything else. Revenue growth, margin percentages and even return ratios move with copper in ways that say little about the business. This section builds the toolkit from the ground up: first the equation that links metal, volume and spread to profit, then a worked example on a single kilometre of cable, then one sub-section per native KPI, an illustrative plant P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps and a ten-minute checklist for reading quarterly results.

The equation: tonnes, spread and the metal that drops out

The core identity is simple. Revenue equals the tonnes of conductor metal sold multiplied by the metal price per tonne plus the value-add per tonne. The metal price is set on the London Metal Exchange and passed through; it inflates revenue but, if passed through cleanly, it leaves profit unchanged. Profit therefore depends on three things management influences, volume, value-add per tonne and the fixed-cost base, plus one thing it does not, the inventory gain or loss when metal moves between purchase and sale. For conductors the same identity is written directly as tonnes multiplied by EBITDA per tonne; for APAR's oils it is kilolitres multiplied by EBITDA per kilolitre; for order-book businesses such as Diamond Power and APAR's conductors, revenue is the opening order book multiplied by the execution rate plus book-and-bill sales.

KPI 1 · Volume growth (tonnes or kilometres)

What it is: the year-on-year change in physical sales, measured in tonnes of metal, kilometres of cable or, for conductors, metric tonnes shipped. It is the true activity signal, because it is the only growth measure that copper cannot inflate. Formula: (tonnes this period ÷ tonnes same period last year) − 1. Worked example: a maker that sold 20,000 tonnes of copper in cable last year and 21,600 tonnes this year grew volume 8%, whatever its revenue did. Drivers: end-demand (grid, housing, data centres), share gains (brand, dealer reach), capacity, and tender wins for institutional makers. Copper price spikes can depress volume by delaying industrial projects or pushing buyers towards aluminium.

Benchmarks: above 15% is good, 8–15% average, below 8% weak, against Crisil's FY27 industry estimate of about 10%. How the six compare: disclosure is patchy. R R Kabel reported about 17% wires and cables volume growth in Q1 FY27; Polycab "mid-to-high single digit" for wires and cables in Q2 FY27 (wires early double digit, cables mid single digit); Finolex wire volumes rose 7% in Q1 FY27; APAR's conductor tonnes fell 6.7% in Q1 FY27; KEI guided to 17–18% for FY26 but does not publish a quarterly figure; Diamond Power discloses utilisation (about 60%) rather than volume. Definition trap: companies mix tonnes, kilometres and "volume" without a unit; a kilometre of house wire carries a fraction of the copper of a kilometre of power cable, so a shift towards wires can raise kilometres while tonnes fall.

KPI 2 · The value–volume gap

What it is: revenue growth minus volume growth, in percentage points. It measures how much of reported growth came from price (mostly metal) rather than physical sales. Formula: value growth (%) − volume growth (%), or more precisely (1 + value growth) ÷ (1 + volume growth) − 1 for the implied price change. Worked example: Polycab's Q2 FY27 wires and cables value growth of 47% against volume of about 7.5% is a gap of about 40 points, or an implied price rise of about 37% (1.47 ÷ 1.075). With metal at about 65% of price and copper up about 47%, metal explains about 30 points; the rest is compounds, list prices and mix (Part 09). Drivers: the metal price, compound prices, list-price actions beyond metal, and mix.

Benchmarks: there is no "good" gap, only an explained or unexplained one. A gap that matches the metal move is healthy pass-through; a gap larger than the metal move suggests pricing power or mix; a positive gap with falling volume, as at APAR's conductors in Q1 FY27 (value +19.9%, tonnes −6.7%), means growth is entirely price and mix, which is sustainable only if the mix shift is real (in APAR's case it is: premium conductors). Crisil's FY27 industry forecast implies a gap of 18–20 points. Definition trap: when copper falls, the gap turns negative and revenue can shrink while volumes grow; reading that as weak demand would be a mistake.

KPI 3 · Metal share of price

What it is: the cost of the copper or aluminium in a product divided by its selling price. It determines how strongly the metal price moves revenue and margin percentages. Formula: metal content (kg) × delivered metal price (₹/kg) ÷ selling price. Worked example: in our kilometre of cable, ₹13.34 lakh of copper in an ₹18.80 lakh price is 71%. For the same current rating in aluminium (4-core 95 sq mm, about 1,026 kg of aluminium a kilometre at about ₹330 a kilogram) the metal cost is about ₹3.4 lakh, roughly a quarter of the copper bill, which is why aluminium dominates utility distribution and why substitution rises when copper spikes. Drivers: the metal price itself, the product (house wire and power cable carry more metal relative to price than special cables), and the value-add the maker can charge.

Benchmarks: the sector range is 62–70% of revenue, by Crisil's estimate and the primers'; a lower metal share signals more engineering or brand content per kilogram. How the six compare: no company discloses it directly. Polycab's Q2 FY27 raw-material share of 76.4% (all materials, not just metal) is the closest published proxy. Definition trap: metal share rises automatically in a copper spike, so a maker whose metal share is rising may simply be selling the same products at higher copper prices, not losing value-add.

KPI 4 · Gross margin

What it is: revenue minus raw-material cost, as a share of revenue. For a cable maker it is the broadest measure of the spread over materials. Formula: (revenue − raw materials consumed ± change in inventory) ÷ revenue. Worked example: our kilometre has ₹14.28 lakh of materials in an ₹18.80 lakh price, a 24.0% gross margin; Polycab's reported Q2 FY27 gross margin was 23.6%, down 3.5 points year on year. Drivers: metal price (higher copper mechanically lowers the percentage), mix (wires and EHV carry higher gross margins than LT aluminium cable), pass-through speed, and inventory gains or losses, which flow straight through this line.

Benchmarks: above 25% is good, 20–25% average, below 20% weak for a branded wires and cables maker; conductor and B2B makers run lower because their products are metal-heavier and their spreads thinner. How the six compare: only Polycab regularly emphasises gross margin; others can be estimated from screener's materials line but bases differ (some include traded goods, some exclude stock changes). Definition trap: a 3.5-point fall in gross margin during a 47% copper rise is mostly arithmetic. To judge pricing power, convert gross margin into rupees per kilogram or per kilometre and compare that.

KPI 5 · The spread: ₹ per kilogram of metal (or ₹ per km)

What it is: the rupee value-add over the metal content, per kilogram of conductor metal or per kilometre of a given cable. It is the cleanest measure of pricing power because the metal price drops out. Formula: (selling price − metal cost) ÷ kg of metal, or the same per km for a standard product. Worked example: ₹5.46 lakh of value-add on 889 kg of copper is about ₹614 a kilogram; EBITDA is about ₹274 a kilogram. If copper rises 10% and the maker passes it through exactly, the spread stays at ₹614 a kilogram even though the margin percentage falls. Drivers: brand premium, product mix up the ladder, conversion efficiency (scrap, power), dealer schemes and freight.

Benchmarks: no company publishes a ₹/kg spread for cables, so benchmarks are INDICATIVE: a branded house-wire maker earns a higher spread per kilogram than an institutional LT cable maker, and EHV the highest. How the six compare: not disclosed (n/a) except APAR's conductor EBITDA per tonne (next KPI). An analyst can approximate it by dividing segment EBIT by disclosed tonnes where available. Definition trap: a spread per kilometre is only comparable for the same cable; mix shifts between thin wires and heavy cables move the average without any change in pricing.

In plain terms

The single most useful question to ask of a cable maker is: how many rupees did you make on each kilogram of copper you sold, and is that number rising? Percentages are distorted by the copper price; rupees per kilogram are not. Companies rarely publish it, so analysts estimate it from segment profit and volume disclosures, and the companies that publish volumes make that much easier.

KPI 6 · Wires & cables segment EBIT margin

What it is: operating profit of the cable segment, after allocated overheads and depreciation, divided by segment revenue. It strips out FMEG, EPC and other segments. Formula: segment EBIT ÷ segment revenue. Worked example: Polycab's wires and cables EBIT was ₹3,430 crore on ₹25,179 crore of revenue in FY26 (13.6%) and about ₹1,190 crore on ₹8,300 crore in Q2 FY27 (14.2%, down 90 basis points year on year despite a 38% rise in rupee EBIT). Drivers: mix (wires versus cables, channel versus institutional, domestic versus export, voltage), scale, inventory gains and losses, and the metal-price denominator effect.

Benchmarks: above 13% is good, 10–13% average, below 10% weak. How the six compare: the measures differ. Polycab reports W&C EBIT; KEI reports a company-wide margin that, on its own definition, includes other income (Q1 FY27: 13.0% company, 12.4% excluding other income); R R Kabel's W&C margin rose 232 basis points year on year in Q1 FY27 (about 9.9% according to one secondary source) and targets 10.5% by FY28; APAR guides to a 10–11% EBITDA framework for cables; Finolex and Diamond Power report no separate W&C segment margin, so their company operating margins (12.1% and 11.2% in Q1 FY27) are the nearest proxies. KPI 8 · FMEG EBIT margin

What it is: the operating margin of the fans, lights, switches and solar products segment. Formula: FMEG segment EBIT ÷ FMEG revenue. Worked example: Polycab's FMEG earned about ₹100 crore of EBIT on ₹993 crore of revenue in Q2 FY27, 10.1%; in FY25 it lost about ₹38 crore on ₹1,654 crore (−2.3%). Benchmarks: above 8% is good, 2–8% average, below zero weak. Definition trap: traded products (Polycab's solar inverters) can inflate segment revenue and absorb fixed costs; check whether a margin jump came from a one-quarter surge in a traded category.

KPI 9 · Channel versus institutional mix

What it is: the share of sales made through distributors and dealers (the "channel" or "retail" business) versus direct sales to utilities, EPC contractors, developers and OEMs ("institutional"). Formula: channel sales ÷ total sales. Worked example: KEI's dealer and distribution sales were 59.09% of its total in Q1 FY27, up from 51.18% a year earlier, and grew 42% year on year. On ₹3,185 crore of quarterly revenue, that is roughly ₹1,880 crore through dealers (our arithmetic). Drivers: dealer count and reach, brand investment, product range in wires and small cables, and the pace of institutional ordering (a burst of tenders lowers the channel share without any channel weakness).

Why it matters: channel sales usually carry steadier margins, faster cash collection and list-price pass-through within days; institutional sales bring volume and engineering credibility but longer credit, tender pricing and price-variation lags. Benchmarks: above 60% channel is good for a branded maker, 45–60% average, below 45% weak (INDICATIVE). How the six compare: KEI 59.1% (Q1 FY27; about 54% in FY26); Polycab does not publish a percentage but said channel sales outpaced institutional in Q2 FY27; R R Kabel and Finolex are predominantly channel; APAR is overwhelmingly B2B; Diamond Power is 100% B2B. Definition trap: KEI's "dealer/distribution" share is sometimes reported as a "retail (B2C)" share; it is the same metric with a different label, and dealers also sell to small contractors, so it is not purely consumer demand.

KPI 10 · Export share

What it is: export revenue as a share of total revenue. Formula: export sales ÷ total sales. Worked example: R R Kabel's 26.2% export share on ₹9,722 crore of FY26 revenue means about ₹2,547 crore was earned abroad. Drivers: certifications (UL, CE, utility approvals), customer relationships, freight and transit times, the rupee, and above all tariffs. Why it matters: exports diversify away from domestic entrants and usually carry a premium for certification, but they bring tariff, logistics and currency risk. Benchmarks: above 20% good, 8–20% average, below 8% weak (for diversification, not necessarily for quality). How the six compare: APAR about 30%, R R Kabel 26.2%, KEI an estimated 10–12%, Polycab 5.4% (target above 10% by FY30), Finolex below 5%, Diamond Power close to zero (chart in Part 11). Definition trap: "international business" sometimes includes overseas subsidiaries' local sales and sometimes only Indian exports; APAR's order-book export share (56.8%) is not its revenue export share (about 30%).

KPI 11 · EHV share and premium mix

What it is: the share of revenue from the top of the ladder: EHV cable (66 kV and above) for cable makers, premium conductors (HTLS, OPGW, copper conductors for rail) for conductor makers. Formula: EHV or premium revenue ÷ segment revenue. Worked example: KEI's EHV sales of ₹186 crore in Q1 FY27 were about 5.8% of its ₹3,185 crore revenue; APAR's premium conductors were 50.3% of conductor revenue, up from 43.7%. Drivers: qualification and approvals, capacity (CCV lines), utility ordering of underground and reconductoring projects. Benchmarks: INDICATIVE: for an EHV-capable cable maker, a rising EHV share from mid-single digits is the signal; for APAR, premium above 50% is good. How the six compare: KEI and APAR disclose; Polycab's EHV plant is not yet commissioned (n/a until FY28); Diamond Power claims 400 kV capability but does not disclose EHV sales. Definition trap: "EHV" sometimes includes EPC services around cable laying; and an EHV order book is not EHV revenue until type tests and site readiness allow delivery.

In plain terms

Three mix numbers tell you whether a cable maker is moving to safer ground: how much it sells through dealers (faster cash, steadier prices), how much it exports (a second market the newcomers do not touch) and how much of its sales come from the top of the voltage ladder (where few rivals can follow). A company improving all three is getting better even if its headline margin barely moves.

KPI 12 · Order-book cover

What it is: for institutional and project businesses, the value of unexecuted orders divided by trailing revenue of that business, a measure of revenue visibility. Formula: order book ÷ trailing twelve-month segment revenue. Worked example: Diamond Power's ₹3,688 crore order book (11 August 2026) against ₹2,298 crore of trailing revenue is about 1.6 times; APAR's ₹10,190 crore conductor book against an estimated ₹13,300 crore of trailing conductor revenue is about 0.8 times. Drivers: utility and EPC ordering, export wins, and execution speed (a fast executor shows a lower cover for the same demand). Benchmarks: above 1.5 times good, 0.8–1.5 times average, below 0.8 times weak for a project business; flow businesses (house wire) have no meaningful order book. How the six compare: Polycab's EPC book of ₹9,000–10,000 crore is 5–6 times its FY26 EPC revenue of ₹1,667 crore; Diamond Power about 1.6 times; APAR conductors about 0.8 times (but with four-year export contracts); KEI's ₹3,585 crore (March 2026) about 0.3 times its total revenue, because most of KEI is a flow business. Definition trap: order books mix currencies, delivery periods and price-variation terms; a book that is 57% export and spread over four years is not equivalent to a 12-month domestic book.

KPI 13 · Working-capital days and cash conversion

What it is: working-capital days measure how many days of sales are tied up in receivables plus inventory minus payables; cash conversion (CFO ÷ EBITDA) measures how much of operating profit turned into operating cash. Formulas: (receivables + inventory − payables) ÷ revenue × 365; operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: Polycab's FY26 operating cash of ₹3,811 crore on ₹4,013 crore of EBITDA is 95%; Finolex's ₹49 crore on ₹620 crore is 8%. Drivers: customer mix (dealers pay faster than utilities), inventory policy, supplier and LC terms, and the copper price (higher metal raises the rupee value of every day of working capital). Benchmarks: working capital below 45 days good, 45–75 average, above 75 weak; cash conversion above 80% good, 50–80% average, below 50% weak. How the six compare: Polycab 30 days and 95%; R R Kabel 41 and 38%; APAR 43 and 50%; Finolex 62 and 8%; KEI 80 and 68%; Diamond Power 82 and 16% (charts and map in Part 13). Definition trap: short cycles can be bought with LC-funded payables, which carry interest; Polycab's 11-day Q2 FY27 cycle is guided back to 45–50 days.

KPI 14 · Finance cost as a share of revenue (the LC cost)

What it is: total finance cost divided by revenue; in this industry it is mostly the cost of letters of credit and acceptances used to buy metal. Formula: finance cost ÷ revenue. Worked example: APAR's ₹474 crore on ₹22,902 crore is 2.1%; on reported borrowings of only ₹956 crore it would imply an impossible ~50% interest rate, which shows the bulk is trade-finance cost. Drivers: metal prices (bigger LCs), payable days, interest rates (RBI repo 5.50% in October 2026) and the share of imported metal. Benchmarks: below 0.5% good, 0.5–1.5% average, above 1.5% weak. How the six compare: Finolex about 0%; KEI 0.5%; R R Kabel 0.8%; Polycab 0.8%; APAR 2.1%; Diamond Power 2.0% (pre-QIP, promoter loans). Definition trap: net-cash figures exclude this trade debt; adding LC-backed payables to borrowings gives a truer picture of how much of the metal pipeline banks are financing.

In plain terms

Look at the interest line before the debt line. A cable maker paying far more interest than its loans would justify is financing its copper through its suppliers' banks. That is cheap and normal, but it means the company is less cash-rich than its balance sheet suggests, and it will need more of that financing every time copper rises.

KPI 15 · Utilisation and capacity additions

What it is: output as a share of installed capacity, and the pace at which capacity is being added. Formula: production ÷ capacity (in km or tonnes for comparable products). Worked example: if industry capacity grows 21% while volume grows 10%, utilisation falls from 75% to about 68% (75% × 1.10 ÷ 1.21; ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers: demand, new lines, entrants, and product-mix shifts that change effective capacity. Benchmarks: above 80% tight (pricing power), 65–80% normal, below 65% loose (price competition likely). How the six compare: Crisil puts industry utilisation at about 75% for FY26; Diamond Power reports about 60% in conductors and cables; others do not disclose (n/a). Definition trap: kilometre capacity is meaningless across products (a house-wire line and an EHV line are measured in the same unit but differ a hundred-fold in copper per kilometre), so compare capacity in tonnes or rupees of output.

KPI 16 · ROCE and capex intensity

What it is: return on capital employed (EBIT ÷ capital employed) and capex as a share of revenue. Together they show whether growth is being bought efficiently. Formulas: EBIT ÷ (equity + debt − sometimes cash); capex ÷ revenue. Worked example: Polycab spent ₹1,480 crore of capex on ₹28,884 crore of FY26 revenue, 5.1%, while earning a 33.2% ROCE. Drivers: asset turns (a wire plant turns over its fixed assets many times a year because revenue includes the metal), margins, cash balances (idle cash lowers ROCE), and working capital. Benchmarks: ROCE above 25% good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak; capex intensity of 2–5% normal in a growth phase, above 8% suggests overbuilding, below 1% under-investment. Definition traps, collected

  • Market size has three bases. ₹1.1 lakh crore (Polycab/MOSL) is the house figure; Crisil's numbers imply about ₹1.5 lakh crore; older primer figures of ₹80,000 crore (FY25) and "₹50,000 crore-plus" are on narrower or earlier bases. Never mix FY25 and FY26 bases.
  • "Organised share" depends on who counts as organised: Crisil's rated companies, all branded players, or something else. Estimates range from about two-thirds to 80%.
  • Value growth is not volume growth. With metal at 65–75% of price, a 47% copper rise adds 30-plus points of value growth with no extra tonnes.
  • EBITDA bases differ. KEI's reported EBITDA includes other income (FY26 ₹1,387 crore = ₹1,229 crore operating + ₹159 crore other income); APAR's company EBITDA (₹2,067 crore) exceeds screener operating profit (₹1,922 crore). This report uses screener operating profit in tables.
  • Interest cost is not debt cost. APAR's ₹474 crore of interest on ₹956 crore of borrowings is mostly LC and acceptance charges hidden in payables.
  • Margin percentages fall mechanically when copper rises, even with an intact rupee spread. A 50–90 basis-point dip in a spike is not proof of lost pricing power.
  • Inventory gains and losses run through gross margin. APAR's oils EBITDA per kilolitre jumped to ₹25,482 from ₹7,004 on a base-oil spike; strip such gains before judging "underlying" margins.
  • Segment names do not line up. Polycab: W&C / FMEG / EPC. KEI: cables & wires / EPC / stainless-steel wire. APAR: conductors / oils / cables (segments sum to more than consolidated revenue). Finolex: electrical / communication / others. R R Kabel: W&C / FMEG. Diamond: one segment.
  • Finolex's P/E is flattered by treasury income: other income of ₹370 crore against operating profit of ₹620 crore, plus a ~₹91 crore share of Finolex Industries' profit; its ₹4,195 crore of "investments" includes that equity-accounted stake, which is not all liquid cash.
  • Diamond Power's March 2026 balance sheet is pre-QIP, with ₹2,536 crore of borrowings and negative net worth (book value −₹11.5 a share). Its ROE and P/B are not meaningful and its 11.8% ROCE is distorted; the FY27 QIP cleared the promoter loans.

How to read a cable maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, then volume growth. Find the volume number first (tonnes, km or a "mid-single-digit" description). If the company gives none, compare revenue growth with the year-on-year copper move to estimate it. Red flag: revenue up, volume down, and no mix explanation.
  2. Gross margin and its rupee equivalent. Note the change in percentage points, then ask whether rupee gross profit grew faster than volume. Red flag: rupee gross profit growing slower than volume, which means the spread per kilogram fell.
  3. Segment margins. Read W&C EBIT separately from FMEG and EPC (Polycab, R R Kabel), and conductor EBITDA per tonne separately from oils (APAR). Red flag: group margin up only because a small segment had a one-off.
  4. Inventory effects. Look for commentary on inventory gains, valuation provisions or "commodity tailwinds". Compare the margin jump with the copper move in the quarter. Red flag: a margin jump in a copper spike from a company that does not hedge.
  5. Other income and EBITDA basis. Check whether reported EBITDA includes other income (KEI) or forex (APAR). Recompute operating EBITDA. Red flag: profit growth driven by treasury income.
  6. Mix KPIs. Channel share (KEI), export growth (Polycab, R R Kabel, APAR), EHV sales (KEI), premium conductor share (APAR), order book (APAR, Diamond, Polycab EPC). Red flag: export decline without a tariff or logistics explanation.
  7. Working capital and cash. At half-year and full-year, read operating cash flow, receivable, inventory and payable days. Red flag: payables stretching to mask rising inventory, or cash conversion below 50% two halves running.
  8. Finance cost. Compare interest with reported debt. Red flag: interest rising sharply while borrowings are flat, which signals more LC funding.
  9. Capacity and capex. New lines commissioned, EHV timelines, utilisation comments. Red flag: repeated slippage of a flagship project (KEI's Sanand EHV and Polycab's EHV plant have both moved later).
  10. Competitive commentary. Any mention of price cuts, dealer schemes, entrant activity or share. Red flag: rising A&P and scheme costs with flat wire margins, which would be the first sign of a dealer-margin war.
In plain terms

When a cable maker reports, ignore the headline sales growth at first. Ask four questions in order: how much more did it physically sell, how many rupees did it keep per kilogram of metal, did any of the profit come from lucky copper timing or treasury income, and did the profit turn into cash? Those four answers tell you more than the rest of the press release.

The valuation equation

A cable maker's multiple is, in effect, a bet on three things: how long volume can compound above the economy's growth rate; how stable the rupee spread per kilogram stays as capacity and entrants arrive; and how much extra value comes from optional businesses (FMEG, EHV, exports) that earn better margins than the core. The entrant risk is a discount on the second term. That framing explains the 2025–26 re-pricing: the entrants did not change volume expectations or the optional businesses, but they raised doubts about spread stability in house wire, so the multiples of the retail-wire leaders fell while APAR's, whose spreads come from conductors and exports, did not.

One way to see what 42.7 times implies: if the group's multiple drifted down to 30 times over five years, a level closer to a mature industrial, earnings would have to grow about 7.3% a year just to keep the share prices flat ((42.7 ÷ 30)^(1/5) ≈ 1.073; ILLUSTRATIVE). To deliver a 12% annual return from that starting point, earnings would need to compound at about 20% a year for five years. That is demanding but not out of line with FY26's 29% PAT growth or Crisil's long investment pipeline, provided spreads hold. The global lens: comparable margins, far higher returns, missing the high end

India's cable leaders earn margins similar to or above the global majors and much higher returns on capital. What they lack is the subsea and HVDC technology where the global premium sits.

The global wire and cable industry is commonly put at $200–220 billion a year, which would make India about 5–7% of it (both figures INDICATIVE, from industry estimates we have not verified). It is dominated by a handful of multinationals: Prysmian of Italy, the global leader, which bought the US building-wire maker Encore Wire in 2024 for roughly $4.2 billion; Nexans of France, Polycab's joint-venture partner in 2008–09, which has refocused on electrification; Southwire, the privately held US building-wire leader; Sumitomo Electric, Furukawa and Fujikura of Japan (Fujikura re-rated sharply on data-centre fibre demand); and LS Cable of Korea, a leader in subsea and HVDC cable now expanding in the US. Chinese makers such as Far East, Hengtong and ZTT compete aggressively in export markets.

What the comparison says

Three conclusions are reasonably robust even with approximate global data. First, India's leaders earn similar or higher margins than the global majors; Polycab's 13.9% is above Prysmian's and Nexans's typical ranges. Second, Indian returns on capital are far higher, because house wire is asset-light and Indian makers carry no legacy subsea or high-voltage project businesses with heavy capital and long build times. Third, Indian valuations are richer, roughly 1.5–2 times global P/E multiples on our INDICATIVE estimate, reflecting faster domestic growth and the scarcity of listed Indian electrification plays.

The gap is at the top of the technology ladder. HVDC and subsea cable, the fastest-growing and highest-value segment globally as offshore wind and long-distance interconnectors expand, is dominated by Prysmian, Nexans, NKT, LS Cable and Sumitomo. India's makers work at up to 400 kV in XLPE AC cable; HVDC cable is imported or supplied by multinationals. India's planned 33 GW of HVDC links in the transmission plan will largely use overhead lines and converter stations rather than cable, but any underground or subsea HVDC element will be an import or multinational opportunity.

There is also a lesson from abroad on consolidation. Prysmian's purchase of Encore Wire showed how global leaders buy into building-wire markets with strong distribution. India's market is still fragmented enough that the leaders can grow by taking share rather than buying it, but the entrants' arrival, combined with Polycab's net cash, makes acquisitions of regional players more likely over time.

In plain terms

By global standards, India's best cable makers are very profitable and very efficient with capital, which is part of why they cost more to buy. Where they are behind is the most advanced kind of cable, the kind laid under the sea or used for very long high-voltage links. That technology is held by a handful of European, Korean and Japanese companies.

Technology and disruption: slow-moving, but not static

Cable technology changes slowly. Six shifts matter for the listed makers: aluminium substitution, fire-safety specifications, higher voltages, reconductoring, the fibre cycle and new routes to market.

A house wire made today looks much like one made thirty years ago, and that stability is part of why brands matter: with little to differentiate the product technically, trust and availability decide. But the industry is not static, and six technical or commercial shifts are reshaping where value sits.

1. Aluminium substitution

When copper spikes, buyers look for ways to use less of it. In LT power distribution, aluminium is already standard; in building wire and some industrial cable, aluminium and copper-clad aluminium (CCA) gain share at the margin. In the US, the cable market is about 66% copper and 33% aluminium by value, according to APAR. For listed makers this is a mix shift rather than a threat: all of them make aluminium products. The risk is at the low end, where unbranded CCA wire sold as copper creates safety problems and price competition.

2. Fire safety and specification creep

Data centres, metros, hospitals and high-rise buildings increasingly specify halogen-free flame-retardant (HFFR), low-smoke zero-halogen (LSZH), electron-beam cross-linked and fire-survival cables. These need better compounds, extra process steps and testing, and they raise the bar for small makers. Specification creep is a quiet tailwind for the organised players and a reason the data-centre market carries better margins than ordinary building wire.

3. Higher voltages: EHV and the HVDC gap

Indian makers now work up to 400 kV in XLPE cable (KEI, with Polycab following from FY28). The next steps, 525 kV and HVDC cable, remain the preserve of multinationals. As Indian cities push more transmission underground and data-centre campuses demand their own high-voltage feeds, EHV demand should grow faster than the cable market as a whole; the domestic makers' challenge is to build the type-test record that utilities require.

4. Reconductoring with HTLS

High-temperature low-sag conductors let a utility carry two to three times more power on existing towers, avoiding new rights-of-way. This substitutes for some new line-building (bad for tower makers) but is strongly positive for premium-conductor makers, above all APAR. Reconductoring is also the main use of HTLS in the US, which is why APAR's export book is rising despite tariffs.

5. The fibre cycle

Optical-fibre cable went through a price collapse in the early 2020s, when Finolex's fibre volumes rose 30% in one year while revenue stayed flat. It is now reviving on data-centre and AI demand: Finolex's communication-cable revenue rose 62% in Q1 FY27, APAR makes telecom and OPGW products, and R R Kabel is buying U M Cables' fibre business. Fibre remains more cyclical than power cable, because fibre prices swing with Chinese capacity.

6. New routes to market

The most important disruption may be commercial rather than technical. UltraTech is using its cement and building-materials dealer network to sell wire; Adani brings captive copper; B2B e-commerce platforms let contractors compare prices across brands. Each erodes, slowly, the advantage of a dense dealer network. The incumbents' response is to deepen it with more products per dealer (FMEG), better electrician loyalty programmes and faster delivery.

In plain terms

No new technology is about to make copper cable obsolete. The changes that matter are slower: cheaper aluminium replacing copper where it can, stricter fire rules that favour the better makers, more cable at very high voltages, smarter conductors that squeeze more power from existing lines, and new ways of selling wire that chip away at the old dealer networks.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The bull case is a decade of grid, data-centre and housing demand meeting a consolidating industry. The bear case is a capacity wave, two well-funded entrants and a copper cycle that could turn.

Both cases start from the same facts: demand is the broadest in twenty years, copper is near a record, capacity is growing faster than volume, and two large groups have entered. They differ on which of those facts dominates over the next three years.

The bull case

  • A long demand runway. ₹9.15 lakh crore of transmission investment to 2032, RDSS, 500 GW of non-fossil capacity, data centres heading from under 2 GW to 8–15 GW, housing, railways and exports, with Crisil counting a ₹10–12 lakh crore pipeline.
  • Consolidation keeps working. Organised players are still taking share from a 30–35% unorganised tail; Polycab gained about four points in FY26, the year the entrants announced.
  • Mix is lifting margins. EHV (KEI +48%), premium conductors (APAR above 50%), exports and a profitable FMEG arm (Polycab 10.1%, R R Kabel breakeven) add margin that copper cannot erode.
  • Earnings have not been touched by the entrants. PAT grew 29% in FY26 and 54% in Q1 FY27; the 2025 de-rating was in multiples, leaving room for re-rating if spreads hold.
  • Balance sheets are strong. Polycab's ₹4,520 crore net cash, sector debt to EBITDA of 0.5–0.6 times, and equity-funded expansion at KEI, APAR and Diamond Power.

The bear case

  • Growth is mostly copper. Revenue up 37–53% on volume of single to low-double digits; when copper falls, revenue shrinks and inventory gains turn into losses.
  • Capacity is outrunning demand. Industry capacity +20–22% by end-FY27 against ~10% volume; utilisation could fall from ~75% towards the high 60s, inviting price competition.
  • The entrants are real and patient. UltraTech's 10.98 lakh km plant and Adani's captive copper target the most profitable pool; a dealer-margin war would erode spreads without any list-price cut.
  • Cash lags profit. Group CFO was 68% of EBITDA in FY26, with Finolex at 8% and Diamond at 16%; LC funding hides trade debt.
  • Valuations still assume a lot. 42.7 times trailing earnings for the group, 55.6 times for APAR and 106 times for Diamond Power; US tariffs (Section 232 at 50% on metal-intensive goods) cloud the export story.

Where we come out

The demand case is the strongest part of the bull argument and the least contested; nobody serious disputes that India will lay far more cable this decade than last. The weakest part of the bull case is the assumption that spreads hold through a capacity wave. The strongest part of the bear case is the arithmetic of capacity versus volume: if both Crisil figures are right, utilisation falls for at least two years. The weakest part of the bear case is the entrant threat to branded house wire, where electrician trust and availability have repeatedly defeated well-funded challengers in Indian consumer markets.

What would change the balance? A sustained fall in Polycab's or R R Kabel's wire-segment margin alongside rising scheme and A&P costs would confirm the bear case on entrants. A copper fall of $2,000 a tonne or more in a quarter would test inventory discipline and show which makers hedge. On the bull side, two more quarters of Polycab volume growth at or above the market with stable spreads, KEI's EHV line starting on time and APAR holding EBITDA above ₹50,000 a tonne would support today's multiples.

In plain terms

Everyone agrees India needs a lot more cable. The argument is about whether so many new factories, including UltraTech's and Adani's, will force prices down before demand catches up, and about what happens to profits when copper eventually falls. The next two or three quarters of results will answer much of that.

The entrants: what to look for

Ultravolt's first full quarter of sales is Q3 FY27 (October–December 2026), and Praneetha's building and flexible wires are due in November 2026. Neither discloses volumes, so the evidence will be indirect: dealer surveys, any change in incumbents' dealer schemes or A&P, any list-price cut by Polycab, Havells, KEI or R R Kabel, and the incumbents' wire volumes relative to the market. Polycab said in October that it had not changed prices. The first incumbent to cut list prices outside a copper move would be the clearest signal that the entrants are biting.

Copper and trade

Citi expects copper to reach $15,000 a tonne by the end of 2026; a proposed US tariff on refined copper is one reason prices are high. Watch the US decision, LME inventories and Chinese demand. On trade, two legal and regulatory items matter for exporters: the challenge to the 10% Section 301 duty and any clarification of how Section 232's 50% metals tariff applies to finished insulated cable. Either could swing APAR's and R R Kabel's US economics.
