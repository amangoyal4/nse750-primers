00

The one-page view: an opinion business that grew while bonds shrank

Five forces explain Indian credit ratings and research. Here they are before the detail.

Two industries share the name "credit ratings and research" in India. The first is the domestic rating business. Eight SEBI-registered agencies give published opinions on whether a borrower will repay a bond, a commercial-paper programme, a securitised pool or a bank loan. The borrower pays the fee. Investors, banks and regulators use the opinion. The business is small. The three listed raters booked about ₹1,395 crore of domestic ratings revenue in their latest full years, and the whole industry, including the unlisted India Ratings, Acuité, Infomerics, Brickwork and Acer, is perhaps ₹1,700–2,000 crore (an indicative estimate, because the unlisted agencies' revenue is not public). But it is one of the most profitable businesses in Indian finance. The domestic rating arms earn 48–60% margins on almost no capital, because their product is an analyst's judgement backed by a licence and a reputation.

The second industry is the export of financial research and analytics from India to global banks, asset managers and the rating agencies' own foreign parents: credit notes for S&P's rating committees, risk models for European banks, league tables of investment-banking revenue, client surveys and wealth-adviser benchmarks. It is larger, lower-margin and exposed to global bank budgets, the rupee and generative AI. CRISIL, the only listed member of this sub-sector, does both. About 70% of its revenue comes from the research engine and about 69% is earned outside India, yet its small domestic rating subsidiary earns close to 39% of group profit. To understand CRISIL you have to understand both industries, and how each one moves.

The five forces

  • Bank credit versus the bond market. Ratings demand comes from two credit channels with different fee yields. Bonds pay more per mandate; bank-loan ratings give volume and stickiness. In 2026 bank credit grew 19.1% (August) while corporate bond issuance fell 6% in the first nine months of the calendar year. Rating revenue still grew 15–29% at the listed agencies. Section 03 explains why.
  • The surveillance annuity and a two-tier oligopoly. Every outstanding rating pays an annual monitoring fee, so this year's revenue follows last year's new mandates. In listed bonds investors effectively choose the agency, and CRISIL takes 46.8% of fresh ratings. In small-company bank-loan ratings, borrowers shop on price, and the default record of the long tail is weaker.
  • Regulation now prices rating quality. RBI's Standardised Approach directions, effective 1 April 2027, cut the risk weight on an AA loan from 30% to 20%, but only if the rating agency's own one-year default rate for AA stays within RBI's ceiling. On some averaging windows, several grades at the big agencies breach those ceilings, so the relief is not assured. SEBI, meanwhile, widened the licence in January 2026.
  • CRISIL's offshore analytics engine. Research, Analytics & Solutions (RAS) is about 70% of CRISIL's revenue. Its margin fell to 17.2% in Q3 CY26 from 21.9% a year earlier. S&P Global entities pay about 13% of CRISIL's revenue. The rupee, the hedge book (struck at ₹88.84 per US dollar for 2026) and generative AI decide this engine's profits.
  • Reputation and defaults. IL&FS defaulted in 2018 while rated AAA and AA. CARE's operating margin fell from 63% to 33%, two chief executives left their posts, and SEBI rebuilt its default-disclosure rules. Every rating agency's value rests on the belief that its AAA means AAA.

The paradox of 2026 sits at the centre of this report. The Indian corporate bond market had a poor year. Rising government-bond yields after the West Asia war began in February, and a rate hike to 5.50% in October, made bank loans cheaper than bonds for many top borrowers. Value raised through private placements fell 8.8% in FY26 on SEBI's own data. Yet Crisil Ratings' revenue grew 25.6% in the July–September quarter of 2026, CARE's ratings revenue grew 19.1% in April–June and ICRA's about 13%. Three mechanisms explain it: bank credit growing at 19%, which multiplies bank-loan ratings; the annual surveillance fees on the large crop of 2024–25 mandates; and a rise in the number of bond issues even as their value fell. Raters bill per issue and per issuer far more than per rupee.

The second big change is regulatory. From April 2027, RBI will let banks hold less capital against loans rated AA, BBB and BB, but it ties each grade's risk weight to the rating agency's own observed default rate. For the first time in Indian banking, the quality of a rating agency's track record has a price that a bank treasurer can calculate. On the data we have, the top agencies pass comfortably at AAA, AA and A on recent windows, but every agency's BB grade breaches RBI's 1% ceiling, and CRISIL's BBB is fractionally over the 0.40% ceiling on SEBI's ten-year window. RBI has not said which averaging window it will use. That single definitional choice, still open in October 2026, will decide how much capital relief Indian banks get and which agencies they prefer.

The third story is CRISIL's own. The company reported its July–September quarter on 23 October 2026: revenue up 20.2% to ₹1,095.2 crore, but profit before tax up only 5.3% and net profit up 5.9% to ₹204.6 crore. Ratings delivered; the research engine did not. RAS revenue grew 16.5%, but its profit fell 8.3%, as Coalition Greenwich renewals pulled into the first half of the year, the FX gain shrank from ₹15.5 crore to ₹2.3 crore and amortisation from the PriceMetrix acquisition rose. At ₹34,745 crore of market value and 38.8 times trailing earnings, CRISIL is priced as a global compounder. Its net profit compounded at about 11% a year from CY22 to CY25.

In plain terms

CRISIL is a small, very profitable Indian rating agency attached to a large, moderately profitable research exporter, controlled by S&P Global. The rating half is booming on bank lending. The research half is slowing and is exposed to the rupee and to AI. The stock price assumes both halves keep compounding.

01

How an opinion earns: the two value chains

A rating is paid for by the borrower, used by the lender and policed by two regulators. Research is paid for by a global bank and billed in dollars.

A credit rating is a published opinion on the probability that a borrower will pay interest and principal on time, expressed on a standard scale from AAA (highest safety) down to D (default) for long-term debt, and A1+ to A4 for short-term paper such as commercial paper. In India almost all ratings are on the Indian national scale, which ranks Indian borrowers against each other. An Indian AAA is not a global AAA: on the global scale India's sovereign sits in the BBB category, the lower part of investment grade, and Indian companies are rarely rated above it there. The national scale matters because it is what Indian investors, banks and regulators use to set limits and prices.

The money is earned twice: once at Step 2 and then every year at Step 5. Step 3 is where the cost sits, and Step 4 is where reputation is won or lost, because every rating is public.

The trigger comes from two places. On the capital-market route, SEBI's issue regulations require a rating from a registered agency for public issues and listed private placements of debt, and investor mandates add their own floors: insurers and pension funds, for example, may invest only in paper rated AA or above. On the bank route, a bank lending to a large company prefers the loan to be rated because an external rating can cut the capital it must hold. That is the bank-loan rating, or BLR, a product born when RBI adopted the Basel II capital rules in 2007–08. CRISIL says it was the first agency to rate a bank loan in India. BLRs are less glamorous than bond ratings, and CARE's management calls them "fairly commoditized", but they make up the bulk of rated entities. Crisil Ratings alone had about 7,300 companies with active ratings at March 2026 and issued more than 1,280 new BLRs in CY25.

The fee is paid by the borrower. That is the issuer-pays model, used by every large rating agency in the world, and it carries an obvious conflict: the party being judged pays the judge. The industry manages that conflict with rules (rating committees exclude business-development staff, analysts rotate, non-rating fees from rated entities are disclosed) and, more powerfully, with reputation. A rating is only worth paying for if investors trust it. In the listed bond market investors effectively choose which agency's rating they accept, and that is why one agency can take nearly half of all fresh ratings while charging more per mandate than its rivals.

In plain terms

The borrower pays, but the lender decides whether the rating is worth anything. An agency whose AAA ratings keep defaulting will find that investors demand a higher interest rate for its AAA paper, and borrowers will stop paying it for ratings. The licence lets an agency rate; only its track record makes the rating valuable.

The second value chain: research exported from India

CRISIL's larger business looks nothing like a rating agency. Its Research, Analytics & Solutions segment sells to global corporate and investment banks, asset managers, insurers and private-credit funds, and it serves S&P Global's rating committees through a dedicated Global Analytics Centre (GAC). The buyers have two motives. The first is cost and scale: Indian analysts can build a credit model, validate a bank's risk model against US or UK supervisory rules, or write first-draft equity research at a fraction of the cost in New York or London. The second is proprietary data the buyer cannot build for itself, such as Coalition Greenwich's league tables of investment-bank revenue and headcount, its client-experience surveys, and PriceMetrix's benchmarks of wealth-adviser productivity.

Where is value captured? The clearest answer comes from comparing margins along the chain. The domestic licence earns the most: Crisil Ratings Ltd made a 59.5% pre-tax margin in CY25 (including treasury income), ICRA's ratings segment a 56.7% segment result in FY26, and CARE's standalone business a 48% operating margin. Proprietary benchmarks earn less but still well: Coalition Development Ltd, the UK entity behind Coalition Greenwich, earned about 25% of turnover as net profit in CY25. Generic research outsourcing earns mid-teens to low-twenties, and captive work for a parent earns a fixed mark-up over cost that neither CRISIL nor ICRA discloses. The ranking is intuitive: the harder a product is to copy, the higher its margin. A licence plus thirty years of default history is very hard to copy; a team of analysts building spreadsheets is not.

The last link in the ratings chain is the one most investors miss: what happens when a borrower stops cooperating. If an issuer stops paying fees or sharing information, the agency cannot simply drop the rating. It must publish an "issuer not cooperating" (INC) rating, usually with a downgrade. From April 2027 RBI attaches a penalty to that label: a loan whose rating is INC carries at least a 100% risk weight for six months and 150% after that. The rule makes it costly for a borrower to walk away from its rating agency and makes the annual surveillance fee even stickier.

02

Size of the pool: a ~₹1,850 crore industry beside a ₹61 lakh crore market

Indian ratings is tiny next to the debt it rates and next to the global agencies. CRISIL's research engine is larger than the whole domestic rating industry.

Start with what can be measured. The three listed agencies disclose their domestic ratings revenue: Crisil Ratings Ltd ₹670.6 crore (CY25), CARE Ratings standalone ₹387.7 crore (FY26) and ICRA's ratings segment ₹336.5 crore (FY26). Together that is ₹1,394.8 crore, or about ₹1,395 crore, mixing CRISIL's calendar year with the others' fiscal year. India Ratings, owned by Fitch, is the fourth large agency and is unlisted; its revenue sits behind paid databases. A database snippet says its net sales grew 11.1% in 2025. Given that it took 19% of fresh listed ratings in FY26, close to ICRA's 20%, a range of ₹300–380 crore is a reasonable indicative estimate. The long tail of Acuité, Infomerics, Brickwork and Acer, mostly small-company bank-loan raters, adds perhaps ₹120–250 crore. The whole domestic pool is therefore roughly ₹1,700–2,000 crore, about US$195 million at today's rupee (INDICATIVE).

CRISIL: a ₹4,310 crore company in a ₹1,850 crore industry

CRISIL's consolidated revenue of ₹3,649 crore in CY25, and ₹4,310 crore in the twelve months to September 2026, is more than twice the size of the entire domestic rating pool. That is the clearest signal that CRISIL is mainly a research company with a rating agency inside it. Its revenue has compounded at about 10% a year over ten years, from ₹1,253 crore in CY14, and about 13% over five years. The ratings-only growth record is shorter but stronger. CRISIL's Ratings segment, which includes the GAC, grew from ₹911.3 crore in CY24 to ₹1,078.7 crore in CY25 (+18.4%) and to ₹973.4 crore in the first nine months of CY26 alone (+23.6%). The industry's long-run growth tracks the stock of rated debt plus pricing, roughly 10–14% a year, with sharp deviations when reputation or the credit cycle intervenes.

The global yardstick

The comparison with the global agencies is humbling. S&P Global Ratings earned US$4,724 million in 2025, about ₹44,800 crore at ₹94.9 per dollar; Moody's Investors Service earned US$4,119 million, about ₹39,100 crore. Each is more than 20 times the entire Indian industry. Even Morningstar DBRS, the distant fourth global agency, earned US$354.4 million (about ₹3,360 crore) in 2025, almost twice India's total. The gap says less about India's economy than about issuer-pays pricing: a US corporate pays a fee on every bond calibrated to a deep, global investor base, while an Indian borrower pays a national-scale fee in a market where most debt is still bank loans. If India's bond market grows toward the 36–40% of GDP seen in China and the US, from about 16–17% today, the pool could grow faster than credit for a long time. That is the industry's structural bull case.

03

Bonds down, raters up: the anatomy of 2026's rating boom Deep dive

Bond issuance fell, yet rating revenue grew 15–29%. Four mechanisms explain the gap, and one of them sets up a risk for 2027.

On almost every headline measure, 2026 was a bad year for the Indian corporate bond market. On Prime Database's calendar-year series, which CRISIL uses, issuance fell 12% in January–March, 26% in April–June and rose 36% in July–September only because the same quarter of 2025 had collapsed by 31.7%. Over nine months, issuance was ₹8,407 billion against ₹8,950 billion, a fall of 6.1%. SEBI's own series of exchange-listed private placements shows the fiscal year FY26 (April 2025 to March 2026) at ₹8,99,736 crore, down 8.8% on FY25. CareEdge measured issuance in April–June 2026 at ₹2.5 lakh crore, down 29.3%. If ratings revenue simply tracked bond value, the agencies should have had a poor year.

They had their best year in a decade. CRISIL's Ratings segment revenue grew 20.2% in Q1 CY26, 21.4% in Q2 and 28.9% in Q3; within it, Crisil Ratings, the domestic agency, grew 25.6% in the July–September quarter. ICRA's ratings revenue rose 14.2% in FY26 and about 13% in Q1 FY27 (₹83.8 crore against ₹74.2 crore). CARE's ratings revenue rose 17% in FY26 and 19.1% in Q1 FY27. The rest of this section explains the gap between the bond market and the raters, because the same mechanisms will decide the next two years.

Mechanism 1: bank credit replaced bonds, and bank loans get rated too

Indian borrowers can raise debt from banks or from the bond market, and in 2026 the price signal pointed firmly to banks. After the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026, the ten-year government-bond yield approached 7% in March, lifting the benchmark against which corporate bonds are priced. Banks, by contrast, were flush with deposits and keen to lend to top-rated corporates. CRISIL's management described "some level of substitution of bonds by bank loans" on its April call, and the October repo hike to 5.50% did not change that: a rate hike "does not favour the bond market because yields further harden". Bank credit growth accelerated from 10.3% in August 2025 to 19.1% in August 2026, and credit to large industry swung from 2.0% growth in June 2025 to 16.6% in June 2026.

For a rating agency, a large-company bank loan is not lost business. It is a bank-loan rating instead of a bond rating. The fee per mandate is lower, and CARE's management describes BLRs as "fairly commoditized", but the count of mandates rises with credit growth. CRISIL itself expected bank credit to grow "around 13%" in FY27 when it spoke in April; actual growth ran about six percentage points ahead. Most of the volume surprise in 2026 ratings revenue came from that gap.

Mechanism 2: the surveillance annuity runs a year behind

Every outstanding rating pays an annual surveillance fee until the debt is repaid or the rating withdrawn. So a large part of this year's revenue is set by last year's new mandates. Subodh Rai, who runs Crisil Ratings, put it plainly in April 2026: surveillance revenue "is driven by the new rating revenues performance in prior year … what you're going to see in '26 will be largely driven by the new revenues we did in 2025 and partly … '24". CY24 and CY25 were strong years for new mandates: calendar 2025 issuance was ₹11,245 billion, slightly above 2024's ₹11,195 billion, and FY25 private placements rose 17.8% in value and 23.2% in number. Those mandates are now paying their first and second surveillance fees.

Mechanism 3: more issues, smaller issues

The third mechanism is the one most commentary missed, and SEBI's statistics show it clearly. In FY26 the value of private placements fell 8.8%, but the number of issues rose 16.0%, from 1,659 to 1,924. The average issue shrank from ₹595 crore to ₹468 crore, a fall of about 21%. CRISIL's slides show the same thing on a calendar basis: the number of bond issuers in January–September 2026 was about 1,191, up 3.8% from about 1,147, even though value was down 6%. Rating fees are charged per instrument and per issuer, with caps on the largest programmes. A mega-issue of ₹10,000 crore by a public-sector financier pays a capped fee of a few tens of lakhs; ten mid-sized NBFC issues of ₹500 crore each pay ten fees. A market with more, smaller issuers is better for rating revenue than a market with fewer, larger ones, even at the same rupee volume.

Mechanism 4: price and mix

The last mechanism is pricing. Crisil Ratings' management said it "acquired a lot of new clients at good prices" and handles pricing "in a very disciplined manner", while declining to give any five-year price trend. CARE's management, for its part, has made "continuously improving the pricing" a formal performance goal. Other short-term instruments helped: commercial-paper issuance rose 7.2% in FY26 to ₹16.9 lakh crore and 18.5% in Q1 FY27 to ₹5.4 lakh crore, and NBFC securitisation grew about 25% in FY26. In a year when the industry's mix shifted from bonds toward lower-fee bank loans, holding or raising price per mandate mattered. The agency with the most investor-preferred brand can raise prices most easily, which is one reason Crisil Ratings outgrew ICRA in the same quarters.

In plain terms

Rating revenue grew in 2026 because four things went right at once: banks lent more to rated companies, last year's record mandates paid their annual fees, the bond market had more (smaller) deals even though it raised less money, and the leading agencies held their prices. Only the first and third depend on this year's credit market.

The risk: the lag works both ways

The surveillance lag that cushioned 2026 cuts the other way later. The new bond mandates written in 2026 were fewer by value, and in Q2 and Q3 fewer by issuer count, than in 2025. They form the base for CY27 and FY28 surveillance revenue. If bank credit growth slows from 19% toward the low teens, which RBI's rate hike and tighter stance make plausible, and the bond market does not recover, the industry could face a year in which both new-mandate fees and surveillance growth slow at the same time. This is an INDICATIVE inference, not a forecast: bank-loan ratings on the 2026 credit surge also feed surveillance, and CP and securitisation volumes are still growing. But the lag means the best time to watch for a slowdown in rating revenue is twelve to eighteen months after bond issuance turns down, not the same quarter.

The upside case is equally mechanical. CRISIL's management sees the bank-versus-bond swing as cyclical, not structural: "the pendulum" swings back when government-bond yields fall or banks run short of deposits. The comparison base also eases: Q4 CY26 laps a weak Q4 CY25 for bonds, and Q3 CY26 already showed issuance up 36% on a depressed base. RBI's 2027 rules, which raise the penalty on unrated and non-cooperating borrowers, add a regulatory push toward more bank-loan ratings from April 2027. For the agencies, a slower bond market is an irritation; a sharp slowdown in bank credit and bonds together, as in FY20–22, is the real threat.

04

The debt market underneath: who borrows, who holds, and why bonds are only 16% of GDP

Rating demand is a function of India's credit structure: bank-heavy, AAA-heavy and dominated by financial issuers.

India's corporate bond market is young, concentrated and growing. At about 16–17% of GDP, it is less than half the size of China's (36%) or the US market (40%) relative to their economies, while bank credit, at about 61% of GDP, does most of the lending. That structure matters for raters in two ways. First, a large share of rated entities are rated for bank loans, not bonds. Second, the bond market itself is dominated by a few hundred financial issuers that raise money frequently, which makes them the agencies' most valuable recurring clients.

Who borrows? On CareEdge's breakdown of outstanding bonds, NBFCs account for 30.2%, private non-financial corporates 27.6%, public-sector undertakings 20.5%, banks 13.0%, and housing-finance companies and others the remaining ~8.7%. Financial issuers made up about 64–66% of FY26 issuance, down from 75–78% earlier, as banks took back NBFC lending after RBI rolled back its November 2023 risk-weight increase on bank loans to NBFCs in April 2025. That policy swing illustrates how quickly regulation moves volume between the bond and loan channels: the 2023 hike pushed NBFCs into bonds in FY25; the 2025 rollback pulled them back to banks in FY26. Readers who follow our NBFC and housing-finance reports will recognise those issuers as the agencies' anchor clients.

Who holds the bonds, and why do they care about ratings? Corporates (about 32%), insurers (19.5%), mutual funds (12.9%) and banks (9.2%) are the largest holders. Each has rules that embed ratings: insurers and pension funds may invest only in paper rated AA or above; debt mutual funds face scheme-level limits by rating; banks use ratings to compute capital. That is why more than 85% of outstanding bonds are rated AAA or AA, and about 95% of FY26 issuance was. A borrower rated below AA has almost no institutional market for its bonds and goes to banks instead. The regulatory use of ratings, rather than investor curiosity, is the foundation of demand. Foreign portfolio investors hold just 5.4%, up from 2% in FY22, which is another reason the national scale, not the global one, dominates.

In plain terms

India's bond market is a club for the highest-rated borrowers, because the biggest buyers are allowed to buy only AA and above. Everyone else borrows from banks. That is why bank-loan ratings, not bond ratings, make up most of the rated companies in India, and why the bond market's growth is capped until lower-rated borrowers can sell bonds too.

The demand drivers, ranked

  • Bank credit to rated-size borrowers. The dominant driver of rated-entity count and bank-loan rating revenue: 15.9–16.1% growth in FY26 and 19.1% by August 2026. ICRA expects bank credit "to drive bulk of our growth" in FY27.
  • Bond and commercial-paper issuance. Higher fee per mandate; sensitive to the government-bond curve, bank pricing and regulation. CP rose 18.5% in Q1 FY27.
  • NBFC, HFC and microfinance funding. Financial issuers are about two-thirds of bond issuance and the core of CP and securitisation; NBFC securitisation grew 25% in FY26.
  • Infrastructure, InvITs, REITs and municipal bonds. SEBI extended the expected-loss rating scale to municipal bonds in FY26; CRISIL projects infrastructure capex to double by fiscal 2030.
  • Regulatory use-cases. RBI's capital rules (with a sharper edge from April 2027), insurer and pension investment limits, mutual-fund limits, and since January 2026 ratings of instruments under other financial regulators.
  • The surveillance stock. 1,942 outstanding ratings on listed securities at March 2026 (SEBI) and many thousands of bank-loan ratings, each paying an annual fee.
  • Credit quality. With banking gross NPAs near 2%, defaults are rare and upgrades outnumber downgrades, a reputational tailwind. Stress brings non-cooperating issuers, withdrawals and unpaid fees.

The structure has a subtle consequence for the agencies' rated universes. Crisil Ratings' default study notes that its median rating moved from BB to BBB in FY22, and about 73% of its ~7,300 rated companies were investment grade by March 2026, against about 24% in March 2013. That is partly better credit and partly selection: many sub-investment-grade borrowers stopped cooperating with their raters once banks' thresholds for using external ratings rose, so they dropped out of the rated universe. RBI's 2027 rules push the other way, with a 150% risk weight on unrated exposures above ₹500 crore and penalties on non-cooperating ratings, which should pull some weaker borrowers back into the rated universe from FY28. That is volume for the bank-loan raters, but at the riskier end of the scale, where default records are weakest (INDICATIVE inference).

The bond market's under-penetration is the long-run opportunity. If corporate bonds rose from about 16–17% of GDP toward 25%, the stock of rated bonds would grow far faster than GDP for a decade, and with it the surveillance base. The obstacles are well known: thin secondary-market liquidity, the AA-and-above investment rules that shut out lower-rated issuers, and the ease of bank borrowing for top companies. SEBI's chairman has spoken repeatedly about deepening the market; CareEdge's June 2026 study asks whether it "can finance India's growth". For the rating agencies, the drivers that matter in any given year remain the bank-bond price gap and the credit cycle, but the direction of travel, toward more market-based debt, is in their favour. Eight licences, two tiers: who rates India

Three of the four leading agencies belong to the global big three. In listed bonds investors pick the rater; in small-company loans, borrowers shop on price.

SEBI had eight registered credit rating agencies at 31 March 2026: Crisil Ratings, ICRA, India Ratings & Research, CARE Ratings, Acuité, Infomerics, Brickwork and Acer Credit Rating, the newest, registered on 21 August 2025. (Our earlier CRISIL primer said nine; eight is correct.) RBI's 2026 list of agencies whose ratings banks may use for capital has seven domestic names, all but Acer, plus CareEdge Global IFSC for non-resident corporates and the three global agencies. The licence is the entry ticket, but it is not what separates the winners.

The first striking feature is ownership. The global big three own three of India's four leading agencies: S&P controls CRISIL (66.64%), Moody's controls ICRA (51.86%) and Fitch owns India Ratings outright. Why two tiers? Investor preference versus price shopping

The issuer pays, but in the listed bond market the investor decides which ratings matter. A mutual fund or insurer that knows an agency's history will price a bond more keenly if that agency rated it, and a borrower that wants the tightest spread pays for the investor's preferred rating. CRISIL's annual report attributes its leadership in corporate bonds to "investor and issuer preference" for quality ratings. The revenue arithmetic supports that: Crisil Ratings earns about ₹9.2 lakh per rated company a year against about ₹7.5 lakh at CARE (INDICATIVE averages that blend bank-loan, bond and structured mandates), a premium of roughly 23% for the investor-preferred rater.

In small-company bank-loan ratings the dynamic is the opposite. The borrower needs a rating only so its bank can use it, the bank cares mainly that the agency is on RBI's eligible list, and the cheapest acceptable rating wins. This is where "rating shopping" risk is highest, and where defaults cluster: in FY25 Acuité and Infomerics together accounted for 9 of the 22 defaults on listed-security ratings that SEBI recorded, and Brickwork's licence was cancelled in 2022 before an appeal tribunal restored it with curbs. The tail earns thin fees on small mandates and carries most of the regulatory risk. RBI's 2027 framework, which ties risk weights to each agency's default record, is likely to widen the gap between the tiers (Section 07).

Downgrades tell a related story. Of 84 downgrades of listed-security ratings in FY26, CARE and India Ratings had 20 each, CRISIL 14 and ICRA 12. Upgrades (149) outnumbered downgrades by 1.8 to 1, a benign credit environment. A higher downgrade count is not automatically bad: an agency that downgrades early, before a default, is doing its job. What investors punish is the cliff-edge, a rating that stays at AA until weeks before a default, as happened with IL&FS in 2018 and DHFL in 2019 (Section 10).

The research-side competition

CRISIL's larger business faces a completely different competitive set. In research and risk outsourcing, its rivals are specialist providers such as Acuity Knowledge Partners (formerly part of Moody's), Evalueserve and eClerx, and the financial-services arms of Genpact and WNS, which our BPO and KPO and IT services reports cover. In benchmarking, it competes with consulting firms and data vendors. The most powerful competitor is the client's own captive centre: virtually every bulge-bracket bank runs a research and risk centre in India. And the data and AI platforms (Bloomberg, LSEG, FactSet, Moody's Analytics, S&P Market Intelligence itself) keep moving into territory that used to need an analyst. There is no licence protecting this business, which is why its margins are a third of the rating licence's.

Five business models, five very different margins

The same word, "rating agency", covers a 60%-margin licence, a cost-plus captive and a 17%-margin research exporter. CRISIL is the only listed company that runs all five models.

To value a company in this sub-sector you first have to decompose it into business models, because each model has its own margin, capital need and growth driver. A single price-to-earnings multiple applied to CRISIL as if it were ICRA or CARE will mislead. The table below sets out the five archetypes that exist in Indian ratings and research.

The rating that sets the risk weight: RBI's 2027 Standardised Approach Deep dive

From 1 April 2027 a bank's capital charge will depend on the rating agency's own default record. It is the biggest change to Indian rating economics since Basel II, and the relief it promises is not assured.

On 27 April 2026 RBI issued the Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks – Capital Charge for Credit Risk – Standardised Approach) Directions, 2026, replacing a draft of 7 October 2025. They take effect on 1 April 2027. For banks they rewrite the risk weights used to calculate capital on corporate loans. For rating agencies they do something new: they make the quality of an agency's past ratings a number that changes a bank's capital, and therefore its lending price, from one agency to the next. Our private banks, PSU banks and small finance banks reports cover the effect on lenders; this section covers what it means for the raters.

What changes: lower base weights

For long-term ratings of corporates, the base risk weights fall at three grades. AA moves from 30% to 20%, the same as AAA. BBB moves from 100% to 75%. BB moves from 150% to 100%. A stays at 50% and anything below BB at 150%. Unrated corporate exposures carry 100%, but if the borrower's aggregate exposure across the banking system exceeds ₹500 crore, the weight rises to 150%; the same threshold applies to unrated MSMEs. Short-term ratings run from 20% (A1+/A1) to 150% (A4/D). On its January 2026 reading of the draft, Crisil Ratings estimated that the lower weights could release about ₹71,000 crore of bank capital on a base of about ₹4.35 lakh crore, and cut lending rates for qualifying AA borrowers by up to about 50 basis points a year, provided every agency qualified.

The catch: each agency's observed default rate

The base weights apply only if the rating agency's track record at that grade is good enough. Paragraph 27(2) and Table 21 of the Directions say the weights are "adjusted for the average one-year ODR for each rating category published by the respective ECRAs". ODR is the observed default rate: the share of borrowers rated at a grade at the start of a year that defaulted within the year. If an agency's average one-year ODR for a grade exceeds RBI's ceiling, loans rated at that grade by that agency move one risk-weight bucket up. An AA rating from an agency whose AA ODR is above 0.10% would carry 50%, the A weight, instead of 20%.

Why the relief is not assured

A worked example: what a qualifying rating is worth to a bank

Take an ILLUSTRATIVE ₹1,000 crore loan to an AA-rated company, with the bank holding capital at a 12% ratio and needing a pre-tax return of about 20% on that capital (roughly a 15% post-tax return on equity). If the rating agency qualifies at AA, the risk weight is 20%, so risk-weighted assets are ₹200 crore and capital is ₹24 crore, costing about ₹4.8 crore a year, or 48 basis points on the loan. Under the old rules (30%), the same loan tied up ₹36 crore of capital, costing about 72 basis points. If the agency's AA default rate breaches the ceiling, the weight moves to 50%: ₹60 crore of capital and about 120 basis points. If the borrower were unrated and its system-wide exposure exceeded ₹500 crore, 150% would apply: ₹180 crore of capital, about 360 basis points.

What it means for the agencies

  • Rating quality becomes a commercial differentiator. The race will be fought at BBB, where mid-corporate bank-loan volume lives. CRISIL looks clean at AAA–A on every window; CARE claims the best short-term default rates at AAA, AA and A. Agencies with weak records in the long tail risk losing bank-loan mandates.
  • Bank-loan ratings get stickier. The ₹500 crore unrated threshold and the INC penalties make walking away from a rating costly. Expect more ratings and fewer withdrawals.
  • Lower-grade volume may return. Borrowers that left the rated universe because external ratings did not help them now face 150% if unrated above the threshold, which should pull some back from FY28 (INDICATIVE).
  • The headline relief is an upper bound. Crisil Ratings' ₹71,000 crore estimate assumed every agency qualifies. On the final text, with BB breaches everywhere and BBB depending on the window, it is a ceiling, not a forecast.
  • Compliance cost rises. Ratings must name the bank and facility, appear in a monthly bulletin and be reviewed within 15 months, or the exposure counts as unrated.

Until now an Indian bank treated every eligible agency's AA the same. From April 2027 it will check each agency's AA track record first. SEBI's rulebook: from licence conditions to outcome metrics

SEBI licenses the agencies and polices their conduct; since IL&FS it has increasingly measured their results. In 2026 it widened what they may rate.

The SEBI (Credit Rating Agencies) Regulations, 1999 set the rules of entry and conduct: who may register, minimum net worth, the rating process, conflicts of interest and restrictions on rating instruments of promoters and associates. Over 27 years SEBI has layered on disclosure and default-recognition rules, and the direction of travel is clear. Early rules governed how agencies were structured. Later rules govern how their ratings perform.

Five eras of Indian ratings, 1987 to 2026

Liberalisation created the agencies, global parents bought them, IL&FS humbled them and a credit boom restored them. 2026 opened a sixth chapter: regulation that scores them.

Indian credit ratings are almost exactly as old as Indian financial liberalisation. CRISIL was incorporated in 1987 as the country's first rating agency, promoted by ICICI and UTI with other financial institutions, and assigned its first ratings the following year. ICRA followed in 1991 and CARE in 1993. The agencies were born to serve markets that liberalisation was opening: commercial paper (for which RBI made ratings mandatory in 1990), company fixed deposits and, later, corporate bonds. The industry's history since then falls into five eras, each with its own lesson for investors.

Era 1, 1987–1995: founding

The early agencies were creatures of India's development-finance institutions, which owned them and supplied much of their early business. Ratings were a new idea, demand came from regulation (CP, deposits) more than from investors, and fees were small. CRISIL listed on Indian exchanges in the early 1990s. The habit of ownership by financial institutions, later replaced by global parents at CRISIL and ICRA, still shapes CARE, whose largest historical holders were Indian banks and institutions.

Era 2, 1996–2014: globalisation and the bank-loan rating

In 1996 Duff & Phelps began rating in India, later becoming Fitch India and, from 2013, India Ratings & Research. S&P took majority control of CRISIL in 2005, and CRISIL had already begun its move into research outsourcing by buying Irevna, an equity-research outsourcer, in 2004–05. Basel II, adopted by RBI in 2007–08, created the bank-loan rating, and with it a much larger rated universe; SMERA (now Acuité, 2005) and Brickwork (2007) were set up to serve small companies. CRISIL bought Coalition, the London-based investment-bank benchmarking firm, on 3 July 2012 (not 2013, as our earlier primer said), and Moody's lifted its ICRA holding to about half through a 2014 open offer. By 2014 three of India's four leading agencies belonged to the global big three.

Era 3, 2015–2021: peak margins, then IL&FS

The middle of the decade was the industry's golden age for margins. CARE earned operating margins of 61–63% in FY15–18 and net profit of about ₹162 crore in FY18. Then, in September 2018, IL&FS defaulted on debt rated AAA and AA, followed by DHFL in 2019 and a string of other failures. The agencies were penalised, two chief executives left, and CARE's revenue and margin collapsed. CRISIL's rating business was largely untouched; it kept growing through research and bought Greenwich Associates on 26 February 2020. Section 10 tells this story in detail because it explains much of today's market structure.

Era 4, 2021–2025: recovery and re-rating

Post-COVID credit growth, a surge of NBFC bond issuance (helped by RBI's November 2023 increase in risk weights on bank loans to NBFCs, rolled back in April 2025) and record bond issuance in FY25 restored the industry. CARE's revenue grew 21% in FY25 and its operating margin recovered to 39–42%. ICRA grew 12–20% a year. CRISIL grew 20% in CY22, stalled in CY24 when global banks cut spending, and grew 12% in CY25. All three agencies rebranded: CRISIL into Crisil Ratings, Crisil Intelligence, Crisil Coalition Greenwich and Crisil Integral IQ in 2024–25; CARE into CareEdge; ICRA unveiled a new identity in July 2026.

Era 5, 2026: bonds down, bank credit up, and outcome-based regulation

The current era began with the West Asia war on 28 February 2026, which pushed the ten-year government-bond yield to nearly 7%, and continued with RBI's rate hike to 5.50% on 7 October. Bond issuance fell, bank credit hit 19% and the agencies grew 15–29% (Section 03). RBI finalised its ODR-linked risk weights in April, SEBI widened the licence in January, ICRA consolidated Fintellix and moved D2K and Fintellix to full ownership, CRISIL closed the PriceMetrix acquisition, and CARE agreed in October to sell 19.8% of its GIFT City subsidiary to SBI and NSE IFSC.

The IL&FS scar: how one default rewired Indian ratings Deep dive

A AAA-rated group defaulted in weeks. Margins collapsed, chief executives left, SEBI rewrote the disclosure rules, and the bond market moved toward the agency it trusted most.

In September 2018 Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services, a large infrastructure lender and developer whose debt carried AAA and AA ratings, began missing payments. Within weeks its ratings fell to D. For investors who held the paper, including mutual funds, provident funds and insurers bound by AA-and-above rules, the lesson was brutal: the rating had told them almost nothing about the risk until it was too late. For the rating industry it was the defining event of the past three decades, and it explains three features of today's market: the depth of SEBI's disclosure rules, the gap between CRISIL and its rivals in listed bonds, and why RBI now wants to see each agency's default rates before it grants capital relief.

What went wrong

The detailed failures were investigated at length, but their shape is familiar from rating crises everywhere. The ratings leaned heavily on expectations of support from a large, systemically important group rather than on the standalone ability of each entity to pay. They underweighted the liquidity mismatch of a group funding long-term infrastructure assets with short-term debt. And they were slow to move: ratings stayed high until default was imminent, then fell several notches at once. Under the issuer-pays model, an agency that downgrades a large, frequent issuer early risks losing it to a competitor; IL&FS showed the cost of that incentive when it is not checked. DHFL in 2019, and the problems at Reliance Capital, Altico and the Yes Bank AT1 bonds, extended the damage across 2019–20. Each produced the same pattern of late, multi-notch downgrades, which investors and regulators came to call the "cliff-edge".

In plain terms

A rating is supposed to fall gradually as a borrower weakens, giving investors time to sell or demand a higher interest rate. With IL&FS the rating stayed at the top until the money ran out, then dropped to default almost at once. An early warning that arrives on the day of the default is not a warning, and the market stopped trusting the agencies that gave it.

The price the agencies paid

SEBI fined ICRA, CARE and India Ratings ₹25 lakh each in December 2019 over the IL&FS ratings and later raised the penalties to ₹1 crore each. Those sums were trivial against the agencies' profits. The leadership consequences were not: ICRA's chief executive was sent on leave and left in 2019, and CARE's managing director and chief executive was removed the same year. The commercial consequences were the largest of all, and they show in CARE's accounts, the cleanest example because CARE is a pure rating agency with no research business to cushion it.

Why CRISIL gained

CRISIL came through the crisis with its rating franchise intact. Its revenue grew from ₹1,748 crore in CY18 to ₹1,982 crore in CY20, mainly through research, but the more lasting gain was in listed bonds. When investors became wary of ratings in general, they leaned toward the agency they trusted most, and issuers followed because an investor-preferred rating lowers the coupon. CRISIL now assigns 46.8% of fresh ratings on listed securities. Its default record in that market is not spotless: both of its two FY26 defaults on listed-security ratings came from investment grade, a watch item, and its SEBI long-run BBB default rate is fractionally over RBI's ceiling. But the gap between its share of listed-bond ratings (47%) and CARE's (4%) is, to a large degree, a legacy of how each agency was perceived after 2018.

In plain terms

After a scandal, the whole industry loses some trust, but the best-trusted agency loses the least and gains share. That is what happened in India after IL&FS. The agency judged most reliable by investors took the bond market; the others recovered by leaning on bank-loan ratings, where investors have less say.

The rules the scar left behind

SEBI's response came in a circular of 13 June 2019 on enhanced disclosures. Rating press releases had to state a liquidity indicator (strong, adequate, stretched or poor) and list the factors that would trigger an upgrade or downgrade. Each agency had to publish cumulative default rates by grade against probability-of-default benchmarks set by SEBI, so that a regulator or investor could see whether an agency's AA was behaving like an AA. A further circular in August 2022 required default rates with and without non-cooperating issuers, closing a loophole in which an agency could flatter its record by excluding borrowers that stopped sharing data. These rules were consolidated in the Master Circular of July 2025, and they produce exactly the data RBI now uses for its 2027 risk-weight test. In that sense the IL&FS scar has become part of the capital rules.

The default record has improved markedly. Defaults among ratings of listed securities halved from 22 in FY25 to 11 in FY26, and defaults from investment grade fell from 11 to 3. Crisil Ratings' annual default rate across its whole portfolio was 0.7% in FY26 (38 defaults), the lowest in 17 years. Part of that is a benign credit cycle, with banking NPAs near 2%; part is the agencies' more cautious practice since 2019; and part is selection, as weaker borrowers left the rated universe. The test of whether the scar has truly healed will come in the next downturn, when the incentive to keep a large issuer's rating high meets the new disclosure regime.

Reading a rater: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain a rating and research company. For each: what it is, how to compute it, a worked example, what moves it, a benchmark, how CRISIL and the reference peers compare, and the trap.

The difficulty is that the most important drivers, such as how many mandates were won, at what fee, and how much of revenue is annual surveillance, are not disclosed by any Indian agency, and that the listed companies mix rating and research businesses with very different economics. In plain terms

Rating revenue is a count of customers multiplied by what each pays, and most customers pay every year without a new sale. Rating cost is a team of analysts. KPI 5 · Share of fresh listed ratings

What it is. An agency's share of the fresh ratings assigned to listed or to-be-listed securities, as counted by SEBI. Formula: agency's fresh ratings ÷ all fresh ratings. Worked example: 339 ÷ 725 = 46.8% for CRISIL in FY26. What drives it: investor preference, which is to say reputation in the bond market, plus relationships with frequent issuers such as NBFCs and public-sector financiers. Benchmark: above 35% is dominant, 15–35% a strong player, below 10% marginal in bonds. Comparison: CRISIL 46.8%, ICRA 20.1%, India Ratings 19.3%, Acuité about 6%, CARE about 4%, Infomerics about 3%, Brickwork about 1%.

The trap. It covers listed securities only, a fraction of rated entities, and it counts ratings, not revenue. CARE's 4% here sits alongside its claim of 24–25% of incremental rating business by count and volume. Our earlier primer's ">60% share" for CRISIL has no source; the verifiable figure is 46.8% of fresh listed ratings.

What moves this share from year to year? Mostly the issuer mix. Frequent financial issuers, such as NBFCs, housing financiers and public-sector lenders, dominate listed issuance and tend to stay with the agency whose rating their investors prefer, so a year in which NBFCs issue heavily favours the bond-market leader. A year in which more first-time corporates and smaller NBFCs come to market spreads mandates more widely. Regulation can move it too: if banks begin to prefer agencies that pass RBI's ODR test, bank-loan mandates may follow the same pattern as listed bonds. Watch the SEBI annual report each August for the update.

KPI 6 · Default rates by grade, and rating stability

What it is. The share of borrowers rated at a grade that default within one year (observed default rate, ODR) or within several years (cumulative default rate, CDR), plus the share of ratings unchanged after a year (stability). Formula: defaults within the horizon ÷ ratings at the start of each cohort, averaged across cohorts. Worked example: on SEBI's long-run window (FY16–26), Crisil Ratings' one-year rate is 0.01% at AAA, 0.06% at AA, 0.09% at A, 0.42% at BBB and 1.97% at BB; ICRA's ten-year average is 0.2% at AAA and 1.0% at BBB. What drives it: the credit cycle, the agency's analytical caution, how quickly it downgrades, and the composition of its book. Benchmark: comfortably below RBI's Table 21 ceilings (AAA ≤0.05%, AA ≤0.10%, A ≤0.20%, BBB ≤0.40%, BB ≤1.0%) is good; at the ceiling is average; above it is weak, and from April 2027 it costs the agency's clients capital. Stability above 80% is good; Crisil's AAA one-year stability is 99.03% and BBB 91.64%.

The trap. There are three different "default rates": SEBI's count of defaults among listed-security ratings (11 in FY26, CRISIL 2); each agency's published CDRs by grade and horizon; and RBI's average one-year ODR used for risk weights. They use different universes and windows. A low SEBI count does not mean an agency passes RBI's test, and RBI has not said which averaging window it will use (Section 07).

A worked ODR calculation (ILLUSTRATIVE). Suppose an agency started each of the last ten years with about 500 BBB-rated borrowers, 5,000 borrower-years in all, and 21 of them defaulted within twelve months of the cohort date. Its average one-year BBB ODR is 21 ÷ 5,000 = 0.42%, just above RBI's 0.40% ceiling, so every BBB loan it rates would move one bucket up: 100% instead of 75%. Had one of those 21 defaults been recognised a month later, outside the twelve-month window, the rate would be 0.40% and the agency would pass. That is how thin the margin is at the grade where most mid-corporate bank-loan ratings sit, and why the treatment of borrowers that stop cooperating, and the choice of window, matter so much. It also explains the incentive problem: an agency close to the ceiling has a reason to be conservative when assigning BBB, which over time pushes borderline borrowers down to BB, where the ceiling is breached anyway.

In plain terms

An agency's default table is its report card. AAA should almost never default within a year; BB defaults much more often. What matters is whether each grade behaves like its label. From 2027 that report card decides how much capital a bank must hold against loans the agency rates.

KPI 15 · Cash conversion and debtor days

What it is. Operating cash flow as a share of EBITDA, and trade receivables expressed in days of revenue. What drives it: client mix and payment terms, the timing of surveillance billing, tax payments and working-capital swings around year-end renewals. Benchmark: conversion above 100% good, 85–100% average, below 85% weak; debtor days below 45 good, 45–75 average, above 75 weak. Comparison: ICRA 103% and 48 days; CARE 107% and 27 days (FY26). The trap. Rising debtor days at a domestic rater can be an early sign of stress: borrowers that delay paying their rating fee are often the same ones heading for non-cooperation or downgrade. At CRISIL, by contrast, a few days' movement usually reflects the timing of large research invoices, not credit quality. Free cash flow at CRISIL (₹688 crore in CY25) comfortably covers its dividend (₹446 crore), which is the practical test of whether the payout is sustainable.

Definition traps, collected

  • Calendar vs fiscal year. CRISIL's own filings call January–December 2026 "FY2026"; ICRA's and CARE's FY26 is April 2025 to March 2026. Never put CRISIL CY25 and ICRA FY26 in one column without saying so.
  • Standalone vs consolidated. CRISIL standalone excludes Crisil Ratings Ltd and includes subsidiary dividends (₹272 crore from Crisil Ratings in CY25). Use consolidated.
  • Segment vs agency. CRISIL's Ratings segment (₹1,078.7 crore) = Crisil Ratings Ltd (₹670.6 crore) + GAC (~₹390–410 crore) + a small residual.
  • "Research" means three things. CRISIL's RAS, ICRA's Risk & Analytics (KnowTech, Fintellix, D2K) and CARE's small non-ratings segment are different businesses.
  • Four counts of activity. Rated entities, outstanding ratings, fresh ratings and rated instruments are different numbers.
  • Three default rates. SEBI's listed-default count, each agency's CDR by grade, and RBI's average one-year ODR use different universes and windows.
  • Three bond-issuance series. SEBI listed private placements (FY26 ₹9.00 lakh crore, −8.8%), Prime Database fiscal (₹10.8 lakh crore, −3.2%) and Prime calendar (CY25 ₹11.2 lakh crore, flat). Name the basis every time.
  • National vs global scale. An Indian AAA is not a global AAA.
  • Payout bases. CRISIL's CY25 payout is 58.2% on the company's DPS and share count; screener shows 57%.
  • Margin bases. Segment profit, segment result, EBITDA and PBT margins are not interchangeable.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in analysing Indian raters come from comparing things that only look alike: a calendar year with a fiscal year, a segment with a company, one kind of default rate with another. Check the basis before you compare the number.

How to read a rating company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the period and the basis. Is it CRISIL's calendar quarter or a fiscal quarter? Consolidated? Note the result date (CRISIL Q3 CY26: 23 October 2026).
  2. Ratings revenue growth. Segment and, if given, the agency alone (CRISIL Q3: +28.9% and +25.6%). Above 15% is strong; compare with bank credit and bond issuance in the same quarter.
  3. Ratings margin, year on year. Same quarter last year, not the previous quarter (CRISIL Q3: 44.9% vs 45.0%). A fall of more than three points with rising revenue is a red flag.
  4. Research revenue and margin. Growth, and margin against the same quarter (17.2% vs 21.9%). Ask what is acquisition, what is FX and what is organic.
  5. The cost lines. Employee cost as a share of revenue (54.2%), professional and associate fees (+37% YoY in Q3), D&A (+38%). Find where the margin went.
  6. FX gain or loss. ₹2.3 crore vs ₹15.5 crore. Strip it to see the underlying trend.
  7. Unallocated items. Amortisation of acquired intangibles and treasury income. Reconcile segment profit to PBT.
  8. Tax rate. A swing (22.6% in Q2 CY26, 25.9% in Q3) can flatter or depress profit growth.
  9. Market commentary. Bond issuance, issuer counts and bank credit from the presentation; for CRISIL, any comment on Coalition Greenwich renewals, the GAC and generative AI.
  10. Dividend and red flags. Interim dividend (₹17, record date 29 October 2026); then look for default counts, regulatory orders, contingent tax claims (CRISIL: ₹779.8 crore disputed direct tax at December 2025) and related-party growth.
In plain terms

For CRISIL, the ten-minute read of Q3 CY26 is: ratings excellent, research soft, amortisation and FX explain the rest, no change to the dividend habit. The question to carry into the February 2027 results is whether research margins recover toward 22% or settle near 17%.

Why the global giants do not rate India directly

In most large markets S&P and Moody's rate borrowers directly on the global scale. In India they operate through local affiliates on the Indian national scale, for three reasons. Indian regulation requires a SEBI-registered agency for domestic issues. Almost every Indian borrower is rated only on the national scale, which ranks Indian credits against each other rather than against the world; on the global scale, India's sovereign sits in the BBB category, and Indian corporates are rarely rated above it. And Indian fee levels, set by a market where most debt is bank loans, would not support a global agency's cost base. CareEdge has pointed out that rating distributions in India and China are far more concentrated in AAA and AA than in the US or UK, where ratings are benchmarked to the international scale. An Indian AAA means "among the safest Indian borrowers", not "as safe as a US Treasury".

In plain terms

The global agencies own India's leading raters rather than competing with them, because Indian borrowers need a rating on an Indian yardstick. That makes CRISIL and ICRA the local franchises of a global duopoly: they share its methods and reputation but earn Indian-sized fees.

Scale, in perspective

CRISIL's entire trailing revenue, ₹4,310 crore or about US$454 million, is about 9.6% of S&P Ratings' revenue and about 2.8% of S&P Global's. Crisil Ratings Ltd, at about US$71 million, is roughly 1.5% of S&P Ratings. Morningstar DBRS alone is about 1.8 times the whole Indian rating industry. Measured against GDP or bond-market size, India's rating pool is small because its bond market is small and its fees are national-scale. That is the long-run opportunity: if Indian corporate bonds grow toward the 36–40% of GDP seen in China and the US, the rating pool could grow much faster than credit for many years. It is also why the global parents keep their Indian affiliates: India is one of the few large markets where rating revenue can still compound at double digits.

Technology and disruption: AI is a tool for raters and a threat to researchers

A regulated opinion with a named committee is hard to automate. An hour of first-draft research is not.

Generative AI affects the two industries in this sub-sector in opposite ways. For rating agencies it is mainly a productivity tool. A rating is a regulated opinion issued by a named agency through a committee that is accountable to SEBI and, from 2027, scored by RBI; neither regulator recognises a model as a rating agency. AI can spread financial statements, monitor news and early-warning signals across thousands of rated companies, draft rationales and speed up surveillance, which means fewer analyst hours per rating and higher margins for incumbents. For research outsourcing, AI is a direct threat to the product: the generic tasks that outsourcers sell by the hour (data extraction, first-draft reports, model documentation) are exactly what large language models do well.

In plain terms

AI will let a rating agency rate more companies with the same analysts, which is good for margins. It will also let a global bank do more of its own research without hiring an outsourcer, which is bad for CRISIL's larger business. The question for CRISIL is whether its benchmarks and specialist risk work grow fast enough to offset the generic work that AI takes away.

Other technology and structural threats

  • Bank captive centres. Nearly every large global bank runs a research and risk centre in India. CRISIL argues these create demand when they set up centres of excellence or need regulatory remediation; over the long run they cap third-party pricing. Our BPO and KPO and IT services reports cover the same shift.
  • Data and platform disintermediation. Moody's private-credit models, AI assistants fed with rating data and the data platforms of Bloomberg, LSEG and S&P Market Intelligence blur the line between rating agency, data vendor and analyst.
  • The parent's own strategy. S&P could move GAC work into its own India operations (S&P Capital IQ India already exists) or automate it. So far the flow has run the other way, with S&P billings up 22% in CY25.
  • New entrants. AI lowers the cost of analysis, but not the cost of a licence, a default history and investor trust. Acer, registered in 2025, had no share of fresh listed ratings in FY26.
  • Cyber and data security. Rating agencies hold unpublished, price-sensitive information; a leak is a regulatory offence and a reputational event.

For investors, the asymmetry matters. The rating licence is likely to become more profitable with AI, because the binding constraints (regulation, accountability, reputation) are not technological. The research engine faces a genuine contest between AI-driven productivity gains, which CRISIL can keep as margin if it moves fast, and AI-driven price deflation, which clients will demand. Management concedes the risk exists but frames AI as a net opportunity. The next two years of RAS margins will show which effect is winning.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian ratings, and CRISIL

The rating franchise looks stronger than ever; the research engine and the valuation carry the risk.

The bull and bear cases for this sub-sector diverge on one question: does the rating agency's structural advantage (a growing debt market, regulation that rewards quality, a sticky annuity) outweigh the cyclical and competitive pressures on the research business that makes up most of CRISIL? Both cases rest on the same facts; they weigh them differently.

The bull case

  • A long runway. Corporate bonds at 16–17% of GDP against 36–40% in China and the US; ₹61.5 lakh crore outstanding and compounding at about 11% a year.
  • A sticky annuity. Surveillance on a record 2024–25 mandate book; INC penalties and the ₹500 crore unrated threshold from April 2027 make ratings stickier.
  • Regulation rewards the leaders. RBI's ODR test turns a clean default record into a price advantage; CRISIL is clean at AAA–A on every window.
  • Investor preference. 46.8% of fresh listed ratings and a ~23% fee premium per rated company over CARE (INDICATIVE).
  • A delayed currency tailwind. CY27 hedges should be struck well above CY26's ₹88.84; the rupee is near ₹95.
  • S&P keeps sending work. Related-party billings up 22% in CY25; the GAC is low-risk cost-plus revenue.
  • Capital-light compounding. No debt, ~₹1,010 crore of treasury, 58% payout, ROCE above 30%.

The bear case

  • The lag turns. 2026's weaker bond mandates feed 2027 surveillance; if bank credit slows from 19%, rating growth could slow sharply (INDICATIVE).
  • The research margin. 17.2% in Q3 CY26 against 21.9%; GenAI deflation, bank captives and vendor consolidation are real.
  • ODR relief may disappoint. CRISIL's BBB is over the ceiling on SEBI's long window; BB breaches everywhere; RBI has not fixed the window.
  • Parent concentration. S&P is ~13% of revenue and rising, controls strategy and takes ₹287.5 crore of dividends a year.
  • Tax contingencies. ₹779.8 crore of disputed direct tax (Dec-25), up 20% in a year.
  • Valuation. 38.8 times trailing earnings, above both S&P (27.4×) and Moody's (29.5×), against profit growth of about 11% a year over CY22–25.
  • Reputation is binary. One large default at a high grade could undo years of share gains, as IL&FS did for CARE.

The sensitivities show where the leverage lies. A 10% swing in domestic rating revenue moves group profit before tax by about 4.5%; a one-point move in the research margin by about 2.5%; a 1% move in the rupee by about 2% before hedges. The research margin, which fell nearly five points year on year in Q3, is therefore the single most powerful lever on CRISIL's earnings in the near term, more powerful than the rating boom. That is why the bear case concentrates there.

In plain terms

The bull case says India's credit market and RBI's new rules make the best rating agencies more valuable every year, and CRISIL owns the best one. The bear case says CRISIL is mostly a research company whose margins are falling, priced as if they will not. Both can be true at once; the price decides which matters more.

For the wider industry, the balance is more favourable than for CRISIL's stock. The domestic rating pool should keep growing faster than nominal GDP as long as bank credit stays strong and the bond market resumes growth, and RBI's 2027 rules should concentrate bank-loan mandates in the agencies with clean records. The main industry-wide risks are a sharp credit slowdown, which would hit both new mandates and, a year later, surveillance; a default scandal at a large agency; and an RBI choice of a long ODR window that strips the capital relief from BBB loans.
