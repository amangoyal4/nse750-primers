00

The one-page view: steel moves the revenue, the spread moves the profit

Eight companies, one input, five different businesses, and a year that split the group cleanly into winners and losers.

The eight companies in this report do not make steel. They buy it, as hot-rolled coil, plate, round billets, wire rod, or as iron ore and coke that they melt themselves, and turn it into something a customer will pay more for: a square hollow section for a warehouse roof, a 48-inch line pipe for a Texas gas field, a cement-lined iron main for a village water scheme, a seamless casing string for an ONGC well, a duplex stainless tube for a refinery heat exchanger, or a mining-crane rope. What they earn is the gap between what the product sells for and what the steel inside it cost. This report calls that gap the conversion spread, and almost everything that matters about these companies follows from how wide it is, how stable it is, and who controls it.

That framing matters because it is the opposite of how steel-makers work. A steel mill's profit rises and falls with the steel price. A converter's revenue rises and falls with the steel price, because the steel is passed through, but its profit should not, as long as it can reprice its product as fast as its input moves. The cleanest proof sits in the last twelve months. Mumbai hot-rolled coil went from ₹54,760 a tonne at its December 2025 trough to ₹76,900 in September 2026, a 40% rise. Over the same period APL Apollo, the largest company in this group, earned between roughly ₹4,700 and ₹5,500 of operating profit per tonne of tube in every quarter: ₹5,161 for FY26 as a whole and ₹5,522 in Q1 FY27. The steel price swung by a third; the spread barely noticed. For the upstream story (mill prices, the safeguard duty, China's exports, coking coal) read the sibling report, Iron & Steel: the mills. This report starts where that one stops, at the mill gate.

Read with the sibling report. Steel prices, the 12% → 11% safeguard duty on flat steel, Chinese export pressure and coking-coal costs are covered in depth in the Iron & Steel sub-sector report. Here those are inputs. The question in this report is what a downstream converter keeps after paying for them.

The year that split the group

FY26 looked dull in aggregate and dramatic underneath. The eight companies' combined revenue was ₹84,061 crore, almost exactly the ₹84,182 crore of FY25. Combined operating profit (EBITDA before other income) fell 10% to ₹9,251 crore and combined net profit fell 15% to ₹5,896 crore. But the totals hide a clean split. APL Apollo's EBITDA per tonne rose 36% and its profit 59%. Welspun Corp posted record operating profit on the back of a US order book. Usha Martin's margin climbed from 17% to 19%. On the other side, the companies that sell water pipe to state governments had their worst year in a decade: Electrosteel Castings' ductile-iron pipe volume fell 25% and its profit 77%; Jindal SAW's profit fell 37% in FY26 and then 78% in Q1 FY27. Maharashtra Seamless drifted lower on a soft drilling cycle, and Ratnamani's carbon-steel pipe book went quiet.

Five businesses that share an input

"Steel products" is a classification of convenience. The eight companies share a raw material and very little else. Branded structural and galvanised tube (APL Apollo, and Surya Roshni's pipe arm) is a high-velocity dealer business with ₹4,000–6,000 of EBITDA per tonne. Large-diameter line pipe (Welspun Corp, Jindal SAW, part of Ratnamani) is an order-book project business where qualification keeps competitors out and plant loading decides the margin. Ductile-iron (DI) water pipe (Electrosteel, Jindal SAW, a new Welspun plant) is really integrated iron-making sold to governments. Seamless tube and oil-country casing (Maharashtra Seamless, Jindal SAW) lives off ONGC's drilling budget behind an anti-dumping wall. Specialty products (Ratnamani's stainless and nickel-alloy tubes, Usha Martin's wire ropes) earn margins of 17–20% on certified, safety-critical engineering. Each of these has a different customer, a different cycle and a different reason to exist, which is why the group's valuations range so widely.

The forces that define the sub-sector

Five forces explain nearly everything in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them.

  • The conversion spread, not the steel price. Profit equals tonnes times (realisation minus input steel minus conversion cost). Steel moves revenue; spread and volume move profit. Quarterly profits are distorted by inventory gains and losses whenever coil moves faster than product prices, by roughly ₹780 a tonne at APL for every ₹3,000 move in coil, and by far more at makers carrying six to nine months of stock (Section 06).
  • Government water spending sets the DI pipe cycle. The wind-down of the first phase of the Jal Jeevan Mission and slow state payments took Electrosteel's volume down 25% and Jai Balaji's DI plant utilisation to about 30%. JJM 2.0 (₹8.69 lakh crore to December 2028) is the recovery trigger, but orders had not visibly converted by Q1 FY27 (Section 08).
  • Global pipeline capex and trade walls decide line pipe. US Section 232 tariffs and anti-dumping duties shut Indian-made large-diameter pipe out of the US, which made Welspun's Arkansas plant a moat: a ~US$1.8 billion single order in August 2026 took its book to a record US$4.4 billion (~₹42,100 crore). The Hormuz shock froze Middle-East shipments for Jindal SAW, Ratnamani and Usha Martin at the same time (Sections 09 and 10).
  • Brand, qualification and integration are the only protection against commoditisation. APL's brand gives it a negative cash cycle and 32% ROCE. API licences and oil-major approvals protect line pipe, casing and alloy tube; when Jindal SAW lost its seamless API monogram, those sales stopped. Owning a blast furnace widens a DI maker's margin at full load and crushes it at 58% load (Sections 07, 11, 12).
  • Valuation follows predictability. Trailing P/E runs from 10.6× to 48.6× and price-to-book from 0.8× to 11.3×. The market pays for stable spreads, negative working capital and visible order books, and discounts government receivables, tender-priced commodities and treasury-heavy balance sheets (Section 19).
In plain terms

These companies are middlemen with machines. They buy steel at whatever the mills charge and sell a finished product at whatever their customers will pay. The best of them, like APL Apollo, have enough brand and scale to set their own selling price, so a steel-price surge flows straight through to customers and their profit per tonne stays steady. The weakest sell to one buyer (a state water board, ONGC) through a tender, so when that buyer stops ordering, the furnaces run half empty and profits collapse. That difference, more than steel prices, is why one trades at 48 times earnings and another below book value.

Two housekeeping notes. First, where this report converts dollar figures into rupees it uses ₹95.7 per US dollar, the rate implied by Welspun Corp's own conversion of its US$4.4 billion order book into about ₹42,100 crore on 21 August 2026; it sits inside the ₹95–96 range the rupee traded in during September–October 2026. Second, the group is concentrated: APL Apollo and Welspun Corp are 65% of the ₹1.97 lakh crore combined market value but only 47% of revenue and 48% of profit. Every aggregate in this report is therefore also shown without APL Apollo, so one company's multiple does not masquerade as an industry's. On that basis the group trades at 31.5× trailing earnings including APL and 27.4× without it.

01

Coil in, spread out: how a steel converter earns

The mill sells a commodity by the tonne. The converter forms, welds, pierces, casts, coats and certifies it, and is paid for whatever it adds.

Every product in this report starts as an intermediate steel product made by someone else, or, in the case of DI pipe, as iron ore and coke. The integrated mills (JSW Steel, Tata Steel, AM/NS India, SAIL, Jindal Steel; all covered in the Iron & Steel report) sell hot-rolled coil and plate for welded pipe and tube, round billets for seamless tube, and wire rod for rope. The converter then does one of four physical things. It forms and welds coil or plate into a tube (electric-resistance welding for small and medium sizes; submerged-arc welding for the large-diameter pipe used in oil and gas trunk lines). It pierces a solid hot round into a seamless tube with no weld at all. It casts molten iron centrifugally into a ductile-iron pipe. Or it draws and strands wire rod into rope. Then it tests the product (hydrostatic, ultrasonic, X-ray), coats it (zinc galvanising, three-layer polyethylene, epoxy, cement-mortar lining) and certifies it against the standard the buyer specifies.

Step 1 is mostly a pass-through. The converter's money is made in Steps 2–4: yield and scale in conversion, the certification that keeps rivals out, and the channel or contract that decides who sets the price.

Why the steel is a pass-through

For a welded tube or a line pipe, mill steel is 70–80% of the product's cost. No converter can earn a return on that slice: it buys coil at the mill's price and must sell the tube at a price that recovers it. What it can do is add value on top, and lose money when the steel price falls faster than it can lower its own selling price (or gain when the reverse happens). The useful mental model is a toll booth on a road whose traffic is steel tonnes. The toll is the spread; the steel is the vehicle passing through. A rise in steel prices makes the vehicles more expensive but does not change the toll, unless the booth has stock on hand whose value changes overnight.

Value is added in five places, and the companies differ precisely in which ones they own:

  • Scale and conversion cost. Mill utilisation, steel yield (how many tonnes of coil go into a tonne of tube; typically 1.03–1.06), and freight-optimised plant locations. APL Apollo's ~11 plants and its Direct Forming Technology (DFT), which saves 3–7% of steel per section, are the benchmark.
  • Brand and distribution. A brand lets a converter set rather than accept the spread. APL's ₹1,500-a-tonne general-segment price rise in January 2025 stuck, which a commodity pipe maker could not have done.
  • Qualification. API licences, oil-major approved-vendor lists, nuclear and power-utility approvals. These take years to earn and can be lost in a day.
  • Coating and finishing. Three-layer polyethylene, concrete weight coating, galvanising and colour coating add margin and stickiness.
  • Location inside a protected market, or backward integration. Welspun's US and Saudi plants sit behind tariff walls and local-content rules. DI pipe makers own blast furnaces and coke ovens, so their margin behaves like an iron-maker's raw-material spread.

In plain terms

Margin percentages mislead in this industry. Because the steel inside the product is passed through at cost, a tube maker's margin looks thin (8%) and a rope maker's looks fat (19%), but the tube maker turns its money over many times a year with almost no stock and gets paid before it pays its suppliers. What matters is profit per tonne and how much capital each tonne ties up, which is why this report leans on EBITDA per tonne and the cash conversion cycle rather than margin alone.

The pass-through has one awkward consequence that runs through every set of quarterly results: revenue becomes a poor guide to business momentum. When coil prices fell from FY25 into late 2025, the group's rupee revenue went sideways even as APL and Surya sold more tonnes. When coil rose 40% in 2026, revenues inflated without any extra activity. An analyst who tracks revenue growth in this sub-sector is mostly tracking the steel price. Tonnes, spread per tonne and order intake are the numbers to watch, and they are covered in the KPI masterclass in Section 15.

02

Five businesses, one input: the archetypes inside "steel products"

A dealer-led tube brand, a project pipe mill, a government-dependent iron foundry, a drilling supplier and two specialty engineers share a raw material and little else.

The best way to understand this group is to stop treating it as one industry. Each archetype has its own customer, its own way of setting price, its own working-capital profile and its own cycle. The table below sets them side by side; the paragraphs after it explain what makes each tick.

A. The branded structural tube: a consumer business made of steel

APL Apollo sells about 3.5 million tonnes a year of hollow sections, galvanised and pre-galvanised tube, coated products and roofing through roughly 800 distributors and 2.5 lakh retailers and fabricators. The ticket size is small, the product moves fast and the customer is a fabricator who wants a standard section delivered tomorrow. That creates the most unusual balance sheet in Indian steel: in FY26 APL's cash conversion cycle was minus 12 days. Distributors pay upfront, steel mills give credit, and inventory turns in under a month. Margin comes from climbing a mix ladder from plain black pipe through galvanised and coated products to heavy DFT sections that replace concrete columns and box plates. Value-added products were about 55% of APL's volume by Q4 FY26. Surya Roshni's pipe arm plays the same game with a more commodity-heavy mix (value-added products about 47% of steel volume) and a conventional 78-day cash cycle. The archetype's threats are the steel majors integrating forward (Tata Steel's Structura brand, JSW's downstream push) and regional unorganised mills that compete on price in the plain-pipe segment.

B. Large-diameter line pipe: a project business behind a qualification gate

Line pipe is bought one project at a time. A pipeline owner specifies the steel grade, wall thickness, coating and testing; mills that hold the right licences and approvals bid; and the winner usually buys plate against the contract, so the steel is passed through and the conversion fee is what is really bid. The order book gives nine to twenty-four months of visibility. The swing factor is plant loading: a large LSAW mill has high fixed costs, so a gap between orders destroys margin, while a full book lets it pick the best-priced work. Location now matters as much as cost. An Indian plant faces 50% US tariffs plus anti-dumping and countervailing duties on large-diameter welded pipe; a US plant is "domestic" and wins share from imports. That is why Welspun's FY26 working-capital days were minus 23 (big customer advances) while Jindal SAW's were plus 66 and rising.

C. DI pipe: an iron foundry with a single customer

A DI pipe maker is an integrated iron-maker. Iron ore and coke go into a blast furnace; hot metal is treated with magnesium so the graphite forms spheres (which makes the iron ductile rather than brittle); the metal is spun into a pipe in a centrifugal casting machine; and the pipe is cement-lined and zinc-coated. Margin depends on four things: the cost of ore and coking coal (shared with blast-furnace steel-makers, and coking coal reached US$278 a tonne in September 2026), the utilisation of a furnace that cannot be idled cheaply, the tender price, which collapses when the industry has spare capacity, and how quickly state governments pay. In FY26 all four went wrong together, which is why Electrosteel's inventory reached 276 days and its cash cycle 278 days.

D. Seamless and OCTG: hostage to the drill bit

Seamless tube is made by piercing a hot solid round and rolling it out, so there is no weld line to fail under pressure. The highest-value use is oil country casing and tubing, sold mainly to ONGC and Oil India. Demand follows India's upstream drilling budget, which follows crude prices and government policy. Chinese mills are the low-cost global producers, so India has protected seamless tube with anti-dumping duty since 2016. Maharashtra Seamless holds about 55% of the Indian market and has a second distinctive feature: ₹3,821 crore of investments against a ₹7,844 crore market value, so its FY26 other income (₹383 crore) was more than half its operating profit (₹669 crore).

E. Specialty engineered: the approval is the product

Ratnamani has about 61,500 tonnes a year of stainless and nickel-alloy tube capacity against roughly 5,10,000 tonnes of carbon-steel pipe. By tonnage it is a line-pipe company; by profit it is an alloy-tube specialist supplying heat exchangers, boilers, fertiliser plants, nuclear reactors, LNG trains and desalination plants, all of which buy only from approved vendors. Usha Martin sold its steel mill to Tata Steel in 2019 and kept a wire-rope business that sells to cranes, mines, elevators, offshore mooring and ropeways in dozens of countries; 57% of its revenue is international. Ropes wear out and are replaced on a schedule, so the business has a consumable-style replacement cycle under its new-equipment demand. Both earn 17–20% margins, and Usha's EBITDA per tonne (an estimated ₹34,000–41,000) is seven to eight times a structural-tube maker's.

In plain terms

A tonne of structural tube makes its maker about ₹5,000. A tonne of wire rope makes its maker about ₹34,000. The difference is not the steel, which is similar; it is what the customer is paying for. The tube buyer pays for availability and price. The rope buyer, who will hang a mine cage or an elevator from it, pays for certification, testing, a service engineer and a brand the safety inspector recognises. The more of that a product carries, the less the steel price matters to its maker.

The hybrid: Surya Roshni

Surya Roshni does not fit any single archetype. About 76% of its revenue comes from galvanised, ERW, spiral and API pipes and DFT hollow sections, making it India's largest GI pipe producer and largest ERW pipe exporter. The other 24% (about ₹1,809 crore in FY26) is the "Surya" lighting, fans and appliances brand, with a new wires and cables business attached. The two halves share a balance sheet and a promoter but not much else, which is why a May 2026 report that the company might demerge them sent the stock up about 25%. No such proposal has been approved by the board or filed with the exchanges. It remains a rumour, and this report treats it as one.

Jindal SAW, finally, straddles three archetypes at once (line pipe, DI pipe and seamless casing, with pellet, coke and pig-iron plants behind them). That diversification was meant to smooth its cycle. In FY26 and Q1 FY27 it did the opposite, because all three of its businesses were hit by different shocks in the same twelve months, a story told in Section 10.

03

Size, growth and who buys the pipe

Two years of flat rupee revenue hide volume growth in tubes, a collapse in water pipe and a boom in American gas pipe.

The eight companies sold ₹84,061 crore of products in FY26, ₹84,182 crore in FY25 and ₹85,392 crore in FY24. Three years of flat-to-falling rupee revenue might suggest a stagnant industry. It is not. APL Apollo's tonnes rose 11% in FY26 and Surya Roshni's steel volume rose 22% in the first half of FY27. Welspun's revenue rose 20% in FY26. The aggregate stayed flat for two reasons: steel prices fell from FY25 into the December 2025 trough, deflating every converter's realisation per tonne, and the water-pipe and oil-casing businesses (Electrosteel, Jindal SAW, Maharashtra Seamless, Ratnamani's carbon-steel book) went through a downturn at the same time. Strip out the steel-price effect and the branded-tube side of the group grew at double digits while the government-dependent side shrank.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: four cost structures

A tube maker spends 80 rupees in every hundred on steel; a rope maker spends under 40. That single difference explains most of the gap in margins.

The cost structure of a converter is dominated by one line, the metal it buys, and the size of that line decides almost everything else. When steel is 80% of cost, as it is for a structural tube, the converter is a high-volume, thin-margin logistics and manufacturing business whose skill lies in yield, freight and turning inventory fast. When the metal is under 40% of cost, as in wire rope, the business is about engineering, labour and service, and steel-price swings barely register. The input prices that matter

Each archetype watches a different set of input prices. For welded tube and line pipe it is hot-rolled coil and plate. Mumbai retail HRC including GST went from ₹60,920 a tonne in March 2025 to a ₹54,760 trough in December 2025, then up to ₹66,940 in March 2026, ₹68,170 in June and ₹76,900 in September 2026. Ex-works prices before GST run 15–20% lower (₹63,000 a tonne in Mumbai on 7 October 2026). The drivers were the safeguard duty on flat steel (12% from April 2025, stepping down to 11.5% and then 11% to April 2028, with a US$675 a tonne floor on imported HRC), the Hormuz energy shock, a rupee at ₹95–96 and coking coal at US$278 a tonne in September 2026; all are covered in the Iron & Steel report.

For DI pipe makers, HRC does not matter at all. Their inputs are iron ore (NMDC lump at about ₹5,400 a tonne in September 2026) and coking coal or coke (hard coking coal at US$229–278 a tonne during Q2 FY27). Integrated makers with their own coke ovens (Electrosteel at Haldia and Srikalahasthi; Jindal SAW's pellet, coke and pig-iron plants) partly hedge this, but in 2026 they faced a cost squeeze on top of a demand trough. Ratnamani's stainless and alloy tubes are priced off nickel, molybdenum and chrome, so nickel swings move its realisations. Zinc matters to every galvaniser and DI coater, polyethylene and epoxy to every line-pipe coater, and wire rod to Usha Martin.

In plain terms

When steel gets more expensive, the converter does not lose money as long as it can raise its own prices just as fast. The danger is timing. A company holding a month of stock gains when prices rise and loses when they fall, and a company holding nine months of stock can see a full quarter's profit swing on a price move it had nothing to do with. For a DI pipe maker the relevant price is not steel at all but iron ore and coking coal, which is why a steel rally did nothing to help Electrosteel.

The steel majors moving downstream

The most serious long-term threat to the tube makers is not another tube maker but their own suppliers. Tata Steel sells hollow sections under the Structura brand with captive coil, and JSW Steel has been building downstream capacity. A mill that owns the coil can, in principle, price a tube off its marginal cost of steel, which an independent converter cannot. So far APL Apollo's distribution reach, DFT range and speed of delivery have kept it ahead, and its share has not fallen. But this is the reason the market watches APL's general-segment spread so closely: if a mill decided to buy share, the plain-pipe end of the market is where it would start.

Imports and China

Imports are a smaller threat in downstream steel than upstream, for three reasons: freight costs on bulky tube are high relative to its value, Indian buyers in water and oil specify domestic standards and approvals, and the government's domestic-steel preference policy favours Indian pipe in public tenders. The exception is seamless tube, where Chinese mills are the world's low-cost producers; India has imposed anti-dumping duty of roughly US$961–1,611 a tonne on Chinese seamless tube since 2016, now extended to 27 January 2027 while a second sunset review runs. A subtle point, developed in Section 06: the safeguard duty that props up Indian flat-steel prices does not cover pipes and tubes, so converters pay a protected price for their input while their output enjoys narrower protection.

Who owns these companies

Seven of the eight are family-controlled, with promoter holdings between 40% and 70%. The exception is APL Apollo, where the Sanjay Gupta family holds 28.18% and foreign and domestic institutions own 55% between them, which makes it an institution-owned compounder in a sector of family firms. The two largest recent changes run in opposite directions. At Electrosteel the Kejriwal family raised its holding from 46.22% to 54.52% over the year to September 2026, including about ₹225 crore of buying on 28–29 September, while foreign investors cut theirs from 19.26% to 8.57%. At Usha Martin the Jhawar family's stake has drifted down to 40.28% from 42.45% a year earlier. There is little multinational or private-equity ownership among the eight; the multinationals in these markets (Bekaert, Alleima, Saint-Gobain PAM) compete as rivals rather than owners.

Steel up, profit flat: the spread and the inventory Deep dive

Coil rose 40% in nine months and the best converter's profit per tonne barely moved. Here is the machine that makes that happen, and the accounting noise around it.

The single most important idea for anyone reading a steel converter's results is that its revenue and its profit answer to different masters. Revenue answers to the steel price. Profit answers to the spread, and quarter by quarter to the inventory. This section takes the machine apart: what the spread is for each archetype, the evidence that the best converters really do hold it through a steel cycle, how big the inventory distortion is, and why converters are structurally squeezed between protected mills upstream and less-protected product markets downstream.

The spread, defined for each archetype

For a welded tube or line pipe, the spread is the product's realisation per tonne minus the coil or plate cost per tonne multiplied by a yield factor (typically 1.03–1.06 tonnes of coil per tonne of tube; DFT lowers it). EBITDA per tonne is that spread minus other materials (zinc, coatings), minus conversion cost (power, labour, consumables), minus freight and selling costs. For a DI pipe the input is not steel but about 1.5–1.7 tonnes of iron ore and 0.6–0.7 tonnes of coke per tonne of hot metal, so the spread behaves like a blast-furnace steel-maker's raw-material spread. For wire rope, wire rod is only 30–40% of the price, so steel moves matter far less than product mix.

In plain terms

When steel prices jump, a converter that bought its steel last month at the old price and sells its tube this month at the new price makes a one-off profit. When steel prices fall, it makes a one-off loss. These gains and losses are real cash, but they do not tell you anything about how good the business is. The trick to reading these companies is to estimate the inventory effect each quarter and look through it to the underlying spread.

The safeguard paradox

India's safeguard duty on flat steel (12% from April 2025, then 11.5% from April 2026 and 11% until April 2028, plus a US$675 a tonne floor on imported HRC) was designed to protect the mills from Chinese and other cheap imports. It works: it supports the price that converters pay for coil. But it covers only flat products (HS 7208–7212, 7225 and 7226). Pipes and tubes (HS 7303–7306) are not covered. So a converter buys its input inside a tariff wall and sells its output with only narrower protections: anti-dumping duty on Chinese seamless tube, Bureau of Indian Standards quality-control orders that keep out uncertified product, freight costs, and qualification barriers in oil, gas and water.

In plain terms

The government protects the steel mills by taxing imported coil, which keeps coil prices up. It does not tax imported pipes and tubes in the same way. The ones that cope are those with a brand customers pay for, a licence competitors cannot easily get, or their own iron supply. Everyone else is caught in the middle.

The policy implication cuts both ways. If the safeguard falls away in April 2028 as scheduled and coil prices fall, converters will take a one-off inventory hit and then benefit from cheaper input, as long as their own selling prices are sticky. If the safeguard is extended or widened, they keep paying more for coil than international peers. The brand that sets its own spread: APL Apollo's franchise Deep dive

How a tube converter turned a commodity into a dealer brand, why its record quarter still left it behind its own guidance, and what 48.6 times earnings assumes.

APL Apollo is the reason this sub-sector has a market value of ₹1.97 lakh crore rather than a fraction of it. At ₹59,772 crore it is worth 86% as much as Jindal SAW, Usha Martin, Ratnamani, Maharashtra Seamless, Surya Roshni and Electrosteel put together (₹69,155 crore), on an operating margin of under 8%. Understanding why the market pays that is the key to understanding what "quality" means in steel conversion.

From converter to brand

Until the mid-2010s APL Apollo was a regional maker of ERW pipe and galvanised tube, the kind of business that takes whatever spread the market gives. Three decisions changed it. First, in 2016–17 it commissioned India's first Direct Forming Technology lines, which can make any square or rectangular section size on one mill without changing rolls and use 3–7% less steel than conventional forming. That let it offer a far wider range of sections than rivals, including heavy sections that could replace concrete columns and fabricated box plates. Second, it built a distribution system of about 800 distributors and 2.5 lakh retailers and fabricators, sold under the "APL Apollo" name with sub-brands such as Apollo Column and SG Premium. Third, it moved its mix up a ladder: from plain black pipe to galvanised and pre-galvanised, to coated products, to heavy DFT structural sections and roofing. Value-added products reached about 55% of volume by Q4 FY26.

The proof that this created pricing power came in January 2025, when APL raised prices in its general (plain) segment by about ₹1,500 a tonne and the increase stuck. General-segment EBITDA per tonne went from about ₹2,000 to over ₹3,500. A commodity pipe maker raising prices by ₹1,500 would simply lose volume to the next mill. APL did not, because a fabricator ordering a full range of sections from one dealer values availability and breadth more than a small price difference on one line. Company-wide EBITDA per tonne moved from roughly ₹3,800 in FY25 (an estimate: ₹1,199 crore of operating profit on about 3.15 million tonnes) to ₹5,161 in FY26, up 36%, and ₹5,522 in Q1 FY27.

The water-pipe air-pocket: Jal Jeevan Mission and the DI price war Deep dive

The government approved ₹8.69 lakh crore for rural water in March 2026. Seven months later the pipe makers were still waiting for the orders.

The ductile-iron pipe business went from its best year to its worst in twenty-four months, and the cause was almost entirely one government programme. The Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM), launched on 15 August 2019 to give every rural household a piped water connection, drove a wave of DI pipe orders that peaked in FY24, when Electrosteel Castings earned a record ₹740 crore. Then the first phase ran out of money, states fell behind on paying their contractors, new DI capacity built for the boom came on stream, and the industry slid into a price war. This section explains the scheme's arithmetic, what the pause did to the pipe makers, and why the approved second phase has not yet shown up in their order books.

The scheme arithmetic

The first phase of JJM had central assistance of ₹2.08 lakh crore approved in 2019-20, with a 2024 target. On 10–11 March 2026 the Union Cabinet approved JJM 2.0: the mission is extended to December 2028 with a total outlay of ₹8.69 lakh crore, of which the central share is ₹3.59 lakh crore (₹1.51 lakh crore more than originally approved). The target is all 19.36 crore rural households; about 15.80 crore (81.61%) already had a tap connection at approval. The emphasis shifts from building infrastructure to delivering a service, with digital IDs for water systems (Sujalam Bharat), formal handover of schemes to village bodies (Jal Arpan) and an operations-and-maintenance push (Jal Utsav).

In plain terms

A DI pipe plant is a blast furnace with a spinning mould attached, and a blast furnace costs nearly as much to run half-empty as full. When orders dropped by a quarter, Electrosteel's fixed costs stayed put and every remaining tonne had to carry more of them, while the price war meant each tonne also sold for less. Why DI was hit harder than any other pipe

Four features combined. DI pipe is close to 100% government-procured, so there is no private demand to fall back on. Capacity was added across the industry in FY23–FY25 for a JJM peak that did not last. Blast furnaces cannot be idled cheaply, so makers kept running and cut prices to fill them. And the government pays slowly even in good times, so working capital ballooned as stock piled up: Electrosteel's inventory reached 276 days and its working-capital days rose from 55 in FY24 to 92 in FY26. Management called it "slower project execution and liquidity constraints" on its Q1 FY27 call. The same buyers also buy large-diameter spiral (HSAW) water-transmission pipe from Welspun, Jindal SAW and Ratnamani, and galvanised pipe from Surya, but those companies have other markets; Surya's +22% steel volume in H1 FY27 shows non-JJM demand (structures, exports) more than offset the gap there.

Why JJM 2.0 has not yet arrived

Approval is not money. Under the restructured scheme, states must re-plan their remaining work, budget their larger share, and clear arrears owed to contractors before new tenders flow, and contractors who have not been paid are slow to bid. The dossier's estimate is a 6–12 month lag from the March 2026 approval to visible pipe volume, which puts H2 FY27 as the earliest recovery window. The evidence so far is consistent with that: Electrosteel's Q1 FY27 standalone profit was ₹5.9 crore and Jai Balaji was still at about 30% utilisation. Electrosteel's stock hit a 20% upper circuit on the day of the Cabinet approval; its earnings have not followed. A further risk is substitution: plastic (HDPE, PVC) pipe already dominates small-diameter distribution, and a scheme focused on service delivery may spend more on maintenance and plastic distribution lines than on new DI trunk mains.

Electrosteel's response: buy more, branch out

Electrosteel's management has responded on two fronts. It has set long-term targets of ₹7,000–8,000 crore of revenue at a 13–13.5% EBITDA margin by FY30–31, and is diversifying: a paints and coatings business targeted at ₹800–1,000 crore of revenue within five years (₹250–300 crore of capex), a valves plant by the end of FY27, the acquisition of T.I.S. Service S.p.A. in Italy, and about ₹700 crore set aside for bolt-on acquisitions. Capital work in progress stood at ₹1,186 crore in March 2026, large for a company earning ₹289 crore of trailing operating profit. And the promoter family raised its holding from 46.22% to 54.52% over the year, including about ₹225 crore bought on 28–29 September 2026, while foreign investors cut their stake from 19.26% to 8.57%, a pattern that looks like a block transfer from one to the other. At 0.80 times book, Electrosteel is the only member trading below its net worth. The investment case is a straightforward bet on JJM 2.0 converting into orders; the risk is that diversification spends cash the core business is not generating.

In plain terms

The government has promised ₹8.69 lakh crore for rural water, but most of it has to come from states that were already late paying for the first phase. Until states clear their old bills and issue new tenders, the pipe makers will not see orders. That could start in late FY27. The pipe makers' shares have priced some of the recovery in already; their earnings have not.

The same water-scheme cycle runs through other parts of the library: pump makers selling into rural water and irrigation in Pumps & Engines, and the EPC contractors who build the schemes and wait for state payments in Civil Construction. When state water payments recover, all three should see it within a quarter or two of one another.

Inside the US wall: Welspun's line-pipe moment Deep dive

American tariffs shut Indian-made large-diameter pipe out of the US. Welspun had a plant in Arkansas. Its order book went to US$4.4 billion and its share price nearly tripled.

Welspun Corp is the clearest example in Indian industry of a trade wall turning into a moat. The company has made large-diameter line pipe in India since the 1990s and built a plant in Little Rock, Arkansas, around 2008. For years that plant was simply a second factory. Then the United States raised a stack of trade barriers against imported steel pipe, and the Arkansas plant became one of the few large-diameter mills inside the wall just as American gas-pipeline building accelerated. The result was a record order book, a near-tripling of the share price and a re-rating from a cyclical multiple to something closer to a growth multiple.

The protection stack

Three layers of US policy work against Indian-made pipe and for US-made pipe.

  • Section 232 tariffs. The 2018 national-security tariff on steel (initially 25%) was extended to pipe, tube and steel derivatives from 12 March 2025, with product exclusions ended, under proclamations issued on 10 February 2025. The steel tariff rate was raised to 50% from June 2025. The dossier treats the 50% rate as applying to pipe and tube as well, but it notes that the pipe-specific application of the 50% rate has not been fully verified; this report says so rather than state it as settled.
  • Anti-dumping and countervailing duties. Large-diameter welded pipe from India has been subject to US AD/CVD orders since 2019. In May 2026 the US Commerce Department's preliminary administrative review gave Welspun a 0.00% dumping margin; the final result is pending.
  • Domestic-content preferences. There is no blanket Buy American rule for privately funded oil and gas pipelines. The Build America, Buy America Act (part of the November 2021 infrastructure law) requires US-made iron and steel in federally funded infrastructure, including water systems. In practice, US midstream buyers prefer domestic mills for tariff certainty and shorter lead times.

Ropes and alloy tubes: the niche specialists

Usha Martin and Ratnamani sell approval, not tonnes. Their margins are double the group's, and their steel-price risk is a fraction of it.

Two companies in the group earn margins of 17–20% on products where steel is a minority of the price and the customer is buying certification, engineering and service. They are the closest thing in Indian downstream steel to the global specialty-materials companies, and they behave differently from the rest of the group in almost every respect: their working capital is long because they stock specialised product close to customers, their exports are high, and their earnings depend on mix rather than on tonnes.

Usha Martin: a rope company that sold its steel mill

Usha Martin started as a wire and wire-rope maker in eastern India in the 1960s and later built a specialty-steel mill, which loaded it with debt. In 2019 it sold the steel business to Tata Steel and kept the rope franchise. Today it sells wire ropes, wire, strand and low-relaxation prestressed concrete (LRPC) strand to cranes, mines, elevators, offshore mooring systems and ropeways in dozens of countries, with about 57% of revenue earned internationally and a place among the world's five largest rope makers. Ropes are consumables: a crane or mine-hoist rope is inspected and replaced on a schedule, so the business has a replacement-demand floor under its new-equipment sales.

FY26 revenue was ₹3,691 crore (+6.2%), operating profit ₹705 crore (19.1%) and profit ₹466 crore (+15%). Q1 FY27 was stronger: revenue ₹1,033 crore (+16%), operating profit ₹208 crore (+44%) and profit ₹142 crore (+41%), on flat volume of about 51,000 tonnes. Seamless, OCTG and the China duty: Maharashtra Seamless splits in three

India's seamless-tube leader is a drilling-cycle business wrapped around a ₹3,800 crore treasury, protected by a duty on Chinese pipe that expires in January 2027, and about to demerge.

Maharashtra Seamless (MSL), incorporated in 1988 as part of the D.P. Jindal group, is India's largest maker of seamless tubes, with 650,000 tonnes a year of seamless capacity (up to 20 inches outside diameter) and 125,000 tonnes of ERW pipe at Raigad, plus 57.5 MW of renewable power. It holds about 55% of the Indian seamless market and about 18% of API-grade ERW pipe. Its main customers are ONGC and Oil India for casing and tubing, plus IOCL, HPCL, BPCL, GAIL and Reliance for line pipe and process tubing. Two features make it unlike any other company in this report: its earnings follow India's upstream drilling budget, and nearly half its market value is a portfolio of financial investments.

Hostage to the drill bit

MSL's revenue has fallen for two years: ₹5,404 crore in FY24, ₹5,269 crore in FY25 and ₹4,674 crore in FY26 (our primer cites an FY23 peak of ₹5,716 crore, which this report could not verify). Because casing demand follows crude prices and government upstream budgets, and because Chinese mills can undercut Indian ones by a wide margin, the business depends on two things it does not control: ONGC's drilling programme and the anti-dumping duty.

The China duty

India first imposed anti-dumping duty on seamless tubes from China (HS 7304, excluding stainless and cast iron) in 2016, after the 2014–16 oil crash left Chinese mills with surplus capacity. The duty, about US$961–1,611 a tonne depending on the producer, was extended in 2021. A second sunset review was initiated in March 2026, on application by Jindal SAW, Kirloskar Ferrous and MSL, who asked for higher duties and for OCTG to be excluded from the review's scope; the review covers tubes up to 355.6 mm outside diameter. Pending the outcome, the duty has been extended from 27 October 2026 to 27 January 2027. If it lapses, Chinese casing and tubing could undercut Indian mills in ONGC tenders; if it is extended or raised, MSL's pricing floor holds.

Rules of the game: duties, quality orders and trade walls

Policy protects the converters' input more than their output at home, and decides who can sell abroad. Eleven rules matter.

Downstream steel is shaped more by trade and procurement policy than most manufacturing. At home, the government protects the mills' flat steel, which raises the converters' input price, while the converters' own products rely on narrower protections: product-specific anti-dumping duty, quality-control orders, domestic-preference rules in public tenders, and the sheer cost of shipping bulky pipe. Abroad, the US, the EU and Saudi Arabia have each built walls that decide which Indian companies can sell there, and from which plant. What the rules add up to

Three consequences follow. First, at home the converters are on the wrong side of the main tariff. The flat-steel safeguard lifts their input cost, while their products enjoy no matching safeguard; their protection is product-specific (seamless duty), standards-based (BIS orders, which also suspended 55 standards in November 2025, loosening some barriers) or procurement-based (domestic preference in PSU tenders). The converters that thrive have a private moat (brand, qualification, integration) on top.

Second, abroad, the plant's location now decides access. Indian-made pipe faces 50% tariffs (on the steel basis; the pipe-specific rate is not fully verified) plus anti-dumping and countervailing duty in the US, a quota with a 50% out-of-quota duty in the EU, and carbon-border costs from February 2027. Companies with plants inside these walls (Welspun in the US and Saudi Arabia) win; companies exporting from India face higher costs or lose access. Surya Roshni's rising US exports of ERW pipe sit squarely in Section 232's path.

Third, the domestic demand switches are policy switches. JJM decides DI pipe volumes; ONGC's budget decides casing; government infrastructure spending decides a part of structural tube. These can turn on and off faster than any private cycle. The pipe makers' best defence is the one Surya and APL have taken: a broad private customer base that does not depend on a single programme.

In plain terms

India taxes imported steel coil to help its steel mills, which makes life harder for the companies that buy that coil to make pipes. Abroad, America and Europe have put up their own walls against imported steel products. The companies that win are those that either have a factory inside the wall (Welspun in Arkansas) or sell something buyers cannot easily get elsewhere. The rest pay more for their steel and face more barriers when they sell.

Two dates dominate the near-term policy calendar: the final findings of the seamless duty review before 27 January 2027, and the start of EU CBAM certificate sales in February 2027. Further out, the scheduled end of the flat-steel safeguard in April 2028 would lower converters' input prices, with a one-off inventory hit in the quarter it happens.

Seven decades of cycles: from Electrosteel's founding to the US order

Line pipe runs on oil and gas capex, DI on government water schemes, tubes on construction, seamless on drilling, rope on mining. Their cycles rarely line up.

The companies in this report were founded across seven decades, and each grew up in a different cycle. Cycle anatomy

The five archetypes run on five clocks. Reading a steel converter: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, what each one means, how the eight companies compare, and the definitional traps that make like-for-like comparison harder than it looks.

A converter's annual report is full of numbers that look comparable across companies and are not. Revenue tracks the steel price. Margin percentages fall when steel rises even if profit is unchanged. "EBITDA" includes other income at one company and excludes it at the next. Order books are quoted in dollars at one and rupees at another. This section builds the toolkit from the bottom up: the core equation, then each native KPI in turn, an illustrative plant P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected in one place, and a ten-minute routine for reading a quarter.

A worked example in round numbers, ILLUSTRATIVE. A tube maker sells 1 lakh tonnes in a quarter at ₹65,000 a tonne, so revenue is ₹650 crore. Now suppose coil rises ₹3,000 a tonne and the company passes it on in full (ignoring yield for simplicity). Realisation rises to ₹68,000 and revenue to ₹680 crore; steel cost rises to ₹57,000; EBITDA per tonne is still ₹5,000 and EBITDA still ₹50 crore. But the margin falls from 7.7% to 7.4%, because the same profit sits on a bigger revenue line. Add one more wrinkle: the company held 10,000 tonnes of coil bought at the old price. Selling the tubes made from it at the new price produces a one-off gain of about ₹3 crore (10,000 × ₹3,000), so reported EBITDA is ₹53 crore and EBITDA per tonne ₹5,300. The franchise has not changed at all; three different headline numbers have.

In plain terms

When steel gets dearer, a converter that passes the cost on makes the same rupees per tonne but shows a lower margin percentage, because revenue has grown. If it was holding cheap stock, it also books a one-off gain. So in a rising steel market, a falling margin can be perfectly healthy and a jump in profit per tonne can be partly luck. Always ask: what happened to rupees per tonne, and how much of that was inventory?

5. The inventory effect

What it is. The one-off gain or loss when input prices move while the company holds stock bought at the old price. Formula: inventory tonnes × change in input price over the period (approximately). Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): APL holds about 0.25 million tonnes; a ₹3,000 a tonne coil move is worth about ₹75 crore, roughly ₹780 a tonne on a 0.96 million-tonne quarter. Jindal SAW, with about ₹8,200 crore of inventory, faces about ₹205 crore from a 5% move on half its stock, roughly 80% of its Q2 FY27 operating profit. What drives it: inventory depth (days), the size and speed of the input move, and whether contracts pass steel through (line pipe often does; spot GI does not). Benchmarks: inventory under 30 days is good for a distribution converter; 30–60 average; above 60 weak. For project and DI makers, under 120 days is good, 120–180 average, above 180 weak. How the group compares: APL 27 days, Surya 64, MSL 140, Welspun 166, Ratnamani 174, Usha 187, Jindal SAW 191, Electrosteel 276 (chart in Section 06).

Definition trap. Companies rarely disclose the inventory effect, and when they do it is often netted into "realisation" or "other operating income". A quarter with rising coil prices will almost always flatter EBITDA per tonne; estimate the effect before comparing it with guidance.

9. Cash conversion cycle and working-capital days

Book-to-bill and order intake: the leading edge of the order book

What it is. New orders won in a period divided by revenue billed in the same period. Formula: order intake ÷ revenue (above 1 means the book is growing). Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): Welspun's book rose from about ₹25,750 crore after Q1 FY27 to about ₹42,100 crore in August 2026; even allowing for two months of execution (roughly ₹2,900 crore at its trailing run-rate), intake in that window was close to ₹19,000 crore against under ₹3,000 crore billed, a book-to-bill of over six for the period, driven by one order. Over a full year a figure between 1.0 and 1.3 is healthy for a line-pipe maker. What drives it: pipeline final-investment decisions, the company's qualifications and plant location, and pricing discipline. Benchmarks: above 1.2 good, 0.9–1.2 average, below 0.9 weak over a trailing year. How the group compares: only Welspun's intake can be inferred; Jindal SAW's book fell from a record of about US$1.48 billion during FY26 to US$1,317 million at year-end, implying book-to-bill below one in the second half. Definition trap: a single large order makes any quarterly ratio meaningless; use four-quarter sums, and check whether the company counts orders when signed, when the letter of intent arrives, or when it is merely the lowest bidder.

In plain terms

The order book tells you how much work is waiting; book-to-bill tells you whether that pile is getting bigger or smaller. A company can have a big book and still be shrinking if it is shipping faster than it is winning. Jindal SAW's book fell through the second half of FY26, which warned of the weak quarters that followed, while Welspun's jumped on one giant order.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA with or without other income. APL (₹1,914 crore company vs ₹1,808 crore operating), Ratnamani (19.6% primer vs 16.9%), Surya (~8% primer vs 6.5%), Electrosteel Q1 FY27 (9.5% company vs 7.0%), Welspun (₹2,371 crore company vs ₹2,236 crore screener; Q1 FY27 ₹756 crore vs ₹692 crore, unresolved).
  • Per-tonne denominators. Volume may include roofing, overseas plants, trading or pig iron. Electrosteel's revenue per pipe tonne is overstated because revenue includes non-pipe products.
  • Margin percentages in a moving steel market. The same rupees per tonne show a lower margin when steel rises.
  • Inventory gains and losses hidden inside realisation; rising-coil quarters flatter EBITDA per tonne.
  • Gross versus net debt. Jindal SAW ₹2,528 crore (company net) vs ₹4,691 crore (gross); screener's "investments" include Welspun's strategic EPIC stake.
  • Consolidated versus attributable profit. Ratnamani's ₹534 crore includes a 49% minority in RFSS (~₹483 crore attributable); Usha's ₹491 crore (primer) vs ₹466 crore (screener).
  • One-offs in profit. Welspun's ₹548 crore EPIC gain in Q1 FY27 and ~₹843 crore of FY25 one-offs; MSL's swinging treasury income.
  • Order books in different currencies, with or without L1 positions, including subsidiaries or pellets, and with very different delivery dates.
  • Cash cycle versus working-capital days for companies with large customer advances (Welspun 85 vs −23).
  • Nameplate versus effective capacity, and year-end versus average.
  • Stale primer bases. Jindal SAW's FY25 revenue is ₹20,829 crore (screener), not ~₹16,215 crore; APL's capacity is 5 Mt, not 4.3–4.5 Mt; Ratnamani's promoter holding is 59.77%, not ~52%; Electrosteel's is 54.52%, not ~50.1%.
In plain terms

Most of the mistakes people make with these companies come from comparing numbers that were measured differently: profit with and without interest income, debt with and without cash, tonnes with and without roofing or trading. Before drawing a conclusion from two companies' figures, check that both are on the same basis. When they are not, convert them, or do not compare them.

How to read a steel converter's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Pull the volume first (it is often released days before results, as APL and Surya do). Compare year on year and on a half-year basis. Red flag: a second consecutive quarter of growth below guidance.
  2. Compute EBITDA per tonne on the operating basis (excluding other income). Compare with the last four quarters and with guidance. Red flag: per-tonne profit falling in a quarter when coil prices rose.
  3. Estimate the inventory effect. Look up the change in HRC (or ore and coal for DI) during the quarter and multiply by inventory tonnes. Strip it out before judging the spread. Red flag: management citing "inventory gains" two quarters running.
  4. Check the margin basis. Is the company's EBITDA including other income? Reconcile with the reported other-income line.
  5. For order-book companies, read intake and book together: book ÷ trailing revenue, new orders versus revenue billed (book-to-bill), and the delivery schedule. Red flag: book-to-bill below one for two quarters.
  6. Read working capital. Inventory and receivable days at the half-year; cash from operations versus EBITDA. Red flag: inventory days rising while volume falls (Jindal SAW, Electrosteel in FY26).
  7. Check net debt on the company's own basis, and whether it rose with capex or with working capital.
  8. Strip one-offs from profit: stake sales, treasury gains or losses, export-incentive catch-ups, exceptional items.
  9. Read the guidance language on volume, per-tonne margin and capex; compare with what was said last quarter. Red flag: guidance cut without detail (Ratnamani, August 2026).
  10. Finish with the external switches for that company: JJM tenders and state payments (DI), US tariff news (Welspun), MENA shipping (Jindal SAW, Ratnamani, Usha), the seamless duty review (MSL), and HRC's direction for next quarter's inventory effect.
In plain terms

Ten minutes is enough if you go in the right order: tonnes, profit per tonne, how much of that was lucky stock timing, whether the margin includes interest income, how much work is in hand, whether cash is getting stuck in stock or unpaid bills, and whether anything in the profit was a one-off. Then check the one or two outside switches that move that particular company. Revenue growth, the first number most people look at, is the last one that matters here.

How to read the table

Start with the two combined rows. Third, the P/E column is distorted at both ends. The native KPI columns explain the differences: the order book is Welspun's whole story, EBITDA per tonne is APL's, and the cash cycle is Electrosteel's problem.

Five things the market pays for

  • A brand plus negative working capital plus high ROCE. APL Apollo: 32% ROCE, a −12-day cash cycle and cash from operations above EBITDA earn a consumer-style multiple (about 49 times earnings, 33 times EBITDA) on an 8% margin. The market is capitalising the stability of a ₹5,000–5,500 spread and 15–20% volume growth.
  • Order-book visibility inside a protected market. Welspun: a US$4.4 billion book (about 2.4 times revenue) and a US plant inside the Section 232 wall took it from roughly 10–12 times earnings to about 30 times in a year.
  • Niche, qualification and exports. Usha Martin and Ratnamani trade at 27–40 times earnings despite flat or falling revenue, because approvals and certification make their margins durable.
  • Government buyers, long working capital and commodity inputs are discounted. Electrosteel and Jindal SAW trade at 0.8–1.5 times book; their P/Es look high only because earnings are at a trough.
  • Treasury-heavy cyclicals with a pending restructuring are discounted most: MSL at about 6 times EV/EBITDA and 1.1 times book.

Investors pay most for profits they can predict. APL Apollo's profit per tonne barely moves and its customers pay in advance, so the market treats it like a consumer company. The water-pipe and casing makers depend on government budgets that switch on and off, so the market values them at not much more than the value of their factories.

Why Indian converters trade at two to five times global multiples

Four reasons, in roughly descending order of durability. First, a domestic growth runway: Indian steel demand is growing 7–9% a year against roughly 0–2% globally, and structural tube is growing faster still as it replaces concrete and fabricated sections. Second, protection: a safeguard on inputs that keeps domestic steel supply orderly, anti-dumping duty and quality orders on some outputs, and, for Welspun, a US plant inside the tariff wall. Third, scarcity: listed downstream steel businesses with growth are few in India, and domestic institutional money concentrates in them. Fourth, APL's consumer-brand positioning, which no global tube maker has achieved.

Global tube and pipe makers trade at cyclical multiples because their end-markets are mature and their earnings swing with oil and construction. The risk to Indian multiples is convergence: if growth stalls, the market can re-rate an Indian converter toward global levels quickly. Electrosteel shows how fast: from a record profit in FY24 to 0.8 times book within one scheme cycle. Tenaris, the global seamless leader, earns a far higher margin than Maharashtra Seamless and trades at a similar EV/EBITDA; the Indian discount there is about the drilling cycle and the treasury, not about quality.

In plain terms

The best Indian pipe and tube makers are valued at several times what similar companies fetch in America or Europe. That premium is paid for India's faster growth and for the protection Indian producers enjoy. It is not guaranteed: if growth slows, the gap can close, and a company that was valued as a growth story can quickly be valued as a cyclical one.

Technology and disruption: sections, plastics, hydrogen and carbon

The biggest technology gains have come from making less steel do more; the biggest threats are plastic pipe, synthetic rope and carbon costs on blast-furnace iron.

Technology in steel conversion moves slowly, but four shifts are reshaping who earns what. Two are opportunities for the group (heavier structural sections and new pipeline specifications), two are threats (substitute materials and carbon costs), and one cuts both ways (energy transition).

DFT and heavy structural sections

Direct Forming Technology, pioneered in India by APL Apollo in 2016–17 and since adopted by Surya Roshni, forms a square or rectangular section directly from a flat strip without first making a round tube, and can change sizes without changing rolls. It saves 3–7% of steel per section, which at current coil prices is worth roughly ₹1,600–4,600 a tonne, a large number against an EBITDA of ₹5,000 a tonne. More importantly, heavy DFT sections can replace concrete columns and fabricated box plates in buildings, which is what expands the structural-tube market from about 9 million tonnes toward an estimated 17 million by FY30. Pre-engineered buildings, warehouses, data centres and solar mounting structures are all steel-intensive formats that favour tube.

Substitutes: plastic pipe and synthetic rope

Plastic pipe (HDPE, PVC, MDPE) already dominates small-diameter water distribution and continues to take share from galvanised and small-diameter DI pipe. DI holds the trunk mains, where pressure, diameter and longevity matter. The risk for DI makers is that JJM 2.0's emphasis on service delivery and maintenance tilts spending toward plastic distribution lines and repairs rather than new DI trunk mains. Welspun hedges this through Sintex-BAPL; Surya is adding wires and cables rather than plastic pipe. In ropes, high-performance synthetic fibre ropes are displacing steel in some offshore mooring applications, while plasticated steel ropes and compacted high-performance strands, both of which Usha makes, are the defence.

New pipeline specifications

Higher-grade line pipe (X70–X80, sour service for gas with hydrogen sulphide) and possible future hydrogen and CO2 pipelines need tougher, lower-hardness steel, different welding and more demanding testing. Welspun and Jindal SAW have publicised hydrogen-blend qualification tests. This is INDICATIVE option value: no order sizes were found, and the dossier's information here comes from memory rather than fetched sources. Nuclear and LNG projects, by contrast, are real demand for Ratnamani's alloy tubes today.

Carbon: CBAM and green steel

The EU's carbon border mechanism became definitive on 1 January 2026, with certificate purchases due from February 2027. It covers iron and steel articles, including tubes and pipes (CN 7303–7306) and wire ropes (7312). Exporters will need verified embedded-emission data, and those whose product carries a lot of carbon will pay more. Integrated DI makers using the blast-furnace route (roughly 2.2–2.6 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of iron, per the sibling dossier) are the most exposed; tube makers buying coil from mills face whatever their supplier's footprint is. Electrosteel's European DI exports, Usha's European rope sales and Ratnamani's and Welspun's European orders all carry this cost from 2027.

In plain terms

The technology that has mattered most is unglamorous: forming tubes in a way that uses less steel and makes heavier sections that can replace concrete in buildings. The threats are also unglamorous: plastic pipes taking small water lines, fibre ropes taking some offshore jobs, and a European carbon tax that will hit blast-furnace iron hardest. Hydrogen pipelines get the headlines but have not yet produced orders.

The energy transition cuts both ways. In the long run, lower oil and gas use would shrink line-pipe and casing demand. In the next five years, the opposite is happening: US gas demand for LNG exports and for power to AI data centres is driving the pipeline building behind Welspun's order book, and LNG and nuclear projects drive demand for Ratnamani's tubes. Investors in the line-pipe makers are effectively betting on the length of that window.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against steel converters

The bull case rests on spreads, protection and a water-spending recovery; the bear case on valuation, inventory reversal and policy risk.

The debate about this sub-sector is really several debates, one per archetype. For APL Apollo it is about growth versus valuation; for Welspun about whether a policy-made moat lasts; for the DI and seamless makers about whether the trough is cyclical; for the specialists about when the Middle East recovers. The columns below set out the strongest version of each side.

The bull case

  • Spreads are sticky. APL Apollo held ₹4,700–5,500 a tonne through a 40% coil swing; branded and qualified converters have shown they can pass steel through.
  • Structural growth in tube. Structural tube is estimated to grow from ~9 to ~17 million tonnes by FY30 as steel replaces concrete; APL is doubling capacity with internal cash.
  • Protected US demand. Welspun's US$4.4 billion book, mostly for FY28–FY29, gives the best visibility in Indian industrials; US gas and LNG demand supports more.
  • Water spending must flow. JJM 2.0 has ₹8.69 lakh crore approved to December 2028; even a partial conversion would lift DI makers from trough utilisation and margins.
  • Clean balance sheets. Five of eight are net cash; promoters are buying at Electrosteel; the trough-cycle names trade near book.
  • Specialists with pricing power. Usha's ~20% margin and net cash, Ratnamani's approvals and new spool business, both levered to a Middle-East recovery.

The bear case

  • Valuation. APL at 48.6× earnings with H1 FY27 volume up only 4% against a 15–20% guide; Welspun at ~38× excluding one-offs; Ratnamani at 40× with falling profit.
  • Inventory reversal. Coil at ₹76,900 is a cyclical high; a correction turns recent inventory gains into losses, most painfully for long-inventory makers.
  • Policy-made moats can be unmade. A cut in US Section 232 tariffs would expose Welspun's US advantage; the seamless duty review could lapse in January 2027.
  • JJM 2.0 may disappoint. States fund ~59%, are still in arrears, and the scheme now favours service delivery over new trunk mains; plastic takes small diameters.
  • Earnings quality. Repeated one-offs at Welspun, treasury swings at MSL, unresolved EBITDA bases, and unverified figures (APL net cash, MSL order book).
  • Mills moving downstream. Tata Structura and JSW could compete harder in plain structural tube.

Weighing the two, the bull case is strongest where the moat is private and visible in cash flow (APL's cash cycle, Usha's margins and net cash) and weakest where it depends on a single policy (Welspun's US tariff wall, MSL's duty, the DI makers' scheme). The bear case is strongest on valuation at the top of the group and on timing at the bottom: the companies with the clearest franchises are fully priced, and the cheap ones need specific external triggers that have not yet arrived. The most useful position is therefore company-specific, not sector-wide.

Three questions decide most of the outcome over the next four quarters. Does APL Apollo's second half deliver the 25%-plus volume growth needed to meet guidance, without the per-tonne spread falling below ₹5,000? Do JJM 2.0 tenders and state payments show up in DI volumes by H2 FY27, as the 6–12-month lag implies? And does the US tariff wall around Welspun's market hold while it delivers the largest order in its history? A "yes" to all three supports the bull case; a "no" to any one hits a different part of the group.

In plain terms

The optimist sees companies that have proved they can protect their profit per tonne, a huge American order book, and a government water programme that has to start spending soon. The pessimist sees the best companies already priced as if everything goes right, steel prices at a peak that will reverse, and moats built by politicians that politicians can remove. Both are right about different companies.
