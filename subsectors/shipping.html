00

The one-page view: record profits, priced as a peak

Two companies, one state-owned and one family-run, are earning more than ever from a war that shrank the oil trade. The market pays them six to eight times earnings because it does not believe the war rates will last.

India has two listed shipowners in the NSE 750. The Great Eastern Shipping Company (GE), founded in 1948 and run by the Sheth family, is the country's largest private shipowner: 40 ships of 3.24 million deadweight tonnes (dwt) across crude, product, gas and dry-bulk carriers, plus an offshore drilling and support business, Greatship, with 4 jack-up rigs and 19 offshore vessels. The Shipping Corporation of India (SCI), formed by the government in 1961, is the national carrier: 58 owned ships of 5.26 million dwt, dominated by tankers, plus liner, bulk, offshore and ship-management businesses and stakes in four LNG-carrier joint ventures. The government owns 63.75% of SCI and has been trying, on paper, to sell that stake since 2019.

Both companies earn in dollars, report in rupees and pay almost no income tax, because Indian shipowners can choose to be taxed on the tonnage of their ships rather than on their profit. Both are price-takers: their combined fleet is about 0.35% of the world's merchant tonnage, so neither can move a freight rate. What they can control is which ships they own, when they buy and sell them, how much of their fleet they lock into fixed-rate time charters, and how they fund all of it. On those choices the two companies sit at opposite ends of the spectrum.

The defining fact of 2026 is the West Asia war. From the start of March the Strait of Hormuz was effectively closed to normal traffic. Seaborne oil volumes fell: "dirty" tanker trade was down 12% year on year in GE's first quarter of FY27, China's seaborne crude imports fell about 35% and product trade fell 16%. Yet tanker earnings went to records, because replacement barrels had to travel from the Atlantic, ships were trapped inside the Gulf, and war-risk insurance and queuing tied up tonnage. Less oil moved; each tonne moved further and fewer ships were free to move it. That is the paradox this report is built around.

The result is the most profitable quarter either company has ever reported. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) GE's consolidated profit after tax was ₹1,309 crore, up 159%, more than twice its profit for the whole of FY22. SCI's was ₹619 crore, up 75%. Together they earned ₹1,928 crore in one quarter (+125%) on revenue of ₹3,852 crore (+53%). Revenue growth of 53% became profit growth of 125% on fleets that barely changed in size: that is shipping's operating leverage in one line.

Four forces explain almost everything in this report. First, ton-miles and disruption, not volumes, set the price of a ship-day: Russia in 2022, the Red Sea in 2023–24 and Hormuz in 2026 all lengthened voyages or trapped ships. Second, supply discipline decides how long a boom lasts, and the orderbook is rebuilding fast: newbuild orders equal 27.1% of the crude-tanker fleet and 34.9% of the large LPG-carrier fleet (August 2026). Third, capital allocation is the one lever management fully controls, and GE (a net-cash asset trader) and SCI (a state carrier whose fleet plan is written in New Delhi) use it in opposite ways. Fourth, India's maritime policy has turned from neglect to active support: a ₹69,725 crore package, preference for Indian-flagged ships in government cargo, and a plan to build an Indian national fleet around SCI, because foreign ships carry about 93–95% of India's sea trade and collect about ₹6 lakh crore of freight a year.

In plain terms

A shipowner rents out steel boxes that cost the same to run whether the rent is $15,000 or $90,000 a day. When a war makes ships scarce, almost every extra dollar of rent falls to profit, and Indian owners pay nearly no tax on it. The market knows ships are scarce only until the shipyards deliver the next wave or the war ends, so it values these companies on what their ships and cash are worth, not on this year's earnings.

01

How a ship earns: rent a box of steel by the day

A shipowner sells ship-days. The price of a ship-day is the time-charter equivalent, and almost everything else in the industry is a cost or an asset price wrapped around it.

A merchant ship is a floating warehouse that moves. Its owner can earn from it in two basic ways. Under a voyage (spot) charter, the owner agrees to carry a specific cargo from one port to another for a lump sum or a rate per tonne, and pays the voyage costs: fuel (bunkers), port dues, canal tolls and brokers' commissions. Under a time charter, the owner hires the ship and its crew to a charterer for a fixed period, from a few months to several years, at a fixed daily rate; the charterer pays the voyage costs and decides where the ship goes. Owners also put ships into pools run by a commercial manager that share earnings across many similar ships, and a few long-term contracts, such as LNG carriers, run for 20–25 years.

Because spot and time-charter earnings are not directly comparable, the industry converts everything to a single price: the time-charter equivalent (TCE), in US dollars a day. TCE is voyage revenue minus voyage costs, divided by operating days. A spot voyage that earns $5 million gross, spends $1.5 million on fuel and port costs and takes 40 days has a TCE of $87,500 a day. GE calls the same measure its time-charter yield (TCY). Everything below TCE (crew, repairs, insurance, depreciation, interest) is broadly fixed per ship per day, which is why a change in TCE flows almost one-for-one into profit.

Alongside sit the flag state (DG Shipping), classification societies, P&I and war-risk insurers, bunker suppliers, ports and canals, crew agencies and the Alang shipbreakers. In a tight market the owner in Step 3 captures most of the surplus; in a glut the charterer and the yard do.

Who captures value depends on the cycle. Ship supply is fixed in the short run because a newbuild takes two and a half to four years, and freight is a small share of the delivered value of the cargo. When ships are scarce, a refiner pays almost any rate because the alternative is no crude: at $200,000 a day, a 45-day Gulf–China round voyage for a two-million-barrel very large crude carrier (VLCC) adds only about $4.5 a barrel. In a glut the opposite happens and owners earn below their cash costs, as crude-tanker owners did in 2021. Over a full cycle, value is captured mostly by asset timing (buying ships cheaply in slumps and selling them in booms) and by a low cost of capital. Operating skill (high utilisation, low off-hire, fuel efficiency and approval by oil majors' vetting inspectors) matters at the margin.

The single concept every reader must own is the ton-mile. Shipping demand is not tonnes of cargo; it is tonnes multiplied by the distance they travel. A barrel shipped from the US Gulf to India uses roughly two and a half to three times the ship-days of a barrel shipped from Ras Tanura to Jamnagar. When sanctions, wars or canal closures force cargo onto longer routes, the world needs more ship-days even if it ships the same, or less, oil. This is why the three great disruptions of the past five years (Russia's re-routed crude in 2022, the Red Sea diversions around the Cape of Good Hope in 2023–24, and the Hormuz closure in 2026) were good for tanker owners, even though the last of them destroyed volume.

In plain terms

TCE is the daily rent a ship earns after paying for the fuel and port bills of the trip. If you remember one number per ship type, make it that one. A Suezmax tanker that costs about $9,000 a day to crew and maintain makes a loss at $10,000 of TCE and a fortune at $90,000.

Indian owners add one local twist. Government and public-sector cargo, such as crude imported by state refiners, LPG imported by the oil marketers and coal for public utilities, is shipped through tenders in which Indian-flagged ships have a right of first refusal (ROFR): they may match the lowest foreign bid and take the cargo. ROFR transfers volume certainty, not a price premium. It is the base of SCI's tanker business and a modest advantage for GE, and it is the tool through which the government now wants to rebuild an Indian fleet (Section 09).

Finally, both companies report in rupees but earn in dollars. Charter hire and freight are priced in US dollars everywhere. A weaker rupee therefore lifts rupee revenue with no change in the underlying business: the rupee moved from an average of about ₹87.6 per dollar in FY26 to ₹94.9 on 19 October 2026, an 8% boost to rupee earnings on the same dollar rates. GE's Q4 FY26 profit of ₹1,044 crore included a ₹236.8 crore foreign-exchange gain. This report quotes earnings rates in dollars a day and profit in rupees, and says which is which.

02

Small at sea, big at home: 0.35% of the world, 40% of the Indian flag

The two members are minnows in a 2.4-billion-tonne world fleet and giants under the Indian flag, which itself carries only about 5–7% of India's own trade.

The world merchant fleet is about 2.4 billion deadweight tonnes across roughly 110,000 ships, and it carries about 12.3–12.5 billion tonnes of seaborne trade a year. GE's 3.24 million dwt and SCI's 5.26 million dwt together are about 0.35% of that fleet. Neither is close to the scale of the global leaders, which run 80–200 ships in a single segment. At home the picture inverts. According to the Directorate General of Shipping, India's flag fleet at 31 December 2025 was 1,592 vessels of 14.02 million gross tonnes (GT) and 21.41 million dwt. The two members' 8.50 million dwt is about 40% of that. By gross tonnage, SCI's 54 overseas-trading ships (2.77 million GT) are the largest Indian overseas fleet and GE's 39 (1.78 million GT) the second, together 37% of India's overseas tonnage.

The bigger story is how little of India's own trade travels on Indian ships. Around 95% of India's trade by volume and 70% by value moves by sea. In 1987-88, under cargo reservation, Indian ships carried 40.7% of India's export-import cargo. Liberalisation in the 1990s removed the privileges; foreign owners with cheaper capital, no Indian tax rules and no Indian crewing obligations won the tenders; and the share fell to 6.53% by FY20. Today foreign-flagged ships carry about 93–95% of India's sea trade and collect freight of about $70–75 billion, or roughly ₹6 lakh crore, a year. That outflow is the number the government quotes when it talks about "import substitution in shipping".

In plain terms

India pays foreign shipowners about ₹6 lakh crore a year to carry its trade. The two listed Indian owners together collect about ₹11,000 crore of revenue. The government wants to close part of that gap, and SCI is its chosen instrument; whether that makes SCI a better investment depends on the price at which it is asked to buy ships.

03

The Hormuz paradox: less oil, more money Deep dive

A war that removed nearly a third of the world's seaborne crude supply from normal trade produced the richest tanker market in history. Understanding why, and why GE's own earnings lagged the headlines, is the key to reading these companies in 2026–27.

On 28 February 2026 war broke out in West Asia. Within days the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 14 million barrels a day of crude (about 30% of seaborne crude trade) and about 3.4 million barrels a day of refined products normally pass, was effectively closed to normal traffic. Hormuz tanker traffic fell about 95% in March. More than 3 million barrels a day of Gulf refining capacity shut, and product trade fell 2.4 million barrels a day in March alone. About 6% of the crude-tanker fleet and 4% of the product-tanker fleet was trapped inside the Gulf. Asia, which takes 84% of Hormuz flows, scrambled for replacement barrels.

Why less oil meant more money

Tanker demand fell in tonnes but rose in effective ship-days, and supply fell faster than demand. Four mechanisms did the work. First, distance: Asian refiners replaced Gulf crude with Atlantic barrels from the US, Brazil, Guyana and West Africa; Atlantic crude trade rose 13% in Q1 FY27, and a barrel from the US Gulf needs two and a half to three times the ship-days of a Gulf barrel. Saudi Arabia diverted exports to its Red Sea port of Yanbu, where loadings exceeded 5 million barrels a day against about 1 million normally. Second, trapped and queuing tonnage: ships inside the Gulf could not trade normally, and the protected shuttles of September tied VLCCs up in queues. Third, war risk: owners refused Gulf voyages or demanded rates that covered premiums of several per cent of hull value per transit. Fourth, product dislocation: about a quarter of the LR2 fleet switched from clean products to dirty crude trades, tightening the product market too.

The route map is bimodal

One more feature of the 2026 market confuses almost every headline: rates differ enormously by route. Routes that load inside or next to the Gulf carry war-risk-inflated rates, while Atlantic routes pay something much closer to normal. On 16 October 2026 the Baltic Exchange's VLCC Middle East Gulf–China route (TD3C) assessed $1.37 million a day, while the MR product-tanker route from Rotterdam to the US East Coast (TC2) paid $11,500, close to breakeven. A "tanker rate" without a route and a date is meaningless in 2026.

In plain terms

The rates you read in the news are what one ship earned on one route on one day, often a route next to a war zone. A shipowner earns an average across many voyages, ballast legs back to the loading area, ships on fixed charters and days in dry dock. In 2026 that average is far below the headline.

What the members actually earned

This is the most common error in writing about Indian shipping in 2026, and an earlier draft of our own research made it. Why does GE's realised crude TCY sit at about two-thirds of the market Suezmax average? Five reasons. Timing: a voyage fixed in March at March's rates is earned over April and May, and some cargoes were fixed before the peak; GE's crude TCY was $61,424 in Q4 FY26 against a market far higher in March. Mix: two of its five crude ships are Aframaxes, and its crude fleet averages 15.5 years, older than the eco-ships that earn the top rates. Routes: GE does not transit Hormuz; all its ships that needed to leave the Gulf have left, and one remains inside on a time charter to ADNOC. It earns on the Atlantic and Red Sea legs, not the war-zone loadings that set the record headlines. Blend: averages include ballast legs, waiting time and off-hire. Peer check: Frontline, a pure spot tanker owner, reported spot TCEs of $111,500 a day for its Suezmaxes and $92,400 for its LR2/Aframaxes in the same April–June quarter. GE's $93,026 crude TCY is consistent with that once its older, Aframax-inclusive fleet is allowed for.

The LPG gap has a different cause. GE's four gas carriers (three VLGCs and one mid-size gas carrier) are all on fixed-rate time charters. When the market tripled, GE's earnings did not move; they fell 5% as one charter rolled. The upside arrives only when charters reprice. Two did in May–July 2026: one at the old base rate plus a profit share worth about 25–30% more at current spot rates, the other at a 25–30% higher fixed rate. The third VLGC comes off charter in the first calendar quarter of 2027, with the market then about $20,000 a day above its current rate. "GE captures the VLGC boom" is therefore wrong for FY27 to date; it will capture a slice of it, late.

In plain terms

A time charter is a fixed-price contract. It protects the owner when rates collapse and costs it money when rates explode. GE chose to fix all its gas carriers and part of its product and bulk fleets; its crude ships it left fully on the spot market. Its Q1 FY27 results show exactly what that choice does in a boom: crude earnings almost tripled, gas earnings slipped.

SCI does not disclose TCE by segment or its spot-versus-charter mix, so its earnings must be read from its segment profit. Its tanker segment (which includes its gas carriers) earned ₹525.23 crore of profit before interest and tax (PBIT) in Q1 FY27, against ₹244.74 crore a year earlier and ₹1,189.54 crore for the whole of FY26: 44% of the previous year's tanker profit in a single quarter. A rough per-ship reading of FY26 tanker revenue, about ₹105–115 crore per ship-year on 31 tankers and gas carriers, suggests SCI's realised earnings were well below GE's, reflecting period charters to public-sector refiners at sub-spot rates and an older fleet; this is an indicative estimate because SCI does not publish the inputs. SCI was also more exposed to the Gulf itself: four of its ships, Desh Garima, Desh Suraksha, Desh Vibhor and Desh Vaibhav, were stuck west of Hormuz on 31 March 2026. Desh Garima reached Mumbai on 22 April; the other three were still awaiting clearance on 8 May. SCI books freight on percentage of completion and reported "no material impact".

The symmetrical risk is the core of the bear case. Several events would release effective tanker supply at once: a durable reopening of Hormuz, a Russia–Ukraine settlement that unwinds sanctions-driven re-routing, a normal Red Sea, and the US proposal to bring "dozens to hundreds" of sanctioned dark-fleet tankers back into mainstream trade. Any of these would shorten voyages or add ships, just as the shipyards deliver the orderbook (Section 05). A war that cuts trade can be the best thing that happens to a tanker owner; a peace that restores it can be the worst. That is why the market pays about six times trailing earnings for GE and seven and a half for SCI.

In plain terms

The war made ships scarce by making voyages long and dangerous, not by making oil plentiful. Peace would make the same ships plentiful overnight. Investors are being paid record profits now in exchange for owning that reversal risk, and the reported numbers will look best a quarter or two after the market has already peaked.

What should an investor take from the paradox? Three rules. First, always ask whether a rate is a market average, a route assessment or a company's realised TCY. Second, look at forward cover: the share of next quarter's days already fixed and at what rate tells you more about the next result than any headline. Third, remember that the asset values that drive NAV (Section 11) react to the expected duration of the boom, not to the daily rate; the five-year-old Suezmax value index stood at 211 in June 2026, about double its early-2022 level, which tells you the market prices this as a long but finite boom.

Demand: miles, not tonnes

Five demand engines drive the members' ship-days: oil trade distance, India's own energy imports, LPG, dry bulk and offshore drilling. In 2026 the first one dwarfs the rest.

Shipping demand is derived demand: nobody wants a ship-day for its own sake. Each of the members' businesses rides a different end-market, and in 2026 those markets moved in different directions. India's energy imports: the members' home cargo

India imports about 4.8–5.0 million barrels a day of crude, and in January–February 2026 roughly 2.6 million barrels a day of it, about half, came through Hormuz. When the strait closed, Indian refiners bought from the US, West Africa, Brazil and Guyana, which is longer-haul cargo, and from Saudi Arabia's Red Sea terminal at Yanbu. For the state refiners, much of that crude moves on tenders where Indian-flagged ships hold a right of first refusal, which gives SCI volume. For LPG, the shift was more dramatic: India buys about 60% of its LPG abroad, and pre-war about 90% of those imports came through Hormuz. By Q1 FY27 the US supplied about 68%. Every one of those cargoes needs a VLGC for several times longer than a Gulf cargo. The refining side of this story, including the losses the oil marketing companies took, is covered in our refining report.

In plain terms

When India had to buy its cooking gas from Texas instead of Qatar and Saudi Arabia, every ship carrying it spent weeks longer at sea. The country did not use much more gas, but it needed far more gas-carrier days, and that is what owners of gas carriers sell.

Sanctions and the shadow fleet: demand-neutral, supply-relevant

One more force shapes tanker demand from the supply side. Since 2022 a "shadow" or "dark" fleet of older tankers has carried sanctioned Russian, Iranian and Venezuelan oil outside mainstream insurance and finance. Industry estimates put it at roughly 1,000 or more tankers, about 17–20% of tanker capacity by dwt (an indicative figure from Lloyd's List and S&P Global estimates in 2025, not re-verified). Those ships are unavailable to mainstream charterers, which tightens the market for compliant owners like GE and SCI. The US Treasury's sanctions on Rosneft and Lukoil in October 2025 pushed Indian refiners away from Russian crude and onto longer-haul cargoes. In October 2026 the US Energy Secretary floated a plan to move "dozens to hundreds" of sanctioned tankers into mainstream trade, which would add supply. Mainstream owners do not trade with the shadow fleet; they benefit from it absorbing tonnage, and they lose if it is legitimised.

In plain terms

Roughly one tanker in six is busy carrying sanctioned oil and is off-limits to normal customers. That shrinks the pool of ships that Indian refiners and Western oil companies can hire. If governments let those ships back into ordinary trade, the pool grows overnight without a single new ship being built.

Coastal shipping, the smallest engine, is a policy story. Cabotage rules reserve much coastal cargo for Indian-flagged ships, and the new Coastal Shipping Act 2025 sets up a coastal licensing framework. SCI's coastal freight rose 10% in FY26 with coastal ship availability of 99.2%, and its export-import liner services ran at 95% utilisation. These volumes are steady but small next to the tanker segment, which earned 92.9% of SCI's FY26 segment profit.

Put together, demand in 2026 is less a growth story than a dislocation story. The world shipped less oil and modestly more dry cargo, but it needed many more tanker and gas-carrier days. For the investor that distinction is central: growth driven by tonnes would persist for years, while growth driven by miles can reverse with a ceasefire. The best guide to which kind of demand you are looking at is whether volumes and earnings are moving in the same direction. In Q1 FY27 they were moving in opposite directions.

05

Supply: the orderbook returns, and the old ships stay

Ship supply is fixed for two to four years, then arrives in waves. In 2026 owners ordered heavily again, while almost nobody scrapped the ageing tankers. Supply, not demand, will decide when this boom ends.

Shipping cycles are mostly supply cycles. A new tanker takes two and a half to four years from order to delivery, so a demand shock produces high rates for as long as it takes the yards to respond, and then the yards respond all at once. The best single measure of what is coming is the orderbook as a percentage of the existing fleet. GE's Q1 FY27 investor presentation, which draws on Clarksons data, gives the August 2026 figures: crude tankers 27.1%, product tankers 20.7%, dry bulk 13.9% and large LPG carriers (65,000 cbm and above) 34.9%. Three months earlier, in May 2026, they were 23.3%, 18.5%, 13.2% and 26.8%. A year before that the crude orderbook was about 11%.

The floor: an old fleet that nobody scraps

Against that wave stands an unusually old fleet. Because rates were high from 2022 and the shadow fleet bought up old tonnage, owners scrapped almost nothing. Crude-tanker demolition was 0.08% of the fleet in 2023, 0.14% in 2024 and 0.48% in 2025, against 4.39% in 2018. Product tankers were similar. About 24% of the crude fleet and 20% of the product fleet is now 20 years or older; for LPG carriers, about 10% is 25 or older. In a downturn those ships become scrapping candidates, which is the industry's natural shock absorber. The arithmetic is simple: if a quarter of the crude fleet is old enough to scrap and the orderbook is 27%, the net fleet change over the next few years depends almost entirely on how quickly the old ships leave once rates fall.

Regulation as a supply brake

Environmental rules quietly reduce effective supply. Since 1 January 2023 the IMO's Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index (EEXI) caps engine power on older ships, and the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) rates each ship's annual emissions from A to E; poorly rated ships slow-steam, which means fewer voyages per year, and lose appeal to charterers. The EU Emissions Trading System has covered shipping since 2024, and FuelEU Maritime since 2025 (Section 09). The Hong Kong Convention on ship recycling entered into force on 26 June 2025; India's Alang yards, which handle about 30% of global scrapping by lightweight tonnage, are largely compliant. India's 2025 package adds a ₹4,001 crore shipbreaking credit note: scrap an old ship in India and receive a credit toward an Indian-built newbuild.

06

Where ₹100 goes: fixed costs, near-zero tax

Once a ship is owned, most of its costs are fixed per day. In a boom, a spot-heavy Indian owner with net cash can turn more than half its revenue into profit, a ratio few industries anywhere can match.

A shipowner's costs fall into four layers. Voyage costs (bunker fuel, port dues, canal tolls, commissions) are paid by the owner only on spot voyages; on time charters the charterer pays them. Vessel operating expenses (crew, repairs and maintenance, insurance, stores, lubricants) are paid every day the ship exists, working or not; crew is about 40% of them. Charter hire paid applies when the owner itself hires in extra ships, and general and administrative costs cover the office. Below EBITDA come depreciation (the ship's cost spread over a 20–25-year life), interest and tax, which for Indian shipowners is almost nothing. Then there is a line most industries do not have in size: other income from treasury yield on cash, foreign-exchange gains and profits on selling ships.

The cost structure can be turned into a daily breakeven per ship. Cash breakeven covers operating expenses, overheads and debt service; profit-and-loss breakeven adds depreciation. Using industry operating-cost benchmarks and the companies' financing structures, GE's tankers need about $10,000–12,000 a day to cover cash costs and its bulkers about $7,000–9,000; on a P&L basis its product tankers need about $15,000–18,000, its crude tankers $18,000–22,000 and its bulkers $12,000–14,000. Price-takers in a vast ocean: industry structure

Global shipping is one of the most fragmented big industries in the world. No owner sets a price, so the members compete on cost, timing and access to cargo, and at home on the Indian flag.

The largest crude-tanker owners in the world (Frontline, CMB.Tech, COSCO Shipping Energy, Bahri, Sinokor, the Angelicoussis group) each control less than 5% of the crude fleet, and the top ten together perhaps 25–30%. Dry bulk is even more fragmented: the largest owner, COSCO, has about 5%. The large-LPG-carrier market is the most concentrated, with BW LPG, Dorian, Petredec, Avance, Kumiai and the trading houses running perhaps 40–50% of about 400 VLGCs. These are indicative shares from industry owner rankings. The consequence is simple: every owner is a price-taker, and the members' 0.35% of world tonnage gives them no influence on rates.

Fragmentation has two causes. Ships are standardised, mobile assets that can be bought second-hand, financed against their own value and run by a third-party manager, so the barriers to entry are low. And a single ship is a viable business, so there is no minimum efficient scale beyond a few dozen vessels. The industry's economics are those of a commodity: returns are set by the balance of supply and demand, and individual owners differ mainly in cost of capital, operating efficiency and the timing of asset purchases.

The Indian landscape

Under the Indian flag the structure looks different. SCI is the largest overseas-trading owner by gross tonnage and GE the second; together they hold 37% of Indian overseas GT and about 40% of Indian-flag dwt. The rest is split among unlisted tanker owners (Seven Islands Shipping, with about 25 ships, is the largest), coastal and offshore operators, and cargo owners. Reliance mostly charters rather than owns; Adani has stated plans for an owned fleet; ONGC and Oil India charter offshore vessels and rigs. Several listed companies sit next to the sub-sector without being members: Essar Shipping (asset-light after restructuring), Global Offshore Services, Seamec (diving support), Jindal Drilling (jack-ups for ONGC), Dredging Corporation, the port operators and the shipbuilders.

The shareholder registers say a lot about how the market sees each company. In plain terms

Nobody controls shipping prices, so the only ways to win are to buy ships cheaply, run them well, borrow cheaply and have cargo others cannot get. GE wins on the first three. SCI's edge is the fourth: government cargo and government policy. Most of this report is about how much each edge is worth.

China deserves a separate note. It builds about 55–60% of the world's new tonnage by gross tonnage and owns the largest fleet by deadweight. The US Section 301 port fees on Chinese-built and Chinese-operated ships, introduced from 14 October 2025, were suspended for a year under the US–China truce of November 2025. Yard choice now carries political weight: the bidders for SCI's eight-VLGC programme paired Indian yards with Korean builders (HD KSOE, Samsung Heavy, Hanwha Ocean), not Chinese ones. For Indian owners the practical effect is that newbuilds from Korea and Japan cost more and the Indian yards that the government wants to use are still learning to build large merchant ships.

Five ways to own a ship

The two members contain five distinct business models, from a spot-heavy asset trader to a utility-like LNG carrier on a 25-year contract. Each earns, risks and is valued differently.

"Shipping" is not one business. A crude tanker trading spot, a gas carrier on a 25-year charter to an LNG importer and a jack-up rig drilling for ONGC share little except that they float. The two listed members together contain five archetypes, and the mix explains most of the difference in their margins, returns and valuations.

Archetype C, Greatship, reprices slowly. Its rigs and vessels work on contracts of one to three years, so its FY26 results (PAT ₹438.85 crore, the best since FY16) reflect day rates agreed in 2023–25. In plain terms

GE is mostly a trader that owns its own stock-in-trade: it makes money from running ships at market rates and from buying and selling them at the right moments. SCI is a public utility for the country's oil and gas cargo with a tanker business attached, and much of its fleet strategy is decided by the government. Same industry, different jobs.

How the models behave in a downturn is the critical test. A spot-heavy owner loses revenue first and fastest when rates fall; it survives on its balance sheet. A charter-heavy national carrier is cushioned by contract cover and ROFR volumes but has less financial room. The record bears this out. GE lost money in FY18 (−₹210 crore) and FY19 (−₹21 crore), when tanker rates were weak and its offshore arm was in a long winter, but its net debt never threatened the business, and it emerged able to buy. SCI lost money in FY19 (−₹63 crore) and earned single-digit returns for most of the decade. Its ten-year average return on equity, FY17 to FY26, was roughly 8% by our estimate.

Investors value these archetypes differently. Spot-heavy owners are valued on NAV, and in a boom usually trade at a discount to it because the market expects rates and asset values to fall; GE trades at about 0.8 times. Contract-backed businesses such as LNG carriers and drilling contractors on long contracts are valued more on EV/EBITDA and dividend yield, often at higher multiples because their cash flows are visible. National carriers carry a governance discount and a policy option: the market prices both the risk that the state uses them for low-return mandates and the chance of a sale or re-rating. SCI's mix of B, D and E is why its FY26 P/E (8.9×) is higher than GE's (7.3×) despite lower returns, a pattern Section 12 examines.

Tax, flag and ₹69,725 crore: the policy turn

For thirty years India let its flag fleet shrink. Since 2021 it has done the opposite: a flag subsidy, a tougher right of first refusal, five new maritime laws, a ₹69,725 crore package and a national-fleet plan built around SCI. Tonnage tax underpins all of it.

Tonnage tax: why the "E" in P/E is almost pre-tax

Since the Finance Act 2004, a qualifying Indian shipping company can opt out of normal corporate tax on its shipping income and pay tax instead on a notional income computed from the net tonnage of each ship (Chapter XII-G of the Income-tax Act 1961, sections 115V–115VZC, carried forward under the new Income-tax Act 2025, whose section numbering we have not checked). The notional income per ship per day rises in slabs with net tonnage; the normal corporate rate then applies to that notional figure, not to the actual profit. Both GE and SCI have opted in. GE's annual report confirms its shipping income is assessed on deemed tonnage income under section 115VA, and that the group opted for the 25.17% rate under section 115BAA on its non-tonnage income, such as Greatship's drilling and treasury income.

Budget 2025-26 extended the regime to inland vessels. Non-shipping income is taxed normally, which is why GE's consolidated rate (2.75% in FY26) sits slightly above its standalone rate.

In plain terms

Indian shipowners pay tax on the size of their ships, not on what the ships earn. In a boom that tax is close to nothing. So when you compare GE's six-times earnings with a normal company's twenty-times, remember that the normal company's earnings are after a 25% tax and GE's are almost before it. The fair comparison is with pre-tax profit.

The Indian flag: right of first refusal and the 2021 subsidy

When Indian public-sector entities hire ships through tenders, Indian-flagged ships may match the lowest bid and take the cargo. A June 2021 DG Shipping order set a priority order for that right: first, Indian-built, Indian-flagged and Indian-owned ships; second, foreign-built ships that are Indian-flagged and Indian-owned; third, Indian-built ships under a foreign flag and foreign ownership. The order is designed to favour Indian shipyards as well as Indian owners. In July 2021 the Cabinet added a ₹1,624 crore, five-year subsidy for Indian-flagged ships in the global tenders of ministries and public-sector companies.

The ₹69,725 crore package (September 2025)

On 24 September 2025 the Cabinet approved a ₹69,725 crore package on four pillars. The Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme (₹24,736 crore, running to 31 March 2036) pays Indian yards direct assistance per vessel and includes a ₹4,001 crore shipbreaking credit note. The Maritime Development Fund (₹25,000 crore) combines a ₹20,000 crore Maritime Investment Fund, 49% government-owned, with a ₹5,000 crore Interest Incentivisation Fund to cut the cost of ship debt; it was first announced in the Union Budget of February 2025. The Shipbuilding Development Scheme (₹19,989 crore) funds shipbuilding clusters, infrastructure and an India Ship Technology Centre, with a target of 4.5 million GT of annual domestic shipbuilding capacity. The fourth pillar is legal, tax and policy reform under a National Shipbuilding Mission. The government projects about 30 lakh jobs and ₹4.5 lakh crore of investment.

New laws, and Europe's carbon price

In 2025 Parliament replaced the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 with the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 and passed the Coastal Shipping Act, the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, the Bills of Lading Act and the Indian Ports Act. The changes include easier registration of Indian-owned ships (including foreign-built ships on bareboat-charter-cum-demise terms), a broader definition of an "Indian vessel" and a coastal licensing framework. Their effect on the members is incremental: they make it easier to bring ships under the Indian flag, which supports the national-fleet plan.

The external rules matter as much. The EU Emissions Trading System covers ships of 5,000 GT and above: 50% of emissions on voyages into or out of the EU and 100% of intra-EU voyages and time at berth. Owners surrendered allowances in 2025 for 40% of 2024 emissions and in 2026 for 70% of 2025 emissions, and from 2027 surrender 100%; methane and nitrous oxide are covered from 2026. The European Commission proposed a targeted revision on 17 July 2026. Most of the cost is passed to charterers under standard contract clauses, but it penalises older, less efficient ships, which charterers avoid on European trades.

The government has decided India should own more of the ships that carry its oil, gas and containers, and it is putting up money, cargo preference and cheaper loans to make that happen. SCI is the main vehicle. GE benefits indirectly through ROFR and cheaper finance but has chosen not to expand on the back of policy.

Seventy-eight years of cycles

The members have lived through cargo reservation, liberalisation, a super-cycle, an eight-year bust, an offshore winter, a pandemic and three wars. The pattern is always the same: scarcity, ordering, glut, scrapping.

Great Eastern Shipping was founded in 1948 by the Sheth and Bhiwandiwalla families; its first ship was the Liberty ship SS Fort Ellice. The Shipping Corporation of India was formed on 2 October 1961 by merging the state's Eastern Shipping Corporation (1950) and Western Shipping Corporation (1956). For three decades Indian shipping grew under cargo reservation, and in 1987-88 Indian ships carried 40.7% of the country's trade. Liberalisation in the 1990s removed those privileges, SCI's shares began trading in 1992 after an initial disinvestment, and the Indian-flag share began a thirty-year slide.

Three wars then rewrote the tanker map. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, followed by the EU crude embargo (5 December 2022), the products embargo (5 February 2023) and the G7 price cap, sent Russian crude on long voyages to India and China and replaced it in Europe with long-haul Atlantic and Middle Eastern barrels. Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping from November 2023 diverted ships around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 3,000–3,500 nautical miles and 10–14 days to an Asia–Europe voyage. The 2026 Hormuz closure then destroyed volume but stretched distances and trapped ships. Each event raised tanker earnings without any increase in oil demand, which is the lesson of Section 03 in historical form.

In plain terms

Shipping booms end when the ships ordered during the boom arrive. The 2008 boom ended that way, and so did the 2020 storage spike. What is unusual about 2022–26 is that four years of high rates came from geopolitics rather than from demand, and owners ordered relatively little until 2025. That is now changing.

Two patterns recur in the members' histories. GE has consistently bought ships in slumps and sold them in booms, keeping debt low enough to survive the troughs; its loss years were small. SCI has consistently been asked to serve policy goals (cargo security, coastal services, island connectivity, now the national fleet) and its returns have been lower and lumpier, with a strategic sale announced in 2019 that has not happened. The next section turns these patterns into the numbers an investor should track.

Management controls the fleet, its age and mix, costs and leverage. The market controls TCY and the rupee. That is why the same company can earn ₹630 Cr one year and ₹2,943 Cr four years later with roughly the same ships.

A worked example with round numbers, labelled illustrative. A product tanker works 350 days a year at a TCY of $40,000 a day. At ₹90 to the dollar that is ₹126 crore of TCE revenue (350 × $40,000 = $14 million). Operating costs and overheads of $10,000 a day for all 365 calendar days come to $3.65 million, about ₹33 crore. Depreciation on a ship bought for $45 million and written off over 25 years is about ₹16–18 crore; call it ₹18 crore. With no debt there is no interest, and tonnage tax is about ₹0.2 crore. Profit after tax is about ₹75 crore. If the TCY falls to $20,000, TCE revenue halves to ₹63 crore and profit falls to about ₹12 crore: a 50% fall in the rate produces an 84% fall in profit. That is operating leverage, and it is why the first KPI an analyst checks is always the rate.

1. Fleet size, deadweight and age

What it is. The number of owned ships, their carrying capacity in deadweight tonnes (cargo plus fuel and stores) and their average age. Gas carriers are also measured in cubic metres. Formula. Average age is usually weighted by ship count; some owners weight by dwt. Worked example. GE owns 40 ships of 3,244,888 dwt with an average age of 14.34 years (August 2026): 5 crude tankers (3 Suezmax, 2 Aframax; 15.47 years), 16 product tankers (2 LR2, 4 LR1, 10 MR; 15.57 years), 4 gas carriers (3 VLGCs and 1 mid-size gas carrier; 17.46 years) and 15 dry-bulk carriers (2 Capesize, 10 Kamsarmax, 1 Ultramax, 2 Supramax; 11.81 years). Greatship adds 4 jack-up rigs (14.80 years) and 19 offshore vessels (16.48 years). SCI owns 58 ships of 5,261,096 dwt averaging 15.50 years: 12 crude tankers, 11 product tankers, 5 VLCCs, 3 gas carriers (31 tankers in all, averaging 17.27 years), 15 bulk carriers (13.41 years), 2 liner ships and 10 offshore supply vessels, and it manages 40 more for government bodies.

What drives it. Purchases and sales; age rises by one year every year unless the owner trades out old ships. Benchmark. Under 10 years is good, 10–15 average, over 15 weak; the world tanker fleet averages about 12–13 years. How the members compare. Both are older than the world average. GE's bulk fleet is young and its gas fleet old; SCI's tanker fleet, at over 17 years, is the oldest block in either company, which matters for oil majors' vetting, fuel efficiency and the flag subsidy. GE contracted three ships for expansion in August–September 2026, taking its owned fleet to 42 in Q3 FY27 and 43 by FY29. Definition trap. Ship counts quoted in summaries are often stale or mislabelled: "4 VLGCs" at GE is really 3 VLGCs plus 1 smaller gas carrier, and our own primer still carries an older 38-ship count.

2. TCE and TCY: the market average versus what you actually earn

What it is. The daily earnings of a ship after voyage costs. Formula. TCE = (voyage revenue − voyage costs) ÷ operating days; for a ship on time charter it is simply the daily hire. Worked example. A Suezmax voyage from West Africa to India grosses $6.0 million, spends $1.8 million on bunkers, port and canal costs and commissions, and takes 45 days including the ballast leg: TCE = $4.2 million ÷ 45 = $93,333 a day. What drives it. The market rate on the routes the ship trades, the timing of fixtures, the ship's fuel efficiency and vetting status, and whether it is on a fixed charter. Benchmark. For a Suezmax, above $50,000 is good, $25,000–50,000 average, below $20,000 weak; for an MR, above $30,000 good and below $15,000 weak; for a Capesize above $25,000 good.

How the members compare. GE reports TCY by segment every quarter; SCI does not report TCE at all. GE's Q1 FY27 TCYs were crude $93,026, product $45,471, LPG $41,528 and bulk $22,601 a day. Its crude TCY has risen every quarter for a year, from $33,865 in Q1 FY26 to $47,281 in Q3 FY26, $61,424 in Q4 FY26 and $93,026 in Q1 FY27. The definition trap is the biggest in the industry: the figures most often quoted (Suezmax $143,199, VLGC $127,330 and so on) are industry averages shown in GE's deck for context, not GE's earnings. A second trap: dividing reported revenue by ship-days does not give TCE, because a spot owner's revenue includes voyage costs it then pays out; gross freight can be 1.5–2 times TCE when fuel is expensive.

When you see a dramatic daily rate in the news, ask three questions: on which route, on which day, and is it a market average or this company's own number? GE's own crude ships averaged $93,000 a day in Q1 FY27. That is a record for GE, and still about two-thirds of the market Suezmax average.

3. Spot versus time-charter mix, and forward cover

What it is. The share of a fleet's days earning floating spot rates versus fixed time-charter rates, and the share of next quarter's days already booked. Formula. Time-charter share = time-charter days ÷ total operating days; forward cover = days fixed for the coming quarter ÷ days available. Worked example. GE has about 25–26% of its shipping capacity on time charter, with its crude fleet entirely spot and its gas carriers entirely on charter. For Q2 FY27 it had fixed 46% of crude days at about $90,000, 61% of product days at about $45,000, 100% of LPG days and 57% of bulk days, plus 75% of rig days and 89–94% of offshore-vessel days. What drives it. Management's view of the cycle and its appetite for volatility; charterers' willingness to pay for long periods.

Benchmark. There is no universal "good" level; the right mix depends on the cycle. High spot exposure is right in an up-cycle and painful in a bust. The question to ask is whether the mix is wrong-footed. How the members compare. GE discloses its mix; SCI does not, though its tanker fleet is substantially on period charters to public-sector refiners, which explains much of its lower realised earnings in a boom and its steadier earnings in a slump. Definition trap. Forward cover tells you about next quarter's revenue, not about this quarter's TCY; and a high cover figure at a low rate (GE's LPG book) can hide a large gap to market.

4. Breakeven per day

What it is. The daily TCE a ship must earn to cover its costs. Formula. Cash breakeven = (opex + G&A + interest + scheduled debt repayment) ÷ operating days; P&L breakeven = (opex + G&A + depreciation + interest) ÷ operating days. Worked example (illustrative). A Suezmax with opex of $8,750 a day, G&A of $1,200, depreciation of $2.0 million a year and interest of $1.4 million a year, working 350 days, has a cash breakeven (excluding repayment) of about $14,400 and a P&L breakeven of about $20,100 a day. What drives it. Purchase price (through depreciation), leverage (interest and repayments), crew costs and the ship's age (repairs). Benchmark. TCE above twice the P&L breakeven is good; 1.2–2 times average; below 1 means losses. How the members compare. Using indicative breakevens, GE's Q1 FY27 earnings ranged from 1.7 times breakeven (bulk) to 4.7 times (crude), as the chart in Section 06 shows; SCI's breakevens are not disclosed but are estimated higher (cash breakeven for its tankers about $12,000–15,000) because of older ships, wage scales and more debt. Definition trap. Companies that quote a "cash breakeven" often exclude debt repayment or dry-docking; always ask what is in it.

5. Operating expense per day

What it is. The daily cost of keeping a ship crewed, maintained, insured and supplied, regardless of whether it is earning. Formula. Vessel operating expenses ÷ calendar ship-days (owned ships). Worked example. GE's standalone Q1 FY27 operating expenses were ₹239 crore against 3,679 revenue days, about ₹6.5 lakh a day, roughly $6,900 at ₹93.7; its G&A of ₹82 crore adds about $2,400 a day. Because revenue days include some in-chartered ships, the per-owned-ship figure is slightly higher. What drives it. Crew (about 40%), repairs, insurance (war-risk cover in 2026), lubricants, and the ship's age and type. Benchmark (indicative industry ranges): VLCC about $9,500–10,500 a day, Suezmax $8,500–9,000, Aframax/LR2 $8,000–8,500, MR $7,000–7,500, VLGC $9,000–10,000, Capesize $6,500–7,000, Supramax $5,000–5,500. How the members compare. Indian-crewed ships run near the low end on crew; SCI's public-sector wage agreements and older fleet push its costs higher, though it does not publish a per-day figure. Definition trap. Some owners quote opex excluding dry-docking, which is capitalised and depreciated; compare like with like.

6. Utilisation and operating days

What it is. The share of calendar days a ship or rig is earning. Formula. Operating (revenue) days ÷ calendar days. For offshore, contracted days ÷ available days. Worked example. A ship that spends 25 days in dry dock every two and a half years and five days a year off-hire for repairs loses about 15 days a year: utilisation of about 96%. GE's shipping revenue days rose from 3,507 in Q1 FY26 to 3,679 in Q1 FY27 as the fleet grew. Its drilling rigs worked about 286 of 364 available days in Q1 FY27, about 79%, because one rig was idle from the end of April through the monsoon. What drives it. Dry-docking schedules, breakdowns, waiting for cargo and, offshore, contract gaps. Benchmark. Shipping above 97% good, 94–97% average, below 92% weak; offshore above 90% good, 75–90% average, below 70% weak. How the members compare. SCI reported coastal ship availability of 99.2% and utilisation of 99% in FY26; GE's shipping utilisation is estimated at 96–98%; GE's offshore cover for Q2 FY27 was 75% for rigs and 89–94% for vessels. Definition trap. Global jack-up "utilisation" of about 70% in GE's deck is on a total-fleet basis; marketed-fleet utilisation, which excludes cold-stacked rigs, is usually 85–90%.

In plain terms

A ship earns nothing on the days it sits in dry dock, but it still pays its crew. For tankers, utilisation is mostly a given; the swing factor is the rate. For drilling rigs it is the opposite: the rate is fixed for years, and a single idle rig for a monsoon season can cost a quarter of the offshore arm's profit growth.

7. EBITDA per operating day

What it is. Cash earnings per ship per working day: a single number that blends rate, mix and cost. Formula. (TCE revenue − opex − G&A) ÷ revenue days, or segment EBITDA ÷ ship-days. Worked example. GE's Q1 FY27 standalone TCE revenue was ₹1,412 crore; less opex of ₹239 crore and G&A of ₹82 crore leaves ₹1,091 crore over 3,679 revenue days, about ₹2.97 lakh a day or roughly $31,700. What drives it. Rate, utilisation, cost and mix. Benchmark. Above $20,000 good, $10,000–20,000 average, below $8,000 weak. How the members compare. GE's FY26 shipping segment earned an indicative $25,000–26,000 per ship-day (segment PBIT of ₹2,637 crore plus about ₹530 crore of shipping depreciation, over about 14,000 revenue days at ₹87.6). SCI's FY26 standalone EBITDA of ₹2,633 crore over 21,170 ship-days is about ₹1.24 lakh a day, roughly $14,000, an indicative figure that includes liner, technical services and management fees. Definition trap. GE's company-reported "EBITDA" includes other income (₹1,619 crore consolidated in Q1 FY27 against screener operating profit of ₹1,338 crore plus ₹281 crore other income); screener's operating profit excludes it.

8. NAV per share and P/NAV

What it is. The estimated market value of the fleet plus net cash and other net assets, per share, and the share price as a multiple of it. Formula. NAV = Σ (broker value of each ship) + net cash − other net liabilities; P/NAV = price ÷ NAV per share. Worked example. GE publishes its NAV every quarter. At 30 June 2026 its consolidated NAV was ₹1,862–1,911 a share (midpoint ₹1,886) and its standalone, shipping-only NAV ₹1,512; at 31 March 2026 they were ₹1,772–1,821 and ₹1,422. At ₹1,510 the shares trade at about 0.80 times the consolidated midpoint; on 3 August, at ₹1,398, they traded at 0.74 times. Multiply the midpoint by about 14.28 crore pre-buyback shares and NAV is about ₹26,900 crore. Take out normalised consolidated net cash of ₹7,726 crore and the implied value of the fleet plus Greatship is about ₹19,200 crore. The market capitalisation of ₹21,564 crore less ₹8,056 crore of reported net cash prices the same ships and rigs at about ₹13,500 crore: the discount sits on the ships, not on the cash.

What drives it. Ship values (Section 05), retained earnings and capital returns. Benchmark. Below 0.7 times is cheap, 0.8–1.0 average, above 1.2 rich; global listed owners trade at 0.7–1.2 times across the cycle, at a discount when the market expects rates to fall. How the members compare. GE's consolidated NAV per share compounded at 26% a year from ₹592 in June 2021 to ₹1,886 in June 2026 (standalone 23%). SCI publishes no NAV; its book value per share was ₹170.95 at September 2025. Our rough estimate, marking SCI's ships at 1.5–1.9 times their depreciated book value, adding its LNG joint-venture stakes and deducting net debt, gives an indicative range of about ₹200–310 a share, which would put the ₹259 share price somewhere around NAV rather than at a clear discount. Definition trap. GE's consolidated NAV is a range because Greatship's rigs are valued as a range; quote the basis. And the gap between standalone and consolidated NAV (about ₹374 a share) is roughly what the method attributes to Greatship.

9. The asset-value index

What it is. An index of second-hand prices for a standard ship of a given type and age, usually five years old. Formula. Current price ÷ base-period price × 100. Worked example. GE's index of five-year-old ships stood at Suezmax 211, MR 185, Capesize 173 and VLGC 137 in June 2026, with a base of about early 2022; crude and product tanker values rose another 5–10% in Q1 FY27. A five-year-old Suezmax that cost about $45 million in early 2022 would therefore cost roughly $95 million now (illustrative). What drives it. Expected future earnings over the ship's remaining life, newbuild prices and yard slots, interest rates and the availability of ship finance. Benchmark. There is no "good" level; the signal is the direction and the gap to newbuild prices. When a five-year-old ship costs close to a newbuild, the market expects the boom to last; when values fall while spot rates are still high, the market expects a turn.

How the members compare. The index matters far more to GE, because it drives GE's NAV and its decisions to buy or sell. Our illustrative sensitivity: GE's NAV implies its ships and rigs are worth about ₹19,200 crore, or about ₹1,345 a share. A 10% move in ship values moves NAV per share by about ₹134, roughly 7%. SCI's book carries ships at depreciated historical cost, so higher ship values do not show in its accounts at all. Definition trap. The index base matters: an earlier reading assumed early 2021 as the base, but the series reads about 103–115 in March 2022, so the base is about early 2022.

10. Orderbook as a percentage of the fleet

What it is. Ships on order in a segment divided by the existing fleet, the best forward indicator of supply. Formula. Orderbook dwt (or cbm for gas carriers) ÷ fleet dwt. Pair it with the delivery schedule and the share of the fleet that is old enough to scrap. Worked example. With a crude orderbook of 27.1%, about 24% of the crude fleet aged 20 years or more, and scrapping running at under 0.5% a year, the crude fleet could grow by about 6–8% a year in 2027–28 if no old ships leave, or by close to nothing if old ships are scrapped as fast as new ones arrive (illustrative arithmetic on the delivery schedule in Section 05). What drives it. Owners' confidence, yard capacity and newbuild prices; ordering rises after a year or two of high rates. Benchmark. Below 10% good, 10–20% average, above 25% weak. How the members compare. GE is most exposed to product and crude orderbooks through its spot fleet; its LPG book is protected for now by time charters but will reprice into the 2027 delivery wave. SCI's planned VLGC expansion lands in the most over-ordered segment. Definition trap. 11. Net cash, and why there are three numbers

What it is. Cash and liquid investments minus debt. Formula. Net cash = cash + bank balances + liquid investments − borrowings (with or without lease liabilities); "normalised" figures adjust debt for currency swaps. Worked example. At 30 June 2026 GE's consolidated net cash was ₹8,056 crore as reported (₹7,726 crore normalised); its standalone net cash, which excludes Greatship, was ₹5,889 crore reported or ₹5,559 crore normalised, the US$593 million the company quotes. Its gross debt was ₹799 crore reported, with the last loan maturing in November 2028. SCI's audited March 2026 balance sheet shows borrowings of ₹2,476.93 crore (plus ₹202.32 crore of lease liabilities) against visible liquidity of only ₹435 crore: cash ₹114.86 crore, other bank balances ₹234.17 crore and current mutual-fund investments ₹85.96 crore. On that basis SCI's net debt is about ₹2,042 crore. SCI's own presentations describe a much larger cash position, and its "other current financial assets" jumped to ₹2,710.95 crore without the contents being disclosed, so its net debt lies somewhere between roughly nil (company definition) and about ₹2,000 crore (balance-sheet cash only).

What drives it. Cash profit less capex, dividends and buybacks. Benchmark. Net cash is good; net debt up to 2.5 times EBITDA average; above 4 times weak. How the members compare. GE has net cash of about 2–2.5 times its annual EBITDA; SCI's net debt is at most about 0.9 times FY26 EBITDA. Definition trap. Screener's "borrowings minus investments" misses both companies' bank deposits held in other assets. An AI summary called GE's ₹8,056 crore "mislabelled gross cash"; it is genuine consolidated net cash. Conversely, SCI is often described as "net-cash", which its audited balance sheet does not support.

12. Return on equity through the cycle

What it is. Profit as a percentage of shareholders' equity, measured over a whole cycle rather than a single year. Formula. PAT ÷ average equity, averaged across ten years or a full cycle. Worked example. GE's company-reported consolidated ROE was 8% in FY22, 28% in FY23, 23% in FY24, 18% in FY25, 19% in FY26 and 30% annualised in Q1 FY27. Its earlier decade was much weaker: PAT of ₹748, ₹1,097 and ₹755 crore in FY15–FY17, losses of ₹210 crore in FY18 and ₹21 crore in FY19, then ₹207 and ₹919 crore, with screener ROCE of 4–11%. SCI's cumulative PAT over FY17–FY26 was ₹6,068 crore on average equity of roughly ₹6,500–8,500 crore, an indicative ten-year average ROE of about 8%, with a best year of about 15% (FY26) and a loss year in FY19. What drives it. Rates, leverage, the size of the cash pile (which earns treasury yields but dilutes ROE) and asset timing. Benchmark. A ten-year average above 12% is good, 7–12% average, below 5% weak. How the members compare. GE's recent ROE is strong, and it argues in its annual report that conventional return ratios are "not appropriate" for shipping because asset appreciation bypasses the P&L; it prefers NAV growth, net debt to equity and cash debt-service cover. Definition trap. Screener's ROE (GE 15.9% for FY26) and the company's (19%) differ by basis; a single boom-year ROE says nothing about the cycle.

13. Payout: dividends and buybacks

What it is. The share of profit returned to shareholders. Formula. (Dividends + buybacks) ÷ PAT. Worked example. GE paid ₹35.10 a share for FY26, ₹501.11 crore, 17% of consolidated PAT; it has paid 18 consecutive quarterly dividends, including ₹14.40 for Q1 FY27. On top, its ₹900 crore buyback had returned ₹450.5 crore by 19 October 2026. SCI paid ₹7.5 a share for FY26, about ₹349 crore, 26% of standalone PAT, up from 2–3% in FY22–FY24 and 36% in FY25. What drives it. Capital needs, management philosophy and, for SCI, the government's guideline for public-sector companies (a minimum dividend of 30% of PAT or 4% of net worth, whichever is higher, under DIPAM's 2020 guideline as we understand it, not re-verified). Benchmark. 30–60% plus buybacks is good for a cyclical asset owner; 15–30% average; below 10% weak. Global tanker owners pay out 50–100%. How the members compare. 17. Definition traps, collected

  • Market average vs realised TCY. The Suezmax $143,199 and VLGC $127,330 in GE's deck are industry averages; GE's own Q1 FY27 TCYs were crude $93,026 and LPG $41,528.
  • Route rate vs average. A Baltic route assessment ($1.37 million a day on TD3C, 16 October) is one route on one day, often next to a war zone.
  • TCE vs revenue per day. A spot owner's revenue includes voyage costs; dividing it by ship-days overstates TCE.
  • Revenue from operations vs total income. GE's FY26 revenue from operations was ₹5,409 crore; "total income" of about ₹6,312 crore includes about ₹900 crore of other income.
  • Company EBITDA vs screener operating profit. GE's reported EBITDA includes other income; screener's excludes it. SCI's "operating EBITDA" (₹882 crore in Q1 FY27) differs from screener's ₹837 crore by about ₹45 crore.
  • Segment "result". GE's FY26 shipping and offshore "results" of ₹2,463 crore and ₹479 crore are segment net profit; segment PBIT was ₹2,637 crore and ₹525 crore.
  • Standalone vs consolidated. GE's standalone NAV (₹1,512) excludes Greatship; consolidated (₹1,862–1,911) includes it. SCI's consolidated PAT includes its LNG joint ventures (₹619 crore vs ₹664 crore standalone in Q1 FY27).
  • Net cash bases. GE: ₹8,056 crore consolidated reported vs US$593 million (₹5,559 crore) standalone normalised. SCI: between roughly nil and about ₹2,000 crore of net debt, depending on whether undisclosed "other financial assets" count as cash.
  • Rupee growth vs dollar growth. FY26 revenue rose 2.4% in rupees but was flat to down in dollars.
  • One-offs. GE's Q4 FY26 PAT included a ₹236.8 crore forex gain; SCI's FY26 other income included ₹169.12 crore of interest on tax refunds, about 12% of PBT.
  • P/E under tonnage tax. Earnings are close to pre-tax; compare with pre-tax multiples elsewhere.
  • Fleet counts. GE's "4 VLGCs" are 3 VLGCs plus 1 mid-size gas carrier; check the date of any fleet list.
  • Orderbook labels. Use the deck itself, not a summary: crude 27.1%, product 20.7%, bulk 13.9%, LPG 34.9% (Aug-26).
In plain terms

Most mistakes about shipping companies come from mixing two numbers that look alike: a market rate with a company's rate, standalone with consolidated, total income with revenue, or rupee growth with dollar growth. Before quoting any figure, say which one it is.

18. How to read a shipping company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Earnings per day first (minute 1). Pull the company's own TCY by segment and compare with the previous quarter and the same quarter last year. Ignore the market-rate slides until you have done this. Red flag: TCY falling while market rates rise (wrong mix or poor timing).
  2. Days (minute 2). Revenue days and the fleet count. More days with the same fleet means fewer dry-dockings; fewer days can mean idle rigs or ships stuck behind a war zone.
  3. Forward cover (minute 3). The share of next quarter already fixed and at what rate. This is the best predictor of the next result. Red flag: low cover going into a falling market.
  4. Costs per day (minute 4). Opex and G&A per day; a jump usually means dry-docking, war-risk insurance or crew costs.
  5. Other income and one-offs (minute 5). Strip out forex gains, ship-sale profits and tax-refund interest to see the run-rate. Red flag: profit growth driven by other income.
  6. Segment split (minute 6). GE: shipping vs offshore PBIT. SCI: tankers vs liner, bulk and technical services, plus the joint-venture share below the line.
  7. Balance sheet (minute 7). Net cash or debt on the company's basis and on the audited balance sheet; capex commitments for newbuilds.
  8. NAV (minute 8). For GE, the new NAV per share and its bridge (cash profit, revaluation, dividends); compare with the share price. For SCI, check book value and any fleet-value commentary.
  9. Fleet moves (minute 9). Ships bought and sold, their ages and prices. Buying old ships at high prices is the classic late-cycle error; selling old ships into strength is the opposite.
  10. Market and supply (minute 10). Only now read the market slides: orderbook, scrapping, asset values and management's view. Red flag: an orderbook rising while asset values stall.

The ten-minute routine works because shipping results are mechanical. If you know the days, the rate and the cost per day for each segment, you can predict operating profit within a few per cent; GE's Q1 FY27 standalone TCE less opex and G&A (₹1,091 crore) sits within about 3% of a bottom-up estimate built from its own TCYs and fleet. What you cannot predict from the quarter is the thing that matters most for the share price: how long the rates last. For that, the orderbook, scrapping and asset values in minutes 9 and 10 are the guide.

In plain terms

Read a shipping result from the inside out: rate, days, cost, then the one-offs, then the balance sheet and asset values. The headline profit is the least informative number in the release, because it mixes the market's luck with the company's choices.

GE: a value fund that owns ships Deep dive

Great Eastern Shipping runs its fleet like a portfolio: buy in slumps, sell in booms, hold net cash, publish NAV every quarter. In 2026 it added two new moves: a buyback below NAV and its first expansion orders in years.

GE's annual report says plainly that conventional return ratios are "not appropriate" for a shipowner, because asset appreciation bypasses the profit and loss account and currency moves distort reported profits. It asks to be judged instead on the growth of its net asset value per share, its net debt to equity and its cash debt-service cover. That is the language of an investment company, and the right way to read GE is as a value fund whose assets happen to be ships, with an operating business attached.

How the NAV was built

GE's standalone (shipping-only) NAV per share rose from ₹540 in June 2021 to ₹1,512 in June 2026. The company's own bridge splits that ₹972 increase into ₹950 of cash profit, ₹176 of fleet revaluation and ₹154 paid out as dividends. Roughly 85% of the gain therefore came from earnings retained, not from a rise in ship prices. That matters: an owner whose NAV rises only because ship prices rose has created nothing that cannot reverse, while one whose NAV rises from cash profits has banked the gains. GE's consolidated NAV, which adds Greatship, rose from ₹592 to ₹1,886 over the same five years, a 26% compound annual rate.

"Replace, not expand", and the 2026 exceptions

For years GE's stated fleet policy has been to replace, not expand: sell older ships into strong markets and buy younger ones, keeping the fleet roughly constant. The FY26 record shows it in action. GE bought a Suezmax (Jag Laadki), an MR (Jag Pranesh), a VLGC (Jag Vijay), two Kamsarmaxes (Jag Amol, Jag Anjali) and an Ultramax (Jag Riddhi), and sold a Suezmax, an MR, a VLGC and a Kamsarmax. In Q1 FY27 it bought a 2014-built MR and a 2019-built Kamsarmax and sold two older MRs; in Q2 it bought a 2015-built LR2 (Jag Laxman) and sold a 2009-built one (Jag Lokesh). Age drives disposals, management says, because old ships face "restricted tradability" with oil majors; and eco-designed ships built from 2013 burn 20–25% less fuel, worth $1,000–2,500 a day.

On the August 2026 call management went further: it would rather hold "the majority of the money" than buy ships at today's prices, and any additions would be "1 or 2 ships… on the margin". Then it made three. On 7 August it contracted a 2015-built Kamsarmax of 81,886 dwt; on 8 September a 2019-built Kamsarmax of 81,609 dwt; and on 28 September a newbuild Suezmax of about 157,000 dwt from a Far East yard, for delivery in the second half of FY29. All three were described as "for expansion" and funded from internal accruals. The owned fleet goes from 40 to 42 in Q3 FY27 and to 43 by FY29. Prices were not disclosed; capital commitments at March 2026 were ₹604.71 crore.

In plain terms

GE's expansion is cautious and well chosen. It bought bulk carriers, where asset prices have risen least and the orderbook is thinnest, and it ordered a tanker that will not arrive until 2028–29, after the big delivery wave of 2027–28. It avoided buying tankers at today's prices for delivery now. That is consistent with an owner that thinks the current boom is real but finite.

There is a cautionary footnote in GE's own record. In March 2025 it booked an impairment on ships bought between end-2023 and mid-2024, after prices fell about 20%. Even a disciplined buyer can buy too high, which is why management stresses the "margin of safety" it gives up by buying at peak prices.

The buyback

On 4 August 2026, asked about buybacks, management said buybacks were treated like any other capital-allocation option, with no target P/NAV, that the removal of buyback tax had taken away a tax premium, and that "if we haven't announced one, we are waiting". Twenty-three days later, on 27 August, the board approved an open-market buyback of up to ₹900 crore at a maximum price of ₹1,530, about a 16% premium to the prior close of about ₹1,316. The indicative maximum is 58,82,352 shares, 4.12% of equity; at least ₹675 crore must be spent; promoters do not participate.

In plain terms

Greatship is GE's slow-moving ballast. What could go wrong at GE? The biggest risk is the one it cannot control: a durable peace or a normalisation of trade routes that pulls tanker rates down just as the orderbook delivers, with its crude fleet fully exposed to spot rates. SCI: between a sale and a national-fleet mandate Deep dive

The Shipping Corporation of India has been on the block since 2019 and, since 2025, at the centre of the government's plan to build an Indian fleet. It cannot fully be both. Its tankers earn the profit; its future is being written in New Delhi.

SCI is India's national carrier, a Navratna company 63.75% owned by the government. It runs 58 owned ships (5.26 million dwt, average age 15.5 years), manages 40 more for government bodies, and holds 26–29.08% stakes in four LNG-carrier joint ventures. Its tankers, which include its gas carriers, earn almost all of its profit. Its future, more than any listed company in this library, depends on decisions made by ministries: the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways, the Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM), and the oil ministry through the public-sector refiners.

The business today: a tanker company with passengers

In FY26 SCI's tanker segment earned PBIT of ₹1,189.54 crore on revenue of ₹3,942.23 crore, 92.9% of total segment PBIT of ₹1,280.56 crore. Liner earned ₹74.84 crore (5.8%), technical and offshore services ₹35.29 crore (2.8%), and bulk lost ₹19.11 crore. In plain terms

The government said in 2019 that it would sell SCI. Since 2025 it has been asking SCI to build a national fleet. Both plans are officially alive. Until one is formally dropped, SCI's share price carries a bit of each: some hope of a takeover premium, and some worry about being made to buy ships for policy reasons.

The national-fleet mandate

The 2025–26 pipeline is long. The investor's question is whether this makes SCI a growing, state-backed fleet manager that deserves a higher valuation, or a captive instrument that will earn sub-market returns on mandated capex. The case for the first: SCI gets cargo backing from its joint-venture partners (the oil companies are both owners and customers), cheaper capital from the Maritime Development Fund, ROFR and subsidy support, and a chance to renew its old fleet with modern ships. SCI is being asked to become the backbone of an Indian national fleet, with the oil companies as partners and customers. That could make it a much bigger and more modern company. Carbon, technology and the peace risk

Decarbonisation will slowly make old ships less valuable and new ones more expensive. Peace could make every ship less valuable at once. The second risk is nearer.

Shipping produces roughly 3% of global greenhouse-gas emissions, and its regulators are tightening. The IMO's 2023 strategy targets net-zero emissions "by or around 2050", with checkpoints of a 20–30% cut by 2030 and 70–80% by 2040 against 2008. Its Net-Zero Framework, a global fuel standard with a price on emissions, was approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025, but its adoption vote was postponed by a year in October 2025; we have not checked its status since. The EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime already apply on European trades (Section 09). The practical effect falls on old ships: poorly rated ships under the CII must slow down, lose charter appeal and, on EU trades, pay more for carbon. Dual-fuel newbuilds able to burn LNG, methanol or ammonia cost roughly 10–25% more than conventional ones.

The nearer disruptor is peace. Several events would release effective tanker supply at the same time: a durable reopening of Hormuz (freeing trapped and queuing ships and shortening voyages), a Russia–Ukraine settlement that unwinds sanctions-driven re-routing, a normal Red Sea, and the US proposal to bring sanctioned dark-fleet tankers into mainstream trade. Each would shorten the average voyage or add ships, and they could coincide with the 2027–28 delivery wave, when product-tanker deliveries alone are scheduled at 7.6% of the fleet in 2027 and LPG at 12.1%. Nobody can time geopolitics; the investor can only measure exposure. On that test GE is more exposed in its earnings (spot crude) but better protected in its valuation (net cash, below NAV); SCI is less exposed in its earnings (period charters) but more exposed through its planned capex.

In plain terms

Cleaner-fuel rules will slowly make old ships worth less and new ones cost more, which hurts owners of old fleets like SCI and rewards owners that renew, like GE. But the bigger, faster risk is that the war ends: ships that are now stuck, queuing or sailing the long way round would be back on the market within weeks, just as hundreds of new tankers arrive from the yards.

Bull vs bear

The bull case is that geopolitics and an old fleet keep ships scarce for longer than the market expects. The bear case is that peace and the orderbook arrive together.

The bull case

  • Disruption is persistent: Hormuz, Russia and the Red Sea have kept voyages long since 2022, and each "normalisation" has been followed by a new shock.
  • An old fleet that must be scrapped: about 24% of crude tankers and 20% of product tankers are 20+ years old, scrapping is near zero, and CII, EEXI and EU ETS rules push old ships out.
  • The lag is in the investor's favour: Q3 FY27 will capture the September–October spike; GE's third VLGC and Greatship's rigs reprice upward into 2027.
  • Cheap on every measure: combined 6.3× trailing PAT, about 4.3× trailing EBITDA, GE at 0.80× NAV with 37% of its value in net cash, tonnage tax making earnings near pre-tax.
  • Capital returns: GE's buyback below NAV and SCI's higher payouts; global peers pay out far more, so there is room to rise.
  • Policy tailwind: the ₹69,725 Cr package, the ₹25,000 Cr Maritime Development Fund, ROFR and the oil-company joint venture give SCI cargo-backed growth.

The bear case

  • Peace releases supply at once: trapped ships, queues, re-routed trades and possibly the shadow fleet could return within weeks.
  • The orderbook is back: crude 27.1%, product 20.7% and LPG 34.9% of fleet, with 2027 the heaviest delivery year.
  • Rates are already retreating: crude routes 13–39% below early-October peaks by 19 October; product and Aframax rates "significantly lower" since Q1.
  • Peak-earnings trap: low P/Es on peak profits are normal in shipping; FY22 combined PAT was ₹1,495 Cr, about a third of FY26.
  • SCI's mandate risk: policy capex at 10–12% IRR hurdles, LPG newbuilds into the most over-ordered segment, an old fleet and net debt.
  • GE's late-cycle moves: expansion orders and buybacks near the top; a March 2025 impairment showed the cost of buying high.

The two cases can be framed with the members' own recent history. If rates fall back to the FY25 pattern, combined profit would look like FY25's ₹3,188 crore; if they settle at the FY26 level, about ₹4,300 crore; if Q1 FY27's pace held for a year, about ₹7,700 crore. At today's combined market value, those correspond to P/Es of roughly 10.5, 7.8 and 4.4 times. The market is pricing something between the first two, which is consistent with a view that the war premium fades within a year but rates do not collapse to the 2021–22 trough.
