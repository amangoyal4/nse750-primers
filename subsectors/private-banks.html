00

The one-page view: nineteen banks, one scarce input

Lending is easy to grow and hard to price. Gathering cheap deposits is hard to grow and decides everything else.

India's 19 listed private sector banks held ₹95.6 lakh crore of deposits at the end of March 2026, a little over a third of everything Indians keep in scheduled commercial banks. They earned a combined standalone net profit of ₹1.97 lakh crore in FY26 and, on 13 October 2026, the stock market valued them at ₹34.9 lakh crore, roughly 2.1 times their combined book value and 17.7 times their FY26 earnings. Two banks dominate. HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank together earned 63% of the group's profit and account for 60% of its market value. The other seventeen range from Kotak Mahindra Bank, a ₹4.4 lakh crore conglomerate with more capital than it can use, to CSB Bank, a ₹5,300 crore Kerala gold-loan lender waiting to learn who its next owner will be.

The headline numbers look calm. Gross bad loans across the banking system fell to about 1.8% in March 2026, the lowest in decades, and every one of the 19 banks is profitable on a quarterly basis again. Under the surface, four forces are reshaping the sub-sector at the same time, and a fifth decides who wins.

  • The deposit war. Savers have moved money out of low-yield savings accounts and into term deposits and mutual funds. The savings share of system deposits fell from 34.6% in March 2022 to 28.7% in March 2026. Every bank now pays more for each marginal rupee, and the banks with the weakest current-and-savings (CASA) franchises pay the most.
  • The rate-cycle whiplash. The RBI cut the repo rate by 125 basis points between February and December 2025. Because most retail and small-business loans are now tied directly to the repo rate, loan yields fell within weeks while deposit costs fell slowly. Margins at the large CASA-rich banks hit multi-year lows in the June 2026 quarter. On 7 October 2026 the RBI raised rates for the first time since February 2023, which starts the reverse process.
  • The end of a credit cycle. Microfinance and unsecured retail loans drove a sharp rise in bad loans in FY25 and FY26 at Bandhan, IndusInd, RBL and IDFC First. Those books are now healing quickly, and profits at the mid-size banks are recovering as provisions fall, just as a stricter expected-credit-loss accounting regime approaches in April 2027.
  • The ownership revolution. Emirates NBD now owns 60% of RBL Bank. Japan's SMBC holds close to a quarter of Yes Bank. Blackstone is taking 9.99% of Federal Bank. Fairfax has emerged as the reported preferred buyer of IDBI Bank. HDFC Bank and Kotak are both changing chief executives within ten weeks of each other. Half of the sub-sector is under new owners or new management.
  • A two-speed sector. ICICI Bank earned a 2.49% return on assets in the June 2026 quarter while HDFC Bank, digesting its 2023 merger, earned about 1.8% and Yes Bank 0.9%. Meanwhile a set of century-old southern banks, Karur Vysya, Tamilnad Mercantile and City Union, quietly earn returns that rival the giants.

In plain terms

A bank buys money from savers and sells it to borrowers. For most of the last decade the hard part was the selling: avoiding borrowers who would not pay back. Today the hard part is the buying. Savers have more choices than ever, so the banks that can still attract cheap, sticky deposits from salaried households and businesses earn the fattest margins and can grow without taking risk. That is why a bank's deposit franchise, not its loan book, is the first thing to study.

The rest of this report follows those forces. Sections 01 and 02 explain how a private bank makes money and how large the sub-sector is. Sections 03 to 07 take the four forces in turn, with deep dives on HDFC Bank's post-merger arithmetic, on how interest-rate changes travel through a bank's margin, and on the wave of foreign ownership. Sections 08 to 10 cover business models, regulation and history. Section 11 is a masterclass on every metric used to judge a bank. Sections 12 to 16 compare the 19 banks, profile each one and explain what the stock market pays for. The final sections cover the global context, technology, the bull and bear cases and a list of indicators to watch, with thresholds.

In plain terms

Three numbers explain most of what happens to a bank's profit: the margin it earns between what it pays depositors and what it charges borrowers (the net interest margin), how much of each loan it loses to defaults (credit cost), and how much it spends running branches and technology (operating cost). Almost every story in this report is a story about one of those three.

01

How a deposit becomes profit

A bank is a spread business wrapped in a regulated balance sheet, with a fee business on top.

Every private bank in this report runs the same basic machine. It collects deposits from households, businesses and governments. It keeps a slice of those deposits idle as required by the RBI: 3% as a cash reserve ratio (CRR) since November 2025, and 18% in government securities as the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR). It lends most of the rest. The difference between the interest it earns on loans and investments and the interest it pays on deposits and borrowings is net interest income (NII). For the 19 banks, NII came to ₹4.20 lakh crore in FY26, about two-thirds of their combined revenue after interest costs.

The other third comes from fees and other income: charges on cards and payments, commissions for selling insurance and mutual funds, foreign-exchange and trade fees, processing fees on loans, recoveries from loans written off in earlier years, and treasury gains on bond portfolios. Fee income is prized because it needs little capital. A rupee of card or wealth fees does not require the bank to hold equity against it the way a rupee of loans does.

Value is created at both ends: in Step 1, by paying less for money than rivals do, and in Step 4, by losing less of it. Step 5 lifts returns because it earns income without consuming capital.

The cost side has three parts. Operating expenses (salaries, branches, technology, marketing and commissions paid to loan agents) typically absorb 40–60% of a bank's net revenue. Provisions set money aside for loans that may not be repaid. Tax takes roughly a quarter of what is left. Two features of this split matter more than any other. First, nearly half of all income is passed straight back to depositors and lenders as interest. A bank that pays half a percentage point less for its money than a competitor, with the same loans, earns several percentage points more on its equity. Second, profit is only about a sixth of income, so small swings in provisions move earnings a lot. When IndusInd Bank had to absorb a derivatives-accounting loss and microfinance defaults in FY26, its profit fell 65%; when IDFC First Bank's provisions dropped from 2.69% of loans to 1.53% in a year, its quarterly profit more than doubled.

The shape of the balance sheet determines how much capital the machine needs. Indian banks must hold common equity (CET1) of at least 8% of risk-weighted assets including the capital conservation buffer, and total capital of 11.5%, with small extra buffers for the systemically important HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank. Most of the 19 hold far more: Kotak's CET1 is 22.4%, RBL's 32.2% after Emirates NBD's investment, and Tamilnad Mercantile's total capital ratio 32.3%. Excess capital is safe but dilutes return on equity, which is why Kotak's 2.1% return on assets translates into only a 12% return on equity.

In plain terms

Return on assets (RoA) tells you how good the machine is. Return on equity (RoE) tells you how good it is for shareholders, and that depends on how much of the bank is funded by their money rather than by depositors. A bank earning 2% on assets with 10 times leverage makes 20% on equity; the same bank holding twice the capital makes only 10%.

Finally, the bank sits inside a group. HDFC Bank owns HDB Financial Services (listed in July 2025), HDFC Life, HDFC ERGO and HDFC AMC. ICICI Bank owns stakes in ICICI Prudential Life, ICICI Lombard, ICICI Prudential AMC and ICICI Securities. Kotak's subsidiaries earned ₹1,357 crore of the group's ₹5,480 crore profit in the June 2026 quarter. These subsidiaries are why consolidated profit can differ a lot from the bank's own: HDFC Bank's FY26 consolidated profit was ₹79,219 crore against ₹74,671 crore standalone, and Kotak's ₹19,288 crore against ₹14,008 crore. This report uses standalone bank figures for the banking metrics and notes consolidated figures where they change the picture.

02

Size, share and the long march from the state banks

Private banks took a decade of market share from the public sector. In FY26 the state banks fought back.

Indian banking is large and still under-penetrated. At the end of March 2026, scheduled commercial banks had ₹213.6 lakh crore of loans outstanding, 16% more than a year earlier, and ₹262.3 lakh crore of deposits, 13.4% more, according to RBI fortnightly data compiled by ICICI Securities. The RBI's own annual analysis puts FY26 credit growth at 14.5% on its preferred basis, and ICRA at 15.9%; the definitions differ, but every measure shows the fastest loan growth since the post-pandemic rebound. By 30 September 2026 credit had grown to about ₹229.9 lakh crore and deposits to about ₹284 lakh crore, a credit-to-deposit ratio of roughly 81%, although those September growth rates are flattered by special foreign-currency deposits explained in Section 03.

The 19 banks in this report hold about 36% of system deposits. The private sector as a whole, including small finance banks and private banks outside the NSE 750, holds somewhat more. The longer arc is the important one. In 2000 private banks held roughly an eighth of system credit; by 2015 about a fifth; by FY25 close to 40%. The decade after 2015 was the great transfer of market share from the public sector banks, which were crippled by the corporate bad-loan crisis, to private banks that had stayed retail and clean. That transfer paused in FY26. The RBI noted that public sector banks again out-grew private banks in credit, and they took 50.8% of the year's incremental deposits against 38.6% for private banks.

Within the 19, the concentration is extreme. The four largest, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank and Kotak, hold 71.2% of the group's deposits, earn 83% of its profit and account for 83% of its market value. Growth over the past decade has been steady and, once the 2023 HDFC merger is included, spectacular. Combined deposits of the banks that make up today's group rose from about ₹20.9 lakh crore in March 2015 to ₹95.6 lakh crore in March 2026, a compound rate of about 15% a year. Some of that reflects listings and mergers rather than organic growth, most obviously HDFC Ltd's ₹6 lakh crore-plus balance sheet folding into HDFC Bank in July 2023. But even the organic rate comfortably beat nominal GDP, which is the point: private banks have been taking share from public sector banks, from non-bank lenders and from the informal sector at the same time.

The outlook is slower. ICRA expects system credit growth of 11.0–11.7% in FY27, citing the war in West Asia, higher oil prices and pressure on deposits, with bad loans edging up to 2.0–2.1% and return on assets easing to 1.2–1.3%. Crisil expects system return on assets of 1.1–1.2%, down from about 1.3% in FY26. Those are system numbers that include the public sector banks. For the private banks the arithmetic is similar but the starting point is higher: their combined return on assets in FY26 was about 1.6%, and their median return on equity a little over 12%.

In plain terms

Private banks spent ten years growing faster than the government-owned banks, because the government banks were busy cleaning up bad loans to big companies. That clean-up is finished. State banks now have capital and are lending again, so private banks have to compete harder for both borrowers and depositors. The easy share gains are over.

03

The deposit war: savers left, term deposits took over

The cheapest source of money in the system is shrinking, and every bank is paying for it.

Indian households used to keep a large share of their money in savings accounts that paid 3–4%. Over the past four years three things changed that habit. Interest rates rose sharply in 2022–23, making term deposits at 7% and above far more attractive. Mutual funds, through systematic investment plans and direct apps, became a mass-market product, pulling savings into equity. And the UPI payment system meant people no longer needed a large idle balance to transact. The result shows up clearly in the RBI's annual survey of deposits.

Almost half of all term deposits are now bulk deposits of ₹1 crore or more: rate-sensitive money that moves to whoever pays most.

The shift matters because of the price gap. A current account costs a bank almost nothing in interest. A savings account at a large bank now pays about 2.5–3% after the 2025 rate cuts. A one-year retail term deposit paid around 6.5–7% in 2025 and a bulk deposit could cost more. When ₹100 moves from a savings account to a term deposit, the bank's cost on that ₹100 roughly doubles. Across a ₹30 lakh crore deposit base, a few percentage points of mix shift is worth thousands of crores of profit a year.

The CASA ratio, the share of deposits held in current and savings accounts, is therefore the single best summary of a deposit franchise. In the June 2026 quarter it ranged from 50.8% at IDFC First Bank to 19% at CSB Bank.

In plain terms

Picture two shops selling the same product at the same price. One gets its stock from a wholesaler at ₹44; the other pays ₹67. The first shop can match any price war and still make money; the second has to take more risk or accept thinner profit. In banking, the "wholesale price" is the cost of deposits, and the cheapest suppliers, IDBI, Kotak, ICICI and J&K Bank, pay about two-thirds of what DCB and Bandhan do.

The FCNR window: a policy-made deposit supply

In mid-2026 the deposit war acquired a new and temporary front. The rupee fell to a record low of 96.96 to the US dollar on 20 May 2026 as the war in West Asia lifted oil prices and foreign portfolio investors sold Indian equities. On 8 June the RBI opened a special window, modelled on a similar scheme in 2013: banks could raise fresh foreign-currency non-resident (FCNR(B)) deposits of three to five years from Indians abroad and swap the dollars with the RBI at a concessional cost, so that the central bank, not the bank, carried the currency risk. Demand was so strong that the RBI closed the window early, for deposits raised up to 31 August.

The effect on bank balance sheets was large. HDFC Bank raised US$11.5 billion, about ₹1.10 lakh crore, and lent US$5.7 billion of it out again through its overseas branches. IDFC First Bank raised ₹34,390 crore, of which ₹24,885 crore was "leveraged": lent back to the same depositors through its GIFT City branch. Yes Bank's September deposits grew 19.5% year on year as reported but 13.2% excluding these deposits. For anyone reading Q2 FY27 numbers, the lesson is to look for the normalised figure. The FCNR money is real, but much of it is matched by loans created for the purpose, and it will run off when the deposits mature.

In plain terms

The RBI wanted dollars to steady the rupee, so it made it attractive for banks to collect dollar deposits from Indians abroad. Banks collected a lot, and some lent the money straight back to the same customers. That pushed up deposit and loan growth in the September 2026 quarter in a way that says nothing about the banks' everyday franchise. Strip it out before comparing.

Three implications follow for the rest of the report. First, banks that already have cheap, granular deposits can grow loans without paying up, which is why ICICI Bank grew loans 19.6% while keeping its cost of deposits at 4.41%. Second, banks whose loan books ran ahead of their deposits, HDFC Bank after its merger and several mid-size banks after 2022, must either slow lending or pay more, and both cost profit. Third, any rise in interest rates from here will reach term deposits with a lag of a year or more, because 69.8% of system term deposits are of one to three years' maturity. The deposit war is therefore not over; it is merely changing shape.
