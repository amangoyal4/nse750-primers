00

The one-page view: nineteen banks, one scarce input

Lending is easy to grow and hard to price. Gathering cheap deposits is hard to grow and decides everything else.

India's 19 listed private sector banks held ₹95.6 lakh crore of deposits at the end of March 2026, a little over a third of everything Indians keep in scheduled commercial banks. They earned a combined standalone net profit of ₹1.97 lakh crore in FY26 and, on 13 October 2026, the stock market valued them at ₹34.9 lakh crore, roughly 2.1 times their combined book value and 17.7 times their FY26 earnings. Two banks dominate. HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank together earned 63% of the group's profit and account for 60% of its market value. The other seventeen range from Kotak Mahindra Bank, a ₹4.4 lakh crore conglomerate with more capital than it can use, to CSB Bank, a ₹5,300 crore Kerala gold-loan lender waiting to learn who its next owner will be.

The headline numbers look calm. Gross bad loans across the banking system fell to about 1.8% in March 2026, the lowest in decades, and every one of the 19 banks is profitable on a quarterly basis again. Under the surface, four forces are reshaping the sub-sector at the same time, and a fifth decides who wins.

  • The deposit war. Savers have moved money out of low-yield savings accounts and into term deposits and mutual funds. The savings share of system deposits fell from 34.6% in March 2022 to 28.7% in March 2026. Every bank now pays more for each marginal rupee, and the banks with the weakest current-and-savings (CASA) franchises pay the most.
  • The rate-cycle whiplash. The RBI cut the repo rate by 125 basis points between February and December 2025. Because most retail and small-business loans are now tied directly to the repo rate, loan yields fell within weeks while deposit costs fell slowly. Margins at the large CASA-rich banks hit multi-year lows in the June 2026 quarter. On 7 October 2026 the RBI raised rates for the first time since February 2023, which starts the reverse process.
  • The end of a credit cycle. Microfinance and unsecured retail loans drove a sharp rise in bad loans in FY25 and FY26 at Bandhan, IndusInd, RBL and IDFC First. Those books are now healing quickly, and profits at the mid-size banks are recovering as provisions fall, just as a stricter expected-credit-loss accounting regime approaches in April 2027.
  • The ownership revolution. Emirates NBD now owns 60% of RBL Bank. Japan's SMBC holds close to a quarter of Yes Bank. Blackstone is taking 9.99% of Federal Bank. Fairfax has emerged as the reported preferred buyer of IDBI Bank. HDFC Bank and Kotak are both changing chief executives within ten weeks of each other. Half of the sub-sector is under new owners or new management.
  • A two-speed sector. ICICI Bank earned a 2.49% return on assets in the June 2026 quarter while HDFC Bank, digesting its 2023 merger, earned about 1.8% and Yes Bank 0.9%. Meanwhile a set of century-old southern banks, Karur Vysya, Tamilnad Mercantile and City Union, quietly earn returns that rival the giants.

In plain terms

A bank buys money from savers and sells it to borrowers. For most of the last decade the hard part was the selling: avoiding borrowers who would not pay back. Today the hard part is the buying. Savers have more choices than ever, so the banks that can still attract cheap, sticky deposits from salaried households and businesses earn the fattest margins and can grow without taking risk. That is why a bank's deposit franchise, not its loan book, is the first thing to study.

The rest of this report follows those forces. Sections 01 and 02 explain how a private bank makes money and how large the sub-sector is. Sections 03 to 07 take the four forces in turn, with deep dives on HDFC Bank's post-merger arithmetic, on how interest-rate changes travel through a bank's margin, and on the wave of foreign ownership. Sections 08 to 10 cover business models, regulation and history. Section 11 is a masterclass on every metric used to judge a bank. Sections 12 to 16 compare the 19 banks, profile each one and explain what the stock market pays for. The final sections cover the global context, technology, the bull and bear cases and a list of indicators to watch, with thresholds.

In plain terms

Three numbers explain most of what happens to a bank's profit: the margin it earns between what it pays depositors and what it charges borrowers (the net interest margin), how much of each loan it loses to defaults (credit cost), and how much it spends running branches and technology (operating cost). Almost every story in this report is a story about one of those three.

01

How a deposit becomes profit

A bank is a spread business wrapped in a regulated balance sheet, with a fee business on top.

Every private bank in this report runs the same basic machine. It collects deposits from households, businesses and governments. It keeps a slice of those deposits idle as required by the RBI: 3% as a cash reserve ratio (CRR) since November 2025, and 18% in government securities as the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR). It lends most of the rest. The difference between the interest it earns on loans and investments and the interest it pays on deposits and borrowings is net interest income (NII). For the 19 banks, NII came to ₹4.20 lakh crore in FY26, about two-thirds of their combined revenue after interest costs.

The other third comes from fees and other income: charges on cards and payments, commissions for selling insurance and mutual funds, foreign-exchange and trade fees, processing fees on loans, recoveries from loans written off in earlier years, and treasury gains on bond portfolios. Fee income is prized because it needs little capital. A rupee of card or wealth fees does not require the bank to hold equity against it the way a rupee of loans does.

The cost side has three parts. Operating expenses (salaries, branches, technology, marketing and commissions paid to loan agents) typically absorb 40–60% of a bank's net revenue. Provisions set money aside for loans that may not be repaid. Tax takes roughly a quarter of what is left. Two features of this split matter more than any other. First, nearly half of all income is passed straight back to depositors and lenders as interest. A bank that pays half a percentage point less for its money than a competitor, with the same loans, earns several percentage points more on its equity. Second, profit is only about a sixth of income, so small swings in provisions move earnings a lot. When IndusInd Bank had to absorb a derivatives-accounting loss and microfinance defaults in FY26, its profit fell 65%; when IDFC First Bank's provisions dropped from 2.69% of loans to 1.53% in a year, its quarterly profit more than doubled.

The shape of the balance sheet determines how much capital the machine needs. Indian banks must hold common equity (CET1) of at least 8% of risk-weighted assets including the capital conservation buffer, and total capital of 11.5%, with small extra buffers for the systemically important HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank. Most of the 19 hold far more: Kotak's CET1 is 22.4%, RBL's 32.2% after Emirates NBD's investment, and Tamilnad Mercantile's total capital ratio 32.3%. Excess capital is safe but dilutes return on equity, which is why Kotak's 2.1% return on assets translates into only a 12% return on equity.

In plain terms

Return on assets (RoA) tells you how good the machine is. Return on equity (RoE) tells you how good it is for shareholders, and that depends on how much of the bank is funded by their money rather than by depositors. A bank earning 2% on assets with 10 times leverage makes 20% on equity; the same bank holding twice the capital makes only 10%.

Finally, the bank sits inside a group. HDFC Bank owns HDB Financial Services (listed in July 2025), HDFC Life, HDFC ERGO and HDFC AMC. ICICI Bank owns stakes in ICICI Prudential Life, ICICI Lombard, ICICI Prudential AMC and ICICI Securities. Kotak's subsidiaries earned ₹1,357 crore of the group's ₹5,480 crore profit in the June 2026 quarter. These subsidiaries are why consolidated profit can differ a lot from the bank's own: HDFC Bank's FY26 consolidated profit was ₹79,219 crore against ₹74,671 crore standalone, and Kotak's ₹19,288 crore against ₹14,008 crore. This report uses standalone bank figures for the banking metrics and notes consolidated figures where they change the picture.

02

Size, share and the long march from the state banks

Private banks took a decade of market share from the public sector. In FY26 the state banks fought back.

Indian banking is large and still under-penetrated. At the end of March 2026, scheduled commercial banks had ₹213.6 lakh crore of loans outstanding, 16% more than a year earlier, and ₹262.3 lakh crore of deposits, 13.4% more, according to RBI fortnightly data compiled by ICICI Securities. The RBI's own annual analysis puts FY26 credit growth at 14.5% on its preferred basis, and ICRA at 15.9%; the definitions differ, but every measure shows the fastest loan growth since the post-pandemic rebound. By 30 September 2026 credit had grown to about ₹229.9 lakh crore and deposits to about ₹284 lakh crore, a credit-to-deposit ratio of roughly 81%, although those September growth rates are flattered by special foreign-currency deposits explained in Section 03.

The 19 banks in this report hold about 36% of system deposits. The private sector as a whole, including small finance banks and private banks outside the NSE 750, holds somewhat more. The longer arc is the important one. In 2000 private banks held roughly an eighth of system credit; by 2015 about a fifth; by FY25 close to 40%. The decade after 2015 was the great transfer of market share from the public sector banks, which were crippled by the corporate bad-loan crisis, to private banks that had stayed retail and clean. That transfer paused in FY26. The RBI noted that public sector banks again out-grew private banks in credit, and they took 50.8% of the year's incremental deposits against 38.6% for private banks.

Growth over the past decade has been steady and, once the 2023 HDFC merger is included, spectacular. The outlook is slower. ICRA expects system credit growth of 11.0–11.7% in FY27, citing the war in West Asia, higher oil prices and pressure on deposits, with bad loans edging up to 2.0–2.1% and return on assets easing to 1.2–1.3%. For the private banks the arithmetic is similar but the starting point is higher: their combined return on assets in FY26 was about 1.6%, and their median return on equity a little over 12%.

In plain terms

Private banks spent ten years growing faster than the government-owned banks, because the government banks were busy cleaning up bad loans to big companies. That clean-up is finished. State banks now have capital and are lending again, so private banks have to compete harder for both borrowers and depositors. The easy share gains are over.

03

The deposit war: savers left, term deposits took over

The cheapest source of money in the system is shrinking, and every bank is paying for it.

Indian households used to keep a large share of their money in savings accounts that paid 3–4%. Over the past four years three things changed that habit. Interest rates rose sharply in 2022–23, making term deposits at 7% and above far more attractive. Mutual funds, through systematic investment plans and direct apps, became a mass-market product, pulling savings into equity. And the UPI payment system meant people no longer needed a large idle balance to transact. The result shows up clearly in the RBI's annual survey of deposits.

Almost half of all term deposits are now bulk deposits of ₹1 crore or more: rate-sensitive money that moves to whoever pays most.

The shift matters because of the price gap. A current account costs a bank almost nothing in interest. A savings account at a large bank now pays about 2.5–3% after the 2025 rate cuts. A one-year retail term deposit paid around 6.5–7% in 2025 and a bulk deposit could cost more. When ₹100 moves from a savings account to a term deposit, the bank's cost on that ₹100 roughly doubles. Across a ₹30 lakh crore deposit base, a few percentage points of mix shift is worth thousands of crores of profit a year.

The CASA ratio, the share of deposits held in current and savings accounts, is therefore the single best summary of a deposit franchise. In the June 2026 quarter it ranged from 50.8% at IDFC First Bank to 19% at CSB Bank.

In plain terms

Picture two shops selling the same product at the same price. One gets its stock from a wholesaler at ₹44; the other pays ₹67. The first shop can match any price war and still make money; the second has to take more risk or accept thinner profit. In banking, the "wholesale price" is the cost of deposits, and the cheapest suppliers, IDBI, Kotak, ICICI and J&K Bank, pay about two-thirds of what DCB and Bandhan do.

The FCNR window: a policy-made deposit supply

In mid-2026 the deposit war acquired a new and temporary front. The rupee fell to a record low of 96.96 to the US dollar on 20 May 2026 as the war in West Asia lifted oil prices and foreign portfolio investors sold Indian equities. On 8 June the RBI opened a special window, modelled on a similar scheme in 2013: banks could raise fresh foreign-currency non-resident (FCNR(B)) deposits of three to five years from Indians abroad and swap the dollars with the RBI at a concessional cost, so that the central bank, not the bank, carried the currency risk. Demand was so strong that the RBI closed the window early, for deposits raised up to 31 August.

The effect on bank balance sheets was large. HDFC Bank raised US$11.5 billion, about ₹1.10 lakh crore, and lent US$5.7 billion of it out again through its overseas branches. IDFC First Bank raised ₹34,390 crore, of which ₹24,885 crore was "leveraged": lent back to the same depositors through its GIFT City branch. Yes Bank's September deposits grew 19.5% year on year as reported but 13.2% excluding these deposits. For anyone reading Q2 FY27 numbers, the lesson is to look for the normalised figure. The FCNR money is real, but much of it is matched by loans created for the purpose, and it will run off when the deposits mature.

In plain terms

The RBI wanted dollars to steady the rupee, so it made it attractive for banks to collect dollar deposits from Indians abroad. Banks collected a lot, and some lent the money straight back to the same customers. That pushed up deposit and loan growth in the September 2026 quarter in a way that says nothing about the banks' everyday franchise. Strip it out before comparing.

Three implications follow for the rest of the report. First, banks that already have cheap, granular deposits can grow loans without paying up, which is why ICICI Bank grew loans 19.6% while keeping its cost of deposits at 4.41%. Second, banks whose loan books ran ahead of their deposits, HDFC Bank after its merger and several mid-size banks after 2022, must either slow lending or pay more, and both cost profit. Third, any rise in interest rates from here will reach term deposits with a lag of a year or more, because 69.8% of system term deposits are of one to three years' maturity. The deposit war is therefore not over; it is merely changing shape.

04

After the merger: HDFC Bank's loan-to-deposit arithmetic Deep dive

The largest private bank swallowed a bond-funded mortgage company and has spent three years paying for it in deposits.

On 1 July 2023 HDFC Ltd, India's largest housing-finance company, merged into HDFC Bank, the bank it had founded in 1994. The deal created one of the world's most valuable banks and removed a holding-company structure that the RBI disliked. It also created a funding problem that still defines HDFC Bank's results in 2026. HDFC Ltd was not a bank. It funded its ₹6 lakh crore-plus loan book mostly with bonds, commercial paper and borrowings from other banks, not with retail deposits. When those loans arrived on HDFC Bank's balance sheet, the combined bank suddenly had far more loans than deposits: its loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR) jumped to roughly 110%.

A bank can live with an LDR above 100% for a while, but it is expensive and fragile. Bonds cost more than deposits. They also do not count as stable funding in the same way, and the bank must still meet its cash-reserve, liquidity and priority-sector rules on a much larger base. So HDFC Bank set itself a multi-year task: replace maturing HDFC Ltd bonds with deposits, and grow loans more slowly than deposits until the LDR returned to a more normal 85–90%.

What the lost CASA costs

A rough calculation shows why the market cares. A fall of about 6.5 percentage points in the CASA ratio on a ₹33 lakh crore deposit base means about ₹2.1 lakh crore that would once have sat in current and savings accounts now sits in term deposits. If the gap between a term-deposit rate and a blended CASA rate is 3.5–4 percentage points, the extra interest cost is ₹7,500–8,500 crore a year before tax, or roughly 17–19 basis points of margin on ₹45 lakh crore of assets. That is an illustrative estimate, but it is the same order of magnitude as the fall in HDFC Bank's margin, which reached 3.26% on total assets in the June 2026 quarter (3.40% on interest-earning assets), the lowest in the bank's history and well below the roughly 4.1% the pre-merger bank used to earn.

In plain terms

HDFC Bank bought a giant mortgage business that came without its own savers. To fund those mortgages it had to go out and attract deposits quickly, and the quickest way was to pay attractive rates on fixed deposits. That worked, but fixed deposits cost far more than savings accounts, so the bank's margin shrank. The merger made the bank bigger; it has not yet made it more profitable per rupee.

Two further drags compound the problem. The merged mortgage book enlarged the base on which priority-sector lending targets are calculated, and any shortfall must be parked in low-yield government funds such as the Rural Infrastructure Development Fund. And HDFC Ltd's home loans earn yields of about 8.5–9%, well below the bank's retail unsecured or business loans, so the merged loan mix is lower-yielding by design. The net result is a return on assets of about 1.8% in FY26 against roughly 2% before the merger.

Where the loan-to-deposit ratio stands now

By the bank's own provisional disclosures, gross advances were ₹30.61 lakh crore and deposits ₹31.71 lakh crore at the end of June 2026, a ratio of about 96.5%. In September 2026 the figures were ₹32.20 lakh crore and ₹33.28 lakh crore, 96.8%, but deposits were flattered by US$11.5 billion of special FCNR(B) deposits, about half of which the bank lent out again through overseas branches. The LDR has stopped falling; HDFC Bank has chosen to resume faster loan growth (gross advances +16.3% year on year in September) rather than push the ratio down further.

The market's verdict, and the new chief executive

Investors have marked HDFC Bank down hard. At ₹714 on 13 October 2026 (after a 1:1 bonus issue in 2025) the shares were about 30% below their 52-week high of ₹1,020, the bank's market value was ₹11.0 lakh crore and it traded at 1.9 times book value and 14.6 times standalone earnings, multiples that were unthinkable for most of its listed life. Foreign portfolio investors have cut their holding for five consecutive quarters, from 48.8% in June 2025 to 39.4% in September 2026, while domestic institutions raised theirs from 35.8% to 43.4%.

Margins explain part of the de-rating. Governance explains the rest. On 18–19 March 2026 the bank's chairman, Atanu Chakraborty, resigned with immediate effect, citing in his letter developments over about two years that conflicted with his personal values; the board said there were no other material reasons, and Keki Mistry was appointed interim chairman. In July the board concluded an internal review of a deposit-mobilisation arrangement with the Maharashtra State Road Development Corporation in 2017 and 2021, found "business overreach rather than any mala fide action", issued warning letters and token ₹1 lakh penalties to the managing director, the CFO and the head of retail assets, and reported the matter to the RBI. In parallel, chief executive Sashidhar Jagdishan decided not to seek another term. On 1 October 2026 the RBI approved Anup Bagchi, a former ICICI Bank executive director, as managing director and chief executive for three years from 27 October 2026, the first time HDFC Bank has been led by an outsider.

In plain terms

For thirty years investors paid a premium for HDFC Bank because it was predictable: steady growth, very few bad loans, no surprises. In 2026 it delivered surprises in margin, in the boardroom and in leadership at the same time. The loan book is still among the cleanest in India (gross bad loans 1.17%), so the question is not survival; it is whether the new chief executive can rebuild cheap deposits and with them the old returns.

The bull and bear reading of HDFC Bank is therefore unusually clean. The bull case says the funding repair is largely done, borrowings will keep running off, the RBI's October rate hike will lift yields on the bank's repo-linked loans faster than its deposit costs, the FCNR deposits give it cheap dollar funding for a few years, and a new chief executive can reset strategy without inheriting an asset-quality problem. The bear case says the CASA loss is structural, because savers are not coming back to low-yield accounts, so HDFC Bank will settle at a permanently lower margin and a return on equity in the low-to-mid teens, closer to Axis than to ICICI. The next four quarterly CASA ratios will decide which reading is right.

Margins through the rate cycle: the repo-link asymmetry Deep dive

When the RBI cuts, loan yields fall in weeks and deposit costs in years. Which banks win depends on which side reprices first.

It then held through August 2026. On 7 October 2026, facing consumer inflation forecast to reach 6.0% in the December 2026 quarter, higher oil prices and a weak rupee, the Monetary Policy Committee raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% and changed its stance to "calibrated tightening". It was the first hike since February 2023.

To understand what this did to bank profits, start with how loans are priced. Since October 2019 the RBI has required banks to link new floating-rate retail and small-business loans to an external benchmark, which in practice is almost always the repo rate. These external-benchmark (EBLR) loans reprice automatically, usually at the next quarterly reset. Older loans are linked to the bank's marginal cost of funds lending rate (MCLR), which moves more slowly as the bank's own funding costs change. Some loans, notably vehicle loans, gold loans, microfinance and many personal loans, carry fixed rates. ICICI Bank publishes the split: 57% of its domestic loans are linked to the repo rate or another external benchmark, 30% are fixed, and 13% are tied to MCLR or older benchmarks.

The sequence runs in reverse when rates rise: repo-linked loan yields go up first, deposit costs later. The October 2026 hike starts that process.

Deposits move far more slowly. Current accounts pay nothing, so a rate cut cannot lower their cost. Savings rates are set by each bank and were cut by most large banks during 2025, typically to 2.5–3%. Term deposits are fixed until maturity, and 69.8% of system term deposits are of one to three years' maturity, so only about a third of a bank's term deposits reprice in any given year. The CRR cut from 4% to 3%, phased in between September and November 2025, partly offset the squeeze by releasing about ₹2.5 lakh crore of cash that banks could lend instead of holding at zero return.

When interest rates fall, a bank whose loans are mostly on floating rates has to cut what it charges borrowers almost immediately, but it cannot cut what it pays on fixed deposits until they mature. Now run it backwards: what the October hike does

A rise in the repo rate reverses the sequence. Repo-linked loans reprice upward at the next reset, while term deposits stay at their contracted rates until maturity. For a bank like Bank A, a 25 basis point hike adds roughly 14 basis points to loan yields within a quarter or two and very little to deposit costs at first, so margins could widen by 10–14 basis points over the next two quarters before deposit repricing catches up. Brokerage Anand Rathi wrote on 1 October 2026 that it expects margins to expand from the second half of FY27 if rates keep rising. The RBI's statement that rate cuts are "off the table" and its inflation forecast of 6.0% for the December 2026 quarter suggest the hiking cycle may not be over.

There are three offsets. First, the deposit war does not pause: if loan growth stays near 15% while deposits grow 12–13% excluding FCNR money, banks will raise term-deposit rates regardless of policy, and Crisil already believes deposit costs have bottomed. Second, the FCNR(B) deposits raised in mid-2026 carry their own swap and interest costs, which pressure margins in the September 2026 quarter. Third, a hiking cycle tends to raise bad loans with a lag, particularly in unsecured retail and small business. The margin tailwind is real but it will be partial and temporary.

In plain terms

The same feature that hurt the big banks in 2025, loans that follow the repo rate, helps them in a rising-rate world. After the October 2026 hike the large banks' loan rates go up quickly while what they pay on fixed deposits stays the same for a while. Expect their margins to improve over the next two or three quarters, unless the fight for deposits pushes deposit rates up at the same time.

For investors, the practical rule is to read every bank's margin through its funding and pricing mix rather than in isolation. A bank whose margin rose during 2025 has not necessarily become better; it may simply be the kind of bank that benefits when rates fall. Conversely, a CASA-rich bank whose margin compressed is likely to see the reverse as rates rise. The truly informative number is the margin relative to peers with the same mix, and the trend in the cost of deposits, which reflects the franchise rather than the cycle.

The credit cycle turns: microfinance, unsecured loans and the road to ECL

FY26 was the year bad loans hit the mid-size banks. FY27 is the year provisions fall, just before the accounting rules tighten.

The bad-loan problem of 2015–2020 was about large corporate borrowers: steel, power and infrastructure companies that could not repay. It crippled the public sector banks and hit ICICI Bank, Axis Bank and Yes Bank hard. That cycle is over. Corporate balance sheets are now the strongest in decades, and system-wide gross bad loans fell to about 1.8% in March 2026. The credit stress of 2024–26 came from a different place: small, unsecured loans to households.

Microfinance is the clearest example. These are loans of ₹30,000–₹1 lakh made to women in groups, mostly in rural and semi-urban India, without collateral. After a lending boom in 2022–24, many borrowers took loans from several lenders at once. When repayments faltered in 2024, the industry's portfolio at risk (loans 31–180 days overdue) climbed to 6.3% by March 2025, according to the industry body MFIN. The RBI and the industry tightened rules on how many lenders a borrower could have, and lenders cut disbursements. By March 2026 the same measure had fallen to 2.0%, and the industry's total portfolio had shrunk about 12% over the year to June 2026.

In plain terms

Small unsecured loans are profitable when almost everyone repays and painful when even a few per cent do not, because there is no collateral to sell. In 2024–25 too many people borrowed from too many lenders and repayment slipped. Lenders stopped lending, rules were tightened and the bad loans were written off. The banks that suffered most now have much lower provisioning bills, which is why their profits are jumping.

Not all clear: the warning signs

The RBI's June 2026 Financial Stability Report flagged three cautions. Unsecured loans account for more than half of all fresh retail slippages. Loans 31–60 days overdue (the SMA-1 bucket) rose in March 2026 even as headline bad loans fell. And small-ticket personal loans originated through fintech partners showed delinquency of about 4% in the 90–179-day bucket. Consumer credit at banks still grew about 17.5%. The large banks have deliberately slowed unsecured lending: ICICI's credit-card book shrank 1.9% in the year to June 2026, and Axis grew cards only 5% and personal loans 7%.

The bigger change is in accounting. On 27 April 2026 the RBI issued final directions moving commercial banks from the current "incurred loss" provisioning model, under which a loan is provided for mainly after it becomes 90 days overdue, to an expected-credit-loss (ECL) model from 1 April 2027. Under ECL, loans are placed in three stages; a loan whose credit risk has risen significantly (Stage 2) needs lifetime expected-loss provisions even if it is still being repaid. Banks with large unsecured or small-business books, or weak historical data, will need more provisions on day one; reports suggest the capital impact can be phased in until March 2031. Crisil expects provisions to rise by 5–10 basis points in FY27 as banks build buffers. Several already are: Axis created a one-time ₹2,001 crore standard-asset provision in the March 2026 quarter, citing the West Asia war, and ICICI holds ₹13,100 crore of contingency provisions.

In plain terms

Today a bank mostly provides for a loan once it has gone bad. From April 2027 it must provide earlier, as soon as the risk of a loan going bad has clearly increased. The total losses do not change, but they are recognised sooner, so the first year of the new rules will take a bite out of reported profit and capital. Banks with large cushions of extra provisions, ICICI and Axis for example, are best placed.

The investment conclusion is that FY27 is likely to be the best year for credit costs at the mid-size banks in this cycle: old bad loans are written off, new stress is low, and ECL has not yet started. From FY28 provisioning will be structurally a little higher for everyone, and highest for the banks with the riskiest books. That argues for valuing the recovery banks on normalised, ECL-adjusted credit costs rather than on the exceptionally low provisions of the next few quarters.

The ownership revolution: foreign capital and new chief executives Deep dive

In eighteen months, a Dubai bank, a Japanese megabank, an American private-equity firm and a Canadian insurer have bought into Indian private banking.

For most of the past thirty years Indian private banks belonged to one of three ownership types. Some had a founder-promoter who held a large but shrinking stake, such as Uday Kotak at Kotak Mahindra Bank or the Hinduja group at IndusInd. Some were "promoterless", widely held by institutions after their sponsors sold out, like HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank and Axis Bank. And the old private banks of the south were owned by thousands of local shareholders with no controlling holder at all. Foreign banks that wanted a large Indian presence had to build branches slowly or buy a failing bank, as Singapore's DBS did with Lakshmi Vilas Bank in 2020.

That has changed. Between August 2025 and July 2026 the RBI allowed, or is in the process of allowing, five large foreign or financial investors to take strategic stakes in Indian private banks, including the first outright foreign control of a profitable Indian bank.

The shift has consequences for how investors should read the banks. A new chief executive usually spends the first year reviewing the balance sheet, which often means extra provisions, write-offs or a reset of growth targets. Rajiv Anand's first year at IndusInd included a quarterly loss in September 2025 and a 2% shrinkage of the loan book. New owners also bring new strategies. Emirates NBD has said it will use RBL as a platform for India–Gulf trade and remittance flows. Fairfax, if it completes the IDBI deal, has reportedly committed that IDBI would be its only Indian bank, which implies it will sell its 40% stake in CSB Bank; CSB's shares fell 44% from their January 2026 high partly on that prospect.

In plain terms

About half of India's listed private banks will have a different owner, chairman or chief executive in 2027 from the one they had in 2024. New leaders often start by cleaning the books, so earnings in their first year can disappoint even if the long-term plan is sound. When reading results from these banks over the next year, separate the one-off clean-up from the underlying trend.

What it means for the rest of the sub-sector

Four implications follow. First, the floor under mid-size bank valuations has risen: if a strategic buyer will pay 1.2–1.5 times book for a recovering bank, the listed peers trading near 1 times book carry an implicit option on a similar deal. Second, governance standards are being forced upward. The IndusInd episode, HDFC Bank's chairman resignation and the MSRDC review show the RBI and boards acting faster than in the past, and new foreign owners bring their own home-regulator scrutiny. Third, competition for deposits and talent will intensify. Well-capitalised, foreign-backed banks such as RBL can pay more for deposits and staff while they build share. Fourth, consolidation is now possible. The RBI has historically resisted forced mergers of healthy private banks, but a foreign-owned RBL, an SMBC-anchored Yes Bank or a Fairfax-controlled IDBI could each become acquirers of the small regional banks over time.

There are risks too. Foreign owners can be patient, but they are also subject to their own home-market problems: a crisis at a parent can trigger a strategic review of the Indian subsidiary. Voting rights in Indian private banks are capped at 26% regardless of shareholding, so even a 60% owner must govern through the board and with the regulator's blessing. And the RBI's fit-and-proper reviews can take years, as IDBI Bank's four-year sale process shows: the expression of interest went out in October 2022, financial bids came in February 2026 and revised bids in July 2026, and as of mid-October no share purchase agreement had been announced.

A bank that lends to the riskiest borrowers charges the most and shows the widest margin, but it also loses the most when times turn. A bank's margin, growth and capital are set as much in Mumbai's Mint Street as in its own boardroom.

The third theme is governance. In plain terms

The RBI is not just the referee; it decides the price of the main raw material (money), how much stock each bank must keep in the warehouse (reserves and liquidity), how much of its own money each owner must put in (capital), and who is allowed to run the shop. A bank analyst who ignores the RBI's next move is analysing only half the business.

Two pending items are worth tracking. Deposit insurance, now ₹5 lakh per depositor, is funded by a flat premium on all banks; proposals for risk-based premiums would modestly raise costs for weaker banks. Three decades, eight shocks

The private banks that survive are those that came through every cycle without a fatal mistake.

India's private banks fall into two generations. The "old private banks", City Union (1904), Karur Vysya (1916), CSB (1920), Tamilnad Mercantile (1921), Karnataka Bank (1924), South Indian Bank (1929), Federal Bank (1931) and J&K Bank (1938), were founded by local business communities long before independence and survived the nationalisations of 1969 and 1980 because they were too small to be taken over. The "new private banks" were licensed after the 1991 reforms: HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis (originally UTI Bank) and IndusInd in 1994, Kotak (converted from a finance company) in 2003, Yes Bank in 2004, and IDFC Bank and Bandhan in 2015. RBL (founded 1943 in Kolhapur) and DCB (from a 1930s cooperative) were old institutions rebuilt as new-style banks.

Every decade since has brought shocks, and each one sorted the strong from the weak. The timeline below lists the eight that shaped today's sub-sector.

    Shock 1: ICICI Ltd reverse-merges into ICICI Bank (2002); Global Trust Bank collapses and is merged into Oriental Bank of Commerce (2004). Kotak Mahindra Finance becomes a bank (2003).

In plain terms

Banking profits move in long waves. For roughly five years after 2014, Indian private banks earned less each year as bad loans to big companies came home to roost. For the next six years, profits soared as those losses were written off and lending boomed. FY26 was the first year in that upswing when profit barely grew. The lessons of history are consistent. The banks that disappeared, Global Trust, Centurion, Lakshmi Vilas, and the one that nearly did, Yes Bank, shared three traits: rapid growth funded by expensive bulk deposits, concentrated lending to a few large or connected borrowers, and governance that let a dominant chief executive override risk controls. The banks that thrived, HDFC Bank and Kotak in particular, did the opposite: granular retail deposits, diversified lending and conservative provisioning. IndusInd's 2025 crisis was a reminder that governance failures can occur even at well-established banks, in that case through accounting for internal derivatives trades rather than through lending.

History also shows how long recoveries take. Yes Bank was rescued in March 2020; six years later its return on assets is still below 1%. IDBI Bank was taken over by LIC in January 2019 and left the RBI's prompt corrective action framework in March 2021; only in FY26 did it earn more than 2% on its assets. The microfinance-hit banks of 2024–25 are recovering faster because their problem was concentrated in short-tenure loans that run off within two years, but full normalisation of returns usually takes two to three years after the peak of stress.

Reading a private bank: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, one equation. Learn how each is built, what good looks like and where banks define them differently.

A bank's annual report runs to hundreds of pages, but its economics come down to a short list of ratios that every analyst, rating agency and regulator uses. This section explains each of them in the order in which they flow into profit, with a worked rupee example, the drivers, the range that counts as good, average or weak among the 19 banks, how the banks compare, and the trap that catches people who compare banks' published figures without checking definitions.

Start with the equation

Every bank's profit can be written as one equation. Interest earned on loans and investments, minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings, gives net interest income. Add fee and other income to get net revenue. Subtract operating expenses to get pre-provision operating profit (PPOP), the bank's earning power before credit losses. Subtract provisions for bad loans, then tax, to reach net profit. Divide each line by average total assets and the same equation becomes the "RoA tree", the most useful single tool in bank analysis.

Of those assets, 92% (₹92,000 crore) earn interest; the rest is cash, fixed assets and other items. If the bank earns a net interest margin of 3.8% on its interest-earning assets, its NII is ₹3,496 crore, or 3.50% of total assets. It earns ₹1,500 crore of fees and other income (1.50%). Loans are ₹70,000 crore, and a credit cost of 0.6% means provisions of ₹420 crore. Pre-tax profit is ₹2,076 crore; tax at 25.2% is ₹523 crore; net profit is ₹1,553 crore, a return on assets of 1.55%. If shareholders' equity is 11% of assets (₹11,000 crore), the return on equity is about 14.1%.

In plain terms

A bank keeps about one and a half rupees of profit for every hundred rupees of assets on its books. That sounds tiny, but because shareholders put in only about eleven rupees of every hundred, the profit on their money is around fourteen per cent. Everything in bank analysis is about nudging that one and a half up or down by a few tenths.

The sensitivities are what make this equation powerful. For the same bank, a 10 basis point change in margin moves profit by about ₹69 crore, or 4.4%. A 50 basis point rise in credit cost cuts profit by about ₹262 crore, or 17%. Moving 5 percentage points of deposits from term deposits at 6.5% to savings accounts at 2.75% saves about ₹159 crore of interest a year before tax, lifting profit by roughly 7.7%. Those three sensitivities, margin, credit cost and deposit mix, explain most of the differences between the 19 banks.

Net interest margin (NIM)

Definition and formula. NIM = net interest income ÷ average interest-earning assets, annualised. It measures the spread a bank earns on the money it has lent and invested. Worked example. A bank with ₹92,000 crore of average interest-earning assets, a yield of 9.0% (₹8,280 crore of interest income) and interest expense of ₹4,784 crore has NII of ₹3,496 crore and a NIM of 3.80%. Drivers. Loan mix (unsecured and microfinance loans yield far more than mortgages or corporate loans), the share of low-yield government securities, the CASA ratio and cost of term deposits, the interest-rate cycle (Section 05) and the amount of idle cash held for CRR and liquidity. Ranges. Above 4.0% is good for a universal bank; 3.2–4.0% average; below 3.2% weak. High-yield lenders run at 5–6% but need correspondingly higher credit costs.

CASA ratio

Definition and formula. CASA ratio = (current account + savings account deposits) ÷ total deposits. Worked example. A bank with ₹1,00,000 crore of deposits, of which ₹12,000 crore are current accounts and ₹26,000 crore savings, has a CASA ratio of 38%. If savings pay 2.75% and term deposits 6.5%, raising the ratio to 43% by attracting ₹5,000 crore more savings in place of term deposits cuts the annual interest bill by ₹187.5 crore. Drivers. Branch density in salaried and business districts, payroll and corporate cash-management mandates, government business, NRI remittance corridors, digital experience and the savings rate the bank is willing to pay. Ranges. Above 40% is good; 30–40% average; below 30% weak.

How they compare. IDFC First (50.8%), IDBI (43.6%), J&K Bank (42.0%), Kotak (40.3%) and ICICI (39.5%) lead; DCB (21.7%) and CSB (19%) trail (chart in Section 03). The definition trap. Period-end and average ratios differ, sometimes by several points: RBL's period-end CASA ratio was 29.2% in June 2026 but its quarterly average was 25.2%. Banks also count "sweep" deposits and fixed-rate savings differently, and a high CASA ratio bought with an above-market savings rate is less valuable than it looks. Pair the CASA ratio with the cost of deposits.

The CASA ratio also interacts with growth. Current and savings balances grow roughly with the incomes and transaction volumes of a bank's existing customers, perhaps 8–12% a year at a mature bank. A bank that wants its total deposits to grow 15–20% must therefore raise the difference through term deposits, which mechanically lowers the CASA ratio. That is why fast-growing banks almost always show a falling ratio and why a stable or rising ratio during strong growth, as at Federal Bank in FY26, is a genuine sign of franchise strength.

In plain terms

Current and savings accounts are the money people and businesses keep in the bank for everyday use. Because they need it handy, they accept little or no interest. The more of a bank's money comes from these accounts, the cheaper its funding. A high CASA ratio is the closest thing banking has to a moat.

Cost of funds and cost of deposits

Definition and formula. Cost of deposits = interest paid on deposits ÷ average deposits. Cost of funds = total interest expense ÷ average interest-bearing liabilities (deposits plus borrowings). Worked example. A bank paying ₹4,784 crore of interest on ₹88,600 crore of average deposits and borrowings has a cost of funds of 5.40%. Drivers. CASA ratio, the share and price of bulk deposits, bond and refinance borrowings, the rate cycle, and the bank's credit rating. Ranges. Below 4.8% good; 4.8–5.6% average; above 5.6% weak, on the FY26 rate environment. How they compare. On reported Q1 FY27 figures ICICI's cost of deposits was 4.41%, Kotak's cost of funds 4.46%, IDBI's cost of deposits 4.59%, IndusInd's cost of funds 5.05%, Federal's cost of deposits 5.21%, Karur Vysya's 5.45%, City Union's 5.56% and IDFC First's cost of funds 5.96% (full approximations for all 19 in Section 03). The definition trap. Some banks report cost of deposits, others cost of funds; the latter includes bonds and borrowings and is usually higher. Year-on-year falls in 2026 mostly reflect the rate cycle, not franchise improvement: compare the change with peers.

Loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR or CD ratio)

Definition and formula. LDR = advances ÷ deposits. Worked example. ₹70,000 crore of loans funded by ₹82,000 crore of deposits gives an LDR of 85.4%. Drivers. Loan demand relative to deposit-gathering ability; regulatory reserves (with 3% CRR and 18% SLR, a bank cannot lend more than about 79% of deposits without other funding such as equity or bonds); mergers with non-bank lenders, as at HDFC Bank. Ranges. 80–90% is comfortable; 90–95% stretched; above 95% means the bank depends on borrowings or must pay up for deposits; below 75% indicates under-used deposits and lower margins. How they compare. HDFC Bank (96.5%), Bandhan (94.3%) and RBL (93.1%) are the most stretched; J&K Bank (75.3%), Karnataka Bank (78.5%) and IndusInd (78.7%) have the most spare capacity (chart in Section 04). The definition trap. Banks differ on whether they use gross or net advances, whether they include inter-bank participation certificates and bills rediscounted, and whether certificates of deposit count as deposits. IDFC First reports a "domestic" ratio that excludes FCNR-funded loans booked at its GIFT City branch; Yes Bank publishes both reported and FCNR-normalised ratios (87.5% and 83.2% in September 2026).

Credit cost

Definition and formula. Credit cost = provisions for loan losses in the period ÷ average advances, annualised. Worked example. A bank with average loans of ₹70,000 crore that books ₹105 crore of loan-loss provisions in a quarter has an annualised credit cost of 0.60% (₹105 crore × 4 ÷ ₹70,000 crore). Drivers. Loan mix (unsecured and microfinance highest), the economic cycle, underwriting discipline, collections capability, and accounting choices such as contingency or "floating" provisions. Ranges. Below 0.5% good; 0.5–1.2% average; above 1.2% weak for a universal bank. Microfinance-heavy banks can run at 2–3% in stress years and still be profitable if their margins are high enough.

How they compare. Federal (0.41%), HDFC (about 0.40%) and Kotak (0.46%) are the lowest; IDFC First (1.53%, down from 2.69%) the highest among those disclosing (chart in Section 06). The definition trap. Banks report net credit cost (after recoveries and upgrades) or gross; some include provisions on investments and contingent items; some divide by average assets rather than loans (Yes Bank reports 0.3% of average assets, which is roughly 0.5% of loans). Large one-time buffers, such as Axis's ₹2,001 crore "West Asia" provision in the March 2026 quarter, distort a single quarter; look at four-quarter averages.

In plain terms

Credit cost is the share of the loan book a bank expects to lose each year. A credit cost of 0.5% means that for every ₹100 lent, the bank sets aside 50 paise a year for loans that will not be repaid. Because a bank's whole profit is only about ₹1.5–2 per ₹100 of loans, a jump in credit cost from 0.5% to 1.5% can wipe out more than half its profit.

Gross and net NPA ratios

Definition and formula. A loan becomes a non-performing asset (NPA) when interest or principal is more than 90 days overdue. Gross NPA ratio = gross NPAs ÷ gross advances. Net NPA ratio = (gross NPAs − specific provisions held against them) ÷ net advances. Worked example. A bank with ₹72,000 crore of gross loans, ₹1,440 crore of NPAs and ₹1,080 crore of provisions on them has a gross NPA ratio of 2.0% and a net NPA of ₹360 crore, about 0.51% of its ₹70,920 crore net advances. Drivers. New slippages, recoveries and upgrades, write-offs (which remove loans from gross NPAs), and sales to asset reconstruction companies. Ranges. Gross below 1.5% good, 1.5–3% average, above 3% weak; net below 0.4% good, 0.4–1% average, above 1% weak.

Provision coverage ratio (PCR)

Definition and formula. PCR = specific provisions held ÷ gross NPAs. It may be stated excluding or including technical write-offs (loans written off in the books but still being pursued). Worked example. ₹1,080 crore of provisions against ₹1,440 crore of NPAs is a 75% PCR. Age of the bad loans (provisions rise with time overdue), collateral, management conservatism. Ranges. Above 80% good; 65–80% average; below 65% weak.

Slippage ratio and the SMA buckets

Definition and formula. Slippage ratio = new NPAs added in the period ÷ opening standard advances, annualised. Special mention accounts (SMA) are loans overdue 1–30 days (SMA-0), 31–60 (SMA-1) and 61–90 (SMA-2), the pipeline for future NPAs. Worked example. A bank with ₹70,000 crore of standard loans that adds ₹350 crore of new NPAs in a quarter has an annualised slippage ratio of 2.0%. Ranges. Below 1.5% good; 1.5–2.5% average; above 2.5% weak. How they compare. Axis's gross slippage ratio was 1.79% in the June 2026 quarter, down from 3.13% a year earlier; Kotak's quarterly slippages fell 27% to ₹1,321 crore; Yes Bank's gross slippages were ₹964 crore (1.4% of advances, annualised); Tamilnad Mercantile reported total SMA at 2.21% of advances, down from 3.05%. The definition trap. Some banks report net slippages (after upgrades and recoveries) and some gross; the RBI's Financial Stability Report noted that SMA-1 rose in March 2026 even as headline NPAs fell, which is exactly why the SMA pipeline matters more than the NPA stock.

Fee income ratio

Definition and formula. Fee income ratio = core fee and commission income ÷ average total assets (or ÷ net revenue). It excludes treasury gains and recoveries from written-off loans, which do not recur. Worked example. A bank earning ₹1,200 crore of core fees on ₹1 lakh crore of average assets has a fee-to-assets ratio of 1.2%; if its net revenue is ₹4,996 crore, fees are 24% of it. Drivers. Card spending and interchange, payments and cash management, distribution of insurance and mutual funds, trade and forex, loan processing fees, wealth management. Ranges. Fee-to-assets above 1.5% good; 1.0–1.5% average; below 1.0% weak. How they compare. IDFC First reported fees of 2.09% of average assets in the June 2026 quarter, helped by its retail and card franchise; Axis earned ₹6,156 crore of fees (up 7%), 90% of them from "granular" retail, transaction-banking, trade and forex sources; Kotak earned ₹2,500 crore; RBL's core fees rose 16% to ₹923 crore; Federal's fee income rose 21.7%. The definition trap. "Other income" in a bank's profit and loss includes treasury gains on bonds and recoveries from old bad loans as well as fees; Yes Bank's and South Indian Bank's other income in FY26 contained large recovery and security-receipt gains that will not repeat. Always separate core fees from the rest.

Cost-to-income and cost-to-assets

Definition and formula. Cost-to-income = operating expenses ÷ net revenue (NII + other income). Cost-to-assets = operating expenses ÷ average total assets. Worked example. ₹2,500 crore of opex against ₹4,996 crore of net revenue is a cost-to-income ratio of 50%; on ₹1 lakh crore of assets it is a cost-to-assets ratio of 2.5%. Drivers. Branch and staff intensity, technology spend, the cost of acquiring customers (agents and marketing), and the revenue side (a high-margin bank can afford a higher cost-to-assets). Ranges. Cost-to-income below 45% good, 45–58% average, above 58% weak; cost-to-assets below 2.2% good, 2.2–3% average, above 3% weak for a universal bank.

In plain terms

CET1, capital adequacy and liquidity coverage

Definition and formula. CET1 ratio = common equity tier-1 capital (paid-up equity and retained earnings, less intangibles and deductions) ÷ risk-weighted assets (RWA). Total capital adequacy (CRAR) adds additional tier-1 bonds and tier-2 capital. Liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) = high-quality liquid assets ÷ expected net cash outflows over 30 days of stress. Worked example. A bank with ₹68,000 crore of RWA (68% of its ₹1 lakh crore assets) and ₹10,200 crore of CET1 has a CET1 ratio of 15%. If unsecured loans attract a risk weight 25 percentage points higher, ₹10,000 crore of such loans adds ₹2,500 crore of RWA and lowers the ratio to about 14.5%. Ranges. CET1 above 15% good; 12.5–15% average; below 12.5% tight. LCR above 125% good; 110–125% average; below 110% tight. How they compare. Charts in Section 09. The definition trap. Some banks quote capital including the current year's profit, others excluding it (Bandhan reported 18.2% including profits); a May 2026 RBI direction on capital computation changed how some items are counted. Excess capital is a cushion against ECL, but it also explains why Kotak's RoE lags its RoA.

The liquidity coverage ratio deserves equal attention since April 2026. Under the revised RBI rules, retail deposits accessible by internet or mobile banking carry an additional 2.5 percentage point run-off assumption, so a bank with a highly digital deposit base needs more liquid assets for the same deposits. A bank that runs its LCR close to 100% has little room to absorb a sudden withdrawal and may have to buy government bonds at the expense of loans, which lowers its margin. Ratios in the June 2026 quarter ranged from 116% at IDFC First to 144% at Kotak; RBL lifted its ratio to 148% in September after receiving Emirates NBD's capital. A falling LCR combined with a rising loan-to-deposit ratio is an early sign that a bank is stretching its funding.

In plain terms

Capital is the shareholders' own money that sits under the bank like a shock absorber. If loans go bad, capital absorbs the loss before depositors lose anything. The RBI insists on a minimum; a bank with much more than the minimum is safer and can grow faster, but earns a lower return on that capital until it puts it to work.

Definition traps, collected

  • NIM denominator. Total assets or interest-earning assets; "core" NIM excluding tax-refund interest. HDFC Bank: 3.26% vs 3.40% for the same quarter.
  • Period-end vs average. CASA ratios, deposits and loans can differ materially (RBL CASA 29.2% period-end vs 25.2% average).
  • Gross vs net, with or without write-offs. PCR including technical write-offs adds 7–15 points; Axis's "aggregate coverage" of 161% counts general provisions.
  • FCNR-normalised figures. Q2 FY27 deposit and loan growth is inflated by the RBI swap window; use the banks' normalised figures (Yes Bank deposits +13.2% normalised vs +19.5% reported).
  • Advances definitions. Gross vs net; including or excluding inter-bank participation certificates, bills rediscounted and securitised loans ("advances under management" at HDFC Bank); including credit substitutes (IDFC First).
  • Credit cost basis. Of loans or of assets; net of recoveries or gross; including one-time buffers (Axis ₹2,001 crore).
  • Standalone vs consolidated. HDFC Bank's FY26 profit was ₹74,671 crore standalone and ₹79,219 crore consolidated; Kotak's ₹14,008 crore and ₹19,288 crore. P/E and P/B look very different on each basis.
  • Agricultural NPAs. Crop-season classification inflates June and December quarters for banks with large agricultural books.
  • Capital including profits. Some banks add the current period's profit to capital ratios before audit.
  • Annualising one quarter. Seasonal or one-off provisions distort a single quarter's RoA and RoE.
In plain terms

Banks follow common accounting rules, but they choose which ratios to put in the headline and how to define them. Before comparing two banks on any number, check three things: is it period-end or average, is it standalone or consolidated, and does it include anything unusual such as written-off loans or one-time provisions. Most apparent "outperformance" disappears once definitions are aligned.

How to read a private bank's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Deposits first. Total deposit growth and CASA ratio, period-end and average. Is deposit growth at or above loan growth? Strip out FCNR or other one-off deposits.
  2. Loan growth and mix. Which segments are growing: secured or unsecured, retail or corporate? Growth well above the system (about 12–15%) needs an explanation.
  3. NIM, and why it moved. Check the yield on advances and the cost of funds separately. Relate the change to the rate cycle and the bank's share of repo-linked loans.
  4. Fees. Core fee growth, excluding treasury gains and recoveries. Is it keeping pace with the balance sheet?
  5. Operating costs. Cost-to-income and cost-to-assets; is the bank investing (branches, technology) or cutting?
  6. Slippages and SMA. New NPAs added, by segment; the SMA-1 and SMA-2 pipeline if disclosed.
  7. Credit cost and buffers. Provisions as a % of loans; any one-time or contingency provisions created or released.
  8. Asset quality stock. Gross and net NPA, PCR excluding write-offs, and write-offs in the quarter.
  9. Capital and liquidity. CET1 including or excluding profit, LCR, and any planned capital raise.
  10. RoA and the outlook. Annualised RoA versus the bank's own target and the peer range; management guidance on margins, growth and credit cost.

Read in that order, a quarter tells a coherent story: where the money came from, where it went, what it earned, what it cost to run and what was lost. The same ten steps applied to HDFC Bank's June 2026 quarter give the picture in Section 04: deposits up 14.7% but CASA down to 32%, loans up 15.4%, margin at a record-low 3.26%, credit cost about 0.4%, gross NPA 1.17%, profit up 5% (about 10% excluding a one-off gain the year before). Applied to IDFC First Bank, the same steps reveal the opposite story: a 50.8% CASA ratio, loan growth of 20.6%, margin up 25 basis points, provisions down by more than a third, and a record profit.

In plain terms

If you have only ten minutes with a bank's results, look at where its money came from (deposits and CASA), what it lent and at what margin, how many loans went bad, and how much capital it has left. Profit growth is the last thing to check, not the first, because it is the sum of all the others.

Who is winning, and on what

ICICI wins on returns, IDFC First on deposits, Tamilnad Mercantile on clean growth, and the market has noticed only some of it.

Three maps capture the competitive landscape. The first plots return on assets against the price-to-book multiple. If the market priced banks purely on current profitability, the dots would form a rising line from bottom-left to top-right. Broadly they do, which is reassuring, but the exceptions are where the debates lie.

ICICI Bank and Kotak occupy the top-right corner: high returns and high valuations. Karur Vysya and City Union are close behind; the market has re-rated both over the past two years as their bad loans fell and returns rose above 1.5%. HDFC Bank and Axis Bank sit in the middle: good returns, de-rated valuations. The interesting outliers are Tamilnad Mercantile and IDBI, which earn returns above 1.8% on assets but trade at about 1.3 times book. In TMB's case the discount reflects decades of disputed ownership and a share register with no promoter and almost no institutional investors; in IDBI's it reflects a tiny free float and uncertainty over the privatisation terms. Federal Bank is the outlier in the other direction: a modest 1.2% return on assets but a 2.1 times book valuation, which prices in the growth plan funded by Blackstone's capital.

The second map separates two ways of earning a margin: cheap funding and high-yield lending.

The top-right corner, where cheap deposits meet rich margins, is the most valuable place in banking, and only Kotak, ICICI and IDFC First are there. Kotak and ICICI arrived through decades of franchise building; IDFC First is there because it pays above-market rates on savings accounts and lends at high yields, a combination that works only if credit costs stay controlled. The top-left corner holds the high-yield lenders, Bandhan, Karur Vysya and RBL, whose margins depend on what they lend rather than on cheap money. The large cluster at about 32% CASA and 3.2–3.3% NIM, HDFC Bank, Federal and Karnataka Bank, is the clearest picture of the deposit war: a once-premium franchise (HDFC) now looks, on these two measures, like a regional bank.

In plain terms

There are two ways to make a fat margin: borrow cheaply or lend expensively. Borrowing cheaply is the better way because it does not require taking more risk. The second map shows that only a handful of banks manage both, and that HDFC Bank, after its merger, has lost much of its cheap-funding advantage.

The third map asks whether growth is being bought with risk.

Almost every bank is growing loans faster than 15% a year, partly because the September 2026 figures for some banks include FCNR-funded loans and partly because the economy is strong. Only IndusInd is shrinking, by design, as its new management cuts microfinance and rebuilds. The banks growing fastest with the cleanest books, Tamilnad Mercantile, City Union and RBL, are the ones to watch for the next cycle of asset-quality tests; rapid growth tends to flatter bad-loan ratios for a year or two because new loans have not yet had time to go bad.

Winning in Indian private banking today means three things at once: cheap deposits, a clean loan book and a return on assets near 2%. ICICI Bank does all three at scale. What the market pays for: return on equity, growth and trust

Banks are priced on book value, and the multiple of book is a bet on how much the bank will earn on that book for how long.

Investors value most companies on their earnings or cash flows. Banks are different, because their earnings depend on accounting choices about provisions and because their raw material, money, is fungible: what matters is how much return a bank can earn on each rupee of shareholders' capital, and for how long. The standard tool is the price-to-book ratio, read against return on equity. A simple formula, the justified price-to-book, links them.

Two further factors sit on top of return on equity. The first is trust. HDFC Bank's de-rating from roughly 2.7 times book in 2023 to 1.9 times today is larger than its fall in return on equity alone would justify; the extra discount reflects uncertainty about governance and strategy after the chairman's resignation and the leadership change. In plain terms

Banks borrow ten rupees for every rupee of their own, so investors worry more about what could go wrong than about next year's profit. That is why a governance scare can cut a bank's valuation by a third even when its loans are fine, and why a strong owner standing behind a bank can lift its valuation even before its profits improve.

For the sub-sector as a whole, the combined valuation of 2.13 times book and 17.7 times FY26 earnings implies a sustained return on equity of about 15% on the formula's assumptions, a little above the group's FY26 figure of about 12.5% on a simple-sum basis. That gap is the market's bet on recovery at the mid-size banks and on margin expansion as rates rise. It is not a demanding bet by the standards of the past decade, when the large private banks routinely traded at three to four times book, but it does assume the deposit war does not get worse.

The quiet compounders of the south

Century-old banks in Tamil Nadu and Kerala now earn returns that rival the giants, on gold, small business and loyal local deposits.

Seven of the 19 banks are "old private banks" from the south: City Union, Karur Vysya and Tamilnad Mercantile from Tamil Nadu; Federal, South Indian and CSB from Kerala; and Karnataka Bank from coastal Karnataka. Founded between 1904 and 1931 by local trading communities, they survived nationalisation because they were small, and for decades they were regarded as sleepy, regional and over-staffed. Most suffered in the 2015–2020 bad-loan cycle, when they lent to mid-sized corporates and infrastructure projects they could not judge well.

The past five years have been different. Each has retreated to what it knows: small businesses and traders in its home region, agricultural and jewel loans backed by gold, and retail lending to communities where it has deep relationships. Combined with low credit costs, falling deposit costs in FY26 and, for some, the rise in gold prices, the result is a group of banks earning returns that rival the largest private banks.

In plain terms

The best of the old southern banks have found a simple, profitable formula: lend to local traders and families against gold or business assets they understand, fund it with deposits from customers who have banked with them for generations, and keep plenty of capital in reserve. It is not glamorous, but Tamilnad Mercantile and Karur Vysya now earn as much on each rupee of assets as Kotak.

Their growth has also accelerated sharply. Tamilnad Mercantile's loans grew 27% and City Union's 25% in the year to June 2026, compared with about 16% for the system. That is a double-edged signal. These banks have the capital and the deposit loyalty to grow faster, and their regional economies, particularly Tamil Nadu's manufacturing and electronics clusters, are expanding. But rapid growth after years of caution is exactly what preceded their last bad-loan cycle, and the gold-loan boom depends on gold prices staying high. A 15–20% fall in gold prices would not cause large losses, because loan-to-value limits leave a buffer, but it would slow growth and could force some borrowers to top up collateral or repay.

Valuations vary widely within the group. Karur Vysya and City Union trade at 2.2–2.4 times book, a full re-rating from about one times five years ago. Federal trades at 2.1 times on expectations of a higher return. Tamilnad Mercantile, Karnataka, South Indian and CSB trade at about one times book or a little more, reflecting ownership or governance questions (TMB's disputed register, Karnataka's 2025 leadership turmoil, CSB's pending owner change) or lower returns. For investors, these banks offer something the giants cannot: the possibility of being acquired. With foreign banks now allowed to buy control, and large Indian banks hungry for deposits, the small, clean, high-capital southern banks are natural targets, although the RBI's consent and the absence of controlling shareholders make any deal slow.

The main risks are concentration and succession. Each bank depends on one or two states, so a regional shock, a flood, a political dispute or a downturn in a local industry hits its whole book. And several have had difficult chief-executive transitions; the RBI's tighter governance rules make board quality more important than ever for banks without a controlling owner.

The global lens: why foreign banks want in

Indian private banks are among the most profitable large banks in the world, in one of the least penetrated credit markets.

Seen from abroad, Indian private banking has an unusual combination of features. The best Indian banks earn returns on assets of 2–2.5%, roughly double what large banks in the United States earn and three to five times what large European and Japanese banks earn. They operate in an economy growing 7% a year in real terms and 12–13% in nominal terms, where bank credit is still only around 60% of GDP by the dossier's estimate (₹213.6 lakh crore of credit against roughly ₹357 lakh crore of nominal GDP in FY26), compared with well over 100% in China and much of the developed world. And their regulator has a strong record of intervening early, as with Yes Bank in 2020 and IndusInd in 2025, so that depositors of failing private sector banks (Global Trust in 2004, Yes Bank and Lakshmi Vilas in 2020) have been protected through RBI-arranged rescues or mergers.

Indian bank valuations have converged toward global levels over the past five years. In plain terms

A large bank in Japan or Europe might earn 50 paise to a rupee of profit on every ₹100 of assets. India's best private banks earn ₹2 or more. That gap, and India's room for credit to grow, is why foreign banks and investors are now prepared to pay well over book value for stakes in mid-size Indian banks.

The global lens also highlights two risks that domestic investors sometimes underweight. The first is currency. Foreign investors earn returns in dollars; the rupee's fall to a record low of 96.96 to the dollar in May 2026 is one reason foreign portfolio investors have been net sellers of Indian bank shares, and why HDFC Bank's foreign ownership fell five quarters in a row. The second is the global cost of capital. Indian bank valuations are partly set by global investors comparing them with US Treasury yields and developed-market banks; if global rates rise or risk appetite falls, Indian banks can de-rate even when their own numbers are unchanged. Against that, domestic institutions, mutual funds, insurers and pension funds, have steadily raised their holdings: at HDFC Bank they now own more than foreign investors do.

Finally, India's banking model differs from the global norm in one important way: the state still owns about half the system. Public sector banks set much of the price of deposits and loans, especially in rural areas and for government business. When they are healthy and growing, as in FY26, they compete hard; when they are weak, as in 2015–2020, private banks gain share easily. Global comparisons that ignore this miss the main competitive constraint on Indian private banks.

Technology and disruption: the branch is not dead, but deposits are digital

UPI, account aggregators and AI are changing how banks gather money and judge borrowers, and how fast customers can leave.

Indian banking has digitised faster than almost any large banking system. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), launched in 2016, now carries the bulk of retail payments by volume; account opening, fixed deposits, mutual-fund investment and many loans can be completed on a phone in minutes. For banks this is both an opportunity and a threat.

The opportunity is cheaper customer acquisition and better data. Axis Bank reported that 98% of its individual customers' financial transactions in the June 2026 quarter were digital, that 66% of new savings accounts were opened on tablets by staff in the field, and that 48% of individual retail term deposits by value were opened digitally. It had about 16 million monthly active users on its app and 40 million customers on WhatsApp banking. IndusInd says it runs more than 50 machine-learning models that evaluate about half a million loans a month and monitor 42 million customers. Account aggregators, the RBI-licensed data-sharing framework launched in 2021, let a bank see a small business's bank statements and GST filings with the customer's consent, which is one reason business-banking loan books are growing 18–28% a year at the leaders.

The threat is mobility. When a customer can move a deposit to another bank, a small finance bank or a liquid mutual fund in a few taps, deposits become more rate-sensitive and potentially more flighty. Three other technology themes matter. First, payments are a loss leader. UPI carries no merchant fee for most transactions, so banks earn little directly; the value lies in the customer relationship and the current-account float. Banks with dominant payment shares, Axis with about 38% of UPI payer volume and a 22% share of merchant terminals, are investing for deposits and cross-sell, not payment revenue. Second, cards and unsecured consumer credit are increasingly originated through fintech and co-branded partnerships; this widens reach but outsources part of the underwriting, which is where the RBI has focused its supervisory concern. Third, technology resilience is now a regulatory issue in its own right: the RBI's 2024 restrictions on Kotak's digital onboarding were imposed because of IT-governance lapses, not credit problems, and cost the bank nearly a year of customer acquisition.

In plain terms

Technology has made banking easier for customers and cheaper for banks, but it has also made it easier for customers to leave. A bank's deposits used to be held in place by the inconvenience of switching. Today a better rate is a few taps away, so banks must earn loyalty through service, relationships and convenience, or pay for it with higher rates.

Will fintechs or big technology firms disrupt the private banks? So far, the answer is no: Indian regulation keeps deposit-taking and most lending inside licensed banks and NBFCs, and payment banks and digital-only lenders have struggled to become profitable (the RBI's 2024 action against Paytm Payments Bank ended its deposit business). The more likely pattern is partnership and absorption: banks provide the balance sheet and licence, fintechs provide distribution and design, and the large banks buy or build the best capabilities. The NBFCs covered in the NBFC sub-sector report are a closer competitive threat in unsecured, vehicle and gold lending than any technology company.

Bull vs bear

The case for and against Indian private banks, in the terms of the four forces.

Investors in Indian private banks in late 2026 are weighing an unusually clear set of opposing arguments. The bull case rests on margins recovering as rates rise, credit costs staying low after the microfinance clean-up, and new owners and leaders lifting returns at banks that have underperformed. The bear case rests on a deposit war that never ends, an accounting change that will raise provisions, a growth slowdown and the risk that rapid recent loan growth produces the next round of bad loans.

The bull case

  • Margins turn up: the 7 October 2026 hike lifts yields on repo-linked loans (57% of ICICI's domestic book) within a quarter, while term-deposit costs lag; Axis called its June 2026 margin the bottom of the cycle.
  • Credit costs stay low: microfinance stress has passed (portfolio at risk 2.0% from 6.3%), corporate balance sheets are strong, and large banks hold big contingency buffers ahead of ECL.
  • Recovery earnings: IDFC First, RBL, Bandhan, Yes and IndusInd have profits far below their potential; each quarter of normal credit costs lifts returns sharply.
  • Strategic capital: foreign owners at RBL, Yes, Federal and prospectively IDBI bring capital, ratings and patience; listed peers at about book value carry takeover optionality.
  • Reasonable valuations: the group trades at 2.1× book and 17.7× earnings; HDFC Bank at 1.9× book is near its lowest valuation in a decade with a clean book and a new CEO.
  • Structural runway: bank credit is about 60% of GDP in an economy growing 12–13% nominal; private banks have taken share for a decade and have the technology lead.

The bear case

  • The deposit war is structural: the savings share of deposits fell from 34.6% to 28.7% in four years and households keep moving to mutual funds; CASA ratios at HDFC, Yes, CSB and J&K fell 2–5 points in a year.
  • ECL from April 2027: earlier provisioning will dent profits and capital, most at banks with unsecured or small-business books and thin buffers.
  • Growth is slowing: ICRA expects system credit growth of 11.0–11.7% in FY27; Q2 FY27 growth is flattered by FCNR deposits and matching loans that will run off.
  • Fast growth breeds bad loans: RBL (+42%), TMB (+27–29%), CUB (+25%) and J&K (+25%) are growing far above the system; unsecured SMA-1 rose in March 2026.
  • Governance and leadership risk: new CEOs at HDFC, Kotak, IndusInd and Yes; HDFC's chairman resignation and MSRDC review; IndusInd's accounting failure. First-year clean-ups are common.
  • Macro shocks: the West Asia war, oil, a record-weak rupee and continued foreign selling; a rate-hiking cycle eventually raises defaults.

In plain terms

The optimists say the hardest part of the cycle, falling margins and microfinance losses, is behind the banks, and the shares are reasonably priced. The pessimists say savers will keep demanding higher rates, new accounting rules will force bigger provisions, and today's rapid loan growth will produce tomorrow's bad loans. On balance, the evidence in this report suggests the sub-sector's near-term earnings are more likely to surprise upward than downward in FY27, driven by low credit costs at the recovery banks and the margin effect of the October hike, while the medium-term risks, the deposit war and ECL, are real but gradual. The dispersion between banks will remain wide. The banks best placed on both horizons are those with cheap, granular deposits, high existing buffers and moderate growth; the banks most exposed are those growing fastest on expensive funding.
