00

The one-page view: a duration trade dressed as a property business

Three listed operators, ₹5,729 crore of revenue, a record demand cycle, and an accounting standard that hides the single number that matters.

A flexible-workspace operator does one thing: it rents a large piece of office space on a long lease, spends money turning it into finished desks, meeting rooms and cafeterias, and then licenses those desks to companies for months or a few years at a time. The money is made in the gap between what clients pay per seat and what the landlord charges per square foot. The risk sits in the gap between the length of the lease (nine to fifteen years) and the length of the client contract (two to four years).

India's listed sub-sector is young. Awfis Space Solutions listed in May 2024, Smartworks Coworking Spaces in July 2025 and WeWork India Management in October 2025. Together they reported ₹5,729 crore of revenue in FY26, up 26.4% from ₹4,531 crore in FY25, and were worth ₹15,383 crore at the close on 23 October 2026. IndiQube Spaces, also listed in July 2025, is a direct competitor but is not in the NSE 750 set covered here; it appears in this report only as a clearly marked reference point, as do the unlisted Table Space and the global operator IWG.

The industry is enjoying the best demand conditions it has ever had. According to CBRE, flexible-space operators took 27% of all office space absorbed in India's main cities in the April–June 2026 quarter, up from about 5% in 2017, and were the largest single occupier category for three quarters in a row. The July–September quarter eased to 24% on CBRE's count. Global capability centres (GCCs), the in-house technology and operations hubs that multinationals run in India, took about 42% of the 66.4 million sq ft absorbed in the first nine months of calendar 2026. Many of those GCCs land first in a flex centre.

Yet the three stocks have gone three different ways. WeWork India is roughly flat over a year and is now 57% of the group's market value on 42.6% of its revenue. Smartworks is down 21%. Awfis is down 64% and trades at 18.7 times reported earnings while WeWork trades at 99 times. The reasons are not mainly about demand. They are about what each company's profit really is once rent is counted, how much fixed rent each has promised to pay, and how each is owned.

The first thing a newcomer must learn is that this sector has three profit lines, not one. Under the Ind AS 116 lease standard, rent on a long lease is removed from operating costs and replaced by depreciation of a "right-of-use" asset and interest on a "lease liability". Because rent is the largest cost an operator has, its reported EBITDA balloons: the three members' combined Ind AS operating profit was ₹3,286 crore in FY26, a 57.4% margin. Put the rent back and the figure collapses to roughly ₹1,027 crore, or 17.9%, on the companies' own adjusted measures. Reported net profit was ₹157 crore, a 2.7% margin, because Ind AS loads lease costs into the early years of each lease and these companies are opening centres fast.

In plain terms

A co-working company is a middleman for office space. It signs a long lease, furnishes the floor and rents the desks out for a few years at a time at roughly two and a half to three times what it pays the landlord. When the desks are full it makes a healthy profit after rent; when they empty, the rent bill does not shrink. The accounting rules move the rent bill into "depreciation" and "interest", which is why the headline margins look enormous and the bottom line looks tiny. Neither tells you how the business is doing. The rent-paid margin, about 10–20% for these three companies, does.

Five forces define the sub-sector, and the report is built around them.

  • Flex's share of India's office demand has risen from about 5% in 2017 to 21% in 2025 and 27% in April–June 2026 on CBRE's numbers, driven by GCCs and by large companies that keep a "core" of long-leased space and put 15–20% of their footprint in flexible space. Cushman & Wakefield, which counts renewals in its denominator, puts the July–September 2026 share at only 16%. The source always matters (Sections 02–03).
  • Three EBITDAs. Ind AS 116 makes reported EBITDA 37–65% of revenue, while rent-paid EBITDA is 10–20%, and every company defines its adjusted measure differently. Reading through this is the analyst's first job (Section 07).
  • Long leases, short licences. The members owe ₹11,239 crore of lease liabilities against net cash excluding leases of only ₹106 crore. Their protection is contracted client revenue: WeWork's locked-in revenue covers its locked-in rent 2.9 times, Smartworks' about 2.1 times, and WeWork breaks even at 56.6% occupancy (Section 08).
  • Three models. WeWork India runs a premium branded straight-lease portfolio; Smartworks leases whole buildings and fills them with enterprise managed offices at the lowest price per seat; Awfis runs the largest network by centre count, with landlords funding much of the fit-out under "managed aggregation", plus a design-and-build arm (Section 06).
  • Ownership and governance. WeWork India is controlled by the Embassy group, which has pledged part of its stake and is also WeWork's landlord and facility manager; Smartworks is founder-controlled with a Singapore holder linked (indicatively) to Keppel; Awfis is a founder-led company whose private-equity backers have been selling (Section 16).

01

From building to seat: how a square foot is turned into a monthly fee

A landlord sells space by the square foot for nine years; an operator sells it by the seat for three. Everything in between is the business.

The chain starts with a developer who builds a Grade-A office tower. In Bengaluru that tower leases at about ₹94 per sq ft per month (CRE Matrix, April–June 2026); in Mumbai's core markets at about ₹167. A conventional tenant, say a bank, would sign a nine-year lease directly, wait nine to twelve months for its own interior contractor and spend ₹2,500–4,000 per sq ft (indicative) on furniture, air-conditioning upgrades, IT cabling and a cafeteria. The flex operator inserts itself between those two parties.

The operator signs the head lease, typically nine to fifteen years with a three-to-five-year lock-in during which it cannot leave, and with rent escalating 4–5% a year or 15% every three years. It then fits out the floor, usually in two to four months (Smartworks says 45–60 days for an enterprise fit-out), and sells the result by the desk. The client pays one all-in monthly fee that bundles rent, the use of the fit-out, power, housekeeping, security, internet, coffee and reception. The client can move in within weeks, does not tie up capital, and can scale up or down at the end of its term.

What the operator sells is not one product but a ladder of them. At the bottom are hot desks and day passes for freelancers; then dedicated desks and private cabins inside a shared centre for start-ups and project teams; then "managed offices", where a whole floor or building is designed to one client's brand and specification and run by the operator for three to five years. Around these sit virtual offices (a registered address and mail handling), value-added services such as parking, food, events and IT, and, at Awfis and increasingly WeWork, a design-and-build business that fits out offices for corporates who keep their own leases.

Where is the value captured? Mainly in three places. First, the spread between the fee per seat and the rent per seat, multiplied by occupancy: WeWork India's total revenue was 2.8 times its rent in Q1 FY27, and Smartworks targets lease-rental revenue of at least twice rent. Second, the ancillary layers sold on the same square foot, which carry little extra cost. Third, the time value of the client's capital: a corporate that would have spent ₹20 crore and a year on its own fit-out pays the operator a premium to avoid both. Landlords capture value as well. Flex operators are now the largest category of tenant in Indian offices, a landlord with a flex anchor fills a new building faster, and some developers own their own flex brands, most visibly the Embassy group, which controls WeWork India.

In plain terms

The operator buys office space wholesale by the square foot on a long contract and sells it retail by the desk on short contracts. The retail price includes the furniture, the services and the right to leave in two or three years. Clients pay the premium because it saves them a year of fit-out work and a large cheque up front. The operator earns it only if the desks stay full.

One more structural point shapes everything later in this report. The landlord relationship can take three forms. A straight lease makes the operator a tenant with fixed rent and all of the vacancy risk; WeWork India and Smartworks run almost entirely on these. A managed-aggregation or revenue-share agreement makes the landlord a partner who funds part or all of the fit-out and takes a share of revenue or profit; this was 57% of Awfis' signed supply in June 2026. A pure management contract leaves the lease and the capex with the owner and pays the operator a fee; it is how IWG, the global leader, is growing, and it is where WeWork India's new design-and-consultancy business points. The choice changes the reported accounts, the capital needed and who loses money when a centre empties.

02

From 5% to 27% of leasing: how flex became India's largest office occupier

A decade ago co-working was a start-up amenity. By mid-2026 it was taking more office space than technology companies or banks.

India passed one billion sq ft of office stock in April–June 2026, about 1,055 million sq ft by CBRE's count. Demand has set records three years running: gross absorption was 82.6 million sq ft in calendar 2025 (CBRE) and 66.4 million sq ft in the first nine months of 2026, up 8% on the same period a year earlier. Within that market the flexible-space segment has grown faster than any other category. CBRE estimates flex operators took 2.2 million sq ft in 2017 and 18.6 million sq ft in 2025, an 8.4-fold rise, and 11.4 million sq ft in the first half of 2026 alone, up 89% year on year.

Why did the share rise so steadily, including through the pandemic? Three reasons stand out. First, occupiers changed how they plan space: CBRE's surveys suggest about 55% of large occupiers now use flex, and the share of an occupier's portfolio held in flex has moved from 5–10% historically to 15–20%. Second, the buyer changed. Enterprises are now 70–80% of flex demand and sign three-to-five-year terms instead of one or two, which makes it worth an operator's while to build entire managed buildings for them. Third, the supply of flex became professional. In 2017 the market was a scatter of start-up spaces; by 2025 Cushman & Wakefield estimated that the ten largest operators took 72% of all flex leasing, and 74% in the first half of 2026.

In plain terms

Flex stopped being a place for start-ups and became a way for large companies to rent office space. A bank or a US technology firm that once signed a nine-year lease and built its own office now often asks an operator to build and run it, and signs for three to five years. That shift, not freelancers, explains why the segment's share of leasing has risen from about one in twenty to about one in four square feet.

The stock of flex space has followed. CBRE's series, published in WeWork India's October 2025 prospectus, shows flex stock in the tier-1 cities rising from about 31 million sq ft before 2020 to 84 million sq ft in 2024, and the actual figure reached about 118 million sq ft by mid-2026, already well above CBRE's own forecast of 102–106 million sq ft for end-2025 and close to its 121–125 million sq ft forecast for end-2026. CBRE's forecast for 2027 is 140–144 million sq ft; CRISIL, which rates many of these operators, expects 140–145 million sq ft by FY28. Flex is now about 11% of India's office stock (an estimate: 118 ÷ 1,055), up from 4–6% before the pandemic.

A sober reading of the growth story has two caveats. Flex at about 11% of office stock is already high by world standards, so the bull case requires India to go further than most markets have. And forecasts by consultants who are paid by the operators, or by research co-sponsored by them (a 2026 Redseer study on GCC demand was co-sponsored by WeWork India and Smartworks), should be read as advocacy. CRISIL's independent estimate, that flex's share of net absorption rises from 14–15% in FY24 to about 20% in FY26 and about 25% by FY28, is more cautious and is the better anchor.

03

Who fills the seats: GCCs, enterprises and the "core plus flex" office

The client has changed from the start-up founder to the multinational's India hub, and that change both lengthens contracts and concentrates risk.

The single most important demand driver is the global capability centre. NASSCOM and Zinnov count about 2,117 GCCs operating 3,728 units in India in FY26, with about US$98 billion of revenue and 2.36 million staff, and more than 100 new GCCs opened during the year. CBRE estimates GCCs took about 28 million sq ft, or 42%, of the 66.4 million sq ft absorbed in the first nine months of calendar 2026; Cushman & Wakefield puts it at 39% and JLL at 41.7% for the first half. These figures match the IT Services report. A GCC typically lands in flex while it hires its first few hundred people, then either expands within the operator or moves to a conventional lease once it has scale. Research co-sponsored by WeWork India and Smartworks estimates that flex is about 18% of GCC leasing today and could reach 32% by 2030; treat the forecast as indicative.

Behind the GCC wave sits a broader change in how large occupiers plan space, usually called "core plus flex". A company keeps a long-leased core for its stable headcount and puts 15–20% of its footprint in flex to absorb uncertainty: new projects, acquisitions, pilots in new cities, teams whose size may halve in two years. This is why enterprise clients dominate every member's revenue. Smartworks says 92% of its revenue comes from enterprises; WeWork India says 77% of its core revenue comes from organisations with more than 200 employees; Awfis, the most diversified, says enterprises and multinationals are 64% of its clients by its own measure, with SMEs and start-ups 34%.

The other big change is sector mix. Information-technology services firms were once the anchor tenants of Indian offices, but tier-1 vendors are hiring fewer juniors and using AI to compress their staffing pyramids. The flex operators have quietly diversified away. WeWork India's FY26 desk sales to IT services and consulting firms fell 43% to 2,684 desks, while sales to banks and financial firms rose 176% to 9,036 desks and to telecom firms 281% to 3,687. At Smartworks, about three-quarters of new revenue in Q1 FY27 came from outside IT, and the IT and IT-enabled-services share of its portfolio fell from about 44% in FY24 to about 35%. Awfis' client base is 39% technology, 21% professional services and 13% financial services.

In plain terms

The typical new flex client in 2026 is the India office of a foreign bank, telecom or software company that is growing fast and does not want to commit to nine years of rent. That client signs for three or four years, pays reliably and often expands. The catch is that its decisions are made in New York or London, so a downturn there arrives here quickly. Indian IT-services firms, the old anchor tenants, are now a shrinking part of the mix.

Three further drivers matter at the margin. Capex avoidance: a corporate fit-out at ₹2,500–4,000 per sq ft (indicative) plus nine to twelve months of project time becomes an operating cost and a 45–60-day move-in. Distributed teams: Smartworks says 35% of its revenue comes from clients present in more than one city, and Awfis says 48% of its clients use more than one centre. Tier-2 cities: Awfis runs 25 centres with about 13,200 seats in tier-2 cities (8% of seats) and Smartworks has entered Indore, Kochi, Coimbatore and Jaipur, where rents and prices are lower but competition is thinner.

Domestic demand is growing too. JLL says Indian occupiers took 47.3% of April–June 2026 leasing, a nine-quarter high, and that "indigenous flex operators" took 57.8% of domestic leasing in the first half. That figure says as much about supply as demand: Indian companies increasingly lease their offices through Indian operators rather than directly.

Against all this sit two clear headwinds. The first is IT-services hiring, discussed above. The second is artificial intelligence. If AI lets a GCC deliver the same output with fewer people, it needs fewer seats; operators argue that AI-related roles are additive (the sponsored Redseer study projects GCC AI headcount rising from 181,000 in 2025 to 730,000 by 2030), but nobody yet knows the net effect. Section 18 returns to the question. For now, the demand data are unambiguous: absorption is at a record, and flex is taking an unprecedented share of it.

04

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: rent first, everything else second

An operator's cost base is dominated by one fixed line it negotiates once every nine years. The rest is housekeeping, power, brokers and a brand fee.

Strip the accounting away and an operator's costs fall into a short list. The largest by far is rent to landlords, about 40–47% of revenue for a straight-lease operator. Next come centre operating costs: common-area maintenance, power, housekeeping, security, repairs and the food and amenities bundled into the seat price, another 20–28%. Then sales costs: brokers who bring enterprise deals are paid 2.5–3.7% of revenue, plus marketing. Then people and overhead, 5–11%. WeWork India alone pays a brand and management fee to WeWork International, 2.43% of revenue in FY26. What is left is rent-paid EBITDA. Below it sit fit-out depreciation (the operator's own capex, written off over about eight to ten years) and a little interest on real borrowings.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 a client pays, roughly ₹40–47 goes straight to the landlord, ₹20–28 is spent running the building, ₹10–15 goes on brokers, staff and head office, and ₹15–20 is left as operating profit. One cost is easy to miss: goods and services tax on fit-outs. Co-working licence fees attract 18% GST, which operators collect from clients and offset against GST paid on their own rent and services. But input tax credit on construction of immovable property is blocked under section 17(5)(d) of the CGST Act, and whether a given fit-out counts as "plant" (credit allowed) or "immovable property" (credit blocked) has been litigated for years. WeWork India received an ₹11.01 crore show-cause notice over input credit for April 2021 to March 2023 on 1 October 2026. For an operator spending ₹500–600 crore a year on fit-outs, the eligibility of credit on that spend is a live cost item, not a footnote.

The lesson for reading cost structures: watch three ratios over time. Rent as a share of revenue (the inverse of the revenue-to-rent multiple) tells you whether pricing is keeping up with what landlords charge. Opex per sq ft tells you whether wage and power inflation is being passed through. And the brokerage line tells you how much of the growth is being bought from intermediaries rather than won directly.

Model 1: WeWork India, the premium branded straight lease

The result is the highest revenue per sq ft of the three, a revenue-to-rent multiple of 2.8 (total) in Q1 FY27, and the highest rent-paid margin: 20.2% IGAAP-equivalent EBITDA in FY26.

WeWork India is not asset-light. Some older descriptions say it was moving to revenue-share or management arrangements; its FY26 and Q1 FY27 disclosures show essentially every centre on a lease, ₹1,014 crore of cash rent paid through lease liabilities in FY26, and a capex cycle of ₹456.5 crore in FY26 and ₹188 crore in Q1 FY27 alone. Its "managed office" product, 23.7% of revenue, is a client product (a dedicated floor for one company), not a landlord partnership. The newer Rivet design-and-build arm and the Member Services marketplace are capital-light, but neither booked material revenue in Q1 FY27.

Model 2: Smartworks, the enterprise campus at the lowest price per seat

Smartworks takes whole buildings, often large ones (66% of its area is in buildings above 300,000 sq ft and 37% above 500,000 sq ft), frequently from non-institutional landlords who own about two-thirds of its area and who accept wholesale rents for a single rated tenant. It fits them out cheaply, at about ₹1,350 per sq ft (rising about 5% a year), packs them densely at roughly 23 seats per 1,000 sq ft of super built-up area, and sells managed offices to mid-sized and large enterprises at what it claims is the lowest price per seat in the market: an estimated ₹8,300 per seat a month in Q1 FY27 (ILLUSTRATIVE). Its MD says the closest competitor is 10–15% more expensive. The model trades price for volume and term: 92% of revenue is from enterprises, the 1,000-plus-seat cohort is 41% of rental revenue, and average lock-in for that cohort is 35 months.

Model 3: Awfis, the managed-aggregation network plus design-build

Awfis has the most centres (242 operational in June 2026), the most clients (3,600-plus) and the broadest geography (18 cities, nine of them tier-2). Its distinguishing feature is managed aggregation: a landlord funds all or part of the fit-out and shares revenue or profit with Awfis instead of charging fixed rent. MA was 57% of Awfis' signed supply in June 2026, down from 62% a year earlier. Awfis spends about ₹50,000 per seat of its own capital (FY22 to nine months of FY24, per its prospectus), against ₹80,000–2,00,000 for top operators on CBRE's 2023 range. That gives it the highest return on capital (the company reports cash ROCE of 55–60%) and the lowest lease liabilities (₹1,450 crore), but also the lowest margin, because the landlord keeps a large share of the spread. Its Transform arm adds design-and-build contracting, 17% of FY26 revenue, at 17–18% gross margin.

07

Three EBITDAs: reading a flex operator through Ind AS 116 Deep dive

The same company reports a 64% EBITDA margin, a 20% "adjusted" margin and a 3% net margin in the same year. Only one of them describes the business, and each company calculates it differently.

Since 1 April 2019 Indian listed companies have accounted for leases under Ind AS 116, the Indian version of the international standard IFRS 16. For most companies it is a technical detail. For a flex operator, whose largest cost is rent on long leases, it rewrites the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash-flow statement, and makes the headline numbers almost useless for judging the business. Getting through this is the first job of anyone analysing the sector.

How the standard rewires the accounts

Under the old Indian GAAP, rent was an operating expense: ₹100 of rent a year went through the P&L as ₹100 of cost every year (smoothed over any rent-free period). Under Ind AS 116, on the day an operator signs a ten-year lease it records the present value of all the future rent as a lease liability and an equal right-of-use (RoU) asset. From then on, rent disappears from operating costs. In its place come two charges below EBITDA: depreciation of the RoU asset, spread evenly over the lease, and interest on the lease liability, which is high in early years when the liability is large and falls as it is paid down.

Three consequences follow. First, reported EBITDA is inflated by the full rent bill: WeWork India's and Smartworks' Ind AS EBITDA margins are about 64%, a figure that has nothing to do with the economics of renting desks. In plain terms

In plain terms

When a flex company says "EBITDA", ask which one. The reported number includes the rent and is meaningless. The adjusted number takes the rent out, but WeWork, Smartworks and Awfis each take it out a slightly different way, so a 20% at one company and a 14% at another are not measured with the same ruler. On a common cash ruler WeWork earns about 24 paise of operating profit per rupee of revenue, Smartworks about 17.5 and Awfis about 10, all after paying the landlord.

Three practical rules follow for any reader of this sector's results. Compare a company only with itself on its own definition, and note any change of definition (Awfis' in Q1 FY27; WeWork's change in Q1 FY27 to amortise large managed-office customisation income over the client's contract instead of booking it upfront). Use cash lease payments from the cash-flow statement as the cross-check against any adjusted figure. And never compute "net debt to EBITDA" by dividing screener's borrowings, which include lease liabilities, by screener's operating profit, which excludes rent: the result double-counts the leases and is meaningless. 08

Long leases, short licences: the mismatch at the heart of the model Deep dive

The operators owe ₹11,239 crore of future rent and have almost no other debt. Whether that is safe depends on how much client revenue is contracted, how long, and at what occupancy the centres stop making money.

Every flex operator runs a mismatch between the duration of what it owes and what it is owed. On one side are head leases of nine to fifteen years with lock-ins of three to five years, during which the operator cannot walk away. On the other side are client licences averaging 26 to 35 months of lock-in. If clients leave faster than leases can be exited, or if new clients can only be found at lower prices, the operator keeps paying full rent on space that earns less. This is how WeWork Inc., the US parent of the brand, failed: it signed leases at peak rents with a client base heavy in start-ups, and when demand turned it filed for Chapter 11 in November 2023. WeWork India was a separate, Embassy-controlled licensee and was not part of that filing.

The size of the promise

At March 2026 the three members carried lease liabilities of ₹11,238.9 crore: WeWork India ₹5,217.5 crore, Smartworks ₹4,571.4 crore (consolidated) and Awfis ₹1,450 crore. This is the present value of rent they have committed to pay. Their other borrowings were small and covered by cash: WeWork India had gross debt of ₹332.8 crore and cash of ₹344.5 crore (net cash ₹11.7 crore), Smartworks gross debt of ₹207.0 crore and liquid assets of ₹263.1 crore (net cash ₹56.1 crore), and Awfis borrowings of ₹50.9 crore against ₹89.6 crore of cash and bank balances (net cash ₹38.7 crore, per its annual report). Combined net cash excluding leases was ₹106.5 crore. By June 2026 heavy capex had moved the three to roughly zero: WeWork net debt ₹31.6 crore, Smartworks net debt ₹5.6 crore, Awfis net cash ₹45 crore.

Two facts temper the stress test. First, occupancy has rarely fallen that far. WeWork India's portfolio occupancy was 83.8% in FY23, dipped to 76.8% in FY25 during a heavy opening phase, then reached a record 86.9% in March 2026. Even the COVID shock was managed through lease renegotiations rather than mass defaults. Second, the operators have levers. Leases can be renegotiated in a downturn (landlords prefer a reduced rent to an empty floor), managed-office clients are often signed back-to-back with landlord terms so that a client exit can trigger a lease exit, and WeWork's management says that when a managed-office client leaves there is "no cost outlay" because fit-out capex has been recovered within the client's term.

The real test of the hedge came in Q1 FY27 at Awfis. An enterprise client with about 3,000 seats across five centres in three cities consolidated into a conventional office in May 2026, after being acquired by a multinational about 15 months earlier. Awfis chose to keep the five centres and carry the cost while refilling them, taking a hit to H1 FY27 margins on a five-to-seven-year view, and it was simultaneously absorbing five-year rent resets on leases signed in 2021 at COVID-low rents, which it can only pass on to clients with a lag of three to four quarters. Its normal churn is 1.5–2% of inventory a month. A single large exit is manageable for a network of 159,000 seats; a wave of them, in a GCC downturn, would not be.

In plain terms

The model is safe when centres are 80–90% full and clients sign for three years or more. It becomes dangerous only if occupancy falls into the 60s and stays there, because rent keeps running while revenue falls. The operators have built buffers (long client contracts, back-to-back leases, revenue-sharing landlords), and a 10-point fall would roughly halve WeWork's profit rather than wipe it out. A 20-point fall would.

The investor's checklist for the mismatch is therefore short. Track mature and committed occupancy every quarter, not just portfolio occupancy. Track locked-in revenue cover where it is disclosed and ask for it where it is not. Track the share of new capacity that is pre-committed before opening (Smartworks' typical pre-commitment is 30–35% of a new building; Awfis signs its "partial managed office" centres only with at least 50% pre-committed). And watch the size of the not-yet-operational pipeline relative to the operating base, because new centres carry rent before they carry clients: Smartworks' new footprint had committed occupancy of only 42% and lock-in cover of 0.8 times in June 2026.

Rules, tax and ratings: an accounting standard matters more than any regulator

No licence is needed to run a co-working centre. The rules that move the numbers are about lease accounting, GST credits, special economic zones and what a credit agency will tolerate.

Flexible workspace is lightly regulated as an activity. There is no sector regulator, no licence and no price control. What shapes the business is a set of rules written for other purposes. The most important, by a distance, is Ind AS 116, covered in Section 07. One detail is worth adding here: when an operator sub-leases an entire floor to a single managed-office client on terms that transfer most of the risks of the lease, it may classify that sub-lease as a finance lease. The client's payments then come off revenue and are booked as a finance-lease receivable, with interest income below the line. Awfis does this and adds the amount back in its "normalised revenue" (₹12 crore in Q1 FY27); its finance-lease receivables had a present value of ₹358 crore at March 2026. It is one reason Awfis' reported revenue and its normalised revenue differ, and why some of its "other income" is really rent.

Securities rules matter because every member leans on non-GAAP measures. SEBI's disclosure rules allow them in presentations if they are reconciled to the audited numbers, which all three do. Awfis went further in Q1 FY27 and had its cash-EBITDA reconciliation checked by MSKA under an agreed-upon-procedures engagement: not an audit opinion, but more assurance than any peer offers. SEBI also kept WeWork India's first draft prospectus "in abeyance" in 2025 before approving the refiled offer, and the October 2025 prospectus remains the richest single source on the company's licence, related parties and unit economics.

Tax is the regulatory item with real money attached. Co-working fees attract 18% GST, the same rate as commercial renting; the GST rate rationalisation of September 2025 left the rate unchanged. For an enterprise client that can claim input credit, GST is a pass-through. For a small business or a client using the space for exempt activities, it is an 18% surcharge on the seat price, which slightly favours direct leases for those clients. The bigger issue is on the operator's own spend. The CGST Act blocks input credit on goods and services used to construct immovable property. The Supreme Court's October 2024 Safari Retreats judgment opened a route to credit where a building or fixture functions as "plant", and the 2025 Budget amended the law retrospectively to narrow it. Fit-out spend therefore sits in a grey zone; WeWork India's ₹11.01 crore show-cause notice for April 2021 to March 2023 is an example of the tax department testing it.

In plain terms

Nobody licenses co-working, so the rules that matter are tax and accounting. The lease standard changes how profits look; GST rules decide whether the tax paid on furniture and fit-outs can be recovered; and credit-rating agencies set the limits on how much debt and how little occupancy an operator can get away with. None of these has yet hurt the model, but GST on fit-outs is a live argument with the tax department.

Credit ratings are the practical discipline. All three members are rated in the single-A band and their real borrowing is small. WeWork India was upgraded to A+ from A− during FY26 and reported an average cost of borrowing of 8.5%, down from 16.7% in FY24 when it was loss-making. Among the reference peers, IndiQube is rated CRISIL A+ and Table Space was upgraded to ICRA A in July 2026. The Reserve Bank's 25-basis-point increase in the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 matters little to these companies' own borrowing costs, since all three are close to net-debt neutral, but it matters to landlords and REITs, whose valuations and appetite for new building move with rates.

Ten years, four shocks: from start-up amenity to a listed sub-sector

Born in 2015, nearly killed by COVID, rescued by GCCs and listed in a seventeen-month rush. The cycle has never yet been tested by a long office downturn.

The Indian flex industry is barely a decade old, and its history can be told in four phases, each ending in a shock. The birth (2014–2017) produced most of today's brands: Awfis was incorporated in December 2014 and opened its first centre in New Delhi in 2015; IndiQube, Table Space, Innov8 and 91springboard followed in 2015–2016; Smartworks was founded in 2016 by Neetish Sarda and Harsh Binani; and WeWork India Management was incorporated in 2016 as the Embassy group's licence of the WeWork brand, opening its first centres in Bengaluru and Mumbai in 2017. Flex leasing that year was 2.2 million sq ft, about 5% of the market.

The venture boom (2018–2019) brought money and a warning. Sequoia (now Peak XV) and ChrysCapital backed Awfis; OYO bought Innov8. Globally, WeWork's attempted 2019 listing collapsed when investors examined its lease obligations, a preview of the duration risk this report keeps returning to. In April 2019 Ind AS 116 put every operator's leases on its balance sheet.

The COVID shock (2020–2021) emptied offices. Operators renegotiated rents rather than defaulting, and the flex share of leasing never collapsed: CBRE's series shows 8% in 2019 and 12% in 2021, because occupiers who did not want to sign nine-year leases in an uncertain world turned to flexible space. Awfis signed a large cohort of landlord agreements in 2021 at COVID-low rents; those are now reaching their five-year resets, a cost pressure in FY27. The GCC wave (2022–2025) then lifted absorption to successive records and flex's share to 16% in 2023, 18% in 2024 and 21% in 2025. WeWork Inc. filed for Chapter 11 in the US in November 2023 without affecting the Indian licensee.

Reading a flex operator: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about a co-working company. Each is defined differently by each operator, so knowing the trap matters as much as knowing the figure.

A flex operator's profit can be written as one equation. Revenue is seats, times the share of seats occupied, times the monthly price per occupied seat, times twelve, plus ancillary income. Rent-paid EBITDA is that revenue minus rent to landlords, minus the cost of running the centres, minus sales costs and fees, minus corporate overhead. Below it, fit-out depreciation and a little interest give adjusted pre-tax profit. Every KPI in this section is one term of that equation, or a measure of how safe that term is.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). Take a 1,000-desk centre in Bengaluru with about 65,000 sq ft of leasable area. The operator pays rent of ₹95 per sq ft per month, about ₹7.4 crore a year. It sells desks at ₹18,000 a month and fills 85% of them: 850 × ₹18,000 × 12 = ₹18.4 crore of core revenue, plus about 12% more from services and passes, for ₹20.6 crore in total. Running the centre costs about ₹5.4 crore, brokers and marketing about ₹1.2 crore, and the brand fee about ₹0.5 crore. The centre earns about ₹6.1 crore of EBITDA, roughly 30% of revenue. Head-office overhead of about ₹2.1 crore takes the company-level margin to about 19%, and depreciation on a ₹17.6 crore fit-out takes about ₹2.2 crore more. The full anatomy, with sensitivities, is laid out at the end of this section.

In plain terms

A centre makes money in one way: fill desks at a price well above what the landlord charges for the space they occupy. Everything else (power, cleaners, brokers, head office) is a smaller and more predictable cost. So the first two questions about any operator are always "how full are the centres?" and "how much more than the rent are clients paying?"

1. Capacity: desks, seats and members

What it is. The number of workstations an operator can sell in centres that are open. Formula. Operational capacity = sum of sellable desks or seats in operating centres; occupied = seats under contract and billing. Worked example. A 65,000 sq ft premium centre at about 15 desks per 1,000 sq ft holds about 1,000 desks; the same floor fitted out as a dense enterprise campus at 23 per 1,000 sq ft holds about 1,500. What drives it. New centres opened, minus centres exited, plus densification of existing floors.

How the members compare (June 2026). WeWork India had 133,600 operational desks (135,800 including "warm shell" space) and 113,400 members, with a target of about 155,000 desks and 10.3 million sq ft by March 2027. Smartworks had 238,000 operational seats and 193,000 occupied, with capacity for 326,000 seats on its leased area and 381,000 on its total secured area. Awfis had 159,000 operational seats, 170,000 including centres under fit-out and 185,000 signed. Together the members operate about 531,000 desks and seats. Benchmark. There is no "good" level; what matters is that capacity grows no faster than the operator can fill it. The members are adding 20–30% a year.

The trap. "Desks", "seats" and "members" are not the same thing. WeWork counts desks as capacity and members as people; a member on a roaming pass uses no fixed desk. Smartworks' seat counts are based on super built-up area and denser layouts. And capacity can be quoted as operational, under fit-out, signed or "total secured including letters of intent"; the last of these was 16.9 million sq ft at Smartworks against 10.4 million operational. Always use operational capacity for anything to do with revenue or occupancy.

Pipeline conversion. The more useful forward-looking figure is the gap between operational and signed capacity, and how fast it closes. WeWork India's committed pipeline of 43,600 desks (19,500 on signed leases and 24,100 under letters of intent) would take it from 133,600 to 179,400 desks; Smartworks has 6.5 million sq ft secured but not yet operating, more than half its operating base; Awfis has about 26,000 seats signed but not yet open. A pipeline that is large relative to the operating base raises future revenue but also future rent, so read it alongside pre-commitment levels and the occupancy of centres opened in the past year.

2. Operational area and the square-foot trap

What it is. The floor area of centres that are open. Formula. Sum of area in operating centres, on the operator's own area definition. Worked example. A floor of 50,000 sq ft of carpet area might be quoted as about 58,000 sq ft "leasable" and about 70,000 sq ft "super built-up" once shares of lobbies, lift cores and common areas are added (ILLUSTRATIVE loadings; actual loadings vary by building). What drives it. New leases, exits and the area convention used.

How the members compare. WeWork India reports 9.1 million sq ft of operational leasable area (12.0 million including letters of intent); Smartworks 10.4 million sq ft of operational super built-up area (SBA), 9.1 million mature and 8.4 million occupied; Awfis about 8.4 million sq ft of chargeable area on its total supply including centres under fit-out (9.1 million signed). Combined, about 28 million sq ft in mixed units, roughly 24% of India's flex stock. Benchmark. Not a quality measure on its own.

The trap. Super built-up area is the largest of the area measures, so Smartworks' "largest by area" claim is partly a matter of units, and any revenue-per-sq-ft comparison between Smartworks (SBA), WeWork (leasable) and Awfis (chargeable) overstates WeWork's lead by an unknown amount. Never rank operators by area without naming the unit, and never compute a per-sq-ft figure across companies without saying the units differ.

3. Occupancy: portfolio, mature and committed

What it is. The share of operational capacity that is sold. Formula. Occupied seats ÷ operational seats, at a date or as a period average. Worked example. 113,400 WeWork members ÷ 133,600 desks gives roughly 85%, close to the company's reported 84.9% for June 2026. What drives it up. Pre-committed openings, renewals, strong sales; down. Speculative openings that start empty, large client exits, a demand downturn.

In plain terms

4. Price per seat and revenue per square foot

What it is. What a client pays for one occupied seat each month, and what the operator earns from each square foot of operational space. Formulas. Price per seat = seat revenue ÷ average occupied seats ÷ months. Revenue per sq ft = revenue ÷ operational area ÷ months. Worked example. Smartworks' Q1 FY27 lease-rental revenue of ₹477.9 crore ÷ about 192,000 average occupied seats ÷ 3 gives about ₹8,300 per seat per month (ILLUSTRATIVE). WeWork India's ₹687 crore of IGAAP revenue ÷ 9.1 million sq ft ÷ 3 gives about ₹252 per sq ft a month.

What drives it. Location and building grade, brand, product mix (private offices command more than managed floors), contractual escalations of 4–7% a year, and discounting in a soft market. WeWork India says it sold desks without discounting in Q1 FY27; Awfis prices new premium inventory 30–50% above its portfolio. How the members compare. WeWork ~₹18,000 per member per month on core revenue, Awfis ~₹9,700 and Smartworks ~₹8,300 per occupied seat (all ILLUSTRATIVE estimates; Section 06 charts them). WeWork's disclosed net average revenue per member was ₹17,096 (FY23), ₹19,015 (FY24) and ₹19,842 (FY25). Benchmark. Absolute price is a positioning choice, not a quality score; what matters is price relative to rent (the next KPI) and the trend.

The trap. No member publishes a like-for-like price per seat every quarter; analysts derive it, and the derivation depends on whether ancillary revenue, managed-office fit-out recoveries and finance-lease rent are included. Smartworks' mix shift toward large cohorts raised its derived price from about ₹7,600 in FY26 to about ₹8,300 in Q1 FY27 without any like-for-like price rise.

8. Breakeven occupancy

What it is. The occupancy at which a centre's revenue just covers its rent and running costs. Formula. Fixed centre costs ÷ (revenue per occupancy point − variable cost per point). Worked example. The illustrative centre's fixed costs are rent ₹7.4 crore, fixed opex ₹4.5 crore and sales ₹1.2 crore, ₹13.1 crore in all; each occupancy point brings ₹0.242 crore of revenue and ₹0.016 crore of variable cost, so breakeven is 13.1 ÷ 0.225 ≈ 58% (ILLUSTRATIVE). What drives it. Rent relative to price (the revenue-to-rent multiple is its mirror image), opex per sq ft, and density.

How the members compare. WeWork India discloses a portfolio breakeven occupancy of 56.6% (Q1 FY27), against actual occupancy of 84.9%: a cushion of 28 points. Smartworks and Awfis: n/a. For Awfis' MA centres the concept is softer, because the landlord's share falls with revenue. Benchmark. 60% or lower good, 60–70% average, above 70% weak. The trap. The disclosed figure is at centre level, before head-office overhead and fit-out depreciation. At company level, after overhead of about 10% of revenue, the illustrative centre's breakeven rises to about 67%, and the cushion is correspondingly thinner.

9. Fit-out capex per seat and payback

What it is. The operator's own capital spent to turn a bare floor into a sellable centre, and how long the centre takes to earn it back. Formulas. Capex per seat = fit-out capex ÷ seats created; capex per sq ft = fit-out capex ÷ area; payback (years) = fit-out capex ÷ annual centre EBITDA at maturity. Worked example. The illustrative centre costs ₹2,700 per sq ft × 65,000 sq ft = ₹17.6 crore, about ₹1.76 lakh a desk, and earns ₹6.05 crore of centre EBITDA a year at 85% occupancy: payback in about 2.9 years, or 35 months, after the ramp-up. What drives it. Building grade, specification (premium finishes, cafeterias, meeting suites), density, client contributions on managed-office deals, import content and construction inflation (Smartworks budgets about 5% a year).

How the members compare. WeWork India: ₹1,60,648 per desk and ₹2,610 per sq ft in FY23, falling to ₹1,32,665 and ₹2,218 by Q1 FY26 (company KPI). Smartworks: about ₹1,350 per sq ft, roughly ₹59,000 a seat at its density (ILLUSTRATIVE). Awfis: about ₹50,000 a seat of its own capital, because landlords fund much of the fit-out in MA centres. Refurbishment adds to the bill: Smartworks spends about 15% of initial capex every three years; WeWork refreshes branded centres in years six to seven at 5–10% of initial capex. Benchmark. Below ₹1,500 per sq ft good, ₹1,500–2,500 average, above ₹2,500 premium (acceptable only with premium pricing); payback under 30 months good, 30–42 average, over 42 weak. The trap. Smartworks' reported gross block includes ₹633 crore of fit-out cost capitalised on behalf of clients and recovered from them; its "normalised" gross block is ₹1,679 crore against ₹2,306 crore reported. Capex per seat is only comparable once client-funded fit-out is stripped out.

10. Lock-in tenure and locked-in revenue cover

What it is. How long clients are contractually bound, and how much contracted client revenue the operator holds against its contracted rent. Formulas. Weighted average lock-in (months) = Σ(seats × remaining or original lock-in) ÷ Σ seats; locked-in cover = remaining client revenue under lock-in ÷ remaining rent under the operator's own landlord lock-ins. Worked example. A 3,000-seat campus with 2,760 seats sold at ₹8,300 a month on 35-month lock-ins carries about ₹80 crore of contracted revenue; if the head lease has a five-year lock-in at ₹9.4 crore a year, locked-in rent is ₹47 crore and the cover is about 1.7 times at signing (ILLUSTRATIVE), rising as seats fill.

How the members compare. WeWork India 2.9 times (₹3,363 crore against ₹1,152 crore, June 2026), up from 2.4 times; Smartworks about 2.1 times overall, 2.7 times mature, 0.8 times new; Awfis n/a. Lock-ins: Smartworks 35 months (1,000-plus-seat cohort), Awfis 26, WeWork average commitment 27 (33 for large enterprises). Section 08 charts both. Benchmark. Cover of 2.5 times or more good, 1.5–2.5 times average, below 1.5 weak; lock-in of 30 months or more good. The trap. WeWork's ₹3,363 crore is remaining contract value, not 27 months of revenue, and Smartworks' "contracted rental revenue" of about ₹5,400 crore includes periods beyond the lock-in. Compare lock-in with lock-in, and tenure with tenure.

11. Client mix, concentration and seat retention

What it is. Who the clients are, how concentrated revenue is, and how many seats renew. Formulas. Top-10 share = revenue from the ten largest clients ÷ total; enterprise share on each company's definition; seat retention = seats renewed ÷ seats up for renewal (definitions vary). Worked example. If ten clients pay ₹130 crore of a ₹600 crore quarterly core revenue, the top-10 share is about 22%, close to WeWork India's 21.6% in Q1 FY27. What drives it. Deal size (managed offices concentrate revenue), sector strategy and pricing discipline at renewal.

How the members compare. Top-10 share: WeWork 21.6% (23.4% in Q4 FY26), Smartworks 23% (down from 39% in FY19), Awfis n/a (its 500-plus-seat clients are 37% of the portfolio and 1–25-seat clients 28%). Enterprise share: 77%, 92% and 64% on their own definitions. Retention: Smartworks 74% in Q1 FY27, down from 89% in Q4 FY26 and 95% a year earlier, which management calls deliberate churn of clients signed four to five years ago at lower prices; WeWork renewals 84% in Q1 FY27 (78% in FY26), with 52% of new desk sales coming from existing members; Awfis n/a, but it exited about 1,800 seats in Q1 FY27. Benchmark. Top-10 below 20% good, above 30% weak; retention 85% or more good, below 75% weak. The trap. A fall in retention can be good (repricing) or bad (losing clients). Test it against mature committed occupancy: at Smartworks that stayed at 92% while retention fell, which supports management's explanation.

In plain terms

A few very large clients make revenue lumpy but sticky; thousands of small ones make it smooth but restless. The question to ask is not just "who are the clients?" but "what happens when the biggest one leaves?" Awfis found out in Q1 FY27 when one client walked out of five centres at once.

12. Lease liabilities ÷ adjusted EBITDA, and net debt excluding leases

What it is. The sector's two leverage measures: committed rent relative to operating profit, and real financial debt net of cash. Formulas. Lease liabilities (balance sheet) ÷ rent-paid EBITDA; net debt ex-leases = bank and bond borrowings − cash and liquid investments. Worked example. WeWork India: ₹5,217.5 crore ÷ ₹499.2 crore = 10.5 times; net debt = ₹332.8 crore − ₹344.5 crore = −₹11.7 crore, i.e. net cash. What drives it. New leases signed (raise liabilities before EBITDA arrives), lease ageing (liabilities amortise), and capex funded by debt.

How the members compare (FY26 / Mar-26). Lease liabilities ÷ adjusted EBITDA: WeWork 10.5 times, Smartworks 14.5 times, Awfis 6.8 times, all members 10.9 times. Net cash ex-leases: WeWork ₹11.7 crore, Smartworks ₹56.1 crore, Awfis ₹38.7 crore; by June 2026 WeWork had net debt of ₹31.6 crore, Smartworks ₹5.6 crore, and Awfis net cash of ₹45 crore. CRISIL estimates the rated sector's net debt at about 1 times EBITDA. Benchmark. Lease multiple below 8 times good, 8–12 average, above 12 stretched; any net cash ex-leases is good. The trap. Screener's "borrowings" line includes lease liabilities (WeWork ₹5,550 crore, Smartworks ₹4,778 crore, Awfis ₹1,501 crore at March 2026), and WeWork's March 2025 figure excludes them, a series break. Never divide screener borrowings by screener operating profit, and never chart screener borrowings over time for this sector.

13. Normalised operating cash flow and free cash flow after capex

Normalised OCF = reported operating cash flow − lease payments (principal and interest) − upfront brokerage where the company adjusts for it; FCF = normalised OCF − capex. Worked example. WeWork India FY26: free cash from operations of ₹585.5 crore less capex of ₹456.5 crore = ₹129.0 crore of free cash flow to the firm. What drives it. Rent-paid EBITDA, working capital (security deposits paid to landlords, client deposits received, upfront brokerage), and the expansion pace.

14. Ancillary revenue share

What it is. Revenue from anything other than the seat licence: services, digital passes, events, food, parking, design-and-build. Formula. Non-seat revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example. WeWork India's Q1 FY27 VAS of ₹57.5 crore plus digital ₹26.2 crore on ₹687 crore of revenue is 12.2%. What drives it. Occupied seats (services follow members), product development, and one-offs such as customisation income and termination fees.

How the members compare. WeWork 12.2% in Q1 FY27 (VAS 8.4%, digital 3.8%), guided to 11–12% VAS plus 3–4% digital; Smartworks' other operating revenue 12.5% in Q1 FY27 and 11% in FY26, up 139% in FY26, but including termination charges; Awfis' Transform 17.2% of Q1 FY27 revenue, a contracting business rather than an ancillary layer on seats. IndiQube (reference) reports 17% VAS. Benchmark. 10–15% of genuinely recurring services is good; the margin matters more than the share (digital at about 80% EBITDA margin per WeWork management; Transform at 17–18% gross). The trap. Lumpy items flatter growth: WeWork's ₹47 crore of customisation income in Q4 FY26 against ₹9.5 crore in Q1 FY27, and Smartworks' unsplit termination income. Ask what share is recurring.

Definition traps, collected

  • Three EBITDAs. Reported Ind AS EBITDA includes rent; each company's adjusted measure removes it differently (WeWork accrual IGAAP, Smartworks lease cash paid, Awfis normalised then cash). Never add or rank them without saying so.
  • Three PATs. Ind AS PAT is depressed for growing operators and can be lifted by deferred-tax credits (WeWork FY25) or other income (Awfis Q1 FY27).
  • "Borrowings" are mostly leases. Use net debt excluding leases for leverage; show lease liabilities separately.
  • Desks, seats and members differ, and so do capacity stages (operational, under fit-out, signed, including LOIs).
  • Leasable, super built-up and chargeable area differ. Name the unit; avoid cross-company per-sq-ft rankings without a caveat.
  • Four occupancies. Portfolio, mature, committed and committed-mature; each company defines "mature" slightly differently.
  • Lock-in is not tenure, client lock-in is not landlord lock-in, and remaining contract value is not annual revenue.
  • Revenue includes lumpy items: customisation income, termination charges, design-and-build contracts, finance-lease rent moved to other income.
  • WeWork India is not WeWork Inc. The US company's bankruptcy history is not the Indian licensee's.
  • Consultants measure differently. CBRE, Colliers, JLL and Cushman & Wakefield give flex shares from 16% to 28% for the same period; calendar-year market data must not be mixed with fiscal-year company data.
In plain terms

Almost every number in this sector comes with a hidden choice: which EBITDA, which occupancy, which square foot, which consultant. The safe habit is to compare each company only with its own history on its own definition, and to name the definition every time you compare across companies.

How to read a flex operator's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its mix (1 minute). Pull revenue from operations and its growth, then split it into seat revenue and the rest. Red flag: headline growth well above seat-revenue growth (Smartworks' 44% headline vs 34% lease-rental growth in Q1 FY27).
  2. Operational capacity and openings (1 minute). Desks or seats operational, added and exited in the quarter. Red flag: large exits not explained.
  3. Occupancy, portfolio and mature (1 minute). Compare with last quarter and last year. Red flag: mature occupancy falling, which cannot be blamed on new centres.
  4. The company's adjusted EBITDA and margin (1 minute). On its own definition; check whether the definition changed. Red flag: margin down without a burst of openings to explain it.
  5. Cash rent paid (1 minute). From the cash-flow statement; divide by seat revenue. Red flag: rent growing faster than seat revenue (Awfis: lease cash +53% vs co-working revenue +27% in Q1 FY27).
  6. Revenue-to-rent and centre margin, where disclosed (1 minute). Red flag: multiple slipping below about 2.5 times.
  7. Contracted cover and lock-in (1 minute). Locked-in revenue ÷ locked-in rent; average lock-in. Red flag: cover falling while capacity grows.
  8. Reported vs adjusted PAT (1 minute). Reconcile the gap to lease accounting, deferred tax and other income. Red flag: reported profit exceeding operating profit because of other income.
  9. Net debt ex-leases and capex (1 minute). Capex against guidance; real debt against cash. Red flag: real debt rising to fund openings.
  10. Guidance and the pipeline (1 minute). Capacity targets, signed-but-not-open area, pre-commitment levels. Red flag: pipeline growing far faster than mature occupancy can absorb.

The global lens: India grows while the world's leader turns into a franchisor

IWG grows 4% a year and pivots to fee-earning contracts. WeWork Inc. went bankrupt. India's operators grow 25–45% a year on leases. Both lessons apply.

The global industry offers two cautionary tales and one template. The first tale is WeWork Inc., which signed long leases at peak rents around the world, filled them heavily with start-ups, failed to list in 2019 and filed for Chapter 11 in November 2023; it emerged in 2024 under the control of Yardi, a property-software company. WeWork India, a licensee controlled by Embassy, was not part of the bankruptcy and is a different business: its leases were signed at Indian market rents, its clients are mostly large enterprises, and it is profitable on a rent-paid basis. But it still depends on the post-bankruptcy WeWork for its brand.

The template is IWG plc, the London-listed owner of Regus, Spaces, HQ and Signature and the world's largest operator. In 2025 it reported system-wide revenue of US$4,453 million (+4%), group revenue of US$3,762 million and adjusted EBITDA of US$531 million (+6%), with 4,609 open centres and 5,979 including its pipeline. Its company-owned revenue fell 1%; its managed and franchised system revenue grew 28% to US$876 million across 1,891 locations. IWG is turning itself into a franchisor that earns fees from landlords and partners, the "hotel model", because fee income needs no fit-out capital and carries no lease. Its net debt of US$715 million, 1.35 times EBITDA, is quoted excluding IFRS 16 lease liabilities, the same convention as the Indian "net cash excluding leases". In India IWG operates 125 centres in 37 cities through franchise and managed partnerships.

AI, hybrid work and disruption: fewer seats per job, more jobs in flex?

The central long-term question is whether artificial intelligence shrinks the white-collar headcount that fills Indian offices. Nobody knows the answer yet; the operators' own research is advocacy.

The flex industry sells seats, and seats are bought for people. Anything that changes how many people a company needs in an office in India changes demand. The largest such force is AI. The IT Services report documents tier-1 Indian vendors hiring fewer junior engineers and using AI tools to compress the traditional staffing pyramid. If the same happens inside GCCs, the 42% of office absorption they now account for could slow sharply. The early evidence is already visible in the operators' client mix: WeWork India's desk sales to IT services and consulting firms fell 43% in FY26, and Smartworks' IT and IT-enabled-services share fell from about 44% of its portfolio in FY24 to about 35% in Q1 FY27.

The operators argue the opposite. A 2026 Redseer study co-sponsored by WeWork India and Smartworks projects GCC AI headcount rising from about 181,000 in 2025 to about 730,000 by 2030 and some 79 million sq ft of net-new AI-led office demand by 2030. Press reports of Embassy Office Parks REIT's Q1 FY27 leasing said 21% came from AI firms. These figures may prove right, but they come from parties with an interest in the conclusion and should be treated as advocacy until independent data confirm them. The fair summary is that AI shifts the mix of office demand (toward engineering, data and product roles, and toward GCCs over outsourcers) while its effect on the total number of seats is unknown.

Hybrid work is the second force, and it cuts both ways. Companies that let staff work from home two or three days a week need fewer desks per employee (desk-sharing ratios of 0.7–0.8 are common, an indicative range), which reduces total demand. But hybrid work also raises demand for exactly what flex sells: well-located, amenity-rich space that can be scaled up and down, and roaming access across cities. WeWork's "All Access" pass and digital products, which earn high margins on spare capacity, are a direct monetisation of hybrid patterns.

The third force is landlords. A REIT or developer can fit out floors itself and offer "plug-and-play" space, cutting the operator out. Embassy (through WeWork India), RMZ (CoWrks) and others already own flex brands. The defence the operators offer is that landlords want long commitments and dislike operating risk, which is true at today's vacancy levels; if vacancy keeps rising from Cushman & Wakefield's 14.4% in Q3 CY2026, more landlords will try. A fourth, smaller force is proptech and fractional ownership. Space-booking, access-control and building-management software (WeWork's resale of Yardi's Workplace software, Smartworks' WorkCtrl, Nexus and WorkBlock platforms, the Awfis app) is mostly a cost and experience lever rather than a disruptor. Fractional and SM-REIT platforms could compete for small occupiers but are not yet material.

In plain terms

If AI means a multinational needs 800 engineers in Bengaluru instead of 1,000, it needs fewer desks, and flex operators feel that first because their contracts are short. The operators say AI will create more jobs than it removes, but their evidence comes from studies they paid for. On the data available today, the mix of clients is changing fast and the total is not yet known.

Bull vs bear: a structural share gain or a cyclical peak with leases attached?

Both cases rest on the same facts: record demand, fast growth and ₹11,239 crore of committed rent. The disagreement is about what happens when the cycle turns.

The bull case

  • Structural share gain: flex rose from 5% of leasing in 2017 to 21% in 2025 and 27% in Q2 CY2026 (CBRE), and gained share even through COVID. Occupiers' flex allocation is moving from 5–10% to 15–20% of portfolios.
  • The GCC engine: about 2,117 GCCs, 100-plus new ones a year, 42% of 9M CY2026 absorption; US tariffs and visa costs push more work offshore to India.
  • Operating leverage: the leased-model operators grew adjusted EBITDA about 71% in Q1 FY27 on 35% revenue growth; Smartworks' margin has risen five quarters running.
  • Protected revenue: locked-in client revenue covers locked-in rent 2.9× at WeWork and ~2.1× at Smartworks; breakeven occupancy at WeWork is 56.6% against 84.9% actual.
  • Consolidation: the top ten take 74% of flex leasing (C&W, H1 2026), and large deals are doubling; scale players win enterprise mandates.
  • Clean balance sheets: all three were net cash excluding leases at Mar-26, and growth has been largely self-funded.

The bear case

  • Duration mismatch: ₹11,239 Cr of lease liabilities, 10.9× combined adjusted EBITDA, against client lock-ins of 26–35 months. A 10-point occupancy fall would roughly halve WeWork's rent-paid EBITDA (illustrative).
  • Concentrated demand: 65% of WeWork's core revenue is from foreign clients and ~46% from North America; a US downturn or AI-driven headcount cuts at GCCs would hit quickly.
  • Supply wave: flex stock growing ~20% a year, record office completions, vacancy rising in Q3 CY2026; Hyderabad, NCR and Pune vacancies at 19–21%.
  • Capex outruns profit: FY27 capex guidance of ₹1,250–1,410 Cr (illustrative sum) against FY26 adjusted EBITDA of ₹1,027 Cr; free cash flow thin or negative.
  • Accounting fog: three EBITDA definitions, lumpy termination and customisation income, other income propping up reported profit at Awfis.
  • Governance and supply of shares: Embassy's pledge and related-party payments, 1 Ariel Way's 15.17%, a 14.9% Singapore holder at Smartworks, sponsor exits at Awfis, and new listings (Table Space, Executive Centre).

Weighing the two, the evidence of FY26 and Q1 FY27 favours the bull case on demand and on the leased-model operators' execution: occupancy is high, margins are rising, contract cover is improving and nobody is borrowing heavily. The bear case is strongest on two points that the current data cannot settle. One is the cycle: the sector has never been tested by a prolonged office downturn with leases this large, and its clients are concentrated in exactly the industries (foreign technology and finance) that cut costs first. The other is valuation: at 16–18 times rent-paid EBITDA, WeWork India and Smartworks are priced for the share gains to continue, while Awfis at about 7 times is priced for its margin squeeze to persist.

The swing factor between the two cases is occupancy in new centres. If the 20,000-plus seats WeWork opened between July and October 2026, and the 3 million sq ft Smartworks is bringing online in the next nine months, fill at the 13–14-month pace management describes, the operating leverage continues and the bull case holds. If they fill slowly in a softer market, the fixed rent on those centres becomes the bear case in real time.

In plain terms

The optimist says companies have permanently changed how they rent offices and India's co-working leaders are the main beneficiaries. The pessimist says this is the top of a hot cycle and the operators have signed nine-year leases to serve three-year customers. Both are partly right. The next year of occupancy data in the new centres will show which part matters more.
