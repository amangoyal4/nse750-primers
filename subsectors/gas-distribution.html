City gas & LNG: cheap gas fades, the tolls cash in

A complete guide to the five NSE 750 companies that move gas to Indian cars, kitchens, factories and fertiliser plants: how the city-gas utilities earn a few rupees on every cubic metre, why the government's shrinking allocation of cheap domestic gas and the 2026 West Asia war crushed those rupees while the LNG and LPG terminal operators booked record profits, which numbers tell you who is winning, and what the market is (and is not) paying for.

00

The one-page view: one molecule, three ways to earn, two opposite years

The city-gas utilities sell gas the government no longer rations generously. The terminal operators charge a toll on gas and LPG that now has to come from further away. 2026 rewarded the second and punished the first.

This report covers five listed companies that sit on different links of India's gas chain. Indraprastha Gas (IGL), Mahanagar Gas (MGL) and Adani Total Gas (ATGL) are city gas distribution (CGD) utilities: they hold exclusive licences to lay pipes in a city or district, buy gas, and sell it as compressed natural gas (CNG) for vehicles and as piped natural gas (PNG) for homes, shops and factories. Petronet LNG runs India's largest liquefied natural gas (LNG) import terminals at Dahej in Gujarat and Kochi in Kerala, where ships unload super-cooled gas that is warmed back into vapour and sent into the national pipeline grid. Aegis Logistics owns port tanks for liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) and liquid chemicals, and also buys and distributes LPG to industry, commercial users and auto-LPG pumps.

Put together, the five earned net revenue of ₹82,136 crore in FY26, operating profit before other income (EBITDA in this report) of ₹11,307 crore, and profit attributable to shareholders of ₹7,852 crore. On 21 October 2026 the market valued them at ₹1.83 lakh crore. Those totals hide more than they reveal. Petronet's revenue is mostly the value of the gas it passes through, so it is 53% of the group's revenue but earns a toll worth only about ₹2.7 per cubic metre. ATGL is 7.2% of the group's revenue and 8.4% of its profit, yet 33.7% of its market value. The two metro utilities, IGL and MGL, together earn more than twice ATGL's profit and are worth half as much.

The year that matters most for understanding this sub-sector is the one that has just happened. In the June quarter of 2026 (Q1 FY27, the latest reported for all five), the three city-gas utilities sold 7.5% more gas than a year earlier and lifted their combined revenue 18%, yet their combined EBITDA fell 30.5%, from ₹1,304 crore to ₹906 crore. The two terminal operators saw revenue fall 42% and EBITDA rise 61%, from ₹1,399 crore to ₹2,249 crore. A sector total ("EBITDA up 17%") is an artefact of adding two businesses that moved in opposite directions, which is why every aggregate in this report is shown by subgroup.

Five forces explain almost everything in this report, and the report is built around them.

  • The cheap-gas squeeze (section 08). Gas from old state-owned fields is sold to city-gas companies under the administered pricing mechanism (APM) at a government ceiling of $7.00 per million British thermal units (mmbtu), when the formula price for October 2026 is $11.22. That gas is running out at 9–10% a year, so the government keeps cutting how much of it the utilities get. APM fell from about 51% of the sector's priority need to 34% in April 2025, and it covered only about 27–30% of IGL's and MGL's Q1 FY27 volume. Every cut forces them to buy dearer gas.
  • CNG against electric vehicles and against petrol (section 09). CNG has gone from about half the price of petrol per unit in 2021 to 85% of it in Delhi today, while electric three-wheelers took 55.8% of passenger-auto sales in September 2026. The threat is real but slower than headlines suggest, because autos are only 7–8% of IGL's CNG.
  • The 2026 West Asia war (section 10). Strikes on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex and disruption in the Strait of Hormuz cut India's biggest LNG supplier, pushed Indian spot gas to $28.4/mmbtu in September and forced the utilities to buy expensive cargoes. Petronet lost most of its Qatar volume and still posted a record quarter.
  • LPG and liquid terminals (section 11). India imports 61% of its LPG. When Gulf supply was rationed, Aegis's distribution business earned a windfall that management itself calls three times normal.
  • Exclusivity, build-out and valuation (section 12). Each city-gas licence is a local monopoly, but the regulator has twice signalled that it could open the oldest networks to rivals. The market pays about 94 times FY26 earnings for ATGL's long build-out and about 12–13 times for IGL and MGL's mature cities.

In plain terms

A city-gas company is a shop that buys gas wholesale and sells it retail through pipes it alone may lay. Its profit is the gap between the two prices, a few rupees on every cubic metre. For fifteen years the government sold it cheap wholesale gas, so the gap was wide. That cheap gas is running out, and 2026's war made the replacement gas expensive. The terminal companies are different: they are paid a fixed fee to unload and store fuel that someone else owns, and when supply is chaotic their fees hold up and their trading desks profit. That is why the same headline year was a disaster for one group and a record for the other.

Three facts set the tone for the rest of the report. First, CGD volume is still growing strongly: national city-gas sales reached 47 million standard cubic metres a day (mmscmd) in FY26, up from 32 in FY22, and CNG cars took 22% of passenger-vehicle sales. Growth is not the problem; the price of the molecule is. Second, the terminals' 2026 profits contain one-offs that will not repeat in the same form: Petronet's Q1 FY27 included ₹494 crore of trading and inventory gains, and its Q4 FY26 included ₹630 crore of overdue 2022 use-or-pay dues. Third, valuation dispersion is wider than in almost any other part of Indian energy, so which company you own matters more than whether you own the sector. The next quarter will test all of this: ATGL reports Q2 FY27 on 22 October, Aegis on 26 October and Petronet on 2 November; IGL and MGL have not announced dates.

01

How a molecule earns: the chain from gas field to gas stove

Gas passes through six owners before it reaches a car or a kitchen. The members own three of those links, and the money per unit is very different on each.

Natural gas is mostly methane. India produces about half of what it uses, from old fields run by ONGC and Oil India (priced under APM), from deepwater and high-pressure fields such as Reliance-BP's KG-D6 (priced under a separate "HPHT" ceiling), and from smaller private blocks. The other half arrives as LNG: gas cooled to −162°C so that it shrinks about 600 times and can be shipped. Once on land, gas moves through a common-carrier trunk pipeline network of 27,598 km, of which GAIL owns about 68%. At each city the CGD company takes the gas at a "city gate" station, reduces its pressure, and runs it through steel and then polyethylene pipes to CNG stations and household meters.

The striking feature of the chain is that the link with the least capital per unit earns the most per unit. A metro CGD company sells gas at a net realisation of ₹52–55 per scm and keeps ₹3.4–9 of it as EBITDA, while the terminal that unloaded the same gas earned about ₹2.7 per scm and the pipeline that carried it about ₹2.1. In normal years the city network earns two to three times the terminal's per-unit fee. In Q1 FY27 IGL's ₹3.4 was only about 1.25 times Dahej's fee, a measure of how badly the squeeze bit. The reason the last mile can earn more is local monopoly plus the customer's alternative: a car owner compares CNG with petrol, not with the wholesale price of gas.

Aegis runs a parallel chain for LPG. A very large gas carrier (VLGC) brings propane and butane from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar or, increasingly, the US to a port terminal at Kandla, Pipavav, Mumbai, Mangaluru or Haldia. From the tanks the product goes by rail, road or pipeline to the bottling plants of the oil marketing companies, which supply India's 32.7 crore household LPG customers, or into Aegis's own distribution business, which sells bulk LPG to factories, cylinders to commercial users and auto-LPG at pumps. The terminal earns a fee on every tonne that passes through whoever owns the product; the distribution arm earns a spread over the Saudi contract price.

In plain terms

Three businesses touch the same family of fuels. The city-gas company is a retailer with an exclusive shopfront. The LNG terminal is a toll gate at the port, paid per unit whether the fuel is cheap or dear. The LPG terminal is the same toll gate for a different fuel, with a trading shop attached. Retailers live on the spread between buying and selling prices; toll gates live on volume and contract length. That difference explains why the same price shock helps one and hurts the other.

02

Size, growth and history: a 188 mmscmd market that refuses to grow, with one segment that will not stop

India's total gas use has been stuck near 65–71 billion cubic metres for five years. City gas has grown 9.5% a year inside that flat total.

India consumed 68,753 million scm of natural gas in FY26, about 188 mmscmd, down 3.6% from a record 71,314 in FY25. Gross domestic production was 34,776 million scm and LNG imports 34,427 million scm (26.0 million tonnes), so imports met 50.1% of demand for the second year running. Gas is only about 6–6.5% of India's primary energy, against a world average near 23–24%, and the government's target of 15% by 2030 will be missed by a wide margin on any reasonable trajectory: total consumption in April–September 2026 was 34,769 million scm, flat on a year earlier.

The table carries the single most useful lesson about Indian gas demand: it is very sensitive to price. When the average landed price of LNG jumped to $16.4/mmbtu in FY23, after the 2022 price shock, imports fell to 19.9 million tonnes and consumption dropped 6.5%. When the price came back to $10.6 in FY25, imports recovered to a record 27.0 million tonnes. The users who walk away when LNG is dear are power plants, refineries, petrochemicals and industry, which can burn coal, naphtha or fuel oil instead. The users who stay are fertiliser plants (whose gas cost is effectively subsidised), household kitchens and CNG vehicles. This is why the April–September 2026 numbers look odd at first sight: in the worst gas-supply shock in a generation, LNG import volume actually rose 4.5%, because the priority users kept buying at higher prices while price-sensitive users cut back. The import bill rose 23% in dollars, from $6.9 billion to $8.5 billion.

Inside that flat total, city gas is the only large segment that grows every year. In rupee terms the city-gas retail market is worth roughly ₹85,000 crore a year before taxes (17,080 million scm at about ₹50/scm of net realisation), and about ₹1.0–1.05 lakh crore including excise and state VAT. The LNG import bill was $13.4 billion in FY26; at the April–September 2026 run-rate it is heading for about $17 billion. Regasification services nationally are worth roughly ₹8,200 crore a year of toll-equivalent revenue (about 1,366 trillion BTU at ~₹60/mmbtu). India consumed 33.2 million tonnes of LPG in FY26 and imported 21.3 million tonnes of it, at a cost of $11.3 billion (₹99,149 crore). All of these rupee sizes are approximate and are labelled so; the volumes are PPAC's.

The history of the sub-sector is a history of the state deciding who gets cheap gas: court-ordered CNG in Delhi from 1998, fifteen years of cheap APM gas that let metro utilities earn ₹6–9 per scm, a licence land-grab in 2018–2022, and since 2022 a run of shocks. Section 13 sets this out as a timeline.

In plain terms

India's gas market is the size of a medium European country's and has not grown for five years, because imported gas is too expensive for power stations and factories that can burn coal or oil instead. City gas is the exception: car owners and households pay for it because their alternatives (petrol and LPG cylinders) cost more. So the listed city-gas companies are growing inside a market that is not, and their volume story is sound. Their problem lies in what they pay for the gas, which is the subject of section 08.

03

Who burns the gas: cars and kitchens pay, factories bargain, fertiliser takes the LNG

The members' demand comes from four very different customers, each with its own price ceiling and its own reason to switch away.

City gas has become a transport business. CNG was 47% of CGD volume in FY22 and 63% in FY26, as CNG volume compounded at 17.9% a year while industrial PNG shrank 2.3% a year. The metros are even more CNG-heavy: CNG was 74% of IGL's Q1 FY27 volume (5.31 million kg a day, about 7.2 mmscmd of 9.66), 73.3% of MGL's (3.496 of 4.766 mmscmd) and about 72% of ATGL's standalone volume (218 of 303 million scm). The driver is a running-cost gap rather than policy: a CNG hatchback in Delhi costs about ₹3.48 per km to fuel against about ₹6.01 for a petrol one, a saving of roughly 42% even after 2026's price increases (section 09 works through the numbers).

Household PNG is small in volume but large in politics. National PNG-D volume was only 1,305 million scm in FY26 (about 3.6 mmscmd, 8% of CGD) spread over 160–177 lakh connections, which works out to about 0.22 scm per connection per day; ATGL's management puts a typical active home at 0.38–0.40 scm a day, which tells you that many connections are not burning gas at all. MGL has about 33 lakh connected homes but only about 23 lakh consuming ones. Margins on household gas are thin because its price is politically sticky, but it matters for two reasons. It secures the highest priority for APM allocation, and since the 2026 LPG shock it has been the government's preferred replacement for imported cooking gas: a new incentive scheme from 1 September 2026 gives each CGD company an extra 200 scm of APM gas for every incremental billed household connection above a threshold, and the government says this cuts the payback on a household connection from about ten years to three. Delhi PNG at ₹50.59/scm is cheaper per unit of heat than the ₹942 domestic LPG cylinder (₹66/kg), because one scm of gas carries roughly the heat of 0.9 kg of LPG.

Industrial and commercial PNG is the price-taker. National industrial CGD volume was 4,668 million scm in FY26 (27% of CGD), down from 5,124 in FY22, and it collapsed in FY23 when LNG spiked. These customers can burn propane, LPG, fuel oil, low-sulphur heavy stock, pet-coke or coal, so their gas is priced against those fuels and gets no APM. That cuts both ways. Under the March 2026 emergency order they were capped at 80% of their six-month average use, and MGL's industrial and commercial volume fell 9.9% quarter on quarter. But because their prices track alternative fuels, which tracked crude, MGL's industrial and commercial realisations rose 70–80% year on year in Q1 FY27. The extreme case is Morbi, the ceramics cluster in Gujarat, whose demand swung from about 0.4 mmscmd to 8 mmscmd and back to about 3 within a few months in 2026 as propane supply came and went. Industrial gas is a swing load.

Petronet's demand is the country's LNG demand, and the chart above shows who that is. Fertiliser plants take almost half, because India's urea and complex-fertiliser makers run on imported gas and their selling prices are set by the government (the gas cost is effectively passed to the subsidy bill). City gas takes about 6,260 million scm, 38% of its own supply in FY26 and 45% by August 2026 as domestic allocation shrank. Refineries, power plants and petrochemical units take the rest, and they are the elastic ones: power plants burned 10.5% less gas in FY26 and run on imported gas only when it is cheaper than coal power, which is rarely (the power generation report covers this). Petronet's utilisation is therefore high when landed LNG is below about $10–11/mmbtu and low above about $14–15.

Aegis's demand is LPG demand, which until 2026 was a steady import story. Consumption rose from 27.5 million tonnes in FY21 to 33.2 million tonnes in FY26 while domestic production stayed near 12–13 million tonnes, so imports climbed from 16.5 to 21.5 million tonnes and import dependence from 56% to 61%. Then the war broke the series. In April–September 2026, LPG consumption fell 15.5% to 13.70 million tonnes, bulk LPG sales fell 61.8%, and active domestic customers fell 1.2% to 3,267 lakh, the first decline on record; refiners were told to maximise LPG output, which rose 25%. For a terminal owner the lesson is that throughput held up because the product still had to be imported, from further away. For a private distributor the lesson is that rationed supply from the state companies sent commercial and industrial buyers to whoever had product in tanks.

In plain terms

The members serve customers with very different bargaining positions. Car owners and households cannot easily switch, so they absorb price increases up to the point where petrol or an LPG cylinder becomes cheaper. Factories switch the moment propane is cheaper, so their volume swings. Fertiliser plants take LNG whatever it costs, because the government pays. Knowing which customer a company depends on tells you how much of a cost shock it can pass on.

04

Where ₹100 goes: a gas retailer keeps eleven, a toll gate keeps twelve, for very different reasons

The members' cost structures look similar in percentage terms and are nothing alike underneath. Gas cost is two-thirds of a city-gas bill; the LNG itself is six-sevenths of Petronet's.

A city-gas company's single largest cost is the gas it buys, and that gas is a blend of four very differently priced sources. APM gas from ONGC and Oil India's old nomination fields costs the ceiling of $7.00/mmbtu, about ₹26.4 per scm at ₹94.9 to the dollar. Gas from new wells in those same fields (new-well gas, NWG) is priced at 12% of the Indian crude basket, which at September 2026's $116 basket is about $13.9, or ₹52 per scm. On top come the pipeline tariff (₹2.14 per scm in Zone 1), compression electricity for CNG, the dealer commission paid to oil companies' petrol pumps that sell CNG, and the network's own staff and maintenance.

Two features of this cost structure drive the rest of the report. Gas cost is set almost entirely outside the company, by a government ceiling and allocation, by crude, Henry Hub, spot LNG and the rupee; management controls only what it signs and what it charges. And because fixed costs are small relative to gas cost, a ₹3–4 per scm swing in the blended gas price, about 8% of IGL's gas cost, can halve EBITDA per scm. In normal years treasury income cushions this: IGL's FY26 other income of ₹661 crore (including joint-venture profits) equalled 35% of its EBITDA.

In plain terms

Two-thirds of a city-gas bill is the gas itself, and the company does not set that price. A difference of a few rupees per cubic metre in what it pays, on a margin of only five to nine rupees, is the difference between a good year and a bad one. Petronet's bill is almost all LNG it passes on at cost, so its revenue line is noise; the fee it earns on top is what matters. Aegis is mostly an LPG trader by revenue and mostly a terminal owner by profit.

The rupee belongs in this section because every molecule the members touch is priced in dollars. APM and HPHT ceilings are dollar figures, new-well gas is a percentage of a dollar crude basket, and LNG is priced off Brent, Henry Hub or spot. From the FY25 average of ₹84.57 to ₹94.9 today the rupee has fallen about 11%, which alone raises rupee gas cost by about 12% with no change in dollar prices: APM at $7 cost ₹23.4 per scm at the FY25 rate and ₹26.4 today. On IGL's blended ₹43.3, each ₹1 of rupee depreciation adds roughly ₹0.45 per scm, worth about ₹150–160 crore a year of EBITDA at its current volume (an illustrative sensitivity). ATGL explicitly cited "currency depreciation" for its 39% rise in rupee gas cost in Q1 FY27.

Industry structure: 307 local monopolies, eight terminals, and a few owners of port tanks

City gas is fragmented by area and concentrated by volume. LNG regasification is concentrated in one company. LPG tankage is mostly owned by the state oil companies, with Aegis the largest independent.

PNGRB, the downstream regulator, has authorised 307 geographical areas (GAs) for city gas, which together cover close to the whole country. Each GA is a local monopoly for laying the network: the licence-holder has 25 years of infrastructure exclusivity, plus a shorter period (five years for older GAs, eight for the 9th–11th bidding rounds) of exclusive right to sell gas within it. The legacy metro and state networks still carry most of the volume. Gujarat, Maharashtra, Delhi and Uttar Pradesh together hold 74% of India's household PNG connections (13.0 of 17.7 million) and 45% of its CNG stations (4,094 of 9,158). The three members alone hold about 24% of the country's CNG stations and about 45% of its household connections.

In plain terms

Every city-gas company is a monopoly in its own towns, so the industry's competition is mostly about who won which towns in the licence auctions and how fast they build. The LNG terminal business is a near-duopoly of location: Petronet's Dahej is the best-connected site on the coast and runs full in normal years, while newer terminals sit half-empty. LPG tanks at ports are scarce because ports, permits and rail links are scarce, which is what gives Aegis its pricing power.

Five business models under one ticker group

The members fall into five archetypes with different revenue drivers, capital needs and risks. Mixing them in one average produces numbers that describe no real company.

The first archetype is the mature metro utility: IGL in Delhi-NCR and MGL in Mumbai. Their networks are dense and largely built; volume grows 5–9% a year, mostly from CNG; and margin depends on the gas-buying book, on how quickly price increases are passed through, and on opex density. They generate cash, pay dividends of about 3% and sit on large treasury balances, but capital spending is rising again (₹1,800–2,000 crore at IGL and ₹1,500–1,800 crore at MGL in FY27) as they push into newer GAs and household connections. Their risks are the APM squeeze, electric substitution in high-usage fleets and, at the edge, open access.

The second is the growth utility building new GAs: ATGL among the listed members, and Torrent, Think Gas, AG&P, Megha and IRM in the wider industry. Volume grows 12–20% a year from a low base; margins depend on spreading fixed network costs over more customers and on industrial and commercial pricing; capital intensity is high (ATGL spent ₹903 crore in FY26), and free cash flow is thin (ATGL's was ₹161 crore in FY26). Risks are slow ramp-ups, penalties for missing the minimum work programme promised in the licence bid, the cost of capital and sponsor risk.

The third is the regasification toll terminal, Petronet: throughput times tariff, plus use-or-pay, its own Qatar margin and occasional trading gains, now with a ₹20,685 crore petrochemical complex that changes its risk profile. The fourth and fifth are Aegis's port tank storage, an annuity on capacity and throughput, largely inside AVTL, and its LPG sourcing and distribution arm, which earns a spread per tonne over the Saudi contract price on mostly working capital and carries price, inventory and sourcing-integrity risk (the Adani Enterprises OFAC settlement of May 2026 concerned Iran-origin LPG; ATGL was not a party).

The rulebook: who sets the price, who allocates the gas, who taxes it

The members' economics are written by a ministry, a price agency, a regulator, state governments and courts. Reading them means reading that rulebook first.

Five sets of rules matter. The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas (MoPNG) decides who gets domestic gas (its 10 August 2022 guideline gives city-gas CNG and household PNG priority for APM gas "only up to the quantity available"), sets the pricing formulas for APM (7 April 2023 notification) and HPHT gas (21 March 2016), and issues emergency orders such as the Natural Gas (Supply Regulation) Order of 9–10 March 2026. PPAC, its price and analysis cell, notifies the monthly APM price and the half-yearly HPHT ceiling and publishes the data this report relies on. PNGRB authorises GAs and pipelines, sets pipeline and network tariffs (including the unified tariff), minimum work programmes, exclusivity and open-access rules, and safety standards; since the Supreme Court's 2015 ruling in IGL v PNGRB it does not set retail CNG or PNG prices. GAIL implements APM allocation and the war-time priority order. State governments levy VAT on gas (5% on CNG in Delhi; 14–15% in states such as Gujarat, UP and Madhya Pradesh) and write the EV policies that compete with CNG.

The unified pipeline tariff is the most recent structural change. Since 1 April 2023 most trunk pipelines have charged a single national tariff by distance band rather than pipeline by pipeline. From 1 January 2026 PNGRB cut the bands to two: ₹54.00/mmbtu up to 300 km and ₹102.86/mmbtu beyond, and it ruled that CNG and household PNG are always billed at the Zone-1 rate wherever they are delivered. PNGRB estimated the saving at about ₹1,000 crore a year, cutting delivered prices by ₹1.25–2.50 per kg of CNG and ₹0.90–1.80 per scm of household PNG. The winners are networks far from the gas source, such as ATGL's GAs in UP, Rajasthan and Odisha, Torrent and Think Gas; Delhi and Mumbai, close to the trunk system, gain little. An 8 October 2026 draft would bring all remaining pipelines (about 10%) into the scheme; comments closed on 23 October.

Taxes shape the competitive position of CNG. Natural gas is outside GST: the law says the GST Council "shall recommend the date" on which GST applies to crude, diesel, petrol, natural gas and jet fuel, and it never has. So CNG bears central excise of 14% plus state VAT, and city-gas companies cannot claim input tax credit on the GST they pay on pipes, compressors and contracts, which we estimate strands ₹100–200 crore a year for a large utility. Bringing gas into GST at a low rate with full credit would help the companies, and a committee reportedly recommended it in April 2026, but states would lose VAT revenue and IGL's managing director said in August that there was "no available evidence" of an imminent change. Taxes on competing fuels matter too: the 26 March 2026 cut in central excise on petrol to ₹11.90 a litre and diesel to ₹7.80 narrowed CNG's tax advantage, although pump prices did not fall (the oil companies absorbed the cut, then raised prices four times in May).

The compressed biogas (CBG) blending obligation requires city-gas companies to blend CBG equal to 1% of their CNG and household PNG volume in FY26, rising to 3% in FY27, 4% in FY28 and 5% from FY29. FY26 compliance was reported at 1.05%. CBG sales rose from 42.7 thousand tonnes in FY25 to 96.5 thousand tonnes in FY26 and 88.4 thousand in April–September 2026 alone, but the FY27 mandate implies about 310 thousand tonnes, so the first half ran at roughly 57% of what is needed; a shortfall or relaxation looks likely. In plain terms

The government sets the price of the cheapest gas, decides how much of it each company gets, decides who is served first in a crisis, and taxes the product at both ends. The regulator decides who may build where and what the pipelines charge. The companies set only the retail price, and even that is capped in practice by what petrol costs. Most of what happens to these stocks starts in a notification, not in a boardroom.

The cheap-gas squeeze: how a shrinking government allocation sets city-gas margins Deep dive

APM gas costs $7.00 when its own formula says $11.22. There is less of it every year, and every cubic metre the utilities lose must be replaced with gas that costs up to twice as much.

The single most important number for a city-gas company is not its volume or its retail price but the share of its gas that comes from APM fields. APM, the administered pricing mechanism, covers gas from ONGC and Oil India's old "nomination" fields, awarded before competitive bidding. Since April 2023 its price has been set by the Kirit Parikh formula: 10% of the Indian crude basket, with a floor of $4 and a ceiling that was $6.50 in FY24–FY25, $6.75 from April 2025 and $7.00 from April 2026. With crude at $90–116 a barrel in the second half of 2026, the formula has been far above the ceiling, so APM is a subsidised molecule: in October 2026 the formula was $11.22 and the price paid $7.00. That $4.22 gap is worth about ₹16 per scm on every APM unit, more than double IGL's entire Q1 FY27 EBITDA per scm.

The subsidy would not matter if there were plenty of APM gas. There is not. In plain terms

The government sells the city-gas companies a fixed ration of gas at about two-thirds of its market value, and the ration shrinks every year because the fields are old. Whatever the ration does not cover, the company must buy at market prices that 2026's war pushed sharply higher. The company that had signed cheaper contracts linked to US gas prices (MGL) kept most of its margin; the one whose imports were linked to oil prices (IGL) lost more than half of it.

The direction is clear even if the pace is not. APM is a depleting asset, and allocation to city gas will keep falling, with politically timed top-ups (the January 2025 restoration; the 200-scm household incentive from September 2026). City-gas margins are moving from "APM-subsidised" economics, ₹7–12 per scm in FY21–FY24, to "market-gas" economics of roughly ₹3.5–8. The official 2026 allocation ratio to city gas was not found in any public source; company disclosures are the best proxy.

In plain terms

An investor in IGL or MGL is really betting on three government decisions (how much APM, at what ceiling, and how fast new-well gas is priced) and one market price (US gas versus oil). Volume growth is the easy part. When reading a quarterly result, find the sourcing mix first; it explains the margin better than anything else.

CNG against EVs and petrol: a bridge fuel being crossed from both ends Deep dive

CNG's price advantage over petrol has shrunk from half to 15% per litre-equivalent. Electric autos already outsell CNG ones. Yet CNG car sales are at a record, and the arithmetic of IGL's mix shows why the decline is slower than the headlines.

CNG's market rests on one comparison: the cost of driving a kilometre on it against the cost on petrol or diesel. The utilities price CNG with that comparison in mind (IGL says it aims to "maintain a healthy delta with MS/HSD", MGL sees "room for CNG price hikes if MS and HSD prices increase"), so petrol is the price umbrella. The umbrella is held by the state oil companies, which lost money in 2026: PSU downstream companies made a combined loss of ₹18,150 crore in Q1 FY27 (see the refining report). That gives CNG pricing a political ceiling. And from the other side, electric vehicles are taking the highest-usage fleets.

War, Qatar and LNG: a two-phase shock that broke Petronet's contract model and paid it anyway Deep dive

From March 2026 India lost most of its Qatari LNG. Petronet's long-term volume fell 89%, its revenue halved, and it reported a record quarter. The city-gas utilities paid for the replacement gas.

The West Asia war began on 28 February 2026. Iranian strikes hit Qatar's Ras Laffan complex and knocked out two of its fourteen LNG trains; Qatar has reportedly said repairs will sideline about 12.8 mtpa of capacity for three to five years (a single-source figure, labelled indicative). Petronet served force majeure notices on QatarEnergy on 2 March and on vessel owners and customers on 3 March; QatarEnergy formally invoked force majeure on 4 March; loadings stopped from 3 March. Qatar had supplied about 40–45% of India's LNG before the war. By September 2026 it shipped only 94 thousand tonnes to India, sixth among suppliers, and QatarEnergy had exported 18 cargoes in the year against 509 a year earlier. On 28 September it extended force majeure to the end of November 2026 for Asian and European buyers, including at least one Indian buyer.

Use-or-pay, vessel claims and the contract book

Use-or-pay is the mechanism that is supposed to make a terminal a toll road. Customers who book regasification capacity pay for it whether or not they use it. (Take-or-pay is different: it obliges a buyer to pay for contracted LNG whether or not it lifts it, and it applies to Petronet's Qatar purchase contract.) Force majeure suspends both. Petronet's use-or-pay receivables stood at ₹719.84 crore gross at 31 March 2026 (₹413.02 crore net of a ₹306.82 crore provision): ₹13.11 crore for calendar 2022, ₹540.50 crore for 2023, ₹117.27 crore for 2024 and ₹48.96 crore for 2025, after ₹120.64 crore was waived against make-up cargoes. The auditor flagged the receivable as an emphasis of matter. Under the mechanism, offtakers' extra tolling cargoes first meet their current-year commitment and any excess retires past dues, so the 2026 surge in third-party tolling could help collections. The owners of the three long-term chartered LNG carriers have claimed ₹89.30 crore to 31 March 2026 "and have continued to do so"; nothing is provided, on legal advice, and the CFO said the charters were suspended under other clauses of the time-charter agreements, not under force majeure. A press figure of about ₹348 crore may be a later cumulative claim; it is unverified.

The contract book changes in April 2028. The current Qatar contract, 7.5 mtpa on a free-on-board basis since 2004, ends then; Petronet ships it in long-term-chartered carriers. The successor sale and purchase agreement, signed on 6 February 2024, is 7.5 mtpa for 20 years from 2028 to 2048 on a delivered (DES) basis, with the same offtake split (GAIL 60%, IOC 30%, BPCL 10%) and a formula price that includes shipping (undisclosed). What remains open is the back-to-back gas sale agreements with the offtakers, which management expects to close in "2–3 quarters". Because the new deal is delivered, the seller carries freight and vessel risk, and most of Petronet's 25-year charters end with the old contract; the charter-hire claims and free-on-board vessel exposure therefore fall away after April 2028. Two ExxonMobil contracts add diversity: about 1.425 mtpa from Australia to Kochi until 2035 and about 1.2 mtpa for 15 years from April 2026, both for BPCL, IOC and GAIL. A 0.5 mtpa tolling contract with a Deepak Fertilisers subsidiary started at Dahej in May 2026.

In plain terms

The war removed the ships that normally fill Petronet's Dahej terminal. Its customers chartered replacement ships themselves and paid Petronet to unload them, so the toll kept coming, and Petronet's small trading desk made a large profit when spot gas prices exploded. The city-gas companies bought that expensive replacement gas, which is where their margins went. Petronet's record quarter is therefore partly a one-off; its underlying toll income was slightly lower than a year earlier.

For the city-gas utilities the war's lasting effects are three. Supplier diversification is now policy, which should mean more US, Omani and African volume in their books. Qatar's damaged trains may stay out for years, keeping Asian spot prices higher and more volatile; S&P measured JKM volatility at an average 94% over March–September 2026 (indicative). And the policy tilt from LPG to piped gas in kitchens, born of the LPG shortage, gives them a political tailwind for household connections. For Petronet the war showed that the toll model survives the loss of its anchor supplier, as long as offtakers can find cargoes and the terminal is the best-connected on the coast.

LPG and liquid terminals: Aegis, the tank owner whose trading arm struck a war dividend Deep dive

India imports three of every five tonnes of LPG it burns. Aegis owns the largest independent set of port tanks to receive it, and in 2026 its distribution arm earned roughly three times its normal margin per tonne.

Aegis Logistics has two businesses that behave nothing alike. The terminals (LPG and liquids) are an annuity: customers rent tank space and pay per tonne or kilolitre handled, contracts run for years, and margins on liquids are about 73–74%. Most of the liquids business and some LPG terminals now sit inside Aegis Vopak Terminals (AVTL), listed on 2 June 2025 after a ₹2,800 crore IPO; AVTL earned operating profit of ₹687 crore on sales of ₹923 crore in FY26. The LPG sourcing and distribution arm buys LPG abroad and sells it to the oil companies and, through its own network, to factories, commercial users and auto-LPG pumps. It is high-volume and, in normal times, thin-margin: about 92% of FY26 revenue and a much smaller share of profit. In FY26 Aegis's consolidated revenue was ₹8,333 crore (+23%), screener EBITDA ₹1,452 crore (company "normalised" ₹1,599 crore), reported PAT ₹1,107 crore and PAT attributable to Aegis shareholders ₹898 crore, after ₹209 crore went to minority holders, chiefly AVTL's outside shareholders.

The 2026 war turned the second business into the profit engine. Aegis's Q1 FY27 normalised EBITDA was ₹727 crore, up 184%: ₹591 crore from the gas division (up 296%) and ₹136 crore from liquids (up 28%). The private-import puzzle

One data conflict needs explaining before the numbers are used together. Exclusivity, build-out and valuation: why the market pays 94 times for one licence book and 12 times for another Deep dive

A city-gas licence is a monopoly with an expiry date and a work programme attached. The market values ATGL's 53 young licences at more than IGL's and MGL's mature cities combined, which only makes sense on very long-dated assumptions.

The economic heart of city gas is the licence. PNGRB authorises one company per geographical area, and that authorisation carries two kinds of exclusivity. Infrastructure exclusivity, for 25 years, means no one else may lay a city-gas network there. Marketing exclusivity, which was five years for older GAs and eight for those awarded in the 9th–11th rounds (and can be extended), means no one else may sell gas to customers on that network. Once marketing exclusivity lapses, a rival could in principle buy gas, pay the incumbent a network tariff set by PNGRB, and sell to the incumbent's customers. IGL's Delhi and MGL's Mumbai passed that point long ago, and one trade report says their exclusivity "expired in 2012". In September 2021 PNGRB issued a notice proposing to declare 54 CGD networks, including Delhi and Mumbai, common or contract carriers, which would have formalised open access. On 27 May 2024 it withdrew that notice. The proposal could be revived, but for now the metros' real moat is incumbency: they own the stations, the meters, the customer relationships and the city permits, and no third-party marketer operates at scale.

Thirty years of cycles: born by court order, fattened by cheap gas, squeezed by scarcity

The members' history has four phases and half a dozen shocks. Almost every turn in city-gas margins was a policy turn; almost every turn in LNG volume was a price turn.

The industry was not born of a market. MGL was formed in 1995 by British Gas and GAIL to supply Mumbai, and IGL in 1998, the year the Supreme Court ordered Delhi's buses, autos and taxis to move to CNG to fight air pollution. Petronet was incorporated the same year by GAIL, IOC, BPCL and ONGC to import LNG, and Dahej opened in 2004 at 5 mtpa. For the next fifteen years the business ran on cheap APM gas and a retail price benchmarked against petrol and diesel, so metro utilities earned ₹6–9 per scm with returns on equity of 20–25%. The PNGRB Act (2006) created the licensing regime; Dahej expanded to 10, 15 and then 17.5 mtpa; Kochi opened in 2013 and sat stranded for years until its pipelines were built; MGL listed in July 2016.

The city-gas margin cycle is a policy cycle. EBITDA per scm at the metro utilities was roughly ₹6–7 in FY19, rose to ₹8–9 in FY21 when APM was priced at $1.79–2.90, collapsed to ₹4–5 in the first half of FY23 when APM jumped to $6.10–8.57 and LNG spiked, recovered to ₹8–12 in FY24 once the Parikh ceiling capped APM at $6.50, and fell back to ₹5–9 in FY26 and ₹3.4–7.9 in Q1 FY27 as the allocation was cut. The pattern repeats: whenever the state gives cheap gas, margins widen and the stocks re-rate; whenever it takes the gas back or the price spikes, margins shrink and the stocks de-rate. The metro utilities' own five-year record shows the squeeze from the other side. In plain terms

City-gas stocks have moved with government decisions about cheap gas more than with anything the companies did. Knowing which kind of cycle you are in, policy or price, tells you what to watch: ministry notifications for the utilities, LNG prices and volumes for the terminal.

The shocks that shaped the current shareholder registers are also worth naming. Reading the KPIs: a masterclass in rupees per cubic metre, trillion BTUs and tonnes Deep dive

Each of the three business models has its own profit equation and its own units. Learn the equations, the dozen numbers that feed them, and the traps in how companies define them, and every quarterly result in this sub-sector becomes readable in minutes.

Start with the city-gas equation, because three of the five members live by it. EBITDA equals the volume of gas sold, in standard cubic metres, multiplied by the spread per cubic metre: net realisation (the selling price after excise and VAT, weighted across CNG, household, commercial and industrial customers) minus gas cost (the blended purchase price plus pipeline transport) minus operating cost (staff, compression power, dealer commissions, maintenance). The diagram marks which terms management controls. It controls opex fully, and volume and realisation partly: volume depends on network build-out and on how many vehicles convert, and realisation is capped in practice by petrol and by politics. It controls gas cost hardly at all, beyond the contracts it signs.

How to forecast it. The most practical method is to rebuild the blend from its parts each quarter: take the company's disclosed mmscmd of APM, new-well gas, HPHT, term LNG and spot, apply the period's price for each (APM ceiling, 12% of the average crude basket, the HPHT ceiling, Brent or Henry Hub times the contract slope, and spot or IGX), convert at the period's rupee rate and 25.2 scm per mmbtu, and add about ₹2.1 for Zone-1 transport. 6. The CNG/petrol price ratio and per-km saving

What it is. The pricing headroom a utility has before CNG stops being attractive. Formula. CNG ₹/kg ÷ petrol ₹/litre in the same city; and, more meaningfully, CNG ₹/km ÷ petrol ₹/km using typical mileage. Worked example. CNG price increases (driven by gas cost), petrol prices (set by oil companies under government influence), and fuel taxes on both. Benchmarks. Below 0.75 good, 0.75–0.90 average, above 0.90 weak, the level at which conversions tend to slow. Members. Delhi 0.85 (IGL), Mumbai 0.80 (MGL). IGL says it keeps CNG about ₹17 a kg below petrol; MGL says CNG is 40–45% cheaper than petrol and about 12% cheaper than diesel.

Definition trap. Per-unit ratios overstate the squeeze, per-km savings understate competition from EVs, and neither captures the cost of converting a vehicle. Compare like with like: a CNG auto against an e-auto (₹2.5–3.0 against ₹0.6–1.0 a km, estimates), a CNG car against a petrol car.

In plain terms

A city-gas company can raise its CNG price until a driver would save more by buying petrol. That ceiling is still comfortably above today's price per kilometre in Delhi and Mumbai, so pricing power exists, but each increase narrows the gap that persuades the next driver to choose CNG. Watch the ratio, not just the price.

13. LPG throughput, static capacity and the ₹/t margin

What it is. For a tank owner: tonnes passing through (throughput), tank space (static capacity), and how often the tanks turn over. For a distributor: tonnes sold and EBITDA per tonne. Formulas. Turns = throughput ÷ static capacity. Distribution margin = (gas-division EBITDA − throughput × ~₹1,000/t − sourcing contribution) ÷ distribution tonnes (management's method). Worked example. Aegis FY26: 5,152 thousand tonnes through about 245,800 tonnes of tanks ≈ 21 turns a year. Q1 FY27 distribution margin ≈ (₹591 crore − ₹112 crore) ÷ 277 thousand tonnes ≈ ₹17,300 a tonne (indicative). What moves it. Import volumes, the share of imports landing on the west coast, evacuation by rail and pipeline, and, for distribution, the gap between Saudi contract prices and what rationed buyers will pay. Benchmarks. Throughput above 5 million tonnes good, 2–5 average; turns above 20 good, 12–20 average, below 12 weak; distribution margin above ₹6,000 a tonne good, ₹3,500–6,000 average, below ₹3,500 weak; liquids terminal margin above 70% good.

18. Definition traps, collected

  • Gross vs net revenue. City-gas revenue includes 14% CNG excise in press releases, not in screener. ATGL ₹6,415 crore gross = ₹5,894 crore net; MGL ₹9,185 crore gross = ₹8,246 crore net.
  • ₹/kg vs ₹/scm. Divide by about 1.4, and remember taxes sit between the pump price and the company's realisation.
  • EBITDA per scm numerators and denominators. Standalone EBITDA over standalone volume, same period; strip one-offs such as OMC trade-margin reversals.
  • Allocation percentages. At least five denominators; use APM mmscmd instead.
  • Standalone vs joint venture. ATGL 303 vs 496 million scm; 707 vs 1,167 stations; IGL's MNGL and CUGL are outside its volume.
  • Utilisation across a capacity change. Dahej's 65.6% is on 22.5 mtpa; on the old 17.5 it would be about 83%.
  • Regas tariff on the wrong volume. Regas revenue ÷ tolling TBTU (₹69), not ÷ total TBTU (₹58).
  • Use-or-pay vs take-or-pay. Different contracts, both suspended by force majeure; use-or-pay receipts are lumpy one-offs.
  • Static vs throughput capacity. Tonnes of tank space are not tonnes handled; kilolitres are not tonnes.
  • Reported vs attributable PAT. Aegis ₹1,107 crore vs ₹898 crore; use attributable for P/E.
  • Net cash. Screener's "investments" include strategic stakes (IGL, ATGL); "borrowings" include leases (Petronet, MGL).
  • Capacity headlines. Petronet is 27.5 mtpa (Dahej 22.5 + Kochi 5), not 22.5; its share of India's LNG is about 66%, not 75%.
In plain terms

Most misreadings in this sector come from comparing numbers built on different bases: gross with net, standalone with joint-venture, old capacity with new, reported with attributable. Before comparing two figures, ask what each one includes. The answer usually explains half of any apparent difference.

19. How to read a city-gas or terminal company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volume first (1 minute). mmscmd by segment for city gas; TBTU and utilisation (on the stated capacity) for Petronet; logistics, distribution and sourcing tonnes for Aegis. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the last quarter, to remove seasonality.
  2. EBITDA per unit (1 minute). EBITDA per scm for city gas; for Petronet, EBITDA in ₹ crore (never margin %); for Aegis, gas-division EBITDA and the implied ₹/t on distribution. Check it against guidance (IGL ₹7, MGL ₹8–9, Aegis ₹7,000+/t).
  3. The gas-cost line (2 minutes). Gas cost per scm and its change; then the sourcing mix: APM mmscmd, new-well gas, HPHT, Henry Hub versus Brent LNG, spot share. A rising spot share or a falling APM volume is the main red flag.
  4. Realisation and price actions (1 minute). Net realisation per scm, CNG price increases during the quarter, and the CNG/petrol ratio in the main city. Large price increases with flat margins mean the company is chasing costs.
  5. One-offs (1 minute). OMC trade-margin reversals, labour-code provisions, use-or-pay receipts, trading and inventory gains, foreign-exchange items. Strip them from both this quarter and the base quarter.
  6. Below EBITDA (1 minute). Other income (treasury and, in consolidated accounts, joint-venture share), depreciation and interest. For Aegis, minority interest; quote attributable profit.
  7. Network and connections (1 minute). Stations added, household connections added and, where disclosed, consuming; industrial and commercial customers. For Aegis, capacity commissioned.
  8. Capex and balance sheet (1 minute). Quarterly capex against the annual guide; borrowings, cash and any shift of debt from non-current to current.
  9. Management's next-quarter comments (1 minute). Gas-cost direction (MGL's "1–2 months of pressure"), allocation news, tariff talks, Qatar status, distribution-margin outlook.
  10. Red flags to note. EBITDA per scm below ₹4 for two quarters; spot share above 10%; APM volume down more than 10% in a quarter; utilisation below 60% outside a force-majeure period; a growing use-or-pay receivable; a capex guide raised without a funding plan; any new contingent liability or auditor emphasis of matter.
In plain terms

A city-gas result is read from the bottom of the cost stack up: what the gas cost, how much of it was cheap, what the company charged, what was left per cubic metre. A terminal result is read from volume and tariff, after stripping one-off receipts. An LPG result is read by splitting the steady tank income from the trading income. Ten minutes on those lines is enough to know whether a quarter was good, and why.

The global lens: a small LNG buyer in a market about to be flooded, and city-gas stocks priced like China's

India buys about 6% of the world's LNG and owns about 5% of its regasification capacity. The coming wave of new liquefaction was the bull case for cheap gas; the 2026 war has delayed it.

Global LNG trade reached a record 437 million tonnes in 2025, up 6.3%, the strongest growth since 2022, according to the International Gas Union. The US was the largest exporter at 110.7 million tonnes, ahead of Qatar (81.5) and Australia; Europe's imports rose 26.1 million tonnes while China's fell 8.9 million. Liquefaction capacity was 524.5 mtpa at the end of 2025, and 68.4 mtpa of new capacity reached final investment decision in 2025 alone, part of about 206 mtpa sanctioned over five years. That wave, led by US projects and Qatar's North Field expansions, was the main reason to expect cheaper LNG and fuller Indian terminals from 2027. The 2026 war has complicated it: two of Qatar's fourteen trains are out, reportedly for three to five years, and spot prices have been both higher and far more volatile.

India's place in that market is modest. Its 26–27 million tonnes a year are about 6% of global trade, making it the fourth-largest importer (35.4 bcm in calendar 2025 per the Energy Institute's Statistical Review). Its 58.5 mtpa of terminals are about 5% of the world's roughly 1,113 mtpa of regasification capacity. Gas is about 6% of India's primary energy against a world average of about 23–24%. India is, however, the world's second-largest importer of LPG (21.45 million tonnes in FY26), after China, which is why Aegis's terminals matter more globally than India's gas terminals do.

Technology and disruption: electrons for autos, biogas by mandate, LNG for trucks

The disruptions that matter are segment by segment. Electric three-wheelers are already decisive; electric cars are a 2030s question; biogas is a cost; LNG trucking and ammonia storage are options.

The biggest structural threat, electric vehicles, is covered in section 09. The useful way to frame it is by segment: autos (7–8% of IGL's CNG) are largely lost in Delhi by 2030, buses have mostly gone, taxis (14%) are a medium-term risk, and private cars (48–50%) a slow-burn risk for the 2030s.

Several smaller technologies cut both ways. Compressed biogas is mandated at 3% of CNG and household PNG in FY27 and 5% from FY29; it is an opportunity for utilities that own plants (MGL is building a 350-tonne-a-day municipal-waste plant on Mumbai municipal land; ATGL sold 1,654 tonnes of CBG in FY26) and a cost for those that must buy it at ₹72–80 a kg. LNG for long-haul trucks is nascent: IGL has begun its first LNG operation in NCR with CONCOR; MGL runs two LNG stations selling about 5 tonnes a day at breakeven; Petronet is expanding truck-loading bays at Dahej (8 to 14) and Kochi (4 to 6) on the thesis of an 8–10 mtpa small-scale LNG market in six to eight years. Hydrogen blending into city-gas networks remains at the pilot stage and is immaterial before 2030. Induction cooking is a slow, minor threat to household PNG and LPG. Smart metering and network automation are opex levers: ATGL's smart-meter joint venture can make 7.2 lakh meters a year and sold 1.83 lakh in FY26.

Bull vs bear: margins that could mean-revert, or a molecule that stays dear

The case for and against the sub-sector turns on gas cost for the utilities, on windfalls for the terminals, and on long-dated growth for the two expensive names.

The bull case

  • Volume is structural: national CGD +9.5% a year FY22–FY26; CNG 22% of car sales; 27,300 vehicle additions a month at IGL; household connections pushed by policy.
  • Cheaper gas is coming: 206 mtpa of new liquefaction sanctioned in five years; Henry Hub at ~$3 vs Brent above $100; utilities adding Henry Hub contracts and hedges.
  • Metros are cheap: IGL and MGL at ~12–13× earnings and ~7× EV/EBITDA, net cash, ~3% yields, priced near Chinese peers on depressed margins.
  • The toll survived the worst case: Petronet lost 89% of its Qatar volume and still grew profit; a delivered-basis Qatar deal from 2028 removes vessel risk.
  • Scarce port tanks: Aegis's capacity and geography fit the shift to US LPG cargoes; long-dated contracts (HPCL to 2038, Hindustan Zinc ammonia).

The bear case

  • The cheap molecule is depleting: APM −9–10% a year; ~27–30% of metro volume; new-well gas at ~$13.9 when crude is $116.
  • Pricing power is capped: CNG at 0.85× petrol in Delhi; petrol set by loss-making oil companies; EVs taking autos, buses and soon taxis.
  • Windfalls flatter the terminals: Petronet's ₹494 Cr trading gains and ₹630 Cr use-or-pay receipt; Aegis's ~₹17,300/t margin vs ₹7,000+ guided.
  • Capital is being redeployed: Petronet's ₹20,685 Cr petchem plant; Aegis's US$809 mn Tristar deal, ₹6,000 Cr raise and CARE watch; MGL borrowing.
  • Valuation and contingent items: ATGL at ~94× earnings with a ₹3,472 Cr JV guarantee; IGL's ₹331 Cr DDA claim; Petronet's ₹720 Cr use-or-pay receivable.

The balance of evidence, as of October 2026, is that the bear case describes the next two quarters better and the bull case the next five years. Q2 FY27 is likely to show weaker city-gas margins than Q1 (indicative, from MGL's and ATGL's own comments), while the terminal operators' windfalls may persist for as long as spot dislocation does. The decisive variables are not company-specific: the APM allocation and ceiling, the Henry Hub–Brent spread, the rupee, and how quickly Qatar's lost capacity and the new US supply arrive.

In plain terms

If cheaper imported gas arrives as the world's new LNG plants open, the city-gas utilities' margins should recover and their low valuations will look like an opportunity. If gas stays expensive, those margins stay thin and the low valuations are deserved. For the terminal companies, the question is how much of 2026's profit was a one-off. For ATGL and Aegis, the question is whether growth arrives fast enough to justify prices that already assume it.
