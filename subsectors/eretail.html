00

The one-page view: ₹6.2 lakh crore priced on order value, not on profit

Seven very different businesses share one stock-market label. Half the value sits in one company, and most of that rests on a single bet: that ten-minute delivery can earn a real margin.

The seven companies in this report sell food, groceries, beauty products, baby clothes, home cleaning and Jaipur-made gemstone jewellery. What they have in common is that the customer orders on a screen and the company, or a partner it controls, delivers. Together they were worth about ₹6.2 lakh crore at the 16 October 2026 close, booked ₹1.31 lakh crore of revenue over the trailing twelve months, and lost ₹5,115 crore after tax in FY26. Only three of the seven made a reported profit in FY26: Eternal (₹366 crore), Vaibhav Global (₹266 crore) and Nykaa (₹204 crore). This is a group the market prices on the size of the flow of orders and on the path to profit, not on today's earnings.

The value is lopsided. Eternal, the company that owns Zomato, Blinkit, District and Hyperpure, is worth ₹3.14 lakh crore, or 51% of the group. Meesho (₹1.05 lakh crore) and Nykaa (₹0.99 lakh crore) follow. Swiggy, the only company that competes with Eternal in both of its main businesses, is worth about a fifth as much (₹64,674 crore) and trades close to its 52-week low. FirstCry and Vaibhav Global together are worth less than 2% of the group. Because Eternal dominates, every combined figure in this report is shown twice: all seven, and the six excluding Eternal.

Five forces explain almost everything in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them.

  • The quick-commerce land-grab. Ten-minute grocery and general-merchandise delivery from neighbourhood "dark stores" grew roughly 120% in FY26 to about a sixth of Indian online retail. Blinkit, Zepto (unlisted) and Swiggy's Instamart did ₹28,195 crore of net order value in the March 2026 quarter. Only Blinkit made money, and only just: 0.6% of order value in Q1 FY27. Half the group's market value rests on Blinkit moving toward the 3.5–5% margin it earns in its oldest cities (Sections 05 and 14).
  • Food delivery as the profit pool. Zomato and Swiggy form a settled duopoly that earned ₹898 crore of adjusted EBITDA in the June 2026 quarter, growing 17–20% a year. That cash funds the quick-commerce war (Section 06).
  • Ownership as strategy. Indian foreign-investment rules ban foreign-owned e-commerce companies from holding inventory. To let Blinkit and Instamart buy and own the goods they sell, Eternal and then Swiggy capped foreign ownership at 49.5%. Swiggy's first vote failed in May 2026 and the second passed on 18 August, with RBI sign-off still pending (Section 07).
  • Monetisation instead of subsidy. The platforms now earn from advertising, platform and handling fees, memberships and the margin on goods they own. Meesho charges sellers no commission at all and still keeps about 32% of net order value as revenue (Sections 10 and 14).
  • A market split by profit trajectory. Investors pay about 2.4 rupees of enterprise value per rupee of Eternal's annualised order value and about 0.7 for Swiggy's. Nykaa and Urban Company trade near 5× their order value; FirstCry and Vaibhav Global near 1× sales. The market rewards being number one in a category with a visible path to margin (Section 18).

In plain terms

These companies are valued less on what they earn today than on how much shopping flows through them and how much of each order they will eventually keep. Food delivery has already shown that a two-player market can keep 3–6 rupees in every hundred. Quick commerce is trying to prove the same thing while three well-funded rivals and two global giants build dark stores as fast as they can. Whether Blinkit's 0.6% becomes 4% decides most of the group's value.

A note on dates. This report is written on 19 October 2026, in the middle of the September-quarter (Q2 FY27) results season. Only Urban Company has reported Q2 FY27 (on 16 October). Eternal reports on 21 October, Swiggy on 22 October, and Meesho and Vaibhav Global on 28 October; Nykaa and FirstCry typically report in early-to-mid November, and Nykaa has already issued a provisional Q2 business update. Every "latest quarter" figure in this report is therefore Q1 FY27 (the June 2026 quarter) unless it is marked as Q2 FY27 for Urban Company or as Nykaa's Q2 update. The company sections say which quarter each figure is from, and the dates appear in the calendar in Section 22.

01

Who keeps what from a ₹100 order

The same rupee of customer spending can be booked as ₹15, ₹29, ₹32 or ₹100 of revenue, depending on whether the platform owns the goods. Read that first, or every comparison goes wrong.

Every business in this report sits somewhere on one chain. A brand, a manufacturer, a restaurant or a trained service professional makes something. A platform lets the customer find it, pay for it and track it. A fulfilment node (a dark store, a warehouse, a sort centre or a restaurant kitchen) prepares it. A last-mile rider or courier takes it to the door. The platforms make money at three points on that chain: by charging the seller (commission and advertising), by charging the customer (delivery, platform, handling and small-cart fees, plus memberships), and, where the law allows, by buying the goods and keeping the retail margin.

Three ways to book the same sale

Marketplace (third-party, or "3P"). The platform never owns the goods. It books only its commission, fees and advertising as revenue. Zomato and Swiggy food, Meesho, Nykaa Fashion and Instamart (until it converts) work this way. Instamart's Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹1,232 crore on ₹7,907 crore of gross order value is a take of 15.6%, so a rupee of Instamart shopping shows up as about 16 paise of revenue.

Inventory-led (first-party, or "1P"). The platform buys the stock, holds it and sells it, so revenue is roughly the full sale value net of GST. Blinkit (from FY26), Nykaa beauty, FirstCry's house brands and Vaibhav Global work this way. When Blinkit switched, Eternal's revenue rose 169% in FY26 to ₹54,364 crore, while the underlying order value grew far less. In Q1 FY27 Eternal's revenue rose 182% but group net order value (NOV) rose 54%. The revenue jump was accounting, not demand.

Managed services. Urban Company sets prices, trains and equips its partners, and takes a commission of about 20–25% plus product sales to partners. Its revenue was 37.5% of net transaction value (NTV) in Q2 FY27 (₹537 crore of ₹1,432 crore).

Where is value captured in 2026? Food-delivery platforms keep about 25–30% of order value as revenue (Zomato's food revenue of ₹3,100 crore on NOV of ₹10,769 crore is 28.8%) and turn 5–6% of order value into adjusted EBITDA for the leader, about 3% for the follower. Quick commerce captures roughly 15–20% of order value through margin, fees and advertising, but most of that is spent on riders and stores, leaving 0–1% for the leader and losses for everyone else. Meesho keeps about 32% of net order value and retains 4–5% as contribution. Category specialists earn retail gross margins (Nykaa 45.9%, FirstCry 36.5%, Vaibhav Global about 63%) and convert 4–11% of revenue into EBITDA after fulfilment and marketing. Advertising is the richest pool everywhere: it has no rider and no warehouse attached, so nearly every rupee of it falls to contribution.

In plain terms

A marketplace reports a small slice of each sale as revenue; an inventory model reports the whole sale. So when Blinkit switched to owning its stock, Eternal's revenue nearly tripled in a year without customers buying three times as much. Always ask "revenue on what?" and use order value (and the company's own definition of it) to judge growth.

Vaibhav Global is the outlier. It is not a platform, it does not sell to Indian consumers, and it is not loss-making. It designs and manufactures low-priced jewellery in Jaipur and sells it to value-seeking households in the US, UK and Germany through its own TV channels and apps, so it captures the manufacturer's margin, the retailer's margin and the broadcaster's audience in one company. It sits in this sub-sector because it is a direct-to-consumer, screen-based retailer, and it is a useful control: a mature, cash-generating e-retailer valued at about 12 times earnings, against a group where the median company has no earnings to value.

02

Size: a ₹6.9 lakh crore online market where a sixth now arrives in ten minutes

Indian online retail grew about 21% in FY26. Two formats, quick commerce and value commerce, went from almost nothing in FY21 to about 30% of it.

Redseer estimates Indian online retail at about US$80 billion of gross merchandise value in FY26, up 21% a year. At ₹86 to the dollar, the conversion rate used throughout this report for estimates, that is roughly ₹6.9 lakh crore. India has 320–340 million people who buy online at least once a year, and more than 250 million of them shop in "value commerce", the low-price, mostly unbranded segment Meesho leads. These figures are third-party estimates rather than audited numbers, and they are labelled INDICATIVE wherever they appear.

The striking change is in the mix. Quick commerce was about US$13–14 billion of GMV in FY26 (roughly ₹1.1–1.2 lakh crore), about 17% of online retail, and grew about 120%. Quick commerce and value commerce together went from about 2% of online retail in FY21 to about 30% in FY26, so value commerce is roughly 13% by subtraction (an estimate). Redseer expects quick commerce to reach 25–30% and value commerce 15–18% by FY30, more than 40% of online retail between them. A separate Infisum study (September 2026) puts broader e-commerce, including services and travel, at US$125 billion in 2024 rising to US$345 billion by 2030, with quick commerce supplying 45–50% of incremental e-retail growth.

Why the quick-commerce size depends on who is counting

Estimates of the quick-commerce market disagree by a factor of four, and the reason is definitions. Redseer put FY25 quick-commerce order value at about ₹64,000 crore and FY26 GMV at US$13–14 billion. Bernstein estimated an FY26 exit run-rate of about US$14 billion of net order value. Mordor Intelligence gives US$3.65 billion for 2026, but that is platform revenue, not the value of goods sold, and should be ignored as a market size. The simplest cross-check is to add up what the three leaders reported: Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart did ₹28,195 crore of NOV in the March 2026 quarter, which annualises to about ₹1.13 lakh crore. Add Flipkart Minutes, BigBasket's BB Now and Amazon Now, and allow for GMV being larger than NOV, and that is consistent with Redseer's figure.

Food delivery and the specialists' markets

No reliable independent estimate of the FY26 food-delivery market was found. The listed duopoly is effectively the market: Zomato's Q1 FY27 NOV of ₹10,769 crore plus Swiggy's food GOV of ₹9,490 crore is ₹20,259 crore a quarter, about ₹81,000 crore a year annualised. That sum mixes two definitions (Swiggy's GOV includes taxes and fees that Zomato's NOV excludes), so the implied 53:47 split understates Zomato; on a like-for-like basis it is probably nearer 55–57%, though that is an estimate that could not be verified.

The specialists play in smaller pools. Online beauty and personal care was about ₹52,000 crore in calendar 2025, up from ₹21,000 crore in 2022 (about 35% a year), and Nykaa holds roughly 25–30% of it. Baby and kids is large but mostly unorganised, with India's fertility rate near 2.0, below replacement. Home services has no credible market size; the informal local plumber and beautician are Urban Company's main rivals. Vaibhav Global plays in US and UK TV and digital shopping, a market dominated by QVC and HSN, where its ₹3,692 crore of revenue is a niche share.

In plain terms

Indian online shopping is roughly ₹6.9 lakh crore a year and growing about a fifth a year. One rupee in six now goes to ten-minute delivery, a format that barely existed five years ago. The exact size depends on whether you count the goods (GMV), the goods net of discounts (NOV) or just the platform's cut (revenue), which is why published figures range from ₹31,000 crore to ₹1.2 lakh crore for the same market.

03

Who orders, how often, and why

Two billion ten-minute orders a year in a handful of metros, a quarter-billion value shoppers in smaller towns, and a premium beauty customer who still wants a store.

Demand in this sub-sector comes from different people in different places. Quick commerce and food delivery are metro habits bought by affluent, time-poor households. Value commerce is a tier-2-and-below phenomenon driven by first-time online shoppers. The specialists serve a premiumising urban middle class, and Vaibhav Global sells to price-sensitive older households in the US and UK. The eight drivers below explain most of the growth, and each has a number attached.

1. The ten-minute habit

Blinkit handled 916.6 million orders in FY26, Zepto 640.2 million and Instamart 412.2 million, together about 1.97 billion quick-commerce orders in a year. Blinkit's figure is about 76 million orders a month (an earlier primer quoted 274 million a month; the 274 million is a quarterly figure, as Blinkit's ₹525 average order value only reconciles on the quarterly basis). With about 27 million monthly transacting customers, a Blinkit user orders roughly 3.4 times a month; an Instamart user (1.4 crore monthly users, 11.5 crore orders in Q1 FY27) about 2.7 times. These frequency figures are calculated, not disclosed. Frequency matters because it fills dark stores: the same rent and staff are spread over more orders.

2. Quick commerce moves beyond groceries

The second driver is category creep. Quick commerce started with milk, bread and snacks and is moving into electronics, beauty, toys and baby care. Redseer notes that quick commerce is still under 5% of fashion, mobiles and electronics, which together make up about 60% of online retail GMV. That is the headroom, and it is also the threat to the specialists: FirstCry's management cites "heightened competitive intensity" in diapers, and Nykaa's mass-beauty range faces the same pressure. For FMCG brands the shift is already large. Quick commerce is over 70% of online sales for Britannia and Tata Consumer and 75% for Dabur; e-commerce is about 19% of FMCG sales in India's top eight metros and is adding about a percentage point a quarter. The Packaged Foods report covers how this is rewiring FMCG distribution and squeezing distributors and kiranas.

3. Tier-2 value shoppers

New user acquisition in Indian e-commerce has "shifted decisively toward Tier 2-plus India", in Redseer's words. Meesho's annual transacting users rose 29% to 274 million in Q1 FY27 (on a trailing twelve-month basis), with about 80% of its monthly users in tier-2 towns and smaller. These shoppers buy a ₹265 kurti or kitchen set, not a ₹525 grocery basket, which is why value commerce has a different economic shape (Section 10).

4. Premiumisation and the return of the store

Nykaa's Q2 FY27 business update showed store like-for-like growth in the early 20s, its best in six quarters, with beauty net sales value growing in the late 20s. Urban, aspirational buyers are trading up in beauty and want to try products in person. That keeps omnichannel relevant: Nykaa added 14 stores in the quarter to 338.

5. Time poverty and the formalisation of services

Urban Company's net transaction value grew 39% in Q2 FY27 and InstaHelp, its 10–15-minute home-help service, handled 6.2 million orders in the quarter, up 63% from the June quarter. Dual-income metro households are paying for trained, background-checked help booked by app instead of finding it informally.

6. Food: frequency and new formats, slower growth

Food delivery is the most mature market. Growth has slowed to high teens or about 20% (Swiggy food GOV +17.4%, Zomato NOV +20% in Q1 FY27). The platforms are adding formats to push frequency: Swiggy's Bolt fast delivery in 400+ cities, 10-minute food (Bistro, Swiggy Snacc, Zepto Café) and budget apps such as Toing. Higher frequency, more than new users, is the lever.

7. The Western value consumer

Vaibhav Global's demand comes from a different place altogether. Its Q1 FY27 revenue rose 12.7% in rupees but was roughly flat in constant currency, held back by disruption from the Middle East conflict early in the quarter and by cautious US and UK spending. Its FY27 guidance is 9–11% revenue growth. A weak rupee flatters its reported numbers.

8. The macro overlay

Indian FMCG volumes slowed to +0.9% in the January–March 2026 quarter, with rural volumes turning negative (NIQ). Quick-commerce demand is concentrated in affluent metros and has so far been insulated from that slowdown. The flip side is concentration risk: the group's growth depends on perhaps 40–50 million urban households.

04

Riders, dark stores and inventory: where ₹100 of order value goes

The supply side of e-retail is not factories but people on scooters, small warehouses in residential lanes and, increasingly, stock the platform owns and can lose.

An e-retailer's costs fall into two groups. Variable costs move with every order: the rider's payout, packaging, the payment-gateway fee, the discount the platform funds, and, for inventory models, the cost of the goods and the stock that expires or is stolen. Semi-fixed costs move with the network: dark-store rent and staff, warehouse leases, technology and the brand marketing budget. The economics of every format in this report come down to how many orders share each unit of semi-fixed cost, and how much is left of each order's take after the variable costs are paid.

The six inputs that matter

  • Last-mile labour. The rider's payout per delivery is the largest variable cost in food and quick commerce. Swiggy cited an annual wage increase for delivery partners and spending on rider availability as the reasons its food contribution margin dipped quarter on quarter in Q1 FY27. On top of payouts, a new social-security levy applies under the Code on Social Security, in force from 21 November 2025: aggregators contribute 1–2% of annual turnover, capped at 5% of what they pay gig workers. Karnataka has added its own welfare fee (Section 12).
  • Dark-store real estate. A dark store is a 2,500–4,000 sq ft neighbourhood warehouse closed to the public, from which pickers fill orders in under two minutes. Blinkit runs about 19 million sq ft of warehousing and stores across 300+ cities; Instamart's 1,143 stores at March 2026 covered 4.8 million sq ft, about 4,200 sq ft each (calculated). The rent and staff are fixed whether the store does 600 orders a day or 1,400.
  • Inventory and shrink. Owning the goods means owning the losses. Blinkit lost about 1.8% of its NOV, roughly ₹308 crore in Q1 FY27, to expiry, damage and theft, according to its shareholder letter as reported by Apparel Resources. Inventory also ties up cash: screener shows 109 inventory days at Nykaa and 133 at FirstCry.
  • Customer acquisition and discounts. In quick commerce, discounting is the swing cost: a couple of percentage points of order value spent on offers can wipe out a margin that is itself under 1%. Instamart deliberately shed more than 4 million unprofitable users in Q1 FY27 to stop paying for them.
  • Packaging, payments and cloud. Payment-gateway charges run about 1–2% of order value (a recalled industry figure, not a company disclosure), with packaging and cloud costs on top.
  • Taxes and tariffs. Since 22 September 2025, local delivery services supplied through e-commerce operators have been taxed at 18% GST, payable by the platform under Section 9(5) (recalled from the 56th GST Council outcome; not re-verified for this report). Platforms either absorb this or pass it on through delivery and platform fees. For Vaibhav Global, the relevant tax is the US import tariff on Indian goods, partly refunded in Q1 FY27 (₹38.4 crore).

Ten minutes to profit: the quick-commerce land-grab Deep dive

Three players built nearly 5,000 dark stores between them. One of them now earns a sliver of profit; two burn ₹800–1,250 crore a quarter; and two of the world's largest retailers have just arrived.

Quick commerce is the force that decides the value of this sub-sector. Blinkit alone accounts for most of Eternal's market value, and Eternal is half the group. Instamart decides whether Swiggy survives as an independent company on its current cash. Quick commerce is also what squeezes FirstCry's diapers and Nykaa's mass beauty. Everything else in the group is either funding this war or being affected by it.

What quick commerce does to FMCG and the kirana

Quick commerce is not just a new channel for brands; it is replacing parts of the old one. E-commerce is about 19% of FMCG sales in India's top eight metros, and traditional trade (the kirana and its distributor) fell from 61.8% to 57.2% of FMCG sales in four quarters, according to NIQ data covered in the Packaged Foods report. The All India Consumer Products Distributors Federation, representing about 4 lakh distributors, complained to the Competition Commission in 2025 about predatory pricing and direct deals; the CCI sought evidence but no investigation order or penalty had been found as of October 2026. For brands, the platform's take through margin and advertising runs about 15–25% of sales (an estimate), which is why quick commerce is both a growth engine and a margin question for FMCG companies.

The war chest

Each player has raised money specifically for this fight. Eternal (then Zomato) raised ₹8,500 crore in a qualified institutional placement in November 2024 and had treasury of ₹17,972 crore at March 2026. Swiggy raised ₹10,000 crore in December 2025, earmarking ₹4,475 crore for quick-commerce fulfilment, and had ₹14,400 crore of cash at June 2026. Zepto's planned fresh issue is ₹8,010 crore. Against that, Swiggy's FY26 operating cash outflow was ₹2,898 crore and Zepto's run-rate burn is about ₹3,500 crore a year, so neither is close to running out, but neither can afford a multi-year subsidy contest with Walmart and Amazon either.

In plain terms

Quick commerce has a leader that just turned profitable, a challenger that has fixed its order-level economics but not its overheads, a private rival with an IPO ready, and two global giants building stores at a hundred a month. The leader's margin of 0.6% shows that the model can work; whether it reaches 3–5% depends on whether the newcomers force everyone back into discounting.

What has to go right for the quick-commerce bull case? Three things. Order density in the newest stores must keep rising toward the 1,200–1,400 orders a day that Redseer identifies as the breakeven range in mature metros. Average order value must stay above about ₹500 as the category mix widens into electronics and beauty. And discounting must stay contained even as Flipkart and Amazon scale. The first two are within management's control; the third is not.

Why the duopoly holds

Food delivery has been a two-player market since January 2020, when Zomato bought Uber Eats India. Foodpanda had already exited in 2019, and Amazon Food shut in 2022. The reason challengers fail is local density. A platform needs enough restaurants in each neighbourhood to attract customers, and enough customers to keep riders busy between orders; the incumbents have both in every city that matters, and a new entrant has neither. Rider utilisation, the share of a rider's hour spent carrying food, drives cost per order, and it is highest for the platform with the most orders per square kilometre. ONDC, the government-backed open network, lowers commissions but has had limited traction so far. Rapido's low-commission "Ownly" (2025) is a challenger worth watching, but evidence of its scale is thin.

The levers left

Swiggy's Capital Markets Day in August 2026 set a target of ₹5,000 crore of food adjusted EBITDA by FY31, about five times the roughly ₹1,000 crore it earned in FY26. Getting there needs order value to compound in the high teens and margin to rise toward Zomato's level, through more advertising, higher membership penetration, 10-minute food formats (Bolt, Snacc), and cheaper formats such as the Toing budget app that bring in new users at lower average order values. Zomato's long-run guidance is about 20% annual growth in food order value.

What threatens the pool

  • The gig-worker levy. 1–2% of turnover, capped at 5% of worker payouts, under the Code on Social Security; plus state welfare fees (Section 12). On Swiggy's FY26 revenue of ₹23,053 crore, 1% would be about ₹230 crore a year (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • GST on delivery. The 18% GST on local delivery through e-commerce operators from September 2025 (recalled, not re-verified) either raises customer prices or comes out of the margin.
  • Competition law. A Competition Commission investigation following the National Restaurant Association of India's 2021 complaint reportedly found exclusivity and price-parity arrangements with restaurants (reported by Reuters in November 2024); a final order is pending.
  • Restaurant pushback and new formats. Restaurants resent commissions of 20%+ and some push customers to order directly. Ten-minute food from quick-commerce kitchens (Bistro, Zepto Café) competes with restaurant delivery for the same snack occasion.
  • Slower growth. Swiggy's food GOV growth slowed from 22.6% in Q4 FY26 to 17.4% in Q1 FY27. At high-teens growth, the pool grows more slowly than the quick-commerce bill it is funding.

The connection to quick commerce is the reason this pool matters so much. Eternal's ₹606 crore of quarterly food profit is six times Blinkit's ₹102 crore and comfortably covers District's ₹62 crore loss and unallocated costs; that is what lets Eternal keep opening 200 dark stores a quarter without raising money. Swiggy's ₹292 crore covers only about 38% of Instamart's ₹778 crore loss, which is why Swiggy has had to raise ₹10,000 crore and still burns cash. In this sub-sector, the weaker food business is also the weaker quick-commerce funder, and the gap compounds.

In plain terms

Food delivery earns the money; quick commerce spends it. Zomato's food profit is big enough to pay for Blinkit's expansion several times over. Swiggy's is not big enough to pay for Instamart's losses, so Swiggy relies on cash raised from investors. Any new cost on food delivery, from a gig levy to a competition ruling, hits the weaker funder hardest.

Who may own the stock: the shareholder register as strategy Deep dive

Indian rules let a foreign-owned e-commerce company run a marketplace but not own inventory. In 2025–26 that turned a cap on foreign shareholding into the most important strategic decision in quick commerce.

Most retail investors would not expect a company's shareholder mix to decide its gross margin. In Indian e-commerce it does. Under the foreign direct investment (FDI) policy set out in DPIIT's Press Note 2 of 2018, which took effect in February 2019, 100% foreign investment is allowed in the marketplace model of e-commerce, but foreign investment is not allowed in the inventory model of multi-brand e-commerce. A marketplace also may not control the inventory of the sellers on it, and the rules limited how much of a platform's sales any single vendor could account for (25% in the 2018 note). These provisions are recalled from the policy text rather than re-verified for this report, but their effect is not in dispute: a company counted as foreign-owned cannot buy and resell groceries itself.

Why ownership decides the inventory model

The test is whether the company is "Indian-owned and controlled" (IOCC): resident Indians must own more than 50% and control the board. A company that meets the test is treated as domestic for FDI purposes, so its downstream investment, such as a quick-commerce subsidiary, can run an inventory model. A listed company with no promoter and a large foreign institutional base fails the test by default. To pass, it must cap foreign ownership below 50%, which in practice means a shareholder resolution setting an aggregate foreign-investment limit (both Eternal and Swiggy chose 49.5%) and then waiting for foreign holdings to fall below it.

It cannot set the selling price (the seller does), it must route goods through seller entities it does not control, and it earns a commission rather than the full retail margin. Owning the inventory lets the platform negotiate directly with FMCG brands for purchase terms, decide assortment and price store by store, bundle advertising with buying, and capture the gross margin itself. Blinkit's experience is that this is worth roughly one percentage point of order value, which at a network margin of 0.6% is the difference between profit and loss. Swiggy guides that the conversion will add about 80 basis points to Instamart's contribution margin, which at its Q1 FY27 level of −0.2% would put the business clearly positive at the contribution line.

The cost: index outflows and a new shareholder base

Capping foreign ownership has a price. Global index providers weight stocks partly by the room foreign investors have to buy them; a hard cap with little headroom can cut a stock's weight. Jefferies estimated about US$400 million (roughly ₹3,400 crore at ₹86/US$) of passive outflows for Swiggy. The visible result at Eternal is a rotation on the register: foreign institutions fell from 39.04% to 33.01% in a year and domestic institutions rose from 30.04% to 36.22%. That is consistent with the foreign-ownership cap at work, though the register does not label the cause.

In plain terms

A foreign-owned Indian platform is allowed to be a shopping mall but not a shop. To become a shop, and keep the shopkeeper's margin, it must make sure Indians own the majority. Eternal and Zepto did this first; Swiggy's shareholders said no in May and yes in August. The prize is about one percentage point of margin; the price is fewer foreign index funds holding the stock.

Why only these companies can do it

The enabling condition is a promoter-less, institution-heavy register in which domestic mutual funds and insurers can absorb the shares foreign holders sell. Eternal, Swiggy and FirstCry have no identifiable promoter. Meesho's founders hold 16.34% and Urban Company's 19.02%, with large VC stakes besides. None of the others needs IOCC status today: Meesho and Nykaa Fashion are marketplaces by design, Nykaa's beauty inventory sits in an entity structured for it (Nykaa's promoters hold 52.07%), and Vaibhav Global sells abroad. But the rule shapes all of them. It explains why Meesho chose a zero-inventory model, why Flipkart and Amazon run marketplaces in India while owning inventory elsewhere, and why the arrival of Flipkart Minutes and Amazon Now as marketplace-model quick-commerce operators competes on different economics from IOCC-structured Blinkit.

What could change

The risk is policy. Traders' bodies such as the Confederation of All India Traders have lobbied against quick commerce and against inventory models in general (recalled), and the IOCC route is a structural interpretation of FDI rules rather than an explicit licence for quick commerce. If DPIIT tightened how IOCC status is tested, or issued an e-commerce policy aimed at quick commerce, the 1P advantage could be challenged. No such move had been announced by October 2026. The other risk is internal: an IOCC company must keep foreign ownership below its cap, which limits how freely foreign investors can buy its shares and can depress its valuation relative to global peers.

In plain terms

The ownership rules explain the shape of the whole industry: why marketplaces dominate, why quick-commerce leaders went through shareholder votes, and why domestic mutual funds now own more of Eternal and Swiggy than foreign institutions do. Watch the RBI's decision on Swiggy and any change in how the government reads the rule.

Industry structure: duopolies, a three-way fight and two global giants

Each segment has consolidated, or is consolidating, to two or three players. The listed companies face their toughest competitors in unlisted subsidiaries of Walmart, Amazon, Reliance and Tata.

Concentration differs sharply by segment, and the listed companies only tell half the story. Food delivery is a duopoly of two listed companies. Quick commerce has three scaled players, only two of them listed, with a fourth and fifth arriving. Value commerce has one listed leader facing two giants' sub-brands. Beauty, kids and home services each have one listed specialist and a mix of unlisted rivals. Vaibhav Global's competitors are in the US.

  • Food delivery: Zomato and Swiggy, about 53:47 on a mixed-basis sum of Q1 FY27 order values, probably 55–57% Zomato like-for-like (estimate). Challengers have failed: Foodpanda exited (2019), Uber Eats India was sold to Zomato (January 2020), Amazon Food shut (2022).

Imports and China

Unlike most retail sub-sectors, imports are a minor issue. Meesho's supply comes mostly from domestic small manufacturers, and more than 80% of its GMV is unbranded. Six business models, six different margin ceilings

The seven companies run six distinct models. Each has its own revenue logic, its own steady-state margin and its own way of failing.

Grouping the companies by model rather than by product is more useful for an investor, because the model decides what the margin can become. A hyperlocal food marketplace and a managed-services marketplace are both "platforms", but one pays a rider per order and the other trains and equips a professional. A value marketplace and a beauty specialist both sell online, but one never owns stock and the other holds 109 days of it. The table sets out the six archetypes and their economics at maturity.

The ₹265 basket: Meesho and the economics of value commerce Deep dive

Meesho charges its sellers no commission and still keeps about 32 rupees of every hundred. Its problem is not the model but the size of the basket: there are only a few rupees of profit in a ₹160 order.

Meesho is the second-largest company in this report by market value (₹1.05 lakh crore) and the purest test of whether a low-price, mass-market Indian marketplace can be profitable. In FY26 it handled ₹70,710 crore of GMV (up 41%) from 267 crore orders placed by 26.4 crore annual transacting users. Net merchandise value (NMV), after cancellations and returns, was ₹41,560 crore. It listed on 10 December 2025 at ₹111 and traded at ₹227 on 16 October 2026, up 105%.

How a zero-commission marketplace earns money

Meesho does not charge sellers a commission on each sale, which is how it attracted about 8.5 lakh small sellers, mostly unbranded manufacturers and traders. It earns instead from three sources. Advertising: sellers pay to rank higher in search and feeds, and in a market of near-identical unbranded products, visibility is everything. Logistics: Meesho charges for shipping, increasingly through Valmo, its own asset-light logistics network that aggregates small courier and sort-centre operators; the primer says Valmo handled about 62% of orders at about 12% lower cost, a figure that could not be verified against a dated filing. Fees: other seller and customer charges. Together these came to 30.4% of NMV in FY26 and 32.0% in Q1 FY27 (₹3,713 crore of revenue on ₹11,614 crore of NMV).

Users, frequency and the tier-2 engine

Meesho's growth comes from both more users and more orders per user. In plain terms

Meesho takes about ₹51 from each ₹160 order without charging the seller commission, mostly through advertising and delivery charges. After paying for delivery and returns it keeps about ₹7, and its overheads still cost about ₹9 an order. It needs to find two or three more rupees per order. Cash, losses and the one-offs

Meesho's reported losses need adjusting. Its FY25 loss of ₹3,942 crore included about ₹2,487 crore of US exit tax when it moved its holding company back to India and about ₹1,346 crore of reorganisation and stock-compensation charges; the underlying FY25 loss was about ₹108 crore. FY26's ₹1,358 crore loss reflects the Valmo investment and growth spending. The quarterly loss has narrowed from ₹491 crore in the December 2025 quarter to ₹166 crore in March 2026 and ₹133 crore in June 2026. Cash from operations was −₹3,875 crore in FY26, distorted by the exit-tax payment and the timing of payments to sellers (an interpretation, not a disclosure); trailing twelve-month free cash flow was −₹537 crore at Q1 FY27. Company cash of ₹6,521 crore at June 2026 gives years of runway at the current burn.

The risks

  • The giants' value brands. Flipkart's Shopsy and Amazon Bazaar copy the low-price, low-commission model, and Reliance's Shein partnership brings global fast-fashion supply.
  • Quick commerce creeping down-market. As quick-commerce assortments widen, some household and personal-care items may migrate.
  • Supply overhang. VC holders (Elevation about 14.5%, Peak XV about 13.6%, Prosus about 13.1%, SoftBank about 9.9% at listing, per the primer) and the lock-in on institutional shares counted toward the promoter contribution, ending around June 2027 (estimate).
  • Valuation. At 7.1 times trailing revenue and 1.39 times FY26 GMV, Meesho is priced for the margin target, not for today. Its shares fell about 6% after Q1 FY27 results on a revenue miss and softer Q2 commentary.

Meesho's Q2 FY27 results are due on 28 October 2026. The numbers to watch are contribution margin (does it hold above 4.5%?), marketplace adjusted EBITDA (how close to zero?) and NMV growth (above 30%?).

Specialists under pressure: beauty holds, diapers leak, home help burns

The four non-platform companies each face quick commerce or its logic in a different way. Nykaa is defending well, FirstCry is losing its most frequent purchase, Urban Company is copying the ten-minute model, and Vaibhav Global's numbers look better than its business.

The common thread is simple. Quick commerce takes high-frequency, low-ticket items: diapers, mass-market shampoo, a lipstick bought on impulse. Specialists defend with what a ten-minute app cannot easily offer: curation, private-label brands, expert advice, stores where customers can try things, and depth of range. How well each specialist does depends on how much of its business is the kind of item a dark store can stock.

Nykaa: the profit compounder, with fashion finally turning

Nykaa (FSN E-Commerce Ventures) is the sub-sector's only consistent profit compounder. Its EBITDA has risen 4.6 times in four years, from ₹164 crore in FY22 to ₹753 crore in FY26, and it generated ₹493 crore of free cash flow in FY26. In Q1 FY27 GMV rose 34% to ₹5,590 crore, revenue rose 29% to ₹2,782 crore, gross margin was 45.9% and EBITDA was ₹236 crore, 8.5% of revenue, up 68%. Profit after tax more than tripled to ₹80 crore.

The important change was in fashion. Nykaa Fashion lost 2.6% of net sales value in FY26 (about −₹37 crore) and turned EBITDA-positive for the first time in Q1 FY27 at +0.1%, while growing GMV 53%. Beauty, which earned 10.3% EBITDA on ₹2,371 crore of net sales value in the quarter, no longer has to carry a loss-making sister business. Nykaa's provisional Q2 FY27 update (early October) reported consolidated GMV growth in the high 20s, beauty NSV growth in the late 20s and fashion NSV growth in the late 40s, 14 new stores taking the total to 338, and store like-for-like growth in the early 20s, the best in six quarters. The shares rose 6.1% on the day.

The India core is profitable and cash-generative. But the market has de-rated it hard: at ₹179 the shares are 61% below the ₹465 IPO price, near the 52-week low of ₹162, and the company is valued at about 1.1 times trailing sales. The bear case is the cohort logic: lose the nappy and you eventually lose the shoes. SoftBank sell-downs, a possible GlobalBees impairment, falling births and the absence of a promoter add to the overhang.

In plain terms

Nykaa sells things people like to browse and try, and its fashion arm has stopped losing money, so it is holding up well against ten-minute apps. FirstCry's best customer habit, buying diapers, is moving to those apps, which cuts its margins even though sales are still growing. The market values Nykaa at about five times its order value and FirstCry at about one times its sales.

Urban Company: a profitable core funding a ten-minute experiment

Urban Company is the only company to have reported the September quarter. In Q2 FY27 its net transaction value rose 39% to ₹1,432 crore and revenue rose 41% to ₹537 crore. Its adjusted EBITDA was −₹144 crore, but that splits into a core business making +₹30 crore and InstaHelp losing ₹174 crore. The net loss widened to ₹182.5 crore from ₹59.3 crore a year earlier. On 16 October its board approved raising up to ₹2,500 crore, subject to shareholder approval; at the current market value that would be about 10% dilution (estimate).

InstaHelp is Urban Company's response to Snabbit and Pronto: a trained helper at the door in 10–15 minutes for cleaning, dishes or laundry, booked by the hour. It handled 6.2 million orders in the quarter, up 63% on the June quarter, and its loss per order has fallen from about ₹447 (Q4 FY26) to ₹346 (Q1 FY27) and ₹279 (Q2 FY27), a 37% reduction in two quarters. But the order value is small: InstaHelp's NTV was ₹71 crore, about ₹115 per order (estimate). A loss of ₹279 per order is about 2.4 times what the customer pays. The unit economics are moving in the right direction and are still far from viable.

Regulation touches every line of an e-retailer's order economics: who may own stock, what riders must be paid into, what tax applies to a delivery fee, and whether a dark store may operate in a residential lane.

GST: who pays the tax on a delivery fee

Since January 2022 the platform, not the restaurant, has collected and paid 5% GST on restaurant food sold through it. From 22 September 2025, local delivery services supplied through e-commerce operators are taxed at 18% under Section 9(5), payable by the platform (recalled; not re-verified). Earlier tax demands on delivery charges, about ₹400 crore for Zomato in December 2023 and similar for Swiggy (recalled), showed how much is at stake in the classification. The same GST changes cut rates on many packaged foods, which helped quick-commerce basket prices.

Competition law: two open files

The Competition Commission has two matters open that bear directly on this group. In food delivery, an investigation triggered by the National Restaurant Association of India in 2021 reportedly found, in the Director-General's report covered by Reuters in November 2024, that Zomato and Swiggy had exclusivity or price-parity arrangements with restaurants; the Commission's final order is pending. In quick commerce, the distributors' federation alleged predatory pricing, below-cost selling and exclusive deals with FMCG brands; the CCI sought evidence in April 2025, and no investigation order or penalty had been found as of October 2026. Either could constrain how platforms price, discount and contract with suppliers.

In plain terms

A platform that keeps ₹24 on a ₹430 food order has little room for new costs. The gig-worker levy, 18% GST on delivery and any competition ruling against discounts or exclusive deals each take a few rupees from that ₹24. None is large on its own; together they could take a meaningful share of the profit unless customers pay more.

Dark stores and consumer protection

Dark stores sit in a grey zone of municipal and food-safety law. FSSAI tightened licensing and inspections in 2025, and Maharashtra's Food and Drug Administration suspended a Zepto dark-store licence in Mumbai in June 2025 over hygiene lapses (recalled). Municipalities in Mumbai, Bengaluru and Gurugram have debated whether dark stores belong in residential areas. Expiry and shrink are now disclosed (Blinkit's 1.8%), which invites scrutiny of how expired stock is handled. On the consumer side, the Department of Consumer Affairs' dark-patterns guidelines (November 2023) and its 2025 campaign against drip pricing target exactly the small "platform fee" and "handling fee" lines that have done most to make food delivery profitable.

In plain terms

The rules that matter most are about who may own stock, what platforms must pay toward riders' social security, and whether small fees and deep discounts survive regulators' scrutiny. Most of these are new or still being written, so the cost is uncertain rather than known, and that uncertainty falls hardest on the businesses with the thinnest margins.

Four cycles in twenty years: subsidy, consolidation, fees, profit

Each segment of Indian e-retail has gone through the same loop: funded players burn cash, the weak exit, the survivors raise fees and turn profitable. Food delivery took about eight years. Quick commerce is in the middle of the loop.

Indian e-retail has a short but crowded history. The first era, from 2007 to 2014, was horizontal: Flipkart (2007), Snapdeal, Myntra and Amazon India (2013) sold books, phones and fashion nationwide. Most of the companies in this report were founded in that era as narrower bets: Zomato as a restaurant-listings site (2008), FirstCry in Pune (2010), Nykaa by Falguni Nayar (2012), Swiggy and UrbanClap (2014). Vaibhav Global, founded in Jaipur in 1989 as a gemstone exporter, pivoted to US and UK TV retail in the mid-2000s.

The second era, 2015–2019, was the subsidy war. Zomato launched food delivery in 2015, Meesho started as a social-commerce app the same year, and venture capital flooded into discounts. Press Note 2 (2018) forced the marketplace-inventory split. By 2020 the weak had gone: Foodpanda exited and Zomato bought Uber Eats India, leaving the food duopoly that still stands.

The third era, 2020–2022, was COVID and the IPO wave. Swiggy launched Instamart in August 2020, Zepto was founded in 2021 and Grofers rebranded as Blinkit in December 2021. Zomato's July 2021 IPO (₹9,375 crore at ₹76) was India's first large consumer-internet listing, followed by Nykaa in November 2021 (₹5,352 crore at ₹1,125 a share before its later bonus). Zomato bought Blinkit in an all-stock deal in August 2022 (about ₹4,447 crore). Then came the 2022 new-age tech crash: Zomato fell below ₹50, and Nykaa issued a 5:1 bonus as its lock-in expired.

The fourth era, 2023 onward, has been about profit and quick commerce. Food delivery turned adjusted-EBITDA positive at both players; Zomato introduced a ₹2 platform fee in August 2023 and earned its first full-year PAT (₹351 crore) in FY24. FirstCry (₹465) and Swiggy (₹390) listed in 2024, Urban Company (₹103, 103.6 times subscribed) and Meesho (₹111, about 79 times) in 2025. Eternal restructured to own Blinkit's inventory, Blinkit turned adjusted-EBITDA positive in Q4 FY26, Swiggy's ownership vote failed and then passed, and Flipkart and Amazon arrived in ten-minute delivery.

Reading e-retail: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every e-retail result is a chain of five numbers: how much order value flowed, how much of it became revenue, how much of that survived the rider and the store, how much survived head office, and how much cash it cost. Learn the chain and the definitions, and any quarterly release becomes readable.

An e-retailer's profit can be written as one equation. Order value is the size of the pipe: how many customers, how often they order and how much they spend each time. The take rate is the share of that order value the platform keeps as revenue. Variable costs are what each order consumes: the rider, the dark-store or warehouse handling, discounts, shrink, packaging and payment. What is left is contribution. Fixed costs (technology, brand marketing, corporate) come off next, leaving adjusted EBITDA. Reported EBITDA then subtracts stock-based compensation, and profit after tax subtracts depreciation (including the right-of-use charge on leased dark stores), interest (including lease interest) and tax, and adds treasury income.

What it is. The rupee value of orders flowing through the platform, the single most-watched number. Formula. Users × orders per user × average order value, measured on the company's chosen basis. The bases. Eternal reports NOV, order value net of customer discounts; it stopped reporting GOV from Q1 FY27. Swiggy reports GOV, which includes taxes, fees and discounts. Meesho reports GMV and NMV (net of cancellations and returns). Nykaa reports GMV and net sales value (NSV). Urban Company reports NTV. FirstCry reports GMV for India and consolidated. Vaibhav Global reports revenue only. Worked example. A ₹500 basket with ₹40 of customer fees and taxes and ₹20 of platform-funded discount might be ₹540 of GOV but ₹480 of NOV, a gap of about 12%; the dossier estimates the same spending can be 15–25% larger on GOV than on NOV. Advertising and fee income

What it is. Money the platform earns from brands, restaurants and sellers for visibility (sponsored listings, banners, sampling), and from customers through platform, delivery, handling and small-cart fees and memberships. Neither carries a rider or a warehouse cost, so almost every rupee falls straight to contribution. Formula. Advertising revenue ÷ order value; fee revenue per order. Few companies disclose either cleanly, so they usually have to be inferred. Worked example. In the ILLUSTRATIVE ₹525 basket, advertising of about ₹26 (5% of NOV) and customer fees of about ₹11 together are ₹37, more than twice the ₹15 contribution. In the ILLUSTRATIVE ₹430 food order, advertising of ₹14 and fees of ₹28 together are ₹42, close to the ₹48 contribution. Drivers. The number of brands competing for shelf space on a small screen, the platform's share of a category's online sales (FMCG brands put more than 70% of their online sales through quick commerce), and customers' tolerance of fees. Benchmarks. No reliable disclosed benchmark; the dossier estimates the platform's combined take on FMCG brands, margin plus advertising, at about 15–25% of their sales. How the companies compare. Meesho is the extreme case: with zero commission, advertising and logistics make up its entire 32% take. Instamart's revenue per order rose ₹11 in one quarter largely through fees and monetisation. Disclosure for the others is n/a. The trap. Advertising looks like free money but it is a cost to the brand or seller; if quick commerce squeezes FMCG margins too hard, or regulators act on drip pricing of fees, the most profitable rupee in the chain is the most exposed.

Shrink and inventory days

What it is. For inventory-led models, shrink is stock lost to expiry, damage and theft; inventory days measure how long stock sits before it sells. Formula. Shrink = value written off ÷ order value; inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods × 365. Worked example. Blinkit's shrink of about 1.8% of ₹17,132 crore of NOV was about ₹308 crore in one quarter, three times its adjusted EBITDA of ₹102 crore. On a ₹525 basket, that is about ₹9.50 an order. Drivers. Perishables share, store age (new stores misjudge demand), forecasting quality, theft control, and supplier return terms. Benchmarks. Shrink: good below 1% of order value, average 1–2%, weak above 2%. Inventory days for specialists: below 90 is lean, 100–135 typical for this group. How the companies compare. Blinkit shrink about 1.8% (average); Nykaa 109 inventory days and FirstCry 133 on screener. Instamart has no shrink line yet because it is still a marketplace; it will once it converts. The trap. Shrink is new as a disclosed number and was reported through press coverage of the shareholder letter; working-capital days also vary by source (Nykaa 22 or 29 days in company material against 4 on screener).

Three conclusions follow. First, throughput is everything: on these assumptions (taking midpoints of take, rider and fixed costs), store contribution breaks even at roughly 1,000–1,100 orders a day; covering the store's share of central technology, brand and corporate costs pushes the breakeven toward the 1,200–1,400 orders Redseer cites. Second, the new-store drag is large: a store doing 600 orders a day loses about ₹6 lakh a month, so a network opening 200 stores a quarter carries hundreds of immature stores at any time, which is why Blinkit's network margin (0.6%) sits far below Delhi NCR's 3.5%. Third, payback is reasonable once mature: with capex of about ₹1–1.5 crore per store including fit-out, cold chain and opening inventory (estimate), a mature store's ₹90 lakh of annual contribution pays back the investment in about 13–20 months, plus the months of losses while it ramps. The model works store by store; the question is whether enough stores reach maturity before competitors open next door and pull throughput down.

The sensitivities are worth knowing. On the mature store, a ₹25 rise in average order value (to ₹550) adds about ₹2.1 lakh of take and ₹1.7 lakh of contribution a month, lifting the store margin from 3.4% to about 4%. A ₹5 rise in the rider payout per order, the kind of increase a gig-worker levy passed through to riders could produce, costs ₹2.1 lakh a month and cuts the margin to about 2.4%. A competitor opening nearby that takes 200 orders a day from the store (down to 1,200) removes about ₹6.3 lakh of take and saves only about ₹3.3 lakh of rider, shrink and payment cost, taking contribution to roughly ₹4.5 lakh, about 2.4%. Density is the asset, and a rival's store is the main threat to it.

In plain terms

A dark store doing 600 orders a day loses about ₹6 lakh a month; the same store doing 1,400 orders a day makes about ₹7.5 lakh. The difference is almost entirely the fixed rent and staff being shared across more orders. That is why every quick-commerce company talks about "density" and why a rival opening a store in the same neighbourhood is so damaging.

Definition traps, collected

  • GOV ≠ NOV ≠ GMV ≠ NMV ≠ NTV ≠ revenue. Each company uses its own measure of the pipe; GOV can be 15–25% larger than NOV for the same spending. Instamart: GOV ₹7,881 crore against NOV ₹5,675 crore for the same quarter.
  • Eternal's revenue jump is accounting. FY26 revenue +169% and Q1 FY27 +182%, against group NOV growth of 54%, because Blinkit now books whole sales. Instamart will show the same optical jump after its conversion.
  • Eternal stopped reporting GOV from Q1 FY27. Historical comparisons must use NOV; older GOV-based figures are not comparable.
  • "Adjusted EBITDA" excludes stock compensation and often lease costs. Eternal ₹555 crore adjusted against ₹594 crore reported; FirstCry ₹486 crore adjusted against ₹262 crore on screener; Swiggy food is profitable while group EBITDA is −₹651 crore.
  • Contribution margin definitions differ between Eternal/Swiggy (after delivery, discounts, store, payment) and Meesho (after logistics and payment). Instamart's Q1 FY27 sign is disputed (−0.2% or +0.2%).
  • Screener "borrowings" are mostly leases. Eternal ₹4,592 crore, Swiggy ₹2,551 crore, Nykaa ₹1,238 crore and FirstCry ₹1,594 crore are dominated by dark-store, warehouse and store leases. Use company cash and treasury.
  • Screener shareholding hides the VCs in "public" or "others", not FII. Swiggy shows FII 8.86% although Prosus alone held about 31% before listing.
  • One-offs swing reported PAT. Meesho FY25 (~₹3,833 crore of exit tax and reorganisation); Urban Company FY25 (a ₹211 crore deferred-tax credit); Vaibhav Global Q4 FY26 tax benefit and Q1 FY27 tariff refund (₹38.4 crore).
  • Annualising quick commerce is hazardous. Store counts, density and seasonality change every quarter; use dated quarterly run-rates, not "FY" figures stitched from different quarters.
  • Period errors. Blinkit's ~274 million orders are quarterly, not monthly; Meesho's ~₹265 is GMV per order, not ₹400–600; Meesho's "₹4,100 crore cash" in its primer is cash from financing, not cash held.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in this sector is defined slightly differently by each company. Before comparing two companies, check the basis (gross or net order value), the period (month, quarter, year), and whether "EBITDA" includes employee share awards and lease costs. Most of the apparent contradictions in published coverage disappear once those three questions are answered.

How to read an e-retail company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Order value and its growth (minute 1). Pull the order-value figure on the company's own basis (NOV, GOV, NMV, NTV) and its year-on-year and quarter-on-quarter growth. Red flag: year-on-year growth healthy but quarter-on-quarter flat in a growing market (Instamart in Q1 FY27).
  2. Segment split (minute 2). For Eternal and Swiggy, separate food, quick commerce and the rest. Is the profitable segment growing faster or slower than the loss-making one?
  3. Take rate or revenue per order (minute 3). Revenue ÷ order value, and revenue per order where given. Rising take with stable growth is healthy; rising take with slowing growth may mean fees are deterring customers.
  4. Contribution margin (minute 4). The order-level test. Red flag: contribution falling while order value accelerates, which usually means discounts are buying the growth.
  5. Adjusted EBITDA by segment, as % of order value (minute 5). Compare with the last four quarters. For quick commerce, check it against net store additions: improving margin while adding stores is the strongest signal.
  6. Stores and throughput (minute 6). Net dark-store additions, and orders per store per day if disclosed. Big additions plus flat order growth means falling throughput.
  7. The reconciliation (minute 7). Adjusted EBITDA to reported EBITDA (stock compensation), then to PAT (depreciation, lease interest, treasury income). Red flag for Eternal-type results: treasury income larger than pre-tax profit.
  8. One-offs (minute 8). Tax credits, tariff refunds, exit taxes, reorganisation charges, inventory write-offs. Vaibhav's Q1 FY27 refund and Q4 FY26 tax benefit are examples.
  9. Cash (minute 9). Cash and treasury against the quarter's burn; runway in years. Any new fund-raise (Urban Company's ₹2,500 crore) and its dilution.
  10. Guidance and competition (minute 10). Management's comments on discounting, new entrants (Flipkart Minutes, Amazon Now) and policy (IOCC approval, gig levy). Red flag: guidance shifting from "margin" to "investment" without a date for breakeven.
In plain terms

Read results from the top of the pipe down: how much was ordered, how much the platform kept, whether each order paid for itself, whether the whole business did, and how much cash it took. Then strip out the one-offs and check the cash. Ten numbers, in that order, tell you most of what the press release will try to tell you in forty pages.

The verdict

Three lessons from abroad

Duopolies earn more. India's food leader earns a higher margin on order value than DoorDash, despite much smaller baskets, because there are only two players and rider costs are low (about ₹40–50 per delivery in the dossier's estimate, against several dollars in the US). Talabat in the Middle East shows similar margins in a similarly concentrated market. The lesson for India is that the food margin is a product of market structure: a credible third player would compress it.

Quick commerce needs Indian conditions. Getir, Gorillas and Gopuff tried ten-minute delivery in Europe and the US and largely failed: riders were expensive, cities less dense, and baskets did not cover the cost of the dark store. India has the density (many orders per square kilometre), cheap last-mile labour and a large population of affluent urban households used to having things delivered. That is why India now has what is probably the world's largest and densest ten-minute market.

A giant can reset the market. Meituan, the most successful local-commerce platform in the world, was profitable and dominant until JD.com and Alibaba launched subsidised instant-delivery offers in 2025, forcing it into a price war that cut its margins and its valuation sharply. The parallel in India is Flipkart (Walmart) and Amazon in quick commerce. Neither needs Indian quick commerce to be profitable on its own, and both have logistics backbones that lower their marginal cost.

Valuation in global context

Indian platforms trade at much higher multiples of order value than their global peers. Eternal's 2.4 times annualised NOV compares with DoorDash at roughly 0.4–0.5 times GOV (approximate). Part of the gap is growth: Eternal's NOV is growing 54% against DoorDash's much slower rate. Part is the Indian market's scarcity premium for listed consumer-internet leaders, with domestic mutual funds now among the largest holders. And part is the bet that quick commerce will become far larger in India than anywhere else.

In plain terms

India's two-player food market is more profitable per order than America's, and ten-minute delivery works in Indian cities in a way it never did in Europe. But China shows that a leader's profits can vanish quickly when a deep-pocketed rival decides to subsidise, which is the risk Flipkart and Amazon pose here. Indian investors pay far more for each rupee of order flow than American investors do for DoorDash's.

Technology and disruption: robots in dark stores, AI in the basket

The threats to this group come less from new technology than from old giants using it, and from formats eating each other: ten-minute food against restaurants, quick commerce against kiranas and specialists, apps against linear TV.

The companies in this report are technology businesses, but the main disruption risks are about competition and format rather than invention. Six deserve attention.

  • Horizontal giants absorbing quick commerce. Flipkart Minutes (about 1,000 stores heading to 1,500), Amazon Now and JioMart can share warehouses, trucks, supplier relationships and customer data with their main e-commerce businesses, lowering the marginal cost of each dark store, and they can subsidise from other profit pools.
  • ONDC. The government-backed Open Network for Digital Commerce lets any seller and any buyer app transact, with lower commissions. It threatens platform take rates in principle, but traction has been limited so far.
  • AI and agentic shopping. If customers increasingly ask an AI assistant to "reorder the usual groceries", the assistant, not the platform's search page, decides which products are shown. That could erode the sponsored-listing advertising that now makes up much of the platforms' profit (an estimate of the risk, not an observed trend).
  • Dark-store automation. Picking robots and micro-fulfilment systems could lift orders per store per day and cut staff costs, improving the economics in Section 14. It would also raise capex per store, favouring the best-capitalised operators.
  • Format cannibalisation. Ten-minute food from quick-commerce kitchens (Blinkit's Bistro, Swiggy Snacc, Zepto Café) competes with restaurant delivery. Quick commerce takes high-frequency items from FirstCry and Nykaa. And quick commerce takes share from kiranas and FMCG distributors: traditional trade fell from 61.8% to 57.2% of FMCG sales in four quarters (NIQ), as covered in the Packaged Foods report.
  • The decline of linear TV. Vaibhav Global must keep migrating customers from TV shopping to apps and digital channels as its audience ages; digital currently earns a lower margin than TV.

What quick commerce is doing to brands

The most visible disruption is to FMCG distribution. For several large brand owners, quick commerce now accounts for the majority of online sales: more than 70% for Britannia and Tata Consumer, 75% for Dabur, 65% for Parle and 58% for ITC (as reported in May 2026 and summarised in the Packaged Foods dossier). This gives the platforms bargaining power over brands' margins and advertising budgets, and it gives the brands a reason to support rival platforms so that no single app controls their online shelf. It is also why the platforms' advertising income, the richest rupee in their model, depends on brands' willingness to keep paying.

Bull vs bear for Indian e-retail

The arguments on both sides are strong, which is why the group's valuation dispersion is so wide.

The bull case rests on structure: two of the segments (food delivery and quick commerce) are consolidating into markets where the leader can earn a durable margin, the companies are well capitalised, and Indian consumption online is growing about a fifth a year with ten-minute delivery taking an ever-larger share. The bear case rests on competition and price: the new entrants are the best-funded retailers in the world, regulation is adding costs to a thin margin, and the market has already paid for much of the upside in the leaders.

The bull case

  • Scale cures margin in quick commerce. Blinkit improved for five straight quarters to 0.6% while adding 200 stores a quarter; its mature cities earn 3.5–5%. Applied to an annual NOV heading above ₹70,000 crore, that is a profit pool of several thousand crore.
  • A cash-generating food duopoly. About ₹898 crore a quarter of adjusted EBITDA growing 17–20%, protected by density and the failure of every third entrant.
  • Ownership unlocks margin. IOCC status lets Blinkit and soon Instamart own inventory, worth about 0.8–1 percentage point of order value.
  • Monetisation levers are early. Advertising, memberships and fees are still growing; Instamart added ₹11 of revenue per order in one quarter.
  • Strong balance sheets. About ₹40,914 crore of company cash and treasury across four platforms; no meaningful financial debt.
  • Specialists hold their ground. Nykaa's beauty earns 10.3% and fashion turned positive; Meesho's contribution recovered to 4.6%.

The bear case

  • Giants with unlimited capital. Flipkart Minutes adds about 100 dark stores a month; Amazon Now is scaling. China's 2025 subsidy war crushed Meituan's margins.
  • Valuation already assumes success. Eternal trades at about 725 times earnings and 2.4 times NOV; Nykaa at 371 times earnings; Urban Company at about 12 times revenue with an EBITDA loss.
  • Regulation adds up. A gig levy of 1–2% of turnover, 18% GST on delivery (recalled), possible CCI orders on exclusivity and predatory pricing, dark-store zoning, and scrutiny of drip-priced fees.
  • The number twos keep burning. Instamart lost ₹778 crore and Zepto about ₹850–900 crore a quarter; Urban Company is raising ₹2,500 crore to fund InstaHelp.
  • Inventory brings new risks. Blinkit's shrink (₹308 crore a quarter) is three times its EBITDA; FirstCry shows what quick commerce does to a specialist's margin.
  • Supply overhangs. VC stakes, lock-in expiries (Meesho around June 2027) and index outflows from foreign-ownership caps.

Where does the balance lie? The operating evidence of the last four quarters favours the bulls on the leaders: Blinkit's margin, Zomato's food margin, Nykaa's fashion turn and Meesho's contribution recovery all moved the right way. The price evidence favours caution: much of that improvement is already in the valuations of Eternal and Nykaa. The swing factor for the whole group is the intensity of competition from Flipkart and Amazon over the next four to six quarters, which no company in this report controls.

In plain terms

The good news is that ten-minute delivery is starting to make money for the leader and food delivery already does. The bad news is that Walmart and Amazon have arrived, regulators are adding costs, and the leaders' share prices already reflect much of the good news. The next year turns on whether the giants force a price war.

Over the next three weeks, five of the seven companies report.
