00

The one-page view: toll gates on a coastline that grows 4% a year

Three listed companies, one of them dominant, earning toll-road margins on a cargo base that grows slowly, under leases that do not last forever.

India moved about 1,668 million tonnes (MMT) of cargo through its ports in FY26: 914.7 MMT through the twelve state-owned "major" ports and 753.1 MMT through the "non-major" ports run under state maritime boards, where almost every private port sits. That total has grown at only 3.8% a year since FY19. Yet the three listed port companies in the NSE 750 grew their combined revenue by 25.9% in FY26 to ₹45,255 crore, at a combined operating margin of 57.8%, and earned ₹14,844 crore of reported profit. Explaining that gap between slow tonnes and fast rupees is most of what it takes to understand this sub-sector.

The listed group is lopsided. Adani Ports and Special Economic Zone (APSEZ) is 85.6% of the trio's revenue, 86.1% of its profit and 82.8% of its market value. It handled 451.0 MMT at its Indian ports in FY26, 27.1% of all Indian cargo, plus 49.8 MMT abroad. JSW Infrastructure handled 122 MT (115 MT in India), about half of it for its own JSW group, and is trying to more than double capacity from 186 to 400 million tonnes a year by FY30. Gujarat Pipavav Port (GPPL) is a single debt-free harbour in Saurashtra controlled by Maersk's APM Terminals; it pays out almost all its profit and its lease from the state ends on 29 September 2028. Any "sector" number in this report is mostly APSEZ, so we show the other two separately wherever it matters.

Five forces shape the business, and the report is built around them:

  • Tonnes grow slowly; revenue grows fast. All-India cargo rose 4.4% in FY26; the trio's revenue rose 26%. The difference is mix (containers, liquids and cars pay more per tonne than coal), acquisitions (NQXT in Australia, Gopalpur, Navkar), international ports, marine services and logistics. It also shows up in profit per tonne: APSEZ's domestic ports made about ₹447 of EBITDA per tonne in Q1 FY27 against about ₹194 at JSW's ports (both indicative, our arithmetic from segment disclosures).
  • The concession clock and the regulator. A port company owns a lease, not a coastline. Gujarat's pioneer leases expire first: Pipavav in September 2028 and Mundra, APSEZ's flagship, in February 2031, with no published framework for extending them. Meanwhile the Major Port Authorities Act 2021 freed tariffs at government ports and the Indian Ports Act 2025, in force from 1 April 2026, moved non-major tariff oversight to new State Maritime Boards. What the state takes matters: GPPL pays a royalty of 3.9% of revenue; JSW pays 18–36% (52.5% at one terminal) at its government-port terminals.
  • Containers and transhipment. Containers are the highest-value cargo and the most contested. APSEZ handled 46% of India's containers in FY26. About three-quarters of India's transhipment boxes are still switched between ships at Colombo, Singapore and other foreign hubs; Vizhinjam, Vadhavan and Great Nicobar are India's attempt to bring them home.
  • Captive versus third-party cargo. JSW's own group provides about 52% of its tonnes but only 38% of its revenue. APSEZ calls 53% of its domestic cargo "sticky". GPPL's biggest customer, Maersk, is also its parent's parent.
  • Geopolitics and trade. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 cut Gulf flows: non-major ports fell 4.1% in March, LPG and LNG at major ports fell 16.6% in April–July, GPPL's liquid cargo halved in Q1 FY27 and JSW's Fujairah terminal handled 0.17 MT against 1.60 MT. Since 24 July 2026 Indian goods face a 10% US Section 301 tariff on top of normal duties.

In plain terms

A private Indian port is a long lease on a stretch of deep water, a breakwater and some berths. Ships pay to come in, cargo owners pay per tonne or per box to move goods across the quay, and once the port is built most costs are fixed, so each extra tonne is mostly profit. That is why margins of 60–75% are normal. The two catches are that India's total cargo grows slowly, so growth has to come from winning share and from higher-paying cargo, and that the lease ends one day, so the value depends on what the state asks for when it is renewed.

2026 has been an eventful year. The war in West Asia began on 28 February and a ceasefire held from 7 April, but Gulf shipping, LPG supply and freight rates stayed disrupted for months. JSW Infrastructure raised ₹7,503 crore in a qualified institutional placement that closed in the June quarter and now has net cash of ₹2,769 crore. On 18 May the US Treasury's sanctions office settled with Adani Enterprises (not APSEZ) for US$275 million over Iranian LPG purchases; in August the US criminal counts against Gautam Adani, Sagar Adani and Vneet Jaain were dismissed with prejudice, and the SEC matter was settled, with the amount disputed. S&P upgraded APSEZ to BBB, level with India's sovereign rating. And in late August the Gujarat Maritime Board sent GPPL a comfort letter to start processing its concession extension, giving it 90 days to file an investment and business plan.

The latest reported quarter for all three companies is Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026). In it, the trio's revenue rose 18.8% to ₹12,598 crore, operating profit rose 14.8% and profit rose only 9.2%, as depreciation and interest climbed faster than sales. No Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) results are out at the time of writing: JSW Infrastructure's board meets on 27 October, APSEZ's on 28 October and GPPL's on 4 November. Some Q2 operating data is already out. GPPL handled 192,000 containers (twenty-foot equivalent units, TEU) in Q2, up 17%, and 360,000 in H1, up 10%. APSEZ's group cargo for H1 FY27 was about 280 MMT, up 15%, as reported by the trade press from its operational update. Government data show major ports handled 472.75 MMT in H1 FY27 (+8.07%) and non-major ports 377.90 MMT (+0.95%).

Valuations tell the market's view of these forces. At the 23 October close APSEZ traded at 29.5 times trailing earnings and about 19 times FY26 EBITDA; JSW Infrastructure at 47.5 times earnings and about 27 times EBITDA, a price for its capacity build; and GPPL at 14.0 times earnings with a 6.6% dividend yield and about 10 times EBITDA, a price for a lease with a maturity date. The rest of this report explains how those numbers are built, what can move them, and which indicators to watch. Related reading in the library: Shipping, Logistics, Shipbuilding and Coal.

01

How a port earns: water, a wall, a quay and a gate

A port sells access to deep water and the right to move cargo across its quay. The owner of the channel earns far more than a tenant on a single berth.

Strip a port down and it is four physical things: a dredged channel deep enough for large ships, a breakwater that calms the sea so ships can work in all weather, berths with cranes, conveyors or pipelines that move cargo between ship and shore, and a yard and gate where cargo is stored and handed to trucks, trains or pipelines. Each piece earns a different charge from a different payer. Ships pay vessel-related charges (port dues, pilotage, towage by tugs, berth hire). Cargo owners pay cargo-related charges, by the tonne for bulk and liquid cargo and by the box for containers, plus storage. Around the port, logistics businesses earn money moving cargo inland, and land near the port can be leased to industry.

The money concentrates in Steps 3 and 4. Who owns Step 4, the developer or a government port authority, decides whether a port earns 70%+ margins or 30–55%.

The most important distinction in Indian ports is between a port developer and a terminal operator. At a private "non-major" port such as Mundra, Jaigarh or Pipavav, the developer holds a 30–50-year concession from a state maritime board for the whole port. It builds the breakwater and channel, owns the berths, sets its own tariffs subject to the board, keeps the vessel-related charges and pays the state a royalty. Gujarat Pipavav's royalty to the Gujarat Maritime Board was ₹45.1 crore in FY26, just 3.9% of its revenue. At a government-owned "major" port, the port authority keeps the channel and the marine services and leases individual berths to private operators under public-private partnership (PPP) contracts, usually for 30 years. The operator bids a share of its revenue to win the berth. JSW Infrastructure's major-port terminals pay revenue shares of 18–36%, and 52.52% at one terminal, according to CARE Ratings. That one difference in who owns the water explains much of the margin gap between the companies.

Once the port is built, costs barely move with volume. Dredging maintenance, staff, power for cranes, insurance and depreciation are mostly fixed; the main variable costs are fuel, some labour and, at government terminals, the revenue share. This is why incremental margins exceed average ones. GPPL added ₹81.6 crore of revenue in Q1 FY27 against the year before and ₹66.7 crore of EBITDA, an incremental margin of about 82% (our arithmetic from its results presentation). APSEZ's domestic ports added ₹764 crore of revenue and ₹515 crore of EBITDA, about 67%. JSW's ports added ₹122 crore of revenue but only ₹40 crore of EBITDA, about 33%, because new terminals were still ramping up and Fujairah lost volume.

The companies are not pure ports any more. APSEZ calls itself an "integrated transport utility": in Q1 FY27 its domestic ports earned ₹5,152 crore of EBITDA out of a group ₹6,541 crore, with international ports, marine services, logistics and SEZ land making up the rest. In plain terms

There are landlords and tenants in Indian ports. A private developer at a state-board port is the landlord of the whole harbour: it keeps the money ships pay to enter, sets its own prices and pays the state a small royalty. A private operator at a government port is a tenant on one berth: the port authority keeps the harbour income and takes a fifth to a half of the operator's revenue as rent. Same cranes, very different margins.

Shipping lines sit at the end of the chain and decide where containers go. A line that makes a port its hub brings transhipment boxes (containers moved from one ship to another) and regular services; a line that leaves can take a large share of a port's container volume with it. That is why the lines co-own terminals: MSC is APSEZ's 50:50 partner at Mundra's CT-3 and has bought 49% of the Ennore container terminal, CMA CGM owns half of CT-4, and Maersk's APM Terminals owns 44% of GPPL. For an investor, a line partner is both a guarantee of volume and a counterparty with strong bargaining power.

02

1,668 million tonnes: a big, steady and slow-growing pool

India's port cargo has grown at 3.8% a year since FY19. Government ports are now growing faster than private ones, for reasons of cargo mix rather than ownership.

India's ports handled 1,667.8 MMT in FY26, up 4.4% on FY25's 1,597.3 MMT. The major ports (the twelve port authorities owned by the Union, from Deendayal at Kandla to Syama Prasad Mookerjee at Kolkata) handled a record 914.7 MMT, up 7.0%. The non-major ports handled 753.1 MMT, up only 1.4%. About 95% of India's trade by volume moves by sea, so port cargo is a direct read of India's physical trade, its energy imports and its coastal shipping.

Three features of this series matter. First, port cargo is not very cyclical in aggregate: only one year in the last eight fell (FY21, Covid, −5.3%). Second, growth is modest: 3.8% a year over seven years, slower than India's real GDP, because India's heaviest cargoes (crude oil and coal) grow slowly, and because a rising share of value is carried in containers, which weigh less per rupee of trade. Third, the leadership has swapped. Non-major ports outgrew the majors through FY24 (FY24: +11.1% against +4.5%), then stalled: +2.6% in FY25, +1.4% in FY26 and +0.95% in H1 FY27. Do not read "major ports beat non-major ports" as "government beat private". Private operators run many of the berths inside major ports (JSW at Paradip, Ennore, Mangalore, Tuticorin and JNPA; APSEZ at Tuna near Kandla, Mormugao and Ennore; PSA, DP World and APM Terminals at JNPA), and that cargo counts as major-port tonnage. The real driver is cargo mix by coast. The non-major total is dominated by Gujarat, whose ports (Mundra, Reliance's Sikka, Dahej, Hazira, Pipavav) handled 64.5% of non-major cargo in H1 FY27, and Gujarat is heavy in crude oil and imported coal. When Tata Power's imported-coal plant at Mundra ran little in FY26, Mundra's own tonnage fell 4.3% to 192.0 MMT; when the war disrupted Gulf crude and LPG, Gujarat's ports felt it first. Deendayal (Kandla), a major port next door, meanwhile grew to 160.1 MMT in FY26 and added 26.85% in H1 FY27, with its container business up sharply.

What the cargo is

Energy dominates the tonnage. In FY26, petroleum, oil and lubricants (crude plus products, including LPG and LNG) were about 469 MMT, or 28% of the total; coal was about 395 MMT (24%); containers about 375 MMT (22.5%, or 25.7 million TEU); iron ore and pellets 112.5 MMT; fertiliser and its raw materials 47.8 MMT; and "other" cargo 262 MMT. Together, crude and coal are about 52% of all Indian port tonnage. That makes ports a play on India's energy imports as much as on its manufacturing exports, and it explains why the 2026 West Asia war and the long-run future of thermal coal both matter so much (Part 12 and Part 19).

Capacity is not the constraint in aggregate. India had about 2,762 million tonnes a year of port capacity in 2025, double the 1,400 MTPA of 2014, against 1,668 MMT of traffic, so national utilisation is about 60%. The major ports alone had 1,680.9 MTPA at the end of FY25 and ran at 50.9%. Yet specific berths are full: Mundra's container terminals hit about 94% utilisation in peak months, GPPL's car terminal ran at about 92% in FY26, and JSW says its existing Kolkata facilities run at 90%. The binding constraint is deep-draft berths for the largest container ships and evacuation by rail and road, not tonnes of nameplate capacity. Average vessel turnaround at Indian ports has fallen from 93 hours to 48 hours over the last decade, according to the government, which has itself unlocked capacity.

In plain terms

India's port cargo grows about as fast as a mature utility's volumes: 4–5% a year in a normal year. Nobody can build a growth company on that alone. The listed port companies grow by taking share from others, by handling higher-paying cargo, by buying ports at home and abroad, and by selling services around the port. When you read a "record cargo" headline, check whether the record is in tonnes, in containers or in revenue; they tell different stories.

The first half of FY27 gives a mixed but resilient picture. All-India cargo was 850.65 MMT, up 4.8%. Coastal cargo (domestic sea trade between Indian ports) was the star, up 14.9% at major ports and 10.8% at non-major ports, helped by the government's coastal-shipping push and coal moved by rail-sea-rail routes. All-India containers rose 9.3% to 13.8 million TEU, despite the new US tariff, partly because shipping lines re-routed transhipment to Indian west-coast ports during the Gulf disruption. Overseas cargo at non-major ports fell 1.2%. The headline rebound is real, but some of it is temporary overflow that may reverse when Gulf hubs normalise.

03

Tonnes crawl, rupees sprint: how 4% cargo becomes 26% revenue

Mix, acquisitions, services and pricing explain why the listed companies grow several times faster than India's cargo, and why their profit per tonne differs by more than two times.

In Q1 FY27 APSEZ's domestic tonnage rose just 2% (115.3 MMT), yet its domestic port revenue rose 12% and its group revenue 19%. JSW's cargo rose 5.6% and its revenue 18%. GPPL's container count rose 3% and its revenue 33%. Over four years the pattern is the same: from FY22 to FY26 APSEZ's revenue compounded at 22.7% a year against 9.6% for its domestic tonnes and 5.9% for all-India cargo. There are five sources of this gap, and an investor needs to know which one is doing the work, because they differ in durability.

1. Mix: not all tonnes pay the same

Different cargo pays different prices across the same quay, and the pecking order varies by port. At APSEZ, management says liquids and containers realise more than coal, and the container and liquid share of its domestic cargo rose about 300 basis points in Q1 FY27, which lifted realisation even with flat tonnes: coal fell from 41.8 to 38.4 MMT while containers rose from 44.4 to 48.1 MMT and liquids from 11.6 to 13.0 MMT. GPPL gave the clearest price list in its Q1 FY27 call: containers about ₹9,500–10,000 per TEU (roughly ₹680–710 a tonne at 14 tonnes a box, indicative), dry bulk ₹650–750 a tonne and liquids ₹650–700 a tonne, with containers 60–65% of revenue and priced in US dollars. Those are far above JSW's average of about ₹390 a tonne, which is mostly coal and iron ore moved for its own group. At GPPL the mix gain in Q1 came from cars: RoRo units rose 53% while liquids, hit by the war, fell 47%. A container berth also turns far more cargo value per metre of quay than a coal berth, which is why ports chase boxes even when the price per tonne is similar.

2. Acquisitions and new geographies

APSEZ's group cargo rose 15% in Q1 FY27 (138.1 MMT), almost all of it from outside India: NQXT, the Australian coal terminal bought from a promoter entity at an enterprise value of A$3.98 billion and consolidated from Q4 FY26, added 10.0 MMT, and Colombo's new West International Container Terminal added 6.9 MMT. International port revenue rose 80% and EBITDA 256%. Over a longer run, APSEZ bought Dhamra, Kattupalli, Krishnapatnam, Dighi, Gangavaram, Karaikal and Gopalpur. JSW consolidated Navkar's logistics business. Acquired growth is real growth, but it costs capital and it does not repeat unless the company keeps buying.

3. Services around the port

APSEZ's marine revenue rose 67% in Q1 FY27 to ₹901 crore as it took its tug and offshore fleet to third parties (135 vessels against 118 a year earlier) and signed a ten-year LNG marine contract in Argentina. JSW's logistics revenue rose 72% to ₹237 crore. These businesses grow faster than ports, but at lower margins: APSEZ marine 44.8%, APSEZ logistics 18.7% (trucking 5.9%), JSW logistics 30.6% (flattered by net booking of rake revenue).

4. Pricing and pass-throughs

At non-major ports the developer sets its own tariffs, and APSEZ also raised revenue in Q1 FY27 from fuel-surcharge pass-throughs, "premium" charges for longer vessel stays and reefer power during the West Asia disruption, routine rate revisions and a weaker rupee. GPPL raised container tariffs from January 2026 and benefits directly from the rupee, because its container revenue is priced in dollars. At ₹94.9 to the dollar in October 2026, that is a tailwind. JSW's take-or-pay contracts with JSW Steel set a floor; management told CARE that Jaigarh and Dharamtar rates have not been cut in ten years.

5. One-offs

Some growth does not repeat. GPPL booked ₹31.6 crore of duty-benefit (SEIS) export-incentive scrips in Q1 FY27 and ₹49.6 crore across FY26; excluding them, Q1 revenue grew about 20%, not 33%, and EBITDA 25%, not 45%. APSEZ's other income in Q1 FY27 included ₹518 crore of dividends from its Mundra container JVs, which is a contra entry against its share of JV profits, not new income. APSEZ also booked a ₹603 crore gain in FY26 from selling 49% of its Ennore container terminal to an MSC affiliate.

04

What fills the berths: energy, steel, boxes, cars and the coast

Port demand is derived demand. Seven flows decide how much cargo India's ports handle, and they are moving in different directions in 2026.

Nobody wants a port for its own sake. Cargo comes because a refinery needs crude, a power plant needs coal, a steel mill needs coking coal and iron ore, a factory needs to ship goods, a carmaker needs to export vehicles, or a coastal state wants to move coal by sea instead of rail. Each flow has its own growth rate, its own price per tonne and its own exposure to shocks. The mix of flows a port sits on matters more than the national total.

Energy imports: half the tonnes

Crude oil, petroleum products, LPG, LNG and coal together make up about 52% of Indian port tonnage. India imports most of the crude it refines, so crude arrives at single-point moorings and jetties in Gujarat (Mundra, Sikka, Vadinar in the Deendayal port area), Paradip and elsewhere. Over FY19–FY25, according to Basic Port Statistics, POL tonnage grew only 1.0% a year at major ports and shrank 1.7% a year at non-major ports, because Reliance's Sikka jetties were flat and refinery runs grew slowly. Coal grew 1.5% a year at majors and 3.0% at non-majors. In H1 FY27, the West Asia war rearranged this pool rather than shrinking it: crude at major ports rose 7.65% (September +40.1%) as refiners rebuilt stocks; thermal coal at non-major ports rose 56.2%; "other coal" at non-major ports fell 30.5%; LPG and LNG fell 20.9% at major ports but rose 7.8% at non-major ports as US-origin cargo replaced Gulf supply at private Gujarat terminals.

Containers: manufactured trade

Containers carry India's manufactured exports and imports: garments, engineering goods, chemicals, electronics, tiles from Morbi, auto parts. All-India container volume was about 25.7 million TEU in FY26. APSEZ describes India's trade as having grown only about 3% in April–June 2026 against a usual 6–8%. Container demand is the most valuable and the most exposed to tariffs and to shipping-line decisions (Part 10).

Cars, fertiliser and food

Auto exports are a small but high-value flow. GPPL handled 229,433 RoRo units in FY26 (+39%) and 123,000 in H1 FY27 (+24%), and Mundra loaded a record 6,548 cars on one vessel in Q1 FY27. GPPL's car terminal, at 250,000 cars a year, was 92% used in FY26, which is why it has signed an MoU with NYK for up to 500,000 cars a year. Fertiliser and its raw materials rose 23.8% at major ports in April–July 2026 and 51.9% at non-major ports in FY26; this cargo depends on monsoon and subsidy decisions. Foodgrain exports swing with government export policy (foodgrains at majors fell 44.7% in FY26, then more than doubled from a small base in early FY27).

The coast and the rails

Coastal cargo is the quiet growth story: it rose 14.9% at major ports and 10.8% at non-major ports in H1 FY27, to about 187 MMT combined. The Coastal Shipping Act 2025, cabotage relaxations and coal moved from eastern mines to western power plants by rail-sea-rail routes all help. Inland, the Western Dedicated Freight Corridor gives double-stack container trains to JNPA, Mundra and Pipavav; railway schemes such as GPWIS (a 10% freight rebate for 15 years on private wagons) and LSFTO (12% for 20 years) encourage port companies to own rakes; and Gati Shakti cargo terminals let them build rail-side depots on railway land. Rail carried about 1.7 billion tonnes of freight in FY26, and the government targets a 45% rail share of freight by 2030. Logistics policy decides which port's hinterland is cheapest to reach, so it shifts share between ports. The library's Logistics report covers the inland side.

In plain terms

Ask what a port's customers make before asking how big the port is. Where ₹100 goes: a capital-heavy business with light running costs

Running a port costs 40–50% of revenue. Paying for it, through depreciation and interest, costs another 12–26%. The three companies split their ₹100 very differently.

A port's costs fall into two buckets. Running costs (staff, power and fuel for cranes, conveyors and tugs, maintenance dredging, repairs, insurance, the royalty or revenue share, and the cost of any trucking or rail services sold) are only 39–51% of revenue for the three listed companies. Capital costs (depreciation of the breakwaters, berths and cranes, and interest on the debt that built them) are large and fixed. The result is a high EBITDA margin but a much lower return on the capital employed, which is typical of infrastructure.

The running costs

  • Operating expenses were ₹11,234 crore at APSEZ in FY26, 29% of revenue, high because trucking and freight-network services are close to pass-through businesses. A pure port runs nearer 15–20%.

India's major ports have run at roughly half their nameplate capacity for years, according to Basic Port Statistics. That looks like a glut, but averages mislead. Who handles India's cargo: one giant, one challenger, many tenants

APSEZ alone handles more than a quarter of India's cargo. Most of the rest moves through government port authorities and global terminal operators that are not listed in India.

India's port industry has an unusual shape: a dozen state-owned landlord port authorities that handle 55% of the cargo but rarely operate the berths themselves; a few global terminal operators (DP World, PSA, APM Terminals) running container berths inside those ports; one dominant private developer, APSEZ, with fifteen ports and terminals across eight coastal states; a second, smaller private group, JSW Infrastructure, built around its parent's steel; captive industrial jetties, above all Reliance's at Sikka; and a long tail of smaller private ports. Only three of these are listed in the NSE 750.

In plain terms

Indian ports are not a crowded market of equals. One private company handles over a quarter of everything and nearly half of all containers; the government owns most of the rest of the capacity but leases the berths out; and the foreign terminal giants operate many of those berths without being listed here. When APSEZ's share moves by a percentage point, that is about 17 million tonnes, more than GPPL handles in a year.

China is a competitor abroad, not at home. Chinese operators such as China Merchants Port (which runs the Colombo International Container Terminal and Hambantota in Sri Lanka) and COSCO compete with APSEZ for Indian Ocean transhipment, while India has kept Chinese capital out of its own ports through security vetting and Press Note 3 since 2020. "Imports" in a port business mean transhipment boxes handled in Colombo or Singapore instead of an Indian port; that leakage is one of the forces in Part 10.

In plain terms

If you own the harbour, the state charges you a few per cent. If you rent a berth in the state's harbour, it charges you a fifth to a half of your sales. The single-asset income port (GPPL) has its own logic. It does not need to grow to be valuable: with ₹708 crore of FY26 operating profit, almost no capex until renewal, no debt and a payout of 98–100%, it is valued on how much cash it can hand back and for how long. The danger is the "how long": a single asset with a lease end date has no other ports to fall back on. The multi-port developer (APSEZ) has the opposite profile: it reinvests most of its cash flow (payout 13%), uses debt, and is valued on growth, network effects and the assumption that leases get renewed. Who sets the rules: a century-old law replaced, a tariff regulator abolished

Two acts in five years rewrote Indian port regulation. Prices at government ports were freed; at state ports, oversight moved to new State Maritime Boards.

India regulates its two kinds of ports differently. The twelve major ports are a Union subject under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways (MoPSW). The non-major ports fall under coastal state governments and their maritime boards, of which the Gujarat Maritime Board, set up in 1982, was the pioneer of private ports. Two recent laws changed both systems, and the details matter for pricing power and for renewals.

The Major Port Authorities Act, 2021

The 2021 Act converted the major port trusts into Port Authorities with commercially minded boards, empowered to set their own scale of rates. It abolished TAMP, which had capped tariffs at major ports and at private terminals inside them through cost-plus formulas (2005), normative tariffs (2008 and 2013) and, from 2018, reference tariffs indexed to inflation. New PPP concessions now set market tariffs. Legacy PPP terminals remain on their indexed reference tariffs, which is one reason older terminals earn less than newer ones. Disputes go to an Adjudicatory Board rather than to the courts first.

The Indian Ports Act, 2025

The 2025 Act replaced the 1908 law for all ports. It creates a statutory Maritime State Development Council, chaired by the Union ports minister with coastal states' port ministers, which advises on a national perspective plan and on tariff-transparency guidelines. It requires each coastal state to set up a State Maritime Board (within six months of commencement) that fixes tariffs at non-major ports, and creates state dispute-resolution committees with appeal to the High Court. It also modernises pollution, safety and disaster-response rules. For private developers, the Act codifies what was already practice in Gujarat: they set tariffs under the concession, subject to the state board. The open question is how active the new boards and the Council's transparency guidelines will be. The Act came into force on 1 April 2026 according to gazette summaries; we could not find a date for its rules.

In plain terms

A private Indian port developer is unusually free to set its prices; there is no national regulator telling it what to charge. Its real constraint is competition from the next port and its contract with the state. Since 2021 government ports have also been freer, which helps their new private tenants but makes the government ports tougher competitors. The rule that matters most for the listed companies is not a tariff rule but a renewal rule, and in Gujarat it has not been written yet.

Concession terms: where the real regulation lives

For a developer, the concession agreement is the regulation. It sets the lease length, the royalty, any revenue share, the capacity the developer must build, and what happens at the end. GPPL's annual report spells out the end-game: if the concession is not renewed, its assets vest in the Gujarat Maritime Board at Depreciated Replacement Value, computed at the expiry date. Gujarat's current port policy offers waterfront concessions of up to 50 years for new ports and six upcoming greenfield ports on 30–50-year build-operate-transfer terms, but it has no published framework for extending the existing pioneer concessions; the state has said Union government approval has been sought. Opposition politicians have alleged that an extension policy would favour the Adani group; the Gujarat government has denied this. Part 9 works through what this means in rupees.

Rail, coast and shipbuilding policy

Ports benefit from policy beyond port law. The GPWIS and LSFTO railway schemes reward private wagon and train ownership with freight rebates; Gati Shakti multimodal cargo terminals give asset-light access to railway land (JSW's Navkar won a ₹48 crore terminal award on 13 October 2026, and JSW's Arakkonam terminal began commercial operations in Q1 FY27); the Coastal Shipping Act 2025 encourages domestic sea trade. The September 2025 maritime package (₹69,725 crore) extends shipbuilding financial assistance to March 2036 (₹24,736 crore), creates the ₹25,000 crore Maritime Development Fund (a ₹20,000 crore investment fund with 49% government participation plus a ₹5,000 crore interest-incentive fund) and adds a ₹19,989 crore shipbuilding development scheme. These matter more for shipyards (see Shipbuilding) than for ports, but they support coastal and port-craft demand.

The regulatory direction is, on balance, supportive for private port companies: more pricing freedom, government ports run more commercially, and a stated national goal of multiplying capacity. The risks are specific rather than general: a renewal framework that raises royalties at legacy Gujarat ports, tariff-transparency guidelines that become de facto caps, political scrutiny of a dominant operator, and environmental clearances that delay new ports (JSW's Keni port is about a year late because Karnataka's coastal-zone authority was not constituted for six to seven months).

The concession clock: Pipavav 2028, Mundra 2031 and the price of renewal Deep dive

A port company owns a lease, not a coastline. Gujarat's pioneer leases run out first, and their renewal terms will set the template for the industry.

Every private port in India operates under a concession: a contract with the state that grants the right to build and run the port for a fixed term, in return for a royalty or revenue share, after which the assets go back to the state. The concession term is therefore the asset life. Most of the time investors ignore it, because 30–50 years is a long time. That is no longer possible for the two Gujarat ports where private Indian ports began. Pipavav's concession ends on 29 September 2028, under two years from now. Mundra's ends on 16 February 2031, about four years away. Gujarat has no published framework for extending them.

Pipavav: the first test

GPPL's concession from the Gujarat Maritime Board dates from 1998 and runs 30 years to 29 September 2028. The company applied for an extension in 2011 and again in 2021. Around 26 August 2026 the GMB issued a comfort letter "for processing its proposal to extend the concession period"; GPPL must now submit its performance record and an Investment and Business Plan for the extended period within 90 days, which points to late November 2026. The shares rose as much as 5.6% to ₹173.75 that day (they closed at ₹157 on 23 October). No terms are public: not the new tenure, not the royalty, not the investment obligation. GPPL has signed a non-binding ₹17,000 crore MoU with the state for investment in containers, liquids and RoRo, conditional on a long-term extension, and says that investment would be its own, not its parent's. It is already building a ₹750–800 crore liquid jetty (CRISIL's estimate) that lifts liquid capacity from 2 to about 5 MMT by March 2027. Management said on 13 August that discussions were "progressing positively" with "no red flags", and that it will give no three-to-five-year guidance until the concession is secured.

Mundra: the bigger prize

Mundra's original concession runs 30 years from 17 February 2001 to 16 February 2031. APSEZ's annual report shows that the sub-concessions for the CT-1, CT-3 (with MSC) and CT-4 (with CMA CGM) container terminals are co-terminus with it, and that the CT-3, CT-3 extension and CT-4 sub-concession agreements still await the state's approval for final signing. The West Basin coal facilities have state approval for a concession "effective till 2040", but the supplementary agreement is still being signed. So 2031 is the cliff for Mundra's container business and most of the port, with coal partly protected to 2040 on paper. APSEZ filed extension requests in 2015 and 2021. Its management said in April that the talks are positive but "we don't control the timing", and in July that "before Mundra concession, we should hear Pipavav concession".

How much hangs on Mundra? Less than a few years ago, but still a lot. Mundra handled 192.0 MMT in FY26, down 4.3%, which was 42.6% of APSEZ's domestic tonnes and, by CARE's count, 38% of group cargo against 66% in FY19. The Mundra cluster earned ₹1,549 crore of EBITDA in Q1 FY27, about 30% of domestic-port EBITDA, at a 70% margin, and Mundra's containers run near capacity in peak months. Management targets a Mundra return on capital "crossing 38–40%". Diversification has reduced the concentration, but Mundra is still the single most valuable port asset in India.

Three levers in any renewal

  • Tenure. A 20-year extension is very different from 30 or 50. Comparators: Kerala gave Vizhinjam 40 years plus a 20-year extension; Andhra Pradesh's concessions at Krishnapatnam and Gangavaram run 30 years plus up to 20; Gujarat's new-port policy allows up to 50.

The leak: transhipment abroad

A large container ship cannot call at every port. Lines run mother ships between hubs and feed smaller ports with feeder ships. India has historically lacked a deep-water hub on the main east-west lane, so boxes bound for or from India are switched at Colombo, Singapore, Port Klang, Jebel Ali or Salalah. APSEZ's management puts the share of India-bound transhipment that happens outside India at 76%. This costs Indian shippers time and money and gives foreign ports the hub fees. Bringing it home is a stated aim of Maritime India Vision 2030.

Vizhinjam: India's first deep-water transhipment port

APSEZ's Vizhinjam port in Kerala, close to the main east-west shipping lane, began commercial operations in December 2024 and was formally commissioned in May 2025. It handled 346,000 TEU in Q1 FY27 (+10%), a record 130,863 TEU in May 2026, and in one month berthed seven vessels of 399 metres or longer, the largest container ships afloat. Phase 2 will take capacity to about 5.7 million TEU. MSC's terminal arm TiL has agreed to invest US$1.397 billion for 49% of Adani Vizhinjam Port, implying about US$2.85 billion for the whole; reported terms split this into US$539 million for the stake and US$858 million tied to finishing the expansion by December 2028, which would also earn a 20-year concession extension. The deal had not closed at the time of writing: it needs Competition Commission clearance and Kerala's prior written consent, because the 2015 concession treats a transfer of 25% or more as a change of ownership, and Kerala said it learned of the deal from the media. APSEZ has said no cash flows until approvals arrive. One data oddity: Vizhinjam's tonnage does not appear in the MoPSW non-major tables (Kerala's boards show 0.028 MMT for H1 FY27), so the all-India totals probably understate cargo by roughly 20 MMT a year (indicative).

Colombo: APSEZ on both sides

APSEZ also runs the Colombo West International Container Terminal in Sri Lanka, which handled 461,000 TEU in Q1 FY27, five times a year earlier, and 6.9 MMT. Phase 2, taking capacity from 24 to 48 MMT, was due to start in October 2026. The neighbouring Colombo International Container Terminal is run by China Merchants Port. Owning both Vizhinjam and a Colombo terminal means APSEZ gains whichever way Indian transhipment flows, although Vizhinjam earns Indian-rupee revenue in an Indian concession while Colombo carries Sri Lankan country risk.

GPPL is controlled by APM Terminals, part of the Maersk group, and Maersk Line alone was about 17% of its FY26 revenue (CRISIL). Its container volume has been flat for three years: about 700,000 TEU in FY24, 694,900 in FY25 and 668,200 in FY26 (−4%), in a year when west-coast ports grew 9% to 19.04 million TEU. It lost about 13 percentage points of relative growth, mainly because Middle East export services were suspended and vessel schedules were unreliable after Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd launched their Gemini network in February 2025. Its "Shaheen" service between the US and Jebel Ali has been suspended since March 2026, a hit of 70,000–80,000 TEU that management assumes lasts all of FY27, replaced from end-June by Maersk's FI2 service. With ad-hoc transhipment calls, Q2 FY27 volume jumped 17% to 192,000 TEU and H1 rose 10% to 360,000. The terminal has 1.35 million TEU of capacity, so it ran at about 49% in FY26, and it could be expanded to 2.4–3.0 million TEU if the concession is extended. Management guides to about 700,000 TEU for FY27.

Its limits are the shipping lines' bargaining power, the government's ability to add deep-water capacity at Vadhavan, and the tariff-sensitive trade that fills the boxes.

Captive cargo: when your biggest customer is your parent

JSW's group supplies half its tonnes at a lower price per tonne; APSEZ calls half its cargo "sticky"; GPPL's parent's group is its biggest customer. Related-party cargo de-risks volume and clouds pricing.

Every listed Indian port company has a structural relationship with some of its customers. For JSW Infrastructure it is explicit: its ports were built to serve JSW Steel and JSW Energy, and the group still supplies about 52% of its tonnes. For APSEZ it is a broader "sticky cargo" base of 53% of domestic volume: preferred-port customers, shipping-line JV partners and Adani-group cargo. For GPPL it is the Maersk group, which owns 44% of the company through APM Terminals and was about 17% of revenue. Captive cargo makes a port's volumes more predictable. It also raises a question that minority shareholders cannot fully answer from the outside: is the captive cargo priced fairly?

CARE expects the group share to rise again as Dharamtar, Jaigarh and the Odisha slurry pipeline, all linked to JSW Steel, come on stream by March 2027. The narrative that third-party share keeps rising is no longer supported by the latest data.

APSEZ: "sticky cargo" and the integrated utility

APSEZ discloses that 53% of its domestic cargo in Q1 FY27 was "sticky": volumes from customers with a preference or commitment to its ports, from container lines that co-own its terminals, and from Adani-group businesses such as power plants and the Mundra industrial cluster. The concentration risks are visible: in Q1 FY27 one customer's plant shutdown cost Krishnapatnam 2.0–2.5 MMT (volume −19%), and Tata Power's imported-coal plant at Mundra ran little through FY26, contributing to Mundra's 4.3% fall. Coal was 38.4 of APSEZ's 115.3 MMT of domestic cargo in Q1 FY27, a third of the total, and much of it is linked to a small number of coastal power plants.

GPPL: the parent as customer and as router

GPPL's related-party relationship is with the Maersk group, which is both its 44% shareholder and its largest customer, and which decides how its own shipping network calls at Pipavav. When Maersk restructured its network into the Gemini Cooperation with Hapag-Lloyd in February 2025, schedule reliability at Pipavav suffered; when Maersk suspended the Shaheen service in March 2026, Pipavav lost 70,000–80,000 TEU; when Maersk added FI2, volume came back. Minority shareholders benefit from the parent's interest in the port, and from its ~100% dividend payout, but they cannot control the routing decisions that drive volumes. The concession renewal adds another twist: the ₹17,000 crore investment plan would be GPPL's own money, so minorities would fund the expansion.

Across all three, the investor's checklist is the same: what share of cargo comes from related or anchor customers, how that share is trending, how related-party revenue compares with related-party tonnes, and whether the contracts are take-or-pay. These lines are in the annual reports' related-party notes and in the investor presentations' cargo splits, and they deserve more attention than they get.

The West Asia stress test: what a Gulf war did to Indian ports Deep dive

The 2026 war reshuffled India's cargo more than it shrank it: liquids, LPG and Gulf-linked trade fell; coal, crude restocking and transhipment rose. Then US tariffs arrived.

On 28 February 2026 war broke out in West Asia. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted, Brent crude peaked near $118 a barrel, LPG cargoes from the Gulf dried up and insurers repriced the region. A ceasefire held from 7 April and Brent eased to about $95, but Gulf shipping, LPG supply and freight schedules stayed disturbed for months. For Indian ports the shock was a natural experiment in exposure: it showed which flows, which ports and which companies depend on the Gulf.

US tariffs: a second shock, from the other side

Trade policy in Washington changed several times in 2026. The US Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February; a Section 122 tariff of 10% applied until 24 July; since then Indian goods have faced a 10% Section 301 tariff on top of normal (MFN) duties (12.5% for China and Vietnam), which is under legal challenge, while Section 232 tariffs of 50% apply to copper and aluminium derivatives. Container tonnes at major ports grew only 0.35% in July, the first month of the new regime, then recovered: September major-port containers rose 20.8%. The tariff seems to have slowed rather than stopped container growth, but the transhipment overflow makes the underlying export trend hard to read. APSEZ said India's trade grew only about 3% in April–June 2026 against a usual 6–8%, and that July was "a little better… not normal".

The money side: rupee, rates and insurance

The rupee weakened to about ₹94.9 to the dollar by 19 October 2026, near a record low of about ₹96.8. Four decades, six shocks: how Indian private ports were built

Gujarat opened the coast in the 1990s; containers, acquisitions and the east coast followed; since 2020 the shocks have come from pandemics, short-sellers, courts, wars and tariffs.

India's private port industry is barely thirty years old. Before the 1990s almost all cargo moved through government port trusts. Gujarat changed that: the Gujarat Maritime Board, set up in 1982, offered private concessions in the 1990s, Pipavav became the first private port (concession 1998) and Mundra followed (concession from 2001). The listed companies are products of that opening and of the consolidation that followed.

Three eras of growth

The Gujarat era (1990s–2000s) built the template: an all-weather, deep-water port on state land, with a rail link to the north-western hinterland, priced freely and paying a small royalty. Pipavav and Mundra both grew on containers as global lines came to India. The consolidation era (2010s–2022) turned APSEZ from a Gujarat company into a national one: it bought or built ports on every coast, while JSW used its parent's steel growth to build bulk ports and win government terminals. APSEZ's domestic share went from about 24% in FY22 to about 27% by FY25 (our arithmetic). The network era (2023 onwards) has been about transhipment, overseas ports, rail and marine services, and enormous capex: APSEZ plans ₹90,000–1,00,000 crore over FY27–31 and 1,000 MMT of domestic capacity by December 2030; JSW 186 → 400 MTPA by FY30; the government Vadhavan and Great Nicobar.

In plain terms

Indian port stocks have been far more volatile than Indian port cargo. Cargo dips only in a pandemic; share prices have swung on short-seller reports, US court cases, lease renewals and equity raises. That gap is useful: when a stock falls on a non-cargo event, the business underneath is usually still compounding at 4–5% volume growth plus mix. When it falls on a lease or a royalty, the business itself has changed.

The 2026 resolution of the Adani legal matters deserves precision, because our earlier primer described it as unresolved. APSEZ's FY26 annual report says the company "has not been named" in the US DOJ and SEC matters, which concerned a non-executive director. Reading a port: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain almost everything about a port company. This section shows how each is built, what drives it, what good looks like, how the three listed companies compare, and where each one misleads.

A port's economics can be written on one line. Revenue is the sum, across every cargo type, of the volume handled times the price per unit, plus the charges ships pay to use the harbour, plus services sold around the port. EBITDA is that revenue minus the royalty or revenue share paid to the state, operating and maintenance costs, staff and power. Because most of those costs are fixed once the port is built, EBITDA rises much faster than revenue when volumes rise. And because the port is expensive to build and the lease is finite, what investors earn depends on how full the port gets, how much it can charge per tonne, and for how long.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE)

Take a port with 50 MTPA of capacity running at 70%, so it handles 35 MT a year. If it earns ₹500 a tonne on average, revenue is ₹1,750 crore. Say its costs are ₹600 crore a year of fixed costs (dredging, maintenance, staff, insurance) plus ₹50 a tonne of variable costs (fuel, power, some labour), or ₹175 crore. EBITDA is ₹1,750 − ₹600 − ₹175 = ₹975 crore, a 55.7% margin and ₹279 a tonne. Now add 10% more cargo, 3.5 MT. Revenue rises by ₹175 crore but costs only by ₹17.5 crore, so EBITDA rises by ₹157.5 crore to ₹1,132.5 crore: a 10% volume gain produces a 16% EBITDA gain, at a 90% incremental margin. The same lever works in reverse, which is why a port that loses a big customer feels it immediately.

The real numbers behave the same way. APSEZ's domestic ports handled 115.3 MMT in Q1 FY27; annualised, that is about 461 MMT. At about ₹604 of revenue and ₹447 of EBITDA per tonne (indicative, our arithmetic), the annual run-rate is about ₹27,860 crore of revenue and ₹20,610 crore of EBITDA. A 1% volume gain at the 67% incremental margin APSEZ achieved in Q1 adds about ₹187 crore of EBITDA a year; a ₹10 a tonne price rise with no extra cost adds about ₹461 crore (both illustrative). Price matters more than volume at the margin, which is why mix and tariffs carry so much of the growth.

In plain terms

Three numbers do most of the work: how full the port is, what it earns per tonne and what the state takes. Fill a built port and profits jump; raise the average price per tonne by shifting to better cargo and profits jump again; let the state take a bigger share and profits shrink by exactly that share. Everything else in this section is a way of measuring one of those three things more precisely.

A word on disclosure before the KPIs. The three companies report very different amounts of detail. APSEZ publishes cargo by port and by cargo type every quarter, TEU by terminal, revenue and EBITDA for six domestic port clusters and four other segments, its market shares and a monthly operational update. JSW publishes cargo by entity, its third-party share, capacity by asset and revenue and EBITDA for ports and logistics, plus a detailed project list with costs and dates. GPPL publishes volumes by cargo type (containers, dry bulk, liquid, RoRo, rail) every quarter, including an early operating update, but no segment revenue: it reports as "one segment", so its per-cargo economics come from management's comments on the earnings call. None of the three publishes revenue or EBITDA per tonne, which is why most of the per-tonne figures in this section are our own arithmetic and labelled indicative. Where a company does not disclose a KPI we show "n/a" rather than estimate it.

1. Cargo volume (MMT): count the right tonnes

What it is: million metric tonnes handled in a period, the base of every other KPI. Formula: tonnes loaded plus unloaded across all berths; containers are converted to tonnes (about 14–15 tonnes per TEU in Indian port statistics: 14.3 at major ports and 15.0 at non-major ports in FY26, our arithmetic). Worked example: APSEZ handled 500.8 MMT in FY26, of which 451.0 MMT was in India and 49.8 MMT abroad. What drives it: India's trade and energy imports, customer plants running or not, share gains, new capacity, acquisitions. Benchmarks: good is domestic growth above 10% a year; average is 4–8%, roughly the national rate (4.4% in FY26); weak is under 3%. APSEZ's domestic growth was 4.7% in FY26 and 2% in Q1 FY27 (weak quarter, blamed on one-off customer outages); JSW's was 4% in FY26 (122 vs 117 MT) and 5.6% in Q1 FY27 (11% ex-Fujairah); GPPL's tonnage rose about 8% a year over FY22–FY26 (indicative).

The trap: group totals mix countries. APSEZ's Q1 FY27 group cargo grew 15% (138.1 MMT) but domestic cargo only 2%; the rest was Australia and Colombo. Always split domestic from international and organic from acquired. JSW's 122 MT includes 6.55 MT handled in the UAE under operating contracts that are excluded from its capacity count.

2. Container throughput (TEU) and transhipment share

What it is: the number of twenty-foot-equivalent container units handled; a 40-foot box counts as two. Formula: boxes lifted across the quay, counting a transhipment box twice (once off, once on) under most terminal conventions. Worked example: GPPL handled 168,659 TEU in Q1 FY27; at about ₹9,750 a TEU that is about ₹164 crore of container revenue for the quarter (illustrative). What drives it: shipping-line services and alliance networks, hinterland trade, rail links, transhipment decisions, draft. Benchmarks: good is TEU growth above 10% a year with a stable transhipment share; average is 5–10%; weak is flat or falling. APSEZ domestic TEU rose 8% in Q1 FY27; GPPL rose 3% in Q1 and 17% in Q2; JSW does not disclose TEU for Mangalore.

The trap: transhipment TEU are double-counted lifts and usually earn less per box than export-import (EXIM) boxes, which also bring rail and storage revenue. A port whose TEU jump because of transhipment overflow, as Mundra's (27% transhipment) and Pipavav's did in Q1–Q2 FY27, may report record volumes with little change in revenue per box. APSEZ capped transhipment to protect EXIM realisation for this reason.

3. Market share: name the denominator

What it is: a company's tonnes or TEU as a share of India's total. Formula: domestic tonnes ÷ all-India tonnes (major plus non-major). Worked example: APSEZ 451.0 ÷ 1,667.8 = 27.0% for FY26 (the company reports 27.1%); in containers, 46% for FY26 and 44.8% for Q1 FY27. What drives it: capacity at the right ports, line partnerships, rail reach, pricing, acquisitions, and competitors' capacity. Benchmarks: for a leader, a share gain of more than 50 basis points a year is good, flat is average and losing share is weak. APSEZ's share was 27.6% in Q1 FY27 against 27.8% a year before, and its container share 44.8% against 45.2%: both slipped slightly. JSW's share is about 6.9% (115 of 1,667.8 MMT, indicative) and GPPL's about 0.8% of tonnes and 2.6% of TEU (indicative).

The trap: the denominator moves. MoPSW's major-port total for FY26 is 914.73 MMT in its table but 915.17 MMT in its press statement; Vizhinjam's tonnage appears to be missing from the non-major series, which may understate the total by about 20 MMT a year (indicative); and only APSEZ publishes its own share. Shares computed by us for JSW and GPPL are indicative.

4. Capacity and utilisation

What it is: nameplate capacity in million tonnes a year (or TEU or cars), and the share of it used. Formula: volume ÷ capacity. Worked example: APSEZ's domestic ports had 653 MMT of capacity at June 2026 and handled 451 MMT in FY26, about 69% (indicative); JSW reports 62.3% for FY26 (FY25 63.9%); GPPL's containers ran at about 49% of 1.35 million TEU, its dry bulk at about 73% of 4 MMT, liquids at about 80% of 2 MMT and cars at about 92% of 250,000 units (indicative). What drives it: volume growth against capacity additions; a big new terminal cuts utilisation for two or three years. Benchmarks: 65–80% is good (busy but not congested); 50–65% average; under 50% weak. Above about 80% a terminal congests, ships wait and customers go elsewhere, so the best ports add capacity before they hit it.

The trap: nameplate capacity is not comparable across companies or cargo types, and aggregate utilisation hides full berths. India's major ports run at about 51% overall, yet Mundra's container terminals touch 94% in peak months and GPPL's RoRo is nearly full. JSW's 186 MTPA excludes the 41 MTPA of UAE terminals it operates but includes cargo from them, which slightly overstates Indian utilisation; and its Jaigarh port runs at only 35–40%.

5. Revenue per tonne (realisation)

What it is: the average price earned per tonne handled. Formula: port revenue ÷ tonnes. Worked example: APSEZ's domestic ports earned ₹6,964 crore on 115.3 MMT in Q1 FY27, about ₹604 a tonne, up from about ₹549 a year earlier (₹6,200 crore on 112.9 MMT), a 10% rise on 2% more tonnes. JSW's ports earned ₹1,208 crore on 31.0 MT, about ₹390, up from about ₹369 (indicative, our arithmetic). GPPL's management gives prices by cargo: ₹9,500–10,000 a TEU, ₹650–750 a tonne for dry bulk and ₹650–700 for liquids. What drives it: cargo mix, tariff changes, vessel-related income, storage, the rupee (for dollar-priced tariffs), fuel pass-throughs and one-off premium charges. Benchmarks: for a container-heavy multi-cargo developer, above ₹550 a tonne is good, ₹400–550 average, below ₹400 weak; for a bulk-heavy port, above ₹400 good, ₹250–400 average (indicative bands).

The trap: companies rarely publish it, so analysts compute it with mismatched numerators and denominators. APSEZ's domestic-port revenue includes harbour (marine and vessel) income; JSW's tonnes include UAE volumes; GPPL's revenue includes export-incentive scrips (₹49.6 crore in FY26). A rising realisation can be mix, price, currency or one-offs, and only one of those is pricing power.

6. EBITDA per tonne: the single best port KPI

What it is: operating profit earned per tonne handled, which combines price, mix and cost efficiency in one figure. Formula: port EBITDA ÷ tonnes. Worked example: APSEZ's domestic ports earned ₹5,152 crore of EBITDA on 115.3 MMT in Q1 FY27, about ₹447 a tonne, up from about ₹411 a year earlier. JSW's ports earned ₹601 crore on 31.0 MT, about ₹194, against about ₹191 a year earlier and ₹202 for FY26 (₹2,462 crore on 122 MT). All are indicative ratios we calculate. GPPL does not report a blended figure; on FY26 operating profit of ₹708 crore and about 14.1 MMT, it is roughly ₹500 a tonne (indicative, derived tonnage). What drives it: everything in KPIs 4 and 5, plus the royalty or revenue share, plus cost control. Benchmarks: for a bulk-heavy port, above ₹250 good, ₹150–250 average, below ₹150 weak; for a container-heavy multi-cargo port, above ₹400 good, ₹250–400 average, below ₹250 weak (indicative).

The trap: the gap between APSEZ and JSW is not mainly efficiency. It is mix (about 42% containers by tonnes against mostly coal and ore), ownership of the harbour (developer against PPP tenant paying 18–52.5% revenue share) and group cargo priced below third-party cargo at JSW. Compare a company with itself over time, or with a peer of the same mix.

7. EBITDA margin: three definitions for one quarter

What it is: EBITDA as a share of revenue. Formula: EBITDA ÷ revenue from operations. Worked example: APSEZ's Q1 FY27 margin was 60.4% on the company's EBITDA of ₹6,541 crore but 57.8% on screener's operating profit of ₹6,253 crore; the ₹288 crore gap matches its share of JV and associate losses, which screener appears to net into expenses (our inference). JSW's was 46.6% on "operating EBITDA" (₹674 crore) and 48.7% on "EBITDA" including other income (₹731 crore). GPPL's was 64.5% reported but about 61% excluding duty-benefit scrips. What drives it: the business mix (domestic ports 74%, marine 44.8%, logistics 18.7% at APSEZ), the royalty, operating leverage and one-offs. Benchmarks: for a developer port, above 65% good, 50–65% average, below 50% weak; for a logistics arm, above 25% good, 12–25% average.

The trap: a falling group margin can be good news. APSEZ's group margin will drift down as logistics and marine grow faster than ports, even if every business improves. Read margins by segment, and check which definition (with or without other income, before or after JV netting) is being quoted.

8. Incremental margin: operating leverage in one number

What it is: the share of each extra rupee of revenue that turns into extra EBITDA. Formula: change in EBITDA ÷ change in revenue, year on year. Worked example: GPPL's Q1 FY27 revenue rose ₹81.6 crore and EBITDA ₹66.7 crore, an 82% incremental margin (part of it from scrips); APSEZ's domestic ports added ₹764 crore of revenue and ₹515 crore of EBITDA, 67%; JSW's ports added ₹122 crore and ₹40 crore, 33% (all indicative). What drives it: whether growth comes from filling existing berths (high incremental margin) or from new terminals with start-up costs, acquisitions, pass-through revenue or low-margin services (low). Benchmarks: above the average margin is good (operating leverage working); at the average is neutral; well below is weak. The trap: it is volatile quarter to quarter and distorted by one-offs; use it as a direction-finder over several quarters, not a single reading.

9. Third-party and "sticky" cargo share

What it is: the share of volume that comes from customers outside the promoter group (JSW's "third-party share") or from customers committed to the port (APSEZ's "sticky cargo"). Formula: third-party tonnes ÷ total tonnes. Worked example: JSW handled 58.8 MT for third parties and 62.8 MT for the JSW group in FY26, a 48% third-party share. What drives it: the parent's expansion (which raises group volume), the port's location and pricing for outside customers, and new third-party-oriented assets such as container terminals. Benchmarks: for a captive-anchored port, above 60% third-party is good (diversified), 40–60% average, below 40% weak. JSW is at 48%, flat since FY25's 49%. APSEZ's sticky share is 53%; GPPL's largest customer, Maersk, was about 17% of revenue.

The trap: these are three different definitions. JSW's is ownership-based, APSEZ's is commitment-based (and includes non-group customers), and GPPL has no published equivalent. A higher third-party share is not automatically better for JSW's minority shareholders if the group cargo is take-or-pay and the third-party cargo is contestable; what matters is the price per tonne on each (Part 11).

10. Concession life remaining

What it is: the years left before the lease on a port ends and its assets revert to the state. Formula: concession end date minus today, ideally weighted by each port's share of EBITDA. Worked example: GPPL has 1.9 years left on 100% of its earnings. At APSEZ, the Mundra cluster (lease to February 2031, 4.3 years) earned ₹1,549 crore of EBITDA in Q1 FY27, about 30% of domestic-port EBITDA and about 24% of group EBITDA (indicative); most of its other ports have decades left. JSW's owned ports run on long concessions (Jaigarh 50 years) and its major-port terminals on about 30 years. What drives it: the date of the original grant, and extensions. Benchmarks: above 25 years good, 10–25 average, under 10 weak, weighted by earnings. The trap: company "average" lives hide concentration. APSEZ's "30+ years" average is true across its portfolio and still compatible with a quarter of its EBITDA sitting on a 2031 lease.

11. Royalty and revenue share

What it is: what the state takes for the right to run the port. Formula: royalty or revenue share paid ÷ revenue (or per tonne). Worked example: GPPL paid a waterfront royalty of ₹45.1 crore in FY26, 3.9% of revenue, or roughly ₹32 a tonne (indicative). At JSW's major-port terminals the revenue share is 18–36% of revenue, and 52.52% at one terminal; at JSW's blended ₹390 a tonne that would be roughly ₹70–140 a tonne (illustrative, since the share applies to each terminal's own revenue). What drives it: the concession contract and, at renewal, negotiation with the state. Benchmarks: for a developer, under 5% is good, 5–10% average, above 10% weak; for a PPP tenant, under 25% is good, 25–40% average, above 40% weak (indicative). The trap: companies seldom disclose it separately; it hides in "operating expenses". APSEZ does not publish a group royalty figure, and a tenant's revenue share may be bid on a different revenue definition from the reported one.

12. Return on capital employed (ROCE)

What it is: operating profit after depreciation (EBIT) as a share of the capital tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ average (equity + debt), or ÷ (fixed assets + working capital). Worked example: screener puts FY26 ROCE at 14.1% for APSEZ, 13.6% for JSW and 28.1% for GPPL. APSEZ reports 16% for FY26 on its own basis (excluding NQXT), with domestic ports at about 23%, marine 13–15%, logistics about 10% and international about 8%; JSW reports 13.4% on a trailing basis at June 2026, down from 17.2% a year earlier as QIP cash swelled the capital base. What drives it: EBITDA per tonne, utilisation, the age of the assets (old, depreciated ports show high ROCE), capital-work-in-progress that earns nothing yet, and cash on the balance sheet. Benchmarks: above 18% good, 12–18% average, below 12% weak. The trap: GPPL's 28% reflects a fully depreciated, debt-free asset with a two-year lease, not superior economics that would survive a renewal with new capex and a higher royalty. APSEZ targets about 20% by FY31, rising one point a year.

13. Net debt to EBITDA

What it is: how many years of operating profit it would take to repay net debt. Formula: (gross debt − cash) ÷ trailing EBITDA. Worked example: APSEZ had gross debt of ₹56,776 crore and cash of ₹12,428 crore at June 2026, net debt of ₹44,348 crore and 1.9 times EBITDA (1.8 times pro forma for NQXT); its guidance ceiling is 2.5 times. JSW was at about 1.2 times at March 2026 but had net cash of ₹2,769 crore at June 2026 after its QIP. GPPL had ₹675.9 crore of cash and no fund-based debt at March 2026. Combined, the three had net debt of ₹40,903 crore (June 2026; GPPL March 2026), about 1.56 times FY26 combined operating profit. What drives it: capex, acquisitions, dividends, equity raises and EBITDA growth. Benchmarks: below 2.0 times good, 2.0–3.0 average, above 3.0 weak for an operating port group. The trap: screener's "borrowings" (₹63,566 crore for APSEZ at March 2026) include lease and other financial liabilities and show no cash, so they overstate net debt; use the company's figure. And a low ratio before a big capex programme says little about the ratio in two years.

14. Capex intensity

What it is: how much a company is investing relative to its size. Formula: capex ÷ revenue (or capex per MTPA of new capacity; see Part 5). Worked example: APSEZ spent ₹15,320 crore in FY26 against guidance of ₹11,000–12,000 crore, about 40% of revenue; it guides ₹12,000–14,000 crore for FY27 (about 30% of the revenue guidance midpoint) and ₹90,000–1,00,000 crore over FY27–31. JSW plans about ₹16,500 crore over FY27–28 (ports ~₹13,000 crore, logistics ~₹3,500 crore), about ₹8,250 crore a year or about 1.2 times its FY27 revenue guidance of ₹6,850 crore; CARE puts its FY27–30 capex at ₹28,000–29,000 crore. GPPL plans about ₹200 crore in FY27, about 16% of trailing revenue (all ratios indicative). What drives it: growth ambition, concession obligations and acquisitions. Benchmarks: there is no single good level; what matters is capex per tonne of new capacity (Part 5) and how fast the new capacity fills. A useful test is capital commitments: APSEZ's contracted commitments rose to ₹25,846 crore at March 2026 from ₹8,248 crore. The trap: capitalised interest on work in progress flatters current profit (screener flags JSW as possibly capitalising interest), and capex guidance is routinely exceeded.

15. Payout and dividend yield

What it is: the share of profit paid as dividends, and the dividend as a share of the share price. Formula: dividends ÷ PAT; dividend per share ÷ price. Worked example: GPPL paid out 98% of FY26 profit (100% in FY25, 103% in FY24); at ₹157 its trailing yield is 6.62%, or about ₹10.4 a share. APSEZ paid ₹7.5 a share for FY26 (₹1,728 crore, 13% payout, 0.44% yield); JSW 12% (0.28% yield). What drives it: the growth pipeline and the balance sheet. Benchmarks: not good or bad in itself: an income port with no growth should pay out close to 100%; a growth port should reinvest. The trap: GPPL's payout is funded partly from cash: its cash fell from ₹1,049 crore to ₹676 crore in FY26 while it paid dividends of about ₹464 crore and started the liquid jetty. With ₹500–600 crore still to spend and 100% payout, cash could fall towards ₹300–400 crore by March 2027 unless the payout eases (indicative).

A new port is a slow-burning investment: it loses money while it fills, earns an ordinary return once it is two-thirds full, and earns a very good return only when it is busy and its debt is paid down. Definition traps, collected

  1. Group cargo is not Indian cargo. APSEZ's 500.8 MMT includes 49.8 MMT abroad; its market share uses domestic tonnes only.
  2. Major versus non-major is not public versus private. Private PPP terminals inside major ports count as major-port cargo.
  3. Every market share needs its denominator stated, tonnes or TEU, all-India or a coast, and which version of the government total.
  4. Per-tonne figures are not comparable across companies without adjusting for mix, harbour ownership and captive pricing.
  5. "EBITDA" has at least three versions: APSEZ company EBITDA (₹6,541 crore in Q1 FY27) against screener's operating profit (₹6,253 crore); JSW's "operating EBITDA" without other income against "EBITDA" with it; GPPL's reported against ex-scrips.
  6. Other income can be a contra entry. APSEZ's ₹853 crore of Q1 FY27 other income included ₹518 crore of JV dividends that reduce the JV share line; underlying other income was about ₹335 crore.
  7. Capacity counts differ. JSW's 186 MTPA excludes 41 MTPA of UAE operating contracts whose cargo it counts; APSEZ's 653 MMT is domestic only (international ~144 MMT).
  8. Third-party share and "sticky cargo" are different measures. One is ownership-based, the other commitment-based.
  9. A company's average concession life can hide a near-term cliff on its most valuable asset.
  10. Screener borrowings are not net debt. They include leases and show no cash.
  11. Reported PAT includes exceptional items: APSEZ's FY26 includes a ₹208 crore net exceptional charge (and a ₹603 crore gain on Ennore whose classification we could not confirm); GPPL's includes an ₹18.8 crore Q4 charge; JSW's includes about ₹68 crore of Fujairah fire loss (company "adjusted PAT" ₹1,644 crore against reported ₹1,547 crore).
In plain terms

Most of the confusion in port analysis comes from mixing bases: group with domestic, company EBITDA with screener operating profit, tonnes that include foreign terminals with capacity that excludes them. Fix the basis first, then compare. When a number looks too good, it is usually a definition, not a miracle.

How to read a port company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Tonnes by port and cargo type (2 minutes). Pull domestic and international tonnes separately, and the split by coal, other dry bulk, containers and liquids. Growth below the national rate (4–5%) is a warning unless explained by a named one-off. Red flag: a single port or customer behind most of the change.
  2. Containers and transhipment. Note TEU growth and, where disclosed, the transhipment share. Transhipment-led growth earns less per box and may reverse.
  3. Revenue by segment, then revenue per tonne. Divide port revenue by tonnes and compare with the same quarter last year. Ask whether the change is mix, tariff, currency or one-offs.
  4. EBITDA by segment, then EBITDA per tonne and incremental margin. Use the same basis as last year (company EBITDA or screener operating profit). Red flag: revenue up, EBITDA per tonne down, with no new terminal ramping up.
  5. Strip the one-offs. Export scrips (GPPL), JV dividends in other income (APSEZ), insurance receipts or fire losses (JSW Fujairah), forex and derivative lines, exceptional items.
  6. Below EBITDA. Depreciation (rising fast when capacity or acquisitions arrive), finance cost, other income (treasury income at JSW should step up after the QIP), share of JV profit, and the tax rate (APSEZ's is low because of tax holidays).
  7. Capacity added and utilisation. Which berths were commissioned, what was spent, and whether capex is ahead of or behind guidance.
  8. Balance sheet. Net debt (company definition), net debt to EBITDA against the stated ceiling (APSEZ 2.5×), cash at the cash-rich companies, and any change in ratings.
  9. Guidance arithmetic. Express the quarter as a share of full-year guidance: APSEZ's Q1 FY27 was about 24.6% of the revenue midpoint and 25.7% of the EBITDA midpoint (on track); JSW's was 21.1% of operating revenue and 22.5% of operating EBITDA guidance, implying a steep second-half ramp.
  10. Concession, regulatory and related-party news. Renewal steps (GMB letters, plan filings), new concessions signed or delayed, tariff orders under the Indian Ports Act, and the related-party revenue line. Red flags: a royalty change, a delayed clearance, or group cargo growing faster than group revenue.

The verdicts

  • APSEZ is winning the market. It has 27% of India's cargo, 46% of its containers, the deepest berths, the line partnerships, the rail network and a growing international and marine business. In plain terms

There is no single winner because the three are playing different games. APSEZ is building a national transport network and has already won the scale game. Outside the listed trio, the biggest competitive forces are government ports and global operators. The global lens: Indian port stocks trade at a premium to the world

APSEZ trades at about 1.3 times ICTSI's EV/EBITDA and more than twice the Chinese state port groups'. JSW has no listed analogue. GPPL is priced like an income port with a maturity date.

Few listed port companies anywhere resemble India's. Most of the world's container terminals are owned by shipping lines (Maersk's APM Terminals, MSC's TiL, CMA CGM's terminals), by state-backed groups (DP World, PSA, China Merchants Port, COSCO Shipping Ports) or by a handful of listed specialists such as ICTSI in the Philippines and Westports in Malaysia. Comparing multiples across them is useful but crude: growth, country risk, asset age and how much of the business is associates or logistics all differ. Technology and disruption: automation, decarbonisation and the size of ships

Ports are slow-changing businesses, but four forces could reshape their volumes and economics over the life of today's concessions.

A port concession runs for decades, so the question is not what changes next quarter but what changes over twenty or thirty years. Four forces stand out: automation and digitalisation that raise the capacity of existing berths; the energy transition, which threatens thermal coal, today a quarter of India's port tonnage; the growing size of container ships and the alliances that deploy them; and the new government mega-ports and rail corridors that redraw hinterlands.

Automation and digital operations

APSEZ describes Vizhinjam as India's first automated port and runs Kaleris terminal-operating software with AI tools across fifteen container terminals; it targets about 91 MMT of extra capacity from efficiency gains by 2030, without new berths. Its upgrade of the port community system cut pre-berthing time by 24%. Nationally, average vessel turnaround has fallen from 93 hours to 48 hours over about a decade, according to the government. Automation helps the incumbents most: it raises the capacity of berths that already exist, at deep-water sites that are hard to replicate.

The energy transition: coal is the long-run volume risk

Coal was about 24% of all-India port tonnage in FY26, and about a third of APSEZ's domestic cargo in Q1 FY27 (38.4 of 115.3 MMT); coal, coke and iron ore were 80% of JSW's tonnes. NQXT, APSEZ's Australian acquisition, is a coal export terminal. As India adds renewable power, imported thermal coal is the cargo most likely to decline over the life of today's concessions, although coking coal for steel should keep growing for longer, and 2026 showed coal can rise when gas is scarce (non-major thermal coal +56% in H1 FY27). The replacement cargoes are LNG, LPG, ammonia (Pipavav's new 36,000-tonne ammonia tank), and eventually green hydrogen derivatives, which need different, often costlier, terminals. APSEZ targets 100% renewable power for its own operations by 2027.

Bigger ships, fewer hubs, stronger lines

The largest container ships now carry about 24,000 TEU and need roughly 16–18 metres of water (approximate). New government ports and corridors

Vadhavan, near Dahanu in Maharashtra, is the biggest supply-side change in prospect: ₹76,220 crore, 298 MTPA including about 23.2 million TEU of container capacity in nine 1,000-metre terminals, with Phase 1 targeted around 2029–30. Its terminals will be bid out under the landlord model, so APSEZ, JSW, DP World or PSA could win them, but it will also compete directly with Mundra and Pipavav for the north-western hinterland via the Western Dedicated Freight Corridor. Great Nicobar's transhipment port, targeted around 2029 for its first phase, will compete with Colombo, Singapore, Klang and, eventually, Vizhinjam. Odisha's proposed Bahuda port (150 MTPA, about ₹21,500 crore under an MoU) adds east-coast bulk capacity. On land, the freight corridors and private rake schemes make inland distance less decisive, which favours ports that own the trains.

In plain terms

The biggest technology change for Indian ports is not robots but geography being redrawn: bigger ships that only a few ports can take, new government mega-ports, and rail corridors that let a port reach further inland. The biggest long-run volume risk is coal. Companies with deep water, rail links, liquid berths and container partners are positioned for all four; companies built around one cargo, one customer or one shipping line are not.

Two smaller risks deserve a line each. Cyber attacks on port systems can halt a terminal for days, and the more automated the port, the bigger the exposure. Sanctions screening has become a live compliance issue: the 2026 OFAC settlement concerned LPG purchases by Adani Enterprises, not port operations, but ports that handle LPG, crude and Gulf cargo now screen vessels and cargo more intensively.

Bull vs bear: compounding toll gates or ageing leases on slow cargo?

The case for and against the sub-sector as a whole, then the swing factors company by company.

The bull case is that Indian ports are scarce, high-margin infrastructure in a growing economy, run by companies that keep finding ways to grow revenue three to five times faster than tonnes. The bear case is that tonnes grow slowly, the easy mix and acquisition gains are behind them, coal is a long-run risk, the leases that underpin the best assets are expiring, and the valuations of the two growth names already assume success.

The bull case

  • Scarce deep water. Only a few ports can take the largest ships; Mundra's containers touch 94% utilisation and GPPL's RoRo 92%, giving pricing power.
  • Mix and services keep compounding. FY22–FY26 revenue CAGR of 22.7% at APSEZ and 23.9% at JSW against 5.9% for all-India cargo; marine and logistics add new revenue pools.
  • Transhipment comes home. 76% of India's transhipment happens abroad; Vizhinjam, Colombo CWIT and future hubs can capture it.
  • Balance sheets are strong. APSEZ at 1.9 times net debt to EBITDA and BBB-rated; JSW and GPPL net cash.
  • Regulation is supportive. Free tariffs at non-major ports, TAMP gone, a national push for capacity, coastal shipping and rail.
  • The legal overhang has eased. OFAC settled with Adani Enterprises; APSEZ was not named in the US case; criminal counts dismissed.

The bear case

  • Slow tonnes. All-India cargo grew 3.8% a year since FY19; APSEZ domestic +2% in Q1 FY27; non-major ports +0.95% in H1 FY27.
  • Lease cliffs. Pipavav ends Sep-2028 and Mundra Feb-2031, with no Gujarat extension framework and unknown royalty terms.
  • Coal dependence. About a third of APSEZ's domestic cargo and much of JSW's; thermal coal faces long-run decline.
  • Capex super-cycle. ₹90,000–1,00,000 crore at APSEZ and ~₹16,500 crore at JSW over two years, with depreciation and interest already outpacing revenue.
  • Valuations assume delivery. JSW at 47.5 times earnings and about 27 times EBITDA; APSEZ at about 19 times EBITDA, well above global peers.
  • Geopolitics and trade. Gulf, Israel and Oman exposure; US tariffs on Indian exports; shipping-line routing power.

What would change the view

  • More bullish: a Pipavav extension at a royalty close to today's 3.9% and a 20-year-plus tenure; APSEZ domestic volume growth back above 6% for two quarters; JSW third-party share rising above 50% with EBITDA per tonne above ₹210; Vizhinjam's MSC deal approved by Kerala and the Competition Commission.
