00

The one-page view: one buyer, one bottleneck, three escape routes

A freight-wagon industry that tripled its output in three years, then lost a fifth of it because the customer could not supply the wheels.

India's listed wagon makers sell a welded steel box on wheels, mostly to one customer, Indian Railways (IR), which decides how many it wants each year through a central tender run by the Railway Board. When IR orders, the factories fill up and profits multiply. When it pauses, revenue falls straight away, because there is no consumer market and no export market big enough to replace it. This report covers the three listed members of the NSE 750 that live inside this cycle: Titagarh Rail Systems, Jupiter Wagons and Texmaco Rail & Engineering. Together they delivered 20,889 wagons in FY26, about 64% of India's estimated output, and booked ₹10,479 crore of revenue.

The past five years have been the most dramatic in the industry's history. National wagon production went from 12,796 in FY22 to a record 41,929 in FY25 after IR placed about 60,000 wagons in June 2022. Combined revenue of the three listed makers nearly tripled, from ₹4,460 crore in FY22 to ₹12,938 crore in FY25, and their combined profit went up roughly tenfold. Then FY26 broke the pattern. The 2022 orders were largely delivered, the next large tender did not arrive, and IR's own wheel factories could not supply enough wheelsets for the wagons that were still on order. IR cut its own wagon additions to a revised estimate of 26,000 against a budget of 38,000. Combined revenue fell 19% and profit fell a third.

Five forces explain almost everything in this sub-sector, and the report is built around them:

  • IR's procurement calendar is the master variable. Revenue tracked IR's ordering almost exactly: +59%, +55%, +18% and then −19% over FY23–FY26. A reported ₹40,000 crore tender for about one lakh wagons has been "deferred… not dropped" (Titagarh, August 2026). Its timing and design decide FY28–FY30.
  • The wheelset is the physical bottleneck. IR requires that wagons it orders carry wheels from its own factories. Its main plant made a record 94,275 wheelsets in FY24, enough for only about 23,600 new wagons even if none went to maintenance. New private forged-wheel plants (Ramkrishna–Titagarh, Jupiter's Odisha project) are the structural change of this cycle.
  • Rail capex is high but plateauing, and wagons are a small slice. The FY27 budget gives IR ₹2,93,030 crore of capex, but rolling stock is ₹65,497 crore of that and wagons perhaps ₹10,000–13,000 crore. With an operating ratio near 99%, almost all of it depends on the Union budget.
  • The three companies are escaping in different directions. Titagarh is turning into a metro and Vande Bharat maker; Jupiter into a wheel, brake and battery-storage group; Texmaco into an EPC contractor, exporter and part-owner of a leasing company.
  • Freight-share policy is the long-run anchor. The National Rail Plan wants rail's share of freight to rise from about 27–28% to 45% by 2030, and loading to reach 3,000 million tonnes. Actual loading grows only 3–4% a year.

The market has already passed its verdict on the three strategies. Titagarh produces about 30% of the group's revenue but carries 44% of its market value; Texmaco produces 42% of revenue but only 20% of the value. Investors are paying for passenger rolling stock and for vertical integration, and are discounting the plain wagon shed. All three have also de-rated sharply in 2026: one-year price returns are −24% (Titagarh), −46% (Jupiter, now at a 52-week low of about ₹173) and −27% (Texmaco). Two things make this sub-sector harder to read than its size suggests. The first is that the reported numbers are on different bases. Titagarh's headline order book of ₹26,635 crore is half joint-venture business that will never appear as its revenue; Texmaco's company "EBITDA" includes other income; IR's tender prices exclude the wheels, which IR supplies itself, so a ₹32-lakh IR wagon and a ₹41.5-lakh private wagon are not different prices for the same thing. The second is accounting quality. Two of the three carry FY26 audit flags: Texmaco's auditors qualified its accounts because it created a ₹700 crore contingency provision directly out of reserves instead of through the profit and loss account, and Titagarh's auditors qualified their opinion on its internal financial controls after the Italian subsidiary Firema had to be written off and FY25 restated. Jupiter has a clean audit but disclosed a SEBI enquiry email about trading in its shares around its FY24 results.

In plain terms

These are factories that build what one customer orders, when it orders, using wheels that customer insists on supplying. That made FY23–FY25 a bonanza and FY26 a drought, even though order books stayed full. The three listed makers are now trying to find customers and products that do not depend on that single tender calendar. Until the next large IR order is signed, their earnings depend on how quickly those alternatives grow, and their valuations still assume the big order will come.

The latest reported quarter is Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026). It looks like a bottom reached on low volume: combined revenue of ₹2,193 crore was 7% higher than a weak Q1 FY26 but 22% lower than Q4 FY26, and the three delivered only 3,479 wagons in the quarter, about 13,900 a year annualised, against stated capacity of roughly 35,000–38,000. Nobody has reported Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) yet. Titagarh's board meets on 29 October 2026; Jupiter and Texmaco have not announced dates and are expected by mid-November. 01

How a wagon earns: from a Railway Board tender to an accepted rake

The product is simple. The route from an order to recognised revenue runs through a regulator, a design approval and a wheel factory the maker does not control.

A freight wagon is a welded steel body sitting on two cast-steel bogies, each carrying two wheelsets (a forged axle with two wheels pressed on), with couplers at each end and an air-brake system underneath. There are a dozen common types, each identified by an IR code. Open, high-sided wagons (the BOXN family) carry coal and ore. Covered wagons (BCN and BCNA) carry cement, fertiliser and grain. Flat wagons (BOST, BOSM, BLC) carry steel coils and containers. Tank wagons (BTPN) carry petroleum, hoppers discharge fly-ash or aggregates from the bottom, and auto-carriers (BCACBM) carry cars. A rake is a full train of 40–60 wagons of one type, and private customers usually buy in rakes.

Wagons are only part of what these companies make. Each has a large steel foundry that casts bogie frames, couplers and other parts, used in its own wagons and sold to rivals and to IR workshops. Titagarh also builds metro cars in stainless steel and aluminium and is the junior partner (with BHEL) building 80 Vande Bharat sleeper trains. Jupiter assembles wheelsets, makes brakes through its Stone India subsidiary and a Czech joint venture, and has moved into truck bodies, containers, electric light commercial vehicles and battery storage. Texmaco runs a rail engineering and electrification contracting business alongside the wagon works. The route from order to revenue

For an IR order, the sequence runs like this. The Railway Board floats a centralised tender for a quantity of a given wagon type. Historically, every qualified bidder has had to match the lowest (L1) price, and the quantity is then split by capacity and past performance, so price competition happens once, at the bid, and market share is allocated rather than won wagon by wagon. Each new design, and each new vendor, needs approval from the Research Designs & Standards Organisation (RDSO) in Lucknow, which means a prototype and trials before series production. IR then issues the wheelsets from its own factories, the Rail Wheel Factory at Yelahanka in Bengaluru and the Rail Wheel Plant at Bela in Bihar. The maker fabricates the body, fits its own bogies and the IR wheelsets, and presents the wagon for inspection by RITES or RDSO. Revenue is recognised only on delivery and acceptance.

A wagon missing its wheelsets is unfinished inventory. That is how a company with a full order book can report falling revenue and rising inventory at the same time, as all three did in FY26.

A private order runs differently. The buyer, such as JSW Steel, a cement company, a container operator or a lessor, registers under one of IR's wagon investment schemes and negotiates price and specification directly with the maker. The maker buys the wheelsets itself (imported or from a private plant) and includes them in the price. If the design is new, it still needs RDSO approval, which is why Jupiter lost about a quarter of its volume in Q1 FY27 while it waited for prototypes of new private designs to be cleared. Exports, such as Texmaco's South Africa and Cameroon orders, follow the customer's own standards and often include long maintenance contracts.

Where the value is captured

Margins rise as you move away from the plain IR wagon. A commodity IR wagon earns roughly 10–12% EBITDA; Titagarh's freight segment earned 12.2% EBIT in FY26, which it attributes to a better mix and foundry efficiency, and management says the figure moves by half to one percentage point depending on the public-private mix, the price variation clause and commodity prices. Components and wheels earn more: Jupiter targets 15% or more on its wheel plant and its brakes, and Titagarh says its wheel joint venture will earn margins "in line with a standard forging business". Passenger cars earn more per unit of factory space: Titagarh's passenger segment made 14.3% EBIT in FY26 and 14.7% in Q1 FY27, though, as Section 19 shows, global passenger builders earn only 4–6%. Long-term maintenance contracts and leasing are the least cyclical slice, and today they are small.

In plain terms

Building the steel box is the low-value part of the job. The money is in the parts that are hard to make (wheels, brakes, propulsion), in the products that carry more value per square metre of factory floor (metro and Vande Bharat cars), and in the contracts that pay for decades (maintenance and leasing). Each listed company is trying to move up one of those ladders.

02

Size and the 2022–25 boom: from 12,800 wagons a year to 41,929 and back

Output more than tripled in three years. Combined profit rose about tenfold. Margins hardly moved.

For the ten years to FY24, India produced an average of 15,875 wagons a year, and the decade before that averaged 13,262 (Press Information Bureau figures). In FY21 and FY22 output was still around 12,500–12,800. Then the 2022 tender cycle arrived, and production rose to 22,790 in FY23, 37,650 in FY24 and a record 41,929 in FY25. FY26 has no published full-year figure, but the Ministry of Railways' year-end review gives 33,703 wagons for January–November 2025. Taking out January–March 2025 (3,651 + 3,880 + 4,135) leaves about 22,000 for April–November 2025, roughly 2,750 a month. Adding December–March at 2,400–2,900 a month gives an INDICATIVE estimate of 31,500–33,600 for FY26, with a central figure of about 32,500, down roughly 22% from FY25.

How big is the market?

Measured by IR's own spending, the wagon market is modest. IR's FY27 budget target of 32,000 wagons at ₹32–40 lakh each implies ₹10,000–13,000 crore a year (INDICATIVE). Private wagon buying has historically run at 7,000–10,000 wagons a year; Texmaco thinks 12,000–15,000 is sustainable, which at ₹40–45 lakh is another ₹3,000–6,500 crore. Wheelsets are a separate pool: Jupiter sizes domestic demand at three to four lakh wheelsets a year (new wagons, replacement and passenger trains), worth roughly ₹7,500–12,000 crore at ₹2.5–3 lakh each (INDICATIVE). Passenger rolling stock adds a market that depends on metro expansion, which the government wants to take from 25 to 50 cities. In dollars, the three listed companies' combined FY26 revenue is about US$1.1 billion at ₹94.9 to the dollar, roughly a third of the US wagon maker Greenbrier's FY25 revenue.

In plain terms

The boom was a volume boom, not a pricing boom. Each wagon earned roughly the same margin in FY25 as in FY22; the factories simply built three times as many. That is why profits can fall faster than revenue when volume drops, and why the next order matters more than any efficiency programme.

A note on the denominators. National production (PIB) counts every wagon made in India: IR orders, private wagons under the investment schemes, public-sector buyers, IR workshop output and some exports. IR's Union Budget target counts only the wagons IR itself adds to its fleet with budget money. In FY25 the gap was about 12,000 wagons (41,929 produced versus 29,889 IR additions), which is broadly the private and export pool. Comparing a company's deliveries with the wrong one of these two numbers is the most common error in writing about this industry, and this report keeps them apart throughout.

03

The master variable: Indian Railways' procurement calendar

Budget targets missed, a revised estimate cut by a third, and a mega-tender that keeps slipping by a quarter or two.

Everything else in this report (margins, return on capital, cash flow and share prices) sits downstream of one decision: how many wagons IR orders, of which types, and when. The Union Budget sets a physical target for IR's own wagon additions each year. The table and chart show how that target has fared. For FY25, IR budgeted 38,000 and achieved 29,889, or 79%. For FY26 it budgeted 38,000 again and then cut the revised estimate to 26,000, 68% of the target. For FY27 the budget estimate is 32,000, 23% above the FY26 revised figure but well below the FY25 and FY26 budgets.

The mega-tender that keeps slipping

The swing factor for FY28–FY30 is a single tender. In May 2026, Mint and Angel One reported that IR was "preparing a tender of ₹40,000 crore for 1,00,000 freight wagons" to be delivered over three to four years, which would mean 25,000–35,000 a year. Arihant Capital expected it within two quarters. By August, Titagarh's management said it had been "deferred by a few months or a couple of quarters, but not dropped" and that priorities had "shifted because of the Middle East war", which began on 28 February 2026 and pushed up crude and freight costs. Texmaco's management, for its part, has said the country needs "no less than 1.5 lakh to 2 lakh wagons" in the near term, and that, as of now, it can "only talk about private and export" orders. No tender notice had appeared on IR's procurement portal by late October 2026.

The reported value implies about ₹40 lakh a wagon (INDICATIVE), noticeably above the ₹32 lakh ex-GST of 2022. Whether that is because steel has risen, because the mix is richer, or because wheels will be included in the price instead of being supplied by IR is not known; no tender document exists yet. The answer matters a great deal. If IR keeps supplying wheels, its own factories cannot equip one lakh wagons in three or four years on top of maintenance demand (Section 05), so either the free-supply rule is diluted or the new private wheel plants are designated as suppliers.

In plain terms

The industry is waiting for one customer to sign one large order. Until it does, each company keeps its sheds running at roughly half speed on private and export work. When it does, the size of the order matters less than two details: how many wagons a year it requires, and who supplies the wheels.

The FY26 cut also shows something about IR's priorities. The wagon target is one of the few items in the railway budget that can be deferred without stopping a train: new lines, electrification and safety work have contractors and political commitments attached, while a wagon order can simply be placed a year later. When the budget tightens (and with an operating ratio near 99%, IR's budget is always tight), wagons are among the first items to be cut, and among the first to be restored when freight traffic or political focus demands it. The next section looks at that budget.

04

Rail capex: high, plateauing, and wagons are a thin slice of it

Railway capital spending nearly tripled in six years and then went flat. Rolling stock is a fifth of it; wagons perhaps a twenty-fifth.

Indian Railways' capital expenditure rose from ₹1,01,985 crore in FY18 to ₹2,62,217 crore in FY24, an average of about 17% a year. Since then it has gone sideways: ₹2,69,361 crore in FY25, ₹2,65,200 crore in the FY26 revised estimate (identical to the budget), and ₹2,93,030 crore budgeted for FY27, up 10.5%. For an industry that depends on IR's wallet, the plateau matters more than the headline. The headline railway budget looks enormous, but the wagon makers live off a small and deferrable corner of it. Rail capex can grow while the wagon order shrinks, as it did in FY26. What the budget says about intent

The FY27 budget did contain signals of long-term intent. It proposed a new east–west Dedicated Freight Corridor from Dankuni in West Bengal to Surat, and sanctioned seven high-speed passenger corridors at about ₹16 lakh crore over their lives. It allotted ₹28,740 crore across ongoing metro and rapid transit projects, mostly outside the railway budget, which matters for Titagarh's metro business. And the wagon target rose from 26,000 to 32,000, which the industry reads as a floor for FY27. But the same documents show what has not changed: freight rates have not been revised since 2018, coal still provides about 48% of freight revenue, and IR's internal resources cannot fund a large wagon order without budget support.

For investors, the practical consequence is that the wagon makers' revenue is a function of three numbers that appear each February: the gross budgetary support to railways, the rolling-stock head within it, and the physical wagon target. The FY28 budget, due on 1 February 2027, will show whether the 32,000 target is being met and whether the mega-tender has been provided for. A rolling-stock head that rises only in line with inflation, combined with lease repayments growing at 40% a year, would mean the mega-tender has to be stretched over more years or financed some other way, for example through private wagon investment and leasing (Section 10). Two other signals are worth tracking. The first is IR's revised estimate for the current year, published with each budget, because the FY26 cut from 38,000 to 26,000 appeared there first. The second is the monthly freight-loading data: if loading accelerates and wagon turnaround stays near five days, the case for more wagons becomes hard to deny.

One more point on scale. Even a ₹40,000 crore, one-lakh-wagon programme spread over four years is about ₹10,000 crore a year, roughly what IR already budgets for wagons. The mega-tender is less a new pot of money than a commitment to keep spending at the FY27 level for several years, with prices and quantities fixed in advance. For the makers, that visibility is worth more than the headline, because it lets them run their sheds at a steady rate and plan wheel and steel purchases. The weakness in FY26 came from the gap between tenders, not from a lack of total spending.

The wheel is the wagon: how a ₹2.5-lakh part stopped a ₹40-lakh product Deep dive

IR insists on supplying the wheels for its own wagons, and its wheel factories could not keep up. Private forged-wheel plants are the structural change of this cycle.

A wagon needs four wheelsets: eight wheels and four axles. For any wagon that Indian Railways orders, those wheelsets must come from IR's own production units. Texmaco's management put it plainly on its Q1 FY27 call: "we are dependent on the supply of Rail Wheel Factory. And that's what is the mandate of the government." The wheelsets are issued to the wagon maker as a "free-supply item", which is why IR's tender prices exclude most of the wheel cost. The rule has a logic (quality control, and keeping IR's own factories busy), but it means that IR's wagon output can never exceed what its wheel and axle lines produce, net of what the existing fleet consumes in replacement.

IR's own capacity: the hidden ceiling

The Rail Wheel Factory at Yelahanka, Bengaluru, is the core of the system. According to a PIB release of September 2024, it produced a record 1,96,265 wheels, 83,054 axles and 94,275 wheelsets in FY24, and a third axle-machining line was being commissioned to add about 40,000 axles a year. The Rail Wheel Plant at Bela in Bihar makes wheels only (axles and assembly happen at RWF). Bela has capacity of one lakh wheels a year and produced a record 42,167 in FY24; snippet reports put FY26 output at 60,699 and the FY27 target at 72,000. SAIL's Durgapur plant makes about 40,000 cast wheels a year. RINL's forged-wheel plant at Rae Bareli, built for about ₹2,300 crore with capacity of one lakh wheels, has "sat largely idle after commissioning disputes" (The Core, February 2026).

The arithmetic is unforgiving. RWF's record 94,275 wheelsets, if every one went into a new wagon and none to replacing worn wheelsets on the existing fleet, would equip about 23,600 wagons. Axles are the tighter constraint: RWF's 1.96 lakh wheels plus Bela's recent output of about 60,000 come to roughly 2.56 lakh wheels, enough for 1.28 lakh wheelsets, but RWF's axle output of 83,054 capped wheelsets at about 83,000 before the new line. Coaches, locomotives and the maintenance of an existing fleet of roughly three lakh wagons draw on the same pool. That is why IR's own wagon additions have topped out at 26,000–30,000 a year (29,889 in FY25, a 26,000 revised estimate in FY26) even when national wagon-building capacity was well above 40,000, and why IR reportedly planned in May 2026 to import 60,000–70,000 axles from China (single-source report, INDICATIVE).

How FY26 went wrong

The shortage was worst in the first half of FY26. Titagarh was "hit over two quarters"; Jupiter's annual report says an "industry-wide shortage of wheelsets constrained wagon production"; and CARE Ratings cites "non-availability of wheel sets from Rail Wheel Factory" for Texmaco. The effect fell hardest on the companies with the most IR business. Texmaco delivered 81.7% of its FY26 wagons to IR and Titagarh's freight book was mostly IR, so both lost volume they could not replace. Jupiter, with a larger share of private orders, could import wheels for those but still lost IR volume. By Q1 FY27, Texmaco said there was no shortage, but by then its IR book had largely run out.

The economics of a wheel

Wheels are a better business than wagons. An ILLUSTRATIVE calculation for the Ramkrishna–Titagarh plant: at roughly ₹1 lakh a wheel and the 2023 tender price of ₹1.88 lakh a tonne, each wheel weighs about half a tonne. A forging-industry EBITDA margin of about 20% (Ramkrishna Forgings itself runs at 20–22%) gives about ₹20,000 per wheel. At full capacity of 2.28 lakh wheels, that is about ₹2,280 crore of revenue and ₹450 crore of EBITDA, of which the IR contract underwrites about ₹800 crore of revenue a year for 20 years. Titagarh's 49% share of the profit after interest, depreciation and tax could be ₹120–140 crore a year at maturity, about equal to Titagarh's entire FY26 reported profit of ₹123 crore. It would be equity-accounted, so it would show up in profit but never in revenue. For Jupiter's plant, ₹2,500–3,000 crore of revenue at 15% EBITDA is ₹375–450 crore a year at maturity (FY29 or later), about 75% attributable to Jupiter after the Lucchini and SIMEST stake, against Jupiter's whole FY26 consolidated operating profit of ₹354 crore. Both figures are ILLUSTRATIVE and depend on ramp-up, price and utilisation.

The component business has its own cycle, as Jupiter showed in Q1 FY27. Its wheelset output, which goes into its own wagons and is sold to rivals and IR, fell from 5,290 in Q4 FY26 to 1,282, a drop of 76% in one quarter, and the subsidiaries swung from about ₹16 crore of EBITDA to roughly break-even. Management did not discuss it on the call. The thesis that a component maker's profit "scales with industry volume" cuts both ways: when industry volume falls, so does the component business.

The endgame: from shortage to surplus?

If both private plants reach capacity and RINL's plant is ever started, India's wheel supply by FY29 could be RWF's ~1.96 lakh, Bela's 0.6–1 lakh, SAIL's 0.4 lakh, RINL's 1 lakh, Ramkrishna–Titagarh's 2.28 lakh and Jupiter's 2 lakh: roughly 8–8.6 lakh wheels a year. Jupiter sizes domestic demand at three to four lakh wheelsets, or six to eight lakh wheels. On those INDICATIVE numbers, the bottleneck could turn into a surplus within three years, which is why Jupiter plans to export half its output and why Texmaco says it is considering wheels but is "not… jumping into a band wagon". Three questions decide who wins. Does IR keep the free-supply mandate, or let wagon makers fit private wheels on IR orders? Does IR buy wheels from the private plants, as it already does through the Ramkrishna–Titagarh contract? And does the mega-tender specify forged 1,000 mm wheels, which favour the new plants? The Ramkrishna–Titagarh contract alone, 80,000 wheels a year, is enough for about 10,000 wagons, which makes Titagarh's joint venture a swing supplier for the very tender Titagarh is bidding on.

In plain terms

For three years the problem was too few wheels. By the end of the decade it may be too many. The companies that own wheel plants (Titagarh through its joint venture, Jupiter directly) gain a higher-margin business and some control over their own supply, but they also take on a new cycle. The investor question shifts from "will there be wheels?" to "who will buy them, and at what price?"

Where ₹100 goes: steel, castings, wheels and an 11% margin

A wagon is mostly bought-in steel. The maker controls the foundry, the shed and the bid price, and little else.

The cost structure of a wagon explains why margins are thin and why they barely change through the cycle. The largest input is steel plate and sections, from SAIL, JSW and ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel, with stainless steel from Jindal Stainless for the newer stainless-bodied designs. On an IR wagon, where the wheels are supplied free by IR, steel and bought-out structural material account for about half the price. The next layer is castings (bogie side-frames, bolsters, couplers and knuckles), which all three companies make in their own foundries, followed by brakes, bearings and springs, mostly bought from Wabtec, Knorr-Bremse, Timken, SKF and NBC. Labour, power and overheads are a small share and are largely fixed in the short run, which is where operating leverage comes from.

Why the margin does not move

Three mechanisms keep the margin in a narrow band. First, IR's price variation clause works both ways: when steel rises, the price rises with a lag; when steel falls, the price falls too, so windfall gains are clawed back. Titagarh says this, together with the public-private mix and commodity prices, moves its freight margin by only half to one percentage point. Second, the L1-matching tender system means that any efficiency one maker achieves tends to be bid away in the next tender, because the lowest bidder sets the price for everyone. Third, the companies have used good years to add capacity, so fixed costs rise with the boom.

The ILLUSTRATIVE operating-leverage arithmetic for Titagarh's freight business shows the potential and the reality. At 600 wagons a month (7,200 a year at ₹39 lakh) freight revenue would be about ₹2,800 crore; at 1,000 a month (12,000 at ₹40 lakh) about ₹4,800 crore. With fixed costs of about ₹250 crore and a variable margin of about 20%, EBIT would rise from about ₹310 crore (11%) to about ₹710 crore (14.8%). Yet management guides an 11–12% freight margin at both ends of that range, which tells you that price competition and the variation clause absorb most of the leverage in practice.

In plain terms

A wagon maker is a converter: it buys steel, adds castings and labour, and sells a finished wagon at a price that is reset with steel costs. It keeps about 11 rupees of every 100 before interest and tax. Getting more volume is the main way to earn more; getting a higher margin requires a different product.

The cash side of the business is less forgiving than the margin. At March 2026 the three carried ₹2,635 crore of receivables (about 92 days of combined revenue) and ₹2,535 crore of inventory, against ₹7,808 crore of equity. In a downturn, inventory rises because wagons wait for wheels or for approvals, receivables stay high because IR pays on its own schedule, and bank guarantees tie up borrowing capacity: Texmaco's bank and corporate guarantees reached ₹1,086 crore at March 2026, about a quarter of its revenue. This is why the group's FY26 free cash flow was negative (−₹373 crore) despite ₹1,076 crore of operating profit, and why rising interest rates (the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026) matter to companies whose interest line already includes guarantee commission. A maker's quality shows less in its margin than in how much of that margin turns into cash, which is the subject of several KPIs in Section 14.

Capacity is not the constraint

The industry has far more capacity than it uses. An industry commentator in February 2026 put theoretical national capacity at about 60,000 wagons a year against output of 35,000–40,000 in the peak. Spare capacity has two consequences. It keeps prices competitive when a tender does arrive, because every qualified builder wants to fill its sheds, and it means that a sudden large order can be absorbed quickly if wheels are available. In 2022–25 national output tripled without any company building a new plant from scratch; Texmaco bought one. The constraint on growth has been the order flow and the wheelsets, not the sheds.

Imports, China and the protected market

Complete wagons are hardly imported at all: HS 8606 imports were ₹86 crore in FY25 and ₹39 crore in FY26, mostly from Korea. RDSO approval, domestic-content rules under the 2017 Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India) Order and IR's design standards act as barriers. Since 2020, rule 144(xi) of the General Financial Rules has restricted bidders from countries sharing a land border with India, which effectively excludes China's CRRC, the world's largest rolling-stock maker, from Indian tenders. The import exposure is in parts, not wagons: ₹4,537 crore of HS 8607 imports in FY26, half of them from China (Section 05). In passenger rolling stock, propulsion, train control and brakes are still largely imported or supplied by multinationals, which is the dependence Titagarh is trying to reduce by building its own propulsion systems.

In plain terms

Competition among wagon builders is real but contained. Foreign rivals are kept out, capacity is ample, and IR's tender rules spread the work among qualified builders at one price. A wagon maker's position depends less on beating rivals and more on how much IR orders and how well it diversifies away from IR.

Concentration of customers is the flip side. Jupiter's annual report shows its three largest customers took 51.6% of FY26 revenue, the largest alone 28.6% (₹834 crore); in FY25 a single customer, almost certainly IR, took 42%. Texmaco sent 81.7% of its FY26 wagons to IR. Titagarh's freight book has been mostly IR. Even after the private-order pivot, a single buyer still accounts for a large share of every company's deliveries.

Three companies, three escape routes from one cycle Deep dive

Titagarh is becoming a passenger-train maker, Jupiter a components and energy group, Texmaco an exporter, contractor and lessor. The evidence so far, and what each route costs.

Every listed wagon maker learned the same lesson from the decade before 2021, when IR ordered 13,000–16,000 wagons a year and the industry lost money: a business with one customer and one product is hostage to that customer's budget. The 2022–25 boom gave all three the cash and the share prices to do something about it. They have chosen different routes, and the FY26 slump is the first real test. This section compares them on the evidence available at Q1 FY27: how much revenue the new businesses already produce, what margins they earn, how much of the order book they represent, and how much capital they need.

Titagarh: from wagon shed to train builder

Titagarh's standalone order book at 30 June 2026 was ₹13,335 crore, of which ₹10,395 crore (about 78%) was passenger rolling stock: 491 metro cars and 1,280 Vande Bharat sleeper cars, plus 74 propulsion sets and 72 traction motors. Freight was only ₹2,470 crore (about 5,300 wagons), and ships about ₹470 crore. The revenue mix is following. Passenger revenue was ₹255.55 crore in FY25 and ₹539.33 crore in FY26 (+111%), while freight fell 25.4% to ₹2,604 crore. In Q1 FY27 passenger revenue reached ₹229.75 crore, 31% of the standalone segment total, up from 11% a year earlier. Car deliveries went from 12 in FY25 to 63 in FY26 and a record 30 in Q1 FY27 alone. Management guides 45–50 cars a quarter for the rest of FY27 and about 200 for the year (within ±10%); 30 plus three quarters at 45–50 gives 165–180, so reaching 200 requires a ramp above 50 a quarter in the second half. Capacity is meant to reach 850 or more cars a year by 2028-29, which at roughly ₹10 crore a car would be about ₹8,500 crore of revenue potential.

Jupiter: from wagon maker to components and energy group

Jupiter's route is vertical integration plus diversification. Its ₹4,550 crore order book at 30 June 2026 contains roughly ₹3,000 crore of wagons (about 80% private, ~7,000 wagons pending), ~₹700 crore of wheelsets, ~₹500 crore of commercial-vehicle bodies and ~₹500 crore of battery energy storage systems (BESS). FY26 unit output shows how broad the business is: 5,498 wagons, 19,939 wheelsets, 26,267 brake discs, 10,909 commercial-vehicle bodies and components, 1,460 containers and 1,321 crossings for railway track. The plan is to make every major wagon component itself and sell the surplus to rivals and IR: wheels and axles from the Odisha plant (Section 05), freight brakes from Stone India (RDSO-approved, commercial production from July 2026, fully captive by the end of 2026, EBITDA-positive from Q3 FY27 and 15% from FY28), and brakes through a 50% joint venture with DAKO of the Czech Republic.

The energy business is the bigger departure. Jupiter Electric Mobility holds a BESS book of "over 500 MWh, valued at more than ₹500 crore", led by a 100 MW / 400 MWh, roughly ₹400 crore, 15-year build-own-operate contract with a West Bengal utility, plus 110 MWh of memoranda of understanding and an October 2026 award for Uttarakhand and a second West Bengal site. Management targets a ₹1,000 crore book by the end of FY27 and ₹5,000 crore in three years, at project returns of 15% or more, but says the business turns EBITDA-positive only in FY28 and that funding (internal accruals or debt) is "not firmed up". On 23 October 2026 Jupiter also signed a non-binding MoU with PESA, the Polish state-owned train maker, for metro, semi-high-speed and high-speed passenger rolling stock.

The evidence so far is mixed. Jupiter's standalone PAT has exceeded its consolidated PAT in every recent quarter, so the subsidiaries and joint ventures (Stone India, the energy business, the wheel plant's pre-operating costs, the DAKO and Kovis ventures) are losing money in aggregate, by about ₹11–12 crore a quarter. The component engine stalled in Q1 FY27, when wheelset output fell 76% quarter on quarter. Exports are effectively nil (₹0.50 crore in FY26), so the export story rests entirely on the future wheel plant. And the balance sheet has turned: borrowings doubled to ₹996 crore at March 2026 and free cash flow was −₹524 crore. Jupiter's route has the highest long-run margin potential and the highest capital need of the three.

Texmaco: exporter, contractor and part-owner of a lessor

Texmaco's route is to keep its position as India's largest wagon shed but sell the output to different customers, and to grow its contracting arm. The change in its freight-car book is the most dramatic shift in the industry: private and export orders were 21% of the book in FY25, 79% at FY26, and 96.4% at Q1 FY27. The order book itself jumped from about ₹5,408 crore at March 2026 to ₹9,923 crore at June 2026, mainly on a South Africa order of about ₹4,100 crore with train operators linked to Transnet: about 2,200 wagons plus 30 diesel locomotives by FY28 (CARE Ratings), with a 15-year maintenance contract worth 30–35% of the value. About half of that revenue falls in FY28; FY27 is prototypes and preparation. A Cameroon order (Camalco, 1,700 open-top wagons in three phases) is also under way.

The contracting arm is in two parts. Infra–Electrical (Bright Power), which builds overhead electrification and power infrastructure, had FY26 revenue of ₹609.68 crore (+66%) and a segment result of 10.8%; it grew another 77% in Q1 FY27. Infra–Rail & Green Energy (track, bridges, signalling, hydro-mechanical) lost ₹27.40 crore on ₹348 crore of revenue in FY26 and was only just profitable (1.4% EBIT) in Q1 FY27; CARE notes ₹765 crore of unbilled revenue in these businesses with delayed recovery. Around this, Texmaco has built a web of partnerships: TrinityRail of the US has joined the leasing joint venture Texmaco Touax (Texmaco's stake falls from 50% to 34%); Wabtec Texmaco (40%) makes air brakes and is developing condition monitoring and a new bogie; Texmaco Nymwag makes tank wagons; Saira Asia does passenger interiors; and a joint venture with RVNL is being incorporated. Management's "Texmaco 2.0" plan aims to double revenue by 2030, with core EBITDA improving 1.2–3 percentage points and a "journey towards the mid-teens".

Freight share: the long-run anchor that moves slowly

The National Rail Plan wants 45% of freight on rail by 2030. Loading grows 3–4% a year and modal share has hardly moved.

The long-run case for more wagons rests on a policy goal. The National Rail Plan (draft December 2020, final 2021) set a target of raising rail's share of India's freight from roughly 27–28% to 45% by 2030, with an average freight-train speed of 50 km/h. The related "Mission 3000 MT" aims for 3,000 million tonnes of annual loading by 2030. Titagarh's management makes the arithmetic point bluntly: loading is "currently at half… they cannot do it with the same number of wagons". If the fleet's productivity stays roughly constant (PRS notes that average wagon turnaround has been about five days and unchanged from FY19 to FY24), doubling tonnage needs roughly double the fleet: from about three lakh wagons to about six lakh, as the National Rail Plan envisages. Spread over five to eight years, that is 35,000–60,000 wagons a year, well above IR's 26,000 in FY26 (ILLUSTRATIVE).

Why rail does not win more freight

Three obstacles explain the gap. The first is price. IR uses freight to cross-subsidise passenger fares, and its freight rates have not been revised since 2018 but remain high relative to road for many commodities; the Economic Survey 2025-26 named high freight rates as a barrier. The second is speed and reliability: average freight speeds on the network are far below the 50 km/h target, and even on the Dedicated Freight Corridors they run at 37–38 km/h against a design aim of 60–65 km/h. The third is the last mile: most shippers need a truck at one or both ends, which adds cost and handling. Road is improving faster, with new expressways and, in time, electric trucks.

The Dedicated Freight Corridors are the main physical fix. The Eastern corridor (1,337 km) is fully commissioned and the Western (1,506 km) is largely complete, and traffic on them rose from 22,389 million net tonne-km in FY23 to 111,898 million in FY25. But PRS reports that the Western corridor "remains underutilised, running 159 trains per day in 2024-25 against its capacity of 380". The FY27 budget proposed a third, east–west corridor from Dankuni to Surat. For wagon makers, the corridors create demand for higher-capacity, faster wagons (Titagarh supplied "hydraulically operated large discharge wagons" for corridor use through Mitsui) and, if used fully, would raise wagon productivity, which cuts both ways: faster turnaround means each wagon carries more freight, so fewer wagons are needed per tonne.

The government's freight targets imply India needs many more wagons. The traffic data say it needs only modestly more each year. Mix shifts that matter more than the total

Even if total tonnage grows slowly, the mix is shifting toward the cargo types that need new, specialised wagons. Coal is about half of loading and 48% of freight revenue, and a long-run energy transition could reduce demand for open BOXN-type wagons. Meanwhile container, automobile, cement, fly-ash, steel and petroleum traffic is growing. Automobile loading on rail rose from 27,522 wagons in FY18 to 1,79,291 in FY24 under the automobile freight-train operator scheme, a six-and-a-half-fold increase. The July 2026 reform package targets fly ash (India produces about 340 million tonnes a year and rail moved about 13 million in FY26) in closed containers, containerised food grain, a single licence for container train operators and rationalised fertiliser tariffs. Each of these creates demand for specific wagon designs, often bought by private owners rather than IR, which is where Section 10 picks up.

From IR's vendor to a lessor: private wagon ownership and leasing Deep dive

Private buyers carried the industry through FY26. The schemes that let them own wagons, and the leasing companies now forming, are the industry's best hedge against IR's calendar.

Wagons on Indian Railways do not have to be owned by IR. For two decades a menu of schemes has allowed customers and investors to buy wagons, run them on IR's network and receive a rebate on freight or a guaranteed allocation of rakes. Until recently this was a niche: perhaps 7,000–10,000 wagons a year at best. In FY26 it became the bridge that kept the sheds open. Texmaco delivered 1,531 private wagons in FY26 (18% of its total) and now has a freight-car book that is 96.4% private and export; about 80% of Jupiter's wagon book is private. National production of about 32,500 against IR's own additions of 26,000 implies that roughly 6,500 FY26 wagons, about a fifth, were bought by someone other than IR (INDICATIVE).

Private orders change the economics in four ways. Realisation is higher, because wheels are included and the designs are specialised: Jupiter's average rose from ₹38 lakh to ₹41.5 lakh between Q1 FY26 and Q1 FY27 on mix. Design and approval risk is higher, because each new type needs an RDSO prototype cycle. Price protection may be weaker, because private contracts are often fixed-price or only partly indexed to steel. And the customer base is wider, which reduces the risk of one budget decision but increases the number of smaller credit exposures. Texmaco thinks 12,000–15,000 private wagons a year is sustainable; that is roughly a third to a half of an IR year.

Leasing: the annuity the industry never had

Leasing is the most interesting development because it turns a one-time sale into a stream of rentals and gives the maker a captive buyer. Texmaco Touax, a joint venture with the French lessor Touax, is the incumbent. In June 2026 it was restructured to bring in TrinityRail, one of North America's largest wagon makers and lessors, with Texmaco's stake falling from 50% to 34%; management confirmed that the three partners may invest about ₹1,800 crore over three to five years to lease about 100 rakes, and calls the result "India's first globally benchmarked railcar leasing platform". Titagarh registered as a Wagon Leasing Company in February 2026 and has a first contract for two rakes. GATX India is buying wagons from Jupiter.

An ILLUSTRATIVE view of what that platform means. ₹1,800 crore for 100 rakes is ₹18 crore a rake, or about ₹36 lakh a wagon at 50 wagons a rake, close to a standard private-wagon price. If the platform bought those rakes evenly over four years, it would place about 25 rakes (roughly 1,250 wagons, ₹450 crore) a year, a useful but modest addition to a market of 30,000–40,000 wagons. For the lessor, leasing is balance-sheet heavy and earns a high EBITDA margin but a low return on equity in the early years, because assets are financed up front and rentals arrive over 20–30 years. For Texmaco, a 34% equity-accounted stake means rentals never show up in its revenue; what it gains is order flow for its sheds and a share of lessor profit.

In plain terms

Private owners and lessors give the wagon makers customers other than IR. They pay more per wagon, they do not depend on IR's wheels, and they order in small, frequent lots. They are not yet big enough to replace an IR tender, but they turned FY26 from a collapse into a slowdown, and leasing could make part of the industry's revenue recurring for the first time.

Three things to watch. First, whether the 2023 pause on new private applications is formally lifted or replaced by the new design and oil-sector rules. Second, how quickly Texmaco Touax deploys the ₹1,800 crore, and whether other global lessors follow Trinity and GATX into India. Third, whether IR itself starts leasing wagons from private lessors instead of buying them through the budget, which would ease the fiscal constraint described in Section 04 and could make the mega-tender partly a leasing programme. The policy direction since 2025 has been consistently toward more private ownership; the open question is scale.

Rules of the game: tenders, approvals, local content and a US tariff wall

The rulebook protects the industry from foreign competition and spreads IR's work among builders. It also caps prices, adds approval delays and now taxes exports of rail parts to the US at 50%.

Most of the rules that shape this industry are written by its customer. The Railway Board runs centralised wagon tenders in which, historically, every qualifying bidder has had to match the lowest (L1) rate, and quantities are then allocated by capacity and past performance. The effect is to make price discovery a one-off event per tender and to guarantee each approved builder a share of the work, which keeps several builders alive but limits how much an efficient one can win. RDSO approval is required for every new design and every new vendor, so entry is slow and each new private design costs months. Contracts carry liquidated damages for late delivery: Titagarh had ₹25.73 crore deducted from FY26 revenue (₹13.37 crore in FY25) and won a ₹15 crore arbitration award on one wagon dispute, which is being challenged. IR also requires performance and advance bank guarantees, which is why finance costs at all three include guarantee commission.

Order books that aren't what they seem Deep dive

Headline backlogs mix revenue the company will book with joint-venture business it never will, decades of maintenance and non-rail projects. An adjusted view changes the ranking.

Order books are the most quoted number in this industry and the least comparable. Each company counts different things, on different time horizons, and some of what they count will never be their revenue. Added together, the three companies' latest standalone or company order books come to ₹27,808 crore (Titagarh ₹13,335 crore + Jupiter ₹4,550 crore + Texmaco ₹9,923 crore), or 2.65 times their combined FY26 revenue. Including Titagarh's joint-venture lines, the total is ₹41,108 crore. This section takes each book apart.

Titagarh: half the headline is joint ventures

Titagarh's headline order book at 30 June 2026 was ₹26,635 crore. Its own presentation splits it into a standalone book of ₹13,335 crore (passenger ~₹10,395 crore, freight ~₹2,470 crore, ships ~₹470 crore) and ₹13,300 crore of joint-venture lines: ~₹6,300 crore for its share of the Ramkrishna–Titagarh wheel joint venture's 20-year IR supply contract, and ~₹7,000 crore for the Vande Bharat maintenance joint venture with BHEL, a 50:50 company that will maintain the 80 sleeper train-sets for 35 years from depots at Shakur Basti (Delhi) and Jogeshwari (Mumbai). Five decades of cycles: losses, rescues, a super-cycle and an air-pocket

The industry has boomed and bust with IR's budgets since the 1970s. The 2022–25 cycle was the largest, and it was financed by the stock market as much as by IR.

Indian wagon building has always been a creature of IR's plans. Texmaco was founded in 1939 by the K. K. Birla group and its rail business later became the separately listed Texmaco Rail & Engineering, now part of the Adventz group of Saroj Kumar Poddar. Commercial Engineers & Body Builders Co (CEBBCO), the company that became Jupiter, was incorporated in Jabalpur in 1979. Titagarh Wagons was incorporated in Kolkata in 1997. All three listed around the last pre-2022 wagon cycle, the freight push of IR's 11th Plan: Titagarh's IPO came in 2008 on most accounts (our company primer gives 2010), and CEBBCO's IPO and Texmaco Rail's listing in 2010.

The decade that followed was hard. IR's wagon procurement settled at 13,000–16,000 a year on average, well below capacity. Titagarh lost ₹147 crore in FY18, ₹23 crore in FY19 and ₹36 crore in FY20, partly because of its Italian subsidiary; Texmaco lost ₹65 crore in FY20; CEBBCO went into distress and was taken over by the Lohia family, who built Jupiter around it. Several public-sector wagon makers, including Burn Standard, Jessop and Bharat Wagon & Engineering, were wound up between 2017 and 2020. The survivors diversified: Titagarh bought Firema in 2015 and won the Pune Metro aluminium-car order, and Texmaco built an EPC business around Kalindee and Bright Power.

Reading a wagon maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

The equation, the twelve numbers that matter, what good looks like, where the three companies stand, and the definition traps that catch most readers.

A wagon maker's results look simple: wagons out, revenue in, a margin of about 10%. In practice, the reported numbers mix units with and without wheels, revenue with and without joint ventures, EBITDA with and without other income, and order books that include maintenance contracts lasting 15 or 35 years. This section builds up from the revenue and profit equation to every native KPI, using round-number examples first and then the three listed companies' actual figures. Each KPI covers what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example, what moves it, a benchmark, how the companies compare and the trap in its definition.

The equation: what the company controls and what it does not

Every wagon maker's revenue can be written as the number of wagons delivered multiplied by the realisation per wagon, plus revenue from everything else (components sold to others, passenger cars, EPC contracts, ships). The number of wagons delivered is itself the smallest of three things: the orders released by customers, the shed's capacity times its utilisation, and the wheelsets available. Profit follows from a contribution margin on that revenue, less fixed costs, less depreciation, interest and tax, plus or minus the share of joint-venture profit.

A worked example in round numbers. Suppose a company delivers 1,500 wagons in a quarter at ₹38 lakh each: ₹570 crore. The single most important number in a wagon maker's results is how many wagons it delivered, because almost every cost except steel stays the same whether the shed is full or half empty. Small changes in volume make large changes in profit, in both directions.

KPI 2 · Capacity utilisation

What it is. Output as a share of what the sheds can build. It tells you how much operating leverage is waiting to be released if orders and wheels arrive. Formula. Annual (or annualised) deliveries ÷ stated annual capacity. Worked example. A 12,000-wagon shed delivering 1,284 in a quarter is running at 1,284 × 4 ÷ 12,000 = 43%. What drives it. The same factors as deliveries, plus the denominator, which companies can and do redefine. Benchmark. Above 80% good, 50–80% average, below 50% weak.

How the companies compare. On FY26 deliveries, Titagarh ran at about 58% of 12,000, Jupiter at about 61% of a 9,000 midpoint, and Texmaco at about 54% of 15,400 installed units. On Q1 FY27 annualised output, the figures fall to about 43%, 51% and 27%. The trap. Capacity is elastic and self-reported. Texmaco quotes 15,400 installed units (including 2,400 at Nymwag), "15,000–16,000" on the call and "2,500–3,000 a quarter" in its presentation; on the presentation figure its Q1 utilisation was 35–42%, not 27%. Jupiter's capacity has been quoted as 6,000, 8,000 and 10,000. Always state whose number you are using.

Utilisation matters for valuation because it tells you how much of a recovery can come without new capital. At roughly 40% utilisation, the three could more than double output on existing sheds if orders and wheels arrived, so a mega-tender would lift profit far faster than revenue, much as FY23–FY24 did. The flip side is that idle sheds still carry depreciation, staff and interest, which is why profit fell faster than revenue in FY26. A company quoting a large capacity it never uses is not showing strength; it is showing the size of its fixed-cost base.

KPI 3 · Realisation per wagon

What it is. Average revenue per wagon delivered, the price term in the equation. Formula. Wagon revenue ÷ wagons delivered. Where a company reports only a freight segment, segment revenue ÷ wagons, which overstates the pure wagon price because it includes castings and other freight sales. Worked example. Titagarh's Q1 FY27 freight revenue of ₹505.31 crore over 1,284 wagons is ₹39.4 lakh; its FY26 freight revenue of ₹2,604 crore over 7,019 wagons is ₹37.1 lakh. What drives it. Customer mix (IR wagons exclude wheels; private and export wagons include them), wagon type (stainless, tank and special-purpose wagons cost more), the price variation clause, and the vintage of the order (a 2022 IR price escalated for steel). Benchmark. There is no universal good or bad; what matters is the trend on a stable mix and whether margin rises with it.

How the companies compare. Jupiter reported ₹41.5 lakh in Q1 FY27, up from ₹38 lakh a year earlier, which management attributes to mix (more non-IR wagons). Texmaco's FY26 annual report gives ₹3,413 crore of wagon revenue on 8,372 wagons, ₹40.8 lakh. Titagarh's ₹37–39 lakh includes castings but is mostly IR wagons without wheels. The trap. Rising realisation can mean the company is buying more wheels for private customers, not that it is earning more per wagon. Roughly ₹9–10 lakh of a private wagon's price is four wheelsets that pass straight through. Also check GST: press reports of award values often include 18% GST, company revenue never does.

KPI 4 · EBITDA (or EBIT) per wagon

What it is. Operating profit earned per wagon delivered. It strips out the wheel pass-through that distorts realisation and is the cleanest measure of whether a maker is earning more on each unit. Formula. Freight-segment EBIT (or EBITDA) ÷ wagons delivered. Worked example. Titagarh's Q1 FY27 freight EBIT of ₹61.68 crore over 1,284 wagons is ₹4.8 lakh a wagon; FY26 freight EBIT of ₹317.93 crore over 7,019 wagons is ₹4.5 lakh. What drives it. Mix, in-house castings, steel buying, price variation timing and, above all, volume, because fixed costs are spread over fewer wagons when output falls. Benchmark. More than ₹5.5 lakh good, ₹3.5–5.5 lakh average, below ₹3.5 lakh weak (INDICATIVE).

How the companies compare. Titagarh, at ₹4.5–4.8 lakh, is in the average band on freight EBIT. Texmaco's freight-car division earned a ₹283.74 crore segment result in FY26 over 8,372 wagons, about ₹3.4 lakh a wagon, but the division also includes the foundry and components, so the pure wagon figure is uncertain. Jupiter reports a single segment, so the only calculable figure is consolidated operating profit ÷ wagons: ₹6.4 lakh in FY26 and ₹5.7 lakh in Q1 FY27, which overstates the wagon margin because it includes wheelsets, CV bodies and everything else. The trap. Segment EBIT is before unallocated corporate costs, and different companies allocate differently. Dividing a consolidated number by wagons gives a figure that looks comparable and is not.

KPI 5 · EBITDA margin, excluding other income

What it is. Operating profit as a percentage of revenue, before depreciation, interest and tax, and excluding other income (interest earned, dividends, one-off gains). Formula. (Revenue − operating costs) ÷ revenue; screener.in's "operating profit" line is this measure. Worked example. Texmaco's FY26: revenue ₹4,377 crore, operating profit ₹391 crore, margin 8.9%. Add other income of ₹56 crore and the company's own "EBITDA" is ₹450 crore, or 10.2%. What drives it. Volume (operating leverage), mix (passenger and components earn more), steel prices relative to the price variation clause, start-up losses in new subsidiaries, and one-off items such as export freight or free-supply material. Benchmark. For a wagon maker, above 13% good, 9–13% average, below 9% weak.

How the companies compare. On FY26: Jupiter 12.1%, Titagarh 10.4%, Texmaco 8.9%. On Q1 FY27: Titagarh 12.3% (helped by a 31% passenger share), Jupiter 9.7% (down from 13.0% a year earlier as subsidiaries made losses and raw materials rose to 73.6% of revenue), Texmaco 7.5% consolidated. Texmaco's standalone Q1 EBITDA of 10.8%, presented as a 161 basis-point improvement, was 7.4% excluding other income, down from 7.8%. CARE Ratings' downgrade trigger for Texmaco is a margin below 7%. The trap. Texmaco includes other income in its EBITDA; Titagarh and Jupiter do not. Comparing company-reported EBITDA margins across the three therefore flatters Texmaco by about 1.3 percentage points on FY26 and 3.4 points on Q1 FY27.

KPI 6 · Order book and book-to-bill

What it is. The value of contracted work not yet delivered, and how many years of revenue it represents. Formula. Order book ÷ trailing twelve-month (or last full-year) revenue. A related flow measure is order inflow ÷ revenue in a period; above one means the book is growing. Worked example. Texmaco's ₹9,923 crore book ÷ FY26 revenue of ₹4,377 crore = 2.3 years. What drives it. Tender wins, private and export orders, and execution (a book shrinks as it is delivered). Benchmark. Above 2.5× good, 1.5–2.5× average, below 1.5× weak.

How the companies compare. Titagarh 4.2× on its standalone book (8.4× on the headline including joint ventures); Texmaco 2.3×; Jupiter 1.6× (₹4,550 crore ÷ ₹2,916 crore). On the adjusted rail-supply basis in Section 12, the figures are about 4.0×, 2.0× and 1.2×. A second view is pending wagons ÷ quarterly deliveries, which shows how long the wagon book alone lasts at the current pace. The trap. Joint-venture orders, long maintenance contracts, build-own-operate projects and non-rail work all inflate the headline. And a high ratio built on a five-year Vande Bharat delivery schedule does not support next year's revenue the way a one-year wagon order does.

KPI 8 · Wheelset output and availability

What it is. For a wheelset maker (Jupiter), the number of wheelsets produced; for any maker with IR orders, the number of wheelsets received from IR relative to the wagons scheduled. Formula. Wheelsets needed = wagons × 4. Worked example. IR's FY27 budget target of 32,000 wagons needs 1,28,000 wheelsets, about 36% more than RWF's record of 94,275. What drives it. IR's RWF and Bela output, import permissions and Chinese supply, and the ramp-up of private plants (Ramkrishna–Titagarh from FY27, Jupiter's Odisha plant from FY27–FY28). Benchmark. No universal one; the test is whether wheelset supply covers the wagon schedule.

How the companies compare. Jupiter produced 19,939 wheelsets in FY26 and only 1,282 in Q1 FY27 (from 5,290 in Q4). Titagarh's 49% joint venture is contracted to supply 80,000 wheels (40,000 wheelsets' worth) a year to IR. Texmaco buys wheels for private and export orders and depends on RWF for IR orders; it reported no shortage in Q1 FY27. The trap. A wheel is not a wheelset: two wheels and an axle make one wheelset, and a wagon needs four. Capacity claims mix the units (Section 05).

The practical questions for each results call are simple. How many wheelsets did IR issue against the quarter's IR deliveries, and is there a backlog of wagons waiting for them? For private orders, where are the wheels coming from and at what price, given Chinese supply and the Hong Kong re-routing seen in trade data? And for the two wheel plants, what were trial volumes, and when does invoicing to IR or exporters start?

KPI 9 · Receivable days

What it is. How long customers take to pay. Formula. Trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. Texmaco's ₹1,248 crore of receivables at March 2026 on ₹4,377 crore of revenue is 104 days. KPI 10 · Inventory days and the cash conversion cycle

What it is. Inventory days measure how long material and unfinished wagons sit in the plant; the cash conversion cycle (CCC) adds receivable days and subtracts payable days to show how long cash is tied up from buying steel to collecting payment. Formula. Inventory ÷ cost of goods (or revenue) × 365; CCC = receivable days + inventory days − payable days. Worked example. Jupiter: 95 receivable days + 188 inventory days − 69 payable days = 214, reported as 213 after rounding. What drives it. Wheel shortages (a wagon without wheels is inventory), RDSO approval delays for new designs, raw-material stocking ahead of orders, and the start-up of new plants. Benchmark. Inventory below 75 days good, 75–120 average, above 120 weak; CCC below 75 good, 75–130 average, above 130 weak.

How the companies compare. FY26 inventory days were 92 (Titagarh), 188 (Jupiter) and 95 (Texmaco); CCC was 113, 213 and 133. Jupiter's figure is the clearest warning sign in the group: its inventory was ₹1,079 crore at March 2026, more than a third of its annual revenue. Titagarh targets normalised net working capital of 30 days in freight and 75 days in passenger by the end of FY27. The trap. Year-end snapshots can be managed by timing deliveries and supplier payments around 31 March. Compare the half-year balance sheet (September) with March for a fairer view.

KPI 11 · Cash conversion: CFO to operating profit, and free cash flow

What it is. How much of the operating profit turns into operating cash flow (CFO), and what is left after capital spending (free cash flow, FCF). Formula. CFO ÷ operating profit; FCF = CFO − capex. Worked example. Texmaco: CFO ₹363 crore ÷ operating profit ₹391 crore = 93%; with capex of about ₹167 crore on screener's basis, FCF was +₹196 crore. What drives it. Working-capital swings (the KPIs above), advances from customers, the timing of IR payments, and capex for new plants. Benchmark. CFO above 80% of operating profit good, 40–80% average, below 40% weak; FCF should be positive over a cycle.

How the companies compare. FY26 CFO was ₹322 crore at Titagarh (97% of operating profit), ₹9 crore at Jupiter (3%) and ₹363 crore at Texmaco (93%); the group converted 64%. Free cash flow was −₹45 crore, −₹524 crore and +₹196 crore, a combined −₹373 crore. Jupiter's cumulative FCF over FY24–FY26 was about −₹1,070 crore, funded by the ₹800 crore QIP, ₹135 crore of promoter warrants and about ₹650 crore of new borrowings. The trap. Jupiter's company presentation can show healthy EBITDA while cash goes the other way; and Titagarh's earlier claim of positive free cash flow of about ₹380 crore in FY26 does not reconcile with CFO of ₹322 crore and capex of about ₹368 crore. Always rebuild FCF from the cash-flow statement.

KPI 12 · Return on capital employed (ROCE)

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a percentage of the capital tied up in the business (equity plus debt). It combines margin and asset turns into one number. Formula. EBIT ÷ (equity + borrowings), usually on average capital. Worked example. A business with ₹1,000 crore of capital earning ₹110 crore of EBIT has an 11% ROCE. If revenue doubles on the same sheds and EBIT rises to ₹250 crore, ROCE becomes 25%, which is what happened at Titagarh between FY22 and FY24. What drives it. Volume above all (asset turns), then margin, then working capital and the capital spent on new plants that are not yet earning. Benchmark. Above 20% good, 12–20% average, below 12% weak; a reasonable cost of capital for these companies is in the low teens.

How the companies compare. FY26 ROCE was 10.9% at Titagarh, 9.1% at Jupiter and 11.2% at Texmaco, down from peaks of 25%, 31% and 14%. Return on equity was lower still: 6.8%, 6.4% and 7.3%, and 6.2% for the group on closing equity. The trap. Texmaco's equity is ₹700 crore lower because of its reserve-funded provision (Section 18), so its ROE and ROCE are flattered relative to a company that took the same charge through profit. Capital-work-in-progress on new plants (Jupiter's ₹270 crore at March 2026) depresses ROCE before the plant earns anything.

Three lessons come out of the anatomy. First, at full volume a well-run shed can earn a ROCE of about 20% on an EBITDA margin of only 11%, because it turns its capital over about twice a year. The same exercise for a passenger line gives a different shape. An ILLUSTRATIVE metro car worth ₹9 crore carries about ₹4.6 crore of bought-in systems (propulsion, train control, air-conditioning, doors, brakes, bogies), ₹1.6 crore of car body and interiors, ₹1.3 crore of engineering, labour, testing and warranty and a small overhead, leaving about ₹1.3 crore of EBIT, close to Titagarh's reported 14–15%. At 200 cars a year that is about ₹1,800 crore of revenue and ₹260 crore of EBIT from a fraction of the floor space a 6,000-wagon shed needs, because one car carries the revenue of 23–25 wagons. The catch is concentration: a few contracts, long warranty periods, liquidated-damages exposure and heavy dependence on imported subsystems.

In plain terms

A wagon shed is a good business when it is full and paid on time, and a poor one when it is half empty and waiting for wheels. The margin hardly changes between the two; the return on capital changes from about 20% to under 10%.

Definition traps, collected

  • Production versus IR additions. PIB's national production (41,929 in FY25) counts every wagon built; the Budget's physical target (29,889 achieved in FY25) counts only IR's own additions. Never divide a company's deliveries by the wrong one.
  • Ex-GST versus GST-inclusive award values. ₹7,838 crore and ₹6,450 crore for the 2022 awards are ex-GST; ₹9,249 crore and ₹7,615 crore include 18% GST.
  • Wheels in or out. IR prices exclude free-issued wheelsets; private and export prices include them. A higher realisation can simply be bought-in wheels.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. Texmaco includes it; Titagarh and Jupiter do not. Use operating profit excluding other income for every comparison.
  • Reported versus pre-exceptional profit. Titagarh's FY26 consolidated PAT is ₹122.82 crore after a ₹64.78 crore Firema exceptional item and a ₹17.15 crore share of joint-venture losses; its four quarterly results, presented before exceptional items, add up to about ₹167 crore.
  • Standalone versus joint-venture order books. Titagarh's ₹26,635 crore headline includes ₹13,300 crore of equity-accounted joint-venture orders.
  • Supply versus maintenance. Texmaco's South Africa order includes a 15-year maintenance contract worth 30–35%; Titagarh's Vande Bharat maintenance sits in a joint venture.
  • Total income versus revenue from operations. Jupiter's "₹2,961 crore" for FY26 is total income; revenue from operations is ₹2,916 crore.
  • Net debt bases. Screener nets investments, many of them strategic stakes; companies sometimes do not. Texmaco reports ₹444 crore, screener ₹290 crore, and CARE counts consolidated debt of ₹1,515 crore including acceptances.
  • Capacity. Self-reported, elastic and frequently restated; always name the source.
  • Reserves versus profit. Texmaco's ₹700 crore contingency provision was charged to reserves, not profit, so FY26 PAT is unaffected but equity is ₹700 crore lower and future ROE is flattered.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in this sector has a twin on a different basis. Before comparing two companies, check that the two numbers are the same thing: with or without wheels, with or without GST, with or without other income, with or without joint ventures, before or after exceptional items.

How to read a wagon maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Wagons delivered. Find the unit count in the presentation or call (Jupiter's operating highlights slide; Titagarh's and Texmaco's commentary). Compare with the year-ago quarter and the previous quarter. Red flag: a fall with no explanation, or no number at all.
  2. Revenue by segment. Split freight, passenger, EPC and other. For Titagarh, the passenger share; for Texmaco, Bright Power and Rail & Green; for Jupiter, the consolidated-minus-standalone gap. Red flag: growth that comes only from non-core lines.
  3. Operating margin excluding other income. Recompute it from revenue and operating costs. Red flag: a company-reported margin that improves while the ex-other-income margin falls (Texmaco Q1 FY27).
  4. Profit per wagon. Freight EBIT ÷ wagons where segments exist. Red flag: a falling figure at stable volume, which points to pricing or cost problems rather than the cycle.
  5. Other income, exceptional items and tax rate. Red flags: other income doubling, a negative tax rate (Texmaco's −18% in Q1 FY27), an exceptional item booked only in the audited annual numbers (Titagarh FY26).
  6. Order book and inflow. Take the standalone figure, subtract joint-venture and non-rail lines, and compare with the previous quarter. Red flag: a book that shrinks for three quarters with no inflow.
  7. Pending wagons and wheel status. How many wagons are left, and are wheels available for IR orders? Red flag: management citing "supply-chain" or "wheelset" issues without numbers.
  8. Working capital. At half-year and year-end, look at inventory and receivables in rupees, not just days. Red flag: inventory rising faster than the order book is executed.
  9. Debt and cash. Gross borrowings, cash and investments; and for Texmaco, bank guarantees. Red flag: borrowings rising while capex projects slip.
  10. Guidance and the tender. What does management say about the IR mega-tender, private orders and the next two quarters' deliveries? Compare with what it said last quarter. Red flag: repeated "next quarter" promises (Jupiter's Q2 "spike" is the one to test now).
In plain terms

Start with wagons delivered and finish with what management says about the next IR tender. In between, recompute the margin on one basis, check where the cash went and strip the order book down to what will actually become revenue. Ten minutes of that will tell you more than the headline profit growth figure.

The ₹700 crore question, and the other audit flags Deep dive

Two of the three companies carry FY26 audit qualifications and the third disclosed a regulator's enquiry. What happened, what it does to the numbers, and what to watch.

For a sector valued on the expectation of future profits, the quality of current profits matters a great deal, and FY26 produced three separate governance and accounting issues. Texmaco's auditors qualified their opinion on its accounts because of a ₹700 crore provision created out of reserves. Titagarh's auditors qualified their opinion on its internal financial controls after the collapse of its Italian subsidiary forced a restatement. Jupiter's audit was clean, but its secretarial auditor reported an email from SEBI in connection with an investigation into trading in its shares. None of these is an allegation of fraud. Each changes how the reported numbers should be read.

Texmaco: a provision that bypassed the profit and loss account

In its FY26 accounts, Texmaco created a "provision for contingencies" of ₹700 crore. The Statement of Changes in Equity shows it was funded by ₹494.05 crore from the general reserve and ₹205.95 crore from retained earnings. The board's rationale (Note 1.37) is that it covers "geo-political factors… trade uncertainties, supply chain disruptions, escalation of costs and foreign exchange risks", contingencies that "would arise neither because of operational issues, nor would be attributable to identifiable individual risks or a single reportable year", and that it was therefore appropriate to carve it out of free reserves. On the Q4 FY26 call, management described it as a safety net for "Texmaco 2.0", including large contracts that might miss their expected outcomes, and confirmed there was no cash impact.

The auditors disagreed. They issued a qualified opinion on the standalone statements because the provision was created "by way of charge to its free reserves… not routed through the profit and loss account" and because "it was not possible to assess the exact time frame of the impact of such risks". Under Indian accounting standards a provision is a liability recognised through profit when there is a present obligation that can be estimated reliably; a general cushion against future risks does not usually qualify, and if it did it would have to go through the P&L. The ₹700 crore now sits on the balance sheet as a liability: Note 1.38 shows closing provisions of ₹736.98 crore including the ₹700 crore, and the consolidated segment note lists "unallocated corporate liabilities ₹700 Cr".

The global lens: a synchronised freight-car slump and thin passenger margins

India's three listed makers are a fraction of global scale, earn freight margins in line with North America, report passenger margins well above Europe's, and trade at several times US multiples.

Outside India, the freight-wagon market is also in a downturn, which matters for anyone who expects exports to fill the gap. In North America, Trinity Industries guides to about 25,000 industry deliveries in 2026, and Greenbrier, citing FTR, expects fewer than 25,000, the lowest since 2010, against an average of about 35,000 since 2020. Greenbrier delivered 22,000 railcars and reported revenue of $3,240 million in its year to August 2025, with core EBITDA of $512 million (16%) and a backlog of 16,600 units worth $2.2 billion; it has since cut its FY26 revenue guidance to $2.4–2.5 billion, and its backlog (13,800 units at May 2026) is the lowest since 2014. Trinity's rail products segment earned an operating margin of just 1.3% in Q2 2026. India's FY26 trough was therefore part of a synchronised freight-car slump, not an isolated event.

Technology and disruption: heavier wagons, faster trains and a coming wheel surplus

Technology changes here are gradual and set by the customer's standards. The real disruptions are a customer that can build in-house, a commodity mix that may shift away from coal, and a wheel market that could flip to oversupply.

Wagon technology moves in steps defined by RDSO. The current steps are higher axle loads (25-tonne designs such as BOXNS, which carry more per wagon), stainless-steel and aluminium bodies (lighter and more corrosion-resistant, used in newer covered and open wagons), and higher speeds. Moving from 100 km/h to 120 km/h or more needs forged wheels instead of cast ones, which is a large part of why the new forged-wheel plants matter, and new bogie designs, such as the bogie intellectual property Texmaco is developing with Wabtec. Each step means a new prototype cycle, so the companies with the strongest design teams and the fastest RDSO approvals gain an edge; Titagarh cites 150 design and R&D engineers.

On the passenger side, the technology frontier is propulsion, train control and car-body materials. Titagarh has supplied propulsion for two EMU rakes, plans MEMU propulsion next, dispatched 891 traction motors in FY26 and is building an in-house aluminium extrusion-to-coach line, which it says would make it eligible for high-speed work. Train-control systems (TCMS and communications-based train control) and much of the propulsion electronics remain dependent on multinational suppliers. Digital condition monitoring, which Texmaco is developing with Wabtec, could in time shift value from building wagons to maintaining them and selling data, as it has at Wabtec; Texmaco has also signed an MoU with Nevomo on maglev and linear propulsion, which is exploratory.

The disruptions that matter more

  • The customer as competitor. IR can move work to its own factories at any time, as it did with the Mumbai MRVC suburban train tender. ICF, RCF and MCF build most of India's coaches and Vande Bharat chair cars. Private passenger makers depend on IR choosing to outsource.
  • First-of-kind execution. The Vande Bharat sleeper is new to every builder. BEML/ICF's first set was flagged off in January 2026; Kinet's prototype has slipped to December 2026; Titagarh–BHEL's first train is due around the end of Q3 or early Q4 FY27. A delay or a safety issue would hit Titagarh's largest contract.
  • Coal and the commodity mix. Coal is about half of IR's loading and 48% of its freight revenue. A long energy transition would reduce demand for open BOXN wagons while container, auto, cement and fly-ash traffic grows, favouring makers with design breadth (Texmaco's eleven types) and private-customer relationships.
  • Road competition. Expressways and, over time, electric trucks compete for exactly the medium-distance, high-value freight that rail needs to win to reach its modal-share target, while rail freight rates have not been revised since 2018.
  • A wheel surplus. Section 05's arithmetic suggests India's wheel capacity could exceed domestic demand by FY29.

Bull versus bear: the case for and against Indian wagon makers

The bull case is a fleet that must double and a tender that must come. The bear case is a customer that has missed its own targets for two years running.

The bull case

  • A fleet that needs to grow. The National Rail Plan wants the wagon fleet to double to about six lakh by 2030; even slower freight growth implies more than IR's 26,000–32,000 a year.
  • A tender in preparation. A ₹40,000 crore, one-lakh-wagon tender is "deferred… not dropped"; when it comes, sheds at about 40% utilisation can roughly double output without new capital.
  • The wheel constraint is being removed. About 4.3 lakh wheels a year of new private forged capacity arrives in FY27–FY28, owned in part by Titagarh and Jupiter.
  • Diversification is working. Titagarh's passenger book is worth three years of revenue at a higher margin; Texmaco's book is 96% private and export; Jupiter is building wheels, brakes and storage.
  • Policy direction. Private wagon design, oil-sector tank-wagon ownership, leasing (with Trinity and GATX in India) and new freight corridors all widen the customer base.

The bear case

  • The customer keeps missing. IR achieved 79% of its FY25 wagon target and cut FY26 to 68%; the FY27 target of 32,000 is still below FY25's budget, and the mega-tender has slipped twice.
  • Fiscal limits. Capex has been flat since FY24, rolling stock gets 3% more in FY27, lease repayments to IRFC grow 41%, and the operating ratio is near 99%.
  • Margins do not expand. Even at the 2022–25 peak the group's margin was under 11%; returns now are 9–11% on capital and about 6% on equity.
  • Balance-sheet and accounting strain. Jupiter's net debt and 213-day cash cycle; Texmaco's qualified audit and 104 receivable days; Titagarh's internal-controls qualification and restatement.
  • Valuation. 36× trailing earnings for the group and 46–48× for Titagarh and Jupiter, against 10–15× for US railcar makers, in a global freight-car downturn.
