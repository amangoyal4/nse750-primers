00

The one-page view: one buyer, one bottleneck, three escape routes

A freight-wagon industry that tripled its output in three years, then lost a fifth of it because the customer could not supply the wheels.

India's listed wagon makers sell a welded steel box on wheels, mostly to one customer, Indian Railways (IR), which decides how many it wants each year through a central tender run by the Railway Board. When IR orders, the factories fill up and profits multiply. When it pauses, revenue falls straight away, because there is no consumer market and no export market big enough to replace it. This report covers the three listed members of the NSE 750 that live inside this cycle: Titagarh Rail Systems, Jupiter Wagons and Texmaco Rail & Engineering. Together they delivered 20,889 wagons in FY26, about 64% of India's estimated output, and booked ₹10,479 crore of revenue.

The past five years have been the most dramatic in the industry's history. National wagon production went from 12,796 in FY22 to a record 41,929 in FY25 after IR placed about 60,000 wagons in June 2022. Combined revenue of the three listed makers nearly tripled, from ₹4,460 crore in FY22 to ₹12,938 crore in FY25, and their combined profit went up roughly tenfold. Then FY26 broke the pattern. The 2022 orders were largely delivered, the next large tender did not arrive, and IR's own wheel factories could not supply enough wheelsets for the wagons that were still on order. IR cut its own wagon additions to a revised estimate of 26,000 against a budget of 38,000. Combined revenue fell 19% and profit fell a third.

Five forces explain almost everything in this sub-sector, and the report is built around them:

  • IR's procurement calendar is the master variable. Revenue tracked IR's ordering almost exactly: +59%, +55%, +18% and then −19% over FY23–FY26. A reported ₹40,000 crore tender for about one lakh wagons has been "deferred… not dropped" (Titagarh, August 2026). Its timing and design decide FY28–FY30.
  • The wheelset is the physical bottleneck. IR requires that wagons it orders carry wheels from its own factories. Its main plant made a record 94,275 wheelsets in FY24, enough for only about 23,600 new wagons even if none went to maintenance. New private forged-wheel plants (Ramkrishna–Titagarh, Jupiter's Odisha project) are the structural change of this cycle.
  • Rail capex is high but plateauing, and wagons are a small slice. The FY27 budget gives IR ₹2,93,030 crore of capex, but rolling stock is ₹65,497 crore of that and wagons perhaps ₹10,000–13,000 crore. With an operating ratio near 99%, almost all of it depends on the Union budget.
  • The three companies are escaping in different directions. Titagarh is turning into a metro and Vande Bharat maker; Jupiter into a wheel, brake and battery-storage group; Texmaco into an EPC contractor, exporter and part-owner of a leasing company.
  • Freight-share policy is the long-run anchor. The National Rail Plan wants rail's share of freight to rise from about 27–28% to 45% by 2030, and loading to reach 3,000 million tonnes. Actual loading grows only 3–4% a year.

The market has already passed its verdict on the three strategies. Titagarh produces about 30% of the group's revenue but carries 44% of its market value; Texmaco produces 42% of revenue but only 20% of the value. Investors are paying for passenger rolling stock and for vertical integration, and are discounting the plain wagon shed. All three have also de-rated sharply in 2026: one-year price returns are −24% (Titagarh), −46% (Jupiter, now at a 52-week low of about ₹173) and −27% (Texmaco). The group trades at 42.1× FY26 profit and about 36× trailing profit on a combined market value of ₹20,352 crore, which still assumes a strong recovery.

Two things make this sub-sector harder to read than its size suggests. The first is that the reported numbers are on different bases. Titagarh's headline order book of ₹26,635 crore is half joint-venture business that will never appear as its revenue; Texmaco's company "EBITDA" includes other income; IR's tender prices exclude the wheels, which IR supplies itself, so a ₹32-lakh IR wagon and a ₹41.5-lakh private wagon are not different prices for the same thing. The second is accounting quality. Two of the three carry FY26 audit flags: Texmaco's auditors qualified its accounts because it created a ₹700 crore contingency provision directly out of reserves instead of through the profit and loss account, and Titagarh's auditors qualified their opinion on its internal financial controls after the Italian subsidiary Firema had to be written off and FY25 restated. Jupiter has a clean audit but disclosed a SEBI enquiry email about trading in its shares around its FY24 results.

In plain terms

These are factories that build what one customer orders, when it orders, using wheels that customer insists on supplying. That made FY23–FY25 a bonanza and FY26 a drought, even though order books stayed full. The three listed makers are now trying to find customers and products that do not depend on that single tender calendar. Until the next large IR order is signed, their earnings depend on how quickly those alternatives grow, and their valuations still assume the big order will come.

The latest reported quarter is Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026). It looks like a bottom reached on low volume: combined revenue of ₹2,193 crore was 7% higher than a weak Q1 FY26 but 22% lower than Q4 FY26, and the three delivered only 3,479 wagons in the quarter, about 13,900 a year annualised, against stated capacity of roughly 35,000–38,000. Nobody has reported Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) yet. Titagarh's board meets on 29 October 2026; Jupiter and Texmaco have not announced dates and are expected by mid-November. Section 22 lists what to look for.

01

How a wagon earns: from a Railway Board tender to an accepted rake

The product is simple. The route from an order to recognised revenue runs through a regulator, a design approval and a wheel factory the maker does not control.

A freight wagon is a welded steel body sitting on two cast-steel bogies, each carrying two wheelsets (a forged axle with two wheels pressed on), with couplers at each end and an air-brake system underneath. There are a dozen common types, each identified by an IR code. Open, high-sided wagons (the BOXN family) carry coal and ore. Covered wagons (BCN and BCNA) carry cement, fertiliser and grain. Flat wagons (BOST, BOSM, BLC) carry steel coils and containers. Tank wagons (BTPN) carry petroleum, hoppers discharge fly-ash or aggregates from the bottom, and auto-carriers (BCACBM) carry cars. A rake is a full train of 40–60 wagons of one type, and private customers usually buy in rakes.

Wagons are only part of what these companies make. Each has a large steel foundry that casts bogie frames, couplers and other parts, used in its own wagons and sold to rivals and to IR workshops. Titagarh also builds metro cars in stainless steel and aluminium and is the junior partner (with BHEL) building 80 Vande Bharat sleeper trains. Jupiter assembles wheelsets, makes brakes through its Stone India subsidiary and a Czech joint venture, and has moved into truck bodies, containers, electric light commercial vehicles and battery storage. Texmaco runs a rail engineering and electrification contracting business alongside the wagon works. The table below sets out the product list and the value per unit, which differs by a factor of about 25 between a wagon and a metro car.

The route from order to revenue

For an IR order, the sequence runs like this. The Railway Board floats a centralised tender for a quantity of a given wagon type. Historically, every qualified bidder has had to match the lowest (L1) price, and the quantity is then split by capacity and past performance, so price competition happens once, at the bid, and market share is allocated rather than won wagon by wagon. Each new design, and each new vendor, needs approval from the Research Designs & Standards Organisation (RDSO) in Lucknow, which means a prototype and trials before series production. IR then issues the wheelsets from its own factories, the Rail Wheel Factory at Yelahanka in Bengaluru and the Rail Wheel Plant at Bela in Bihar. The maker fabricates the body, fits its own bogies and the IR wheelsets, and presents the wagon for inspection by RITES or RDSO. Revenue is recognised only on delivery and acceptance.

A wagon missing its wheelsets is unfinished inventory. That is how a company with a full order book can report falling revenue and rising inventory at the same time, as all three did in FY26.

A private order runs differently. The buyer, such as JSW Steel, a cement company, a container operator or a lessor, registers under one of IR's wagon investment schemes and negotiates price and specification directly with the maker. The maker buys the wheelsets itself (imported or from a private plant) and includes them in the price. If the design is new, it still needs RDSO approval, which is why Jupiter lost about a quarter of its volume in Q1 FY27 while it waited for prototypes of new private designs to be cleared. Exports, such as Texmaco's South Africa and Cameroon orders, follow the customer's own standards and often include long maintenance contracts.

Where the value is captured

Margins rise as you move away from the plain IR wagon. A commodity IR wagon earns roughly 10–12% EBITDA; Titagarh's freight segment earned 12.2% EBIT in FY26, which it attributes to a better mix and foundry efficiency, and management says the figure moves by half to one percentage point depending on the public-private mix, the price variation clause and commodity prices. Components and wheels earn more: Jupiter targets 15% or more on its wheel plant and its brakes, and Titagarh says its wheel joint venture will earn margins "in line with a standard forging business". Passenger cars earn more per unit of factory space: Titagarh's passenger segment made 14.3% EBIT in FY26 and 14.7% in Q1 FY27, though, as Section 19 shows, global passenger builders earn only 4–6%. Long-term maintenance contracts and leasing are the least cyclical slice, and today they are small.

In plain terms

Building the steel box is the low-value part of the job. The money is in the parts that are hard to make (wheels, brakes, propulsion), in the products that carry more value per square metre of factory floor (metro and Vande Bharat cars), and in the contracts that pay for decades (maintenance and leasing). Each listed company is trying to move up one of those ladders.

Two features of the cash cycle matter as much as the margin. IR pays on its own cycle after acceptance, so receivables sit at 70–105 days of revenue across the three. And because IR contracts include a price variation clause indexed to steel and other inputs, a sudden rise in steel prices is passed through with a lag, while private contracts are often fixed or only partly indexed. The more private business a company takes, the more it needs to manage steel-price risk itself.

02

Size and the 2022–25 boom: from 12,800 wagons a year to 41,929 and back

Output more than tripled in three years. Combined profit rose about tenfold. Margins hardly moved.

For the ten years to FY24, India produced an average of 15,875 wagons a year, and the decade before that averaged 13,262 (Press Information Bureau figures). In FY21 and FY22 output was still around 12,500–12,800. Then the 2022 tender cycle arrived, and production rose to 22,790 in FY23, 37,650 in FY24 and a record 41,929 in FY25. FY26 has no published full-year figure, but the Ministry of Railways' year-end review gives 33,703 wagons for January–November 2025. Taking out January–March 2025 (3,651 + 3,880 + 4,135) leaves about 22,000 for April–November 2025, roughly 2,750 a month. Adding December–March at 2,400–2,900 a month gives an INDICATIVE estimate of 31,500–33,600 for FY26, with a central figure of about 32,500, down roughly 22% from FY25.

The striking line is the operating margin. Revenue nearly tripled between FY22 and FY25, but the combined margin rose only from 9.4% to a peak of 10.9% in FY24. The profit explosion came from below the operating line: interest and depreciation were roughly fixed while revenue tripled, so PAT went from ₹70 crore to ₹730 crore. Return on capital employed jumped for the same reason: Titagarh reached 25% and Jupiter 31% in FY24 because the same sheds turned over two and a half to three times as much revenue, not because each wagon became more profitable.

How big is the market?

Measured by IR's own spending, the wagon market is modest. IR's FY27 budget target of 32,000 wagons at ₹32–40 lakh each implies ₹10,000–13,000 crore a year (INDICATIVE). Private wagon buying has historically run at 7,000–10,000 wagons a year; Texmaco thinks 12,000–15,000 is sustainable, which at ₹40–45 lakh is another ₹3,000–6,500 crore. Wheelsets are a separate pool: Jupiter sizes domestic demand at three to four lakh wheelsets a year (new wagons, replacement and passenger trains), worth roughly ₹7,500–12,000 crore at ₹2.5–3 lakh each (INDICATIVE). Passenger rolling stock adds a market that depends on metro expansion, which the government wants to take from 25 to 50 cities. In dollars, the three listed companies' combined FY26 revenue is about US$1.1 billion at ₹94.9 to the dollar, roughly a third of the US wagon maker Greenbrier's FY25 revenue.

In plain terms

The boom was a volume boom, not a pricing boom. Each wagon earned roughly the same margin in FY25 as in FY22; the factories simply built three times as many. That is why profits can fall faster than revenue when volume drops, and why the next order matters more than any efficiency programme.

A note on the denominators. National production (PIB) counts every wagon made in India: IR orders, private wagons under the investment schemes, public-sector buyers, IR workshop output and some exports. IR's Union Budget target counts only the wagons IR itself adds to its fleet with budget money. In FY25 the gap was about 12,000 wagons (41,929 produced versus 29,889 IR additions), which is broadly the private and export pool. Comparing a company's deliveries with the wrong one of these two numbers is the most common error in writing about this industry, and this report keeps them apart throughout.

03

The master variable: Indian Railways' procurement calendar

Budget targets missed, a revised estimate cut by a third, and a mega-tender that keeps slipping by a quarter or two.

Everything else in this report (margins, return on capital, cash flow and share prices) sits downstream of one decision: how many wagons IR orders, of which types, and when. The Union Budget sets a physical target for IR's own wagon additions each year. The table and chart show how that target has fared. For FY25, IR budgeted 38,000 and achieved 29,889, or 79%. For FY26 it budgeted 38,000 again and then cut the revised estimate to 26,000, 68% of the target. For FY27 the budget estimate is 32,000, 23% above the FY26 revised figure but well below the FY25 and FY26 budgets.

The pattern behind those numbers is a tender cycle, not a smooth programme. In April 2022 IR floated a tender for about 90,000 wagons worth roughly ₹31,000 crore, which drew 16 bidders. In June 2022 it placed about 60,000 of them for ₹23,500 crore, with delivery due by FY26. The mega-tender that keeps slipping

The swing factor for FY28–FY30 is a single tender. In May 2026, Mint and Angel One reported that IR was "preparing a tender of ₹40,000 crore for 1,00,000 freight wagons" to be delivered over three to four years, which would mean 25,000–35,000 a year. Arihant Capital expected it within two quarters. By August, Titagarh's management said it had been "deferred by a few months or a couple of quarters, but not dropped" and that priorities had "shifted because of the Middle East war", which began on 28 February 2026 and pushed up crude and freight costs. Texmaco's management, for its part, has said the country needs "no less than 1.5 lakh to 2 lakh wagons" in the near term, and that, as of now, it can "only talk about private and export" orders. No tender notice had appeared on IR's procurement portal by late October 2026.

The reported value implies about ₹40 lakh a wagon (INDICATIVE), noticeably above the ₹32 lakh ex-GST of 2022. Whether that is because steel has risen, because the mix is richer, or because wheels will be included in the price instead of being supplied by IR is not known; no tender document exists yet. The answer matters a great deal. If IR keeps supplying wheels, its own factories cannot equip one lakh wagons in three or four years on top of maintenance demand (Section 05), so either the free-supply rule is diluted or the new private wheel plants are designated as suppliers.

In plain terms

The industry is waiting for one customer to sign one large order. Until it does, each company keeps its sheds running at roughly half speed on private and export work. When it does, the size of the order matters less than two details: how many wagons a year it requires, and who supplies the wheels.

The FY26 cut also shows something about IR's priorities. The wagon target is one of the few items in the railway budget that can be deferred without stopping a train: new lines, electrification and safety work have contractors and political commitments attached, while a wagon order can simply be placed a year later. When the budget tightens (and with an operating ratio near 99%, IR's budget is always tight), wagons are among the first items to be cut, and among the first to be restored when freight traffic or political focus demands it. The next section looks at that budget.

04

Rail capex: high, plateauing, and wagons are a thin slice of it

Railway capital spending nearly tripled in six years and then went flat. Rolling stock is a fifth of it; wagons perhaps a twenty-fifth.

Indian Railways' capital expenditure rose from ₹1,01,985 crore in FY18 to ₹2,62,217 crore in FY24, an average of about 17% a year. Since then it has gone sideways: ₹2,69,361 crore in FY25, ₹2,65,200 crore in the FY26 revised estimate (identical to the budget), and ₹2,93,030 crore budgeted for FY27, up 10.5%. For an industry that depends on IR's wallet, the plateau matters more than the headline. The money is still very large, but it is no longer growing fast enough to lift every line item at once, and IR has to choose.

How the money is raised explains why wagons are vulnerable. IR's operating ratio, the share of its revenue consumed by working expenses, is 98.82% in the FY26 revised estimate and 98.40% in the FY27 budget. That leaves almost nothing from operations to invest, so about 95% of the FY27 capex (₹2,78,030 crore) comes as gross budgetary support from the Union government, with ₹12,000 crore of extra-budgetary resources (market borrowing through IRFC) and a small balance from internal resources. IR's investment programme is therefore a line in the Union budget, competing with defence, roads and subsidies. PRS also notes that IR's capital-output ratio rose from 418 paise in 2017-18 to 704 paise in 2023-24, meaning each rupee of capital generates less output than before. A finance ministry looking for savings has a ready argument.

Within the total, the rolling-stock head (locomotives, coaches, Vande Bharat trains and wagons) was ₹60,625 crore in FY25, ₹58,895 crore in the FY26 budget, ₹63,373 crore in the FY26 revised estimate and ₹65,497 crore in the FY27 budget, up 3%. That head funds 1,800 electric locomotives and 10,392 coaches in FY27 as well as wagons. At 32,000 wagons and ₹32–40 lakh each, wagons need ₹10,000–13,000 crore (INDICATIVE): roughly a sixth of the rolling-stock head and about 4% of total capex. A separate and fast-growing line, "leased assets – payment of capital component", repays IRFC for rolling stock bought in earlier years under lease. It rises from ₹28,157 crore to ₹39,650 crore in FY27 (+41%). Past wagon booms are now a fixed charge on today's budget.

In plain terms

The headline railway budget looks enormous, but the wagon makers live off a small and deferrable corner of it. Rail capex can grow while the wagon order shrinks, as it did in FY26. What matters for these companies is the physical wagon target in the budget documents, not the total capex number in the Finance Minister's speech.

What the budget says about intent

The FY27 budget did contain signals of long-term intent. It proposed a new east–west Dedicated Freight Corridor from Dankuni in West Bengal to Surat, and sanctioned seven high-speed passenger corridors at about ₹16 lakh crore over their lives. It allotted ₹28,740 crore across ongoing metro and rapid transit projects, mostly outside the railway budget, which matters for Titagarh's metro business. And the wagon target rose from 26,000 to 32,000, which the industry reads as a floor for FY27. But the same documents show what has not changed: freight rates have not been revised since 2018, coal still provides about 48% of freight revenue, and IR's internal resources cannot fund a large wagon order without budget support.

For investors, the practical consequence is that the wagon makers' revenue is a function of three numbers that appear each February: the gross budgetary support to railways, the rolling-stock head within it, and the physical wagon target. The FY28 budget, due on 1 February 2027, will show whether the 32,000 target is being met and whether the mega-tender has been provided for. A rolling-stock head that rises only in line with inflation, combined with lease repayments growing at 40% a year, would mean the mega-tender has to be stretched over more years or financed some other way, for example through private wagon investment and leasing (Section 10). Two other signals are worth tracking. The first is IR's revised estimate for the current year, published with each budget, because the FY26 cut from 38,000 to 26,000 appeared there first. The second is the monthly freight-loading data: if loading accelerates and wagon turnaround stays near five days, the case for more wagons becomes hard to deny.

One more point on scale. Even a ₹40,000 crore, one-lakh-wagon programme spread over four years is about ₹10,000 crore a year, roughly what IR already budgets for wagons. The mega-tender is less a new pot of money than a commitment to keep spending at the FY27 level for several years, with prices and quantities fixed in advance. For the makers, that visibility is worth more than the headline, because it lets them run their sheds at a steady rate and plan wheel and steel purchases. The weakness in FY26 came from the gap between tenders, not from a lack of total spending.

The wheel is the wagon: how a ₹2.5-lakh part stopped a ₹40-lakh product Deep dive

IR insists on supplying the wheels for its own wagons, and its wheel factories could not keep up. Private forged-wheel plants are the structural change of this cycle.

A wagon needs four wheelsets: eight wheels and four axles. For any wagon that Indian Railways orders, those wheelsets must come from IR's own production units. Texmaco's management put it plainly on its Q1 FY27 call: "we are dependent on the supply of Rail Wheel Factory. And that's what is the mandate of the government." The wheelsets are issued to the wagon maker as a "free-supply item", which is why IR's tender prices exclude most of the wheel cost. The rule has a logic (quality control, and keeping IR's own factories busy), but it means that IR's wagon output can never exceed what its wheel and axle lines produce, net of what the existing fleet consumes in replacement.

IR's own capacity: the hidden ceiling

The Rail Wheel Factory at Yelahanka, Bengaluru, is the core of the system. According to a PIB release of September 2024, it produced a record 1,96,265 wheels, 83,054 axles and 94,275 wheelsets in FY24, and a third axle-machining line was being commissioned to add about 40,000 axles a year. The Rail Wheel Plant at Bela in Bihar makes wheels only (axles and assembly happen at RWF). Bela has capacity of one lakh wheels a year and produced a record 42,167 in FY24; snippet reports put FY26 output at 60,699 and the FY27 target at 72,000. SAIL's Durgapur plant makes about 40,000 cast wheels a year. RINL's forged-wheel plant at Rae Bareli, built for about ₹2,300 crore with capacity of one lakh wheels, has "sat largely idle after commissioning disputes" (The Core, February 2026).

The arithmetic is unforgiving. RWF's record 94,275 wheelsets, if every one went into a new wagon and none to replacing worn wheelsets on the existing fleet, would equip about 23,600 wagons. Axles are the tighter constraint: RWF's 1.96 lakh wheels plus Bela's recent output of about 60,000 come to roughly 2.56 lakh wheels, enough for 1.28 lakh wheelsets, but RWF's axle output of 83,054 capped wheelsets at about 83,000 before the new line. Coaches, locomotives and the maintenance of an existing fleet of roughly three lakh wagons draw on the same pool. That is why IR's own wagon additions have topped out at 26,000–30,000 a year (29,889 in FY25, a 26,000 revised estimate in FY26) even when national wagon-building capacity was well above 40,000, and why IR reportedly planned in May 2026 to import 60,000–70,000 axles from China (single-source report, INDICATIVE).

In plain terms

IR told the wagon makers it would provide the wheels, then could not make enough of them. The makers had orders, sheds and steel, but a wagon without wheels cannot be delivered, so it sat in inventory and earned nothing. A ₹2.5-lakh component held up a ₹40-lakh product, and it will do so again on the next big order unless the supply rules change or new wheel plants arrive in time.

How FY26 went wrong

The shortage was worst in the first half of FY26. Titagarh was "hit over two quarters"; Jupiter's annual report says an "industry-wide shortage of wheelsets constrained wagon production"; and CARE Ratings cites "non-availability of wheel sets from Rail Wheel Factory" for Texmaco. The effect fell hardest on the companies with the most IR business. Texmaco delivered 81.7% of its FY26 wagons to IR and Titagarh's freight book was mostly IR, so both lost volume they could not replace. Jupiter, with a larger share of private orders, could import wheels for those but still lost IR volume. Inventory piled up: Jupiter's inventory days reached 188 in FY26, and the group's inventory stood at ₹2,535 crore at March 2026. By Q1 FY27, Texmaco said there was no shortage, but by then its IR book had largely run out.

The supply line had already been disturbed once. Until February 2022, much of India's imported wheel supply came from Ukraine. The Russian invasion cut that route and China filled it. Department of Commerce trade data for HS 8607 (parts of railway rolling stock, which includes wheels, axles, bogies, brakes and couplers) show imports almost doubling from ₹2,802 crore in FY23 to ₹5,049 crore in FY24, with China's share rising from 46% to 69%. Imports eased to ₹4,072 crore in FY25 and rose again to ₹4,537 crore in FY26, when China's direct share fell to 52% but Hong Kong's jumped from ₹0.4 crore to ₹471.5 crore, consistent with re-routing of Chinese-origin parts (an inference, INDICATIVE).

Passenger trains are even more import-dependent. Jupiter's managing director says that "today, India does not produce any wheelset" for passenger rolling stock; metro and Vande Bharat wheels come almost entirely from Lucchini RS (Italy) and Bonatrans (Czech Republic). There is also a technical constraint. Cast wheels, which IR and SAIL make, are limited to about 100 km/h; faster trains and heavier, faster freight need forged wheels. Older wagon designs use 840 mm wheels, while newer high-axle-load designs need 1,000 mm wheels, so capacity in one size does not substitute for the other.

The private response: about 3.3 lakh units a year of new forged capacity

Two listed-company projects are changing this, and The Core estimates new private capacity of about 3.28 lakh forged wheels and wheelsets a year for roughly ₹4,500 crore of investment. (The figure mixes units: 2.28 lakh wheels at one plant and one lakh wheelsets, equal to two lakh wheels, at the other. Counted in wheels, it is about 4.3 lakh.)

  • Ramkrishna Titagarh Rail Wheels (Chennai). A 51:49 joint venture of Ramkrishna Forgings and Titagarh, with capacity of 2,28,000 forged wheels a year, which Titagarh calls Asia's second largest. Project cost is about ₹2,000 crore, of which ₹500 crore of equity has been infused by the partners. It won IR's 2023 wheel tender at ₹1.88 lakh a tonne, against ₹2.75 lakh from Bharat Forge and ₹2.89 lakh from SAIL, and holds a 20-year contract to supply 80,000 wheels a year to IR, about 40% of capacity, at roughly ₹1 lakh a wheel. Hot trials and samples ran in August 2026, and Titagarh expects invoicing within FY27.
  • Jupiter Tatravagonka Railwheel Factory (Khordha, Odisha). The axle line is due in FY27 and the wheel line in FY28. The revenue target is ₹2,500–3,000 crore at full ramp with EBITDA of 15% or more, and half the output is meant for export. Jupiter took the venture to 100% ownership in Q1 FY27, then agreed that Lucchini RS and the Italian state finance institution SIMEST will take 25% for about ₹290 crore, cutting Jupiter's own equity commitment from ₹900 crore to about ₹600 crore. The economics of a wheel

Wheels are a better business than wagons. An ILLUSTRATIVE calculation for the Ramkrishna–Titagarh plant: at roughly ₹1 lakh a wheel and the 2023 tender price of ₹1.88 lakh a tonne, each wheel weighs about half a tonne. A forging-industry EBITDA margin of about 20% (Ramkrishna Forgings itself runs at 20–22%) gives about ₹20,000 per wheel. At full capacity of 2.28 lakh wheels, that is about ₹2,280 crore of revenue and ₹450 crore of EBITDA, of which the IR contract underwrites about ₹800 crore of revenue a year for 20 years. Titagarh's 49% share of the profit after interest, depreciation and tax could be ₹120–140 crore a year at maturity, about equal to Titagarh's entire FY26 reported profit of ₹123 crore. It would be equity-accounted, so it would show up in profit but never in revenue. For Jupiter's plant, ₹2,500–3,000 crore of revenue at 15% EBITDA is ₹375–450 crore a year at maturity (FY29 or later), about 75% attributable to Jupiter after the Lucchini and SIMEST stake, against Jupiter's whole FY26 consolidated operating profit of ₹354 crore. Both figures are ILLUSTRATIVE and depend on ramp-up, price and utilisation.

The component business has its own cycle, as Jupiter showed in Q1 FY27. Its wheelset output, which goes into its own wagons and is sold to rivals and IR, fell from 5,290 in Q4 FY26 to 1,282, a drop of 76% in one quarter, and the subsidiaries swung from about ₹16 crore of EBITDA to roughly break-even. Management did not discuss it on the call. The thesis that a component maker's profit "scales with industry volume" cuts both ways: when industry volume falls, so does the component business.

The endgame: from shortage to surplus?

If both private plants reach capacity and RINL's plant is ever started, India's wheel supply by FY29 could be RWF's ~1.96 lakh, Bela's 0.6–1 lakh, SAIL's 0.4 lakh, RINL's 1 lakh, Ramkrishna–Titagarh's 2.28 lakh and Jupiter's 2 lakh: roughly 8–8.6 lakh wheels a year. Jupiter sizes domestic demand at three to four lakh wheelsets, or six to eight lakh wheels. On those INDICATIVE numbers, the bottleneck could turn into a surplus within three years, which is why Jupiter plans to export half its output and why Texmaco says it is considering wheels but is "not… jumping into a band wagon". Three questions decide who wins. Does IR keep the free-supply mandate, or let wagon makers fit private wheels on IR orders? Does IR buy wheels from the private plants, as it already does through the Ramkrishna–Titagarh contract? And does the mega-tender specify forged 1,000 mm wheels, which favour the new plants? The Ramkrishna–Titagarh contract alone, 80,000 wheels a year, is enough for about 10,000 wagons, which makes Titagarh's joint venture a swing supplier for the very tender Titagarh is bidding on.

In plain terms

For three years the problem was too few wheels. By the end of the decade it may be too many. The companies that own wheel plants (Titagarh through its joint venture, Jupiter directly) gain a higher-margin business and some control over their own supply, but they also take on a new cycle. The investor question shifts from "will there be wheels?" to "who will buy them, and at what price?"

Where ₹100 goes: steel, castings, wheels and an 11% margin

A wagon is mostly bought-in steel. The maker controls the foundry, the shed and the bid price, and little else.

The cost structure of a wagon explains why margins are thin and why they barely change through the cycle. The largest input is steel plate and sections, from SAIL, JSW and ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel, with stainless steel from Jindal Stainless for the newer stainless-bodied designs. On an IR wagon, where the wheels are supplied free by IR, steel and bought-out structural material account for about half the price. The next layer is castings (bogie side-frames, bolsters, couplers and knuckles), which all three companies make in their own foundries, followed by brakes, bearings and springs, mostly bought from Wabtec, Knorr-Bremse, Timken, SKF and NBC. Labour, power and overheads are a small share and are largely fixed in the short run, which is where operating leverage comes from.

Why the margin does not move

Three mechanisms keep the margin in a narrow band. First, IR's price variation clause works both ways: when steel rises, the price rises with a lag; when steel falls, the price falls too, so windfall gains are clawed back. Titagarh says this, together with the public-private mix and commodity prices, moves its freight margin by only half to one percentage point. Second, the L1-matching tender system means that any efficiency one maker achieves tends to be bid away in the next tender, because the lowest bidder sets the price for everyone. Third, the companies have used good years to add capacity, so fixed costs rise with the boom.

The ILLUSTRATIVE operating-leverage arithmetic for Titagarh's freight business shows the potential and the reality. At 600 wagons a month (7,200 a year at ₹39 lakh) freight revenue would be about ₹2,800 crore; at 1,000 a month (12,000 at ₹40 lakh) about ₹4,800 crore. With fixed costs of about ₹250 crore and a variable margin of about 20%, EBIT would rise from about ₹310 crore (11%) to about ₹710 crore (14.8%). Yet management guides an 11–12% freight margin at both ends of that range, which tells you that price competition and the variation clause absorb most of the leverage in practice.

In plain terms

A wagon maker is a converter: it buys steel, adds castings and labour, and sells a finished wagon at a price that is reset with steel costs. It keeps about 11 rupees of every 100 before interest and tax. Getting more volume is the main way to earn more; getting a higher margin requires a different product.

The cash side of the business is less forgiving than the margin. At March 2026 the three carried ₹2,635 crore of receivables (about 92 days of combined revenue) and ₹2,535 crore of inventory, against ₹7,808 crore of equity. In a downturn, inventory rises because wagons wait for wheels or for approvals, receivables stay high because IR pays on its own schedule, and bank guarantees tie up borrowing capacity: Texmaco's bank and corporate guarantees reached ₹1,086 crore at March 2026, about a quarter of its revenue. This is why the group's FY26 free cash flow was negative (−₹373 crore) despite ₹1,076 crore of operating profit, and why rising interest rates (the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026) matter to companies whose interest line already includes guarantee commission. A maker's quality shows less in its margin than in how much of that margin turns into cash, which is the subject of several KPIs in Section 14.

Capacity is not the constraint

The industry has far more capacity than it uses. An industry commentator in February 2026 put theoretical national capacity at about 60,000 wagons a year against output of 35,000–40,000 in the peak. On company figures, Texmaco has 15,400 vehicle units of installed capacity including 2,400 at its Czech-linked tank-wagon unit Texmaco Nymwag (CARE Ratings), though its own presentation gives a standing run-rate of 2,500–3,000 a quarter, or 10,000–12,000 a year, and management calls capacity "elastic, 15,000–16,000". Titagarh has 12,000 a year (1,000 a month) and is deliberately running at 600–650 a month until the IR tender is clear. Jupiter's capacity has been described as rising from 6,000 toward 8,000–10,000. Spare capacity has two consequences. It keeps prices competitive when a tender does arrive, because every qualified builder wants to fill its sheds, and it means that a sudden large order can be absorbed quickly if wheels are available. In 2022–25 national output tripled without any company building a new plant from scratch; Texmaco bought one. The constraint on growth has been the order flow and the wheelsets, not the sheds.

Imports, China and the protected market

Complete wagons are hardly imported at all: HS 8606 imports were ₹86 crore in FY25 and ₹39 crore in FY26, mostly from Korea. RDSO approval, domestic-content rules under the 2017 Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India) Order and IR's design standards act as barriers. Since 2020, rule 144(xi) of the General Financial Rules has restricted bidders from countries sharing a land border with India, which effectively excludes China's CRRC, the world's largest rolling-stock maker, from Indian tenders. The import exposure is in parts, not wagons: ₹4,537 crore of HS 8607 imports in FY26, half of them from China (Section 05). In passenger rolling stock, propulsion, train control and brakes are still largely imported or supplied by multinationals, which is the dependence Titagarh is trying to reduce by building its own propulsion systems.

In plain terms

Competition among wagon builders is real but contained. Foreign rivals are kept out, capacity is ample, and IR's tender rules spread the work among qualified builders at one price. A wagon maker's position depends less on beating rivals and more on how much IR orders and how well it diversifies away from IR.

Concentration of customers is the flip side. Jupiter's annual report shows its three largest customers took 51.6% of FY26 revenue, the largest alone 28.6% (₹834 crore); in FY25 a single customer, almost certainly IR, took 42%. Texmaco sent 81.7% of its FY26 wagons to IR. Titagarh's freight book has been mostly IR. Even after the private-order pivot, a single buyer still accounts for a large share of every company's deliveries.
