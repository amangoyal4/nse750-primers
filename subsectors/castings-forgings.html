Castings & forgings: four furnaces, four different fortunes

A complete guide to the four castings and forgings companies in the NSE 750: a mining wear-parts exporter, a titanium and superalloy aspirant, a multi-technology auto supplier with a European half, and a crankshaft machinist turning into a shell-maker. They share molten metal and almost nothing else. This report explains how each turns a tonne of steel or titanium into profit, why the market values the smallest of them at more than the other three combined, and which numbers to watch as tariffs, wars and the vehicle cycle pull them in different directions.

00

The one-page view: one label, four unrelated businesses

The four companies melt or hot-shape metal. After that, their customers, cycles, margins and valuations have almost nothing in common, and an average across them hides more than it shows.

"Castings & Forgings" sounds like one industry. In the NSE 750 it is four. AIA Engineering casts high-chrome steel balls and liners that grind ore inside the world's copper, gold and iron-ore mills and cement plants, and the balls wear away in use, so mines buy them every month. PTC Industries is a Lucknow investment-casting foundry that has spent five years building India's first private aerospace-grade titanium melting, casting and forging complex, and is now qualifying parts for Safran and Airbus. CIE Automotive India is a multi-technology auto-component supplier, controlled by Spain's CIE Automotive, that forges, stamps, casts and machines parts for Indian vehicle makers and, through its European subsidiaries, for European car and truck makers. Balu Forge Industries machines crankshafts for export aftermarkets and has pivoted part of its new Belagavi forging capacity into artillery-shell bodies.

The four share a process family. Each heats metal until it can be poured into a mould (casting) or squeezed into a die (forging), and then machines, heat-treats and inspects the result. That shared process is why the market data vendors file them together. But the demand curve each one rides is unrelated to the others: tonnes of ore milled in Chile and Ghana; aircraft build rates in Toulouse and engine output at Safran; Indian tractor and two-wheeler production and German car output; and defence budgets in Delhi and the NATO capitals. In a normal year, these four curves do not peak together, which is why any sector-wide average of growth or margin is close to meaningless.

The clearest way to see how different they are is to put revenue next to market value. On the latest full year (FY26 for the three March-year companies, calendar 2025 for CIE) the four sold ₹15,536 crore of goods and earned ₹2,458 crore of reported profit after tax. The market values them at ₹92,762 crore. CIE produces 60.5% of the combined revenue but only 15.1% of the market value. PTC produces 3.9% of the revenue and 4.1% of the profit, yet commands 42.9% of the market value: on 21 October 2026 the smallest company by sales was worth more than AIA, and nearly three times CIE. Strip PTC out and the other three trade at about 22.5 times their combined profit; leave it in and the group's multiple jumps to 37.7 times. That single fact shapes this report: three of the companies are valued on what they earn today, and one is valued on what it might become.

Six forces define the group and organise the rest of this report. First, the height of the qualification wall: every company's margin maps onto how hard it is to become an approved supplier, from an aerospace titanium casting at the top to a jobbing auto forging near the bottom. Second, mining throughput and AIA's "conversion" thesis: the claim that mines will keep switching from forged steel balls to AIA's longer-lasting high-chrome balls, set against the fact that AIA's tonnage in FY26 (258,002 tonnes) was below FY22 and 13% below its FY24 peak. Third, the Western aerospace and defence supply-chain reshuffle after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which opened a door for Indian titanium (PTC) and artillery-shell bodies (Balu). Fourth, the vehicle cycle, which in 2026 means Indian production growing in double digits while European output shrinks; CIE straddles both. Fifth, cash conversion: the companies' accounting margins look similar, but how much of that profit arrives as cash differs enormously. Sixth, tariffs, carbon borders and input costs, including the US Section 232 duty that since 6 April 2026 charges 50% on the full value of grinding balls and many other steel articles.

01

From melt to money: how hot metal becomes a margin

Casting pours liquid metal into a shape; forging squeezes hot solid metal into one. The money is made not on the metal, which is passed through, but on qualification, yield and machining.

Start with the two words. A casting is made by melting metal, usually in an electric induction furnace, and pouring it into a mould. The mould can be sand (cheap, for engine blocks and pump bodies), a permanent steel die (for aluminium parts made at high speed), or a ceramic shell built around a wax pattern that is melted out before the metal is poured, the "lost-wax" or investment-casting method used for turbine blades and other thin-walled, precise parts. Casting can make complicated shapes in one step, but the metal's grain structure is random, so a casting is generally weaker in fatigue than a forging of the same alloy.

A forging is made by heating a solid steel bar (a billet) to around 1,150–1,250 °C and pressing or hammering it between dies. The metal is never liquid. Its grain is squeezed to follow the part's shape, which gives forgings the best fatigue strength for parts that are loaded and unloaded millions of times: crankshafts, axle beams, steering knuckles, connecting rods, gear blanks. Forging is capital-heavy (an 8,000-tonne press costs tens of crores) and wasteful of material (the "flash" squeezed out at the edges is scrap), so the forged blank is normally machined on CNC lathes and grinders to its final dimensions.

Every one of the four sells the same thing underneath: processed metal with a guarantee attached. The metal itself (steel scrap, ferro-chrome, special-bar-quality steel, titanium sponge, aluminium ingot) is bought at market prices and, in nearly every contract, passed through to the customer with a lag of one quarter or so. The company earns its margin on everything it adds: the yield it gets from each tonne melted, the speed and reliability of its lines, the machining that turns a rough blank into a finished part, and above all the right to supply at all, which is granted only after a customer has tested the part, audited the plant and signed off the process. In aerospace that sign-off can take two to five years; in mining a trial in a working mill takes three months to two years; in autos a new part is typically approved in six to eighteen months.

The four companies' value chains differ in where they start and end. AIA buys scrap and ferro-chrome, melts and casts in Gujarat, heat-treats, and ships to stock-points run by its Vega subsidiaries in Chile, Peru, Australia, Africa and North America, from which balls are delivered to the mill and consumed continuously. It captures value by selling the mine a lower total cost of grinding (fewer kilograms of media per tonne of ore and better throughput) rather than by marking up the metal. PTC's chain is the longest: titanium sponge (bought, mostly imported) is melted with master alloy and scrap into ingots in vacuum furnaces, which are then cast or forged, machined (in its UK subsidiary Trac Precision or in Lucknow), inspected and certified before going to Safran, Airbus, Blue Origin or Indian defence integrators. CIE buys special-bar-quality steel from Indian and European mills, cuts, heats, forges, trims, heat-treats and machines it, and delivers to the assembly lines of Mahindra, Maruti, Tata, tractor makers and, in Europe, carmakers and truck makers. Balu historically bought or made forgings and machined them into finished crankshafts for export aftermarkets and engine makers; it is now integrating backward into heavy forging at Belagavi and forward into empty shell bodies supplied to ammunition integrators.

The ladder in the chart is the first thing an expert notices. Margin follows the height of the approval wall, not the tonnage. Aerolloy, PTC's titanium-casting subsidiary, earned a 45% EBITDA margin in the Jun-2026 quarter because there are very few places in the world that can melt and cast flight-critical titanium to Safran's standard. AIA's 28–29% operating margin on what looks like a commodity steel ball comes from the fact that a mine will only switch suppliers after months of trial in its own mill, and AIA and its Belgian rival Magotteaux are the two established names in high-chrome media. Balu's 27% reflects its machining and aftermarket niche. CIE India's 16–18% is a good number for an auto Tier-1, where the carmaker's purchasing department sets the price each year. Trac Precision, the UK machining job shop PTC bought in December 2024, earned 8.5%, the margin of a business anyone with the right machines can enter. Below them all sits the typical Indian MSME foundry at around 8–14% EBITDA.

In plain terms

Nobody in this industry gets rich by selling metal. They get paid for being trusted to turn metal into a part that will not fail. The harder it is to win that trust, the fewer competitors there are, and the higher the margin. That is why a titanium casting for a jet engine earns three times the margin of a forged car part, even though both start as a lump of metal heated until it glows.

Two consequences follow. First, revenue growth is a poor guide to profit growth in this sub-sector, because metal prices move revenue up and down without changing the margin in rupees. When ferro-chrome or steel prices rise 10%, a pass-through supplier's revenue rises a few per cent and its margin percentage falls, even though it is no worse off. Analysts therefore prefer measures per tonne or per part. Second, the moat is mostly invisible in the accounts. A qualification is not an asset on the balance sheet, so the value of PTC's Safran and Airbus approvals, or of AIA's trial record at a Chilean copper mine, shows up only in future orders. That makes these businesses hard to value from published numbers alone, and explains why the market's estimates of them swing so widely.

02

India's foundry floor: the world's second-largest, and mostly small

India pours about 16 million tonnes of castings and forges about 3 million tonnes a year. The four listed members are a sliver of that tonnage and a much larger share of its high-specification value.

India is the world's second-largest producer of both castings and forgings, behind China. The Institute of Indian Foundrymen (IIF) put castings output at 15.86 million tonnes in 2024-25, up from 15.16 million tonnes in 2023-24, worth about US$22 billion (roughly ₹2.1 lakh crore at ₹94.9 to the dollar), from about 5,000 foundries of which roughly 90% are MSMEs. The figure is single-sourced (it comes from IIF's president at the 74th Indian Foundry Congress in February 2026, as reported by the trade press) and should be treated as indicative. An older and still widely quoted estimate of about 12 million tonnes dates from an earlier Foundry Congress and should not be used for 2024-25. China produces around 50 million tonnes a year, roughly three times India's output.

Forging is a smaller and more concentrated industry. The Association of Indian Forging Industry (AIFI) puts output at about 2.9 million tonnes on 4.8 million tonnes of capacity, so roughly 60% utilisation, with about 30% exported and about US$3.8 billion invested. Automobiles take about 60–65% of forgings (commercial vehicles, cars, two-wheelers and tractors), with the rest going to industrial machinery, railways, oil and gas, defence and aerospace. Bharat Forge, which is not in this sub-sector's list, is the largest Indian forger by a wide margin; the other listed forgers include Ramkrishna Forgings, Happy Forgings, MM Forgings and Sona BLW, as well as CIE and Balu.

The castings mix is dominated by grey iron (roughly two-thirds of tonnage), followed by ductile (SG) iron, steel and aluminium and other non-ferrous metals, each around a tenth. Foundries cluster by tradition: Rajkot and Ahmedabad in Gujarat, Kolhapur and Pune in Maharashtra, Coimbatore and Chennai in Tamil Nadu, Belagavi in Karnataka, Batala and Jalandhar in Punjab, Howrah in Bengal and Agra in Uttar Pradesh. Most are family-owned units that pour grey-iron castings for pumps, valves, tractors and local engineering firms at thin margins. Pollution rules have slowly squeezed them: foundries are in the "red" or "orange" categories of the pollution boards, cupola furnaces (which burn coke) have been pushed towards electric induction furnaces, and the National Green Tribunal has forced foundries in the Delhi region and around the Taj Mahal to switch to piped natural gas. That steady rise in the cost of being small is a slow consolidation force.

In plain terms

India's foundry industry is huge in tonnes and tiny in companies. Thousands of small units make ordinary iron castings. The four listed members sell perhaps 0.4–0.5 million tonnes of metal products between them (an INDICATIVE estimate), about 2–3% of national tonnage, but they sit at the top of the ladder: high-alloy consumables, aerospace metals, Tier-1 auto parts and defence components. Their fortunes depend on their own niches, not on national foundry output.

Balu sells to commercial-vehicle, agricultural, oil-and-gas and power-generation engine makers and their aftermarkets, and increasingly to defence and aerospace customers.

Mining: ore milled, not mine capex

Grinding media demand follows the tonnes of ore that pass through a mine's grinding mills, not the size of the mining industry's capital budget. A concentrator grinding copper, gold or iron ore consumes roughly 0.3–1.0 kilograms of media per tonne of ore, depending on how hard the rock is and the type of mill (an INDICATIVE industry range). Two long trends work in AIA's favour. Ore grades keep falling (average copper head grades have slid from around 1% to around 0.6% over two decades), so more rock has to be milled for every tonne of metal. And copper and gold prices were strong through 2025 and 2026, with gold at record highs, which keeps mines running at full throughput and willing to spend on optimisation. Against that, AIA's management told analysts in August 2026 that its mining trials in Chile, Peru and Ghana were "taking longer than anticipated", and its mining tonnage fell 3.8% in FY26. Section 07 explains why.

The non-mining part of AIA's business (cement, coal-fired power and aggregates) is mature. High-chrome media are already standard in cement ball mills, and new cement plants increasingly use vertical roller mills, which need far less grinding media. Indian cement capacity additions of 40–50 million tonnes a year keep this business growing in line with Indian construction, which is one reason AIA's "others" tonnage rose 9.8% in FY26 even as mining fell. About 35.55% of AIA's FY26 consolidated sales were made in India, more than its "exporter" label suggests; readers interested in the cement leg can see our cement report.

Aerospace: the bottleneck is castings and forgings

Commercial aircraft demand is not the problem. Defence: budgets, indigenisation and the shell shortage

Two defence trends support PTC and Balu. In India, the government reserves about 75% of the capital procurement budget for domestic industry and has issued positive-indigenisation lists of several hundred items that must be bought from Indian suppliers. Titanium castings for missiles (PTC supplies a BrahMos sub-system and castings), a titanium cradle for the 105 mm gun on the Indian Light Tank (ordered by DRDO's ARDE lab) and artillery components for Gun Factory Kanpur all sit within that push. Abroad, Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 exposed a severe shortage of 155 mm artillery shells in Europe and the United States; the EU set a target of 2 million shells a year. Balu's automated empty-shell line (nameplate 360,000 bodies a year), a five-year binding MoU with a NATO-affiliated buyer, a 30,000-unit order for 152 mm shells from an Indian energetics company and a 10,000-unit pilot order for 155 mm extended-range shells all ride this wave.

In plain terms

If copper prices fall and mines slow down, AIA feels it. If Indian tractor sales slump after a bad monsoon, CIE feels it. If Airbus slows its build rate or Safran delays a qualification, PTC feels it. If a ceasefire cuts NATO's appetite for shells, Balu feels it. Very few events hit all four at once, which is the one useful thing about owning them together.

Two forces do hit all four, though less evenly. The first is the rupee, at about ₹94.9 to the dollar on 19 October 2026 and close to a record low of about ₹96.8. A weaker rupee raises the rupee value of AIA's and Balu's export sales and of PTC's dollar and pound revenues, while making imported titanium sponge and alloys dearer. AIA hedges very little (its forward cover in August 2026 was about US$21 million, against roughly US$300 million of annual sales outside India), so it booked a ₹170 crore foreign-exchange gain in FY26. The second is energy: the West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, pushed Brent crude to about US$118 a barrel before a ceasefire on 7 April, and raised freight, gas and LPG costs. CIE blamed it for an 80 basis-point fall in its Indian margin in the Jun-2026 quarter; AIA cited ocean freight of US$8,000–9,000 a container; Balu said the conflict disrupted its India–UAE operations in the March 2026 quarter.

04

Scrap, chrome, titanium, power: where ₹100 of revenue goes

Metal is the largest cost for every member except the aerospace caster, but it is largely passed through. Energy, freight and labour are the costs that actually squeeze margins.

Each member buys a different basket of inputs, and that basket explains much of its margin behaviour. AIA's main charge is steel scrap, to which it adds high-carbon ferro-chrome (high-chrome grinding media contain roughly 12–28% chromium) and, for some grades, molybdenum and nickel; then it spends heavily on electricity to melt the charge in induction furnaces and on ocean freight to ship heavy balls across the world. CIE and Balu buy special-bar-quality (SBQ) alloy steel from Indian and European mills and spend on gas or electricity to heat billets, and on machining. PTC buys titanium sponge, master alloys and titanium scrap for its vacuum furnaces, plus nickel and cobalt for superalloys; its biggest cost is people, because aerospace casting and inspection is labour- and skill-intensive.

The pass-through mechanism is the most important thing to understand about input costs here. Nearly every contract in the sector links price to the cost of metal, either by formula (CIE's steel clauses reset prices each quarter against an index) or by habit (AIA re-prices repeat orders when scrap and ferro-chrome move). The lag matters. When metal prices rise, the supplier pays the new price immediately but bills the customer at the old price for one or two quarters, so its margin dips; when metal prices fall, the reverse happens and margins widen temporarily. Over a full cycle the effect washes out, but in any one quarter it can move margins by one or two percentage points, and it inflates and deflates revenue with no change in the underlying business.

The costs that are not passed through quickly are the ones that cause lasting damage: electricity, gas, freight and wages. These bit in 2026. The West Asia war raised gas and LPG prices for forging furnaces and heat-treatment lines, pushed up consumables, and disrupted container shipping. CIE said its "countervailing measures" (price negotiations and efficiency projects) would show up over the next two quarters. AIA, which ships tens of thousands of tonnes of heavy steel balls by sea every quarter, is the most exposed to freight; it partly protects itself with captive renewable power, which cuts its exposure to state electricity tariffs.

Titanium: the input with geopolitics attached

Titanium deserves its own paragraph, because for PTC the source of the metal is part of the product. Aerospace-grade titanium starts as ilmenite or rutile sand, which is chlorinated into titanium tetrachloride and reduced with magnesium into porous "sponge" (the Kroll process). Sponge, master alloy and scrap are then melted together under vacuum into ingots. Only a handful of countries make aerospace-grade sponge: chiefly the United States, Japan, Russia, Kazakhstan and China, plus a small Indian plant at Chavara in Kerala. China's share of global titanium-metal production rose from about 40% in 2019 to more than 75% in 2025, according to vendor figures quoted in PTC's annual report, but Western aerospace qualification of Chinese titanium is limited by politics and export controls. Russia's VSMPO-AVISMA, historically the largest supplier to Western aerospace, remains the dominant producer of aerospace-grade mill products outside China; one weak, single-source estimate says it still supplies 25–30% of Western aerospace titanium. Canada and the UK have sanctioned VSMPO; the United States and the EU have not listed it directly. PTC has signed a sponge-supply memorandum with AMIC TOHO Titanium in Saudi Arabia and an MoU with the Odisha government for a proposed sponge plant, for which no cost or timeline has been disclosed.

In plain terms

Rising metal prices make these companies' revenue look bigger and their margins look thinner, without changing much else. The costs to worry about are the ones customers do not reimburse quickly: power, gas, shipping and wages. For PTC the issue is different: the question is not what titanium costs, but whose titanium a Western jet-engine maker will accept.

A crowded floor, a narrow top: who competes with whom

Indian castings are fragmented; global grinding media is a duopoly at the top with Chinese forgers underneath; aerospace castings are an oligopoly of Western giants.

Structure depends on the layer of the industry. At the bottom, Indian castings are very fragmented: about 5,000 units, roughly 90% of them MSMEs, and the largest listed foundries (AIA, Nelcast, Alicon, Kirloskar Ferrous, Electrosteel, Steelcast, PTC and others) together account for well under 15% of national tonnage, an INDICATIVE estimate. Forgings are more concentrated at the top: AIFI represents more than 200 companies, but Bharat Forge alone sells around ₹9,000–10,000 crore on a standalone basis, and the top eight listed forgers probably account for 40–50% of organised forging value (INDICATIVE). Neither national number tells you much about the four members, because each competes in a global niche with its own set of rivals.

Grinding media: a high-chrome duopoly fighting forged incumbents

Grinding media come in two families. Forged steel balls are the traditional product in mining: cheaper per tonne, tougher, but faster-wearing. High-chrome cast media, AIA's speciality, are harder and wear more slowly, so a mine that switches typically consumes fewer kilograms per tonne of ore. In high-chrome, AIA and Magotteaux are the two established global suppliers; report vendors put AIA at about 19% of the high-chrome market and Magotteaux at about 16%, but these shares are INDICATIVE and have not been verified from primary data. Magotteaux, based in Belgium, has been owned since 2012 by Chile's Sigdo Koppers, which paid about US$790 million for it. In forged media, the leaders are Moly-Cop (owned by the US private-equity firm American Industrial Partners since 2017), Chile's ME Elecmetal, South Africa's Scaw and Brazil's Gerdau, plus a fast-growing group of Chinese forgers. AIA's management said in August 2026 that about 20 Chinese forging companies now supply mines worldwide and that "the top two mines in Chile today use Chinese forged media". It added that there is no Chinese presence in high-chrome yet.

Archetype A, the engineered consumable. AIA sells something that is destroyed in use. A grinding ball shrinks as it tumbles in the mill until it is too small to work and passes out with the ore; liners and diaphragms wear and are replaced on a schedule. A mine running a mill at full throughput therefore reorders every month, which gives AIA an annuity-like revenue stream tied to tonnes of ore milled rather than to the mine's capital budget. AIA's rent comes from the total-cost-of-ownership argument: if its balls wear 25–40% more slowly than forged balls and improve throughput, the mine saves money even at a higher price per tonne. That rent is protected by the trial process, which ties up a mill for months and makes switching costly for the mine. The drawback is working capital: to guarantee supply, AIA holds large stocks at Vega depots near its customers.

Archetype B, the qualification platform. PTC is building capabilities (vacuum melting, large castings, open-die forging) that very few companies have, then spending years getting each part number qualified with each customer. Revenue arrives in stages: development orders (small), qualification batches, low-rate initial production, and finally serial supply for the life of an engine or aircraft programme, which can run for decades. Margins on qualified parts are high, because once a part is approved the customer cannot switch without re-qualifying another supplier. The cost is capital spent years before revenue, inventory tied up in parts going through qualification, and binary risk: a failed qualification can strand an investment.

Archetype C, the processing fee on steel. CIE earns a conversion margin on steel it buys and passes through. Its rent is modest because carmakers set prices annually and multi-source most parts, but it has two advantages: scale across six technologies (forging, stamping, aluminium and iron casting, gears, composites, magnets), which lets it offer a carmaker several parts from one supplier, and negative or low working capital, because carmakers pay in about a month while steel suppliers give longer credit. Operating leverage is high: a 10% rise in volume on a largely fixed cost base lifts EBITDA by more.

Archetype D, the machining premium. Balu buys or makes forgings and machines them into finished crankshafts and components, selling into export aftermarkets where it can earn more than an OEM-dedicated supplier. Its margin of about 27% reflects that niche. The same machining skill has been redeployed into shell bodies, which need tight tolerances and certification. The model's weakness is credit: aftermarket distributors and new export customers are given 140–180 days to pay, according to CRISIL, so profit arrives as receivables before it arrives as cash.

The ball that eats itself: AIA's consumable duopoly and the conversion thesis Deep dive

AIA's profit has doubled since FY22 while its tonnage has gone nowhere. The bull case rests on mines switching to high-chrome balls; the evidence of the last two years is that the switch has stalled.

AIA Engineering, founded in 1978 by Bhadresh Shah as Ahmedabad Induction Alloys and listed in 2005, is the second-largest maker of high-chrome grinding media and mill internals in the world. It makes balls, liners, diaphragms and discharge systems for ball mills and semi-autogenous (SAG) mills at its Gujarat plants, with installed capacity of 4,36,000 tonnes a year (after closing the Bangalore plant of its listed subsidiary Welcast Steels), and sells through Vega subsidiaries in the UAE, the US, Chile, Australia, South Africa, Indonesia, China, Ghana and elsewhere. In FY26 it sold 258,002 tonnes, booked ₹4,420 crore of revenue from operations and earned ₹1,269 crore of profit. It has almost no debt (₹10 crore at March 2026) and a treasury book that management describes as "₹4,500 crore to maybe ₹5,000 crore".

The economics of the product explain the business. A grinding ball literally eats itself: it is consumed by abrasion and impact inside the mill until it is too small to be useful. A mine therefore buys media continuously, in proportion to the ore it grinds, much as a printer owner buys ink. The pitch for high-chrome media over traditional forged steel balls is total cost of ownership. High-chrome balls are harder and wear more slowly, so a mine consumes fewer kilograms per tonne of ore (an INDICATIVE 25–40% fewer); they also tend to hold their shape, which can improve grinding efficiency and metal recovery. The mine pays more per tonne of media and less per tonne of ore ground. That is why AIA can earn a 28–29% operating margin on a product that, to an outsider, is a steel ball.

The conversion thesis, and the number that undermines it

The bull case for AIA has always been conversion. AIA's own disclosures and its subsidiary Welcast's have described a mining grinding-media pool of roughly 2.5 million tonnes a year, of which only about a fifth uses high-chrome; the rest is forged. In August 2026 management framed it as a "2–2.5 million ton" total opportunity, narrowing to "1–1.5 million tons" in gold, copper and iron ore (media plus liners), with Latin America alone consuming more than 500,000 tonnes of grinding media, a market it said "can become a 300,000–400,000-ton market for us". With 436,000 tonnes of capacity and 258,000 tonnes of sales, AIA has room for roughly 178,000 more tonnes without building a new plant. On paper, that is many years of growth.

The difficulty is the volume record. FY26 tonnage was below FY22. The mining share of volume fell from 65.0% to 61.9%. There was, on these numbers, no measurable conversion in FY26.

Why conversion has stalled

Management's August 2026 call offered four explanations, and the trade data add a fifth. First, trials are slow: AIA's mining trials in Chile and Latin America (a second mill at an existing customer), a Peruvian copper mine and a Ghanaian gold mine were "taking longer than anticipated because of certain technicalities", and a trial in a working mill can take from three months to two years. Second, Chinese forged media have moved into AIA's target accounts; management said the top two Chilean mines now use Chinese forged balls and about 20 Chinese forgers supply mines worldwide, which raises the price gap that AIA's TCO argument has to overcome. Fourth, timing and mix: mining volume fell about 5,400 tonnes from the March to the June 2026 quarter, which management attributed to "timing, product mix, order execution". Sponge to spar: India's titanium chain and the ₹39,800 crore question Deep dive

PTC is building what India has never had: a private, integrated aerospace-grade titanium and superalloy chain. The market already values it as if the build will succeed. The accounts show how early it still is.

PTC Industries was incorporated in Lucknow in 1963 as Precision Tools & Castings and spent most of its life making investment and sand castings in steel and alloys for valves, pumps, cryogenic LNG equipment and marine use, often for export. In the last five years it has reinvented itself around a subsidiary, Aerolloy Technologies, built on 50 acres allotted in the Lucknow node of the Uttar Pradesh Defence Industrial Corridor. The goal is to do on one site what normally takes several companies in several countries: melt titanium and nickel superalloys under vacuum into ingots, cast and forge them into aerospace and defence parts, machine and inspect them, and certify them to Safran's, Airbus's or a defence laboratory's standard. In December 2024 it also bought Trac Precision Solutions, a UK machining business, for ₹181.2 crore.

The reason this matters goes back to 2022. Russia's VSMPO-AVISMA had been one of the largest suppliers of titanium to Western aerospace. After the invasion of Ukraine, Boeing stopped buying from it, Airbus began phasing out direct Russian supply, and Canada and later the UK imposed sanctions. Western engine and airframe makers set out to qualify new sources of titanium and of castings and forgings, which were already the tightest bottleneck in an industry trying to raise build rates. India, with low costs, a growing defence-industrial policy and no political objection from Western buyers, was an obvious candidate, but it had, in PTC's own words, "no aerospace-grade titanium melting capacity of its own" until PTC built it.

CIE's two halves: a growing Indian supplier and a shrinking European one, on a calendar year Deep dive

CIE Automotive India is two businesses under one ticker. India grows in double digits with a slipping margin; Europe shrinks in euros with a recovering margin. Reading it requires knowing which half moved, and which calendar it is on.

CIE Automotive India is the Indian arm of Spain's CIE Automotive, a multi-technology auto-components group. It was formed in 2013 when CIE took a controlling stake in Mahindra's forging and component businesses, including Mahindra's European forgers; it bought Bill Forge in Bengaluru in 2016 for about ₹1,331 crore; it exited its German forging units in 2022 at a heavy loss; and it was renamed after Mahindra sold out in 2023. Its plants make forgings, stampings, aluminium and iron castings, gears, composites and magnets in India, and forgings and gears in Spain, Lithuania and Italy (Metalcastello), with a forging plant in Mexico that has been reported within the "Europe" segment since 2025 even though Mexico is not in Europe. In calendar 2025 it reported revenue from operations of ₹9,406.5 crore, operating profit of ₹1,362 crore (14.5% on screener's basis) and PAT of ₹828 crore.

The calendar trap and the revenue trap

Two accounting conventions catch out almost every reader. First, CIE's financial year ends in December, not March. Its latest full year is CY2025, three months earlier than the others' FY26. Its Q2 CY26 is April–June 2026, the same period as the others' Q1 FY27, and its Q3 CY26 (results due on the evening of 22 October 2026) matches their Q2 FY27. For a like-for-like comparison with the March-year companies, use the twelve months to June 2026: screener shows sales of ₹9,997 crore, operating profit of ₹1,482 crore and PAT of ₹903 crore. Second, CIE's own "sales" figure excludes other operating income (scrap sales and export incentives), which screener includes. In CY25 the company reported "sales" of ₹9,122 crore against revenue from operations of ₹9,406.5 crore; in Q2 CY26 the gap was exactly the ₹77.3 crore of other operating income (₹48.9 crore in India, ₹28.4 crore in Europe). Company margins (16–17%) are on the smaller denominator; screener's (14–15%) on the larger one. This report uses screener for cross-company comparisons and the company's figures for the India-Europe split, and never mixes them in one ratio.

In plain terms

When CIE says "Q2", it means April to June. When AIA, PTC and Balu say "Q2", they mean July to September. And CIE's "16.4% EBITDA margin" and screener's "14.9% operating margin" for the same quarter are both correct, measured on different revenue figures. Always check which quarter and which revenue line you are reading.

Tariff walls and carbon borders: the rules that now shape export economics

Since April 2026 the US charges 50% on the full value of grinding balls and many steel castings and forgings. Europe's carbon border tax is now live. At home, defence procurement rules tilt orders towards Indian suppliers.

Trade policy matters more to this sub-sector than to most Indian industrials, because three of the four members earn much of their revenue abroad and the fourth (CIE) is starting a US export programme. The US tariff regime changed three times in 2026, and the change that matters most for castings and forgings is the one that received least attention.

The US: three layers, one that bites

The broad "reciprocal" tariffs the US imposed under emergency powers (IEEPA), including the extra 25% penalty on India linked to Russian-oil purchases from August 2025, were struck down by the US Supreme Court on 20 February 2026. A temporary 10% surcharge under Section 122 ran until 24 July 2026. Since then India has faced a 10% Section 301 duty on top of the normal (MFN) tariff, after being placed in the 10% band of a forced-labour investigation covering 17 economies (China and Vietnam are at 12.5%); the duty is under legal challenge, and a second Section 301 investigation, into excess manufacturing capacity, also names India.

For castings and forgings, though, the binding constraint is Section 232, the national-security tariff on steel, aluminium and copper and their "derivatives". CBP's steel-derivative list, effective 18 August 2025, includes HTS 7325.91 (grinding balls and similar articles for mills), 7325.99 (other cast articles of iron or steel) and the 7326 lines (other articles of iron or steel, including many forged and stamped parts); the August 2025 inclusions also covered 8483.10 (transmission shafts and crankshafts) and several 8708 auto-part lines. These goods pay 50%. A proclamation of 2 April 2026 (91 FR 18201) made the change that matters: from 6 April 2026 the 50% applies to the full customs value of the article, regardless of its metal content, rather than only to the value of the steel in it. Separately, medium- and heavy-duty vehicle parts have paid 25% since 1 November 2025 (90 FR 48451), in a proclamation that explicitly cites US dependence on imported "transmission shafts, castings, forgings"; passenger-vehicle and light-truck parts have paid 25% since May 2025. Goods under Section 232 do not also pay the Section 301 layer, and the 232 measures generally do not stack on each other, though CBP's treatment of each entry needs checking.

How much does this matter to each member? Less than the headline suggests, because none of the four depends heavily on the US. AIA's mining customers are mainly in Latin America, Australia and Africa; India's grinding-ball exports to the US were about US$20 million in 2024, roughly a tenth of the total, and AIA's own US share is not disclosed but is small. Balu's commercial-vehicle and industrial crankshafts to the US face 25% or 50%, depending on classification. CIE's new ₹200 crore-a-year machined iron-castings programme for "American customers" is the most directly exposed new revenue stream; management has not said how the duty is shared. PTC's US customers (Blue Origin, Honeywell) buy aerospace parts that are generally covered by separate civil-aircraft rules, though this report has not verified the treatment line by line. A further risk is the US Department of Defense's identification of castings and forgings as one of four areas of pressing supply-chain concern, which supports US foundries' requests to extend the lists. The next inclusion window for auto parts is expected in January 2027.

Europe: CBAM goes live

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026. Importers of covered iron and steel products, including some articles in chapters 72 and 73 (such as 7318 and 7326), must report embedded emissions and buy certificates priced off the EU carbon market. For Indian castings and forgings exported to the EU in covered codes, that is a new cost and a new compliance burden, with more weight on suppliers whose electricity is coal-heavy. It is relevant to AIA (European cement customers), to Balu and to any CIE India exports to Europe; whether grinding balls (7325) fall within the covered codes has not been verified for this report. The flip side is that foundries running electric induction furnaces on renewable power, as AIA partly does with its captive wind and solar and its new hybrid plant, will carry a lower certificate cost than coke-fired MSMEs.

In plain terms

The US now charges a 50% tax on the whole value of steel grinding balls and many other steel castings and forgings, and 25% on truck and car parts. That mostly closes the US to Indian suppliers in those products unless they have something US buyers cannot get elsewhere. Europe now charges for the carbon in imported steel goods. Neither is decisive for these four today, because their big markets lie elsewhere, but both narrow the export routes for anyone hoping to grow by shipping more steel to rich countries.

At home: indigenisation, corridors and pollution rules

Indian policy pulls the other way for the defence-exposed members. The Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP-2020) and the Ministry of Defence's positive-indigenisation lists (several hundred items that must be sourced domestically) push orders to Indian suppliers, and about 75% of the capital procurement budget is earmarked for domestic industry; Balu cites a FY26 defence budget of ₹7.84 lakh crore and national targets of ₹3 lakh crore of defence production and ₹50,000 crore of exports by FY30. The Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu Defence Industrial Corridors provide land and approvals, and PTC's Aerolloy complex sits in the Lucknow node. PTC has "Green Channel" status with the Directorate General of Quality Assurance, which speeds acceptance. Exports of shells and other military items need SCOMET licences from the DGFT, which matters for Balu's NATO-linked contracts. Large-calibre ammunition has been opened to private manufacturers, though shell bodies were long subcontracted.

Booms, busts and wars: six decades in one timeline

Forgers follow the vehicle cycle with leverage, AIA follows ore milled with an annuity's smoothing, aerospace follows build rates and qualification, and defence follows budgets and wars. The four rarely peak together.

The history of these companies is a history of separate cycles that occasionally collide. Before 2000, India's foundries and forges were MSME clusters serving pumps, valves, textile machinery and a small auto industry; PTC (1963) and AIA (1978) were born in that world. The 2003–2008 commodity super-cycle brought a mining capex boom and the first Indian auto-part exports; AIA listed in 2005 and built its Vega network. The 2008–09 crash hit forgers hard on both continents. In 2013 CIE took control of Mahindra's forging and component businesses, including European plants in Germany, Italy, Lithuania and the UK. Between 2016 and 2020 AIA's mining business overtook cement and its capacity passed 300,000 tonnes.

The 2020s brought three shocks in quick succession. Covid in 2020 and the freight and commodity spike of 2021–22 inflated AIA's realisations and revenue (FY23's ₹4,909 crore was a peak) while volumes barely moved. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 then did three things at once: it pushed Western aerospace to look for non-Russian titanium (PTC's opportunity), created a NATO artillery-shell shortage (Balu's), and sent European energy prices soaring, which led CIE to exit its German forging business at a loss in 2022. And in 2025–26 trade policy and war returned: US Section 232 tariffs on auto parts and steel derivatives, the Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling, the West Asia war of February–April 2026, and the Section 301 regime from July 2026.

Reading a melt shop: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen measures that explain how a castings or forgings business makes money, how to calculate each from published numbers, what good looks like, and where the definitions trip people up.

Every company in this sub-sector, whatever it makes, runs on the same basic equation. It sells a quantity of processed metal (tonnes of balls, numbers of crankshafts, kilograms of titanium castings) at a price, buys the metal at a market price that it passes on with a lag, and pays for energy, freight, people and overheads. What is left is operating profit. The KPIs below are ways of seeing each term of that equation separately, so you can tell whether a change in profit came from selling more, charging more, a metal-price lag, a cost shock or an accounting line that has nothing to do with operations.

A worked example in round numbers. A foundry sells 1,000 tonnes of high-chrome media at ₹170 a kilogram: revenue is 10,00,000 kg × ₹170 = ₹17 crore. Its cash costs are ₹122 a kilogram (metal ₹65, power and freight ₹35, people and overheads ₹22), or ₹12.2 crore. Operating EBITDA is ₹4.8 crore, or ₹48 a kilogram and a 28% margin. Now suppose ferro-chrome and scrap rise by ₹10 a kilogram and the foundry cannot re-price for one quarter: EBITDA falls to ₹3.8 crore (₹38/kg, 22%). When it does re-price to ₹180, EBITDA returns to ₹48/kg in rupees, but the margin is now 26.7%, because revenue is higher. AIA's FY26 numbers run on exactly this arithmetic: 258,002 tonnes × about ₹171/kg = ₹4,420 crore of revenue; expenses of ₹3,158 crore (about ₹122/kg); operating profit ₹1,261 crore, about ₹49/kg.

The same equation applies to the other three, with different terms doing the work. For CIE, the "tonnes" term is vehicles built multiplied by CIE's content per vehicle, so management controls content (new parts won, about ₹500 crore a year of new business in H1 CY26) but not production; steel is passed through each quarter, and energy is the term that hurt in 2026. For Balu, the volume term is parts machined and shells delivered, and the price term carries a premium for machining and certification; the term that has gone wrong is not in the profit equation at all but in the cash that follows it. For PTC, volume is the number of qualified part numbers multiplied by each customer's annual call-off, and the price term is high because few suppliers exist; the constraint is the time it takes to qualify, which sets when volume arrives. Reading results is largely a matter of identifying which term moved.

In plain terms

Profit equals how much you sell times the gap between what you charge and what it costs you to make it. Metal costs move the price and the cost together, so they change revenue a lot and profit only a little and only for a while. The things that really move profit are selling more, charging more for the same metal (better mix, more machining), and keeping energy, freight and wages under control.

1. Tonnage sold and capacity utilisation

What it is. The physical volume a company sells in a period, and that volume as a share of what its plants could produce. Formula: utilisation = tonnes produced (or sold) ÷ installed capacity. Worked example: AIA sold 258,002 tonnes in FY26 on 4,36,000 tonnes of capacity, so utilisation was about 59%; the Jun-2026 quarter's 64,644 tonnes annualise to about 259,000 tonnes, the same level. What drives it: customers' output (ore milled, vehicles built), new customer wins, inventory cycles at distributors, and for exporters the timing of shipments. Benchmark: above 80% is tight and supports pricing; 60–80% is normal; below 60% means fixed costs are spread thinly and there is little reason to add capacity. AIA is just below 60%; India's forging industry is about 60% (2.9 million tonnes on 4.8 million).

Only AIA discloses tonnage regularly. PTC discloses melt capacity (VAR about 1,500 tonnes a year of ingot, PAM about 600, EB 5,000 under installation) but not output; Balu discloses forging and machining capacity (100,000+ and 45,000 tonnes a year, rising to 150,000 and 80,000) but not volumes; CIE discloses neither. The definition trap: capacities are not comparable across the four. AIA's is finished grinding media; PTC's is ingot (a casting uses a fraction of its ingot); Balu's forging tonnes are raw forgings, much of which become scrap in machining. Never add them, and never multiply an ingot capacity by a part price.

What it is. Operating profit earned on each kilogram sold: the cleanest single measure of a tonnage business's economics, because it strips out the metal-price effect on revenue. 5. Raw-material share and alloy pass-through

What it is. Metal and alloy costs (including the change in inventories) as a share of revenue, and how quickly changes are passed to customers. Formula: (materials consumed + change in inventory) ÷ revenue from operations. Worked example: AIA's materials were 38.36% of revenue in FY26, down from 40.53% in FY25; PTC's net material cost was 17.6%. 8. Order book and book-to-bill

What it is. The value of orders received but not yet delivered, and the ratio of new orders to revenue in a period. Formula: order cover in months = order book ÷ (annual revenue ÷ 12); book-to-bill = orders received ÷ revenue billed. Worked example: AIA's order book was ₹977 crore at 1 July 2026 (₹868 crore at 1 April); against FY26 revenue of ₹4,420 crore that is about 2.7 months of sales. What drives it: for AIA, conversions and large mine contracts; for Balu and PTC, defence and aerospace programme awards. Benchmark: for a consumable, a short order book is normal (customers reorder monthly) and the direction matters; for defence and aerospace suppliers, more than two years of revenue in the book is strong, one to two years average.

How they compare: AIA ₹977 crore; Balu says defence, aerospace and rail are about 50% of its order book but discloses no value; PTC discloses no order book and its Airbus, Safran, BrahMos and DRDO orders carry no published values; CIE reports new business won (about ₹500 crore a year of peak revenue in H1 CY26), which is a lifetime-revenue concept, not an order book. The definition trap: a development or qualification order (most of PTC's) is not a production order, and an MoU (Balu's NATO arrangement, PTC's Safran production MoU) is not a firm order. Do not add them to anything.

For AIA, the useful reading is the change in the order book alongside tonnage. A rising book in a quarter of flat shipments (as between April and July 2026) suggests orders are building ahead of deliveries, often because a large mine contract has been signed; a falling book with falling shipments is the warning sign. For a defence supplier, the reading is the ratio of firm orders to annual revenue and the delivery schedule: Balu's 30,000-shell order "with supplies from June 2026" tells you when revenue should start, but not at what price.

In plain terms

An order book only means something if you know its size and what is in it. AIA tells you both (about three months of sales). Balu tells you what share is defence but not how much. PTC tells you neither, and many of its headline "orders" are paid development work that may or may not lead to years of production.

9. Working capital, the cash conversion cycle and CFO/EBITDA

What it is. How long cash is tied up between paying for inputs and collecting from customers, and how much of operating profit turns into operating cash flow. Formulas: cash conversion cycle (CCC) = inventory days + debtor days − payable days; cash conversion = cash from operations (CFO) ÷ operating EBITDA. Worked example: Balu's FY26 debtor days of 140 on ₹1,107 crore of sales imply receivables of about ₹425 crore; its CFO of ₹32 crore against operating profit of ₹300 crore is a conversion of about 11%. AIA's CFO of ₹592 crore against ₹1,261 crore is about 47%; CIE's CY25 CFO of ₹1,258 crore against ₹1,362 crore is about 92%; PTC's FY26 CFO was negative ₹69 crore against ₹134 crore. What drives it: credit terms, stock held at overseas depots (AIA), qualification work in progress (PTC), new-customer credit (Balu) and supplier credit (CIE).

Benchmarks: CCC under 60 days is good, 60–150 average, above 150 weak; cash conversion above 90% is good, 60–90% average, below 60% weak. On FY26 figures (CIE CY25), only CIE is good on both, and its working capital deteriorated in H1 CY26 (net working capital from −₹384 crore to +₹291 crore). The definition trap: a single year's cash conversion can be distorted by growth (working capital rises with sales) and by one-off receivable timing; use a three-to-five-year cumulative ratio. Balu's cumulative FY22–FY26 CFO (excluding FY23, not available) was about ₹90 crore against about ₹586 crore of PAT.

Each member's working-capital story is different, and the differences matter for valuation. AIA's is structural and deliberate: it holds stock near mines so a customer never runs short, and that stock rose in FY26. PTC's is a phase: parts going through qualification sit as work in progress until they are accepted and billed, so inventory should fall as a share of revenue once serial production starts; if it does not, something is stuck. CIE's had been a strength (suppliers financing customers) until receivables doubled in the first half of 2026; whether that reverses in the second half is a test of whether it was timing or a change in terms. Balu's is the most concerning, because long credit to new and aftermarket customers is how it has won business, and the auditor has drawn attention to receivables whose collection has been "impacted/interrupted".

Two sensitivities show why utilisation and working capital matter. Definition traps, collected

  • Calendar mismatch. CIE's year ends in December. Its "Q2 CY26" is April–June 2026, the others' Q1 FY27. Its latest full year (CY25) is three months older than the others' FY26.
  • Three EBITDA bases. AIA's and PTC's "company EBITDA" include other income (treasury, FX); CIE's company margin uses "sales" excluding other operating income; screener's operating profit uses revenue from operations. Compare like with like.
  • AIA's three revenue lines. Net sales ₹4,355.5 Cr; revenue from operations ₹4,419.9 Cr (adds export benefits); total income ₹4,893.9 Cr (adds other income). Realisation per kg changes with each.
  • Quarter mislabelling. Some secondary sources compared AIA's Q1 FY27 with Q4 FY26 and called it year-on-year; the true base is 60,156 tonnes (Q1 FY26), not 70,138.
  • Capacity units. AIA's tonnes are finished media; PTC's are ingot (with the 5,000 tpa EB furnace still being installed at March 2026); Balu's are raw forging tonnes. Never add them.
  • Inorganic growth. PTC's FY26 +96% includes Trac's first full year (₹247 Cr of revenue at a ₹0.66 Cr profit).
  • Booking location. AIA's standalone exports go mostly to its UAE subsidiaries; Balu's UAE subsidiary earns 44% of group revenue at 0% tax. Read consolidated geography.
  • Balance-sheet timing. Screener's PTC market cap includes the October 2026 QIP shares; its balance sheet does not include the ₹1,800 Cr cash. CIE's net cash differs between December (₹1,881 Cr) and June (₹1,416 Cr) and between screener and company bases.
  • Orders versus MoUs. Development orders, qualification orders and MoUs are not firm production orders and carry no disclosed values.
  • Working-capital snapshots. CIE's negative cycle was a December snapshot that reversed by June; use several balance-sheet dates.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in this sub-sector come from comparing numbers that look alike but are measured differently: different year-ends, different revenue lines, different EBITDA definitions and different kinds of capacity. Before comparing any two numbers, ask "which period, which revenue line, which basis?"

How to read a castings & forgings company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the calendar. Which quarter is it, and which quarter is the year-ago comparison? For CIE, Q3 CY26 is July–September 2026. For the others, Q2 FY27 is the same period.
  2. Pull volume first (AIA). Total tonnes and mining tonnes against the same quarter last year. Red flag: mining tonnes down year on year, or management again declining to guide.
  3. Compute realisation and EBITDA per kg. Revenue and operating profit (before other income) divided by tonnes. Red flag: realisation up but EBITDA/kg down (costs not passed on).
  4. Separate operating profit from other income. Note the FX gain and treasury income. Red flag: profit growth that comes mostly from "other income".
  5. Split the segments. PTC: Aerolloy income and margin versus Trac. CIE: India and Europe sales growth and EBITDA margin, Europe in euros. Red flag: Aerolloy flat quarter on quarter; CIE India margin below 16.7%.
  6. Compare growth with the end-market. CIE India against SIAM/IHS production; AIA against copper and gold output; Balu's defence share against its order-book claims. Red flag: growth below the market for a third quarter.
  7. Read the input-cost commentary. Ferro-chrome, scrap, steel, aluminium, gas, freight. Red flag: "elevated" costs plus "pass-through next quarter" for two quarters in a row.
  8. Check working capital (half-yearly balance sheets). Debtor days, inventory days, and cash from operations against profit. Red flag: Balu debtors above 130 days (CRISIL's former trigger); CIE receivables rising again; PTC inventory build without matching revenue.
  9. Look for orders with values. AIA's order book; any PTC or Balu order with a number attached. Red flag: announcements without values or with only MoUs.
  10. Finish with capital allocation. Capex against guidance (AIA ₹350–400 Cr; CIE H2 step-up), use of PTC's QIP money, AIA's plans for its cash. Red flag: capex that adds no capacity, or new equity raised by a company with negative cash flow.

Technology and disruption: rolls, batteries and printers

Each member faces a different technology threat, and most of them move slowly. The fastest-moving change is in how much carbon a tonne of metal carries.

Grinding technology (AIA). High-pressure grinding rolls (HPGR) and vertical stirred mills crush and grind ore with far less use of balls than a conventional ball mill, and are increasingly specified in new concentrators. They do not replace the installed base of ball and SAG mills, which will run for decades, but they cap media intensity in new capacity. Cement made the same shift earlier: vertical roller mills have displaced ball mills in new plants since the 2000s, which is why AIA's cement business is mature. AIA's response is to sell more mill internals and its new-generation discharge system (NGDS) as a package with media, priced per kilogram; it says it has modified more than 12 mills "with 100% success rate". Ceramic media in fine regrind mills are a smaller substitute.

Electric vehicles (CIE, Balu). Battery vehicles have no crankshaft, connecting rods or engine gears, so they delete part of what forgers sell, while adding demand for e-axle gears, aluminium motor and inverter housings and battery enclosures. CIE's exposure is smaller than its forging label suggests: only 35% of Indian sales are forgings, and much of that is chassis, axle and steering parts that electric vehicles still need. Balu's crankshafts go mostly to commercial-vehicle, generator, marine and agricultural engines, which electrify slowly, and its aftermarket serves an installed base of combustion engines that will last decades.

Additive manufacturing and near-net shaping (PTC, Balu). 3D-printed titanium and nickel parts, such as the LEAP engine's printed fuel nozzle, can replace some investment castings; equally, PTC's ingots and powders could feed printers. Near-net forging, flow-forming and hot isostatic pressing reduce the machining allowance on forgings, which is where Balu adds value. Both shifts are gradual and require their own qualifications. New titanium-sponge routes (electrolytic processes) could cut sponge costs over time; PTC's electron-beam cold-hearth furnace, once running, is designed to recycle titanium scrap, which is a cost advantage if aerospace customers qualify recycled input.

Decarbonisation. Under CBAM and customer carbon targets, an induction-furnace foundry running on renewable power has a growing advantage over a coke-fired cupola. AIA's captive wind and solar and its new hybrid project help here; MSME foundries without such power are the likely losers.

None of the technology threats is sudden. New mines will use fewer balls, electric cars need no crankshafts, and some castings will be printed. But the installed base of mills, engines and aircraft is vast and long-lived, so these changes shape growth over a decade rather than profits next year.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

Because the four are so different, the bull and bear cases are really four pairs of arguments. These are the strongest on each side.

The bull case

  • Qualification moats are real: AIA's mill trials, PTC's Safran and Airbus approvals and CIE's long OEM relationships are slow to win and slow to lose, and they support margins well above the 8–14% of a typical Indian foundry.
  • Built capacity: AIA can add about 178,000 tonnes without a new plant, and each point of mining conversion is worth an ILLUSTRATIVE ₹260–330 crore of revenue.
  • A once-in-a-generation supply reshuffle: Western aerospace wants non-Russian titanium and castings capacity; NATO and India want shells. PTC and Balu are among very few Indian listed ways to supply both.
  • Strong balance sheets: AIA and CIE hold net cash; PTC has about ₹1,800 crore of new equity to fund its build-out.
  • Cheap incumbents: AIA and CIE together trade at about 22.6 times profit and 16 times net-cash-adjusted operating profit, after falling 22–25% since mid-2026.
  • Natural diversification: mining, vehicles, aircraft and defence rarely peak together; the group's combined margin was flat at 19.4% in the Jun-2026 quarter even as AIA's fell.

The bear case

  • AIA's growth story has stalled: tonnage below FY22, mining tonnes down 3.8% in FY26, no FY27 guidance, Chinese forged media at Chile's largest mines, and profit flattered by a weak rupee.
  • PTC's price assumes success: at 317 times earnings, the ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic requires roughly eightfold revenue growth at high margins within the better part of a decade, with no order values disclosed.
  • Balu's profits are not yet cash: CFO of ₹32 crore on ₹259 crore of PAT, an auditor's emphasis of matter, pending tax assessments and a withdrawn negative-outlook rating.
  • CIE is losing ground: Indian growth below most vehicle segments, Indian margin slipping, European volumes falling in euros, and working capital reversing.
  • Trade barriers are rising: 50% US duty on the full value of steel castings and forgings, 25% on vehicle parts, CBAM in Europe and protection in Brazil and Canada.
  • Working capital is heavy everywhere but CIE: only 33% of the March-year companies' operating profit arrived as cash in FY26.

Weighing the two, the bear case is about the present and the bull case about the future, and the balance differs for each company. For AIA and CIE, the market has already priced in much of the bear case at mid-20s and mid-teens multiples; the bull case needs evidence (mining tonnes, Indian margins). For PTC, the bull case is already in the price, and the risk lies in timing. For Balu, the deciding question is whether cash flow catches up with reported profit.
