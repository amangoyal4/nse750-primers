00

The one-page view: a thin fee on a very expensive metal

Nine listed companies, one commodity none of them controls, and a shift of customers from family jewellers to chains that keeps working even when India buys fewer grams.

Indian jewellery is a ₹6.5–7 lakh crore market (an indicative composite; India Ratings puts it above ₹6 lakh crore) in which roughly ₹85–90 of every ₹100 a plain-gold buyer pays is the metal itself. The jeweller earns a making charge, a premium on any diamonds and stones, and a small spread on coins. That is why an organised chain's operating margin is only 6–9%, and why good chains still earn more than 20% on the capital they employ: they turn their stock quickly and fund much of it with someone else's gold.

Five forces define the sub-sector in 2026, and this report is organised around them.

  • The gold price, the rupee and now the customs duty set the revenue line. Domestic gold rose about 60% on average in FY26. Industry jewellery value rose 43% while tonnage fell 21%. Then, on 13 May 2026, the government raised the effective import duty on gold and silver from about 6% to 15% to defend the rupee and the current account. World gold is now about 26% below its January record of roughly $5,590 an ounce, but Indian gold is only about 17% below its ₹1.81 lakh peak, at around ₹1.50 lakh per 10 grams, because the duty and a weaker rupee absorbed most of the fall.
  • Volume is falling while value rises. WGC data show Indian jewellery demand of 75 tonnes in April–June 2026, down 15% on the year, but worth ₹1.13 lakh crore, up 34%. ICRA expects FY27 industry volumes to fall another 15%.
  • Formalisation keeps compounding. Organised chains hold an estimated 37–42% of the market, up from about 5% in 2000. Listed chains reported same-store sales growth of 19–44% in the June and September 2026 quarters while national tonnage shrank. The September-quarter updates: Titan jewellery +21%, Kalyan +26%, Senco +31%, P N Gadgil +22%.
  • Reported margins are inflated by one-offs. Titan reported a June-quarter profit of ₹1,777 crore, but ₹1,353 crore after stripping a ₹407 crore customs-duty inventory gain and a mark-to-market gain on advance gold purchases. Senco's 11.7% FY26 operating margin will reset to 7.5–7.8% on its own guidance. Reading a jeweller now means normalising its margins first.
  • Who funds the gold decides who survives. Gold metal loans, franchisee capital, customers' old gold and savings-scheme deposits let the best chains grow without raising equity. The same leverage destroyed PC Jeweller after 2018, and it is visible again in 700–1,015 days of inventory at two of the nine.

The valuation spread is the investor's hook. At the 16 October 2026 close, trailing price-to-earnings multiples ran from 9.7× for Senco to 211× for BlueStone, with Titan at 67.6×. All nine together trade at about 60× FY26 profit. Take Titan out and the other eight trade at about 37×. Part of that spread is quality (Titan's brand, governance and studded mix), part is earnings that are temporarily inflated or depressed by gold, and part is simply that some of these companies have barely begun to make money.

In plain terms

A jeweller is a shop that sells a commodity whose price it does not set, plus a little design, trust and diamond on top. When gold gets dearer, the shop's revenue rises almost automatically, its old stock becomes more valuable, and its customers buy fewer grams. When the government changes the import duty, the value of every gram on the shelf jumps or drops overnight. The good chains make money from the bits on top of the metal and avoid betting on the metal itself. The bad ones confuse a gold rally with skill.

The rest of this report works through the business from the bottom up: what a jeweller sells and where the margin hides (sections 1–4), the 2026 duty shock (section 5), formalisation and the store race (section 6), the six business models (section 7), regulation (section 8), how inventory is funded and hedged (section 9), the diamond price collapse (section 10) and the history of the cycle (section 11). Section 12 is the KPI masterclass and section 13 shows how to normalise reported margins. Sections 14–18 compare the nine companies and their valuations; the rest covers the global lens, technology, the bull and bear cases, what to watch and a glossary.

01

How a jeweller makes money: the margin lives on top of the metal

The gold passes through at close to cost. Profit comes from making charges, stones, brand trust and the speed at which stock turns.

Follow a gram of gold from the mine to a bride's neck. India mines almost none of its own gold: imports supplied 82% of the country's gold in the April–June 2026 quarter, recycled old gold about 12% of FY26 supply, and domestic mining about 2%. FY26 imports were 735 tonnes worth $72.4 billion, roughly ₹6.9 lakh crore at ₹95 to the dollar. Imports come in through banks and other nominated agencies, and increasingly through the India International Bullion Exchange (IIBX) in GIFT City. The banks either sell the gold outright or lend it to jewellers as a gold metal loan (GML), a loan repaid in grams rather than rupees.

Refiners and bullion dealers then convert bars into the alloys and wires that manufacturers use. Manufacturing is split between hundreds of thousands of karigars (artisans) in clusters such as Bengal, Rajkot, Coimbatore, Thrissur and Mumbai's Zaveri Bazaar, and a growing set of organised factories. Sky Gold in Navi Mumbai is the listed example; it makes plain gold jewellery for Malabar, Kalyan, Joyalukkas, GRT and Senco. Retail chains outsource much of their plain-gold range; Titan also runs its own plants at Hosur and Pantnagar.

The money concentrates in Step 4, but only on the part of the bill that is not metal. The loop from Step 5 back to Step 1 (old gold exchange) is now the largest single source of "new" metal at many chain counters.

At the counter, the bill has three parts. The first is the metal, priced at that morning's rate for the purity sold (22-carat for most Indian jewellery; 18k and 14k for diamond settings and lightweight pieces). The second is the making charge, which covers labour, wastage and the jeweller's margin; brand chains charge roughly 8–25% of metal value, depending on the intricacy of the piece, while many family jewellers advertise "zero wastage" offers to undercut them. The third is 3% GST on the whole. Any diamonds or coloured stones are priced on top, usually per carat, at a much higher mark-up.

Three consequences follow. First, revenue growth tells you mostly about the gold price, so comparing a jeweller's revenue growth with a normal retailer's is meaningless. Second, the levers that management actually controls are the mix (more studded, more lightweight, fewer coins), the making-charge discipline, the speed of stock turns and the cost of funding the stock. Third, any gold the company owns outright becomes a bet on the gold price: if it rises, the company books an inventory gain inside its gross margin; if it falls, the loss lands in the same place. Section 9 explains how chains hedge, and section 13 shows how to strip these gains out.

The aftermarket matters more than most outsiders realise. Customers bring old jewellery to exchange for new pieces, and in 2026 that has become the main way Indian families pay for gold. Old jewellery is also collateral: banks and gold-loan companies such as Muthoot Finance and Manappuram lend against it (see the NBFC sub-sector report), which reinforces the household habit of holding wealth as wearable gold. That habit is the foundation of the whole industry.

In plain terms

On a ₹1 lakh necklace, about ₹85,000 is gold the jeweller bought at roughly the same price it sells at. The jeweller's real business is the ₹12,000 making charge and whatever it earns on diamonds. Everything else in the analysis, from store rents to gold loans, is about how much of that ₹12,000 survives to the bottom line and how little capital is tied up in the ₹85,000 of metal.

02

Volume down, value up: a market counted in grams and paid in rupees

India bought a fifth fewer grams of jewellery in FY26 and paid 43% more for them. The gap between the two is the single most important thing to understand about the numbers.

India is the world's largest jewellery market by some measures, with 27% of global jewellery demand in the April–June 2026 quarter according to the WGC, and it is first or second globally with China in most years. Measured in rupees, the market is worth roughly ₹6.5–7 lakh crore in FY26. That figure is an indicative composite: India Ratings describes the market as "valued at over Rs 6 trillion", and our build starts from about 425 tonnes of jewellery at an average of roughly ₹1.18 lakh per 10 grams (about ₹5 lakh crore of metal) and adds making charges, studded pieces, silver and platinum. At ₹95 to the dollar this is about $68–74 billion. Several company documents still quote "$90–95 billion", which was calculated at an older exchange rate with higher assumptions; this report uses rupee figures throughout.

The pattern is not new. In FY20 and FY21, gold rose 17% and 30% and the value of jewellery sold actually fell, because the COVID lockdowns and the price shock hit together. Bars and coins rose from 188 tonnes in FY22 to 296 tonnes in FY26, from a quarter of the country's gold demand to 41%. When prices spike, households that buy gold as savings switch from jewellery, where they pay making charges and GST on making, to coins, exchange-traded funds and digital gold. India's gold ETFs added 23.5 tonnes in the first half of 2026, up 163%. The government stopped issuing new Sovereign Gold Bonds after February 2024, which pushed some of that savings demand back to physical coins. Jewellers sell coins too, but at a gross margin of 1–3%, so a shift from jewellery to coins hurts profit even when it does not hurt revenue.

The first half of calendar 2026 confirms the trend: 141.2 tonnes of jewellery, down 17.1%, worth ₹2.13 lakh crore, up 40%, with the average MCX price up 69%. April–June 2026 also brought the lowest recycling in eleven quarters (19 tonnes, as households held on to old gold that had doubled in value), net bullion imports down 22% to 98 tonnes, and an unusual build-up of about 21 tonnes of retail inventory, well above the 13-year average of 15 tonnes, as chains stocked their new stores.

Exports are a separate business. India's gem and jewellery exports were about $27.7 billion in FY26, down from roughly $32 billion in FY24, and are dominated by cut and polished diamonds; India cuts about 90% of the world's natural diamonds. Shipments to the United States fell more than 45% between April 2025 and January 2026 under high US tariffs. Among the nine listed companies, only Sky Gold (exports about 11–12% of revenue) and the GCC businesses of Titan and Kalyan have material revenue outside India, so this report concentrates on domestic demand.

In plain terms

Indian families did not stop buying jewellery when gold doubled. They kept spending roughly the same, or more, in rupees and took home fewer grams. That is good for a jeweller's revenue line and neutral to slightly bad for its profit, because the making charge is earned per piece and per gram. When you read that a chain grew revenue by 40%, first ask how much of that was simply gold costing 60% more.

03

Who buys, and with what: weddings, festivals, savings and old gold

Bridal demand is the base load, savings demand swings with price, and in 2026 the customer increasingly pays with gold she already owns.

About 60% of Indian jewellery demand is bridal, roughly 30% is daily wear and about 10% is fashion, according to ICRA. India has around one crore weddings a year, and for most families the jewellery bought at a wedding is both adornment and the bride's financial security. That makes wedding demand inelastic in value: families keep the budget, or raise it, and adjust grams, caratage or design. The muhurat (auspicious-date) calendar moves the quarters. An Adhik Maas (an inauspicious extra lunar month) in May–June 2026 removed about three weeks of demand, according to Titan's management, and Navratri fell entirely in the September 2025 quarter but in the December quarter in 2026, which flattered Q2 FY26 and hurt the Q2 FY27 comparison for Kalyan and P N Gadgil.

Festivals add the peaks. Akshaya Tritiya (April or May) and Dhanteras and Diwali (October or November) are national; Durga Puja matters for Senco in the East, Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Onam in Kerala and Gudi Padwa in Maharashtra. Rural India accounts for an estimated 55–60% of gold demand (a long-standing WGC estimate), so the monsoon and farm incomes matter; the WGC flagged a weak monsoon as a 2026 risk. Diaspora money matters too: Thangamayil blamed slower remittance-funded buying in southern Tamil Nadu for a weak start to its September 2026 quarter, and the 2026 West Asia war cut footfall in the Gulf, where Titan's Damas business lost ₹67 crore at the EBIT level in the June quarter and Kalyan's Middle East revenue grew only 12% in September.

Gold as savings: the investment switch

Every Indian gold purchase is partly a savings decision. When the price runs up quickly, that savings motive shifts from jewellery to bars, coins, ETFs and digital gold, which carry no making charge. Bars and coins rose from 188 tonnes in FY22 to 296 tonnes in FY26. For a jeweller the coin buyer is still a customer, and many use coins to bring people into the store, but the margin on a coin is close to nothing. P N Gadgil is deliberately cutting coin sales: coins and bars fell from 23.3% of its retail revenue in Q2 FY26 to 15.8% in Q2 FY27, and its e-commerce revenue, mostly coins, fell 84%.

How buyers respond to price

The response to a price shock is measurable. In FY26 the domestic gold price rose about 60% and jewellery tonnage fell about 21%, an implied price elasticity of roughly −0.35 (an estimate): each 10% rise in price cut grams by about 3.5%. In April–June 2026 the price was up 59% and tonnage down 15%. Buyers adjust in three ways: fewer grams per purchase, lower caratage, and more trading-in of old gold. Titan's June-quarter numbers show the first effect cleanly: its average ticket size rose about 31% while gold prices were about 38% higher, so the grams in each purchase fell.

Old gold: the new currency at the counter

The biggest change in 2026 is how customers pay. With gold near ₹1.5 lakh per 10 grams, a family that bought jewellery in 2019 at about a third of today's price is sitting on a large gain, and the easiest way to use it is to trade it in. Recycled gold paid for 46% of Kalyan's revenue in the June 2026 quarter, more than 55% in June itself, and management is targeting 55–60%. Senco's figure was about 44% for FY26. The WGC reports that retailers saw exchange volumes rise 10–20%, reaching up to 70% of sales in some cases.

Exchange is good for volumes and for working capital: the retailer receives metal instead of buying it, which reduces imports and funding needs. It is less good for margins. Retailers compete on how fairly they value old gold, often run exchange promotions, and the old piece has to be melted, refined and remade. Kalyan said its higher exchange share and exchange promotions were among the reasons its June-quarter operating margin fell by a full percentage point. Section 12 sets out benchmarks: an exchange share of 25–40% is healthy, 40–55% is average, and above 55% starts to dilute margins.

In plain terms

Half the jewellery sold at some big chains in mid-2026 was paid for with older jewellery. The family walks in with a 2019 necklace now worth twice what it cost, and walks out with a new, lighter one. The chain keeps its sales growing without anyone importing new gold, but it gives away some margin to win that trade-in.

Luxury, studded and the younger buyer

At the top end, demand is driven by premiumisation rather than by gold. Ethos's average selling price reached about ₹2.26 lakh per watch in the June 2026 quarter, and luxury and high-luxury models were 71% of its watch sales. Titan's watch business grew 22% in the June quarter and 30% in September. Younger urban buyers lean to lightweight, studded and lower-carat jewellery for daily wear; the WGC has noted a shift to "lighter-weight, lower-carat and studded jewellery". These buyers are where BlueStone, CaratLane and Kalyan's Candere compete, and where lab-grown diamonds are most likely to change behaviour (section 10).

04

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: eighty rupees of metal, six of profit before interest

A jeweller's cost stack is dominated by an input it buys at market price. The controllable costs are small, so small differences in them decide who earns a return.

Start with the inputs. Gold makes up 75–90% of a gold jeweller's cost of goods. Its rupee price is set by four things: the international price in dollars (LBMA in London, COMEX in New York), the rupee-dollar exchange rate, Indian customs duty (15% since 13 May 2026, 6% before), and a domestic premium or discount that reflects local supply and demand. India traded at a discount to world prices from mid-February to March 2026, when a sharp global sell-off met cautious Indian buyers. None of these four is under the jeweller's control.

Diamonds are the second input, and their prices are falling (section 10). Natural rough is sold mainly by De Beers and Alrosa; De Beers cut prices by 10–25% at its first sale of 2026. Lab-grown diamonds have collapsed: India's export price fell from about $389 a carat in 2022 to about $71 in January–May 2026. Cheaper diamonds raise the gross margin on studded pieces if retail prices hold, which is one reason Titan and BlueStone report higher studded margins this year.

Below gross margin, the operating costs are labour, rent, advertising and overheads. Then comes financing. A gold metal loan costs roughly 2.5–4.5% a year on the metal value, against 8–9.5% for a rupee working-capital loan; franchisee capital costs the brand nothing; and customer advances under gold-savings schemes are a cheap float. For Ethos, the Swiss franc is effectively an input price: the company says the franc's roughly 26% rise against the rupee cost it about ₹19 crore of gross margin in FY26 (a primer figure we could not verify independently).

The 2026 duty shock: world gold fell, Indian gold barely moved Deep dive

On 13 May 2026 India raised the effective import duty on gold and silver from 6% to 15%. It changed the price customers pay, the volume they buy and, for one or two quarters, the profits jewellers report.

The decision was announced on 12 May and took effect the next day through Customs Notifications 15, 16 and 17 of 2026. The basic customs duty on gold and silver went from 5% to 10% and the Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess (AIDC) from 1% to 5%, taking the effective import duty from about 6% to 15%. Including the 3% IGST charged on imports, the total effective incidence roughly doubled, from about 9.2% to about 18.4%. The government cited the weak rupee and the current-account deficit, swollen by the import bill of the West Asia war. The Prime Minister also appealed to households to curb gold purchases, a form of moral suasion last seen in 2013.

The hike came on top of another disruption. The annual notification that exempts imported gold from IGST was not issued for FY27, so 3% IGST applied from April 2026 and banks briefly halted gold shipments. Official trade data then showed gold import tonnage down about 70% in the first weeks after the hike, and the WGC expected Indian jewellery plus bar-and-coin demand to fall by 50–60 tonnes as a result. Some reports also claimed that imports of finished jewellery had been moved to a "restricted" category; we could not confirm that and do not rely on it.

If you lived in New York, gold got a quarter cheaper between January and October 2026. What the shock did to demand

Volumes fell, as intended. WGC data show Indian jewellery demand of 75.1 tonnes in April–June 2026, down 15% on the year, though value rose 34% to ₹1.13 lakh crore. What the shock did to reported profits

A duty change moves the value of every gram already in stock. A jeweller that bought gold at a landed cost including 6% duty can, after 13 May, sell it at a market price that includes 15%. The difference is a one-off inventory gain that shows up inside gross margin, not as "other income". Gold metal loans do not protect against this, because a GML hedges the gold price, not the tax; ICRA notes that duty changes "remain out of their scope". Duty can be hedged only by buying through MCX or IIBX.

Will the duty stay?

History says high duties do not last. The 2013 increase was wound back after smuggling surged and the 80:20 export rule distorted trade; the 2022 increase to 15% was reversed in the July 2024 Budget for the same reason. The 2026 hike is tied to the rupee and the current account, so its life depends on oil, the war and capital flows. The next obvious decision point is the Union Budget on 1 February 2027. A cut would hand jewellers an inventory loss (as in July 2024) but revive tonnage; keeping 15% supports reported margins for a while but leaves volumes under pressure and rewards smugglers.

In plain terms

When the government raised the duty, every gram on a jeweller's shelf became about 8% more valuable overnight. Some companies put that windfall in their profit, which is legitimate accounting but not repeatable business. If the duty is cut again, the same thing happens in reverse. A sensible reader treats both as weather, not climate.

Why the chains are winning

  • Purity is now provable, and compliance is costly. Mandatory hallmarking (2021) and the six-digit HUID code on every piece (from April 2023) made purity a legal minimum. That narrowed one marketing edge of the chains, but raised the cost of compliance for small jewellers and made buyers expect certified purity everywhere.
  • Transparent pricing and buyback. Chains publish making charges and guarantee buyback or exchange at a stated value, which matters to a family converting old gold.
  • Gold-savings schemes. Ten- or eleven-month instalment plans, with the last instalment paid by the jeweller, lock in customers for the next big purchase. They are capped below a year because longer schemes count as deposits under company law.
  • Access to cheap gold. Banks lend gold metal loans only to rated, audited firms, so the chain pays about 3% a year for its stock while a family jeweller pays 9% or more, or uses its own capital.
  • Tax and cash rules. GST (2017), PAN on purchases of ₹2 lakh and above (2016), the ban on cash receipts of ₹2 lakh or more, and anti-money-laundering reporting push larger purchases into formal channels.
  • Franchise co-option. ICRA notes that some unorganised jewellers are now partnering with brands as franchisees, bringing their local trust and capital into a national system.

National chains against regional champions

The most interesting contest is between the national brands and the regional champions. Thangamayil is the trusted name across Madurai, Tirunelveli, Dindigul and the temple towns of southern Tamil Nadu; it opened four stores in Chennai between June and August 2026, its first real test outside its home catchment. Kalyan, meanwhile, launched a Tamil regional sub-brand, Akshaya Thanga Maligai, in Chennai in August 2026, with four franchised stores planned for FY27: a direct attempt to win buyers who prefer a local name, aimed squarely at Thangamayil and Lalithaa.

The economics of the race favour capital-light models. A gold store needs ₹20–50 crore of inventory. FOCO, FOFO and COCO: who owns what

Three acronyms describe store ownership, and they matter more than any other structural choice.

  • COCO (company-owned, company-operated). The company pays for the fit-out, the rent, the staff and the gold. It keeps the whole margin and carries the whole capital. Thangamayil is essentially all COCO; about 93% of P N Gadgil's revenue comes from COCO stores; Ethos is COCO.
  • FOCO (franchise-owned, company-operated). This is Kalyan's model. The franchisee invests ₹20–50 crore in the fit-out and the inventory; Kalyan runs the store, books the revenue and shares the margin. Of 342 Kalyan showrooms in India at March 2026, 222 were FOCO, and all 84 Kalyan India openings planned for FY27 are FOCO.
  • FOFO (franchise-owned, franchise-operated). The franchisee owns and runs the store and buys stock from the brand at a wholesale margin. This is Senco's model (76 FOFO and 9 FOCO among 201 stores at March 2026) and that of Tanishq's franchisees.

The luxury watch model is different again: Ethos owns stock bought in Swiss francs, earns a much higher gross margin, and depends on how many pieces brands such as Rolex, Patek Philippe and Audemars Piguet allocate to it. Its growth is gated by supply, not demand.

PC Jeweller does not fit neatly. It was a national franchise-led chain before 2018, collapsed in the jewellery credit crunch, and is now rebuilding through franchise stores after settling with its banks. Its current margins are not those of a normal showroom business (section 13).

In plain terms

There are only two ways to grow a jewellery chain fast: use your own money to fill new shops with gold, or persuade someone else to. Kalyan and Senco ask local businessmen to pay for the gold in exchange for a share of the profit. Thangamayil and P N Gadgil pay for it themselves and keep all the profit. The first approach earns less per shop but needs far less capital, which is why investors have rewarded it.

08

Rules of the game: hallmarks, duties, cash limits and a trade deal for watches

Jewellery is lightly regulated on price and heavily regulated on purity, payment and imports. Almost every rule of the past decade has pushed buyers toward the chains.

No regulator sets the price of jewellery, and no licence is needed to open a jewellery shop beyond the normal business registrations. What the state controls is purity, the way gold enters the country, the way customers pay and the tax on each step. Eight bodies matter:

  • Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS): hallmarking, the HUID code and purity grades.
  • Ministry of Finance and CBIC: customs duty, the AIDC cess and IGST on imports.
  • Reserve Bank of India: which banks may import gold, the rules for gold metal loans, gold-loan rules for lenders, and the (now largely closed) Gold Monetisation Scheme and Sovereign Gold Bonds.
  • DGFT and the Ministry of Commerce: import policy, the IIBX bullion exchange and free-trade agreements.
  • GST Council: 3% GST on gold and jewellery, 5% on job-work making charges, since July 2017.
  • SEBI: listed-company disclosure, including how jewellers report savings schemes.
  • Ministry of Corporate Affairs: gold-saving schemes count as deposits if they run beyond 365 days, which is why every chain's scheme lasts 10 or 11 months.
  • Anti-money-laundering rules: jewellers became reporting entities for cash transactions of ₹10 lakh and above in December 2020.

Hallmarking and HUID: purity becomes the law

The BIS launched voluntary hallmarking in 2000. It became mandatory on 23 June 2021 in 256 districts, for 14, 18 and 22 carat (later also 20, 23 and 24 carat). From 1 April 2023 only jewellery carrying a six-digit Hallmark Unique Identification (HUID) code may be sold, and old four-mark hallmarks were banned. Coverage has widened in phases, to 361 districts by phase 4 in 2024 and 380 districts in phase 6, effective 2 March 2026. More than 40 crore items have been HUID-tagged. The status of 9-carat gold is unclear: reports in July 2025 said 9k hallmarking had become mandatory, while a BIS statement in March 2026 described 9k as a "voluntary grade". Either way, 9k now has a standard, which helps lightweight jewellery and lab-grown-diamond mounts.

Savings products the state has stepped back from

Two government schemes aimed to divert household savings from physical gold. The Gold Monetisation Scheme (2015) let households deposit gold with banks for interest; its medium- and long-term components were discontinued from 26 March 2025 and mobilisation was always small. Sovereign Gold Bonds (2015–2024) paid interest plus the gold price; no new tranches have been issued since February 2024. Bonds now maturing are redeemed at around ₹1.5 lakh per 10 grams, several times their issue prices, a wealth effect that could support some jewellery demand in 2026–28 (an estimate, not yet visible in data). With these routes closed, savings demand has flowed to coins, ETFs and digital gold. The RBI's 2025 gold-loan rules (loan-to-value caps and purity norms) affect jewellers indirectly: a liquid gold-loan market makes gold more useful as a household asset.

Trade rules: US tariffs and the EFTA watch deal

Export rules matter to only a few of the nine. The United States imposed a 50% tariff on many Indian goods, including jewellery, from 27 August 2025 (25% "reciprocal" plus a 25% penalty linked to Russian oil); one search summary gave 25–32%, so the exact level applied to jewellery is not certain. An India–US interim framework in early 2026 reportedly cut the jewellery rate to 18%, and a Section 301 action from 24 July 2026 reportedly added 10%. These later steps rest on trade-press reports and should be treated as INDICATIVE. US-bound gem and jewellery exports fell more than 45% between April 2025 and January 2026. Sky Gold, with exports of 11–12% of revenue mostly to the Middle East, is the only listed member with visible exposure, and its US share is not disclosed.

The India–EFTA Trade and Economic Partnership Agreement (TEPA), in force since 1 October 2025, matters for watches. Swiss wristwatches (customs headings 9101 and 9102) faced a 20% most-favoured-nation duty plus a 2% surcharge; under TEPA that duty falls to zero over about seven years, with a first-year rate of roughly 18.9% according to PwC. Retail price cuts of 10–20% are expected over time, which should lift demand and could widen Ethos's margins if brands do not capture the benefit themselves. Section 17 covers the effect. Separately, in 2025 consumer-affairs authorities issued guidance that "diamond" on its own means a natural stone and that lab-grown stones must be labelled as such; the final legal status should be checked.

In plain terms

The government does not tell jewellers what to charge, but it decides how pure their gold must be, how customers may pay, and how much tax is added when gold enters the country. Each of those rules makes life harder for a small, informal jeweller and easier for a chain with auditors, bank lines and a compliance team. The import duty is the exception: it hits everyone, and it changes whenever the rupee needs defending.

Pending decisions to track: whether the 15% duty is rolled back once the current account eases (the 1 February 2027 Budget is the next opportunity); a fix for the lapsed IGST exemption; clarity on 9-carat hallmarking; relief from the US Section 301 action; the TEPA watch-duty schedule; any change to RBI rules on gold metal loans; and the Lalithaa IPO, which would add another regional comparator.

Who funds the gold: metal loans, hedging and the balance sheet Deep dive

A jeweller's balance sheet is a pile of gold and a set of promises about who owns it. Reading it correctly separates the safe compounders from the next PC Jeweller.

A jewellery store holds a great deal of metal for its size. A mid-size store carries ₹20–50 crore of gold. A chain with 300 stores holds ₹10,000 crore or more. How that gold is paid for decides three things at once: how much profit is lost to interest, whether the company is exposed to the gold price, and whether it can survive a credit squeeze. There are five ways to fund the stock, and the good chains use all of them.

  • Gold metal loans (GML). A bank lends physical gold, measured in grams, for up to about 180 days at a lease rate of roughly 2.5–4.5% a year on the metal value. The jeweller makes jewellery from it, sells it, and repays the bank in grams (or in rupees at the price on the repayment date). Because the loan is in grams, a rise in the gold price raises the liability exactly as much as it raises the value of the stock. The position is price-neutral.
  • Rupee working-capital loans. Cash credit at roughly 8–9.5%. Gold bought with cash is unhedged unless covered separately.
  • Franchisee capital. In FOCO and FOFO stores the franchisee buys the inventory. It costs the brand nothing and carries no price risk for the brand.
  • Customers' gold. Old gold taken in exchange, and, for a manufacturer, "advance gold" supplied by retail customers. Both reduce what has to be bought.
  • Customer advances. Monthly instalments into gold-savings schemes are a float that funds stock until the customer redeems.

In plain terms

A big "borrowings" number on a jeweller's balance sheet is not automatically dangerous. If it is gold borrowed from a bank and repaid in gold, it is closer to stock on consignment than to a loan. The danger is rupee debt funding gold that nobody has hedged, especially when the stock sits on the shelf for years. That combination is what sank PC Jeweller in 2018.

Case study: how PC Jeweller broke, and what it shows

PC Jeweller was founded in Delhi's Karol Bagh in 2005 and by January 2018 had a market value of about ₹23,100 crore. In February 2018 the Nirav Modi letter-of-undertaking fraud at Punjab National Bank made banks wary of the whole jewellery trade. Gold-loan and working-capital lines were cut, Gitanjali and Nakshatra collapsed, and PC Jeweller's market value fell about 83% in four months. Revenue fell from ₹9,610 crore in FY18 to ₹604 crore in FY24 as the company lost access to the gold it needed to trade; bank dues of about ₹3,466 crore went into default.

The recovery followed a one-time settlement with 14 banks on 30 September 2024 and about ₹2,702 crore of new equity through warrants, which took the share count to about 980 crore. Borrowings fell from ₹4,150 crore (March 2024) to ₹2,151 crore (March 2025) and ₹1,167 crore (March 2026), and the company reportedly cleared all consortium dues around 25 September 2026 (a single press report; confirm against the exchange filing). The lesson for the sector is that a jeweller's business depends on continuous access to gold. A chain that funds itself with rupee debt and slow-moving stock can go from profitable to insolvent in a single credit cycle, regardless of its brand.

Inventory accounting: why gains hide in gross margin

Jewellers value inventory at cost, usually a weighted average. When the gold price rises, the cost of old stock sold is below the price received, and the difference appears as higher gross margin. Hedged gold does not produce this effect, because the matching liability rises too, but unhedged stock does, and so does any change in duty. The size of the gain depends on how much unhedged stock the company carries and for how long, which is why inventory days matter so much (section 12). A company with 1,000 days of stock bought years ago at lower prices, as PC Jeweller appears to have, can report very high margins for as long as that old stock lasts.

Studded jewellery earns roughly three times the gross margin of plain gold. In 2026 the stones themselves are deflating, natural slowly and lab-grown spectacularly.

The surest way for a gold jeweller to earn more per rupee of sales is to sell fewer grams of gold and more diamonds. A plain-gold piece carries a gross margin of about 11–13%; a studded piece 30–40% or more. Diamond prices are falling on two fronts. The threats are three.

  • Value perception. If buyers learn that a lab-grown stone indistinguishable to the eye costs a fraction of a natural one, the premium on natural studded jewellery is harder to defend, and retail prices may have to follow input costs down.
  • New competitors. LGD lets direct-to-consumer brands such as Limelight, Jewelbox, Fiona and Solitario sell "diamond jewellery" at entry prices without a heritage brand. They compete for exactly the young urban buyer that BlueStone and CaratLane target.
  • Buy-back promises. Chains offer lifetime exchange on natural diamonds, often at a stated percentage of the current price. In plain terms

Diamonds are where jewellers make their real money, and the stones are getting cheaper. For now that helps, because shops have not cut their prices as fast as their costs fell. The risk is that customers notice. If a lab-made diamond looks the same and costs a fifth as much, the extra that a brand can charge for a natural stone starts to shrink.

Will Indian buyers accept lab-grown diamonds?

The evidence so far is thin. Consumer-affairs guidance requires that "diamond" on its own mean natural and that lab-grown stones be labelled, which helps transparency. Titan reportedly launched an LGD brand in 2025 (named "beYon" in reports we could not verify), and BlueStone gives no LGD disclosure despite a 68% studded mix. LGD may in fact help the gold chains: it gives a buyer priced out of heavy gold a way to buy a sparkly, light piece at a low price, and it suits 9k and 14k mounts. It may hurt the pure studded brands more, because their premium rests on the stone. Investors should look for any company that starts disclosing natural and lab-grown sales separately; until then, studded-share comparisons across companies mix two very different products.

Five decades of shocks: how the cycle works

Rising gold flatters revenue and margins while volume quietly falls; falling gold hurts unhedged stock but brings buyers back. Duty moves and credit shocks cut across both.

The organised jewellery industry is young. Titan began as a Tata–TIDCO watch joint venture in 1984 and launched Tanishq in 1994–96, initially with an export focus, before pivoting to India with a purity guarantee and karat-meters in its stores that let customers test their own gold. Kalyan opened its first showroom in Thrissur in 1993. In 2000 the organised share was about 5%. The industry's history since has been a sequence of shocks, each of which pushed customers toward the chains even as it hurt some of them.

  1. 2012–14, the current-account crisis. Duty rose from 2% to 10%, the 80:20 rule tied imports to exports, and the RBI restricted gold loans to some segments. Chains with bank gold lines gained; smuggling rose.
  2. 2016–18, the formalisation shocks. PAN for purchases of ₹2 lakh and above (January 2016), demonetisation (November 2016) and GST (July 2017) squeezed cash-based jewellers. Then the February 2018 Nirav Modi fraud froze bank credit to the trade; Gitanjali and Nakshatra collapsed and PC Jeweller's value fell about 83% in four months. A jeweller credit winter ran from 2018 to 2020.
  3. 2020–22, COVID and hallmarking. Lockdowns crushed FY21, mandatory hallmarking arrived in June 2021, and Kalyan's March 2021 IPO restarted listings.
  4. 2022–24, the land-grab. Kalyan pivoted to franchise-owned stores and re-rated; Titan bought out CaratLane's minority for ₹4,621 crore (2023); HUID-only sales began; Senco listed; the July 2024 duty cut to 6% cost Titan about ₹290 crore of inventory value.
  5. 2024–26, the gold super-cycle. Gold rose from about $2,000 an ounce in early 2024 to about $5,590 in January 2026. P N Gadgil (2024) and BlueStone (2025) listed; PC Jeweller settled with its banks; tonnage fell while value soared, and inventory gains inflated FY26 margins; Titan bought 67% of the Gulf jeweller Damas.
  6. 2026, the policy reversal. Gold had its sharpest monthly fall in about 13 years in March, the duty went back to 15% in May, and the Prime Minister asked households to buy less gold. Value growth nonetheless held at 21–31% in the September-quarter updates.

In plain terms

The jewellery cycle runs backwards to most retail cycles. When gold gets expensive, shops look busier on paper and more profitable, while actually selling less metal. When gold gets cheaper, the reverse happens: customers return, but profits dip. Titan's management put it simply: "any softening in gold prices would support demand." The skill is to read through the metal to the number of customers and grams.

Reading a jeweller: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Sixteen numbers, how each is calculated, what good looks like, how the nine compare, and the trap hidden in each one. Then a store P&L, a scorecard and a ten-minute results routine.

Every jeweller's results can be rebuilt from one equation. Revenue is the number of stores times the number of buyers each store serves times the average ticket. The ticket is the grams in the purchase times the gold price times one plus the making charge, plus the value of any stones. Gross profit is what the jeweller keeps on making charges, stones and coins, plus or minus the gain or loss on any gold it holds unhedged. Below gross profit come the store costs, then interest on the gold. The diagram marks which terms management controls and which it does not.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the terms combine. A store serves 20,000 buyers a year. The average buyer takes home 15 grams of 22-carat jewellery at ₹15,000 a gram, with a 12% making charge: 15 × ₹15,000 × 1.12 = ₹2.52 lakh per ticket. Revenue is 20,000 × ₹2.52 lakh = ₹50.4 crore. If gold rises 20% and buyers cut their grams by 7%, the ticket becomes 13.95 × ₹18,000 × 1.12 = ₹2.81 lakh, and revenue rises 11.6% to ₹56.2 crore with the same number of buyers. Nothing about the store's skill has changed. If 20% of the ticket value moves from plain gold to studded jewellery, gross profit rises by about ₹2.4 crore on ₹56 crore of revenue (20% × ₹56.2 crore × 21 points of extra margin), lifting store EBITDA by roughly two-thirds. Those are the two movements every KPI below is trying to separate: price, which the store does not control, and mix and customers, which it does. All figures in this example are ILLUSTRATIVE.

In plain terms

A jeweller's sales go up for three reasons: more shops, more customers per shop, or a bigger bill per customer. The bill per customer goes up mostly because gold got dearer, a little because the customer chose diamonds, and it goes down when the customer buys fewer grams. Good analysis splits the growth into those pieces before deciding whether the company is doing well.

1. Same-store sales growth (SSSG or like-for-like)

What it is. Revenue growth at stores open for more than a year, so that new openings do not flatter the number. Formula. SSSG = (this period's revenue at mature stores ÷ the same stores' revenue a year earlier) − 1. It decomposes as (1 + buyer growth) × (1 + ticket growth) − 1, and ticket growth is roughly gold-price change plus mix change minus the fall in grams per ticket. Worked example. If buyers rise 5% and the ticket rises 31%, SSSG ≈ 1.05 × 1.31 − 1 = 37.6%. That is Titan's June 2026 quarter, where domestic jewellery grew 38% and reported like-for-like growth was 33%.

Drivers. Up with the gold price, wedding dates, festival timing and exchange promotions; down with Adhik Maas months, high-base comparisons and price-driven gram cuts. Benchmark. Good: above the gold-price change plus 5 points; average: roughly equal to the gold-price change; weak: below it. How the nine compare. Thangamayil 44.4% and BlueStone 39% (June quarter), Titan 33%, P N Gadgil retail 25.5%, Kalyan India about 20% and Senco 19% (September quarter), Ethos 13.2% (chart in section 6). Sky Gold and PC Jeweller do not report it. Definition trap. Companies define it differently: Titan's like-for-like covers domestic jewellery only; P N Gadgil's covers retail, not franchise or e-commerce; Kalyan's is for India. And in a gold rally almost all of it is price. A 25% SSSG with gold up 30% means fewer grams through the door.

2. Buyer growth

What it is. The change in the number of unique customers who bought. It is the cleanest volume signal in the sector because it is untouched by the gold price. Formula. Buyers this period ÷ buyers in the same period last year − 1. Worked example. 21,000 buyers against 20,000 a year earlier is 5% buyer growth, even if revenue rose 38%. Drivers. Store openings in new catchments, brand campaigns, lightweight and entry-price ranges, savings-scheme maturities; down when price shocks push first-time and small-ticket buyers out. Benchmark. Good above 10%; average 3–8%; weak below zero. How the nine compare. Only Titan discloses it: roughly flat for three quarters of FY26, high single digits in the March 2026 quarter, +5% in June and mid-single digits in September. Definition trap. Titan reports buyers for its domestic jewellery brands, not for CaratLane or the group, and only as an approximate growth rate. Nobody else reports it at all, so investors must infer it from revenue, price and grams.

3. Grammage (volume) growth

What it is. The change in grams or kilograms of gold jewellery sold. Formula. 4. Stud ratio (studded share)

What it is. Studded jewellery sales as a share of total jewellery sales. Formula. Studded revenue ÷ jewellery revenue (some companies exclude coins from the denominator). Worked example. ₹2,700 crore of studded sales in ₹10,000 crore of jewellery is a 27% stud ratio; at a 21-point margin premium, each extra point of ratio adds about ₹21 crore of gross profit on that base. Benchmark. Good above 25%; average 12–25%; weak below 12%. How the nine compare. 5. Gross margin, adjusted

What it is. Revenue minus the cost of goods sold, as a share of revenue, after removing gold-price and duty inventory gains or losses and hedge mark-to-market. Formula. (Revenue − COGS − inventory and duty gains + inventory losses) ÷ revenue. Worked example. A chain reports ₹1,000 crore of revenue and ₹170 crore of gross profit, including a ₹25 crore duty gain. Reported gross margin is 17%; adjusted is 14.5%. Drivers. Stud ratio, making-charge discipline, coin share (each coin sale dilutes it), exchange promotions, diamond input prices and, for watches, the franc. Benchmark. Good above 20% for a studded-rich national brand; average 13–17%; weak below 12%; B2B is structurally 7–9%. How the nine compare. BlueStone's adjusted gross margin was 37.4% in the June 2026 quarter; Titan's consolidated adjusted gross margin 22.7%; ICRA's model store 13%; Sky Gold about 8.5%. Others do not publish an adjusted figure, and screener's cost lines do not allow a clean one. Definition trap. BlueStone's gross margin has been quoted at about 38% (IPO era), 42% (FY26, another basis) and 37.4% (Q1 FY27 adjusted): always check whether the gain is in or out.

Gross margin is where gold windfalls hide. If a company held gold that became more valuable, its gross margin jumps without any customer paying more for craftsmanship. Always look for the line in the results presentation that says "excluding inventory gains" or "adjusted", and use that number.

6. EBITDA or EBIT margin, adjusted

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation and interest (EBITDA) or after depreciation (EBIT), as a share of revenue, excluding inventory and duty gains and excluding other income. Formula. (Reported EBITDA − one-off gains − other income) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Titan's jewellery EBIT in the June 2026 quarter was about ₹2,200 crore, quoted by ICICI Direct as about 14% of revenue. Removing the ₹386 crore jewellery duty gain and about ₹155 crore of mark-to-market gains leaves about ₹1,660 crore, which ICICI puts at an 11.7% margin, flat on the year and in line with Titan's ~11% guidance. (The two percentages appear to use different revenue bases: ₹1,660 crore is 11.7% of about ₹14,200 crore, while ₹2,200 crore is 14% of about ₹15,700 crore. Benchmark. Good above 9%; average 6–9%; weak below 5%. How the nine compare. Reported FY26: PC Jeweller 20.1%, BlueStone 15.9%, Ethos 13.3%, Senco 11.7%, Titan 9.5%, Kalyan 7.2%, Sky Gold 6.9%, Thangamayil 6.6%, P N Gadgil 5.9% (chart in section 4). 7. Inventory days

What it is. How long, on average, stock sits before it is sold. Formula. Inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365 (screener's basis); some analysts use revenue instead of COGS, which gives a lower number. Worked example. ₹20 crore of stock and ₹47 crore of annual cost of goods gives 20 ÷ 47 × 365 ≈ 155 days. ICRA puts its model store at 145 days in normal times, rising to 195 if volumes fall 25%. Drivers. New stores (each needs a full display before it sells), studded mix (diamonds turn slower), make-to-order versus ready stock, bridal seasonality, and weak demand. Benchmark. Good below 130 days; average 130–220; weak above 250. A B2B maker should be below 60; a luxury watch retailer at 200+ is normal. How the nine compare. Sky Gold 50, Thangamayil 144, Kalyan 167, Titan 222, Ethos 222, Senco 286 (up from 220), BlueStone 700 and PC Jeweller 1,015 (P N Gadgil not available on the same basis). Definition trap. Franchise models hold less stock on the brand's balance sheet, so a FOCO chain looks leaner than a COCO chain with the same store-level stock. And very high days can hide old stock carried at old prices, a source of inventory gains when sold.

8. Hedge ratio

What it is. The share of gold inventory protected against price moves. Formula. (Grams under gold metal loans + grams hedged with futures + customer-supplied grams) ÷ grams of inventory. Worked example. A chain with 1,000 kg of stock, 600 kg on GML and 200 kg sold forward on MCX is 80% hedged; a 10% gold-price move changes the value of its open 200 kg by ₹30 crore at ₹15,000 a gram. Drivers. Access to GML lines (rated, audited companies), management philosophy, and cost: hedging gives up the windfall in a rally. Benchmark. Good about 100%; average 50–80%; weak below 40%. How the nine compare. Titan and Kalyan describe themselves as essentially price-neutral through gold-on-lease and GML. Senco hedges about 100% of daily sales and 40–60% of inventory. BlueStone, P N Gadgil, PC Jeweller and Ethos do not give a clear ratio. Definition trap. 10. Leverage: net debt including gold loans, and interest cover

What it is. How much the company owes relative to what it earns, and how comfortably operating profit covers interest. Formula. Net debt incl. GML ÷ EBITDA; interest cover = EBITDA ÷ interest. 11. Old-gold exchange share

What it is. The share of revenue paid for with old gold traded in by customers. Formula. Value of old gold accepted ÷ revenue. Worked example. If ₹460 crore of a ₹1,000 crore quarter is settled with old gold, the exchange share is 46% (Kalyan, June 2026 quarter). Drivers. The size of the household gain on old jewellery (very large in 2026), exchange promotions, trust in the chain's valuation, and the gap between buying and selling rates. Benchmark. Healthy 25–40%; average 40–55%; above 55% starts to dilute margins through promotions and refining costs. How the nine compare. Kalyan 46% (above 55% in June 2026; target 55–60%); Senco about 44% for FY26; WGC reports up to 70% at some retailers (chart in section 3). Others do not disclose. Definition trap. Exchange reduces cash needs and imports, so it can make working capital look better while margins fall. A rising exchange share alongside falling operating margin is the signature of growth being bought with promotions.

Definition traps, collected

  • Revenue is mostly gold. Compare revenue growth with the gold price and with grams, never with a normal retailer's growth.
  • Titan has three revenue bases. ₹87,584 crore on screener includes bullion and digital gold; "income excluding bullion" is about ₹76,078 crore (₹76,797 crore on ICICI Direct's basis); domestic jewellery is a third figure again. State which you use.
  • EBITDA bases differ. Company EBITDA often includes other income: P N Gadgil ₹704 crore against screener's ₹617 crore; Kalyan ₹2,491 crore against ₹2,562 crore; Senco ₹969 crore against ₹984 crore.
  • Borrowings include gold loans. Show net debt with and without GML where possible; screener's figures here also exclude cash.
  • Inventory gains sit inside gross margin. Strip price and duty gains before comparing margins across companies or years.
  • Calendar versus fiscal. WGC data are calendar quarters (Q2 CY26 = Q1 FY27); ICRA re-cuts them to fiscal years.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. Thangamayil's figures are standalone because its consolidated page is empty; it has no material subsidiaries.
  • Tax. PC Jeweller pays almost no tax on carried-forward losses, so its P/E is not comparable.
  • Store counts mix formats. Titan's 3,758 include watches and eyewear; Kalyan's 546 include Candere and international; Senco's include lifestyle stores.

In plain terms

Almost every headline number in a jeweller's results has a twin that tells a different story: revenue with and without bullion, margin with and without gold gains, debt with and without borrowed gold, store counts with and without other formats. Before comparing two companies, make sure you are comparing the same twin.

How to read a jeweller's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the gold price and the duty. Note the average domestic gold price change for the quarter against the same quarter last year, and whether duty changed. This is your baseline for everything else.
  2. Divide revenue growth by the gold-price change. (1 + revenue growth) ÷ (1 + price change) − 1 approximates volume growth after store additions. Negative is normal in 2026; worse than the industry's −15% is a warning.
  3. Read SSSG and buyer growth. SSSG below the price change means fewer grams at mature stores. Positive buyer growth (Titan) is the best sign of real share gain.
  4. Check the mix. Stud ratio up, coin share down and exchange share stable are good; the reverse explains a margin fall before you see it.
  5. Find the inventory gain. Search the presentation or call for "inventory gain", "customs duty", "mark-to-market" or "adjusted". Restate gross and EBITDA margins without it.
  6. Compare adjusted margin with guidance. Titan guides about 11% jewellery EBIT; Senco 7.5–7.8% EBITDA for FY27. A beat that comes only from gains is not a beat.
  7. Look below EBITDA. Other income and the tax rate (PC Jeweller's ~0%) can make profit growth look better than the business.
  8. Read the balance sheet. Inventory days, borrowings split between GML and rupee debt, and any change in hedging policy. Rising days with rising rupee debt is the main red flag.
  9. Check operating cash flow (half-yearly). Profit growth with persistently negative cash flow means the profit is sitting on the shelf as gold.
  10. Finish with the network. Stores opened, franchise share of openings, and any new region. Openings funded by franchisees are cheap growth; openings funded by rupee debt are not.

Red flags in one line: margins above peers without a studded or luxury explanation; inventory days rising for several quarters; a hedging policy described only vaguely; profit growth well ahead of cash flow; promoter pledges or steady promoter selling; and a business update that stresses value growth while staying silent on grams or buyers.

In plain terms

Ten minutes is enough if you follow the order: gold price first, then how much of the sales growth is left after you take gold out, then customers and diamonds, then the one-off gains, then the debt and the stock on the shelf. Most surprises in jewellery results come from skipping the first and the fifth steps.

PC Jeweller reported a 20.1% operating margin in FY26 and about 28% in the June 2026 quarter, two to three times Titan's, with almost no tax (carried-forward losses) and ₹196 crore of other income. Who is winning: three maps and a scorecard

Thin margins with high returns beat rich margins with slow stock. Growth and valuation line up only loosely, and the studded lever explains more of the margin than scale does.

The first map plots operating margin against return on capital. It shows the sector's central paradox clearly. Six of the nine sit in a tight cluster of 6–12% margin and 20–27% ROCE: the gold chains and the manufacturer that turn their stock fast. The three outliers to the right, PC Jeweller, BlueStone and Ethos, earn far higher margins and far lower returns, because their inventory (legacy gold, diamonds, Swiss watches) turns slowly. Titan sits close to the centre of the cluster on margin but is the only one combining that with national scale.

The second map sets valuation against growth. If the market paid simply for growth, the dots would rise from left to right. They do not. Sky Gold and Thangamayil grew fastest in FY26 and trade at 40–49× earnings; Titan and Ethos grew more slowly and trade at 66–68×; Senco and P N Gadgil grew at middling rates and trade at 10–20×. The market is paying for brand durability (Titan), luxury runway (Ethos) and capital-light growth (Kalyan, Sky Gold), and discounting earnings it considers gold-inflated or governance-sensitive (Senco, P N Gadgil, PC Jeweller).

The third map shows the studded lever. Among the gold jewellers, margin rises with stud ratio: P N Gadgil and Sky Gold at 1–11% studded earn 6–7%; Kalyan and Titan at 27–31% earn 7–10%; BlueStone at about 68% earns 16% before its heavy lease and interest costs. Senco's reported 11.7% sits far above the line for its 12% stud ratio, which is exactly what an inventory gain looks like; its guided 7.5–7.8% puts it back where its mix says it should be.

Ethos and the EFTA glide path

Ethos sells watches whose prices are set in Switzerland. Its growth depends on three things outside India's gold market: how many pieces brands such as Rolex, Patek Philippe and Audemars Piguet allocate to it, the Swiss franc, and Indian import duty. The franc's roughly 26% rise against the rupee in FY26 cost it about ₹19 crore of gross margin. Brand allocation is the most fragile: a brand can cut allocations, demand exclusive boutiques, or open its own stores.

The India–EFTA trade agreement, in force since 1 October 2025, is the largest structural change for this business. Swiss wristwatches faced a 20% most-favoured-nation duty plus a 2% surcharge. The global lens: lower margins, higher returns, far higher multiples

India is the world's largest jewellery market by some measures. Its listed jewellers earn thinner margins than global brands, better returns on equity, and two to five times their valuations.

India accounted for 27% of world jewellery demand in the April–June 2026 quarter, according to the WGC, and it is first or second with China in most years. It cuts and polishes about 90% of the world's natural diamonds and held 35.7% of global lab-grown diamond exports in 2025. Yet its jewellery industry looks very different from the West's. Indian buyers want gold, mostly 22-carat and mostly plain; studded jewellery is 10–30% of sales at the listed chains, against 50–90% for many Western brands. That single difference explains most of the gap in margins.

Indian jewellers trade at two to five times the multiples of global peers. Three things justify part of that. First, formalisation: about 60% of the Indian market is still with family jewellers, so chains can grow faster than the market for a long time, whereas Western markets are mature. Second, scarcity: Indian domestic investors have few listed consumer franchises of this quality to buy, and steady domestic fund inflows support their prices. Third, returns: high ROE with little equity means more of the growth can be self-funded. The part that is not justified by fundamentals is the assumption that margins stay flat while the gold price, duty and diamond prices all move. Chow Tai Fook's 2024–25 experience, when a gold-price spike crushed Chinese jewellery volumes and forced a shift to pricing by piece, is the closest warning for Indian chains. Laopu Gold's re-rating shows the opposite path: design-led gold sold at a premium per piece rather than per gram.

For Ethos, the closest listed analogue is Watches of Switzerland, which trades at about 10–12× earnings. The difference reflects India's early stage in luxury and the EFTA duty cuts ahead, but also shows how far the multiple could fall if growth slowed to a mature market's pace.

In plain terms

Indian jewellers earn less per rupee of sales than Cartier or Pandora because Indians buy mostly plain gold, which carries a thin margin. They earn more on the money invested because they borrow gold cheaply and sell it fast. And investors pay far more for them than for foreign peers, because India still has decades of customers moving from local shops to chains. That premium is earned only if growth lasts.

Technology and disruption: lab-grown stones, lighter gold, apps and digital savings

No technology threatens the bridal gold purchase. Several are changing how much of it is gold, where it is bought and what else competes for the savings rupee.

The bridal jewellery purchase is one of the most technology-resistant transactions in Indian retail. Families want to see, touch and weigh the gold, negotiate over making charges and exchange old pieces, and they want a trusted name. That is why BlueStone, born online, now makes about 93% of its sales in stores. But around that core, five technologies are reshaping the business.

Lab-grown diamonds

Section 10 covers the price collapse (export prices down 82% since 2022). The disruption is twofold: LGD lowers the entry price of "diamond" jewellery, opening studded to buyers priced out of heavy gold, and it lets new direct-to-consumer brands (Limelight, Jewelbox, Fiona, Solitario and others) compete without a heritage brand. It also threatens the perceived value of natural stones, and therefore the premium and buy-back promises of the established studded brands. Titan reportedly launched an LGD brand in 2025; BlueStone and CaratLane are exposed in both directions. The sector lacks disclosure: no listed company yet splits natural and lab-grown sales.

Lighter, lower-carat jewellery

CNC machining, 3D-printed moulds and electroformed hollow jewellery cut the grams in a piece without cutting its size or look. With gold at ₹1.5 lakh per 10 grams, this is the industry's main answer to price: the same necklace at two-thirds of the weight. Sky Gold's lightweight cast jewellery is the listed B2B play. The hallmarking of 14-carat and 9-carat grades supports the trend. For retailers, lighter pieces mean higher making charges per gram and often better margins, but fewer grams to sell.

Online and quick commerce

Digital-first studded brands are growing fastest: CaratLane +32% and Candere +64% in the September 2026 quarter, Candere +112% in June. Quick-commerce apps sell gold and silver coins in minutes, and digital-gold platforms let customers buy fractions of a gram for as little as ₹1 a day. These channels win lightweight, studded and gifting purchases; bridal stays in the store.

Digital gold, ETFs and the savings rupee

Gold ETFs and digital gold compete with jewellery for the savings part of the purchase. India's gold ETFs added 23.5 tonnes in the first half of 2026, up 163%, and bars and coins reached 41% of gold volume in FY26. With Sovereign Gold Bonds no longer issued, some savings demand has moved to coins, which jewellers sell at near-zero margin, and some to ETFs, which bypass them entirely. This is a slow leak of the "jewellery as savings" habit, most visible when prices spike.

Smartwatches

Cheap smartwatches, many from China, have eroded the mass-market analogue watch. Titan's smart wearables are declining in single digits. Luxury mechanical watches, Ethos's market, are unaffected; if anything, the smartwatch has turned the traditional watch into a pure status and collecting purchase.

AI design, virtual try-on and RFID

Chains are using AI-assisted design, virtual try-on in apps and RFID tags for inventory control. These improve efficiency (faster stock counts, lower shrinkage, faster design cycles) rather than threaten anyone, and the larger chains can afford them more easily.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The bull case rests on formalisation and returns; the bear case on gold, policy and valuation. Both are visible in the 2026 numbers.

The two cases below set out the strongest version of each argument for the sub-sector as a whole. Individual companies sit at very different points between them, as the scorecard in section 15 shows.

The bull case

  • A long formalisation runway. About 60% of a ₹6.5–7 lakh crore market is still with family jewellers; organised share is expected to rise from 37–42% to 45–50% by FY30. Chains grew 21–31% in the September 2026 quarter while national tonnage fell.
  • High returns on little capital. Gold metal loans, franchise capital and customers' old gold let the best chains earn 20%+ ROCE on 6–10% margins and grow without new equity.
  • A margin lever that is moving the right way. Stud ratios are rising at Titan, Kalyan and Senco, and falling diamond input costs widen studded margins for now.
  • Resilient value demand. Weddings are budget-led; families cut grams, not spending. Exchange of old gold, worth two to three times its purchase price, funds new buying.
  • Policy that favours chains. Hallmarking, HUID, GST, cash rules and access to cheap gold loans all tilt the field toward audited, multi-store operators.
  • Optionality outside gold. Titan's watches, CaratLane and EyeCare, and Ethos's EFTA duty glide path, add growth not tied to the gold price.

The bear case

  • Volumes are shrinking. Jewellery tonnage fell 21% in FY26 and 15% in April–June 2026; ICRA expects another 15% fall in FY27. Value growth depends on gold staying high.
  • Profits are flattered. FY26 and FY27 earnings include gold and duty gains: Titan's ₹407 crore in one quarter, Senco's margin resetting from 11.7% to 7.5–7.8%, BlueStone's quarterly profit smaller than its inventory gain.
  • Policy risk cuts both ways. A duty cut would trigger inventory losses (as in July 2024); keeping 15% invites smuggling and moral suasion against buying gold.
  • Exchange and coins dilute margins. Old gold above 55% of sales and investment demand at 41% of volume both pull mix toward low-margin business.
  • Balance-sheet strain in parts. Five of nine had negative operating cash flow in FY26; inventory runs 700–1,015 days at two; borrowings nearly doubled at P N Gadgil; BlueStone's founder stake is 37% pledged.
  • Valuation. 60× FY26 earnings for the group and 68× for Titan, two to five times global peers, assume high growth with stable margins through a period of falling grams and deflating diamonds.

Weighing the two, the structural case is stronger than the cyclical one. Formalisation, studded mix and capital-light growth are durable; the 2026 combination of record rupee gold, a 15% duty and one-off inventory gains is not. The most likely path is that listed chains keep gaining share while reported margins normalise downward over FY27–FY28 as duty gains run off. Companies whose valuations already assume normalised earnings (Senco, P N Gadgil) are less exposed to that normalisation than those priced on the inflated numbers; companies whose profits depend on unhedged gold or legacy stock (Thangamayil, PC Jeweller) carry the most earnings risk.

In plain terms

The good news is that Indians keep moving their jewellery buying from local shops to chains, and that will continue for years. The bad news is that 2026's profits were helped by gold and tax moves that will not repeat, while Indians are buying fewer grams. Investors in the sector are betting that the first trend outlasts the second.
