00

The one-page view: a trough year, a fast rebound and a split market

Seven companies, ₹49,778 crore of revenue, and five quite different businesses that share one raw material and, for half of them, one dominant customer country.

"Textiles" in the NSE 750 means seven companies that turn cotton (and, at the margins, polyester and viscose) into something a buyer will pay for. Vardhman Textiles mostly sells yarn and fabric. K.P.R. Mill spins its own yarn, knits it, dyes it and sews it into T-shirts for European brands, and also runs a sugar mill. Welspun Living and Indo Count make towels and bedsheets for American big-box retailers. Trident does both yarn and towels, plus paper made from wheat straw. Arvind weaves denim and shirting fabric and is building a technical-textiles arm that sells protective fabrics and filtration media. Alok Industries is a giant integrated mill that went bankrupt in 2017, was rescued by Reliance Industries and JM Financial ARC, and still cannot cover its interest bill.

Together they reported ₹49,778 crore of revenue and ₹2,019 crore of net profit in FY26. Strip out Alok's ₹744 crore loss and the other six made ₹2,763 crore on ₹46,063 crore of revenue, at a combined operating margin (EBITDA before other income) of 12.2%. That is a respectable industrial margin, but it hides a year that was, for the US-facing half of the group, the worst since the 2023 destocking slump.

The reason is one policy decision in Washington. From 7 August 2025 the United States imposed a 25% "reciprocal" tariff on Indian goods under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), and from 27 August it doubled the total to 50% as a penalty for India's purchases of Russian oil. For a towel maker selling more than half its output to America, a 50% extra duty on top of the normal (most-favoured-nation, or MFN) rate is not a cost that can be passed on in full. Welspun Living's FY26 profit fell 67%; Indo Count's fell 49% on flat revenue. Then the regime collapsed in stages: a roughly 18% rate under an announced India–US arrangement for about two weeks in February 2026, a 6–3 US Supreme Court ruling on 20 February 2026 striking down the IEEPA tariffs altogether, a temporary 10% surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act from 24 February that lapsed on 24 July 2026, and since then a 10% Section 301 duty that puts India level with Bangladesh and Pakistan (according to Welspun's investor deck) and 2.5 percentage points cheaper than China, Vietnam and Turkey.

The first full quarter under the lower tariffs, April–June 2026 (Q1 FY27), showed how fast the business snaps back when the policy shock lifts: Indo Count's revenue rose 26%, Arvind's 25% and Welspun's 24%. Vardhman's operating margin jumped to 18% from 13% in FY26 as the gap between cotton and yarn prices widened. Investors had already moved: Welspun's share price roughly doubled off its tariff low and now trades at 76 times trailing earnings; Indo Count at 57.5 times. Those multiples are on depressed earnings, so they look extreme; the market is paying for the recovery before it is visible in annual numbers.

Revenue is not where the value sits. K.P.R. Mill (KPR) is only the fifth-largest by sales but is worth ₹36,677 crore, a third of the group's market value, because it earns a 19% margin and a 19.6% return on capital employed (ROCE) with net cash. Vardhman, the largest by revenue, is worth ₹15,249 crore and trades at 18 times earnings because its ROCE was 8.6% in FY26. The gap between those two companies is the clearest lesson in the sub-sector: owning the chain all the way to a finished garment, with captive power and a European customer base, has produced steadier margins than selling yarn into a commodity market. In plain terms

Indian textile companies all start with cotton, but they are paid for very different things. A spinner is paid the difference between yarn and cotton prices. A towel exporter is paid for reliability and price by a handful of American retailers, and a US tariff comes straight out of that price. A garment maker that owns every step from yarn to a sewn T-shirt keeps the margin that other companies hand to each other at every step. The 2025–26 tariff year showed which of these models is fragile.

Five forces explain almost everything in this report, and the sections are arranged around them:

  • US trade policy (Section 04). About half the listed profit pool depends on American home-textile demand. The tariff went from 25% to 50% to roughly 18% to zero (after the court ruling) to a 10% surcharge to a 10% Section 301 duty in under twelve months. Section 301 duties are themselves under legal challenge, and refunds of the struck-down IEEPA tariffs are unresolved.
  • The trade map rotating in India's favour (Section 05). The India–UK trade agreement entered into force on 15 July 2026; the India–EU agreement was concluded on 27 January 2026, with ratification pending and entry into force expected in 2027.
  • The cotton-to-yarn spread (Section 06). Indian cotton has a minimum support price (MSP) that rose 44% in five years, an 11% import duty that is waived and reinstated, and a state buyer of last resort. The spread between cotton and yarn moved Vardhman's margin from 24% to 10% and back to 18% on almost unchanged revenue.
  • Integration and downstream mix (Sections 09 and 10). Margin is captured at the garment end and in technical textiles, not in the commodity middle. Brands, US factories and protective fabrics are the escape routes from private-label pricing.
  • Capital intensity and working capital (Section 14). Mills stock a season of cotton after harvest, carrying inventory of 128 to 244 days, and spinning and processing need heavy, recurring capex. That caps returns outside KPR, and it is why a debt-funded scale bet (Alok) can sink a company.

Three points frame the rest of the report. First, the two US-heavy exporters (Welspun and Indo Count) had the worst FY26 and the best Q1 FY27, so trailing figures understate their earning power and trailing multiples overstate their expense. Second, the spinners (Vardhman and the yarn half of Trident) are moving on a different clock: the cotton-yarn spread, which turned in their favour in early 2026. Third, Alok sits outside every comparison. Its equity of −₹21,527 crore and ₹26,106 crore of borrowings make it a balance-sheet question that only its sponsor, Reliance, can answer. We show aggregates with and without it throughout.

In plain terms

FY26 was a bad year for reasons that have mostly reversed. The question for investors is not whether the US-facing companies recover (Q1 FY27 says they are recovering) but whether the recovery is already in the share prices. At 76 times and 57 times trailing earnings, Welspun and Indo Count need margins back in the low-to-mid teens within a year or two to look reasonable.

01

From kapas to a US shelf: how the money is made

Every listed member owns a different slice of a long chain, and the slice decides the margin.

The textile value chain starts in a cotton field and ends on a retail shelf, and almost every step was historically a separate business. A farmer grows kapas (seed cotton). A ginner separates the lint (the usable fibre, about a third of the weight) from the seed and presses it into bales of 170 kg; traders quote prices per "candy" of 356 kg. A spinner cards, combs and twists the lint into yarn, losing roughly 15–20% of the fibre as waste along the way, much of which is sold back into lower-grade uses. A weaver or knitter turns yarn into grey (unfinished) fabric. A processor bleaches, dyes, prints and finishes it. A cut-make-trim (CMT) unit turns fabric into a garment, a towel or a sheet set, and an exporter ships it to a retailer or brand that sets the final price.

The listed companies are distinguished by how many steps they own. KPR owns Steps 2–4 with captive power; Vardhman stops mostly at Step 3; Indo Count buys most of its yarn and concentrates on Steps 3–4; Alok owns all of 1–4 including polyester, and still loses money.

Each handoff in the chain carries a margin for the seller and a working-capital burden for someone. When a company owns several consecutive steps, two things happen. It keeps the margin that would otherwise go to the next supplier, and it can schedule the whole chain around its own orders, cutting waste and inventory. The cost is capital: a fully integrated mill needs spindles, looms or knitting machines, a dye house with an effluent treatment plant, cutting and sewing halls, and often a captive power plant. Integration pays only if the factories run full.

The second dimension is what the customer is buying. A spinner sells a near-commodity: 30s or 40s count combed cotton yarn has a published market price, and buyers can switch suppliers within a week. A home-textile exporter sells a programme: a US retailer contracts for a season's worth of towels in specified colours, weights and packaging, and the exporter's value lies in delivering on time at a price. A garment exporter to European brands sells a combination of fabric development, compliance (labour, chemicals, traceability) and speed. A technical-textile maker sells a specification: a flame-retardant fabric, a geotextile or a filter medium that has to pass a test, with switching costs to match.

The ladder is clear. Margin is captured at the garment end (KPR) and in technical textiles (Arvind's AMB), not in the home-textile middle. Home textiles earn mid-teens margins only in good cotton and tariff years. Spinning earns whatever the spread allows. And the one company that tried to own every step with borrowed money, Alok, shows the risk of integration without pricing power: when the capital cost exceeds the integration margin, the chain is a liability, not an asset.

There is also a man-made fibre (MMF) chain running in parallel. Purified terephthalic acid (PTA) and monoethylene glycol (MEG), mostly from Reliance, become polyester chips, then partially oriented yarn (POY), fully drawn yarn (FDY) and polyester staple fibre (PSF). Viscose staple fibre (VSF) comes from wood pulp, dominated in India by Grasim. Globally about two-thirds of fibre consumed is synthetic, but India's industry, and these seven companies, remain cotton-heavy. Alok is the exception, with about 1,250 tonnes a day of polyester capacity, which is the industrial logic behind Reliance's interest in it.

In plain terms

A bale of cotton passes through five or six hands before it becomes a towel. Each hand takes a margin and holds some stock. Companies that own several steps keep more of the margin, but they also have to keep all their factories busy. The most profitable listed textile company owns the whole chain into a finished garment; the least profitable owns the whole chain too, but borrowed too much to build it.

How each member makes its money

Vardhman Textiles sells cotton and blended yarn (about 52% of revenue), woven fabric (grey capacity 220 million metres a year, processed 210 million metres), sewing thread through its associate stake in the A&E joint venture, acrylic fibre (21,000 tonnes a year) and a small but growing shirt business (2.2 million pieces a year rising to 4.5 million by end-FY27). Exports were about 43% of sales in FY25. It is the cleanest listed proxy for the cotton-yarn spread.

Welspun Living makes terry towels and bath rugs (it claims #1 in the US), bed linen (top two in the US), rugs and carpets, flooring, spunlace nonwovens and, since 2025, pillows inside the US. In Q1 FY27 the US was about 59% of its revenue, from the company's statement that non-US sales were about 41%. Its value lies in scale and reliability as a private-label supplier to big-box retailers, plus a growing branded share (about 18% of sales).

Arvind weaves denim (17.5 million metres in Q1 FY27) and woven shirting fabric, stitches more than 11 million garments a quarter, and runs Advanced Materials (AMB): protective fabrics for defence and industry, composites and, since 6 May 2026, a roughly 61% stake in the US geotextile and filtration maker Dalco-GFT. Arvind is not the brand company: Arvind Fashions (US Polo Assn., Arrow, Flying Machine) was demerged in 2018 and is listed separately; apparel retail is covered in our speciality retail report.

Trident splits its revenue roughly into cotton yarn (about 44% of Q1 FY27 sales), bath linen (29%), bed linen (14%) and paper and chemicals made from wheat straw (14%), all from Punjab and Madhya Pradesh. The three cycles offset each other, which is why its FY26 profit held flat while Welspun's fell by two-thirds.

K.P.R. Mill spins about 350,000 spindles of yarn, knits and dyes fabric, and sews T-shirts and innerwear (181 million pieces sold in FY26 against 204 million of capacity) for brands that reportedly include H&M, Marks & Spencer and Decathlon. It also owns a 20,000-tonne-a-day sugar mill and a 470 kilolitre-a-day ethanol distillery in Karnataka, plus about 192 MW of captive wind, co-generation and solar power. Garments were about ₹3,179 crore of FY26 revenue, yarn and fabric about ₹2,256 crore and sugar and ethanol about ₹1,173 crore.

Indo Count Industries calls itself the world's largest bed-linen maker, with about 153 million metres of capacity after buying GHCL's home-textile plants at Vapi and Bhilad. More than 90% of revenue is exported, and about 70–75% of exports go to North America. Its "new business" (utility bedding such as comforters and pillows, US-made products and brands including the owned Wamsutta and licensed Tommy Hilfiger) was ₹387 crore in Q1 FY27, about a third of turnover.

Alok Industries has about 450,000 cotton spindles and roughly 1,250 tonnes a day of polyester capacity, plus weaving, knitting, processing, home textiles and garments, mostly at Silvassa. Reliance holds 40.01% and JM Financial ARC 34.99%. Revenue fell from ₹7,310 crore in FY22 to ₹3,715 crore in FY26; Q2 FY27 revenue rose 14.5% to ₹1,078 crore but the loss widened to ₹208 crore.

02

Size, exports and who buys: a ₹3.16 lakh crore basket growing at 2%

Indian textile and apparel exports have barely grown for a decade; the listed companies grew by taking share inside a flat pie.

India exported ₹3,16,335 crore of textiles, apparel and handicrafts in FY26, up 2.1% on FY25, according to Ministry of Textiles data. Ready-made garments (RMG) were the largest piece at ₹1,39,350 crore (+2.9%). Cotton yarn, fabric and made-ups, which is where towels and bedsheets sit, were ₹1,02,400 crore (+0.4%). Man-made fibre yarn, fabric and made-ups were ₹42,688 crore (+3.6%), and handicrafts excluding carpets ₹15,855 crore (+6.1%). The residual, about ₹16,042 crore, is carpets, jute, silk and wool.

These totals should be treated as indicative. The dollar series is definitionally inconsistent: parliamentary material puts FY26 at "more than US$33.5 billion", while the Press Information Bureau put FY25 at US$37.75 billion, because the two series cover handicrafts, carpets and jute differently. Dividing ₹3,16,335 crore by the FY26 average rate of ₹89.6 gives about US$35.3 billion. We use rupees throughout and flag the coverage problem rather than pick a series.

Over a decade, India's textile and apparel exports have compounded only about 3–5% a year in dollar terms, and India's share of world textile and apparel exports is about 4.1%. India supplies only around 4–5% of US and EU apparel imports, against China, Bangladesh and Vietnam, each two to six times larger. In the EU, India's apparel import share was about 4.9% in 2024, down from 5.8% in 2018. The government's target of a US$350 billion textile economy by 2030, including US$100 billion of exports, would need export growth several times faster than anything delivered in the past decade.

The home-textile story is better. In 2025 India was the largest supplier of bed and bath linen (HS code 6302) to the United States at US$1.99 billion (about ₹17,800 crore at the FY26 average of ₹89.6 per dollar, or ₹19,000 crore at today's ₹95.5), ahead of China at US$1.45 billion, whose shipments fell 27% as US tariffs on China rose. India, China and Pakistan together supply 85.5% of the category. That is the market Welspun, Indo Count and Trident's towel and sheet business fight over.

Six demand engines

The seven members sell into six distinct end-markets, and each moves on its own driver:

  • US big-box home textiles. Welspun (US about 59% of sales), Indo Count (about 70–75% of exports to North America) and Trident's towel and sheet arm. Demand follows US housing turnover, retailer inventory cycles and private-label programmes. Q1 FY27 home-textile exports at Welspun rose about 28%; Indo Count's core bed-linen volume reached 23 million metres, up 12% on the previous quarter.
  • EU and UK knitwear and apparel. KPR's EU exposure is about 23–25% of revenue, and its customers are European brands. The UK agreement, in force since 15 July 2026, removes duties of up to 12%. Welspun's UK and Europe sales rose more than 20% in Q1 FY27.
  • Domestic consumption. Vardhman and Arvind sell much of their fabric to Indian garment makers; Welspun's domestic consumer business grew 21.3% in Q1 FY27; Trident is building domestic brands.
  • Defence, industrial and infrastructure (technical textiles). Arvind's AMB revenue was ₹650 crore in Q1 FY27, up 85%, including ₹157 crore from Dalco-GFT; management cited a "normalisation of defence procurement". Welspun's advanced textiles are about 4% of sales.
  • Yarn exports to Asia. Vardhman's Q1 FY27 growth came from strong export yarn demand, with better cotton-yarn spreads and a weaker rupee.
  • Ethanol blending. KPR's 470 KLPD distillery sells to oil marketing companies; ethanol volume fell to 146 lakh litres in Q1 FY27 from 323 lakh a year earlier, an allocation and feedstock issue still to be explained in full.

In plain terms

India's overall textile exports are barely growing, but in one category, towels and bedsheets for American shoppers, India is already the largest supplier. 03

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: half to fibre, a tenth to profit

A textile P&L is dominated by one input the company does not control and one customer it cannot dictate to.

For an integrated towel or sheet exporter in a normal year, roughly ₹42 of every ₹100 of revenue goes on cotton, yarn and fibre. Dyes, chemicals, packing and accessories take another ₹8, power and fuel ₹8, employees ₹11, and freight, warehousing and other overheads ₹18. That leaves an EBITDA of about ₹13. Depreciation takes ₹4, interest ₹2 and tax about ₹2, leaving net profit of about ₹5. The inventory chart explains why textile companies need so much capital even when their receivables are tight. Indian cotton is harvested between October and March, and quality cotton of a given staple length is available in volume only then, so serious spinners buy several months of requirement during the season. Vardhman carries about 244 days of inventory; KPR, which also stocks cotton for its garment chain, 167 days. Suppliers' credit, customer advances and buyer-financing programmes fund part of it, which is why working-capital days are much lower than inventory days at Welspun (34), Trident (16) and Arvind (6, because Arvind's payables run to about 160 days). Alok's working capital is negative (−101 days) because its sponsor effectively funds it.

The practical consequence is that cotton purchasing is a large, concentrated bet made once a year. A mill that buys heavily at the top of the season and then sees cotton fall will sell yarn priced off the lower market cost while carrying expensive stock; that is how inventory losses wiped out spinner profits in FY23. Conversely, a mill that stocks up before a rise books inventory gains that flatter one or two quarters. Part of Vardhman's Q1 FY27 margin jump likely reflects cotton bought cheaply in the 2025–26 season against rising yarn prices; management attributed it to "improved spreads".

In plain terms

Half of a textile company's revenue goes straight out the door to pay for cotton, and the company has to buy most of a year's cotton in a few months. That makes profit very sensitive to the cotton price at the moment of purchase, and it ties up a lot of cash in bales sitting in warehouses. A ten-rupee move per kilo in cotton can be the difference between a good year and a bad one.

The supply side has one more structural feature: capacity is cheap to add in small increments and expensive to retire. India has an estimated 6.1–6.2 crore spindles (implied by CRISIL's statement that Vardhman's 12.3 lakh spindles are about 2% of the installed base). Thousands of small mills in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Punjab and Maharashtra run them, many family-owned, many supported by state power subsidies and past interest-subsidy schemes (the Technology Upgradation Fund Scheme, TUFS). When spreads are good, they run flat out and add spindles; when spreads are poor, they keep running to cover fixed costs. That behaviour keeps spinning margins mean-reverting and stops any single mill from setting prices.

The tariff roller-coaster: 25%, 50%, 18%, zero, 10%, 10% Deep dive

How a US duty on Indian goods went through six settings in a year, who paid for it, and why FY26 is the trough rather than the new normal.

No single number explains Indian textile earnings in 2025–26 better than the extra duty the United States charged on Indian goods. It changed six times in under a year, under three different US laws, and one of those laws was struck down by the Supreme Court. For any figure in this report it matters which regime it was earned under, so we begin with the sequence.

The sequence has a shape that matters for investors. The damage was concentrated in the 50% window, which ran from 27 August 2025 to early February 2026, about five and a half months that sit almost entirely inside FY26 (April 2025 to March 2026). Before it, the 10% baseline and the brief 25% rate were manageable. After it, the rate fell back to about 18% for roughly two weeks, then to zero on the IEEPA leg when the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 on 20 February 2026 that the statute did not authorise the tariffs. The administration replaced them four days later with a 10% surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act, a provision that allows temporary duties of up to 15% for up to 150 days. That surcharge lapsed on 24 July 2026 and was replaced the same day by duties under Section 301, the statute the US Trade Representative (USTR) uses against "unreasonable or discriminatory" foreign practices, this time framed as a forced-labour action.

Under Section 301 India pays 10% on top of MFN, the tier for countries that have their own bans on imports made with forced labour. China, Vietnam, Turkey and most other countries pay 12.5%. According to Welspun's investor deck (a company source, not a USTR notice we have seen), Bangladesh and Pakistan are also in the 10% tier. If that is right, India sits level with its two closest rivals in towels and sheets and 2.5 points below China and Vietnam, its best relative position in the US market since the tariff war began.

None of this is settled. Section 301 duties are under legal challenge, including suits filed in early August 2026. Refunds of the IEEPA tariffs collected between April 2025 and February 2026 are unresolved, and if US importers recover them, some may try to reopen prices with their Indian vendors for the concessions they extracted during the 50% months. That is speculative, but it is the kind of retroactive negotiation that private-label buyers are known for. And no text of an India–US interim trade agreement has been published, so the "framework" that briefly produced the 18% rate has no legal footprint to rely on.

In plain terms

For five and a half months, an Indian towel cost an American importer 50% more in extra duty than it had the year before. Then the duty fell back to 10%, and courts and lawyers are still arguing over the legal basis. Today India pays the same as Bangladesh and Pakistan and less than China and Vietnam. That is good news, but it rests on a tariff that could be challenged, changed or replaced.

Who paid: exporters gave up margin to keep the shelf

A tariff is paid by the importer of record, here a US retailer or its sourcing agent. Who bears it economically depends on bargaining power. The large Indian home-textile exporters made a deliberate choice: keep the programmes (the seasonal contracts that fill their factories) by giving price concessions, rather than lose shelf space to Pakistan or China that would be hard to win back. Indo Count's management said as much, and our Indo Count primer describes the roughly ₹123 crore fall in FY26 profit as the price of that decision. The cost appears in the margin line rather than the revenue line.

The evidence fits. Indo Count's FY26 revenue was flat (₹4,151 crore to ₹4,141 crore) but its company-reported EBITDA fell from ₹573 crore (13.7%) to ₹461 crore (11.0%) and its net profit from ₹250 crore to ₹127 crore. Welspun's revenue fell 11% and its margin fell from 12.3% to 8.4% on the screener basis (about 14% to 9.1% on the company's basis), and its profit fell 67%. Smaller apparel exporters, without the balance sheets to absorb duty, lost volume instead: US textile and apparel imports from India fell 18.2% in January 2026 and 28.7% in February 2026 year on year, while imports from Vietnam grew about 5%, according to CITI.

In plain terms

The Indian companies did not raise their prices to cover the tariff; they cut them a little so their American customers would keep ordering. A small price cut on a thin-margin product wipes out most of the profit. That is why their revenue held up but their profit halved, and why profit snapped back as soon as the duty fell.

The trade map rotates: UK open, EU signed off, US at parity

For the first time since the 2005 quota exit, Indian textiles face a trade map that is moving in their favour in three markets at once.

Tariffs are relative. A towel from India competes with towels from Pakistan, China and Turkey; a T-shirt competes with Bangladesh, Vietnam and Cambodia. What matters is not the duty India pays but the gap between India's duty and its rivals'. For most of the past two decades that gap ran against India: Bangladesh, Cambodia and Pakistan enjoyed duty-free or reduced-duty access to the EU under least-developed-country or GSP+ preferences, while Indian garments paid the full EU rate of up to about 12%. Vietnam signed an EU free trade agreement that took effect in 2020. India had no agreement with either of its two biggest Western markets.

That is changing on three fronts at once.

  • The UK. The India–UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), signed in July 2025, entered into force on 15 July 2026. It removes UK import duties of up to 12% on Indian textiles and apparel, putting India on terms at least equal to Bangladesh's in the UK. KPR, with its European brand customers, and Welspun, with its #1 towel position in the UK, are the direct beneficiaries. Welspun's UK and Europe sales already rose more than 20% in Q1 FY27.
  • The EU. India and the EU concluded negotiations on a free trade agreement on 27 January 2026. Some Indian press reports described it as "signed"; EU-process sources say the European Commission asked the Council to authorise signature on 11 September 2026, with signature targeted for December 2026 and entry into force expected in 2027. We treat it as concluded, not in force. When it takes effect, it should remove EU duties of up to about 12% on Indian garments and home textiles.
  • The US. Under Section 301, India now pays 10% on top of MFN, the same as Bangladesh and Pakistan (per Welspun's deck) and 2.5 points less than China, Vietnam and Turkey. Welspun says talks on a bilateral trade agreement are "advanced"; no text has been published.

Older agreements add at the margin: the India–Australia ECTA has been in force since 29 December 2022, and the India–EFTA trade agreement (Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein) since 1 October 2025. Welspun estimates the home-textile market in India's free-trade partners at more than US$85 billion, a useful indication of the scale of the opportunity even if no exporter will capture more than a fraction of it.

There is a caution. Trade agreements remove duties; they do not create competitiveness. Bangladesh's garment industry exports roughly US$40 billion a year (from memory), several times India's garment exports, and it built that position on lower labour costs, scale in basic garments and decades of buyer relationships, not just duty preferences. In plain terms

Indian textile exporters used to pay higher import duties than their rivals in Europe and Britain. Now the UK has removed its duties on Indian goods, the EU has agreed to do the same from about 2027, and in the US India pays a little less than China and Vietnam. That is the best combination the industry has had in twenty years, but lower duties only open the door; the companies still have to win the orders.

The cotton paradox: the world's biggest grower, with expensive fibre Deep dive

A rising support price, an on-off import duty and a state buyer of last resort decide the spread that sets every spinner's margin.

India is one of the world's two largest cotton producers, yet its spinners regularly complain that Indian cotton is expensive. Both statements are true, and the reason is policy. The government sets a minimum support price (MSP) for kapas, buys through the Cotton Corporation of India (CCI) whenever market prices fall below it, and charges an 11% duty on imported cotton except when it waives the duty. Those three levers put a floor under Indian cotton that rivals in Bangladesh and Vietnam, which import US, Brazilian and African cotton duty-free, do not face.

The support price: up 44% in five seasons

The MSP for medium-staple kapas was ₹5,726 a quintal in 2021-22, ₹6,080 in 2022-23, ₹6,620 in 2023-24 and ₹7,121 in 2024-25 (these four from memory of CACP and PIB announcements, so INDICATIVE). It rose to ₹7,710 in 2025-26 (+8.3%) and to ₹8,267 for 2026-27 (+7.2%), with long-staple cotton at ₹8,667. The source for ₹8,267 does not state the season explicitly, but the 7.2% step over ₹7,710 is consistent with it being the 2026-27 rate. From ₹5,726 to ₹8,267 is a 44% rise in five seasons, faster than yarn prices and far faster than global cotton.

The MSP is a farmer-income policy, not a textile policy, and it works as one: when market prices fall below MSP, CCI buys. In the 2025-26 season it bought more than 10.4 million bales (of 170 kg), about 4% more than the year before, and it holds 4–5 million bales in stock. That makes CCI the swing supplier to the mills. When it sells its stocks through auctions, it effectively sets the domestic price; when it holds them, prices firm.

Comparing prices requires care with units. MSP is quoted per quintal (100 kg) of kapas, which yields only about 33–35% lint by weight. Mills buy lint, quoted per candy (356 kg). Global prices are quoted in US cents per pound on ICE futures. In April 2026 the benchmark Shankar-6 variety traded at about ₹60,500 a candy, or about ₹170 a kilogram of lint. ICE futures were in the high 70s of cents per pound in June 2026; at 78 cents, that is about US$1.72 a kilogram, or ₹164 at ₹95.5. On those snapshot numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE, and from different months), Indian cotton was about 4% dearer than the global benchmark before freight and duty, and an Indian mill importing cotton would have paid 11% duty on top of that outside the waiver windows.

In plain terms

The government guarantees cotton farmers a minimum price that has risen by almost half in five years. When the market price falls below it, a state agency buys the crop. That protects farmers but keeps the mills' main raw material expensive compared with what Bangladeshi or Vietnamese mills pay for imported cotton. When the gap gets too wide, the government suspends the import duty for a few months.

The import duty: switched off, on and off again

The basic import duty on raw cotton (HS 5201) is 11%: 5% basic customs duty plus a 5% agriculture infrastructure and development cess (AIDC) plus the social welfare surcharge. The government waived it from 19 August 2025 to 30 September 2025, extended the waiver to 31 December 2025, reinstated the duty in January 2026, and waived it again from 1 June to 31 October 2026 (Notification 19/2026-Customs of 30 May 2026). The current waiver expires at the end of this month. The precedent from 2025 is that a waiver can be extended once, but the 2026-27 crop is arriving from October and farm groups oppose imports at harvest time, so an extension is far from certain.

For spinners, the duty matters most for extra-long-staple cotton (US Pima, Egyptian Giza, Australian), which India grows little of and which premium yarns and bedsheets need. A waiver lowers the cost of these imports by about a tenth. For home-textile exporters selling high-thread-count sheets, it also matters for compliance: some US retailers want traceable, non-Xinjiang cotton, and imported US cotton with documentation is one way to supply it.

The crop: three estimates, one uncertain balance

Nobody agrees on the size of India's 2025-26 cotton crop. Industry figures cited in trade press put it at about 291 lakh bales; the US Department of Agriculture at about 305 lakh; the Government of India at 306.92 lakh. A gap of 16 lakh bales, about 5% of the crop, is the difference between a comfortable and a tight balance, and it is one reason cotton prices rose 8.5% in a single month in April 2026. For 2026-27, planted area is reported up about 7%. The government's Mission for Cotton Productivity (2026-27 to 2030-31, ₹5,659 crore outlay) targets 498 lakh bales by 2031, which would require yields far above India's current levels, among the lowest of the major producers.

The man-made fibre side: a quieter policy win

Polyester and viscose matter less to these seven companies than cotton, but a policy change in November 2025 helped the whole chain. Quality-control orders (QCOs), which required imports of PTA, MEG, polyester staple fibre and various polyester yarns to carry Bureau of Indian Standards certification, were rescinded by the Ministry of Chemicals on 12 November 2025; the viscose staple fibre QCO was withdrawn by the Ministry of Textiles on 18 November 2025. The orders had effectively restricted imports and kept domestic MMF prices above international levels. Their removal cheapens polyester and viscose for Indian converters, which helps blended-fabric makers and, at the margin, Alok's downstream business, while removing a cushion for domestic fibre producers such as Reliance and Grasim.

In plain terms

A spinning mill makes money on the gap between what it pays for cotton and what it gets for yarn, minus running costs. That gap is small, so a 10% jump in cotton can wipe out two-thirds of the profit, and a 5% rise in yarn prices can add half. In mid-2026 the gap widened in the mills' favour. Whether it stays wide depends on a duty decision due by 31 October, the size of the new crop, and how much cotton the government agency buys and sells.

In plain terms

Cotton policy pulls in two directions. Higher support prices help farmers but make Indian yarn dearer. Import-duty waivers help mills but upset farmers at harvest. The government switches between them as the gap between Indian and world prices widens and narrows, which is why spinners' profits move in cycles that have little to do with how well the mills are run.

Industry structure: fragmented at the spindle, concentrated on the shelf

The largest Indian spinner has 2% of the country's spindles; three countries supply 85% of America's towels and sheets.

Indian textiles are fragmented upstream and concentrated downstream. Spinning is the most fragmented stage: Vardhman, the largest spinner, holds about 12.3 lakh spindles, roughly 2% of India's installed base, according to CRISIL. Thousands of mills, many in the Tamil Nadu cluster around Coimbatore and Tiruppur, in Gujarat, Punjab and Maharashtra, sell into the same yarn market. Listed mid-sized spinners include Nahar, Sutlej, Ambika Cotton, Nitin Spinners and Sri Ramalinga; many more are unlisted family businesses. No single mill can move the yarn price, which is why spinning margins revert to the mean.

Home textiles for export are the opposite. A handful of Indian companies, Welspun, Indo Count, Trident, Himatsingka Seide and a few others, together make India the largest supplier of bed and bath linen to the United States, and they face a small number of very large buyers: US big-box retailers and department-store groups that run private-label programmes. Welspun claims the #1 position in US towels and bath rugs and a top-two position in sheets; Indo Count claims to be the world's largest bed-linen maker. On the buying side, a single retailer can account for a large share of an exporter's sales; neither company publishes customer concentration in detail, but the industry's history (including Welspun's 2016 episode with a US retailer over mislabelled "Egyptian cotton" sheets) shows how much leverage a top customer has.

The real rivals: Pakistan in towels, China in synthetics

In cotton towels the real competitor is Pakistan, not China. In the first quarter of 2023, Pakistan supplied 37% of cotton terry-towel units imported into the US, India 36% and China 14%. In cotton sheets, India, Pakistan and China together supplied about 94% of US imports in early 2024. In man-made-fibre (polyester microfibre) sheets, China dominates, and India and Pakistan each had under 5% of US imports in the first half of 2025. That split explains why India's tariff position relative to Pakistan matters more to Welspun and Indo Count than its position relative to China, and why the Section 301 tiering (India and, per Welspun, Pakistan both at 10%) is described by the companies as parity rather than advantage.

Imports and China

India's textile imports are small relative to exports but strategic. Extra-long-staple cotton comes from the US, Egypt and Australia (partly under the Australia ECTA quota), duty-free during waivers. Man-made fibre and filament imports, mainly from China, surged before the quality-control orders and are likely to rise again now they are rescinded. In plain terms

Thousands of mills spin yarn in India, so none of them controls the price. But only a few big companies make towels and sheets for American stores, and they sell to a few very large buyers who hold most of the bargaining power. India's main rival in towels is Pakistan, not China. The listed companies are almost all family-run, and the funds that own them favour the two with the most distinctive businesses, Arvind and KPR.

Why archetype decides valuation

Investors pay for predictability. Alok's working capital is funded by its sponsor, which is not a model any other company can copy.

In plain terms

Before comparing two textile companies, ask which kind of textile company each one is. A spinner's profit swings with cotton prices; a towel exporter's swings with US trade policy; a garment maker's has been the steadiest. Comparing their price-to-earnings ratios without that context is like comparing a farm with a supermarket because both sell food.

Trident shows why the archetypes matter even within one company. In Q1 FY27 its yarn arm earned a 15.3% EBIT margin, its bed linen 16.3% and its paper 17.6%, but its bath linen only 5.3%. A single blended margin of 17% hides a towel business under severe pressure and a yarn business enjoying the spread upswing. Investors who own Trident for its towels and investors who own it for its yarn are buying different exposures, and the blended number tells neither group what it needs.

In plain terms

The steadiest margins belong to the company that sells finished T-shirts; the most erratic belong to the one that sells yarn. The towel and sheet makers sit in between in normal years, but they can fall sharply when Washington changes the rules. Alok sits on its own: its problem is not its margin but its debt.

What the market is paying for at KPR

Integration. KPR runs the chain from cotton to finished garment: spinning about 350,000 spindles, knitting, dyeing and processing fabric, then cutting and sewing 181 million garments in FY26 against 204 million pieces of capacity (about 89% utilisation). Each step that would otherwise be a purchase from another company is an internal transfer, so KPR keeps the spinner's spread, the knitter's margin, the processor's margin and the garment maker's margin. A garment that sells for about ₹176 (FY26 garment revenue of about ₹3,179 crore divided by 181 million pieces, our estimate) contains roughly 0.2 kg of fabric whose yarn costs about ₹60 (ILLUSTRATIVE). The remaining ₹116 or so pays for knitting, dyeing, cutting, sewing, trims, logistics and margin, and KPR captures nearly all of it.

Captive power. About 192 MW of wind, solar and sugar-bagasse co-generation covers most of the power that spinning and processing need. Power is 8–12% of a mill's costs; owning it removes a cost that grid-dependent spinners pay at commercial tariffs.

The right customers. KPR's garment customers are European brands, which source on quality, compliance and lead time as well as price, and which were not subject to the US tariff. KPR's FY26 profit rose 6% while Welspun's fell 67%. The UK agreement (in force) and the EU agreement (expected 2027) are both tailwinds for exactly this customer base.

Self-funded growth. In August 2026 KPR approved ₹1,225 crore of capex, internally funded, aimed at about ₹2,000 crore of additional turnover: a 45-million-piece garment plant in Odisha (₹450 crore, due in the first quarter of FY28), a 2.5-million-piece sweater unit, 10,000 tonnes a year of new processing at Perundurai, knit-fabric modernisation of 15,000 and 20,000 tonnes a year at Arasur and Neelambur, and spinning modernisation, with completions from the third quarter of FY27 to the fourth quarter of FY28. At the FY26 realisation of about ₹176 a piece, the Odisha plant alone could add about ₹790 crore of garment revenue at full utilisation (ILLUSTRATIVE).

The case for Vardhman's discount narrowing

Vardhman's discount is explained by its single-digit ROCE through most of the cycle, but three things could narrow it. First, the spread: Q1 FY27's 18% margin, if sustained for a year on FY26 revenue, would lift EBITDA from ₹1,239 crore to about ₹1,780 crore, and the EV/EBITDA multiple would fall to about 8.6 times (ILLUSTRATIVE; a single quarter is not a year). CRISIL's outlook for the Vardhman group (which may include Vardhman Special Steels) is FY27 revenue of ₹12,000–12,200 crore and a margin of 13.5–14%, citing the wider cotton-yarn spread, a more conservative view. Second, capex: about ₹3,000 crore over FY25–FY27 (CRISIL; trade press cites about ₹3,660 crore) is going into yarn, nylon and polyester fabric, digitisation and green energy, including a 26% stake in a 27 MW Serentica renewable project signed on 5 October 2026, and a shirt unit doubling to 4.5 million pieces. Some of that moves Vardhman downstream. Third, the balance sheet: CRISIL rates it AA+, net debt is somewhere between near-zero and ₹400 crore, and it carries ₹1,828 crore of investments, so it can keep investing through a downturn.

Against that: FY26 free cash flow was −₹579 crore, ROCE will be diluted by new capacity before it lifts, and Vardhman's mix is still about half yarn. Its shirt business, at 4.5 million pieces, would be about 2% of KPR's garment capacity. The premium gap will not close because Vardhman becomes KPR; it may narrow if the spread holds and the market starts to value Vardhman on mid-cycle rather than trough earnings.

Escaping private label: brands, US factories and technical textiles

The home-textile exporters live on programmes priced by a few retailers; each listed company is trying a different route out.

Private-label home textiles are a good business in a good year and a squeezed one otherwise. The exporter makes a towel or a sheet set to a retailer's specification, under the retailer's label, at a price negotiated programme by programme. The retailer can re-tender the programme each season, move it to Pakistan or China, or demand a share of any cost shock, as it did with the 2025–26 tariff. The exporter's protection is scale, reliability and product development; it does not own the customer. Each listed home-textile maker is now pushing in three directions to change that: owned or licensed brands, factories inside the United States, and adjacent products with different economics.

Welspun: capacity built for more than it sells

Welspun's Q1 FY27 deck shows how much room it has before it needs new capital. Its bath-linen plant (96,400 tonnes a year) ran at 83%, but its bed-linen capacity (108 million metres) ran at only 60%. Rugs ran at 74%, spunlace nonwovens at 48%, needle-punch at 36%, flooring at about 40% of effective capacity and wet wipes at 18%. Its US pillow plants (Ohio and Nevada, 10.1 million pieces effective) ran at 55%, with Ohio above 80% and Nevada producing only from 15 June 2026.

Producing inside the US: tariff-proofing a slice of revenue

Both Welspun (pillows in Ohio and Nevada) and Indo Count (utility bedding in North Carolina, with US facilities 60–65% utilised) now make products inside the United States. A pillow or comforter filled and sewn in the US from imported components pays duty only on the components, not on the finished product, and can be marketed as US-made. These are small businesses today, but they sidestep the policy risk that wrecked FY26 and put the exporters closer to the retailer's distribution centres. The trade-off is US labour and overhead costs, which are much higher than India's; the plants make sense only for bulky, low-labour products where freight and duty are large shares of the landed cost.

Arvind has taken the most distinctive route, into Advanced Materials (AMB): flame-retardant and ballistic fabrics for defence and police, industrial fabrics, composites and, since 6 May 2026, the US geotextile and filtration business Dalco-GFT. Trident's Group CEO, Samir Joshipura, resigned with effect from 30 September 2026, and Rahul Roongta re-joined as Group CFO on 14 October 2026. In plain terms

Arvind raised new money from investors and borrowed more to buy an American company, nearly doubling its net debt in three months. Welspun's owning family sold a slice of shares after the price doubled. Trident's chief executive left. None of these changes the industry, but each changes what an investor in that company is buying.

Alok: the rescue that has not yet worked

Alok Industries is the cautionary tale of Indian textiles. It expanded aggressively with debt through the 2000s and early 2010s into a fully integrated cotton and polyester chain, accumulated borrowings of more than ₹30,000 crore, and was among the first large companies referred to insolvency under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code in 2017. Reliance Industries and JM Financial ARC won it with a resolution plan of about ₹5,000 crore, approved by the National Company Law Tribunal in 2020 and implemented around September 2020. Reliance's interest was industrial: Alok's polyester capacity sits naturally downstream of Reliance's PTA and MEG.

Six years later, Alok still cannot cover its interest. In FY26 it earned ₹28 crore of operating profit on ₹3,715 crore of revenue, against interest of ₹615 crore and depreciation of ₹263 crore, for a loss of ₹744 crore. Its balance sheet carries ₹26,106 crore of borrowings (press reports cite ₹25,616 crore; the difference is likely leases or preference instruments), and its net worth is −₹21,527 crore: equity capital of ₹497 crore plus reserves of −₹22,024 crore. (Our Alok primer quotes the reserves figure as net worth; the correct net worth is −₹21,527 crore.) Reliance provides a corporate guarantee on the term debt. Reports that Reliance now accounts for Alok as an associate rather than a subsidiary could not be verified.

Its shares are worth something only because Reliance might one day restructure the debt; how much minority shareholders get in that case is entirely in Reliance's hands.

Policy: the state's hand on fibre, factories and exports

Support prices, import duties, production incentives, textile parks and export rebates: the government is present at almost every step of the chain.

Few Indian industries are shaped by as many policy levers as textiles. The state sets the floor price of the raw material, decides whether to tax its import, subsidises new capacity, builds industrial parks, rebates embedded taxes on exports and negotiates the trade agreements that decide market access. Some of these levers pull against each other: a higher cotton support price helps farmers and hurts spinners; a duty waiver does the reverse. For investors, the practical point is that a large share of the sub-sector's earnings variance comes from decisions taken in New Delhi and Washington rather than in company boardrooms.

A century of cycles: quotas, cotton spikes, a bankruptcy and a tariff war

The listed companies are survivors of six distinct shocks; each left a mark on how they are built and financed.

The oldest member, Arvind, was founded by the Lalbhai family in Ahmedabad in 1931, when the city was India's Manchester. KPR began in Coimbatore in 1984 under K.P. Ramasamy and his brothers and listed in 2007. Indo Count started as a spinning unit in Kolhapur in 1988. The modern shape of the industry, however, was set in the twenty years since the Multi-Fibre Arrangement ended on 1 January 2005. Under the MFA, rich countries had allocated import quotas by country, guaranteeing India a share whatever its costs. When quotas ended, China captured most of the freed share, and Indian mills (Welspun, Trident, Alok, Vardhman) responded with a capacity-building wave in 2005–12, much of it subsidised by TUFS.

The 2010–11 cotton super-spike was the first great cycle of the modern era. ICE cotton futures exceeded 200 cents a pound, Indian cotton reached about ₹62,000 a candy in March 2011 (from memory), and the government restricted cotton exports in 2010 and 2012. Spinners boomed and then crashed as prices collapsed and they were left with expensive inventory.

The 2016 Welspun–Target episode, in which a major US retailer found that sheets sold as "Egyptian cotton" did not contain it, was a reminder of retailer power and reputational risk. Welspun lost business for a time, built a traceability system (Wel-Trak) and recovered; the lesson about customer concentration stuck.

Alok's collapse, 2017–20, ended the debt-funded integration model. A company with borrowings above ₹30,000 crore was referred to insolvency, and creditors recovered a fraction through the Reliance–JM Financial plan.

In 2018 Arvind demerged its brands and retail business into Arvind Fashions and its engineering arm into Anup Engineering, leaving a focused textiles and advanced-materials company.

The FY22 boom followed the pandemic: restocking demand and cotton at about ₹1 lakh a candy in May 2022 widened yarn spreads to record levels. Vardhman's margin reached 24% and its profit ₹1,551 crore; KPR's margin reached 25%. The FY23–FY24 bust followed as US and EU retailers destocked and cotton fell: Vardhman's margin dropped to 10% in FY24, Welspun's to 9% in FY23. In 2024–25, China+1 sourcing and the August 2024 political crisis in Bangladesh pushed buyers toward India; Indo Count bought Wamsutta, and Welspun gained US share. Then came the FY26 tariff shock and, in FY27, the reset described in Sections 04 and 05.

Reading a textile company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twelve numbers explain almost every textile result. What each one means, how to compute it, what good looks like, where the seven companies sit, and the traps in each definition.

Every textile company's profit can be written as one equation: how much it sells, at what price, minus what the fibre cost, minus what it cost to convert the fibre into the product, minus what it gave away to get the product into the customer's hands. Some of those terms management controls; some it does not. The first job in reading any textile result is to separate the two.

In plain terms

A textile company's profit is what is left after paying for cotton and running the factories, out of what customers pay. The cotton price and the customer's price are mostly set outside the company. What the managers really control is how efficiently the factories run and how full they are. So when profit jumps or falls, first check whether prices moved; only then credit or blame the managers.

1. Volume and capacity utilisation

What it is. Volume is the physical quantity sold: tonnes of yarn or towels, million metres of fabric or bed linen, million pieces of garments. Utilisation is output divided by installed capacity. Formula: utilisation = production ÷ capacity for the same period. Worked example: Welspun's bath-linen plant has 96,400 tonnes a year of capacity; Q1 FY27 output was 20,048 tonnes, or about 80,200 tonnes annualised, which is 83%.

What drives it. Orders first (retailer programmes, brand seasons, yarn export demand), then operational factors: power outages, labour availability, floods (Welspun Vapi and Indo Count Bhilad in 2026), and the ramp-up of new lines. Utilisation matters because conversion costs are largely fixed: as Section 03 showed, roughly ₹40 of every ₹100 of a mill's costs do not fall when volume falls. Benchmark: good is 85% or more, average 70–85%, weak below 70%.

In plain terms

Realisation is simply the average selling price per towel, metre or T-shirt. It goes up when a company sells better products, when cotton costs are passed on, or when the rupee weakens. Only the first of those says anything about the company's strength.

3. The cotton-to-yarn spread

What it is. The difference between the price of yarn and the cost of the cotton in it. Formula: yarn price (₹/kg) − [cotton lint price (₹/kg) × input ratio (about 1.15–1.20) − waste recovery (₹/kg)]. Worked example: April 2026: ₹290 − (₹170 × 1.18 − ₹10) ≈ ₹99 a kilogram (ILLUSTRATIVE). After about ₹70 of conversion cost, EBITDA is about ₹29 a kilogram, 10% of the yarn price.

What drives it. Cotton supply (crop size, CCI stocks, MSP, import duty), yarn demand (domestic weavers and knitters, exports to China, Bangladesh and Vietnam) and the rupee. The spread is the single biggest driver of Vardhman's earnings and of the yarn halves of Trident and KPR. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): below 2% of FOB is good, 2–5% average, above 5% weak. The FY26 evidence implies concessions large enough to take 3–4 points off blended margins. Read the margin bridge, not the press release.

In plain terms

A price cut of 3% on a towel that earns a 13% margin takes almost a quarter of the profit. Companies do not announce these cuts; they show up as "pricing pressure" in the results commentary. When a US-facing exporter's revenue holds up but its margin falls, a concession to buyers is the first suspect.

8. Inventory days and working-capital days

What it is. How many days of sales are tied up in stock, and in stock plus receivables minus payables. Formulas: inventory days = inventory ÷ revenue × 365 (screener uses revenue; some analysts use cost of goods); working-capital days = (inventory + receivables − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: Vardhman's 244 inventory days on ₹9,869 crore of revenue means about ₹6,600 crore of stock at year-end; its working-capital days of 156 mean about ₹4,200 crore is funded by the company rather than by suppliers.

What drives it. The cotton season (mills buy October–March and stock through the year), retailer terms (home-textile exporters often hold finished goods in US warehouses), and supplier credit (Arvind's ~160 payable days). Benchmarks: working capital below 45 days good, 45–100 average, above 100 weak; inventory below 130 days good, 130–200 average, above 200 weak. On those measures, FY26: Trident (16 WC days, 128 inventory days) and Arvind (6 and 177) look good on working capital; Vardhman (156 and 244) and KPR (99 and 167) are weak or average, by choice, because they stock cotton. The chart is in Section 03.

The trap: year-end (31 March) is the peak of the cotton-buying season, so March balance sheets overstate the average inventory a mill carries; September balance sheets understate it. Compare like with like (March with March), and remember that a mill with low inventory days at the wrong time of year may simply not have bought its cotton yet, which could be a bet on lower prices.

The capital side of the anatomy deserves a sentence of its own. The plant ties up about ₹663 crore: ₹550 crore of depreciated machinery and buildings and about ₹113 crore of working capital (45 days of ₹917 crore of sales). EBITDA of ₹118 crore less depreciation of ₹37 crore gives EBIT of about ₹81 crore, a pre-tax return of about 12%. If the plant had to carry Vardhman-style inventory of 240 days instead of 45 days of working capital, capital employed would rise by several hundred crore and the return would fall toward single digits, which is the arithmetic behind the low ROCE of the cotton-stocking mills. If instead the plant were bought second-hand or inside a subsidised park at half the cost, the return would roughly double, which is why PM MITRA parks and acquisitions of distressed capacity (such as Indo Count's purchase of GHCL's plants for about ₹610 crore) can change a company's economics.

Three lessons fall out of the anatomy. First, net profit is about 5% of revenue in a normal year, so a cost shock of 3–5% of revenue (a tariff concession, a cotton spike, a flood) can erase it. Second, utilisation is the lever management controls: at 60% the plant earns a 6.2% EBITDA margin, at 90% 15.1%, with the same prices. That is why Welspun's bed-linen plant at 60% is both a weakness today and an opportunity tomorrow. Third, a pre-tax ROCE of about 12% for a well-run plant in a normal year is only around the cost of capital. Textile manufacturing creates value only with high utilisation, integration that removes a supplier's margin, or a product that earns more than the commodity price.

In plain terms

A typical towel factory keeps about five rupees of every hundred as profit. Running it at 90% of capacity instead of 60% more than doubles its operating margin, because wages and overheads do not shrink when output falls. Filling the factory matters more than almost anything else a manager can do.

Definition traps, collected

  • Revenue basis. "Total income" includes other income; "revenue from operations" does not. Indo Count's FY26 total income was ₹4,211 crore, its sales ₹4,141 crore. Vardhman's standalone revenue (₹9,652 crore in our primer) differs from consolidated (₹9,869 crore). Use consolidated revenue from operations.
  • EBITDA basis. Company EBITDA usually includes other income; screener's operating profit does not. The gap was 0.7 points at Welspun and 1.45 points at Indo Count in FY26. Compare guidance only with company-basis figures.
  • "The US tariff". Six settings in under a year under three laws. Always say which regime (IEEPA 25% or 50%, the ~18% arrangement, Section 122 at 10%, Section 301 at 10%) a figure was earned under.
  • US share denominators. Share of revenue, of exports, or of a product's exports. Welspun ~59% of revenue; Indo Count ~70–75% of exports.
  • Cotton units. MSP is per quintal of kapas (seed cotton, about a third lint); mill prices are per candy (356 kg) of lint; world prices are cents per pound. ₹60,500 a candy ≈ ₹170/kg of lint; 78¢/lb ≈ ₹164/kg at ₹95.5.
  • Capacity. Nameplate vs effective; spindles are not comparable across counts; Arvind's denim capacity is internally inconsistent in our primer.
  • Net debt. Company figures vs screener proxies (borrowings minus investments, which misses cash in other assets) vs rating-agency group figures. Vardhman: ~₹30 crore proxy vs ₹350–400 crore CRISIL.
  • Seasonal balance sheets. March is peak cotton inventory; compare March with March.
  • Exceptional items. Arvind's Q1 FY27 PAT was ₹80 crore before exceptional items and ₹58 crore after; Alok's Q2 included a ₹35.7 crore exceptional loss. State which one you are using.
  • "New business" and "branded". Self-defined buckets; judge them by segment margin, not by name.

How to read a textile company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find the archetype and the regime (1 minute). Spinner, garment maker, home-textile exporter, technical, or distressed? Which US tariff regime and which cotton season does the quarter sit in? Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) is the first quarter fully under Section 301 at 10%.
  2. Revenue growth, split into volume and price (1 minute). Take segment volumes (tonnes, metres, pieces) and realisations from the presentation. Volume growth with flat price is demand; price growth with flat volume may be cotton pass-through or currency.
  3. Strip out the rupee (30 seconds). Compare the quarter's average rupee with the year-earlier quarter. A 5% weaker rupee adds roughly 5% to export revenue with no change in the business.
  4. Gross margin, against cotton (1 minute). Did gross margin move with or against the cotton-yarn spread? For converters, compare with yarn prices. A rising gross margin while cotton rises suggests cheap inventory or real pricing power; find out which.
  5. EBITDA margin, on the right basis (1 minute). Note whether the company includes other income. Compare with guidance on the same basis (Welspun low teens; Indo Count 13%).
  6. Segment margins (1.5 minutes). For Trident, look at bath vs bed vs yarn vs paper; for Arvind, textiles vs AMB; for KPR, textiles vs sugar; for Welspun, home textiles vs flooring. The blended margin hides the trend.
  7. Utilisation and capacity (1 minute). Are the plants fuller? Any new capacity (KPR Odisha, Welspun Nevada, Indo Count US) dragging margins during ramp-up?
  8. Working capital and net debt (1.5 minutes). Inventory and receivable days versus the same quarter last year; net debt versus the last quarter. A jump in inventory without a jump in sales is a warning; so is net debt rising faster than capex explains.
  9. Geography (30 seconds). US vs non-US growth; UK and EU after the agreements; domestic consumer.
  10. Commentary red flags (1 minute). "Pricing pressure", "customer support", "inventory correction", "one-time" items that recur, management changes, flood or power disruptions, and any change in how a KPI is defined. Then check the next quarter's board date and guidance changes.
In plain terms

When a textile company reports, first ask what kind of textile company it is and what the US tariff and cotton price were that quarter. Then check whether sales grew because it sold more or because prices and the rupee moved, whether the margin is measured the same way as last time, and whether stock or debt is piling up. Ten minutes with those questions tells you more than the headline profit number.

Technology & disruption: automation, traceability and the synthetic shift

Five forces are changing how textiles are made and sold; most favour the larger, integrated, better-capitalised players.

Textile manufacturing looks like an old industry, but its economics are being reshaped by forces that will matter more over the next five years than any single tariff decision.

  • Automation. Compact spinning, automatic cone winders, air-jet looms and increasingly automated cutting and sewing reduce the labour needed per kilogram or per piece. That lowers costs for those who invest, raises the capital needed per spindle and loom, and narrows India's labour-cost advantage over Bangladesh and the gap between Asian and near-shore production. It favours companies with balance sheets to fund modernisation: KPR's ₹1,225 crore programme includes spinning and knit-fabric modernisation, and Vardhman is spending heavily on digitisation.
  • Traceability and forced-labour rules. The US Section 301 action that now sets India's tariff is itself keyed to forced-labour rules: countries with their own import bans on forced-labour goods get the lower 10% tier. US enforcement against Xinjiang cotton (under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act) has made fibre-origin documentation a condition of doing business with large retailers. Indian cotton with traceability (Kasturi's blockchain records; Welspun's Wel-Trak) is an advantage that Bangladesh, which uses imported and often Chinese-origin fabric, finds harder to match.
  • The synthetic shift. Roughly two-thirds of the fibre consumed worldwide is synthetic, mainly polyester; India's industry, and these seven companies, remain cotton-heavy. Athleisure, performance wear and microfibre home textiles are synthetic categories where China dominates (China is the top US supplier of polyester sheets; India and Pakistan each had under 5% in the first half of 2025). The November 2025 withdrawal of quality-control orders cheapens synthetic inputs for Indian converters, and the PLI scheme targets MMF apparel and technical textiles, but none of the listed members is yet a significant MMF garment maker.
  • Making it in America. Welspun's pillow plants in Ohio and Nevada and Indo Count's North Carolina utility-bedding plant put a slice of production inside the US tariff wall. Their scale is small and their costs high, but they are a hedge against policy and a way to serve retailers' demand for faster replenishment.
  • Climate and physical risk. Floods hit Welspun's Vapi operations and shut Indo Count's Bhilad plant for about 20 days in 2026 (insured). A tornado and power failures cut Alok's Silvassa utilisation in FY25. Water use and effluent from dye houses are regulated tightly in Tamil Nadu and Gujarat; captive renewable power (KPR's ~192 MW, Vardhman's Serentica stake) reduces both cost and emissions, which European buyers increasingly audit.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian textiles now

The trade map has turned in India's favour and the trough is behind; but the valuations already assume the recovery, and the policy risk has changed shape rather than disappeared.

The bull case

  • The trough is behind. FY26 contained the 50% tariff window; Q1 FY27 revenue rose 16.6% for the six ex-Alok, with Indo Count +26%, Arvind +25% and Welspun +24%.
  • Best trade map in two decades. UK duties gone (15 Jul 2026), EU agreement concluded (entry into force expected 2027), US tier at 10% against 12.5% for China, Vietnam and Turkey.
  • The spread has turned. Vardhman's margin 18% in Q1 FY27 against 13% in FY26; Trident's yarn at 15.3% EBIT; KPR's gross margin up 480 basis points.
  • Operating leverage in spare capacity. Welspun's bed linen at 60%, Indo Count's core at about 70% and US plants at 60–65%: volume growth without heavy capex.
  • Mix shift is real. Indo Count's new business at a third of revenue; Arvind's AMB at 15% margins and two-fifths of segment EBITDA.
  • Strong balance sheets. KPR net cash, Vardhman AA+, Welspun's net debt halved to ₹775 crore through the trough.

The bear case

  • Recovery already priced. Six ex-Alok at ~35× trailing and ~20× EV/EBITDA; Welspun 76×, Indo Count 57.5× need guidance met.
  • Policy risk has changed form, not gone. Section 301 is under legal challenge; IEEPA refunds could trigger buyer claw-backs; no India–US agreement text exists.
  • Towel pricing is weak. Trident's bath EBIT fell to 5.3% even at the 10% tariff; Pakistan competes at the same rate.
  • Cotton policy cuts both ways. MSP up 44% in five seasons; the duty waiver expires on 31 October; crop estimates disagree by 5%.
  • Low returns. Five of six healthy members earned ROCE below 10% in FY26; global peers trade at a fraction of Indian multiples.
  • Company-specific overhangs. Arvind's debt-funded US acquisition; Welspun's promoter sale; Trident's CEO exit; Indo Count's leverage and flood; KPR's flat textile volumes; Alok's ₹26,000 crore of debt.

Weighing the two, the fundamental direction is positive and the valuation cushion is thin. The trade map, the cotton spread and spare capacity all point to better FY27 earnings than FY26, and the Q1 FY27 numbers confirm it. But much of that improvement is already in the share prices of the US-facing exporters, and the tariff that underpins it rests on a statute being contested in court. The companies least dependent on any of these variables, KPR for quality and Vardhman for balance-sheet value, sit at opposite ends of the valuation range. Arvind combines growth and a moderate multiple but has taken on acquisition risk. For Alok, nothing in the bull case matters until its debt is restructured.

In plain terms

The business news for Indian textiles is the best in years: lower trade barriers, better cotton spreads, and plenty of spare factory capacity to fill. The investment case is less clear, because share prices have already risen to reflect much of that, and the American tariff rules could change again.

Two items on the calendar matter most. The first is the cotton duty decision on 31 October: an extension supports spinners' spreads into the new season; a lapse with a large domestic crop is manageable; a lapse with a short crop would squeeze spinners and yarn buyers alike. The second is the Q2 FY27 results season, the first quarter entirely under the 10% Section 301 duty. If Welspun holds a margin of 12.5% or more and Indo Count shows revenue near ₹1,375 crore a quarter despite its flood, the recovery case strengthens; if towel pricing (Trident's bath margin) stays weak and Welspun's margin slips, the high trailing multiples become harder to defend. Engineering exporters face the same US regime; our pumps and engines report covers how it affects them.

In plain terms

Over the next few weeks, watch two things: whether the government keeps imported cotton duty-free after 31 October, and whether the towel and sheet exporters' profit margins hold up in the July–September quarter. After that, the EU trade deal's signature (targeted for December) and KPR's new factory (due in 2027) are the big dates.

Textile reporting mixes agricultural, industrial and trade-law vocabulary. The terms below are the ones a reader needs to follow company presentations and this report.
