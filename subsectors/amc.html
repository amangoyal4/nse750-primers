00

The one-page view: a toll on India's savings that the market has just re-priced

The business is simple, the economics are exceptional, and 2026 showed both sides of it: flows held at records while prices, profits and valuations fell.

An asset management company (AMC) runs mutual funds. It does not lend, it does not underwrite risk and it barely needs capital. It charges a small annual fee on the money it manages, and because that money is pooled into schemes that it already runs, each extra rupee costs it almost nothing to handle. A leading Indian AMC such as HDFC AMC keeps about 79 paise of every rupee of fee revenue as operating profit, pays out most of its earnings as dividends and needs no new equity to grow. That is why five of India's six listed AMCs earn returns on equity above 25%, and why investors have been willing to pay 30–40 times earnings for them.

The fee is a toll on the household's shift from bank deposits and gold into financial assets. India's mutual-fund industry managed ₹82.6 lakh crore at the end of September 2026, about six times what it managed ten years earlier. Monthly systematic investment plan (SIP) inflows reached a record ₹32,490 crore in September 2026 from 10.06 crore contributing accounts. Equity funds took in money for the 67th month in a row. On flows alone, 2026 was the best year the industry has had.

Yet the six listed AMCs in the NSE 750 now trade 18–38% below their 52-week highs, and Canara Robeco AMC sits below its October 2025 issue price. Three things explain the gap between record flows and falling share prices. First, about 57.5% of industry assets are in equity-oriented funds, so when the Nifty fell to 22,780 on 28 September 2026 (roughly 13% below its peak, an approximate figure), the assets on which fees are charged fell with it. Second, AMCs mark their own large investment books to market every quarter, so reported profits swung with the market: HDFC AMC's Q2 FY27 profit rose only 4% and ICICI Prudential AMC's 6%. Third, the supply of AMC shares jumped. SBI Funds Management, the industry leader with about 15% of assets, listed on 21 July 2026, ICICI Prudential AMC had listed in December 2025 and Canara Robeco in October 2025. Investors who had four listed AMCs to choose from in mid-2025 now have seven, and the scarcity premium went with it.

Five forces define this sub-sector

  • Flows are the floor, markets are the multiplier. SIPs give AMCs a growing monthly annuity, but revenue is a percentage of asset value, so a falling market cuts income even when money keeps coming in. Costs are fixed, so profits fall faster than assets.
  • SEBI sets the price, and the April 2026 reset was passed down the chain. The new Base Expense Ratio (BER) regime cut what a scheme may charge. AMCs responded by cutting what they pay distributors, and their own yields went up, not down. The live risk is SEBI's May 2026 consultation on distributor commissions, which could stop that pass-through.
  • The equity-share race sets the valuation. A rupee in an active equity fund earns an AMC four to five times what a rupee in a liquid fund earns. AMCs gaining equity share (Nippon, at a seven-year high) trade at around 41 times earnings; those losing it (UTI, ABSL) trade at 22–28 times.
  • "Total AUM" misleads. UTI reports ₹23.4 lakh crore of group assets but has under ₹1 lakh crore in active equity funds. ABSL's total assets passed ₹10 lakh crore after it won a ₹6.1 lakh crore EPFO mandate that earns almost nothing. Only mutual-fund quarterly average AUM, split by asset class, tells you what pays.
  • Squeezed from below, diluted from above. Passive funds rose from 9.8% to 18.6% of industry assets in five years; app-native AMCs from Zerodha, Groww and Jio BlackRock price close to zero. Meanwhile three sponsor sell-down IPOs in nine months added about ₹21,700 crore of AMC stock to the market.

The reader should come away with one framework. An AMC's value equals the assets it manages, times the fee each rupee earns, times the share of that fee it keeps, times the multiple the market will pay for growth in all three. The first term depends on markets and flows, the second on product mix and SEBI, the third on scale and discipline, and the fourth on whether the AMC is winning market share in equity. In 2026 the first term fell, the second surprisingly rose, the third held, and the fourth compressed across the board. The rest of this report takes each term in turn.

In plain terms

An AMC is a fee collector sitting on top of India's savings. When markets rise and savers keep investing, the fee base grows on its own and almost all the extra fee drops to profit. In 2026 savers kept investing at record levels, but the market fell, so the fee base shrank, profits slowed and the share prices fell harder than the market. Meanwhile three new AMC listings gave investors more choice. Which AMC gains equity share, and whether SEBI lets AMCs keep pushing fee cuts onto distributors, now decides which of these stocks re-rates.

Where this report sits in the library: distributors, wealth managers and registrars, who live on the other side of the same fee, are covered in Other Financial Services; SBI Funds Management is discussed as a subsidiary in PSU Banks; the parents of HDFC AMC and ICICI Prudential AMC are in Private Banks; and the life insurers that share sponsors and bank channels with these AMCs are in Life Insurance.

01

How an AMC earns: a fee on every rupee, every day

The AMC manufactures funds and charges a percentage of assets; distributors, registrars and custodians are paid out of the same pot, but only the AMC's slice reaches its income statement.

A mutual fund in India is a trust. Savers buy units in a scheme; the scheme's money sits with a custodian; a SEBI-registered AMC is appointed as investment manager and paid a fee for running it. That fee, accrued daily as a percentage of the scheme's net assets, is the AMC's "revenue from operations". There is no product to ship, no inventory and no credit risk. If a fund falls 10% the AMC's fee on that fund falls 10%; if the fund doubles, the fee doubles, and the AMC's costs barely move.

The scheme pays more than just the AMC's fee. Every scheme has a Total Expense Ratio (TER), the annual charge deducted from the fund's assets. Out of the TER come the distributor's trail commission (for regular plans), the registrar and transfer agent (CAMS or KFintech), the custodian, the auditor, and, since April 2026, separately disclosed statutory levies and brokerage. What is left is the AMC's management fee. For an equity regular plan with a TER of about 1.6%, roughly ₹55–60 of every ₹100 of TER goes to the distributor, ₹35–38 to the AMC and ₹5–8 to scheme costs and levies. Those splits are approximate and vary by scheme size and commission contract.

Because the distributor is paid from the scheme, not from the AMC's own account, commission costs barely show up in an AMC's income statement. HDFC AMC's "fees and commission" expense is only 1–2% of its operating costs. The exception is ICICI Prudential AMC, whose fees and commission line (₹132.9 crore in Q2 FY27, up 30%) is material, most likely because of its portfolio management and alternatives businesses, which pay distributors from the AMC's own revenue. This matters when you compare margins: ICICI's costs per rupee of assets look higher partly because its revenue is also higher.

Why the business is so profitable

Four features combine. The first is that costs are mostly people and technology, fixed in the short run. A fund manager who runs a ₹10,000 crore scheme can run a ₹50,000 crore scheme with the same team. HDFC AMC's mutual-fund investment team grew from about 30 to about 40 people, a far smaller rise than in the assets it manages. The second is that the AMC needs almost no capital: it must invest a slice of its own money in its schemes (SEBI's "skin in the game" rule) and hold a minimum net worth, but nothing like a bank's capital requirement. The third is that the distributor's pay sits outside the AMC's books. The fourth is the SIP: a saver who sets up a monthly debit keeps investing without being asked, which gives an AMC a stream of new money that does not depend on its sales force in any given month.

The result is a business in which a leader converts 65–80% of fee revenue into core operating profit and 55–70% into net profit, pays out 75–100% of that profit, and still grows. HDFC AMC's FY26 return on equity was 32.9% and its return on capital employed 42.9%, with zero borrowings and ₹9,362 crore of investments on the balance sheet. ICICI Prudential AMC's return on equity was about 85% on a thin equity base after years of high payouts (our primer's figure; screener.in does not yet show it).

In plain terms

The saver pays the fund, the fund pays the distributor and the AMC, and the AMC keeps most of its slice as profit because running an extra rupee costs almost nothing. When a saver switches from a regular plan to a direct plan, the saver and the distributor feel the change; the AMC hardly notices. That is why the AMC is the best-placed link in the chain, and why any regulation that cuts the scheme's total charge first lands on whoever the AMC can push it onto.

There is one structural weakness in the model. Because revenue is a percentage of assets, and because more than half of assets are in equity, the AMC is effectively long the stock market. It cannot hedge that exposure without hedging its own clients. The AMC also carries a second exposure: its own investment book, often several thousand crore of rupees in its own schemes and in bonds, which is marked to market through the profit and loss account every quarter. In a falling market both hit at once. Section 03 shows what that did in 2026.

02

₹82.6 lakh crore and why it keeps growing

Industry assets compounded about 20% a year for a decade, driven by the slow move of household savings out of deposits and gold and by the monthly SIP habit.

India's mutual-fund industry managed ₹82.64 lakh crore of assets at the end of September 2026, measured as closing net assets reported to AMFI. That was down 5% from ₹87.08 lakh crore at the end of August, mostly because companies pulled ₹1.83 lakh crore out of liquid and debt funds before quarter-end, but up 9.3% from a year earlier. At the end of March 2026 the figure was ₹73.73 lakh crore, 12.2% above March 2025. Over ten years, from roughly ₹12.3 lakh crore in March 2016, assets compounded at about 19.6% a year; over five years, from about ₹31.4 lakh crore in March 2021, at about 18.6%. The revenue pool

Multiply average assets of about ₹75 lakh crore in FY26 by a blended AMC yield of 40–42 basis points and the whole industry's fee revenue comes to roughly ₹30,000–32,000 crore a year (an estimate; AMFI does not publish it). What drives demand

Eight forces bring money into funds. Three are structural and slow; three are cyclical; two are institutional and mostly low-fee.

  • Financialisation of household savings. Equity and mutual funds rose from about 2% of household financial savings in FY12 to more than 15% in FY25, while bank deposits fell from about 58% to about 35% (Economic Survey figures as cited in our ICICI primer). Mutual-fund assets are still only about 21% of GDP, against more than 100% in developed markets.
  • The SIP habit. Monthly SIPs of ₹32,490 crore come from 10.06 crore accounts; SIP assets of ₹17.86 lakh crore are 21.6% of the industry. SIP money is sticky and price-insensitive: it kept rising through the 2026 market fall.
  • Investor reach. About 6.2–6.3 crore unique investors (derived from company market-share data, not an AMFI release), growing about 11% a year. Nippon alone reaches 39% of them; HDFC 28%.
  • Smaller towns. The "beyond top-30 cities" (B30) share of industry assets is 18.5%. Members run higher: Canara Robeco 23.6%, Nippon 20.5%, HDFC 19.2%. The extra 30 bp TER once allowed for B30 money was suspended in March 2023.
  • New products. 721 passive schemes, thematic funds, multi-asset funds and the new SIF category keep the shelf growing.
  • Market levels. The amplifier rather than a driver. FY25 was strong, FY26 mixed, and the first half of FY27 a drawdown.
  • Institutional mandates. EPFO and other retirement bodies hand large mandates to AMCs, which inflate "total AUM" but pay very low fees.
  • Corporate treasury money. Liquid and money-market funds swing by ₹1–2 lakh crore around quarter-ends and advance-tax dates; they earn 12–13 bp.

In plain terms

India's fund industry has grown about 20% a year for a decade because households are slowly moving money from deposits and gold into markets, and because tens of millions of people now invest a fixed sum every month. The six listed AMCs in this report manage close to half of it and earn more than half of its fees, because more of their money is in equity, which pays the best fee. The pool is about ₹30,000 crore of fees a year, and every 0.01% change in what funds may charge moves it by about ₹826 crore.

Forecasts are scarcer than history. No consensus industry forecast was found. Broker models assume around 13% a year AUM growth for HDFC AMC over FY26–28, and industry growth of 12–15% a year would put assets near ₹100 lakh crore by about FY28; both should be read as INDICATIVE. The figure that matters more for AMC profits is the mix within that growth. If passive and liquid money grow faster than active equity, industry assets can rise 15% while fee revenue rises 10%. That is the quiet risk behind every headline about record AUM.

03

Flows are the floor, markets are the multiplier

2026 split the two halves of the AMC model apart: savers kept investing at record rates while the market, and with it AMC profits, went the other way.

The single most useful sentence about an Indian AMC is that flows set the floor and markets set the multiplier. Net new money (SIPs, lump sums, less redemptions) adds to assets steadily, in good months and bad. Market movement then multiplies the whole book, up or down, every day. Over a decade the two have compounded together, which is why industry assets grew six-fold. In any single year, though, the multiplier dominates: a 10% move in the Nifty on an equity book of ₹47 lakh crore is a ₹4.7 lakh crore swing in assets, many times what a month of SIPs brings in.

The 2026 numbers show both halves. September 2026 brought record SIP inflows of ₹32,490 crore, equity fund net inflows of ₹38,307 crore (the 67th consecutive positive month) and about 63 lakh new SIP registrations. In the same month the Nifty closed at 22,780 on 28 September, which this report estimates at roughly 13% below its peak of about 26,300 (only the 22,780 close is verified; the peak and the percentage are INDICATIVE). Industry AUM fell 5% in the month, from ₹87.08 lakh crore to ₹82.64 lakh crore. AMC stocks fell further than the index, and earnings growth slowed sharply.

Why profits fall faster than assets

The mechanism is operating leverage. An AMC's costs (salaries, technology, marketing, offices) are mostly fixed over a year. Revenue moves one-for-one with average assets. Is the SIP floor as solid as the headline?

The aggregate SIP number is at a record, but the mix underneath is shifting in three ways. First, cancellations rose: ABSL cited "higher cancellation rates across the industry" in its Q1 FY27 commentary. The industry's SIP stoppage ratio for 2026 was not sourced for this report, so the size of the rise cannot be stated. Second, tickets are getting smaller: HDFC AMC's average SIP ticket fell from about ₹2,800 to ₹2,500 in a quarter. Third, flows are concentrating in the winning schemes. Canara Robeco's monthly SIP collection fell 12% year on year to ₹676 crore and its SIP accounts 8%, ABSL's monthly SIP fell 10% in a quarter to ₹1,090 crore, while Nippon's systematic flows rose 13%.

In plain terms

AMC profits move with the stock market twice over: once because the fee is a percentage of fund values, and again because the AMC's own investments are marked to market every quarter. Savers kept investing in 2026, which stopped assets from falling as fast as the market, but it could not stop profit growth from slowing. When you read an AMC's quarterly result, strip out the treasury income first; otherwise a good quarter can look bad and a bad one good.

The implication for investors is that AMC stocks behave like a geared bet on the equity market with a growth kicker from flows. In a rising market, rising assets, operating leverage and treasury gains all point the same way, and AMC stocks outrun the index. In a falling one, all three reverse together, and flows only cushion the fall. The 2026 de-rating was this mechanism working on the way down, compounded by new listings. It also explains why the trough of the market is usually close to the trough of AMC earnings growth: once the market stabilises, the flow floor reasserts itself within a quarter or two.

A. The scaled active-equity leaders

B. Volume, passive and reach

Nippon Life India is a different machine. It has no parent bank in India, so it built the widest retail reach in the industry through independent distributors and digital channels: 24.1 million unique investors, 39% of the industry total, and 78% of new purchases made digitally. C. The legacy turnarounds

ABSL and UTI both carry legacies that hurt their economics. ABSL was a debt-heavy, corporate-money house that lost equity share for most of the last six years, from about 8.2% in FY20 to 4.06% in FY26. Both have high costs relative to their yield, so their margins (55–60% at ABSL, about 47% at UTI) are well below the leaders'. D. The equity niche

Canara Robeco is the purest equity book in the set: 91% of its ₹1.24 lakh crore QAAUM is in equity-oriented schemes, 86% of its investors are individuals and 23.6% of its assets come from beyond the top 30 cities, thanks to Canara Bank's branch network. Who paid for SEBI's fee reset? Deep dive

SEBI rewrote the fee rules on 1 April 2026 and the AMCs guided to a hit. Two quarters later their yields were higher, because the cut was passed to distributors. The next rule may stop that.

SEBI regulates the price of a mutual fund directly. It caps the Total Expense Ratio a scheme may charge, by asset class and by scheme size, and it decides what may and may not be charged on top. Every few years it tightens the cap, and every time the market asks the same question: how much of the cut will the AMC absorb? The 2026 reset is the largest change to the fee structure since 2018, and the evidence from its first two quarters gives a clear answer that most of the AMC primers in this library did not anticipate.

What changed on 1 April 2026

The SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026 were approved by the SEBI board in December 2025, released in January 2026 and took effect on 1 April 2026, replacing the 1996 regulations. For fees, the central change was to unbundle the old TER into separately disclosed parts. The Base Expense Ratio (BER) is now the charge for managing the money, and it must cover the AMC's management fee, the distributor's commission and the scheme's running costs (registrar, custody, audit). Brokerage, regulatory fees and statutory levies (securities transaction tax, stamp duty, exchange charges and GST) are now shown separately and sit outside the BER. TER is simply the sum.

What the AMCs said would happen

Before the regime took effect, HDFC AMC guided to a gross hit of 3–4 bp on its yield, which it said would be "largely offset". Other AMCs gave similar guidance, and the primers in this library framed the reset as a 3–4 bp headwind. That framing made sense: a cut to the total a scheme may charge has to land somewhere, and the AMC's management fee is one of only two places it can land.

What actually happened

Yields went up. HDFC AMC's revenue yield on QAAUM was 45.4 bp in Q4 FY26, the last quarter of the old regime. It rose to 47.0 bp in Q1 FY27 and 48.3 bp in Q2 FY27. So who paid? The distributors

The phrase that matters is "commission optimisation". The BER caps what the scheme may charge for management plus distribution together. An AMC facing a lower cap can keep its own fee and cut the trail commission it pays distributors, as long as its commission contracts allow it. From 1 April 2026 the large AMCs did exactly that. The evidence from the other side of the chain is consistent: Prudent Corporate Advisory, one of the largest mutual-fund distributors, reported that its commission yield fell 3 bp in a quarter to 57 bp, which it attributed to a GST-related change. The distributor story is told in full in our Other Financial Services report.

There is a mechanical limit on how much of any cut an AMC can pass on. It can only cut commissions on regular-plan assets; direct-plan assets pay no commission, so a lower cap on a direct plan comes straight out of the AMC's fee. At HDFC AMC, direct plans are 32% of equity assets and 44.7% of all assets. So in a stylised world where a cap falls by 3 bp across the board and the AMC passes all it can to distributors, the AMC still absorbs roughly a third of the cut on equity. That is about 1 bp, which is close to the "3–4 bp gross, largely offset" guidance. The sensitivity table below sets the numbers out on a ₹1 lakh crore active equity book.

In plain terms

SEBI lowered what a fund may charge. The AMCs did not take a smaller fee; they paid distributors less instead. Their own fee per rupee actually rose in the first two quarters. The only part of the cut they cannot pass on is on direct plans, where there is no distributor to squeeze, and that part is small. The losers of the April 2026 reset were distributors and brokers, not the fund companies.

The twist: SEBI's May 2026 consultation on commissions

The pass-through works only as long as AMCs are free to set distributor commissions. In May 2026 SEBI issued a consultation paper on distributor commissions. Its final form was not known as of 19 October 2026. If the final rule caps or standardises trail commissions, or fixes the share of the BER that must go to distribution, the AMCs lose the lever they used in April. Any future cut to the BER would then land on the AMC fee. On the six members' combined assets of about ₹38.3 lakh crore, every basis point that lands on the AMC is worth about ₹383 crore of revenue a year, about 2.4% of their FY26 revenue from operations, and almost all of it would come out of operating profit.

There are two other ways yields can still erode. The telescopic slab structure means a scheme's allowable fee falls as it grows, so the most successful schemes earn the least per rupee; ABSL puts this drag at 1–2 bp a year for mix and slabs together. And passive drift, the steady shift of money into index funds and ETFs at under 10 bp, lowers the blended yield without any regulator acting. Neither is new, and both are visible in the quarterly yield line. The commission rule is the one that could change the slope.

A short history of who absorbed each cut

The 2026 pattern has precedent. When SEBI banned entry loads in August 2009, distributors lost their upfront fee overnight; industry assets stagnated for four years and many small distributors left, but AMC margins recovered once trail commissions became the norm. When SEBI cut TER slabs and banned upfront commissions in October 2018 (effective April 2019), AMCs again cut trail rates and their yields fell only modestly. Each time, the AMC used its bargaining power over a fragmented distributor base. The difference now is that SEBI appears to be looking at the commission itself, and that the large distributors (national platforms, banks and wealth firms) are more concentrated and more able to push back than in 2009.

One scheme per category

SEBI's October 2017 categorisation circular allowed each AMC only one scheme in each defined equity category. Before it, AMCs ran several overlapping large-cap or multi-cap funds, often inherited through mergers. The rule forced mergers of overlapping schemes and capped how far an AMC could grow by launching new active funds. It also concentrated assets in fewer, larger schemes, which pushes those schemes into the lower fee slabs. The routes around it are product types the rule does not restrict in the same way: index funds and ETFs, sector and thematic funds, fund-of-funds, and since 2025 the Specialised Investment Fund.

Skin in the game and the treasury book

SEBI requires AMCs to invest a slice of each scheme's assets, set by the scheme's risk level, in the scheme itself, and AMCs seed new funds and AIFs with their own money (ABSL seeds up to 10% of an AIF's size). The intent is to align the manager with the investor. The side effect is that every listed AMC carries a large investment book, ₹700 crore to more than ₹9,000 crore, that is marked to market each quarter. That is the source of the treasury swings in Section 03. It also means an AMC's net cash is not all free cash: part of it is regulatory capital that cannot be paid out.

Cross-holdings: why UTI's sponsors keep selling

SEBI's rule that a sponsor holding 10% or more of one AMC may not hold 10% or more of another bites hardest at UTI. Its four PSU sponsors (SBI, LIC, Bank of Baroda and Punjab National Bank) together hold about 45%, and SBI and LIC also sponsor their own AMCs. They have been reducing their holdings since the 2020 IPO and are legally pushed to continue. T. Rowe Price, the US asset manager, owns about 23%, which screener.in classifies inside "public" as a foreign body corporate rather than as a foreign institutional investor. UTI therefore has no controlling owner, a steady supply of sponsor shares, and a register in which the largest holders are either compelled sellers or a foreign manager whose own business is under pressure. Press reports in August and December 2022 that the Tata group was in talks to buy a majority stake were denied by UTI at the time; no 2026 deal or filing was found for this report, so those reports are history, not a live event.

Minimum public shareholding: ICICI's float

ICICI Prudential AMC listed in December 2025 with only a small float. After the IPO, promoters held 87.59% (ICICI Bank about 53% and Prudential about 34.6%, an estimated split), leaving 12.4% in public hands. Listing rules require 25% public shareholding within a set period; the 2025 amendments gave very large issuers a longer glide path, but the details were not verified for this report. To reach 25%, about 12.6% of the company would need to be sold, worth about ₹17,300 crore at the 19 October 2026 market value (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic). Prudential, which has been reducing its stake since 2025, is the obvious seller. That is a supply overhang that does not depend on the business at all.

In plain terms

Apart from fees, three rules matter most to an AMC shareholder. The skin-in-the-game rule forces AMCs to keep large investment books, which is why their quarterly profits jump around. The cross-holding rule forces UTI's government-owned sponsors to keep selling its shares. The public-float rule means Prudential will probably have to sell several thousand crore of ICICI Prudential AMC shares. None of these changes the business; all of them affect the share price.

The rulebook also explains why AMCs are capital-light in practice but not in theory. Net worth requirements are modest, but skin in the game, seed capital and the need to hold a buffer for expenses above the caps tie up part of each AMC's investment book. When an AMC reports a payout ratio near 100%, it is paying out the earnings while keeping the regulatory capital. UTI's FY26 payout of 127% of profit, on a year when its treasury book lost money, was possible only because profit was temporarily depressed, not because the capital was spare.

The equity-share race: who is winning the rupee that pays Deep dive

Equity assets earn four to five times what liquid assets earn, so the AMC gaining equity share compounds faster and is valued higher. Share momentum, not size, sets the multiple.

If you could know only one number about an AMC, the most useful would be the direction of its share of industry equity assets. Equity is where the fee is. HDFC AMC earns 61 bp on active equity and 13 bp on liquid funds; a rupee won in equity is worth almost five rupees won in liquid. An AMC that gains equity share is therefore growing its most valuable assets faster than the market, and because costs are fixed, that growth drops almost entirely into profit. An AMC that loses equity share can still grow its total assets, through passive, debt or institutional mandates, and look healthy on headline AUM, while its revenue and profit fall behind.

The stock market has priced this precisely. The six members' trailing P/E multiples line up almost exactly with the direction of their share in 2026: Nippon, at a seven-year high in market share, trades at about 41 times; ICICI Prudential and HDFC, large but losing a little equity share this year, at 39 and 34 times; ABSL, whose equity share has halved in six years, at 27.5 times; UTI, losing share fastest, at about 22 times; and Canara Robeco, whose SIP book is shrinking, at about 19 times.

In plain terms

Investors pay up for the AMC that is winning new equity money, not for the one with the most money. Watch two numbers: an AMC's share of industry SIP inflows and its share of industry equity assets. Style fashion can swing new money between AMCs within months, which is why even the leaders' multiples fell in 2026.

Two cautions mark the limits of this framework. Share gains bought with passive or low-fee money do not carry the same value as share gains in active equity; Nippon's overall share gain is partly ETFs earning under 10 bp, which is why its equity share (7.38%) and SIP share (9.84%) are the better test. And share is cyclical by style: the houses winning flows in a mid-cap rally can lose them just as quickly in a large-cap or value phase. The durable advantages are reach (investors and distributors), breadth of the fund range, and a long record across styles. On those, ICICI Prudential, HDFC and Nippon remain well ahead of the other three, whatever the next few months of flow data show.

AUM that pays, and AUM that doesn't Deep dive

Headline "total AUM" adds pension, PMS and passive mandates that earn a few basis points to equity funds that earn sixty. Re-weight the book by what it earns and the league table changes.

AMCs like to report big numbers, and the biggest number available is "total AUM" or "group AUM": every rupee the group manages in any form, including mutual funds, portfolio management mandates, alternative investment funds, offshore funds, advisory mandates and pension money. That number can be many times the mutual-fund book. It is a poor guide to revenue, because the largest additions in recent years have been institutional mandates that pay almost nothing. The rule in this report is simple: compare AMCs only on mutual-fund QAAUM, and within that on active-equity QAAUM.

UTI: ₹23.4 lakh crore that earns like a mid-sized fund house

UTI AMC reports group AUM of about ₹23.4 lakh crore (March 2026), which would make it larger than SBI Funds. Most of it is not mutual-fund money. It includes the NPS assets managed by UTI Pension Fund (about ₹4 lakh crore, roughly 24% of NPS, at fees of a few basis points), EPFO and other retirement mandates, and offshore and advisory money. Its mutual-fund QAAUM is ₹3.93 lakh crore. Within that, its active equity QAAUM is ₹94,567 crore, essentially flat year on year, a 2.60% share of industry equity, down from 3.01% a year earlier. ICICI Prudential is the case where the non-mutual-fund book adds real revenue. It runs about ₹27,000 crore in portfolio management, about ₹17,000 crore in AIFs (plus ICICI Venture's ~₹4,628 crore AIF book transferred in April 2026) and about ₹29,000 crore of advisory mandates, around ₹73,000 crore in all. Alternatives can earn up to about 2% gross. That is why its revenue from operations divided by mutual-fund QAAUM gives about 57.7 bp, while the company's own mutual-fund yield is about 48 bp: non-MF revenue is roughly 15–17% of the total (an estimate). HDFC AMC, by contrast, describes its non-MF revenue as immaterial, and its AIF yields of 80–90 bp come on a small base.

Six decades, five shocks: how the industry got its shape

From a government monopoly to a listed, bank-dominated oligopoly, through a fund crisis, a decade-long distribution winter, a credit shock and two fee resets.

India's fund industry has been reshaped by five shocks, and each one left a mark on today's listed AMCs. Understanding them explains why UTI is promoter-less, why Nippon owns what was Reliance's fund house, why ABSL carries a debt legacy, why the industry is so concentrated, and why every fee change is followed by a fight over distributor pay.

1963–1993: the UTI monopoly

The Unit Trust of India was created by an Act of Parliament in 1963 and launched its flagship US-64 scheme in 1964. For 24 years it was the only mutual fund in the country. Public-sector banks were allowed to set up funds in 1987, when SBI Mutual Fund and Canbank Mutual Fund (later Canara Robeco) were among the first non-UTI funds, and private and foreign AMCs were permitted in 1993. Canara Robeco's lineage as one of the 1987 bank funds is real, but our Canara primer's claim that it is "second only to UTI" in vintage overlooks SBI Mutual Fund, which started the same year.

1994–2003: the joint ventures and the US-64 crisis

The private era produced most of today's large houses. Birla Sun Life (now ABSL) was set up in 1994, Reliance Mutual Fund (now Nippon India) in 1995, the ICICI–Prudential joint venture in 1998 and HDFC Mutual Fund in 1999–2000. SEBI's first Mutual Fund Regulations came in 1996. The defining shock of the period was the US-64 crisis of 2001, when UTI's flagship, which paid guaranteed-looking returns on a portfolio that could not support them, had to be rescued. In 2003 UTI was split into the Specified Undertaking of UTI (SUUTI), which took the guaranteed schemes, and UTI AMC, a commercial fund house owned by four public-sector sponsors. That split is why UTI has no single promoter today. HDFC AMC bought Zurich's Indian fund business the same year.

2009–2014: the post-entry-load winter

SEBI banned entry loads in August 2009, removing the 2.25% upfront charge that had paid distributors. Distribution collapsed, many small distributors left, and industry assets stagnated between roughly ₹6 lakh crore and ₹10 lakh crore for several years. The industry learnt to pay distributors through trail commissions out of the expense ratio instead. SEBI added the B30 incentive in 2012 and mandated direct plans in January 2013. T. Rowe Price bought 26% of UTI AMC in 2010 for about ₹650 crore.

2014–2020: the SIP boom, the credit shock and the fee reset

AMFI's "Mutual Funds Sahi Hai" campaign from 2017 and a long equity bull market brought retail SIP money in volume. Reliance Nippon Life Asset Management listed in November 2017, the first listed pure-play AMC, and HDFC AMC listed in August 2018 at ₹1,100 a share. Then came the second shock: the IL&FS default in September 2018 and exposures to Anil Ambani group and DHFL paper hit debt funds and Reliance MF in particular, leading to Nippon Life's takeover of the AMC in September 2019 and its rebranding to Nippon India. SEBI's October 2018 TER cut and trail-only rule, effective April 2019, was the third shock. Franklin Templeton's wind-up of six debt schemes in April 2020 was the fourth, and it set back retail debt funds for years. UTI AMC listed in October 2020.

2020–2026: the SIP super-cycle, the listing wave and the BER reset

Demat accounts and SIPs exploded after 2020, with monthly SIPs rising from about ₹8,000–9,000 crore to over ₹26,000 crore by 2024 and ₹32,490 crore by September 2026. ABSL listed in October 2021. abrdn exited HDFC AMC in 2022–23, and HDFC Ltd merged into HDFC Bank in July 2023, making the bank the AMC's parent. Debt funds lost indexation in April 2023. Then the fifth shock, which is still playing out: three sponsor sell-down IPOs in nine months (Canara Robeco, ICICI Prudential, SBI Funds), the BER regime from April 2026, and a market correction of roughly 13% (INDICATIVE) that cut AMC stocks by 18–38% even as flows set records.

Reading an AMC: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen numbers explain almost everything about an asset manager. Each one below comes with its formula, a worked example, benchmarks, the six members' figures and the trap that catches people.

An AMC's accounts are short and its business is simple, which makes it easy to read the wrong number with confidence. "AUM" has four meanings, "yield" is computed on different bases, "operating margin" on screener.in is not the operating margin, and reported profit can move opposite to the business in any quarter. This section builds the reading from the bottom up: first the equation that generates profit, then each KPI in turn, then an illustrative AMC P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps in one place, and a ten-minute routine for reading quarterly results.

1. MF QAAUM and market share

What it is. Quarterly average AUM of the mutual-fund business: the average of daily net assets across all schemes in the quarter. Market share is the AMC's QAAUM divided by the industry's, as published by AMFI each quarter. Formula: share = AMC QAAUM ÷ industry QAAUM. Benchmarks: good is above 10% and rising; average is 4–10% and stable; weak is below 4% or falling by more than 25 bp a year. 9. Treasury income and core PAT

What it is. Other income is mostly mark-to-market gains or losses, interest and dividends on the AMC's own investment book. Core PAT is profit after tax excluding it. Formula: core PAT ≈ core operating profit × (1 − tax rate); treasury share = other income ÷ profit before tax. Worked example: HDFC's FY26 other income of ₹500 crore was about 13.5% of its ~₹3,711 crore profit before tax; ABSL's ₹214.5 crore was about 16% of its pre-tax profit; in Q1 FY27 ABSL's ₹162 crore of other income was about 39% of pre-tax profit, which is why reported profit jumped 65% quarter on quarter while core PAT fell. Drivers: the size of the book (₹0.7k–9.4k crore), its equity share, and the market in the quarter. Benchmarks: there is no "good" level; the test is whether you can explain each quarter's swing, and whether core PAT is growing. A treasury share above 20% of pre-tax profit in any quarter means the headline growth number is not the business. Members: see the treasury table in Section 03; UTI's book lost ₹108 crore in Q3 FY26 and swung FY26 profit down 42%. Trap: tax effects travel with treasury income. HDFC's Q2 FY27 PAT included a ₹46.8 crore tax reversal; core PAT growth of 8.5% is the clean figure. Use core P/E (market cap ÷ core PAT) for valuation where available: HDFC trades at about 39 times trailing core PAT of ₹2,592 crore against 34 times reported PAT.

12. Price to AUM, P/E and core P/E

What it is. Three ways the market prices an AMC: market value as a percentage of mutual-fund AUM, market value over trailing profit, and market value over trailing core profit. 13. Return on equity and dividend payout

What it is. Return on equity measures profit against shareholders' funds; payout measures how much of profit is returned as dividends. For a capital-light business the two are linked: high payouts keep equity low and ROE high. Formula: ROE = PAT ÷ average equity; payout = dividend per share × shares ÷ PAT. Worked example: HDFC's FY26 dividend of ₹54 a share on about 42.8 crore shares is about ₹2,310 crore, 81% of its ₹2,858 crore profit; with equity of about ₹9,229 crore its ROE was 32.9%. Drivers: margin, payout policy, the size of the treasury book (which inflates equity) and regulatory capital needs. Benchmarks: ROE of 30% or more is good, 20–30% average, below 15% weak; payout of 75–100% is typical, 40–75% average, below 40% low. Members: ROE ICICI about 85% (primer; screener does not show it), Nippon 34.5%, HDFC 32.9%, Canara Robeco 30.3%, ABSL 25.2%, UTI 10.3%. In plain terms

Valuing an AMC comes down to how much of its money is in equity, how much of each fee rupee it keeps, and whether it is gaining share. A rupee of equity fund money is worth roughly 12 paise of AMC market value at a typical multiple, a rupee of debt or liquid money about 4 paise, and a rupee of index money about 1 paisa. That is why ICICI Prudential and HDFC are valued at 10–12% of their fund assets and UTI at under 3%.

How the KPIs connect: one chain, not thirteen numbers

The thirteen KPIs are not independent; they form a chain, and reading them as a chain is what separates an analyst from a list of ratios. Reach (unique investors, B30, distributor count) drives SIP share. SIP share, sustained over several quarters, drives equity share. Equity share, together with the passive and debt books, sets the equity mix. The mix sets most of the yield. The yield, against a largely fixed cost base, sets the core margin and core operating profit per rupee of assets. Core profit growth, adjusted for the treasury book, drives the multiple. And the multiple, applied to an AUM base that moves with markets, gives P/AUM.

Seen this way, the six members separate cleanly. HDFC is strong at every link except the first two this year: its reach is growing (17.7 million investors, up 22%), but its equity share slipped 30 bp in two quarters as debt shrank and style turned against it, and the market docked its multiple from about 40 times in July to 34 times. Nippon is strong at the first three links and weaker at the fourth and fifth, because ETFs dilute its yield; the market pays for the first three. ICICI is strong at the middle links (mix, yield, margin) but its flow share has halved, the first link to turn. ABSL and UTI are weak at the first three links, so strength further down cannot rescue the multiple. Canara Robeco has a strong mix but weak reach growth and sub-scale costs, so its good fourth link sits between two weak ones.

The chain also tells you which numbers lead and which lag. Flow data (SIP share, net equity flow share, monthly AMFI figures) lead by one to three quarters. Asset share and mix lag by a quarter or two. Yield and margin lag further, and move only a basis point or two at a time unless regulation intervenes. Reported profit is the noisiest and least informative of all in any one quarter. An investor who watches the first link monthly will usually see a re-rating or de-rating coming before it shows up in the results that the market reacts to.

Finally, the chain shows where regulation can break it. A BER cut acts on the yield link; a commission rule acts on the split within the yield; a scheme-categorisation change acts on the product shelf, and therefore on reach and mix. None of them acts on reach itself, which is why the AMCs with the widest investor bases have historically recovered fastest from each fee reset.

Definition traps, collected

  • Four kinds of AUM. MF QAAUM (market-share basis), closing AUM (can differ 5% or more), total or group AUM (inflated by near-zero-fee mandates), and equity AUM (with or without ETFs). Compare only MF QAAUM and active-equity QAAUM.
  • Equity share is not one number. HDFC reports active equity-oriented share; Nippon excludes ETFs; UTI's "70:30" includes ETFs; ICICI reports equity schemes.
  • Yield bases differ. ICICI's company MF yield (~48 bp) versus revenue ÷ MF AUM (57.7 bp); ABSL's yield on management fees; Nippon's primer 37 bp on total AUM versus ~41 bp on MF QAAUM.
  • Screener "Sales" is not revenue from operations. For HDFC, ABSL, UTI and Canara Robeco it includes treasury gains, so screener "OPM" overstates the core margin. Use company revenue from operations.
  • Reported PAT follows the treasury book. Use core PAT and core P/E; check for one-off tax items such as HDFC's ₹46.8 crore reversal in Q2 FY27.
  • SIP versus systematic flows. Some AMCs include STPs from their own liquid funds; folios are not unique investors.
  • Quarter mismatches. In October each year some AMCs have reported the September quarter and others have not; any "industry" aggregate mixes periods.
  • Consolidated versus standalone. UTI's FY26 profit is ₹472 crore consolidated, ₹460 crore standalone and "about ₹404 crore" in our primer; Canara Robeco has only standalone figures on screener.

How to read an AMC's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Pull MF QAAUM and market share (minute 1). Compare with the previous quarter and a year ago. Share up = winning; share flat while equity markets rose = losing in disguise. Red flag: overall share down more than 25 bp a year.
  2. Pull equity QAAUM and equity share (minute 2). Use the company's own basis and compare with its own history. Red flag: equity share down two quarters running.
  3. Check SIP flows and SIP share (minute 3). Is SIP share above equity share? Is the monthly SIP number rising? Does it include STPs? Red flag: SIP collections falling while the industry's rise.
  4. Compute the yield (minute 4). Quarterly revenue from operations × 4 ÷ QAAUM. Compare with last quarter on the same basis. Red flag: a fall of more than 2 bp without a mix explanation, which could mean the commission pass-through is ending.
  5. Compute opex/AUM and the core margin (minute 5). Opex × 4 ÷ QAAUM; core OP ÷ revenue. Note ESOP charges and one-offs (CSR, VRS). Red flag: costs growing faster than revenue for two quarters.
  6. Separate treasury income (minute 6). Other income this quarter versus last and a year ago. Compute core PAT growth. Red flag: headline PAT growth that comes mostly from other income.
  7. Read the tax line (minute 7). Look for reversals or unusual effective rates.
  8. Check the non-MF business (minute 8). PMS, AIF, offshore and pension assets and their revenue contribution. Red flag: a "total AUM" jump with no revenue to match (a mandate effect).
  9. Read the regulatory commentary (minute 9). What management says about BER, commissions, the May 2026 consultation, telescopic slabs and new products such as SIFs.
  10. Price it (minute 10). Update P/AUM and core P/E. Compare with the peer ladder: is the multiple consistent with the share trend you found in steps 1–3?
In plain terms

Read an AMC result in this order: is it gaining share, especially in equity? Is its fee per rupee holding? Are its costs under control? Only then look at profit, and strip out the gains or losses on its own investments before you do. Most market overreactions to AMC results come from reading step 6 before steps 1 to 5.

Why Indian AMCs earn roughly double

  • Higher yields. An Indian active equity fund earns the AMC about 58–67 bp after distribution; US blended fees, heavily weighted to passive, are far lower.
  • Distribution outside the P&L. In India the scheme pays the distributor; many global managers pay distribution and platform fees from their own revenue.
  • A low passive share. Passive is 18.6% of Indian fund assets against roughly half in the US. Every point that shifts lowers blended yields.
  • The SIP annuity. A growing, monthly, automatic inflow from tens of millions of savers, with little equivalent in mature markets where the flows are mostly retirement-plan rollovers and net outflows from active funds.
  • Concentration. The top five Indian AMCs hold over half the assets, and each runs a full shelf at scale.

Why the valuation premium is about growth, not quality alone

Indian AMC P/Es of 19–41 times compare with roughly 10–23 times for listed global managers (INDICATIVE). The premium is mostly a growth premium. Indian industry assets have compounded at about 19–20% a year for a decade; global asset managers' organic growth is in low single digits, with market moves supplying most of their asset growth. Indian margins are higher, but even BlackRock, with the best global franchise, trades at only about 20–23 times. The global experience is the cautionary part. T. Rowe Price, UTI's large shareholder, is the textbook case of an active-equity house losing assets to passive year after year, with its multiple falling to the low teens. That is the path the bear case fears for Indian active managers if passive's share keeps doubling every five years.

Passive, direct and the apps: the squeeze from below

Index funds, direct plans and app-owned AMCs all push fees down. So far they have lowered prices for savers and distributors more than for AMCs; the next phase could be different.

Technology has changed how Indians buy funds faster than it has changed what they buy. App-based investing (Groww, Zerodha Coin, Paytm Money, the AMCs' own apps) has driven the rise of direct plans and of first-time investors, and MF Central, the registrars' joint platform, has made it easier to see and switch holdings across AMCs. Those changes mostly hurt distributors. The threat to AMCs comes from the products the apps favour and the AMCs the apps themselves now own.

1. Passive drift

Passive funds (index funds and ETFs) rose from 9.8% of industry assets in March 2021 to 18.6% in March 2026, with assets of ₹13.73 lakh crore growing to about ₹15.6 lakh crore by July 2026, roughly 25% a year. There are now 721 passive schemes. Index fund expense ratios can be below 10 bp, and the AMC's revenue yield on passive is 6–10 bp against about 60 bp on active equity. Every percentage point of industry assets that moves from active equity to passive therefore cuts industry revenue by roughly ₹400 crore a year on today's base (ILLUSTRATIVE: 1% of ₹82.6 lakh crore × ~50 bp). Passive's growth also strengthens the case for regulating active fees: data showing that many active funds underperform their benchmarks after fees (S&P's SPIVA India reports are widely cited) is the argument SEBI hears most often.

3. The direct-plan shift and the platform toll

Direct plans are 44.7% of HDFC AMC's assets and 43% of ABSL's. For the AMC this has been benign, because the fee per rupee is about the same in both plans and the direct investor pays no commission. But the platforms that bring direct investors (Groww, Zerodha, Paytm Money and others) are now large enough to ask AMCs for "distribution" or "partnership" fees for prominence, data or integration. If that becomes standard, part of the commission saved by direct plans flows back out of the AMC's own revenue, which is what happens at global managers. This is an area to watch rather than a measured trend; no figures were found.

4. New products: SIFs and quantitative strategies

SEBI's Specialised Investment Fund framework (February 2025) lets AMCs run long-short and other strategies inside a mutual-fund wrapper for investors with ₹10 lakh or more. ABSL launched the first, a hybrid long-short fund, and plans two more; HDFC plans two launches in the next two quarters and has hired two senior professionals for the business; one SIF collected ₹377 crore in September 2026. SIFs can carry higher fees, including performance-linked fees now permitted under the BER rules, so they are a new fee pool for AMCs with the talent to run them. They also need different skills, which favours the large houses.

5. Lower switching costs and key-person risk

MF Central, run by the registrars CAMS and KFintech, gives investors a single view of their holdings across AMCs and makes switching easier. Combined with app-based investing, it means flows follow recent performance faster than before, which magnifies style-cycle swings in flow share (Section 08). Key-person risk also remains: a star manager's departure or a long stretch of underperformance in one style can move billions of rupees of flow within months, as HDFC found in 2019–21.

Bull vs bear: a toll road or a fee-compression story?

The bull case rests on the flow annuity and on AMCs' bargaining power; the bear case on passive drift, regulation of commissions, style risk and supply.

The debate about Indian AMCs is not about whether they are good businesses; on returns, capital needs and cash generation they plainly are. It is about two questions. Will the asset base keep compounding at mid-teens rates for another decade, and will the AMCs keep their share of the fee on that base? The bull and bear cases below set out the strongest version of each side for the sub-sector as a whole.

The bull case

  • The flow annuity is intact. Record SIPs (₹32,490 crore a month) and 67 consecutive months of equity inflows through a ~13% market fall show that household financialisation is structural, not cyclical. MF assets are about 21% of GDP against more than 100% in developed markets.
  • AMCs won the fee fight. The April 2026 reset was meant to cut fees; HDFC's yield rose from 45.4 to 48.3 bp and ABSL's held at 43.3 bp. AMCs have shown they can push regulatory cuts onto a fragmented distributor base.
  • Operating leverage works both ways. With costs at 10–20 bp of assets, a market recovery lifts profit faster than assets. Treasury books that hurt in 2026 add back in a rebound.
  • The de-rating has done its work. Multiples have fallen 4–10 turns in four months; the group trades at about 35 times trailing earnings and 9% of AUM, with dividend yields of 2–4.6% at several members.
  • Leaders are widening the moat. Reach (Nippon's 24 million investors, HDFC's 3.2 million additions in a year), new pools (SIFs, AIFs, GIFT City) and scale economies favour the large listed houses.

The bear case

  • Commission regulation ends the pass-through. SEBI's May 2026 consultation could cap or standardise trail; the next BER cut would then land on AMC fees, worth about ₹383 crore of revenue per basis point for the six.
  • Passive drift compresses yields without any regulator. Passive doubled its share to 18.6% in five years; each point that shifts from active costs the industry about ₹400 crore of revenue a year.
  • Market beta is double. Equity is 57.5% of AUM, and treasury books add a second exposure; a 20% fall cuts core operating profit by about 25% at a leader before treasury losses.
  • Share is volatile and the leaders are losing it. ICICI's net-equity-flow share fell from ~19% to ~11% in five months; HDFC's active equity share slipped; mid- and small-cap specialists and SBI Funds are taking flows.
  • Supply keeps coming. ICICI must roughly double its float (~₹17,300 crore at today's price), SBI and Amundi hold ~88% of SBI Funds, UTI's sponsors must sell, and Canara Robeco's promoters hold 75%.

Weighing the two

The bull case is strongest on the long run and on the economics of the leaders. The bear case is strongest on the next 12–18 months and on the weaker members. Both cases agree on more than they appear to. Both accept that flows are structural; they differ on how much of the fee AMCs keep. Both accept that passive is growing; they differ on whether the leaders can collect enough passive volume to offset the yield. And both accept that supply is coming; they differ on whether there are enough domestic buyers (SIP-fed mutual funds themselves among them) to absorb it at current prices.

The test that settles most of it is observable within two or three quarters. If the HDFC, ICICI and ABSL yields hold within 1–2 bp of their Q2 FY27 levels after SEBI's commission decision, and if SIP inflows stay above ₹30,000 crore a month, the bull case on the leaders is largely intact and the remaining question is market levels. If yields fall 3 bp or more without a mix explanation, or SIP inflows break below ₹30,000 crore for consecutive months, the bear case gains its first hard evidence.

The optimists say Indians will keep moving savings into funds for decades and the AMCs have shown they can protect their fees. Two further habits help. First, read every monthly or quarterly number against its own basis: closing AUM against closing AUM, QAAUM against QAAUM, company yield against company yield. Most of the apparent surprises in AMC data come from mixing bases rather than from the business. Second, keep a running note of what each AMC said about yields, commissions and SIP quality in the previous quarter, so that the next result can be checked against it.

If SIPs stay above ₹30,000 crore and yields hold, the business is fine and the share prices will follow the market. If either breaks, the de-rating of 2026 has further to run.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • AMC (asset management company): the SEBI-registered company that manages a mutual fund's schemes and earns a management fee.
  • AUM (assets under management): the value of money managed. Always check which kind: closing, quarterly average, total/group, or equity.
  • QAAUM: quarterly average AUM, the average of daily assets over a quarter; the basis for AMFI market share and for fees.
  • MAAUM: monthly average AUM.
  • AMFI: Association of Mutual Funds in India, the industry body that publishes monthly data and registers distributors.
  • TER (Total Expense Ratio): the annual charge a scheme deducts from its assets; since April 2026 it equals BER plus brokerage plus levies.
  • BER (Base Expense Ratio): the capped charge for managing the money, covering the AMC fee, distributor commission and scheme running costs.
  • Telescopic slabs: fee caps that fall as a scheme's size rises.
  • Trail commission: the annual commission an AMC's scheme pays a distributor on assets the investor keeps; upfront commissions are banned.
  • Direct plan: a version of a scheme with no distributor commission and a lower TER.
  • Regular plan: the version sold through distributors, carrying trail commission.
  • SIP (systematic investment plan): a fixed monthly investment debited automatically.
  • STP (systematic transfer plan): a scheduled transfer from one fund (usually liquid) to another (usually equity) within the same AMC; not new money.
  • B30: assets from investors beyond India's top 30 cities.
  • Unique investors: distinct PANs holding at least one fund; unlike folios, which count accounts.
  • Revenue yield: revenue from operations as a percentage of average AUM, in basis points.
  • Basis point (bp): one hundredth of a percentage point; 58 bp = 0.58% = ₹0.58 per ₹100 a year.
  • Core operating profit: revenue from operations less employee and other operating costs, before treasury income.
  • Core PAT: profit after tax excluding treasury (other) income.
  • Treasury book: the AMC's own investments, including seed capital and skin-in-the-game holdings, marked to market through profit.
  • Skin in the game: SEBI's requirement that an AMC invest a risk-based share of each scheme's assets in the scheme.
  • P/AUM: market capitalisation divided by mutual-fund QAAUM.
  • Passive fund: an index fund or ETF that tracks an index rather than picking stocks.
  • ETF: exchange-traded fund, a passive fund bought and sold on the stock exchange.
  • SIF (Specialised Investment Fund): a SEBI category from 2025 for long-short and similar strategies, with a ₹10 lakh minimum.
  • PMS: portfolio management services, separately managed accounts for wealthy clients or institutions.
  • AIF: alternative investment fund, a private pooled fund (private credit, real estate, private equity, long-short).
  • NPS: National Pension System, regulated by PFRDA; UTI Pension Fund is one of its managers.
  • EPFO: Employees' Provident Fund Organisation, India's largest retirement fund, which hands large low-fee mandates to AMCs.
  • OFS (offer for sale): an IPO in which existing shareholders sell shares and the company raises no new money.
  • MPS (minimum public shareholding): the rule that at least 25% of a listed company's shares must be held by the public.
  • Cross-holding rule: SEBI's bar on a sponsor holding 10% or more of two AMCs.
  • MF Central: the registrars' joint platform for viewing and servicing holdings across AMCs.
  • RTA: registrar and transfer agent (CAMS, KFintech), which keeps investor records for AMCs.
