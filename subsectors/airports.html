00

The one-page view: a flat year that was also the first profitable one

India's airports handled fewer passengers in 2026 than in 2025, yet the largest listed operator posted its first annual profit in over a decade. The reason sits in a regulator's spreadsheet, not in the departure hall.

An Indian airport is a regulated toll gate with a shopping mall and a property business attached. The toll, the money airlines and passengers pay to land, park and pass through, is set every five years by the Airports Economic Regulatory Authority of India (AERA) using a formula that allows the operator a fair return on the assets it has built and no more. The mall, made up of duty free, food, advertising, car parks and cargo, is lightly regulated and is where most of the upside lives. The property business, hotels and offices on airport land, sits largely outside the regulator's reach. Over all three sits the grantor, usually the Airports Authority of India (AAI), which leased the airport to a private operator in return for a share of gross revenue that runs from 4% at Hyderabad to 45.99% at Delhi.

That structure explains the year. All-India airport passengers, as counted by AAI at the terminals, grew only 1.9% to 420.09 million in FY26 and then fell 2.9% in April–September 2026, with international traffic down 7.1% after the West Asia war shut Gulf corridors from 28 February 2026. July, August and September were each down 4.7–5.9%. Yet GMR Airports, which runs Delhi, Hyderabad and Goa's new Mopa airport and since mid-2026 Nagpur and Visakhapatnam's new Bhogapuram airport, grew FY26 revenue from operations 42% to ₹14,807 crore, company EBITDA 47% to ₹6,150 crore, and swung from a ₹817 crore loss to a consolidated profit of ₹472 crore. Its traffic grew 0.9%.

Two things did that, and neither was demand. First, AERA's fourth control-period order for Delhi (CP4), effective 16 April 2025, raised the approved aeronautical yield per passenger from about ₹145 to ₹360; Delhi's aero revenue rose from ₹1,153 crore in FY25 to ₹3,011 crore in FY26. Second, GMR took Delhi duty free in-house from 28 July 2025 and Hyderabad's from 10 September 2025, so the holding company now books gross duty-free sales rather than a concession fee; its standalone revenue jumped from ₹1,263 crore to ₹4,242 crore. One was a regulatory reset, the other a change in who runs the shops.

The industry is a three-way split. By FY26 terminal passengers, computed from AAI's airport-wise data, GMR's three airports handled 27.3% of India's traffic, Adani Airport Holdings' eight airports 22.7%, Fairfax-controlled Bengaluru (BIAL) 10.6% and Cochin (CIAL) 2.7%. Five forces explain almost everything about this industry, and the report is built around them. First, the tariff machine: aero charges equal an allowed return on the regulatory asset base plus operating costs, depreciation and tax, minus 30% of non-aero revenue, reset every five years with true-ups, and fought over at the tribunal (TDSAT) and the Supreme Court. Second, the grantor's cut: the revenue share or per-passenger fee the operator bid to win the concession, which at Delhi takes 42 rupees of every 100 earned. Third, traffic against capacity: a flat 2026 driven by airline problems (see the airlines report) colliding with new airports at Navi Mumbai, Noida and Bhogapuram. Fourth, non-aero and the city side: duty free, food, cargo and Aerocity, where an operator can earn more than the regulated return. Fifth, leverage, ADP and valuation: ₹34,000 crore of net debt, a French co-promoter selling down, and a share price that pays roughly double what Europe pays for airports.

In plain terms

Traffic tells you how busy an airport is. The regulator's order tells you how much each passenger is worth. In 2026 the second mattered far more than the first: Delhi earned roughly two and a half times as much aero revenue per passenger as a year earlier while handling slightly fewer people.

The headline numbers also need care, because airports report on bases that look alike and are not. GMR's "52% EBITDA margin" is on net income, after the revenue share paid to grantors. On total income it is 40.5%, and screener's operating margin, which excludes other income, is 39%. Consolidated profit of ₹472 crore became ₹175 crore for GMR's own shareholders, because AAI owns 26% of Delhi and Hyderabad. GMR's widely quoted "187 million" is airport capacity in million passengers per annum, not passengers handled. And at ~23× EV/EBITDA on the consolidated headline, the stock looks expensive. Strip out AAI's minority share and treat ADP's convertible bonds as the equity they will become, and it trades at about 27× trailing EBITDA. Europe's listed airports trade at 9–12×.

Where does that leave the industry in November 2026? Profitable at the operator level for the first time in years. The newly reset Delhi tariff is in the base, the Hyderabad reset cut prices from 1 September 2026, and the court fights that could add more to Delhi are not expected to pay before FY28. Debt has stopped rising at GMR, but it has not started falling. Ahead lie a privatisation round of 11 more AAI airports, the first listing of Adani's airports, and a recovery in traffic that management guides at only +3–4% for FY27. The rest of this report takes each of those threads apart.

01

How an airport earns: a toll gate, a mall and a landlord in one

Every rupee an Indian airport collects comes from one of three businesses, and each of them is shared with someone before the operator sees it.

Start with who is in the chain. The grantor owns the land and the right to run the airport: AAI for Delhi, Mumbai, Hyderabad and the leased AAI airports, or a state government for Goa's Mopa, Noida, Navi Mumbai, Nagpur and Bhogapuram. The concessionaire builds and runs the airport for 30–60 years: GMR, Adani, Fairfax's BIAL, Zurich Airport at Noida, CIAL in Kerala, or AAI itself at about 125 airports. The economic regulator, AERA, sets aero charges at every airport with more than 3.5 million passengers a year. Its orders can be appealed to TDSAT and then to the Supreme Court. Safety and security sit with DGCA, BCAS and CISF, and policy with the civil aviation ministry.

Then come the three businesses. Aeronautical revenue is what airlines and passengers pay for the airport's core service: landing charges per tonne of aircraft weight, parking per hour, and the User Development Fee (UDF) collected inside every ticket. It is regulated. Non-aeronautical revenue is everything a passenger or airline spends that is not a regulated charge: duty free and retail, food and lounges, advertising, car parks, cargo handling, space rentals and ground-handling concessions. It is unregulated in price, but under India's hybrid till 30% of it is used to lower the aero charges. City-side development (CPD), meaning hotels, offices, hospitals and logistics on airport land, earns upfront premiums and annual rent. At Delhi it counts in gross revenue for the 45.99% share.

Real estate is smaller than often claimed. DIAL's CPD rentals of ₹949 crore plus Mopa's ₹7 crore were about 6.5% of GMR's FY26 revenue from operations, not the ~15% sometimes quoted. The three Indian airports' aero revenue (₹4,954 crore) was roughly a third of consolidated revenue, and non-aero, including the holding company's duty-free and cargo businesses, made up the remaining ~60%.

The single most important fact about where the money goes is that the grantor gets paid first. In FY26 Delhi kept ₹37.7 of EBITDA from every ₹100 of gross income after paying AAI ₹42.2 and running costs of ₹20.1. Hyderabad, a greenfield airport with a 4% revenue share, kept ₹62.6. Mopa, still ramping up and paying 36.99%, kept ₹31.4 and then lost money after depreciation and interest. Opex as a share of revenue differs much less between them than the concession fee does.

In plain terms

An airport operator is a tenant that pays rent as a percentage of its sales. At Delhi the landlord takes about 42% off the top. At Hyderabad it takes 4%. That one clause, signed in 2006 and 2004, explains why Hyderabad makes more profit per passenger than Delhi even though Delhi's passengers spend more.

Below EBITDA come depreciation on the terminals and runways and interest on the debt that built them. Those two lines are large because airports are built ahead of demand and financed mostly with borrowing: at DIAL they took ₹33 of every ₹100 in FY26, and at Mopa they took ₹113. Tax is often nil because past losses are carried forward. So the industry's economics come down to five things: how many passengers come through, what the regulator lets you charge each of them, how much they spend beyond that, how much the grantor takes off the top, and what the debt costs. The rest of the report works through them in that order.

In plain terms

The hybrid till means the regulator counts part of the shop and food profits when it sets landing fees. For every ₹100 of non-aero revenue, ₹30 is used to lower the charges airlines and passengers pay, and the operator keeps the benefit of the other ₹70. Running better shops therefore raises profit, but not one-for-one.

02

420 million and stalling: size, growth and a lost year

India's airport traffic tripled from its COVID trough by FY25, then slowed to 1.9% in FY26 and turned negative in 2026. The forecasters had expected 440–450 million.

AAI counts every passenger who passes through an Indian airport terminal. A domestic trip therefore appears twice, once at the departure airport and once at the arrival airport. On that basis Indian airports handled 420.09 million passengers in FY26: 339.45 million domestic (+1.4%) and 80.64 million international (+4.2%). The airline industry's own count, passengers carried by Indian airlines as reported by DGCA, was about 167 million domestic in the same year, half the AAI figure for the reason just given. Airports live on AAI numbers, and this report uses them throughout.

The long view is less exciting than the "fastest-growing aviation market" label suggests. Pre-COVID FY19 traffic was 344.7 million. FY26 was 420.1 million, a compound growth rate of only about 3.5% a year from FY20, because the rebound years (+63.7% in FY22, +73.3% in FY23) only restored lost ground. Real growth resumed in FY24 (+15.0%) and FY25 (+9.5%), then faded. In 2025 ICRA expected FY26 at 440–450 million; the actual was 420 million, a miss of roughly 5%. Its August 2026 forecast for FY27 is 424–432 million (+1–3%), down from 435–445 million (+4–6%) only five months earlier.

The months tell the story of 2026. January (+4.0%) and February (+0.5%) were normal. In March the West Asia war began, Gulf airspace closed and international traffic collapsed 18.5%. April was −5.4% overall, May briefly positive at +4.5% on summer holidays, and then July to September fell 4.7%, 5.6% and 5.9%. By then the drag had moved from international to domestic: domestic terminal passengers fell 6.0% in August and 6.3% in September. This matches the DGCA series in the airlines report (domestic carried −6.3% and −6.5%), so both counts show a domestic market that shrank as fares rose.

How big is the industry in money? Adding the large operators' FY26 revenue gives an INDICATIVE total: GMR ₹15,201 crore gross income, Adani's airports ₹13,081 crore, BIAL about ₹4,690 crore, CIAL ₹1,220 crore and AAI roughly ₹20,500 crore (an estimate based on FY25), or about ₹54,700 crore. About ₹7,400 crore of AAI's income is revenue share and fees paid by GMR and Adani, so the net industry figure is closer to ₹47,000 crore. GMR alone is about 28% of the gross figure. That is small next to the airlines that use these airports: IndiGo's FY26 revenue alone was ₹84,962 crore. But airport revenue is more stable and far more profitable per rupee.

In plain terms

India has a large population but few people fly: fewer than one trip per person every five years. That is the long-run case for airports. The short-run problem is that in 2026 airlines raised fares sharply and had fewer planes to fly, so fewer people flew. Airports cannot set ticket prices, but they live with the result.

ICRA's airport-sector outlook remained Stable in August 2026, even as its airline outlook was Negative. Its sample of DIAL, BIAL, GHIAL, CIAL and AAI is expected to grow revenue 6–8% in FY27, faster than the +1–3% traffic forecast, because tariffs and non-aero spend keep rising. ICRA expects operating margins near 50% in FY27 and FY28 and sees more than ₹1 lakh crore of airport capex over four to five years. Airport revenue therefore does not follow traffic one for one: regulated yields and spend per passenger carry part of the weight, which is the thread of the next sections.

03

Who flies, and through which gate: demand drivers in 2026

Airport demand is airline supply plus fares plus geography. In 2026 two airlines' problems shaped traffic at every Indian airport, and one hub kept growing.

An airport does not sell tickets, so its demand depends on decisions taken by airlines. India's domestic market is a near-duopoly: in September 2026 IndiGo carried 65.4% of domestic passengers and the Air India group 26.8%, together 92.2%. GMR's annual report shows the effect directly: two customers, each above 10% of revenue, together paid ₹3,447 crore, about 23% of FY26 revenue from operations. They are not named, but they can only be IndiGo and the Air India group. Their decisions to cut routes, ground aircraft and raise fares landed directly in airport traffic in 2026. The airlines report covers those causes in detail. Here we focus on how they show up at the gate.

  • Fares and fuel. IndiGo's yield rose 21% in Q1 FY27 and 34% in Q2 FY27 as jet fuel spiked after the West Asia war. Passengers responded: AAI domestic terminal passengers fell 6.0% in August and 6.3% in September 2026.
  • Airline capacity. Pratt & Whitney engine groundings (about 40 IndiGo aircraft still grounded in January 2026), DGCA's schedule cuts after IndiGo's 3–5 December 2025 meltdown (2,507 cancellations), SpiceJet's collapse to 0.6% share and Air India's widebody reductions all removed seats.
  • The Gulf corridors. International traffic is 19% of India's airport passengers, 24% of GMR's Indian traffic, and heavily tied to Gulf labour and diaspora routes. The war cut international traffic 18.5% in March 2026.
  • Hub formation. Air India's "Easy Connect" transfer strategy and IndiGo's international growth concentrate long-haul connections at Delhi, which entered OAG's 2026 megahub ranking at #28, the only Indian airport in the top 50.
  • Cargo. Freight grew 11.7% in H1 FY27 as cargo diverted from sea and Gulf hubs. Delhi set a record of 1.15 million tonnes in FY26.
  • Spend per passenger. Delhi duty-free spend per departing passenger rose to ₹1,095 in H1 FY27 (+5%) and Hyderabad's to ₹929 (+20%). That is still about a third of what Paris earns per passenger.

The result was a sharp split between airports. Delhi grew 5.0% in April–September 2026 while Mumbai fell 8.2%, Hyderabad 11.7%, Chennai 10.9% and Kolkata 7.3%. Delhi was the only top-five Indian airport with more seats in October 2026 than a year earlier, according to OAG data cited by GMR. Some of Mumbai's fall is a transfer to Adani's new Navi Mumbai airport: the two together grew 1.9%. Hyderabad's fall came from IndiGo's route rationalisation from January 2026 and the airport's Gulf-heavy international mix. GMR's own network therefore moved in two directions at once: its largest airport gained share while its second-largest lost it.

In plain terms

When airlines shrink, they cut the thinner routes first and keep the busy hub flights. That is why Delhi kept growing in 2026 while Hyderabad and Goa shrank: Air India and IndiGo concentrated their best long-haul and connecting flights at Delhi. GMR owns both kinds of airport, so its total barely moved (+0.8% in H1 FY27).

Seasonality adds a predictable pattern on top. Indian airport traffic peaks from October to March, with festivals, winter travel and the December–January visiting-friends-and-relatives season, and again in May. July to September, the monsoon, is the trough. GMR's Q2 FY27 traffic was 8.2% below Q1. Delhi handled 18.1 million passengers in Q2 against 20.4 million in Q1. Management's FY27 guidance of +3–4% assumes H2 traffic about 10% above H1, based on these seasonal patterns and on airline slot filings that it says are already open for booking at Delhi and Hyderabad. Since H1 was +0.8%, the H2 recovery has to do most of the work.

Over a decade, the drivers are the familiar ones: low air-travel penetration (India makes roughly 0.12–0.15 trips per person per year against about 0.5 in China and 2.5 in the US, by IATA and ICAO estimates), rising incomes, and more than 1,500 aircraft on order at IndiGo and Air India, which assume 8–10% annual growth. The risk to the airport case lies in the gap between those order books and what passengers can afford at 2026 fares. The airline industry is expected to lose ₹36,000–38,000 crore in FY27 on ICRA's estimate. Airports are paid per passenger and per landing whether airlines make money or not, but airlines in loss fly fewer routes, and those lost flights are airport revenue foregone.

04

Who owns India's gateways: two platforms, a Canadian investor and the state

Private operators carry about two-thirds of India's air passengers through roughly 14 large airports. The state still owns the network, the land and a slice of almost every private airport.

India's airport map is unusual by global standards. About 125 airports are run by AAI, a statutory corporation that also provides air navigation services, yet the 14 or so large airports leased to private operators carry about 64% of passengers. Within the private segment, two groups dominate. GMR has run Delhi since 2006 and Hyderabad since it opened in 2008, opened Goa's Mopa in January 2023, took over Nagpur on 25 June 2026 and opened Visakhapatnam's new Bhogapuram airport on 17 August 2026. Adani won all six airports offered in the 2019 privatisation round, bought control of Mumbai from GVK in 2020–21, and opened Navi Mumbai on 25 December 2025. Between them they handled half of India's airport passengers in FY26.

In plain terms

AAI plays four roles at once. It runs about 125 airports itself, it is the landlord that leased the big ones to private operators, it collects a share of their revenue, and it owns 13–26% of the companies that run them. When GMR wins a fee dispute against AAI, AAI loses money as landlord and gains a little as shareholder.

AAI's own finances depend heavily on the private airports. It reported a profit of ₹6,522 crore in FY26 against ₹7,233 crore in FY25, on revenue of roughly ₹20,000–21,000 crore (an estimate from FY25 annual-report readings). The six Adani airports leased in 2019 paid it about ₹3,200 crore in FY25, inflated by a one-time ₹2,232 crore "under-recovery" receipt, and Delhi plus Mumbai paid about ₹4,200 crore in revenue share. In FY26 Delhi alone paid ₹3,232 crore, which makes GMR's Delhi concession AAI's largest single source of cash. By one reading of AAI's annual report, 91 of its 122 airports lost about ₹1,600 crore while the other 31 earned ₹2,740 crore. Kolkata and Chennai, the two largest airports AAI still runs, are its most profitable. Both are obvious privatisation candidates.

Competition between airports is limited by geography. Mumbai's two airports are both Adani's. Hyderabad and Bengaluru are the only commercial airports in their cities, and BIAL's concession carries 150-km exclusivity to 2033. Three real contests exist. In Delhi NCR, Noida (Zurich) opened on 15 June 2026 and AAI's Hindon civil enclave grew to 1.13 million passengers in FY26. Noida's 0.21 million passengers in April–September 2026 were a rounding error against Delhi's 38.5 million, so there is no visible cannibalisation yet. In Goa, GMR's Mopa competes with AAI's Dabolim, a Navy airfield, and its share of Goa traffic fell from about 49% in Q4 FY26 to about 42% in April–September 2026. In Visakhapatnam, the old civil enclave closed when Bhogapuram opened, so GMR inherited the city's traffic.

The structure is likely to concentrate further. Private operators held about 64% of passengers in FY26. Navi Mumbai and Noida ramping up, the 11-airport round described in section 06 and the government's stated plan to take PPP airports from 14 to 25 under the National Monetisation Pipeline point to something closer to 70% private by FY30 (INDICATIVE). GMR, Adani and BIAL together already handle 60.6% of national traffic. Whether the next round goes mostly to Adani, mostly to GMR, or to a new entrant such as Vinci will decide whether India ends the decade with a duopoly or a more crowded field. The civil aviation ministry plans to cap how many bundles any one bidder can win, which points to the government preferring the latter.

The tariff machine: how AERA turns an asset base into a price per passenger Deep dive

Every five years a regulator decides what an airport may earn from airlines and passengers. The formula is simple. The fights over its inputs run for a decade and sit at the tribunal and the Supreme Court.

The Airports Economic Regulatory Authority of India was set up under the AERA Act of 2008 and has regulated tariffs since 2009. It covers every "major airport", raised in 2019 from 1.5 million to 3.5 million passengers a year, in five-year control periods (CPs). For each CP the operator files a business plan with capex, traffic, opex and a cost of capital. AERA publishes a consultation paper, takes comments from airlines and others, and issues a tariff order that sets landing, parking and UDF rates for each year of the period. The operator, airlines or passenger groups can appeal to the TDSAT, and the TDSAT's orders can be appealed to the Supreme Court.

The formula

AERA calculates an Aggregate Revenue Requirement (ARR), the total aero revenue the airport may collect over the CP. It is the allowed return on the regulatory asset base, plus depreciation, plus operating costs, plus tax, minus 30% of non-aero revenue under the hybrid till, plus or minus true-ups for past over- or under-recovery. Divide the ARR by forecast passengers and you get the yield per passenger (YPP), the figure every order is summarised by. The individual charges are then set to collect that yield.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the pieces interact. All figures are ILLUSTRATIVE. Suppose an airport's regulatory asset base is ₹20,000 crore and AERA allows a 12% return: ₹2,400 crore. Add depreciation of ₹1,000 crore, operating costs of ₹1,600 crore and tax of ₹200 crore, for ₹5,200 crore. The airport expects ₹4,000 crore of non-aero revenue, so 30% (₹1,200 crore) is deducted, leaving an ARR of ₹4,000 crore a year. With 100 million forecast passengers the yield is ₹400 per passenger. If only 90 million turn up, the airport collects about ₹3,600 crore. To the extent the order puts volume risk on users, the ₹400 crore shortfall is a true-up added to the next period's charges. If the airport sells more duty free than forecast, it keeps 70% of the excess within the period.

In plain terms

The regulator works out what the airport needs to earn a fair return on what it has built, subtracts part of the shop income, and divides by the number of passengers it expects. That number is the price per passenger. Build a new terminal and the asset base grows, so the price rises once the terminal is in use. Fill the airport faster than expected and the price per head falls at the next reset.

Three variables the operator does not control

Three inputs drive almost every fight. The first is the asset base and which assets count. Capex enters the RAB only when AERA accepts it as efficient and, under the newer "put to use" approach, only when the asset is commissioned. At Hyderabad, GMR says AERA's approach adds yield in a step when the ₹13,800 crore expansion completes in about September 2029, rather than smoothing it over the period. The second is the till. A single till (100% of non-aero used to lower aero charges) was AAI's historic model. Dual till (0%) is AERA's default for new PPP airports under its amended 2021 guidelines. The hybrid till (30%) applies at Delhi, Mumbai and Hyderabad. The third is the traffic forecast: a higher forecast spreads the same ARR over more passengers and lowers the yield. In 2026, with traffic falling, operators that accepted optimistic forecasts will recover the gap only through future true-ups.

Delhi CP4: the order that made GMR profitable

Delhi's fourth control period runs from 1 April 2024 to 31 March 2029. AERA's Order No. 20/2024-25, dated 28 March 2025 and effective 16 April 2025, approved a YPP of ₹360, up from ₹145 in CP3, an increase of about 150%. The increase reflected Delhi's Phase 3A capex (the fourth runway, the T1 expansion and an eastern cross taxiway) entering the asset base, plus deficits carried forward from earlier periods. AERA was careful with politics. The domestic UDF, paid by about 80% of Delhi's passengers, stayed at ₹129. International UDF was reset at ₹650 for departing economy passengers, ₹275 for arrivals and ₹810 for business class, and landing charges rose. Delhi's realised aero yield went from about ₹145 in FY25 to ₹383 in FY26 and ₹406 in H1 FY27.

The pattern since 2023 is clear. The tribunal keeps ruling for operators, and AERA keeps appealing to the Supreme Court and deferring implementation. In plain terms

Winning at the tribunal is not the same as getting paid. The regulator has a right of appeal and uses it, so a favourable ruling turns into cash only when the Supreme Court decides, often years later. The tariff an airport is actually collecting today matters far more than the one a tribunal says it deserves.

The regulator also intervenes outside the five-year cycle. AERA's Order 01/2026-27 of 7 April 2026 cut domestic landing and parking charges at all major airports by 25% for three months as West Asia relief for airlines, with the under-recovery to be trued up later. It shows a regulator that weighs airline health and fares alongside airport returns. That is why the domestic UDF at Delhi stayed at ₹129 even as the yield more than doubled, and why Hyderabad's passenger charges came down. Over the next decade the direction of travel looks like this: returns on new assets protected, passenger-facing charges held down, and more of the operator's upside pushed into non-aero, where the regulator takes only 30%.

The grantor's cut: concessions and the privatisation map Deep dive

Bidders win Indian airports by promising the government a share of future revenue. Those promises, made in 2006, 2019 and 2026, decide more of an airport's profit than any manager can.

An Indian airport concession has two money terms that matter. The concession fee is what the operator promised the grantor to win the airport: a percentage of gross revenue in the older deals, a fixed fee per passenger in the newer ones. The term runs 30 years plus a 30-year extension at Delhi and Mumbai, and up to 50–60 years at newer airports. Both were set by competitive bidding at the time of award and cannot be changed by the regulator. AERA can allow a concession fee as a cost in the tariff only where the concession says so, and at Delhi it does not. The fee is therefore a straight deduction from the operator's profit.

From percentages to per-passenger fees

The 2019 round changed the model. AAI offered six airports (Ahmedabad, Jaipur, Lucknow, Thiruvananthapuram, Guwahati and Mangaluru) on 50-year leases with bids expressed as a fee per domestic passenger. Adani won all six with fees of ₹177, ₹174, ₹171, ₹168, ₹160 and ₹115 respectively. AAI had collected about ₹2,032 crore of per-passenger fees by June 2024. The per-passenger model gives the grantor a cleaner, traffic-linked income that does not depend on how the operator classifies revenue. It leaves the operator all the upside from non-aero spend and real estate, but full exposure to traffic. Six ways to run an airport: the business-model archetypes

An airport's age, its concession vintage and who granted it decide its economics more than its size does. India has six distinct models, and GMR owns examples of four.

Grouping India's airports by business model, not by operator, explains why per-passenger profits range from about ₹240 to ₹735. The brownfield mega-hub (Delhi, Mumbai) has the most traffic and the richest non-aero spend but pays the highest revenue share. The 2000s greenfield (Hyderabad, Bengaluru) pays almost nothing to the grantor and earns the best margins, but relies more on regulated aero revenue and so is more exposed to tariff cuts. The new-generation greenfield (Mopa, Navi Mumbai, Noida, Bhogapuram) must ramp traffic against a heavy fee and large depreciation and interest, so it loses money for years. The leased AAI brownfield (Adani's six) pays a per-passenger fee and must invest to expand. AAI's own network cross-subsidises loss-making regional airports. And the platform business, duty free, cargo and food run by the airport group itself, is a newer model that GMR adopted in 2025.

Three new airports and a terminal conversion opened in the 2025–26 window. Traffic fell. Airports are 30–60 year assets, but each new terminal starts with depreciation and interest from its first day.

India built a lot of airport capacity in 2025 and 2026. Why build into a downturn? Because airports are planned five to ten years ahead and concessions often require capacity triggers. Mumbai's single-runway system was full, so Navi Mumbai was overdue. Noida was awarded in 2019 and Bhogapuram in 2020, long before anyone expected a war in the Gulf. The economics of a new airport are a J-curve: depreciation and interest start from the commercial operations date, when the asset is capitalised and interest stops being added to the project cost, while traffic, retail and real estate take years to build. GMR's Q2 FY27 results show it clearly. Bhogapuram earned about ₹42 crore of revenue in its first 46 days and lost ₹82 crore, and GMR's capital work in progress fell ₹3,573 crore between March and September 2026 as the airport moved onto the fixed-asset register. Group interest and depreciation rose. Adani's numbers show the same effect at about five times the scale: its airports swung from a pre-tax profit of ₹303 crore in H1 FY26 to a loss of ₹484 crore in H1 FY27 as Navi Mumbai's costs arrived.

In plain terms

A new airport is expensive from its first day and profitable only years later. Interest that was quietly added to the construction cost before opening starts hitting the profit and loss account on opening day, while passengers, shops and offices arrive gradually. That is why both GMR's and Adani's profits fell in 2026 even as their operating income rose.

Does the 2026 dip change the long-run value of these assets much? Less than the headlines suggest, but it matters in two ways. First, the regulator sets yields by dividing an asset base by forecast traffic. Lower traffic forecasts at the next reset raise the yield per passenger, partly protecting the operator, while past shortfalls are recovered only through true-ups where the order allows them. Second, at greenfield airports on per-passenger fees or with large loans, the first few years of traffic decide whether the project can service its debt without new equity. Navi Mumbai and Noida both opened at a fraction of capacity, and Bhogapuram inherited only the old airport's roughly 3 million passengers.

Delhi is the exception that shows why scale matters. At about 74% utilisation, with 106 million passengers a year of capacity against 78.7 million handled, it has room to grow without new capex until it approaches 90 million. Management has said it plans no airport expansion or new-airport capex, only operational capex of ₹1,000–1,500 crore a year. That is the right posture for a group whose debt has stopped rising but not yet started falling. Hyderabad's ₹13,800 crore expansion, which AERA will add to the asset base when it is put to use, is the large exception, and its timing will be one of the decisions to watch.

For the industry the clear lesson of 2026 is that capacity is a lagging bet on traffic and the regulator decides who carries the risk. Airlines kept fares high and capacity tight in 2026, while airports added gates. If traffic recovers to the 8–10% growth that airline order books assume, today's new capacity will look prudent by FY30. If it settles at 3–5%, the newest greenfields will take longer to reach break-even and the grantors who wrote per-passenger fees into their concessions will wait longer for meaningful payments.

Delhi's open questions

Four things will decide DIAL's next five years. Platform, not landlord: non-aero, duty free and Aerocity Deep dive

Since 2025 GMR has stopped renting its terminals to retailers and started running the shops itself. It is the biggest strategic change in Indian airports since privatisation, and it moves profit away from the regulator, the grantor and AAI's minority stake.

For two decades an Indian airport operator was a landlord to its shops. Duty-free operators, food chains and advertising agencies paid the airport a concession fee, usually a percentage of sales with a minimum guarantee, and the airport booked that fee as non-aero revenue. GMR changed the model in 2025. From 28 July 2025 at Delhi and 10 September 2025 at Hyderabad, GMR Airports Ltd (GAL), the listed parent, runs duty free itself, buying stock and booking gross sales. It also runs Delhi's Cargo Terminal 1 under a concession to 2036 plus 30 years, car parks and retail. Its 70% subsidiary GMR Hospitality has won Delhi T3's food and beverage concession to May 2036, consolidated from Q3 FY27. Management calls this the "GAL Platform".

Why insource? Follow the leakages

The logic is about where the margin lands. When an outside operator ran Delhi duty free, it kept the retail margin, and DIAL received a concession fee. Three parties then took a slice of that fee before GMR's shareholders saw it: AAI took 45.99% as revenue share, the regulator used 30% of it to lower aero charges under the hybrid till, and AAI's 26% minority took its share of what was left. Now GAL, 100% owned by the listed company, keeps the retail margin. It still pays DIAL a concession fee, so AAI still collects its revenue share on that fee, but the operating profit of the shop sits outside DIAL. Each rupee of retail margin that moves from a third party to GAL reaches GMR's shareholders with fewer deductions on the way.

In plain terms

Before 2025 GMR was the landlord of Delhi's duty-free shop and collected rent. Now it runs the shop and keeps the shopkeeper's profit too. The rent still goes to the airport company, which still pays AAI its share, but the shopkeeper's margin no longer passes through the airport company's revenue share and minority stake.

The cost is visible in the accounts. Material costs, mainly duty-free stock, tripled from ₹316 crore in FY25 to ₹982 crore in FY26. Platform businesses earn retail margins, not landlord margins: management puts the consolidated non-aero EBITDA margin at about 20%, and Delhi Duty Free earned ₹219 crore of EBITDA on ₹1,144 crore of income in H1 FY27, after paying its concession fee. GMR's group EBITDA margin on net income has drifted from 53% to 49% partly for this reason, as well as because of Bhogapuram's start-up costs. Revenue grows much faster than profit when a landlord becomes a retailer, and the margin percentage falls even as rupee profit rises.

Thirty years, five resets: the history that shaped the industry

Airport traffic cycles are short. Airport profit cycles are set by regulatory resets and concession changes that come every five years or so, and the industry's history is a sequence of them.

Until the early 2000s every major Indian airport was run by AAI, formed in 1995 from the merger of the International Airports Authority and the National Airports Authority. Under-investment was chronic and terminals were congested. Privatisation came in two forms. Greenfield concessions at Hyderabad (GMR) and Bengaluru (a Siemens, Unique Zurich and L&T consortium) were signed in 2004 and opened in 2008 on 4% revenue shares. The 2006 OMDA concessions handed Delhi to a GMR-led consortium with Fraport and Malaysia Airports, and Mumbai to GVK with ACSA and Bidvest, with AAI keeping 26% of each and taking 45.99% and 38.7% of revenue. AERA was created in 2009 and its first control period triggered a tariff war that is still in court seventeen years later.

Five resets stand out. The first, privatisation in 2004–06, set the concession terms that still decide margins. The second, AERA's creation in 2009, turned airport pricing into a quasi-judicial process with appeals. In plain terms

Airport profits jump when a new tariff order or contract structure takes effect, not gradually as traffic grows. To predict an airport's profit, start with the dates of its next tariff reset and its open court cases, then look at passenger numbers.

The lesson for anyone valuing the industry: the cycle that matters is regulatory. Delhi's next reset, CP5, begins in FY30. Hyderabad's runs to March 2031. Bhogapuram's first proper order is due around January 2027. Bengaluru's CP4 order was still awaited in late 2026. In between, true-ups, tribunal awards and Supreme Court rulings can add or remove large sums. Traffic shocks such as COVID or the 2026 war hurt for a few quarters. The regulatory calendar decides the step changes in profit.

Reading an airport: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain almost everything about an Indian airport operator. Each has a formula, a benchmark and a trap, and several are defined differently by different companies.

Airport accounts look like any other company's, but the numbers that matter sit in investor decks, regulatory orders and footnotes rather than the income statement. This section explains each native KPI in turn: what it is, how it is calculated, a worked example, what moves it, what good looks like, how GMR and its reference peers compare, and the definition trap to avoid. It then puts the pieces together in an ILLUSTRATIVE anatomy of one airport-year, a benchmark scorecard, a list of traps and a ten-minute checklist for reading quarterly results.

The equation

An airport's profit can be written as one chain. Passengers multiplied by what each is worth (regulated aero yield, plus non-aero income, plus city-side rent) gives gross income. Subtract the grantor's revenue share to get net income, subtract operating costs to get EBITDA, then subtract depreciation, interest and tax. The operator controls some of these terms and not others. It controls opex, non-aero spend and real-estate leasing. It influences traffic through airline marketing and incentives. It does not control the aero yield, which AERA sets, or the revenue share, which was fixed when the concession was bid.

A worked example with round numbers, all ILLUSTRATIVE: an airport handles 100 million passengers. Each brings ₹400 of aero revenue, ₹500 of non-aero and ₹100 of city-side rent, so gross income is ₹10,000 crore. Depreciation of ₹1,300 crore and interest of ₹2,000 crore leave ₹700 crore before tax. In plain terms

Passengers times what each is worth gives you the top line. The grantor's cut and the operating costs come off before EBITDA, and the cost of the debt comes off after. Of those four steps, management controls only the costs and part of the spending per passenger.

1. Passengers and capacity utilisation

What it is. Terminal passengers, arrivals plus departures, on AAI's basis, and the ratio of passengers to the terminal's rated capacity in million passengers per annum (mppa). Formula: utilisation = annual passengers ÷ capacity. Example: Delhi handled 78.7 million against 106 mppa of capacity in FY26, so utilisation was 74%. Drivers: airline capacity and fares, the economy, the Gulf corridors, hub strategy and new competing airports push it up or down. New terminals cut utilisation overnight. Benchmark: 70–90% is good, with headroom but no congestion. 50–70% or 90–100% is average. Below 50% (new airports) or above 100% (queues and slot caps) is weak. Comparison: Delhi ~74%, Hyderabad ~90% in FY26 and ~80% annualising H1 FY27, Mopa ~70%, Nagpur ~104%, Navi Mumbai ~27% and Noida ~3–4% annualised.

In plain terms

Yield per passenger is the regulated price. Where it is above what the regulator approved, the extra usually comes back off future charges. Where it is below, the shortfall can usually be recovered later. Either way the regulated return over a full control period is what counts, not any one quarter.

5. Regulatory asset base (RAB) and fair rate of return

What it is. The RAB is the depreciated value of the aero assets AERA accepts. The fair rate of return (FRoR) is the post-tax weighted average cost of capital AERA allows on it. Formula: return allowed = RAB × FRoR; RAB grows with approved capex put to use and shrinks with depreciation. Example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a ₹20,000 crore RAB at a 12% FRoR earns a ₹2,400 crore return before costs, about ₹240 of yield per passenger at 100 million passengers. Drivers: capex entering the base (Delhi's Phase 3A in CP4; Hyderabad's ₹13,800 crore expansion around September 2029), AERA's view of efficient capex, the cost of equity it allows (historically about 14–16%) and tribunal rulings on the opening base (the Delhi/Mumbai CP1 hypothetical RAB case). Benchmark: an FRoR at or above the operator's actual cost of capital is good. GMR's own impairment tests use discount rates of 12.0–12.5%. Comparison: AERA's RAB and FRoR for Delhi CP4 and Hyderabad CP4 could not be sourced (n/a); the regulator's site was unavailable.

Why does the RAB matter so much to valuation? Because over a control period aero revenue converges on the allowed return on it, so the RAB sets a floor under an airport's regulated cash flows. An airport that builds a terminal efficiently and gets it accepted into the base earns a near-certain return on that capex, which is why operators push to have capex approved before construction and argue about every asset AERA excludes. The flip side is that the RAB depreciates: without new investment the allowed return shrinks each year, and so does the aero yield.

9. Duty-free spend per passenger (SPP)

What it is. Duty-free sales divided by departing passengers. Formula: SPP = duty-free sales ÷ departing passengers, or penetration × average transaction value. Example (ILLUSTRATIVE): if 14% of departing passengers buy and the average basket is about ₹7,800, SPP is about ₹1,095, Delhi's H1 FY27 level. Drivers: the international share of traffic, store size and location, pricing against downtown and online, product mix (liquor, tobacco, beauty), digital pre-order and allowance rules. Benchmark: above ₹1,000 is good for India, ₹600–1,000 average, below ₹600 weak. Comparison: Delhi ₹1,095 (+5%), Hyderabad ₹929 (+20%). Paris earns about ₹3,230 per passenger across all retail (INDICATIVE conversion of ~€31).

The trap. Some operators divide by all passengers, some by departing passengers only, and some by international departing passengers only. The denominator can change the figure by a factor of four. GMR's SPP divides by departing passengers. Also check whether "duty free" includes arrival duty free and specialty retail.

10. EBITDA margin: on gross income or on net income

What it is. EBITDA as a percentage of income, where the choice of denominator changes the answer by ten points or more. Formula: margin on net income = EBITDA ÷ (gross income − revenue share), GMR's basis. Margin on gross = EBITDA ÷ gross income. Screener's OPM = operating profit excluding other income ÷ revenue from operations. Example: GMR FY26 EBITDA of ₹6,150 crore is 52% of net income (₹11,757 crore) but 40.5% of gross income (₹15,201 crore). Screener's OPM is 39% (₹5,757 crore ÷ ₹14,807 crore). Drivers: the revenue share, the mix of low-margin platform businesses, start-up costs at new airports, and tariff resets. Benchmark: on net income, above 60% is good, 45–60% average, below 45% weak. Comparison: DIAL and GHIAL 65% of net, group 49–52%; Aena 58.3% of revenue (9M 2026), BIAL ~70%.

14. Contracted real-estate lease book

What it is. Future minimum lease rentals receivable under non-cancellable operating leases, disclosed in the notes to the accounts. Formula: sum of contracted rent by maturity band, nominal and undiscounted. Example: GMR's FY26 annual report shows ₹1,027 crore within a year, ₹4,310 crore in one to five years and ₹34,170 crore beyond five years, about ₹39,500 crore in total. Drivers: new leases signed (Aerocity One at ₹270 per sq ft per month), lease tenor (up to 42 years), renewals and escalations, offset by rent recognised each year. Benchmark: a book many times annual city-side revenue signals locked-in income; a growing book is good. We know of no published benchmark. Comparison: DIAL's CPD rentals of ₹949 crore in FY26 against the ₹39,500 crore book, about 42 years of current rent. Other Indian operators do not disclose comparable figures (n/a).

The trap. The book is nominal: ₹34,170 crore received over decades is worth far less in present value. Part of every rupee of Delhi city-side rent goes to AAI through the 45.99% revenue share. Upfront premiums already received are not in it. The figure covers the whole group, not only Delhi, though Aerocity dominates.

The lease book still deserves a place in any valuation, because it is the most predictable cash stream an airport has. Aero revenue depends on a regulator and non-aero on passengers' spending, while contracted rent depends mainly on tenants staying solvent. Management's plan to sell completed buildings to a REIT or to long-term funds would turn part of this book into cash up front, which could fund deleveraging at the holding company.

17. Definition traps, collected

Most mistakes in reading airport companies come from comparing numbers that look alike but are built differently. The list below collects the traps flagged above in one place.

  • Three layers of revenue. Gross income, revenue share and net income. GMR's margins are on net income; screener's OPM is on revenue from operations and excludes other income. ₹6,150 crore of EBITDA is 52%, 40.5% or (screener's ₹5,757 crore) 39%, depending on basis.
  • AAI vs DGCA passengers. AAI's 420 million counts each domestic trip at both ends; DGCA's domestic carried count was about 167 million.
  • Capacity is not traffic. "187 million" is GMR's capacity in mppa; passengers were 121.6 million (114.6 million in India).
  • Consolidated vs attributable profit. FY26 consolidated PAT ₹472 crore, owners ₹175 crore; Q2 FY27 consolidated ₹65 crore, owners −₹15 crore.
  • Intercompany duty-free fees. Summing SPV revenue share overstates the group's by about ₹1,197 crore in FY26.
  • Hybrid till is not dual till. 30% of non-aero revenue lowers aero charges; the operator does not keep all non-aero profit.
  • Aero is a step function. Delhi's aero revenue rose 161% in FY26 on a regulatory reset, not organic growth.
  • UDF is not yield. Delhi's ₹129 domestic UDF coexists with a ₹383 aero yield.
  • CGF classification. Cargo, ground handling and fuel farm are aero at Hyderabad and non-aero at Delhi.
  • Non-aero IPP is GMR's own construct, inflated in FY26 by gross duty-free sales; Adani's ₹961 is a different measure.
  • Net debt excludes FCCBs held by ADP (₹2,910 crore), which are equity in substance; screener's gross borrowings include them and leases.
  • Medan lags a quarter, Cebu is no longer owned, and Crete (21.64%) and Medan (49%) are not consolidated line by line.
  • Headline vs effective revenue share. Delhi's 45.99% is about 42% of gross after the 2022 arbitral exclusions.
  • Ramp-up interest. Interest is capitalised until a new airport opens, then hits the P&L in full, as at Bhogapuram from 17 August 2026.

18. How to read an airport company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Passengers by airport, YoY and QoQ (one minute). Check the investor deck's traffic page against AAI's monthly data. Flag any airport down more than 5% and ask whether the cause is airlines (route cuts), geography (Gulf exposure) or competition (a new airport nearby). Remember Q2 (Jul–Sep) is the seasonal trough.
  2. Aero yield per passenger (one minute). Compare it with the approved yield in the current tariff order. A big jump without a new order usually means a true-up, a parking windfall or a mix shift, and will reverse. Red flag: management annualising a quarter's yield.
  3. Non-aero income per passenger and duty-free SPP (one minute). Look for 7–8% underlying spend growth plus penetration gains. Separate one-offs such as insourcing, new stores and terminal openings.
  4. Gross income, revenue share and net income (one minute). Check that revenue share moved in line with gross income. If it fell faster, ask whether a dispute was settled or the base redefined.
  5. EBITDA and its margin, on a stated basis (one minute). Recompute the margin on gross income. A falling margin with rising rupee EBITDA usually means lower-margin platform revenue; a falling margin with flat EBITDA usually means new-airport start-up costs.
  6. The per-airport P&L (one minute). Read Delhi, Hyderabad, Mopa and the new airports separately. A group number can hide a strong Delhi and a weak Hyderabad, as in Q2 FY27.
  7. Interest and depreciation (one minute). A step-up usually means an asset has opened and capitalisation has stopped. Ask for the run-rate and when it stabilises.
  8. Consolidated vs attributable PAT (one minute). Read the minority line. Rising consolidated profit with falling owners' profit means the profitable airports are minority-shared and the loss-makers are 100% owned.
  9. Net debt, cash and refinancing (one minute). Check net debt by entity, whether cash is temporarily inflated by pre-funding, the cost of new debt against the debt it replaced, rating actions and the next bond maturity.
  10. Regulatory and legal update plus guidance (one minute). New tariff orders, TDSAT or Supreme Court rulings, AERA appeals, AAI fee disputes, and management's traffic, non-aero and capex guidance. Red flags: quantified benefits from rulings still under appeal, or traffic guidance that needs an implausible second half.
In plain terms

For an airport, start with passengers by airport and the regulated yield, then follow the money down through the grantor's share, costs, interest and minorities to what shareholders keep. Most surprises sit in the yield, the minority line or a court ruling, not in the passenger count.

Who is winning: scale, quality and balance sheets

On scale GMR leads India, on per-passenger quality Bengaluru leads, on growth pipeline Adani leads, and on balance sheet the Europeans lead. The maps show where each stands.

"Winning" in airports means three things: controlling the busiest gateways for decades, earning a high return per passenger on them, and funding growth without overstretching the balance sheet. No Indian operator leads on all three. GMR controls the country's busiest and only growing hub, but pays the most to its grantor. Adani has caught up in earnings and has the larger pipeline in Navi Mumbai, but its profit before tax has turned negative as that airport's costs arrive. BIAL earns the best margins and per-passenger profits on a 4%-type concession with AAA-rated debt, but it is a single airport. CIAL is debt-free and highly profitable on a small base.

The first map makes the duopoly visible. GMR and Adani sit together in the top-right quadrant, with GMR ahead on passengers (114.6 million against 95.3 million) and Adani slightly ahead on EBITDA per passenger (₹566 against ₹537). Their trailing EBITDA to September 2026 was almost identical, about ₹6,500 crore each. Inside GMR the spread is wide: Hyderabad near the top, Delhi below the midline because of the revenue share, and Mopa still ramping up in the bottom-left. BIAL, on fewer passengers, earns more per head than any of them.

The second map puts GMR in the global context. The two best-run listed European operators, Aena and Zurich, sit top-left with leverage of 1.5–2.2× and margins of 56–58%. GMR is towards the bottom-right with Fraport, both carrying more than 5× EBITDA in net debt after large expansion programmes. The difference is partly structural: Aena and Zurich own their main airports outright, while GMR pays 42% of Delhi's revenue to a grantor and lets AAI keep 26% of the equity. Better refinancing and Delhi dividends to the holding company would move GMR left. Higher non-aero margins and lower revenue-share leakage would move it up. Management's target of 4–4.5× would still leave it to the right of ADP.

Four comparisons stand out. Scale: Aena's nine-month revenue alone is more than three times GMR's annual revenue, and its EBITDA about five times GMR's. GMR's market cap of ₹1.06 lakh crore is below ADP's (~₹1.20 lakh crore) and about a quarter of Aena's (~₹4.50 lakh crore). Valuation: GMR trades at roughly twice the European multiple on the headline and closer to two and a half times on the look-through basis.

The map makes an awkward point. In 2026 GMR's traffic grew less than Aena's or Zurich's: +0.8% in H1 FY27 against +4.1% for Aena's group and +6% at Zurich. GMR's multiple is not paying for current growth. It pays for the expectation that India's airport traffic, after its 2026 pause, returns to the 8–10% a year that airline order books assume, against the 1–4% that European operators guide to, and that GMR's non-aero and real-estate businesses compound on top. The private Indian market seems to share that view: Adani's pre-IPO round valued its airports at a similar multiple. Fairfax's own DCF of Bengaluru, at about 10–11×, does not.

In plain terms

European airports are mature businesses that grow a few per cent a year, so investors pay about ten times their annual EBITDA. Indian airports might grow two or three times as fast for many years, so investors pay more. In 2026 GMR's traffic barely grew, so its higher price rests on what investors expect in future, not on the current year.

Regulation differs too, and the differences cut both ways. Britain regulates Heathrow on a single till, the toughest model for operators. France has applied a dual till at Paris since 2011, and Spain at Aena since 2017 under the DORA framework, so commercial income stays entirely with the operator. India's 30% hybrid sits in between. European regulators are also pressing charges down: Zurich cut airport charges about 10% from 1 October 2026 and Aena's DORA III for 2027–31 was approved on 15 September 2026. AERA's Hyderabad order and its April 2026 landing-charge relief are part of the same global trend of regulators putting passengers and airlines first, even where operators have invested heavily.

Two links between the global peers and India matter directly. ADP is selling down its GMR stake to cut its own leverage from 3.7× to 3.1×, a reminder that the French state's priorities, not GMR's, drive that decision. And Zurich's Noida airport is EBITDA-negative in 2026 and targets break-even in 2027, which shows how long even an experienced global operator expects an Indian greenfield to take. For GMR the global lens offers both a warning (Fraport's leverage and low multiple after a long build) and an aspiration (Aena's margins and balance sheet once the build is done).

The fine print: minorities, disputes and two big customers

Several things that matter to GMR's shareholders sit outside the headline numbers: AAI's minority stakes, fee disputes worth more than a year's profit, negative book equity and a dependence on two airlines.

Start with the minority interest. AAI owns 26% of DIAL and of GHIAL, GMR's two profitable airports. GMR owns 100% of Mopa, Bhogapuram and Nagpur, two of which lose money, subject to NIIF's convertible debentures at Mopa and Bhogapuram. In FY26, consolidated PAT of ₹472 crore split into ₹175 crore for owners and ₹297 crore for minorities. Other comprehensive income was −₹136 crore, so total comprehensive income to owners was only ₹24 crore. In H1 FY27, ₹213 crore of consolidated profit became ₹76 crore for owners, and in Q2 FY27 a ₹65 crore consolidated profit was a ₹15 crore loss for owners. Until the new airports turn profitable, owners' profit will grow more slowly than the headline.

Technology and disruption: what could change the airport business

Airports face no import competition and little direct substitution, but technology is changing how passengers move through terminals, where long-haul flights depart from, and how shops sell.

The biggest structural threat to a hub airport is the aircraft that makes hubs unnecessary. Narrowbody long-range jets such as the A321XLR, first delivered to an Indian airline in January 2026, have the range to link Indian tier-2 cities directly with the Gulf, South-East Asia and parts of Europe. Point-to-point international flights from Lucknow, Pune or Kochi would bypass Delhi and Mumbai. So far the opposite has happened: Air India's "Easy Connect" and IndiGo's international push have concentrated connections at Delhi, which reached #28 in OAG's 2026 megahub ranking, and GMR's airports carry 34–35% of India's international passengers. But the long-run risk to hub transfer traffic is real.

  • High-speed rail and expressways. The Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed line, under construction, will compete on a 500-km corridor. Expressways shift short trips to road. Neither threatens Delhi's long-haul role.
  • Climate and ESG. DIAL and GHIAL hold Airport Carbon Accreditation Level 5, the highest, and GHIAL's energy mix was 89% renewable in 2024. Sustainable aviation fuel mandates (1% blending for international flights by 2027) fall mostly on airlines but can add to fuel-farm and storage requirements.

  • MRO opportunity. The engine-maintenance crunch from Pratt & Whitney and CFM LEAP shop visits is an opening. GMR Aero Technic has signed with StandardAero to become an authorised on-wing engine service centre for Asia-Pacific.

Technology also changes how regulators think about capacity. Faster processing lets an existing terminal handle more passengers, which can delay the capex that would enter the regulatory asset base, a mixed blessing for an operator paid a return on assets. Dynamic pricing of aircraft parking at Delhi, which produced the 2026 over-recovery, shows how data-driven charging can raise revenue and, in turn, draw the regulator's attention.

In plain terms

New long-range narrowbody jets could one day let more Indians fly abroad directly from smaller cities instead of changing at Delhi or Mumbai. For now airlines are concentrating flights at Delhi, which helps GMR. Meanwhile faster security and boarding mean fewer staff, and more time for passengers to spend in the shops.

None of these forces is likely to change the industry's economics in the next three years as much as the tariff calendar or the traffic recovery will. Over ten to twenty years, the risk to watch is the A321XLR's effect on hub transfer traffic and Gulf competition, since Dubai, Doha and Abu Dhabi already compete with Delhi for India–Europe and India–US connections, and Indian carriers' share of India's international traffic was only about 43% in FY26. The opportunity is that a well-run Delhi hub keeps more of that traffic in India, with more non-aero spend per connecting passenger.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian airports

The bull case rests on decades of traffic growth, regulated returns and an expanding commercial business. The bear case rests on the regulator, the grantor, the debt and the price.

The two cases below are written for the sub-sector as a whole, with GMR as the listed way to own it. Both are reasonable readings of the same facts. The difference is mostly about how fast traffic recovers, how much the regulator and courts give back, and how quickly debt falls.

Bull case

  • Long runway. India makes about 0.12–0.15 air trips per person a year against about 0.5 in China. Airline order books of more than 1,500 aircraft assume 8–10% annual growth once fares normalise.
  • Regulated floor. Aero revenue earns a return on the asset base set by formula, with true-ups for traffic shortfalls. Delhi's CP4 runs to FY29 at an approved ₹360 per passenger.
  • Court upside. TDSAT has ruled for DIAL on CP1, CP2/CP3 and CP4. If the Supreme Court agrees, true-ups flow from FY28; none is in the numbers.
  • Commercial compounding. Non-aero guided to grow 15–17% a year; duty-free penetration of only 11–14%; spend per passenger about a third of Paris's; a platform model that keeps the retailer's margin.
  • Real estate. ₹39,500 crore of contracted rent, a 1 mn sq ft Aerocity building leasing at ₹270/sq ft/month, hotels opening, and a REIT or asset-sale route to cash.
  • Cheaper money. Investment-grade GHIAL, upgraded DIAL and GAL ratings; every 100 bp off ~₹40,000 crore of debt adds ~₹400 crore of pre-tax profit (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Debt peaked. No expansion capex; Bhogapuram's final tariff (~Jan-2027) and Delhi dividends should start reducing leverage toward 4–4.5×.

Bear case

  • Traffic stall. India's airport traffic fell 2.9% in H1 FY27; ICRA cut its FY27 forecast by 3 points in five months; two financially stressed airlines decide most of the schedule.
  • Regulator tilt. AERA cut Hyderabad's UDF, held Delhi's domestic UDF at ₹129, imposed a 25% landing-charge cut for three months and appeals every tribunal loss. Delhi's parking windfall will be clawed back.
  • The grantor's cut. Delhi pays ~42% of gross revenue to AAI forever; new concessions require ₹300–600 per passenger; the 2026 round may be bid away.
  • Minority leak. AAI owns 26% of the profitable airports; GMR bears 100% of new-airport losses; owners' profit was −₹15 crore in Q2 FY27.
  • Leverage. Net debt flat at ₹34,000 crore for a year (5.3×); holding-company interest cover ~1.1×; ₹1,500 crore bullet in Feb-2027 and ₹4,400 crore in Aug-2028.
  • Disputes. ₹1,758 crore of unprovided COVID-period fees could become payable to AAI.
  • Price. ~23× consolidated and ~27× look-through EBITDA against 9–12× in Europe and ~10–11× for Fairfax's BIAL mark; ADP sold at ~26% below market.

Where do the two cases meet? Both agree the industry's long-run demand is strong and the 2026 dip is cyclical. They disagree about who captures the value of that growth. The bull case assumes the operator keeps most of it through non-aero, real estate and regulated returns on new assets. The bear case assumes the regulator, the grantor and AAI's minority take most of it, and that the share price already discounts more than the operator will keep. The evidence over the next 12–18 months will come from four places: the Supreme Court's rulings on Delhi, Bhogapuram's final tariff, the size of winning bids in the 11-airport round, and whether GMR's net debt actually starts to fall.
