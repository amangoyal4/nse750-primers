00

The one-page view: an industry that just survived its own boom

Indian microfinance grew 25% in a year, lost 27% of its book in seven quarters, and by mid-2026 had asset quality back at pre-crisis levels. The cycle is the business.

Microfinance in India means one thing above all: a collateral-free loan of ₹30,000 to ₹1,00,000 to a woman from a low-income household, usually made through a group of five to ten women who meet every week or fortnight and vouch for one another. About 6 crore women held such loans in August 2026, owing ₹3.2–3.3 lakh crore. The lenders are a mix of non-bank microfinance companies (NBFC-MFIs), commercial banks, small finance banks (SFBs) that began life as microfinance companies, and other NBFCs. The borrowers use the money for cows and goats, a tailoring machine, a small shop's stock, school fees or simply to smooth the lumpy income of a farm-labour household.

The industry is defined by a cycle that repeats every few years. Loans grow fast because the product is simple and yields 20–24%; lenders pile into the same villages; borrowers take a third and fourth loan to repay the first; then something breaks: a heatwave, an election promise of loan waivers, a state law, a funding freeze. The 2024–26 cycle was the worst since Andhra Pradesh in 2010. The industry portfolio, as tracked by the Microfinance Industry Network (MFIN), peaked at ₹4,33,697 crore in March 2024, fell for seven straight quarters to ₹3,14,728 crore in December 2025 (−27.4%), and has since edged up to ₹3,28,708 crore in June 2026, still 24% below the peak. Loans overdue 31–180 days (PAR 31–180) went from 2.2% in March 2024 to 6.3% in March 2025 and back down to 1.6% in June 2026.

The listed lens for this report is CreditAccess Grameen (CAGL), the only microfinance pure-play in the NSE 750 and India's largest NBFC-MFI. It holds 7.3% of the industry and 16.8% of NBFC-MFI loans (March 2026, company figures) and is the clearest case study of what separates the survivors from the casualties. In FY25, the worst year, CAGL earned ₹531 crore while its four listed NBFC-MFI peers lost ₹2,297 crore between them. In Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) it reported its highest-ever quarterly profit, ₹502 crore, with a 23.2% return on equity. And yet its share price fell 35% between late July and early November 2026, in two legs that coincided with the next set of worries: a proposed cap on insurance commissions and a weak monsoon in its home states (late September), then Q2 results that brought a cut in lending rates and drought-related restructuring requests in Maharashtra (late October). The timing points to these causes; no single one is confirmed.

The eight forces that shape the sub-sector

  • 1. The credit cycle. Growth and losses run 12–18 months out of phase. Industry lending grew 24.5% in FY24; PAR tripled in FY25; the book then shrank for seven quarters. Every number in this report should be read by asking "where in the cycle is this?"
  • 2. Over-indebtedness and the guardrails. The cause of the bust was too many lenders per borrower. The industry's self-regulator, MFIN, capped lenders at four (from August 2024) and then three (from April 2025), and capped each borrower's total microfinance plus unsecured retail debt at ₹2 lakh.
  • 3. Funding liquidity. Banks both fund NBFC-MFIs and compete with them. When banks stopped lending in FY25–26 the book kept shrinking long after delinquencies fell; funding to NBFC-MFIs then jumped 91% year on year in Q1 FY27.
  • 4. State politics. Karnataka (Act 15 of 2025), Tamil Nadu (Act 40 of 2025) and Bihar (a bill passed in February 2026) all legislated against coercive recovery. The risk is a collective change in willingness to repay, which no single lender can diversify away inside a state.
  • 5. Regulatory loosening. The RBI's 2022 framework freed pricing; in June 2025 it cut the share of assets an NBFC-MFI must hold in microfinance from 75% to 60%; in February 2025 it partly reversed the 2023 risk-weight increase on bank loans to NBFCs.
  • 6. The weak 2026 monsoon. Rainfall was 87% of the long-period average, the fourth lowest since 2001, with the South Peninsula at 76%: a risk concentrated in CAGL's states rather than across the industry.
  • 7. Rates and margins. The RBI raised the repo rate from 5.25% to 5.50% on 7 October 2026. CAGL's cost of funds was 9.1% and its net interest margin a record 15.1% in Q2 FY27; it has started cutting lending rates by 50 basis points.
  • 8. The structural shift. Share is moving from banks to NBFC-MFIs (banks 33.2% → 25.3% of the industry; NBFC-MFIs 39.4% → 44.3%, March 2024 to June 2026), and borrowers are graduating from group loans to individual and secured loans. A proposed insurance-commission cap (2–2.5% on credit-life) threatens a fee line worth about 25–30 basis points of CAGL's return on assets.

Three conclusions run through the report. First, through-cycle credit cost, not yield, decides who wins. Yields across lenders sit within a few points of each other; the difference between CAGL's FY25 credit cost of 7.7% of AUM and the 15–25% that wiped out peers' equity was where they lent, to whom and how many other lenders those borrowers had. Second, funding is the transmission mechanism: credit problems became an industry contraction because bank lines froze, and growth restarted only when they thawed. Third, the industry is turning into rural retail finance. Group loans are flat or shrinking at the best lenders, while individual business loans, secured loans and housing grow, enabled by the 60% qualifying-asset rule. That shift changes what "microfinance growth" means and how a microfinance lender should be valued.

In plain terms

Microfinance is a high-yield, high-frequency lending business whose losses arrive in waves. In a calm year a good lender earns 4–6% on its assets and more than 20% on its equity; in a bad year it can lose a year or two of profit, and a weak one can lose half its capital. The industry has just come through a bad wave. The best lender is earning record profits; the question for investors is how soon, and how hard, the next wave arrives.

The report ends with technology, the bull and bear cases, what to watch and a glossary.

01

How a ₹50,000 loan earns: the product and the value chain

A group loan to a woman, repaid weekly at a village meeting, funded by bank loans and bonds, checked against a credit bureau: every link in the chain is a place where profit is made or lost.

The core product is the joint-liability group (JLG) income-generation loan. A lender's loan officer forms a group of five to ten women from the same village, who know each other and agree to stand behind each other's repayments. Several groups make a centre (in CAGL's language a kendra), which meets weekly or fortnightly at a fixed time. The loan has no collateral, no guarantor beyond the group, and a short tenure of one to three years, repaid in small equal instalments. At CAGL the income-generation loan runs from ₹5,000 to ₹2,00,000 at 18.00–23.75% a year over one to three years, and smaller life-cycle loans (home improvement, water and sanitation, education, medical, festival and emergency) of ₹1,000–50,000 run 11–104 weeks.

Ticket sizes have grown steadily. NBFC-MFIs disbursed an average of ₹45,024 per loan account in FY24. MFIN's August 2026 data put the average loan size across the industry at ₹62,427, and CAGL's average outstanding per group-loan borrower was ₹62,300 in September 2026, up from ₹55,500 a year earlier. Part of that is inflation and part is the guardrails: with fewer lenders allowed per borrower, the remaining lenders lend larger amounts to the borrowers they keep, which MFIN describes as "a degree of consolidation around existing borrowers".

Value is captured at Step 2 by the lender with the lowest cost of funds, the lowest cost to serve and, above all, the lowest losses through the cycle. Steps 3 and 5 are where losses are prevented; Step 1 is where a crisis is transmitted.

Around the core loan sit three other product families. The first is the self-help group (SHG) bank-linkage model, NABARD's parallel system in which banks lend to groups of 10–20 women, often with state interest subvention. It is large: 55.6 lakh SHGs received bank credit in 2024-25 and fresh loans totalled ₹2,08,283 crore, with roughly ₹3 lakh crore outstanding (secondary summaries of NABARD's Status of Microfinance report, indicative). These loans are not in MFIN's ₹3.29 lakh crore and are a subsidised, state-driven channel rather than a commercial one. The second family is graduation products: individual business loans, secured business loans against property, affordable housing, two-wheeler and digital personal loans. CAGL's ranges show how prices fall as security rises: individual business loans ₹1,000–3,00,000 at 18–24%; secured business loans ₹3–20 lakh at 16–23.5%; affordable housing ₹2–24 lakh at 13–19.5%. The third is fee and insurance income: processing fees and the commission earned for selling credit-life insurance, the only two charges MFIN allows lenders to deduct from the loan amount.

Banks play two roles at once, and that dual role is the most important structural fact in the chain. They are the largest lenders to NBFC-MFIs (bank term loans were 61.5% of CAGL's borrowings in September 2026) and a large direct competitor (25–33% of the industry portfolio over 2024–26), lending either through their own branches or through business correspondents (BCs) such as IndusInd's Bharat Financial Inclusion. Bank lending to NBFC-MFIs for on-lending counts towards priority-sector targets, which is a structural reason banks fund the sector. When banks pull back, NBFC-MFIs feel it twice: first as lost wholesale funding, later as less competition for borrowers.

Why does a 20–24% interest rate exist, and why do borrowers pay it? The cost of serving a ₹50,000 loan repaid in 50–100 weekly instalments is high: CAGL's operating expenses run at about 5% of its loan book every year, against about 1–2% for a bank's mortgage book. Funding costs NBFC-MFIs 9–12%. Expected losses through the cycle run at 3–4% a year. Add those up and a lender needs a yield in the low 20s simply to earn a reasonable return. For the borrower, the comparison is not with a bank loan she cannot get but with the informal moneylender. The MFIN–NCAER borrower survey (2024-25) found only 1% of microfinance borrowers also borrowed informally, against 46% in 2011, and an average borrower's repayment-to-income ratio of 18.7%.

In plain terms

A microfinance lender is a retail bank without deposits, running a weekly door-to-door collection service. The interest rate looks high because the loan is small, the instalments are many and the collection is done in person. What makes the business work is that the group meets every week, so missed payments are seen within days and the borrower's neighbours are part of the enforcement.

Where is value captured? Not at the lender with the highest yield. CAGL's group-loan rates top out at 23.75%, a little below many peers. The dispersion that matters is in credit cost (CAGL's FY25 credit cost was 7.7% of AUM while Fusion's and Spandana's losses equalled 55% and 33% of equity), in opex (CAGL has held opex at 4.5–5.1% of AUM for a decade while sub-scale peers run 6–11%), and in funding cost (CAGL's 9.1% against Spandana's 12.3%). The second structural advantage is distribution: CAGL's 2,326 branches and roughly 15,000 loan officers visit more than four lakh villages every week, and that same network can sell individual and secured loans to the borrowers it already knows, at almost no extra customer-acquisition cost.

02

₹3.3 lakh crore, 6 crore women: how big the market is

The industry's book is back to roughly its March 2023 size, but it now serves about 1.7 crore fewer borrowers than at the peak, through far fewer loans per borrower.

MFIN's Micrometer is the industry's standard yardstick. It counts the gross loan portfolio (owned plus managed, i.e. including loans sold through securitisation or direct assignment) reported to the microfinance credit bureau by all regulated lenders. On that basis the industry grew from ₹3,48,339 crore in March 2023 to its peak of ₹4,33,697 crore in March 2024, a gain of 24.5% in a single year, with 7.8 crore unique borrowers and 14.9 crore loan accounts. FY24 disbursements were ₹3,35,473 crore through 7.26 crore loans. Then the book shrank every quarter until December 2025.

Fewer borrowers, fewer loans each

The most striking number in the industry is the borrower count. MFIN's August 2026 note gave 6.06 crore unique borrowers and 9.27 crore loan accounts, against 7.8 crore and 14.9 crore at the March 2024 peak. That is about 1.7 crore fewer borrowers and 5.6 crore fewer accounts. The figure usually quoted, that "about 50 lakh clients have lost access to formal finance" (MFIN, September and December 2025), was an older and narrower estimate of exclusion; the net fall in the bureau count is roughly three times larger. Some of it is reclassification (loans moved to the retail bureau) and some is the end of multi-lending: accounts per borrower fell from about 1.9 to about 1.5 (derived). But a large number of low-income women who had microfinance loans in 2024 no longer do.

Who lends: the lender-type split

At the peak, NBFC-MFIs held 39.4% of the industry (₹1,70,903 crore), banks 33.2% (₹1,44,022 crore), SFBs 17.1% (₹74,278 crore), other NBFCs 9.3% and not-for-profit MFIs 0.9%. By June 2026 NBFC-MFIs had 44.3% and banks 25.3%. The Jun-26 split of the remaining 30.4% between SFBs and NBFCs is not published; CAGL's annual report, using bureau data, gives March 2026 shares of NBFC-MFIs 43.7%, banks 26.4%, SFBs 15.6% and NBFCs plus others 14.3%. One caution: MFIN's own Q4 FY26 release printed the banks' March 2026 share as 32.7%, which does not reconcile with its December 2025 (26.7%) or June 2026 (25.3%, "slightly lower than … the previous quarter") figures, nor with the banks' reported −30% year-on-year change. We treat 32.7% as a misprint and use 26.4%.

Where the loans are

East and North-East India hold the largest regional share, 36.6% in March and June 2026, with the South second. The South's share is the clearest marker of the boom and bust: it rose from 28% in March 2023 to 31% in March 2024, when it had almost caught up with the East, and has since fallen (−1.4 percentage points year on year to June 2026). That is the Karnataka and Tamil Nadu story in one series. The three largest states by portfolio are Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, and the top ten states hold about 83% of the industry (June 2026). Bihar alone had about 1 crore clients and ₹48,569 crore outstanding from RBI-regulated lenders in February 2026, about 15% of the industry.

How large could the market be? CAGL estimates the broader rural and semi-urban "micro-retail" credit pool, including gold, personal, consumer and business loans to the same households, at about ₹70 lakh crore (March 2026, its own and CRIF estimates), of which classic microfinance is a small slice. It also cites government data on about 23.5 crore low- and middle-income households as the addressable base by 2030. Those are company estimates and should be read as direction rather than forecast. The more useful sizing fact is that 46% of CAGL's customers borrow only from microfinance lenders, while 43% also hold business loans, 30% gold loans, 21% personal loans and 17% consumer loans elsewhere (CRIF data in CAGL's deck). The microfinance borrower is now a multi-product retail borrower, which is precisely why MFIN brought unsecured retail loans inside the ₹2 lakh cap.

In plain terms

The market is about the size it was in early 2023, but it is shaped differently: fewer women, larger loans, fewer lenders each, and a bigger share held by specialist microfinance companies than by banks. Headline "industry growth" figures now understate lending to these households, because some loans have been relabelled as retail and some borrowers have graduated to individual loans that sit outside the microfinance count.

03

Who borrows, and why they repay

Demand is the easy part: rural women want working capital and there is little else on offer. Repayment depends on wages, weather, migration and the social glue of the weekly meeting.

The typical borrower is a woman in a rural or semi-urban household whose income comes from several sources: farm labour, a few animals, a small trade, remittances from a migrant husband or son. The MFIN–NCAER survey (2024-25) put the average borrowing household's income at ₹25,844 a month, the average loan at ₹51,222 over 23 months, and the share of borrowers holding credit-linked insurance at 84.4%. The RBI's 2022 rules cap eligibility at a household income of ₹3 lakh a year and cap total loan repayments at 50% of monthly household income. MFIN argued in September 2026 that the ₹3 lakh threshold, unchanged since 2022, "merits review" because incomes and prices have risen: as household incomes cross the line, good borrowers drop out of the definition.

The demand drivers

  • Rural livelihoods. Loans fund livestock, petty trade, tailoring and farm-allied activity, but repayment depends on wages and remittances. That is why monsoons, heatwaves and floods drive delinquency: CAGL reported a temporary PAR rise in August–September 2026 from flooding in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh, and a "minor increase in monthly PAR accretion" from lower wage income and migration in drought-hit areas.
  • Women's work. CAGL cites India's female workforce participation rising from about 24% (FY18) to about 46% (FY24). More women with their own income strengthens both demand and repayment capacity.
  • New-to-credit customers. About 35% of CAGL's new borrowers each quarter have no previous credit history (2.3 lakh new borrowers in Q2 FY27, 4.9 lakh in H1). Microfinance remains the entry point to formal credit.
  • Graduation. Roughly 6–8% of CAGL's customers a year move up to individual products. Their credit histories make them attractive to banks, gold lenders and fintechs too.
  • Pent-up demand after the credit crunch. MFIN said in March 2026 that the good 2025 monsoon was "creating pent up demand", and in September 2026 that "a slowdown in demand does not appear to be a concern". Supply, not demand, has been the constraint.

The competitive set for the borrower's wallet has widened. Gold loans, digital personal loans and consumer-durable finance reached the same households during the 2021–24 credit boom. A borrower who held three microfinance loans could also hold a phone-app loan and a gold loan, none of which the microfinance bureau saw. That is the root of the over-indebtedness that MFIN's guardrails now attack, and why the ₹2 lakh cap includes unsecured retail loans.

Why women repay: the mechanics of joint liability

Joint liability works through information and social pressure rather than through law. Group members screen each other before the loan (nobody wants to guarantee an unreliable neighbour), see each other every week, and know quickly when a household is in trouble. The weekly meeting makes the loan officer's job one of continuous monitoring rather than occasional recovery. It also makes repayment a public act, which is why "external incitement" (political or activist calls not to repay) is so damaging: it removes the social cost of default for a whole village at once. MFIN's diagnosis of FY25 named exactly that, together with heatwaves, over-leveraging and the Karnataka issue.

Repayment has two failure modes, and they behave differently. Inability to pay follows income shocks: drought, floods, illness, the loss of a migrant's wages. It tends to be local and temporary, and lenders manage it with rescheduling and by pausing new loans in the affected district. Unwillingness to pay follows political or social shocks: loan-waiver promises, coercion allegations, state laws. It spreads across all lenders in a state, does not respond to rescheduling and can persist for years, as Andhra Pradesh showed after 2010. Over-indebtedness links the two: a borrower servicing four loans is pushed from inability into unwillingness by a small shock.

In plain terms

Borrowers repay because the loan matters to them, the group watches, and there is usually a next, larger loan to look forward to. Each of those three supports can fail: incomes fall in a drought, the group's pressure stops working when politicians or activists say repayment is optional, and the promise of a next loan disappears when the lender itself runs short of funding. The 2024–26 crisis knocked out all three at once in some states.

Demand for the product is therefore not the investor's main worry. Analysts on CAGL's October 2026 call asked about the opposite problem: customers who want to borrow but cannot be renewed because they have hit the ₹2 lakh cap or the three-lender limit. "Many have hit the limit," the chief executive said. CAGL wants to add more than 1 lakh new customers a month (about 80,000 now) partly to replace the roughly 3 lakh it wrote off in the past 12 months. Supply of credit, the guardrails and the definition of who counts as a microfinance borrower are what bind.

Funding: the biggest single cost

NBFC-MFIs cannot take deposits, so they borrow wholesale. CAGL's September 2026 mix was bank term loans and cash credit 61.5%, foreign ECBs 21.2%, securitisation and direct assignment 5.8%, financial-institution term loans 3.1%, private NCDs 2.9%, foreign NCDs 2.2%, NBFC term loans 1.9% and public NCDs 1.4%. NBFC-MFIs "generally maintain 10–15% of their total assets in cash and liquid investments" (MFIN); CAGL's ₹3,502 crore was 10.2% of assets in September 2026. Microfinance is labour-intensive. In Q2 FY27, ₹254.5 crore of CAGL's ₹381.6 crore of operating expenses including depreciation (67%) was staff cost. Seven quarters down: anatomy of the 2024–26 crisis Deep dive

A heatwave, an election, a fourth lender, a state ordinance and a funding freeze, in that order. The credit problem was solved by mid-2025; the book kept shrinking until the money came back.

The 2024–26 crisis is the defining event for every company in this report, and it is worth reconstructing quarter by quarter, because the order of events explains why the damage was so uneven and why the recovery took so long. It had three overlapping causes, all named in MFIN's own releases: over-indebtedness, a funding freeze, and state politics. Its symptoms arrived in sequence: first delinquency, then lender caution, then shrinkage, then recovery in quality while the book still shrank.

Phase 1: the boom (FY23–FY24)

After the RBI's March 2022 framework freed pricing and harmonised rules across lenders, microfinance grew at 20–30% a year. Industry portfolio rose 24.5% in FY24 to ₹4,33,697 crore; CAGL's yield rose from 18.3% (FY22) to 20.9% (FY24); Fusion (November 2022) and Muthoot Microfin (December 2023) listed. The South's share of the industry rose from 28% to 31%. Multi-lending deepened: CRISIL found that by December 2024 about 23% of borrowers had four or more microfinance lenders. Meanwhile, the same households took gold loans, phone-app personal loans and consumer finance that the microfinance bureau did not see. In November 2023 the RBI raised risk weights on bank lending to NBFCs and on consumer credit, an early signal that the regulator saw unsecured lending running hot.

Phase 2: the break (Q1–Q3 FY25)

The first shock was physical: the summer of 2024 brought a severe heatwave across north and central India, coinciding with a long general election that disrupted field work. Collections slipped. MFIN's first guardrails, issued on 8 July 2024 and applicable from 1 August 2024, capped lenders per borrower at four and total microfinance debt at ₹2 lakh. They were the right medicine but, applied to an over-lent book, they immediately stopped the refinancing that had kept some borrowers current: a borrower who could no longer get a fifth loan could not repay the fourth. In October 2024 the RBI barred four NBFCs (Asirvad, Arohan, DMI Finance and Navi) from sanctioning loans over pricing concerns, and on 25 November 2024 MFIN announced Guardrails 2.0: three lenders, ₹2 lakh including unsecured retail loans, and no lending to borrowers more than 60 days overdue on more than ₹3,000. Q3 FY25 (October–December 2024) was the worst quarter: CAGL lost ₹99.5 crore, Fusion lost ₹719 crore, and industry PAR 31–90 and 91–180 were 3.2% and 3.3% in December 2024.

The human cost

The borrower count is the measure that matters most outside the stock market. MFIN's 7.8 crore unique borrowers in March 2024 had become 6.06 crore by August 2026. Loan accounts fell from 14.9 crore to 9.27 crore. Some of this is healthy (fewer loans per borrower) and some is statistical (loans reclassified), but a large group of women lost access to the only formal credit they had. CAGL alone wrote off about 19 lakh borrowers between FY21 and FY26, and roughly 3 lakh in the twelve months to September 2026. A written-off borrower carries a bureau record that bars her from new microfinance loans under the guardrails' 60-day rule. MFIN's earlier estimate that about 50 lakh clients had "lost access to formal finance" is therefore a floor rather than the full count.

In plain terms

The crisis began as a lending problem (too many loans to the same women), turned into a political problem (state laws that made collection harder), and then into a funding problem (banks stopped lending to lenders). By late 2025 borrowers were repaying normally again, but the industry kept shrinking because lenders had no money to lend. It started to grow only when the money came back.

Three lessons from the anatomy

  • Losses lead, funding lags. PAR peaked around December 2024–March 2025; funding bottomed about a year later. Investors watching only delinquency called the recovery too early; those watching only growth called it too late.
  • The cure tightened the disease first. The guardrails were necessary, but applying them to an over-lent book removed the refinancing that kept marginal borrowers current, so delinquency rose before it fell. Any future tightening will have the same short-term effect.
  • Reported shrinkage overstates the real fall. The 24% fall in industry portfolio includes reclassification of bank loans to retail bureaus and graduation to individual loans outside the microfinance count. Credit to low-income households fell, but by less than the headline says.

The crisis also sorted lenders cleanly. Large, well-rated NBFC-MFIs with rural, low-overlap books and long liability maturities (CAGL above all) stayed profitable and were funded throughout. Mid-size lenders with urban, East or North-heavy, multi-lender books lost a third to a half of their equity (Fusion, Spandana). Banks reclassified and retreated. Small MFIs could not borrow at all. Section 16 returns to why CAGL's losses were shallower; Section 07 to how funding transmitted the shock.

Three lenders, ₹2 lakh: the guardrails that rewired the industry

An industry body, not the regulator, set the rules that ended the boom. They cap growth per borrower, favour the primary lender and make ticket size, not borrower count, the growth engine.

Two different rulebooks govern Indian microfinance and they are often confused. The first is the RBI's Master Direction on microfinance loans of 14 March 2022, effective 1 April 2022 and last updated 17 July 2025. It defines a microfinance loan as a collateral-free loan to a household with annual income up to ₹3,00,000, caps a household's total loan repayments at 50% of its monthly income, requires each lender's board to set a ceiling on rates and charges that "should not be usurious", bars prepayment penalties, and applies the same rules to banks, SFBs, NBFCs and NBFC-MFIs. The second is the MFIN guardrails, issued by MFIN as the RBI-recognised self-regulatory organisation and binding on its members. These are the rules that actually tightened lending in 2024–25.

A lender cannot now grow by adding a third, fourth or fifth loan to an existing borrower; it can grow only by lending more to borrowers it already serves (up to the ₹2 lakh cap), by finding new borrowers, or by moving borrowers to products outside the microfinance definition. Who the guardrails favour

The rules favour the primary lender: the one that already has the borrower's relationship and can raise her loan size within the ₹2 lakh cap while others are locked out by the lender count. CAGL is the only microfinance lender to 45% of its customers and one of two to another 36%, which is why it could keep growing its book through FY26 while the industry shrank. They penalise late entrants and urban multi-lending books, where the borrower already has three lenders and a phone-app loan. And they push all lenders towards graduation products, which do not count towards the lender limit when they are secured, though unsecured retail loans do count towards the ₹2 lakh.

The guardrails also create friction at the top of the customer base. CAGL's management said on the October 2026 call that some good customers cannot be renewed because they have reached the ₹2 lakh cap: "Many have hit the limit". Those customers either graduate to an individual loan (CAGL's retail-finance book) or leave. Combined with the ₹3 lakh household-income ceiling (which MFIN wants reviewed), the guardrails are now the main constraint on growth for the best lenders, rather than demand or funding.

In plain terms

The industry wrote itself a rule that no woman may owe more than ₹2 lakh in unsecured loans or borrow from more than three microfinance lenders. That stopped the debt spiral. It also means a lender can now grow mainly by lending more to women it already knows, which rewards the lenders who got there first and pushes everyone towards individual and secured loans.

Is the regime durable? Self-regulation has two weaknesses. Enforcement depends on members and on bureau data quality, and non-members (some NBFCs and fintechs) are not bound, although the inclusion of unsecured retail debt in the cap brings their loans into the calculation through the bureau. And an SRO under pressure from members to restart growth may relax rules sooner than a regulator would. Against that, the RBI's 2022 framework gave MFIN explicit responsibility as an SRO and moved the regime "from being prescriptive to principles based". For now the guardrails are working: the share of borrowers with four or more lenders has collapsed, PAR is below pre-crisis levels and the industry is growing again, slowly.

07

Funding: the transmission belt between banks and borrowers

Banks lend to microfinance companies and compete with them. When the banks stopped, a credit problem became an industry contraction; when they returned, growth restarted.

For an NBFC-MFI, funding is not a background input. Its loans are short (1–3 years), so it must refinance a large part of its liabilities every year, and its ability to renew borrowers' loans depends on fresh wholesale money. A borrower who repays faithfully in the expectation of a larger next loan stops repaying when the next loan does not come. That feedback loop is why a funding freeze can turn a contained credit problem into a sector-wide shrinkage, and why MFIN described the sector in March 2026 as still facing "liquidity issues" a year after PAR had peaked.

The chain ran from the RBI to banks to MFIs to borrowers. In November 2023 the RBI raised risk weights on bank loans to NBFCs (by 25 percentage points where the NBFC's external rating would otherwise attract less than 100%) and on consumer credit, which made bank lending to NBFC-MFIs more expensive in capital. As delinquency rose in FY25, banks' internal limits for the sector tightened, and covenants embedded in loan agreements were breached. CAGL's FY26 annual report is unusually candid about this. At 31 March 2025 the strongest lender in the industry was out of compliance on seven facilities, and it obtained waivers on all of them.

The thaw

Three things restarted the flow. The RBI's partial rollback of risk weights on 25 February 2025 (effective 1 April 2025) withdrew the November 2023 add-on on bank exposures to NBFCs and stopped treating microfinance loans as 125%-weighted consumer credit (they now carry 100%, or 75% where they qualify as regulatory retail); other unsecured consumer credit stayed at 125%. Policy rates fell through 2025 (repo from 6.50% to 5.50% by June 2025 and 5.25% by December 2025), with a CRR cut adding liquidity. And the government's guarantee scheme gave banks a partial backstop. By Q1 FY27, funding to MFIN-member NBFC-MFIs was up 91.4% year on year. CAGL signed a US$175 million ECB in October 2026, syndicated by Standard Chartered and DBS (its fifth ECB syndication in five years, replacing a maturing US$200 million facility at a lower rate) and raised ₹940 crore of domestic NCDs in FY27 to date.

Guarantees, not bailouts: CGSMFI 2.0 and CGFMU

Two different guarantee schemes are often confused, and the difference matters for who benefits. CGSMFI 2.0 (Credit Guarantee Scheme for Microfinance Institutions, launched by the Department of Financial Services through NCGTC in March 2026) guarantees banks' loans to MFIs. The beneficiary is the lending bank; the MFI is the intermediary, and the funds must go to incremental lending to RBI-defined microfinance borrowers. Cover is 80% of the amount in default for small MFIs (AUM below ₹500 crore), 75% for medium (₹500–2,000 crore) and 70% for large (₹2,000 crore and above), replacing a flat 75%. Per-MFI caps were ₹100 crore, ₹200 crore and ₹300 crore, with the large-MFI cap later raised to ₹1,000 crore within 20% of AUM. At least 5% of guarantees are reserved for small and 10% for medium MFIs; the guarantee fee is reported at 0.50% a year; and the scheme ran until 31 August 2026 or ₹20,000 crore of guarantees, whichever came first. Only ₹770 crore had been sanctioned by 10 June 2026. All CGSMFI 2.0 details come from secondary summaries and are INDICATIVE; whether the scheme was renewed after August is not known.

CGFMU (Credit Guarantee Fund for Micro Units, also NCGTC) guarantees pools of the lender's own loans to micro borrowers. CAGL's CFO described the economics on the October 2026 call: an annual premium of about 1.3–1.5%, cover of about 75% of claimed losses, and a cap on total claims of 15% of the pool. Banks and SFBs "already covered a significant portion" of their microfinance books this way. CAGL has not yet used a microfinance guarantee; it had applied and was waiting for a "budgetary approval" expected "in a week or two" from 22 October, and plans to cover "a few thousand crore" over the next few years. That is a borrower-pool guarantee, not CGSMFI 2.0.

In plain terms

Microfinance companies lend to women with money they borrow from banks. The largest lenders never needed it, because their ratings and foreign lenders kept them funded.

The structural lesson is that the funding moat is real but narrowing. CAGL's 9.1% cost of funds sits near the bottom of the NBFC-MFI range, but Satin reports an adjusted 8.3%, Fusion's marginal cost is 9.35% and Muthoot Microfin, now also AA-/Stable, pays 9.93%. As funding returns, the spread between the best and the median lender compresses, and the advantage shifts back to credit selection and opex. The weak point remains the small and medium MFIs, which depend on banks, guarantees and securitisation, and which will be first to lose funding in the next downturn.

When states legislate: Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Bihar and the ghost of Andhra

Coercion-prevention laws arrive whenever borrower stress meets an election. The risk is behavioural, correlated across lenders and impossible to diversify within a state.

Microfinance is regulated by the RBI, but repayment happens in villages governed by state politicians, police and district administrations. Every major Indian microfinance crisis has had a state-government trigger or amplifier. In October 2010 Andhra Pradesh, then the industry's largest market, passed an ordinance after borrower suicides that required lenders to register with district authorities, restricted collection and effectively froze repayments; SKS Microfinance lost most of its Andhra book, and the RBI's Malegam Committee (January 2011) led to the creation of the NBFC-MFI category in December 2011, with margin caps, income limits and a two-lender cap. Assam passed a Microfinance Act in 2020 and ran a relief scheme in 2021. The 2024–26 cycle added three more states.

Why the laws hurt even regulated lenders

The Karnataka and Tamil Nadu laws were aimed mainly at unregistered moneylenders and at coercive recovery, and RBI-regulated entities were largely outside their registration requirements. The damage came through behaviour rather than law. When a state makes coercive recovery a non-bailable offence, every loan officer becomes cautious about pressing for repayment, every borrower hears that collection agents can be arrested, and local politicians and activists can tell a village that repayment is optional. MFIN listed "external incitement" and the "Karnataka issue" among the causes of FY25 stress. In Bihar, where the 2026 bill explicitly excluded RBI-regulated lenders, MFIN still felt it necessary to warn borrowers "not to fall prey to rumours".

The risk is correlated in a way that credit risk usually is not. A drought hits some districts; a law hits every lender in the state at once, and lenders cannot price it in advance. It is also persistent. Andhra Pradesh, once the largest microfinance state, took most of a decade to return to the national map. For investors, the practical measures are a lender's concentration by state and its share of each state's market, because a lender that is a large share of a state's microfinance is the most visible target if politics turn.

In plain terms

A state government cannot change the RBI's rules, but it can make it dangerous for a loan officer to knock on a borrower's door, and it can make borrowers believe they do not have to pay. When that happens, every lender in the state suffers together, however careful it was. That is why investors watch where a microfinance lender is concentrated as closely as they watch its overdue loans.

What would make the political risk smaller? What would make it larger: a drought year before a state election, a visible lender with a large share of a state's market, and a return to multi-lending. The 2026 monsoon, discussed in Section 10, has supplied the first ingredient in CAGL's home states.

The rulebook loosens: pricing freedom, the 60% rule and cheaper bank money

Since 2022 the RBI has moved from prescribing prices to policing outcomes. The 2025 changes gave NBFC-MFIs room to diversify and cut their funding cost; an insurance-commission cap now threatens a fee line.

For a decade after the Andhra crisis, Indian microfinance was the most tightly prescribed lending business in the country. NBFC-MFIs operated under a margin cap of 10–12% over their cost of funds, an interest-rate ceiling, separate rural and urban income limits (₹1.25 lakh and ₹2 lakh), a two-lender cap and a requirement that 85% of net assets be microfinance loans. Banks and SFBs lending to the same borrowers faced none of these limits, which tilted the field against the specialists.

The RBI's March 2022 framework changed that. It defined microfinance by the borrower (household income up to ₹3 lakh, rural or urban) rather than by the lender, applied the same rules to every regulated lender, removed the margin cap and rate ceiling in favour of board-approved pricing policies that "should not be usurious", and introduced the 50%-of-income repayment cap. Pricing freedom was the single largest change: CAGL's portfolio yield rose from 18.3% in FY22 to 20.9% in FY24 as lenders priced for risk and cost. It also made over-lending possible, because a lender that prices freely can afford riskier borrowers, until the losses arrive.

The 60% rule: a licence to diversify

On 6 June 2025 the RBI cut the NBFC-MFI qualifying-asset requirement to 60% of total assets net of intangibles, from 75% of total assets. Two consequences followed. The immediate one was technical relief: as microfinance books shrank in the bust while cash and other assets did not, some NBFC-MFIs were drifting towards breaching the 75% floor. The strategic one was the licence to build a retail book: CAGL's retail finance rose from 5.9% of AUM in March 2025 to 23.3% in September 2026, and Muthoot Microfin's non-JLG share rose from 17% to 31% between March and September 2026. Without the change, an NBFC-MFI that wanted to keep graduating customers would have had to either lose them or convert to a different licence.

Risk weights: the cost of bank money

The RBI's November 2023 increase in risk weights made bank lending to NBFCs and consumer credit more capital-intensive. Its partial rollback on 25 February 2025, effective 1 April 2025, withdrew the 25-percentage-point add-on on bank exposures to NBFCs and removed microfinance loans from the 125% consumer-credit bucket (they now carry 100%, or 75% where they qualify as regulatory retail). Unsecured consumer credit stayed at 125%. For banks, that lowered the capital cost of funding NBFC-MFIs and of their own microfinance books; for NBFC-MFIs it lowered the spread banks needed to charge them.

Policing pricing

Pricing freedom came with a warning. On 17 October 2024 the RBI barred four NBFCs (Asirvad Micro Finance, Arohan Financial Services, DMI Finance and Navi Finserv) from sanctioning and disbursing loans, citing pricing that it considered usurious. The restrictions were lifted in 2025, but the signal stuck: a yield materially above peers is a regulatory as well as a credit risk. MFIN's Guardrails 2.0 added that boards must review rates "to ensure that efficiency gains are passed on to clients", and CAGL's 50 bp cut in October 2026 is partly an answer to that expectation.

The next rule: capping credit-life commissions

Almost every microfinance loan carries credit-life insurance (84.4% of borrowers in the MFIN–NCAER survey), and the lender earns a commission for selling it. On 23 September 2026 the insurance regulator IRDAI issued a consultation paper, "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution", proposing to cap credit-life commissions paid to lenders at 2% for single-premium and 2.5% for multi-year policies, and credit-health at 5%. Comments closed on 31 October 2026 after an extension (more than 650 responses); the start date has been reported variously as January 2027 or FY28. CAGL estimates the impact at about 25–30 basis points of ROA "in case the commissions drop to indicated draft guidance", and says it is "better placed … to cover it through pricing". On about ₹33,000 crore of assets, 25–30 bp is roughly ₹80–100 crore a year of pre-tax income (INDICATIVE), most of CAGL's ₹136 crore fee and commission line.

In plain terms

The RBI now lets microfinance lenders set their own prices, lets the specialist lenders put up to 40% of their money into other kinds of loans, and has made it cheaper for banks to lend to them. Rain and rates: the 2026 drought and the end of falling funding costs

The monsoon failed where CAGL lends most, and the RBI raised rates for the first time since 2023, just as microfinance margins hit a record. Risk-adjusted NIM, not headline NIM, is what to watch.

The 2026 south-west monsoon delivered 759.4 mm of rain, 87% of the long-period average of 868.6 mm: the fourth-lowest total since 2001 and the thirteenth lowest since 1901 (IMD). The deficit was unevenly spread. Central India, the monsoon's core zone for agriculture, was normal at 97%. North-West India received 94.1%. The South Peninsula received 76%, its fourth-lowest since 1901, and North-East India 74%, with the East and North-East combined the driest since records began in 1901. June was 65% of normal, July 101%, August 84% and September 92%. Seventeen of 36 meteorological subdivisions, covering 42% of the country's area, were deficient.

Does drought cause defaults?

Historically, microfinance repayment has been more resilient to drought than to politics. CAGL's CFO said on the October 2026 call that "historically, we've not seen this kind of drought … our data at least indicates that we've not had any significant impact because of drought", but added that "this year, we'll have to watch out because it's very widespread". Microfinance households have diversified incomes, and many borrowers are not farmers. The channels that do bite are lower farm-labour wages, migration (a migrant borrower misses centre meetings) and the knock-on effect on small shops and livestock. CAGL's monthly PAR 15+ accretion, its measure of new delinquency, has edged up from a trough of 0.07% of AUM in March 2026 to 0.16% in June and 0.26% in September 2026, which the company attributes partly to lower wage income and migration in monsoon-deficient regions and partly to floods. In about six districts of central Maharashtra borrowers have asked for more time and some for restructuring; CAGL has stopped new sourcing there.

In plain terms

A bad monsoon does not usually make microfinance borrowers default en masse, because most of them do not depend on a single crop. It makes some of them late, especially where men migrate for work. The danger is when a drought meets a political campaign, as it has started to in Maharashtra. This year the rain failed most in the south, where CAGL lends most, rather than in the east, where the industry lends most.

In plain terms

Five kinds of institution lend to the same women. Specialist microfinance companies are the most focused and, at their best, the most profitable. Banks and small finance banks have cheaper money from deposits but higher overheads and other businesses to run. The shake-out of 2024–26 rewarded the large specialists with cheap funding and rural, low-overlap borrowers, and punished mid-sized specialists and banks that were heavily exposed.

Five crises in five decades: the history that sets the multiple

From Grameen and NABARD's first groups to Andhra 2010, demonetisation, COVID and the 2024–26 bust. In CAGL's own decade, the "normal" year has been the exception.

Modern microfinance began with group lending to women in Bangladesh (Grameen Bank's project in 1976, the bank in 1983) and, in India, with SEWA Bank (1974) and NABARD's self-help-group bank-linkage pilot (1992). The commercial model arrived in the late 1990s and 2000s: Grameen Koota, CAGL's ancestor, was founded in Karnataka in 1999; SKS Microfinance listed in August 2010 to great fanfare. Private equity poured in, and Andhra Pradesh became the most crowded market in the world.

What the history teaches

Three crises in ten years means the "normal" year (credit cost of about 2%) has been the exception. CAGL's ten-year average credit cost is about 3.8% of AUM; its own stated cross-cycle averages are an ROA of 3.4% and an ROE of 13.9%. That is the number that should anchor its valuation, rather than the 5.8% ROA and 23.2% ROE of its best quarter. The global record tells the same story: Bolivia's over-indebtedness crisis (1999–2000), Morocco (2008), Nicaragua's "no pago" movement (2009) and Andhra Pradesh (2010) all followed the same sequence of rapid growth, multi-lending and political backlash. India's 2024–26 cycle fits the pattern almost exactly.

In plain terms

Every five years or so, something goes badly wrong in Indian microfinance: a state law, demonetisation, a pandemic, a borrowing binge. A lender that is very profitable in the good years but loses its capital in the bad years is not a good business. The ones that survive are the ones that earn enough in good years and lose little enough in bad years to keep growing through all of them.

The cycles have also become shorter and more frequent as the industry has grown. Andhra 2010 to demonetisation was six years; demonetisation to COVID four; COVID to the 2024 bust four. Each crisis has been followed by regulatory change (the 2011 NBFC-MFI regime, the 2022 harmonisation, the 2024–25 guardrails), each change has broadened the business, and each recovery has been led by the lenders with the strongest balance sheets. The question for the next cycle is whether the guardrails, which attack the cause of the last one, can lengthen the gap.

1. Gross loan portfolio (GLP) and AUM

What it is. The total loans a lender manages: loans on its balance sheet plus those sold through direct assignment (DA) or securitisation that it still services. MFIN's industry "portfolio" is the same idea measured from bureau data. Formula. AUM = on-book loans + off-book DA/PTC loans; growth = closing ÷ opening − 1. Worked example. CAGL's AUM was ₹30,946 crore in September 2026, of which ₹23,746 crore group loans and ₹7,200 crore retail finance; up 19.5% year on year and 2.1% in the quarter. What moves it. Disbursement minus repayments minus write-offs; with 1–3 year loans, about half the book repays every year, so AUM responds within two or three quarters to a change in disbursement. Benchmark. In a normal year, 20–25% growth is good, 12–20% average; below 10% signals stress, but above 35% is a warning of over-lending. The trap. Never compare a company's on-balance-sheet "loans" (as shown on data sites) with another's AUM, and remember that AUM growth now hides falling group-loan books: CAGL's group loans fell 2% between March and September 2026 while retail finance grew.

2. Active borrowers and average ticket

What it is. The number of unique borrowers with a live loan, and the average outstanding per borrower. Formula. AUM ÷ borrowers. Worked example. CAGL: ₹30,946 crore ÷ 44.98 lakh = ₹68,800 average outstanding (group loans ₹62,300; retail finance ₹1,05,300). What moves it. New customers (CAGL adds 2.2–2.5 lakh a quarter, 35% new to credit), attrition (historically about 15% a year: half graduate, the rest stop borrowing or default), write-offs (about 3 lakh at CAGL in the year to September 2026) and the guardrail cap on how far a ticket can grow. Benchmark. Customer growth of 8–10% a year alongside AUM growth of 20–25% is healthy (CAGL's Project Shakti targets); customer growth far below AUM growth means growth is coming from bigger tickets, which hits the ₹2 lakh ceiling eventually. The trap. Borrower counts differ by whether a company counts all live accounts or unique borrowers, and whether retail customers are included.

3. Disbursement

What it is. New loans paid out in the period. Formula. Sum of loans disbursed; often compared with AUM as a "turnover" ratio. Worked example. CAGL disbursed ₹6,355 crore in Q2 FY27 (+19.4%) and ₹12,461 crore in H1 FY27 (+15.6%). Its FY26 disbursement of ₹24,859 crore was about 90% of its average FY26 AUM (derived). What moves it. Funding availability above all (Section 07), then seasonality (Q4 is the strongest quarter: CAGL disbursed ₹8,313 crore in Q4 FY26 against ₹5,767 crore in Q3), then the guardrails. Benchmark. Disbursement growing in line with AUM is normal; disbursement falling for two quarters while AUM is flat means the book is ageing and will shrink. The trap. Disbursement growth after a crisis flatters: a lender that disbursed almost nothing a year earlier can show +40% while lending less than it did before the crisis.

4. Branches, loan officers and productivity

What it is. The distribution network and how hard it works. Formula. Borrowers per loan officer; AUM per branch. Worked example. CAGL: 44.98 lakh borrowers ÷ 15,089 loan officers ≈ 298 each; ₹30,946 crore ÷ 2,326 branches ≈ ₹13.3 crore per branch. What moves it. Ticket size (bigger loans raise AUM per officer), branch vintage (new branches take two to three years to fill), technology (digital collections free officer time) and attrition. Benchmark. 250–400 borrowers per officer is typical for weekly or fortnightly JLG; much higher suggests thin monitoring. The trap. Branch counts include retail-finance branches with very different economics, and some lenders count BC outlets or banking units (Bandhan reports 4,414 EEB banking units alongside 2,001 bank branches).

5. Portfolio yield

What it is. Interest earned on loans as a percentage of average loans. Formula. Interest on loans ÷ average loan book. Worked example. CAGL's yield was 22.3% in Q2 FY27, up from 20.7% a year earlier; on its own P&L, ₹1,777.1 crore × 4 ÷ ₹30,633 crore average AUM ≈ 23.2% (INDICATIVE, on a different base). What moves it. Pricing (set by the board under the RBI's "not usurious" principle and MFIN's pass-on rule), product mix (secured loans yield less), and interest reversals: when a loan turns bad, accrued interest is reversed, lowering yield. CAGL's interest de-recognition on Stage 3 loans fell from ₹76.9 crore (Q2 FY26) to ₹37.8 crore (Q2 FY27), which flattered the yield. Benchmark. 20–22% with low credit cost is good; 22–25% average; above 26% is a pricing-risk flag (the RBI acted against four NBFCs in October 2024). Spandana's 24.9% and Satin's 23.0% sit above CAGL. The trap. Rising yield after a crisis is often lower reversals, not higher prices; and a rate cut (CAGL's 50 bp from October 2026) takes four to six quarters to show.

6. Cost of funds

What it is. Interest paid on borrowings as a percentage of average borrowings. Formula. Finance cost ÷ average borrowings; the marginal cost is the rate on new borrowings. Worked example. CAGL's average and marginal cost of borrowing were both 9.1% in Q2 FY27; it guides 9.2–9.3% to March 2027 after the repo hike. What moves it. Policy rates (with a lag set by the fixed/MCLR/repo mix), the lender's rating (AA- vs A makes 100–300 bp of difference), the mix of ECBs and NCDs, and the state of the funding market. Benchmark. Below 9.5% is good for an NBFC-MFI; 9.5–11.5% average; above 12% weak. SFBs and banks fund at 6.5–8% from deposits but carry CRR/SLR. The trap. Companies define it differently: Satin reports 8.3% "adjusted for MTM/forex"; Fusion quotes a marginal 9.35%; some include processing fees on borrowings and some do not. The like-for-like figure is finance cost ÷ average borrowings from the P&L.

7. Net interest margin (NIM)

What it is. Net interest income as a percentage of the earning base. Formula. (Interest income − interest expense) ÷ average AUM or average earning assets. Worked example. CAGL reported NIM of 15.1% in Q2 FY27 (H1 14.7%); on average AUM the P&L gives about 16.7% (INDICATIVE): the company's base is larger. FY26 NIM was 13.4% (FY25 12.9%). What moves it. Yield minus cost of funds, scaled by how much of the book is debt-funded: a lender with more equity earns a higher NIM on the same spread. Benchmark. Above 13% good; 10–13% average; below 9% weak. The trap. NIM is not comparable across lenders that use different denominators; and NIM without credit cost means little. CAGL's own anchor is risk-adjusted NIM (NIM − credit cost) of at least 10%.

9. Credit cost

What it is. The charge to profit for bad loans. Formula. (Expected-credit-loss provisions + write-off impact − recoveries) ÷ average AUM. Worked example. CAGL's Q2 FY27 credit cost was ₹299.2 crore, 0.99% of AUM non-annualised (0.77% from new PAR, 0.22% from raising its provisioning rates); H1 FY27 annualised 3.43% (2.98% "normative" plus 0.45% macro-prudential buffer). For one ₹50,000 two-year weekly loan with about ₹27,000 average outstanding (ILLUSTRATIVE): interest at 22% earns about ₹5,900 a year, funding costs about ₹1,950, opex about ₹1,350, and credit cost about ₹540 at 2% or ₹2,160 at 8%; pre-tax profit is about ₹2,050 in a normal year and about ₹440 in a bust, and the loan breaks even at a credit cost of about 10% of its average balance. What moves it. The cycle; borrower overlap; geography; how quickly the lender writes off; and provisioning choices (buffers can be built or released). Benchmark. Through-cycle below 2.5% good, 2.5–4% average, above 5% weak. CAGL's ten-year average is about 3.8%. The trap. Three bases are in use: quarterly ₹, non-annualised % and annualised %. 11. GNPA and NNPA: always ask "at how many days?"

What it is. Gross non-performing assets (Stage 3 loans) as a share of the book; net NPA after provisions. Formula. New slippages minus write-offs and recoveries; restructuring; sales to asset reconstruction companies. Benchmark. GNPA below 2% good, 2–4% average, above 5% weak, always on a stated dpd basis; NNPA below 0.75% good. The trap. The dpd basis; and one-quarter collapses in GNPA (ESAF 5.40% to 2.84%, Suryoday 6.60% to 2.97% in Q2 FY27) usually mean write-offs or sales, not recoveries (unverified for these two).

12. Provision cover (ECL)

What it is. Total expected-credit-loss provisions as a share of exposure, and Stage 3 coverage. Formula. ECL ÷ exposure at default (EAD). Worked example. 13. Write-offs

What it is. Loans removed from the books as unrecoverable. Formula. Write-offs ÷ opening AUM. Worked example. The lender's policy (CAGL writes off quickly after Stage 3), the size of the previous cycle's slippage, and recoveries. Benchmark. Through-cycle 2–3% of AUM is typical; above 5% means the book is still clearing a crisis. The trap. 14. Collection efficiency

What it is. Collections received as a share of what was due. Formula. Collections ÷ scheduled dues, excluding or including arrears and prepayments; the "X-bucket" version measures only borrowers who were current at the start of the period. Worked example. CAGL Q2 FY27: 97.9% excluding arrears, 98.1% including; X-bucket 99.71% in September 2026. Fusion's X-bucket was 99.77%, Muthoot Microfin's collection efficiency 98.11%, Bandhan's EEB about 98% (June 2026). Benchmark. Excluding arrears above 98.5% good, 96–98.5% average, below 95% weak. The trap. Including arrears and prepayments can push the ratio above 100% in a recovery; always use the "excluding arrears" or X-bucket number, and note that it covers only the live book, not written-off loans.

In plain terms

A 98% collection rate sounds excellent, but in microfinance it is only average. If 2% of what falls due each week is not paid, and loans run for two years, the unpaid amounts pile up to several per cent of the book. The best lenders collect more than 99.5% from borrowers who were up to date.

15. Return on assets and return on equity

What it is. Profit as a share of average total assets and of average equity. Formula. PAT ÷ average assets; PAT ÷ average equity; ROE = ROA × assets ÷ equity. Worked example. CAGL Q2 FY27 annualised: ROA 5.8%, ROE 23.2%; H1 FY27 5.9% and 23.8%; FY26 2.7% and 10.7%; FY25 1.9% and 7.7%; FY24 5.6% and 24.9%. What moves it. Every term in the equation above, plus leverage. Benchmark. ROA above 4% good, 2.5–4% average, below 2% weak; ROE above 18% good, 12–18% average, below 10% weak. CAGL's FY27 guidance (raised on 22 October 2026) is ROA 5.2–5.8% and ROE 21–24%; Project Shakti's ten-year targets are ROA 4.0–4.5% and ROE 18–20%. The trap. A single quarter annualised overstates the year when the quarter is unusually good; and screener's "ROE" for CAGL (about 10.5%) is FY26, not trailing.

16a. Liquidity and asset-liability match

What it is. The cash and liquid investments a lender holds, and how the maturity of its loans compares with the maturity of its borrowings. Formula. Cash and liquid investments ÷ total assets; cumulative maturity gap = assets maturing within a bucket − liabilities maturing in the same bucket. Worked example. CAGL held ₹3,502 crore of liquidity in September 2026, 10.2% of total assets, plus ₹6,759 crore of undrawn sanctions and a ₹5,925 crore pipeline. At 31 March 2026 (standalone), about ₹8,247 crore of advances were due to mature within six months against about ₹6,168 crore of borrowings, plus about ₹935 crore of liquid investments: a positive gap of about ₹3,000 crore (derived, INDICATIVE). What moves it. The lender's choice of long-dated funding (77.6% of CAGL's borrowings have an original tenure of two years or more), the short tenure of its loans, and the market. Benchmark. 10–15% of assets in liquidity is the industry norm (MFIN); below 8% leaves little room in a freeze. The trap. Liquidity is a cost in good times: 10% of assets earning 6–7% against a 9% cost of funds drags NIM by roughly 0.2–0.3 percentage points. Lenders that cut it to flatter margins are the ones that freeze first.

16b. Borrower retention, vintage and lender overlap

What it is. The share of borrowers who take a repeat loan, how long they have been with the lender, and how many other microfinance lenders they use. Formula. Repeat borrowers ÷ borrowers eligible for renewal; share of borrowers by number of lenders on the bureau. Worked example. CAGL's retention was 88% in H1 FY27 (85% in FY26, 88% in FY23–FY24); 27% of its borrowers had been with it for more than six years and 47% for less than three (53% a year earlier); 45% had no other microfinance lender. What moves it. Service quality, competition, the guardrails (which force non-renewal when a borrower has too many lenders or too much debt) and graduation. Benchmark. Retention above 85% good, below 70% weak; sole-lender share above 40% good. The trap. High retention can coexist with high risk if the lender is renewing over-indebted borrowers; read it with lender overlap and PAR.

17. Price to book

What it is. Market value as a multiple of shareholders' equity. For lenders it replaces EV/EBITDA. Formula. Market cap ÷ equity (or price ÷ book value per share). A fair P/B ≈ (sustainable ROE − growth) ÷ (cost of equity − growth). Worked example. CAGL at ₹1,050 on 3 November 2026: market cap ₹16,851 crore ÷ September 2026 equity of ₹8,919 crore = 1.89×. With a sustainable ROE of 18%, growth of 12% and a cost of equity of 15%, fair P/B ≈ (18 − 12) ÷ (15 − 12) = 2.0× (ILLUSTRATIVE). Benchmark. Above 2.5× means the market believes through-cycle ROE is above 18%; 1.2–2.5× average; below 1× means the market doubts book value or returns. The trap. Book value after a crisis may still carry under-provided loans; and the right ROE is the through-cycle one (CAGL's ten-year average is 13.9%), not the best quarter.

20. Definition traps, collected

  • GNPA at 60 or 90 days. CAGL's group-loan GNPA is recognised at 60+ dpd (retail 90+); most peers use 90+. Compare on PAR 90+ (CAGL 1.11%).
  • Industry "portfolio" shrank partly by relabelling. About 5% of the September 2025 book moved from the microfinance bureau to retail bureaus, mostly at banks; banks' −28.5% (June 2026) is part real, part reclassification.
  • MFIN's lender-type shares are not internally consistent. The banks' 32.7% share printed for March 2026 is a misprint; use about 26.4%.
  • Two rulebooks. The RBI's ₹3 lakh household-income limit and 50% repayment cap are separate from MFIN's ₹2 lakh per-borrower debt cap (including unsecured retail) and three-lender limit.
  • AUM growth hides group-loan shrinkage. CAGL's 19.5% AUM growth includes a group-loan book down 2% since March 2026.
  • Three bases for credit cost. Quarterly ₹, non-annualised % and annualised %; never multiply a quarter by four without saying so.
  • NIM and ROA use different denominators. NIM on AUM or earning assets; ROA on total assets; ROE on average equity. Quote the period every time.
  • Banks and SFBs are not pure plays. Bandhan's secured share is 57.3%; Equitas is about 86% secured. Use them for market share and funding, not as valuation comparisons.
  • "NBFC-MFI" is a licence, not a description. It requires 60% of assets in microfinance since June 2025; other NBFCs may hold up to 25%.
  • Cost of funds definitions vary. Average vs marginal, with or without MTM and forex adjustments.
  • Collection efficiency can exceed 100% when arrears and prepayments are included; use the excluding-arrears or X-bucket version.
In plain terms

Almost every microfinance number comes with a definition choice that can move it by a percentage point or more. Before comparing two lenders, check how many days overdue counts as "bad", whether the loan book includes loans sold to others, whether a ratio is for one quarter or annualised, and whether the industry figure includes loans that have been relabelled as retail.

21. How to read a microfinance lender's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. AUM and its split (1 minute). Total AUM, group vs individual/secured, and QoQ and YoY growth. Red flag: group loans shrinking while the headline grows, without a stated reason.
  2. Borrowers and ticket size. Active borrowers, new borrowers, and average outstanding. Red flag: AUM growth more than ten points above borrower growth for several quarters, which signals concentration near the ₹2 lakh cap.
  3. Disbursement. Compare with the same quarter last year and with repayments. Red flag: disbursement falling for two quarters.
  4. New delinquency first. PAR 15+ accretion or PAR 0+/30+ by month and state. This is the leading indicator; it moves two to four quarters before credit cost. Red flag: two consecutive monthly increases in a large state.
  5. PAR 90+ and GNPA, with the dpd basis. Red flag: GNPA falling only because write-offs rose.
  6. Credit cost and write-offs. Convert to an annualised % of AUM and compare with guidance. Red flag: credit cost below guidance while PAR is rising.
  7. The ECL roll-forward. New provisions, write-off reversals, buffers built or released. Red flag: profits boosted by buffer releases.
  8. Yield, cost of funds and NIM. Check whether yield is rising from price or from lower interest reversals; check the marginal cost of funds. Red flag: NIM rising while marginal cost of funds rises too.
  9. Opex/AUM and cost-to-income; ROA, ROE and CRAR. Red flag: opex/AUM rising for reasons other than investment in new branches.
  10. Liquidity, funding and guidance. Cash as % of assets, undrawn sanctions, new borrowings, rating actions; then compare guidance with last quarter's. Red flag: liquidity below 8–10% of assets, or guidance cut on credit cost.

Applied to CAGL's Q2 FY27, the ten-minute read goes like this. AUM +19.5%, but group loans −2% year to date: the growth is retail finance. Borrowers +1.3%: ticket-size growth. PAR 15+ accretion up from 0.07% (March) to 0.26% (September), with Maharashtra's early-bucket PAR rising from 0.7% to 1.0%: the first warning. PAR 90+ 1.11% and GNPA 1.79% at 60+ dpd: clean. Credit cost 0.99% non-annualised, including ₹88 crore of buffer-building: conservative. NIM 15.1% on falling reversals and cheaper funding, but a 50 bp price cut and a repo hike ahead: peak margins. ROA 5.8%, CRAR 26.9%, liquidity 10.2%: strong. Guidance raised on ROA and NIM, held on credit cost at 3–4% with management steering to the top half. A record quarter with the first signs of the next turn, which is how the market read it on 23 October 2026.

In plain terms

Read a microfinance result from the back of the cycle to the front: new overdue loans first, then bad loans, then the provisions charged against them, and only then the margin and the profit. Profit tells you about the last six months; new delinquency tells you about the next six.

The quality premium: why CAGL fell less, and where it is exposed Deep dive

In FY25 CAGL earned a 1.9% ROA while Fusion and Spandana lost a third to a half of their equity. The reasons are rural borrowers, sole-lender relationships, cheap long money and lean branches. The price is concentration in three states.

The bust tested every lender with the same shocks. CAGL's credit cost rose from 2.1% of AUM in FY24 to 7.7% in FY25 and 6.7% in FY26, enough to cut its ROA from 5.6% to 1.9% and its ROE from 24.9% to 7.7%. That was painful, but it was profitable in every year except one loss-making quarter (Q3 FY25, −₹99.5 crore). Fusion's FY25 loss of ₹1,225 crore cut its equity by 42%; Spandana lost ₹1,035 crore in FY25 and ₹699 crore more in FY26, and its balance sheet shrank 46% from its March 2024 peak. Why the difference?

Five sources of the premium

  • Rural, low-overlap borrowers. 87% of CAGL's borrowers are rural (FY26). 45% have no other microfinance lender, 36% one other. Rural borrowers with a single lender were the least exposed to the multi-lending spiral that drove urban and peri-urban losses.

  • Early recognition. CAGL classifies group loans as non-performing at 60 days overdue rather than 90, provisions accordingly, and writes off quickly (₹1,968 crore in FY26). It also carries a 0.45% macro-prudential buffer. Early recognition makes the crisis visible sooner and the recovery cleaner.
  • Cheap, long, diversified funding. AA-/Stable from three agencies, 80-plus lenders, 21% of borrowings from foreign ECBs, 77.6% of borrowings with tenure of two years or more, a positive asset-liability gap and 10% of assets in liquidity. When banks froze, CAGL's lines were waived, not called.
  • Lean, scaled branches. Opex has stayed at 4.5–5.1% of AUM for a decade, so pre-provision profit runs at 9–13% of AUM. That is the shock absorber: CAGL's breakeven credit cost is roughly 10–12% of AUM; a mid-size peer's is perhaps 6–8% (INDICATIVE).

From kendra to household: the retail-finance pivot Deep dive

The best microfinance lenders are turning into rural retail lenders. CAGL's retail book went from 5.9% to 23.3% of AUM in eighteen months, enabled by the 60% rule and driven by borrowers who outgrow the group loan.

The group loan has a ceiling. Under the guardrails a borrower may owe no more than ₹2 lakh in microfinance and unsecured retail loans, and under the RBI definition her household must earn no more than ₹3 lakh a year. A woman who has borrowed from CAGL for seven years, built a small business and a credit score above 700 will hit one or both limits. If CAGL cannot lend her more, another lender will. The retail-finance (RF) book is CAGL's answer: individual business loans, secured business loans, affordable housing and two-wheeler loans sold mainly to its own graduating customers through its existing branches.

The industry is pivoting too

Every listed NBFC-MFI is moving the same way. The end point is a rural retail lender that uses the group loan as its customer-acquisition channel and makes an increasing share of its profit from the graduates.

In plain terms

The group loan is how a microfinance lender meets a woman; the individual loan is how it keeps her once she outgrows the group. The RBI's 2025 rule change lets specialist lenders put 40% of their money into those bigger loans. The bet is that a lender who has watched a borrower repay every week for seven years knows more about her than a bank that has just met her.

CAGL's own targets frame the ambition. Its Project Shakti plan sets ten-year aims of 20–25% AUM growth a year, 8–10% customer growth, an ROA of 4.0–4.5% and an ROE of 18–20%, with growth from both organic retail lending and acquisitions in secured lending. On the Q1 FY27 call management set a medium-term target of ₹50,000 crore AUM by calendar 2028, roughly 60% above September 2026. A widely quoted target of half the book in retail by FY30 does not appear in any company document we have seen. Two implications follow for analysis. First, "microfinance growth" will understate CAGL's growth and the industry's lending to the same households, because graduates leave the microfinance count. Why a bank might want it, and what would change

For a bank, CAGL offers India's best-run rural lending franchise: 2,326 branches, 45 lakh borrowers, a decade of data on low-income households, and a priority-sector book that banks must otherwise buy. What would change is just as important: the qualifying-asset rule would become irrelevant if the business moved inside a bank; deposit regulation, CRR/SLR and banking supervision would apply; and the strategy might tilt towards the bank's products. The private banks report covers the potential acquirers. For CAGL's minority shareholders the key questions are whether a holding-company deal triggers an open offer (an indirect acquisition of control generally would under Indian takeover rules, though the details depend on the structure) and whether a new owner keeps CAGL listed.

The global lens and the price of a cycle

India runs one of the world's largest microfinance markets. Its best lender now trades in line with Mexico's Gentera, at about 1.9× book, after a 35% fall since July 2026.

At ₹3.29 lakh crore, India's MFIN-measured microfinance market is about US$34.6 billion at ₹94.9 to the dollar (October 2026), down from about US$45.7 billion at the peak on the same exchange rate. Global microfinance loan portfolios are often estimated at US$150–200 billion (INDICATIVE, weak), which would put India at roughly a fifth; Bangladesh (Grameen Bank, BRAC) and Indonesia's state-led micro-lending through Bank Rakyat Indonesia are the other giants. CAGL's AUM of ₹30,946 crore is about US$3.3 billion, similar to the loan book of Gentera, the Mexican group that owns Banco Compartamos.

Lessons from abroad

The international record is a sequence of the same mistake: rapid growth, multiple lending to the same borrowers, then a political or social backlash. Bolivia (1999–2000), Morocco (2008), Nicaragua's "no pago" movement (2009) and Andhra Pradesh (2010) all followed it, and Compartamos' 2007 IPO set off a global debate about the ethics of high-yield lending to the poor. The markets that recovered best built credit bureaus, lender limits and borrower-protection codes, which is what India's guardrails now do. The global lesson for valuation is that the market capitalises a microfinance lender's through-cycle return, not its peak return: Gentera and CAGL both trade at about 1.9× book on peak-year ROEs in the 20s.

Technology and disruption: apps, bureaus and the weekly meeting

Digital collection makes microfinance cheaper, but it erodes the centre meeting that makes it work. Data is replacing field judgement in underwriting; fintech and gold lenders compete for the same women.

The weekly centre meeting is microfinance's core technology: it collects cash, monitors borrowers and enforces joint liability. Digital tools are now changing each of those functions. At CAGL, 26.3% of collections were digital in Q2 FY27 (18.1% a year earlier and 24.2% in Q1 FY27); its Grameen Mahi app had 17.1 lakh active registered customers, 38.1% of borrowers. Muthoot Microfin reports 47% digital collections. Yet more than 99% of CAGL's regular collections still happen at centre meetings, and CAGL notes "reducing centre meeting attendance" as a trend to manage.

Four shifts to watch

  • From social collateral to data. Bureau checks, account-aggregator data, image recognition and automated risk monitoring (all in CAGL's deck) shift the underwriting edge from the loan officer's judgement to data. That favours scale lenders with long histories, and it makes the group less essential for individual loans.
  • Competition from fintech and gold. Digital personal loans and gold loans reached the same households in 2021–24, creating much of the multi-lending the guardrails now count. 30% of CAGL's customers hold a gold loan elsewhere; CAGL keeps customers with gold loans to 10–12% of its base.
  • Bureau definitions. Whether a loan is reported to the microfinance or retail bureau changes reported market size and guardrail enforcement. MFIN has asked the RBI to treat all unsecured loans to households below the ₹3 lakh income limit as microfinance.
  • Cyber and fraud risk. Spandana reported a ₹7.80 crore cyber-fraud incident on 1 November 2026. Digital collections and app-based disbursement widen the attack surface for lenders with thin technology budgets.

Climate is the other disruptor. The 2024 heatwave, the August–September 2026 floods in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, and the 2026 drought all fed directly into PAR. Lenders are responding with district caps, weather-linked rescheduling and insurance, but no microfinance lender in India yet prices climate risk explicitly. The fertilisers report covers the rural-income side of the monsoon.

In plain terms

Paying by phone is cheaper for lender and borrower, but the weekly meeting is what makes women repay loans without collateral. The lenders that win will use technology to cut costs and spot trouble earlier without losing the meeting, and will have to defend their customers against fintech and gold lenders offering faster money.

None of this suggests technology will displace the scale microfinance lenders soon. The borrower's cash income, the need for weekly contact and the regulatory requirement to assess household income keep the model field-heavy. What technology changes is the cost curve: a lender that can run 400 borrowers per officer instead of 300, with collections half digital, has a structurally lower opex ratio. CAGL's opex has been flat at about 5% of AUM; the next step-down in the industry's cost base will come from digital collection and data-led underwriting rather than from more branches.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian microfinance

The bull case is a recovered industry with clean books, cheaper regulation and a consolidating market. The bear case is a cycle that comes round every four or five years, starting this time in CAGL's own states.

The bull case

  • Clean books. Industry PAR 31–180 is 1.6% (June 2026), below pre-crisis; the over-indebted borrower has been written off or capped; 95% of the book is within the guardrails.
  • Funding is back. NBFC-MFI funding +91.4% year on year (Q1 FY27); CGSMFI 2.0 and the risk-weight rollback lower bank funding costs; ratings are rising (Muthoot Microfin to AA-).
  • Share gains. Banks have retreated (25.3% of the industry from 33.2%), and the guardrails favour primary lenders like CAGL.
  • The retail runway. The 60% rule lets the best NBFC-MFIs follow their graduating borrowers into individual and secured loans at low acquisition cost.
  • Valuation. CAGL at 1.9× book and ~8.5× annualised H1 earnings; peers at 0.84–0.89× book; the price already implies an average rather than a peak year.

The bear case

  • The cycle. Three crises in CAGL's last ten years; PAR 15+ accretion has tripled from its trough; peak margins (NIM 15.1%) are being given back through rate cuts.
  • Drought in the south. South Peninsula rainfall 76% of normal; Karnataka and Tamil Nadu are 47% of CAGL's AUM; restructuring requests in Maharashtra.
  • Politics. Karnataka and Tamil Nadu laws stay on the books; Maharashtra farmer-death attributions have begun; Bihar's bill adds rumour risk.
  • Fees and rates. IRDAI's credit-life cap (25–30 bp of CAGL's ROA); repo up to 5.50% with lagged pass-through to funding costs.
  • Growth ceiling and overhang. Borrower growth of 1.3%; the ₹2 lakh cap binds; a holding-company deal or further CAI sales could weigh on the stock.

Weighing the two, the industry's position is stronger than at any time since early 2024: delinquency is low, funding has returned and the regulatory direction is supportive. But microfinance profits are at their most fragile precisely when they look best, because lenders cut prices and expand into new products while the next shock builds. The swing factors for the next twelve months are the drought's effect on southern and western collections, the IRDAI decision and whether the group-loan book resumes growth. For CAGL specifically, the bull case needs credit cost to stay near 3.5% and Karnataka and Maharashtra to remain calm; the bear case needs only one of its three main states to turn.

The balance of evidence favours a slow, steady recovery for the industry rather than a repeat of the FY24 boom: the guardrails cap how fast lenders can grow per borrower, banks are cautious, and small MFIs remain short of capital. That is a better environment for the scale lenders than for the industry as a whole. It also means that headline industry growth will stay modest even as CAGL and Satin grow 20–30%, because their gains come partly at the expense of banks and smaller lenders and partly from retail products outside the microfinance count.

In plain terms

The optimist sees an industry that has cleaned up, with its best lender earning record profits at a modest price. The pessimist sees an industry that has a crisis every few years, with the next one possibly starting in the very states where its best lender is concentrated. Both are looking at the same numbers; they disagree on how soon the next shock comes.
