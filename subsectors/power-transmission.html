00

The one-page view: a state-written order book, throttled by execution

Demand for new wires is planned years ahead by the government. Profits depend on how fast lines can be built, what return each one was allowed or bid at, and what investors charge for capital.

Power transmission is the part of the electricity business that nobody sees until it fails. The lines above 220 kilovolts carry power from a solar park in the Kutch desert or a coal plant in Chhattisgarh to the substations outside Delhi, Mumbai and Bengaluru. They earn a fee for being available, not for the electricity they carry. Two companies in the NSE 750 make this their main business: Power Grid Corporation of India (POWERGRID), the state-controlled national transmission utility, and Adani Energy Solutions (AESL), the largest private transmission developer, which has added Mumbai distribution, smart meters and, since 2026, a power-trading platform.

Together they were worth ₹3,76,415 crore on 23 October 2026 and reported a combined FY26 profit of ₹18,321 crore (reported, including minority interests). The weights are lopsided. POWERGRID produced 87% of that profit but holds only 60% of the combined market value. AESL earns 13% of the profit and is valued at 40%. The market is paying about 14 times earnings for a near-sovereign annuity and more than 60 times FY26 earnings (about 40 times trailing earnings to September 2026) for growth, optionality and a trading business whose profits arrived only this year.

Five forces define the sub-sector, and this report is built around them.

  • A capex supercycle throttled by execution. The National Electricity Plan for transmission budgets ₹9.15 lakh crore for FY23–32, and the Central Electricity Authority's newer plan for 900 GW of non-fossil capacity adds ₹7.93 lakh crore for FY27–36. Yet the country added only 8,830 circuit-km of lines in FY25 and 12,139 in FY26, against an original FY26 target of 24,400. POWERGRID's capex has risen from ₹9,060 crore in FY22 to ₹39,967 crore in FY26, and its free cash flow has fallen from ₹31,102 crore in FY23 to ₹3,687 crore.
  • Two return regimes. Lines nominated to POWERGRID under the Regulated Tariff Mechanism (RTM) earn a cost-plus 15.5% return on equity (15.0% for assets commissioned from April 2024). Lines won in tariff-based competitive bidding (TBCB) earn whatever the 35-year bid implied, and recent bids look thin: AESL's Vizag win prices its tariff at roughly 10.2% of estimated cost, which on standard financing implies an equity return of only 7–8% (indicative). 83% of POWERGRID's works in hand are now TBCB.
  • The bottleneck is equipment and land. A 765 kV transformer takes 20–24 months to arrive. Right-of-way compensation is being re-priced state by state. In H1 FY27 POWERGRID delivered 89% of its full-year line target while private developers delivered 27%.
  • AESL beyond the wires. Mumbai distribution, 14.8 million smart meters and a new Energy Solutions Platform (power trading on long-term contracted renewable capacity) made up about half of AESL's H1 FY27 operating EBITDA. The trading engine alone produced 24%, mostly sourced from Adani Green.
  • Cost of capital and valuation. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and the 10-year government bond yield reached 7.47%. POWERGRID's earnings yield of about 7.0% now sits below the sovereign yield. Both stocks are at or near 52-week lows.

In plain terms

A transmission line is a toll road for electrons that gets paid whether or not traffic flows, as long as the road is open. POWERGRID owns most of the existing roads and earns a return set by a regulator. AESL builds new roads it wins in auctions, runs Mumbai's suburban power supply, installs smart meters and has started trading power. The government has already decided that India needs roughly twice as many roads; the hold-up is getting the steel, transformers and land fast enough, and deciding who pays what return to build them.

01

How a wire earns: from a planner's map to a 35-year toll

A transmission project passes through six hands before it earns a rupee. The money is decided at the start (the tariff) and in the middle (the build); operations are cheap.

Every inter-state transmission line in India begins as a line on a planner's map. The Central Electricity Authority (CEA) writes the National Electricity Plan and rolling five-year plans for the inter-state transmission system (ISTS). The Central Transmission Utility of India (CTUIL), carved out of POWERGRID in 2021, identifies specific schemes, usually to evacuate a renewable-energy zone or to strengthen a corridor. The National Committee on Transmission (NCT) then decides the route to market: nomination to POWERGRID under the Regulated Tariff Mechanism, or an auction under tariff-based competitive bidding. It also sets an "NCT cost", the planner's estimate of what the scheme should cost. The Ministry of Power notifies the decision.

For a TBCB scheme, a bid process coordinator (RECPDCL or PFCCL, subsidiaries of the state lenders REC and PFC) incorporates a shell company, runs qualification and a request for proposals, and closes with an electronic reverse auction in which bidders compete on the levelised annual tariff they will accept for 35 years. The winner buys the shell for a token sum; that is why POWERGRID's filings on 12 October 2026 record "acquisitions" of the Jam Khambhaliya and Lakadia REZ companies for ₹18–20 crore each. The Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) adopts the tariff under section 63 of the Electricity Act and grants a licence. For an RTM scheme, POWERGRID builds first and files a cost-plus tariff petition under section 62.

Value is captured in Step 2 (the tariff a bidder accepts or the regulator allows) and Step 3 (whether the line is built on cost and on time). Steps 4 to 6 are low-cost and low-risk once the asset works.

Construction is where the risk sits. EPC contractors (KEC International, Kalpataru Projects, Tata Projects, L&T, and the Adani group's own EPC arm for AESL) erect towers and string conductors. Equipment makers (Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, GE Vernova T&D, BHEL, CG Power, Toshiba and, since 2026, four Chinese-linked suppliers) deliver transformers, reactors, gas-insulated switchgear and HVDC converter valves. Right-of-way, the permission to cross private land, is negotiated with farmers and state administrations. A TBCB line earns nothing until its commercial operation date (COD); a delayed RTM line keeps its money locked in capital work in progress (CWIP), earning no regulated return until it is capitalised.

Once a line is live, billing is mechanical. Under the General Network Access regime introduced in October 2023, every designated ISTS customer (state distribution companies, open-access buyers, some generators) books a quantity of network access and pays a pooled monthly charge. Grid-India's National Load Despatch Centre (NLDC) computes the charges and the CTUIL pays each licensee its annual charge, one-twelfth a month. POWERGRID's management put the point simply on its Q1 FY27 call: its tariff is "rupees per annum". An under-used line earns the same as a congested one. Payment security comes from letters of credit and the Late Payment Surcharge Rules of 2022, and it works: POWERGRID collected 101.2% of its FY26 billing (catching up arrears) and had about 10.7 days of receivables outstanding.

Because operating costs are tiny, transmission margins are among the highest of any listed business. POWERGRID's consolidated operating expenses were ₹7,875 crore on total income of ₹47,684 crore in FY26, an EBITDA margin of about 83%. AESL's transmission segment ran at a 92% operating EBITDA margin in H1 FY27. What separates a good transmission company from a poor one is therefore not cost control but three things: the tariff it accepted or was allowed, the capital cost and timing of each project, and the cost of the debt and equity that fund it. 02

The grid and its money pool: 5 lakh circuit-km, ₹47,500 crore a year

The physical grid is large and growing about 2.5% a year. The inter-state money pool is smaller than commonly assumed, and POWERGRID's group owns most of it.

On 31 March 2026 India's transmission network at 220 kV and above stood at 5,06,513 circuit-km (ckm) of lines and 14,50,526 MVA of transformation capacity, according to CEA data reproduced in both members' FY26 annual reports. A circuit-km counts each circuit separately: a 300-km double-circuit line is 600 ckm. Inter-regional transfer capacity, the ability to move power between the five regional grids, was 1,20,340 MW, of which POWERGRID owns 1,01,180 MW (about 84%). HVDC terminal capacity is about 33,500 MVA, more than half at ±800 kV. The network crossed 5 lakh ckm on 14 January 2026 when the Bhadla II–Sikar II 765 kV line was energised.

Two corrections follow. First, the common assumption that the ISTS pool is ₹60,000–65,000 crore a year overstates it; NLDC's own number is closer to ₹47,500 crore. Second, AESL's slice of today's inter-state revenue is small. Most of its operating transmission is intra-state or regulated by states (Maharashtra's MEGPTCL, Rajasthan and Gujarat lines, Mumbai transmission) plus the CERC-regulated ATIL. The contest between the two members is a contest for new awards, not for today's revenue.

Intra-state transmission, owned mainly by state utilities such as MSETCL, GETCO and RVPN, adds a pool of similar size that is not centrally published. Combining the two, the total Indian transmission revenue pool is plausibly ₹1.0–1.2 lakh crore a year (INDICATIVE estimate). Two benchmarks anchor the scale: the GNA quantum in December 2025 was 1,23,255 MW, so the average ISTS charge was about ₹3.2 lakh per MW per month, roughly ₹0.44 per kWh at a 100% load factor; and the two listed members together collected about ₹74,321 crore of statutory revenue in FY26, of which less than ₹55,000 crore was transmission.

In plain terms

Every month, the users of India's inter-state grid pay about ₹3,960 crore into one pot. POWERGRID and its subsidiaries take out about ₹83 of every ₹100. AESL's inter-state lines take out about ₹6. The rest is shared by a long tail of private owners and infrastructure trusts. The race is over who builds the lines that will be added to that pot over the next decade.

The members' own revenue histories show the two models. POWERGRID's statutory revenue rose from ₹37,744 crore in FY20 to ₹46,733 crore in FY26, a compound growth rate of 3.6%, while profit grew 6.3% a year from a low base; FY23–FY26 profit was flat at ₹15,400–15,900 crore. AESL's revenue grew from ₹11,416 crore to ₹27,588 crore (15.9% a year), driven by Mumbai distribution, construction income on new lines and, in FY27, trading. Its profit was lumpy until FY26: ₹922 crore in FY25 after a one-off other-income loss of ₹1,395 crore in Q1 FY25, then ₹2,393 crore in FY26 and ₹3,897 crore in the twelve months to September 2026.

03

A plan written by the state: ₹9.15 lakh crore, then ₹7.93 lakh crore more

Demand for transmission is not a market forecast. It is a government plan to connect 500 GW of clean power by 2030 and 900 GW by 2036, and it is already behind.

The demand side of transmission is unusual: there are no customers choosing between products. Planners decide what is needed, and licensees are paid to build it. The anchor document is the National Electricity Plan (Transmission) for 2023–32, published in 2024. It envisages 1,91,474 ckm of new lines and 1,274 GVA of new transformation capacity, an investment of more than ₹9.15 lakh crore, and a rise in inter-regional capacity from about 120 GW to 168 GW by 2032. Its purpose is to integrate 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 and about 600 GW by 2032, together with 47 GW of battery storage and 31 GW of pumped hydro.

The ₹9.15 lakh crore is often misquoted as an inter-state/intra-state split. It is in fact split by period: roughly ₹4.25 lakh crore for FY23–27 and ₹4.91 lakh crore for FY28–32. For the second period the plan allocates ₹3.91 lakh crore (80%) to the inter-state system and ₹0.99 lakh crore (20%) to intra-state networks, with 76,787 ckm, 4,97,855 MVA and 32,250 MW of HVDC. The widely quoted "₹4.75 lakh crore" was the draft plan's FY23–27 total (₹4,75,804 crore, of which ISTS ₹3,13,950 crore). Across the whole plan, the implied inter-state share is about 70–75% (indicative).

Demand driver 1: renewable evacuation

The anchor driver is renewable energy, and the problem is geography. India's best solar and wind sites are far from load: Khavda in Kutch (a ~30 GW park), Bikaner, Barmer, Bhadla and Fatehgarh in Rajasthan, Anantapur and Kurnool in Andhra Pradesh, Koppal, Gadag and Davanagere in Karnataka, Krishnagiri in Tamil Nadu, and 13 GW planned in Ladakh. Every line POWERGRID commissioned in Q1 FY27 was, in management's words, "by and large associated with RE generation". Installed capacity reached 533 GW at March 2026, of which 283 GW was non-fossil. The transmission cost per gigawatt is rising as sites get remoter: the 900 GW plan implies roughly ₹2,000 crore of transmission per GW of additional non-fossil capacity (indicative).

Demand driver 2: peaks, data centres and new loads

Peak demand met rose from 203 GW in FY22 to 250 GW in FY25, dipped to 245 GW in a mild FY26, then set a record of 270.82 GW on 21 May 2026. CEA projects a peak of about 388 GW by FY32 and 459 GW by FY36. Because wires earn on availability, peaks matter through planning rather than through revenue: higher peaks mean more lines, not more income per line. New point loads add to the case. POWERGRID cites about 71 GW of potential data-centre load and ₹13,103 crore of green-hydrogen transmission already before the NCT (Kakinada, Vizag, Tuticorin); it has won a Kakinada green-hydrogen scheme. Storage is becoming a transmission asset class. In plain terms

India has decided to roughly double the size of its high-voltage grid within a decade, because its new solar and wind farms sit in deserts and hills far from the cities that use the power. The money is promised and the projects are being awarded. The lines that can't keep up: a funded plan, an unbuilt grid

India has missed its transmission targets for three years running. The miss is not about money; it is about transformers, land and timelines that were set too tight.

The most important fact about Indian transmission in 2026 is that execution, not demand, is the binding constraint. The plan is approved, the money is available from cheap state-backed lenders, and the awards keep coming. What does not keep up is the physical build. National line additions peaked at about 16,750 ckm in FY21, held near 14,000–15,000 ckm in FY22–FY24, then collapsed to 8,830 ckm in FY25, a decade low. FY26 recovered to 12,139 ckm, but only after the government cut the year's target by 37% in July 2025, from 24,400 ckm to 15,382 ckm. Measured against the original target, the country built half of what it planned.

Why the plans and the build diverge

CTUIL's rolling ISTS plan to 2030–31 envisages 77,228 ckm, 6,97,747 MVA and ₹5,41,481 crore of additions, of which about 42,000 ckm and 414 GVA (₹2.83 lakh crore) were already under construction. A plan that expects four times last year's ISTS build is a wish list, and the slippage is structural.

Four causes recur in company calls and ministry reviews. Equipment lead times have stretched (section 5). Right-of-way disputes have intensified as states adopt market-linked compensation. Scheduled commercial operation dates were set unrealistically short: as little as 18 months for a 765 kV double-circuit line, which the government has since revised to 26–30 months or more. And commissioning depends on other people's assets: a substation is useless until the line into it is finished, and a line built for a solar park earns only "deemed" COD if the park is late. Each cause lengthens the period in which capital is spent but not earning.

05

Transformers, farmers and where ₹100 goes

The supply chain for a high-voltage grid is short, global and sold out. Land is local, political and getting dearer. Both decide whether a bid makes money.

A transmission line is simple to describe and hard to build. It needs steel towers, aluminium conductors, insulators and, at each end, a substation full of transformers, reactors and switchgear. For long-distance renewable corridors it increasingly needs HVDC converter stations. The towers and conductors come from a competitive domestic industry. The large transformers, reactors and converter valves come from a handful of global and Indian manufacturers whose order books are full, because the United States and Europe are rebuilding their grids and data centres are adding load everywhere at once.

The Ministry of Power's review in March 2026 put lead times at 20–24 months for a 765 kV transformer, about 15 months for 400 kV and 11–15 months for 220 kV. For a line with an 18-month SCOD, that alone was fatal: the transformer could not arrive before the deadline. POWERGRID says supply stress is "not as bad as before", that manufacturers are adding capacity and that it now procures in bulk rather than project by project. The government has also permitted four Chinese-linked companies to supply, a reversal of the 2020 restrictions that should ease supply but raises grid-security questions. HVDC converter stations are tighter still: only Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy and GE Vernova build them at scale, and slots are booked years ahead.

Right-of-way: the cost that is not in the plan

The second bottleneck is land. A transmission line does not buy the land under it; it buys the right to place towers and string conductors across it, and compensates owners for tower bases, corridors and lost crops. The Ministry of Power's compensation guidelines, which link payments to market land values, have been adopted by Delhi, Haryana, Gujarat and other states. POWERGRID told analysts that this higher right-of-way cost is not included in the ₹7.9 lakh crore estimate for the 900 GW plan. Technology helps at the margin: insulated cross-arms narrow the corridor by about 20%. But the trend is visible in POWERGRID's accounts. Its contingent liabilities for land, tree and crop compensation claims rose 13% in FY26 to ₹3,974 crore, out of total contingent liabilities of about ₹7,373 crore.

Who sets the return: CERC's formula and its two tiers

For regulated lines, the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission writes the profit formula five years at a time. Its 2024–29 rules quietly cut the return on every new asset.

For RTM lines, profit is not earned in a market; it is calculated. CERC's Terms and Conditions of Tariff Regulations, 2024, notified on 15 March 2024 for the control period FY25–FY29, set out each component of the annual fixed cost (AFC) that a regulated transmission asset may recover from users. The formula rewards three things: the amount of equity deployed in commissioned assets, keeping those assets available above a normative level, and running them within cost norms. It does not reward traffic, and it does not punish a line that carries little power.

Two consequences follow. Second, the legacy book keeps rolling off its 12-year depreciation schedule. In plain terms

The regulator lets POWERGRID earn a fixed percentage on the shareholders' money tied up in each regulated line. For lines switched on before April 2024 that percentage is 15.5%; for newer lines it is 15%. Because older lines have paid off their loans, their tariffs shrink every year even though the profit on them does not, which is why POWERGRID's revenue can stall even when it is adding assets.

Availability, incentives and the 99.75% line

Availability is where management can add profit. A regulated AC line recovers its full AFC if monthly availability reaches 98.00%; HVDC bipoles need 95.00% (85% in their first 12 months). Above 98.50% (97.50% for HVDC) the charge rises in proportion to availability, and the increase stops at 99.75%. The maximum incentive is therefore about 1.27% of AFC. Trippings beyond two a year add 12 outage hours each, and outages that block generation evacuation count double. POWERGRID's investor charts plot availability against a 99.75% line, and some commentary calls that a "normative threshold"; it is in fact the incentive cap. POWERGRID achieved 99.84% in FY26 and earned ₹664 crore of incentive (₹485 crore in FY25), about 4% of profit.

Other levers in the rulebook

Several other rules shape reported earnings. Where CERC's order on a new asset lags its commissioning, the licensee accrues interest on the differential tariff at SBI MCLR plus 150 basis points; this gave POWERGRID ₹705 crore in FY26 but only ₹22 crore in Q1 FY27 (₹257 crore in Q1 FY26) once orders were issued, which explains most of the Q1 profit dip. Deferred tax is recoverable from beneficiaries, so POWERGRID carries a regulatory deferral asset that moves whenever tax rates change; in Q4 FY26 a remeasurement at new-regime rates produced an ₹832 crore tax credit on one side and a reversal of the deferral asset on the other. The first amendment raised the self-insurance allowance to 0.12% of capital cost, and the second, on 20 March 2026, allowed licensees to install battery storage at existing substations under RTM.

Award policy sits above the tariff rules. The Tariff Policy of 2016 requires inter-state transmission to be competitively bid, with exceptions for strategic, urgent or upgrade work. The NCT decides case by case whether grid-strengthening goes to the incumbent; POWERGRID's management says there is "no specific rule". Recent RTM allocations include the Pang–Kaithal HVDC from Ladakh (approved by the Cabinet in October 2023 at ₹20,773.70 crore; NCT cost now ₹22,942 crore, still "awaiting communication from GoI") and offshore-wind evacuation (₹13,142 crore). Both are excluded from POWERGRID's works in hand until confirmed. Distribution is regulated by states: AEML in Mumbai earns 15.5% on wires (14% base plus 1.5% if wires availability is at least 99.5%) and 17.5% on retail supply under MERC's 2024 rules.

RTM vs TBCB: the economics of a 35-year bid Deep dive

The return on India's new transmission lines is migrating from a number the regulator sets to a number bidders accept. On recent evidence, that number is thin.

Two contracts govern every inter-state line in India, and they could hardly be more different. Under the Regulated Tariff Mechanism, the licensee builds, the regulator audits the cost, and the tariff is calculated to give a 15.0–15.5% post-tax return on 30% equity, recalculated each control period and grossed up for tax. Under tariff-based competitive bidding, the licensee promises a fixed annual tariff for 35 years before a single tower is erected; if the build costs more or takes longer, the shortfall is the developer's, and if it costs less, the gain is too. Since 2016 the second route has been the default. In June 2026, 83% of POWERGRID's ₹1,75,700 crore of works in hand was TBCB, against 14% RTM; AESL's under-construction book is almost entirely TBCB.

The accounting row matters more than it seems. POWERGRID accounts for its TBCB subsidiaries as finance leases: it books a lease receivable and lease income rather than an asset and depreciation, and funds the subsidiaries largely with inter-company loans (standalone loans to related parties were ₹56,730 crore at March 2026, up 59%, earning the parent ₹3,350 crore of interest). AESL uses the service-concession model, under which it books construction revenue and a matching receivable while a line is being built. Neither company discloses project-level profit, and POWERGRID's management, asked repeatedly for separate TBCB figures, said only that returns are "well-calibrated" and that it "will examine" more disclosure. Outsiders therefore have to reverse-engineer TBCB returns from tariffs and estimated costs.

The key ratio: annual tariff ÷ project cost

The cleanest available clue is the ratio of the levelised annual tariff to the project's estimated capital cost. A line that costs ₹1,000 crore and bids ₹130 crore a year has a 13% tariff yield; at ₹100 crore a year, 10%. Because operating costs are about 8% of tariff and the tariff is flat for 35 years, that single ratio, together with the debt cost and tenor, largely determines the equity return. The opposite puzzle sits at POWERGRID. On its Q1 FY27 call, an analyst observed that the ₹2,200 crore of annual revenue from FY27's first six wins "seems large" relative to typical revenue per rupee of gross block, which would imply richer tariffs; management promised to share the NCT cost offline. With no project-level disclosure from either company, the defensible conclusion is a range rather than a point estimate. What can be said is that a bid at a 10% tariff yield only works if the bidder can build well under the planner's estimate, refinance cheaply after commissioning, capture construction margin in-house, or sell the asset to an infrastructure trust at a premium.

In plain terms

When a company bids for a transmission line, it promises to accept the same fee every year for 35 years. If that yearly fee is only about a tenth of what the line costs to build, as with AESL's Vizag win, the shareholders' return after paying the banks works out to about 7–8% a year, roughly what a government bond pays today. To earn more, the builder has to spend less than planned, borrow more cheaply later, or sell the finished line to someone happy with a lower return.

Who wins the auctions, and why

POWERGRID is the largest TBCB winner by a distance. Its cumulative share of inter-state TBCB tariff was about 44% at March 2026 and about 47% by July 2026. Four advantages explain who wins. Cost of capital is first: POWERGRID funds TBCB equity entirely from internal accruals (₹9,965 crore operational and ₹3,408 crore under construction at June 2026) and borrows near the sovereign rate, so it can accept a lower tariff for the same return. Procurement scale is second: bulk orders for transformers and reactors give the incumbent priority in factory queues. Vertical integration is third: AESL can capture construction margin through the Adani group's EPC arm (₹1,269 crore of FY26 related-party EPC purchases), which lowers the effective cost of a thin bid. Recycling is fourth: a developer that can sell operating lines to an InvIT at an 8–9% yield earns a development margin even on a modest 35-year IRR. Private developers without one of these advantages have increasingly ceded the field, which is part of why the private sector built only 27% of its FY27 target in H1.

The policy question is whether thin bids are a problem. For consumers they are a gain: a 10% tariff yield instead of RTM's front-loaded 17–19% saves billions over a line's life. For the system they carry risk, because a developer that has bid too low has every incentive to delay, litigate change-in-law claims or under-maintain. For investors the practical point is that TBCB growth is worth less per rupee than RTM growth, and the market should value POWERGRID's 83% TBCB pipeline and AESL's ₹87,222 crore book with that in mind.

In plain terms

POWERGRID wins about half of all auctioned lines because it can borrow almost as cheaply as the government and buys equipment in bulk. AESL wins a large share of what is left because it builds through its own group and can raise equity. Both are now bidding at levels where the return looks modest unless they build cheaply and refinance later, which makes the new pipeline less valuable per rupee than the regulated lines of the past.

Four decades of the grid: from nomination to auction

Transmission has a capex cycle rather than an earnings cycle. Profits lag spending by two to four years, and the defining shocks have been blackouts, policy changes and, for one member, a short-seller and a US indictment.

1989–2013: building the national grid. The National Power Transmission Corporation was incorporated in 1989 and renamed Power Grid Corporation of India in 1992. It took over the transmission assets of the central generating companies, built regional grids and the links between them, introduced 765 kV lines and HVDC, listed in 2007 and became a Maharatna in 2013. The northern and eastern grid collapses of July 2012, which left about 600 million people without power in the world's largest blackout, accelerated inter-regional investment. On 31 December 2013 the southern grid was synchronised with the rest, creating "One Nation, One Grid".

2006–2019: the auction route and the private consolidator. Competitive bidding guidelines for transmission arrived in 2006 and the first inter-state TBCB awards around 2010–11. Sterlite Grid and Adani Transmission emerged as the main private challengers; Reliance Infrastructure exited. Adani Transmission was demerged from Adani Enterprises and listed in 2015, bought TBCB SPVs and, in August 2018, completed the roughly ₹18,800 crore purchase of Reliance Infrastructure's Mumbai integrated utility, which became Adani Electricity Mumbai (AEML). Qatar Investment Authority bought 25.1% of AEML in 2019–20. The 2016 Tariff Policy made competitive bidding the default for inter-state lines.

2019–2023: POWERGRID's flat patch. With the inter-regional backbone largely built, POWERGRID's capex fell to ₹9,000–12,500 crore a year and CWIP shrank from ₹35,177 crore in FY20 to ₹12,854 crore in FY22. Profit plateaued at about ₹15,400–15,600 crore in FY23–FY25. In 2021 POWERGRID listed PGInvIT (₹7,735 crore raised, including a ₹2,736 crore offer for sale), the CTUIL was separated from it, and the government launched the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS) that would later fund smart meters. Adani Transmission was renamed Adani Energy Solutions in July 2023, after the Hindenburg report of January 2023 halved Adani group share prices.

The cycle, characterised

Transmission does not have a demand cycle in the usual sense; revenue does not fall in a recession, because tariffs are paid for availability. It has a capex cycle: a burst of awards and spending, followed two to four years later by capitalisation and earnings. POWERGRID went through one such cycle in FY08–FY19 and is entering the next. The lag is visible in its CWIP, which rose 2.8 times in two years, and in a profit line that has not moved. The risks in this kind of cycle are execution (the capital sits idle longer) and rates (the value of a long-dated, fixed-return stream falls when bond yields rise). Both are biting in 2026.

Reading a wire: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Sixteen measures that explain a transmission company, how each is defined, what good looks like, how the members compare, and where the definitions trip people up.

A transmission company's results look dull: revenue up 2%, margins at 83%, profit flat. The interesting information is in the operating disclosures, and most of it concerns assets that are not yet earning. This section explains the measures that matter, in the order an analyst would use them. It starts with the profit equation, works through each native KPI, builds an illustrative project from scratch, scores the members against benchmarks, collects the definition traps and ends with a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

The profit equation

Every transmission profit can be broken into five terms. The first, return on regulated equity, is set by CERC; the company controls only how much equity it gets into service and when. The second, the TBCB spread, is set at bid time; afterwards the company controls only build cost, timing and refinancing. The third, availability incentive, is controlled by operations but capped at about 1.27% of tariff. The fourth, adjacencies, depends on the company's strategy. The fifth, regulatory drag and one-offs, is mostly arithmetic the company cannot avoid.

1. Gross block and regulated equity

What it is. The gross block is the original cost of all commissioned assets; regulated equity is the part of approved capital cost on which CERC allows a return (normatively 30%). For a regulated utility, regulated equity is the earnings base. Formula. Regulated equity ≈ 30% × approved capital cost of commissioned RTM assets; expected regulated profit ≈ regulated equity × allowed ROE. Worked example. A utility with ₹1,00,000 crore of approved RTM assets has ₹30,000 crore of regulated equity; at 15.5% it should earn about ₹4,650 crore of post-tax profit from those assets, whatever its actual financing. Drivers. Capitalisation adds to it; nothing in the regulated formula subtracts from it except asset retirement, so regulated equity rises steadily in a build cycle. Benchmarks. Growth above 8% a year is strong, 4–8% average, below 4% weak.

The members. POWERGRID's consolidated gross fixed assets were ₹3,20,334 crore at March 2026 (including gross TBCB lease receivables) and ₹3,25,671 crore at June 2026; the standalone gross block was ₹2,75,736 crore. 2. Capitalisation versus capex and CWIP

What it is. Capex is cash spent on assets; capitalisation is the value of assets put into commercial service in a period. Only capitalised assets earn a regulated return or start a TBCB tariff. Formulas. Closing CWIP ≈ opening CWIP + capex − capitalisation. Capitalisation ratio = capitalisation ÷ opening CWIP. Worked example. A company starts the year with ₹36,000 crore of CWIP, spends ₹40,000 crore and capitalises ₹28,000 crore: CWIP ends at about ₹48,000 crore and its capitalisation ratio is 78%. Drivers. Equipment delivery, right-of-way, regulatory DOCO approvals and the readiness of connected generators. Benchmarks. Capitalisation at or above guidance is good, 85–100% average, below 85% weak; a capitalisation ratio above 70% of opening CWIP is good and below 50% signals a pile-up.

The members. POWERGRID capitalised ₹28,206 crore in FY26, 113% of its ₹25,000 crore guidance and about 78% of opening CWIP of ₹36,341 crore (company basis), on capex of ₹39,967 crore. 4. Availability

What it is. The share of time a transmission element is available to carry power, weighted by the value of each element. Formula. Under CERC rules, transmission availability factor for the month = weighted available hours ÷ total hours, after penalties for trippings beyond two a year (12 hours each) and double weighting for outages that block generation. Worked example. A line with ₹100 crore of annual charges at 99.0% availability recovers about ₹100.5 crore; at 97.0% it recovers about ₹99.0 crore. Drivers. Maintenance practice, weather, tripping rates, spare transformers. Benchmarks. At or above 99.75% is good (the incentive cap), 99.0–99.75% average, below 98% weak (charges cut).

The members. POWERGRID has run between 99.82% and 99.85% every year since FY22 (99.84% in FY26, 99.80% in Q1 FY27), with trippings of 0.26 per line in FY26. AESL's revenue-weighted availability was 99.70% in FY26, 99.63% in H1 FY27 (99.71% a year earlier) and 99.8% in Q2. The trap. AESL's number is provisional and revenue-weighted across a smaller, younger network; POWERGRID's 99.75% chart line is the incentive cap, not a normative threshold.

6. Tariff per ckm and yield on gross block

What it is. Two ways of asking how much revenue each unit of network earns. Formulas. Yield on gross block = transmission revenue ÷ gross block; tariff per ckm = transmission revenue ÷ operational ckm. Worked example. ₹44,000 crore of transmission charges on ₹3,20,000 crore of gross block is a 13.7% yield; on 1,85,000 ckm it is about ₹24 lakh per ckm a year. Drivers. Asset age (older RTM assets past their loan period earn less), voltage and substation mix (substations and HVDC earn far more per ckm than lines), and the RTM/TBCB mix. Benchmarks. There is no universal norm; the trend matters more than the level. A falling yield on a growing block signals regulatory drag or low TBCB tariffs.

The members. POWERGRID earned transmission charges of ₹43,962 crore on consolidated gross fixed assets of ₹3,20,334 crore in FY26, a yield of about 13.7%, or about ₹23.8 lakh per ckm on 1,84,960 ckm. For AESL, annualising H1 FY27 transmission operating revenue (₹3,117 crore) gives roughly ₹31 lakh per operational ckm (ILLUSTRATIVE; the segment includes some regulated assets and service-concession income). The trap. Per-ckm comparisons ignore substations (POWERGRID has 291 and 6,34,516 MVA), so a network rich in substations looks artificially lucrative per kilometre.

7. TBCB levelised tariff ÷ project cost

What it is. The single best outside indicator of how aggressive a TBCB bid was (section 7). Formula. Levelised annual tariff ÷ estimated or actual capital cost. Worked example. A ₹600 crore annual tariff on a ₹5,000 crore project is a 12% yield, which in the illustrative model implies about a 10–11% equity IRR. Drivers. Competition in the auction, the bidder's cost of capital, its procurement advantage, and how conservatively the planner estimated cost. Benchmarks. Above 13% good, 11–13% average, below 11% aggressive.

The members. AESL's under-construction book yields about 12.3% (₹10,708 crore ÷ ₹87,222 crore); Vizag about 10.2% on trade-press cost estimates. 8. Return on equity: regulated versus reported

What it is. Regulated ROE is the rate CERC allows on regulated equity; reported ROE is accounting profit divided by book equity. Formula. Drivers. Incentives and efficiencies push reported ROE above the regulated rate; idle equity, CWIP funded by equity, low-return TBCB and equity raised ahead of assets push it below. Benchmarks. 14. Collection efficiency and receivable days

What it is. Whether the money billed actually arrives. Formulas. Collection efficiency = cash realised ÷ amount billed; receivable days = outstanding ÷ (annual billing ÷ 365). Worked example. ₹40,684 crore collected on ₹40,201 crore billed is 101.2%; ₹1,100 crore outstanding on ₹40,000 crore of annual billing is about 10 days. Drivers. The Late Payment Surcharge Rules of 2022, letters of credit and state finances. Benchmarks. At or above 100% good; below 95% weak; receivable days under 30 good. The members. POWERGRID: 101.2% in FY26 and 104.03% in Q1 FY27; receivable days 26.4 in FY24, 10.7 in FY26, 12.1 in Q1 FY27. AEML: 99.75% collection in H1 FY27. The trap. In plain terms

Indian electricity has a long history of state distribution companies paying late. For the national grid, that problem has largely been fixed: since the 2022 late-payment rules, POWERGRID collects slightly more than it bills each year as old arrears come in, and its customers owe it less than two weeks of revenue.

15. Dividend payout and the cash squeeze

What it is. The share of profit paid out as dividends, and whether operating cash flow covers both capex and dividends. Formulas. Payout = dividend per share ÷ EPS; cash coverage = (operating cash flow − capex) ÷ dividends paid. Worked example. A utility with EPS of ₹17 paying ₹9 has a 53% payout; if it generates ₹41,000 crore of operating cash, spends ₹33,000 crore of cash capex and pays ₹8,400 crore of dividends, the gap is funded with about ₹400 crore of new debt, before any growth in working capital. Drivers. Government dividend policy for state-owned companies, the capex cycle and the regulated return. Benchmarks. For an incumbent, a 50–70% payout covered by free cash flow is good; a payout funded by borrowing during a build cycle is a warning, because it adds debt that has to be serviced from future tariffs.

The members. POWERGRID paid ₹9.00 a share for FY26 (interims of ₹4.50 and ₹3.25 and a final of ₹1.25), a 53% payout and about ₹8,371 crore in cash; it paid ₹48,712 crore of dividends over five years. Dividends per share have fallen from ₹12.06 in FY24 and ₹10.50 in FY25 as capex rose, and the payout ratio from 67% in FY23–FY24 to 53–54% in FY25–FY26. At ₹244 the stock yields about 3.7%. AESL pays no dividend and has had negative free cash flow in four of the last five years (−₹3,435 crore in FY26). The trap. A stable dividend per share can hide a payout financed by borrowing; read it alongside free cash flow, which for POWERGRID fell to ₹3,687 crore in FY26.

Definition traps, collected

  • ckm versus route-km. A double-circuit line counts twice. Compare ckm with ckm.
  • Operational versus headline network. AESL's 29,802 ckm includes 9,779 ckm under construction; operational is 20,023 ckm.
  • Capex versus capitalisation versus commissioning. Only capitalised assets earn; POWERGRID's FY26 capex was ₹39,967 crore, capitalisation ₹28,206 crore.
  • Operational versus statutory revenue. AESL's ₹18,296 crore FY26 "operational revenue" excludes service-concession construction income and traded goods; statutory revenue was ₹27,588 crore and total income ₹28,325 crore.
  • Screener operating profit versus company EBITDA. POWERGRID FY26: ₹32,831 crore versus ₹39,677 crore, because regulatory-deferral movements are classified differently.
  • Regulatory deferral balances. Deferred tax is recoverable from customers, so tax-rate changes move a regulatory asset (₹9,877 crore → ₹6,515 crore in FY26) as well as the tax line. EPS excluding these movements was ₹20.11 against ₹17.13 reported.
  • Tax credits in profit. POWERGRID's FY26 PAT growth of 2.6% includes an ₹832 crore deferred-tax credit; excluding it, profit fell about 2.7% (ILLUSTRATIVE). Neither figure is a clean run-rate.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. POWERGRID's two PATs are almost identical (₹15,921 crore and ₹15,928 crore) because TBCB subsidiary profit returns to the parent as interest and dividends.
  • Works in hand versus UC book versus meter book. Cost of projects, cost of projects, and ten-year revenue potential; never add them.
  • Ind AS versus non-Ind AS meter EBITDA. AESL's H1 FY27 smart-meter EBITDA was ₹352 crore (Ind AS) or ₹654 crore (non-Ind AS); never mix them.
  • Reported versus attributable profit. AESL's FY26 profit was ₹2,393 crore including minorities and ₹2,282.54 crore attributable, after QIA's 25.1% share of AEML.
  • One-time regulatory income and prior-period true-ups. AESL adds "one-time regulatory income" to EBITDA; POWERGRID booked ₹617 crore of prior-period income and ₹705 crore of differential-tariff interest in FY26. Strip both before reading growth.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in this sector has a twin that means something different: network that includes unbuilt lines, revenue that includes construction work, profit that includes tax credits, order books that measure different things. Before comparing two numbers, check that both are operational, both are on the same accounting basis and both cover the same period.

How to read a transmission company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Capitalisation first. Pull the quarter's capitalisation and compare it with a quarter of annual guidance (POWERGRID: ₹7,500 crore a quarter for ₹30,000 crore). This is the leading indicator of next year's earnings. Red flag: capitalisation below 70% of run-rate for two quarters.

  2. Capex and CWIP. Note capex and the change in CWIP. Rising CWIP with flat capitalisation means the build is stuck.

  3. Transmission charges, not total income. Growth in tariff revenue, split into new assets and regulatory drag where the company gives the bridge (Q1 FY27: +₹788 crore new, −₹560 crore drag).

  4. Strip the one-offs. Remove differential-tariff interest, prior-period income, tax remeasurement and "one-time regulatory income". What remains is the run-rate.

  5. Availability and incentive. Above 99.75% is at the cap; any fall below 99.5% deserves a question.

  6. Order intake. New TBCB wins, their tariff and, if available, the NCT cost; compute tariff ÷ cost. Red flag: wins below an 11% yield.

  7. Segment split for diversified players. For AESL, separate transmission, distribution, meters and trading; compute growth ex-trading.

  8. Leverage on one basis. Net debt ÷ trailing EBITDA, using the same definition each quarter; note equity raises.

  9. Collections. Collection efficiency and receivable days; a sudden rise in receivables is the earliest sign of counterparty stress.

  10. Guidance and calendar. Capex, capitalisation and commissioning guidance; dates for big auctions; any regulatory orders pending. Red flag: guidance cut without an explanation tied to specific projects.

In plain terms

Read a transmission result backwards from what a newspaper headline reports. Start with what was switched on, then what was won and at what price, then how much of the profit was one-off. Revenue and profit growth in any single quarter tell you the least.

Who is winning: scale, returns and the price of each

With two members, the useful comparison is against reference points: global grid operators, the other Indian owners and the regulated benchmark itself.

Who is winning in Indian transmission depends on the race. In the race to build, POWERGRID is clearly ahead in 2026: it delivered 89% of its full-year line target in H1 FY27, capitalised 113% of guidance in FY26 and is executing faster than at any time in a decade. In the race to win new work it is also ahead, with about 47% of cumulative inter-state TBCB tariff and a 32% win rate by count in FY26, while AESL's share of bids fell to 17% in H1 FY27. In the race for growth in reported profit, AESL is far ahead, but mostly because of trading. In the race for investor favour, neither is winning: both stocks are at or near 52-week lows after the October rate hike.

The first map sets the members against Europe's listed grid operators. POWERGRID, at an 83–85% margin and 9.2 times EBITDA, is priced like Terna and Redeia, which earn far lower allowed returns (roughly 5–7% in Europe) but sit in countries with much lower bond yields. AESL is priced for growth, at 22 times FY26 EBITDA on a 32% blended margin. The combined figure, 11.5 times, is close to the European level, which is a coincidence of mix rather than a meaningful benchmark.

The second map is the more telling. If allowed returns were the main driver of value, POWERGRID would trade at the highest multiple. It trades at the lowest, because the value of a fixed 15% return depends on the cost of capital against which it is measured, and India's 10-year bond yields 7.47%. US utilities earn about 10.5% allowed returns on far lower bond yields and trade at 18–24 times. AESL's 40-times trailing multiple sits far above anything its implied TBCB returns (roughly 10–11% on its book, ILLUSTRATIVE) would justify; the premium is for growth and for adjacencies that are not regulated wires.

The third map compares current scale with future work. POWERGRID is in a class of its own on both axes. AESL is pipeline-heavy: ₹87,222 crore of projects against 20,023 operational ckm, so its value depends more on delivery than POWERGRID's does. The other private owners and trusts are an order of magnitude smaller on both axes, which explains why, in practice, the large HVDC auctions come down to POWERGRID, AESL and Resonia.

Smart meters and RDSS: the meter as a ten-year annuity Deep dive

A government scheme has sanctioned 20 crore smart meters and installed 28% of them with 21 months to go. AESL holds the largest private book, concentrated in one state utility.

Smart metering looks like a distribution topic, but it has become one of AESL's growth engines and a small business for POWERGRID, and its economics resemble transmission more than retail. Under the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS), launched in July 2021 with an outlay of ₹3.03 lakh crore (gross budgetary support ₹97,631 crore), state distribution companies hire an Advanced Metering Infrastructure Service Provider (AMISP) to design, build, finance, own, operate and eventually transfer a fleet of smart meters with their communication network and software. The AMISP is paid a fixed fee per meter per month, typically for 120 months. It is, in effect, a ten-year availability contract with a discom as counterparty.

The scheme's smart-meter component sanctions 20.33 crore meters (19.79 crore consumer meters, 52.53 lakh distribution-transformer meters and 2.05 lakh feeder meters) worth ₹1.31 lakh crore, with gross budgetary support of ₹24,173 crore. Installation has been slow but is accelerating: 3.90 crore by December 2025, 4.69 crore by March 2026 and 5.73 crore by 30 June 2026, 28.2% of the sanctioned total, according to parliamentary replies; across all schemes, 7.24 crore smart meters had been installed. The scheme is due to end on 31 March 2028. Installing the remaining ~14.6 crore in 21 months would need about 1.9 crore a quarter, nearly double the recent pace of about 1 crore. Either the deadline is extended or installations roughly double.

The economics of a meter

The economics can be sketched with round numbers. AESL's book of ₹29,519 crore across 24.6 million meters over 120 months implies an average fee of about ₹100 per meter per month (ILLUSTRATIVE; contract fees range widely). At that fee the 14.8 million installed meters would generate about ₹1,780 crore a year once all are billing. Actual non-Ind AS meter revenue in H1 FY27 was ₹731 crore, about ₹1,460 crore annualised, because billing lags installation (cumulative meter-months billed were 74.5 million in H1). The non-Ind AS EBITDA margin was 89%. If a meter costs ₹6,000–7,000 installed (an indicative, single-source estimate), each earns about ₹1,070 a year of EBITDA, a payback of roughly six years and an unlevered return of about 10% over ten years (ILLUSTRATIVE). The IntelliSmart price works out to about ₹1,370 per contracted meter.

In plain terms

A smart-meter contract pays a fixed fee for each installed meter every month for ten years. AESL has signed up nearly 25 million meters, more than half of them for one Maharashtra utility, and has installed about 15 million. The pushback risk

Consumer acceptance is the scheme's weak point, and prepaid metering is the flashpoint. In May 2026 Uttar Pradesh moved all smart meters to postpaid mode and suspended prepaid replacements after billing complaints; about 83 lakh of its 86.5 lakh smart-meter users had been on prepaid. The Union Power Minister told the Lok Sabha in April 2026 that prepaid was never mandatory. West Bengal suspended residential installations in June 2025. None of AESL's contracts is in those states, but the pattern matters for any AMISP: postpaid conversion changes the discom's cash-collection benefit (and hence its enthusiasm), and political resistance slows installation, which is when the AMISP's capital is most exposed. Communication failures between meter and head-end drive many of the billing complaints, so execution quality is part of the risk. AESL appointed a chief information security officer on 22 October 2026, a reminder that a meter network is also a cyber-security exposure.

What the multiples imply

A simple check shows what the market is assuming. For a company earning a steady ROE with constant growth, price-to-book equals (ROE − g) ÷ (cost of equity − g). POWERGRID trades at about 2.26 times book with a 15.3% ROE. Assuming 5% long-run growth, the implied cost of equity is about 9.6% (ILLUSTRATIVE), only about two percentage points above the sovereign yield, which is low for an equity but consistent with a near-sovereign, regulated cash flow. For AESL, at 4.8 times book and a 9.4% ROE, the same formula only works if investors expect ROE to rise sharply as the under-construction book and meters start earning, or growth to stay high for many years. The market is paying for AESL's future returns, not its present ones.

In plain terms

When government bonds pay 7.47% with no risk, a share whose profits equal 7% of its price looks less attractive, even if those profits are very safe. That is the main reason POWERGRID's share price fell to a one-year low in October 2026. Nothing about its business changed; the alternative got better.

The global lens

Globally, transmission businesses look remarkably alike operationally and very different financially. Terna in Italy, Redeia in Spain and National Grid's UK transmission arm run EBITDA margins of roughly 70–80%, similar to POWERGRID's 83% and AESL's 92% on transmission. The business model travels; the cost of capital does not. Indian allowed returns (15.0–15.5% nominal post-tax) are roughly twice European regulated returns (about 5–7%; the UK's RIIO-T3 framework from April 2026 allows about 6% real) and well above the US federal base ROE of about 10.5% for transmission. China's State Grid, the world's largest utility with more than a million km of lines, is state-owned and unlisted.

HVDC, storage and the technology of the next grid

The technology risks in transmission are mostly about supply, not obsolescence. Contracts already signed are safe; the shape of future awards is not.

The biggest technology shift in Indian transmission is the move to high-voltage direct current for long renewable corridors. HVDC loses less power over long distances, lets operators control flows precisely and, in its voltage-source-converter (VSC) form, can connect weak grids and undersea links. India already has about 33,500 MVA of HVDC terminal capacity, more than half at ±800 kV. POWERGRID counts a pipeline of 22 HVDC schemes totalling about 127 GW: 14 in the 900 GW plan, 6 for Brahmaputra-basin hydro and 2 cross-border or undersea links (a 500 MW VSC link to Sri Lanka and a ±320 kV VSC link from Paradeep to the Andaman Islands). The constraint is converter supply, not demand: only three or four global manufacturers build HVDC valves at scale.

For AESL, HVDC is the centre of its future. Its two under-construction HVDC projects, Bhadla–Fatehpur (1,906 ckm, ₹3,557 crore a year) and KPS III–South Olpad (1,168 ckm, ₹2,392 crore a year), carry about 55% of its TBCB tariff under construction, and both are scheduled for 2029. AESL describes itself as the only private player with four HVDC lines (two operating: Mundra–Mohindergarh and Mumbai). A delay in converter delivery on either project would shift a large share of AESL's contracted growth by a year or more; an early commissioning would do the reverse. The Barmer HVDC auction (₹24,974 crore) will show whether AESL wants to add a third.

UHVAC, HVDC and batteries compete as planning options. India has tested 1,200 kV alternating current; HVDC is preferred for bulk long-distance flows; and battery storage co-located with solar can reduce the peak transmission capacity needed per gigawatt of panels. POWERGRID's view is that batteries complement rather than replace HVDC. Co-located storage is a long-run risk to the volume of new awards, but it does not touch existing RTM or TBCB contracts, which are paid on availability. CERC's 2026 amendment turns the threat partly into an opportunity, since transmission licensees can now own storage at their substations under the regulated route.

Grid stability is creating new regulated asset classes. As inverter-based renewables displace spinning coal turbines, the grid loses inertia and short-circuit strength. Synchronous condensers and STATCOMs restore it; POWERGRID won India's first synchronous-condenser TBCB scheme (Fatehgarh-II) in FY27. Digital operations lower costs and land needs: POWERGRID claims the world's first 765 kV digital substation, indigenous 220/132 kV mobile gas-insulated substations, insulated cross-arms that cut corridor width by about 20%, Asia's first synthetic-ester transformer, and drone and AI-based line patrols.

In plain terms

The grid of the 2030s will lean on direct-current super-highways from deserts and mountains to cities, plus batteries and stabilising machines that the old coal-based grid did not need. None of this threatens lines already built, because they are paid for being available. It does decide who wins the next decade's contracts, and the bottleneck is that only a handful of factories in the world make the key HVDC equipment.

Bull vs bear: the case for the wires and the case against

The bull case is a state-guaranteed decade of investment bought at a 52-week low. The bear case is thin bids, slow builds, a rate cycle and a trading engine dressed as a utility.

The sub-sector divides opinion less on demand, which nobody disputes, than on three questions: whether execution will catch up with the plan, whether the returns on new work will be adequate, and what the cost of capital will be. The two members sit at opposite ends of each question, so the arguments below are framed for the sub-sector and then applied to each company.

The bull case

  • A plan that cannot be cancelled. ₹9.15 lakh crore of NEP spend plus ₹7.93 lakh crore for the 900 GW plan; inter-regional capacity must rise from 120 GW to 168 GW, and curtailment makes the need visible.
  • Execution has turned at the incumbent. POWERGRID delivered 89% of its FY27 line target in H1 and capitalised 113% of guidance in FY26; ₹50,419 crore of CWIP is stored-up earnings for FY28–FY29.
  • Regulated returns locked to 2029. 15.0–15.5% post-tax ROE with tax gross-up, full tariff at 98% availability, and collections above 100% of billing.
  • Valuation reset. POWERGRID at 14.3 times earnings, 9.2 times EBITDA and a 3.7% dividend yield at a 52-week low; a fall in bond yields would re-rate it quickly.
  • AESL's growth engines. ₹87,222 crore under construction, 14.8 million meters installed with 22.3 million more to come via IntelliSmart, AEML's regulated base growing 11%, and a trading platform moving toward long-term contracts such as the 2,500 MW MSEDCL deal.
  • Legal overhang lifted. US fraud counts against three Adani executives dismissed with prejudice; AESL was never named.

The bear case

  • Thin auction returns. Vizag priced at about 10.2% of cost, consistent with a 7–8% equity IRR (indicative); 83% of POWERGRID's pipeline and nearly all of AESL's is TBCB.
  • Lower returns on new regulated assets. 15.0% ROE and slower depreciation for assets from April 2024, plus a FY30–34 reset that could cut further.
  • Execution and land. Private developers built 27% of their FY27 target in H1; AESL added no operational ckm in six months; land-compensation claims at POWERGRID rose 13%.
  • Rates. The 10-year G-sec at 7.47% exceeds POWERGRID's earnings yield; one or two more hikes are possible.
  • Earnings quality at AESL. 24% of H1 operating EBITDA from a trading engine that sources about 80% of supply from Adani Green, with FY26 related-party purchases of about ₹22,207 crore.
  • Cash. POWERGRID's free cash flow fell to ₹3,687 crore and its dividend per share has fallen; AESL's free cash flow is negative and its QIP buyers are 24% under water.

Applied to POWERGRID, the debate is mostly about timing and rates. The bull case needs capitalisation to hold near ₹30,000 crore a year and bond yields to stabilise; the bear case needs only for capitalisation to slip and yields to keep rising. At 14 times earnings with a 3.7% yield, much of the bear case already appears to be priced. Applied to AESL, the debate is about quality. The bull case needs the HVDC projects to commission on schedule in 2029, the meter book to keep billing and the trading engine to convert to long-term contracts; the bear case needs only for trading margins to normalise toward ₹650–1,300 crore a year (ILLUSTRATIVE) while the HVDCs slip, which would leave a ~40 times trailing multiple resting on transmission and meter profits that have not yet arrived.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • AFC (annual fixed cost): the yearly tariff a regulated (RTM) transmission asset may recover: return on equity, interest, depreciation, O&M and working-capital interest.
  • AMISP: Advanced Metering Infrastructure Service Provider; installs, finances and runs smart meters for a discom for a monthly fee per meter.
  • Availability (TAFM / NATAF): the share of time a transmission element is available; full tariff at 98% (AC) or 95% (HVDC), incentive from 98.5% (AC) capped at 99.75%.
  • BPC: bid process coordinator (RECPDCL or PFCCL) that runs TBCB auctions.
  • Capitalisation: the value of assets put into commercial service; only capitalised assets earn.
  • ckm (circuit-km): line length counted per circuit; a double-circuit line counts twice.
  • COD / SCOD / DOCO: commercial operation date; scheduled COD in the contract; date of commercial operation declared for a regulated asset.
  • CTUIL: Central Transmission Utility of India, the inter-state system planner, separated from POWERGRID in 2021.
  • CWIP: capital work in progress, money spent on assets not yet in service.
  • DIC / GNA: designated ISTS customer; General Network Access, the regime since Oct-2023 under which users book network access and pay per MW.
  • ESP: AESL's Energy Solutions Platform, a trading business on contracted renewable and storage capacity.
  • GIS: gas-insulated substation, compact switchgear used where land is scarce.
  • HVDC / VSC: high-voltage direct current; voltage-source converter, the HVDC technology suited to weak grids and undersea links.
  • InSTS / ISTS: intra-state and inter-state transmission systems.
  • InvIT: infrastructure investment trust that owns operating assets and distributes cash (PGInvIT, IndiGrid).
  • IR capacity: inter-regional transfer capacity between India's five regional grids (1,20,340 MW at Mar-26).
  • MVA: mega-volt-ampere, the unit of transformation (substation) capacity.
  • NCT / NCT cost: National Committee on Transmission, which decides the award route and estimates scheme cost.
  • NEP: National Electricity Plan; the transmission volume for 2023–32 envisages ₹9.15 lakh crore of investment.
  • RAB: regulated asset base on which a distributor earns its allowed return (AEML ₹10,456 Cr).
  • RDSS: Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (2021), which funds smart meters and discom upgrades.
  • Regulatory deferral account: a balance for amounts recoverable from or payable to customers under regulation, e.g. deferred tax at POWERGRID.
  • Regulatory drag: the fall in an RTM asset's tariff once its normative loan is repaid, while its ROE stays constant.
  • Right-of-way (ROW): permission and compensation for running lines and towers over private land.
  • RTM: Regulated Tariff Mechanism, cost-plus tariff under section 62 of the Electricity Act.
  • SCA income: construction revenue booked under service-concession accounting while lines or meters are built.
  • TBCB: tariff-based competitive bidding; a 35-year levelised tariff won in a reverse auction under section 63.
  • Works in hand (WIH): POWERGRID's estimated cost of projects won or allotted but not yet commissioned, including amounts already spent.
