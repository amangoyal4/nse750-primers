Indian general insurance: losing on risk, earning on the float

A complete guide to India's non-life insurers: why an industry that pays out more in claims and costs than it earns in premium still makes money, how health overtook motor as the biggest line after the GST cut, why motor is a price war on one side and a bond fund on the other, what the government's PSU insurers and the national reinsurer are really worth, and how a regulator rewriting commissions and accounting at the same time changes every number you read.

00

The one-page view: an industry that loses on risk and earns on money it holds

₹3.36 lakh crore of premium, a 113% combined ratio, and profits that come almost entirely from investing other people's money until claims fall due.

General insurance is the business of selling one-year (and sometimes multi-year) promises against bad things: a hospital bill, a car crash, a factory fire, a failed harvest, a cyclone. India's general insurers wrote ₹3,36,121 crore of gross direct premium (GDPI) in FY26, up 9%, or 11.1% if you strip out an accounting change that now spreads multi-year policies over their life. That makes India roughly the 15th-largest non-life market in the world, yet the premium is still only about 1% of GDP, around a quarter of the global average.

The single most important fact about the industry is that, taken as a whole, it does not make money from insurance. In FY26 the sector's combined ratio, which is claims plus expenses as a share of premium, was 113%. For every ₹100 of premium earned, insurers paid out or spent about ₹113. BCG's sector round-up puts the underwriting loss at roughly ₹30,000 crore. The industry still reported a profit, roughly ₹10,000 crore after tax (down 23%) and a return on equity of about 6%, because it earned about ₹36,000 crore of investment income on the premiums and reserves it holds before claims are paid. That pool of held money is called the float, and it is the real engine of the business.

Averages hide three very different industries. The four government-owned multi-line insurers (New India, United India, Oriental and National) ran a combined ratio of around 124–128% and, apart from New India, earned negative returns; BCG's FY26 chart shows three of the four with negative solvency margins, a reading we treat as indicative because it comes off a chart. The private multi-line insurers ran about 109% and earned mid-teens returns. The standalone health insurers (SAHIs) ran close to 100–104% and grew fastest, lifting their share of the market from 12% to 14% in a single year.

Five forces shape everything else in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them:

  • The float economy. Underwriting loses money for the industry; investment income on reserves pays the bills. Winners are the insurers that keep the combined ratio near 100% while holding the most invested float per rupee of capital. A 1-point move in ICICI Lombard's combined ratio is worth about ₹230 crore of pre-tax profit.
  • Health's rise. Health is now 44% of premium and growing about 17% a year, helped by the removal of GST on individual policies from 22 September 2025. It is also a fight on three fronts: medical inflation, hospitals that want higher tariffs, and multi-line insurers buying retail share.
  • Motor's two halves. Own-damage (OD) cover is in a price war (combined ratio 132%, return on equity −34%), while third-party (TP) cover, priced by the government and unrevised since June 2022, earns +22% because its claims take years to settle and the reserves sit invested.
  • The PSU problem. Government insurers are losing share about a point a year, run combined ratios in the 120s to 140s, and face either recapitalisation, privatisation or share sales to meet the 25% public-float rule.
  • A regulator rewriting the rules. The 1/n premium rule (October 2024), the GST exemption (September 2025), Ind AS accounting (April 2026) and now a proposal to squeeze expenses of management to 20% of premium move reported numbers more than the underlying business does.
In plain terms

A general insurer collects your premium today and pays claims later. If it prices well, the claims and costs come to a little less than the premium and it keeps the difference. In India, the industry as a whole prices badly: it pays out ₹113 for every ₹100 it earns. It survives because it invests the premiums in bonds and shares between collecting them and paying claims, and the investment income more than covers the gap. So the two questions that matter for any Indian insurer are: how close to break-even is its underwriting, and how much money does it get to invest per rupee of shareholders' capital?

The investment case follows from those two questions. The market pays 4–6 times book value for the private insurers that combine near-break-even underwriting with growth (Go Digit 5.7×, ICICI Lombard 4.4×, Star Health 4.2× on its IGAAP book, Niva Bupa 3.8×) and about 1 times book or less for the government-controlled pair (New India 1.0×, GIC Re 0.77×). That spread, from roughly ₹0.77 to ₹5.70 of market value per rupee of book, is one of the widest in any Indian financial sub-sector, and section 17 explains how much of it is justified.

Two reading rules apply throughout. First, always ask which basis a number is on. Star Health's FY26 profit is ₹557 crore under the old Indian accounting rules (IGAAP) but ₹911 crore under the new Ind AS standard; Niva Bupa's is ₹131 crore versus ₹365 crore. Star's FY26 premium is ₹18,606 crore as reported under the 1/n rule but about ₹20,369 crore on the older upfront basis. Second, always ask which quarter: ICICI Lombard has already reported Q2 FY27 (to September 2026), while the other five are still at Q1 FY27 (to June 2026). This report labels both every time.

In plain terms

Insurance accounts in India are in the middle of three rule changes at once, so the same company can show three different "premiums" and two different "profits" for the same year. None of that changes the cash the business generates. Before comparing two insurers, put them on the same basis; if you cannot, compare each company only with its own history.

01

How a general insurer earns: premium in, claims out, money in between

The value chain runs from a policyholder through a distributor and an insurer to a reinsurer, and every link takes a share of the premium.

Indian law requires premium to be paid before cover begins (section 64VB of the Insurance Act), so an insurer is always paid first and pays later. That timing gap is the reason the business exists in its current shape. A policy is sold by a distributor, priced and underwritten by a primary insurer, partly passed on to a reinsurer, and eventually settled through a hospital, a garage, a surveyor or a court. Each step takes a cut, and the cuts have grown faster than premium in recent years, which is why the regulator is now intervening.

Value is captured in two places: by the insurer that prices retail risk well and holds long-tail reserves (the float), and increasingly by distributors whose commissions have outrun premium growth.

Each step deserves a word. Distribution in India is fragmented and expensive. Star Health runs more than 8.5 lakh agents; ICICI Lombard works through 830 brokers, 351 corporate agents (mostly banks) and about 1.7 lakh agents and point-of-sale persons; Go Digit sells almost entirely through brokers, dealers, aggregators and partner apps. In motor, the car or two-wheeler dealer usually decides which insurer a buyer gets, and dealers have used that power to push commissions up. The regulator's September 2026 consultation paper cites IRDAI data showing motor premium up 34% between FY23 and FY25 while motor commission rose 259%.

The primary insurer is where the risk sits. It decides whom to cover and at what price, sets aside reserves for claims that have happened but not yet been settled (including those not yet reported, known as IBNR), and invests those reserves until they are paid. India has 36 licensed general insurers in H1 FY27, up from 4 in FY01: the four PSU multi-line companies, about 20 private multi-line companies, seven standalone health insurers and two specialised state insurers (the Agriculture Insurance Company for crop and ECGC for export credit).

Reinsurance is insurance for insurers. Indian insurers pass on about 31% of their direct premium: 82% of fire, 56% of crop, 49% of other commercial lines, but only about 20% of motor and 19% of health, because those are many small, predictable risks. By law, 4% of most policies must first be offered to GIC Re (the "obligatory cession"), and GIC Re also gets first right of refusal on other cessions. A reinsurer pays the ceding insurer a commission on the business it accepts, which partly offsets the ceding insurer's acquisition costs.

Claims settlement is where the industry's arguments live. In health, insurers negotiate package rates with hospitals and fight over tariffs; in 2025 several insurers suspended cashless treatment at Max Healthcare's 22 hospitals and the hospitals' association threatened the same against Star Health (section 8 and the Hospitals report). In motor own-damage, garages and parts prices set the cost. In motor third-party, compensation for death and injury is set by Motor Accident Claims Tribunals and is uncapped, which is why those claims take three to seven years to settle and why the reserves are so valuable to invest.

Large, lumpy risks (a refinery, a port, a district's harvest) are mostly reinsured; many small, predictable risks (cars, family health policies) are mostly kept. By archetype, PSU insurers cede only 16% of premium against 44% for the large privates.

Where is value captured? Three places. First, in retail lines with pricing power and renewal stickiness: individual health, personal accident and small-business property. A retail health customer who has served a three-year waiting period for pre-existing conditions rarely switches, so renewals are an annuity. Second, in long-tail lines that generate float, chiefly motor TP and liability, where even a combined ratio above 100% can earn a good return because the reserves sit invested for years. Third, increasingly, in distribution: brokers, dealers and banks have taken a rising share of premium, which is precisely what the IRDAI's 2026 commission proposals target.

In plain terms

Selling a policy, carrying the risk, insuring the insurer and paying the claim are four separate jobs, often done by four different firms. The insurer is the only one that both takes the risk and holds the cash. That is its weakness (it pays when things go wrong) and its strength (it earns interest on the cash while it waits).

The same structure exists in life insurance, but the economics are different: a life insurer's float lasts decades and most of its profit comes from managing savings, while a general insurer's float lasts months (health) to years (motor TP) and profit hinges on the annual price of risk. The Life Insurance report covers that side, including the distribution reforms that apply to both.

02

Size, growth and the 1/n blur: a ₹3.36 lakh crore pool compounding at 13%

Premium has quadrupled in eleven years, but two accounting changes and a crop retreat make the last three years look slower than they were.

Indian general insurance is a large market that is still small for the economy it serves. GDPI was about ₹10,087 crore in FY2001, ₹29,498 crore in FY2008 and ₹3,36,121 crore in FY26, a compound growth rate of about 14.5% a year since FY08. In H1 FY27 the industry wrote ₹1,79,611 crore, up 8.9% on the same half a year earlier. On a nominal GDP of roughly ₹357 lakh crore, FY26 premium is about 0.94% of GDP; the global average for non-life premium is around 4%. That gap, rather than any single product, is the long-run growth argument.

The growth path has three phases. From FY15 to FY20, premium more than doubled, driven by the launch of the government's crop scheme, PMFBY, in Kharif 2016 (GDPI jumped about 32% in FY17), by mandatory multi-year motor TP for new vehicles from September 2018, and by steady health growth. COVID slowed FY21 to about 5%, but it also made health cover a household priority, and FY22–FY24 grew 11–16% a year. Then FY25 and FY26 slowed to 6% and 9%. Part of that slowdown is real: crop premium fell 27% in FY26 to about ₹22,000 crore as state tenders were re-bid at lower rates and some insurers walked away. But a large part is an accounting change.

Why reported growth understates the business: the 1/n rule

Until September 2024, an insurer that sold a three-year car TP policy or a five-year two-wheeler TP policy, or a multi-year health policy, could book the entire premium as written premium in the year of sale. From 1 October 2024, the IRDAI requires such long-term policies to be recognised one-nth per year of the term. A ₹15,000 five-year two-wheeler TP policy that used to add ₹15,000 to GDPI in the year of sale now adds ₹3,000 a year for five years. The cash is still collected upfront; only the reported premium changes.

The effect is a temporary hole in reported growth until the stream of one-nth slices from earlier years builds back up, roughly from H2 FY27. ICRA estimates FY26 GDPI growth at 11.1% excluding the 1/n effect, against 9% reported. Companies now publish both: ICICI Lombard calls the old basis the "n basis", Star Health the "N basis" and Niva Bupa "without 1/n". For a multi-year-heavy book the difference is large: Star's FY26 premium is ₹18,606 crore (1/n, +11%) or about ₹20,369 crore (N basis, +16%); Niva's is ₹8,586 crore (+27.0%) or ₹9,433 crore (+27.4%).

In plain terms

Since October 2024, if you buy a five-year policy, your insurer can only count one-fifth of your payment as this year's sales, even though it has all the money in the bank. That makes the industry look as if it is growing more slowly than it is, and it makes insurers that sell many long policies (two-wheeler TP, multi-year health) look slowest of all. Always check whether a growth rate is "1/n" (reported) or "n" (as if the old rule still applied).

03

What buys insurance: hospital bills, new vehicles, factories and the monsoon

Each line has its own demand driver, and only one of them, retail health, is driven by individual choice.

Demand for general insurance is a patchwork. Motor TP is compulsory by law. Crop insurance is bought largely by the state on farmers' behalf. Group health is bought by employers. Fire and engineering cover are bought by companies, often because a lender requires it. Only retail health, personal accident and some home and travel policies are bought by individuals choosing to protect themselves, which is why that segment carries the best margins and attracts the most competition.

Health: under-insurance, medical inflation and a tax cut

Out-of-pocket spending is still the largest source of health spending in India, at roughly 40–50% of current health expenditure on National Health Accounts estimates. A single hospitalisation can wipe out a family's savings, so demand for cover rises with income and with awareness, both of which jumped after COVID. Medical costs rise faster than general prices: Niva Bupa's own retail claims-cost index has compounded at 7.5% a year since FY19 (5.4% over five years), lower than the double-digit "medical inflation" quoted in the press because insurers negotiate package rates and steer patients to preferred hospitals. Rising costs push up premiums, which raises the rupee size of the market even when the number of insured people grows more slowly.

The GST exemption on individual health policies from 22 September 2025 (previously taxed at 18%) was the biggest single demand event in the line's recent history. Retail health premium was reported up about 19% to ₹48,952 crore in the eleven months to February 2026, a figure from a single secondary source that we treat as INDICATIVE. By Q2 FY27 industry retail health was growing 32% a year, and the listed players were growing faster still.

Motor: vehicle sales, the vehicle park and premiumisation

Every vehicle on Indian roads must carry third-party cover, and most financed vehicles also carry own-damage cover. New-vehicle sales therefore feed the line directly (and through multi-year TP policies sold at purchase), while the much larger stock of older vehicles feeds renewals. Auto sales grew 10.4% in FY26 but motor premium grew only 9.2%, because own-damage prices fell in a price war and because the government-set TP tariff has not been raised since June 2022. Electric vehicles get a 15% discount on TP premium but cost more to repair. The Two-Wheelers and Auto Components reports cover the vehicle cycle that drives this line.

Commercial lines: capex and reinsurance prices

Fire, engineering, marine and liability cover follow industrial investment, trade and infrastructure. Fire premium rose 13% in FY26, but large corporate renewals are being priced aggressively and insurers such as ICICI Lombard have walked away: its fire book shrank by 21–32% year on year in Q1 and Q2 FY27. Because 82% of fire premium is reinsured, global reinsurance prices also set the floor for Indian commercial rates; a hard reinsurance market in 2023–24 helped, and a softer one in 2025–26 has hurt.

Crop: a policy decision, not a purchase

PMFBY runs in 25 states and union territories for Kharif 2026. Farmers pay capped premiums of 2% (Kharif), 1.5% (Rabi) and 5% (commercial and horticultural crops); the Centre and states split the rest 50:50 (90:10 in north-eastern and Himalayan states). The Union Budget for 2026-27 allocates ₹12,200 crore. Between 2016-17 and 2023-24, farmers paid ₹32,475 crore of premium and received ₹1,72,138 crore of claims, more than five times what they paid. Crop premium therefore rises and falls with state tenders and with insurers' appetite, which turned negative after loss-making years: crop GDPI fell 27% in FY26, and ICICI Lombard's crop share of its book fell from about 5% in FY25 to 0.3% in H1 FY27.

Catastrophes: the swing factor

Floods and cyclones drive commercial and reinsurance pricing. The Chennai floods of 2015 cost insurers roughly ₹5,000 crore (approximate), Cyclone Michaung in 2023 about ₹2,200 crore, and ICICI Lombard booked a ₹118 crore catastrophe loss from a Gujarat event in Q2 FY27. In plain terms

Most general insurance in India is bought because someone else insists on it: the law (car TP), a lender (fire), an employer (group health) or the state (crop). Those buyers shop on price and switch freely. The one large pool of people who choose to buy, and then rarely leave, is families buying their own health cover. That is why every insurer is chasing retail health, and why it is the most fought-over line in the market.

Claims: 70–100% of earned premium

Claims are the largest cost by far, and their drivers differ by line. In health they are hospital tariffs, the cost of drugs and implants, the share of senior citizens in the book, and fraud. In motor own-damage they are parts and labour, with electric vehicles and cars carrying driver-assistance sensors costing more to repair. In motor TP they are court awards for death and injury, which rise with wages and with Supreme Court rulings on how compensation is calculated, and which have no upper limit. In fire they are the frequency of catastrophes. The PSU insurers' ₹98 of claims per ₹100 reflects a book heavy in group and government health, motor TP written at the regulated price, and commercial risks they were slow to reprice.

Commission: the fastest-growing cost

Operating expenses: staff, branches, technology and tax

Operating costs run at ₹13–20 per ₹100 of NEP. Reinsurance and capital: the two inputs that set capacity

An insurer cannot write unlimited premium. It needs capital to hold the regulatory solvency margin (at least 1.5 times the required amount) and it needs reinsurance to cap its losses on large and correlated risks. Reinsurance costs money: the insurer gives up premium and receives a reinsurance commission and claim recoveries in return. Capital is consumed by growth: Niva Bupa's solvency ratio fell from 3.03× to 2.25× in about fifteen months as it grew premium by 27–32% a year. These two inputs are the industry's version of "capacity": an insurer that grows fast without either will eventually have to stop selling or raise money.

In plain terms

For an average Indian insurer, ₹100 of earned premium turns into about ₹81 of claims, ₹15 of commission and ₹18 of salaries, rent and systems, which is ₹114 in all. Government insurers pay out almost all their premium in claims before any costs at all. Private multi-line insurers keep claims to about ₹75 but pay more to the people who sell their policies. Health specialists keep claims lowest but have the least money to invest, because hospital bills get paid quickly.

The picture also explains why the ₹100 of NEP is the wrong place to stop. Two insurers with identical underwriting losses can produce very different profits depending on how much money they hold per rupee of premium and how well they invest it. A large private multi-line insurer earns ₹21 of investment income per ₹100 of NEP; a standalone health insurer earns ₹8. That difference, not underwriting skill, is the main reason large privates earned a 15% return in FY26 and health specialists 1%. The next section takes that float apart.

In plain terms

If two shops both sell at a 10% loss but one of them holds its customers' cash for three years before delivering and the other for three weeks, the first can earn enough interest to make a profit and the second cannot. That is the difference between a motor TP book and a health book.

The industry lost about ₹30,000 crore on underwriting in FY26 and earned about ₹36,000 crore on investments. Understanding the float is understanding the business.

The float is the money an insurer holds between collecting premium and paying claims. It has two parts. The unexpired-risk (unearned premium) reserve is premium for cover not yet delivered; on a one-year policy bought in January, about three-quarters is still unearned at the end of March. The claims reserves are money set aside for claims already incurred but not yet paid, including those not yet even reported (IBNR). Add shareholders' capital, and you have the insurer's investment book. The insurer earns the investment return on all of it but owes the policyholders only the claims.

What the money is invested in

The IRDAI sets investment rules: a minimum share in government securities, limits on equity and on exposure to any single group, and a ban on most speculative assets. In plain terms

A large part of what Indian insurers report as "profit" is really the return on a bond-and-equity portfolio. When interest rates are high or the stock market is rising, profits look good even if underwriting is getting worse. When the stock market falls, the PSU insurers in particular have nothing left to cover their underwriting losses. Always split an insurer's profit into the underwriting part and the investment part before judging it.

The limits of living on the float

Float economics has three weaknesses. First, it rewards growth in long-tail lines even when they are underpriced, which is one reason motor TP and group health have been written at losses for years. Second, it makes reported profit hostage to the bond and equity markets: a fall in yields of one percentage point would take roughly 2–3 points off the ROE of a leveraged private insurer, by the sensitivities above. Third, reserves are estimates. If TP court awards rise faster than assumed, an insurer has to strengthen reserves, which shows up as a sudden jump in claims for past years. New India's Q1 FY27 loss, driven by a jump in its claims ratio to 103.4% on motor TP, is a reminder of how that looks.

The listed insurers sit at different points on this spectrum. ICICI Lombard and GIC Re combine large floats with near-100% combined ratios and earn 15–17% returns. New India has a large float but a combined ratio of 121–122%, which the float cannot fully cover in a bad quarter. Star Health and Niva Bupa have small floats and must make money on underwriting itself. Go Digit has a large float relative to its size (about 2× premium, thanks to its motor TP book) but a combined ratio still above 105%. The rest of this report keeps returning to that two-part test: how close to 100%, and how much float per rupee of capital?

The unlisted competition

The listed six are a minority of the market, and several of the strongest competitors are unlisted. Foreign capital and the import question

The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, passed in December 2025 and effective 5 February 2026, raised the foreign-investment limit to 100% (the DPIIT press note took effect on 2 May 2026), from 74% since 2021 and 49% since 2015. That makes it possible for foreign partners in joint ventures, such as Mitsui Sumitomo at Chola MS, Sompo at Universal Sompo or Zurich at Kotak, to take full control, and for Indian partners to sell out. Generali already controls Generali Central (74%). There are no imports in the usual sense, but the "imported" input is foreign reinsurance capacity: foreign reinsurance branches and Lloyd's India compete for cessions after GIC Re's obligatory share and its right of first refusal. The 2025 Act cut the net owned funds a foreign reinsurer must hold to open an Indian branch from ₹5,000 crore to ₹1,000 crore, which should bring in more capacity and more competition for GIC Re.

In plain terms

Foreign insurers can now own Indian general insurers outright. Expect some joint ventures to be bought out by the foreign partner and some Indian groups to sell out; either way, the number of well-capitalised competitors is not going down. For GIC Re, the same law makes it cheaper for foreign reinsurers to set up in India and compete for its business.

Scale private multi-line: the float machine

ICICI Lombard is the listed example. The model's weakness is that it competes with everyone: with the PSUs on large commercial risks, with Go Digit on motor, and with the SAHIs on retail health. Its FY27 combined ratio is drifting up as motor OD losses rise and commercial prices soften.

Digital challenger: cheap to run, expensive to sell

Go Digit was built to service policies through software rather than branches, and its operating cost per policy is low. But it buys most of its business from brokers, dealers and aggregators, so its commission bill is high, and it is concentrated in motor, the most price-competitive line. The model works if technology lowers claims costs (faster, cheaper surveys and fewer fraudulent claims) enough to bring the combined ratio below 100%. In Q1 FY27 it chose to shrink premium 8.4% rather than write motor business where "commission and premium rates were insufficient".

PSU multi-line: the equity book

New India is the largest insurer in India, with the widest commercial reach and an international business in about 26 countries. It has also run combined ratios of 117–122% for years. Its profits come from investment income, especially gains realised on a century-old equity portfolio. When the stock market is strong and it sells shares, it reports a profit; when claims spike and it has fewer gains to book, as in Q1 FY27, it reports a loss. The other three PSUs are in a weaker position still.

Standalone health: underwriting is everything

Star Health and Niva Bupa sell almost nothing but health cover. Health claims are paid quickly, so the float is small (investment assets of about 1.2 times annual premium, against about 2.2 times at ICICI Lombard) and the SAHIs earn only ₹8 of investment income per ₹100 of NEP. They must make money on underwriting itself. The prize is the retail renewal annuity: Niva's retail renewal rate was 95.8% in Q1 FY27 and Star's persistency over 100% on a premium basis, because customers who have served waiting periods are reluctant to switch. The risk is that claims costs (hospital tariffs, medical inflation, ageing) rise faster than the insurer can reprice.

National reinsurer: a privileged seat at every table

GIC Re receives a legally guaranteed 4% of most policies written in India, gets the first look at other reinsurance placed by Indian insurers, and so sees data on the whole market. It also writes about a quarter of its premium abroad. Its combined ratio has improved from 108.8% (FY25) to 106.0% (FY26) and 104.9% (Q1 FY27) after it cut crop and loss-making foreign treaties, and its investment book is the largest in the group. It earns a 14–15% return and trades at the lowest valuation, which section 10 examines.

Health after the GST cut: a boom, a base effect and a fight over the hospital bill Deep dive

Health is now 44% of the market and the main profit pool, but specialists, multi-line insurers and hospitals are all fighting over the same rupee.

Health insurance in India covers three very different businesses under one name. Retail (individual and family) policies are bought by households, renewed year after year, and priced by the insurer; this is where the money is made. Group policies are bought by employers, rebid every year, and often written at a loss to win the account. Government scheme business (PM-JAY and state schemes) is tendered and runs near or above 100%. Industry statistics add personal accident and travel cover to all three. The result is that "health" as a whole ran a 110% combined ratio and a −7% return on equity in FY26 on BCG's numbers, while the best retail books made solid profits.

The boom: GST exemption plus pent-up demand

From 22 September 2025, individual health and life policies became exempt from GST, which had been charged at 18%. Customers saw prices fall by roughly 15%, and the effect on demand was immediate. Health premium growth accelerated from about 10% in H1 FY26 to 17% for the full year, and by Q2 FY27 industry retail health premium was growing 32% a year. The boom has a built-in fade. From Q3 FY27, year-on-year comparisons start to include months that already had the tax cut, so growth rates will drop even if underlying demand stays strong. Star Health has guided to moderation in H2 FY27 and to insurance-revenue growth of 15–16% for FY27 (higher in FY28). Investors who extrapolate Q2 FY27 growth rates will be disappointed; the right comparison from October 2026 is growth on a post-exemption base.

The tax cut also had a cost. Because exempt supplies do not qualify for input tax credit, insurers lost the GST credit on commissions, advertising, rent and claims processing for individual health policies. They absorbed most of it: Star's Ind AS expense ratio still fell from 30.8% to 30.2% in FY26 and Niva's from 39.2% to 36.6%, as scale offset the lost credit. Insurers also trimmed some distributor payouts.

In plain terms

Removing GST made health insurance about 15% cheaper for families from September 2025, and they bought much more of it. But growth rates compare this year with last year, and from October 2026 last year already includes the cheaper prices. So the headline growth numbers will fall over the next few quarters even if nothing goes wrong. That slowdown is arithmetic, not a crisis.

The share fight: specialists versus multi-line insurers

The standalone health insurers took the market's growth in FY26: their share of total GDPI rose from 12% to 14%, with premium growth of about 21%. Star Health remains the retail leader, with roughly 31% of retail health premium on our primer's estimate (INDICATIVE, not re-verified for FY26) and about 40% of all SAHI premium. Star and Niva together write ₹27,192 crore, about 59% of SAHI premium of roughly ₹45,700 crore. Niva Bupa is gaining fastest among the specialists: its retail-health market share rose from 10.0% to 11.1% in the year to Q1 FY27. But the multi-line insurers are now pushing hard through their bank channels. ICICI Lombard's retail health share rose from 3.3% in FY25 to 4.1% in FY26 and 5.0% at the September 2026 exit, funded partly by deliberately shrinking its fire and crop books.

The specialists' advantage is twofold. Ind AS makes the specialists look better, and that is by design

Under the old IGAAP rules, an insurer expensed all acquisition costs (mostly commission) in the year a policy was sold, while the premium was earned over the cover period. A fast-growing retail health book therefore looked unprofitable: every new customer cost a full commission up front. Under Ind AS (the Indian version of IFRS 17), acquisition costs are spread over the coverage period, and part of the investment book is marked to market through the profit and loss account. The result is a large jump in reported profit for growing retail books. Star Health's FY26 profit is ₹557 crore under IGAAP (−14%) and ₹911 crore under Ind AS (+16%); Niva Bupa's is ₹131 crore under IGAAP and ₹365 crore under Ind AS. The cash is identical. Star also publishes a "normalised" PAT that replaces the actual investment return with an 8% yield to strip out mark-to-market swings: Q1 FY27 normalised PAT was ₹386 crore against ₹550 crore reported.

In plain terms

Old Indian accounting made a health insurer pay for a new customer's commission all at once, even though the customer pays premiums for years. New accounting spreads that cost over the policy, which is closer to the economics but makes profits jump. When you see Star's profit "rise" from ₹557 crore to ₹911 crore, nothing changed except the accounting rulebook.

Who sets the price of a hospital bed: insurers versus hospitals

The health insurer's largest cost is the hospital bill, and the two sides have been in open conflict. Hospitals argue that insurers delay approvals, deduct unfairly and refuse to accept tariff increases; insurers argue that hospitals inflate bills for insured patients and that package rates should rise more slowly than costs. The May 2024 IRDAI master circular raised the stakes by requiring cashless authorisation within one hour and discharge approval within three hours, which cut both ways: it protected patients, but left insurers less time to scrutinise bills.

Motor: a frozen third-party price, an own-damage price war and a commission reset Deep dive

Motor is a ₹1.08 lakh crore line that is really two businesses: one priced by the government and earning on its float, one priced by competition and losing money.

Every motor policy in India has two parts. Third-party (TP) cover pays for death, injury and property damage caused to others; it is compulsory under the Motor Vehicles Act and its price is notified by the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways in consultation with the IRDAI. Own-damage (OD) cover pays for damage to the insured's own vehicle; it is optional (though lenders require it) and has been freely priced since detariffing in January 2007. In FY26 the industry wrote about ₹1.08 lakh crore of motor premium, 41% OD and 59% TP. The two halves have opposite economics.

Third-party: a regulated price that has not moved since June 2022

The last TP tariff revision took effect on 1 June 2022. Since then, wages (which drive court awards for death and injury), medical costs and vehicle values have all risen. The IRDAI proposed an average increase of about 18% in 2025, with 20–25% for some categories, but no notification had been found by October 2026. Go Digit's management said in July 2026 that TP rates had been "flat for five years". Because TP claims are uncapped and settled by Motor Accident Claims Tribunals over several years, an unchanged tariff slowly erodes reserves; insurers that under-reserve today find out in three or four years' time, as New India's Q1 FY27 claims ratio of 103.4% (driven by motor TP) illustrates.

The 1/n rule bites hardest here. Since September 2018, new cars must carry three-year TP cover and new two-wheelers five-year cover, paid upfront. Until October 2024 that premium was booked in the year of sale; now it is spread over the term. Motor-heavy insurers with large new-vehicle books (Go Digit, ICICI Lombard) therefore saw the largest drop in reported premium growth, even though the cash still arrives on day one and adds to the float. Electric vehicles get a 15% TP discount under a 2019 IRDAI rule but cost more to repair under OD.

In plain terms

The government sets the price of compulsory third-party motor cover and has not raised it in over four years, while the cost of compensating accident victims keeps rising. Insurers still make money on it because they hold the premium for years before paying, but that cushion thins every year the price stays frozen. A TP tariff rise would be the single most valuable regulatory decision for motor-heavy insurers.

Own-damage: the price war

OD is where competition plays out. Insurers discount OD premiums to win the customer (and the dealer), knowing the TP part of the policy is profitable. The result is an industry OD combined ratio of 132% and a −34% return in FY26. ICICI Lombard's OD loss ratio rose from 65.2% in FY25 to 68.7% in FY26 and to about 85% in Q2 FY27 (our read of its investor presentation, approximate). Its overall motor loss ratio rose from 64.2% to 66.3% in FY26. Go Digit responded differently: in Q1 FY27 it walked away from private-car, standalone-OD and older-car business where "commission and premium rates were insufficient", shrinking its total gross written premium by 8.4% and its motor market share from about 6.5% (H1 FY26) to 5.6%. ICICI Lombard kept growing: its motor premium rose 13.0% in H1 FY27, in line with the industry's 13.1%, for a 10.3% share.

What would change motor economics

  • A TP tariff notification. An average 18% rise on about ₹63,000 crore of TP premium would add roughly ₹11,000 crore of annual premium at the same claims cost (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic), a large boost to the most float-rich line.
  • The commission reset. Nil commission on new-vehicle TP for distribution entities and tighter expense caps would shift money from dealers and brokers to insurers, if insurers do not compete it away through OD discounts.
  • Repair costs. EVs and cars with driver-assistance sensors raise OD claim severity; usage-based (pay-as-you-drive) OD add-ons, allowed since 2022, could improve pricing for low-mileage drivers.

The PSU problem and GIC Re's privilege: share sales, solvency and a shrinking cession Deep dive

The government owns a third of the market through insurers that lose money on underwriting, and both listed PSUs must still sell shares to meet the 25% public-float rule.

The four PSU multi-line insurers were created when the government nationalised 107 general insurers in 1972–73 and merged them into GIC and four subsidiaries: New India, United India, Oriental and National. GIC became the national reinsurer and the four were delinked from it in 2000–02. Half a century later, they still write about a third of India's premium, but on BCG's FY26 numbers the PSU multi-line group ran a loss ratio of 98%, a combined ratio of 128% and a return on equity of −4%, and its share is slipping about a point a year (35% to 34% in FY26). The problem is structural rather than cyclical: high wage and pension costs, books concentrated in group health, government schemes and motor TP, and slow pricing.

The regulatory reset: a commission squeeze and a new accounting rulebook at the same time Deep dive

Between 2023 and 2026 the regulator freed commissions, changed how premium is booked, removed GST on health, opened the sector to 100% foreign ownership and switched accounting standards. Now it wants to cut expense limits by a third.

Few Indian sectors have had as many rule changes in as short a time. Each one moved reported numbers. Together they mean that an analyst comparing FY24 with FY27 is comparing different accounting, different premium recognition, different tax and different commission regimes. This section explains the two changes that matter most from here: the IRDAI's September 2026 proposal to cap distribution costs, and the move to Ind AS accounting.

The road to the 2026 paper

In April 2023 the IRDAI scrapped product-level commission caps and replaced them with a single overall limit on expenses of management (EoM): commission plus operating costs, capped at 30% of gross written premium for multi-line insurers and 35% for standalone health insurers. The aim was flexibility; the effect, as the IRDAI's own data now show, was a bidding war for distribution. Motor commission rose 259% over FY23–FY25 against premium growth of 34%; retail health commission rose 118% against premium growth of 53%; broker pay including rewards rose 173% against broker-placed premium growth of 37%. Some insurers breached the overall cap and needed forbearance: Niva Bupa's auditors highlighted an emphasis of matter on pending EoM-exceedance forbearance in Q1 FY27. In crop, insurers discounted aggressively partly to bring their overall EoM ratio within limits.

What the September 2026 consultation paper proposes

On 23 September 2026 the IRDAI published "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution", with comments due by 25 October 2026. It covers life and general insurers. For general insurers, the key proposals reported so far are:

  • An EoM glide path to 20% of premium within five years, with FY2027-28 as year one (verified). Secondary reports say the base would move from gross written premium to domestic GDPI and that the path would run 30% → 25% → 20%; these details are from secondary sources and not checked against the paper itself.
  • Nil commission on motor TP for new vehicles when sold through distribution entities, and 2.5% for agents.
  • Health first-year commission of about 15% (distribution entities) and 20% (agents), with renewal commission around 5%, as reported by secondary sources (labelled as reported, not verified against the paper).
  • A single Insurance Distribution Entity licence with optional open architecture; commission defined as all-inclusive (rewards and incentives count); an uplift for rural and small-town business.
  • A ban on compulsory bundling of insurance with loans, and measures on motor-dealer influence over the choice of insurer.

The proposals are consistent with those set out for life insurers in the same paper, which the Life Insurance report covers in detail. Distributor stocks reacted sharply: PB Fintech (PolicyBazaar) fell about 36% around the paper, according to our life-insurance research.

Who wins and who loses if it passes (our reading)

The proposals move money from distributors to insurers. Motor-heavy manufacturers should gain most: if dealers and brokers are paid nothing for new-vehicle TP, Go Digit and ICICI Lombard keep more of each premium, provided they do not hand the saving back through OD discounts. PSU insurers, already at about 21–23% EoM, are close to the target and barely affected. Standalone health insurers are the most exposed if the glide path applies to them: Star's expense ratio is about 30% and Niva's 35–37%, and their agency models are built on first-year commissions above the reported 15–20% caps. Distributors (aggregators, brokers, motor dealers and bank channels) lose income. There is a risk for insurers too: a sharp cut in agent pay could slow retail health sales, the one segment where agents genuinely create demand.

In plain terms

The regulator thinks insurers are paying too much to the people who sell their policies, and wants to cap total running costs at 20% of premium within five years, against 30–35% today. Insurers that sell mostly through car dealers would gain; insurers that depend on large agent armies, like the health specialists, may have to rebuild how they sell. Nothing is final until the regulator issues rules after the 25 October 2026 comment deadline.

From 1 April 2026, Ind AS (Schedule IIA, the Indian equivalent of IFRS 17) became the primary accounting basis for insurers, with two years of parallel IGAAP reporting and a one-year deferral available on application by 30 April 2026. The IRDAI says 11 insurers implemented it in FY27 and the rest will follow in FY28. Among the listed six, Star Health and Niva Bupa report Q1 FY27 under Ind AS, and Go Digit's results are now presented under Ind AS (according to secondary reports). ICICI Lombard's Q2 FY27 release still uses IGAAP-style ratios (claims ÷ NEP, expenses ÷ NWP), which suggests it took the deferral, but this is not confirmed. New India and GIC Re have not confirmed either way; GIC Re's Q1 call still referred to IGAAP net worth.

A century of cycles: nationalisation, detariffing, a crop boom and the reset

Every phase of the industry's history left a mark that still shows in today's numbers: the PSUs' cost base, the motor price war, the crop retreat and the regulatory backlog.

Indian general insurance has gone through five phases since independence, and each explains a feature of the market today. The private era (to 1972) produced New India, founded in 1919 by Sir Dorabji Tata. Nationalisation (1972–2000) merged 107 insurers into GIC and four subsidiaries under a Tariff Advisory Committee that fixed prices; it built the PSUs' branch networks and their high fixed costs. Liberalisation (2000–2007) let private insurers in: ICICI Lombard, Bajaj Allianz, Royal Sundaram, Tata AIG, IFFCO-Tokio and Reliance all started in 2000–01, GIC was designated the national reinsurer, AIC was formed in 2002 and Star Health became the first standalone health insurer in 2006.

Detariffing on 1 January 2007 freed the prices of fire, engineering and motor OD, and a decade-long price war followed: fire rates fell an estimated 60–80% and the motor TP pool, which ran until 2012, produced heavy losses. The health and crop boom (2014–2019) brought the foreign-investment limit to 49% (2015), PMFBY (2016, lifting premium about 32% in FY17), three IPOs in 2017 (ICICI Lombard in September, GIC Re in October, New India in November) and mandatory multi-year motor TP (2018). The COVID years (2019–2022) saw the PSU merger called off with a capital injection (2020), large COVID health claims, the foreign-investment limit raised to 74% (2021), ICICI Lombard's merger with Bharti AXA General (completed September 2021) and Star Health's IPO (December 2021, at ₹900). Since 2023 the industry has been in the regulatory reset described in section 11.

Reading a general insurer: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about an Indian non-life insurer, provided you know which basis each is on and where each one misleads.

Ordinary company metrics do not work for insurers. Revenue, operating margin and ROCE as shown on screening websites mix premium, investment income and reserve movements into meaningless totals, and an insurer has no "EBITDA". The right toolkit starts from the profit equation of an insurer, then measures each term: how much premium, how much of it is kept, how much is paid out, how much it costs to sell and service, how much is invested and at what yield, how much capital supports it, and what the market pays for that capital.

ROE ≈ [(1 − combined ratio) × NEP ÷ net worth + yield × investment assets ÷ net worth] × (1 − tax rate). The first term is underwriting leverage, the second is investment leverage.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the pieces fit. Take an insurer with ₹1,000 crore of net worth, ₹2,000 crore of net earned premium and ₹3,500 crore of investment assets. At a 104% combined ratio it loses 4% × ₹2,000 crore = ₹80 crore on underwriting. At an 8% yield it earns ₹280 crore on investments. PBT is ₹200 crore; at 25% tax, PAT is ₹150 crore and ROE is 15%. Now move each lever by a small amount. If the combined ratio rises to 106%, underwriting loses ₹120 crore and ROE drops to 12%. If the yield falls to 7%, investment income drops to ₹245 crore and ROE to 12.4%. If the insurer grows premium 20% at the same ratios without new capital, ROE rises, but solvency falls. That trade-off between growth, underwriting, yield and capital is what every KPI below measures.

In plain terms

An insurer's return comes from two sources stacked on top of each other: whatever it makes or loses on the insurance itself, multiplied by how much premium it writes per rupee of capital, plus whatever it earns on its investments, multiplied by how much money it invests per rupee of capital. A good insurer is one where the first number is close to zero or positive and the second is large and safely invested.

1. Premium: GDPI, GWP and growth (and the 1/n basis)

What it is. The insurer's sales. GDPI (gross direct premium income) is premium written directly in India and is the market-share basis; GWP adds reinsurance accepted (and, for New India, foreign premium); for a reinsurer such as GIC Re, all premium is accepted business. Formula: growth = this period's premium ÷ the same period last year − 1. Worked example: an insurer writing ₹10,000 crore of GDPI this year against ₹9,000 crore last year grew 11.1%; if ₹600 crore of five-year TP policies were spread under 1/n (only ₹120 crore booked this year), its "n basis" premium would be ₹10,480 crore and growth 16.4%.

What drives it. Volume (policies sold), price (premium per policy), mix (health versus motor versus commercial) and, since October 2024, the share of multi-year policies. Benchmarks: good is growth of 5 points or more above the industry with a stable combined ratio; average is within 2 points of the industry; weak is growth bought with a worsening combined ratio. FY26 industry growth was 9% (11.1% ex-1/n); Q2 FY27 7.0%.

What it is. The insurer's GDPI as a share of industry GDPI, overall or by line. Formula: company GDPI ÷ industry GDPI. Worked example: New India's ₹42,822 crore ÷ ₹3,36,121 crore = 12.7%. 3. Product mix

What it is. The split of premium by line: health (retail, group, government), motor (OD, TP), fire, marine, engineering, liability, crop and others. Why it matters: each line has different loss ratios, float duration and capital needs, so mix is the starting point for every forecast. Worked example: ICICI Lombard's H1 FY27 mix was health, travel and PA 37%, motor OD 19%, motor TP 18%, fire 10%, marine 4%, crop 0.3% and others about 11.7%, against health 29% and crop 5% in FY25: the company has moved about 8 points of its book into health in two years. Benchmarks: there is no "good" mix in the abstract, but a book with more retail health, TP and specialty commercial lines and less group health, government schemes and crop earns more. The trap: industry "health" includes group and government business; always ask for the retail share (section on health KPIs below).

4. Retention ratio

What it is. The share of premium the insurer keeps after reinsurance. Formula: NWP ÷ GWP. Worked example: GWP ₹1,000 crore with ₹300 crore ceded gives a 70% retention. Drivers: line mix (fire and crop are heavily ceded), capital (a thinly capitalised insurer cedes more), and the obligatory 4% to GIC Re. Benchmarks: 70–85% good, 55–70% average; below 50% suggests a fronting business that earns mostly reinsurance commission; above 90% with catastrophe exposure is risky. The trap: a falling retention can flatter the combined ratio (the reinsurer takes the bad risks and pays commission) while shrinking the float and future profit. Look at retention together with the net combined ratio.

In plain terms

Retention tells you how much of the risk the insurer really carries. A health specialist keeps most of its premium because hospital bills are small and predictable. A big commercial insurer passes on most of a refinery's premium to reinsurers because one fire could wipe out a year's profit. Keeping more means more profit in good years and more pain in bad ones.

5. Loss (claims) ratio

What it is. The share of earned premium paid out or reserved for claims. Formula (IGAAP): net incurred claims ÷ NEP, where incurred claims include paid claims plus the change in claims reserves (including IBNR). Drivers: price adequacy, risk selection, claims management (fraud control, hospital packages, surveys), catastrophes, medical and repair inflation, court awards and reserve releases or strengthening. Benchmarks: good is below 65% for retail health, below 68% for private motor and below 60% for fire; average 68–78%; weak above 85% (PSU 98%, crop 92%).

6. Expense ratio and expenses of management (EoM)

What it is. The cost of selling and running the business. Formulas: the IGAAP expense ratio is (net commission + operating expenses) ÷ NWP; the IRDAI's EoM ratio is (gross commission + operating expenses) ÷ GWP and is the metric the cap applies to (30% multi-line, 35% SAHI); under Ind AS, companies show an expense ratio on insurance revenue. Benchmarks: good below 28% for multi-line and 32% for SAHIs; average 28–32% and 32–35%; weak is above the cap.

10. Investment leverage and float intensity

What it is. How many rupees the insurer invests for each rupee of its own capital (investment leverage), and for each rupee of premium (float intensity). 11. Solvency ratio

What it is. The regulator's capital test. Formula: available solvency margin ÷ required solvency margin (a factor-based formula driven by premium and claims). Worked example: available capital of ₹550 crore against a required margin of ₹250 crore is 2.2×. Drivers: profits retained, capital raised, growth (which raises the requirement), and investment marks (only part of fair-value gains count). Benchmarks: good above 2.2×; average 1.7–2.2×; weak below 1.6×; below 1.5× is a breach. The listed six range from New India's 1.80× (Jun-26) to GIC Re's 4.32× (chart in section 10); the three unlisted PSUs read as negative on BCG's chart (INDICATIVE). The trap: a high solvency ratio is not always good; it can signal idle capital that depresses ROE. A falling one at a fast grower (Niva 3.03× → 2.25×) signals a future capital raise.

Solvency also explains strategy. GIC Re's 4.32× gives it room to write more business or pay larger dividends, and its capital is the reason it can absorb a catastrophe year without a raise. ICICI Lombard at 2.74× and Go Digit at 2.43× can grow at industry pace from retained earnings. Niva Bupa at 2.25× is growing premium 25–30% a year and consuming capital as it goes; at that pace it would approach the 1.5× floor within a few years unless profits rise or it raises equity or sub-debt. New India at 1.80× has the thinnest cushion among the listed six and little room for a large reserve strengthening. A move to a risk-based capital regime, long under development, would change these numbers again: insurers with concentrated catastrophe exposure or large equity books would need more capital, and those with diversified short-tail books might need less.

13. Health-book quality: retail share, claims settlement and renewal

What it is. Three measures of a health book's durability. Retail share = retail health premium ÷ total health premium; claims settlement ratio = claims settled ÷ claims reported, by number; renewal rate or persistency = renewed premium (or policies) ÷ premium due for renewal. Worked example: a book of ₹1,000 crore with ₹750 crore retail is 75% retail. Benchmarks: retail share above 70% good, 40–70% average, below 40% weak; claims settlement above 95% good, 90–95% average, below 90% weak. Star's book is about 95% retail (group about 2.5% of GWP in Q1 FY27), its retail claims settlement ratio 91% and its persistency 102% on a premium basis. Niva's book was 66.9% retail in FY26 and 74.8% in Q1 FY27, with a 95.6% claims settlement ratio and a 95.8% retail renewal rate. The trap: persistency above 100% on a premium basis reflects price increases and upgrades at renewal, not more than every customer renewing.

14. Valuation: price-to-book and price-to-earnings

What it is. What the market pays per rupee of book value and of profit. Formulas: P/B = market cap ÷ net worth; P/E = market cap ÷ PAT. Justified P/B ≈ (ROE − growth) ÷ (cost of equity − growth). Worked example: an insurer with 17% ROE, 10% growth and a 12% cost of equity justifies (17 − 10) ÷ (12 − 10) = 3.5× book. Benchmarks: no fixed range; P/B should track sustainable ROE and growth. The six range from 0.77× (GIC Re) to 5.71× (Go Digit) on 19 October 2026 (section 17). The trap: screener P/E for Star and Niva mixes IGAAP and Ind AS quarters; P/B for PSUs uses a book that excludes much of their fair-value gains.

Why P/B rather than P/E as the anchor? Because an insurer's earnings swing with catastrophes, reserve releases and investment gains from year to year, while book value moves more slowly and is the capital that generates future returns. A sensible routine is to compute P/B on the most comparable book available, set it against a through-the-cycle ROE rather than the latest year's, and use P/E only as a cross-check on a normalised profit (for example Star's normalised PAT at an 8% yield, or ICICI Lombard's profit excluding unusually large capital gains). For the PSUs, add back the fair-value change account to get an economic book; for the Ind AS reporters, check whether book value is on the new or the old basis before comparing with IGAAP peers.

ICICI Lombard (IGAAP). Net earned premium of roughly ₹23,000 crore on an average net worth of about ₹17,700 crore gives underwriting leverage of about 1.3×. A 103.4% combined ratio therefore costs about 3.4% × 1.3 ≈ 4.4 points of pre-tax ROE. Investment leverage of about 3.4× at an 8.47% yield adds about 28.8 points. The investment term is more than six times the underwriting term: ICICI Lombard's return is mostly a function of how much float it holds and how well it invests it, with underwriting discipline mainly protecting that return from leaking away.

Star Health (Ind AS). Insurance revenue of ₹17,999 crore on a net worth of about ₹9,000 crore (between the March and June 2026 Ind AS figures) gives underwriting leverage of about 2.0×. New India (IGAAP). New India earned ₹11,112 crore of investment income in FY26, equal to about 39% of its consolidated net worth of ₹28,531 crore, a far bigger investment term than any private insurer. Yet its standalone PAT was only ₹1,384 crore. The difference, roughly ₹9,700 crore before the effects of tax, was consumed mostly by underwriting at a 122.4% combined ratio, including the ₹3,525 crore wage revision. New India shows the limit of the float model: the investment engine is large, but when underwriting loses more than 20 points on a book that is larger than net worth, the engine runs mostly to stand still.

In plain terms

Break an insurer's return into two pieces: what it makes on insurance and what it makes on investments. For ICICI Lombard and GIC Re, almost everything comes from investments. Knowing which piece drives a company tells you which risks matter most for it.

Definition traps, collected

  • Screener "sales", "OPM" and "ROCE" are meaningless for insurers. Use premium, combined ratio, PAT, ROE and P/B. Never quote an insurer's "EBITDA margin".
  • Four premium numbers. GDPI ≠ GWP ≠ NWP ≠ NEP. GIC Re has no GDPI. New India's GWP includes foreign premium.
  • 1/n versus "n". Since October 2024, reported growth understates business written; companies publish both. Star's FY26 premium is ₹18,606 crore (1/n) or about ₹20,369 crore (N basis).
  • IGAAP versus Ind AS. Star FY26 PAT ₹557 crore versus ₹911 crore; Niva ₹131 crore versus ₹365 crore. Not every listed insurer has switched; ICICI Lombard probably deferred (unconfirmed).
  • Combined ratio definitions. Company IGAAP (mixed denominators), restated "n", Ind AS CISR and BCG's own figures all differ; PSU ratios include one-offs.
  • "Health" is three businesses. Retail, group and government health have opposite economics; industry figures also include PA and travel.
  • Book value for PSUs. Much of the fair-value gain on equities sits outside book (GIC Re ₹53,124 crore ex-FVCA versus ₹90,790 crore including it).
  • Standalone versus consolidated. GIC Re's Q1 FY27 PAT is ₹1,922 crore standalone and ₹1,744 crore consolidated; New India's FY26 PAT ₹1,384 crore standalone and ₹1,417 crore consolidated.
  • Market-share series. "Private share" sometimes excludes SAHIs (52.3%) and sometimes includes them (66%).
  • Quarter mismatch. As of 19 October 2026, ICICI Lombard is at Q2 FY27 and the other five at Q1 FY27.

How to read a general insurer's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the basis first (one minute). IGAAP or Ind AS? 1/n or "n"? Standalone or consolidated? Write it down before reading any number.
  2. Premium growth against the industry. Pull GDPI (or GWP) growth and compare with the GI Council's industry figure for the same months. Ask what was exited (crop, fire, OD) and what grew (retail health).
  3. Mix shift. Note the change in health, motor OD, motor TP and commercial shares. A rising retail health share is usually good; a rising group or government share usually is not.
  4. Loss ratio, by line if possible. Look for motor OD (the price-war line), health (medical inflation and hospital tariffs) and any catastrophe loss called out separately. Red flag: a jump of more than 3 points without a named catastrophe.
  5. Expense ratio and EoM. Is it falling with scale? Is the company inside the 30% or 35% cap, or asking for forbearance? Red flag: an auditor's emphasis of matter on EoM.
  6. Combined ratio, reported and adjusted. Note the reported figure, the company's preferred basis ("n", ex-catastrophe, ex-wage) and the gap between them. A widening gap is a warning.
  7. Investment income split. Separate interest and dividends from realised and mark-to-market gains. Red flag: PAT growth that comes entirely from capital gains while the combined ratio worsens (ICICI Q2 FY27 is a mild example).
  8. Solvency. Compare with the 1.5× minimum and with last quarter. Red flag: a fast decline at a fast grower, which points to a capital raise.
  9. ROE and book value. Annualise carefully (quarterly gains are lumpy) and check whether the book includes fair-value reserves.
  10. The footnotes. Auditor qualifications (New India's Q1 FY27 review), reserve strengthening, regulatory actions, related-party reinsurance and management's guidance on the next two quarters.
In plain terms

When an insurer reports, ask five questions in order: which accounting rules, did it grow faster than the market, are claims and costs rising or falling as a share of premium, how much of the profit came from selling investments, and is its capital cushion shrinking? If you can answer those, you understand the quarter.

Who is winning: growth with discipline, float with discipline, or neither

Three maps place the six on the trade-offs that matter: underwriting against growth, return against price, and float against underwriting.

Winning in general insurance means growing without giving away price, holding enough float to earn on, and having the capital to keep going. No listed insurer wins on every count. The health specialists combine the best underwriting with the fastest growth but have small floats. ICICI Lombard and GIC Re have large floats and near-break-even underwriting but are growing below the market. Go Digit has a large float for its size but has paused growth to fix underwriting. New India has scale and float but loses heavily on underwriting.

Map 1 is the cleanest summary of FY26. Niva Bupa grew 27% at a CISR of about 101.5%, which is why it trades at a premium despite a tiny IGAAP profit. Star Health sits in the "profitable" half with mid-teens growth on its preferred basis. Go Digit grew 16% at 105.7%, a combination the market tolerated in FY26 but which the company itself has since stopped, choosing to shrink in Q1 FY27. ICICI Lombard and GIC Re grew below the industry at 103–106%, by design: both are exiting unprofitable lines (crop, large fire, foreign treaties). New India sits alone in the bottom-right.

Map 2 shows how differently the market treats each rupee of return. If valuations simply tracked current ROE, the dots would sit on an upward-sloping line. Instead they form two clusters. The private insurers sit high on the chart whatever their current ROE, because investors are paying for growth and for an expected improvement in underwriting (Go Digit at 5.7× book on a 12% ROE is the clearest case). The PSUs sit low whatever their ROE, because investors discount government ownership, share sales and dependence on equity gains. Switching Star and Niva to Ind AS moves them right, toward a 10–11% ROE, which makes their valuations look less stretched but still well above ICICI Lombard's on a return-adjusted basis.

Map 3 ties the report's central argument together. The further right an insurer sits, the more combined ratio it can afford; the lower it sits, the less it needs to afford. ICICI Lombard and GIC Re are in the comfortable zone. Go Digit's float is ample for its size, so its problem is purely underwriting: a move from 105.7% to 100% would put it beside ICICI Lombard. The health specialists sit low and left, which works only as long as claims stay controlled. New India is the only name in the top half, where the float cannot carry the loss.

Why the PSU discount persists

GIC Re and New India together earn ₹11,079 crore of FY26 IGAAP profit and trade at 7.7 times that profit and 0.86 times book. The discount has four sources. Supply: the government must sell about 2.4% of GIC Re and about 10.4% of New India to meet the public-float rule, and markets price in future offers at a discount (GIC Re's June 2026 OFS was priced about 9% below market). Earnings quality: much of their profit is realised equity gains, which are lumpy and depend on the market. Governance and mandate: state-owned insurers have historically written social-mandate business and settled wage revisions that private boards would resist. Hidden value: on the other side, their books understate fair value (GIC Re's fair-value reserve is worth roughly ₹37,700 crore, the difference between its ₹90,790 crore and ₹53,124 crore net worth figures), which is why the discount on an economic book is even deeper than it looks.

Why the private premium persists

The private insurers trade at 38.7 times FY26 IGAAP earnings (33.7 times using Ind AS profit for Star and Niva) and 4.7 times book. Investors pay for three things: long-run premium growth in the low-to-mid teens, as penetration rises from about 1% of GDP; operating leverage, as fixed costs spread over a larger book; and the possibility that regulation (commission caps, a TP tariff rise) shifts economics in manufacturers' favour. The risks to that premium are the ones this report has catalogued: FY27's rising combined ratios, the GST base effect in health, and the chance that the expense glide path hurts the agency-led health specialists more than it helps motor insurers.

In plain terms

Government-owned insurers are cheap partly because the government keeps selling their shares and partly because their profits depend on the stock market. Private insurers are expensive because investors expect India to buy a lot more insurance over the next decade and the private players to win most of it. Both views can be right at the same time.

The global lens: worse underwriting, richer yields, higher multiples

Indian insurers run combined ratios that would be unacceptable in mature markets, but earn comparable returns because Indian bond yields are higher and growth is faster.

India is the 15th-largest non-life insurance market in the world and the second largest in emerging Asia (Swiss Re, calendar 2024: about US$36 billion of premium, shown in dollars only for global comparison). It is also one of the least penetrated, at about 1% of GDP against a world average of about 4%. Three comparisons with global insurers explain how the Indian industry differs.

First, underwriting. The best global insurers make money on underwriting itself: combined ratios in the high 80s to mid 90s are normal for well-run property and casualty companies, and Progressive has built a decades-long record in US personal auto at around 90%. India's best large insurers run 100–105%. Part of that gap is structural: detariffed commercial lines have been underpriced for two decades, motor TP is government-priced, and fragmented competition keeps rates low. Part is accounting: the IGAAP expense ratio's NWP denominator inflates Indian combined ratios relative to IFRS-style measures. But most of it is real, and it is why the IRDAI is pushing on distribution costs.

Second, yields and leverage. Indian government bonds yield roughly 6.5–7% against about 4% in the US, and Indian insurers' realised returns are 7–8.5%. Combined with investment leverage of 2–4 times net worth, that lets an Indian insurer earn a mid-teens ROE at a 103% combined ratio, roughly what a US or European insurer earns at 90–95%. The flip side is that Indian insurer returns are more exposed to falling interest rates: if Indian yields converged toward global levels, Indian insurers would need better underwriting to hold their returns.

Third, valuation. Indian private insurers trade at 4–6 times book, far above global multi-line peers at 1–2 times. The premium reflects growth: Indian premium grows 10–20% a year in nominal terms against 5–8% in mature markets, from a penetration base a quarter of the global average. GIC Re is the exception, trading below the global reinsurance range despite comparable ROE, which isolates the size of the Indian PSU discount. GIC Re ranks about ninth among global reinsurers by premium (AM Best, non-IFRS 17 basis) and holds an A- (Excellent) rating.

In plain terms

A good American or Japanese insurer makes a profit on its insurance and then earns modest interest on top. A good Indian insurer loses a little on its insurance and makes it up with much higher interest. That works as long as Indian interest rates stay well above global ones. If they fall, Indian insurers will have to price risk the way global leaders do.

Technology and disruption: cheaper selling, faster claims, new risks

Technology is changing how policies are sold and claims are settled, but the biggest disruptions so far have come from regulation, not from start-ups.

General insurance is a data business, and the industry's technology agenda has four parts: selling more cheaply, deciding claims faster and more accurately, pricing risk more finely, and protecting customer data. Progress has been real but uneven, and none of it has yet changed the industry's basic economics as much as the regulatory reset.

Digital distribution and embedded insurance

Aggregators (led by PolicyBazaar), OEM and dealer platforms, fintech apps and travel sites now sell a large share of motor, health and travel policies, and many policies are "embedded" in another purchase: travel cover with a flight, device cover with a phone, motor cover with a car loan. Go Digit is the listed proxy for partner-led digital distribution; Acko has the highest direct-to-consumer share of any insurer on BCG's data. Incumbents are catching up: Star Health's direct-to-consumer channel was 16% of fresh retail sales in Q1 FY27, up 142% year on year, and 97% of its fresh policies were sourced digitally (often by agents using its app). Digital selling lowers servicing cost but not necessarily acquisition cost, because aggregators and platforms charge for the customer; the IRDAI's proposal to define commission as all-inclusive is aimed partly at those payments.

Bima Sugam: the regulator's own marketplace

Bima Sugam, the IRDAI-promoted online marketplace, could do more to reduce acquisition costs than any private platform if it works as planned, by letting customers compare and buy standard motor and health policies with low or no commission. Its information site has been live since 17 September 2025; transactional sales were targeted for end-September 2026 but are not confirmed live, and the September 2026 consultation paper says it should be "operational in 4–6 months". It has been delayed repeatedly, so treat it as an option, not a forecast.

AI in underwriting and claims

Insurers are using machine learning for fraud detection, automatic claim adjudication and instant underwriting. Niva Bupa auto-decides 48% of retail health proposals; ICICI Lombard uses face-scan underwriting for some health products. In motor, photo- and video-based surveys cut the time and cost of settling own-damage claims. The prize is a lower loss ratio (less fraud, better selection) and a lower expense ratio (fewer staff per claim). The risk is that models trained on historical data misprice new risks such as EV repairs or climate-driven floods.

EVs, driver assistance and usage-based cover

Electric vehicles get a 15% discount on TP premium but cost more to repair, especially battery packs; cars with driver-assistance sensors also cost more per own-damage claim. Usage-based (pay-as-you-drive and pay-how-you-drive) own-damage add-ons have been allowed since 2022 and let insurers price low-mileage and safer drivers more accurately. OEMs are also embedding insurance at the point of sale, which could shift bargaining power further toward the vehicle maker and dealer, the very channel the IRDAI's proposals target.

Cyber and data risk

Insurers hold sensitive health and financial data. Star Health suffered a data breach in September 2024, with customer data offered for sale through Telegram; the company sued Telegram and Cloudflare. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act adds compliance obligations and penalties. Cyber insurance is also a small but growing product line for commercial insurers, which brings its own accumulation risk.

Climate and catastrophe modelling

More frequent floods and cyclones in Assam, Chennai, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana are raising catastrophe loads in pricing and reinsurance programmes. Parametric covers, which pay out automatically when a measured trigger (rainfall, wind speed) is breached, are emerging for states, farmers and businesses, and could reshape crop insurance if claims assessment under PMFBY can be automated.

In plain terms

Technology is making insurance cheaper to service and faster to claim, and apps and websites now sell a large share of policies. But the platforms that bring the customer still charge for it, so selling costs have not fallen much. The regulator's own marketplace and its commission rules may end up doing more to cut costs than any start-up.

Hospital integration and the care model

A longer-term disruption in health would be insurers owning or tightly contracting care delivery, the model of US managed-care companies, to control the hospital bill directly. Indian insurers have moved partway, through preferred-provider networks, package rates and wellness programmes, but none owns hospitals at scale. If hospital tariff fights intensify (section 8), deeper integration, or regulation of hospital prices for insured patients, could shift the balance of power.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian general insurers

The bull case rests on penetration, health and a regulator that is fixing distribution economics. The bear case rests on soft pricing, claims inflation and valuations that assume improvements not yet delivered.

The bull case

  • A long growth runway. Non-life premium is about 1% of GDP against about 4% globally; even at constant penetration, premium grows with nominal GDP, and health has compounded at 18% for a decade.
  • Retail health is a durable profit pool. Renewal rates above 95%, waiting periods that lock customers in, and the GST exemption have widened the market; the specialists run CISRs at or below 100%.
  • The regulator is on manufacturers' side. Nil commission on new-vehicle TP, a 20% EoM end-point and curbs on dealer influence would shift money from distributors to insurers.
  • Float economics favour the large privates. ICICI Lombard earns 17–18% at a 103% combined ratio, and a TP tariff rise would add to the most float-rich line.
  • Share gains from the PSUs. The government insurers are losing about a point of share a year, and three of four look undercapitalised; private insurers are the natural winners.
  • GIC Re looks cheap. A mid-teens ROE, 4.3× solvency and a 4% dividend yield at 0.77× book, with the turnaround still in progress.

The bear case

  • The cycle has turned soft. Motor OD is in a price war (132% combined ratio), large commercial risks are being discounted, and FY27 combined ratios are rising at ICICI Lombard and Go Digit.
  • Claims inflation without price relief. TP tariffs frozen since June 2022, court awards and repair costs rising, and hospitals pushing tariffs up.
  • The health boom fades. From Q3 FY27, growth laps the GST cut; competition from bank-led multi-line insurers is intensifying.
  • Regulation cuts both ways. An EoM glide path to 20% could force the agency-led health specialists to rebuild distribution; Niva already exceeds the current cap.
  • Profits lean on markets. Investment income exceeds underwriting losses for the industry; a fall in yields or equities would hit returns directly.
  • Valuations assume improvement. Go Digit and Niva Bupa are priced for ROEs of about 16–18% they have not yet earned; the PSUs face continuing government share sales.

Weighing the two, the strongest parts of the bull case are structural (penetration, retail health, the direction of regulation) and play out over years, while the strongest parts of the bear case are cyclical (soft pricing, the GST base effect) and visible in the next few quarters. That mismatch is typical of insurance. It means the near-term newsflow is likely to be weaker than the long-term story, and that the companies best placed to ride out a soft cycle, those with large floats and disciplined underwriting, deserve more credit than those relying on growth alone.

The regulatory items are the swing factors in both directions. A TP tariff increase and a motor commission cap would add materially to the earnings of motor-heavy insurers within a year. An EoM glide path applied fully to standalone health insurers would force a costly change in how they sell. Both decisions sit with the government and the IRDAI, and both could arrive within the next twelve months.

The bull and bear cases also split by company. ICICI Lombard is the cleanest expression of the bull case with the least regulatory risk. Star Health and Niva Bupa are the purest plays on retail health, carrying the most exposure to both the GST base effect and the expense proposals. Go Digit is the highest-beta bet on motor regulation. GIC Re and New India are bets on PSU reform and on the value of their investment books more than on underwriting.

A useful way to hold the two views together is to separate time horizons. Over one to two years, the evidence will come from quarterly combined ratios, the shape of the health slowdown after the GST anniversary, and what the IRDAI finally adopts. Over five to ten years, the evidence will come from penetration, from whether the private insurers keep taking share from the PSUs, and from whether the industry as a whole ever reaches an underwriting profit. An investor who is right about the second horizon can still lose money on the first, which is why entry valuation matters so much in this sector.

In plain terms

The long-term case for Indian general insurance is strong: Indians are badly under-insured and health insurance is becoming a household purchase. The short-term picture is weaker: prices are soft, claims are rising and the health boom is about to slow. Investors who buy now are paying for the long term and accepting a bumpy next year.
