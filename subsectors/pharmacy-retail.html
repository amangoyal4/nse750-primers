Indian pharmacy retail: the chemist's counter goes corporate

A complete guide to the business of selling medicines in India: how a family chemist and a pharmacy chain each make money on a margin set by convention and capped by the state, why chains hold only about an eighth of a ₹2.46 lakh crore medicine market after twenty years, why private label is the only big lever and has a trust ceiling, how wage inflation, quick commerce and the government's own pharmacies press on the counter, and what MedPlus, the one listed pure-play chain, tells us about all of it.

00

The one-page view: a regulated margin, sold across a family counter

India buys about ₹2.46 lakh crore of medicines a year. Most of it still passes over the counter of an owner-run chemist; chains hold roughly an eighth and gain about a point of share a year. The fight is over who captures the slice between the manufacturer's price and the patient.

Every Indian medicine has a printed maximum retail price (MRP), set by the manufacturer within rules written by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA). Between that price and the factory sit a carrying-and-forwarding agent, a stockist and a retailer, each paid by a margin that is a trade custom rather than a law: roughly 10% for the stockist and 20% for the chemist on a branded drug. Pharmacy retail is the business of living on that 20%, and of finding ways to keep more of it than the custom allows.

The audited Indian Pharmaceutical Market (IPM) was ₹2,45,943 crore in the year to March 2026 (Pharmarack PharmaTrac), up 8.6% in value but only 0.6% in units: growth came almost entirely from price and mix. In 2023, the last year for which an industry-quality channel split exists (the Indian Pharmaceutical Alliance's study with Pharmarack), 54% of that market reached patients through standalone chemists, 31.3% through hospitals and institutions, 11.6% through organised chains (about ₹28,000 crore), 2.5% through online pharmacies and under 1% through the government's Jan Aushadhi kendras. The same study projects chains at 17.4% of the market, ₹72,832 crore, by 2030.

The chains are a short list. Apollo Pharmacy had 7,440 stores at the end of June 2026 and, adding the 222 net openings Apollo reported for the September quarter, about 7,662 at the end of September (an indicative derivation). MedPlus had 5,642. Davaindia, the generic-pharmacy chain owned by Zota Health Care, had 2,840 smaller outlets. The government's Jan Aushadhi scheme had 20,149 kendras at 30 June 2026. Against them stand an estimated eight to twelve lakh licensed retail chemists; nobody has published a census, and the trade body AIOCD's figure of about 12.4 lakh members includes wholesalers and pharmacists.

Only one of these businesses is listed as a pure pharmacy chain: MedPlus Health Services (NSE MEDPLUS, BSE 543427), so it is the single member of this sub-sector and the lens we use throughout. Apollo's pharmacy arm sits inside Apollo Hospitals, Davaindia inside a drug maker, and the online players (PharmEasy, Tata 1mg, Truemeds) are unlisted. We bring them in as reference points, never as members, and we never add their numbers to MedPlus's.

Five forces shape the industry, and the report is built around them:

  • Formalisation. Chains grow two to three times faster than the market by taking share from the 54% standalone channel. Their moat is cluster density: many stores close together, fed from one warehouse.
  • Mix, not markup. The retailer's slice of a branded drug is fixed by custom and, for price-controlled drugs, by formula. Private label, the chain's own generics and household goods, is the only large lever on gross margin, and it has a ceiling set by customer trust.
  • Labour and rent against capped prices. Medicine prices rise at most 10% a year (actual average about 5.6%), while wages and rents do not wait. Karnataka raised minimum wages by an average 60% and Telangana by "25% plus" from June 2026.
  • The channel war. Online pharmacies have crept to an indicative 3–3.5% of the market, quick-commerce apps now deliver medicines in ten minutes, and the law governing both is a draft that was never notified.
  • The state as price-setter and competitor. The NPPA caps prices on about 17–18% of the market and has now twice capped trade margins on cancer drugs; the government runs its own 20,000-outlet generic chain.

The numbers that matter most for the listed company are less flattering than its headline store growth. MedPlus grew revenue 21.3% to ₹2,037 crore in Q2 FY27 (Sep-26), but its gross margin fell 110 basis points to 25.1%, its pre-Ind AS operating EBITDA margin was 4.8%, and profit after tax was flat at ₹56 crore; first-half profit fell 8.8%. The causes run straight through the five forces: a deliberate retreat from an over-pushed private-label drive, franchise stores that carry a lower gross margin, the June wage shock, and continued discounting to hold customers against online rivals. The market has noticed. At ₹619 on 13 November 2026 the stock was worth ₹7,440 crore, 35.2 times trailing earnings, down from roughly 39 times in mid-2026.

In plain terms

A chemist is a shop that is allowed to keep about a fifth of the price printed on each strip of tablets, and must pay its rent, its pharmacist, its electricity and its unsold expired stock out of that fifth. A chain can keep a little more because it buys in bulk, runs its own warehouses and sells its own cheaper generics. It also gives some back in discounts to win customers. The whole industry argument is about how much of that fifth survives once rent and wages rise and the government, the online apps and the customer each take their share.

The single most common misreading of this industry, and one our own MedPlus primer made, is to treat the "mature-store" margin a chain reports (about 11.9% for MedPlus in FY26) as the margin the whole company will reach once its young stores grow up. That figure is measured before warehouse, delivery, head-office and marketing costs, which absorb about six points of revenue. Fully loaded, MedPlus's older stores earned 6.7% in Q4 FY26 and 4.3% in Q1 FY27. Section 06 takes that apart; the KPI masterclass in section 12 explains every number a reader needs to avoid the same trap.

01

How a pharmacy earns: a customary 20%, and what chains add to it

A medicine passes through four sets of hands before it reaches a patient. The retailer's share is set by habit and regulation, so a pharmacy chain makes money by removing a middleman, changing the mix of what it sells and making each square foot work harder.

The Indian medicine supply chain is long and fragmented. A manufacturer ships to a carrying-and-forwarding (C&F) agent in each state, which holds stock for a fee of about 2–3%. The C&F agent sells to stockists (distributors) at the price-to-stockist (PTS); there are more than 60,000 independent distributors and about 20 organised ones, which held about 10% of distribution in 2023 and are expected to reach 25–30% by 2030. The stockist sells to the retailer at the price-to-retailer (PTR), earning a customary 10%. The retailer sells to the patient at or below MRP, earning a customary 20% on branded generics (16% is the figure written into the formula for price-controlled drugs). The goods and services tax (GST) on most medicines was cut from 12% to 5% on 22 September 2025, lowering MRPs but leaving percentage margins unchanged.

On ₹100 of MRP for a typical branded prescription drug, the sibling branded-formulations report's convention-based arithmetic gives about ₹68.6 to the manufacturer, ₹7.6 to the stockist, ₹19.0 to the chemist and ₹4.8 to GST at 5%. These are customary shares, not statutory ones, except where the NPPA has intervened. An independent chemist rarely keeps the full ₹19: it gives regulars a few percent off, writes off expired stock that the manufacturer will not take back, and absorbs credit to doctors' clinics and nursing homes. A realistic gross margin for a branded-heavy independent is 16–18% (indicative).

A chain improves on that in four ways. First, it self-distributes: MedPlus runs its own warehouses (about 33 days of warehouse inventory and around 51,000 SKUs across them), Apollo's stores are supplied by Apollo HealthCo, and Reliance supplies its own outlets. That captures part of the stockist's slice, worth perhaps 5–8% of retail value (indicative). Second, it negotiates scale discounts and year-end rebates from manufacturers; MedPlus's CFO cited a 40-basis-point swing in one quarter from the timing of such a supplier discount. Third, it sells private label: generics and household goods made to its order by contract manufacturers, where the chain keeps much of what would have been the brand owner's share. Fourth, it adds services such as diagnostics and paid membership plans.

Chains also give money back. MedPlus offers 20% off bills above ₹1,000 (trimmed to 19% for non-UPI payments from 7 July 2026), and sells a membership plan whose price rose from ₹99 to ₹149, with 44–45 lakh active holders in June 2026. Online pharmacies, which once gave 25–40% discounts, now give 8–14%. The net result is that a chain's gross margin ends up around 25–26%, roughly eight points above a branded-heavy independent, not the 40% the custom-plus-private-label arithmetic might suggest.

In plain terms

When you buy a branded blood-pressure tablet, the chemist keeps about ₹19 of every ₹100. When you accept the chain's own version of the same molecule at ₹60–70, the chain may keep ₹35–40, even though you paid less. That is why every chain wants you to switch, why the pharmacist behind the counter is often paid an incentive to suggest it, and why pushing too hard can lose a customer for good.

Two further features of the economics deserve emphasis because they run through the whole report. Medicines are mostly paid for out of pocket: India's insurance and government schemes cover hospital stays, not outpatient prescriptions, so the patient is price-sensitive and loyal to whoever is cheapest and closest. And demand is steady: a diabetic or hypertensive patient refills every month regardless of the economy. That combination, a steady, price-sensitive, local demand for a product whose price is fixed by the manufacturer, is what makes pharmacy retail a game of convenience, assortment and cost rather than of pricing power.

02

Size, growth and the channel map

The medicine market grows about 9–10% a year, mostly on price. Retail-type channels carry about two-thirds of it, and chains are the fastest-growing slice. No official number exists for the chains' size today; the best estimate puts them at ₹30,000–40,000 crore.

The best measure of the market is the audited Indian Pharmaceutical Market, compiled by Pharmarack (PharmaTrac) and AIOCD-AWACS from stockist and retailer data. Pharmarack's moving annual total (MAT) for March 2026, which matches FY26, was ₹2,45,943 crore, up 8.6% in value and 0.6% in units. The same body had put 2023 at about ₹2.42 lakh crore after 9.1% annual growth from 2019, and projects ₹4.6 lakh crore by 2030, a 9.6% compound rate. One caveat runs through every use of these figures: the published releases do not say whether the market is valued at MRP or at trade prices, so they should not be added to retail margins or compared directly with a retailer's revenue.

Calendar 2026 has been stronger. Monthly value growth ran 10–13.5% from March to September, against Pharmarack's own January forecast of 7.8–8.1%. Volume, flat for most of the year, turned positive in June (+3.3%) and September (+3.4%). The GST cut to 5% from 22 September 2025 lowered shelf prices; whether it lifted volumes is not established. Price growth has been held down by the NPPA's annual revision for scheduled drugs (+0.65% in April 2026, tied to the wholesale price index) and by the 10% cap on increases for the rest; actual increases on non-scheduled drugs averaged about 5.6% a year in 2020–25.

The therapy mix explains why pharmacy demand is sturdier than the headline suggests. In March 2026 anti-diabetic medicines grew 15.5%, cardiac 14.6%, neuro and central nervous system 11.6%, while anti-infectives grew only 4.0% and gastro 4.3%. Chronic medicines, the monthly refill basket of diabetes, blood pressure, cholesterol and thyroid patients, account for roughly 37–40% of the market and grow two to three times faster than acute ones. That basket is where chains and online pharmacies compete hardest, because a patient who refills every month is worth winning with a membership card, a reminder call or a home delivery.

The market is also overwhelmingly branded. In 2023 branded generics, off-patent molecules sold under a company's brand name and promoted to doctors, were 87% of the IPM (about ₹2.10 lakh crore). Trade generics, sold to chemists on margin rather than promoted to doctors, were 10% (about ₹24,000 crore) and growing faster, at about 15% a year. Patented drugs were about 3% and unbranded "generic generics" about 0.5% (₹1,200 crore). That structure matters to a retailer: the doctor's prescription names a brand, the brand's MRP fixes the retailer's margin, and the only way to earn more is to persuade the patient to accept a substitute.

The channel map

The IPA/Pharmarack study remains the only industry-quality split of the market by channel; no rating agency has published a newer one. Applied to the 2023 market, it gives the following picture. Standalone pharmacies carried about ₹1.31 lakh crore; hospitals and institutions about ₹75,700 crore; organised chains ₹28,000 crore; online pharmacies about ₹6,000 crore; Jan Aushadhi about ₹1,500–2,000 crore. By 2030 the study expects standalone to fall to 42% of a much larger market, institutions to rise to 34.2% as doctors consolidate into hospitals, and chains to reach 17.4% or ₹72,832 crore, implying mid-teens annual growth. Other estimates exist (a brokerage put "organised" retail including e-commerce at 18% in 2023), but they mix definitions; we prefer the industry body's split.

What are the chains worth today? There is no published figure, so we build it two ways, both indicative. In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 Indians spend on medicines, about ₹12–15 is now spent in a chain store, about ₹3 online, about ₹1 at a government generic shop, about ₹30 inside a hospital and the rest, roughly half, at the neighbourhood chemist. The chains' share has been rising by about a point a year. At that pace the independent chemist will still be the biggest seller of medicines in India in 2030.

Non-medicine sales add to the retail pool but not to the IPM. About a fifth of MedPlus's pharmacy revenue is household and wellness goods, branded and private label, and consultants that count these sales put the whole "retail pharmacy" market at ₹2.0–2.3 lakh crore. Those estimates come from press releases with no published method, so we do not rely on them. The useful takeaway is simpler: a chain store is partly a convenience store, and that helps it get more visits from each customer than a chemist that sells only medicines.

03

From ten lakh counters to dense clusters

Force 1, formalisation. Chains win share because they stock more, run cheaper supply and give a discount, but they win cities rather than the country. Cluster density, many stores close together around one warehouse, is the moat.

The independent chemist has survived two decades of predictions of its demise for good reasons. It is close, often within walking distance; it extends informal credit; it knows its customers; its owner works long hours for no salary; and it is frequently in premises the family owns, so it pays little or no rent. On an indicative composite, a branded-heavy independent earns a gross margin of 16–18%, pays 3–6% of sales in rent if it rents at all, 5–7% for family labour and one or two staff, and 1–2% for electricity and other costs, leaving an operating margin of 3–6% before the owner's own salary. Its inventory of 30–45 days is largely financed by its stockist's credit.

A crude calculation shows how small each counter is. If standalone chemists carry 54% of a ₹2.46 lakh crore market and there are 8–12 lakh of them, each sells about ₹11–17 lakh a year, roughly ₹1–1.4 lakh a month (indicative; the IPM valuation basis and the chemist count are both uncertain). A MedPlus store sells about ₹12 lakh a month, ten times more. Scale shows up as reliability: chains stock 30,000+ SKUs across their networks (MedPlus about 51,000 across warehouses) and can promise that a prescription will be filled in full, which a small shop cannot.

Chains grow by taking customers from that standalone pool, and the evidence is that they do so city by city. MedPlus's founder built the chain from Hyderabad from 2006 on a single principle: open stores close to each other, even at the risk of taking sales from one's own shops. Management says plainly that it does not track same-store sales growth for that reason ("we continuously add stores close by to our own stores"). A dense cluster lowers delivery cost per store, lets one warehouse turn its stock faster, makes a store-fulfilled two-hour delivery promise credible, and makes the brand visible on every second street. It also makes the chain the default choice for a whole neighbourhood, which is the closest thing pharmacy retail has to a moat.

The geography shows the cluster logic. At 30 September 2026, by our reading of the company's map (±2 stores), MedPlus had about 1,145 stores in Tamil Nadu, 1,048 in Karnataka, 941 in Telangana, 764 in West Bengal, 728 in Andhra Pradesh and 644 in Maharashtra, with small and growing footprints in Odisha (193), Chhattisgarh (63), Madhya Pradesh (57) and Kerala (46), and a single store in Delhi. Four southern states hold about 69% of the network. Management describes its expansion as "contiguous": Maharashtra, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Kerala next, then "northwards and westwards" once each new state is profitable. Where the two big chains meet, they meet head-on. Apollo had 1,337 stores in Tamil Nadu, 1,011 in Telangana and 938 in West Bengal at 30 June 2026, almost exactly matching MedPlus's density in those states. MedPlus is more than twice Apollo's size in Maharashtra (about 644 against 272); Apollo is national, with hundreds of stores in Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Rajasthan and Haryana where MedPlus has almost none. Demand supports the build. India has more than ten crore adults with diabetes and over twenty crore with hypertension (ICMR-INDIAB, 2023); medicines are the largest component of out-of-pocket health spending; and chronic refills grow faster than the market. Two forces push the other way. Doctors are consolidating into hospitals, which keep the prescription (and the pharmacy sale) in-house; the institutional channel is expected to rise from 31.3% to 34.2% of the market by 2030. And government generics, from Jan Aushadhi to Davaindia's private generic model, are adding outlets far faster than the chains, albeit small ones (section 10).

In plain terms

A chain does not beat the corner chemist everywhere at once. It picks a city, opens dozens of stores close together, supplies them from one warehouse, and becomes the obvious place to go. Then it moves to the next state. That is why MedPlus is a giant in Hyderabad and almost absent in Delhi, and why its expansion speed depends on how fast it can build new clusters rather than on how big the national market is.

The share shift is real but slow, roughly a point of the medicine market a year at best. For a listed chain that means its growth comes from two sources it can control, new stores and more sales per store, and one it cannot, the market's own 9–10% nominal growth. MedPlus's revenue per average store has been flat at ₹1.35–1.4 crore for four years (FY23–FY26, indicative arithmetic), because each year's new and smaller-town stores pull the average down as fast as older stores mature. Its growth has come almost entirely from store count. That is why the 800-net-store target for FY27, of which 312 were delivered in the first half, is the most important single number for its revenue this year.

Where ₹100 goes: four ways to run a pharmacy

The same ₹100 of medicine sales supports very different cost structures. A dense chain lives on a 25% gross margin and a 20% cost base; an independent on 17% and family labour; a franchise store splits the margin; an online pharmacy spends its margin on delivery.

Start with the only listed pure-play chain. In FY26 MedPlus earned a gross margin of 26.2% on revenue of ₹6,892 crore, after supplier rebates and inventory provisions. Its operating costs, measured the way management measures them (before Ind AS 116, so that store rent counts as a cost), were 20.9% of revenue: employee costs about 12.8% (the annual report puts employee benefits at 12.70% of total income, ₹884.5 crore), rent 4.0% (₹272.5 crore), and everything else, power, packaging, card and UPI fees, warehousing, marketing and technology, about 4.1% by subtraction. That left an operating EBITDA of ₹365.8 crore, 5.3% of revenue, and a profit after tax of ₹219.6 crore, 3.2%.

In plain terms

Every way of selling medicines in India ends up with a profit of about ₹3–7 per ₹100 of sales, once all the costs are counted. The chain earns more on each strip of tablets but pays for staff, rent and warehouses; the corner chemist earns less but pays itself nothing; the app saves on rent but pays for riders and advertising. What separates winners from losers is volume per outlet and how much capital each rupee of sales ties up.

Capital intensity is the final dimension. MedPlus carried inventory of ₹1,521 crore at 30 September 2026 against trade payables of ₹364 crore: net working capital of 54 days of revenue. A company-operated store ties up an indicative ₹20–22 lakh (about ₹14 lakh of stock, ₹5 lakh of fit-out and ₹2–3 lakh of rental deposit), which it earns back at the store level in one and a half to two years, and in three to four once overheads are allocated. A franchise store ties up almost nothing of MedPlus's money. An online player's capital goes into central warehouses and delivery fleets. A distributor such as Entero ties up receivables. Whichever model a reader looks at, the right question is the same: how many rupees of profit does each rupee of capital produce, after every cost?

The private-label pendulum Deep dive

Force 2, mix not markup. A chain cannot raise the price of a branded drug, but it can sell its own version. MedPlus pushed that lever hard in FY25, lost customers, and has spent FY27 pulling back. The pendulum shows where the trust ceiling sits.

Private label is the single biggest lever a pharmacy chain has on its gross margin, because it is the only one that changes who captures the manufacturer's share. When a patient buys a branded drug, the chain is a price-taker on a customary margin. When the same patient accepts the chain's own product containing the same molecule, made by a contract manufacturer and sold at a lower shelf price, the chain captures a large part of what would have been the brand owner's margin. MedPlus sells 1,750+ private-label SKUs (900+ medicines across chronic, acute and over-the-counter categories, and 850+ non-medicine items from packaged food to cleaning products), made by "three or four" contract manufacturers that also supply large drug companies, with every batch tested in its own NABL-accredited laboratory.

The arithmetic of one switch

Take a ₹100 branded chronic refill. The chain's gross margin on it is about ₹19. Offer the patient the chain's own version of the molecule at ₹60–70, saving the customer 30–40%; the chain's gross margin on the private-label pack is perhaps ₹35–40 (illustrative). Sales fall by a third, but gross profit roughly doubles. Moving one point of revenue from branded pharma (about 19% margin) to private-label pharma (about 55%) adds about 36 basis points of gross margin on paper. Management's own rule of thumb, repeated on the Q1 FY27 call, is that every 20–30 basis points of private-label share adds about 10 basis points of gross margin, which implies 33–50 basis points per point of share. The two methods agree.

So why not sell nothing but private label? Because the prescription names a brand, the doctor may object, and the patient's trust in an unfamiliar name is fragile. India's medicine market is 87% branded generics for a reason: patients and doctors use brands as a proxy for quality in a market where regulatory enforcement is uneven. Private label also carries an inventory risk the chain does not bear on brands. MedPlus's private label is made to order and cannot be returned; branded products are 90–95% returnable to the manufacturer when they expire. The company provisions about 0.8–1.0% of sales for inventory losses overall, but up to about 5% on private label.

The swing: from push to retreat

The annual reports show the push clearly. MedPlus's private-label share was 10.1% of revenue in FY21, 12.7% in FY22, 13.6% in FY23 and 14.26% in FY24; then 19.29% in FY25 and 21.88% in FY26. From FY25 the annual report describes the ratio as private-label share "to consolidated revenue", and the FY24 figure is close to the private-label pharma-only share in the company's quarterly decks. The jump from 14.3% to 19.3% therefore probably includes a change of basis (non-medicine private label being added). The often-quoted line that private label was "about 13% at the IPO" belongs to the old basis and cannot be compared with today's 21%.

In plain terms

MedPlus discovered that its customers would accept its own medicines up to a point, and that past that point they would walk to a rival who sold them the brand their doctor wrote down. It overshot, took a year of weak sales at its older stores, and is now rebuilding slowly. The lesson for the whole industry is that private label is a dial to turn carefully, not a switch.

The rest of the industry

Every chain is on the same path at a different speed. The IPA/Pharmarack study puts private label at 15–18% of chain sales and expects 25–30% by 2030. Apollo reported "private label and generics" at 15.53% of pharmacy sales in Q3 FY26 (a search excerpt of its own materials; a different basis from MedPlus's) and on its Q2 FY27 call blamed part of a margin dip on hiring for a private-label and generics team. Davaindia sells only its parent Zota's own generics, at a 60% gross margin and steep discounts to brands. Jan Aushadhi sells only government-branded generics. At the other end, online pure-plays such as Truemeds were built on generic substitution, offering a cheaper equivalent on every order.

Policy is slowly tilting the field towards private label. The National Medical Commission's 2023 regulation requiring doctors to prescribe by generic name was put in abeyance after protests, but if it returns the dispensing decision would move from the doctor to the counter, which favours chains with their own generics. The NPPA's trade-margin caps on cancer drugs (section 10) cut the rupee margin on brands in capped categories, which makes private label relatively more valuable. And Jan Aushadhi's 50–80% discount advertising teaches patients that generics are acceptable. Against that, a 2026 consumer who has been oversold once is harder to win back, and medicines regulators are inspecting chains more often.

The investment conclusion is narrower than the bull case in our MedPlus primer suggested. Private label is not "climbing" towards some higher natural share; it is oscillating around 20–23% of MedPlus's revenue, with the company now trying to rebuild by a few tenths of a point a quarter. At the management rule of thumb, rebuilding from 21.4% to the 23.3% peak would add roughly 65–95 basis points of gross margin (indicative), meaningful for a business earning 5% at the operating line, but a multi-quarter project that depends on customers not walking away again.

The 11.9% illusion Deep dive

A mature MedPlus store earns about 12% EBITDA at the store level. The company does not, and will not simply by ageing. The gap is warehouse, delivery, head-office and marketing cost, about six points of revenue, and it does not shrink as stores mature.

Pharmacy chains report a "mature store" margin because it answers a real question: does a store, once it has found its customers, make good money on the capital it ties up? MedPlus's answer is yes. Its stores open more than twelve months earned a store-level EBITDA of 11.9% in FY26, 13.1% in Q4 FY26, 10.4% in Q1 FY27 and 11.7% in Q2 FY27, on a store-level operating return on capital of 62–80% annualised. Our MedPlus primer, written in August 2026, drew the natural but wrong conclusion: that the company-wide margin of about 5.3% "should climb toward the mature one", and that the gap was "the drag from young, sub-scale stores". The company's own disclosures show that most of the gap is something else.

What the store-level number leaves out

Store-level EBITDA is the store's gross profit minus the costs incurred inside the store: counter staff and pharmacists, rent and store running costs. It excludes everything that happens before the goods reach the store or above it: central and regional warehouses, trucks and delivery hubs, the head office, technology, marketing, procurement and the pre-opening costs of new stores. In a chain that self-distributes, those costs are large. MedPlus's own bridge for Q4 FY26 allocates them: the 12-month-plus stores, which earned 13.1% at the store level, earned ₹113.3 crore, or 6.7% of their revenue, once all non-store costs were allocated. In Q1 FY27 the CFO gave the same calculation on the call: "On allocating all non-store-related costs, the operating EBITDA of stores greater than 12 months would be INR707 million, which translates to a margin of 4.3%." Store-level that quarter was 10.4%. The gap was 6.4 points in Q4 FY26 and 6.1 points in Q1 FY27.

What slowing store openings would really do

The bridges put a number on the "young store drag". Pre-opening costs run at ₹7.3–7.6 crore a quarter for 220–295 openings, about ₹3 lakh per store (indicative), or about ₹30 crore a year. Young stores swing between a ₹3.6 crore quarterly loss and a ₹1.4 crore profit. Together the cost of growth is about ₹30–45 crore a year, 0.5–0.7% of revenue. If MedPlus stopped opening stores tomorrow, its margin would rise by roughly 50–70 basis points, not the 600 or so that a convergence from 5.3% to 11.9% implies. In plain terms

A mature MedPlus store looks like it earns ₹12 on every ₹100 it sells. Wages and rent rise; medicine prices are capped

Force 3. A pharmacy's revenue per pack is tied to MRPs that rise about 5–6% a year. Its costs are people and premises, which do not wait. The June 2026 minimum-wage notifications in Karnataka and Telangana showed how quickly a point of margin can disappear.

The price side of a pharmacy's economics is set in Delhi. About 17–18% of the medicine market is covered by the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM 2022, 384 medicines), whose formulations carry ceiling prices calculated as the average price of brands with at least 1% share, with a 16% retail margin built in; 935 scheduled formulations had ceilings in July 2026. Those ceilings move once a year with the wholesale price index: +0.65% in April 2026, and close to zero since 2024. The other 82% of the market is "non-scheduled": manufacturers may raise MRP by at most 10% a year, and in 2020–25 they raised it by about 5.6% a year on average. On top of price, volume grew only 0.6% in FY26. A pharmacy's rupee margin per pack therefore grows at roughly the rate of MRP inflation plus mix, about 6–9% a year in a normal year.

The cost side is local. Staff cost is decided by state minimum-wage notifications, by the wages offered across the street by quick-commerce and delivery companies, and by the number of registered pharmacists available; rent by city property markets. In FY26, before any of the 2026 wage notifications, MedPlus's employee benefit expense rose 21.8% to ₹884.5 crore while revenue rose 12.3%, taking staff cost from 11.74% to 12.70% of total income (FY26 annual report). Rent rose 14.0% to ₹272.5 crore, roughly in line with store growth. The new national Labour Codes, effective 21 November 2025, changed the definition of wages and added ₹6.5 crore of one-time past-service cost for gratuity and leave encashment in FY26.

In plain terms

A pharmacy cannot raise the price of a strip of tablets when its wage bill jumps. When Karnataka raised the minimum wage by about 60% in one go, MedPlus had to find the money elsewhere: a slightly smaller discount for card payers, a pricier membership card, fewer bonuses. That worked this time. It will not work every time, and every chain and independent in those states faces the same bill.

Why labour is the structural squeeze

The wage notifications are the visible part of a deeper change. MedPlus's management said its staff were being drawn away by "lower wages, especially when compared to employees in the quick commerce business": a young person with a two-wheeler can earn more delivering groceries than standing behind a pharmacy counter. Registered pharmacists, legally required in every licensed store, are scarcer still. The result is rising attrition, rising wages and the temptation to cut corners on staffing, which in turn invites the drug-licence suspensions described in section 10. Global chains report the same pressure: RD Saúde said its margin was "still pressured by investments in employee pay and benefits made at the end of 2025", and Tsuruha's operating margin fell to 3.8% on higher selling and administrative costs. Pharmacy retail is a labour business everywhere.

Rent and the Ind AS 116 drag

Rent is the smaller cost but has its own accounting twist. MedPlus's average store is about 539 square feet, and the network covered 2.9 million square feet in June 2026 against 2.5 million a year earlier. Q2 FY27 rent was ₹80.75 crore, up 19.7% against revenue up 21.3%, or about ₹48,000 per store per month (indicative). Under Ind AS 116, leases are capitalised: the company books a right-of-use asset and a lease liability, and replaces rent with depreciation and interest. MedPlus's lease liabilities were ₹1,424.4 crore at 31 March 2026, up 27% in a year, and lease commitments maturing beyond five years almost doubled to ₹716 crore as longer leases were signed. Because lease interest is front-loaded, a young and growing lease book makes reported profit lower than cash profit: the Ind AS treatment cut MedPlus's FY26 PAT by about ₹43.7 crore (pre-Ind AS PAT was ₹263.3 crore against reported ₹219.6 crore), and by about ₹14 crore in Q2 FY27 alone.

For the industry, the arithmetic is unforgiving. Two ways to own a pharmacy chain Deep dive

MedPlus's listed company owns its stores and books retail sales. Apollo's listed company owns the wholesale supply, the app and a stake in the business that owns the stores. The two look similar on a store count and are not comparable on almost anything else.

India's two largest pharmacy chains were built under the same foreign-investment rules and came out with opposite structures. Indian policy has long barred foreign direct investment in multi-brand retail, which covers a pharmacy selling many companies' products. A listed company with large foreign shareholders could therefore not simply own a national chain of shops. Apollo Hospitals, whose pharmacy began in 1987 as an adjunct to its first hospital in Chennai, solved this by splitting the business: its listed subsidiary, Apollo HealthCo, runs wholesale distribution to the stores and the Apollo 24|7 digital platform, while the stores themselves sit in a separate company. Apollo's 4 November 2026 results filing names it: the stores are run by Apollo Pharmacies Ltd, a subsidiary of Apollo Medical Pvt Ltd (AMPL), which is an associate, not a subsidiary, of Apollo HealthCo.

In plain terms

Buying MedPlus shares means owning the shops. Buying Apollo Hospitals shares, and in future Apollo Healthtech shares, means owning the wholesaler that supplies Apollo's shops, the Apollo 24|7 app and part of a company that owns the shops. Apollo's pharmacy business looks more profitable on paper partly because the costliest part of running a pharmacy, the rent and staff in each store, is on somebody else's books.

The investment consequence is that Apollo Healthtech, when it lists, will not be the clean valuation comparator for MedPlus that many expect. It will be a distributor and digital platform with a captive retail customer, closer to Entero plus an app than to a store chain. Its margin will reflect wholesale economics and platform investment; its growth will reflect Keimed's open-market distribution as well as Apollo's stores. What it will provide is a second listed pharmacy-adjacent business of real scale, which may change how the market values the whole category. For the industry, the two structures are also two strategies: MedPlus bets on owning the customer at the counter and capturing the whole retail margin; Apollo bets on owning the prescription, through its hospitals, doctors and app, and the supply chain behind it.

Two structural facts deserve a final note. First, the Apollo design concentrates store-level risk, wage shocks, licence actions and rent, in an entity outside the listed group, while the listed group earns a steadier wholesale margin; that is a real advantage for its shareholders. Second, MedPlus's franchise push borrows a piece of Apollo's logic: by letting franchisees own stores, it shifts store costs and capital off its own books while keeping supply and brand. The two models are converging at the edges.

The channel war: apps, ten-minute delivery and the store as a warehouse

Force 4. Online pharmacy took a decade to reach an indicative 3–3.5% of the market, burned through billions and is now close to breakeven. Quick commerce is the new attack on the acute basket. The chains' answer is to make every store a fulfilment node.

Online pharmacy arrived in India around 2015 with a simple pitch: a discount of 25–40% off MRP, delivered to the door. PharmEasy, 1mg, Netmeds, Medlife and later Truemeds raised venture money on the assumption that India would follow China's path to a large online medicine market. The pitch ran into three walls. The law was never settled (section 10); chemists' associations fought every step; and the discounts were funded by investors rather than by any cost advantage, since a single medicine order is small, bulky relative to its value and often urgent. COVID-19 helped briefly, when the government's GSR 220(E) notification of March 2020 allowed licensed retailers to deliver medicines to homes. Then came consolidation: Reliance bought 60% of Netmeds' parent for about ₹620 crore in August 2020, Tata Digital took majority control of 1mg in June 2021, and PharmEasy merged with Medlife and bought Thyrocare for about ₹4,546 crore in 2021.

The bust followed. PharmEasy withdrew its IPO in 2022 and raised ₹2,400 crore in a 2023 rights issue led by Manipal at roughly a 90% cut to its peak valuation. Discounts across the online channel fell to 8–14%. In 2023 online pharmacy was 2.5% of the medicine market by the IPA/Pharmarack count, with more than 60% of sales in chronic medicines and an average order of ₹1,200–1,300. On our bottom-up estimate the channel reached ₹7,500–8,500 crore in FY26, about 3–3.5% of the IPM (indicative): still small, but growing faster than the market rather than stalling.

Narrowing losses cut both ways for store chains. The irrational discounting of 2018–22, when online players gave away a fifth of MRP, is over, which takes pressure off chain discounts. But the survivors are stronger, better funded by conglomerates (Tata, Reliance) or hospital groups (Apollo), and now growing faster than the market. A channel that grows 20% a year from 3% of the market reaches about 6% in four years; that would be a material share of the chronic-refill basket that chains depend on.

Quick commerce: the acute basket under attack

The newest front is ten-minute delivery. Zepto launched Zepto Pharmacy in August 2025 after a 12-month pilot (Mumbai, Bengaluru, Delhi-NCR, Hyderabad); Swiggy Instamart delivers medicines with PharmEasy; Blinkit, Flipkart Minutes and PhonePe's Pincode offer similar services. A Telangana Today report in August 2026 described quick-commerce apps fulfilling prescriptions after "30-second" tele-consultations, with Hyderabad chemists calling it a grey zone. No credible figure for quick-commerce medicine sales exists in the public domain, so the threat cannot be quantified. Its target is clear: the acute and over-the-counter purchase, a fever, a cough syrup, an antacid, bought in a hurry in a metro. MedPlus earns 45% of its pharmacy revenue in five metros. So far the company's numbers do not show damage: metro stores keep growing, and the metro share drifted from 47% to 45% mainly because of faster growth elsewhere.

MedPlus's chief executive dismissed the model on the Q1 FY27 call: "almost all of these businesses are bleeding cash … The day people figure out how to actually give a 20% discount, give a free delivery and give it in 10 minutes, we'll be more than happy to go to that model." The point is fair on economics, since a dark store selling medicines needs a licence and a pharmacist just as a chemist does, but quick commerce has already done one kind of damage: it competes for the same young workers that pharmacies employ, and at higher wages.

In plain terms

Apps tried to sell medicines cheaper than shops by spending investors' money on discounts. That phase is over: the apps now lose little or nothing, but give much smaller discounts. Ten-minute delivery apps are the new rival for the urgent purchase in big cities. Nobody yet knows how much they sell, and nobody has shown that selling medicines in ten minutes makes money.

The store as the warehouse

The chains' response is omnichannel fulfilled from stores. MedPlus takes orders online and delivers them from its nearest store, promising two-hour delivery or store pickup; by Q2 FY27 it ran 735 delivery hubs serving 3,441 pincodes, and its omnichannel revenue had grown from about ₹88 crore in Q2 FY26 to about ₹111 crore, roughly 5.5% of revenue (read from a chart). Apollo's 24|7 platform has 49 million registrations, about 8 lakh daily active users and about 53,000 daily medicine orders, and management wants daily orders up 20–30% in six months on a flat cost base. Both models dispatch each order from a licensed retail store. That is the most defensible structure if the rules tighten, because the Madras High Court's 2024 order allows online sales only by licensed chemists, and it uses the store's existing stock and staff rather than a separate warehouse.

The likely end-state is not online against offline but a split of the basket. Chronic refills, which can be scheduled, suit home delivery from whoever offers the best price and reminder service; that favours scaled platforms and store chains with apps. The state at the counter: price-setter, competitor, inspector Deep dive

Force 5. The government caps the price of a sixth of what a pharmacy sells, has begun capping trade margins directly, runs a 20,000-outlet generic chain of its own, has left online pharmacy in legal limbo for eight years, and licenses every counter.

Few retail businesses have the state as closely involved in their economics as Indian pharmacy. The government appears in four roles. As price-setter, through the NPPA and the Drugs (Prices Control) Order 2013 (DPCO), it fixes ceiling prices on essential medicines and caps annual increases on the rest. As competitor, through the Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana (PMBJP), it franchises government-branded generic pharmacies. As rule-maker for new channels, it has published, and never finalised, rules for online pharmacy. As inspector, through state drugs control administrations, it licenses and suspends individual stores. Each role compresses the branded rupee margin a little, and together they explain why private label and density matter so much.

Price-setter: from ceilings to trade-margin caps

The ceiling system is described in section 07: NLEM drugs (about 17–18% of the market) carry formula ceilings with a 16% retail margin built in and move each April with the wholesale price index; other drugs may rise at most 10% a year. Since 2019 the NPPA has also used a stronger tool. Paragraph 19 of the DPCO lets it act in "extraordinary circumstances" in the public interest, and in February 2019 it used that power to cap the total trade margin on 42 non-scheduled cancer drugs at 30%, cutting the MRPs of 526 brands by up to 90%. On 8 October 2026 the authority found extraordinary circumstances again; on 17 October it gazetted a cap on 137 non-scheduled anti-cancer drugs, a list recommended by an expert committee of the Directorate General of Health Services, under which the total trade margin from price-to-stockist to MRP may not exceed 30% of MRP. The order took effect on 9 November 2026. Manufacturers had seven days to report revised prices; MRPs were expected to fall by up to 70%, about 600 brands by more than half; the government estimates patient savings of about ₹2,500 crore a year. Apollo's CFO said on the Q2 FY27 call that the impact would be quantified once revised MRPs arrived.

The government's own chain is the fastest-growing pharmacy network in the country by outlets. In plain terms

The government runs its own chain of about 20,000 small generic pharmacies, and wants 25,000 by March 2027. Each one sells very little, about ₹1 lakh a month, and many lose money. Rule-maker: the e-pharmacy limbo

Online pharmacy operates under rules that do not exist. The Health Ministry published draft Drugs and Cosmetics (Amendment) Rules on e-pharmacy as GSR 817(E) in August 2018; they were never notified and never withdrawn. Courts filled the gap. The Delhi High Court restrained unlicensed online sales in December 2018 in Zaheer Ahmed v Union of India, and on 4 May 2024 gave the ministry a "last and final opportunity" to frame a policy within four months, a deadline that has passed. A Madras High Court single judge directed the Centre to notify the 2018 rules in December 2018; on appeal, a division bench (Practo v Tamil Nadu Chemists and Druggists Association, July 2024) ordered the status quo until the Centre frames policy or the Delhi case is decided, with online sales only by licensed chemists. The operating basis for home delivery remains GSR 220(E) of 26 March 2020, a COVID measure. A draft Drugs, Medical Devices and Cosmetics Act, which would replace the 1940 Act and require a licence for online sale, was submitted by the drugs controller to the Health Ministry on 15 October 2025; it had not been introduced in Parliament by November 2026.

Four decades, one cycle

From the family chemist to the first chains, the online boom and bust, and MedPlus's private-label swing. The defining events, and what each did to the economics of the counter.

For most of independent India's history, medicines were sold by family chemists supplied on credit by local stockists, under price controls that covered far more drugs than today. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act of 1940 and its 1945 Rules created the retail licence and the pharmacist requirement that still define the industry. Apollo Pharmacy opened in 1987 in Chennai as an adjunct to India's first corporate hospital. The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority was set up in 1997. Until the mid-2000s, organised pharmacy retail barely existed.

The first wave of chains came between 2006 and 2015. MedPlus was founded in Hyderabad in 2006 by Gangadi Madhukar Reddy and built dense South Indian clusters; Apollo scaled nationally; Wellness Forever (Mumbai), Guardian (Delhi NCR), Frank Ross (Kolkata, Emami group) and Noble Plus (Gujarat) grew regionally. The Jan Aushadhi scheme was launched in 2008 and relaunched as the PMBJP in 2015–16. The DPCO 2013 moved price control to market-based ceilings on NLEM drugs. Foreign-investment rules shaped structures: Apollo separated store ownership from distribution.

The online boom ran from 2015 to 2021, financed by venture capital and conglomerates. Draft e-pharmacy rules in 2018 and the court orders that followed froze the legal framework; COVID-19 in 2020 brought home delivery under GSR 220(E); Reliance bought Netmeds and Tata bought 1mg. MedPlus listed in December 2021 at ₹796 a share, raising about ₹1,398 crore (₹600 crore fresh, the rest an offer for sale including Warburg Pincus's vehicle). The bust followed: PharmEasy's IPO was withdrawn in 2022 and its 2023 rights issue priced the company at about a tenth of its peak; online discounts fell to 8–14%.

In plain terms

The industry has been through one full swing in a decade: investors poured money into apps that gave medicines away at a loss, then stopped; chains raced to open stores, then slowed; MedPlus pushed its own medicines hard, then backed off. Each swing ended in the same place, a thin margin earned slowly by being close to the customer, and each taught the same lesson about how little room there is to buy growth in a business whose prices are set by somebody else.

The pattern is worth stating because it recurs. When capital is cheap, someone tries to buy share in Indian pharmacy with discounts or store openings, margins fall, and the share gain proves smaller than hoped. When capital tightens, the survivors cut discounts, slow openings, push private label and rebuild margin, until a cost shock or a regulator takes some of it back. The listed chain's earnings have followed that rhythm: profit fell in FY23, recovered strongly in FY25–FY26, and slipped in H1 FY27. Investors who extrapolated FY26's 46% profit growth were extrapolating the top of a swing.

Reading a pharmacy chain: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number a pharmacy chain reports, what it means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, and where the definitions hide traps. MedPlus is the worked example throughout because it is the only listed chain that discloses store-level economics.

A pharmacy chain's results look simple: revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, store count. Each of those words hides a choice of definition that can move the answer by several percentage points. The chain may count franchise stores as its own; it may report EBITDA before or after the lease accounting standard Ind AS 116; it may quote a store-level margin that excludes a quarter of its costs; it may describe private label as a share of pharmacy revenue or of total revenue. This section builds the business from its equation, then takes each key performance indicator (KPI) in turn: what it is, the formula, a worked rupee example, what moves it, a benchmark, how MedPlus compares, and the trap.

The equation: from a store count to reported EBITDA

A pharmacy chain's operating profit is the product of a few physical quantities and a few ratios. Revenue is the number of stores multiplied by revenue per store. Gross profit is revenue multiplied by gross margin, itself a blend of branded and private-label margins less discounts. Store EBITDA is gross profit less the costs incurred inside stores: staff, rent, utilities. Operating EBITDA, on the company's own pre-Ind AS definition, is store EBITDA less the costs above the store: warehouses, logistics, head office, technology and marketing, plus pre-opening costs. Reported EBITDA under Ind AS 116 then adds rent back, because the standard reclassifies rent into depreciation and interest.

In plain terms

A pharmacy chain makes money in five steps: open stores, sell enough in each, keep about a quarter of each sale as gross profit, pay the store's own costs, then pay for everything behind the stores. Each step has its own number, and each number can be quoted in a flattering way. The rest of this section is a guide to which version to trust.

KPI 1: Stores and net additions (owned versus franchise)

What it is. The number of pharmacy stores at period end, and the change in the period: openings minus closures. Formula. Net additions = openings − closures; store growth = net additions ÷ opening store count. Worked example. In Q2 FY27 MedPlus opened 219 stores and closed 53, a net 166, taking the count from 5,476 to 5,642 (+3.0% in the quarter); over twelve months it added 712 net, +14.4%. What drives it. Capital, site availability, the speed of building new clusters, and since FY26 the willingness of franchisees to sign up. Benchmark. For a growth chain, 12–18% annual net store growth is healthy; above 25% (MedPlus in FY23) usually depresses margins; below 8% means the chain has chosen margin over growth. How the chains compare. In Q2 FY27 Apollo added 222 net stores to about 7,662 (indicative), MedPlus 166 to 5,642, Davaindia 15 to 2,840 (20 opened, 5 closed, all closures franchise).

The trap. Since FY26, MedPlus's store count includes franchise stores: about 310 of FY26's 618 net additions and 131 of Q1 FY27's 146. A franchise store adds revenue (the company books its supply sales) but almost no company capital, a smaller gross profit and a different risk. Closures now include franchisee withdrawals: 27 of Q1 FY27's 52 closures were franchisees giving up, and "franchisee withdrawn" was a named reason in Q2. Always ask how many of the net additions are company-owned. The FY27 guidance of 800 net additions "including franchisee outlets" requires about 488 in H2 after 312 in H1, roughly 244 a quarter against 146 and 166 so far.

KPI 2: Revenue per store

What it is. The average annual sales of a store. Formula. Pharmacy revenue ÷ average stores in the period, annualised. Worked example. Footfall, conversion and bill value; store age (a year-one store sells far less); location (metro stores sell more); format (MedPlus's average store is about 539 square feet; about 47–48 are larger formats); price inflation; and private-label mix, which lowers the rupee bill even as it raises margin. Benchmark. For an Indian chain store, above ₹1.6 crore is strong, ₹1.1–1.6 crore average, below ₹1.0 crore weak. How they compare. MedPlus about ₹1.43 crore; Apollo an indicative ₹1.9 crore of retail value; Davaindia about ₹24 lakh; a Jan Aushadhi kendra about ₹12 lakh; China's Yifeng about ₹2.2 crore; Brazil's RD Saúde about ₹24 crore.

The trap. Revenue per store can stagnate while every individual store grows, because each year's new stores (and new, smaller towns) pull the average down. MedPlus's consolidated revenue per average store was ₹1.59 crore in FY22, then ₹1.39, ₹1.37, ₹1.35 and ₹1.37 crore in FY23–FY26. Growth came from store count, not productivity. A rising share of franchise stores, whose supply sales are booked at wholesale value, lowers the measured figure further.

KPI 3: Growth of stores older than twelve months (not SSSG)

What it is. Year-on-year revenue growth of the stores that have been open for more than twelve months. Retailers elsewhere report same-store sales growth (SSSG) on a fixed set of stores; MedPlus explicitly does not: "we do not really track SSSG per se … we continuously add stores close by to our own stores". Formula. Revenue this period of stores open more than twelve months ÷ revenue of those same stores a year earlier − 1 (the company's description); the qualifying base changes every period. Worked example. In Q1 FY27, stores older than twelve months produced ₹1,643.6 crore, 94% of pharmacy revenue, and grew 15.3%; in Q2 FY27 the figure was 13.2%. What drives it. Maturing of the previous year's openings (a strong tailwind after a big opening year), medicine inflation (about 6–9%), private-label substitution (which lowers the bill), competition and cannibalisation from new stores nearby. Benchmark. Above 12% strong, 6–12% average, below 5% weak; the company's own "number to shoot for" is about 10%.

The trap. Because the cohort includes stores in their second year, still ramping up, this measure runs well above a true like-for-like figure after a year of heavy openings. RD Saúde reports both: same-store growth of 10.1% and "mature" same-store growth of 8.4% in 3Q26. A reader should treat MedPlus's 13.2% as an upper bound on like-for-like growth and compare it with total revenue growth (21.3% in Q2 FY27): the gap is the contribution of new stores.

KPI 4: Private-label share (two bases)

What it is. The share of sales coming from the chain's own brands. Formula. Private-label revenue ÷ the stated denominator, which may be pharmacy revenue or consolidated revenue, and may or may not include non-medicine private label. Worked example. In Q2 FY27 MedPlus's re-based series shows private-label medicines at 10.7% and other private label at 10.7% of pharmacy revenue, 21.4% in total. What drives it. Staff incentives, the breadth of the range (1,750+ SKUs), membership plans that bundle discounts, product availability (a diaper supply disruption cut Q1 FY27's non-pharma share) and customer trust. Benchmark. Above 22% is high for an Indian chain, 12–22% average, below 12% low; the IPA/Pharmarack study puts the chain average at 15–18%, rising to 25–30% by 2030. How they compare. MedPlus 21.4% (re-based); Apollo 15.53% "private label and generics" in Q3 FY26 on its own basis; Davaindia close to 100% (own generics only).

The trap. MedPlus has used at least three bases. The annual reports moved from what appears to be private-label pharma only (14.26% in FY24) to share of consolidated revenue (19.29% in FY25, 21.88% in FY26). The Q4 FY26 deck series (23.3% in Q4 FY25 to 21.9% in Q4 FY26) included franchise sales in the denominator. The Q2 FY27 deck re-based to pharmacy revenue, so Q1 FY27 reads 20.8% there and 20.0% in the call. Never chart two bases on one line, and never compare "about 13% at the IPO" with 21% today.

KPI 5: Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after the cost of goods sold. Formula. (Revenue − cost of goods) ÷ revenue, where cost of goods is net of supplier rebates and includes inventory provisions. Worked example. Q2 FY27: revenue ₹2,037.1 crore, gross margin ₹510.9 crore, 25.1%. What drives it. Private-label mix (management: 20–30 basis points of share ≈ 10 basis points of margin); customer discounts (20% on bills above ₹1,000, 19% for non-UPI payments since July 2026); supplier year-end rebates (40 basis points swung between Q4 and Q1); inventory provisions (about ₹12 crore a quarter, 0.8–1.0% of sales, up to 5% on private label); franchise mix (about 50 basis points lower); and category mix (FMCG versus medicines). Benchmark. For an Indian chain, above 26% strong, 22–26% average, below 22% weak; an independent chemist runs at an indicative 16–18%; online players at about 20–22%. How they compare. MedPlus 25.1% (Q2 FY27); API Holdings 21.4% (Q1 FY27); Zota/Davaindia 60.2% (generic-only); Apollo HealthCo does not report a retail gross margin.

The trap. The quarterly gross margin is noisy. Q4 typically includes supplier year-end rebates and provision releases; Q1 reverses them. Compare a quarter with the same quarter a year earlier, not with the previous quarter, and check whether a gross-margin gain came with weaker older-store growth (the FY25 pattern).

KPI 6: Discount rate and membership plans

What it is. The average reduction from MRP given to customers, and the paid loyalty plans that unlock it. Formula. Discount rate = (sales at MRP ex-GST − net revenue) ÷ sales at MRP ex-GST; MedPlus reports "GMV" as MRP less GST. Worked example. If about 40% of revenue comes from bills above ₹1,000, cutting the discount on those bills by one point is worth roughly 40 basis points of gross margin (indicative). The membership price rise from ₹99 to ₹149 on 44–45 lakh active holders was worth about ₹10–11 crore a year, by the CFO's estimate, about 15 basis points of revenue. What drives it. Competition from the other chain in the same street, online discounts (8–14% now, down from 25–40%), and the chain's need to grow its branded customer base. Benchmark. There is no published industry discount rate; the useful benchmark is the direction of change and whether gross margin holds when discounts are cut. How they compare. MedPlus's headline discount is among the deepest of the chains; online players give 8–14%; Jan Aushadhi and Davaindia compete on list price rather than discount.

The trap. Membership revenue and discount savings both pass through gross margin, so a price rise on the membership looks like a margin improvement in the business. It is real, but it is a one-off step, not a trend. The non-UPI distinction also shifts card fees: UPI is free to the merchant, cards are not.

In plain terms

A pharmacy chain pays customers to come back, through discounts and membership cards. When it trims the discount by a point or charges more for the card, its gross margin rises at once, but only once. Watch whether customers keep coming after the change; MedPlus's older stores still grew 13% in the quarter after it did both.

KPI 7: Store-level EBITDA

What it is. The operating profit of stores before any costs incurred above the store. Formula. (Store gross profit − store staff − store rent − store running costs) ÷ store revenue, for a defined cohort (MedPlus: stores older than twelve months). Worked example. The illustrative store below: gross profit ₹36.7 lakh − rent ₹5.8 lakh − staff ₹11.0 lakh − running costs ₹3.0 lakh = ₹16.9 lakh, 11.7% of ₹144 lakh of sales. What drives it. Gross margin, revenue per store (fixed costs spread over more sales), wage levels and rent per square foot. Benchmark. Above 12% strong, 9–12% average, below 9% weak, for a mature Indian chain store. How they compare. MedPlus 11.7% (Q2 FY27), 11.9% (FY26); Davaindia's oldest cohort (234 stores opened FY21–24) earns 15–17% store EBITDA on a 60% gross margin, its newest cohorts −5% to −7%.

The trap. This is the number behind the "11.9% illusion" (section 06). It excludes about six points of warehouse, logistics, head-office and marketing cost, so it is a ceiling for no one. Use it only to judge whether stores are worth opening.

KPI 8: Fully allocated and operating EBITDA (pre-Ind AS)

What it is. Operating profit after every cost, with rent treated as an operating expense. MedPlus's "operating EBITDA" also excludes ESOP cost and interest income. Formula. KPI 10: Store ROCE versus company ROCE

What it is. Return on capital employed at two levels. Formula. Store-level operating ROCE = (store EBITDA − store depreciation) ÷ (store inventory + store capex + refundable rent deposit), annualised. Company operating ROCE (MedPlus's definition) = operating EBIT ÷ average capital employed excluding cash, which adds warehouse inventory, warehouses and head-office assets. Screener ROCE uses total capital including lease liabilities and cash. Worked example. The illustrative store: (₹16.9 lakh − ₹1.0 lakh) ÷ ₹21.5 lakh = 74%. Benchmark. Store ROCE above 75% strong, 55–75% average, below 55% weak; company ROCE above 25% strong, 15–25% average, below 15% weak. How MedPlus compares. Store ROCE 72.0% (Q2 FY27), 80.0% (Q4 FY26); company ROCE 20.7% (Q2 FY27 annualised), 26.3% (Q4 FY26); screener ROCE 12.6%.

The trap. The 70–80% store number is the one quoted in presentations ("store ROCE over 70%"). The return a shareholder earns on the company's capital is the 12–26% range, depending on whether leases and cash are counted. KPI 11: Inventory days and cash conversion

What it is. How long stock sits before it is sold, and how much working capital the business ties up. Formula. Inventory days = inventory ÷ revenue (or cost of goods) × 365; net working capital days = (inventory + receivables − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. MedPlus's inventory of ₹1,521 crore at September 2026, against Q2 FY27 revenue annualised at about ₹8,150 crore, is about 68 days of revenue; payables of ₹364 crore bring net working capital to the reported 54 days. What drives it. Store age (year-one stores held 86 days of their own revenue in Q2 FY27 against 34 days for stores older than twelve months), warehouse stock (about 33 days), the breadth of the range, private-label orders (made to order, non-returnable) and supplier credit. Benchmark. Net working capital below 45 days strong, 45–60 average, above 60 weak; year-one store inventory below 75 days good, above 100 weak. How MedPlus compares. Screener inventory days (on cost of goods) improved every year from 117 (FY23) to 99 (FY26); the cash conversion cycle from 91 to 79 days.

The trap. Operating cash flow swings with inventory. In Q2 FY27 MedPlus's operating cash flow was ₹37 crore, 37.7% of operating EBITDA, because inventory rose ₹126 crore; free cash flow after capex (about ₹78 crore) and lease payments (about ₹78 crore) was −₹84 crore. Screener's free-cash-flow figure (₹373 crore in FY26) does not deduct lease payments.

KPI 12: Young-store share and closure rate

What it is. The share of the network less than two years old, and the rate at which stores are shut. Formula. Stores under 24 months ÷ total stores; annualised closures ÷ opening store base. The pace of openings, site selection, relocations and franchisee withdrawals. Benchmark. Definition traps, collected

  • Three EBITDAs. MedPlus's company operating EBITDA (pre-Ind AS, ₹365.8 crore, 5.3% in FY26), its Ind AS EBITDA (₹678.7 crore, 9.8%, including interest income) and screener's operating profit (₹608 crore, 8.8%). State which.

  • Private label on several bases. Annual-report basis changed in FY25; deck series re-based in Q2 FY27; old and new differ by up to 0.8 points for the same quarter.
  • FY averages versus quarterly trend. "Private label +2.6% over FY25" is an annual-average comparison; the quarterly trend inside FY26 was flat to down.
  • Franchise stores in the count. Since FY26 store counts, net additions, closures and the young-store bridge include franchise stores with different economics.
  • No SSSG. MedPlus reports growth of stores older than twelve months on a changing base; it is not same-store sales growth.
  • Wholesale is not retail. Apollo HealthCo's revenue is mostly sales to Apollo's stores; retail value is higher by roughly the retail margin.
  • IPM is not the retail market. The audited IPM includes hospital supply (31.3%), excludes non-medicine sales, and does not state whether it is valued at MRP or trade prices.
  • Borrowings are leases. Screener's ₹1,424–1,549 crore of MedPlus "borrowings" are lease liabilities; the company has net cash of about ₹544 crore.
  • Store ROCE versus company ROCE. 72% at the store, 20.7% for the company ex-cash, 12.6% on screener's basis.
  • Free cash flow before leases. Screener's FCF ignores lease payments (about ₹265 crore in FY26); the company's own Q2 FY27 FCF after leases was −₹84 crore.
  • Chemist count. AIOCD's 12.4 lakh includes wholesalers and pharmacists; "8–12 lakh retail chemists" is a convention, not a census.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number about Indian pharmacy retail can be quoted two or three ways. The store count may include franchise shops; the margin may be at the store or for the company, before or after the rent rule; private label may be a share of one total or another. Before comparing any two numbers, check that they were measured the same way.

How to read a pharmacy chain's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Stores (minute 1). Openings, closures, net additions, and how many were franchise. Compare the run-rate with the annual guidance (MedPlus: 800 net in FY27; ~244 a quarter needed in H2). Red flag: closures rising faster than openings, or franchise withdrawals climbing.
  2. Revenue and its split (minute 2). Total growth versus growth of stores older than twelve months; the gap is new-store contribution. Red flag: older-store growth below medicine inflation (about 6–9%).
  3. Gross margin, same quarter last year (minute 3). Not the previous quarter, because Q4 carries rebates and provision releases. Red flag: margin up while older-store growth falls (the FY25 pattern).
  4. Private label, on a stated basis (minute 4). Direction versus the company's target (+0.25–0.5 points a quarter). Red flag: a basis change without a restated history.
  5. Operating EBITDA, pre-Ind AS (minute 5). The company's own margin; then check the store-level and fully allocated bridge. Red flag: a widening gap between store-level and fully allocated margins (rising overheads).
  6. Staff cost and rent (minute 6). As a share of revenue and per store. Red flag: staff cost growing faster than revenue for a second year.
  7. Working capital (minute 7). Inventory, payables, net working capital days, year-one store inventory. Red flag: inventory growing faster than revenue without a matching opening surge.
  8. Cash (minute 8). Operating cash flow as a share of operating EBITDA, capex, lease payments, and the closing cash balance. Red flag: free cash flow after leases negative for several quarters.
  9. Reported profit and the Ind AS gap (minute 9). PAT versus pre-Ind AS PAT; interest and depreciation growth versus revenue. Red flag: PAT flat on 20% revenue growth (Q2 FY27 at MedPlus) without a clear one-off.
  10. Everything else that moves the stock (minute 10). Regulatory disclosures (licence suspensions, NPPA orders), promoter pledges, capital allocation (buyback, capex), and guidance changes. Red flag: guidance withdrawn or capital plans reversed.

A reader who runs through those ten steps for MedPlus's Q2 FY27 results gets a clear picture in minutes: 166 net stores (behind the guidance run-rate), revenue +21.3% with older stores +13.2%, gross margin 25.1% (−110 basis points year on year), private label 21.4% re-based (−0.6 points), operating EBITDA 4.8% (pharmacy 4.4%), operating cash flow at 37.7% of EBITDA on an inventory build, PAT flat at ₹56 crore with lease interest and depreciation growing faster than sales, and a ₹130 crore buyback announced two weeks later. A growing chain, under margin pressure, with good stores and a cautious market.
