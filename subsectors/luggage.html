00

The one-page view: the challenger took the lead, then crude took the margin

Two listed companies, one winning and one rebuilding, in a market that is growing faster than either of them.

India's listed luggage industry is two companies. Safari Industries (India), the value-led challenger rebuilt by Sudhir Jatia after he bought it in 2011, and V.I.P. Industries, the brand house founded in 1968 that made "VIP" the Indian word for a suitcase. In FY26 they earned a combined ₹3,905 crore of revenue, 1.1% less than a year earlier. Their combined operating profit, before other income, was just ₹30 crore, a margin of 0.8%, and together they reported a net loss of ₹170 crore. Those combined numbers hide two opposite stories. Safari earned ₹2,047 crore of revenue, a 13.2% EBITDA margin and ₹168 crore of profit. VIP's revenue fell 14.7% to ₹1,858 crore, its operating loss was ₹219 crore on the company's own measure, and it lost ₹338 crore after a ₹78 crore one-off gain from selling property.

FY26 was the first year in which Safari's revenue was larger than VIP's, and the lead held in the latest reported quarter, Q1 FY27 (April to June 2026): ₹589 crore against ₹578 crore. Safari's annual report also cites Euromonitor ranking "Safari" as India's No. 1 luggage brand by retail value for the third year running. Our two stock primers still describe Safari as the number two behind VIP. On revenue that is no longer true, and this report uses the correct order throughout. VIP's own long-standing claim of "about 38% of the organised market" is also out of date: CRISIL Ratings put VIP's share at about 29% in FY26, down from over 40% a few years earlier.

The market itself is not the problem. Motilal Oswal (MOSL) sized Indian luggage at about ₹17,000 crore at consumer prices in calendar 2024, up from ₹15,500 crore in 2023, and forecast 12% annual growth to ₹26,700 crore by 2028. CRISIL's more recent figure, from August 2026, is about ₹20,000 crore growing at 6–8% a year. Either way the market grew by high single digits or better while the two listed companies' combined revenue went sideways from ₹3,950 crore in FY25 to ₹3,905 crore in FY26. The growth went to digital-first brands such as Mokobara, to Samsonite's American Tourister and Kamiliant ranges, to marketplace private labels and to cheap Chinese imports.

Five forces explain almost everything in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them:

  • The market-share swap. Safari built an in-house, value-priced manufacturing machine at Halol and Jaipur and sold through hypermarkets, canteen stores and marketplaces. VIP lost about three years to over-stocked channels, discounting, an inventory clean-up and a change of owner. Sections 08 and 11 are the deep dives.
  • Make versus import. A trolley moulded in India from polypropylene earns a gross margin roughly ten points higher than a finished one imported from China, even after only a 15% customs duty. Bangladesh is both companies' soft-goods base. Section 09 works through the arithmetic.
  • Raw materials. Shells are made of crude-derived plastics. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 pushed Indian polymer prices up by 40–60% within weeks, and both companies' gross margins fell in Q1 FY27 despite price rises of 4–6%. Section 05.
  • Travel and occasions. Domestic air passengers grew only 1.4% in FY26 and fell 6.34% year on year in August 2026. Weddings, school terms and the festive season are the other demand engines. Section 03.
  • The brand and channel fight. Online marketplaces have made "50% off MRP" permanent, quick commerce sells cabin bags in ten minutes, direct-to-consumer brands spend 20% or more of revenue on advertising, and a premium brand, Carlton, changed hands through a courtroom. Section 10.

The stock market has already passed judgement, though not a flattering one for either company. On 23 October 2026 Safari traded at ₹1,226, within a rupee of its 52-week low and about half its 52-week high of ₹2,507, for a market value of ₹6,006 crore, 36.4 times trailing earnings. VIP traded at ₹264, at its 52-week low, worth ₹3,757 crore and 12.9 times a book value shrunk by two years of losses. That price is 32% below the ₹388 a share that Multiples Private Equity paid for control in 2025. Investors are paying a premium multiple for Safari's record and refusing to pay the deal price for VIP's recovery.

In plain terms

A suitcase business makes money in three places: the factory that moulds the shell cheaply, the brand that persuades a family to pay for it, and the shop or website that sells it at a discount that never ends. Safari won the last five years by being cheapest in the factory and widest in the shop. VIP lost them by stuffing its dealers with stock it then had to clear at a loss. In 2026 both were hit by the same thing: the plastic in every shell is made from oil, and oil spiked when war broke out in West Asia.

The rest of this report explains each of those mechanisms with the numbers behind them. Sections 01 to 07 cover how the business works, its size, demand, costs, raw materials, structure and business models. Sections 08 to 11 are the deep dives on the leadership swap, the make-or-import decision, the channel war and VIP's turnaround. Section 14 is the KPI masterclass: every number that matters, how to calculate it, what good looks like and where companies define it differently. 01

From a ₹6,000 tag to ₹2,200 of revenue: how a suitcase earns

Most of what a shopper pays never reaches the brand. The factory decides the gross margin; the shelf decides the price.

Indian luggage is four product families. Hard luggage is the rigid, four-wheeled spinner trolley whose shell is injection-moulded from polypropylene (PP) at the value end or thermoformed from polycarbonate (PC) sheet at the premium end. It now accounts for more than half the market and is still gaining, according to VIP's FY26 annual report; for VIP itself it was about 60% of FY25 revenue. Soft luggage is the fabric trolley and duffel, cut and sewn from nylon or polyester. Backpacks, for school, college, office and travel, are bought all year round. Handbags and accessories are a fashion adjacency, which matters mainly for VIP's Caprese brand.

The value chain runs from a petrochemical plant to a family's car boot. Resin makers such as Reliance supply PP, and importers supply PC. A luggage company either moulds the shell itself and buys or makes the wheels, handles and locks, or it buys the finished bag from a vendor in Bangladesh or China. The brand owner designs the range, guarantees it for three to ten years and pays for advertising. Then the bag passes through one of six channels: hypermarkets and department stores (modern trade), dealers and multi-brand shops (general trade), the armed forces' Canteen Stores Department (CSD), exclusive brand outlets, online marketplaces and quick-commerce apps, and the brand's own website.

The brand controls Steps 2 and 3. It negotiates Step 4. It cannot control Step 1 or Step 5. The money is made or lost in the gap between Step 2's cost and Step 4's price.

The single most important fact about the economics is how little of the price on the tag the brand receives. A typical value-brand 65 cm hard trolley might carry a maximum retail price (MRP) of ₹6,000. Almost nobody pays that. The routine online or hypermarket price is around 40% lower, about ₹3,600. Goods and services tax (GST) at 18% is included in that price, so ₹549 goes to the government and ₹3,051 remains. The retailer or marketplace seller then keeps its margin, about 28% in this illustration, so the brand books roughly ₹2,197 of revenue. That is about 37% of MRP and 61% of what the shopper paid.

Three things follow from that arithmetic. First, the brand's gross margin, the revenue left after the cost of the goods, is decided mostly in the factory. A shell moulded in-house from ₹265 of resin costs far less than a finished trolley bought from a vendor, so a company that makes more of its own range earns more on each rupee of sales. Safari's company-reported gross margin was 47.1% in FY26; Samsonite's, globally, was 59.6%.

Second, the shelf decides the realised price. The retailer's margin and the consumer discount are larger slices than the brand's own profit, which is why every company is pushing into channels where it keeps more: its own exclusive stores, its own website, and quick commerce. A five-point deeper discount, if the brand funds it, costs three to four points of gross margin. Third, the brand's own operating costs, advertising (A&P), freight to warehouses and stores, staff and rent, take another 33–35 points of revenue at a mass brand, leaving a healthy EBITDA margin of 12–15%. Samsonite, with premium prices and global scale, earns about 17%.

In plain terms

The tag price is fiction. Of ₹3,600 actually paid for a ₹6,000 suitcase, the government takes about ₹550 and the shop about ₹850, so the brand gets about ₹2,200. If it moulded the shell itself, the bag cost it about ₹1,080 to make; if it bought the bag finished from China, about ₹1,310. That ₹230 difference per bag is why Safari built factories.

Revenue for the whole company is therefore the number of pieces sold multiplied by the average realisation per piece, added up across categories and channels. Gross margin depends on the mix of in-house and bought-in goods and on resin and currency. EBITDA depends on how much of the gross margin survives advertising, freight and staff. The KPI masterclass in Section 14 opens with that equation as a diagram. Nearly every swing in either company's results since 2023 can be traced to one of four inputs: discount depth, resin price, the in-house share, or inventory that had to be cleared below cost.

02

A ₹17,000–20,000 crore market that grew while its listed leaders stood still

Every sizing agrees the market is growing. The listed pair's combined revenue has been flat for two years.

There is no official statistic for the size of India's luggage market, so every number is an estimate, and the estimates use different definitions. The two most useful come from MOSL and CRISIL Ratings. MOSL, initiating coverage in September 2025, put the market at about ₹6,000 crore in Covid-hit calendar 2020, ₹15,500 crore in 2023 and ₹17,000 crore in 2024, and forecast ₹26,700 crore by 2028, a 12% compound annual growth rate. VIP's FY26 annual report repeats the ₹17,000 crore figure. CRISIL, in an August 2026 rating rationale on VIP, described a "~₹20,000 crore Indian luggage industry" growing at 6–8% a year, with direct-to-consumer brands growing at 15–20%.

From FY23 to FY26 the combined revenue of the two listed companies grew at about 5.8% a year, against a market that grew at roughly 8–10%. In FY25 and FY26 it did not grow at all. Within that total, Safari compounded at about 20% a year from FY20 to FY26 while VIP grew at 1.4%. The listed pair is therefore losing share of the market collectively even as one of its members gains share from the other. The gainers are the companies outside the listed universe: Samsonite India, whose revenue we estimate from its Hong Kong filings at about ₹1,760–1,850 crore in 2025 (INDICATIVE), and the direct-to-consumer cohort, where Mokobara alone went from ₹53 crore of revenue in FY23 to ₹230 crore in FY25.

In plain terms

Indians spend somewhere between ₹17,000 and ₹20,000 crore a year on luggage at shop prices, and that spending is growing by perhaps 6–12% a year. The two listed companies sell about ₹3,900 crore of it at factory-gate prices, and that figure has not grown for two years. One of them, Safari, is growing fast; the other, VIP, shrank. The new growth is going to Samsonite and to online brands most investors have never been able to buy.

Exports are negligible for both listed companies: 0.23% of Safari's standalone turnover and 2.73% of VIP's in FY26. India is a net importer of finished luggage and bags. Under customs heading HS 4202, India imported about US$455 million of trunks, suitcases and bags in 2024, of which 61% came from China and 14% from Bangladesh, as Section 09 shows. Those imports, at landed prices of around ₹500–600 a suitcase, are part of the reason the mass tier is so price-competitive.

03

Flights, weddings and school terms: what fills a suitcase

Air travel sets the tone for premium hard luggage. Weddings, trains and back-to-school carry the mass market. In 2026 the first engine stalled.

Luggage is bought for an occasion. The occasions that matter most are a flight, a wedding, a move to college or a new school year, a festival gift and the replacement of a bag that broke. Each favours a different product. Air travel, particularly with checked baggage, sells large and medium hard trolleys. Rail and road trips sell soft luggage, duffels and cheaper trolleys. Weddings sell sets of two or three matched pieces and premium gifts. School and college terms sell backpacks. Understanding which engine is running explains both the volume and the mix in any quarter.

Domestic air traffic was the best leading indicator for branded hard luggage through the post-Covid boom. Passenger numbers recovered from about 530 lakh in FY21 to 1,654 lakh in FY25, and the listed pair's revenue more than tripled over the same period. Then the engine stalled. FY26 grew only 1.4%, to 1,677 lakh, as aircraft were grounded for Pratt & Whitney engine problems and IndiGo's December 2025 crew-rostering disruption cut capacity. The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 then cut Gulf flights from 300–350 a day to 80–90, raised fuel surcharges and pushed fares up. The effect shows up in what people buy, not only how much. Safari's FY26 annual report describes "geo-political headwinds resulting in a loss of demand for international travel" and a "shift towards domestic destinations" that "impacted the size mix away from larger sized luggage to medium sized luggage and cabin size". Low-cost airlines' cabin-only fares and checked-bag fees push in the same direction. A cabin trolley sells for much less than a large check-in trolley, so a shift in mix towards smaller bags lowers the average realisation per piece even when volumes grow. That is one reason Safari's volumes rose 16–17% in Q1 FY27 while its revenue rose 11.5%.

In plain terms

When fewer Indians fly abroad and more take short domestic trips with cabin bags, luggage makers sell more bags for less money each. Safari sold about 16–17% more pieces in April–June 2026 but earned only 11.5% more revenue. A fall in flights also hurts the premium end most, because the expensive large trolley is the one people buy for a long international trip.

Weddings are the second engine and the most important for premium sets. MOSL cites estimates of over 4.8 million weddings in the November–December 2024 season alone, 67 auspicious dates in FY26 (20% more than a year earlier) and destination weddings growing at 8–10% a year, adding a 10–15% annual uplift to premium luggage sales. These are trade-body estimates and should be read as indicative. The wedding calendar concentrates demand in November to February and April to May, and a year with more auspicious dates is a better year for two- and three-piece trolley sets and for gifting.

Trains, buses and domestic tourism carry the mass market. Domestic tourist visits rose to about 3.06 billion in 2024 from 2.51 billion in 2023, according to the Ministry of Tourism figures MOSL quotes, and IRCTC sold about 14.6 lakh e-tickets a day in Q1 FY27. That traffic buys soft trolleys, duffels and Aristocrat- or Genius-priced hard luggage, often in general-trade shops in tier-2 and tier-3 towns. CRISIL notes that local manufacturers still dominate sales volume there. Backpacks are the steadiest engine: bought for school (March to June), college, office and travel, and the reason Safari calls the category its fastest-growing.

The combination matters for forecasting. A quarter in which flights are weak but weddings and school terms are strong will show healthy volumes, a lower average price and a mix tilted towards value hard luggage and backpacks, which is roughly what Q1 FY27 looked like. The next demand test is the festive and wedding season from October 2026 to February 2027, which falls in the third and fourth fiscal quarters. CRISIL expects VIP's recovery to be "supported by festive demand in the second half of fiscal 2027". The same air-traffic data drives our Travel Services and Hotels reports, which are useful cross-checks on how the travel economy is doing.

In plain terms

A luggage company spends about half its revenue on the bags themselves. In a good year about ₹13–16 of every ₹100 is left as operating profit. Two sensitivities follow from the cost stack, and both are estimates. Operating leverage: roughly a fifth of revenue is fixed in the short run (permanent staff, rent, depreciation and the planned part of advertising), so a 10% swing in volume moves EBITDA margin by about two points either way. Resin and currency: raw material is 55–60% of operating cost, according to CRISIL, and resin itself perhaps 10–15% of revenue for a hard-luggage-heavy maker, so a 10% rise in polypropylene prices costs about 1.0–1.5% of revenue until prices are raised, usually one or two quarters later. Crude in the shell: polypropylene, polycarbonate and the 2026 war premium

Every hard trolley is a few kilograms of oil-derived plastic. When West Asia went to war, the shell got dearer within weeks and the price tag followed a quarter later.

A value hard trolley is a polypropylene shell; a premium one is polycarbonate or a PC-ABS blend. Polypropylene is made from propylene, which comes from naphtha cracking or refinery off-gas, so its price follows crude oil and naphtha with a lag of a few weeks. Polycarbonate is made from bisphenol-A, itself made from phenol and acetone, so it follows benzene and propylene. Nylon and polyester fabric for soft luggage follow PTA, MEG and caprolactam, also crude derivatives. Aluminium trolley tubes follow the London Metal Exchange. Almost every material in a suitcase is a globally traded commodity priced in dollars, and Indian luggage makers are price-takers for all of them.

Measured consistently, the organised market is split almost equally between three companies, one of them unlisted in India, with digital brands taking the next tenth.

In plain terms

There are five ways to run a luggage business. Make it yourself and sell it cheaply everywhere (Safari). Own famous brands and a huge dealer network (VIP). Own premium global brands (Samsonite). Safari vs VIP: how the leader changed Deep dive

In 2020 Safari was 40% of VIP's size. In FY26 it was 110%. The swap was built in the factory, the hypermarket and the stockroom, not in advertising.

In FY20, the last year before Covid, VIP had revenue of ₹1,714 crore and Safari ₹686 crore. VIP was two and a half times larger, earned a 17% operating margin and returned 29% on its capital. Six years later Safari's revenue was ₹2,047 crore against VIP's ₹1,858 crore, Safari earned ₹168 crore while VIP lost ₹338 crore, and Safari's market value was 1.6 times VIP's. Few Indian consumer categories have seen a leadership change this complete in so short a time. It happened in four stages, and the numbers for each are in the companies' own reports.

Stage one: the challenger's playbook (2011–2020)

Sudhir Jatia, who had spent more than three decades in the luggage trade, acquired Safari in 2011, with a 77% majority stake according to the company, and became managing director in April 2012. Both of our primers describe him as a former VIP managing director; Safari's own documents say only that he has more than thirty years in the luggage industry, and several of Safari's senior executives are ex-VIP. The strategy was a classic challenger's: sell good-enough branded luggage at prices well below VIP's, put it where value shoppers already were (the new hypermarket chains, the armed forces' canteen stores and, from the mid-2010s, Amazon and Flipkart), and keep costs lower than the incumbent's. VIP, meanwhile, was moving its soft-goods production to its own plants in Bangladesh and building a portfolio of brands from Alfa to Carlton.

Stage two: Safari builds factories (2019–2025)

The decisive step was manufacturing. Safari had relied on Chinese vendors for most of its range. From the late 2010s it moved polypropylene hard luggage into its own moulds at Halol in Gujarat (the company dates its captive PP luggage plant to FY21–22), added a second Halol unit in its subsidiary Safari Manufacturing Ltd, and in December 2024 started commercial production at a greenfield plant at Jaipur. By June 2024 the subsidiary's Halol unit had capacity of 3.5 lakh pieces a month and was running at 94%; it added another lakh. Jaipur was expanded from 5 to 6.5 lakh pieces a month by Q1 FY27. Section 09 explains why that was worth about ten points of gross margin on every bag moved from a Chinese vendor to an Indian mould. It also gave Safari control of design, quality and speed: it could launch a new colour in weeks rather than wait for a container.

Made in Halol, Jaipur or Bangladesh — or imported from China Deep dive

Where a bag is made sets about ten points of gross margin. Customs data show how much India still imports, and that the listed pair is one of its own biggest suppliers.

Every Indian luggage brand faces the same decision for every product: mould it, have it made by a dedicated vendor, or buy it finished from China. For hard luggage the answer has shifted decisively towards Indian moulding since 2018. For soft luggage and backpacks, which are cut and sewn by hand, it has stayed abroad, in Bangladesh and China. The decision is worth roughly ten points of gross margin on a hard trolley, and it explains why the listed pair's margins differ, why Bangladesh's politics are an Indian luggage risk, and why the price floor of the mass market is set at the border.

The make-or-import arithmetic

Take the illustrative 65 cm PP spinner trolley from Section 01 again, which earns the brand ₹2,197 a piece after GST and the retailer's margin. Made in-house it costs about ₹1,080: ₹265 of resin, ₹480 of wheels, telescopic handle, zips, lock and lining, ₹250 of conversion (power, contract labour and mould wear) and ₹85 of packaging and inward freight. Bought finished from a Chinese factory, a comparable trolley might cost about US$11 free on board, ₹1,045 at ₹94.9 to the dollar. Add ₹80 of freight and insurance for a cost-insurance-freight value of ₹1,125. Basic customs duty on HS 4202 is 15%, ₹169, plus a social welfare surcharge of 10% of the duty, ₹17. The landed cost is about ₹1,310. Integrated GST at 18% is also charged at the border but is credited back to a registered importer, so it is not a cost.

Two points stand out. First, a 15% duty is modest protection. The make-or-import gap is mostly the cost of the factory's margin, shipping and inventory time in China, not the tariff. Our first-pass research assumed a 20% duty; the Customs Tariff schedule shows 15%, so the gap is about ten and a half points rather than fourteen. Second, the gap survives a resin shock. Even with polypropylene at its war-peak price, the in-house trolley costs about ₹1,185, still ₹125 less than the import, because only the resin line moves. Indian moulding is a structural advantage, not a tariff artefact.

In plain terms

A trolley that sells for the same price earns its brand about 51% gross margin if moulded in India and about 40% if bought ready-made from China. Customs duty explains only part of that; the rest is the Chinese factory's own profit and the cost of shipping. That is why Safari built two plants and is now making its own wheels.

Fifty per cent off, forever: marketplaces, quick commerce, D2C insurgents and a courtroom brand

The online shelf made discounts permanent, digital brands bought growth with advertising, and the premium rung of the ladder changed owner by court order.

For most of VIP's history luggage was sold by dealers in a showroom, where a shopper could lift the bag, test the wheels and haggle. That channel still exists, and offline sales remain the majority for both listed companies. But three changes since 2015 have reshaped how luggage is priced and who gets paid. Online marketplaces made prices instantly comparable and turned the "40–50% off MRP" sticker into a permanent feature. Direct-to-consumer brands used those marketplaces and social media to build national brands without dealers. And quick-commerce apps began selling cabin trolleys and backpacks for delivery in ten minutes.

Neither listed company publishes a full channel split, and no primary industry split exists. The low-reliability estimates in circulation put e-commerce at 20–30% of luggage sales. The only primary datapoint is VIP's: its May 2026 investor presentation showed standalone online revenue of ₹215 crore out of ₹965 crore in H1 FY26 (22%) and ₹159 crore out of ₹884 crore in H2 FY26 (18%). Online had fallen 34–36% year on year by design, a "conscious call to reduce primary" shipments to marketplace sellers who had been discounting VIP stock. Working back, VIP's online share in FY25 was probably about 27–30%. That is consistent with the industry estimates.

Why online pricing hurts

Marketplaces did not simply add a channel. They changed the price. In a showroom the discount was negotiated bag by bag; online it is visible to everyone, ranked by algorithm and matched within hours. Platform private labels from Amazon, Flipkart and Myntra and unbranded Chinese imports sit next to branded bags on the same search page. A brand that holds its price loses its ranking; one that cuts it trains shoppers to wait for the next sale. The result is that online realisation per piece is lower than offline for the same bag, and that any brand with surplus stock, as VIP had in FY24–FY25, ends up clearing it online at the deepest discounts, which drags down the price everywhere else. VIP's decision to cut online shipments in FY26 was an attempt to stop that spiral.

In plain terms

Online, every suitcase is compared with every other in seconds, and the cheapest one usually comes first. So brands end up selling at a permanent "50% off". When VIP had too much stock, it ended up dumping it online, which made everyone's prices worse. In FY26 it deliberately sold less online to stop that.

A reported funding round valued it at about ₹1,930 crore. Nasher Miles, founded in 2017, sells colourful value-premium hard luggage online; it reported ₹167.8 crore of revenue in FY24, up 15.5%, and a ₹6.3 crore loss. Uppercase, which positions itself as sustainable and India-made, reported ₹83 crore in FY25 (+34%) and a ₹35 crore loss on ₹23 crore of marketing. Assembly, from a Kolkata manufacturing family, sells design-led backpacks and travel gear but has published no figures we could find. It is a further step in the shift of luggage from a considered purchase towards an everyday one, which helps volumes and hurts price.

The Carlton switch: a brand changes hands in court

The most unusual event in the brand fight happened in a courtroom. VIP had sold Carlton, a UK-origin premium brand it acquired in 2004, as the top rung of its Indian portfolio. A company called Carlton Shoes Ltd held a Class 18 registration for CARLTON in India from 6 May 1994; VIP's own registrations dated from 1995 and 2006. Because both were registered, the case turned on passing off. In July 2023 the Delhi High Court granted an interim injunction against VIP. On 1 July 2025 a Division Bench (VIP Industries Ltd v. Carlton Shoes Ltd, 2025:DHC:5042-DB) dismissed VIP's appeal, holding that global goodwill was not enough: prior use in India governs, and VIP had adopted CARLTON for luggage in India only in 2006, an adoption the court called "deliberate". On 1 August 2025 the Supreme Court disposed of VIP's appeal, allowing six months to sell existing Carlton stock, and on 23 January 2026 extended that to 31 May 2026. The civil suits continue; VIP says it has "strong merit".

Less than a month later, on 18 February 2026, Safari signed a 20-year licence, renewable for another 20, with Carlton Retail Pvt Ltd of New Delhi ("Carlton London") for the CARLTON mark in Class 18, including luggage, in India. The licence fee is the higher of ₹45 lakh or 5% of net sales a year, and Safari pays an interest-free refundable security deposit of ₹99.5 crore in tranches. The licensor is not a related party. Whether Carlton Retail is connected to Carlton Shoes is not documented in anything we found. Safari plans to launch Carlton as its super-premium brand around October 2026, initially with products imported from China. VIP's FY26 annual report and investor decks list only VIP, Skybags, Aristocrat, Alfa and Caprese. Both of our primers still show Carlton as VIP's premium brand; that is out of date.

Luggage is a lightly regulated product. The customs duty matters less than often assumed. In plain terms

When the government cut GST on most clothes and shoes to 5% in September 2025, it left suitcases and backpacks at 18%. Imported luggage pays a 15% customs duty plus a small surcharge, which helps Indian factories a little but not much. There is no special safety or quality rule for luggage, and the rule that restricted imports of plastic granules was scrapped in November 2025, which helps the moulders.

Two other rules shaped the past year. Trademark law decided the Carlton case: the Delhi High Court's ruling that prior use in India, not global goodwill, governs passing off is a precedent any brand acquirer should note. Takeover rules shaped VIP's ownership: because the open offer was priced at ₹388 and the market price stayed near it, almost no shareholder tendered, leaving Multiples in control with under a quarter of the shares and a shareholders' agreement. For the companies themselves, the four labour codes in force from November 2025 raise compliance costs for plants that rely on contract workers, and Bangladesh's scheduled graduation from least-developed-country status in November 2026 will alter the incentives behind VIP's seven plants there.

US tariff changes in 2026, the Supreme Court's striking down of the IEEPA tariffs in February, the temporary Section 122 tariff and the 10% Section 301 duty in force since July, matter little to Indian luggage makers directly, because exports are under 3% of their turnover. They matter indirectly if Chinese exporters redirect suitcases from the US to India, which would put further pressure on the import price floor. Five decades, four shocks: from Blow Plast to a private-equity boardroom

A moulded-plastics company that became a national brand, a challenger rebuilt from near-bankruptcy, a pandemic, a price war and an oil shock.

The history of Indian luggage is short and dominated by one company until recently. VIP was incorporated in January 1968 under the Dilip Piramal group as a wholly owned subsidiary of Blow Plast Ltd, which merged into VIP in fiscal 2007. Its moulded hard suitcases, made at Nashik, turned "VIP" into a generic word for a suitcase in many Indian homes. Over the following decades VIP added brands for every tier: Alfa and Aristocrat for value, Carlton for premium in 2004, Skybags for young buyers in 2012 and Caprese handbags in 2013. Safari Industries (India) was incorporated in 1980 (the brand dates from 1974) and remained a small moulded-luggage maker until Sudhir Jatia bought it in 2011.

Four shocks define the period since. The first was Covid: in FY21 the two companies' combined revenue fell 60%, from ₹2,400 crore to ₹947 crore, and both made losses (Safari −₹21 crore, VIP −₹97 crore). The second was the boom that followed: revenge travel took combined revenue to ₹3,294 crore in FY23 and margins to their peaks. The third was the price war of FY24–FY25, when D2C brands, cheaper Chinese imports and Safari's own value pricing collided with VIP's over-stocked channel. The fourth is the 2026 West Asia oil shock, which raised resin prices just as the price war was easing. Through all four, ownership changed too: Jatia at Safari in 2011–12, Multiples at VIP in 2025.

Reading a luggage company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number that explains a luggage company's results: what it is, how to calculate it, what good looks like, how Safari and VIP compare, and how each one can mislead.

Luggage companies disclose less than most consumer companies. Neither listed member reports volumes in pieces every quarter, neither gives a full channel split, and only one discloses its manufacturing capacity. So reading them well means combining a small number of reported figures, gross margin, EBITDA, inventory, cash, with a few proxies that can be calculated from the notes to the accounts. This section works through each, in the order an analyst would use them, and ends with a benchmark scorecard, a list of definition traps and a ten-minute checklist for the next quarterly result.

The equation: how a piece becomes revenue and profit

The whole business reduces to one chain. Revenue is the number of pieces sold multiplied by the average realisation per piece, the amount the company books after GST and the retailer's margin, summed across hard luggage, soft luggage, backpacks and accessories. Subtract the cost of materials and finished goods bought in, and what is left is gross profit. Subtract advertising and promotion, staff, freight, contract labour and the other operating costs, and what remains is EBITDA. Depreciation, interest and tax then take the company to net profit.

A worked example with round numbers makes the chain concrete. Suppose a company sells one crore pieces a year at an average realisation of ₹1,800. Revenue is ₹1,800 crore. If materials and bought-in goods cost 52% of revenue, ₹936 crore, gross profit is ₹864 crore, a 48% gross margin. That is close to Safari's FY26 shape. Now change one input at a time. Sell 10% more pieces at the same price, and if fixed costs are about a fifth of revenue, EBITDA rises by about ₹60 crore and the margin by about two points, to roughly 14.8%. Cut realisation by 4% through a deeper discount or a smaller-bag mix, and revenue falls ₹72 crore while the cost of making each piece stays the same, so EBITDA falls by up to ₹72 crore, almost a third. Selling 4% fewer pieces instead would cost only about ₹24 crore, because the variable costs of those pieces disappear with them. Price matters far more than volume.

In plain terms

A luggage company's profit is a short sum: how many bags, times what it gets for each, minus what they cost, minus advertising, staff and trucks. Getting 4% less for every bag hurts profit about three times as much as selling 4% fewer bags, because the cost of making each bag does not fall when the price does.

Revenue growth: separate volume from price and mix

Reported revenue growth is the product of volume growth and the change in realisation per piece: (1 + revenue growth) = (1 + volume growth) × (1 + realisation change). The split matters because the two have opposite implications for margins. Volume growth with stable prices spreads fixed costs and lifts margins. Value growth driven only by price rises usually means input costs are being passed through, with no margin gain. Volume growth with falling realisation, the pattern of 2024–26, means the company is winning pieces by cutting prices or selling smaller bags.

Safari is the only member whose volume growth is known, and only from broker notes: in Q1 FY27 volume grew about 16–17% while revenue grew 11.5%. Solving the equation, 1.115 ÷ 1.165 = 0.957, so realisation per piece fell about 4.3%, despite price increases of 4–6% taken in May. The decline came from mix: more cabin and medium bags as international travel fell, more value-priced hard luggage, more backpacks. VIP does not disclose volumes; its gross inventory fell from 45 lakh to 28 lakh units in FY26, which gives a sense of scale but not of sales. A good benchmark for revenue growth in this industry is above 15%; 8–15% is average and below 8% weak. The trap is VIP's FY26: its −14.7% was partly a deliberate cut in sell-in to dealers, so sell-out to consumers fell less. Its Q2 FY27 growth will look strong for the opposite reason.

Gross margin

Gross margin is revenue minus the cost of goods (materials consumed, purchases of finished goods and the change in inventory), divided by revenue. It is the single best summary of a luggage company's economics, because it captures three things at once: the price it realises, the share of its range it makes itself and the cost of its inputs. In Safari's Q1 FY27 results, the arithmetic is: materials ₹201.08 crore + purchases ₹84.90 crore + inventory change ₹40.32 crore = ₹326.3 crore of cost of goods on ₹588.58 crore of revenue, a cost ratio of 55.4% and a gross margin of 44.6%.

In-house manufacturing share

The in-house share is the proportion of a company's range that it makes in its own plants rather than buying finished. No company reports it directly, but a value proxy can be calculated from the cost-of-goods note: raw material consumed ÷ (raw material consumed + purchases of stock-in-trade). For Safari that was 638.80 ÷ (638.80 + 412.26) = 60.8% in FY26 and 201.08 ÷ (201.08 + 84.90) = 70.3% in Q1 FY27. The proxy understates the true in-house share of revenue, because raw material for an in-house bag costs less than a bought-in finished bag of the same value, and in Q1 FY27 it is flattered slightly by higher resin prices. The direction of change is what matters.

The value of the share follows from the make-or-import gap of about ten and a half points in Section 09. If a company's blended gross margin is the weighted average of 50.8% on in-house pieces and 40.3% on imported ones, each ten-point rise in the in-house share adds about one point to gross margin. At 50% in-house the blend is 45.6%; at 70% it is 47.7%; at 80% it is 48.7%. These are ILLUSTRATIVE, using the single-trolley economics, and real companies have lower-margin backpacks and soft goods in the bought-in share. VIP does not disclose enough to calculate the proxy. Good is above 70% by value for a hard-luggage-led brand, 50–70% average and below 50% weak.

Channel mix

Channel mix is the share of revenue from each route to market: modern trade, general trade, CSD, exclusive stores, e-commerce, quick commerce, the brand's own website, institutional sales and exports. Online sales tend to realise less per piece but need less dealer credit; exclusive stores realise more but carry rent and staff; CSD offers volume at government-negotiated prices. VIP is the only member to have disclosed any split: online was 22% of standalone revenue in H1 FY26 and 18% in H2 FY26, after a deliberate cut, and probably 27–30% in FY25. Definition traps, collected

  • "No. 1" means three things. Euromonitor ranks brands (Safari No. 1); company revenue ranks companies (Safari ₹2,047 Cr > VIP ₹1,858 Cr in FY26); VIP's "~38% of organised" is an old company claim, and CRISIL now says ~29%.
  • Market size is at shop prices; revenue is not. The ₹17,000–20,000 Cr market includes GST and retail margins. Dividing company revenue by it understates share.
  • Standalone vs consolidated. Safari's plants sit mostly in a subsidiary, so standalone revenue ≈ consolidated but profit differs (Q1 FY27 standalone PAT ₹28.9 Cr vs consolidated ₹47.8 Cr). VIP's Bangladesh profits sit in subsidiaries. Use consolidated.
  • Screener operating profit ≠ company EBITDA. VIP FY26: −₹241 Cr vs −₹218.6 Cr.
  • Gross-margin proxies overstate or mislead. Screener's material-cost proxy gives Safari ~49% vs 47.1% reported; VIP's FY26 proxy is distorted by a reclassification.
  • Lease liabilities are not borrowings. Safari's ₹116 Cr is leases; it has nil borrowings and ₹283 Cr of liquid cash.
  • EBITDA after Ind AS 116 excludes rent. Safari's lease-adjusted margin is about two points lower than reported.
  • Exceptional items. VIP's FY26 PAT of −₹338 Cr includes a ₹78.2 Cr gain from asset sales to related parties; pre-exceptional loss −₹416 Cr.
  • One-time provisions vary by source. VIP's FY26 inventory provisions: ₹122 Cr (CRISIL) or ~₹130–132 Cr (company deck, standalone).
  • Sell-in vs sell-out. Revenue measures shipments to dealers and platforms, not purchases by shoppers. VIP's FY26 revenue fell more than its consumer sales.
  • Capacity units. Lakh pieces per month, not million; and Safari's disclosed capacity excludes one plant.
  • Plant counts. VIP has 9 plants per its BRSR (2 India, 7 Bangladesh); its MD&A says 10.
  • Free float. VIP's 36.45% "public" includes Samvibhag Securities' 8.44%, a consortium co-investor; the true float is ~28%.

How to read a luggage company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and YoY growth (1 minute). Compare with the same quarter last year, never the previous quarter: Q1 is the seasonal peak. Note any base effect (VIP's Q2 FY26 was ₹406 Cr).
  2. Volume growth, if given (1 minute). Usually only in commentary or broker notes. Solve for realisation: (1 + revenue growth) ÷ (1 + volume growth) − 1. A gap above 5 points means mix or discounting is eating price.
  3. Gross margin (1 minute). Add materials, purchases and inventory change; divide by revenue. Compare with the same quarter last year. Red flag: a fall of more than 200 bp without a resin explanation.
  4. The in-house proxy (1 minute). Materials ÷ (materials + purchases of stock-in-trade). Rising is good; a fall may mean a new imported range (Carlton) or capacity constraints.
  5. Operating costs (1 minute). Employee cost and other expenses as % of revenue. A&P growing much faster than revenue is a warning unless gross margin is rising.
  6. EBITDA and its basis (1 minute). Company EBITDA vs data-provider operating profit; one-offs (provisions, liquidation support) separately disclosed?
  7. Below EBITDA (1 minute). Depreciation (watch new plants), interest (leases vs borrowings), exceptional items (asset sales), tax rate.
  8. Inventory and channel commentary (1 minute). At half-years, the balance sheet: inventory vs last year and vs revenue growth. In decks: channel days, units, SKU counts. Red flag: inventory growing faster than revenue for two periods.
  9. Cash and debt (1 minute). Borrowings vs leases; cash; bank-line utilisation; rating actions. Red flag: falling cash with negative EBITDA.
  10. Guidance and price actions (1 minute). Price hikes taken or planned, resin commentary, new brands, capacity utilisation. Check against Brent, the rupee and DGCA traffic for the quarter.

Applied to Q1 FY27, the checklist gives a clear read. Safari: revenue +11.5% with volume +16–17% (realisation −4%); gross margin 44.6%, down 120 bp despite ~10% input inflation; in-house proxy up from 61.5% to 70.3%; EBITDA 12.8% vs 15.0%, as staff and other costs grew faster than revenue; PAT ₹47.8 crore, −5.4%; nil borrowings. VIP: revenue +3% after seven quarters of decline; gross margin 41%, down 400 bp on war-related costs; no provisions; EBITDA −₹7 crore; loss ₹54 crore; cash down to about ₹91 crore by July; a CRISIL information advisory and a ₹500 crore raise to follow. One company is managing a margin squeeze from strength; the other is managing a recovery from a weak balance sheet.

In plain terms

When the next results arrive, look at five things in order: sales growth against the same quarter last year, gross margin, how much the company made in its own plants, operating profit with one-offs stripped out, and cash. That is enough to know whether a luggage company is winning, holding on or in trouble.

Lighter shells, robot arms and ten-minute delivery: technology and disruption

Luggage is a low-tech product with a fast-changing look. The disruptions that matter are in design speed, materials, automation and how bags are sold, not in gadgets.

A suitcase has changed less in twenty years than almost any consumer durable: a shell, four wheels, a handle, a zip and a lock. The technology that matters in this industry is therefore mostly in how it is made and sold. Six changes are reshaping the economics, and each favours a different kind of company.

Design velocity. The D2C brands launch colour-led ranges quickly and use social media, where luggage has become, in Safari's words, "an 'outfit' for … social media travel content". That shortens replacement cycles, which helps volume, and forces incumbents to match their pace, which raises SKU counts, design costs and inventory risk. VIP's answer was more than 80 launches providing about half its Q1 FY27 revenue, alongside a 25–30% cut in total SKUs. The winners will be companies that can launch fast without leaving old colours in the stockroom.

Materials. The main product innovation is lighter, tougher shells: polycarbonate and PC-ABS blends at the premium end, better PP grades at the value end, recycled or bio-based shells as a sustainability claim (Uppercase's positioning), and aluminium at the very top. Safari's investments in PC sheet extrusion, including sheet-size optimisation that cut offcuts and raised extruder capacity, and in-house wheels show the margin in getting materials right. Lighter shells also matter for airline weight limits, an increasingly important selling point as carriers charge for checked bags.

Automation. Injection moulding and assembly can be automated far more than cut-and-sew soft goods. Safari has added robotic manipulator arms on moulding lines at Halol and Jaipur, and PL Capital reports that a manpower shortage has constrained Jaipur's ramp-up, which is an argument for more automation. The labour codes in force since November 2025 raise the cost of contract-heavy staffing. Over time, automation should widen the cost advantage of Indian moulding over imported hard luggage, while soft goods stay in low-wage Bangladesh and China.

Bull vs bear: a runway of unbranded buyers against a crowded, cyclical shelf

The case for Indian luggage is conversion from unbranded to branded. The case against is that the conversion keeps going to someone other than the listed pair.

The bull and bear cases for the sub-sector rest on the same facts read differently. Roughly half the market is still unbranded, incomes are rising, travel and weddings are growing over time, and Indian moulding is structurally cheaper than imports. But the listed pair's combined revenue has been flat for two years, the incremental branded share has gone to Samsonite and digital brands, margins swing with resin and discounting, and one of the two members is still loss-making and short of cash.

The bull case

  • Branded conversion runway: about half of a ₹17,000–20,000 Cr market is unbranded; MOSL forecasts 12% annual growth to ₹26,700 Cr by 2028, and premium is growing fastest.
  • Structural cost edge: moulding in India beats importing by about ten points of gross margin even at war-peak resin prices; Safari's in-house share rose from 61% to 70% in one year.
  • Safari's position: India's largest luggage company and No. 1 brand, net cash of ₹283 Cr, ROCE ~19%, 11 lakh pieces a month of capacity and a premium brand (Carlton) from late 2026.
  • VIP's low base: a clean stockroom, a new operator-owner and a Q2 FY26 base of ₹406 Cr make strong reported growth likely; CRISIL expects positive operating profit by end-FY27.
  • Input relief: a Brent fall below ~$85 and a return of polymer prices to pre-war levels would restore 1–3 points of gross margin in H2 FY27.
  • Valuations reset: both stocks are near 52-week lows; Safari has de-rated from ~44× to 36× earnings.

The bear case

  • Share leakage: the listed pair grew ~5.8% a year over FY23–26 against a market growing ~8–10%; D2C brands grow 15–20% and Samsonite is expanding in India.
  • Demand stall: CRISIL sees only 6–8% industry growth; domestic air traffic grew 1.4% in FY26 and fell 6.34% in August 2026; ICRA's aviation outlook is Negative.
  • Cyclical margins: Safari's EBITDA margin fell from 18.5% to 10.5% in five quarters in the price war and is 12.8% now; resin and the rupee (~₹95/$) are adverse.
  • Price floor at the border: Chinese suitcases land at ~₹500–580; a 15% duty offers modest protection; US tariffs could divert more Chinese supply to India.
  • VIP's balance sheet: CRISIL A-/Negative, cash ~₹91 Cr, a ₹500 Cr raise likely to dilute 13–16%, related-party asset sales and a free float of only ~28%.
  • Valuation gap: Safari at ~21× EV/EBITDA vs Samsonite at ~5× requires years of above-market growth.

Weighing them, the strongest part of the bull case is the cost advantage, which is measurable and durable, and the strongest part of the bear case is the share leakage, which is also measurable and has lasted two years. The industry will grow; the question is who captures it. For Safari, the test over the next year is whether volume growth in the mid-teens can be held while gross margin returns to 46–47% and EBITDA to 13–14%. For VIP, it is whether gross margin gets back above 42% and EBITDA turns positive before the fund raise has to be priced. A combination of Brent below $85, positive air-traffic growth and a good wedding season would make the bull case look right in the second half of FY27; a further fall in air traffic, a rupee beyond ₹97 and a deeply discounted VIP equity issue would make the bear case look right.

In plain terms

Optimists say half of India still buys unbranded bags, so branded makers have years of growth ahead, and Indian factories make bags more cheaply than China can ship them. Pessimists say the listed companies have not grown for two years while newer brands and Samsonite took the growth, that profits swing with oil and discounts, and that VIP still needs money. Both are partly right.
