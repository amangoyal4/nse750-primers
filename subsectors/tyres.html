Indian tyres: record demand meets record rubber

A complete guide to India's five listed tyre makers: how a tyre turns rubber, oil and steel into profit, why the best vehicle demand in years arrived alongside the worst raw-material bill in a decade, why Balkrishna's off-highway export machine behaves nothing like the four road-tyre majors, and how roughly ₹10,000 crore a year of capex is being built into a margin squeeze.

00

The one-page view: record sales, halved margins

India's tyre makers are selling more tyres than ever, at higher prices than ever, and keeping less of each rupee than at any time since 2022.

Five listed companies make up this sub-sector in the NSE 750: MRF, Apollo Tyres, CEAT, JK Tyre & Industries and Balkrishna Industries (BKT). Together they booked ₹1,02,448 crore of consolidated revenue in FY26 (+10.6%), ₹15,476 crore of operating profit (a 15.1% margin) and ₹6,514 crore of reported net profit (+15.7%). On the 23 October 2026 close the market valued them at ₹1,36,483 crore. Those totals hide the first thing to understand about this group: it is two businesses wearing one label.

Four of the five, MRF, Apollo, CEAT and JK, are domestic road-tyre makers. They sell truck and bus tyres, car and SUV tyres, two-wheeler tyres, farm tyres and some off-road tyres, mostly in India, through dealers who sell to vehicle owners replacing worn tyres, and to vehicle manufacturers who fit new ones. Their combined FY26 revenue was ₹91,625 crore at a 14.4% operating margin. The fifth, BKT, is an export specialist in off-highway tyres for tractors, harvesters, mining trucks, loaders and port equipment. It sells by the tonne, prices largely in euros and dollars, owns its own carbon-black plant, and earned a 21.3% margin on ₹10,823 crore of revenue. Averaging the two models produces a "sector margin" that describes nobody, so this report keeps them apart wherever it matters.

The second thing to understand is that FY27 has split the year into a demand story and a cost story, and the cost story is winning. On demand, the picture is the best in years. The cut in GST on tyres from 28% to 18% on 22 September 2025, together with lower GST on cars, two-wheelers, trucks and tractors, released pent-up buying: SIAM's domestic vehicle sales rose 19.8% in the first half of FY27 (April–September 2026), with passenger vehicles up 28.3% and two-wheelers up 17.6%, and commercial vehicles up 28.7% in the July–September quarter. JK Tyre's original-equipment (OEM) sales rose 65% in Q2 FY27. Revenue at the two companies that have already reported that quarter, CEAT and JK, rose 23.4% combined.

On cost, the picture is the worst since the Russia–Ukraine spike of FY22–23. Natural rubber, the single largest input, hit a record ₹284.50 a kilogram for RSS-4 grade at Kottayam on 13 October 2026, above even the Bangkok price. Crude oil, which drives synthetic rubber, carbon black, nylon cord and process oils, went through two shocks after the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026: US EIA data put dated Brent at a monthly average of $117.29 a barrel in April, $114.16 in September and roughly $126 across 1–20 October, with a high of $135.51 on 2 October. The rupee, at about ₹94.9 to the dollar, makes every imported input dearer. CEAT says its raw-material basket will be about 27% higher by December 2026 than at the end of March. Price increases of 11–15% so far this year cover roughly half of that.

Five forces shape everything else in this report. First, the raw-material spread and its lag: raw materials take 60–66 rupees of every 100 of a domestic maker's revenue this year, and price increases trail cost increases by one to two quarters, longer with vehicle makers. Second, the demand boom and its mix: replacement buyers (about two-thirds of domestic demand) carry the margin, while the OEM boom adds volume at prices that reset a quarter late. Third, two businesses in one sub-sector: BKT's export, off-highway, euro-priced model faces the European and American farm cycle, a US tariff regime that now treats off-highway tyres (about 10%) very differently from road tyres (25% plus about 4%), the EU deforestation rule from 30 December 2026, and its own turn onto Indian highways with Pirelli. Fourth, the capex and leverage cycle: JK's ₹4,980 crore Chennai programme and its CARE AA− rating trigger, CEAT's CAMSO purchase and debt at 1.93× EBITDA, BKT's ₹6,800 crore plan and Apollo's deleveraging. Fifth, overseas bets and restructuring: Apollo's closure of its Dutch plant, CEAT's integration of Michelin's former CAMSO business, and JK's Mexican subsidiary. Behind all five sit a 2018 cartel penalty of ₹1,610 crore on four of the members that the Supreme Court has still not decided, the risk of cheap imports from China and South-East Asia, and India's safety and labelling rules for tyres.

The market has already reacted. Over the year to 23 October 2026, MRF and CEAT fell 26%, JK 25%, Apollo 23% and BKT 9%. On reported FY26 profit the five trade at 21.0× combined; strip out one-off items and the multiple on pre-exceptional profit is about 19.3× (an indicative figure, explained in the peer table). The domestic mid-caps, Apollo, CEAT and JK, trade at about 6.9× EV/EBITDA on FY26 numbers, a level the sector usually reaches near the bottom of a margin cycle. The question for the next two quarters is whether that bottom is now, as CEAT and JK told investors in October, or later, as the September–October oil prices suggest.

In plain terms

A tyre company buys rubber and oil-based chemicals, cooks them into tyres, and sells them to people whose old tyres have worn out and to companies building new vehicles. When rubber and oil get dearer, it has to raise its own prices, but it can only do that gradually, and vehicle makers only accept increases every few months. This year rubber and oil rose very fast while demand was excellent, so the companies are selling more tyres than ever but making less on each one. BKT is different: it sells tractor and mining tyres abroad, mainly in euros, and its costs rose far less.

01

From rubber bale to replacement dealer: how a tyre earns

A tyre is about a third rubber, a quarter carbon black and the rest steel, fabric and chemicals. The factory converts commodities at a thin spread; the brand and the dealer network decide who keeps the margin.

Every tyre in this report starts as a list of commodities. Natural rubber (NR) comes mainly from smallholder plantations in Kerala and from Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam and Côte d'Ivoire. It arrives as smoked sheets (RSS grades) or as technically specified block rubber (ISNR-20, TSR-20), and it is the backbone of truck and bus tyres, where it makes up the large majority of the rubber in the tread and carcass. Synthetic rubber (styrene-butadiene and polybutadiene for treads and sidewalls, butyl and halobutyl for the air-tight inner liner) is made from crude-oil derivatives, and passenger-car tyres use more of it. Carbon black, a fine soot made by burning heavy oil under controlled conditions, reinforces the rubber and makes up roughly a quarter of a tyre's weight; PCBL, Himadri and Birla Carbon are the main Indian suppliers, and BKT makes its own (see the carbon black report). Steel cord and bead wire give radial tyres their belts and their grip on the rim. Tyre-cord fabric (nylon for bias tyres, polyester for car carcasses) and a long list of chemicals and oils (sulphur, zinc oxide, accelerators, antioxidants, silica, process oil) complete the recipe.

Manufacturing is a sequence of mixing, shaping and cooking. Giant internal mixers (Banbury mixers) blend rubber, carbon black and chemicals into compounds. Extruders and calenders turn those into treads, sidewalls and rubber-coated plies of fabric or steel. Builders assemble the layers on a drum into a "green" tyre, which is then cured under heat and pressure in a mould that forms the tread pattern. Every tyre is inspected, and truck tyres are often X-rayed. The process is capital-heavy (presses and moulds are expensive, and each size needs its own mould) and energy-heavy, but the conversion cost is small relative to the raw materials: in a normal year the materials bill is 55–60% of revenue at a domestic maker, and in FY27 it is running at 60–70%.

The money concentrates in Step 4. A maker with a strong brand and a dense replacement network can pass on cost increases faster and charge more per kilogram; a maker that leans on vehicle makers gets volume but waits a quarter for every price change.

The product range is wide, and each category behaves differently. The table below sets out the main types. The biggest single value pool in India is the commercial-vehicle tyre: truck and bus radials (TBR), the older bias or cross-ply truck tyres (TBB) that are still used where trucks are overloaded and roads are rough, and light-truck tyres. Passenger-car and SUV radials (PCR/UV) are the most brand-driven category. Two-wheeler tyres are the largest by count. Farm and off-the-road (OTR) tyres are heavy, low-volume and specialised; this is BKT's home ground.

Weight matters because raw materials are bought by the kilogram. A 56 kg truck radial uses roughly 19 kg of natural rubber, 15 kg of carbon black and 8 kg of steel; a 9 kg car radial uses far less rubber in absolute terms and proportionally more synthetic rubber. That is why truck-tyre-heavy makers feel a natural-rubber spike first and hardest, and why company-level disclosures on product mix (section 08) are essential for reading the squeeze. Apollo's India business and JK each earn 53–56% of revenue from truck and bus tyres; CEAT earns only 27% there and 28% from two- and three-wheelers.

Then there are the channels. Replacement, the tyres a fleet owner or a car owner buys when the old ones wear out, is about two-thirds of domestic demand by ICRA's reckoning. It is sold through company depots, distributors, thousands of multi-brand dealers and branded outlets (MRF's tyre-and-service shops, Apollo Zones, CEAT Shoppes, JK Steel Wheels). JK alone lists more than 6,250 dealers and distributors and over 900 brand shops; MRF has more than 5,000 active dealers. Replacement prices can be changed within weeks, and the brand earns a premium because buyers, especially fleet owners who calculate cost per kilometre, trust the product. OEM supply to vehicle makers is the other third. It is negotiated on annual contracts with raw-material pass-through formulas that typically reset a quarter late, and it carries thinner margins, but it seeds the replacement market: a car that leaves the factory on Apollo tyres is more likely to get Apollo tyres next time. Exports were worth ₹27,312 crore in FY26 for the industry, a record.

In plain terms

The factory part of the tyre business is a fairly thin conversion margin on rubber and oil. The profit comes from what happens after the tyre leaves the factory: a trusted brand, a dealer on every highway, and the ability to raise prices quickly when costs rise. That is why the same commodity squeeze hits companies differently, and why MRF, with the deepest replacement network, usually holds its margin best.

Finally, the tyre has an afterlife: truck casings are retreaded, often more than once (JK alone has more than 95 retread centres), and end-of-life tyres fall under India's extended producer responsibility rules from July 2022, which cost Apollo ₹116 crore in FY26, or 0.41% of revenue.

02

A ₹1 lakh crore industry that compounds at about 10% a year

The industry is roughly ₹1 lakh crore of annual sales, exports more than a quarter of what it makes, and has grown at about 10% a year through a decade of commodity swings.

The Automotive Tyre Manufacturers' Association (ATMA), whose members make the vast majority of India's tyres, puts industry turnover at roughly ₹1 lakh crore for FY26, or about $10.5 billion at ₹94.9 to the dollar. That figure comes from ATMA statements reported in the trade press in mid-2026; ATMA's own website was unreachable for this report, so treat it as a single-source headline. MRF's annual report gives a cross-check on growth rather than size: it estimates that the tyre industry grew about 10% in FY26 while tyre exports grew 9%. ICRA, the rating agency, put industry revenue growth at 13.9% for FY26 and 15.4% for Q1 FY27.

The five members' consolidated revenue (₹1,02,448 crore) is slightly larger than ATMA's industry figure, which looks odd until you remember what the consolidated numbers include: Apollo's European business (Vredestein and its Hungarian plant), JK's Mexican subsidiary JK Tornel, and CEAT's CAMSO entities around the world. Stripping those out, the members' India-made business is very roughly 75–80% of ATMA's industry total (an INDICATIVE estimate). The rest belongs to multinational subsidiaries (Bridgestone, Michelin, Continental, Yokohama), to Goodyear India and TVS Srichakra, which are listed but outside the NSE 750, and to smaller makers.

03

The GST-2.0 rush: demand is the easy part

A tax cut, a low base and easy financing produced the strongest vehicle sales in years. Replacement, OEM and export demand all grew, but the mix moved towards the channel that pays a quarter late.

On 22 September 2025 the GST Council's "GST 2.0" overhaul cut the tax on most new pneumatic tyres (HSN 4011) from 28% to 18%, and on tractor tyres and tubes to 5%. At the same time it cut GST on small cars and two-wheelers up to 350cc from 28% to 18%, on trucks and buses from 28% to 18%, and on tractors from 12% to 5%. ATMA, chaired by MRF's Arun Mammen, welcomed the removal of tyres from what had been treated as a luxury slab. Apollo announced price cuts of ₹300–2,000 per tyre to pass on the full benefit: ₹300–1,500 on passenger tyres and about ₹2,000 on a truck radial. Mechanically, a tyre that cost a buyer ₹12,800 at 28% GST costs ₹11,800 at 18% if the ex-tax price is unchanged, a fall of about 7.8%.

For tyre makers, vehicle sales matter in two ways. They drive OEM demand immediately, because every new vehicle leaves the factory on four, six, ten or two new tyres plus a spare. They drive replacement demand later, as the bigger vehicle population wears out its tyres: a car tyre lasts three to five years, a truck radial one to two lakh kilometres before retreading. Replacement demand also depends on how much vehicles are used, which tracks freight volumes, mining and infrastructure work, rural incomes and fuel prices. ICRA's June 2026 outlook expected FY27 domestic volumes to grow 5–7%, led by replacement. The companies' own numbers ran well ahead of that in H1.

JK Tyre's Q2 FY27 call (23 October 2026) gave the most detailed picture: domestic sales up 32% year on year, OEM up 65% (truck-radial OEM up 76%, car-radial OEM up 35%), replacement up 20%, tipper tyres up about 20% against industry growth of 11–12%, two- and three-wheeler tyres up about 85% (mainly scooters), and exports up 14% on the previous quarter. Apollo's India revenue in Q1 FY27 rose 15.6% to a record ₹5,462 crore, its best growth in 14 quarters, led by volume across replacement, OEM and exports. CEAT's Q2 standalone volume rose about 10%, but its replacement volume grew only in low single digits because a production bottleneck in July and August limited what it could ship; OEM and exports grew in low double digits.

In plain terms

Out of every hundred rupees a tyre maker takes in, about sixty to sixty-five go straight back out to pay for rubber, oil-based chemicals, steel and fabric. That leaves thirty-five to forty to pay workers, run the factory, ship the tyres, advertise and earn a profit. When the material bill rises by five rupees, almost all of it comes out of profit, because the other costs do not shrink. That is why a five-point rise in material costs can cut a margin of thirteen to under ten.

BKT's hundred rupees look quite different. Its raw-material bill was 49.3% of total income in FY26 (company standalone basis), about eleven points lower than a domestic road-tyre maker. Three things explain the gap. It makes its own carbon black, so one of the main inputs comes in at cost rather than at a supplier's price. It imports natural and synthetic rubber duty-free against its export obligations under the Advance Authorisation scheme. And its products earn far more per kilogram: ₹336 per kg in FY26 and about ₹364 in Q1 FY27 (on total income), because off-highway tyres are sold in small batches across thousands of sizes to replacement buyers abroad. Freight is higher (about 5% of revenue, per management, and rising with container rates), sponsorship of European football and US motorsport adds a little to marketing, and the remainder leaves an EBITDA of about 22–23 rupees.

An oligopoly behind a licence wall

Four Indian families and a handful of multinationals make almost every tyre sold in India. Since June 2020, importing a new car, truck or two-wheeler tyre has needed a licence.

Indian tyre-making is concentrated. The four domestic majors in this report, MRF, Apollo, CEAT and JK, together with BKT, the multinational subsidiaries (Bridgestone India, Michelin India, Continental India, Yokohama India) and a few smaller makers (TVS Srichakra, Goodyear India, the remnants of Birla Tyres) account for more than nine-tenths of organised production. What keeps the structure stable is trade protection. In 2015–17 Chinese truck radials flooded the replacement market; one industry claim at the time put imports at about 40% of truck-radial replacement, nine-tenths of it Chinese. India imposed anti-dumping duties of $245.35–452.33 a tonne on Chinese truck and bus radials in September 2017 for five years, and the duties lapsed around 2022. The decisive step came on 12 June 2020, when the Directorate General of Foreign Trade moved new pneumatic tyres for cars, buses, trucks and motorcycles (HS 4011.10, 4011.20 and 4011.40) from "free" to "restricted", meaning every import needs a licence. Imports collapsed. CareEdge's October 2026 rationale on MRF still cites the licence regime, together with trade remedies, as the reason Chinese competition is contained. On top of that sit basic customs duty of about 10% on tyres and the BIS quality-control order of 2009, which requires every plant that ships tyres to India, including foreign plants, to hold a BIS licence.

In plain terms

Since 2020 a foreign company cannot simply ship a container of cheap tyres into India; it needs an import licence, and its factory needs Indian certification. That wall is the main reason the Indian makers can raise prices together when rubber gets dearer without losing the market to Chinese or Thai tyres. If the wall came down, the price ceiling for Indian tyres would be set in China.

The import threat has not vanished; it has changed shape. Chinese tyre makers moved capacity to Thailand, Vietnam and Cambodia to escape American anti-dumping duties, and some of that output aims at India's premium car and truck segments through licensed routes. Historically, Chinese tyres also entered at a concessional rate of about 8.6% under the Asia-Pacific Trade Agreement. CEAT says its export price increases are limited by "premium European manufacturers and lower-priced Chinese brands", so the Chinese ceiling already binds abroad. India remains a large net exporter of tyres: ₹27,312 crore of exports in FY26 against a small import bill. Import data by country for FY24–FY26 could not be obtained for this report.

The final structural feature is retreading. A truck-tyre casing is often retreaded one to three times by organised tread-rubber suppliers or by thousands of small shops. Radial casings retread less often on poor roads, and better highways reduce damage, so retreading slowly shrinks as radialisation rises. The GST cut narrowed the price gap between a new tyre and a retread, and retreaders complained of being treated less favourably than new tyres. For the listed makers, retreading is both a competitor to new truck tyres and a small business of its own.

In plain terms

When the rupee falls and rubber rises, the first group suffers and the second can even benefit. Adding them together hides both stories.

First, BKT is already more Indian than its reputation: India was 36.3% of its FY26 volume and 39.7% in Q1 FY27, when Indian revenue exceeded European revenue in a quarter for the first time. 07

Rules of the road: tax, trade walls, labels and a cartel case

GST, rubber duties, import licences, BIS and AIS-142 standards, waste-tyre rules, the EU deforestation regulation, a split US tariff, and a ₹1,788 crore competition case that has been pending for eight years.

Indirect tax. Since 22 September 2025, most new pneumatic tyres attract 18% GST, down from 28%, and tractor tyres and tubes 5%. Vehicles were cut at the same time. The tax change lowered the street price of a tyre by about 7.8% (section 03) and has made FY27's price increases easier for buyers to absorb.

Customs on inputs. Dry natural rubber pays basic customs duty of 25% or ₹30 a kilogram, whichever is lower. At current prices the ₹30 cap binds, because 25% of ₹280 would be ₹70. Compounded rubber (natural rubber pre-mixed with chemicals, HS 4005), which industry used to import at 10% to sidestep the rubber duty, was raised to 25% in the February 2023 Budget. Despite that, compound imports rose from 2.45 lakh tonnes in 2024-25 to 3.49 lakh tonnes in 2025-26 while straight rubber imports fell from 5.51 to 4.59 lakh tonnes. Exporters can import rubber duty-free under the Advance Authorisation scheme against an export obligation, which is one reason BKT's material costs are lower. The tyre industry lobbies for lower duty and easier authorisations; the Rubber Board and Kerala's planters lobby the other way.

Customs on tyres. New tyres pay basic customs duty of about 10%, and since 12 June 2020 imports of car, truck, bus and motorcycle tyres need a DGFT licence (section 05). The BIS quality-control order of 2009 requires certification for every tyre sold in India.

Performance standards. AIS-142, notified by the road transport ministry (GSR 479(E), 28 June 2022), sets limits for rolling resistance, wet grip and rolling noise for car (C1), light commercial (C2) and truck and bus (C3) tyres. Reported application dates are 1 October 2022 for new tyre types, 1 April 2023 for existing types on rolling resistance and wet grip, and 1 June 2023 for noise; the notification itself was not opened for this report, so treat the dates as snippet-grade. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency has a star-labelling programme for tyres based on rolling resistance, but no evidence was found of a mandatory phase. The standards push the industry towards silica compounds and low-rolling-resistance designs, which suit the larger makers with research budgets.

Waste tyres. The Hazardous and Other Wastes Amendment Rules of July 2022 (GSR 593(E)) made producers and importers responsible for recycling end-of-life tyres, with targets reported as 35%, 70% and 100% of tyres placed on the market (by weight, on a two-year-lagged base) for 2022-23, 2023-24 and 2024-25. Producers meet the obligation by buying certificates from registered pyrolysis and crumb-rubber recyclers. The cost is small: Apollo's was ₹116 crore in FY26, 0.41% of revenue.

In plain terms

Most of India's tyre rules protect the incumbents more than they burden them. The import licence and BIS certification keep cheap foreign tyres out; the rubber duty helps Kerala's planters but costs tyre makers less when prices are high because it is capped at ₹30 a kilogram; and the performance and recycling rules cost the big makers well under one percent of sales. The rules that could genuinely hurt sit abroad: in the EU and in Washington.

The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR). Rubber and tyres are covered products. After two postponements (the latest by Regulation (EU) 2025/2650), the rules apply from 30 December 2026 for large and medium operators and from 30 June 2027 for micro and small ones. The company that first places a tyre on the EU market must file a due-diligence statement showing that the rubber in it was not grown on land deforested after 2020, backed by geolocation of the plots it came from. For a tyre made from rubber bought through traders from thousands of Kerala, Thai and Ivorian smallholders, that traceability is hard. The European Commission's May 2026 package did not reopen the text but plans a delegated act to trim the product scope. The exposure in this sub-sector sits with BKT (Europe was 40% of FY26 volume), Apollo's Vredestein (made in Hungary, but from imported rubber) and CEAT-CAMSO's European sales.

The US tariff stack. American tariffs on Indian tyres have changed four times in fourteen months, and they now differ by product rather than by company. In August 2025 most Indian goods faced 50%; an interim India–US framework in early February 2026 set a reciprocal rate of 18%; on 20 February 2026 the US Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on which both were based, making the 18% void; a 10% Section 122 tariff applied until 24 July 2026; since then a 10% Section 301 duty applies on top of the normal (MFN) duty, and it is under legal challenge. But road tyres were never only on that track. Car and light-truck radials (HTS 4011.10 and 4011.20.10) have been in the Section 232 auto-parts annex since 3 May 2025, at 25% plus the roughly 4% MFN duty, and the IEEPA ruling did not touch Section 232. CEAT said on its 15 October call that its truck radials also pay the 25% auto-parts rate; that HTS-level coverage should be verified.

Rubber at ₹285: anatomy of the FY27 squeeze Deep dive

Natural rubber at a record, crude oil in a second spike, a rupee near its low: the raw-material basket rose 25–30% in six months, and price increases have recovered about half of it.

Every tyre cycle is a race between two indices: the cost of the raw-material basket and the price of tyres. Analysts model tyre earnings as the price index minus the raw-material index lagged by a quarter, and every earnings forecast in the sector is really a view on natural rubber and crude oil. In FY27 the cost index has run far ahead. CEAT's management told investors on 15 October 2026 that its basket rose about 15% in Q1 over the March 2026 exit level, another 8–9% in Q2, and should rise about 3% more in Q3, a cumulative 27% by December. JK said its basket rose about 20% in Q1 over Q4 FY26 and another 9% in Q2, about 30% over six months, with 4–5% more expected in Q3. Apollo saw about 17% in Q1 and expected about 8% more in Q2. Against that, price increases since 1 April have been 12–13% in replacement and 14–15% to OEMs at CEAT, 11–12% at JK, and 9–11% at Apollo, which says it needs 15–16%.

Leg one: natural rubber at a record

The domestic benchmark, RSS-4 grade at Kottayam, set a record of ₹284 a kilogram on 1 August 2026 and another of ₹284.50 on 13 October. For comparison, it averaged about ₹152 in April–December 2023 and about ₹202 in April–December 2024, and was around ₹184 at points in 2024 (figures from parliamentary answers and trade reports; the monthly Rubber Board series was unreachable for this report). It touched ₹262 on 14 May 2026. Apollo, which disclosed its purchase prices, paid an average of ₹225 a kilogram in Q1 FY27 and more than ₹260 in Q2; Crisil put Q1 at ₹240–260. Internationally, CEAT said the Singapore TSR-20 benchmark rose from about $2,200 to $2,600 a tonne during Q2. The unusual feature of 2026 is that the domestic price moved above the international one: on 13 October Bangkok RSS-4 converted to ₹282.16 a kilogram, below Kottayam's ₹284.50. Normally domestic rubber trades at a premium only because of the import duty; now importing offers no saving even before the ₹30 duty.

The second leg of the squeeze is oil. This matters for the timing of the recovery. Crude-linked inputs reach tyre makers' cost lines one to two months after the oil price moves, through synthetic-rubber and carbon-black contract formulas and inventory. In plain terms

Two different things got expensive at once. Rubber, which grows on trees in Kerala and South-East Asia, hit a record because India grows far less than it uses. Oil, which is the raw material for synthetic rubber, carbon black and nylon, spiked twice because of the war in West Asia. Rubber prices react to weather and plantations; oil prices react to wars and shipping lanes. In 2026 both moved the same way, and the second oil spike in September and October has not yet reached the companies' accounts.

Why price increases lag, and by how much

Tyre makers cannot reprice instantly. In replacement, the industry moves in small, frequent steps a week or two apart (CEAT says peers' increases have been within 0.25–0.5 percentage points of each other), because a large single increase invites dealers to stock up before it and buyers to trade down after it. In Q2 FY27 CEAT's realised price effect was about 8% (5.5% of it in July). With vehicle makers, contract formulas pass on raw-material changes with about a three-month lag, and negotiations are, in Apollo's words, "never easy". Exports are slowest, because Indian makers price 15–25% below the top European brands and Chinese brands set a floor. And most makers carry 60–90 days of raw-material inventory (BKT's MD&A), so reported costs trail spot prices in a rising market by up to a quarter, which flatters margins on the way up and hurts them on the way down.

Booms, busts and squeezes: the tyre cycle since 1926

A century of licence-era bias tyres, a long radial transition, an overseas buying spree, and a margin cycle that has swung between 10% and 17% in five years.

India's tyre industry is old. Dunlop India began making tyres at Sahaganj in 1926; Firestone and Goodyear followed. MRF began in 1946 as K. M. Mammen Mappillai's toy-balloon unit in Madras and started making tyres in 1960 with technical help from Mansfield Tire & Rubber of the US. CEAT, of Italian origin, was incorporated in India in 1958 and acquired by the RPG group in 1982. Apollo Tyres was incorporated in 1972 and commissioned its Perambra plant in Kerala in 1977, the same year JK Tyre's Kankroli plant started. BKT's off-highway business began in 1987. For most of the licence era, Indian tyres were bias (cross-ply) tyres, the industry was protected, and the companies competed mainly on distribution.

Three long trends have reshaped the business since. Radialisation: car tyres went almost entirely radial by the 2010s, and truck tyres followed more slowly because bias tyres tolerate overloading and rough roads; truck-tyre radialisation is commonly put at 55–60% of tonnage by FY24–25 from about 15–20% around FY10 (an INDICATIVE industry estimate; no official series). JK, which built India's first truck-radial capacity at Banmore in the late 1990s, calls itself the pioneer. Globalisation: Apollo bought Dunlop South Africa (2006, later sold) and Vredestein of the Netherlands (2009), and in 2013 tried and failed to buy Cooper Tire of the US for $2.5 billion; JK bought Tornel of Mexico in 2008 for about $68 million; Yokohama bought India-based Alliance Tire Group in 2016; CEAT bought Michelin's CAMSO compact-line business in 2024–25. BKT stayed organic. Capacity building: Apollo's Hungarian plant (2017, about €475 million) and Andhra Pradesh plant (2018), BKT's Bhuj complex (ramped 2015–17), and today's ₹30,000 crore industry programme reported by ATMA.

In plain terms

Tyre profits move in waves. When rubber and oil are cheap and prices have already gone up, the companies make a lot of money; when rubber and oil jump, profits can fall by half or more within a year. Reading a tyre maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about a tyre company: how many tonnes it sold, at what price, through which channel, at what raw-material cost, how full its plants are, what it spends to grow, and what that does to its balance sheet and returns.

The equation, and which parts management controls

A tyre maker's operating profit can be written as one equation. For each segment (truck, car, two-wheeler, farm, off-highway), multiply the tonnes or units sold by the realisation per kilogram or per tyre; add the segments up to get revenue; subtract the raw-material cost per kilogram times the tonnes; subtract conversion costs (power, labour, plant overheads); and subtract selling costs (freight, advertising, dealer incentives). What is left is EBITDA. For BKT the realisation term is a euro or dollar price per kilogram multiplied by the exchange rate, and there is a small extra line for carbon black sold to third parties.

Revenue becomes ₹3,629 crore, raw materials ₹2,430 crore, and EBITDA ₹349 crore, a 9.6% margin. Revenue grew 21% and EBITDA fell 11%. That is, almost to the decimal point, CEAT's Q2 FY27.

In plain terms

A tyre company's profit is the number of tonnes it sells, times what it gets per kilogram, minus what the rubber and chemicals cost per kilogram, minus the cost of running the factories and selling the tyres. The company can push its prices and its mix, but it cannot control the cost of rubber or oil. When those costs jump faster than prices, revenue can rise by a fifth while profit falls.

1. Volume: tonnes and units, by segment

What it is. The physical quantity sold. BKT reports tonnes every quarter. The domestic four report volume growth only in words on earnings calls (MRF holds no calls), sometimes by channel and segment, never as a consistent series. Formula. Volume growth = this period's tonnes (or units) ÷ the same period last year − 1. Worked example. BKT sold 93,770 tonnes in Q1 FY27 against 80,664 a year earlier: 93,770 ÷ 80,664 − 1 = 16.2%. What drives it. Vehicle sales for OEM, the vehicle population and usage for replacement, freight and mining activity for truck and off-highway tyres, monsoon and farm incomes for farm tyres, and market share. Benchmark. Industry volume growth of 5–7% is normal (ICRA's FY27 call); double digits means share gains or an unusual boom; below 3% is weak.

How the members compare. BKT's tonnage was nearly flat for two years (2,92,628 t in FY24, 3,15,273 t in FY25, 3,17,356 t in FY26) and then jumped in Q1 FY27 on a weak base and a strong Indian season. CEAT produced more than 4.5 crore tyres in FY26, up from 3.96 crore, partly through CAMSO. JK's domestic volumes rose about 25% in Q1 FY27 and 32% in Q2. CEAT's standalone volume rose about 10% in Q2. Apollo's India revenue rose 15.6% in Q1 on what it called volume-led growth but did not quantify volume. MRF discloses nothing. The trap: "volume" may mean units or tonnes, and a shift from two-wheeler to truck tyres raises tonnes far more than units. JK's 85% growth in two- and three-wheeler tyres adds many units and few tonnes.

2. Price and mix: splitting value growth

What it is. The part of revenue growth that comes from charging more per unit, either by raising list prices or by selling a richer mix of sizes, segments and channels. Formula. (1 + revenue growth) = (1 + volume growth) × (1 + price/mix growth); so price/mix = (1 + revenue growth) ÷ (1 + volume growth) − 1. Worked example. CEAT's consolidated revenue grew 26.2% in Q2 FY27, and management said about 40% of that came from volume (including the CAMSO acquisition), roughly 10.5 points. Price and mix then contributed about 1.262 ÷ 1.105 − 1 ≈ 14%, close to its 13% consolidated price increase plus some premium-mix gain. What drives it. Raw-material pass-through, the shift to 17-inch-plus car tyres and radial two-wheeler tyres, replacement versus OEM mix, and currency for exporters. Benchmark. In a normal year price/mix adds 2–5%; in a pass-through year 10–15%; negative price/mix signals a price war or a slump in premium demand.

The trap. Acquisitions and mergers inflate both volume and revenue growth. CEAT's Q1 and Q2 FY27 growth includes CAMSO, which was consolidated from 1 September 2025, and JK's standalone numbers are not comparable with FY25 because Cavendish was merged into the standalone entity (effective 22 December 2025, appointed date 1 April 2025). Always ask how much of "growth" is organic. The domestic-four growth reported in this report (+11.3% in Q1 FY27) is therefore partly price and partly acquisition, with organic volume growth probably in high single digits.

3. Channel mix: replacement, OEM and exports

What it is. The share of sales to replacement buyers (through dealers), to vehicle makers, and abroad. Formula. Replacement revenue ÷ total revenue (or tonnes ÷ tonnes; say which). Worked example. If a maker sells ₹6,400 crore to replacement, ₹2,300 crore to OEMs and ₹1,300 crore abroad, its mix is 64/23/13, which is JK's FY26 split per CareEdge. What drives it. The vehicle cycle (OEM booms dilute the replacement share, as JK's 63/27/10 in Q1 FY27 shows), the maker's dealer reach, and export strategy. Why it matters. Replacement carries the margin and passes costs through in weeks; OEM passes them through a quarter late at lower margins; exports are slowest to reprice. Industry estimates put replacement margins 5–8 points of EBITDA above OEM, though no company discloses channel margins (INDICATIVE). Benchmark (INDICATIVE). 60%+ replacement is strong, 45–60% average, below 45% weak for a domestic maker.

4. Raw-material cost as a share of sales, and gross margin

Benchmark (INDICATIVE). For a domestic maker, RM% at or below 58% is good, 58–63% average, 64% or more weak; equivalently a gross margin of 40%+ good, 35–40% average, 35% or less weak. 5. Price-hike pass-through and the lag

What it is. How much of raw-material inflation a maker has recovered through prices, and how quickly. Formula. Pass-through = cumulative price increase ÷ cumulative basket inflation over the same period; the price needed to hold rupee gross profit = RM share × basket inflation. Worked example. CEAT: basket +27% by December against prices +13% to September, so pass-through is about 48%. With raw materials at 60% of revenue, holding rupee gross profit needs 0.60 × 27% ≈ 16% of price, so CEAT is about three points short in rupee terms and six to eight points short of its comfort-zone gross margin. What drives it. Replacement share (faster), OEM formulas (one-quarter lag), export competition (slowest), inventory (60–90 days of raw materials flatter costs early and hurt later), and industry discipline. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). 70% or more of inflation recovered within six months is good; 50–70% average; below 50% weak.

How they compare. CEAT about 48%; JK about 38% (11–12% against about 30%); Apollo India about 38% (9–11% against about 26%), and Apollo Europe further behind (3–4% taken of about 10% needed); BKT roughly in balance at the margin (about 5% price against about 5% more raw-material inflation), from a much lower raw-material share. MRF does not disclose its price increases. The trap. Companies often quote price increases "taken" (announced list-price changes) rather than "realised" (what flowed into revenue in the quarter). CEAT's realised price effect in Q2 was about 8%, against 12–13% announced in replacement by September. Ask which number you are being given.

6. EBITDA margin: three definitions

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax as a share of revenue. Formula. EBITDA ÷ revenue. Worked example. MRF's FY26: screener operating profit ₹4,910 crore on ₹31,149 crore = 15.8%. What drives it. Gross margin minus conversion and selling costs; operating leverage (CEAT's other expenses fell from 20.6% to 18.2% of revenue in Q2 as volume rose). Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Domestic road tyres: 15%+ good, 11–15% average, 10% or less weak. Off-highway exporter: 24%+ good, 19–24% average, 18% or less weak.

The trap is the definition. There are at least three bases in circulation for the same company. Screener's "operating profit" excludes other income. JK's company "EBITDA" includes other income (₹343 crore, 7.1%, in Q2 FY27, against screener's ₹334 crore, 6.9%). CEAT's company EBITDA (₹452 crore, 9.5%) includes its share of the Sri Lankan joint venture, against screener's ₹457 crore (9.6%). Rating agencies use PBILDT, which can differ by more: CareEdge's MRF Q1 FY27 PBILDT margin was 13.77%, against screener's 11.8%. Never mix bases in one chart. This report uses screener operating profit in all tables and quotes company figures in prose with their basis.

Realisation (price, mix, FX) minus raw-material and conversion costs per kilogram. Benchmark (INDICATIVE, off-highway). ₹80,000+ good, ₹65,000–80,000 average, below ₹65,000 weak.

What it is. Three lessons follow. First, a new plant at a normal 13% margin earns a single-digit return for years; the industry's 14–19% ROCE comes from blending new plants with old, depreciated ones, which is why a company in a heavy build phase (BKT today, JK and Apollo next) sees its ROCE fall before it rises. Third, utilisation is a powerful lever: at 95% instead of 85%, the same plant sells about 3,300 more tonnes, and if conversion costs stay flat, EBITDA rises by roughly ₹30–35 crore. This is the economics behind the industry's eagerness to fill plants with OEM volume even at thinner margins.

In plain terms

A brand-new tyre factory earns a poor return at first, because it has cost a lot and the debt that paid for it still needs servicing. The same factory, ten years on, earns twice the return on the same profit because it has been mostly paid off. Companies spending heavily on new plants today are accepting a few years of lower returns in exchange for growth later, and a cost squeeze makes those years harder.

Definition traps, collected

  • Three EBITDAs. Screener operating profit excludes other income; JK's company EBITDA includes it; CEAT's includes its Sri Lankan JV share; CareEdge's PBILDT can differ by two points (MRF Q1 FY27: 13.77% vs 11.8%).
  • Units vs tonnes vs revenue. BKT reports tonnes, CEAT and JK tyres a day or a year, others only revenue growth. A mix shift changes each measure differently.
  • Consolidated vs India. Apollo's 79% replacement share and 57% RM ratio are consolidated (Europe included); its India figures are 66% and 65.9%.
  • Acquisitions and mergers in growth. CEAT's growth includes CAMSO from 1 Sep 2025; JK's standalone FY26 includes Cavendish; neither is organic.
  • Market share. MRF's ~31.9% is units (CareEdge); most other shares are self-reported for one segment and channel. The often-quoted "35%" for MRF is unsupported.
  • Natural-rubber "price". Domestic RSS-4 (₹/kg, Kottayam), international RSS-3/RSS-4 (Bangkok) and TSR-20 (Singapore, $/t) are different grades and markets; say which.
  • Price increases "taken" vs "realised". Announced list increases run ahead of what reaches revenue in the quarter (CEAT Q2: ~8% realised).
  • Net debt bases. Screener ignores some bank balances; companies net them; rating agencies add acceptances, dealer deposits and leases. Apollo's March 2026 net debt is ₹3,613 crore, ₹1,600 crore or roughly ₹7,000 crore gross depending on whose basis you use.
  • Reported vs pre-exceptional profit. Apollo's FY26 profit includes a ₹1,033.5 crore exceptional charge and a ₹573.7 crore one-off tax credit; JK's a ₹146 crore exceptional; CEAT's a ₹71 crore exceptional. Quarterly growth rates on depressed bases (Apollo's Q1 FY26 profit of ₹13 crore) are meaningless.
  • Capacity. BKT's tyre and carbon-black achievable capacities are both 3,60,000 tonnes a year, genuinely equal; MRF discloses no tonnage capacity at all.

How to read a tyre company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, then split it. Find the YoY revenue growth and management's volume comment. Price/mix = (1 + revenue growth) ÷ (1 + volume growth) − 1. Strip out acquisitions (CAMSO, Cavendish). Red flag: revenue growth mostly from price while volume lags the industry.
  2. Gross margin, quarter on quarter. Compute 100 − material cost %. A move of more than two points in a quarter is the squeeze or the recovery in action. Red flag: gross margin falling while the company says price increases are "fully absorbed".
  3. The raw-material outlook. Note management's basket guidance for the next quarter and compare it with natural rubber (RSS-4) and Brent over the past two months. Red flag: guidance below what spot prices imply.
  4. Price increases taken and planned. Record cumulative hikes since the cycle began, by channel. Divide by basket inflation to get pass-through. Red flag: pass-through below 50% after two quarters.
  5. EBITDA margin on one basis. Use screener's operating margin for comparison and note the company's own figure and what it includes. Red flag: margin "beats" driven by other income or one-offs.
  6. Channel and segment commentary. Replacement vs OEM vs export growth; truck vs car vs two-wheeler. Red flag: OEM-led growth in a cost spike (thin, lagged pricing) or replacement weakness (lost share or a bottleneck).
  7. Below EBITDA. Check depreciation (rising with new plants), interest (rising with debt), exceptional items and the tax rate. Red flag: profit propped up by a tax credit or a treasury gain (MRF's other income rose to ₹195 crore in Q1 FY27).
  8. Debt and working capital. Compare borrowings or net debt with the previous quarter; compute net debt ÷ annualised EBITDA. Red flag: debt up sharply in one quarter (JK +₹1,243 crore in six months) or a ratio approaching the rating agency's trigger.
  9. Capex and capacity. Note quarterly capex, the full-year guidance and new capacity timing. Red flag: capex guidance unchanged while margins halve and debt rises.
  10. Overseas and special items. Apollo Europe's margin, JK Tornel's results (consolidated minus standalone), CAMSO's implied revenue and EBITDA, BKT's realised currency and the US tariff line. Red flag: subsidiaries losing money for longer than guided.

Done in that order, the ten steps take about ten minutes with the results release, the investor presentation and the previous quarter's numbers side by side. They answer the three questions that matter in this sub-sector: is demand real or just price, is the spread recovering or still closing, and can the balance sheet carry the capex through the squeeze?

Who is winning: pricing power, balance sheets and mix

In a squeeze, the winners are the companies whose margins bend least and whose balance sheets do not have to bend at all. On that test MRF and BKT lead, Apollo is close behind, and CEAT and JK are carrying the most risk.

Winning in a commodity squeeze has two parts. The first is pricing power, which shows up as the margin a company holds when costs jump. The second is balance-sheet strength, which decides whether a company can keep investing, keep paying dividends and keep its credit rating while profits fall. The first map below plots both: the latest-quarter operating margin against net debt as a multiple of FY26 EBITDA.

The map sorts the group cleanly. BKT is in a class of its own on margin and has modest leverage, though its net debt is rising with capex (about ₹1,725 crore at June 2026 on the company's basis). MRF has the strongest balance sheet in the group (CARE AAA, ₹7,572 crore of liquid funds) and held 11.8% in Q1 FY27; its gross margin fell as much as CEAT's, but its lower overheads and treasury income cushioned the result. Apollo held 11.7% in Q1 with a balance sheet it has spent five years repairing; its India margin fell 160 basis points to 12.0% and Europe's to 8.9%. CEAT and JK are where the squeeze bites hardest: thinner margins, rising debt, and rating-agency triggers within sight.

The second map turns to value: what investors pay (trailing P/E) against what the business earns on its capital (FY26 ROCE). In a rational market, higher returns should command higher multiples. The tyre group does not quite follow that rule.

Apollo is the clearest "high-ish return, low multiple" case, which reflects scepticism about Europe, a new capex cycle and a CFO departure. JK sits close to the centre, but on a debt-adjusted basis (EV/EBITDA 6.7×) it is the cheapest, for reasons the first map makes obvious.

BKT's off-highway machine turns onto the highway Deep dive

India's low-cost global specialist in farm and mining tyres earned 21–24% margins for a decade. Now it is building car and truck tyres for India, with Pirelli as partner, while Europe, America and a new EU deforestation rule test the old engine.

Balkrishna Industries makes off-highway tyres: rear tractor tyres the height of a man, flotation tyres for harvesters and slurry tankers, radial tyres for loaders and dump trucks, solid tyres for forklifts and port machinery, and tyres for forestry and turf equipment. It sells more than 3,600 sizes in more than 160 countries, most of them in small batches to replacement buyers, and it sells by the tonne. Its model is an arbitrage: Indian manufacturing costs, captive carbon black and duty-free imported rubber on one side, and European and American replacement prices, set in euros and dollars by Michelin, Bridgestone and Trelleborg (now Yokohama), on the other. That arbitrage produced operating margins of 17.5–24.2% in every year from FY22 to FY26.

FY26: a weak year for the old engine

FY26 was BKT's worst year in four. Consolidated revenue rose only 3.6% to ₹10,823 crore, operating margin slipped to 21.3%, and profit after tax fell 24.9% to ₹1,243 crore. Volume was flat at 3,17,356 tonnes. Three things went wrong: European dealers destocked after a strong FY25, the US raised tariffs on Indian goods to 50% in August 2025, and foreign-exchange swings turned a ₹202 crore realised gain in FY25 into a ₹164 crore loss. The old engine's three tests: tariffs, the euro and the EU deforestation rule

US tariffs. BKT's position in the US has improved relative to its domestic peers. Off-highway tyres now pay about 10% plus a low MFN duty under the Section 301 regime, against 25% plus about 4% for Indian car and truck radials under Section 232. BKT is also exempt from US anti-dumping duty on Indian off-road tyres and pays a countervailing duty of only 0.57% after the review effective 19 March 2026. Management said the tariff rate is "settling at 10% levels" and expects the US to return towards 15–16% of revenue. It has applied for refunds of IEEPA duties paid before the Supreme Court ruling, a possible one-off gain with unknown timing. The Section 301 duty is itself under legal challenge.

The euro. About two-fifths of BKT's volume is sold in Europe, and a stronger euro against the rupee is a direct tailwind. The euro traded around ₹108–110 in October 2026; BKT's Q1 hedges realised about ₹102. A rupee at ₹94.9 to the dollar raises realisations, but it also raises the cost of imported rubber, so the net effect depends on the hedge book and on how much rubber BKT buys in dollars.

EUDR. From 30 December 2026, any company placing rubber products on the EU market must prove, with plot-level geolocation, that the rubber was not grown on recently deforested land. BKT is the first placer for its own European sales, and Europe is roughly 38–40% of its volume. Compliance means tracing rubber back through traders to smallholdings in India, Thailand, Indonesia and West Africa. The cost is likely to be manageable for a company of BKT's scale, but a failure to file, or a buyer's refusal to accept a statement, would block sales. Watch for BKT's disclosure on readiness in its Q2 and Q3 FY27 results.

In plain terms

BKT's old business sells tractor and mining tyres to farmers and contractors in Europe and America. This year three outside forces matter most to it: America's tariffs (now gentler on BKT's tyres than on Indian car and truck tyres), the value of the euro, and a new EU rule from 30 December 2026 that requires proof that the rubber in every tyre did not come from cleared forest. None of these is fatal; each can move profits by a few per cent.

The new engine: on-highway tyres and the Pirelli agreements

BKT is spending heavily to become a domestic road-tyre maker as well. It began supplying truck and bus radials in India in April 2026, has launched selected two-wheeler tyres, and has appointed distributors across India for every category including cars, under new brands (Loadxpert, Milexpert, Zenova and Thyros) with a 24-hour rider-assistance programme. A commercial-vehicle radial pilot ran in Q4 FY26 and a passenger-car pilot is scheduled for Q3 FY27, with car tyres planned from November 2026. Management calls FY27 a portfolio-building year and FY28 the year on-highway becomes "serious business". Its target is about ₹5,000 crore of on-highway revenue by 2030, roughly 20% of a ₹23,000 crore company and about 5% of the domestic market, with off-highway at about 70% and third-party carbon black at about 10%. It targets a blended EBITDA margin of 23–25%. It has repeatedly declined to give on-highway margin guidance.

The capex bill and what it does to returns

BKT's tyre plants (four of them, the largest at Bhuj in Gujarat) have achievable output of 3,60,000 tonnes a year, the same as its carbon-black capacity after Phase II (including 30,000 tonnes of advanced carbon). Of the ₹6,800 crore plan, about ₹3,800 crore had been spent by June 2026 and about ₹3,000 crore remains, with ₹2,500–3,000 crore due in FY27 (₹1,000 crore spent in Q1). Capital commitments rose from ₹924 crore to ₹2,712 crore during FY26. Free cash flow was −₹749 crore in FY26, and net debt rose to about ₹1,725 crore by June 2026 (gross debt about ₹4,690 crore, cash about ₹2,965 crore).

The effect on returns is already visible: ROCE fell from 17% in FY25 to 11% in FY26. Building into the squeeze: balance sheets and the capex cycle Deep dive

Record capex, record raw-material prices and higher interest rates are arriving together. CEAT and JK added ₹2,036 crore of debt in six months; JK's operating margin is below its rating trigger.

The industry is in the largest capacity build in its history. ATMA puts the investment at about ₹30,000 crore over four to five years, and ICRA expects capex at 7–10% of revenue in FY27. Every member is spending: Apollo more than ₹3,000 crore in FY27 within a board-approved ₹5,810 crore programme for FY27–FY29; BKT ₹2,500–3,000 crore; JK ₹1,600–1,800 crore in FY27 rising to ₹2,000–2,500 crore a year in FY28–FY30 under its ₹4,980 crore Chennai programme; CEAT ₹1,400 crore standalone plus ₹100–150 crore for CAMSO; and MRF about ₹1,400 crore in FY26 on a cash-flow proxy, with ₹1,034 crore of capital work in progress at March 2026. The reason is simple: plants are running at 85–95% utilisation and demand is strong. The problem is timing. The capex is being committed into the sharpest margin squeeze since FY22, and working capital is swelling because every tonne of rubber in inventory costs a quarter more than it did in March.

In plain terms

Credit-rating agencies set limits on how much debt a company can carry relative to its profit, and how low its margin can go, before they cut its rating. A lower rating makes borrowing dearer just when the company is borrowing most. JK's margin is already below its agency's line, and CEAT's debt ratio was close to its line even before this year's squeeze. Both need the promised second-half recovery to arrive on time.

Higher interest rates add to the pressure. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, and JK's and CEAT's borrowing costs will reprice upwards on floating-rate loans. JK's interest bill is about ₹430 crore a year; each percentage point on ₹6,000 crore of debt adds about ₹60 crore, or roughly 8% of FY26 profit. MRF, with net cash, benefits from higher yields on its treasury.

The risk beyond FY27 is overbuilding. The FY18–FY20 cycle offers a precedent: capacity came on stream, raw materials eased, and price competition followed. If the current wave of car- and truck-radial capacity arrives in FY28–FY29 just as SIAM's tougher comparison base slows vehicle growth and as BKT-Pirelli and Yokohama's new Visakhapatnam plant add premium car-tyre supply, the industry's price discipline will be tested from the supply side rather than the cost side. The companies best placed for that are those that enter it with low debt: MRF, Apollo (if FY27 capex stays on plan) and BKT. Those with the least room are those building most aggressively relative to their balance sheets, JK first and CEAT second.

Overseas bets: Enschede, CAMSO and Tornel

Three domestic makers own factories abroad. Apollo has just closed one, CEAT has just bought two, and JK is modernising the pair it bought in 2008. None is yet earning Indian margins.

Apollo and Europe. Apollo bought Vredestein of the Netherlands in 2009 and opened a greenfield plant at Gyöngyöshalász in Hungary in 2017 (about €475 million). For years the Dutch plant at Enschede, with high energy and labour costs, dragged on European returns. Apollo asked its Dutch works council for advice on closure on 25 April 2025, reached a settlement in September 2025, and stopped production in June 2026 as planned, affecting about 500 jobs. About 750,000 tyres a year moved, mostly to Hungary; some 14–15-inch sizes moved from Hungary to India, Spacemaster spare tyres to Vadodara, and Enschede's 20-tonne-a-day high-end agricultural line to an off-take partner. The cost appears in FY26 as a ₹1,033.5 crore consolidated exceptional charge (₹1,000.1 crore of impairment and restructuring provisions, plus ₹25.9 crore for the new labour codes and ₹7.5 crore of VRS), with about €50 million of settlement cash still to be paid in FY27. The Dutch subsidiary's assets are now carried at ₹494 crore after a ₹433 crore impairment.

The payoff is still ahead. In Q1 FY27 Europe (excluding the Reifencom distribution business) earned revenue of €147 million (+0.5%) and EBITDA of €13 million, an 8.9% margin against 10.8% a year earlier, depressed by overlapping costs while Enschede wound down and Hungary ramped; excluding the overlap, management put it at about 11%. Europe also needs about 10% of price increases to cover raw materials and had taken only 3–4% by August. The CFO who ran the Enschede project announced on the Q1 call that he is leaving after more than 22 years; no successor was named in the documents reviewed.

On margins, the Indian makers are not outliers. Global majors report segment or adjusted EBIT margins of roughly 6–16% (Goodyear and Sumitomo at the low end, Hankook and Pirelli at the high end), and Michelin's EBITDA margin is around 18–19% (approximate). The global lens matters most in three places: the premium end of the Indian car-tyre market, where Bridgestone, Michelin, Yokohama, Continental and now Pirelli (through BKT) compete for the 16-inch-plus SUV buyer; off-highway tyres, where BKT and CEAT-CAMSO take share from Michelin, Bridgestone, Yokohama Off-Highway (ATG plus Trelleborg Wheel Systems) and Titan International; and trade, where Chinese capacity of more than a billion tyres a year, increasingly made in Thailand and Vietnam, sets the long-run price ceiling in India's export markets.

Even India's biggest tyre company is small by world standards: Michelin and Bridgestone are each about nine times MRF's size. Indian makers earn similar margins to the global giants and are valued more highly because India is growing faster and keeps imports out. The giants matter to Indian investors mainly as competitors for premium car tyres at home and as the companies BKT is taking share from abroad.

Technology and disruption: radials, rims, EVs and Chinese capacity

No single technology threatens the business, but several slow shifts move value towards the makers with research budgets and premium capacity.

Tyres change slowly, and the industry's disruptions are gradual rather than sudden. The most important are shifts in what vehicles need. Electric vehicles are heavier and deliver instant torque, which wears tyres faster (JK says EV tyres wear about 5–10% faster; industry claims run higher), and they need low rolling resistance and low noise. That favours makers with engineering depth: CEAT says it has about a quarter of car and SUV EV fitments and more than 30% of electric two-wheeler fitments, and JK sells a dedicated EV range. Premiumisation, the shift to larger rims and SUVs, raises realisation per kilogram and favours the multinationals and the domestic makers that have invested in 16-inch-plus capacity. Radialisation of truck tyres continues, from an INDICATIVE 55–60% of tonnage, and slowly shrinks retreading.

Materials and manufacturing are changing too. JK markets a tyre with 80% sustainable material and an ISCC Plus-certified range; silica-based "green" tyres lower rolling resistance and help meet AIS-142 limits; and regulators in California and the EU are scrutinising 6PPD, the antioxidant that protects rubber from ozone, because of its toxicity to coho salmon, which could force reformulation worldwide. JK has launched India's first car tyre with an embedded sensor, and Pirelli's Cyber Tyre is in the same family. BKT's capex includes automation. None of these changes the economics quickly, but together they raise the research and capital bar, which favours scale.

That rewards companies that spend on research and premium factories. The biggest outside risk is not a technology but a policy change that lets cheap imported tyres back in.

Bull vs bear

The bull case says the squeeze is a timing problem in a structurally strong, protected industry. The bear case says the second oil spike, record capex and an OEM-heavy boom make FY27–FY28 harder than the market expects.

The bull case

  • Demand is real. Vehicle sales up about 20% in H1 FY27, plants at 85–95% utilisation, replacement demand compounding with the vehicle population, and GST 2.0 leaving buyers able to absorb price increases.
  • Pricing discipline holds. The industry has pushed through 11–15% of increases without visible share loss, and further 4–5% rounds are planned; the price needed to protect rupee profit (about 16%) is within reach.
  • The spread mean-reverts. FY22–23 and FY25 squeezes were followed by strong recoveries (FY24 margins of 16%); natural rubber at a 20-year high invites supply response and demand substitution.
  • Protection. Import licensing and BIS certification keep Chinese tyres out; the industry is a net exporter with record exports.
  • Valuations reset. Mid-caps at about 6.9× EV/EBITDA and all but BKT near 52-week lows, after falls of 23–26% in a year.
  • Optionality. BKT-Pirelli, CEAT-CAMSO and Apollo's restructured Europe could each add to FY28–FY29 profits.

The bear case

  • The second oil leg is not yet in the numbers. Brent averaged $114 in September and about $126 in early October; Q3 FY27 costs may rise more than the 3–5% guided, delaying the trough.
  • The boom is in the wrong channel. OEM-led growth reprices a quarter late, and SIAM's H2 base is the toughest of the cycle.
  • Balance sheets are re-levering into the squeeze. CEAT and JK added ₹2,036 crore of debt in six months; JK's margin is below its rating trigger; repo is up to 5.50%.
  • Overbuilding. About ₹30,000 crore of capacity arriving in FY28–FY29 into slower demand, with BKT-Pirelli and Yokohama adding premium car-tyre supply.
  • Trade and regulation abroad. 25% US tariffs on Indian road tyres, EUDR from 30 December 2026, freight disruption in West Asia.
  • Legal and governance tails. ₹1,610 crore of unprovided CCI penalties on four members, a second CEAT matter, promoter-linked purchases at Apollo, and MRF's 4% payout.

Where does the balance lie? The industry's long-run economics are sound: a protected, concentrated market with growing demand, a replacement annuity and a proven ability to pass costs through over time. The question for the next two quarters is timing, and the evidence is mixed. The companies' guidance points to a Q2 or Q3 trough and an H2 recovery; the oil market points to more cost pressure in Q3. Investors should treat the November results from MRF, BKT and Apollo, and the Q3 basket guidance from all five, as the deciding data.
