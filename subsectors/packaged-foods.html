00

The one-page view: a weak market, strong leaders and a falling multiple

Eight listed companies, five different businesses, one paradox: the best earnings run in years has coincided with the sharpest de-rating in years.

Indian packaged food is the business of buying farm commodities in bulk, converting them into small, branded, shelf-stable packs, and pushing those packs through roughly 12–13 million shops to about 290 million households. The eight NSE 750 companies in this report sold ₹78,631 crore of goods in FY26, up 13.1% on FY25, and earned ₹8,611 crore of profit after tax, up 10.4%. They are worth ₹5.02 lakh crore on the stock market, and one of them, Nestlé India, accounts for almost half of that value.

The defining fact of the last twelve months is a divergence. NielsenIQ (NIQ), which tracks what Indian shops actually sell, found that all-India FMCG volume grew only 0.9% in January–March 2026, with rural volume shrinking 0.8%, and reports for April–June 2026 put rural volume down about 5%. In the same period Nestlé India's sales rose 25.4% in Q1 FY27 and 20.5% in Q2 FY27, led by volume, and Tata Consumer's India branded volume grew 13%. The two facts reconcile only one way: large, urban-skewed brands with heavy modern-trade and quick-commerce exposure took share from small, regional and rural-heavy players. The average shop got quieter; the biggest brands got busier.

Three shocks shaped the period. First, commodities whipsawed: cocoa fell from about US$12,900 a tonne at its December 2024 peak to roughly $3,200 by March 2026, while tea auction prices rose 9.9% in January–July 2026, chilli rose 78% and Heritage Foods' landed milk cost rose 11%. Second, GST 2.0 on 22 September 2025 cut most packaged foods to a 5% rate, which handed a maker selling a ₹10 pack about 12% more money per pack; most gave it back as extra grams rather than a lower price. Third, the 2026 West Asia war pushed crude above $109 a barrel and plastic packaging resin up 30–50%, eating much of the GST gain.

The market's verdict has been to pay less for the same companies. Nestlé India trades at 61 times trailing earnings against about 78 times in June 2026. Britannia hit a 52-week low at 44 times. Bikaji has fallen about 35% in a year, and Orkla India trades 24% below its November 2025 IPO price at 25 times, the cheapest multiple in the group. Meanwhile, in the private market, Temasek paid about ₹8,500 crore for roughly 10% of Haldiram's, implying a value around seven times Bikaji's.

The four forces that define this sub-sector, and that give this report its deep-dive sections, are: the divergence between a stalling market and surging leaders (Part 04); the commodity see-saw that drives almost every margin move (Part 05); the affordability architecture of the ₹5 and ₹10 pack, now strained by GST 2.0 and inflation (Part 06); and the repricing of staples valuations against a private-market benchmark that still pays up for category leaders (Part 17). Quick commerce, parent-company royalties and acquisitions run through all four.

In plain terms

Indian packaged food is a good business in a bad patch. The leaders earn high returns on very little capital because shops pay them quickly and suppliers wait, and their brands let them pass on costs over time. But 2025–26 brought a tax cut that mostly turned into bigger packs, a rural slowdown that hurt small and regional brands, and an energy shock that raised packaging and freight costs. The biggest names grew fastest, yet investors cut the price they pay for each rupee of profit because they now doubt how long the growth and margins last.

Two practical warnings before the detail. The eight are not one business: they range from Nestlé's 23% EBITDA margin and 85% return on capital to Heritage's 6% margin on fresh milk, and the report groups them into five archetypes in Part 02 and Part 09. And growth numbers in this period need a basis check: Q2 FY26 (July–September 2025) was depressed by trade destocking ahead of GST 2.0, so the year-on-year growth rates reported for Q2 and Q3 FY27 are flattered by a weak base.

01

How a ₹10 pack makes money: from wheat field to kirana shelf

The brand owner keeps most of the value; the trade takes a fixed slice; the farmer is paid a commodity price that can move faster than the pack price.

Every company in this report runs the same basic machine. It buys agricultural inputs (flour, edible oil, sugar, milk, cocoa, coffee, tea leaf, chilli, gram flour, potato), transforms them in factories it owns or contracts, wraps them in laminates, plastic and cartons, and sells them through a chain of intermediaries to shops that sell to the consumer. Money is made in the gap between the commodity price paid at one end and the brand price charged at the other, less the cost of reaching the shelf.

Where the shelf price goes

The simplest way to see the economics is to follow one ₹10 pack of biscuits after GST 2.0, using cost ratios calibrated to Britannia's FY26 accounts (about 42% gross margin and 18% EBITDA margin). The consumer pays ₹10. The government takes 5% GST, which is ₹0.48 because the tax is included in the price. The retailer buys at a margin of about 10% (₹0.95) and the distributor and super-stockist keep about 5% of their selling price (₹0.41). The company receives about ₹8.16. Out of that it buys roughly ₹4.08 of ingredients and ₹0.65 of packaging, pays people, advertising, freight and factory overheads, and is left with about ₹1.47 of EBITDA and ₹1.03 of profit after tax.

The dairy case is the inversion that catches out newcomers. In a biscuit, the farmer's wheat is a small fraction of the shelf price; in a pouch of milk, the farmer is paid about two-thirds of what the processor collects. A ₹1 a litre mismatch between what Heritage pays farmers and what it charges consumers moves its quarterly EBITDA by about a fifth. A biscuit maker has a cushion of 40% gross margin; a dairy has a cushion of about ₹12–13 a litre on a ₹60 litre.

In plain terms

On a ₹10 biscuit pack, roughly ₹4 buys the ingredients, ₹1.84 goes to the tax office and the shops, and the maker keeps about ₹1 as profit. That ₹1 is precious because the factory that made the biscuit costs little relative to how many packs it churns out, and because shops pay for stock faster than the maker pays its suppliers. In milk, the maths is much tighter: the farmer gets about two-thirds of the price, and a small rise in the price of raw milk can wipe out a big slice of profit.

One more feature of the chain explains why the leaders are hard to dislodge: reach. Britannia's products are in more than 5 million outlets, Tata Consumer's in about 4.5 million (1.8 million served directly), and Nestlé's rural programme covers about 216,000 villages. A new brand can make a good biscuit; getting it into a million kiranas, keeping it fresh, and collecting the cash is the expensive part. That is why acquisitions (Capital Foods, Organic India, Comfort Click) usually involve putting someone else's brand into an existing distribution machine.

02

One bucket, five businesses: size, growth and what "packaged foods" means

The market is large but poorly measured; the eight listed companies are about 6% of it and run five very different economic models.

Published estimates of the size of India's packaged-food market come from syndicated research houses, not from government or industry bodies, and they do not agree. Ken Research puts it at about US$142 billion in 2025, growing 11% a year; ReportCubes puts it at $137 billion in 2026, growing 7% a year. At an assumed ₹95.5 per dollar that is roughly ₹13.1–13.6 lakh crore, but both definitions include staples such as packaged edible oil, rice and flour that our eight companies barely sell. Treat the figure as INDICATIVE: it says the market is big and fragmented, not how big any single category is.

Category sizes are worse. For biscuits, a figure of ₹1.16 lakh crore for 2025 circulates in trade press citing IBEF, while an older figure puts the domestic biscuit market at ₹35,000 crore. The two cannot both be right on the same definition. A bottom-up triangulation from company revenues (Britannia's biscuits of about ₹13,500 crore, Parle's ₹15,568 crore of FY25 revenue, plus Mondelez, ITC and regional brands) suggests an organised biscuit market of roughly ₹50,000–60,000 crore at company realisation. This report uses the triangulated range and flags the others as unreconciled.

The five businesses inside the bucket

The classification groups these eight because they all sell food-like products in packs, but the economics fall into five archetypes, and almost every comparison in this report has to be made within an archetype rather than across the bucket.

  • The MNC premium platform (Nestlé India). Maggi, Nescafé, KitKat, Cerelac. Gross margin about 55%, EBITDA 23–24%, return on capital about 85%, a 4.5% royalty to the Swiss parent.
  • The mass staples and bakery majors (Britannia, Tata Consumer). Britannia is roughly 70% biscuits with bread, cake, rusk and a cheese joint venture; Tata Consumer sells tea, salt and a widening basket of Sampann staples, Ching's, Smith & Jones and Organic India, plus Tetley and Eight O'Clock abroad. EBITDA 14–18%.
  • The regional and ethnic challengers (Bikaji, Orkla India, Mrs Bectors). Namkeen from Bikaner; Eastern spices and MTR breakfast mixes from the South; Cremica biscuits, English Oven bread and McDonald's buns from Punjab. EBITDA 13–17%, higher capital intensity, heavy regional concentration.
  • The health and wellness brand house (Zydus Wellness). Sugar Free, Glucon-D, Complan, Nutralite, plus the Nycil and Everyuth personal-care brands, and since September 2025 the UK online supplements seller Comfort Click. Seasonal, brand-heavy and now leveraged.
  • The fresh dairy processor (Heritage Foods). Liquid milk, curd, paneer and ice cream in the South. A procurement-spread business with a 5–6% EBITDA margin; benchmark it against Hatsun or Dodla rather than Nestlé.

In plain terms

Nobody knows precisely how big Indian packaged food is: credible-sounding estimates for biscuits alone differ by a factor of three. What is clear is that the eight listed companies are a small slice, about 6%, of a large and fragmented market, and that they are really five different kinds of business wearing one label. A gap between Nestlé's margin and Heritage's says more about what they sell than about how well either is run.

Exports are a minor part of the story for most members but central for two. Nestlé India exports about 4.5% of sales (₹290 crore of ₹6,378 crore in Q1 FY27). Tata Consumer earns about 28% of revenue internationally through Tetley in the UK and Canada and Eight O'Clock coffee in the US. Mrs Bectors sends roughly a quarter to a third of its output abroad (the primer's "about a third" and one broker's 35% are not reconciled with an annual-report segment split). Orkla India earns about a fifth abroad, mostly from Eastern's Gulf diaspora customers. Zydus Wellness, after Comfort Click, earns an estimated 35–40% outside India from UK and European online supplement sales.

03

What drives demand: formalisation, convenience and a rural engine that stalled

Structural drivers are intact; the cyclical driver of 2024–25, rural India, turned negative in 2026.

Demand for packaged food rests on four slow-moving forces and one fast-moving one. The slow forces are the shift from loose to branded products, urbanisation and smaller households, premiumisation, and the extension of distribution into villages. The fast force is affordability: how much money mass-market households have left after rent, fuel and food inflation, and whether the ₹5 and ₹10 packs they buy still feel worth the money.

The structural drivers

  • Loose to branded. Namkeen, spices, pulses, tea and dairy still carry large unbranded shares; industry estimates put snacks and spices at 40–60% loose, though that range is not verified. Tata Sampann staples grew 46% in FY26 and 58% in Q1 FY27; Bikaji grew volume 9.5% in FY26. Investors paid about ₹8,500 crore for 10% of Haldiram's in 2025 largely on the length of this runway.
  • Convenience. Nuclear families and working women buy breakfast mixes, ready-to-cook gravies, instant noodles and bread. Orkla India's convenience foods grew 11.9% in Q1 FY27 and its digital-commerce sales grew 38.1%.
  • Premiumisation and "better for you". Protein bars, millet snacks, sugar substitutes (Sugar Free holds about 96% of its category), organic food (Organic India grew 27% in Q1 FY27) and premium coffee. Each lets a company sell fewer grams for more rupees.
  • Reach. Nestlé's RURBAN programme covers about 216,000 villages; Tata Consumer directly covers 16,000+ villages and added 3,000 in FY26; Britannia runs about 30,000 rural preferred dealers. Every new direct outlet is incremental volume for a brand already in the market.

The cyclical driver that broke

Through calendar 2024 and the first half of 2025, rural India was the engine: NIQ reported rural consumption growing roughly twice as fast as urban. By early 2026 that had reversed. In January–March 2026 rural volume fell 0.8% while urban volume grew 2.2%; NIQ blamed a high base and "pressure on key price points (5/10 packs)". In April–June 2026, reports of NIQ's next snapshot put rural volume down about 5% and rural value down 2.9%, with the food basket's volume down 1.1%. Weak rural wage growth, food inflation returning above 4% in April 2026, and the energy shock from the West Asia war all bit at once.

The rural reversal hit hardest where the price point is the product. NIQ's data show that in rural India the ₹5 and ₹10 packs carry 80% of biscuit volume, 73% of chocolate, 90% of salty snacks and 58% of noodles and vermicelli. NIQ's own headline for the quarter was that inflation is breaking the magic-price-point model in rural India.

Through which pipe: the channel end-markets

About 89% of offline FMCG still moves through traditional trade: kiranas, paan shops and wholesalers. In January–March 2026 that channel grew 1.3% in value and shrank 1.7% in volume. Modern trade, at 11% of offline value, grew 17.4% in value and 22.7% in volume, helped by store openings outside the metros (store count up 19% since April–June 2023) and by falling prices on its shelves. E-commerce, including quick commerce, is about 7% of urban FMCG and 19% in the top eight metros, and its volume grew about 35% in April–June 2026. Institutional sales (QSR buns at Mrs Bectors, Nestlé Professional, Britannia's food service) and exports complete the map.

Five reasons both numbers can be true

1. Share shift from small to large. NIQ's own commentary for the quarter separated "small and mid players", which saw a stronger slowdown, from "large and giant players", which were better positioned. In a downturn, large brands keep their shelf space, fund promotions, and benefit when kiranas cut the number of brands they stock to conserve working capital. Nestlé is the extreme case: it raised advertising and promotion spending by more than 50% in Q4 FY26 and 46% in Q2 FY27, the most aggressive volume push among the staples.

2. Urban over rural. The listed leaders sell disproportionately in cities; the regional challengers and unorganised brands depend more on the heartland. With urban volume at +2.2% and rural at −0.8% in January–March 2026 (and rural about −5% in the following quarter), a portfolio's urban weight explains a lot of its growth.

3. Channel mix. Modern trade volume grew 22.7% and e-commerce volume about 35%, while traditional trade shrank. Quick commerce is now more than 70% of online sales for Britannia and Tata Consumer. Brands built for those channels (premium chocolate, coffee, breakfast cereals, convenience foods, fresh bread) grew; brands that live in rural general trade did not.

4. A flattered base. GST 2.0 took effect on 22 September 2025, and distributors and kiranas ran down stock carrying the old, higher MRP through September and October before restocking. That depressed Q2 FY26 and Q3 FY26 sales and flatters the growth rates of Q2 and Q3 FY27. Nestlé's 20.5% Q2 FY27 growth is partly a base effect, though its Q1 FY27 growth of 25% came before the base turned easy.

5. Grams, not packs. After GST 2.0 many makers kept the ₹10 price and added grams. That lifts tonnage "volume" without one more pack being sold, which is why NIQ's volume (+0.9%) ran ahead of its units (−0.6%). Companies that report underlying volume in tonnes can show growth that NIQ would count as flat units. Part 06 works through the arithmetic.

In plain terms

The shops of India, taken together, sold about the same amount of packaged goods in early 2026 as a year before. The big brands sold much more. That means smaller and regional brands sold less: the big names won shelf space in cities, in supermarkets and on delivery apps while village shops cut back. Is the share shift durable?

Three arguments say the shift is structural. Distribution is being rebuilt around channels where scale wins: modern-trade chains negotiate centrally, and quick-commerce platforms stock a limited assortment in each dark store, which favours the top two or three brands per category. Advertising is moving to digital and retail media, where large budgets buy placement on the platform itself. And the costs of compliance, packaging and working capital have risen for small manufacturers, who also lost some of their price advantage when GST 2.0 lowered the tax gap between branded and loose goods in several categories.

Two arguments say part of it will fade. The base effect from the GST destocking ends after Q3 FY27, so reported growth rates will mechanically slow. And the leaders bought some of their growth: Nestlé's A&P step-up cannot rise at 46–50% a year indefinitely, and its EBITDA margin held at about 24% only because cheap cocoa and benign wheat offset the extra spending. When management flagged "rising food inflation" on 15 October 2026, the stock fell 7–9% intraday, which tells you the market is already asking this question.

The commodity see-saw: why margins move when cocoa, tea and milk do Deep dive

Every member converts farm commodities into packs whose prices move in slow, discrete steps. Most margin moves in 2024–26 trace back to the input basket, not to demand.

A packaged-food company has two price lists that never move in step. Its input prices change every day on commodity exchanges, tea auctions, mandis and dairy co-operative notices. Its own prices change a few times a year, in steps, because a ₹10 pack has to stay ₹10, retailers resist frequent changes, and every MRP change requires reprinting packs and clearing old stock. Gross margin is the gap between the two lists, and it widens or narrows with a lag of one to two quarters whenever commodities move.

The years 2024 to 2026 delivered some of the largest commodity swings in the industry's history. Cocoa futures rose to about US$12,900 a tonne in December 2024, several times their long-run level, after poor West African crops, and then collapsed to roughly $3,200 by late March 2026 (one later, undated report put cocoa back near $6,600 on Ghana's crop outlook; the current level is disputed). Arabica coffee went above $4 a pound in February 2025 before easing in 2026. Tea was expensive in 2024, cheap in 2025, and expensive again in 2026. Milk procurement prices rose steadily. And in March–April 2026 the West Asia war lifted crude above $109 a barrel, taking plastic packaging resin up 30–50% and freight with it.

In plain terms

A food company's profit margin is mostly decided by what it pays for ingredients and how quickly it can raise its own prices afterwards. The four levers, and which ones are low quality

When costs rise, managers have four levers. They can raise the price, which is easy on family packs and premium SKUs and almost impossible on ₹5 and ₹10 packs. They can cut grams, which keeps the price point but risks consumers noticing. They can shift the mix toward higher-margin products and bigger packs. Or they can cut advertising and promotion to protect the reported margin. The first three defend economics; the fourth defends a quarter. Watching which lever a company pulls is one of the best quality signals in this sub-sector.

GST 2.0 and the ₹10 pack: a tax cut that became extra grams Deep dive

On 22 September 2025 most packaged foods moved to a 5% GST rate. At a fixed ₹10 price the maker's realisation jumped about 12%, and most of it went back to consumers as heavier packs, not lower prices.

India's consumer goods are built around price points rather than prices. The ₹5 pack and the ₹10 pack are the currency of rural and lower-income urban India: shoppers pay with a single coin or note, retailers do not need change, and the maker competes on how much product it can put inside a fixed price. This "magic price point" architecture is the most important structural feature of mass packaged food, and it means that cost changes show up in grams rather than in rupees.

GST 2.0 was the largest tax change the industry had seen since GST itself in 2017. From 22 September 2025 the main slabs became 5% and 18%, with a 40% rate for sin goods. Biscuits, chocolates and cocoa products, instant coffee and malt-based drinks moved from 18% to 5%; namkeen, noodles, pasta, sauces, butter, ghee and cheese moved from 12% to 5% (sources conflict on whether namkeen was at 12% or 18% before; 12% is the more common reading); UHT milk and paneer went to nil. Carbonated and caffeinated beverages went the other way, from 28% to 40%.

The 2026 cost shock reversed the gift

Six months later the West Asia war reversed much of the benefit. Suppose palm oil rises 20% and packaging 30% on our ₹10 pack. Palm fat goes from ₹1.06 to about ₹1.27 and packaging from ₹0.65 to about ₹0.85: about ₹0.41 more per pack. Gross margin falls from 42% to about 37%, and EBITDA from 18% to about 13% if nothing is done. The industry's levers are well rehearsed: a grammage cut of 6–7% recovers roughly ₹0.30–0.35; price increases on family packs (Britannia raised a Good Day family pack by ₹10); mix toward ₹20 and ₹30 premium packs; and, if necessary, a temporary cut in A&P. The typical outcome is an EBITDA margin of 16–17%: Britannia reported 16.8% in Q1 FY27 and Bikaji 13.5% against 14.8% a year earlier.

The price-point model under strain

The deeper worry is not one quarter's margin but the price-point system itself. When grams are cut repeatedly, a ₹5 pack eventually becomes too small to satisfy, and the maker must either accept lower margins or move the entry price to ₹7, ₹10 or ₹15. Each move risks losing the shoppers who could only afford the lower point. NIQ reported in 2026 that companies are "rationalizing assortments, adjusting pack sizes" and that entry-price SKUs are losing relevance. For Britannia, where ₹5 and ₹10 packs carry 80% of rural biscuit volume across the category, and for Bikaji, where ₹5 packs alone carry 75% of rural salty-snack volume, this is the central risk to volume over the next two years.

There is a policy subtlety too. Because GST 2.0 was presented as relief for households, makers faced pressure to show that the benefit reached consumers; adding grams was a visible way to do so without breaking price points. That makes it harder, politically and commercially, to take the grams back out quickly when costs rise. The categories where GST did not change, spices, tea and salt, are the cleanest control group: Orkla India, with no GST gift and chilli up 78%, grew volume only 1.7%, while Tata Consumer's salt revenue grew 7%.

In plain terms

The ₹5 and ₹10 pack is how most Indians buy biscuits and snacks. It survives cost increases by quietly shrinking, but it can only shrink so far. The GST cut let makers add grams; the 2026 oil and packaging shock pushed them to take grams out again. If entry packs get too small or too expensive, the poorest shoppers buy less, and that is the volume risk for the mass-market makers.

Taken together, the verdict on GST 2.0 is clear enough to state plainly. It gave the large brands a pricing and grammage lever and a restocking bounce in the quarters after September 2025, and it helped listed leaders gain share as kiranas restocked the brands they trusted. It did not durably revive rural mass volume, which fell in the first two quarters of 2026, and the West Asia cost shock neutralised much of the per-pack gain within six months.

Quick commerce rewires the pipe: the ten-minute shelf

E-commerce is already 19% of FMCG in India's eight largest metros, and quick commerce is most of it. It favours premium, urban, impulse brands and squeezes the distributor.

For forty years, packaged-food distribution in India was a ladder: company to carrying-and-forwarding agent, to super-stockist, to distributor, to several million kiranas, with a sales force walking beats to take orders. Modern trade (supermarket chains such as DMart and Reliance) added a second ladder in the 2000s. Quick commerce, the ten-minute delivery apps run by Blinkit, Zepto, Swiggy Instamart, Flipkart Minutes and BigBasket Now, has added a third since 2022, and in the big cities it is growing fast enough to take share from both.

NIQ's data for the top eight metros show the speed. Traditional trade's share of FMCG value there fell from 61.8% to 57.2% in four quarters to January–March 2026; e-commerce rose from 16.3% to 19.0%, gaining close to a percentage point a quarter; and in North and East metros e-commerce has overtaken modern trade. Across all 52 metros e-commerce is 15.8%, and across urban India about 7%. NIQ describes the gains as "largely substituting traditional trade", and says quick commerce drives most of e-commerce's growth.

Why the ten-minute shelf favours some brands

A quick-commerce dark store stocks a few thousand items in a small warehouse a couple of kilometres from its customers. Shelf space is scarce, so each category carries the top few brands and a handful of premium or new items. Orders are small, frequent and urban, and the typical basket leans toward breakfast, snacks, beverages, dairy and impulse treats. Payment is digital, so consumers pay less attention to price points; a ₹30 or ₹50 pack is as easy to buy as a ₹10 one. All of this favours brands that are already large, premium or convenience-led: Nestlé's coffee and chocolate, Britannia's cakes and croissants, Tata Consumer's Sampann, Ching's and Organic India, MTR breakfast mixes, and English Oven bread.

Brands built for rural general trade gain less. Bikaji's core is bhujia in Rajasthan, Assam and Bihar sold through kiranas; Heritage sells fresh milk through its own routes and parlours. Both are less exposed to quick-commerce economics, which cuts both ways: less margin dilution, but less participation in the fastest-growing channel. Bikaji says e-commerce and quick commerce are its fastest-growing channels, but from a small base.

The cost of the new shelf

Quick commerce is not a free lunch. Platforms earn commissions, charge for advertising and placement, and levy fees; trade estimates of the all-in take run at 15–25% or more of the selling price, which is INDICATIVE because no company discloses it. That compares with roughly 12–20% of MRP kept by the general-trade chain. Platforms also run deep discounts that can undermine a brand's printed price in the consumer's mind, and they are building their own private labels in staples and snacks. On the other side of the ledger, inventory sits with the platform for days rather than weeks, there are no credit losses, and the brand gets data on what sells in which neighbourhood.

Imports and China

Unlike electronics, appliances or chemicals, Indian packaged food faces almost no competition from imported finished goods. Food is bulky, perishable, taste-specific and priced at ₹5 and ₹10, which makes importing uneconomic. China is not a material competitor in finished packaged food; Korean and Chinese-style noodles are a flavour trend that Indian makers sell themselves. The import exposure is in inputs: about 60% of India's edible oil is imported, cocoa and much specialty coffee come from abroad, packaging resins track global petrochemical prices, and some dairy ingredients are imported. That makes the rupee and global commodity prices a cost issue, not a competition issue.

In plain terms

In most categories one or two brands dominate the branded shelf, but a large share of what Indians eat is still sold loose or by local makers. Some of the strongest competitors are not on the stock market at all: Parle in biscuits, Haldiram's in snacks, Mondelez in chocolate and Amul in dairy. Foreign competition comes through the ingredients India imports, not through imported packs.

The structure explains why acquisitions are the preferred route to new categories. Building a national brand from scratch takes a decade of advertising and distribution spending; buying one that is regionally strong and plugging it into an existing network is faster. Tata Consumer bought Capital Foods (Ching's, Smith & Jones) for ₹5,100 crore and Organic India for ₹1,900 crore in 2024. Orkla added Eastern Condiments in Kerala to MTR in Karnataka. The open question in each case is whether the acquirer's distribution actually accelerates the brand, and Part 11 looks at what these purchases have done to returns on capital.

Why the models differ

Negative working capital. Archetypes A and B are financed by their trade. Distributors pay within days, inventory turns quickly, and suppliers are paid in 60–130 days. Nestlé's working capital is about −24 days of sales and Britannia's about −28 days: the business generates cash as it grows rather than consuming it. Proximity to demand. Bread has a shelf life of days, and namkeen and biscuits are bulky and low in value per kilogram. Those plants raise capital intensity and lower returns compared with a business that can serve the country from a few large factories.

Brand versus commodity content. In a jar of Nescafé the coffee is a modest share of the price; in a litre of milk the milk is two-thirds of it. The higher the brand content, the higher the gross margin and the more of a commodity shock the company can absorb before the EBITDA margin moves.

Built versus bought. Nestlé and Britannia grew their brands internally over decades, so their balance sheets carry little goodwill and their returns on capital are very high. Tata Consumer and Zydus bought large parts of their portfolios, so their capital employed includes the premium paid for the brands. Food safety: the risk that can empty a shelf overnight

The defining regulatory event in Indian packaged food remains the June 2015 Maggi ban. FSSAI ordered the noodles off shelves over lead and MSG labelling; Maggi was unavailable for about five months until the Bombay High Court quashed the ban, and it relaunched in November 2015. Nestlé India recovered its share within about a year, which is a testament to the brand, but the episode showed that a single regulatory order can wipe out a third of a company's revenue for a season. In April 2024 a Public Eye report on added sugar in Cerelac sold in India triggered FSSAI scrutiny, and the Commerce Ministry asked e-commerce platforms to stop listing drinks such as Bournvita under "health drinks". Proposed front-of-pack warnings on foods high in fat, sugar and salt are the next item on the agenda; if notified, they would affect chocolate, biscuits, noodles and malt drinks.

Labelling law and the grammage lever

Standard pack sizes were deregulated in 2017, which is what allows companies to change grams freely while keeping the price point. Since 2022 makers have had to display the unit sale price (price per gram or per kilogram) on packs, which makes shrinkflation more visible to careful shoppers. The combination explains the industry's behaviour: grammage is the main lever, and it is legal and routine, but it is no longer invisible.

The Nestlé royalty: the one cost minorities can vote on

Nestlé India pays its Swiss parent a general licence fee, the royalty, of 4.5% of net sales for the use of brands, technology and know-how. A century in seven shocks: how the industry got its shape

Brands founded before independence, MNCs that came for milk, a wave of IPOs and acquisitions, and a run of commodity, tax and safety shocks that each reset margins.

The oldest members of this group predate modern India. Britannia began baking biscuits in Kolkata in 1892. Mavalli Tiffin Rooms opened in Bengaluru in 1924 and began selling packaged mixes in the 1970s, when Emergency-era price controls made running a restaurant difficult. Nestlé built its first Indian factory at Moga in Punjab in 1961, choosing a milk district because its first products were dairy-based. Maggi noodles and Tata Salt, India's first branded iodised salt, both launched in 1983.

The 1990s and 2000s were the branding decades. Nestlé built Nescafé and KitKat into national brands. The Wadia group gained control of Britannia in 1993 and bought out Danone's stake in 2009 after a long dispute. Heritage Foods was founded in 1992 by N. Chandrababu Naidu, Bikaji launched in Bikaner in 1993 as an offshoot of the Haldiram family tree, and Mrs Bectors became McDonald's bun supplier in India in 1996. Tata Tea bought Tetley in the UK in 2000 and Orkla ASA bought MTR in 2007. The 2010s and early 2020s then brought a listing and acquisition wave: Zydus bought Heinz India's brands in 2019, Tata Consumer Products was formed in 2020, Orkla took control of Eastern Condiments in 2020–21, Mrs Bectors listed in December 2020 and Bikaji in November 2022.

The seven shocks

  • 2015, the Maggi ban. Five months off shelves showed how fast a food-safety order can erase a category leader's sales, and how fast a strong brand can recover them.
  • 2017, GST. A single national tax replaced a tangle of state levies, formalising distribution and favouring organised brands over small manufacturers.
  • 2020, the pandemic. Pantry-loading lifted demand for staples, noodles and biscuits; distribution disruptions favoured companies with direct reach.
  • 2022, the Ukraine inflation shock. Wheat, palm oil and milk spiked; makers cut grams and gross margins hit their trough.
  • 2024–25, commodity shock 2.0. Cocoa peaked near US$12,900 a tonne in December 2024; coffee and tea spiked; urban demand slowed while rural led.
  • September 2025, GST 2.0. Most packaged foods to 5%; trade destocking in September–October; grammage added at the ₹10 price point.
  • 2026, the West Asia war. Crude above $109, packaging resin up 30–50%, freight up; rural volume turned negative; staples de-rated.

In plain terms

Indian packaged food has been through a shock every two or three years: a safety ban, two tax overhauls, a pandemic, and three bouts of commodity inflation. Each time the big brands lost some margin for a few quarters and then recovered it, usually by shrinking packs and nudging up prices. The pattern is so regular that the useful question in any shock is not whether margins recover but how long it takes and who loses share along the way.

Two lessons from this history carry into the analysis that follows. First, margins in this industry mean-revert around an archetype-specific level: Britannia's gross margin has oscillated within a few points for years, and Nestlé's has stayed in the mid-50s. A forecast that assumes a margin far outside the historical band needs a specific reason. Second, the shocks favour scale. After the Maggi ban, the 2017 GST, the pandemic and GST 2.0, organised brands with direct distribution came out with more share than they went in with, because small competitors lacked the working capital, compliance resources or shelf relationships to ride out the disruption.

A worked example with round numbers makes the leverage visible. Suppose a company's products reach 1 million outlets directly, and each outlet buys ₹1,500 a month of its goods at company realisation: ₹18,000 a year, or ₹1,800 crore of revenue. At a 42% gross margin it earns ₹756 crore of gross profit. It spends 6% of revenue (₹108 crore) on A&P and 18% (₹324 crore) on people, freight, plants and overheads, leaving ₹324 crore of EBITDA, an 18% margin. Now add 10% more outlets at the same throughput. Revenue rises by ₹180 crore. Gross profit rises by ₹76 crore; freight on the extra volume costs about 6% (₹11 crore); advertising and most overheads do not change. EBITDA rises by about ₹65 crore, an incremental margin of 36%, twice the average. That operating leverage is why distribution expansion is the most valuable growth lever in this industry, and why a loss of throughput hurts so much. These figures are ILLUSTRATIVE.

In plain terms

A food company's sales are the number of shops that carry its products, times how much each shop sells, times the price. Its profit is what is left after ingredients, advertising and running costs. Because the advertising and factories are largely fixed, each extra shop or extra pack sold adds far more to profit than the average pack does; each lost one costs far more too.

1. Underlying volume growth

Worked example: a company selling 100,000 tonnes last year and 107,700 tonnes this year has volume growth of 7.7%, which is Bikaji's Q1 FY27 figure. What drives it: new outlets, rural incomes, the health of price points, launches, A&P, and competitors' pricing. Benchmarks: 8% or more is good for a branded food company, 3–8% average, below 3% weak; NIQ's whole market was 0.9% in January–March 2026. What it is. The share of sales made through online channels, and within that through quick commerce. Formula: online sales ÷ total sales. The definition trap. "Quick commerce is 70% of online" is a share of online, not of total sales. Companies also differ on whether sales to a platform's wholesale arm count as e-commerce or modern trade.

How to judge it. A rising online share is only good news if the economics hold. Pair the share with three other numbers each year: gross margin (does it fall as online grows, because platforms demand deeper trade terms?), A&P ratio (does platform advertising push it up without matching growth?), and receivable days (platforms and modern-trade chains pay more slowly than distributors). A company growing online at 30–40% with all three stable is building a durable channel. One whose gross margin slips a point for every two points of online share is paying the platforms for its growth, and that cost will compound as the channel scales.

10. Royalty as a % of sales

What it is. The licence fee paid to a parent for brands and technology. Formula: royalty ÷ net sales. Worked example: Nestlé India's 4.5% on FY26 net sales came to ₹1,024.5 crore; at 5.25% it would have been about ₹174 crore more. Benchmarks: 3% or less is light; 3–5% typical for MNC subsidiaries; above 5% requires a minority vote under SEBI rules. How they compare: Nestlé 4.5%; the other seven pay no comparable parent royalty (Orkla India's arrangements with Orkla ASA are not verified). The definition trap: royalty is only one channel of value to a parent; central-service charges, import pricing of ingredients and dividends are others. Compare total related-party payments, not just the royalty line.

11. Milk spread and value-added share (dairy)

What it is. For a dairy, the gap between the average selling price of milk and the landed cost of procuring it, and the share of revenue from value-added products (curd, paneer, lassi, ice cream). Formula: spread = selling ₹/litre − landed procurement ₹/litre; VAP share = VAP revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Heritage sold milk at ₹60.34 a litre in Q2 FY27 and paid ₹47.73 landed, a spread of ₹12.61 (20.9%). A ₹1 a litre rise in procurement cost not passed on, on about 17.3 lakh litres a day for 92 days, costs about ₹16 crore a quarter, roughly a fifth of Q2 FY27 EBITDA of ₹74 crore. Benchmarks: a spread of ₹14 or more a litre is good, ₹11–14 average, below ₹11 weak; a VAP share of 35–40% or more lifts blended margins materially. How Heritage compares: spread ₹12.6 (about ₹13.9 a year earlier, derived from the reported +11% cost and +6% price changes); VAP 35.4% of revenue excluding ghee and butter (31.3% a year earlier).

13. Growth-business share

What it is. The share of revenue from the company's newer, faster-growing categories, as opposed to its mature core. Formula: revenue from designated growth categories ÷ total (or domestic) revenue. Worked example: Tata Consumer's growth businesses (Sampann, Capital Foods, Organic India, ready-to-drink, Soulfull and others) grew 47% in Q1 FY27 and reached 36% of India revenue, up from about 31% in FY26; if they keep growing at 40% while the core grows at 5%, they would reach about half of India revenue within about two years. Benchmarks: rising share with stable gross margin is good. How they compare: Tata Consumer 36%; Britannia aims for a 50:50 split between biscuits and adjacencies (currently about 70:30); Bikaji's western snacks grew 21.3% in Q1 FY27; Heritage's VAP 35.4%. The trap: each company chooses which categories count as "growth", and the list can change.

In plain terms

Nearly every metric in this industry can be flattered by a definition: volume in tonnes rather than packs, margins excluding one-offs, reach counted as shops ever served, growth businesses chosen after the fact. The habit that protects you is to compare each company with its own past on the same definition, and to cross-check the story against the cash-flow statement.

14. Cash conversion and payout

What it is. How much of the operating profit actually arrives as cash, and how much of the profit is returned to shareholders. Formulas: cash conversion = cash flow from operations (after tax and working-capital changes) ÷ EBITDA; payout = dividends ÷ PAT. Worked example: Tata Consumer generated ₹2,422 crore of operating cash flow in FY26 on EBITDA of ₹2,792 crore, a conversion of about 87%; after paying about 25% tax, anything above 75% means working capital released cash or at least did not absorb it. Drivers: tax, working-capital swings, seasonal stock-builds, and acquisitions consolidated part-way through a year. Benchmarks: conversion of 80% or more is good for a branded food company, 60–80% average, below 60% weak; payouts above 60% are normal for high-ROCE leaders that cannot reinvest all their cash.

Definition traps, collected

  • Tonnes versus packs. After GST 2.0, tonnage "volume" rose with heavier packs; NIQ's volume was +0.9% while units were −0.6% in January–March 2026.
  • The distorted base. Q2 and Q3 FY26 were depressed by GST destocking, so Q2 and Q3 FY27 growth is flattered.
  • Nestlé's calendar. Nestlé India's "FY24" was a 15-month transition year (January 2023 to March 2024, revenue ₹24,394 crore). Compare FY25 (₹20,202 crore) with FY26 (₹23,155 crore), never FY24 with a 12-month year.
  • Zydus's two businesses. FY26 includes about seven months of Comfort Click; reported revenue growth of 46% is mostly acquisition, and reported PAT is depressed by acquisition interest and amortisation (Q1 FY27 reported ₹119 crore vs about ₹168 crore adjusted).
  • Standalone versus consolidated. Nestlé's Q2 FY27 PAT was ₹1,026.8 crore standalone and about ₹1,010 crore consolidated; its FY26 PAT appears as ₹3,545 crore in some sources (standalone) and ₹3,499 crore consolidated (used here).
  • Net cash. Data-service "net cash" (investments minus borrowings) misses cash and bank balances: it shows Tata Consumer with net debt of ₹1,377 crore when the company reports net cash of about ₹2,978 crore. Use company figures where available.
  • Gross margin classification. Packaging, traded goods and contract manufacturing are classified differently across companies.
  • Reach. Total versus direct outlets versus villages; billed ever versus billed monthly.
  • Online share. Quick commerce "70% of online" is not 70% of sales.
  • Growth businesses. Company-chosen baskets that can be redefined.
  • Price/mix. A residual that silently includes grammage, acquisitions and currency.
  • Seasonality. Compare Zydus and Bikaji year on year, never sequentially.

How to read a packaged-food company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, and its base. Pull reported revenue growth and check the year-ago quarter: was it depressed (GST destocking in Q2–Q3 FY26), inflated by pantry-loading, or boosted by an acquisition (Zydus) or currency (Tata Consumer's international arm)? Strip those out first.
  2. Volume versus price/mix. Find the underlying volume figure and ask whether it is tonnes or packs. Subtract it from revenue growth to get price/mix. Volume below 3% with price/mix above 8% is an inflation-led quarter that may not last.
  3. Gross margin, against the commodity basket. Compare gross margin with a year ago and the previous quarter, and match the move to the company's main inputs (milk, wheat, palm, cocoa, coffee, tea, chilli, packaging). A gross margin rising on falling commodities is a windfall, not a skill.
  4. A&P growth against sales growth. If A&P fell while gross margin also fell, the EBITDA margin was protected by under-investment. If A&P rose faster than sales (Nestlé's +46%), the company is buying volume, which is fine if volume responds.
  5. The EBITDA bridge. Reconcile EBITDA margin change to gross margin, A&P, staff, freight and other costs. Freight and power jumped in 2026 with diesel and crude; check whether management quantified it.
  6. Category and segment growth. Look at the growth engines: Tata Consumer's growth businesses, Bikaji's western snacks, Heritage's value-added products, Orkla's convenience foods, Britannia's adjacencies. Are they growing faster than the core and at similar gross margins?
  7. Channel and geography commentary. Note e-commerce and quick-commerce growth, modern trade, and any comment on rural versus urban. A company that says "rural remained subdued" while growing volume is taking share; one that blames rural for falling volume is losing it.
  8. Below the line. Check other income (treasury gains can flatter PAT), exceptional items (Labour Code provisions, impairments, contingent-consideration gains), and acquisition amortisation and interest.
  9. Working capital and cash flow. At half-year and full-year, check inventory and receivable days and operating cash flow. Rising inventory before a season is normal for Zydus and Bikaji; rising receivables at a company that used to have negative working capital is a red flag, often a sign of channel stuffing or of more sales through slower-paying modern trade.
  10. Pricing actions and outlook. Record any price increases, grammage changes or new price points announced, and management's view of next quarter's input costs. Red flags: guidance for margin expansion while the commodity basket is rising; volume declines explained only by "weather" for several quarters running; a sudden cut in A&P; a new CEO resetting the base (as at Britannia from December 2025).
In plain terms

Ten minutes with a results release is enough if you ask the questions in order: was last year's quarter normal; did the company sell more or just charge more; did ingredients get cheaper or dearer; did it spend more or less on advertising; and is the cash coming in as profits say? Most surprises in this sub-sector are visible in one of those five answers.

Why staples multiples swing so far

The reason a modest change in sentiment produces a large change in price is mathematical. A simple dividend-growth model values a company at a P/E of roughly payout × (1 + g) ÷ (cost of equity − g), where g is the long-run growth rate. For a company like Nestlé, paying out 66% of profit, with an 11% cost of equity, the result is extraordinarily sensitive to the growth assumption when growth is close to the cost of equity. At 10% long-run growth the formula gives about 73 times earnings; at 9.5%, about 48 times; at 9%, about 36 times; at 8%, about 24 times. A one-point cut in the growth investors believe in can halve the justified multiple.

Global packaged-food giants grow slowly at modest multiples. India's listed food companies earn higher returns and trade at two and a half to four times the multiple.

Packaged food is a mature, slow-growing industry in developed markets. Nestlé S.A., Mondelez, PepsiCo, Orkla ASA and Grupo Bimbo grow revenue in low-to-mid single digits, earn operating margins in the low-to-high teens, and trade at roughly 12 to 20 times earnings. Technology and disruption: apps, private labels, labels and the GLP-1 question

The threats to incumbents come less from new technology in the factory than from new gatekeepers on the shelf and new rules on the pack.

Making a biscuit or a packet of bhujia has not changed much in decades; making it efficiently has, through automation, better forecasting and digital distribution tools. The real disruption risks in Indian packaged food are about who controls access to the consumer and what the consumer is told about the product. Seven matter.

  • Quick-commerce gatekeepers. Platforms that control a narrow, data-driven shelf can demand higher fees, sell placement to the highest bidder and launch their own brands. Part 07 covers the economics; the strategic risk is that the platform, not the brand, owns the relationship with the urban consumer.
  • Private labels in modern trade. DMart, Reliance (which relaunched Campa and runs aggressive price-led staples and snack brands) and the platforms are building own-label ranges in staples, biscuits and snacks. Private labels have taken large shares in Europe; in India they are still small but growing fastest in exactly the urban channels that are growing fastest.
  • New-age and D2C brands. Protein bars, millet snacks, clean-label products and premium coffee have attracted dozens of venture-backed brands. They fragment the premium niches incumbents want to grow into; incumbents respond by buying them, as ITC did with Yoga Bar, Tata Consumer with Organic India and Zydus with Comfort Click.
  • Health regulation and labelling. Front-of-pack warnings on foods high in fat, sugar and salt, sugar limits in infant foods after the 2024 Cerelac controversy, and the reclassification of "health drinks" could hit chocolate, biscuits, noodles and malt drinks. Reformulation costs money and can change taste.
  • GLP-1 drugs and diet trends. Appetite-suppressing weight-loss drugs have begun to reduce snack consumption among users in the US. In India this is a long-dated risk, limited by cost and access, but it is part of a broader shift toward protein and away from sugar that favours some portfolios (Zydus's Sugar Free, protein lines) over others.
  • Climate and commodity volatility. Cocoa, coffee and tea have become structurally more volatile as weather extremes hit concentrated growing regions; hotter, wetter or cooler summers shift demand for seasonal brands, as Zydus found in Q1 FY27.
  • Digital distribution. Distributor management systems, retailer ordering apps and AI-driven beat planning raise the productivity of direct reach. This is an advantage for scale players who can afford the systems and the data, and a reason the gap between large and small brands may widen.

The danger to big food brands is not a better biscuit-making machine. It is that delivery apps and supermarket chains decide which brands customers see and start selling their own, that new labelling rules make sugary snacks look less appealing, and that weather makes key ingredients more expensive and less predictable. The big companies have the money to adapt, by buying new brands and investing in digital sales; the smaller ones have less room.

On balance, technology and channel change favour scale. The companies with the largest A&P budgets, the deepest data and the broadest portfolios can negotiate with platforms, reformulate products and buy promising challengers. The risk for investors is less that the incumbents lose than that they keep winning at lower margins, as more of each rupee of sales goes to platforms, advertising and compliance.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian packaged food

The long-term case is intact; the near-term case depends on rural demand, commodity costs and whether the leaders' share gains survive the end of an easy base.

The two cases below are for the sub-sector as a whole. Individual companies sit at very different points: Nestlé's debate is about valuation and the durability of its growth spurt, Heritage's about the milk spread, Bikaji's about rural volume and margins, Tata Consumer's about returns on acquisitions.

The bull case

  • A long runway: much of India's snacks, spices, staples and dairy is still sold loose; branded penetration and per-capita spending remain a fraction of other large emerging markets.
  • Leaders are winning share: Nestlé's volume-led 20–25% growth and Tata Consumer's 13% India volume show that scale, distribution and modern channels are pulling share from small and regional players.
  • Exceptional economics: negative working capital, 56–85% ROCE at Britannia and Nestlé, and high payouts mean growth is self-funded.
  • Commodity relief can return: cocoa has collapsed from its peak; if milk, tea and oil normalise and crude falls after the West Asia shock, gross margins recover with the usual lag.
  • Valuations have reset: Britannia at a 52-week low and 44×, Orkla India at 25×, Bikaji down about 35% in a year; the group ex-Nestlé is at about 50× FY26 profit against much higher levels in recent years.
  • Private-market support: Haldiram's was valued at roughly seven times Bikaji's market cap; a listing would anchor sector valuations.

The bear case

  • The market is not growing: NIQ FMCG volume was +0.9% in January–March 2026 and rural volume about −5% in April–June; the leaders' growth is share gain, promotion and base effect.
  • Margins face a squeeze: milk +11% at Heritage, tea +9.9%, chilli +78%, packaging +30–50%, crude above $109; FY26's cheap-input windfall is reversing.
  • The price-point model is breaking: repeated grammage cuts and ₹15/₹20 packs risk losing the poorest shoppers, who buy 80–90% of rural biscuits and snacks in ₹5/₹10 packs.
  • Channel costs are rising: quick commerce takes an estimated 15–25%+ and is building private labels; distributors are under strain.
  • Still expensive: about 58× FY26 profit for the group and 37× EBITDA; global peers trade at 12–20× earnings.
  • Company overhangs: Nestlé's royalty and Milk & Nutrition volume decline; Britannia's CEO transition; TCPL's 9% ROCE; Zydus's leverage; Orkla ASA at the 75% ceiling; Heritage's spread.

Weighing the two, the structural case is stronger than the cyclical one. Nothing in 2025–26 changed the facts that Indians buy much of their food loose, that branded penetration rises with incomes, and that the leaders' economics are exceptional. What changed is the near-term path: a rural slowdown, an input-cost cycle turning against the industry, a channel shift that raises distribution costs, and an easy base that will soon become a hard one. Investors who own these stocks for the long run are being offered somewhat better prices than in recent years; investors who need near-term earnings upgrades face a difficult few quarters.

In plain terms

The long-term reasons to own Indian food companies have not changed: a huge, still partly unbranded market and businesses that make high returns without needing much money. The short-term problems are real: villages buying less, ingredients and packaging costing more, and delivery apps taking a bigger cut. Share prices have already fallen to reflect some of this, but not to levels that would be called cheap by global standards.
