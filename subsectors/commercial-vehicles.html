00

The one-page view: the trucks are selling faster than the trucking pays

India sold more commercial vehicles in FY26 than ever before and is selling them faster still. Underneath, the people who buy trucks and the companies that build them are both earning less per vehicle.

A commercial vehicle (CV) is a capital good bought on credit by someone who has to earn its price back from freight. That single fact explains most of this sub-sector. Demand does not move with GDP; it moves with the fleet owner's confidence that the truck will cover its monthly instalment, and with a lender's willingness to fund roughly nine of every ten purchases. When freight is plentiful, diesel is stable and credit is easy, operators buy ahead and volumes rise far faster than the economy. When any one of those three turns, they stop buying almost overnight.

In October 2026 the industry is at a visible peak of activity. Domestic wholesales in FY26 were 10,79,871 units, up 12.6%, the first year to beat the FY19 high of roughly 10.07 lakh. Retail registrations tracked by the dealers' federation FADA were 10,60,906 (+11.7%). The current year is stronger again: SIAM's figures for July–September 2026 show 3,09,063 units (+28.7%), with medium and heavy vehicles (M&HCV) up 37% and exports up 72.5%. The first half of FY27 is up 23.8% at 5,74,255. Much of this is the cut in GST on trucks and buses from 28% to 18% on 22 September 2025, which lowered on-road prices by about 7.8%, plus infrastructure and mining demand, an ageing fleet, the Delhi-NCR fleet-renewal scheme PARIVARTAN and a weak rupee that makes Indian trucks cheap abroad.

The signals underneath point the other way. Diesel rose ₹7.50 a litre in four steps from 15 May 2026, about 8%. Freight rates did respond, rising about 7% on average between February and May according to ICRA, so the pass-through was real but partial, and CRISIL's utilisation index for trucks slipped below its year-earlier level. Fuel is 45–55% of a fleet operator's revenue, and 75–80% of the sector is small operators with thin buffers. The transporters' body AIMTC says 20–25% of India's trucks are idle, a figure from a single, interested source that is best treated as indicative. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, the first hike of this cycle. ICRA, reaffirming its view on 31 August, expects FY27 domestic CV growth of only 4–6% and M&HCV trucks of 1–3%, which means it expects the second half to shrink year on year once the post-GST surge enters the base.

The makers are feeling the squeeze before the buyers stop. Commodity costs, steel above all, rose through 2026. Ashok Leyland's standalone EBITDA margin was 13.0% in FY26 and 10.1% in Q1 FY27, even with the help of cheaper opening inventory; management expects Q2 to carry the peak of the commodity hit. Its EBITDA per vehicle, an indicative figure we derive from reported numbers, fell from about ₹2.60 lakh to about ₹1.99 lakh, partly seasonally. Mahindra's auto segment earned a 7.7% PBIT margin in Q1 FY27 against 9.3% in Q4 FY26. SML Mahindra, the only member to have reported the September quarter, earned an operating margin of 4.3% against 7.6% a year earlier, and its profit fell 44%.

The market itself is a Tata-led oligopoly. On FADA's September 2026 retail count, Tata Motors held 34.7%, Mahindra & Mahindra 26.7% (almost all of it pickups and small CVs), Ashok Leyland 18.6% and Volvo Eicher (VECV) 8.0%; nobody else reached 5%. Tata Motors' CV business has been a separately listed company since its demerger on 1 October 2025, and is bidding €3.91 billion for Italy's Iveco in a tender that closes on 26 October. It is not a member of this sub-sector list, so it appears throughout as a clearly marked reference rather than inside any combined figure.

The five members are an uneven group, and how they are added up decides what the numbers say. Mahindra & Mahindra is 74% of their combined revenue and market value, but its profit comes mostly from SUVs, tractors and Mahindra Finance; its CV slice is an indicative ₹30,000–37,000 crore, 15–19% of its revenue. The financial story in this report therefore leads with the other four: Ashok Leyland, India's number-two truck maker and number-one bus maker; Force Motors, which owns the 10–26 seat van market and assembles engines for Mercedes-Benz and BMW; Olectra Greentech, an electric-bus maker whose customers are mostly companies of its own promoter group; and SML Mahindra, a small bus and light-truck maker that Mahindra bought in 2025 and is turning into its vehicle for trucks above 3.5 tonnes.

Five forces shape everything that follows, and each has its own section. First, the cycle: record volumes with late-cycle signals, set against a forecast of falling second-half sales. Second, the trucker's P&L, the best leading indicator of truck demand, now squeezed by diesel, weaker utilisation and the first rate hike of the cycle. Third, margins falling while volumes rise, the tell-tale of commodity inflation outrunning price increases. Fourth, the structure: one leader, a strong number two in heavy trucks, a dominant pickup maker and a ring of specialists. Fifth, electric buses, where the winners of government tenders have been the companies that can finance and operate a twelve-year contract, not the companies that build the most diesel buses.

In plain terms

Truck makers sell to small businesses that live on the gap between freight income and the cost of diesel, drivers and loan instalments. In 2026 that gap narrowed just as sales hit a record. History says sales follow the gap with a lag, so the more useful question for an investor is not how many trucks were sold last month but how much a typical truck is earning its owner this month.

Valuations already reflect some of this. Since our company primers were written in June–August 2026, the members' market values have fallen 1–33%: Olectra by about a third, Force by about 11%, Mahindra and Ashok Leyland by about 7%. The four members excluding M&M trade at about 23.6 times FY26 profit, against roughly 6–11% operating margins and single-digit to mid-teens price-earnings multiples at the large European and American truck makers, whose own cycle turned down in 2025. The Indian premium rests on the view that this market is younger, less penetrated and better at pricing than the developed ones. The next six months, in which the post-GST base and the operator squeeze arrive together, will test that view.

01

From steel to freight: how a truck turns into someone's income

A CV maker sells a bare chassis or a finished vehicle to a dealer, a lender funds the buyer, a body builder finishes it, and an owner-driver spends ten to fifteen years earning it back. Profit pools sit at every step, and the maker controls only some of them.

The commercial-vehicle market is a ladder sorted by gross vehicle weight (GVW), the maximum laden weight a vehicle is certified to carry. At the bottom are small CVs and pickups under 3.5 tonnes, the Tata Ace, Mahindra's Jeeto, Supro and Bolero Pik-Up, Maruti's Super Carry and Ashok Leyland's Dost, which carry vegetables, parcels, gas cylinders and building material over short urban and rural routes. Above them sit light trucks of 3.5–7.5 tonnes, intermediate and medium trucks of 7.5–16 tonnes, and heavy trucks above 16 tonnes: multi-axle rigid haulers, tippers for mines and construction sites, and tractor-trailers for long-distance freight. Buses run in parallel, from 10–26 seat vans such as Force's Traveller to 12-metre city and intercity buses.

Three definitions matter immediately. SIAM, the manufacturers' society, splits the market only two ways: LCV (light, up to 7.5 tonnes, including small passenger carriers) and M&HCV (above 7.5 tonnes). Companies use their own splits: Mahindra reports "LCV under 3.5 tonnes", Tata reports heavy CVs, intermediate and light-medium CVs (ILMCV, about 5–16 tonnes) and small CVs and pickups, and Ashok Leyland's "LCV" means its Dost and Bada Dost range of about 1.25–7.5 tonnes. A market share is only meaningful inside one definition, which is why this report always says whose split it is using.

Both figures are indicative, built from company margins and volumes rather than disclosed by anyone, but the order of magnitude explains why the M&HCV share of a maker's mix, not its unit count, decides its profit. It also explains why Mahindra can sell more CVs than Ashok Leyland and earn far less from them.

Three other profit pools sit beside the vehicle. Spares and service earn roughly twice the vehicle margin and follow the size of the parc rather than this year's sales: Ashok Leyland's spare-parts revenue was ₹4,450 crore in FY26, about 10% of its standalone revenue. Captive finance keeps sales going in downturns and earns a lending spread, but it loads a maker's consolidated balance sheet with borrowings: Hinduja Leyland Finance (HLF) has assets under management of ₹60,310 crore, which is why Ashok Leyland's consolidated debt is ₹63,936 crore even though its vehicle business holds net cash. Engines, aggregates and defence add precision-manufacturing income: Force Motors' engines and aggregates for Mercedes-Benz and BMW were about 30% of its FY26 turnover. Steel buyers and sellers will find the upstream side of this chain in our iron & steel and steel products reports, and the component suppliers in auto components.

In plain terms

A truck maker earns most of its money on the heaviest trucks and on the spare parts those trucks need for the next decade. Small pickups sell in large numbers but leave little profit each. So the same headline growth in units can mean very different profit growth depending on which end of the ladder is growing.

The electric-bus business sits awkwardly in this chain. State transport undertakings rarely buy e-buses outright. They sign gross-cost contracts (GCC) under which an operator owns and runs the bus for 10–12 years and is paid per kilometre. The OEM sells the bus to that operator, so the twelve-year capital and the state's credit risk sit with whoever wins the contract. For Olectra, that operator is almost always Evey Trans, a company in its own promoter group, which is why Section 11 treats e-buses as a question of who funds them rather than who builds them.

02

10.8 lakh trucks and buses: the size of the market and the shape of its record

FY26 beat a seven-year-old peak, and the first half of FY27 is running a quarter higher again. The arithmetic of the second half decides whether FY27 is a new top or the start of a decline.

India's domestic CV market sold 10,79,871 vehicles in FY26 on SIAM's wholesale count, which measures dispatches from factories to dealers. The split was 4,22,998 M&HCVs (3,55,849 trucks and 67,149 buses) and 6,56,873 LCVs (5,95,276 goods carriers and 61,597 small passenger carriers). Trucks drove the growth: M&HCV trucks rose 15.7% and LCV goods carriers 12.5%, while M&HCV buses were flat (−0.2%) after a record FY25. At an average ex-factory realisation of perhaps ₹16–18 lakh, the market is worth an indicative ₹1.75–1.95 lakh crore before GST, most of it in heavy vehicles: about 4.23 lakh M&HCVs at ₹30–35 lakh each account for ₹1.3–1.5 lakh crore.

Exports are the quieter part of the record. The forecast arithmetic is the most important calculation in this section. H1 FY27 sold 5,74,255 units, so H1 FY26 was about 4,63,860 and H2 FY26, the post-GST surge, about 6,16,010. If H2 FY27 merely matches H2 FY26, FY27 ends near 11.90 lakh, up 10%. ICRA's 4–6% forecast implies FY27 of 11.23–11.45 lakh, which needs H2 FY27 to fall about 7–11% year on year. That is not an exotic outcome. The base is the strongest half-year in the industry's history; the GST benefit has been passed on and will not repeat; diesel and rates have risen; and PARIVARTAN, which could replace up to about two lakh old vehicles in Delhi-NCR, had only about 1,450 registrations by the end of August. Ashok Leyland's management, by contrast, guided to high-single-digit M&HCV growth for FY27 and called itself "conservative beyond October". The gap between those two views is the central debate for the sector this winter.

In plain terms

The first half of this year was strong partly because the same months last year were weak: the tax cut came only in late September 2025. From October onwards, every month is compared with a month that already had the tax cut. Even if demand stays healthy, growth rates will fall sharply, and a forecaster expecting 4–6% for the year is really forecasting a decline in the second half.

Penetration is the long-run argument. India has perhaps 7–8 trucks per thousand people, against more than 50 in the United States, and road carries roughly two-thirds of the country's freight. Long-run growth of 6–8% a year is the consensus among rating agencies. The structural brake is rail: the dedicated freight corridors and the National Rail Plan aim to take bulk and container traffic off long-haul roads, which caps growth at the heavy end over the next decade. Neither argument decides the next two quarters; the cycle does.

03

Why a fleet owner buys: replacement, freight growth and the pre-buy

New CV demand is replacement of an ageing parc plus growth in freight, pushed forward or back by price changes, regulation and credit. Right now almost every pusher is spent or turning.

The demand equation is simple to write down. New CV sales equal replacement (the parc multiplied by a scrappage rate) plus growth (the increase in freight tonne-kilometres divided by the work one truck can do), plus or minus pre-buying and deferral when prices, taxes or emission rules change. Every term is gated by two conditions: the operator must be earning enough to want another truck, and a lender must be willing to fund it. Because the growth term is a change in freight rather than its level, truck sales swing two to four times as much as freight volumes do, in both directions.

Replacement is the bedrock. India's CV parc is large and old: the industry deferred purchases for four years from FY19 to FY22, first because of axle-load norms and the NBFC credit freeze, then because of BS-VI price increases and COVID, so a large cohort of trucks bought in FY16–FY19 is now at or beyond the age when maintenance costs rise sharply. Formal scrappage has been slow: the 2021 National Vehicle Scrappage Policy created registered scrapping facilities and automated fitness testing, a road-tax rebate of up to 15% for transport vehicles bought against a scrappage certificate, and OEM discounts of 1.5–3%, but it has not produced a wave of scrapping on its own. PARIVARTAN is the most aggressive version yet, and its uptake is the test of whether incentives can force replacement.

Growth depends on which freight is growing. An indicative split of M&HCV truck demand puts construction, infrastructure and mining tippers at 30–35%, long-haul tractor-trailers and multi-axle haulage at 35–40%, agriculture, cement and regional bulk at 15–20%, and tankers, special vehicles and defence at 5–10%. LCV and SCV demand is overwhelmingly last-mile distribution, e-commerce, farm produce and small-business logistics. That is why steel and cement dispatches and highway awards matter for heavy trucks, while consumption and rural incomes matter for pickups.

Pre-buying and deferral are the swing terms. Every emission step, from BS-IV in 2017 to BS-VI in April 2020 and BS-VI Phase 2 in April 2023, raised prices (by roughly 8–12% for BS-VI) and caused buying ahead of the deadline and a slump after it. The AC-cabin rule produced a smaller version in Q2 FY26. GST 2.0 worked the other way: buyers who expected the cut delayed purchases in mid-2025 and then bought in a rush after September. There is no large emission deadline before about 2028–30, so the next swing is more likely to come from credit or freight than from regulation.

Buses follow a different rhythm. India's state transport undertakings run roughly 1.5 lakh buses, well short of service norms, and private school, staff and tourist operators replace on safety and age rules. In plain terms

Truck buyers bring purchases forward when they expect prices to rise and delay them when they expect a cut. The tax cut of September 2025 pulled demand into the following twelve months; the absence of any new emission deadline means nothing is pulling demand forward now. What is left is real freight growth and replacement, and both depend on whether the trucks already on the road are making money.

Credit is the final gate. About 85–90% of new CVs are financed, by NBFCs (Shriram Finance alone has about ₹1.47 lakh crore of CV loans, 46.9% of its book, growing 19% a year), by banks and by captive lenders such as HLF and Mahindra Finance. CRISIL expects NBFC vehicle-loan books to reach about ₹11 lakh crore by March 2027, growing 16–17% a year. The direct effect of a 25 basis-point rate rise is small, about ₹500 a month on a ₹40 lakh five-year loan, but lenders' appetite matters more than the rate. In both the FY14 and FY20 downturns, it was NBFC caution, not the price of money, that turned a slowdown into a collapse. Our NBFC report covers the lenders' side.

The trucker's P&L: the leading indicator nobody publishes Deep dive

Half of a truck's revenue goes on diesel, most operators run one to five trucks, and the loan instalment is due every month whether the truck is loaded or not. When that arithmetic tightens, truck orders follow within a few quarters.

No official series tracks what an Indian truck earns its owner. The pieces exist, though: ICRA's road-logistics work on operator cost structures, CRISIL's freight and utilisation indices, the transporters' associations' own fuel-adjustment formulas, and the asset quality of the lenders who fund the trucks. Put together, they describe an operator whose costs jumped in May 2026, whose freight rates rose to cover most of it, and whose trucks are now running fewer loaded kilometres than a year ago. That last part is what matters for the OEMs.

Where an operator's rupee goes

ICRA's June 2026 deck gives the cost structure of a diesel fleet operator as a share of revenue: fuel 45–55%, drivers 10–15%, maintenance 8–12%, tyres and lubricants 8–12%, other costs 6–10%, leaving an operating margin of 8–12%. CRISIL's freight-index note puts fuel at 50–60% of operating costs. Small fleet operators, those with fewer than five trucks, are 75–80% of the sector. An owner-driver with a single truck has no corporate overhead to cut, so a change in diesel or in loaded kilometres passes straight into his take-home cash.

The May 2026 shock and how much of it was passed on

The West Asia war that began at the end of February 2026 pushed up crude, freight and LPG prices (our refining report covers how pump prices are set). The transporters' association AITWA introduced a fuel adjustment factor on 20 May that adds 0.65% to freight for every ₹1 rise in diesel, which on ₹7.50 is about 4.9%.

Pass-through could be fast because most road freight is bought on the spot market. ICRA estimates that 80–95% of full-truckload industrial freight is spot, agriculture (about a quarter of freight) is 90–100% spot, MSME freight (another quarter) 70–80%, and even large corporates' freight (about half) is 40–70% spot. Contract freight for large shippers lags by a quarter or two, but the market as a whole re-prices within weeks. That is why rates are a poor warning signal on their own: they adjust to costs. The variable that does not adjust is how much work there is per truck.

Utilisation: the part of the P&L that did not recover

CRISIL's fleet utilisation index stood at 98.7 in April 2026, against 100 in April 2025, and its freight index CRISFrex, which rose to about 101.4 after GST 2.0, softened in April as excess fleet met weaker cargo. The cause is the sales boom itself: twelve months of record truck sales added capacity faster than freight grew. AIMTC, the transporters' congress, says 20–25% of India's roughly 90 lakh trucks are idle. The figure comes from an interested party and a single source, so it is indicative, but it points the same way as CRISIL's index. The direct cost of the rate rise is small: 25 basis points on a ₹40 lakh, five-year loan adds about ₹500 a month to the EMI, about 0.6%. The indirect effect is larger. Lenders respond to rising delinquencies by tightening loan-to-value ratios and insisting on stronger borrowers, and the small operator who needs 90% finance is the first to be turned away. In FY20 it was exactly this, the NBFC freeze after the IL&FS default, that turned an axle-load-driven slowdown into a collapse. The signals to watch are NBFC CV disbursement growth and 30-plus-day delinquencies each quarter: disbursement growth below 10% with rising early delinquencies would be the credit gate starting to close.

In plain terms

Almost nobody buys a truck with cash. As long as lenders keep lending, sales can stay high even while operators' earnings slip. The moment lenders see repayments slow and tighten their terms, the marginal buyer disappears. Interest rates themselves matter less than the lenders' nerve.

Taken together, the operator data describe a late-cycle market rather than a broken one. Freight volumes are near records, rates have re-priced for diesel, and credit is available. But utilisation is down, idle capacity is rising, the first rate hike has arrived and the October-to-March comparison base is the strongest in the industry's history. Every previous downturn in Indian trucking began with this combination of more trucks than loads and a turn in credit. The OEMs' order books will be the last place to show it.

Three troughs in fifteen years: the cycle that sets the valuation

Indian M&HCV volumes have fallen by roughly half three times since 2011. Each fall began with the same three triggers: tighter credit, a regulatory shock and a squeeze on operators.

Heavy trucks are among the most cyclical products in the Indian economy. An operator can postpone a replacement for two or three years by spending more on maintenance, and a whole industry can postpone at once. When it does, an M&HCV maker's volumes can halve in two years, and operating leverage turns a 10–13% EBITDA margin into a loss. LCVs swing less, because they serve consumption and replacement cycles are shorter, but they swing too.

The first modern downturn ran from FY12 to FY14, when mining bans in Karnataka and Goa, a stalled infrastructure programme and high interest rates cut M&HCV volumes by roughly 45%. The recovery from FY15 to FY19 was driven by infrastructure spending, the BS-IV transition in April 2017 and GST in July 2017, which removed check-posts and made trucks more productive, and ended with the FY19 peak of about 10.07 lakh domestic CVs. Then four shocks arrived in sequence. In July 2018 the transport ministry raised permissible axle loads by roughly 20–25%, adding capacity to the existing fleet overnight. In September 2018 IL&FS defaulted and NBFC funding froze. In April 2020 BS-VI took effect, after a period of deep discounting on BS-IV stock. And COVID closed the economy. Domestic CV sales fell about 44% from FY19 to FY21 and M&HCVs by about 59%.

The cycle matters for valuation because the market prices CV makers on current earnings, and current earnings are near a peak. A mid-cycle valuation therefore needs a view on what a normal year looks like, not just a multiple of this year's profit.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: a materials business with a thin top layer

Bought-in materials and components take about seventy rupees of every hundred a diesel truck maker earns. A one-point move in that number is worth almost a tenth of the operating profit.

A CV maker is, financially, an assembler of purchased steel and components. Ashok Leyland's raw-material and component cost was 71.5% of standalone revenue in Q1 FY27, up 90 basis points on a year earlier, and about 70–71% for FY26 as a whole. Employee costs take another 5–6 rupees and other expenses (power, freight-out, warranty, advertising, expensed R&D) 10–12. What is left, the EBITDA, was 13 rupees in FY26 and 10 in Q1 FY27. Below that line the standalone vehicle business pays little interest, because it holds net cash, and earns some other income; after depreciation of 1.5–2 rupees and tax of about a quarter of pre-tax profit, about 8 rupees reach net profit (FY26: ₹3,566 crore on ₹44,007 crore, 8.1%).

In the June 2026 quarter the four members outside Mahindra sold 11% more, earned 1% more EBITDA and lost a full point of margin. The cause is steel and other inputs running ahead of price increases, and the worst of it may be in the September quarter.

In FY26 the spread widened, because demand was strong enough for makers to hold prices and cut discounts while input costs were benign. In 2026 the spread is narrowing. Steel prices rose through the first half of the calendar year, the West Asia war pushed up crude-linked inputs such as tyres, and the weak rupee raised the cost of imported electronics and cells. Tata Motors: why the leader's numbers need care

Tata Motors' CV business, a non-member, printed a screener operating margin of about 16% in Q1 FY27 against 9% for FY26 as a whole. In plain terms

Truck makers can pass rising costs on to buyers, but only with a delay and only while demand is strong. In 2026 costs rose first and prices followed slowly, so each extra truck earned less. The second half of FY27 is when prices should catch up, which is also when the volume comparison turns hardest. The makers need the first to happen before the second bites.

The sector's defences are better than in past cycles. Discounts are low after two years of disciplined pricing, so there is room to cut them rather than cut list prices; dealer stock is moderate; and spare parts and non-vehicle businesses cushion the vehicle margin. The risk is the combination: if commodity costs stay high into a volume slowdown, discounts will widen just when price increases are needed, and the sector's margin could fall towards the 8–10% range seen in FY23 rather than recover to 13%.

Rules of the road: tax cuts, cabin air-conditioning and a scheme to scrap Delhi's old trucks

Regulation sets the price of a truck, what it must contain and when the old one must go. In this cycle the biggest rule change was a tax cut, and the next ones are safety and fuel-efficiency rules that are still drafts.

Policy moves CV demand more than it moves most industries, because a truck is a regulated, taxed, financed and registered asset whose price is a large share of its owner's net worth. Since 2017 the list of rule changes that have swung demand by high single digits or more includes GST itself, axle loads, two emission steps, a scrappage policy, a tax cut and a fleet-renewal scheme. The ones that matter now are below.

GST 2.0: the tax cut that made the record

From 22 September 2025, GST on trucks and buses (goods carriers under HSN 8704 and buses under HSN 8702) was cut from 28% to 18%, alongside small cars, two-wheelers up to 350 cc and auto parts. Ambulances moved from 28% to 18% as well, which helps Force, and tractors from 12% to 5%, which helps Mahindra's farm business. The compensation cess on vehicles was abolished. For a truck with an ex-tax price of ₹35.6 lakh, the tax-inclusive price fell from about ₹45.6 lakh to about ₹42.0 lakh, 7.8%. For an owner-driver who borrows most of the price, the cut lowered both the down payment and the monthly instalment, which is why it worked so quickly on demand.

Axle loads, scrappage and fitness

In July 2018 the transport ministry raised permissible axle loads by roughly 20–25%, so the existing fleet could carry more overnight; it was one of the four shocks behind the FY20 collapse. Overloading enforcement through weigh-in-motion systems and FASTag-linked checks works in the opposite direction, quietly raising demand for trucks. The National Vehicle Scrappage Policy of August 2021 set up registered scrapping facilities and automated testing stations, phased in mandatory automated fitness tests for transport vehicles, offered a road-tax rebate of up to 15% on a new transport vehicle bought against a scrappage certificate, and secured OEM discounts of 1.5–3%. Government vehicles older than 15 years have been deregistered since April 2023.

PARIVARTAN: the most aggressive renewal scheme yet

The Union Cabinet approved PARIVARTAN on 3 June 2026 and registrations opened on 14 July. It covers 37 districts of Delhi-NCR and BS-IV-and-older CVs, an eligible base of about 1.9 lakh trucks and 16,329 buses, with an outlay of ₹9,585 crore (Centre ₹5,041 crore). Buyers who scrap an old vehicle and buy a new BS-VI or electric one get:

  • a 100% motor-vehicle tax exemption for 10 years and a waiver of registration fees;
  • a 5% interest subvention on the loan;
  • a mandated minimum OEM discount of 8%;
  • fuel vouchers of ₹1,200–4,800 a month for five years for BS-VI diesel or CNG vehicles, or a one-time ₹64,000–2.56 lakh for electric ones.

Fifteen OEMs (Force among them) and 42 lenders participate. Uptake has been slow: about 1,450 registrations by 29 August 2026, against a ministry hope of 25,000 by October. SIAM cites the scheme as a support for October–March demand. Two points matter for the makers. The pool, about two lakh vehicles, is equal to roughly 18% of a year's domestic CV sales, so even partial success would be material. But the 8% discount comes out of the OEMs' margin on every PARIVARTAN sale, so it is a volume support at a lower price.

The rules still to come are mostly about safety and fuel efficiency, which will make trucks dearer but are not yet final. Two further policy threads matter. Defence: positive-indigenisation lists and the 2020 Defence Acquisition Procedure favour domestic OEMs; Ashok Leyland supplies Stallion trucks and delivered 1,288 defence vehicles and 1,414 kits in FY26, Force supplied about 600 Gurkhas to the Army, and SML makes special vehicles (Tata's defence business sits outside the CV company). Trade: Indian CVs do not go to the United States, so the 2026 US tariff changes barely touch the members; exports go to the Gulf, Africa, South Asia and Latin America, where the weak rupee is a tailwind and Chinese exporters the main competition. Electric-bus policy, the most consequential thread for two of the members, has its own section next.

Who owns the bus? E-buses go to capital, not to incumbents Deep dive

India's electric-bus market is small, about 4,900 buses in FY26, but tens of thousands have been awarded under twelve-year contracts. The winners are the companies that can finance and run them, and Olectra's winner is its own promoter group.

The electric bus is the one part of the CV market where the business model has already changed. A state transport undertaking (STU) that wants e-buses rarely buys them. It runs a tender for a gross-cost contract (GCC): the winning operator buys the buses and charging equipment, maintains them, usually supplies drivers, and is paid a fixed rate per kilometre for 10–12 years, with a minimum assured distance of roughly 180–220 km a day. The STU keeps fare revenue and provides depots. The operator then buys buses from an OEM. So the question "who builds the most e-buses" is downstream of "who wins GCC tenders", which is downstream of "who can raise twelve years of project finance against a state government's promise to pay".

Reading a CV maker: the KPIs that matter and the traps in each Deep dive

A CV maker's profit is units times what it earns on each, less what it pays for materials and its own fixed costs. Every native KPI below is one term of that equation, or an early warning of where it is heading.

Most of the confusion in reading CV results comes from three sources: the industry uses several incompatible counts of the same thing (units, shares, buses), companies report on different bases (standalone or consolidated, with or without lending subsidiaries, with or without other income), and the business is so seasonal that the previous quarter is almost always the wrong comparison. The masterclass starts with the equation, then takes each KPI in turn: what it is, how to compute it, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, a benchmark, how the members compare and the trap in its definition.

In plain terms

A truck maker's profit depends on four things: how many trucks it sells, what it gets for each, what the steel and parts cost and what its factories and staff cost. Only the price and the factory costs are fully in its hands. A small change in volume or steel moves profit a lot, because the profit is a thin layer on top of large costs.

1. Volumes by segment, and market share on a stated basis

What it is. Units sold, split by segment (M&HCV trucks, buses, LCV goods, LCV passenger) and by destination (domestic, export). Benchmark: a segment leader holds above 35%; 15–35% is a strong challenger; below 10% is a niche.

3. Material cost, gross margin and the pass-through lag

What it is. Raw materials and components consumed, adjusted for inventory change, as a share of revenue; gross margin is 100 minus that share. Formula: (purchases + opening inventory − closing inventory) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Force Q1 FY27: material cost ₹1,749 crore ÷ revenue ₹2,440 crore = 71.7%. What moves it: steel, castings, tyres, precious metals, cells and the rupee on the cost side; price increases, mix and discounts on the revenue side; and the inventory effect, because materials and finished vehicles bought earlier at lower prices are expensed when sold.

Pass-through has a simple rule of thumb. To keep EBITDA per vehicle unchanged in rupees, a maker must raise its price by the material share times the material inflation: at a 72% material share, a 3% rise in the basket needs a 2.2% price increase. To keep the margin unchanged in percentage terms, it needs slightly more. Ashok Leyland's cumulative FY27 increases of about 2.25% on M&HCVs and more than 3.5% on LCVs are consistent with a basket inflation of roughly 3–5%, which matches Mahindra's description of 400–500 basis points of commodity inflation, though Mahindra did not define its base.

The inventory effect is the subtle part. In a quarter when costs are rising, a maker that sells from older, cheaper stock reports a lower material share than its current purchase costs imply; the following quarter, when the dearer stock is sold, the material share jumps even if prices have risen. Ashok Leyland's Q1 FY27 margin benefited from cheaper opening inventory while its vehicle inventory rose from about 6,000 to 8,000 units, so Q2 inherits both the dearer stock and the commodity peak. In plain terms

Price increases in this industry trail cost increases by a quarter or two. In between, the maker's profit dips. A quarter that looks surprisingly good during a cost spike may simply be selling cheaper old stock; the bill arrives the next quarter.

8. Working capital and debtor days

What it is. The cash tied up in receivables and inventory, net of payables. Formula: debtor days = receivables ÷ revenue × 365; working-capital days = (receivables + inventory − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: Olectra's 156 debtor days on FY26 revenue of ₹2,312 crore imply receivables of about ₹988 crore (2,312 × 156 ÷ 365). Every 30 days of extra collection time locks up about ₹190 crore, slightly more than its whole FY26 profit of ₹180 crore. What moves it: the customer (dealers pay diesel OEMs quickly, often through channel finance; STUs and SPVs pay e-bus makers slowly), inventory build ahead of price increases or seasons, and the fiscal-year-end push.

11. Financing: CV loan growth and asset quality

What it is. The growth and health of the loan books that fund CV purchases. Formulas: CV AUM growth = this year's CV loans ÷ last year's − 1; gross stage-3 (GS3) = loans more than 90 days overdue ÷ total loans. Worked example: Shriram Finance's CV loans of ₹1,47,034 crore ÷ ₹1,23,132 crore a year earlier = +19%; its GS3 was 4.64%. What moves it: operator cash flows, interest rates, lender risk appetite, used-vehicle prices. Benchmark: GS3 below 3% is good, 3–5% average, above 5% weak; disbursement growth falling below 10% with rising early delinquencies is the warning.

How the members are exposed. About 85–90% of new CVs are financed. Ashok Leyland's captive HLF (AUM ₹60,310 crore, +20%; net NPA 2.1% with its housing arm) finances an approximate 20–30% of its sales, an industry estimate rather than a disclosed figure. Mahindra Finance is a large financier of Mahindra's rural pickups. A captive lender helps sell vehicles when banks retreat, but its losses land on the parent's consolidated accounts. The trap: CV loan books include used vehicles, which carry weaker asset quality; a headline GS3 can be stable while new-vehicle disbursements are slowing. And a consolidated "debt" figure that includes a captive lender's borrowings says nothing about the vehicle business's leverage.

13. The operator's freight-to-diesel ratio

What it is. The ratio of freight-rate change to fuel-cost change for truck operators, the single best leading indicator of truck demand. Formula: freight needed to hold the operator's margin = fuel share of revenue × diesel price change; ratio = actual freight change ÷ needed freight change. Worked example: with fuel at 45–55% of revenue and diesel up about 8%, freight needs to rise about 3.6–4.4% (CRISIL's own rule, 2.5–2.8% per ₹5 a litre, gives 3.75–4.2% for ₹7.50). ICRA observed about 7%, a ratio comfortably above 1 on rates alone. What moves it: pump prices, excise decisions, fleet capacity relative to cargo, and the spot share of freight. Benchmark: a ratio above 1 with stable utilisation is good; above 1 on rates but with falling utilisation (today) is average; below 1 is weak and historically precedes a fall in truck orders by two to four quarters.

How it reads now. In the 28-tonne example of Section 4, a 4% freight increase fully covers the diesel rise, but 10% fewer kilometres takes the owner's monthly surplus from ₹38,000 to about ₹16,900, the same damage as an unrecovered hike. CRISIL's utilisation index at 98.7 (April 2026, against 100 a year earlier) and AIMTC's indicative 20–25% idle fleet say loaded kilometres, not rates, are the problem. The trap: freight indices are built from spot quotes on major routes and can rise while the average truck earns less, because rates per load are not revenue per truck; pair any rate index with a utilisation measure.

16. Definition traps, collected

  • Three volume scoreboards. SIAM wholesale (FY26 10,79,871), FADA/Vahan retail (FY26 10,60,906) and company dispatches including exports. Tata's 35.7% is Vahan; Ashok Leyland's 30.3% M&HCV share is wholesale. Never put them in one table without labels.
  • "LCV" means three things. SIAM: up to 7.5 tonnes including small passenger carriers. Mahindra: under 3.5 tonnes. Ashok Leyland: its Dost and Bada Dost range. Tata uses "SCV & pickup" for under 3.5 tonnes and "ILMCV" for about 5–16 tonnes.
  • Four counts of buses. SIAM M&HCV buses (67,149), SIAM LCV passenger carriers including vans (61,597), e-bus registrations or KPMG's demand (~4,900) and tender awards (delivered over two to three years). Force's Traveller is a "bus" to SIAM and a "van" to everyone else.
  • Mahindra is a member but not a CV company. Its ₹1.99 lakh crore of revenue is mostly SUVs, tractors and Mahindra Finance; its CV slice is an indicative ₹30,000–37,000 crore.
  • SML is counted twice in "all five". Mahindra has consolidated SML (58.97%) since 1 August 2025, about 0.7% of the five's revenue.
  • Ashok Leyland standalone is not consolidated. The consolidated 19.1% margin and ₹63,936 crore of borrowings belong mostly to HLF. Use standalone for margins, EBITDA per vehicle and net cash.
  • "EBITDA" differs by source. Screener OP excludes other income; company EBITDA may include it or exclude one-offs. Force: ₹1,593 crore vs ₹1,484 crore; Olectra: ₹352 crore vs ₹330 crore.
  • One-offs in the base. Force's FY25 profit included ₹446 crore of other income; Ashok Leyland's FY26 included a ₹308 crore Labour Code charge; Tata's FY26 includes a pre-demerger quarter.
  • Seasonality. Q4 is the peak and Q1 the trough for diesel OEMs; Q1 is the peak for SML's school buses. Compare a quarter with the same quarter a year earlier.
  • Per-vehicle figures include non-vehicles. Revenue and EBITDA per vehicle include spares, engines, defence and insulators.
  • Olectra's revenue is mostly a sale to its own group. About 75% of FY26 revenue went to Evey SPVs and MEIL.
  • Order books are not all firm. An award, a contract, a financed SPV and a delivery schedule are different stages.
  • Net cash is a date. Ashok Leyland's ₹5,899 crore is a March figure; June's was ₹2,252 crore.
  • Growth rates in H2 FY27 are distorted by GST 2.0. The comparison base from October 2026 includes the post-cut surge.

17. How to read a CV maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the units you already have. Monthly dispatches give the quarter's volume before results. Split domestic and export, M&HCV and LCV, and note which segment drove growth.
  2. Compute realisation per vehicle. Revenue ÷ units, against the same quarter last year. If it rose less than material inflation, pricing lagged; if it fell, look for mix or discounts.
  3. Read the material-cost share. Compare it with the same quarter last year. A jump of 50 basis points or more is the commodity hit; check the commentary for inventory effects that may flatter this quarter or the next.
  4. Check EBITDA margin and EBITDA per vehicle, year on year. Never against the previous quarter for a seasonal business. For Ashok Leyland, the Q2 FY27 benchmark is Q2 FY26's 12.1%.
  5. Strip out other income and one-offs. Profit growth driven by treasury income, tax items or a low base (Force FY25) is not operating improvement.
  6. Pick the right basis. Standalone for Ashok Leyland's vehicle business and Mahindra's autos; consolidated only for group value. Ignore consolidated debt that belongs to a lender.
  7. Look at the balance-sheet movement. Net cash or debt against March and against a year ago; inventory in units where disclosed (Ashok Leyland's rose from about 6,000 to 8,000 in Q1 FY27).
  8. Track the non-vehicle lines. Spares, defence, power solutions, engines and insulators: are they growing faster or slower than vehicles?
  9. Update the guidance and the book. Industry growth guidance (Ashok Leyland's high-single-digit M&HCV vs ICRA's 1–3%), price increases taken, e-bus order book and delivery guidance.
  10. Scan for red flags. Widening discounts, rising dealer stock, receivables rising faster than revenue (especially from related parties), new promoter pledges (40.1% of Ashok Leyland's promoter shares are pledged), auditor qualifications and changes of CFO.
In plain terms

Read a truck maker's results in this order: how many vehicles, at what average price, at what material cost, leaving what profit per vehicle compared with the same quarter last year. Then check whether the profit came from operations or from one-offs, and whether cash and stock moved the wrong way. Ten numbers, in that order, tell most of the story.

Mahindra sells more commercial vehicles than anyone except Tata, almost all of them under 3.5 tonnes. Its plan to matter in trucks and buses runs through SML, the listed company it bought in 2025 and is now merging its own truck business into.

Mahindra & Mahindra is the largest member of this sub-sector by a distance, and the one whose CV business is hardest to see. Its FY26 consolidated revenue of ₹1,98,639 crore and market value of about ₹3.59 lakh crore come mostly from SUVs, tractors (43.6% of the Indian market), Mahindra Finance and a portfolio of listed and unlisted holdings that includes a 28.06% stake in Tech Mahindra. Last Mile Mobility: the electric small-vehicle franchise

Mahindra Last Mile Mobility (LMM) sells three-wheelers and small electric CVs (Treo, Zor Grand, e-Alfa) and is India's largest electric CV maker. It sold 42,387 vehicles in Q1 FY27 against 25,189 a year earlier and held 39.5% of electric three-wheelers. This matters to the CV story because ICRA's powertrain economics (operating margins of 30–35% for electric fleets against 8–12% for diesel) are pulling short-haul cargo towards electric three-wheelers and small CVs, and Ashok Leyland's own annual report names e-3W substitution of LCVs as a risk. Mahindra owns both sides of that substitution.

Trucks and buses: MTBD plus SML

Above 3.5 tonnes Mahindra has been a minnow. Its truck and bus division (MTBD), formed after it bought out Navistar's share of their joint venture, makes the Blazo heavy trucks, Furio light and intermediate trucks and Cruzio buses. Together with SML, Mahindra's FY26 annual report gives its "Trucks & Buses" share as 6.0% of the market above 3.5 tonnes. Mahindra now publishes a separate monthly "Trucks & Buses Business" release covering both: in H1 FY27 MTBD sold 8,465 units (+31%), 5,965 goods carriers and 2,500 passenger vehicles, and SML 9,340 (+15%), 6,806 passenger and 2,534 cargo, a combined 17,805 (+22%). In September the combined figure was 2,912 (+53%), with MTBD up 87% and SML 18%.

Mahindra's longer-term ambition, per our primer, is a share of 10–12% above 3.5 tonnes by FY31 and more than 20% by FY36.

In plain terms

Mahindra dominates small pickups but is a bit player in trucks. Its plan is to put its own small truck business and the bus maker it bought into one listed company, SML Mahindra, and grow it with Mahindra's purchasing power, dealers and finance arm. The plan is credible on paper, but SML's latest quarter shows how hard it is to make money at small scale in heavy vehicles.

SML Mahindra on its own

SML is a single-plant maker at Ropar in Punjab, with capacity of about 24,000 vehicles a year. In FY26 it sold 16,632 vehicles (+17%): 11,220 buses and 5,412 trucks, on revenue of ₹2,838 crore (+18%), operating profit of ₹278 crore (9.8%) and net profit of ₹160 crore. Its bus franchise, about 16% of intermediate and light buses, rests on school and staff buses, which are steady, safety-code driven and seasonal; its trucks are a small presence. It returned 31% on capital and 35% on equity, and borrowings fell to ₹38 crore by September 2026. Its Q2 FY27 margin of 4.3% (7.6% a year earlier) and 44% fall in profit are covered in Section 7. On 19 October the company also announced that its CFO had left; an interim CFO is in place and a successor joins on 1 January 2027.

The valuation is the issue. At 54 times trailing earnings and 14.2 times book value, SML is priced for the merged business Mahindra intends to build, not for the business it has. The risks are integration (two dealer networks, two product lines, contract manufacturing for the parent), scale (even combined, 17,805 units in a half-year against Tata's 4.28 lakh in a year), input costs, and the first e-bus still to be rolled out. For Mahindra, the risks to the larger CV slice are a rural slowdown (Shriram flagged weak rainfall), electric small CVs from Tata (Ace EV) and others, and the cost of building a credible heavy-truck business against three entrenched leaders. Once MTBD moves into SML, the overlap between Mahindra's and SML's accounts will grow, and the double count in any "all five" figure will grow with it.

In plain terms

Ashok Leyland is the member that gains most when heavy-truck sales boom and loses most when they fall. Who is winning: growth everywhere, margin nowhere, and a valuation map that ignores both

Three maps place the members on growth against margin change, scale against return, and valuation against current margin. The first shows a shared problem; the third shows the market pricing stories rather than quarters.

The first map plots each member's latest-quarter revenue growth against the change in its margin over the same quarter a year earlier. Every member sits below the zero line: all are growing, and none has expanded its margin. The spread is in the size of the squeeze. Mahindra's auto segment lost only about 20 basis points of PBIT margin on 32% revenue growth (a segment that mixes SUVs with pickups); Olectra grew 66% and lost 200 basis points; SML grew 21% and lost 330. Ashok Leyland and Force grew more slowly and lost 70–100 basis points.

The second map sets scale against return on capital, using FY26 units on a logarithmic axis and each company's cleanest ROCE. Ashok Leyland (standalone 38%) and Tata (35.9%) combine scale with high returns. Force earns a similar return on a fraction of the volume because of its engine contracts. SML's 31% is a peak-year number on a small base, and Olectra's 21% reflects a business still building scale. Mahindra's dot uses its standalone ROCE, which covers SUVs and tractors as well as pickups.

The third map sets valuation against the latest quarter's margin. In a market that priced current earnings, the dots would rise from left to right. Instead the two highest multiples belong to the two lowest-scale, most story-driven members: SML at 54 times earnings with a 4.3% margin, and Olectra at 46.6 times with 11.8%. The cluster of Ashok Leyland, Force, Mahindra and Tata sits at 19–23 times. The market is paying for SML's merger with MTBD and Olectra's order book, not for their quarters.

The global lens: India is out of phase with the world's truck cycle

In 2025 the big European and American truck makers' margins fell by one to three points on a down-cycle. Indian makers printed records. That divergence, and China's export machine, frame how the members should be valued.

The world's heavy-truck industry is dominated by four groups outside China: Daimler Truck (Mercedes-Benz, Freightliner, FUSO and, in India, BharatBenz), the Volvo Group (Volvo, Renault Trucks, Mack and 45.6% of VECV), Traton (Scania, MAN, International) and PACCAR (Kenworth, Peterbilt, DAF). All four reported calendar 2025 results showing the effect of a North American and European downturn. Daimler Truck's industrial revenue fell 10% to €45.9 billion and its adjusted return on sales to 7.8% (8.9% in 2024) on 4,22,510 units (−8%), with 2026 guidance of 6–8%. Volvo's net sales fell to SEK 479.2 billion (526.8) and its adjusted operating margin to 10.7% (12.5%). Traton's revenue fell 7% to €44.1 billion and its adjusted return on sales to 6.3% (9.2%), on 3,05,486 units (−9%; trucks −14%), with Scania alone at 10.7%; it guides to 5.3–7.3% for 2026. PACCAR's revenue fell to $28.44 billion (33.66) with 1,44,200 deliveries and a record $6.87 billion from parts.

Technology and disruption: electrification arrives from the bottom and the city first

Electric three-wheelers and city buses already compete on cost; long-haul diesel trucks are safe this decade. The disruption that matters soonest is to the profit pools in small CVs and buses, and to who controls batteries.

Electrification of commercial vehicles is arriving in a predictable sequence, set by duty cycle, payload and charging. Vehicles that run short, fixed routes and return to a depot every night electrify first; vehicles that run long, variable routes with heavy payloads electrify last. ICRA's operator economics show why: an electric fleet earns a 30–35% operating margin against 8–12% for diesel, but only where the route fits the battery. In September 2026 electric vehicles were 4.04% of CV retail registrations.

Electric trucks are at the start. PM E-DRIVE set aside ₹500 crore for e-trucks, with incentives of up to about ₹9.6 lakh a truck linked to scrapping an old one. A maker that cannot localise its battery and drivetrain will find both its margins and its tender eligibility at risk.

In plain terms

Electric vehicles are already cheaper to run for small delivery vehicles and city buses, so those markets will change first; long-distance diesel trucks will not change for another decade. The companies most affected are the ones strongest in small vehicles and buses. The biggest dependency is on Chinese batteries and magnets, which is why localisation now matters as much as the vehicle itself.

Software and services are a new profit pool. Tata's Fleet Edge connected-vehicle platform has passed one million vehicles; Ashok Leyland runs i-Alert; telematics enable usage-based insurance, predictive maintenance and pay-per-use contracts that tie the operator to the OEM's service network for the vehicle's life. Safety technology will add cost: if the draft ADAS rule is finalised, emergency braking, lane-departure and drowsiness warnings will add an indicative ₹0.5–1.5 lakh a vehicle; Ashok Leyland is also exploring an autonomous Stallion for defence. Finally, rail is the slow structural competitor: the Eastern and Western Dedicated Freight Corridors and the National Rail Plan's goal of about 45% rail share by 2030 target exactly the long-haul bulk and container freight that heavy tractor-trailers carry. That caps long-haul HCV growth at the margin, while raising demand for shorter feeder trips that lighter trucks serve.

Bull vs bear: a structural growth market at a cyclical peak

Both cases are strong. The bull case is about the next decade; the bear case is about the next four quarters. The valuation has to bridge them.

The bull case

  • Structural under-penetration: perhaps 7–8 trucks per thousand people, road carrying about two-thirds of freight, and long-run growth of 6–8% a year.
  • Replacement is overdue: four years of deferral (FY19–FY22), PARIVARTAN's two-lakh-vehicle pool, scrappage and fitness rules.
  • Pricing discipline: a concentrated market (HHI about 2,350) has kept discounts low; FY26 margins of 13% beat the FY19 peak.
  • Cushions: spares (10% at AL), engines (30% at Force), defence, exports at a weak rupee, and net-cash manufacturing balance sheets (about ₹6,154 Cr ex-M&M at March).
  • Electrification as opportunity: Mahindra leads e-3Ws, Olectra and Switch together hold about half the e-bus market, and 46,000 e-buses are awarded.
  • Corporate catalysts: HLF's listing, the MTBD–SML merger, the resolution of the BEST dispute.

The bear case

  • Base effect and forecast: ICRA expects FY27 growth of 4–6% and M&HCV trucks of 1–3%, which implies H2 FY27 falling about 7–11% YoY.
  • Operator squeeze: diesel +8%, utilisation below a year ago, an indicative 20–25% of trucks idle, the first repo hike, and NBFC books at a record.
  • Margins already falling: −100 bp ex-M&M in Q1 FY27 on a manufacturing basis, and Q2 expected to be the commodity peak; SML at 4.3%.
  • Peak-cycle multiples: 20–54× earnings on peak ROCE, against approximately 7–16× globally and 3% ROCE at AL as recently as FY22.
  • E-bus model risk: thin per-km economics, state payment risk and Olectra's 75% related-party revenue.
  • Governance flags: AL's 40.1% promoter pledge; Force's new objects clause and thin disclosure; Olectra's group dependence.

The market has already moved some way towards the bear case. Since the company primers were written between June and August 2026, the members' market values have fallen by between 1% and 33%, led by Olectra, whose de-rating followed the BEST dispute and a weaker margin. The fall is not uniform, and it is not obviously aligned with the quarter's fundamentals: SML, which reported the weakest quarter, has fallen least over the period (though it fell 10% on the day of its results).

The balance of evidence points to a slowdown rather than a bust. The three triggers of past collapses are only partly present: credit is tightening but not freezing, operator economics are squeezed by utilisation rather than broken by rates, and there is no regulatory shock comparable to the 2018 axle-load change or the 2020 BS-VI transition. Against that, the base effect is the largest in the industry's history, and margins are falling before volumes have turned. The most plausible path is a sharp fall in reported growth rates from October, margins that bottom in Q2 or Q3 FY27 if price increases hold, and a debate in 2027 about whether FY28 can grow on an FY27 that was flat or down.

In plain terms

The long-term case for Indian trucks and buses is good: the country needs many more of them, and the makers are disciplined. The short-term case is weaker: last year's tax cut makes this winter's comparisons very hard, fuel and interest costs have risen, and the makers' profits per vehicle are already slipping. Shares have fallen somewhat, but they still price these companies as if this year's profits are normal.

For each member the balance differs. Ashok Leyland is the cleanest expression of the cycle in both directions, with a value-unlock option in HLF. Mahindra's CV exposure is small relative to its value and centred on the most resilient segment. Force's engine income and net cash give it the best downside protection, offset by governance questions and thin disclosure. Olectra's case rests on converting an order book whose financing and collection depend on its own group. SML's case rests almost entirely on the integration Mahindra has promised. In every case the next two results, for the September and December quarters, will tell investors more than any forecast.
