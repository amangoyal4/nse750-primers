The one-page view: a toll gate on every imported tonne

India imports two-thirds of its cooking gas and most of its bulk chemicals. All of it has to sit in a shore tank first, and the owner of that tank is paid whether prices rise or fall.

A liquid or gas terminal is a patch of port land with a jetty pipeline, a set of steel tanks and a gate where road tankers, rail wagons or a pipeline take the cargo inland. A ship arrives with 44,000 tonnes of liquefied petroleum gas from Texas or Ras Tanura, or 8,000 tonnes of methanol from Oman, or palm oil from Malaysia. The cargo is pumped ashore, stored for a few days or a few weeks, and then sent on to a bottling plant, a chemical factory or a refinery. The terminal never owns the product. It rents out space by the cubic metre or charges a fee for every tonne that passes through, and that is why a well-run terminal keeps three-quarters of its revenue as operating profit.

This report covers the whole Indian industry: the independent tank farms at Kandla, Jawaharlal Nehru Port (JNPA), Haldia, New Mangalore, Pipavav and Kochi; the LPG import terminals owned by Indian Oil, Bharat Petroleum and Hindustan Petroleum; joint ventures such as IndianOil Petronas; port owners such as Adani Ports, which bought the Indian Oiltanking business; the private-equity consolidator Cisternina, which KKR controls; and the state's strategic crude reserves. Only one pure-play terminal company is listed in the NSE 750: Aegis Vopak Terminals (AVTL), the joint venture of Aegis Logistics and the Dutch storage group Royal Vopak. We use it as the lens throughout, because its filings, prospectus and earnings calls are the best public window into how the industry's economics actually work.

Five forces shape the industry, and the report is built around them.

  • "Ten days of gas." India imports about 64–66% of the LPG it burns, and before 28 February 2026 roughly 90% of those imports came through the Strait of Hormuz. The West Asia war cut March 2026 LPG consumption by 13.6% and halved imports for months. The government responded with a 30-day LPG reserve directive (29 May 2026) and, by press reports, a ten-year plan costing about US$42 bn that would add 4 MMT of LPG storage. India's port terminals hold roughly 745,000 tonnes. Who builds the gap, and who is paid to run it, is the industry's biggest open question (section 03).
  • The roll-up. AVTL grew from 556,000 cubic metres (cbm) of liquid tanks and 67,000 tonnes of LPG storage in November 2021 to 1.67 mn cbm and 225,800 tonnes today, mostly by buying terminals, many of them from its own promoter group. Aegis builds; AVTL buys; the public owns 13%. The architecture of that model, its transfer prices and the coming promoter sell-down are covered in section 10.
  • "Liquids earn, gas waits." AVTL's liquid tanks earned about 11.6% pre-tax on capital employed in H1 FY27; its newer LPG and ammonia terminals earned about 6.5%, because they run at about a fifth of their design throughput. Whether gas turns rise is the single most important operating variable in the sector (section 11).
  • The landlord. Almost every terminal sits on land leased from a port authority or a private port concessionaire. Rents are reset off market value, and some leases end on fixed dates: all of AVTL's Pipavav sub-leases expire on 29 September 2028, the date Gujarat Pipavav Port's own concession ends (section 09).
  • Leverage and the valuation gap. AVTL's finance cost doubled in a year to ₹40 crore a quarter as acquisition debt replaced the debt its IPO repaid. Its shares trade at about five times Vopak's multiple. The arithmetic behind that gap is in section 17.

In plain terms

A terminal is a toll booth on a road that every importer must use. It does not care whether LPG costs $500 or $700 a tonne; it cares how many tonnes drive through and how much it cost to build the booth. India's road is busy because the country makes too little of its own gas and chemicals. The questions for an investor are whether more traffic will come through this particular booth, whether the landlord will raise the rent, and how much has already been paid for the booth's future traffic.

The money is good when the tanks are full. AVTL earned an EBITDA margin of 74.4% in FY26 and a record 78.6% in Q2 FY27 (July to September 2026). Royal Vopak, the world's largest independent tank-storage company, earns about 58% on a proportional basis. But margin is not return. AVTL's return on capital employed was 7.6% on screener's trailing basis, because most of its LPG capacity was bought or built in the last 18 months and runs well below design. Its trailing profit after tax of ₹329 crore fell even as EBITDA rose 9%, because interest and depreciation on the new terminals arrived before the volume did.

Three facts frame everything that follows. First, terminal demand is a by-product of import dependence: each new Indian refinery or chemical plant that substitutes an import takes volume away from a port tank. Second, terminal capacity is cheap to build compared with what the market pays for it. AVTL's group-built LPG terminals cost roughly ₹0.9–1.2 lakh per tonne of storage against ₹2.4–8.2 lakh for oil-company terminals, yet the stock market values AVTL at about ₹1.6 lakh per cubic-metre-equivalent of capacity, several times replacement cost. Third, the industry is small. Indian third-party liquid storage and LPG terminalling together generate an estimated ₹3,400–4,400 crore of revenue a year, less than the major ports' own revenue and a fraction of Vopak's global business. That is why consolidation, by AVTL, by KKR's Cisternina and by Adani, is the dominant corporate theme.

01

How a terminal earns: ship, tank, hinterland

The terminal sits between the ocean freight and the inland pipe. It captures a fixed fee on a cargo it never owns, and its returns are set by three numbers: what the tank cost, how often it turns, and the tariff.

Bulk liquids and liquefied gases cannot be unloaded straight into a lorry. A very large gas carrier (VLGC) holds 44,000–50,000 tonnes of LPG, a chemical tanker 5,000–40,000 tonnes in dozens of segregated parcels. The ship must discharge in a day or two, because charter rates and demurrage run to tens of thousands of dollars a day, while the hinterland can absorb the cargo only at the pace of road tankers, rail rakes or a pipeline. The shore tank is the buffer between those two speeds. That simple mismatch is the industry's whole reason to exist.

The terminal's edge comes from Step 2 (a scarce waterfront plot and a deep jetty) and Step 4 (a connection to a pipeline or railway that lets the same tank turn more often).

What gets stored. The products differ sharply in how they are kept, who owns the tanks, and how the terminal is paid. LPG is stored either in pressurised spheres and mounded bullets at ambient temperature, or in fully refrigerated double-wall tanks at about −42 °C for propane, which is what a VLGC-scale import terminal needs. Chemicals need stainless or coated tanks, nitrogen blanketing and dedicated lines, segregated by hazard class. Edible oils need heated mild-steel tanks and food-grade lines. Ammonia needs refrigerated tanks at −33 °C and a toxic-gas safety regime. Crude for strategic reserves sits in unlined rock caverns.

Two revenue models. Liquid terminals are landlords. A customer reserves, say, 10,000 cbm of tankage for a year or more and pays a monthly rate per cubic metre whether the tank is full or empty; AVTL's management put it plainly on the August 2026 call: "if I give a capacity, whether it is occupied by the customer or not, I get paid." Handling, heating and blending charges come on top. LPG terminals are toll roads. They charge a fee for each tonne that crosses the jetty, usually with a contractual minimum volume. AVTL's standard LPG fee is ₹1,175 per tonne, about ₹1,200 for principals, and management says it is the same at every terminal in the country. Cargo sits in the tank for only three or four days, so LPG terminals compete on how many times they can fill and empty a tank in a year, not on price.

Where the value is captured. The terminal's fee is small per tonne but nearly all of it is profit. AVTL's LPG fee averaged about ₹1,237 per tonne in FY26 (gas revenue of ₹482.6 crore over 3.9 MMT) and its gas segment kept about ₹1,063 of that as EBITDA. The LPG trader and distributor, in AVTL's case its parent Aegis Logistics, earns five or six times more per tonne, a shade above ₹7,000 in the September 2026 quarter against about ₹4,000 in 2024–25, but that margin swings with LPG prices and supply. The port captures wharfage and land rent. Pipelines and railways capture the inland leg, and a terminal tied to a trunk pipeline earns more turns from the same tanks. The OMCs keep captive tankage for security of supply and outsource throughput where they lack a jetty or land: Hindustan Petroleum, for example, holds an exclusive agreement for AVTL's Haldia LPG terminal until FY2038.

In plain terms

The liquid business is a warehouse that charges for reserved shelf space. The LPG business is a toll road that charges for every truck that passes. Both keep most of what they charge, because the costs (staff, power, insurance, maintenance) barely change with volume. The difference is that the warehouse is paid even when shelves are empty, while the toll road is paid only when traffic flows, apart from minimum-volume clauses.

Why margins look so different across the industry. A pure terminal books only its fee as revenue, so its EBITDA margin is 70–80%. A company that also buys and sells the product books the product's value as revenue: IndianOil Petronas, which sells LPG as well as storing it, reported about ₹3,355 crore of revenue at an 11.6% margin in FY23, and Aegis Logistics' consolidated margin is 17–23%. Comparing margin percentages across these models is meaningless. The right comparisons are EBITDA per tonne or per cubic metre, and return on capital.

The three numbers that decide returns. Every terminal's return can be reduced to capital cost per unit of capacity, the number of times that capacity is used in a year, and the tariff per use. A liquid tank costing ₹20,000 per cbm that earns ₹3,500 per cbm a year at 85% reservation produces about ₹3,000 of revenue per cbm and, at a 75–80% margin, roughly ₹2,300–2,400 of EBITDA, a gross yield of about 12% before depreciation. An LPG terminal costing ₹1.18 lakh per tonne of storage that turns its tanks 18 times a year at ₹1,240 a tonne earns ₹22,300 of revenue per tonne of storage, a gross yield near 19%. The same LPG terminal at nine turns earns half that. Port land rent, which under Indian accounting sits below EBITDA as lease depreciation and interest, then takes its share. Section 13 works through each of these numbers in detail.

The final piece is evacuation. A terminal at Kandla connected to the Jamnagar–Loni pipeline (3.25 MTPA) and, soon, the Kandla–Gorakhpur pipeline (8.25 MTPA, about 96% complete) can move LPG inland far faster than one that relies on road tankers alone. Pipavav has a 32-bay LPG rail gantry; New Mangalore is adding one. In 2024, India's LPG pipelines moved 10.1 MMT against public-sector LPG sales of 29.6 MMT, and Jamnagar–Loni ran at 99% utilisation. For that reason the industry's returns depend on infrastructure that terminal owners do not control: the pipelines are built by the OMCs and GAIL, the railway sidings by Indian Railways, the jetties by port authorities. 02

Tonnes, tanks and rupees: how big the business is

The physical flows are large and growing; the rupee pool that terminals capture from them is small, about ₹3,400–4,400 crore a year.

Measured in cargo, the industry is one of the biggest flows through Indian ports. Petroleum, oil and lubricants (POL) including crude were 272.3 MMT at the major ports alone in FY26, up 16.2% and 29.8% of all major-port cargo. Across all Indian ports, POL including LPG and LNG was about 469 MMT of 1,668 MMT. Deendayal Port at Kandla, India's busiest major port, handled a record 160.1 MMT in FY26, of which 20.41 MMT was liquid cargo, up 11.5% by the port's own records, and 2.61 MMT was LPG, up 25%. India traded about 15.7 MMT of bulk liquid chemicals in FY24 and imports 15–16 MMT of vegetable oils a year. Ammonia imports run at 2.3–2.6 MMT a year.

LPG is the cleanest series because PPAC publishes it monthly. Consumption rose from 24.9 MMT in FY19 to 33.2 MMT in FY26, a compound rate of about 4.2%, driven by the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY) connections that brought cooking gas to about 10.6 crore poorer households. Domestic production stayed flat at about 12–13 MMT, so imports carried all the growth, rising from 13.2 MMT to 21.8 MMT. That gap is the demand base for every LPG import terminal in the country.

If part of that higher yield persists, the import pool that terminals serve grows more slowly than consumption.

In plain terms

For every three cylinders of cooking gas India burns, about two arrive by ship. That ratio has been climbing for a decade because demand grew and refineries did not make more LPG. A terminal owner is effectively betting that the ratio stays high. The 2026 war showed that refineries can squeeze out more LPG in an emergency, which is good for the country and a mild headwind for importers' toll gates.

Two definitional points matter. Liquid capacity is measured in cubic metres, which are identical to kilolitres. LPG capacity is measured in tonnes of static storage, meaning what the tanks hold at one moment. Since LPG weighs about 0.51–0.58 tonnes per cubic metre, a tonne of LPG storage is roughly 1.8–2.0 cbm of tank volume. Liquid and LPG capacities should never be added, and the old figure of "about 6.6 mn kl" of third-party liquid storage that circulated in 2025 overstated the base because it applied CRISIL's 25.53% share to AVTL's later, larger capacity.

The rupee pool. Translating tonnes into revenue requires estimated tariffs, so the following figures are INDICATIVE. Independent liquid storage: about 5.84 mn cbm, 75% reserved, at ₹2,800–3,600 per cbm a year, gives roughly ₹1,200–1,600 crore. Together, roughly ₹3,400–4,400 crore a year. AVTL's ₹440 crore of liquid revenue is between a quarter and a third of the liquid pool, consistent with its capacity share once its above-average tariffs are allowed for; its ₹483 crore of gas revenue is about a sixth to a fifth of the notional LPG pool.

The forward view is set by capacity. Management targets about 2.2 mn cbm of liquid capacity by the end of FY27 and close to 3 mn by the end of FY28, a gross block of ₹10,000 crore by March 2027, cumulative capex of about US$1.2 bn "by next year" and about US$5 bn by 2030–31, and at least 25% a year growth in LPG throughput. PL, the only broker whose initiation the dossier read in full, models revenue of ₹1,224 crore in FY27 rising to ₹2,851 crore in FY29. These are company ambitions and a broker's estimates, not commitments, and the industry pool they would have to come from grows at single digits. Most of the growth therefore has to come from taking share, from new kinds of storage (strategic LPG, ammonia, inland depots) or from customers outsourcing tankage they now own.

03

"Ten days of gas": India's LPG security gap after Hormuz Deep dive

The 2026 West Asia war turned a commercial storage industry into a national-security question. India holds about eight to ten days of LPG in its import terminals; the government now wants thirty.

Before 28 February 2026, India's LPG supply chain worked on a just-in-time basis. Roughly 60% of the country's LPG requirement came from Qatar, the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, in the Petroleum Minister's words to Parliament in March 2026, and about 40% was made at home. Since imports were about two-thirds of consumption, the Gulf supplied about 90% of everything India imported, and nearly all of it sailed through the Strait of Hormuz. Import terminals held a few days of stock; bottling plants held four to seven days; the whole system, counting product in pipelines and at distributors, held about 18 days on average by PPAC's estimate. That was enough for a world where a VLGC arrived every day or two.

When the United States and Israel went to war with Iran on 28 February, Gulf loadings stopped, Qatar's gas exports halted and tanker insurance rates soared. India's LPG imports collapsed: 792,000 tonnes arrived in March 2026 against 1.885 million a year earlier, a fall of 58%. The government invoked an LPG Control Order on 8 March, directing refineries to maximise LPG yield, and output rose 28% within five days. On 12 March commercial LPG supply was capped at 20% of normal so that household cylinders could be protected. March consumption fell 13.6% on PPAC's final data. A ceasefire on 7 April did not restore normal shipping; renewed tanker attacks in October pushed Brent back to about $110.

The pain was not spread evenly. In April–September 2026, household (domestic packed) LPG fell 13.5%, but bulk LPG and direct private imports fell 71%, while auto-LPG rose 31% from a tiny base. Where the gas came from instead. The supply map was redrawn in weeks. According to Kpler-based press reports, the United States supplied 12–14% of India's LPG imports in January and February 2026, 55% in May and 73% in July; the Gulf's share fell from about 81% in February to 16% in May before recovering to about 30% in August. A cargo from the US Gulf Coast takes four to six weeks to reach India against about a week from Ras Tanura, so the same monthly demand needs more cargo in transit and more storage at the receiving end. US cargoes arrive on VLGCs, which favours terminals with refrigerated tanks and deep jetties able to take a whole 44,000-tonne cargo.

The stock-take. How much storage does India actually have? Who would pay. The reported plan would be funded by a levy of ₹1.29 per kilogram of LPG, about ₹18 on a 14.2 kg cylinder, plus ₹1.43 per standard cubic metre of natural gas. At FY26 volumes the LPG levy would raise roughly ₹4,300 crore a year (INDICATIVE). That is meaningful money but it lands on top of an OMC system already absorbing huge losses: domestic LPG under-recovery rose from about ₹80 to about ₹510 per cylinder in Q1 FY27, and the inter-ministerial group put under-recoveries across all fuels at nearly ₹2 lakh crore in the first quarter of 2026. The ₹30,000 crore compensation package often quoted against these losses was approved for FY25's roughly ₹40,000 crore of pre-war LPG losses, not for 2026. The fiscal side of the story is covered in refining & marketing.

Contract or build. For the independent terminals, everything hinges on one decision that has not yet been made. If the OMCs meet the directive by contracting third-party capacity, renting tanks rather than building them, AVTL, with the only multi-port independent LPG network, is the natural counterparty, and its capital cost per tonne is a third to a quarter of theirs. If the state builds rock caverns of the kind South Asia LPG runs at Vizag (60,000 t), or if the OMCs build in-house, the independents gain little beyond construction-era volumes. BPCL's plan suggests at least one OMC intends to build. Petronet LNG has been reported to be considering about 3 MMT of propane storage at Dahej. AVTL's management told analysts on 3 November that "infrastructure is always welcome… India is short," that it is "talking very seriously" about strategic storage, and that it must be "done at the right cost, at the right time." As of mid-November 2026, no strategic-storage contract has been announced by any independent operator.

In plain terms

The government wants roughly three times more LPG in storage than the country's ports can hold today. The cheapest builder is a private company that already owns a third of the import tankage; the default builders are the state oil companies, which have historically paid two to four times more per tonne. Whichever way the decision goes, it will be the largest single change to the industry's size in its history. Until a contract is signed, it remains a possibility, not a revenue line.

How the toll gate held up. The war was a useful test of the terminal model. AVTL's gas revenue fell only 3.5% year on year in Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), to ₹107.2 crore, on throughput of about 0.9 MMT, and recovered to ₹142.0 crore (+12.5%) on 1.1 MMT in Q2, while India's imports were down 48% in the first quarter and 38% in the second. Management gave three reasons: none of the group's chartered ships was stuck in the Gulf; sourcing was diversified (partly through ITOCHU); and the parent's distribution business drew stock out of the tanks quickly, freeing space. Take-or-pay minimums cushioned the rest. AVTL's share of India's LPG imports therefore rose from about 18% in FY26 to about 31% in H1 FY27 (INDICATIVE). The OMCs' own terminals absorbed most of the import collapse, because refinery LPG replaced imported LPG at their inland plants.

The resilience cuts both ways. It shows that a multi-port operator with VLGC-capable jetties and diversified sourcing is more robust than a single-port terminal. It also shows that the import pool can shrink fast when refiners are told to make more LPG. If the higher refinery yield of 2026 becomes structural, India's import dependence falls from about two-thirds towards half, and the national pool of throughput that independent terminals compete for is smaller than pre-war forecasts assumed. For the industry, the strategic-storage push is the offsetting opportunity: storage that is paid for being full, rather than for being used.

04

Beyond LPG: chemicals, edible oil, fuels and ammonia

Liquid tanks serve four other import streams. None is growing fast in tonnes; the industry's liquid growth comes from location and mix more than from market volume.

LPG dominates the headlines, but the liquid side of the industry, chemicals, edible oils and petroleum products, is the steadier earner. AVTL's liquid revenue grew 27.8% in FY26 and 25.9% in H1 FY27, and the liquid segment earns a higher return on capital than gas. Understanding where that liquid demand comes from, and how fragile it is, matters as much as the LPG story.

Chemicals. India imports most of its methanol, acetic acid, phenol, benzene, toluene and xylenes, all of its styrene monomer and many intermediates. Each imported tonne needs port tankage before it reaches a factory in Gujarat, Maharashtra or Tamil Nadu. CRISIL put India's bulk liquid-chemicals trade at about 15.7 MMT in FY24, almost unchanged from 15.8 MMT in FY19, and expects about 18–19 MMT by FY29, roughly 3% a year. In value terms the trend is weaker still: Commerce Ministry data show organic-chemical imports (HS chapter 29) falling for three straight years, from US$29.0 bn in FY23 to US$25.4 bn in FY26, as Chinese oversupply pushed prices down. Tank demand follows tonnes, not dollars, so falling values are partly a price effect, but the tonnage series is flat too. More on the chemical cycle in commodity chemicals.

Petroleum products. Coastal cargo at the major ports grew 8.3% in FY26, and Deendayal's coastal traffic jumped 39%. AVTL's Pipavav terminal (36,000 tonnes, commissioned on 10 August 2026) is anchored by a 15-year take-or-pay contract with Hindustan Zinc for part of its capacity, to serve a new phosphate-fertiliser plant. Fertiliser import values jumped 76% in FY26 as prices rose. Section 19 covers ammonia as an energy-transition play.

In plain terms

Outside LPG, the cargoes that fill Indian tanks are not growing much in weight: chemical imports have been flat for five years and edible-oil imports move with duty changes. A tank company can still grow its liquid revenue quickly by owning tanks in the right places (Mumbai-region tanks rent for about twice Kandla's rate) and by winning share from small operators. That is a different growth engine from a rising tide, and it has a natural limit.

What a tank costs, and where ₹100 of revenue goes

Capital is the dominant input. The cost of a tonne or a cubic metre of storage varies tenfold across builders, and that spread decides who earns a return.

A terminal has few operating costs. Its main inputs are a waterfront plot (leased from a port authority or private port), steel and construction (tank shells, spheres, refrigerated double-wall tanks, pipelines, fire systems), power and nitrogen (for blanketing, heating and the boil-off compressors that keep refrigerated LPG and ammonia cold), a small workforce and a great deal of capital. Tanks have a design life of about 40 years. AVTL's employee costs were ₹51.8 crore in FY26, 5.6% of revenue; depreciation and finance cost together took 34%.

Capital cost per unit. This is the number that decides returns, and it varies enormously. For LPG import terminals, the spread runs from under ₹1 lakh to over ₹8 lakh per tonne of static capacity. Part of that reflects technology (refrigerated tanks for VLGC imports are cheaper per tonne at scale than small pressurised terminals), part reflects timing and site, and part reflects who builds. The Aegis group, which builds terminals and then sells them to AVTL, has consistently built at the bottom of the range.

That pattern, rising EBITDA and flat-to-falling profit during a build-out, is typical of infrastructure businesses and will persist while AVTL keeps borrowing faster than new capacity fills.

For a typical independent liquid-only operator, the picture is less flattering. Companies such as Ganesh Benzoplast report EBITDA margins of 20–30%, because they mix storage with lower-margin businesses, pay higher land rents per cubic metre, and run at lower utilisation (Ganesh Benzoplast reported about 60%). IMC, India's second-largest independent liquid operator by capacity, reported negative operating EBITDA in FY23 on revenue of just ₹253 crore against more than 1.1 mn cbm of tankage, suggesting either very low tariffs, heavy costs or both. Scale, location and contract quality separate a 75% margin from a 20% one in the same business.

Who owns India's tanks: one listed pure-play, a crowded unlisted field

Independent liquid storage is fragmented across dozens of port-level owners; LPG import storage is split between the state oil companies, their joint ventures and one multi-port independent.

India's port storage falls into four ownership groups. The state oil marketing companies (Indian Oil, Bharat Petroleum, Hindustan Petroleum) own most LPG import terminals, product depots and 214 bottling plants, as cost centres rather than businesses. Joint ventures between OMCs and international partners run several LPG terminals: IndianOil Petronas at Ennore and Haldia, South Asia LPG (HPCL with TotalEnergies) in a rock cavern at Vizag, and Adani–Total's terminal at Mundra. Independent and third-party operators rent tanks to anyone: AVTL, IMC, Cisternina (KKR), Indian Oiltanking (now Adani), and dozens of single-port tank farms at Kandla, JNPA and Haldia. And the state holds strategic crude through Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Ltd (ISPRL). Only AVTL among them is a listed pure-play; Aegis Logistics, its listed parent, mixes terminals with LPG trading and distribution.

Liquid storage. On CRISIL's basis, AVTL is the largest third-party liquid tank operator, with 1.49 mn cbm and a 25.53% share at December 2024, and 1.67 mn cbm today. IMC Ltd, the unlisted Kolkata group that began as India Molasses Company, is second with about 1.13 mn cbm across 14 ports, the widest footprint in the country. KKR-controlled Cisternina is assembling a platform around Ganesh Benzoplast's terminals (about 500,000 kl including tanks under construction) and targets 1.5 mn kl. Adani Ports, which bought Oiltanking's 49.38% of Indian Oiltanking (IOTL) for ₹1,050 crore in 2022, claims to be "India's largest third-party liquid storage company" with 3.6 mn KL; but 1.9 mn KL of IOTL's 2.4 mn KL is build-own-operate-transfer (BOOT) tankage dedicated to Indian Oil at Paradip, which CRISIL's definition excludes. Both claims are true on their own definitions. The accurate description of AVTL is "the largest independent, multi-port tank-terminal operator on CRISIL's basis."

In plain terms

Owners behind the owners. Several global groups sit behind the Indian players. Royal Vopak owns 42.23% of AVTL and is the world's largest independent tank-storage company. KKR's Asia Pacific Infrastructure Fund III controls Cisternina, which agreed on 30 September 2026 to buy Ganesh Benzoplast's storage and rail-logistics business for ₹1,154 crore. Oiltanking of Germany, once a significant Indian player through its IOC joint venture, exited in 2022 when it sold to Adani. The industry is attracting infrastructure capital because the assets are long-lived, contracted and scarce, and because India is one of the few large markets where import dependence is still rising.

Imports and China. Unlike manufacturing sub-sectors, storage faces no import competition: a tank in Kandla cannot be replaced by a tank in Singapore. The industry is a beneficiary of import dependence. China matters through trade flows. A Chinese chemical surplus means more cheap imports into India, more tank demand and more pressure on Indian chemical makers; Indian anti-dumping duties then cut those imports and the tank demand with them. The real competitive threat is domestic substitution, from new refineries, crackers and methanol plants, and from customers who build their own tanks.

In plain terms

Rules of the waterfront: regulation and policy

Four layers of rules govern the industry: port land and concessions, hazardous-materials safety, LPG pricing and marketing, and since 2026 a strategic-storage policy still being written.

Ports and land. The Major Port Authorities Act, 2021, in force from 3 November 2021, replaced the 1963 Major Port Trusts Act. It abolished the Tariff Authority for Major Ports for the 12 major ports and let each port board set its own tariffs and lease land under its own policy. Land is allotted under the Land Policy for Major Ports (2014, with revised guidelines in 2023) by tender or auction, on either an upfront premium or an annual lease rent, for periods typically up to 30 years. Rent is revised periodically with reference to market value; a Deendayal Port dispute record describes rent of "6% of market value per annum… revised every five years." Non-major ports such as Pipavav and Mundra fall under state maritime boards, now governed by the Indian Ports Act, 2025, which replaced the 1908 act. Port concessions follow model agreements, usually for 30 years. The ports side of these rules is covered in ports; section 09 covers what they mean for terminal owners.

Safety and environment. Every terminal needs licences from the Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation (PESO) under the Petroleum Act and Rules, and LPG and ammonia tanks fall under the Static and Mobile Pressure Vessels (Unfired) Rules. Oil Industry Safety Directorate standards (OISD-144 for LPG installations, OISD-118 for layouts) govern design. Hazardous chemicals fall under the 1989 MSIHC Rules. Coastal Regulation Zone clearances and state pollution-board consents add to the list. Ammonia adds toxic-gas emergency planning, including off-site plans, which is one reason only two or three third-party ammonia terminals exist. These rules are a real barrier to entry: a new LPG or ammonia terminal takes years of approvals as well as construction.

LPG policy. Domestic LPG prices are administered: a 14.2 kg cylinder cost ₹942 in Delhi on 1 August 2026, and the OMCs absorb the gap to import parity, compensated periodically by the government. PMUY beneficiaries receive a targeted subsidy of ₹300 per cylinder for up to 12 refills a year; PMUY subsidy cost ₹12,212 crore in FY26. Private companies may import LPG and sell it for non-domestic use ("parallel marketing"); Aegis is the largest. The 2026 crisis proved the state's rationing toolkit: the Control Order to maximise refinery LPG (production reached a record of about 92,000 tonnes a day by late May), the commercial supply cap, and household priority. Aadhaar biometric authentication for subsidised refills started on 1 October 2026 and may trim recorded household demand by curbing diversion to commercial use. A PNG incentive scheme approved on 18 August 2026 gives city-gas companies 200 scm of cheap domestic gas for each new billed household connection, nudging urban kitchens away from LPG (see city gas distribution).

In plain terms

The government controls the price of household cooking gas, decides who gets supplied first in a shortage and can order refineries to make more of it. A terminal owner cannot change any of that. What it can do is be the cheapest, most reliable place to land a cargo, so that whichever way policy moves, the tonnes still need its tanks.

Strategic storage: the 2026 change. Until this year, India's strategic stock policy covered only crude oil. ISPRL's Phase 1 caverns, built between 2008 and 2018 at Vizag, Mangalore and Padur, hold 5.33 MMT, about nine to ten days of crude consumption; PIB reported them full in 2021. Phase 2 (6.5 MMT at Chandikhol in Odisha and a second Padur cavern) was approved in July 2021 as a public-private partnership. The Padur-II concession was signed on 1 October 2025, and about 400 acres have been acquired at Chandikhol, where the request for proposals was still under review in August 2026, with a reported cost of about ₹9,000 crore. Feasibility studies are complete for a 1.75 MMT Mangalore extension, and Bina (Madhya Pradesh) and Bikaner (Rajasthan) are being studied. A parliamentary committee has urged 90 days of crude cover.

Trade and tax. Customs-bonded storage lets traders hold imported cargo without paying duty until it leaves the tank, which makes terminals part of the trade-finance chain. Storage services attract 18% GST; AVTL is contesting a ₹50.8 crore disallowance of input-tax credit, its only material contingent liability. The policy direction is, on balance, favourable to the industry: more strategic storage, faster port approvals under the 2021 act and continued import dependence. The two policy risks are land and the build-versus-contract choice. Port boards now set rents themselves, and the state oil companies, which are the main beneficiaries of any strategic-storage budget, have a long habit of building their own tanks.

The landlord's reset: ports, leases and concessions Deep dive

Almost every Indian terminal stands on leased port land. Rents reset off market value, leases expire on fixed dates, and at Pipavav AVTL's contracts outrun its land tenure.

A terminal owner owns its tanks but rarely the ground under them. Major ports lease plots for up to 30 years; private ports sub-lease land within their own concession, which itself ends on a fixed date. The lease typically sets an annual rent with periodic revisions, a minimum guaranteed throughput (MGT) that the terminal must move across the port's jetties each year, and bank guarantees to secure both. When the rent resets or the lease ends, the landlord has leverage that the tenant's take-or-pay contracts with its own customers do not offset. This is the industry's largest non-operating risk, and until AVTL's prospectus was published in 2025 it was almost invisible to outside investors.

The Pipavav cliff. This is the single most important lease fact in the industry. Gujarat Pipavav Port, run by APM Terminals, holds a concession from the Gujarat Maritime Board that ends on 29 September 2028. All five of AVTL's Pipavav sub-leases end on the same day. Yet Pipavav now carries some of AVTL's most important assets: 70,800 tonnes of LPG storage with a 32-bay rail gantry, 116,620 cbm of liquid tanks, the new 36,000-tonne ammonia terminal backed by a 15-year take-or-pay contract with Hindustan Zinc, and a new 15-year take-or-pay contract to move more than 0.5 MTPA of petroleum products by rail. AVTL's customer contracts run well past 2038; its land tenure runs less than two years from today. The Gujarat Maritime Board sent GPPL a comfort letter in late August 2026 to begin processing an extension, giving it 90 days to file an investment and business plan. AVTL has disclosed nothing about the terms on which its sub-leases would roll over.

In plain terms

Imagine signing a 15-year contract to supply a factory from a warehouse whose lease expires in two years. You are probably safe, because the landlord wants the rent and the port wants the traffic. But you have no contractual guarantee, and when the landlord renews its own lease with the state it may want better terms from you. That is AVTL's position at Pipavav: the rent is tiny today; the risk is what the renewal costs.

The likeliest outcome is an extension. Pipavav is a profitable port, the state wants investment, and an extension process has formally begun. Port concessions in India are usually extended with new investment commitments, often with a higher revenue share for the state; Mundra's concession runs to February 2031 under similar arrangements. The risk is in the terms. If GPPL pays more to GMB, it may seek more from its sub-lessees, and a terminal that cannot be moved has little bargaining power. The ports report covers the concession-renewal theme across the sector (ports).

Kandla's expired leases and the Deendayal dispute. How leases appear in the accounts. Under Ind AS 116, lease payments are capitalised: the present value of future rent is booked as a lease liability (₹1,626 crore at September 2026, of which ₹1,501 crore non-current) and a matching right-of-use asset, and the rent disappears from operating costs. Instead, the income statement shows depreciation on the right-of-use asset and interest on the lease liability, both below EBITDA. Two consequences follow. First, reported EBITDA overstates cash operating profit by the amount of rent, roughly ₹120 crore a year for AVTL. Second, lease liabilities are about 40% of AVTL's total debt-like obligations, which is why net debt must always be quoted both with and without them (₹2,164 crore without, ₹3,790 crore with, at September 2026).

Why the structure exists, and what it implies. The arrangement has a commercial logic. Aegis has a long record of building LPG terminals cheaply and quickly, and keeping construction risk in the parent spares AVTL's public shareholders the risk of delays and cost overruns. Vopak, which must approve the deals as the other promoter, has its own interest in fair prices. The independent directors approve each transaction and the shareholders vote on the large ones. The prices paid, about ₹0.94–1.18 lakh per tonne for LPG, are well below what the OMCs pay to build. But three implications follow for anyone analysing the industry through AVTL. First, AVTL's "capex per unit" is a negotiated transfer price, not a market construction cost. Second, the parent captures a development margin that does not appear in AVTL's accounts. Third, because Aegis owns 44.7% of AVTL but 100% of the developer, its incentives on price differ from those of AVTL's minority holders, who own about 13%.

Revenue runs the other way too. In FY26 Aegis group companies paid AVTL about ₹146 crore, roughly 16% of revenue: Aegis Gas (LPG) about ₹74 crore in throughput and storage fees, Aegis Logistics about ₹49 crore, and Sea Lord Containers about ₹23 crore. The parent is at once AVTL's developer, a seller of assets, a customer, a lessor (₹3.1 crore of lease rent) and the operator of competing terminals in Mumbai.

In plain terms

Aegis acts like a property developer that builds warehouses and sells them to a listed landlord it half-owns. The landlord gets finished, working assets at prices below what the state oil companies pay. The developer gets a sale price it helped set. Outside shareholders own a small slice of the landlord and rely on the independent directors and on Vopak, the other half-owner, to keep the prices fair. Nothing about this is unusual in Indian infrastructure, but it means the cost of AVTL's growth is set in a negotiation, not a market.

The overhang. Indian listing rules require promoters to bring their holding down to 75% within three years of listing, so AVTL's promoters, at 86.94%, must sell or dilute about 11.9 percentage points, roughly 13.2 crore shares worth about ₹3,790 crore at ₹286, by 2 June 2028. In plain terms

Only about one share in eight of AVTL trades freely. By mid-2028 the two owners must put roughly as many shares again into the market. If they sell their own shares, the cash goes to them; if AVTL issues new shares, the cash funds new terminals. Either way, the market has to absorb a lot of stock, and the price it pays will be set by how convincingly the new terminals are filling.

Is AVTL the only vehicle? The last governance question is scope. Aegis Logistics keeps its Mumbai (Trombay) liquid and LPG terminals (about 275,000 cbm and 21,000 tonnes) outside AVTL; the chairman said on 3 November 2026 that "Mumbai… is part of Aegis Logistics and not part of Aegis Vopak." Both promoters have strong reasons to keep AVTL's valuation high, and neither has a reason to sell quickly.

Five decades, four shocks: how the industry got here

From state monopoly to private LPG imports, strategic caverns, a listed roll-up and a war that put storage on the national agenda.

State monopoly, 1970s–1990s. For most of independent India's history, petroleum imports moved through installations owned by the state oil companies and the port trusts. Private tank farms were small and mostly served chemicals and edible oils at Kandla, which grew into India's main liquid-chemicals and vegetable-oil port. After the 1990s liberalisation, Indian Oil formed Indian Oiltanking with Germany's Oiltanking, and private operators such as IMC expanded port by port.

The private LPG chain, 1990s–2000s. Aegis Logistics, whose roots go back more than 50 years, built India's first private LPG import terminals at Mumbai and then Pipavav, and pioneered auto-gas and parallel-marketed LPG once private imports for non-domestic use were allowed. This was the seed of today's AVTL: an independent company that could land LPG for anyone, not just for its own marketing arm.

Strategic caverns and the Ujjwala boom, 2005–2020. ISPRL built 5.33 MMT of rock-cavern crude storage at Vizag (2015), Mangalore (2016) and Padur (2018). In May 2016 the government launched PMUY, giving free LPG connections to poor households. LPG consumption rose at about 6% a year from FY14 to FY26, and imports rose from about 8 MMT in FY15 to 21.8 MMT in FY26. Import terminals at Kandla, Haldia, Mangalore and elsewhere filled up; the industry's growth for a decade was essentially the PMUY import wave.

Joint venture, roll-up and IPO, 2021–2025. In 2021 Vopak bought into Aegis's terminal business, and the joint venture began acquiring terminals at Kandla, Haldia, Kochi, Mangalore and JNPA. The Major Port Authorities Act changed port governance the same year. In May–June 2025 AVTL listed, repaid debt and bought the Mangalore LPG terminal. Globally, the storage cycle peaked in 2022–23, when oil contango and European energy-security buying filled tanks; Vopak's occupancy then eased to about 91% by 2025, with chemical storage demand described as weak.

The war and the policy turn, 2026. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 was the industry's defining shock: LPG imports halved, rationing returned, the United States became India's main supplier, and the government issued its first LPG reserve directive. AVTL's gas volumes dipped in Q1 FY27 and recovered in Q2, while its liquid business kept growing; its EBITDA margin reached a record 78.6%. In the same months KKR's Cisternina agreed to buy Ganesh Benzoplast's storage, Aegis Logistics agreed to buy Tristar, and AVTL commissioned India's first independent ammonia terminal.

Three lessons run through this history. First, the industry grows in steps tied to policy (PMUY, port reform, strategic storage) rather than smoothly with GDP. Reading a terminal: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about a tank-storage business. Each has a formula, a benchmark and a trap.

A terminal's economics fit on one line for each business. For liquids, revenue is capacity times the share of it that is reserved, times the monthly rate per cubic metre, times twelve, plus handling extras. For gas, revenue is static capacity times turns a year times the fee per tonne, with take-or-pay minimums as a floor. EBITDA is revenue minus a largely fixed cost base. Below EBITDA come depreciation on 40-year tanks, depreciation and interest on capitalised port leases, interest on debt and tax. Each term in that chain is a KPI, and each is treated below.

In plain terms

Three questions explain a terminal: how many tanks, how full or how busy, and at what price. Everything else (margins, returns, debt) follows from those three and from what the tanks cost to build. When a quarter disappoints, it is almost always because one of the three moved, or because interest and depreciation on new tanks arrived before the customers did.

1. Installed capacity: cubic metres and tonnes

What it is. The physical size of the network: liquid tank shell capacity in cubic metres (identical to kilolitres), and LPG or ammonia static capacity in tonnes, meaning what the tanks can hold at once. Formula: the sum of nameplate tank volumes, or tonnes for pressurised and refrigerated gas. Worked example: AVTL's 1,673,383 cbm comprises Kandla 952,276, Haldia 226,890, Mangalore 193,152, Pipavav 116,620, JNPA 101,900 and Kochi 82,545; its 225,800 tonnes of LPG comprise Mangalore 82,000, Pipavav 70,800, Kandla 48,000 and Haldia 25,000. What moves it: construction, acquisitions and, occasionally, decommissioning or lease loss. Benchmark: scale matters because a multi-port network can offer customers several landing points; above about 1 mn cbm across four or more ports is national scale in India, while Vopak's 35.8 mn cbm is the global reference. The trap: never add cbm and tonnes (a tonne of LPG occupies about 1.8–2.0 cbm), and always check whether a figure is installed, commissioned or "including announced." AVTL's "2.14 mn cbm" and "355,100 t" include projects not yet built; Aegis Logistics' group figures (about 2.5 mn KL, 374,800 t) include the Mumbai terminals that are outside AVTL.

2. Utilisation and occupancy

What it is. The share of liquid capacity that customers have reserved or occupy. Formula: contracted (or occupied) cbm ÷ installed cbm, averaged over the period. 3. Liquid revenue per cubic metre (₹/cbm/month)

What it is. The average rent a liquid terminal earns on its tanks. No Indian operator publishes a tariff card, so the figure must be derived. Formula: liquid revenue ÷ average installed cbm ÷ 12 (per cbm of capacity), or ÷ (capacity × utilisation) ÷ 12 (per occupied cbm). Worked example (INDICATIVE): ₹440.5 crore ÷ 1.673 mn cbm = ₹2,633 a year, or ₹219 a month per cbm of capacity; at 75% utilisation, about ₹292 per occupied cbm a month. H1 FY27 annualised gives ₹3,051 a year (₹254 a month) on the same capacity. What moves it: location (JNPA earns about ₹6,000 a year, roughly twice the blended average), tank specification (stainless, heated or nitrogen-blanketed tanks earn more), product mix, contract length and ancillary services. Benchmark: above ₹350 per occupied cbm a month is premium; ₹220–350 is average; below ₹200 is weak. How companies compare: Aegis Logistics' group liquid division reports revenue above ₹3,600 per cbm a year (₹300 a month) including the Mumbai terminals; AVTL's blended figure is lower because Kandla, with lower rents, dominates. The trap: revenue per cbm includes throughput and handling charges, not just rent, and it is diluted by newly commissioned tanks. A rising figure on unchanged capacity, as in H1 FY27, signals better mix or pricing; a rising figure on rising capacity may only mean the new tanks are in a pricier port.

4. LPG throughput and throughput turns

What it is. Throughput is the tonnage of LPG (or ammonia) moved through the terminal in a period; turns are how many times a year the static capacity is filled and emptied. Formula: turns = annual throughput ÷ average static capacity. Worked example: AVTL handled 3.9 MMT in FY26 on static capacity that grew from 70,800 to 225,800 tonnes during the year, an average of roughly 190,000 tonnes, so about 20 turns (INDICATIVE). In H1 FY27 it handled 0.9 + 1.1 = 2.0 MMT on 225,800 tonnes, under 18 turns annualised. IndianOil Petronas handles about the same 3.9 MMT on about 70,800 tonnes, about 55 turns. What moves it: evacuation capacity (pipelines, rail gantries, tanker bays), jetty capability (VLGC berths cut freight and attract cargoes), OMC cargo allocation, import volumes and whether the terminal is used for fast throughput or for longer storage. Benchmark: good is above 40 turns, average 20–40, weak below 15. India's import terminals averaged 42.7 turns in FY24. The trap: "design turns" are not turns. CRISIL credits AVTL's terminals with 84.75 design turns and Aegis's with 86.96, against a national design average of 48.49; actual AVTL turns were about a quarter of design. Also watch for "logistics volume" at Aegis group level (5.1 MMT in FY26), which includes the Mumbai terminal outside AVTL.

What it is. The price a terminal charges per tonne of LPG crossing its jetty. Formula: stated tariff, or derived as gas revenue ÷ throughput. 6. EBITDA margin

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax as a share of revenue. Formula: (revenue − staff − other operating costs) ÷ revenue. Under Ind AS 116, port lease rent is excluded from operating costs. Worked example: Q2 FY27 revenue ₹270.8 crore, EBITDA ₹212 crore, margin 78.6%, up from 74.7% a year earlier. Benchmark: for a pure terminal, above 70% is good, 50–70% average, below 50% weak. How companies compare: AVTL 74–79%; Vopak 58% proportional; Ganesh Benzoplast about 23%; IPPL 11.6% (books LPG sales). 8. Segment return on capital employed

What it is. The pre-tax return each business earns on the capital it uses. Formula: annualised segment result (after depreciation, before interest and unallocated costs) ÷ segment capital employed. Worked example (INDICATIVE): H1 FY27 liquid segment result ₹142.3 crore × 2 ÷ ₹2,459 crore = 11.6%; gas ₹148.8 crore × 2 ÷ ₹4,605 crore = 6.5%; total 8.2%. Screener's company-wide ROCE is 7.6%. What moves it: utilisation and turns (the numerator), acquisitions and capex (the denominator, which jumps before revenue arrives), and depreciation policy. Benchmark: above 13% pre-tax is good (Vopak targets a 13–17% operating cash return and earned 16.4% in the first nine months of 2026); 9–13% average; below 8% weak. How companies compare: IPPL 46% (FY23, an old, depreciated, fast-turning base); Ganesh Benzoplast 17.6% (FY24); Aegis Logistics 13.3%; AVTL 7.6%. The trap: segment results exclude about ₹42 crore a year of unallocated costs; capital employed is a period-end figure, which understates returns right after a large acquisition; and screener flags that AVTL "might be capitalising the interest cost," which would lift both capital employed and future depreciation. Vopak's "operating cash return" is a cash measure before depreciation, so it runs higher than an EBIT-based ROCE on the same assets.

9. Capex per cubic metre and per tonne

What it is. What a unit of storage cost to build or buy. Formula: project cost ÷ cbm (liquids) or ÷ tonnes of static capacity (gas); better still, ÷ tonnes of annual throughput the terminal can realistically handle. Worked example: AVTL Kandla LPG, ₹450 crore for 48,000 tonnes = ₹93,800 per tonne of storage, or ₹1,130 per tonne of its 4.0 MTPA design throughput; New Mangalore, ₹968 crore for 82,000 tonnes = ₹1.18 lakh per tonne. Aegis Mumbai liquids, ₹125 crore for 64,000 cbm = ₹19,500 per cbm. What moves it: technology (refrigerated versus pressurised), site preparation and marine works, steel prices, whether jetties and pipelines are included, and, for acquisitions, the negotiated price. Benchmark: LPG below ₹1.2 lakh per tonne is good, ₹1.2–2.5 lakh average, above ₹3 lakh weak; greenfield liquid tanks below ₹15,000 per cbm good, ₹15,000–25,000 average, above ₹25,000 weak. The trap: AVTL's figures for group-built terminals are related-party transfer prices; and cost per tonne of storage flatters large refrigerated tanks that turn slowly. Cost per tonne of actual throughput is the real test of a terminal's capital efficiency, and on that measure AVTL's new terminals look less cheap until turns rise.

12. Customer concentration and related-party revenue

What it is. How dependent the terminal is on its largest customers, and on its own promoter group. Formula: largest customer's revenue ÷ total; top-5 and top-10 shares; related-party revenue ÷ total. 13. Lease tenure remaining

What it is. How long the terminal can stay on its land. Formula: years to lease expiry, ideally weighted by rent or, better, by asset value at each site. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): weighting AVTL's December 2024 rent roll by years remaining gives roughly 18 years: Kandla's main plots run to 2045–46, Mangalore to 2045–51, JNPA to 2053. Weighted by assets, the picture is worse, because Pipavav, with LPG, liquid and ammonia assets and two 15-year take-or-pay contracts, has under two years left, and three Kandla CRL plots are already expired and held over. What moves it: renewals, concession extensions (Pipavav's depends on Gujarat Pipavav Port's own extension), and new leases with fresh 30-year terms. Benchmark: more than 20 years remaining on core sites is good, 10–20 average, under 10 weak; any core site under five years is a red flag until renewed. The trap: rent-weighted tenure flatters a portfolio whose cheapest land carries the most valuable assets, as at Pipavav; and a lease that runs 30 years may still have rent resets every five. Because AVTL does not report lease terms in its annual report, the prospectus table (December 2024) remains the only port-by-port source; any new site acquired since then, such as the Haldia LPG terminal or the JNPA expansion land, adds tenure that outsiders cannot yet see. 14. Cash conversion (CFO ÷ EBITDA)

What it is. How much of EBITDA turns into operating cash flow. Formula: cash from operations ÷ EBITDA. Worked example: FY26 cash from operations ₹702 crore ÷ EBITDA ₹686.5 crore = 102%. Three lessons fall out of the arithmetic. In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading this industry come from mixing bases: last year's numbers before and after a restatement, cubic metres and tonnes, the parent and the subsidiary, debt with and without rent. Before comparing any two numbers, check that they measure the same thing over the same period for the same entity.

How to read a terminal company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Segment revenue first (one minute). Pull liquid and gas revenue and compare with the same quarter last year and the previous quarter. Liquid growth on unchanged capacity means better rates or mix; gas growth should be checked against throughput.
  2. Gas throughput (one minute). Find the MMT figure on the call or in the deck (Q1 FY27 0.9, Q2 FY27 1.1). Divide gas revenue by throughput: a result far above ₹1,175–1,300 a tonne suggests take-or-pay shortfall payments or ammonia income; below suggests discounting.
  3. Turns (one minute). Annualise throughput and divide by static capacity. Below 20 is a red flag for the new terminals; a rise towards 30–40 is the single best sign of the bull case.
  4. Liquid revenue per cbm (one minute). Annualised liquid revenue ÷ capacity. Track the trend; a fall after new capacity is normal for a quarter or two.
  5. EBITDA and margin (one minute). A margin near 78% has little upside; a drop of more than two points without a new-terminal explanation is a warning.
  6. Segment results and capital employed (one minute). Compute annualised segment EBIT ÷ capital employed for each segment. Liquids above 11% and gas rising from 6–8% is the path to watch.
  7. Finance cost and debt (one minute). Finance cost ÷ EBITDA, and the balance-sheet split between bank debt, leases and short-term borrowings. Rising short-term debt without an equity plan is a red flag.
  8. Profit after minorities (one minute). Use EPS × shares for attributable profit; the consolidated PAT line includes minority shares.
  9. Related-party notes (one minute). Look for new framework agreements, payments to the group for acquisitions (₹547 crore in H1 FY27), and changes in related-party revenue.
  10. Project calendar (one minute). Check commissioning dates against the last call: JNPA phase-1 liquids (slipped from Q1 to Q3 FY27), JNPA LPG, Kandla CRL-4, Kochi, the KGPL connection and the Pipavav VLGC jetty. Slippage delays revenue but not interest.

Who is winning: scale, speed and price

Three maps place the one listed member against the reference operators: how big versus how busy, how wide versus how large, and how much return versus how high a multiple.

With a single listed company, "who is winning" is a question about positions in the industry rather than a league table of stocks. Three positions matter. In LPG, the winners turn their tanks fastest; scale without speed is expensive. In liquids, the winners hold the most capacity across the most ports, because customers value multiple landing points and because single-port operators are exposed to one landlord. And in the stock market, the winners are those whose valuation is supported by returns; a high multiple on a low return is a bet on the future, not a reward for the present. The maps below put AVTL (the member, shown in green and labelled) against the reference operators on each.

The first map captures the gas story in one picture. AVTL owns about three times as much LPG storage as the next operator but turns it about a third as often as IndianOil Petronas. That is not inefficiency so much as timing: two-thirds of AVTL's storage arrived in the last 18 months, and large refrigerated tanks are slower to fill than small pressurised ones. But it means AVTL's gas segment wins on capacity and loses, for now, on productivity. If the pipeline and rail connections arrive and turns climb towards the national average, AVTL moves straight up the chart into the "large and fast" corner that no Indian operator occupies today.

The second map shows why the liquid market is consolidating. No operator yet combines AVTL's size with IMC's reach, and the operators at the bottom-left, single-port farms with a few hundred thousand cubic metres, are the natural targets. AVTL wins on capacity and on market position within its ports (two-thirds of Kandla's third-party tankage, over half of Haldia's, Mangalore's and Kochi's). Its gaps are the south-east (Chennai, Ennore, Vizag) and, until J2 is built, the Mumbai region, where tanks earn the highest rents and where Aegis's own Mumbai terminal and Ganesh Benzoplast, now heading to Cisternina, are entrenched.

The third map is the investor's map. On returns, AVTL is ordinary: its 7.6% ROCE is in line with Kinder Morgan's and Stolt-Nielsen's and below Vopak's. On valuation, it is in a category of its own, at about five times the multiple of every global peer. The justification offered by bullish analysts is growth: AVTL's EBITDA compounded at 44% a year from FY23 to FY26, against single digits for global peers, and PL expects about 48% a year to FY29. The map makes the condition plain: AVTL's dot must move right (returns rising as turns and utilisation improve) faster than it moves down (the multiple falling as growth slows) for today's price to work.

JNPA phase-1 liquids (about 100,000 cbm) in Q3 FY27; JNPA LPG "next year," lifting group LPG handling capacity from about 20 to 29 MTPA. At least 25% annual growth in LPG throughput. Ammonia take-or-pay with Hindustan Zinc starting later in FY27, at 20–25% first-year utilisation, with a target of eight to ten ammonia terminals by 2030. New business lines under discussion: strategic storage ("talking very seriously"), inland depots and industrial terminals. "Generally speaking, we have a better second half."

What management did not give. Segment EBITDA for the quarter ("we gave only consolidated EBITDA"); any strategic-storage contract; the take-or-pay share of revenue or contract tenors; the identity of the 25% customer; the terms of the Pipavav sub-lease renewal; or whether AVTL or Aegis will book the ammonia distribution margin. Ratings are supportive: India Ratings rates AVTL IND AA/Positive and IND A1+, and it has ₹1,690 crore of listed NCDs secured on its Mangalore, Kandla and Pipavav assets. Capital commitments were ₹1,676.5 crore at March 2026.

In plain terms

The company is doing what it said it would: adding tanks, keeping costs low and growing revenue in the mid-teens even through a war. Profits are not yet growing because the new tanks were paid for with borrowed money and the gas terminals are not yet busy. The next two or three quarters, with JNPA's first tanks and the ammonia contract starting, should show whether revenue begins to outrun interest.

Why 47× EBITDA: leverage, funding and the valuation gap Deep dive

The stock market values India's only listed terminal company at five times the global multiple. Here is what that price assumes and how the build-out is being paid for.

At ₹286 a share on 13 November 2026, AVTL's market capitalisation was ₹31,688 crore. Add ₹2,495 crore of bank and NCD borrowings, ₹1,626 crore of port-lease liabilities and ₹142 crore of minority interests, subtract ₹331 crore of cash, and the enterprise value is about ₹35,620 crore. That is 47.5 times trailing EBITDA of ₹750 crore, 51.9 times FY26 EBITDA and 45.4 times annualised H1 FY27 EBITDA. On earnings, the stock trades at 104 times trailing attributable profit and 7.1 times book value. Since listing, its EV/EBITDA has averaged about 33 times and peaked near 48; the current level is at the top of its own short history.

What the price assumes (ILLUSTRATIVE). Reverse the arithmetic. In plain terms

Buying AVTL at today's price is like paying for a house on the rent it will earn in three years' time, after the extension has been built and let. If the extension is finished on time and fully let, the price was fair; if it is late or half-empty, the buyer has overpaid. Meanwhile the owner needs to borrow or raise more money to finish the extension, and it is close to its borrowing limit.

Why the gap with Vopak may persist, and why it may not. The case for a premium is real. AVTL's EBITDA grew 44% a year from FY23 to FY26, against single digits for Vopak; India's import dependence and storage deficit are structural; AVTL's margins are higher; and a strategic-storage contract could add a decade of growth. Indian infrastructure stocks with long growth runways have historically traded at high multiples. The case against is equally clear. Growth so far has been largely acquired, much of it from the promoter group; the returns on new gas assets are below the cost of capital; the float is small and about to double; and Vopak itself, the most informed holder, values its 42.23% stake inside its own market value of about €6.0–6.2 bn at under ten times EBITDA. AVTL's market value is about half Vopak's on roughly one-seventeenth of its EBITDA. The gap closes either through AVTL's EBITDA tripling, as the bulls expect, or through the multiple compressing. A reader should ask what multiple AVTL deserves in FY30, when growth slows towards single digits, and work backwards from there.

Rates matter too. The RBI's increase of the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, with the rupee near ₹94.9 to the dollar, raises the cost of the floating and short-term debt that bridges AVTL's acquisitions and makes long-duration growth stocks more sensitive to the discount rate. A company whose value lies mostly in cash flows after FY29 is more exposed to that than one earning its keep today.

The global lens: what a mature network looks like

Royal Vopak owns 42% of AVTL and is the world's largest independent tank-storage company. Its numbers show where an Indian network might end up and what the market pays for it.

Royal Vopak stores 35.8 mn cbm across its subsidiaries (20.9 mn cbm on a proportional basis) in about 23 countries. In 2025 it reported IFRS revenue of €1,299 mn, proportional revenue of €1,916 mn and proportional EBITDA of €1,184 mn, a 58% margin, with proportional occupancy of 91.4% and a proportional operating cash return of 15.6%. Net profit of €604 mn included the €113 mn gain on AVTL's listing and a €181 mn impairment reversal. In the first nine months of 2026, proportional EBITDA rose to €906.4 mn, occupancy held at about 91%, the operating cash return reached 16.4%, and the company raised its full-year outlook to €1,200–1,220 mn on 4 November. Net debt is 2.42 times EBITDA. Vopak plans to invest €4 bn by 2030 in gas, industrial and energy-transition infrastructure, has committed about €650 mn to energy-transition projects, and is returning about €1.7 bn to shareholders, including a €500 mn buyback.

Ammonia, hydrogen and the next tank: transition and disruption

The same waterfront that stores LPG and methanol today could store ammonia, biofuels and carbon dioxide tomorrow. The transition is an option for terminal owners; substitution of imports is the threat.

Ammonia, today's opportunity. India imports about 2.3–2.6 MMT of ammonia a year, roughly 15% of its needs, and CRISIL expects about 3.3 MMT by FY29. Almost all of it goes to fertiliser plants near the coast, which mostly own their own refrigerated tanks. AVTL's 36,000-tonne Pipavav terminal, commissioned on 10 August 2026 at a cost management puts at about US$50 mn (it said "₹525 crore"; at ₹94.5 per dollar that is closer to ₹475 crore), is the first independent ammonia terminal of its kind in India. A 15-year take-or-pay contract with Hindustan Zinc covers part of its capacity for a new phosphate-fertiliser plant; management expects 20–25% utilisation in the first full year against a theoretical throughput of about 1 MMT, and storage fees of about ₹2,750 a tonne, roughly 2.5 times LPG's. ITOCHU paid ₹80.32 crore for 10% of the ammonia subsidiary, valuing the equity at about ₹803 crore, and will rise to 25% within three years. Management talks of eight to ten ammonia terminals by 2030, with Kandla next; PL models ammonia storage rising to 144,000 tonnes by FY29.

The economics are promising but small at first (ILLUSTRATIVE). At 250,000 tonnes a year, storage-only revenue would be about ₹69 crore and EBITDA about ₹55 crore, an 11% EBITDA yield on a ₹500 crore asset. If AVTL also captured the distribution margin of about ₹7,000 a tonne that management described, gross margin would rise by about ₹175 crore; but whether AVTL or its parent books that margin is unresolved, and it is a trading margin with trading risk. The first take-or-pay revenue should appear in Q3 FY27.

Hydrogen, tomorrow's option. The National Green Hydrogen Mission targets 5 MMTPA of green hydrogen by 2030, with hubs at Kandla, Paradip and Tuticorin. Most green hydrogen intended for export would travel as green ammonia, which needs export-side refrigerated storage at the port. AVTL signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding with Larsen & Toubro on 14 August 2026 for the potential development of ammonia terminals at Kandla, near a planned L&T green-ammonia facility. Nothing is contracted, and Indian green-ammonia export projects have yet to reach final investment decisions at scale. Methanol bunkering at Kandla, ethanol storage for E20 blending and liquefied CO₂ are smaller adjacent options; existing liquid tanks can often be repurposed. Vopak has committed about €650 mn globally to energy-transition infrastructure, which shows the direction of the industry's capital.

The disruptions. The largest threat to terminal volumes is not technology but substitution of imports. New refineries and petrochemical complexes reduce imports of LPG and chemicals at the margin; PL estimates refinery LPG output rises only from 13.1 to about 14 MMT by FY29, but the 2026 Control Order showed that yields can rise much faster under instruction. City gas and induction cooking erode urban LPG: active LPG connections fell for the first time in 2026, and a new incentive gives city-gas companies cheap gas for each new household connection (see city gas distribution). Pipelines cut both ways: they raise the value of a connected terminal but also let the OMCs serve more bottling plants from their own terminals. Ships are getting larger, which favours terminals with VLGC-capable jetties, such as Kandla today and Pipavav once APM's jetty is ready (see shipping). Finally, safety: LPG and ammonia accidents are rare but severe, and a single incident could bring licence suspensions and higher insurance costs across the industry.

In plain terms

The tanks of 2035 may hold ammonia and biofuels as well as LPG and chemicals, and the companies that own well-located waterfront land will be the ones that get to store them. But the transition is slow, the first ammonia terminal will run at a quarter of capacity in its first year, and the bigger near-term risk is that India makes more of its own LPG and chemicals and imports less.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the toll gate

The industry's structural case is strong; the listed company's price already assumes most of it.

The bull case

  • Structural import dependence: about two-thirds of India's LPG and most bulk chemicals are imported, and every tonne must pass through a shore tank.
  • A storage deficit made visible: eight to ten days of LPG in port terminals against a 30-day directive and a reported 4 MMT plan; the cheapest builder of storage is the private sector.
  • Operating leverage in place: AVTL's LPG tanks run at about 18–20 turns against a national average of 43; reaching 40 turns would add about ₹630 crore of revenue with no new capex (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Location pricing in liquids: revenue per cbm up 16% on unchanged capacity; JNPA J2 adds 318,100 cbm at premium rents.
  • Consolidation runway: fragmented single-port operators, rising land costs and private capital (Vopak, KKR, Adani) point to a few national platforms.
  • Resilience proven: the fee model absorbed the 2026 war with a single soft quarter.

The bear case

  • Valuation: about 47× EBITDA and 104× earnings already discount something close to the broker's FY29 EBITDA, against a 9.5× global benchmark.
  • Returns below cost of capital: gas segment about 6.5% pre-tax; group ROCE 7.6%; turns below the national average.
  • Funding and supply: net debt 5.1× EBITDA including leases, ₹1,959 crore of short-term debt, ~₹580 crore of headroom to the cap, and ~₹3,800 crore of promoter stock due by June 2028.
  • Related-party architecture: assets bought from the promoter at negotiated prices; 16% of revenue from group companies; scope of the listed vehicle unclear.
  • Landlord risk: Pipavav sub-leases end 29 Sep 2028; rents reset off market value; three Kandla leases held over.
  • Concentrated, powerful customers: one customer (likely HPCL) is 25% of revenue, and the OMCs also build their own terminals.

Both cases can be true at once, and for the industry they largely are. India needs more storage, the private sector builds it more cheaply, and consolidation will reward scale. The disagreement is about price and timing for the one listed vehicle. The bull needs three things to go right together: gas turns rising as pipelines and rail connect, JNPA filling at premium rents, and either a strategic-storage contract or steady share gains, all funded without heavy dilution at lower prices. The bear needs only one thing to slip: a delay that leaves interest and depreciation running ahead of revenue for another year, at a valuation that leaves little cushion. The 2026 results so far are consistent with both: revenue and EBITDA up in the mid-to-high teens, profit down.
