How a building block turns a cheap feedstock into a margin, why China decides that margin more than any Indian factory does, why three years of falling profit did not stop the biggest capex wave in the sub-sector's history, and what the sharp June-2026 quarter does and does not prove.

00

The one-page view: six makers of the molecules other makers need

A sub-sector that sells almost nothing a consumer would recognise, earns a spread it does not set, and has just spent three bad years building plants for the good ones.

Every pesticide, painkiller, laminate sheet, dye, tyre and polycarbonate helmet starts as a handful of simple molecules: phenol and acetone, nitrochlorobenzene, methylamine, ethyl acetate, resorcinol. The six companies in this report make those molecules. Their customers are other chemical companies, which buy them by the tanker, react them a few more times and sell the result. Together the six earned ₹28,249 crore of revenue and ₹2,087 crore of net profit in FY26, and the market values them at about ₹77,000 crore.

They look like six different businesses. Deepak Nitrite is mostly a phenol and acetone plant at Dahej with an older nitrite and intermediates business attached. Aarti Industries runs benzene and toluene through nitration, chlorination and hydrogenation into dozens of derivatives and now sells a large volume of a fuel additive. Atul is a 79-year-old diversified house with about 900 products, built on chemistries it makes itself, such as p-cresol and resorcinol. Alkyl Amines and Balaji Amines turn methanol, ammonia and ethanol into amines, solvents and derivatives for drug and crop-chemical makers. Laxmi Organic is India's largest ethyl acetate producer and its only maker of diketene derivatives.

Underneath, they share one economic engine. Each buys a feedstock that trades on world markets and sells a product that also trades on world markets. Profit is the gap between the two, multiplied by how many tonnes the plants can push through. Neither price is set in India. For the last four years both have been set, more than anywhere else, in China, which built capacity in almost every one of these molecules faster than its own economy could absorb it and exported the surplus.

That is why the numbers look the way they do. Combined revenue rose from ₹16,915 crore in FY21 to ₹28,249 crore in FY26, but the combined EBITDA margin fell almost every year, from 24.7% to 13.8%. Net profit in FY26 was 44% below the FY22 peak. Return on capital fell to or below a reasonable cost of capital (about 11–13% pre-tax) for four of the six, and below what a lender charges for two of them. And yet the group carried ₹5,419 crore of capital work-in-progress at March 2026, unfinished plants that will add depreciation and interest before they add profit.

Then the June-2026 quarter arrived. Combined revenue rose 35%, combined EBITDA more than doubled and combined net profit rose 153%. Part of that was a genuine recovery in spreads. Part was a one-off: a conflict in West Asia disrupted Gulf chemical supply and lifted feedstock prices, so companies sold product made from cheaper inventory at higher prices. Management teams and brokers expect some of that to reverse in the September quarter. The rest of this report explains how to tell the two apart.

In plain terms

These companies are converters. They buy one traded chemical, change it, and sell another traded chemical. When China has too much of the product they sell, the selling price falls towards the cost of the raw material and the conversion margin shrinks, however well the Indian plant runs. That happened from 2022 to 2025. The June-2026 quarter showed how fast the margin can come back when supply tightens, and also how hard it is to know, from one quarter, whether it will stay.

The five forces this report is built around

  • China sets the price. China added 1.35 million tonnes of phenol capacity in 2025 alone and now has more than 11 million tonnes, about 18 times India's demand. Its surplus, not Indian demand, has set the ceiling on what Indian makers can charge (sections 04 and 05).
  • The spread game. Profit is product price minus feedstock cost. Spreads moved the combined margin from 24.7% to 13.8% and back to 19.2% in a single quarter, on roughly the same volumes (sections 06 and 07).
  • The capex super-cycle. The group kept building through the downturn. Whether ₹5,419 crore of unfinished plant, and Deepak's ₹11,500 crore downstream programme, earn more than their cost of capital is the central question for FY27–FY29 (section 10).
  • End-market cycles. Agrochemical customers destocked for two years and have not yet restocked; pharma held; dyes and polymers were soft; fuel additives became a new market (section 03).
  • Integration and contracts as the moat. The companies that own their feedstock chain or hold multi-year contracts kept steadier margins than those selling single-step products into the spot market (sections 11 and 12).

The valuation is the last piece of the puzzle. Investors are paying for a recovery in spreads and for the new plants to fill. The September-quarter results, due from late October 2026, will be the first test of whether the June quarter was the start of that recovery or a supply shock that has already passed.

01

How a building block earns: from a tanker of benzene to a drum of intermediate

Each reaction step adds value and removes competitors. The money sits in how many steps a company controls and how close its product is to a commodity.

A building block is a molecule made in one or two reaction steps from a basic petrochemical, sold to a customer who will put it through more steps. The basic petrochemicals (benzene, toluene, propylene, methanol, ammonia, ethanol, acetic acid, chlorine) come from refineries, crackers, gas plants, sugar mills and electrolysers. They are cheap, traded worldwide and priced daily. The building blocks made from them are also traded worldwide, but by fewer producers, and the further down the chain a product sits, the fewer companies can make it safely and to specification.

Value per kilo rises left to right; the number of competitors falls. Most of the six earn most of their money at Step 1 or Step 2, which is why spreads dominate their results.

Three things decide how much of the chain's value a company keeps. The first is how many steps it runs in-house. Aarti takes benzene through nitration, chlorination and hydrogenation, so it earns a margin at each step rather than only the last one. Atul makes its own p-cresol and resorcinol, then sells dozens of products made from them. The second is how hard each step is. Nitration is violently exothermic, ketene (the precursor of diketene) is toxic and unstable, and amine plants handle corrosive, flammable gases. Hazard and environmental clearance are real barriers to entry; Laxmi has been India's only diketene derivatives maker for years for exactly this reason. The third is who sets the price. A product with dozens of producers worldwide, such as phenol or ethyl acetate, is priced off the global market. A product designed into a customer's registered herbicide process is priced by negotiation, often under a multi-year contract.

In plain terms

The closer a company sells to the raw material, the more its profit depends on two prices it does not control. Moving down the chain (more steps, harder chemistry, a contract, a brand) does not remove that dependence, but it dilutes it. That is why Atul, with nine businesses built on its own feedstocks, had the smallest margin swing of the big three, and why Alkyl Amines, whose FY21 profit was inflated by an extraordinary shortage, had the largest.

The rest of this report keeps returning to the same three questions for each company: how many steps does it control, how hard are they, and who sets the price at the point where it sells. Those three answers explain most of the difference between a 6% margin and a 25% one.

There is also a less visible source of value: the relationship with the customer. An agrochemical or drug maker that has qualified a supplier's intermediate has tested its impurity profile, audited its plant and often registered it with regulators as part of its own manufacturing process. Switching supplier means repeating that work, which can take six to eighteen months. That is why a qualified Indian supplier can keep a customer even when a Chinese competitor offers a lower price for a few months, and why new plants take time to fill even when demand exists. Geography matters too. Five of the six have major plants in the Gujarat chemical belt (Dahej, Jhagadia, Vapi, Ankleshwar, Valsad) or in Maharashtra's industrial areas, close to ports, feedstock pipelines and each other. Clusters share infrastructure such as effluent treatment and jetties, and they make it easier to sell one company's by-product as another's raw material. The cost of entry is not only the plant: it is environmental clearance, which can take years for hazardous chemistry, and a safety record that customers trust.

02

Size, growth and the import bill: a large industry with a small global share

India is the world's sixth-largest chemical producer but buys about 3% of the world's chemicals, and still imports more than it exports.

India's chemical sector, broadly defined to include petrochemicals, agrochemicals, fertiliser chemicals and specialties, was worth about US$278 billion in FY24 according to the India Brand Equity Foundation (IBEF), up from US$178 billion in FY19, and IBEF projects US$300 billion by FY28. Narrower definitions give smaller numbers: Choice Institutional Equities projects the industry at about US$351 billion by FY31 at an 8.4% annual growth rate, which implies a base in the mid-US$230 billions for FY26. The gap is a definition problem, not a disagreement about direction. Both describe an industry growing faster than the Indian economy in money terms.

The more useful number for this sub-sector is India's share of the world. NITI Aayog's July 2025 report on the chemical industry puts India at 3–3.5% of global chemical consumption in 2023 and about 3.5% of global chemical value chains, against a target of 5–6% by 2040. India is the sixth-largest producer and third in Asia, yet a net importer: NITI estimated the chemical trade deficit at about US$31 billion in 2023. On IBEF's narrower series, India imported US$28.5 billion of chemicals in FY26 and exported US$20.5 billion (₹1,81,568 crore of exports). The headroom for import substitution is large, and and it underpins the bull case for every company in this report.

Specialty chemicals, the part of the industry that includes intermediates, are harder to size. Market-research houses put the Indian specialty chemicals market at about US$67 billion in 2025, and forecast slow growth of 3.6–3.8% a year in dollar terms to the early 2030s, well below the roughly 12% a year recorded between 2020 and 2025. They cite exactly the forces this report describes: normalising prices and Chinese competition. These estimates come from IMARC and Ken Research, not from a rating agency, so treat them as an order of magnitude. ICRA's August 2026 sector view is qualitative but consistent: healthy domestic volumes, pricing held back by global overcapacity and aggressive Chinese competition, and a mild moderation in margins as raw-material costs rise.

In plain terms

India buys a small slice of the world's chemicals and still imports a lot of what it uses, so there is plenty of room for domestic producers to grow by replacing imports. But revenue growth in this sub-sector says little about profit growth. The group's revenue grew about 11% a year over five years; its profit fell. The rest of the report explains why.

One more caution on growth numbers. Revenue in this sub-sector rises when feedstock prices rise, because companies pass higher costs through in their selling prices. Aarti's management said exactly that about its June-2026 quarter: revenue rose 42% "mainly on account of higher input prices" that it passed on to customers, while volumes fell slightly from the previous quarter. A year of fast revenue growth can therefore be a year of flat volumes and flat margins. Volume and spread, not revenue, are the numbers to track.

The long-term growth case rests on Indian demand rather than on world markets. IBEF cites projections that India's chemicals and petrochemicals demand could roughly triple to about US$1 trillion by 2040. Foreign direct investment into chemicals (other than fertilisers) reached about US$24 billion cumulatively between April 2000 and March 2026, and India's chemical exports in the first two months of FY27 were about ₹36,336 crore (US$3.85 billion). The drivers are familiar: construction and furniture (phenolic resins and laminates), packaging and printing (ethyl acetate and solvents), pharmaceuticals (amines and acetonitrile), crop protection and, increasingly, automotive and electronics plastics (polycarbonate and epoxy). For the six companies, though, the binding constraint over FY23–FY26 was price, not demand. Indian consumption of phenol doubled between FY19 and FY25, and Deepak's phenol profit still fell, because the price at which that phenol could be sold was set by Chinese supply. Growth in demand tells you how many tonnes the industry can sell; it does not tell you what each tonne will earn.

03

Who buys the molecules: five end-markets on five different clocks

Demand is borrowed from agrochemical, pharma, polymer, dye and fuel customers, and each of them was in a different phase of its cycle in 2026.

Nobody buys tri-ethylamine or para-nitrochlorobenzene for its own sake. Demand for an intermediate exists only because a customer downstream is making a herbicide, a drug, a laminate, a dye or a fuel blend. That makes demand derived, and derived demand amplifies the customer's cycle. When an agrochemical company decides to run down its inventory, it stops ordering intermediates entirely for a few months, so the intermediate maker sees a fall far sharper than the fall in pesticide spraying.

Agrochemicals: the cycle that broke the sub-sector

Agrochemicals are the largest single end-market for the group, and the one that did the most damage. During the post-Covid shortage of 2021–22, distributors and formulators around the world over-ordered. When Chinese supply returned in 2023 and prices fell, they stopped buying until their stocks ran down. That destocking lasted roughly two years. By late 2026 the evidence says it is over but that restocking has not begun. A September 2026 brokerage review found that inventory normalisation was progressing but that broad-based restocking had yet to start; Syngenta's crop-protection sales in Latin America fell 11% in the first half of 2026, and FMC's first-quarter revenue fell 4%. In India, UPL's June-quarter crop-protection volumes fell 3% and PI Industries' agrochemical exports fell 8% in volume.

China is again the pressure point. Chinese pesticide output was reported up about 23% year on year in early 2026, its herbicide exports rose 13% in volume in the first quarter, and its pesticide price index fell 4.4% in July 2026 alone. Chinese suppliers have reportedly cut prices by 30–33% on molecules India is trying to make locally. For Aarti, Atul and the amine makers, that means agrochemical volumes may recover before agrochemical prices do.

Pharma, polymers, dyes and the new fuel market

Pharmaceuticals are the steadiest customer. Indian generic-drug makers use acetonitrile, DMF and NMP as reaction solvents and amine derivatives as building blocks, and they buy on qualification and reliability as much as price. That is why Alkyl and Balaji, with roughly half their sales going to pharma, kept volumes stable even when prices collapsed. Laminates and plywood drive Indian phenol demand through phenolic resins, and Indian phenol consumption roughly doubled between FY19 and FY25 to above 600,000 tonnes. Dyes and pigments, an old Indian strength, were soft through mid-2026: Choice Institutional Equities noted in August that dyes, pigments, polymers and parts of agrochemicals faced weak downstream demand and customer resistance to input-linked price rises.

The newest end-market is fuel. Aarti now sells mono-methyl aniline (MMA), an octane booster blended into petrol, from a plant expanded from 290,000 to 360,000 tonnes a year in July 2026, and its "Energy" business volumes rose 57% in the June quarter. Balaji commissioned India's first commercial dimethyl ether plant (100,000 tonnes a year) in May 2026 for LPG blending and aerosols. These markets are large but behave like fuels: they depend on refinery cracks and on policy, not on chemistry skill. Aarti's West Asian sales fell from about 15% of revenue to about 2% in the June quarter because of the conflict there.

In plain terms

The sub-sector's customers were not all in the same mood. Drug makers kept buying; crop-chemical makers had stopped and have only started buying again cautiously; dye and polymer makers were pushing back on prices. A company's mix of customers therefore explains a large part of why its year was good or bad, before any question of how well it ran its plants.

The practical implication for FY27 is that the next leg of volume recovery depends mostly on agrochemicals. If global restocking begins in the 2027 season, the companies with the biggest agrochemical exposure, Aarti and Atul's Life Science segment, gain the most operating leverage. If it does not, pharma-heavy amine makers and fuel-linked volumes will carry the group.

04

China sets the price: how one country's surplus became India's ceiling Deep dive

Between 2022 and 2025 China built capacity in almost every molecule this group makes, ran it at three-quarters full, and exported the rest at whatever price cleared the market.

The most important number for an Indian phenol, amine or ethyl acetate producer is not set in India. It is the price at which a Chinese plant with spare capacity is willing to sell a cargo abroad. Add freight, insurance and any Indian duty to that price and you get the landed cost of imports, the ceiling above which an Indian customer will simply import. In a normal market the ceiling sits comfortably above the Indian maker's cost. In 2023–25 it fell close to, and in some molecules below, that cost.

The build-out

China's chemical investment boom of 2019–2024 was driven by integrated refinery-to-chemicals complexes, provincial industrial policy and cheap credit. Capacity arrived in waves, and it arrived regardless of demand, because the plants were planned years earlier. Phenol shows the pattern in its cleanest form. According to SunSirs, a Chinese commodity-data group, Chinese phenol capacity passed 10.48 million tonnes a year in 2025 and then 11.26 million tonnes after Zhenhai Refining's 650,000-tonne plant started in July. New capacity of 1.35 million tonnes came on in 2025 alone. About 890,000 tonnes of older plants went into long-term shutdown, so the net addition was about 460,000 tonnes, and the industry operating rate rose to only about 75% after Jilin Petrochemical's new unit started. China, which a decade ago imported phenol, expected to export more than 850,000 tonnes in 2025. S&P Global expected a further 680,000 tonnes of Chinese phenol capacity in 2026.

Put those numbers next to India. Indian phenol demand was a little above 600,000 tonnes in FY25 (CRISIL estimated about 650,000). China's 2025 net capacity addition alone was roughly three-quarters of India's whole market, and its exports were larger than India's entire consumption. Deepak Phenolics' Dahej plant, the largest in India (200,000 tonnes nameplate in 2018, and producing an estimated 350,000 tonnes now if its 57% market share is right), is about 3% of Chinese capacity.

What the surplus did to prices and spreads

The surplus went straight into price. The average East China phenol price fell 13.4% in 2025 and ended the year at RMB 5,750–5,850 a tonne, its lowest in nearly five years. In Asia, phenol traded at a discount to its own feedstock, benzene, in the first half of 2024; a producer was paid less for the finished product than the main raw material cost. In plain terms

Chinese plants did not need to be cheaper than Indian ones to hurt them. They only needed spare capacity and a willingness to sell the extra tonnes abroad for whatever they would fetch. Why the pressure may be easing, and why it may not

Three developments in 2025–26 changed the balance at the margin. First, China removed export VAT rebates. China+1: the other side of the same coin

The China+1 thesis says that Western agrochemical, pharma and polymer companies, after the supply shocks of 2020–22, want at least one large non-Chinese supplier for each critical intermediate, and that India is the natural candidate. This is real and visible in contracts: Aarti signed a US$150 million multi-year supply agreement with a global agrochemical innovator in FY26, Atul's crop-protection and pharma customers include global majors, and Deepak's customers in optical brighteners and pigments are global. But China+1 is a volume and relationship argument, not a price argument. A Western customer will pay a modest premium for supply security; it will not pay 30% above the Chinese price for long. The thesis therefore helps Indian companies fill plants, while China still sets how much each tonne earns.

In plain terms

China is both the problem and the reason for the opportunity. Its overbuilding made customers want a second supplier, which is good for Indian volumes; the same overbuilding keeps prices low, which is bad for Indian margins. Policy changes in Beijing, such as removing export rebates, matter as much to an Indian intermediates maker as any decision its own management takes.

For an investor the practical consequence is that the first thing to check in any quarter is not the company's own commentary but the China data: operating rates, export volumes and export prices for the molecules it sells. A Deepak result can be forecast reasonably well from the Asian phenol-benzene spread and Chinese phenol export volumes before the company reports a number.

05

Trade defence and its limits: duties help prices, withdrawn standards help costs

India's anti-dumping system protects individual molecules for five years at a time; its quality-control orders were rolled back in 2025; and US tariffs moved three times in a year.

When imports are sold in India below their normal value and injure domestic producers, an Indian industry can petition the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR). DGTR investigates for about a year, recommends a duty, and the Finance Ministry decides whether to notify it. Duties usually last five years and can be extended after a "sunset review". For this sub-sector, anti-dumping duty (ADD) is the main policy lever, and the companies are frequent petitioners.

Quality-control orders: protection that was taken away

Between 2019 and 2024, the government issued Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) quality-control orders (QCOs) on dozens of chemicals and polymers. For this group, the QCO rollback cut both ways. It lowered and steadied feedstock costs for Laxmi (acetic acid), Alkyl and Balaji (methanol) and Aarti (toluene), which is good for their spreads. It also removed a non-tariff barrier on products some of them sell or plan to sell. If the polycarbonate QCO has gone, Deepak's planned 165,000-tonne polycarbonate plant will compete against unrestricted imports when it starts in 2028.

It is the gross profit of a building block. For most of this group, raw materials are 55–65% of revenue, so a modest move in either price makes a large move in the spread, and a larger move again in EBITDA, because the other costs (power, people, maintenance) barely change with price.

In plain terms

Raw materials are such a large share of these companies' costs that small price changes are magnified many times on the way to profit. A business that keeps ₹14 of every ₹100 as operating profit only needs its selling price to slip by 5% to lose more than a third of that profit. That is why investors in this sub-sector watch commodity spreads every week, and why reported revenue growth tells you very little.

Inventory: the hidden swing

Most converters hold four to eight weeks of raw materials and finished goods (Aarti's inventory was 129 days of cost of sales on screener's measure in FY26, Deepak's 57). When feedstock prices jump, product made from cheaper stock is sold at the new, higher price, creating an inventory gain. When feedstock prices fall, the opposite happens. These gains and losses run through raw-material cost, so they inflate or depress reported EBITDA margin for a quarter or two, then reverse. Aarti's management estimated inventory and currency gains of ₹50–60 crore in the June-2026 quarter; Atul's results show a ₹188 crore drawdown in inventory that helped margins. Section 07 deals with that quarter in detail.

Contracts change the formula, not the physics

Long-term contracts, which Aarti and Atul use for part of their volume, usually price the product as feedstock cost plus a fixed or indexed conversion fee. That moves most of the feedstock risk to the customer, but with a lag of one to three months, and the conversion fee is renegotiated at renewal. The spread on contract business is steadier but not fixed. The 2020 cancellation of Aarti's first long-term contract shows the other protection such contracts carry: the customer paid ₹631 crore in termination fees, booked in the December 2021 quarter.

To understand a quarter in this sub-sector, ask three questions in order. Did the gap between product and raw-material prices widen or narrow? The spread is also why valuation is so hard. A multiple applied to a peak-spread year will overvalue the company; one applied to a trough-spread year will undervalue it. Entry barriers in this sub-sector are real but not permanent. Between FY22 and FY26 the group's plant base grew 86% while revenue grew 13%. Whether the new capacity earns its cost of capital decides the next five years.

Does the new capacity earn its cost of capital?

A useful test is to strip out the unfinished plant. ROCE excluding CWIP divides operating profit (EBIT) by capital employed minus the work-in-progress, asking how well the plants that are actually running perform. On that basis Deepak's FY26 return rises from 11% to 14.3%, Balaji's from 11% to 14.8%, Aarti's from 7% to 8.1%, Laxmi's from 5% to 5.9%, while Alkyl (17.4%) and Atul (14.6%) barely change. Even at the bottom of the spread cycle, Deepak's and Balaji's running plants earned roughly their cost of capital; Aarti's and Laxmi's did not.

The cost of capital itself is a judgement. With Indian ten-year government bonds near 6.5%, an equity risk premium of 6–7% and a cyclical business, a reasonable post-tax cost of equity is 12–14%; corporate borrowing costs about 8–9%. A blended pre-tax hurdle of 11–13% is a fair benchmark for a moderately geared intermediates company (INDICATIVE). Plants that earn less than that across a cycle destroy value even if they grow revenue.

The J-curve of a new plant

New chemical capacity does not earn its full return on day one. Customers must qualify the product (often a 6–18 month process for pharma and agrochemical customers), the plant must be debottlenecked, and volumes ramp over two to three years. Meanwhile depreciation and interest start on commissioning. The result is a J-curve: returns dip when a large plant starts, then climb if utilisation reaches the planned level. Balaji's guidance for its dimethyl ether plant is typical: 30–40% utilisation initially in FY27, 50–60% by the end of FY27, and 80–90% in later years, subject to approvals and market adoption.

Import substitution, and where it runs out: phenol, acetonitrile, polycarbonate Deep dive

Replacing imports created the sub-sector's best investment of the last decade. The next round is larger, later in the cycle, and faces domestic as well as Chinese competition.

Import substitution is the simplest growth story in Indian chemicals. Find a molecule India consumes in volume and mostly imports; build a plant at world scale near a port; sell to Indian customers at slightly below the landed cost of imports. The domestic maker saves its customers freight, insurance, port handling and customs duty, and gives them shorter lead times and rupee invoicing. The margin it earns is roughly the cost of importing, on top of whatever spread the global market allows.

The phenol precedent

Deepak proved the model. Before November 2018 India made very little phenol and imported most of it. The limits: when substitution is complete

Import substitution has a natural end. The domestic maker earns import parity only while imports remain the marginal supplier. Once domestic capacity exceeds demand, the marginal tonne is an Indian tonne looking for a buyer, and prices fall towards export parity: the Asian price minus freight to the destination. The swing between import parity and export parity can be US$150–250 a tonne, enough to remove most of a commodity producer's margin. Indian phenol is about to test this. Haldia Petrochemicals' 300,000-tonne plant, scheduled for inauguration on 14 October 2026, plus Deepak's own plans for new phenol capacity (reported at around 300,000 tonnes in 2025, and described in August 2026 as debottlenecking towards about 400,000 tonnes), could take Indian supply above Indian demand of roughly 600,000–700,000 tonnes within two to three years. Deepak will then increasingly consume its own phenol and acetone in bisphenol-A, MIBK and IPA, which is part of the point of its downstream programme.

Polycarbonate: the second bet

India imports essentially all of its polycarbonate, the clear, tough plastic in helmets, headlamps, LED housings, medical devices and electronics. Deepak's plan repeats the phenol playbook one step further down: build the country's first polycarbonate plant at 165,000 tonnes a year, fed by its own bisphenol-A, which is made from its own phenol and acetone. It is buying a proven plant rather than designing one, by dismantling Trinseo's Stade facility in Germany and rebuilding it at Dahej; partial equipment had arrived by August 2026, civil work had started, and Deepak signed a long-term contract for an on-site hydrogen and carbon-monoxide (HyCO) plant to be commissioned in 2028.

Three things make this bet harder than the first. It is bigger relative to the company: ₹11,500 crore for the propylene and polycarbonate programme against ₹5,837 crore of equity, versus a phenol plant that was large but within reach. It comes later in the global cycle: Chinese polycarbonate and BPA capacity expanded strongly over 2020–25 (Wanhua and others integrated phenol through to polycarbonate), and European producers have been consolidating, including ADNOC's acquisition of Covestro. And the policy cushion may be thinner: the November 2025 polymer QCO withdrawal list reportedly includes polycarbonate, which would leave imports unrestricted when Deepak starts. A polycarbonate plant also needs product qualification with moulders and brand owners, which takes time.

In plain terms

Making locally what India used to import is a good business while imports still fill the gap, because the local maker can charge almost what the import costs. It stops being special once there is enough local capacity, because then local makers compete with each other on price. Phenol in India is reaching that point now. Acetonitrile: substitution behind a duty

Acetonitrile shows a third variant. It is an essential pharma solvent, and most of the world's supply comes as a by-product of acrylonitrile manufacture. When acrylonitrile plants run hard, acetonitrile floods the market; when they cut back, it becomes scarce. Alkyl built an on-purpose plant at Dahej in FY22, at the peak of a pandemic price spike, and then watched prices fall as Chinese and other supply returned. The anti-dumping duty on acetonitrile from China, Russia and Taiwan, notified in June 2025, restored some pricing. The lesson is that substituting a by-product is harder than substituting a main product, because the foreign supplier's cost of the by-product can be close to zero.

In plain terms

Import substitution works best for a product that foreign plants make on purpose, that India imports in large volume, and that a local plant can make at world scale. It works less well for a by-product, for a product where the local market is small, or for one where several local companies build at once.

The broad conclusion is that import substitution remains the best source of growth for this group, but the easy wins are taken. The next ones (polycarbonate, ethylenediamine, specialty fluorochemicals) require more capital per rupee of revenue, more customer qualification and more tolerance for Chinese competition than phenol did in 2018.

Integration and contracts: the moats that actually held

Owning your feedstock, selling more steps of the chain, and signing take-or-pay contracts did not prevent the downturn, but they decided who came through it in good shape.

If spreads are set abroad, a company's only defences are to need fewer bought-in inputs, to capture more steps of value per tonne, or to lock in volume and a conversion fee by contract. Each of the six uses some mix of the three. The evidence from FY22–FY26 is that these defences reduced the damage without preventing it.

Contracts: rent, not fishing

Aarti's long-term contracts show what a well-written contract is worth. Forward integration and the toll booth

Laxmi's diketene business shows the value of a hard step. Ketene, made by cracking acetic acid, is toxic and unstable, and must be converted immediately into diketene and then into esters, amides and arylides. Laxmi acquired the technology and business from Clariant and is the only Indian maker of diketene derivatives, with about 55% of the domestic market. Every diketene derivative sold in India passes through Laxmi's plant or is imported. That position held up in the downturn better than ethyl acetate did, although Specialties' EBITDA margin still fell 3.1 percentage points year on year in the June-2026 quarter as raw-material prices moved.

The amine makers use the same logic on a smaller scale. Both make base methyl- and ethylamines and then convert a rising share into derivatives and specialty solvents in their own plants. Balaji's subsidiary is moving into ethylenediamine, cyanides and EDTA, a different amine family. The more of their base amines they consume internally, the less of their earnings depends on the most commoditised, most China-exposed rung.

In plain terms

The best protection in this sub-sector is to do something few others can do, or to sign customers up for years at a time. Neither makes a company immune to a glut, but both mean that when the glut ends, the company still has its customers and its margins recover first.

For investors, integration and contracts are visible mainly through two numbers: the stability of the EBITDA margin across the cycle and the return on capital at the trough. On both, Atul and Alkyl score best, Deepak and Balaji are average, and Aarti and Laxmi are still proving that their newer integration steps will pay.

The sub-sector's recent history is one boom and one bust. Reading an intermediates company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Ten numbers explain almost every result in this sub-sector. Each has a formula, a benchmark and a trap. This section works through all of them, then shows how to read a quarter in ten minutes.

Analysts who cover consumer companies watch volumes, prices and advertising. Bank analysts watch loan growth, margins and bad loans. For a chemical intermediates maker, the equivalent set is narrower and more mechanical: how much it can make, how much it does make, what each tonne earns over its raw materials, what it costs to convert, and how much capital is tied up doing it. Almost everything else in the accounts is a consequence of those five terms.

EBITDA = capacity × utilisation × (realisation − feedstock cost per unit) − conversion cost. Return on capital = (EBITDA − depreciation) ÷ (plant + working capital). Management chooses capacity and controls conversion cost; it influences utilisation; the market sets the spread.

The equation explains why the same management team can report a brilliant year and a poor one in succession without doing anything differently. Capacity and conversion cost move slowly and are largely in management's hands. The spread moves every week and is largely outside them. Utilisation sits in between: a reliable plant with qualified customers runs fuller, but in a glut even the best plant may have to cut output or sell at a loss. Good analysis starts by asking which term moved.

A worked example in rupees

Take a plant built to make 50,000 tonnes a year of a building block (ILLUSTRATIVE, with round numbers chosen to resemble the sub-sector's averages). It runs at 80%, so it makes and sells 40,000 tonnes. The product sells for ₹150 a kilo and the raw materials in each kilo cost ₹90, so the spread is ₹60 a kilo. Revenue is 40,000 tonnes × ₹150 = ₹600 crore. Raw materials cost ₹360 crore, leaving a spread of ₹240 crore. Conversion costs are ₹48 crore of power and utilities, ₹30 crore of employees and ₹54 crore of other expenses (freight, packing, maintenance, effluent treatment, selling), together ₹132 crore. EBITDA is ₹108 crore, an 18% margin.

The plant cost ₹500 crore to build and is depreciated over 20 years, ₹25 crore a year, so operating profit (EBIT) is ₹83 crore. It needs ₹120 crore of working capital (73 days of sales in stock and receivables, net of payables). Capital employed is therefore ₹620 crore and the pre-tax return on capital is 13.4%, just above a typical 11–13% hurdle (INDICATIVE). Now cut the spread by ₹10 a kilo, roughly what a wave of Chinese exports can do in a quarter: the spread falls to ₹200 crore, EBITDA to ₹68 crore (−37%) and ROCE to 6.9%. Raise the spread by ₹10 instead and EBITDA rises to ₹148 crore and ROCE to 19.8%. A one-sixth move in the spread moves the return on capital by about half.

The example also shows where management can and cannot add value. It chose the ₹500 crore plant and the 50,000-tonne size years ago; that decision is now sunk. It can lift utilisation by winning customers and keeping the plant reliable, worth about ₹10 crore of EBITDA for every five points of utilisation in this example. It can cut conversion cost through energy efficiency, renewable power or better yields: Deepak says its renewable power contracts save about ₹20 crore a year. But a ₹10 a kilo swing in the spread, worth ₹40 crore, dwarfs all of these, and it arrives from outside. Good management in this sub-sector is mostly about being the lowest-cost, most reliable plant when the spread is thin, so that it survives the trough and captures the most when the spread widens.

In plain terms

Most of the money a chemical plant takes in goes straight back out to buy raw materials. What is left has to cover the people, the power and the cost of the plant itself. Because that leftover slice is small, a small change in prices makes a big change in profit. This is the single most important fact for understanding the sub-sector.

KPI 1 · The conversion spread

What it is. The difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the raw materials needed to make one unit of it, using the chemical recipe's ratios. For phenol: phenol price plus about 0.6 times the acetone price, minus about 0.85 times the benzene price and about 0.46 times the propylene price. For ethyl acetate: price minus about 0.68 times acetic acid and about 0.52 times ethanol.

Worked example. In the plant above, ₹150 − ₹90 = ₹60 a kilo, or ₹240 crore a year at 40,000 tonnes. Each ₹1 a kilo of spread is worth ₹4 crore of EBITDA.

Drivers. Chinese operating rates and export prices; feedstock prices (benzene, propylene, methanol, ammonia, ethanol, acetic acid); freight; Indian duties; demand from downstream customers; and, for co-products such as acetone, the price of the co-product.

Benchmarks. For phenol over benzene in Asia, market sources put break-even at US$200–300 a tonne; above about US$300 is healthy, below US$200 is loss-making for a non-integrated producer. For ethyl acetate, Laxmi's record of US$215 a tonne in the June-2026 quarter compares with what brokers describe as normal levels roughly half that (INDICATIVE). How the six compare. No company in the group publishes its own realised spread. Deepak's phenolics EBIT margin (24% in the June-2026 quarter) and Laxmi's EBITDA margin are the closest public proxies.

For the others, analysts build their own spread indicators from public prices. For Aarti, the gap between nitrochlorobenzene export prices and benzene is the starting point, adjusted for chlorine and nitric acid; for its fuel additive, the gasoline-naphtha crack (about US$15–20 a barrel in mid-2026, per management) matters more. For the amine makers, the spread between Chinese methylamine or DMF export prices and methanol plus ammonia is the closest public guide. For Atul, there is no single spread; its many small ones average out, which is precisely why its margin is the most stable of the large three. When a company's reported margin moves against its spread indicator for more than two quarters, something company-specific is happening, such as a mix change, an outage, or a new plant's start-up costs.

The trap. Published market spreads are spot quotes; companies sell partly under monthly or quarterly formula contracts and from inventory, so their realised spread lags the market by one to three months. Do not expect a spot move to show up in the same quarter.

KPI 2 · EBITDA margin

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, divided by revenue. On screener.in it is called "operating profit" and excludes other income; companies often present EBITDA including some other income.

Formula and example. EBITDA ÷ revenue. The worked plant: ₹108 crore ÷ ₹600 crore = 18%. The group in FY26: ₹3,911 crore ÷ ₹28,249 crore = 13.8%.

Drivers. The spread (the biggest), product mix, utilisation (fixed costs spread over more tonnes), power and fuel prices, and one-offs such as inventory gains. Because revenue moves with feedstock prices, margin can fall when feedstock rises even if the rupee spread is unchanged: the same ₹60 spread on a ₹170 price is a lower percentage than on ₹150.

Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Above 20% is good for an intermediates maker; 13–20% average; below 13% weak. Laxmi's management guides that its ethyl acetate business earns mid-single-digit margins over a cycle, so its benchmark is lower.

KPI 6 · ROCE, and ROCE excluding work-in-progress

What it is. Return on capital employed: operating profit (EBIT) divided by the capital, equity plus debt, used to earn it. Formula. ROCE = EBIT ÷ (equity + borrowings), usually on average capital; ROCE excluding CWIP removes capital work-in-progress from the denominator. Example. In plain terms

Return on capital asks a simple question: for every ₹100 tied up in plants and stock, how many rupees of profit come back each year? Below about ₹11–13, the company would have done better to repay its lenders and hand cash back to shareholders. Four of the six were at or below that line in FY26 on reported figures.

KPI 7 · Capital work-in-progress and capex intensity

What it is. CWIP is the cost of plants under construction, carried on the balance sheet until commissioning. Capex intensity is capital spending divided by revenue. Formula and example. CWIP share = CWIP ÷ (net fixed assets + CWIP). Deepak: ₹1,828 crore ÷ (₹3,270 + ₹1,828) crore = 35.9%. Capex intensity, using screener's net investing outflow as a proxy: Balaji ₹344 crore ÷ ₹1,419 crore = 24.2% of revenue.

Drivers. The size and timing of projects; delays (Aarti's Zone IV is three to six months late); and capitalised interest, which adds to CWIP rather than to the interest line. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). CWIP below 10% of operating assets is a business in harvest mode; 10–25% normal growth; above 30% means a third of the capital base earns nothing yet. Capex intensity of 5–8% of revenue is maintenance plus modest growth; above 15% is heavy expansion.

Putting the KPIs together: three read-throughs

Deepak Nitrite. Capacity is large and fully used; the spread fell sharply in FY24–FY26 and recovered from the March-2026 quarter; conversion cost is competitive. Its FY26 numbers (12.5% margin, 2.41 times asset turn, 11% ROCE, 14.3% excluding CWIP) describe a good plant earning a poor spread while carrying a large construction project. The KPIs to watch are the phenolics EBIT margin, CWIP converting into revenue (MIBK and acetophenone from August 2026), and net debt as the polycarbonate programme draws down. Haldia's entry adds a new risk to utilisation that did not exist before.

Aarti Industries. Capacity has grown much faster than profit; asset turn is the lowest in the group (1.29 times on net block, 0.97 including CWIP); ROCE is 7–8%; net debt is 3.6 times EBITDA. Its spread is not obviously worse than peers' (EBITDA margin 14.1%); its problem is the denominator. The KPIs that matter are volumes in the new Zone IV blocks, energy-business utilisation at 360,000 tonnes, and EBITDA against its own ₹1,800–2,200 crore FY28 target. If EBITDA reaches the bottom of that range with capex falling to ₹700–800 crore and below, ROCE roughly doubles from here; if Zone IV slips further, it stays near the cost of debt.

Laxmi Organic. A thin spread (6% margin in FY26) on a commodity base makes it the most operationally leveraged of the six: its EBITDA nearly quadrupled in the June-2026 quarter on 40% more revenue. Its KPIs are the ethyl acetate spread (record US$215 a tonne in that quarter, normalising since May), the Specialties margin, and whether the fluorospecialty and Dahej capacity produce revenue in FY27–FY28. With ROCE at 5% and CWIP at 31% of operating assets, the KPIs say the investment case rests on plants that have not yet earned anything.

In plain terms

Read together, the KPIs tell a different story for each company even though all six went through the same downturn. Deepak's problem was the price of its product; Aarti's is the amount of capital it has put in; Laxmi's is how thin its basic margin is. Each one needs a different thing to go right.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA has three definitions. Screener's operating profit excludes other income; company presentations often include some; brokers adjust again. Aarti's June-2026 EBITDA appears as ₹336 crore (ICICI Direct), ₹382 crore (screener) and ₹385 crore (company presentation).
  • Revenue gross or net of GST. Aarti's June-2026 revenue was ₹2,627 crore including ₹240 crore of GST collected and ₹2,387 crore net. Mixing the two overstates growth or margin.
  • Consolidated or standalone. Alkyl Amines' consolidated page on screener stops at FY20; use standalone. Atul's consolidated figures include joint ventures and minority interests.
  • ROCE basis. Average or year-end capital; with or without CWIP; with or without treasury investments. The same company can show 11% or 14% depending on the choice.
  • Net debt basis. Strategic investments counted as cash (Atul) and bank deposits missed in "other assets" both distort it.
  • Capacity basis. Nameplate at commissioning, debottlenecked effective capacity, or an "installed" total that adds products sharing multi-purpose plants.
  • Segment results gross of inter-segment sales. Atul's and Deepak's segment revenues add up to more than group revenue.
  • Inventory and currency gains inside margin. Aarti's ₹50–60 crore and Atul's ₹188 crore inventory drawdown in the June-2026 quarter.
  • Capitalised interest. Interest on borrowings for unfinished plants is added to CWIP, not expensed; screener flags Deepak, Aarti and Laxmi.
  • Tax. Aarti's effective tax rate was negative in FY24–FY26 (−15% in FY26), which inflates reported PAT relative to pre-tax profit.
  • One-offs and accounting changes. Laxmi's FY26 profit included a one-off litigation gain of about ₹41 crore and benefited from depreciation falling from ₹124 crore to ₹77 crore.
In plain terms

Before comparing two companies on any number, check that both numbers were calculated the same way. In this sub-sector, the same company's profit margin, return on capital and debt can each differ by several points depending on the source. Most apparent "surprises" in results turn out to be definition changes.

How to read an intermediates company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Split revenue into volume and price. Find the volume line in the presentation or call; if revenue grew much faster than volume, it is price pass-through, not demand.
  2. Compare EBITDA margin with last quarter and last year, and with the market spread. Check the relevant spread indicator (phenol–benzene, ethyl acetate, Chinese amine prices) for the quarter; the company's margin should move with it, with a lag.
  3. Look for inventory and currency gains. Management usually quantifies them on the call; subtract them to find the underlying margin.
  4. Read the segment results. Which segment moved the total? A commodity segment surge (Deepak Phenolics) is less durable than a specialty one.
  5. Separate other income and one-offs. Treasury income (Atul), litigation gains (Laxmi) and termination fees (Aarti in FY22) do not repeat.
  6. Check depreciation and interest for step-ups. A jump usually means a new plant has been commissioned; its revenue should follow within two to four quarters.
  7. Check the tax rate. An unusually low or negative rate (Aarti) means PAT growth overstates operating improvement.
  8. Track capex and CWIP against promised dates. Note commissioning delays (Aarti's Zone IV: three to six months) and any change in total project cost.
  9. Check debt and working capital. Rising receivable days or inventory can absorb the quarter's profit; compare net debt with the previous quarter.
  10. Listen for the next quarter's spread and China commentary. Management's view of Chinese supply, anti-dumping cases and customer restocking is the best guide to the following quarter.
In plain terms

The ten steps reduce to three questions: was the quarter driven by price or by volume; how much of the margin was one-off; and are the new plants on time? A reader who answers those three will understand a result better than one who only reads the headline profit growth.

Applied to the June-2026 quarter, the checklist gives a clear verdict. Revenue growth was mostly price; margins included identifiable one-offs at Aarti and Atul and an unusually wide spread at Laxmi; Deepak's phenolics improvement had started before the West Asia shock and so looks more durable; the new plants at Balaji and Aarti were on or near schedule while Aarti's Zone IV was late. That is a picture of a genuine but modest recovery with a temporary boost on top.

In plain terms

The technology race in this sub-sector is mostly about doing the same chemistry more cheaply and more safely, and about owning the raw materials. The genuinely new products, such as polycarbonate, dimethyl ether and fluorospecialties, carry new markets and new risks.

The main disruptive threat is not a new technology but Chinese scale applied to old ones. Chinese producers have integrated from coal and crude through to finished intermediates in single complexes, which lowers their costs in ways an Indian standalone plant cannot easily match. The Indian response, visible across the group, is to integrate where possible, specialise where not, and accept that in pure commodities the Chinese cost curve will set the price.

Environmental compliance is a cost that is rising for everyone and is itself a quiet competitive filter. Plants handling nitration, chlorination and amines generate acidic, saline and organic effluent; most new plants in Gujarat are built for zero liquid discharge, recovering water and solid salts rather than releasing effluent. Global customers increasingly audit suppliers' environmental and safety performance; Aarti reports an EcoVadis Platinum rating (score 87 out of 100, top 1%), a credential that matters to European agrochemical and coatings buyers choosing a non-Chinese source. Companies that invest early in cleaner processes can turn compliance from a cost into a reason to be chosen.

Bull versus bear: a recovery priced in advance

The bull case needs spreads to normalise and new plants to fill; the bear case needs only for China's surplus to persist a little longer.

The six companies are valued at ₹76,972 crore, about 37 times FY26 profit and 29 times trailing profit to June 2026. Both cases agree that FY26 was a trough. They disagree on how far and how fast earnings recover, and on whether the capital already committed will earn its keep.

The bull case

  • The cycle has turned. Combined EBITDA margin rose from 12.3% to 19.2% between the June quarters of FY26 and FY27; Deepak's had already reached 18% in the March quarter, before the Gulf disruption.
  • China's subsidy is shrinking. Export VAT rebates on 249 products were removed from April 2026; older capacity is closing in Japan and Europe; anti-involution policy targets price wars.
  • Capex is mostly spent. Aarti's capex falls to ₹700–800 crore in FY27 and lower from FY28; Atul's is finished; new plants at Balaji, Aarti and Laxmi start contributing in FY27–FY28, so returns can rise quickly on a stable capital base.
  • Import substitution still has runway. Polycarbonate (100% imported), ethylenediamine, acetone and fluorospecialties are large opportunities.
  • Better trade terms. The US tariff fell from 50% to 18% in February 2026; the India–EU agreement gives zero-duty access to most chemical exports.

The bear case

  • Part of the recovery was a war. Inventory gains and a record ethyl acetate spread inflated the June quarter; brokers and managements expect margins to normalise in the September quarter.
  • China is still adding capacity. Another ~680,000 tonnes of phenol in 2026; pesticide output +23%; price cuts of 30–33% on molecules India localises.
  • Domestic oversupply in phenol. Haldia's 300,000 tonnes from October 2026 plus Deepak's own expansion could push Indian phenol prices from import parity towards export parity.
  • Returns below the cost of capital. Combined ROCE of 9.7% in FY26; Aarti and Laxmi below 10% even excluding unfinished plant; borrowings 2.3× FY22 levels and Deepak's set to rise sharply.
  • The valuation already assumes recovery. 20 times EV/EBITDA against roughly 6–12 times for global peers (INDICATIVE).

What the price implies

One way to judge the valuation is to ask what profit level would make today's price look normal. At ₹76,972 crore, the group trades at 36.9 times FY26 profit and 28.6 times trailing profit. If combined profit returned to its FY22 peak of ₹3,757 crore (a level inflated by Aarti's one-off termination fee), the multiple would fall to 20.5 times. A mid-cycle profit of about ₹3,500 crore, roughly what the June-2026 quarter's run-rate implies after removing a sensible share of one-offs, would put the group on 22 times. In other words, the market is already paying a full multiple on a mid-cycle recovery that has not yet been confirmed.

In plain terms

Investors are already paying today for profits these companies have not earned since FY22. If the recovery arrives, the price looks reasonable in hindsight. If the June quarter turns out to have been mostly a one-off, the profits needed to justify the price are a long way off, and the companies with the most debt and unfinished plant will feel it most.

The bull and bear cases are not equally balanced across the six. The bear case bites hardest where leverage, unfinished plant and commodity exposure combine (Aarti, Laxmi and, from FY27, Deepak). The bull case pays most where operating leverage is highest (the same three). Atul, Alkyl and Balaji sit closer to the middle: less downside from debt, less upside from a thin base. That symmetry is why this sub-sector rewards a clear view on the spread more than stock-picking on quality alone.

In plain terms

If you can watch only a few things, watch the price gap between phenol and benzene, what happens to Indian phenol prices once Haldia's plant runs, Chinese export prices for amines and agrochemicals, and the September-quarter margin. Those four will tell you more about the next year than any company presentation.

One more signal deserves attention: the pace at which capital work-in-progress turns into revenue. Across the six, ₹5,419 crore of plant was under construction at March 2026. If the recovery is real and the projects are on time, that figure should fall through FY27 while revenue rises, and ROCE should climb back towards the low-to-mid teens by FY28. If it does not fall, or if revenue does not follow, the capex cycle will have been the sub-sector's most expensive mistake since the boom that started it.

How to use the signals in practice: check the spread indicators monthly, the Chinese export and operating-rate data quarterly, and the companies' own disclosures when they report. When the external indicators and the companies' commentary point the same way, the next quarter is usually predictable. When they diverge (for example, a company reporting strong margins while its spread indicator is falling), the gap usually closes within one or two quarters in favour of the external indicator. Inventory gains, contract lags and one-offs can delay the convergence; they rarely prevent it. The September-2026 quarter is a good first test of this discipline: the external indicators (ethyl acetate spreads moderating since May, feedstock prices easing after the Gulf disruption) point to lower margins than in June, while several companies' commentary points to continued recovery. The results will show which signal was right.

TermMeaning
Acetonitrile (ACN)A solvent used heavily in pharma manufacturing and lab analysis; globally mostly a by-product of acrylonitrile.

China+1The strategy of global buyers to add a non-Chinese supplier for critical inputs.

CWIPCapital work-in-progress: the cost of plants still under construction, not yet earning revenue.
DGTRDirectorate General of Trade Remedies, the Commerce Ministry body that investigates dumping.
DiketeneA reactive chemical made from acetic acid via ketene; the base of a family of pharma, agro and pigment intermediates.
DMF, NMPDimethylformamide and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, amine-derived solvents used in pharma, fibres and batteries.
EBITDAEarnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; operating profit before the cost of the plant and of debt.
EV/EBITDAEnterprise value (market value plus net debt) divided by EBITDA; a valuation multiple that allows for debt.
Ethyl acetateA fast-drying solvent made from acetic acid and ethanol; used in inks, packaging, coatings and pharma.
Import parity / export parityThe price at which imports land (world price plus freight and duty) or at which exports net back (world price minus freight).
Inventory gainExtra margin from selling product made with cheaper raw materials after prices rise; reverses when prices fall.
Methylamines, ethylaminesAmines made from methanol or ethanol with ammonia; building blocks for pharma, agro and rubber chemicals.
MMA (mono-methyl aniline)An octane-boosting fuel additive made by Aarti from the benzene chain (not methyl methacrylate).
NCB, DCB, PDANitrochlorobenzene, dichlorobenzene and phenylenediamine: benzene-chain intermediates for agro, dye, pharma and polymer uses.
Nitration, chlorination, hydrogenationReactions that add a nitro group, chlorine, or hydrogen to a molecule; each a step up the value chain and a safety challenge.
p-CresolAn aromatic chemical used in fragrances, antioxidants and agro/pharma intermediates; Atul holds about 55% of world capacity.

ROCEReturn on capital employed: operating profit (EBIT) divided by equity plus debt.
SpreadProduct price minus the cost of the raw materials in one unit of product; the gross profit of a building block.
Take-or-pay contractA long-term supply contract in which the customer pays for committed volume whether or not it takes delivery.
UtilisationOutput as a share of the plant's capacity.
