00

The one-page view: an export machine whose volume engine has stalled

For 25 years revenue grew with headcount. GenAI, visa walls and client-owned centres are breaking that link, and the rupee is hiding the damage.

India's listed IT services companies sell one thing: the time of skilled engineers, packaged as projects, managed services and outsourced operations for large companies in the United States and Europe. Eighteen of them sit in the NSE 750 under "Computers – Software & Consulting". Together they booked ₹8.65 lakh crore of revenue in FY26, earned ₹1.28 lakh crore of profit after tax, employ roughly 18 lakh people and were worth ₹23.8 lakh crore on 13 October 2026. Five of them, TCS, Infosys, HCLTech, Wipro and Tech Mahindra, account for 84% of the revenue and 81% of the market value.

The economics that built this industry were simple. A client pays a rate per hour, per person or per contract. The Indian vendor delivers most of the work from India at a fraction of the cost of a Western engineer. The spread between what the client pays and what the vendor pays its people produced operating margins of 20–25% and returns on capital of 30–60% for two decades, with almost no debt and almost no physical assets. Revenue grew because headcount grew: more people, billed at stable rates, kept busy at 80–88% utilisation.

That link between people and revenue is what is breaking. NASSCOM's FY26 estimate shows industry revenue up 6.1% but jobs up only 2.3%. The four largest listed companies are growing at 1–3% in constant currency, their weakest stretch outside a global recession. HCLTech's chief executive told investors in April 2026 that around 40% of the industry's work is "AI disrupted" and could shrink 3–5% a year, and that a $100 million contract scope now prices at about $80 million. Meanwhile the US has made the old onsite model far more expensive, and American and European clients have built more than 2,100 captive centres in India that now employ 23.6 lakh people, more than all eighteen listed vendors combined.

Five forces explain almost everything that follows in this report:

  • GenAI deflation of the "run" book. Maintenance, testing, support and infrastructure operations, the long-tenure work that funds the industry, is being repriced as AI tools cut the effort needed. Productivity gains are passed to clients at renewal, so revenue per unit of scope falls every year.
  • A demand pool moving from labour to capital. Worldwide IT spending is rising fast, but the new money goes to data centres, chips and software. Labour-based services outside cloud infrastructure grow at roughly 3–4%, and US discretionary project spending is in its fourth soft year.
  • Protectionism and insourcing. The $100,000 H-1B payment, a wage-weighted visa lottery and threatened outsourcing taxes have closed the rotational onsite model, while global capability centres (GCCs) take strategic work in-house. GCCs are now both the biggest competitor and a growing customer.
  • Margins propped up by the rupee. The rupee slid from about ₹74 to the dollar in FY21 to about ₹95.5 in FY27 so far. Each 1% of depreciation adds roughly 20–30 basis points to operating margin. Reported margins look flat because currency gains are paying for wage hikes, price cuts and AI investment.
  • Scale against agility. The 13 mid and small companies grew revenue at 17.7% a year over FY20–26 against 9.7% for the top five, and trade at 29× earnings against 16×. Several are now buying growth with debt: Coforge's $2.35 billion Encora deal and Persistent's €1.27 billion Nagarro take-private.

The market has already re-priced the sector. The Nifty IT index fell 31% from its November 2024 peak to October 2026 and 21.6% in calendar 2026 alone, against a 10% fall in the Nifty 50. Tier-1 stocks trade at 13–19 times trailing earnings, levels last seen around 2009 and 2013. Kotak Institutional Equities argues those prices assume only 3–4% annual dollar growth in perpetuity. The debate for investors is whether the AI transition looks like the 2014–2019 cloud transition, when legacy work shrank but new digital work more than replaced it, or something deeper, because this time the technology substitutes for the engineer rather than moving the engineer to a new platform.

In plain terms

Indian IT companies have been selling engineers' time to Western companies for decades at a healthy markup. Now software can do a growing share of that work, the US is making it harder to send Indian engineers onsite, and clients are opening their own Indian offices. Revenue at the biggest firms has nearly stopped growing in dollar terms. Profits still look fine mainly because a weaker rupee turns each dollar earned into more rupees. The open question is how much new AI work replaces the old work that is shrinking.

Three facts about comparability run through every table in this report. First, rupee growth flatters: with the rupee about 10% weaker than a year earlier, a company growing 2% in dollars reports roughly 11–14% growth in rupees. Constant-currency growth is the only like-for-like measure. Second, margins are reported on different bases: screener.in's "operating profit" is before depreciation and other income (an EBITDA proxy) and runs 2–5 points above company-reported EBIT. Third, two members are not classic IT services businesses: Sonata Software books about ₹7,772 crore of low-margin Microsoft licence resale in India inside its ₹10,701 crore revenue, and Black Box is a network and data-centre integrator whose revenue includes hardware pass-through. Their revenue, margin and revenue-per-employee figures are flagged wherever they appear.

01

How the money is made: from a client's IT budget to a billable hour in Pune

The listed companies sit in one layer of a six-layer chain, and value is leaking upward to platforms and inward to captives.

An IT services contract starts in a client's technology budget. A bank, retailer or manufacturer splits its spending into two pools. The "run" pool keeps existing systems working: application maintenance, production support, infrastructure and cloud operations, testing, help desks. Historically it absorbed 60–70% of enterprise IT spend. The "change" pool funds new projects: a cloud migration, a new digital channel, an ERP replacement, a data platform. Change spending is discretionary, short-cycle and higher-margin; it is the first thing a chief financial officer cuts when interest rates rise or a recession looms.

The listed companies capture the delivery layer. AI is pushing value up to Layer 3 (platforms selling "digital labour" directly) and across to Layer 6 (captives keeping strategic work in-house).

The Indian vendors live in the fourth layer: system integration and managed services. Their edge was the global delivery model. A small team sits at the client's site to understand requirements and manage the relationship; the bulk of the engineers, typically 75–85% of the effort, sit in Bengaluru, Pune, Chennai, Hyderabad or Noida. Infosys's onsite effort was 22.8% in Q4 FY26; LTM's was 14.2% in Q1 FY27. An offshore engineer is billed at perhaps $25–35 an hour and costs the vendor ₹8–20 lakh a year; an onsite consultant bills $80–110 an hour but costs four to eight times more. Blending the two produces the 20–25% operating margins of the best firms.

Three ways to charge

How a contract is priced decides who keeps any productivity gain, which matters enormously now that AI tools cut the effort needed for many tasks.

  • Time and materials (T&M). The client pays for hours or people actually used. If an AI coding assistant halves the hours, revenue halves. T&M is roughly 46–57% of revenue at Mphasis, Coforge, Mastek and Birlasoft.
  • Fixed price. The vendor commits to a deliverable for a set fee. If the work takes fewer hours, the vendor keeps the saving, until the next contract is priced. Fixed price is 43–50% at Coforge, Mphasis, Mastek and Birlasoft, and 30.8% at Happiest Minds, up from 23.1% a year earlier.
  • Managed services and outcomes. Multi-year contracts (typically 3–7 years) to run a function at an agreed service level, often with built-in annual productivity give-backs of 3–5%. HCLTech's Q1 FY27 margin fell 110 basis points partly because of these scheduled give-backs. Newer variants price per transaction, per ticket or per AI agent.

Where the margin comes from

Margin in this business is the difference between the price of an hour and the cost of an hour, multiplied by how many available hours are actually billed. Three levers dominate. The pyramid: a project staffed with many juniors (cheap) under a few seniors (expensive) costs less per billed hour than an all-senior team. The offshore mix: every point of work moved from New Jersey to Chennai raises margin. And utilisation: an engineer on the bench costs the same as one who is billing. Currency is the fourth lever, outside management's control: revenue is mostly in dollars, euros and pounds, and about 55–60% of costs are rupee salaries. A weaker rupee widens the spread automatically.

Capital needs are trivial. Capex runs at about 2–3% of revenue (offices, laptops, data-centre equipment for managed services), working capital is mostly receivables of 55–95 days, and none of the large companies carries meaningful financial debt. The top five convert 97–117% of profit into free cash flow and return 80–100% of it through dividends and buybacks. That combination is why TCS earns a 63% return on capital employed and why, even after the 2026 sell-off, the group trades at more than four times book value.

In plain terms

An IT services company rents out skilled people. It makes money when it charges more per hour than it pays, keeps most of its people busy on paying work, and does most of the work in India where salaries are lower. It needs almost no factories or debt. The risk is that clients need fewer hours for the same job, either because software now does part of it or because they hire the engineers themselves.

02

Size, growth and the slowdown: a ₹28 lakh crore industry growing at half its old speed

India's tech industry earned $315 billion in FY26; the eighteen listed companies are under a third of it, and growth has halved since the 2004–2014 era.

NASSCOM, the industry association, estimates India's technology industry revenue at $315 billion in FY26, up 6.1%, equal to roughly ₹28.2 lakh crore at the FY26 average exchange rate of ₹89.6 to the dollar. That figure includes hardware, software products, engineering and R&D services (ER&D) and business-process management (BPM), as well as the captive centres of multinationals. Classic IT services, the core of the listed universe, is $149 billion of it, growing about 4.2%. Exports are $246 billion (78%); the domestic market about $69 billion.

Exports, by destination and currency

The Reserve Bank of India's annual survey gives the cleanest view of the export base. Software services exports (all firms, including captives, excluding overseas subsidiaries) were $221.4 billion in FY26, up 8.2%. Computer services were $153.4 billion (69%) and grew about 11%; IT-enabled services, mostly BPO, were $68.0 billion and grew only about 2%, an early sign of AI substitution in rules-based processing work. The United States took 54.1% and Europe 31.8% (the UK alone 15.4%). Invoicing is 72.6% in US dollars, 9.6% euros and 6.0% sterling, which is why the rupee–dollar rate matters so much.

For the listed companies the domestic market is small. HCLTech earns 3.4% of services revenue in India; TCS about 5–6%, and its India revenue fell 10.3% quarter on quarter in Q2 FY27 as a large government deal ran down. India matters more for Black Box, Datamatics (12%), Happiest Minds (18%) and, above all, Sonata, whose domestic Microsoft licence business is 73% of its group revenue.

The industry used to grow 15–25% a year; now it grows 5–6%, and the biggest listed firms grow even more slowly in dollar terms. Their rupee revenue still rises because each dollar now buys about ₹95 rather than ₹75.

03

Where demand comes from: the CIO's budget is moving from people to chips

Global IT spending is booming, but the growth is in data centres and software. Labour-based services, the pool Indian vendors fish in, grows at 3–4%.

Gartner's April 2026 forecast put worldwide IT spending at $6.31 trillion in 2026, up 13.5%, a figure that sounds like a boom for anyone selling technology. The composition tells a different story. Data-centre systems are forecast to grow 55.8% (after 51.6% in 2025) as hyperscalers and enterprises build AI capacity. Software grows 15.1%. IT services, at $1.87 trillion the largest segment, shows a headline growth of about 9%, but Gartner now includes infrastructure-as-a-service, cloud consumption bills paid to Amazon, Microsoft and Google, in that line. Kotak Institutional Equities, citing a different Gartner cut, puts services growth at 3.1% in 2025 and 4.2% in 2026. This report treats 3–4% as the growth of the pool that Indian vendors actually address.

This is the "AI capex crowd-out". Chief information officers have finite budgets. Every dollar spent on GPU clusters, model subscriptions and new software licences has to come from somewhere, and the easiest place to find it is the maintenance and support spend that Indian vendors dominate. Kotak's phrase is blunt: "new dollars flowing to AI infrastructure rather than services". The irony is that the same companies building data centres are also clients: TCS is building HyperVault data centres with OpenAI as a 100 MW anchor tenant, and Black Box's order backlog rose 83% to $949 million on hyperscaler projects. For application-services firms, though, the capex boom is a competitor for budget.

The United States: a fourth soft year

North America takes 54% of India's software exports and 56% of HCLTech's services revenue; it is 74% of LTM, 79% of Persistent, 83% of Birlasoft, 85% of Mphasis and 90% of Latent View. US discretionary spending has been weak since mid-2022, when rate rises hit technology, mortgage and banking clients. An analyst on HCLTech's April 2026 call called FY27 "the fourth year of poor demand". The deals that are being signed are about cost: HCLTech describes its deal mix as "cost take-out, vendor consolidation and modernization"; Wipro's large deals are "structural cost optimization" in which clients "consolidate multiple vendors into a single integrated operating model". Accenture's Q4 FY26 bookings show the same pattern globally: a book-to-bill of 1.4 in managed services against 1.0 in consulting. Clients are signing multi-year run contracts that promise savings, not discretionary projects that promise growth.

US tariff politics touch the sector indirectly. Services are not tariffed: the reciprocal tariff on Indian goods peaked at 50% in August 2025 and fell to 18% under the February 2026 interim framework. But tariffs squeeze the budgets of manufacturing, retail and automotive clients, and they move the rupee. Wipro's engineering and manufacturing unit shrank 8.9% in constant currency in Q1 FY27; Infosys cited a large European automotive client as a drag.

Industry verticals: banking is the bright spot

Banking, financial services and insurance (BFSI) is the largest vertical, typically 30–35% of tier-1 revenue and 68% at Mphasis. It is also the 2026 growth leader: Cognizant's financial services grew 11.7% in constant currency in its June 2026 quarter; TCS's BFSI grew 2.5% quarter on quarter in Q2 FY27; Zensar's banking share jumped to 49% on a $210 million deal. US banks are, at the same time, the most aggressive builders of their own Indian captive centres. Telecom, life sciences and manufacturing are the weak spots.

Europe and the rest of the world

Europe is 28–33% of revenue for most large vendors and grew faster than the US in FY25 (14.3% against 4.9% in RBI data), helped by UK mega-deals: TCS with Marks & Spencer, and TCS's purchase of Porsche's consulting arm MHP with a five-year, €1.25 billion partnership. In 2026 Europe has weakened on autos, manufacturing and geopolitics, though Tech Mahindra's European revenue grew 12.1% in Q1 FY27. Mid-tier firms are using acquisitions to build European scale: LTM's planned purchase of Randstad's technology consulting units (France, Germany, Benelux, Australia) and Persistent's Nagarro take-private would lift Europe to about 22% of each company's revenue.

What offsets the squeeze

Two forces push the other way. The first is vendor consolidation: a client that cuts from eight vendors to three creates a large contract for each winner, which is why bookings are at records even as growth stalls. It is close to zero-sum for the industry, and winners usually price 15–20% below the incumbent. The second is AI services demand. TCS's annualised AI revenue rose from $1.8 billion in Q3 FY26 to more than $3.1 billion in Q2 FY27, over 10% of its revenue; Infosys reports 8.2% of revenue as AI-related; HCLTech's "Advanced AI" revenue grew 78% to $219 million in the September quarter; NASSCOM puts industry AI revenue at $10–12 billion. Data engineering, AI governance, agent building and the modernisation of legacy systems that AI has made cheaper are growing, but companies define "AI revenue" differently and some of it is relabelled existing work.

In plain terms

Companies are spending much more on technology, but most of the increase is going to chips, data centres and software, not to the people who maintain systems. US clients are mostly signing deals that promise to save money rather than build new things. Banks are still spending; telecom and manufacturing clients are cutting. AI work is growing quickly, but from a small base and with loose definitions.

04

GenAI deflation: when a $100 million deal becomes an $80 million deal Deep dive

The industry is moving from selling volume to sharing productivity. Revenue per unit of scope now falls every year, and the effect is only starting to show.

For 25 years the industry's revenue equation was close to headcount × utilisation × rate. Productivity improved slowly and was shared slowly: analysts on HCLTech's April 2026 call noted that renewals of managed-services contracts historically carried a 10–15% price cut over the life of the deal, about 3–5% a year, and new scope more than made up for it. Generative AI changes the slope. Coding assistants and agents cut the hours needed to write and test code, document systems, resolve support tickets and run infrastructure. Clients know this, read the same vendor marketing and demand that the savings show up in the price.

HCLTech's chief executive, C. Vijayakumar, gave investors the clearest framework in April 2026. Roughly 40% of the industry is "AI disrupted": application development and support, traditional infrastructure operations, customer support. That pool "can shrink 3% to 5% CAGR for a few years and can eventually be 25% of the enterprise spend". Around 55% is "AI amplified": data, cybersecurity and cloud, which can grow "at about 10% plus". And 5% is "AI native", growing at 30% and able to reach "20% plus of the market in five years". He added that this deflation is incremental to the old 3–5% renewal give-backs, and that "very little has really played out in already reported numbers. We expect this to happen in FY'27 and onwards."

Three channels of deflation

  • Renewal re-pricing. Managed-services contracts signed in FY21–22, typically five years long, come up for renewal in FY26–28. Clients now write AI productivity commitments into the new terms. Sizing it: an illustrative portfolio calculation

HCLTech's numbers allow a rough calculation. Weighted by the market's mix (40% disrupted at −4%, 55% amplified at +10%, 5% native at +30%), the services market would grow about 5.4%. But Indian tier-1 portfolios are over-weight the disrupted bucket: application maintenance, infrastructure operations and BPO are perhaps 50–60% of their revenue. Re-weighting to 55% disrupted, 40% amplified (growing a little slower, at 8%) and 5% native gives about 2.5% growth, close to the FY27 guidance of 1.5–3.0% at Infosys and 2.5–4.0% at HCLTech. HCLTech itself says the 3–5% deflation on disrupted work translates into a 2–3% headwind for its own portfolio. The same arithmetic says the arithmetic only improves if the native bucket scales faster than 30% a year or the amplified bucket grows faster than 10%.

In plain terms

Picture a client that paid ₹100 a year to have its software maintained. AI tools now let the vendor do the same job with fewer people, and the client knows it, so at renewal it pays perhaps ₹80–90. The vendor's profit can survive if it really does use fewer people, but its revenue shrinks. To keep growing it must win new kinds of work (data, cloud, AI projects) faster than the old work shrinks. For the largest Indian firms, that race is roughly even today.

The offsets

Three things work in the vendors' favour. First, AI-native work is real and growing: TCS's AI revenue run-rate rose about 70% in three quarters to over $3.1 billion; HCLTech's Advanced AI revenue grew 78%; Mastek's AI order book went from $7 million to $64 million in four quarters; Happiest Minds' generative-AI unit grew to 5.2% of revenue. Second, AI makes previously uneconomic projects viable. Infosys's CEO said legacy modernisation with AI costs "60% lower than what they would have done before", which could unlock a wave of mainframe and legacy migrations that clients had postponed for years, the classic pattern where cheaper production raises total demand. Third, vendor consolidation rewards the firms with the best AI tooling, because clients choosing three vendors instead of eight pick those who promise the largest savings.

The precedent most often cited is 2014–2019, when cloud and software-as-a-service cannibalised legacy infrastructure and application work. Industry growth fell to 6–10% but did not collapse, because "digital" work grew from about 20% to 40% of revenue at 25–30% a year. The difference this time is that AI substitutes for the engineer's labour directly rather than moving the engineer to a new platform. Managements say deflation is a "curve" to stay ahead of; Kotak argues that "rupee depreciation has masked the impact so far".

Who is most and least exposed

Exposure depends on what a company does and how it prices. The most exposed are firms with large maintenance, testing, infrastructure-operations and BPO books priced on time and materials: Wipro, Tech Mahindra's business-process arm, the BPM-heavy Datamatics and Hexaware's 11% BPS segment, and parts of TCS and Infosys. Less exposed, at least for now, are product-engineering firms whose clients build new software (Persistent, Coforge's Encora unit), analytics and data businesses (Latent View, with 35% "primary AI" revenue), and physical-infrastructure work (Black Box, which benefits from AI capex). Contract structure also matters: fixed-price and outcome contracts let the vendor keep productivity gains until renewal, which is why Happiest Minds lifted its fixed-price share from 23% to 31% in a year and Latent View is pushing towards milestone pricing.

In plain terms

Deal values are a weaker guide than they used to be. A company can announce record bookings while its revenue barely moves, because each contract now covers more work for less money and replaces older, pricier contracts. To judge whether AI is helping or hurting a company, look at revenue growth and revenue per employee rather than headline deal wins.

Visas, tariffs and GCCs: the onsite model is closing and the client's own India centre is opening Deep dive

The US has made rotational onsite staffing prohibitively expensive, while clients hire 23.6 lakh people in their own Indian captives. Vendors are being paid to build their replacements.

For three decades the Indian model depended on moving engineers between India and client sites. Staff on H-1B (specialty-occupation) and L-1 (intra-company transfer) visas did requirements, transition and production support onsite, then rotated home. Since 2017 that model has been squeezed, and in September 2025 the United States all but closed it.

The September 2025 proclamation and what followed

Presidential Proclamation 10973, effective 21 September 2025, restricted the entry of H-1B workers unless the petition was accompanied by a payment of $100,000. It applied to new petitions for people outside the US, not to existing visa holders, extensions or in-country changes of status. It cited the IT industry's share of H-1B approvals, which rose from 32% in FY2003 to over 65%. A Department of Homeland Security rule published on 29 December 2025 replaced the random lottery with a wage-weighted selection (Level IV wage = four entries, Level I = one) for the FY2027 cap season, and the Department of Labor proposed higher prevailing wages in March 2026.

The US government's own September 2026 extension notice shows the effect. The largest IT staffing and outsourcing firms cut their combined H-1B registrations from 24,946 to 2,055, a 92% fall. Consular processing requests fell about 97%. The $100,000 was paid on only "over 700" petitions in a year: the fee worked as a near-ban, not a revenue source. Registrants with a US master's degree rose from 45.1% to 66.1% of the pool.

Why the revenue impact is small and the model impact is large

Every listed company has told investors the fee was "not material", and on revenue they are right. RBI data show that onsite delivery by people travelling from India (Mode 4) was only $18.4 billion of FY26 software exports; cross-border delivery from India (Mode 1) was $202.7 billion. Vendors had already localised: about 80% of Wipro's US workforce is hired locally; TCS says its green-card filings have been in single digits for two years. But the consequences show up elsewhere:

  • Onsite cost rises. New onsite roles must be filled by US hires or subcontractors costing perhaps 1.5–2 times an Indian H-1B. Subcontracting ratios have risen at fast-growing mid-tiers (Persistent from 18% to 23% of revenue, Coforge from 9% to 20%, partly acquisitions).
  • Ramp-ups slow. Large transitions used to fly in teams for knowledge transfer. Zensar cited "travel, visa and training" costs worth 1.5 points of margin in Q1 FY27 during a mega-deal transition; Latent View put H-1B filing and travel costs at about 1.1 points of EBITDA in Q4 FY26.
  • Work moves offshore, which cuts revenue. Infosys said a lower onsite mix took about 70 basis points off FY26 growth and guided onsite effort down another 75–100 basis points in FY27. Offshore work earns a higher margin but a lower price.

The tail risk: an outsourcing tax

The HIRE Act (Halting International Relocation of Employment Act), introduced by Senator Bernie Moreno in September 2025, would impose a 25% excise tax on payments by US companies to foreign persons for services that benefit US consumers, and deny a tax deduction for those payments. One law-firm calculation puts the effective cost of a $100 offshore invoice at about $125 plus the lost deduction, against about $79 after tax today, roughly 58% more. It would hit captives as well as vendors. As of 2026 it is stalled in the Senate Finance Committee; a unanimous-consent attempt was blocked. It is a low-probability, high-impact risk, and its revival would matter more to valuations than any visa rule.

In plain terms

The US has made it very expensive to send new Indian engineers to American client sites, and courts have only paused the $100,000 charge. Indian firms had already moved most work back to India, so revenue barely suffers. What suffers is flexibility and cost: when a client needs people onsite, the vendor must hire Americans or contractors at much higher pay.

GCCs: the client's own India centre

A global capability centre (GCC) is a captive subsidiary that a multinational runs in India to do its own technology, engineering, analytics and operations work. The NASSCOM–Zinnov 2026 landscape counts 2,117 GCCs with 3,728 units, about 23.6 lakh employees and $98.4 billion of revenue in FY26, up from $64.6 billion in FY24. The 2030 projection made in 2024 ($99–105 billion) has nearly been reached already.

Competitor and customer at once

As a competitor, a GCC takes work in-house, usually the most strategic engineering, data and AI work, and pays mid-level engineers 20–40% more than vendors, with double-digit increments. Latent View's largest technology client insourced work that "eroded almost USD 7–8 million" of its business in 2026, enough to halve its growth guidance. Infosys's CEO says there is no trend of GCCs handing work back to vendors, "but there are some examples".

As a customer, GCCs buy set-up advisory, staff augmentation, managed services for sub-scale functions and, increasingly, build-operate-transfer (BOT) contracts. Infosys won a BOT deal worth more than $500 million to build and run a Hyderabad GCC for Truist with about 4,500 staff, operating it for five years before handing it over. TCS has launched a GCC business unit and took over Best Buy's India GCC; LTM's Randstad deal includes transforming Randstad's India GCC; Wipro bought Olam's captive, Mindsprint; Mphasis says its rest-of-world growth was "led by GCCs". Everest Group sizes the vendor opportunity linked to GCCs at more than $25 billion in 2026, growing about 25% a year.

The net effect is modestly negative for the vendors' share of client wallets. A BOT contract is a three-to-five-year annuity that ends with the vendor transferring its own staff to the client: in effect, the vendor is paid to build its replacement. The companies best placed are those with engineering depth and BOT experience (HCLTech, LTM, Hexaware, Mphasis, Persistent) and those willing to buy captives outright to lock in long contracts.

In plain terms

Big foreign companies increasingly hire their own engineers in India rather than paying an Indian IT firm. Those captive centres now earn about as much as all eighteen listed firms combined and employ more people. Sometimes the IT firm gets paid to set up the captive and run it for a few years, but at the end the client takes it over, and the revenue stops.

Utilisation, the share of available hours that are billed, is where the industry found margin in FY24–26. The margin bridge: the rupee has been paying for flat margins Deep dive

Reported margins look steady. Underneath, about 150–200 basis points a year of currency gain is being spent on wage hikes, price cuts, restructuring and AI investment.

Given four years of weak demand, the stability of Indian IT margins is remarkable. TCS's company-reported EBIT margin was 25.0% in FY26 and 24.0% in Q2 FY27; Infosys has held 20–22% throughout; HCLTech guides 17.5–18.5%. The reason is not that costs are under control in any simple sense. It is that the rupee has fallen from about ₹74 to the dollar in FY21 to about ₹95.5 in the first half of FY27, and a weaker rupee mechanically widens the spread between dollar prices and rupee salaries.

How sensitive is a margin? An illustrative unit

The KPI section (Section 13) builds an illustrative 10,000-person delivery unit earning a TCS-like 24.4% EBIT margin. Shocking that unit one variable at a time shows which levers matter most. A 1% weaker rupee adds about 30 basis points. One extra point of utilisation adds about 90 basis points, because the extra revenue comes with no extra cost. A 7% offshore wage hike (3% onsite) costs about 220 basis points before any offset. A 3% price cut on half the revenue costs about 110 basis points if headcount does not change, but only about 40 if the vendor cuts staff on that work by the same 3%, which is Kotak's point that deflation is margin-neutral only when delivery cost falls with price.

Hedges, and the cost of a currency reversal

Companies hedge part of their expected dollar inflows, typically 6–24 months ahead. When the rupee falls faster than expected, hedges contracted at older rates generate losses that partly offset the translation gain. Persistent lost ₹105 crore on hedges in Q1 FY27 (book of $500 million at ₹93.3 against a spot rate near ₹95); Coforge booked about $10 million of hedge losses with $14 million more to come; Happiest Minds lost ₹11 crore. LTM's outstanding hedges were $5.0 billion at an average ₹95.17. The flip side matters more: if the rupee stabilises or strengthens, the cushion disappears and the underlying deflation will show up directly in reported margins. This is the main reason the sector is more exposed to a rupee reversal than its stable margin history suggests.

In plain terms

Indian IT firms are paid mostly in dollars and pay their staff in rupees. When the rupee weakens, each dollar of revenue turns into more rupees, while most costs stay the same, so profit margins rise without anyone doing anything. Over the past two years that boost has been worth roughly 1.5–2 percentage points a year, and companies have spent it on pay rises, discounts to clients and AI investment. If the rupee stops falling, the margins have to be defended the hard way.

In plain terms

When a company says "margin up 20 basis points", always ask how much came from currency. At Infosys in Q1 FY27 currency added 70 basis points, so the business underneath actually lost 50.

How the model shapes AI exposure

Fixed-price and managed-services contracts let the vendor keep productivity gains until the contract is renewed, then the client claims them. Time-and-materials contracts pass savings to the client immediately as fewer billed hours. BPM work priced per employee is the most directly substitutable: RBI data show IT-enabled services exports grew only about 2% in FY26. Yet Datamatics, the most BPM-heavy member, posted record margins (EBITDA 18.7% in FY26, 19.7% in Q1 FY27) because it used its own automation platforms to cut the effort behind its fixed-price document and publishing work. The model is not destiny; how a firm prices and whether it owns the tools matter as much.

Platform and partner relationships are a quieter source of difference. Coforge, Hexaware and Sonata depend heavily on specific software ecosystems (Pega, Microsoft, Oracle and others); Mastek's Oracle Cloud practice is 27% of its revenue; Birlasoft's ERP practice, built on Oracle JD Edwards and SAP, is 32%. When a platform changes its partner programme, as Microsoft did by billing some large customers directly, the reseller loses margin overnight.

In plain terms

Not every company in this group does the same thing. Most rent out engineers, but Sonata mainly resells Microsoft licences in India and Black Box mainly installs network cables and equipment for data centres. Their revenue includes a lot of hardware or software bought from others, so their margins look tiny and their revenue per employee looks huge. Judge them on their own terms, not against TCS.

11

Regulation and policy: labour codes at home, visa walls and tax threats abroad

Indian rules mostly affect cost (wages, tax, data); foreign rules affect the delivery model and, in the tail case, the whole offshore proposition.

IT services is lightly regulated compared with banking, pharmaceuticals or telecom. There are no licences to sell software services, no price controls and no capacity approvals. Policy matters through four channels: the cost of labour, the tax on profits, the rules on handling data, and the rules on moving people and money across borders. In 2025–26 all four changed at once.

India: tax

Most of the industry pays the 25.17% corporate rate under Section 115BAA, available since FY20. The historical tax shield, Special Economic Zone (SEZ) units under Section 10AA, has been closed to new units since 1 April 2020. Existing units keep their 15-year ladder (100% exemption for five years, 50% for five, then 50% subject to reinvestment), which runs off through the early 2030s. Effective tax rates are therefore drifting up towards 25–26%: TCS's was 24.6% in FY26, Infosys guides 29–30% for FY27 including overseas taxes. Exports of services are zero-rated under GST, but refunds tie up working capital. Transfer pricing is the other recurring friction: Mastek is appealing a FY2022-23 assessment with about ₹31 crore of tax at stake.

India: the Labour Codes

The four Labour Codes (Wages, Industrial Relations, Social Security, Occupational Safety) took effect on 21 November 2025. The provision that matters for IT is the definition of wages: basic pay plus dearness allowance must be at least 50% of total remuneration. Many IT pay structures kept basic pay low and allowances high, especially for junior grades. Raising the wage base raises gratuity, leave encashment and provident-fund accruals. Companies booked one-time charges in the December 2025 quarter and face a recurring cost that we estimate at 20–40 basis points of margin for the tier-1s, to be absorbed through the pyramid and automation.

The STPI scheme of 1991 and the SEZ Act of 2005 were the original enablers of offshore delivery; today's policy matters less for the vendors than for their captive competitors.

United States: visas, labour certification and the outsourcing tax

The H-1B changes are covered in Section 5: the $100,000 payment (collection blocked by the courts since 24 July 2026, extension to September 2027 in place), the wage-weighted lottery and the proposed higher prevailing wages. In October 2026 the US suspended the PERM green-card labour-certification programme; TCS, Infosys and Wipro each told the exchanges they expected no material impact. The HIRE Act's 25% excise tax on payments for foreign services remains stalled. State-level bills on offshoring disclosure, particularly for call centres, add friction for BPM firms.

Trade, Europe and the UK

US tariffs apply to goods, not services. The reciprocal tariff on Indian goods peaked at 50% in August 2025 (including a 25% penalty linked to Russian oil) and fell to 18% under the February 2026 interim framework. The effects on IT are second-order: through clients' budgets and through the rupee. For Black Box, tariffs on imported cable and networking gear matter more than any visa rule. In Europe, the EU AI Act's obligations phase in over 2025–27, creating compliance and AI-governance work. In the UK, public-sector procurement rules and HM Government digital budgets drive Mastek, whose government and education vertical is 43% of revenue.

Six decades of cycles: every downturn so far has ended with more offshoring

From body-shopping in the 1970s to the AI sell-off of 2026, each shock reshaped the model. The question now is whether this one breaks the pattern.

Indian IT's history is a sequence of shocks that each, in the end, pushed more work offshore. The dot-com bust of 2001–03 cut prices by 10–15% but made cost-cutting clients more willing to send work to India. The 2008–09 financial crisis slowed growth to about 5% in FY10, yet margins held because utilisation rose and the rupee weakened. The 2013 rupee crash, from about ₹54 to ₹68 to the dollar, was a windfall. The 2014–19 cloud transition cannibalised legacy work but created a larger "digital" business. COVID produced a dip and then the strongest boom in two decades. Each episode taught the same lesson: offshoring accelerates when clients need to cut costs.

Reading an IT services company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twelve numbers explain almost every quarter. Each is defined differently by somebody, and the differences are where investors get misled.

An IT services company publishes more operating data than almost any other Indian business: growth in constant currency, deal wins, headcount, utilisation, attrition, client buckets, receivable days. That is a gift and a trap. The gift is that you can see the machine working. The trap is that every company defines half these numbers its own way. This section starts with the equation that links them, works through each metric with a rupee example, compares the eighteen companies, and ends with a checklist for reading a results release.

A useful shortcut follows from the equation: EBIT margin ≈ 1 − (average cost per head ÷ revenue per head) − non-people costs. If revenue per employee rises faster than cost per employee, margins rise; if AI deflation cuts the price per unit of work and the company does not cut people, revenue per head falls and margins follow.

A worked example: what 10,000 engineers earn

Take an ILLUSTRATIVE delivery unit of 10,000 people, 22% onsite and 78% offshore, run at 85% utilisation. That gives 8,500 billed people: 1,870 onsite and 6,630 offshore. Each billed person works about 1,800 billable hours a year. Offshore time bills at $28 an hour and onsite at $95, and the exchange rate is ₹95 to the dollar.

  • Offshore revenue = 6,630 × 1,800 × $28 = $334 million.
  • Onsite revenue = 1,870 × 1,800 × $95 = $320 million.
  • Total = $654 million = ₹6,213 crore, or $65,400 per employee, almost exactly HCLTech's reported revenue per employee.
  • Employee cost: 7,800 offshore staff at an average ₹12 lakh = ₹936 crore, plus 2,200 onsite staff at $110,000 each = ₹2,299 crore, total ₹3,235 crore (52.1% of revenue).
  • Other costs: subcontractors 12% (₹746 crore), travel and visas 1.5% (₹93 crore), facilities and IT 3% (₹186 crore), sales and administration 4% (₹249 crore), depreciation 3% (₹186 crore).
  • EBIT = ₹6,213 − ₹4,695 = ₹1,518 crore, a 24.4% margin, close to TCS. Add treasury income of ₹93 crore, deduct 25% tax, and PAT is about ₹1,208 crore (19.4%).

Notice that onsite staff are 22% of the people but generate 49% of the revenue and absorb 71% of the employee cost. This is why the onsite–offshore mix moves both revenue and margin, and why visa rules that force onsite work offshore cut revenue while lifting margins.

In plain terms

Revenue is roughly the number of people, times the share of their time a client pays for, times the hourly price. Profit is what is left after paying those people and their contractors. Almost every metric below measures one piece of that sum.

KPI 1: Constant-currency revenue growth

Definition. Growth in revenue after removing the effect of exchange-rate movements: this period's revenue, translated at last period's exchange rates, divided by last period's revenue. Wipro's definition is typical: current-period volumes at prior-period rates. Companies report it year on year and quarter on quarter.

Worked example. A company earns $1,000 million this year against $980 million last year (growth 2.0%). Last year's average rate was ₹86, this year's ₹95. Rupee revenue rises from ₹8,428 crore to ₹9,500 crore, up 12.7%. The 10.7-point difference is translation. If part of the revenue is in euros or pounds that moved against the dollar, dollar growth and constant-currency growth also differ; the constant-currency figure removes both effects.

Drivers. Volume (people billed), price (rates, give-backs, mix), new clients and acquisitions. Constant-currency growth still includes acquisitions unless the company reports an "organic" figure: Coforge's Q1 FY27 quarter-on-quarter growth was 22.3% reported, 1.1% organic and 5.2% organic excluding planned exits.

Benchmarks. For a tier-1, more than 6% a year is good, 3–6% average and below 2% weak; for a mid-tier, more than 12% is good, 7–12% average and below 5% weak. Quarter on quarter, above 2% is strong, 0.5–2% normal and below zero weak.

KPI 2: Deal TCV, bookings and book-to-bill

Definition. Total contract value (TCV) is the total value of contracts signed in a period over their full life. Book-to-bill divides bookings by revenue for the same period; above 1 means the order book is growing.

Worked example. A company signs a five-year, $500 million managed-services contract in a quarter in which it bills $400 million. The deal alone gives a book-to-bill of 1.25. But it adds only about $100 million a year of revenue once fully ramped, after a 12–18-month transition, and if it replaces an older contract worth $120 million a year from the same client, the net effect on revenue is negative even though bookings look excellent.

Drivers. Vendor consolidation, renewals of large contracts, mega-deals (over $500 million), and the pricing of each deal. Under AI deflation, the same scope books at a lower value: HCLTech's "$100 million is now $80 million".

Benchmarks. On total bookings, a book-to-bill above 1.3 is strong, 1.0–1.3 average and below 1.0 weak. A net-new share above 50% (Infosys reported 55% for FY26 and 61% in Q1 FY27) is a sign that deals add revenue rather than renew it.

In plain terms

A big deal announcement tells you a client signed a long contract. It does not tell you how much new money the company will earn next year, because the contract may replace an older one, may take a year to start, and may be priced lower than it would have been two years ago. Look at what happens to revenue over the following four quarters.

KPI 3: EBIT margin and the margin bridge

Definition. EBIT (earnings before interest and tax, often called "operating margin") divided by revenue, after depreciation and amortisation but before treasury income. The bridge decomposes the change from one period to the next into drivers: currency, wages, utilisation, pricing, subcontracting, amortisation, one-offs.

Worked example. In the 10,000-person unit, EBIT is ₹1,518 crore on ₹6,213 crore of revenue: 24.4%. A 7% offshore wage hike and 3% onsite hike add ₹135 crore of cost and cut the margin to about 22.3%. A 3% weaker rupee adds roughly 90 basis points back. Reported margin: about 23.2%, "down only 120 basis points", while the underlying business lost more than two points.

Drivers. Wage cycles (usually one quarter a year), utilisation, pyramid, onsite mix, subcontracting, pricing and productivity give-backs, currency and hedges, acquisition amortisation and restructuring.

Benchmarks. Company-basis EBIT above 22% is strong for a tier-1 (17–22% average, below 16% weak); for a mid-tier, above 18% is strong, 14–18% average, below 13% weak.

KPI 4: Revenue per employee

Definition. Annualised revenue in US dollars divided by average headcount over the period. HCLTech's own version divides last-twelve-month revenue by the average of the last five quarter-end headcounts.

Worked example. A company bills $1,660 million in a quarter with average headcount of 147,200. Drivers. Onsite mix (onsite staff bill three to four times more), pricing, pyramid, the share of BPM staff (who bill far less), pass-through revenue and productivity.

Benchmarks. Above $65,000 is strong for an IT-services-only firm, $50,000–65,000 average, below $45,000 weak, unless the firm has a large BPM workforce.

KPI 5: Utilisation

Definition. Billed effort divided by available effort, usually reported both excluding and including trainees. Infosys's FY26 utilisation was 84.4% excluding trainees and 81.1% including them.

Worked example. In the 10,000-person unit, raising utilisation from 85% to 86% puts 100 more people on billable work. At the blended rate that adds about ₹73 crore of revenue with no extra cost, lifting EBIT margin by roughly 90 basis points. The lever works in reverse: Persistent's utilisation fell 150 basis points in Q1 FY27 as it hired ahead of a large ramp, and Mphasis's fell from 83.6% to 81.0%.

Drivers. Fresher batches (trainees sit on the bench), hiring ahead of deal ramps, project completions, leave seasons and the decision to keep a bench for agility.

Benchmarks. Excluding trainees, 85–88% is strong, 81–85% average, below 80% weak; above 89% is also a warning sign, because there is no bench to absorb new work.

KPI 6: Attrition, net additions and freshers

Definition. Last-twelve-month voluntary attrition is the number of employees who resigned in the past year divided by average headcount. Net additions are closing minus opening headcount; fresher intake is campus hires.

Worked example. A 10,000-person unit with 13% attrition loses 1,300 people a year. Replacing them at a recruitment and training cost of perhaps ₹1–2 lakh each costs ₹13–26 crore, plus lost productivity while new staff ramp up. At 25% attrition, as in FY23, the bill doubles and wage inflation follows because replacements are hired at market rates.

Drivers. The demand cycle, GCC hiring (which pays 20–40% more for mid-level engineers), wage-hike timing and the company's culture and brand.

Benchmarks. 10–14% is healthy; 14–18% average; above 20% is costly. Below 8% is not ideal either: the pyramid ages and costs rise.

KPI 7: Subcontracting and onsite mix

Definition. Subcontracting cost as a percentage of revenue: payments to contractors and other vendors who deliver part of the work. Onsite mix is the share of effort (or revenue, or headcount) delivered at client locations.

Worked example. In the 10,000-person unit, subcontractors cost 12% of revenue. If visa limits force the unit to replace 200 onsite H-1B engineers with local US contractors and subcontracting rises to 14%, the extra ₹124 crore of cost cuts EBIT margin by about 200 basis points. Moving onsite work offshore instead, from 22% to 20% of staff, cuts revenue by about ₹190 crore but saves about ₹209 crore of onsite pay against ₹30 crore of extra offshore staff, leaving EBIT roughly flat and the margin about 60 basis points higher on lower revenue.

Drivers. Speed of ramp-ups, visa availability, niche skills the company does not have in-house, and acquisition accounting (acquired firms' contractors). Benchmarks: subcontracting below 10% of revenue is lean, 10–15% average, above 18% heavy; a gently falling onsite mix is margin-positive, a rising one usually means ramp-up or visa costs.

The definition trap. Screener's "manufacturing cost" line for IT firms mixes subcontractors with third-party software and hardware; HCLTech reports "outsourcing costs"; Infosys groups subcontracting with third-party items. Onsite mix may be stated in effort (Infosys, LTM), revenue (Wipro, Mphasis, Coforge) or headcount (Mastek, Happiest Minds), and the revenue share is always higher than the effort share.

KPI 8: Client concentration

Definition. The share of revenue from the largest client, top five, top ten and top twenty, usually on a trailing-twelve-month basis, plus "client buckets": the number of clients paying more than $1 million, $10 million, $50 million and $100 million a year.

Worked example. Latent View's top five clients provide 57% of revenue. When its largest technology client insourced work worth about $7–8 million a year in early 2026, roughly 6% of revenue disappeared, and FY27 growth guidance fell from about 19% to about 12%. HCLTech's top five provide 11.4%; losing the same share of a single client would barely register.

Drivers. Large deals with a few clients (mid-tier growth is often built this way), pruning of small accounts, acquisitions that add new clients. Benchmarks: top-10 below 25% is diversified, 25–40% average, above 40% (or a single client above 10%) concentrated. A rising count of $50 million-plus and $100 million-plus clients signals successful "mining".

KPI 9: Days sales outstanding (DSO)

Definition. Receivables divided by revenue, expressed in days: how long, on average, the company waits to be paid. Companies differ on whether unbilled revenue (work done but not yet invoiced) and contract assets are included.

Worked example. The 10,000-person unit earns ₹6,213 crore a year, or about ₹17 crore a day. At 60 days DSO it has ₹1,021 crore tied up in receivables; at 90 days, ₹1,532 crore. The 30-day difference is ₹511 crore of cash, a third of a year's EBIT, that the company cannot pay out or invest.

Drivers. Client payment terms (large clients increasingly impose 60–90 days), milestone billing in fixed-price and project work, delayed remittances in some geographies (Mastek's Middle East), government clients, and the build-up of unbilled revenue during large transitions. Benchmarks: below 65 days strong, 65–80 average, above 85 weak.

KPI 10: Free-cash-flow conversion

Definition. Free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital expenditure) divided by profit after tax. Above 100% means the company turns all its reported profit, and then some, into cash.

Worked example. A company reports PAT of ₹1,000 crore with ₹150 crore of depreciation. If receivables rise by ₹100 crore and capex is ₹120 crore, free cash flow is roughly ₹1,000 + ₹150 − ₹100 − ₹120 = ₹930 crore, a 93% conversion. If a large acquisition adds a non-cash amortisation charge, PAT falls but cash does not, and conversion rises above 100%.

Drivers. Working capital (DSO), capex intensity (2–3% of revenue normally; more for data-centre ambitions), the gap between book and cash tax, non-cash charges and treasury income (which counts in PAT but sits outside operations). Benchmarks: above 100% strong, 85–100% average, below 80% weak.

KPI 11: AI revenue share

Definition. Revenue that the company attributes to AI services, as a share of total revenue or as an annualised run-rate. There is no common standard.

Worked example. TCS's AI revenue run-rate of $3.1 billion against quarterly revenue of $7.642 billion (about $30.6 billion annualised) is about 10%. If half of that is existing data and automation work relabelled, the genuinely new AI revenue is closer to 5%, and its growth is what offsets deflation elsewhere.

Drivers. AI-native projects (agent building, AI factories, data platforms), AI embedded in managed-services deals, and the company's labelling choices. Benchmarks: above 10%, disclosed consistently and growing faster than total revenue, is good; 5–10% average; below 5% or undisclosed weak.

KPI 12: Return on capital and what is done with the cash

Definition. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is EBIT divided by the capital tied up in the business (equity plus debt, or total assets less current liabilities). Companies also publish return on invested capital (ROIC), which strips out surplus cash: HCLTech's last-twelve-month ROIC was 41.2%, and 49.4% in its services business. The payout ratio is dividends plus buybacks divided by profit or free cash flow.

Worked example. The 10,000-person unit needs about ₹1,000 crore of receivables, ₹400–500 crore of laptops, offices and data-centre equipment and little else, perhaps ₹1,500 crore of operating capital in all. Against ₹1,518 crore of EBIT that is a pre-tax return of about 100% on operating capital. Reported ROCE is lower, 30–60% for the best firms, because companies also hold large cash balances and goodwill from acquisitions, both of which sit in the denominator.

Drivers. Margin, working capital, acquisition goodwill (which lowers ROCE immediately after a deal) and the amount of idle cash. This is why the industry returns most of its cash: TCS paid out 81% of FY26 profit, HCLTech 97.6% of net income, Tech Mahindra about 94%; Infosys bought back ₹18,000 crore of shares and Wipro ₹15,000 crore in the past year. A company that stops paying out, as Persistent and Coforge have to fund acquisitions, is changing its model. Benchmarks: ROCE above 40% strong, 25–40% average, below 20% weak; a payout of 85–100% of free cash flow is normal for a mature tier-1.

In plain terms

An IT services company needs very little money to run: no factories, little stock, just offices, laptops and the bills its clients have not yet paid. That is why the best of them earn returns on capital that most industries never see, and why they hand most of their profit back to shareholders. When one of them starts borrowing to buy another company, it is changing the shape of the business.

How the KPIs connect

The metrics are not independent, and reading them together is more useful than reading any one alone. Strong bookings with flat revenue and rising revenue per employee point to deflation absorbed by productivity: the company is winning work but pricing it lower and staffing it leaner. Rising headcount, falling utilisation and flat margin point to a deal ramp that should show up as revenue in one or two quarters; if it does not, the ramp has slipped. A rising margin with constant-currency growth below 2% and a walk dominated by currency means the underlying business is weakening behind a rupee cushion. Rising DSO with rising unbilled revenue on fixed-price work can mean milestones are slipping. And rising concentration with a falling count of mid-sized clients, as at Birlasoft, means the company is becoming more dependent on a few relationships even if the headline growth looks stable.

Definition traps, collected

  • Rupee growth against constant-currency growth. With the rupee about 10% weaker year on year, rupee growth overstates real growth by 8–12 points. Use constant currency, and ask for the organic figure.
  • TCV means different things. Total contract value (TCS), large deals above $50 million (Infosys) or $30 million (Wipro), net-new only (HCLTech, Mphasis), new wins only (Tech Mahindra), order intake (Coforge, LTM), 12-month backlog (Mastek), or nothing (Hexaware, Latent View, Happiest Minds, Datamatics).
  • Margins on four bases. Screener OPM (EBITDA proxy), company EBIT, company EBITDA, and "EBITDA including other income" (Happiest Minds; Hexaware until CY25). Company EBIT may also exclude restructuring and Labour Code charges.
  • Segment against group. Wipro's 16.0% is the IT services segment; Sonata's meaningful margin is its international services arm, not the group; Black Box's revenue includes hardware.
  • Utilisation with or without trainees, gross or net of leave, and sometimes redefined (Hexaware excluded platform staff in 2026).
  • Revenue per employee depends on mix: BPM staff (Tech Mahindra, Wipro, Datamatics), onsite share (Mastek) and pass-through revenue (Sonata, Black Box) swamp productivity differences.
  • Attrition can be voluntary or total, IT-only or company-wide, trailing or annualised quarterly.
  • Headcount includes acquisitions: Coforge's Encora added 9,256 people in one quarter; Wipro's Mindsprint hid a 2,500 organic decline.
  • DSO billed against total: differences of 25–40 days between billed and billed-plus-unbilled measures.
  • PAT before or after minority interest (Coforge on screener), with or without one-offs (HCLTech FY26 ₹16,652 crore reported against ₹17,361 crore excluding Labour Codes).
  • Calendar against fiscal year: Hexaware's "FY26" in this report is calendar 2025, and its latest quarter (April–June 2026) is the same calendar quarter as peers' Q1 FY27.
  • AI revenue is self-defined and often includes relabelled work.

How to read an IT services company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find the constant-currency growth, quarter on quarter and year on year, and the organic figure if there was an acquisition. Ignore rupee growth until you have it.
  2. Compare with guidance. Did the company meet, beat or cut its annual or next-quarter range? A guidance cut matters more than a one-quarter miss.
  3. Read the EBIT margin and the walk. How many basis points came from currency? Subtract them to see the underlying trend.
  4. Check deal wins on the company's own definition and the net-new share. Then check whether last year's big wins are now showing up as revenue.
  5. Look at headcount, utilisation and attrition together. Falling headcount with flat revenue means productivity (or deflation absorbed by cutting staff); rising headcount ahead of revenue means a ramp or a bench build.
  6. Scan verticals and geographies for a single large decline (a telecom client, a European automaker, an Indian government deal).
  7. Check client concentration and buckets. Did the top client shrink? Did the $50 million-plus count rise?
  8. Check cash: DSO (billed and unbilled) and free cash flow against PAT for the quarter and the trailing year.
  9. Read the AI disclosure sceptically: is AI revenue growing faster than total revenue, and is total growth accelerating?
  10. Note the one-offs and the calendar: wage-hike timing, restructuring charges, hedge losses, tax reversals, and when the next results and any pending acquisitions or mergers are due.
In plain terms

The fastest way to understand an IT company's quarter: find growth in constant currency, see how much of the margin came from the rupee, check whether last year's deal wins have turned into revenue, and check whether profit turned into cash. Everything else is detail.

How to read this table

Start with the combined row. The eighteen companies earned ₹8.65 lakh crore of revenue in FY26 at a 22.1% screener operating margin and a 14.8% PAT margin, and the market values them at 17.7 times trailing profit. That multiple is weighted heavily by the tier-1s, which trade at 13–19 times; the mid and small companies together trade at about 29 times.

Read growth in two columns, not one. The rupee growth column shows FY26 against FY25 and includes both currency and acquisitions. Coforge's 35.9% includes Cigniti; Persistent's 23.5% is close to organic; TCS's 4.6% in rupees was a 2.4% decline in constant currency. The constant-currency column is the latest quarter, year on year: it is the better guide to the current pace of the business, and it shows the four largest companies at 1–3%.

Read margins with the basis in mind. The OPM column is screener's EBITDA proxy and is comparable across the eighteen; the EBIT column is each company's own number and is 2–3.5 points lower for the same company. Sonata's 6.9% and Black Box's 8.8% are not low-quality versions of TCS's 27.1%; they are different businesses with pass-through revenue.

Read valuation against growth and cash. The cheapest stocks on P/E (Mastek 12.1×, Wipro 12.7×, Zensar 13.7×, Sonata 13.8×, Infosys 13.9×, Birlasoft 13.9×) are those with the slowest growth; the most expensive (Black Box 57.3×, Persistent 47.6×, Coforge 44.6×) have the fastest or most visible growth. Free-cash-flow conversion adds a quality filter: Mphasis's 50% and Black Box's 6% mean their reported profits are not yet fully backed by cash. Tech Mahindra's 28.4× reflects a margin turnaround that is still in progress, not current profitability.

Read concentration as a risk discount. Latent View (57% from five clients), Birlasoft (43.4%) and Mphasis (40%) can lose a fifth of their growth in a quarter from one client decision. The tier-1s cannot. Finally, note the corporate events that will change several rows within a year: Persistent's Nagarro acquisition (closing by early 2027), Coforge's full-year Encora consolidation, LTM's Randstad purchase, TCS's MHP purchase, and Happiest Minds' merger into ITC Infotech, after which it will cease to be listed separately.

In plain terms

The table lets you compare the eighteen companies on size, profit, growth and price in one view. Who is winning: growth leaders, margin defenders and the squeezed middle

Four maps show the trade-offs: growth against valuation, margin against growth, concentration against productivity, and collection speed against cash conversion.

No company in this group has everything. The maps below place each company on two dimensions at a time. Together they show three clusters. The margin defenders (TCS, Infosys, HCLTech) earn the highest margins and returns, have the most diversified client books and convert profit into cash, but grow at 2–3%. The growth leaders (Persistent above all, then Mphasis, Tech Mahindra, LTM, Hexaware, Happiest Minds) grow at 6–17% on mid-teens margins, with higher concentration and higher valuations. The squeezed middle (Wipro, Zensar, Birlasoft, Sonata's services arm, Mastek) is cheap because it is not growing.

The growth-against-valuation map shows how directly the market is paying for growth. The tier-1s sit in a tight cluster at 1–3% growth and 13–19 times earnings; HCLTech carries the highest multiple of the four because it is the only one that raised FY27 guidance. Persistent stands alone at the top right, growing 16.5% at 47.6 times earnings. The middle band, 6–10% growth at 20–28 times, mixes recovering turnarounds (Tech Mahindra, LTM) with mid-tiers whose growth has wobbled (Hexaware cut its CY26 guidance; Latent View lost a large account).

The margin-against-growth map shows that the top-right quadrant, fast and highly profitable, is empty. TCS and Infosys sit far right on margin and low on growth; Persistent is high on growth with a margin no better than Wipro's. The companies to watch are those trying to move up-and-right: Tech Mahindra under its Project Fortius plan (14.4% EBIT, target 15%, with 6.6% growth) and HCLTech, which combines 18–19% margins ex-restructuring with the best growth among the big four.

The concentration map reveals what mid-tier growth is built on. The tier-1s cluster on the left (top-5 clients under 15%). Persistent, Mphasis, Birlasoft and Latent View each depend on five clients for a third or more of revenue. Revenue per employee is a mix effect more than a productivity ranking: Mastek is high because of onsite UK staff, Datamatics low because it is a BPM business.

The cash-quality map separates the companies whose profits are fully backed by cash from those whose are not. Most of the group sits above 90% conversion with DSO between 40 and 75 days. The outliers are Mphasis (95 days, 50%), the acquisitive Coforge and LTM (below 80%), Latent View (70%, partly because its PAT includes treasury income) and Black Box, whose receivables doubled as data-centre projects billed on milestones.
