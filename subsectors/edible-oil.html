Indian edible oils: a refining business run by customs

00

The one-page view: three rupees in every hundred

India imports more vegetable oil than any other country. Three listed companies refine, pack and trade a large slice of it, and their profits depend more on Delhi's customs tariff and on the timing of their stock than on how much oil Indians cook with.

India uses roughly 26 million tonnes of edible oil a year (CRISIL's FY26 consumption estimate) and grows less than half of it. In 2023-24 domestic production was 12.18 million tonnes and imports were 15.66 million tonnes, which puts official import dependence at 56.25% (PIB, December 2025). In the oil year to October 2025 the country imported 16.0 million tonnes of edible oil and paid ₹1.61 lakh crore for it (SEA). Almost all of that arrives as crude oil in tankers from Indonesia, Malaysia, Argentina, Brazil and the Black Sea, and is refined, packed and distributed in India. That last step, turning a tanker of crude palm or degummed soybean oil into a one-litre pouch on a kirana shelf or a 15-kilo tin in a restaurant kitchen, is the business this report covers.

Three companies in the NSE 750 do it at scale. AWL Agri Business (formerly Adani Wilmar, now controlled by Singapore's Wilmar International) owns Fortune, the country's largest edible-oil brand, and sold 6.85 million tonnes of oil, food and industrial products in FY26. Patanjali Foods is the old Ruchi Soya, bought out of insolvency by the Patanjali group in 2019 and since turned into a mix of edible oil (Ruchi Gold, Mahakosh, Nutrela, Patanjali), packaged food, biscuits, personal care and India's largest private oil-palm plantation programme. Gokul Agro Resources is a Gujarat refiner-trader that sells mostly in bulk to other businesses, exports biodiesel to Europe and runs four port refineries. Together they had FY26 revenue of ₹1,38,978 crore, operating profit (screener basis) of ₹4,569 crore and a market value of ₹67,995 crore on 22 October 2026.

The single most useful fact about this industry is how thin the margin is. Of every ₹100 of revenue, about ₹95–97 goes to buy the oil, seeds and grain. What is left pays for refining, packing, freight, advertising and staff, and leaves an operating profit of about ₹3. A bulk refiner such as Gokul kept ₹2.8 of every ₹100 in FY26; AWL ₹2.9 (₹3.1 on its own "normalised" basis); Patanjali, with a quarter of its revenue in branded food and personal care, ₹4.4. Profit after tax is about ₹1.5 per ₹100. When a margin is that thin, a 1% move in the price of the oil sitting in a company's tanks moves profit by a third. That is why the five forces below, not consumer demand, decide who earns what.

The five forces that define the sub-sector

  • The duty differential decides who refines. Since May 2025 crude edible oil has paid about 19 percentage points less import duty than refined oil. The cut of 24 September 2026 (Notification 31/2026-Customs) lowered both rates by five points and kept the gap. Refined imports have shrunk to 5% of the mix, and 92% of what is left (682,000 tonnes) comes duty-free from Nepal under SAFTA.
  • Inventory and hedging swing the P&L more than demand. Volumes grow 2–6% a year; profit per tonne swings 30–50%. Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) was an inventory-gain quarter for all three companies. The September duty cut and Malaysian palm stocks of 3.51 million tonnes make Q3 FY27 the quarter to watch for losses.
  • Import dependence ties margins to other countries' decisions. Indonesia's B50 biodiesel mandate (live from 1 July 2026), Argentine soybean crushing, the Black Sea sunflower crop, West Asia freight and a rupee near ₹94.9 to the dollar all feed straight into Indian costs.
  • Value is migrating to adjacencies. Patanjali's food and personal-care business is 28% of revenue and 61% of segment EBITDA; AWL is building a food and FMCG arm; Patanjali is planting oil palm; Gokul is exporting biodiesel. The stock market pays for the adjacencies, not the oil.
  • Working capital is the hidden balance sheet. Imports are financed with letters of credit and supplier acceptances that do not appear as "borrowings" on screener. AWL shows ₹1,109 crore of borrowings, but its rating agency counts net debt of ₹4,425 crore.
In plain terms

An Indian edible-oil company is a very large pipe. Crude oil flows in from abroad, gets cleaned, packed and sold, and the company keeps about three rupees of every hundred that pass through. The pipe is profitable only because the government taxes refined imports more heavily than crude ones, and it is most profitable in quarters when the price of oil rises while the company is still holding stock it bought cheaper.

The latest numbers show both the upside and the trap. In Q1 FY27 the three members' combined revenue rose 19% to ₹36,667 crore, but operating profit rose 75% to ₹1,440 crore and profit after tax 65% to ₹810 crore. Volumes barely moved: AWL's edible-oil tonnage was up 2%. What changed was the rupee cost of crude palm oil, which averaged about ₹1,18,500 a tonne before duty in April–June 2026 against about ₹91,400 a year earlier (an INDICATIVE calculation from SEA's monthly dollar prices and the exchange rate). Companies that held oil bought at the old price sold it at the new one. AWL's edible-oil profit reached ₹5,030 a tonne, 30% more than a year earlier; Patanjali's edible-oil margin doubled to 5.22%; Gokul posted its best quarterly margin on record, 3.9%.

The same mechanism runs in reverse. When oil prices fell 40–50% in 2023, AWL lost money in two consecutive quarters and earned only ₹148 crore for FY24. On 24 September 2026 the government cut the effective duty on crude palm and soybean oil from 16.5% to 11%, which lowers the replacement cost of every tonne in the pipeline. Malaysian palm stocks rose 25% in one month to 3.51 million tonnes at the end of September. None of the three has yet reported a quarter that contains these events. AWL reports Q2 FY27 on 27 October 2026, Gokul's board meets on 28 October, and Patanjali, which reported Q2 FY26 on 1 November last year, has not yet announced a date. AWL has already said its Q2 volume rose 12% and revenue 24%.

01

From crude tanker to kitchen: how a tonne of oil earns its margin

The money is made in four places: the refining spread, the duty gap, the timing of inventory, and whatever higher-margin product sits next to the oil on the shelf.

Edible oil looks like a consumer business and behaves like a commodity-processing one. The product is a pouch of Fortune soyabean oil or a tin of Vitalife palmolein, but the economics are those of a port-side processing plant: buy a globally traded raw material in dollars, pay customs duty, convert it at a small spread, and sell it in rupees within six to ten weeks. Branding, distribution and adjacent foods sit on top of that engine and can add a few hundred rupees a tonne; inventory gains and losses can add or remove a few thousand.

The products

India eats eight or nine oils, and the mix matters because each one has a different source, duty and margin. Palm oil is the cheapest and the most imported; soybean and sunflower oil are imported crude and refined in India; mustard, groundnut, rice-bran and cottonseed oil are mostly crushed from Indian seed. Every member sells several, and all three also sell the by-products of crushing and refining.

The value chain

All three members run the same core model: import, refine, pack and sell from port-based plants. The crude oil is bought in dollars, usually on "CIF India" terms (cost, insurance and freight paid to an Indian port), and often through a Singapore trading desk. Wilmar's trading arm plays that role for AWL; Gokul has its own Singapore subsidiaries, Maurigo and Riya International. The oil is pumped into tank farms at Kandla and Mundra in Gujarat, Haldia in West Bengal, Krishnapatnam and Kakinada in Andhra Pradesh, Mangalore and Chennai. Customs duty is paid when it leaves the bonded tank. It is then refined (degummed, neutralised, bleached and deodorised), fractionated in the case of palm (split into liquid olein and solid stearin), packed into pouches, bottles, jars and tins, and shipped to distributors, institutions and export buyers.

Most of the rupee value is created in Step 1 and captured by foreign growers. Indian processors earn their margin in Steps 3–5, and gain or lose a much larger sum on the price of the oil they hold between Step 2 and Step 5.

Two smaller chains run alongside. Domestic seed crushing (soybean in Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra, mustard in Rajasthan, groundnut and cottonseed in Gujarat) turns seed into crude oil and de-oiled cake; about 80% of a soybean by weight comes out as meal, which is sold to feed makers or exported. Crush margins are squeezed between the government's minimum support price for the seed and the export price of the meal. Third, and newest, is domestic oil palm: farmers grow it under state-allotted "factory zones" and must sell the fresh fruit bunches to the processor that holds the zone, at a price linked to international crude palm oil. Patanjali runs six mills on this model (section 15).

Both end up with an EBITDA of about ₹3. The brand does not raise the margin much; it raises the stability of volume and the price the stock market will pay for it.

Where the profit actually comes from

Strip a refiner's earnings into their sources and five profit pools emerge. The first is the refining and packing spread: the difference between the realised price and the landed cost of crude plus conversion, roughly ₹1,500–3,000 a tonne of EBITDA in a normal year for a bulk refiner (an INDICATIVE range). The second is the duty arbitrage: because refined imports pay about 19 points more duty than crude, a domestic refiner can price its product up to that gap above its own cost before an imported refined tanker becomes competitive. The third is inventory and hedging: rising prices produce gains on stock bought earlier, falling prices or duty cuts produce losses. The fourth is the adjacent FMCG business: Patanjali's biscuits earned a 15.35% EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27, its Nutrela soya chunks over 18% and its personal-care business 19.4%, against 5.22% on its edible oil in a boom quarter. The fifth is upstream or specialty: oil-palm milling (Patanjali), oleochemicals and specialty fats (AWL's Industry Essentials) and biodiesel exports (Gokul).

The last point about the chain is what capital it needs. Very little of it is plant. AWL's edible-oil segment employed ₹11,607 crore of capital in the twelve months to June 2026, and ₹9,344 crore of that was net working capital: oil in tanks and in transit, and money owed by customers, less money owed to suppliers. The segment's 14% return on capital came from turning its assets over 5.1 times a year at a 2.8% EBIT margin. Returns in this industry are an asset-turn story, not a margin story. A refiner that runs its plants full and gets its suppliers to finance its stock can earn a high return on a tiny margin; one that holds long inventories on its own money cannot. That single fact explains most of the gap between Gokul's 37% return on capital and Patanjali's 12% (sections 10 and 16).

In plain terms

A refinery's own work, cleaning and packing the oil, earns only a sliver. The bigger sums come from things around it: a tax gap that keeps imported refined oil out, the luck or skill of holding stock when prices rise, and the higher-margin biscuits, soaps or soya chunks sold through the same trucks. When you look at an edible-oil company, ask which of those it is really earning from this quarter.

02

A 26-million-tonne habit: size, growth and the import bill

Consumption grows slowly and steadily. The import bill does not: it moved from ₹63,000 crore to ₹1.61 lakh crore in six years on almost the same tonnage.

Measuring India's edible-oil market depends on which number you pick, and the sources disagree for good reasons. CRISIL estimates FY26 consumption at about 25.5–26 million tonnes, growing 2.8–3.0% a year (five-year average 2.7%). PIB's official arithmetic adds 2023-24 domestic production (12.18 million tonnes) to imports (15.66 million tonnes) to get a supply figure of about 27.8 million tonnes, which includes stock-building and some non-food use. IMARC, quoted in Patanjali's annual report, puts the 2025 market at 25.33 million tonnes. This report uses about 26 million tonnes for consumption (CRISIL) and about 28 million tonnes for supply (PIB) and names the basis each time.

Per-person consumption: doubled in rural India

The government's household consumption survey shows how far the habit has spread. Rural households used 5.76 kilos of edible oil per person a year in 2004-05 and 10.58 kilos in 2022-23, an 84% rise; urban use rose from 7.92 to 11.78 kilos. Add restaurants, street food and processed food, and apparent availability works out at about 19–20 kilos per person (an INDICATIVE figure, supply divided by population). That is well above the 10–12 kilos nutritionists recommend, and the National Mission on Edible Oils set an awareness goal to "maintain consumption level of 19.00 kg/person/annum till 2025-26", in effect asking Indians to stop increasing their oil use. A government that wants consumption to plateau is one reason long-run volume growth is unlikely to rise much above 2–3% a year.

The value: an import bill that tracks the rupee

By value the picture changes completely. SEA's figures show India paid ₹66,942 crore for its vegetable-oil imports in OY2017-18 and ₹1,61,126 crore in OY2024-25: 2.4 times as much for about 10% more oil. The rest is price and currency. Dollar prices of crude palm oil swung from $774 a tonne in October 2020 to $1,828 in March 2022 and back below $900 in 2023, and the rupee weakened from about ₹74 to the dollar (2020-21 average) to ₹93 (2025-26 average). SEA estimates the bill for the current oil year, which ends this month, at about ₹1.75 lakh crore, up 9%, "driven by higher volumes and rupee depreciation". On commerce-ministry TradeStat data for the fiscal year, imports under the three main tariff headings (palm, soybean and sunflower oil) cost ₹1,68,977 crore in FY26, up 17%, and were 2.5% of India's merchandise import bill.

How big is the market in rupees?

No primary source gives a retail market value, so this report builds one from the refiners' side. CRISIL estimated industry revenue at the refinery gate at about ₹2.6 lakh crore for FY26, based on its 82 rated refiners (about 40% of the industry), but that forecast assumed realisations would fall 4.5–5%. Prices instead rose: AWL's edible-oil realisation was up about 13% and the import value of the three main oils 17%. Adjusting for that gives an INDICATIVE refinery-gate value of ₹2.9–3.1 lakh crore for FY26, and perhaps ₹3.3–3.6 lakh crore at retail once GST, trade margins and loose oil are added (also INDICATIVE). This report uses "₹2.6–3.1 lakh crore at the refinery gate, CRISIL basis".

Against that, the three listed members sold roughly ₹1.10 lakh crore of edible oil in FY26: AWL's edible-oil segment ₹59,788 crore, Patanjali's ₹29,314 crore, and Gokul's oils (about 89% of ₹24,077 crore) roughly ₹21,400 crore. That is an INDICATIVE 36–42% of refinery-gate value, though Gokul's share includes bulk, export and biodiesel sales. By volume the members handle an INDICATIVE 7.7–8.1 million tonnes of edible oil, about 30% of national consumption: AWL 4.26 million tonnes (disclosed), Patanjali about 1.9–2.1 million tonnes and Gokul about 1.5–1.7 million tonnes (both estimates, because neither company discloses tonnes).

In plain terms

Indians are eating a little more oil every year, about 2–3% more, and the government would like that to stop. The money spent on it has grown far faster, because most of it is bought abroad in dollars and the rupee has weakened. For a refiner, that means revenue can jump 20% in a year when almost nothing about the business has changed. Always ask how many tonnes were sold before you credit a company for growth.

Growth over the next few years should look similar. SEA and USDA expect imports of 16.5–17 million tonnes in OY2026-27, with sunflower imports up by as much as 30% after the September duty cut. AWL guides edible-oil volume growth of 5–6% for the rest of FY27 and Patanjali 3–5%, both above the market rate and dependent on taking share from regional and loose sellers. The oilseeds mission's target of 25.45 million tonnes of domestic oil by 2030-31 implies a requirement of roughly 35 million tonnes (INDICATIVE); if the mission falls short, as its oil-palm leg already is (section 15), imports keep rising.

03

Who eats the oil: households, restaurants and the switch between oils

Total demand barely responds to price. The choice of oil responds a great deal, and that is where the refiners' volumes and margins move.

Edible-oil demand is the product of three things: population, oil per person, and the slow move from loose oil sold from a drum to packed and then branded oil. The first grows about 1% a year; the second has been rising but is approaching the level the government wants to hold; the third is the one the listed companies live on. Households take about 64% of consumption and restaurants, caterers, street food and food processors the other 36% (IMARC via Patanjali's annual report; the institutional share is an INDICATIVE residual).

Oils are substitutes, and buyers switch fast

In aggregate, Indians do not cut their oil use much when prices rise; they switch to a cheaper oil. Palm oil is normally the cheapest, so it fills the gap whenever soft oils are dear. In 2025 the usual order broke. Indonesia's biodiesel programme pulled palm oil into fuel, and palm traded above soybean oil for long stretches. Indian buyers responded within months: palm's share of edible-oil imports fell from 59% in OY2022-23 to 47% in OY2024-25, the lowest since 2019-20, and soybean oil imports jumped 59% to a record 5.47 million tonnes. In April 2026 sunflower oil imports doubled to 434,000 tonnes while palm fell to a 12-month low of 514,000 tonnes.

The seasons and the festivals

Demand peaks from September to November, when the festival season (Navratri, Durga Puja, Diwali) brings frying, sweets and gifting. Retailers and distributors fill their pipelines in August and September. The government explicitly timed the duty cut of 24 September 2026 to ease prices before the festivals; Business Today reported that vegetable-oil prices were up nearly 20% over the previous year. A weak monsoon also matters, because it hits the domestic soybean and groundnut crops: rainfall was 32% below normal in mid-June 2026 according to AWL's presentation, although kharif soybean area still reached 19.4 million hectares.

Standard pack sizes: the end of the 810-gram litre

A quieter change may matter more than any channel. In plain terms

People do not eat much less oil when it gets dearer; they buy a cheaper kind. That makes total volume steady and the mix volatile. The listed brands grow faster than the market because shoppers are slowly moving from loose oil to packs and from small packers to big brands, and the new standard pack sizes and online shopping both push in that direction.

For the companies, the demand side therefore offers steady but unspectacular growth. AWL grew edible-oil volume 6% in FY26 (helped by the duty gap discouraging refined imports), 2% in Q1 FY27 and 3% in Q2 FY27. Patanjali's food volume rose 5% in Q1 FY27 with prices up 12%. Gokul's three-year volume growth of about 26% a year (company chart) is a capacity and export story more than a domestic-demand one. 04

Jakarta, Buenos Aires and the rupee: the supply side

More than half of India's oil is decided abroad. In 2026 an Indonesian fuel mandate, a war in West Asia and a record-weak rupee set Indian refiners' costs.

Because India imports about 56% of its edible oil and the domestic price of local oils follows the import price, the cost base of every member is set by a handful of external variables: palm-oil output and policy in Indonesia and Malaysia, the soybean crush in Argentina and Brazil, the sunflower crop around the Black Sea, the price of crude petroleum, freight rates and the rupee. India buys about a fifth of world trade in palm, soybean and sunflower oil, so it is a large customer, but it is a price-taker.

Who India buys from

The origin table has shifted sharply. In the eleven months to September 2026, Argentina supplied 3.44 million tonnes (2.71 million of soybean oil and 0.73 million of sunflower oil), more than either Malaysia (2.98 million tonnes) or Indonesia (2.91 million tonnes). Russia supplied 1.29 million tonnes, almost all sunflower oil; Brazil 1.26 million tonnes; Thailand 0.77 million tonnes; Nepal 0.68 million tonnes of duty-free refined oil; Ukraine 0.36 million tonnes. Argentina is now India's single largest supplier, a result of palm losing its price discount.

Large Malaysian stocks are a bearish signal for palm prices in the second half of FY27, which is good for Indian demand but bad for any Indian refiner holding expensive stock.

Freight, war and the West Asia shock

The war in West Asia that began on 28 February 2026 closed the Strait of Hormuz for weeks, pushed Brent crude to about $118 a barrel at the peak and, until the 7 April ceasefire, disrupted shipping across the region. AWL says sea freight doubled at the peak and was still about 50% above pre-war levels by mid-year. Expensive diesel also raised the pull of vegetable oil into biodiesel worldwide, which supported palm and soybean prices. Patanjali noted that soybean-oil futures rose about 40% by the end of March 2026 before stabilising. For India, the war delivered higher oil prices, higher freight and a weaker rupee at the same time: the conditions in which long-inventory holders make money and branded packers struggle to pass on costs quickly.

The domestic seed floor

Domestic oilseeds are priced off the government's minimum support price, which rose 3.5–8.1% for the 2026-27 kharif crops and 6.7% for mustard (₹6,613 a quintal for 2027-28) in the same weeks that the crude-oil duty was cut. Section 6 explains why that squeezes inland crushers.

In plain terms

An Indian refiner's raw material is priced in Kuala Lumpur, Jakarta and Buenos Aires and paid for in dollars. In 2026 an Indonesian fuel law, a war that raised shipping costs, and a rupee at record lows all made that raw material dearer. Refiners that already had oil in their tanks gained; anyone who had to pass the higher price to shoppers immediately did not. When the rupee or palm prices turn, the gains reverse just as quickly.

The supply side also explains why the members describe their hedging so differently. AWL says "the brand itself is our biggest hedge": it does not take speculative positions and hedges by selling forward, holding 30–35 days of raw-oil stock because a voyage takes 30–40 days. Patanjali says openly that it "carries long positions in edible oils, so the resulting commodity price inflation has actually been a net positive", using a "physical-oriented hedging strategy rather than reliance on futures alone". Gokul describes "disciplined hedging" with agro-commodity futures "where appropriate" and credits a "favourable sourcing mix" for its Q1 FY27 margin. In a rising market, all three statements produced gains. In a falling one they will produce very different losses.

05

Nineteen points of protection: the crude–refined duty gap Deep dive

The level of import duty sets the price Indians pay. The gap between the duty on crude oil and on refined oil decides whether that oil is refined in Kandla or in Kuala Lumpur.

If you remember one policy number about this industry, make it the differential: the extra customs duty paid on refined edible oil over crude edible oil. Since 31 May 2025 it has been 19.25 percentage points for palm, soybean and sunflower oil. When the government cut duties again on 24 September 2026 (Notification No. 31/2026-Customs, dated 23 September 2026) it lowered the crude and refined rates by the same five points, and for sunflower oil it cut both by ten, so the gap survived intact. That gap is the reason India's port refineries are busy, the reason refined palmolein has almost stopped arriving from Malaysia and Indonesia, and the reason the most important "import" story left is a pipeline of duty-free refined soybean oil coming in from Nepal.

How the duty is built

News reports quote the basic customs duty (BCD), but the importer pays more. Crude edible oils also carry the Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess (AIDC), currently 5%, introduced in the 2021 Budget, and every import pays the Social Welfare Surcharge (SWS) at 10% of the BCD plus AIDC. Crude palm oil since 24 September 2026 therefore pays 5% BCD + 5% AIDC + 1% SWS = 11% effective. Refined palmolein pays 27.5% BCD + 2.75% SWS = 30.25%. Crude sunflower oil, now at nil BCD, still pays 5.5% (the cess plus its surcharge), against 24.75% on refined sunflower oil. The SEA's published table confirms all four rates. Rapeseed and cottonseed oil were left at 38.5% (crude) and 49.5% (refined), which keeps imported mustard-type oils out entirely.

The protection is four to six times the refiner's whole profit per tonne. Domestic refiners do not need to be more efficient than Malaysian ones; the tariff does the work. If the refined duty were cut to the crude rate, India's idle refining capacity would be exposed overnight.

The arithmetic makes two points. First, the domestic refiner keeps only a small part of the ₹19,000 a tonne; competition among Indian refiners passes most of it to consumers as lower prices than an import would bring. The differential is a shield, not a subsidy that shows up as margin. Second, the shield is far larger than any efficiency gap. A refiner that is 20% less efficient than a Malaysian competitor loses perhaps ₹600–800 a tonne; the tariff gives it ₹19,000. Which is why the industry lobbies hard on the differential, and why, in May and June 2026, it was asking for higher duties rather than lower ones.

In plain terms

The government charges about 11% duty on crude oil and about 30% on oil that has already been refined abroad. That difference is worth roughly ₹19,000 a tonne, several times what an Indian refinery earns on each tonne. As long as it stays, Indian plants do the refining. Watch the gap, not the headline rate: a cut that lowers both rates equally changes prices but leaves the business model alone.

Why the government cut both rates in September 2026

By September 2026 edible-oil prices were up about 20% on a year earlier, retail CPI inflation was 5.5% and wholesale inflation 11.4% (ICRA's summary of the September prints), the RBI had raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October, and the festival season was weeks away. The industry had asked for higher duties in May and June. The government went the other way on the level but protected the structure: five points off both crude and refined palm and soybean oil, and ten points off both crude and refined sunflower oil, where the crude rate fell to nil. Sunflower got the deeper cut because it was the dearest oil ($1,463 a tonne in Q2 FY27) and because southern and western states eat most of it. SEA expects sunflower imports to rise by as much as 30%, to about 3.5 million tonnes, in OY2026-27.

The cut has a cost for the refiners in the short run. Every tonne of duty-paid oil in their tanks and pipelines on 24 September is now worth about 5.5% less in replacement cost (11% for sunflower). On ₹1 lakh a tonne that is about ₹5,500, more than a quarter's EBITDA per tonne. Some of the stock is protected: oil held in bonded port tanks pays duty only when it is cleared, at the rate then in force, so it benefits from the cut. SEA counted 1.24 million tonnes in port tanks and 0.84 million tonnes in the pipeline on 1 October 2026. The duty-paid pipeline and factory stock carries the markdown. Policy beyond the tariff: missions, support prices, packs and stock limits

The government wants three things at once: cheaper oil for consumers, higher prices for oilseed farmers and less dependence on imports. The members live in the space between them.

Customs duty is the loudest lever but not the only one. Delhi also sets minimum support prices for oilseeds, funds two national missions to grow more oil at home, regulates pack sizes and labels, can impose stock limits under the Essential Commodities Act, and taxes the product under GST. Each lever has a different effect on the listed companies, and in 2026 several pulled in opposite directions.

The two missions: grow more oil at home

The National Mission on Edible Oils – Oil Palm (NMEO-OP), approved in August 2021 with an outlay of ₹11,040 crore (centre ₹8,844 crore, states ₹2,196 crore), aims to add 6.5 lakh hectares of oil palm by 2025-26 and raise domestic crude palm oil to 11.2 lakh tonnes by 2025-26 and 28 lakh tonnes by 2029-30. The National Mission on Edible Oils – Oilseeds, approved in October 2024 with ₹10,103 crore over 2024-25 to 2030-31, targets primary oilseed output of 69.7 million tonnes by 2030-31 (from 39 million in 2022-23) and domestic edible-oil production of 25.45 million tonnes, about 72% of projected need. Minimum support prices: a rising floor for seed

Support prices set the floor for domestic oilseeds and so the cost of domestic crushing. They rose sharply in 2026. Higher MSPs encourage farmers to plant oilseeds, which serves the missions, but they raise crushers' input costs at the same moment that a lower crude-oil duty makes imported oil cheaper. Stock limits: the lever nobody wants pulled

In October 2021, with prices spiking, the government imposed stock limits on edible oils and oilseeds under the Essential Commodities Act, capping how much a refiner, wholesaler or retailer could hold. They were lifted in 2022-23. Stock limits are the most damaging policy for an integrated refiner, because they force it to run down the inventory that is both its raw material and its hedge. Patanjali's chief executive raised the risk unprompted on the Q1 FY27 call, warning of possible "commodities control order and essential commodities" action if food inflation persists. With wholesale inflation at 11.4% in September 2026, the risk is not academic.

Packs, labels and food safety

The Legal Metrology change of June 2026 (nine standard pack sizes, weight shown on volume packs, effective from about September) is covered in section 3. Two older rules also shape the market. FSSAI requires vitamin A and D fortification of edible oil supplied through many state and central schemes, limits trans fats to 2%, and allows blended oils only as two-oil blends with no oil above 80%. And some states have, at times, banned the sale of loose edible oil (a 2021 order on loose mustard oil is the best-known). Each of these raises the cost of doing business for unbranded sellers more than for the national brands.

GST and the consumer-affairs ministry

Edible oil carries 5% GST, unchanged by the September 2025 rationalisation (GST 2.0). That rationalisation did help the adjacencies: Patanjali says about 55% of its FMCG portfolio benefited from the GST cut. The consumer-affairs ministry also leans on the industry directly: after the September 2026 duty cut it asked the edible-oil associations to pass the full benefit to retail prices. A company that complies quickly cuts its selling price while still holding stock bought at the old duty, which is how a duty cut becomes an inventory loss.

In plain terms

The government is pulling several levers at once. It pays farmers more for oilseeds, it cuts the tax on imported crude oil to keep shop prices down, it funds a long plan to grow palm oil at home, and it keeps the option of limiting how much stock companies can hold. For a refiner, the useful ones are the duty gap and the pack-size rule; the dangerous ones are stock limits and pressure to cut prices on oil already in the tank.

The overall direction is clear even when the signals conflict. Policy favours domestic processing (the duty gap), domestic farming (MSPs and missions) and lower consumer prices (the duty level), and it is increasingly hostile to unorganised sellers (packs, labels, fortification). That combination is good for large, compliant refiners with brands, and for anyone with a stake in domestic oil palm. It is less good for inland crushers caught between support prices and cheap imports, and for any refiner whose profit depends on prices rising while it holds stock.

Who refines India's oil: concentration, capacity and the long tail

Two thousand brands, five big organised players with about half the market, and three of them listed. The industry has far more refining capacity than it needs.

No primary source publishes national market shares for edible oil, so the structure has to be pieced together from company claims, rating-agency reports and arithmetic. AWL's FY26 annual report gives the best handle: it describes "branded penetration in edible oils" above 75%, more than 2,000 brands, the top five organised players together holding only about 50% of the market, and about 50% held by regional brands. Those are company statements without a cited source. An older CRISIL rationale (2022) said about 60% of the industry was "serviced by the unorganised sector". The truth for 2026 is probably that most oil is now sold in some kind of pack, a large part of it under small regional brands, and that the national leaders hold well under half the volume.

Capacity: far more than the country needs

AWL says its 6.0 million-plus tonnes of annual refining capacity is about 25% of India's total, which implies national refining capacity of roughly 24 million tonnes a year (an INDICATIVE derivation). India consumes about 26 million tonnes, of which about 15 million tonnes is imported crude that needs refining at a port plant; the rest is domestic oil, much of it crushed and refined inland or sold as filtered kachi ghani oil. Gokul, at about 83%, is the outlier.

Overcapacity is the hidden reason the duty gap matters so much. A refinery's fixed costs (plant, people, power contracts) do not change much with volume, so each extra tonne processed in an underused plant earns close to the full spread. If refined imports came back, they would push utilisation even lower and spread the same fixed costs over fewer tonnes. It is also why capacity additions in this industry rarely make sense on their own: AWL is investing to bring tolled food production in-house and to add specialty chemicals, not to add generic refining, and Gokul is expanding because it runs full.

In plain terms

One brand, Fortune, is clearly the biggest, with about 18% of branded sales. After that the market is crowded with multinationals, co-ops and hundreds of regional names. India has many more refineries than it needs, so most plants run half to three-quarters full. That keeps competition sharp and margins thin, and it makes the tariff that protects domestic refining far more valuable than any single company's efficiency.

Concentration is likely to rise slowly. Standard pack sizes, quick commerce, fortification rules and the cost of hedging favour scale. But the long tail survives because edible oil is a regional product (mustard in the north and east, groundnut in Gujarat, coconut in Kerala, sunflower in the south), because small crushers sit next to the seed, and because loose and locally packed oil still wins on price in rural markets. AWL's own description, that regional brands hold about half the market, suggests the national brands are taking share by about a point a year, not by leaps.

Inventory is the P&L: forty years of booms, busts and duty shocks

Edible-oil profits peak when prices are rising, not when they are high. The industry's history is a sequence of companies that confused an inventory gain with a business.

A refiner holds six to eleven weeks of oil: cargo at sea, crude in port tanks, oil in process and packed stock with distributors. CRISIL puts the industry's typical crude inventory at 40–50 days and the lag before price changes reach the shelf at 15–25 days. When the price of oil rises during that window, the company sells at the new price stock it bought at the old one; when it falls, the reverse. Hedging with futures and forward sales reduces the swing but rarely removes it, because India has no deep domestic futures market for palm or soybean oil and because branded sellers cannot change shelf prices every week. The result is a sector whose earnings follow the direction of prices.

Sizing the next swing

The inventory effect can be estimated roughly. AWL held about 45 days of inventory in FY26 (screener; CareEdge says 43), which on ₹74,731 crore of revenue is about ₹9,200 crore of stock (INDICATIVE). A 5% move in oil prices on that stock is about ₹460 crore, roughly 22% of FY26 operating profit, before hedging. The September 2026 duty cut works the same way on duty-paid stock. If AWL had about a month of unhedged, duty-paid crude (₹5,000 crore or so), a 5.5-point cut would mark it down by ₹250–275 crore (INDICATIVE). That is an upper bound: bonded stock in port tanks benefits from the cut, forward sales offset part of it, and management may phase price cuts. Patanjali, which says it runs long positions, is the most exposed in a falling market; Gokul, with 37 days of inventory and supplier-funded stock, the least in proportion.

In plain terms

Refiners make most of their surprise profits and losses on the oil they are holding, not the oil they sell next month. When prices go up after they buy, they win; when prices or duties fall after they buy, they lose. Q1 FY27 was a winning quarter for all three. The duty cut of 24 September and full Malaysian tanks mean the next two quarters could show the opposite, even if the companies sell more oil than ever.

The lesson of forty years is that the industry's disasters came from treating the cycle as a trend: Ruchi borrowed to expand at the top of the 2008–2012 boom, KS Oils collapsed in 2012, and AWL's shares have never regained their April 2022 high of about ₹878 (₹179 on 22 October 2026). Read any edible-oil quarter by asking first whether prices rose or fell during it, and only then whether the company did well.

Reading an oil refiner: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Revenue tells you almost nothing in this industry. Tonnes, profit per tonne, days of inventory and the basis of every debt number tell you nearly everything.

An edible-oil company's revenue is mostly the pass-through price of a world commodity converted at a weak currency. A 20% rise in revenue can mean the company sold no more oil at all. To judge one of these businesses you need a different set of numbers, most of them per tonne or per day, and you need to know exactly how each company defines them, because the three members define several of them differently. This section starts with the profit equation, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, what good looks like, how the members compare, and the trap in its definition. It ends with an anatomy of one typical refinery, a scorecard, the traps collected in one place, and a ten-minute routine for reading a quarterly result.

The profit equation

For the edible-oil engine, EBITDA is volume multiplied by a per-tonne spread, plus or minus the result on inventory and hedges. The spread is the realised price per tonne less the landed cost of the crude oil, less the cost of refining and packing, less the cost of selling. Landed cost is itself the dollar price, times the exchange rate, times one plus the effective duty, plus port and storage costs. For a group such as AWL or Patanjali, the oil engine is one line among several: group EBITDA is edible-oil tonnes times EBITDA per tonne, plus food or FMCG revenue times its margin, plus industrial or plantation tonnes times their per-tonne profit, less unallocated costs.

In plain terms

KPI 1 · Volume (tonnes sold)

What it is. The physical quantity sold in the period, usually in million metric tonnes (MMT). It is the only number that strips out price. Formula: sum of tonnes sold across oils and packs; for a segment, segment tonnes. Worked example: AWL sold 6.85 MMT in FY26, of which edible oil 4.26 MMT (+6%), Industry Essentials 1.35 MMT and Food & FMCG 1.24 MMT; revenue of ₹74,731 crore therefore averaged about ₹1.09 lakh a tonne across the company and about ₹1.40 lakh a tonne in edible oil.

What drives it. Market growth (2–3% a year), share gains from loose and regional oil, the duty gap (which moves refining onshore), capacity and distribution reach, and channel stocking. In a volatile quarter the trade buys hand-to-mouth: AWL blamed Q1 FY27's 2% growth on the "primary pipeline drying up" as distributors waited for prices to settle. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): edible-oil volume growth above 6% is strong, 2–6% average, below 2% weak. How the members compare: only AWL discloses tonnes. Patanjali and Gokul do not, so their volumes are estimates: Patanjali about 1.9–2.1 million tonnes of edible oil (its FY26 edible-oil sales of ₹27,323 crore divided by AWL's realisation of about ₹1.40 lakh a tonne) and Gokul about 1.5–1.7 million tonnes of edible oil within 1.7–1.9 million tonnes of total throughput (83% utilisation of a 2.0 million-tonne refinery, plus crushing and biodiesel).

KPI 2 · EBITDA per tonne

What it is. Operating profit earned on each tonne sold, the single best measure of the refining-and-selling spread. Formula: segment EBITDA ÷ segment tonnes (for edible oil), or company EBITDA ÷ total tonnes (company-wide). Worked example: AWL's edible-oil segment earned ₹492 crore of EBITDA on 0.98 million tonnes in Q1 FY27: ₹492 crore ÷ 9.8 lakh tonnes = ₹5,020, reported as ₹5,030 a tonne. For FY26, ₹1,884 crore ÷ 4.26 million tonnes ≈ ₹4,420 (INDICATIVE, calculated from disclosed figures).

What drives it. The inventory result, the duty gap, mix (mustard and rice bran earn more than palmolein), branded versus bulk, plant utilisation and advertising. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): for edible oil, above ₹4,500 is good, ₹3,000–4,500 average, below ₹2,500 weak. AWL guides ₹4,000–4,500 for FY27; CareEdge expects its company-wide figure to settle at ₹3,500–3,700.

KPI 3 · Realisation per tonne

What it is. The average net price received per tonne. Formula: segment revenue ÷ segment tonnes. Worked example: AWL's edible-oil revenue was ₹59,788 crore in FY26 on 4.26 million tonnes, about ₹1,40,350 a tonne, against ₹49,736 crore on 4.0 million tonnes in FY25 (about ₹1,24,300): a 13% rise in price on 6% more tonnes.

What drives it. World oil prices, the rupee and duty (pass-through of landed cost), the mix of oils (sunflower and mustard sell above palm), the pack mix (small consumer packs above bulk tankers) and pricing power. Benchmark: there is no "good" level; the useful test is whether realisation rose more or less than landed cost. How the members compare: only AWL's can be calculated; Gokul's average across bulk, export and biodiesel is lower because bulk palmolein and biodiesel sell below branded soft oils.

KPI 4 · Landed cost and the duty

What it is. The rupee cost of one tonne of crude oil at the refinery gate. Formula: CIF price in dollars × rupees per dollar × (1 + effective duty) + port, storage and handling. Worked example: crude palm oil at $1,050 × ₹94.9 = ₹99,645; at the 11% duty in force since 24 September 2026, add ₹10,961; with ₹1,500 of port costs, landed cost is about ₹1,12,100 a tonne (ILLUSTRATIVE). At the earlier 16.5% duty it was about ₹1,17,600.

What drives it. Three multipliers, each outside management's control: the dollar price (palm, soybean and sunflower markets), the exchange rate and the duty. Sensitivities on the example (ILLUSTRATIVE): each $10 a tonne adds about ₹1,050; each rupee of depreciation adds about ₹1,165; each point of duty adds about ₹1,000. Benchmark: what matters is landed cost relative to the selling price and relative to the cost when the stock was bought. How the members compare: all three face the same landed cost for the same oil; differences come from purchase timing, origin and freight. Gokul credits a "favourable sourcing mix" through its Singapore desk; AWL buys about a third of its imports from Wilmar Trading.

KPI 5 · Import parity spread

What it is. The gap between what it would cost to import the finished product and what it costs to make it at home. It measures how much pricing room the duty structure gives the domestic refiner. Formula: refined import price × FX × (1 + refined duty) − [crude price × FX × (1 + crude duty) + conversion cost]. Worked example: ₹1,33,500 for imported refined palmolein against about ₹1,14,000 for domestic refining gives a spread of about ₹19,000 a tonne (ILLUSTRATIVE; section 5).

What drives it. The crude–refined duty differential (19.25 points), the price gap between crude and refined oil abroad (Indonesia and Malaysia tax crude exports more heavily to favour their own refiners, which narrows it), and duty-free routes such as Nepal. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): a spread well above the refiner's EBITDA per tonne means imports are shut out; a spread below about ₹5,000 (the 8.25-point era) lets refined imports take 10–13% of the market.

KPI 6 · Gross profit per tonne

What it is. Revenue less the cost of materials, per tonne: the spread before conversion, selling and overheads. Formula: (revenue − raw materials consumed ± change in inventory) ÷ tonnes. Worked example: AWL reported gross profit of ₹11,505 a tonne in FY26 (CareEdge), ₹11,038 in Q1 FY26, ₹12,415 in Q4 FY26 and ₹12,496 in Q1 FY27. With company-wide EBITDA of ₹3,425 a tonne in FY26, about ₹8,080 a tonne went on conversion, packing, freight, advertising and overheads. Gokul's FY26 gross profit, (₹24,077 crore − ₹22,890 crore) on roughly 1.8 million tonnes, is about ₹6,300–6,600 a tonne (INDICATIVE).

How the members compare. Only AWL publishes gross profit per tonne, so the comparison has to be done on margins. Gokul's raw-material bill was 95.1% of FY26 revenue, leaving a gross margin of about 4.9%; AWL's ₹11,505 a tonne on a company-wide realisation of about ₹1.09 lakh is roughly 10.6%; Patanjali's gross margin was 15.3% in its Q2 FY26 presentation, lifted by biscuits, foods and personal care. The ordering is the reverse of the ordering by return on capital. A business that spends more below the gross line, on brands, distribution and packaging, needs a higher gross margin just to stand still, and it carries more fixed cost that does not fall when volume does. That is the price of the stability a brand buys. It is also why branded companies feel a falling market twice: once in the inventory markdown, and again in the advertising and trade spending they cannot cut quickly without losing shelf space to regional packers.

What drives it. The same factors as EBITDA per tonne, plus mix: food staples and branded small packs carry more gross profit and more cost. Benchmark: watch the trend and the ratio of EBITDA to gross profit (AWL about 30%; Gokul over half, because it spends little below the gross line). The definition trap. Some companies include packing material in raw materials and some in other expenses, and AWL's "normalised" gross profit includes commodity-derivative results that its statutory P&L puts in other income. Compare one company over time, not across companies.

KPI 7 · Branded share

What it is. The share of a company's edible-oil sales made under its own consumer brands rather than in bulk or as private label. Formula: branded sales ÷ total edible-oil segment sales, by value. KPI 8 · Inventory days and working-capital days

What it is. Inventory days measure how long the company holds stock; working-capital days add receivables and subtract payables, measuring how many days of sales the company finances itself. Formula: inventory ÷ revenue × 365 (screener uses revenue; some analysts use cost of goods); working-capital days = debtor days + inventory days − payable days. Worked example: Patanjali FY26: debtors 22 days + inventory 79 − payables 25 = 76-day cash cycle; screener's working-capital days (which include other current items) are 51. On ₹40,170 crore of revenue, each day is about ₹110 crore of cash.

What drives it. Voyage times (30–40 days), the size of long positions, FMCG and plantation stock, supplier credit and bank acceptances. Benchmark (INDICATIVE): inventory below 40 days good, 40–60 average, above 70 weak; working-capital days below 10 good, above 50 weak.

Definition traps, collected

  • Oil year versus fiscal year. SEA's import data run November–October; company results run April–March; TradeStat runs April–March. Never put OY tonnes next to FY volumes without saying so.
  • Edible versus vegetable oil. SEA's headline "vegetable oil" includes about 0.3 million tonnes of non-edible oil a year (15.46 versus 15.16 million tonnes in Nov-25 to Sep-26).
  • BCD versus effective duty. A "5% duty" on crude palm oil is 11% effective; "nil" on crude sunflower is 5.5%.
  • Screener operating profit versus company EBITDA. AWL normalises commodity-derivative results into EBITDA (FY26: ₹2,343 crore versus screener ₹2,131 crore; Q1 FY26: ₹519 crore versus ₹366 crore). Patanjali's company EBITDA (~₹1,932 crore) differs from screener's ₹1,762 crore. Gokul's annual report shows ₹716 crore including other income against screener's ₹676 crore.
  • Reported versus normalised profit. Patanjali's FY26 PAT of ₹1,815 crore includes a ₹788 crore credit for earlier years' tax; at a normal 25% rate it would be about ₹1,016 crore (range ₹1,013–1,162 crore depending on exceptional items).
  • Per-tonne definitions. Segment EBITDA per tonne, company EBITDA per tonne and rating-agency PBILDT per tonne are three different numbers.
  • Market share bases. Fortune's 18.1% is of the branded market; AWL's ~29% is on e-commerce and quick-commerce platforms; Nutrela's ~40% (a company claim) is of branded soya chunks; Patanjali's ~75% is a share of its own sales.
  • Net debt. Screener borrowings exclude acceptances and buyer's credit; rating agencies and company frameworks include them.
  • Per-share history at Patanjali. The 2:1 bonus (record date 11 September 2025) tripled the share count to 108.75 crore; divide any earlier per-share figure, including the ₹650 follow-on offer price, by three.

In plain terms

Most bad comparisons in this sector come from mixing bases: oil years with financial years, headline duty with effective duty, one company's EBITDA definition with another's, or a share of the branded market with a share of all sales. How to read an edible-oil company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check what prices did in the quarter (1 minute). Pull SEA's landed prices for crude palm, soybean and sunflower oil and the average rupee rate for the quarter, and compare with the quarter before. Rising rupee costs mean expect inventory gains; falling costs or a duty cut mean expect losses. This tells you what "normal" should look like before you open the results.
  2. Volume first (1 minute). Edible-oil tonnes and growth (AWL discloses them; for Patanjali and Gokul look for management comments). Volume growth above 5% is genuine share gain; below 2% usually means channel destocking.
  3. EBITDA per tonne or segment margin (2 minutes). AWL: edible-oil EBITDA per tonne against the ₹4,000–4,500 guidance. Patanjali: edible-oil margin against the 2–4% "construct" and FMCG margin separately. Gokul: overall margin against its 2.2–3.9% band. Anything far above the band in a rising-price quarter is mostly inventory gain.
  4. Reconcile the basis (1 minute). For AWL, compare reported and "normalised" EBITDA and check the other-income line for derivative gains. For Patanjali, check the tax rate (three quarters of FY26 had negative tax). For Gokul, compare EBITDA with and without other income.
  5. The adjacencies (1 minute). AWL Food & FMCG revenue and margin (guidance 3–4% margin, high-teens growth); Patanjali FMCG revenue and EBITDA (FY27 target ~₹12,500 crore revenue), biscuits and personal-care margins; Gokul's export share and biodiesel.
  6. Working capital and debt (2 minutes). Inventory, receivables and payables at half-year (only in H1 and annual results), operating cash flow, borrowings and, where available, the rating agency's net debt. Red flag: inventory days rising while prices fall, or borrowings rising faster than revenue.
  7. Policy and pipeline commentary (1 minute). Management's view on the duty, the Nepal flow, stock limits, B50 and the rupee, and how much stock was bought before the 24 September duty cut.
  8. Red flags to note (1 minute). Margins far above peers without a price explanation; large "other income" swings; related-party purchases rising; a negative tax rate; capital advances to promoter entities; volume not disclosed when it used to be.
In plain terms

Start with the oil price and the rupee, not the company. Then ask: how many tonnes, how much per tonne, how much of that is the stock moving in price, and who paid for the stock. If you can answer those four questions you have understood the quarter.

The hidden balance sheet: letters of credit, acceptances and three versions of net debt

Every tanker of crude oil is paid for with bank paper. Most of that paper never shows up as "borrowings", which makes edible-oil balance sheets look lighter than they are.

An Indian refiner rarely pays cash for its oil when it is loaded. It opens a letter of credit (LC) with its bank, often with 90 to 180 days of credit (a "usance" LC), or arranges buyer's credit from a foreign bank, or accepts a bill drawn by the supplier that its bank guarantees. The oil arrives, is refined and sold, and the bill is settled weeks later from the sale proceeds. Accounting standards let much of this sit under trade payables or "other financial liabilities" (acceptances) rather than borrowings. The interest is real, the bank exposure is real, and in a falling market the obligation to pay a fixed dollar amount for oil that is now worth less is very real. But a quick look at screener's borrowings line will miss most of it.

AWL: ₹1,109 crore or ₹4,425 crore?

AWL is the clearest case. Screener shows borrowings of ₹1,109 crore and investments of ₹1,082 crore at March 2026, so net debt of almost nothing. AWL's own annual report lists financial capital of "equity ₹10,441 crore; debt ₹7,831 crore". CareEdge, which rates AWL AA-/Stable and has sanctioned bank lines of ₹18,111 crore in view, puts net debt at ₹4,425 crore (₹5,106 crore a year earlier) after unencumbered cash of ₹2,333 crore, and total debt at 2.88 times PBILDT. The difference, about ₹6,700 crore of acceptance-type funding, sits in screener's "other liabilities" of ₹13,186 crore. A tell-tale sign: AWL's FY26 interest expense was ₹707 crore, 64% of its year-end screener borrowings. No company pays 64% interest; it pays a normal rate on a much larger balance than the one screener calls debt.

Who is winning: returns, adjacencies and who funds the stock

On returns and growth, Gokul. On scale and share, AWL. On what the market will pay for, Patanjali, though the gap is narrowing as investors weigh its related-party perimeter.

"Winning" in edible oil means different things depending on the yardstick. Three maps capture the main ones: how margin turns into return on capital, how much of the business sits outside commodity oil, and who carries the cost of the inventory. A scorecard then puts the qualitative judgements in one place. The stock market's own verdict over the past year is stark: Gokul's shares rose 19%, AWL's fell 32% and Patanjali's fell 40% (screener, 22 October 2026).

AWL vs Patanjali: two FMCG stories bolted to one commodity Deep dive

Both companies are trying to turn an edible-oil franchise into a food and consumer-goods business. They are doing it from opposite ends, with very different parents, and the market is pricing the difference.

AWL and Patanjali are the two members that tell investors they are more than oil. AWL's pitch is that the reach of Fortune (135 million households, 9.7 lakh outlets served directly, 63,000 rural towns) can carry flour, rice, pulses, besan, sugar and ready-to-cook foods at scale. Patanjali's is that its refining business is now the cash engine for an FMCG house that sells biscuits, ghee, soya chunks, toothpaste and soap. On 22 October 2026 the market valued Patanjali at ₹38,845 crore and AWL at ₹23,220 crore, even though AWL's revenue is 1.9 times Patanjali's. This section asks whether that gap is earned.

AWL's food business: growing fast, earning little

AWL's Food & FMCG segment sold 1.24 million tonnes for ₹6,473 crore in FY26, after volume fell 5% as it exited low-margin government rice contracts. It earned segment EBITDA of ₹315 crore, about 4.9% (INDICATIVE, calculated), on ₹3,517 crore of capital, for a 6% return on capital. Growth has since accelerated: Q1 FY27 revenue rose 22% to ₹1,726 crore at a 6% margin, and the Q2 update says revenue rose 33% to more than ₹2,000 crore for the first time, with "other foods" (besan, pulses, soya nuggets, sugar, sattu, poha) above 35% of the segment and growing about 50%, and Fortune pulses above ₹100 crore a quarter. Two acquisitions add to it: GD Foods (Tops sauces, pickles and noodles, revenue up 23% in Q1 and over 30% in Q2, with a 52% material margin), and the Madhur sugar brand licensed from Shree Renuka Sugars for a 0.5% royalty, selling about 15,000 tonnes a month for ₹700–800 crore a year.

The margin is the problem. India's palm plantation bet: NMEO-OP and Patanjali's 1.16 lakh hectares Deep dive

The government wants India to grow its own palm oil. One listed company holds a fifth of the national planted area and a large share of the output, and calls it an annuity.

Palm oil is about half of India's edible-oil imports, and it is the one oil the country could, in principle, grow at scale: the oil palm yields four to five tonnes of oil a hectare at maturity, several times any annual oilseed. The National Mission on Edible Oils – Oil Palm, launched in August 2021, set out to plant 6.5 lakh hectares of new oil palm by 2025-26 and raise domestic crude palm oil (CPO) to 11.2 lakh tonnes by 2025-26 and 28 lakh tonnes by 2029-30. By November 2025, 2.5 lakh hectares had been added and national coverage stood at 6.20 lakh hectares, but CPO output was only 3.80 lakh tonnes in 2024-25, about a third of the 2025-26 target (PIB). Andhra Pradesh and Telangana produce 98% of it; new planting is moving into the North-East.

How the model works

Oil palm in India is grown by farmers on their own land, not on company estates. Each state allots a "factory zone" to a processor, which must supply seedlings, advise farmers, set up collection centres and buy all the fresh fruit bunches (FFB) produced in its zone. The price the processor pays is set by a formula linked to international CPO prices and the oil extraction rate (the share of the fruit's weight that becomes oil); under the mission, a "viability price" protects farmers if world prices fall. The government raised planting support from ₹12,000 to ₹29,000 a hectare. A palm takes about four years to bear fruit and about seven to reach prime yield, which then lasts into its twenties. For the processor the business is a mill: buy fruit at a formula price, extract oil and kernel, and sell the CPO to its own or other refineries.

Patanjali: the largest private planter

Patanjali Foods inherited Ruchi Soya's oil-palm zones and has expanded them aggressively. Its planted area rose from 1,00,997 hectares in September 2025 to 1,10,722 hectares at March 2026 (21,176 hectares added in FY26) and 1,15,861 hectares at June 2026, with 80,579 farmers, 48 nurseries holding about 15 million sprouts, 238 collection centres and six mills able to process 1.15 million tonnes of fruit a year. States have allocated it 6.63 lakh hectares of zones in 12 states, more than the whole country's current planted area. Its target is 5 lakh hectares planted by 2032. (One of our own primers carried an outdated ~71,000 hectares; the company's figures above supersede it.)

The national gap, and the other members

Even if everything goes to plan, domestic palm will not replace imports. In plain terms

The government is paying farmers to plant oil palm so India buys less from Indonesia and Malaysia. Patanjali is the biggest company in that programme, with about a fifth of the planted area. Its plantation business will grow every year as young trees mature, but its profits still rise and fall with world palm prices, and the national programme is well behind its targets.

The risks are agronomic, environmental and political. Oil palm is water-hungry, which matters in Telangana and Andhra Pradesh; North-Eastern expansion raises biodiversity concerns; the viability price depends on continued government funding; and factory-zone allocations can be revisited by states. For investors, the plantation is a long-dated asset that the current share price barely separates from the oil business. It will be worth more when Patanjali discloses its profit.

The global lens: India's refiners next to the world's oil traders

The world's edible-oil business is run by a handful of giant traders and planters. India's listed refiners are small next to them, earn more per tonne at home, and trade at higher multiples.

Globally, edible oil sits inside the agribusiness giants: Cargill, ADM, Bunge and Louis Dreyfus in the Americas, and Wilmar, Golden Agri-Resources and the Malaysian planters (IOI, SD Guthrie, KLK) in Asia. They own plantations or crushing plants at origin, ships, trading desks and refineries in consuming countries, and earn across the chain. Wilmar, AWL's parent, reported 2025 revenue of US$70.4 billion, EBITDA of US$4.27 billion (6.1%) and core net profit of US$1.28 billion. Its Food Products division, which includes consumer-pack and bulk edible oil, flour, rice and sugar, sold 34.7 million tonnes for US$30.9 billion and earned profit before tax of about US$450 million, roughly US$13 a tonne.

Two comparisons stand out. First, margin per tonne: AWL's edible-oil EBITDA of about ₹4,420 a tonne in FY26 is about US$47, far richer than Wilmar's US$13 a tonne of pre-tax profit for its global food business (a different measure, after depreciation and interest, and a different mix, so the comparison is indicative only). Second, valuation: Wilmar trades at roughly 10–12 times earnings and below book value, ADM at 12–15 times, Bunge at 9–11 times and the Malaysian planters at 15–20 times (approximate). The Indian members trade at 14–20 times reported earnings (Patanjali about 33 times normalised) and 2.2–4.2 times book. Indian investors pay a premium for consumer-brand optionality at AWL and Patanjali and for growth at Gokul, not for margin.

India's weight in world trade is large. It buys about a fifth of all internationally traded palm, soybean and sunflower oil, and Indonesian, Malaysian and Argentine policy often reacts to Indian demand. But Indian refiners do not own the origin: none of the members has a foreign plantation of scale (Gokul's Indonesian associate is small), and only AWL, through Wilmar, has privileged access to an origin trader. Upstream integration is where the global industry's best returns are made in good palm years (upstream margins of 20–40%, approximate), and it is the part of the chain Indian listed companies are least exposed to, apart from Patanjali's domestic plantation.

In plain terms

Compared with the world's agribusiness giants, India's listed oil companies are small, but they earn more on each tonne at home because India protects its refiners and Indians buy branded packs. Investors pay more for them than for the global giants because they hope the Indian brands will grow into food companies.

Technology and disruption: apps, packs, fuel and traceability

Nothing will replace cooking oil soon. What is changing is how it is sold, what else it is used for and what buyers abroad demand to know about it.

Edible oil is not a technology business, and the disruptions that matter are commercial and regulatory rather than scientific. Five of them are worth tracking.

  • Quick commerce and e-commerce. Online channels favour brands, because loose oil cannot be listed. AWL claims about 29% of edible-oil sales on e-commerce and quick-commerce platforms, and quick commerce is about a third of its alternate-channel volume, growing 64% by value in Q1 FY27. Patanjali targets 20% of revenue from modern trade and online within 18 months. The risk for brands is platform bargaining power over margins; the gain is share.
  • Standard packs. The nine mandatory pack sizes from September 2026 end pack engineering by small packers and make price comparison easy, which should help national brands.
  • Fuel competes for the oil. Indonesia's B50, renewable diesel and sustainable aviation fuel tie vegetable oil to petroleum prices; when diesel is dear, palm is pulled out of food. That keeps palm from regaining its old discount and raises volatility. It also creates an outlet for by-products: Gokul's biodiesel plant turns acid and waste oils into an export product (see our refining report for the fuel side).
  • Traceability and ESG. The EU's deforestation regulation applies to palm and soy sold into Europe, not to Indian domestic sales, but it matters for exporters. AWL says 95% of its palm procurement is traceable; Gokul is RSPO and ISCC-EU certified, which its biodiesel exports require.
  • Health and the oil-reduction push. Trans-fat limits, fortification and government campaigns to cut oil use are a slow drag on per-person consumption, and a reason for brands to push premium oils (rice bran, mustard, blends) where margins are higher.

Inside the companies, the technology that matters most is risk management: the systems that track positions across physical stock, forward sales and futures in several currencies. The industry's collapses (KS Oils in 2012, Ruchi's losses in 2016–17) were risk-management failures, not product failures, and a scale player's best defence is a disciplined hedging book. Energy is the other lever: Gokul's 15 MW captive solar plant cut its power cost by about 45%, and Patanjali's 60.85 MW of wind supplies about a third of its energy. Alternative fats from precision fermentation are a negligible factor for the foreseeable future.

In plain terms

Cooking oil itself will not be disrupted. What is changing is where it is bought (more on apps, which only stock brands), what size the packs are (now standardised), and how much of the world's oil gets burned as fuel instead of eaten. All three tend to favour the large, branded, well-hedged companies.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The structure is protected and the brands are gaining share. The near-term earnings are exposed to a price cycle that has just turned.

The bull case

  • Protected refining: the 19.25-point duty gap survived the September 2026 cut, refined imports are down to 5% of the mix, and policy has consistently protected domestic processing since May 2025.
  • Share gains: branded leaders grow two to three times the market (AWL's edible-oil volume 7.7% a year over nine years), helped by standard packs, quick commerce and fortification rules.
  • Mix: FMCG, foods, specialty chemicals, plantation and biodiesel all earn more than refining; Patanjali's FMCG and AWL's foods are growing at 20–33%.
  • Gokul's compounding: a 40% capacity expansion, biodiesel exports and a 37% return on capital at 14 times earnings.
  • Cleaner AWL: one specialist promoter, no Adani overhang, institutional buying, and a valuation of 0.31 times revenue.

The bear case

  • Inventory losses ahead: the 24 September duty cut, Malaysian stocks at 3.51 million tonnes and any rupee recovery could reverse Q1 FY27's gains in Q3 FY27.
  • Leaks and policy risk: 682,000 tonnes of duty-free refined oil from Nepal, pressure to pass on duty cuts, and the threat of stock limits with wholesale inflation at 11.4%.
  • Thin margins, rising rates: 3% operating margins leave little room for a 5.50% repo rate going higher on large LC and acceptance books.
  • Governance and quality at Patanjali: ~₹2,000 crore a year of related-party flows, a ₹600 crore advance to the promoter, the Ashav award and a tax-flattered FY26.
  • Unproven adjacencies: AWL's foods earn 3–6% and a 6% segment return; Gokul's model relies on supplier credit and has no earnings call.

The balance between the two depends on the horizon. Over three to five years the structural points (duty protection, share gains, mix) favour the larger branded companies and Gokul's expansion. Over the next two quarters the cyclical points dominate: Q1 FY27 was an inventory-gain quarter, the price and duty signals have since turned, and history says edible-oil profits fall faster than revenue when that happens. An investor's entry point in this sector is best judged against normal per-tonne profits (AWL's ₹4,000–4,500 guidance), not against Q1's peak.

In plain terms

The long-term picture is good for the big brands: protected refining, steady share gains and growing food businesses. The short-term picture is risky: the profits of the last quarter came partly from rising prices, and prices and duties have just moved the other way.
