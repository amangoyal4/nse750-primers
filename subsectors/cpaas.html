00

The one-page view: a toll road where the aggregator keeps the small change

Two listed companies route most of India's business messaging. They bill ₹8,826 crore a year and keep about a quarter of it.

Every time a bank sends a one-time password, an airline sends a boarding update or a retailer pushes a sale offer, a message passes through a company most customers have never heard of. These companies are called CPaaS providers (communications platform as a service) or, in Indian regulatory language, aggregators and telemarketers. They connect thousands of enterprises to the telecom networks and messaging apps that actually deliver the message to a phone. India has two listed ones in the NSE 750: Tanla Platforms, the largest domestic aggregator, and Route Mobile, a global aggregator that has been 74.85% owned by Belgium's Proximus group since 2024.

The business is easy to describe and hard to make rich. An enterprise pays the aggregator a price per message, around 13 to 20 paise for a domestic transactional SMS. The aggregator pays the terminating telco a fee for delivering it, around 10 to 15 paise. What is left, a paisa or three per message, has to pay for software, people, compliance and profit. Across FY26 the two listed companies billed a combined ₹8,826 crore, but their combined gross profit was only ₹2,182 crore (24.7%). Roughly ₹75 of every ₹100 they billed went straight out to Jio, Airtel, Vi, foreign mobile operators, Meta (for WhatsApp) and Google and the telcos (for RCS). This is the first fact to keep in mind: revenue in CPaaS is mostly a pass-through, and gross profit is the real size of the business.

Eight forces shape this sub-sector

The rest of this report is organised around the forces that decide who earns what in Indian enterprise messaging. In order of importance:

  • Channel migration and a change of landlord. Messages are moving from SMS to WhatsApp and RCS (rich business messaging in the phone's default app). The aggregator keeps the customer, but Meta and Google become price-setters next to the telcos. Tanla's OTT share (WhatsApp, RCS, Truecaller) rose from 15% of total revenue in FY24 to 36.2% in Q2 FY27.
  • Telco pricing power over termination. Three private telcos deliver nearly every message and set the commercial A2P (application-to-person) fee. The aggregator's enterprise gross margin is pinned at about 18–20% as a result.
  • Regulation as moat and tax. TRAI's DLT system, URL whitelisting, message traceability, the 140/1600 number series and the Third Amendment of September 2026 each add fixed compliance cost and push small operators out. Telcos received 31.1 lakh spam complaints in calendar 2025, most of them about unregistered senders.
  • The collapse of the ILD OTP pool. International SMS into India (a Google or Meta login code sent to an Indian number) was the richest margin in the chain. AIT clean-ups, channel switching by global tech and a June 2025 DoT rule shrank it. Route's revenue per transaction fell from about 29.4 paise in FY25 to 24.8 paise in Q2 FY27.
  • Consolidation into telcos and big platforms. Proximus bought Route (2024), Tata Communications bought Kaleyra (2023), Tanla bought ValueFirst from Twilio (2023 and 2026). A listed company that is 75% owned by a foreign telco trades at a structural discount.
  • AI cuts both ways. It powers fraud prevention (Tanla's Wisely.ai) and conversational agents, but it also helps enterprises reach customers through cheaper channels and may shift pricing from per message to per outcome.
  • Cash-rich balance sheets. The two companies hold ₹2,803 crore of cash against ₹11,082 crore of combined market value, so buybacks, dividends and acquisitions are a large part of the equity case.
  • The rupee at about ₹94.9 per US dollar. Route earns about two-thirds of its revenue outside India, so a weak rupee flatters its reported growth.

Two companies, opposite directions

The single most striking fact about the sub-sector today is that its two members are moving apart. Tanla is re-accelerating. Its year-on-year revenue growth has risen for six straight quarters, from −0.8% in Q2 FY25 to +25.3% in Q2 FY27 (₹1,352 crore), driven by WhatsApp (volumes doubled year on year), wallet-share gains at large banks and two months of the newly acquired ValueFirst overseas business. Stripping out that acquisition, organic growth was still about 22.6% (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic: ₹13,517 mn less ₹285 mn, divided by ₹10,785 mn). Route is stagnating while repairing its margin. Q2 FY27 revenue rose 5.4% to ₹1,180 crore and gross profit 6.8%, but adjusted EBITDA fell 11.1% to ₹118 crore (10.0% margin) and the company's guidance of about 12% for FY27 now needs about 14% in the second half.

In plain terms

A CPaaS company is a broker of delivery. It takes a message from a bank, checks it against the rules, chooses the cheapest reliable route and hands it to the telco or WhatsApp. The telco or Meta keeps most of the money. The broker earns a small fee on a very large number of messages, so a one-paisa change in what the telco charges can wipe out a third of its profit on that message. The companies that do well either own something the telcos need (Tanla's anti-spam and registry software) or move up into software their customers pay for separately.

For readers new to the sub-sector, the most useful single habit is to read every CPaaS result from the gross-profit line down, not from revenue. A revenue beat can come from low-margin one-off campaigns (Tanla's Q2 FY27 letter mentions "one-time campaigns from select customers"), from a weaker rupee, or from a channel whose fee is mostly passed through to Meta. Gross profit, gross profit per message and the share of gross profit that survives to EBITDA tell you whether the business actually got better.

01

How a message makes money: from a bank's server to your phone in two seconds

The value chain has five links. The aggregator sits in the middle and is squeezed from both ends.

An enterprise message in India travels through a chain that TRAI designed in 2018. The bank, retailer or government department is called the principal entity (PE). It registers itself, its sender IDs ("headers", such as HDFCBK) and every message template on a telco's distributed-ledger platform (DLT). It then hires one or more telemarketers, the regulatory name for aggregators such as Tanla's Karix and ValueFirst units, Route, Kaleyra, Gupshup, Infobip and Sinch's Indian arm. The aggregator integrates with the enterprise's systems through an API or the SMPP protocol, chooses a route, tags each message with the PE identity, template ID and the declared PE-to-telemarketer chain, and hands it to the terminating telco. The telco's DLT system checks the message against the registered header, template, URL whitelist and consent records, accepts or rejects it, and delivers it to the handset. The telco then charges the aggregator a termination fee.

Two features of the chain matter most for investors. The first is that the aggregator is the only link with no natural monopoly. The enterprise can split its traffic across several aggregators (large banks routinely use three or four), the telco controls the last mile, and Meta controls the WhatsApp app. The aggregator competes on reliability, delivery speed, compliance tooling and price, which keeps its take thin. The second is that the regulator has made the chain longer and stricter, and that favours scale. Since December 2024 every message must carry a declared PE-to-telemarketer chain, and a message with an undeclared chain is rejected. That ended the anonymous resale of traffic through layers of small brokers and pushed volume toward aggregators with direct telco connections, large compliance teams and bank guarantees already lodged with the telcos.

In plain terms

The aggregator is a middleman between thousands of businesses and a handful of networks. The businesses can switch brokers, and the networks set their own prices. What lets a broker earn more than a sliver is scale (better rates and guarantees with telcos), compliance (the rules now reject sloppy traffic), and products sold on top of the pipe. Tanla has an unusual extra: it also sells the rule-checking software to the telcos themselves.

One last piece of plumbing explains a lot of accounting. Telcos ask aggregators for bank guarantees or prepayments before they grant termination capacity at good rates. Tanla had ₹189.3 crore of bank guarantees outstanding at 31-Mar-2026, and Route ₹225.7 crore. Route also prepays some vendors for committed volumes, and in FY26 it wrote off ₹135.87 crore of such advances after volumes fell short. The supply chain therefore turns part of the aggregator's balance sheet into a credit exposure to telcos and other vendors, a theme Section 15 covers in detail.

02

Size, growth and the profit pool: a ₹11,000–14,000 crore market that keeps about ₹3,000 crore

Market-research numbers overstate the market two to three times. The useful figure is gross profit, and the listed pair hold a large share of it.

There is no official measure of the Indian enterprise-messaging market. TRAI publishes complaint data but not A2P volumes, and the market-research estimates in circulation disagree by a factor of three. One firm puts the "India A2P messaging market" at US$4.69 billion in 2025 (about ₹44,500 crore at ₹94.9 per dollar), another at US$3.02 billion (about ₹28,700 crore), and a third puts the narrower "India CPaaS market" at about US$1.40 billion (about ₹13,300 crore). The larger estimates cannot be reconciled with the revenue of the companies that actually carry the traffic and should not be used.

A bottom-up estimate is more defensible. Tanla's India-billed revenue was ₹3,248.5 crore in FY26 (73.5% of its total, by customer location in the audited accounts), and CARE Ratings credits Tanla with about 35% of India's CPaaS segment by volume. Grossing up gives a domestic enterprise-messaging spend in the region of ₹11,000–14,000 crore a year, including the telco and Meta fees that pass through. At a blended gross margin of 22–25%, the profit pool available to all aggregators is roughly ₹2,500–3,500 crore. Both numbers are ILLUSTRATIVE estimates built from company data and should be read as ranges.

Growth: a boom, a squeeze and a split

Using the two listed members as a proxy for the market, the sub-sector has had three phases. From FY20 to FY22, the digital-payments and e-KYC boom drove combined revenue up about 34% a year: UPI, card tokenisation, e-commerce and the March 2021 enforcement of DLT scrubbing all multiplied OTP and alert volumes. From FY23 to FY25, volumes kept growing but prices fell, and revenue growth slowed from 33% to 8%. Tanla's profit peaked in FY22 (₹539 crore) and Route's in FY24 (₹389 crore). In FY26, combined revenue grew only 2.6%: Tanla +9.7% and Route −3.7% as Route lost international OTP traffic.

In plain terms

The headline "market size" numbers for Indian messaging are mostly noise. What matters is that businesses spend somewhere around ₹11,000–14,000 crore a year to send messages, most of which goes to telcos and Meta, and that all the aggregators together keep about ₹3,000 crore of it. The two listed companies' combined gross profit of ₹2,182 crore includes a lot of non-Indian business at Route, but Tanla alone holds a large slice of the Indian pool.

Management's own view of growth is modest. On the 27-Apr-2026 call, Tanla's executive director put industry CPaaS revenue growth at about 8–10% a year and said Tanla expects to grow "definitely more than 10%" by gaining share. Tanla gives no numeric guidance, and every shareholder letter repeats that it "does not provide any guidance". Route guided for "mid to high single digit" revenue growth in FY27. H1 FY27 delivered +21.7% at Tanla (helped by WhatsApp and the ValueFirst overseas acquisition) and +7.4% at Route, so the market is growing roughly as management described while Tanla takes share.

03

Who buys the messages: 250 enterprises, banks first, global tech leaving

Demand is concentrated in a few hundred large senders. Banks are growing; global technology companies are moving their OTPs elsewhere.

Enterprise messaging demand in India is far more concentrated than the number of senders suggests. More than 3 lakh principal entities are registered on DLT, but Tanla's management said on its April 2026 call that 75–80% of Indian enterprise CPaaS spend comes from the top ~250 customers, with the remaining 15–20% spread across a tail of 50,000–60,000 businesses. Tanla says it is present at about 80% of those top accounts and is competing for wallet share inside them. New logos named on that call included the Reserve Bank of India and Bank of Baroda, and at one of the largest e-commerce companies Tanla's wallet share passed 50% once OTT channels were added.

The six demand pools, in order of size:

  • Banking, financial services and insurance (BFSI). OTPs for UPI, cards and net banking, mandatory debit alerts, statements and collections. This is the anchor. Tanla's 22 customers billed at more than ₹50 crore a year grew 42.5% year on year in Q2 FY27, "with BFSI customers driving growth". FinServ was Route's largest vertical at 15% of FY26 revenue. BFSI also buys fraud protection: a top-five private bank using Wisely ATP blocked 91 million scam messages in FY26 across 21 million customers.
  • Global technology and digital natives. Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, Uber and others send login codes to Indian numbers, mostly as international (ILD) traffic. This was the richest and most volatile pool. Route's "digital native" vertical fell from 22% of revenue in FY24 to 11% in FY26 after "a specific US headquartered customer switched to alternate channels of communication".
  • E-commerce, logistics, quick commerce and travel. Order and delivery notifications, increasingly on WhatsApp utility messages. Route's e-commerce share fell from 10% (FY24) to 4% (H1 FY27) after losing ILD traffic from a global major.
  • Government and public services. Aadhaar, DigiLocker, IRCTC and tax OTPs. Tanla described Government volumes as small and price-sensitive; PSU banks are "a very good share" of volume.
  • Telecom operators as customers. They buy DLT platforms (Trubloq), anti-spam (Wisely at Indosat) and firewalls (Route has 12 deployed). Demand here is driven by regulation and fraud, not by message volume.
  • Conversational commerce and AI agents. Catalogues, bots and agents on WhatsApp and RCS, priced by subscription or outcome. Real but small: Route paid ₹33.7 crore for Heltar's AI customer-engagement business in July 2026.

Where ₹100 of billing goes: three-quarters to the networks, a tenth to run the business

The cost structure is simple. One line, termination and channel fees, decides almost everything.

A CPaaS company's income statement has one dominant cost: what it pays the networks to deliver the messages. Tanla calls it "cost of services" and Route calls it "purchase of messaging services". It includes domestic telco termination, international operator termination, Meta's WhatsApp fees, RCS fees and cloud hosting. In FY26 it was 73.4% of Tanla's revenue and 77.1% of Route's (₹3,400.9 crore). Everything else, the people, the software platforms, the sales teams, the compliance staff, the data centres and the head office, cost about ₹10–11 of every ₹100. The remainder is EBITDA, which was ₹16.4 per ₹100 at Tanla and ₹11.9 per ₹100 (adjusted) at Route.

In plain terms

Of every ₹100 a messaging company bills, about ₹75 is not really its money: it collects it from the customer and hands it to a telco or to Meta. The company runs on the remaining ₹25, spends about ₹10 on itself and keeps ₹12–16. So small changes in what the networks charge matter much more than anything the company does with its own costs.

The key cost inputs, and who controls them

  • Domestic telco termination. The largest cost. Commercial A2P SMS termination is negotiated with Jio, Airtel and Vi, which together serve the vast majority of Indian subscribers. Telcos describe the TRAI-specified deterrent termination charge on commercial SMS as ₹0.07 and asked TRAI in December 2025 to raise it to about ₹0.12. Vendor quotes put wholesale rates at about ₹0.10–0.12 for Jio, ₹0.13–0.15 for Airtel and ₹0.16–0.18 for Vi (INDICATIVE, vendor-sourced).
  • International termination. Set freely by foreign operators. The global average rose from about US$0.07 to about US$0.10 per SMS between late 2023 and early 2025 (mobilesquared).   • Meta and Google channel fees. WhatsApp moved to per-message pricing on 1-Jul-2025; the India marketing rate rose to ₹0.8631 on 1-Jan-2026; free in-window utility messages ended on 1-Oct-2026. Jio repriced RCS on 1-Sep-2026.
  • People. Tanla had 1,043 permanent employees at 31-Mar-2026 and an average of 1,081 in Q2 FY27 including ValueFirst overseas. Route has about 786 (505 in India). Tanla's employee cost rose 14.5% year on year in Q2 FY27.

05

Two landlords: who really owns the margin in Indian CPaaS Deep dive

The aggregator pays rent to the telco on SMS and to Meta and Google on the new channels. Both landlords have been raising the rent.

The simplest way to understand the economics of an Indian CPaaS company is to treat it as a tenant. On every SMS it pays rent to the terminating telco. On every WhatsApp message it pays rent to Meta. On every RCS message it pays rent to the telco and, through the telco, to Google. The tenant can choose where to shop, but it cannot change the rent. Over 2023–2026 every landlord has raised it, and the tenant has been able to pass on only part of each rise to the enterprises it serves. CARE Ratings puts it directly: a "change in telco pricing directly impacts Tanla's realisations and margins, limiting its ability to independently raise prices".

The per-message bridge

Route Mobile publishes billable transactions, which makes it possible to build the economics of an average message from audited and company-reported numbers. In Q2 FY27 Route billed ₹11,797 mn on 47.6 billion transactions. That is 24.8 paise of revenue per transaction. It paid about 19.2 paise to operators and channel owners, leaving 5.55 paise of gross profit. Indirect costs took 3.06 paise, leaving 2.49 paise of adjusted EBITDA, and after depreciation, other income and tax, about 1.73 paise of PAT. Two things stand out. The landlords take more than three-quarters of every paisa billed, and the aggregator's EBITDA is only about one-tenth of what the enterprise pays. And small price moves by a landlord swamp everything else. A domestic SMS works the same way. In an ILLUSTRATIVE example, a mid-size bank pays ₹0.150 per transactional SMS, the telco charges ₹0.120, and the aggregator keeps ₹0.030 of gross profit (20%, close to Tanla's Enterprise gross margin of 20.0% in Q2 FY27). If the telco raises its fee by one paisa and the aggregator cannot pass it on, gross profit falls to ₹0.020, a cut of one-third. On a billion SMS a month, that one paisa is ₹1 crore of monthly gross profit.

In plain terms

The aggregator keeps about a quarter of what it bills and earns about a tenth. When a telco or Meta raises its price by a small amount, the aggregator either loses a large slice of its profit on that message or has to argue the increase through to hundreds of corporate customers, many of whom are banks with strong buying power. That is why these companies' margins move in steps whenever a landlord changes its tariff.

Landlord one: the telcos

Three private telcos deliver nearly all Indian A2P traffic, and the domestic commercial A2P price is negotiated rather than regulated. The telcos reportedly raised bulk SMS charges by about 25–30% (roughly 2.5 paise) from 1-Aug-2023; one 2023 account suggests the figures may have referred mainly to international SMS, so the size of that rise should be treated as reported rather than confirmed. In the interconnection-norms review in December 2025, Jio, Airtel and Vi asked TRAI to raise the deterrent termination charge on commercial SMS from ₹0.07 to about ₹0.12, and to set ₹0.50 a minute on A2P calls. TRAI's final TCCCPR Third Amendment (18-Sep-2026) allowed terminating telcos to charge up to ₹0.05 a minute on A2P calls, a tenth of the ask but a new revenue line for telcos and a new cost for CPaaS voice products. It takes effect 60 days after notification, which had not happened by 1-Nov-2026.

From SMS to WhatsApp and RCS: the channel shift is now visible in the numbers

OTT channels have grown from 15% to 36% of Tanla's revenue in about two and a half years. The aggregator keeps the customer but changes landlord.

For a decade the Indian business message meant an SMS. That is changing fast. WhatsApp has hundreds of millions of Indian users, supports rich formats, buttons and payments, and since July 2025 is priced per message. RCS brings similar features into the phone's default messaging app, and Apple's support for RCS in recent iPhone software made it a two-operating-system channel. For enterprises the attraction is engagement: a WhatsApp delivery update with a tracking button is read and acted on far more often than a plain SMS. For aggregators the shift is a mixed blessing. The customer stays, but the rent goes to a different landlord, and that landlord publishes its price.

Does OTT help or hurt the margin?

The common assumption that WhatsApp and RCS are "higher margin" than SMS needs care, because neither company discloses its OTT gross margin. On WhatsApp the aggregator collects Meta's fee inside its revenue and adds a markup, typically a few percent to the low teens of the Meta fee, plus platform or journey fees. That tends to give more gross profit per message but a lower gross margin as a percentage of revenue. In an ILLUSTRATIVE comparison, an SMS OTP priced at ₹0.150 with a ₹0.120 telco fee earns ₹0.030 (20%), while a WhatsApp authentication message priced at about ₹0.135 with Meta's ₹0.115 fee earns about ₹0.020 (about 15%). A WhatsApp marketing message priced at about ₹0.95 against Meta's ₹0.8631 might earn ₹0.08–0.09 but only about 9–10% of revenue. Tanla's gross margin fell 48 basis points quarter on quarter in Q2 FY27 because of a "change in revenue mix", which fits this pattern.

The channel shift therefore changes three things at once. It inflates revenue relative to gross profit, because Meta's fee is larger than a telco's SMS fee for marketing traffic. It changes the identity of the price-setter, from three Indian telcos that negotiate with aggregators to a global platform that sets a public rate card. And it creates a new dependency: Meta can, and has, changed prices, free tiers and categories on its own timetable, typically on 1 January, 1 April, 1 July or 1 October. A BSP's business plan now has to assume that its largest supplier will change the rules several times a year.

In plain terms

Businesses are moving their messages from SMS to WhatsApp and RCS because customers read them more. The messaging companies still carry the traffic, but more of each rupee now goes to Meta or Google. Revenue looks bigger, profit per message can go up or down, and the company's most important supplier is now a global platform that changes its prices whenever it likes.

The winners in this shift so far are aggregators with scale in BFSI and large-enterprise accounts, where WhatsApp utility and authentication volumes are huge, and with partner status at Meta and Google. Meta and Google each named Tanla's subsidiaries partner of the year for India (Karix and ValueFirst were Google growth partners of the year for 2025; Karix won Meta's partner award), and Tanla's MaaP platform has carried more than 2 billion RCS messages. The losers are SMS-only aggregators and those whose traffic was concentrated in international OTPs, which are moving to WhatsApp authentication-international at ₹2.50 or to app-based verification.

The regulator's rail: Trubloq, consent and the TRAI rulebook Deep dive

India regulates commercial messaging more tightly than almost any other country. Each tightening has raised costs and pushed volume toward scale players.

India's commercial-messaging regime rests on the Telecom Commercial Communications Customer Preference Regulations, 2018 (TCCCPR), notified by TRAI in July 2018 and phased in over 2019–2021. TCCCPR required every commercial sender to register on a distributed ledger run by the telcos, along with every header, every message template and every consent record, and required telcos to scrub every commercial message against that ledger in real time. Enforcement of DLT scrubbing in March 2021 briefly disrupted traffic across the country. It also created a new market: software to run the ledger. Tanla's Trubloq, launched in 2020 and described in its annual report as "the world's largest" blockchain DLT, is one of the platforms the telcos use. Tanla renamed itself Tanla Platforms in the same year.

The economics of Trubloq are unusual. The telco pays Tanla a platform fee for each scrubbed transaction, a fraction of a paisa, on hundreds of billions of transactions. The fee is a cost for the telco, not for the enterprise, so it is not passed through any aggregator's revenue. Tanla's Platforms line, which also includes Wisely.ai and the MaaP RCS platform, earned ₹395 crore of revenue in FY26 at a 98.5% gross margin. One caution: a figure of "about 62% of India's A2P SMS processed through Trubloq", used in earlier coverage, does not appear in Tanla's annual report, letters or transcripts and remains unsourced. It measures scrubbing share across telcos in any case, not Tanla's messaging share (CARE puts that at about 35% by volume), and the two must not be added or compared.

Almost all of the spam Indians complain about comes from ordinary phone numbers, not from the registered business senders that use Tanla or Route. The ILD profit pool collapses: when global tech stopped texting India

International OTPs into India were the fattest margin in the chain. Three forces shrank them at once, and Route carried most of the exposure.

The word "international" means three different things in Indian CPaaS, and confusing them causes most analytical errors about Route Mobile. First, ILD A2P into India: a message generated outside India, typically a login code from Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon or Uber, that terminates on an Indian number. It is billed at the telcos' international termination rate, which is under forbearance (the telcos set it freely) and is many times the domestic rate. Second, revenue from foreign-headquartered customers: Route's customers by headquarters were 37% Asia, 33% Americas, 18% Europe and 3% Africa in H1 FY27. Third, termination outside India: Route terminated 35% of its H1 FY27 revenue in India, so about 65% ended on foreign networks. The ILD pool is the first of these, and it is the one that has collapsed.

Three forces hit it together. The first was a global clean-up of artificially inflated traffic (AIT), also called SMS pumping, in which fraudsters trigger large volumes of OTPs to numbers they control and share in the termination revenue. Route's FY26 presentation says "AIT clean-up across the industry triggered a broad-based decline in A2P SMS volumes globally". The second was channel switching by global tech. A US-headquartered digital native, Route's largest customer in that vertical, moved its traffic to "alternate channels of communication", and Route lost ILD traffic from a global e-commerce major. The third was regulatory: the DoT's 19-Jun-2025 Office Memorandum required all foreign-originated SMS to arrive through ILD gateways and barred domestic A2P from using them, which ended relabelling arbitrage and pushed every genuine international message onto the most expensive route.

A new ₹10.1 crore "management support services" charge from the Proximus group appeared in FY26. Third, cash allocation: three-quarters of every dividend goes to the parent, and the parent influences how the rest of the ₹1,356 crore cash is used. Fourth, management turnover: Route had three CEOs and three chairmen in about 15 months (Section 16).

In plain terms

When a foreign company owns three-quarters of an Indian listed company, minority shareholders depend on decisions made elsewhere: what prices the parent's other businesses pay, how the cash is used, and whether the parent ever buys out the rest. Until those questions are answered, investors usually pay less for each rupee of profit. Route Mobile is a clear example; Tanla, still run by its founder, does not carry this discount.

None of this implies that minorities are being treated unfairly; the related-party transactions are disclosed, approved and described by management as priced at cost plus a margin. It does mean that a large slice of Route's revenue and margin depends on transfer pricing inside a group whose strategy is set in Brussels, and that the stock's valuation reflects that dependence. For Tanla the equivalent governance points are smaller: related-party transactions of ₹21 lakh in FY26 (professional services from an LLP in which a director is a partner), no royalty or brand fee, a ₹5 lakh exchange fine for a board-composition lapse resolved in October 2024, and a 2020 insider-code breach by a designated person that led to disgorgement of ₹56,000.

Three cycles in twenty years: VAS bust, DLT boom, ILD reset

The sub-sector moves in a rhythm of regulatory shock, volume disruption and consolidation, laid over a long shift from SMS to apps.

Indian enterprise messaging has been through three distinct cycles. The first was the mobile value-added services (VAS) era of the 2000s. Tanla, incorporated in Hyderabad in 1995 (the company dates its founding to 1999), listed in January 2007 as an SMS-aggregation and VAS business that rode the international SMS boom of 2007–08. A bust followed in 2009–12, tinged by governance concerns. Route Mobile was founded in Mumbai in 2004 by Sandipkumar and Rajdipkumar Gupta, reportedly with about US$2,000 of starting capital, and grew by connecting to operators worldwide rather than by depending on Indian telcos.

The second was the DLT and digital-payments boom of 2018–2022. Tanla bought Karix Mobile in FY2018-19, making it the largest enterprise SMS aggregator in India. TRAI's TCCCPR 2018 created the DLT system and Trubloq. UPI, e-KYC and e-commerce multiplied OTP volumes. Tanla took an impairment and reported a loss of ₹211 crore in FY20, then earned ₹356 crore in FY21 and a peak of ₹539 crore in FY22; its share price rose from about ₹60 in 2020 to about ₹2,000 in January 2022 before falling about 70% that year. Route listed in September 2020, raised ₹8,675 mn through a QIP in 2021 and went on an acquisition spree (Masivian in Colombia, 365squared in Malta, Cellent, Start Corp, Call2Connect, TeleDNA, M.R. Messaging in Dubai).

Reading a CPaaS company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers tell you almost everything about a messaging company. Most of them start below the revenue line.

CPaaS results are easy to misread because the headline number, revenue, is mostly money that belongs to someone else. This section works through the metrics that matter, in the order an analyst should look at them: what each one is, how it is calculated, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, what good looks like, how Tanla and Route compare, and the trap hidden in its definition. It closes with an ILLUSTRATIVE anatomy of a typical enterprise-messaging book, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps collected in one place and a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

The equation, worked in rupees

Every aggregator's profit comes from one equation. For each channel, gross profit equals volume multiplied by the gap between the price the enterprise pays and the fee the landlord charges. EBITDA is gross profit less the company's own indirect costs. PAT follows after depreciation, other income, finance costs and tax. The company controls some of these terms and not others, and the split is the key to judging management.

In plain terms

KPI 1. Revenue, and why it misleads

What it is: total billing to customers, including the telco, Meta and Google fees the company passes through. Formula: Σ volume × price. Worked example: Tanla's Q2 FY27 revenue was ₹13,517 mn, up 25.3%. Of the quarter-on-quarter rise of 10.2%, 7.9 points came from the existing business and 2.3 points from two months of ValueFirst overseas (₹285 mn), and the letter adds that the quarter "benefited from one-time campaigns from select customers", which were not quantified. What drives it: volume, channel mix (WhatsApp marketing inflates revenue because Meta's fee is large), acquisitions, the rupee (Route earns about two-thirds outside India) and one-off campaigns. Benchmark: industry growth of about 8–10% a year per Tanla management; good is above 15%, weak below 5%. How they compare: Tanla +25.3% and Route +5.4% in Q2 FY27; H1 FY27 +21.7% and +7.4%. The trap: revenue growth can come from low-margin channels or from FX. Never value a CPaaS company on EV/revenue; use EV/gross profit or EV/EBITDA, and check whether gross profit grew as fast as revenue.

KPI 2. Gross margin

What it is: the share of revenue left after landlord fees. Formula: (revenue − cost of services) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Route Q2 FY27: (₹11,797 mn − ₹9,157 mn) ÷ ₹11,797 mn = 22.4%. What drives it up: more platform or software revenue, more domestic SMS OTPs (richest percentage margin), better routing, RCS where aggregators have exclusive arrangements. Down: telco or Meta price rises not passed on, more WhatsApp marketing, loss of high-margin customers, competitive bids at large banks. Benchmark: blended above 28% is good, 22–28% average, below 20% weak; for a pure enterprise aggregator, 18–22% is normal. How they compare: Tanla 26.1% (Q2 FY27), Route 22.4%; Tanla's Enterprise line alone 20.0%. The trap: a falling gross margin is not always bad. If it falls because WhatsApp volumes doubled, gross profit in rupees may still be rising strongly, as at Tanla in Q2 FY27 (gross margin −48 bps quarter on quarter, gross profit +8.2%).

KPI 9. OTT and new-product share

What it is: the share of revenue from WhatsApp, RCS and other app-based channels (Tanla's "OTT") or from newer products (Route's "new products"). Formula: OTT revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Tanla's OTT share was 36.2% of total revenue in Q2 FY27, about ₹4,890 mn of ₹13,517 mn (ILLUSTRATIVE back-calculation). What drives it: enterprise adoption, Meta's category mix (marketing at 7.5 times utility), RCS pricing, partner status with Meta and Google. Benchmark: above 35% good, 15–35% average, below 15% weak, judged as a measure of channel readiness. How they compare: Tanla 15% (FY24) → 23% (FY25) → 31% (9M FY26) → 36.2%; Route new products 8% of FY26 revenue and about 10.9% in Q2 FY27. The trap: two definitions. Tanla's OTT shares are of total revenue (some earlier coverage labelled them as shares of enterprise revenue). Route's "new products" mixes channels (RCS, WhatsApp) with AI and other software. KPI 10. Customer concentration

What it is: how much revenue depends on the largest customers. Formula: revenue of top-N customers ÷ total. Worked example: Route H1 FY27 top 10 = 42% of ₹23,312 mn, about ₹9,790 mn. What drives it: the shape of the Indian market (75–80% of spend from about 250 enterprises), wins and losses at banks and global tech, intra-group traffic. Benchmark: top 10 below 30% good, 30–45% average, above 45% weak. How they compare: Route top 10 42%, top 5 32%, top 50 75% (H1 FY27). Tanla 19 customers = 46% of 9M FY26 revenue (CARE); 22 customers above ₹50 crore a year = about 43% (Q2 FY27). The trap: Tanla's standalone annual report says two customers each above 10% made up more than 86% of standalone revenue. That standalone entity is mainly the platform and holding company, so this reflects telco platform customers and inter-company billing, not group concentration. And at Route, the largest customer is a sister company (Telesign, 14.9% of FY26 revenue), so concentration risk is partly governance risk.

In plain terms

In Indian messaging a few big banks and tech companies matter enormously. Losing one, or seeing one move its login codes to an app, can take several percent off revenue in a quarter. When a company says its top ten customers are 40% of revenue, ask who they are, whether any is related to the company, and whether any has started using WhatsApp or app-based verification instead.

KPI 11. Net revenue retention

What it is: this period's revenue from customers who were customers a year ago, divided by their revenue a year ago. Formula: revenue from the prior-year cohort now ÷ that cohort's revenue then. Worked example: a cohort that billed ₹100 crore last year and ₹119 crore this year has NRR of 119%, Tanla's Q2 FY27 figure. What drives it: wallet-share gains, new channels sold to existing customers, price changes, churn. Benchmark: above 115% good, 100–115% average, below 100% weak. Twilio's comparable "dollar-based net expansion" was 114% in Q3-2026. How they compare: Tanla 119%; Route 105% (H1 FY27 against H1 FY26, deck) but "98% for the year" per management on the 24-Jul-2026 call, after one key customer's disruption. The trap: the definition and period vary (quarter, half, trailing year), none is audited, and FX can flatter it for a company billing in dollars. Use it as a direction-of-travel signal and always quote the basis.

KPI 15. ROE and ROCE

What it is: profit as a share of shareholders' equity (ROE) or of capital employed (ROCE). Formula: PAT ÷ average equity; EBIT ÷ (equity + debt). Benchmark: ROE above 20% good, 12–20% average, below 12% weak. How they compare: Tanla ROE 21.4%, ROCE 26.3% (screener; 26.5% including cash on the company's basis); Route ROE 12.6%, ROCE 17.4%. What it is: how much of the stock price is backed by cash on the balance sheet. Formula: company-reported cash and equivalents (including fixed deposits and liquid funds) ÷ market capitalisation. Benchmark: there is no "good" level, but above about 30% the market is usually signalling doubt about how the cash will be used or how durable the earnings are; below 10% the cash is not a valuation factor. Putting the KPIs together: what the two companies' dashboards say

Read as a set, the KPIs tell a consistent story. Tanla scores well on growth (gross profit +23.1%), operating leverage (EBITDA ÷ gross profit 60.7%), retention (119%), collections (DSO 63 days) and cash conversion, and only average on margins because its Enterprise line is a normal aggregator business. Its weak spots are concentration (19 customers at 46% of revenue) and the stalled Platforms line, whose share of group gross profit is shrinking. The direction of travel matters: its EBITDA ÷ gross profit has slipped six points in two years, and whether the go-to-market hiring and the ValueFirst overseas integration "bear fruit", as management put it, will show up first in that ratio.

Route scores average on gross margin and gross profit per transaction, weak on operating leverage (44.9%) and cash conversion (15% in H1 FY27), and average on concentration and retention once the intra-group nature of its largest customer is understood. Its strengths are not in these dashboards at all: a debt-free balance sheet with cash at half its market value, 280+ direct operator connections and a parent with a global wholesale network. The KPI that would change the view fastest is cash conversion. If the H1 FY27 vendor advance is consumed as services in H2 and cash from operations recovers toward the 75–100% guide, the most serious question about Route's earnings quality would ease; if not, the FY26 pattern would be repeating.

Definition traps, collected

  • Revenue is a pass-through. About 75% of what the members bill belongs to telcos, Meta and Google. Compare businesses on gross profit, not revenue, and never apply software revenue multiples.
  • Three EBITDAs at Route. Reported, adjusted (minus capitalised development, plus one-offs) and screener operating profit differ by up to 5%. Tanla's reported EBITDA is close to screener operating profit. State the basis every time.
  • Screener's "other income" for Route contains the exceptional. Q2 FY26 shows other income of −₹109 crore and a tax rate of 1,042%. Do not compute "core PAT" from screener rows without adding back ₹135.9 crore pre-tax (about ₹119 crore after tax).
  • PAT versus owners' PAT. Route's FY26 PAT of ₹257 crore includes ₹17.9 crore attributable to minority interests; owners' PAT was ₹239 crore, and EPS (₹37.94) is on that basis.
  • Platform/Enterprise is not an audited segment. Tanla's audited accounts have one segment; the split is a management disclosure.
  • OTT share is of total revenue, not enterprise revenue, and it is a revenue share, not a gross-profit share.
  • Market-share claims use different denominators. "About 35% of India CPaaS by volume" (CARE, Tanla) is a messaging share; any "Trubloq share of scrubbing" is a platform share across telcos. Do not add or compare them.
  • "International" means three things: ILD into India, foreign-headquartered customers, and termination abroad.
  • Billable transactions are not SMS sent. Route's count includes OTT and voice, domestic and international.
  • Cash has four definitions. Tanla's ₹1,447.4 crore (Sep-2026) includes fixed deposits over 12 months, liquid funds and ₹58.4 crore of restricted margin money; CARE's narrower "cash and bank" was ₹859 crore at Dec-2025; screener's "investments" miss most of it.   • "Borrowings" are leases. Tanla's ₹53–75 crore and Route's ₹39–42 crore are lease liabilities; Route reported "no outstanding debt" at 31-Mar-2026.
  • NRR has two bases at Route (105% H1 deck; 98% "for the year" on the call).
  • Customer counts differ. Tanla "3,200+ enterprises" (company) versus "2,000+ established enterprises" (CARE); Route "3,100+ clients" (FY26 deck) versus "2,400+ active billable clients" (Q2 FY27 deck).
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in this sub-sector has a footnote that changes its meaning. Before comparing two figures, check that they measure the same thing over the same period on the same basis. The most common mistakes are treating revenue as if it were the company's own money, mixing Route's three EBITDA figures, and confusing cash with fixed deposits and restricted margin money.

How to read a CPaaS company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with gross profit, not revenue (minute 1). Pull gross profit and its year-on-year growth. If gross profit grew much slower than revenue, find out why: channel mix, acquisition, FX or one-off campaigns.
  2. Check the gross margin and its explanation (minute 2). A move of more than 1 point quarter on quarter needs a reason. "Change in revenue mix" usually means more WhatsApp; "traffic disruption" means a customer problem.
  3. Compute EBITDA ÷ gross profit (minute 3). If it fell, look at indirect costs: headcount, go-to-market spend, legal and professional fees, acquired businesses.
  4. Reconcile the EBITDA basis (minute 4). At Route, compare reported and adjusted EBITDA and list the add-backs (breach fees, capital-advance allowances, ESOP). Recurring "one-offs" are a red flag.
  5. Read the unit economics (minute 5). Route: billable transactions, revenue and gross profit per transaction. Tanla: Platforms revenue and margin, Enterprise gross margin.
  6. Check channel and product mix (minute 6). Tanla's OTT share; Route's new-products revenue; any comment on Meta or RCS price changes.
  7. Look at customers (minute 7). Top-10 share, cohort growth, retention, new logos, and any named loss or disruption.
  8. Go to the cash-flow statement (minute 8). Cash from operations against EBITDA, change in receivables and payables, and any "advance to vendor" or rise in other financial assets. This is where the biggest red flags have appeared.
  9. Check cash, guarantees and below-the-line items (minute 9). Company cash (not screener investments), restricted cash, exceptional items, forex gains in other income and the tax rate.
  10. Compare with guidance and the calendar (minute 10). Route guides revenue, margin, cash conversion and dividend; Tanla gives none. Note regulatory dates (Third Amendment notification, consent framework) and landlord price changes for the next quarter.

Red flags worth an immediate follow-up: gross profit falling while revenue rises for two quarters; adjusted EBITDA add-backs in three consecutive quarters; cash from operations below half of EBITDA; a jump in advances to suppliers or in "other financial assets"; payable days falling fast while receivables do not; a new exceptional write-off; any unexplained change in related-party sales; and auditor emphasis-of-matter paragraphs. Green flags: Platforms or software revenue growing faster than the group; EBITDA ÷ gross profit stable or rising while gross profit grows; DSO falling; retention above 110%; and payouts rising with cash.

Same pipe, different fates: why Tanla and Route split Deep dive

Two companies with the same revenue and the same product list are growing at 25% and 5%. Customers, geography, platforms and ownership explain the gap.

In FY26 Tanla and Route billed within ₹10 crore of each other. Both sell SMS, WhatsApp, RCS, voice and email; both have telco-side products; both are debt-free and cash-rich. Yet in Q2 FY27 Tanla grew revenue 25.3% and Route 5.4%, Tanla's gross profit grew 23.1% and Route's 6.8%, and Tanla's EBITDA rose 20.8% while Route's adjusted EBITDA fell 11.1%. The market values Tanla at three times Route. Four differences explain why.

Difference one: who the customers are

Tanla's revenue is anchored in Indian BFSI and large domestic enterprises, the part of the market where WhatsApp utility and authentication volumes are exploding and where banks are buying fraud protection as well as delivery. Its 22 largest customers grew 42.5% in Q2 FY27, "with BFSI customers driving growth". Route's growth customers were global technology companies and international OTP senders, the pool that collapsed: its digital-native vertical fell from 22% to 11% of revenue between FY24 and FY26, and its e-commerce vertical to 4%. When the richest customer pool in your book shrinks, no amount of operational excellence offsets it quickly.

Difference two: geography, corrected

Earlier descriptions of Tanla as almost entirely domestic were wrong. Its audited FY26 accounts show 73.5% of revenue (₹3,248.5 crore) from Indian customers and 26.5% (₹1,169.2 crore) from customers abroad, mostly global tech and OTT companies billed for traffic into India. Route is the mirror image: only 19% of FY26 revenue was booked in Indian entities and 35% of H1 FY27 revenue terminated in India. So the contrast is roughly three-quarters Indian against roughly one-fifth to one-third Indian, depending on the measure, not 90/10. The direction of the conclusion stands: Tanla is exposed mainly to the Indian domestic market, which is growing, and Route mainly to international termination and ILD, which is not.

The vendor-advance problem: buying termination in advance in a falling market Deep dive

Route Mobile lost about ₹135 crore on a minimum-commitment contract, and it is prepaying vendors again. The auditor has flagged it; the counterparty is not named.

In global A2P messaging, aggregators secure good termination rates by committing volume in advance. An operator or a large hub will offer a lower price per message, or capacity on a congested route, in exchange for a minimum guaranteed volume, sometimes paid up front. When volumes grow, this is a sensible way to buy cheaply. When volumes fall, the prepayment becomes a credit exposure to the vendor and the commitment becomes a loss. Route Mobile's FY26 accounts show this happening at scale, and its H1 FY27 cash flow shows the practice continuing. Nothing in the disclosures suggests wrongdoing: the matter is a commercial contract that went badly, disclosed in detail in the annual report and flagged by the auditor.

What happened: the ₹135.87 crore write-off

In Q2 FY26 (quarter to September 2025), Route recognised an exceptional charge of ₹135.87 crore, which turned the quarter's reported PAT into a loss of ₹18.8 crore. Route's FY26 annual report (Note 33.4) explains it in two parts. ₹107.96 crore related to a minimum-guaranteed SMS volume contract: a subsidiary had prepaid ₹196.61 crore to a vendor for the committed volume of the first two years. By 31-Mar-2024 management judged the contract onerous under Ind AS 37 and provided ₹26.70 crore. Volumes then fell well short, as the ILD and AIT shifts of 2024–25 cut traffic. Route sought renegotiation (14-Nov-2024), invoked arbitration against the "vendor subsidiaries" (24-Mar-2025), and the vendor counter-claimed and terminated the agreement (7-Apr-2025). After a hearing at the Singapore International Arbitration Centre (SIAC) in September 2025, Route wrote off the remaining net advance. ₹27.91 crore related to an advance for messaging services paid the previous year to a different vendor that "has ceased its business operation"; recovery is being explored.

In plain terms

Route paid a supplier in advance for two years of messaging capacity, expecting to use it. The traffic did not come, the supplier would not refund the money, and an arbitration panel in Singapore agreed with the supplier. Route lost about ₹135 crore. It was a bad commercial bet, not a hidden fraud, but it shows how a messaging company's balance sheet can carry risks that never appear in the profit line until the loss is booked.

Is it happening again?

Write-downs at Route are a pattern rather than a one-off. FY25 had a net exceptional of ₹18.45 crore: a ₹50.07 crore fair-value gain on contingent consideration for M.R. Management describes its approach as "build versus buy" and has said AI-company valuations are "skyrocketing".

Route's approach is changing. It raised its regular dividend from ₹11 a share in FY26 to a "sustainable" ₹16.5 for FY27, "payable each quarter as usual", and has declared ₹4 + ₹4 so far. It bought back ₹120 crore of shares in 2023, before the Proximus deal. Its acquisitions since have been small capability bolt-ons, such as Heltar's AI engagement business for ₹33.7 crore (₹23.9 crore upfront, ₹9.8 crore deferred for 18 months), plus "scale" deals that management calls "more involved decisions". Analysts on the May 2026 call pressed on low treasury yields (below 3–4% excluding FX) and on a payout of about 30%; the CFO said a "meaningful amount" of cash goes into vendor prepayments and that treasury policy would be reviewed. With RBI's repo rate at 5.50% (October 2026), idle cash earns modestly, which strengthens the case for higher payouts at both companies.

In plain terms

Both companies have far more cash than their businesses need. Tanla returns a good part of its profit through dividends and buybacks and spends some on acquisitions. Route's cash is half of its share price, but its owner decides how it is used, and some of it has gone into advance payments to suppliers. For a minority shareholder, the cash is only worth what the company eventually hands back.

The rupee at about ₹94.9 per dollar

Currency is a meaningful swing factor, especially for Route, which terminates about 65% of its revenue outside India and books most of it in UK, Malta, US and Dubai entities. A weaker rupee raises Route's reported rupee revenue and gross profit even if nothing changes in dollar terms, and produces forex gains in other income: ₹177.6 mn in Q2 FY26 and ₹181.5 mn in Q4 FY26, against only ₹10.2 mn in Q2 FY27. That swing explains most of the year-on-year fall in Route's Q2 FY27 adjusted PAT (about ₹167 mn of the ₹178 mn decline, ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic). Tanla's exposure is smaller but real: 26.5% of its revenue comes from overseas customers, ValueFirst's Middle East and South-East Asia business adds more, and it holds ₹274.6 crore in US-dollar accounts in Dubai and Singapore.

The practical rule is to strip FX before calling Route's growth organic. Two other currency effects matter. Meta's move to INR billing for Indian WhatsApp accounts (mandatory by 31-Dec-2026) removes FX risk for Indian BSPs on WhatsApp fees, which were previously billed in dollars. And international termination rates, set in dollars or euros by foreign operators, rise in rupee terms when the rupee falls, so Route's cost of services moves with its revenue. The net effect on gross margin is small; the effect on reported growth rates and on other income is not.

The global lens: India's CPaaS is cheap, thin and unusually efficient

Indian members run at half the gross margin of US platforms but convert more of their gross profit into EBITDA than almost anyone.

Global CPaaS is worth roughly US$30 billion a year (Juniper's 2025 estimate), with other estimates of US$31.6–35.9 billion for A2P SMS plus CPaaS. A2P SMS itself is flat to declining globally, with one forecaster projecting a −0.8% compound rate to 2031, after the 2024–25 AIT clean-ups. India is perhaps 4–5% of global CPaaS revenue (INDICATIVE) but a much larger share of message volume, because Indian domestic prices are among the lowest in the world: industry estimates of domestic per-SMS termination put India below South Africa (about ₹0.27), the US (about ₹0.7), Brazil (about ₹5.4) and Argentina (about ₹8.4), INDICATIVE. The two listed Indian members together billed about US$0.93 billion in FY26 at ₹94.9 per dollar, against Twilio's single-quarter revenue of US$1.56 billion.

The comparison needs one adjustment before it is useful. US peers increasingly report revenue net of carrier pass-through fees, or show the pass-through separately. Bandwidth reports "cloud communications revenue" excluding about US$76 mn of messaging surcharges (32% of its Q3-2026 revenue) and earns a 22% adjusted EBITDA margin on that net figure (about 15% on total revenue). Twilio excludes US$53.1 mn of A2P carrier fees "passed at cost" from its organic growth. The Indian members report gross: telco and Meta fees sit inside their revenue. Comparing gross margins across these bases overstates the gap. Comparing on gross profit, and on the share of gross profit that reaches EBITDA, is the only fair basis.

Three conclusions follow. On scale, the Indian members are small: Twilio's quarterly revenue (about ₹14,800 crore at ₹94.9) is nearly six times the two members' combined quarterly revenue (₹2,531 crore in Q2 FY27). On margin, Indian gross margins of 22–26% are close to the A2P-heavy global peers that report gross (LINK 24.0%, Sinch about 33.5%) and roughly half of Twilio's, which includes software. On efficiency, Tanla stands out: its EBITDA margin of 15.9% beats Sinch (12.5%), LINK (13.3%), Proximus Global (9.5%) and Infobip (8.6% in 2025), and its EBITDA ÷ gross profit ratio is the highest in the set. Route's 10.0% adjusted margin is in line with the global aggregator norm and slightly above its own parent's.

In plain terms

Compared with global messaging companies, the Indian pair are small and keep a thin slice of each rupee billed, because Indian telcos and Meta take most of it. But Tanla in particular runs very lean: it turns more of the money it does keep into profit than Twilio or the big European players. Comparisons on revenue mislead; comparisons on gross profit are fair.

Route's parent provides a useful cross-check. Proximus Global (BICS, Telesign and Route) reported Q3-2026 revenue of €369 mn (−2.0%), a direct margin of €106 mn (+0.8%) and EBITDA of €35 mn (−8.1%), and raised its FY2026 EBITDA guidance to €125–135 mn citing "stabilizing CPaaS SMS". Annualised revenue of about €1.45 billion makes the group's earlier target of €2–2.5 billion of international revenue by 2026 obsolete. Proximus Global's EBITDA fell 8% year on year while Route's adjusted EBITDA fell 11%, so Route's weakness is part of a group-wide pattern rather than unique to the Indian listed company. Sinch reports Q3-2026 results on 5-Nov-2026, which will give another read on global A2P volumes and pricing.

AI: fraud shield, conversational agent or price deflator

AI creates the most promising new revenue in the sub-sector and the most credible long-term threat to the per-message model.

Artificial intelligence affects CPaaS in two opposite ways. On the opportunity side, it makes possible products that are sold on value rather than volume: real-time scam detection for telcos and banks, conversational agents that run customer journeys on WhatsApp and RCS, and analytics that improve conversion. On the threat side, it helps enterprises reach customers through cheaper or owned channels, lets large language-model platforms connect directly to messaging apps, and accelerates the move away from SMS OTPs toward passkeys, app push and silent network verification. Which effect dominates will decide whether the sub-sector's thin take rate widens or narrows.

The fraud shield

Tanla's Wisely.ai is the most developed example in India. In its FY26 shareholder letters and annual report, Tanla describes Wisely scanning more than 500 billion messages a year (more than 1.5 billion a day) across SMS, WhatsApp and voice, protecting more than 210 million consumers and dropping scam messages in under 50 milliseconds with a false-positive rate below 0.2%. Its case study of a top-five Indian private bank reports 21 million customers protected, 91 million scam messages blocked, 3.2 million fraudulent links blocked and 1.2 million scammer numbers flagged in FY26, with 30–40% fewer smishing complaints and a claimed "₹360+ crore annual economic impact". At Indosat in Indonesia it protects more than 100 million users. These are company claims, not audited figures, but they show a product priced per protected subscriber per month rather than per message, with Platforms-type margins. TRAI's Third Amendment, which requires AI/ML spam flagging by receiving telcos (Regulation 21A), adds a regulatory reason to buy such tools.

The conversational agent

Route's AI strategy is built around bolt-ons and partnerships. In July 2026 it bought Heltar Technologies' AI customer-engagement business for ₹33.7 crore, its first "AI-led bolt-on", and in August 2026 it launched a messaging app on Salesforce's AgentExchange. Twilio's 49% gross margin and 114% dollar-based net expansion show what a software layer on top of messaging can look like; the Indian question is whether the members can lift blended gross margin above about 30% this way.

The price deflator

The threats are concrete. Global tech has already moved much of its ILD OTP traffic off SMS (Section 8). Passkeys, app push and WhatsApp authentication at ₹0.115 (₹0.094 at volume) are cheaper than a delivered SMS once DLT and compliance costs are counted. Network APIs (silent number verification, SIM-swap checks under the GSMA Open Gateway and CAMARA standards) can replace OTPs entirely; Route has a silent-verification deployment at Aakash Education, and Proximus's Telesign and Konera are in this market, so Route has some exposure to the replacement as well as to the thing being replaced. Meta's Cloud API lets large enterprises connect more directly to WhatsApp, reducing the BSP's role. And large language-model platforms with native access to messaging channels could, in time, orchestrate customer conversations without a CPaaS layer.

Cybersecurity is the other side of AI-era risk. Route's Colombian subsidiary Masivian (Masiv) suffered a cyber incident in June 2026; systems in South America were isolated, no group impact was claimed, and remediation professional fees of ₹82.6 mn were added back in H1 FY27 adjusted EBITDA (₹13.6 mn in Q1 and ₹69.0 mn in Q2, against management's July expectation of "very minor" further cost). An aggregator handling billions of OTPs and bank alerts is a high-value target, and incident costs, client trust and regulatory scrutiny will rise together.

In plain terms

AI can help messaging companies sell something more valuable than delivery, such as blocking scams for banks or running customer chats, and those products keep far more of each rupee. But AI and new login technology also let businesses send fewer paid messages. So far the Indian companies earn almost all their money the old way, per message, and the new AI products are still small.

Bull vs bear: the case for the sub-sector, and against it

The bull case rests on BFSI, WhatsApp, platforms and cash. The bear case rests on landlords, the ILD decline and governance.

The bull case

  • Demand is structural. UPI, digital lending, e-commerce and government services keep multiplying OTPs and alerts; BFSI is buying fraud protection as well as delivery, and Tanla's largest cohort grew 42.5% in Q2 FY27.
  • The channel shift is being captured. Tanla's OTT share rose from 15% to 36.2% of revenue; WhatsApp volumes doubled; Meta and Google named its subsidiaries partner of the year.
  • Regulation favours scale. 84% of spam complaints concern unregistered senders; each TRAI rule pushes traffic toward registered, telco-integrated aggregators and creates revenue for telco-side platforms.
  • A 98% margin line with options. Tanla's Platforms earned about 42% of FY26 EBITDA; the consent framework, Wisely ATP and the Third Amendment's AI-flagging rule could restart growth.
  • Cash and payouts. ₹2,803 Cr of combined cash, no bank debt, about ₹409 Cr returned in FY26, and Route's dividend guide up 50% to ₹16.5.
  • Valuation is undemanding in aggregate. 12.0× trailing earnings and 6.4× EV/EBITDA for the combined members; Route trades at 0.95× book with cash at 49% of market value.

The bear case

  • Two landlords set the rent. Telcos have asked TRAI to raise the SMS deterrent charge to ₹0.12; Jio tripled RCS marketing rates; Meta changes prices on its own timetable. A one-paisa unrecovered increase cuts aggregator gross profit by a third.
  • The richest pool is shrinking. ILD OTPs into India are in "structural, secular decline"; Meta is rebuilding the international premium (₹2.50 authentication-international) on its own rails.
  • Operating leverage is slipping. Tanla's EBITDA ÷ gross profit fell from 67% to 61% in two years; Route's from 54% to 45% in one.
  • Platforms has stalled. +6.4% in Q2 FY27, dependent on a delayed regulatory rollout and on a regulator that could reallocate the role.
  • Earnings quality at Route. ₹135.87 Cr written off on vendor advances, two auditor emphasis paragraphs, a new ₹116.8 Cr advance, 15% cash conversion in H1, three CEOs in 15 months, and 16% of revenue sold to sister companies on cost-plus terms.
  • Concentration and disintermediation. 19 customers are 46% of Tanla's revenue; passkeys, network APIs and Meta's Cloud API can each remove volume from the pipe.

Weighing the two, the sub-sector's long-run economics are better than its FY22–FY26 profit record suggests but worse than its FY27 growth rate suggests. The demand side is strong and broadening; the supply side is structurally hostile, because the landlords own the networks and the apps. The companies that create value will be those that move more of their gross profit into products the landlords buy rather than sell, and that turn the cash they generate into returns for minority shareholders. On current evidence Tanla is further along both paths, and its valuation already reflects much of that. Route's discount reflects real problems, but it also means that evidence of clean cash conversion and stable unit economics would matter a great deal to its valuation.

Three tests can settle the argument over the next four quarters. First, whether Tanla's Enterprise gross margin holds at about 20% as the WhatsApp mix grows and if a telco price reset arrives. Second, whether Tanla's Platforms line grows faster than 10% once the consent framework and the Third Amendment are implemented. Third, whether Route's cash from operations returns to at least 75% of EBITDA in H2 FY27, with the H1 vendor advance consumed rather than written off.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • A2P (application-to-person): a message sent by a business system to a person, such as an OTP or alert. P2P is person-to-person.
  • Aggregator / telemarketer (TM): a company that connects enterprises to telcos and messaging apps; "telemarketer" is TRAI's regulatory term.
  • AIT (artificially inflated traffic): fraud in which fake OTP requests generate termination revenue for the fraudster; also called SMS pumping.
  • BSP (business solution provider): a company authorised by Meta to sell WhatsApp Business messaging.
  • Billable transaction: Route's unit of volume, including SMS, OTT and voice events, domestic and international.
  • Consent framework: the planned system for recording customers' consent to receive commercial messages, linked to DLT.
  • CPaaS (communications platform as a service): cloud platforms that let businesses send messages and calls through APIs.
  • DLT (distributed ledger technology): the blockchain-based registry of senders, headers, templates and consent that every Indian commercial message is checked against.
  • DND / UCC: Do Not Disturb preferences; unsolicited commercial communication, the regulatory term for spam.
  • EBITDA ÷ gross profit: the share of gross profit left after the company's own costs; the key operating-leverage ratio.
  • Emphasis of matter: an auditor's paragraph drawing attention to a disclosure without qualifying the opinion.
  • Gross profit (GP): revenue minus cost of services (telco, Meta, Google and foreign operator fees, plus cloud).
  • Header: the registered sender ID shown on an SMS, such as a bank's six-letter code.
  • ILD A2P: international long-distance A2P traffic, a message originated abroad and terminated on an Indian number.
  • MaaP (messaging as a platform): the RCS business-messaging platform that telcos run; Tanla supplies one.
  • MPS (minimum public shareholding): SEBI's rule that at least 25% of a listed company's shares are held by the public.
  • NRR (net revenue retention): revenue from last year's customers this year ÷ their revenue last year.
  • OTP: one-time password used to authenticate a login or payment.
  • OTT channels: app-based messaging such as WhatsApp, RCS and Truecaller business messaging.
  • PE (principal entity): TRAI's term for the business sending commercial messages.
  • RCS (rich communication services): branded, interactive messages in the phone's default messaging app.
  • Template: a pre-registered message format; content outside registered templates is rejected.
  • Termination fee: what the network that delivers a message to the handset charges the sender's aggregator.
  • Traceability: TRAI's requirement that every message carry the declared chain of companies that handled it.
  • Trubloq: Tanla's DLT platform used by telcos to scrub commercial traffic.
  • Wisely.ai / Wisely ATP: Tanla's AI anti-scam platform for telcos and banks; ATP is priced per protected subscriber per month.
