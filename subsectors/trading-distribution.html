00

The one-page view: three businesses, one balance sheet

The bucket mixes a technology distributor, a pharma roll-up and a state trading house with no trade. What unites them is a balance sheet that earns a thin spread on someone else's goods.

This sub-sector is easy to misread because its three members look nothing alike. Redington is a ₹1.19 lakh crore technology distributor that moves iPhones, laptops, servers and cloud subscriptions across India, the Middle East, Turkey and Africa. Entero Healthcare is a ₹6,591 crore pharma and medical-technology distributor assembled from 51 acquisitions since 2018. MMTC is a 1963 state trading company that the Commerce Ministry told to stop doing business in April 2022 and that now holds cash, a 26% stake in India's leading gold refinery, and a pile of legacy litigation while it waits for a closure decision. They should be read as three archetypes, not three peers.

Yet a single idea runs through all of them. A distributor does not make the product it sells. It buys goods from a brand owner, holds them for a few weeks, sells them on credit to thousands of smaller customers, and collects the cash later. It is paid for three things: the capital it ties up, the credit risk it takes, and the reach it provides. That is why the scoreboard is unusual. Margins are tiny (Redington keeps about ₹2 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales; Entero about ₹4), but because the same rupee of capital goes round the conveyor belt nine or ten times a year, the return on that capital can still be 18–22%. Everything that matters in this report follows from that arithmetic: the working-capital cycle, the vendor's terms, the cost of borrowing, and the discipline to say no to customers who pay late.

What happened in the last four quarters

Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) was the best quarter in the sector's listed history, and a large part of it was price rather than volume. Redington's revenue rose 34% to ₹34,966 crore on its company basis, its EBITDA rose 67% and its profit attributable to shareholders rose 77% to ₹486 crore; India revenue grew 63%. The cause was the steepest memory-chip price rise on record: PC prices rose 25–50% over two to three quarters while PC unit growth stayed in single digits, according to Redington's CEO. Entero's revenue rose 38% to ₹1,940 crore and its EBITDA margin reached 5.0%, the full-year target, in the first quarter of the year. MMTC earned ₹104 crore of consolidated profit almost entirely from interest on deposits and its share of the gold refinery.

But the boom was paid for with the balance sheet. Redington's operating cash flow was −₹885 crore in the quarter as receivables and inventory swelled, and the company's own debt-to-equity ratio of 0.39× at June 2026 implies gross debt of roughly ₹4,200 crore, up from ₹2,637 crore in March (an indicative figure derived from the ratio; the June balance sheet is not published). Then the Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, unanimously, its first hike since February 2023. For a business that borrows to finance its customers, that turns the cost of the boom up a notch at the very moment the industry trackers (IDC, Counterpoint) warn that PC sell-in may start to fall from late 2026 and smartphone units may drop more than 15% in the second half.

In plain terms

A distributor works like a lender that also runs a warehouse. It pays the brand, holds the goods for a month, lets the shop pay in two months, and keeps a sliver of the price for its trouble. Its profit depends less on the sliver than on how fast the cash comes back and how cheaply it can fund the gap. In 2026 the goods got more expensive, so the same boxes produced more revenue and more profit, but they also tied up more cash just as money became dearer.

  • Is Entero's roll-up creating value for its own shareholders? Section 12 splits organic from acquired growth and follows the profit that leaks to minority partners.
  • What is MMTC actually worth? Section 13 builds an illustrative sum of the parts: cash, the MMTC-PAMP stake, liabilities, an audit qualification and a closure that has been "awaited" for years.

01

How a distributor earns: renting out a balance sheet

The vendor captures the product margin. The distributor is paid for capital, credit risk and reach, and the way each value chain is built decides how much of each it supplies.

Every distribution business answers the same question for its suppliers: how do you get a product from one factory or one importer to tens of thousands of points of sale, each of which buys in small quantities, wants it tomorrow and expects to pay later? A brand like HP or Sun Pharma could build that network itself, but it would have to finance every shop's credit, run hundreds of warehouses and chase thousands of small receivables. The distributor does it for many brands at once, which spreads the fixed cost of warehouses, delivery fleets, credit teams and IT systems across a far larger volume. That shared cost base, plus the willingness to carry inventory and receivables, is the product a distributor sells.

Redington runs 168 warehouses, 71 sales offices, ~450 brands and ~5,300 staff in 40 markets (Q1 FY27 deck). The money it earns is the spread between what it pays in Step 1 and charges in Step 3, plus vendor rebates.

A technology distributor is paid in four ways. The first is the front margin, the gap between the vendor's price to the distributor and the distributor's invoice to the reseller. The second is back-end income: rebates for hitting sell-in targets, price protection when a vendor cuts list prices on stock already in the warehouse, and marketing funds. The third is interest charged to channel partners who pay late, which Redington books in other income. The fourth is service fees, mostly logistics: its ProConnect arm earned over ₹1,000 crore of revenue in FY26 at a 9–11% EBITDA margin, with 28% of it captive (the logistics report covers third-party logistics economics). Against these sit warehousing, freight, staff, IT, credit losses, inventory write-downs and, above all, interest.

Trade convention on branded generics is roughly 20% to the retailer on PTR and 10% to the stockist on PTS (an industry convention, not a regulation). Entero's 10–11% gross margin sits on top of that convention, lifted by scale purchase discounts and higher-margin medtech.

Pharma distribution has the same skeleton but a heavier body. A chemist stocks thousands of products and orders small quantities daily, so the distributor has to carry far more items (Entero lists 83,400+ SKUs), deliver more often, manage expiry dates and run cold chains for products such as insulin and the new GLP-1 obesity drugs. Suppliers give less credit than technology vendors do, and chemists take longer to pay. The result is a business that needs roughly double the technology distributor's margin to earn a similar return, as section 09 shows.

MMTC's chain is included because it shows what happens when a distributor's only advantage is a licence. MMTC never had to win customers on service or speed; government policy delivered them. When the licence went, so did the business. Its peak turnover was about ₹30,000 crore in FY21 on screener figures; FY26 turnover was ₹2 crore. What remains is a balance sheet, which is why the deep dive in section 13 values it as one.

In plain terms

Strip away the products and all three members sell the same thing: the use of a balance sheet. Redington lends its balance sheet to technology brands and their resellers, Entero to drug makers and chemists. MMTC once lent the government's licence; now it simply holds the government's cash. The products change the margin and the speed; the job does not change.

Where the value is captured

In technology distribution the brand captures the product margin and the distributor earns a utility-like return for capital and reach. Redington's FY26 gross profit was ₹6,042 crore on company revenue of ₹1,19,347 crore; operating costs absorbed roughly ₹3,630 crore and company EBITDA was ₹2,414 crore. Where Redington captures more than a utility return is in places where the brand cannot easily go itself: the long tail of small resellers in India's tier-2 towns, Gulf and African markets where global distributors are thin, and software and cloud, where it bundles subscriptions for thousands of small resellers who cannot deal with AWS or Microsoft directly. About 74% of its software revenue is recurring, according to the FY26 annual report.

In pharma, value is shifting from pure fulfilment toward whoever controls demand. Entero's management describes its medtech business as "demand generation" rather than fulfilment, which is why it earns a higher margin. Cold-chain GLP-1 drugs (₹10–12 crore a month) and private label play the same role. The traditional stockist's 8–10% trade margin, by contrast, is under policy pressure, as section 04 explains.

03

Forces I: conveyor-belt economics

Four structural forces set the returns of every distributor in every year: the thinness of the margin, the length of the cash cycle, the power of the vendor and the price of money.

Sections 03 and 04 set out the eight forces that explain this sub-sector. The first four, here, are permanent features of the business model. The next four, in section 04, are the shocks that made 2026 unusual. A ninth force, the slow wind-down of state trading at MMTC, is specific to one company and gets its own deep dive in section 13.

Force 1 · Thin margins, high velocity

Distribution margins look alarming to anyone used to manufacturing. Redington's gross margin in FY26 was 5.06% and its operating margin 1.97%; in Q1 FY27 the gross margin slipped to 4.90%. Entero's gross margin was 10.3% (11.4% in Q1 FY27) and its operating margin 4.0% (5.0%). These numbers are normal for the trade. Ingram Micro, the largest US technology distributor, earned a 6.67% gross margin and a 1.67% GAAP operating margin in 2025; McKesson, the largest US drug wholesaler, earned 3.61% and 1.54% in the year to March 2026.

What makes the margins workable is velocity. Redington's year-end capital employed (equity of ₹10,160 crore plus borrowings of ₹2,842 crore, including leases) was about ₹13,000 crore against ₹1,19,162 crore of revenue: the capital turned about 9.2 times in the year. An EBIT margin of about 1.8% multiplied by 9.2 turns gives a return on capital of roughly 17% on year-end balances; screener reports 18.4% and the company 19.7% on average capital excluding the Arena impairment. The lesson is that margin percentage is the wrong scoreboard. In Q1 FY27 Redington's gross margin fell 23 basis points, yet its gross profit rose 28% and EBITDA 67%. Rupees of gross profit, cost-to-serve and working-capital days are what count.

Force 2 · Working capital is the whole game

If margin is the sliver, working capital is the bill for earning it. A distributor's net working capital is its inventory plus what customers owe it, minus what it owes suppliers. Measured in days of sales, Redington's was 30 days in FY26 and 32 days in Q1 FY27 on the company's own basis (inventory 31 days, receivables 59, payables 58), down from 37 a year earlier. Entero's net working capital was 59 days at March 2026 and 61 days at June 2026 (66 a year earlier). Screener's year-end cash-conversion cycle is longer for both: 34 days for Redington and 74 for Entero.

Each day matters because of scale. At Redington's FY26 revenue, one day of working capital is about ₹326 crore of capital. At a funding cost of around 9%, every extra day costs about ₹29 crore a year before tax, roughly 1.2% of EBITDA. CRISIL, which rates Redington AA+, expects its net working capital to drift to 36–40 days as large enterprise orders grow; if it did, Redington would need ₹1,300–2,600 crore of extra capital at FY26 revenue. That is the hidden cost of the 2026 boom.

The US comparison is instructive. In plain terms

Every day a distributor waits for its customers to pay is a day it has to borrow to pay its suppliers. At Redington's size, one extra day of waiting costs about ₹326 crore of borrowing. A distributor that keeps its days short can grow without asking its bankers for much more; one that lets them slip can grow its revenue and shrink its cash at the same time.

Force 3 · The vendor holds the cards

A distributor is only as strong as its brand line-up, and the brands know it. Apple alone was 31% of Redington's FY26 revenue (30% in FY25); in Q1 FY27 it was 31%, down from 34% a year earlier as other brands grew faster. The top five vendors were 60% of revenue in FY26 and the top ten 75% in Q1 FY27. Vendors set the distributor's buying price, the rebate schedule, the credit period, the price-protection rules when list prices fall, and, crucially, how many distributors they appoint. A vendor that adds a rival distributor, sells direct to large retailers or opens its own stores can cut a distributor's share overnight.

For a distributor the level of prices sets revenue; the change in prices sets the risk. When the increases stop, the channel's pre-bought stock stops gaining value and buyers wait.

Memory is 30–40% of the bill of materials of a 2026 phone and a large share of a laptop's, so device prices followed. IDC had warned in March that PC prices, already up more than 10%, "could rise another 15–20%"; the CEO's figure is above that path. Rashi Peripherals, the other listed Indian technology distributor, grew revenue 62% in the same quarter, confirming the effect was industry-wide.

For a distributor, price inflation is double-edged. It lifts revenue and gross-profit rupees on the same number of boxes, and channel partners buy ahead of the next price rise, which pulls demand forward. That is why Redington's Q1 FY27 profit rose so fast. But the same inflation raises the rupee value of every unit of inventory and every receivable, so working capital swells. And pre-buying borrows from future quarters: IDC warned on 1 September that "sell-in of PCs might start dwindling from Q4 2026 onwards" and that 2027 would be "more challenging". Redington's CEO, for his part, expects prices to "stabilize at these prices" and said Apple was the last vendor to raise them. Section 11 works through the arithmetic. The consumer electronics report covers the same shock from the manufacturer's side, and the computer hardware report from the brand's.

In plain terms

If a laptop that cost ₹50,000 last year costs ₹65,000 now, a distributor that sells the same number of laptops reports 30% more revenue without doing anything differently. It also needs 30% more cash to hold the same stock and wait for the same customers to pay. When prices stop rising, the extra revenue stops too, but the cash tied up in pre-bought stock does not come back until it is sold.

In plain terms

Half of Redington's business is outside India, and that half had a bad year for reasons unrelated to how well it is run: a war closed the main shipping lane, and Turkey's inflation made a subsidiary loss-making. Because Redington owns only about half of the Turkish business, it bears only about half of those losses, which is why its own shareholders' profit came out higher than the group total.

Force 8 · Pharma consolidation meets trade-margin caps

Indian pharma distribution is one of the most fragmented trades in the economy. The trade's own estimates put the number of stockists at about 65,000, and the chemists' association AIOCD claims about 12.4 lakh members (chemists and distributors). National platforms hold less than 10% of the market. Entero describes the situation as "many sellers, but … buyers basically limited to 1 or 2 organized players like us". That fragmentation is the runway for consolidation: a distributor with better IT, purchasing scale and access to capital can buy family-run stockists, plug them into a common platform and lift their margins. It is also the reason margins are higher than in the US, where three wholesalers dominate.

Two forces push the other way. The first is vertical integration: the Apollo promoter family's wholesale distributor Keimed is being folded into Apollo HealthCo (with ₹2,475 crore from the private-equity firm Advent), which targets about ₹25,000 crore of revenue at roughly 7% EBITDA; the National Company Law Tribunal approved the scheme on 5 May 2026 and a listing is targeted for Q4 FY27. Reliance (through Netmeds), MedPlus (which self-distributes to its own stores) and PharmEasy's business-to-business arm also compete. The second is price regulation.

On 17 October 2026 the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) gazetted a trade-margin rationalisation for 137 non-scheduled anti-cancer drugs: the total trade margin, from the price to the stockist up to MRP, is capped at 30% of MRP from 9 November 2026. MRPs are expected to fall by up to 70%, and the government estimates patient savings of about ₹2,500 crore a year. A 2019 pilot did the same for 42 cancer drugs and cut the MRPs of 526 brands by up to 90%. Press reports say the trade has also discussed a 30% cap for all non-price-controlled medicines; that wider cap is a press report only, not a notified policy.

Who holds the market: an oligopoly, a fragmented trade and a defunct monopoly

Technology distribution is a scale oligopoly in each country. Pharma distribution is a long tail. State trading has gone from monopoly to nothing.

Market structure explains why the members earn what they earn. In technology distribution, scale is self-reinforcing: vendors prefer a few distributors with national coverage, strong credit ratings and good systems, and resellers prefer a distributor that stocks every brand. The result in each country is two or three large broadliners, a handful of specialists and a long tail. Redington says it is number one in India and in the Middle East and Africa (Omdia data) and among the top ten worldwide, despite having no presence in the US, Europe or China.

Technology distribution: compliance as a moat

Most IT hardware, phones and software carry 18% GST, and input credit flows through the distributor. Since August 2023 e-invoicing has been mandatory for businesses above ₹5 crore of turnover, alongside e-way bills for goods in transit. These rules help large, organised distributors, because they make it harder for grey-market traders to undercut prices by avoiding tax. The GST 2.0 rate rationalisation that took effect on 22 September 2025 cut rates on televisions and air-conditioners to 18% but left PCs and phones where they were. Customs duties on phones and components, and the "import management system" authorisations that replaced the August 2023 laptop licensing notice, push brands toward local assembly, which changes where a distributor picks up stock but not what it does.

Accounting rules matter as much as tax rules. Ind AS 115 decides whether a distributor is a principal (books full revenue) or an agent (books only its margin); much cloud and software is agent business. Ind AS 29 requires hyperinflation accounting for Turkish subsidiaries. Outside India, the UAE introduced a 9% corporate tax in 2024 and Saudi Arabia charges 15% VAT. US trade policy is a background factor: the IEEPA tariffs were struck down on 20 February 2026, a temporary 10% Section 122 tariff ran until 24 July 2026, and since then a 10% Section 301 tariff applies on top of most-favoured-nation duties. That affects India's phone exports more than domestic distribution.

Pharma distribution: licensing, price control and the trade-margin cap

A pharma distributor needs a wholesale drug licence for each premises (Forms 20B and 21B), a registered pharmacist or competent person, storage and cold-chain compliance, and adherence to Good Distribution Practices. Prices are regulated under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order 2013, administered by the NPPA. Medicines on the National List of Essential Medicines (roughly 18% of the market) carry ceiling prices revised annually with wholesale inflation; the ceiling price embeds a 16% retailer margin, and wholesalers customarily receive about 8% on these products. Non-scheduled medicines are free to set prices, subject to a 10% annual increase limit, and that is where trade margins can be large.

Paragraph 19 of the DPCO lets the NPPA act "in public interest" on non-scheduled drugs, and it is the legal basis for trade-margin rationalisation. After the 2019 pilot on 42 cancer drugs, the October 2026 order extends the 30%-of-MRP cap to 137 cancer drugs, with manufacturers required to file revised prices within seven days. A Parliamentary Standing Committee noted in August 2026 that there is no timeframe for a permanent trade-margin framework in the DPCO. Draft e-pharmacy rules from 2018 remain un-notified; AIOCD held a nationwide strike against e-pharmacies on 20 May 2026.

Booms, busts and an ending: six decades of the trade

Technology distribution earns the most when prices rise and channels pre-buy, and the least when stock loses value and receivables stretch. Pharma is steadier but policy-exposed. State trading had one cycle, and it ended.

The cycle in this sub-sector is a working-capital cycle. When vendor prices rise and resellers stock up ahead of further increases, a technology distributor's revenue and profit jump, collections are good and inventory gains value. When prices stall or fall, resellers stop ordering, inventory loses value, vendors negotiate price protection and receivables stretch as weak resellers struggle. The COVID PC boom of FY21–FY22 and its aftermath are the template.

The cash-conversion machine: anatomy of distributor ROCE Deep dive

Why a 2% margin can earn 20% on capital, worked from Redington's and Entero's own numbers, and what the same arithmetic says about the cost of the 2026 boom.

The single most useful equation in this sub-sector is also the simplest. Return on capital employed is the operating margin multiplied by how many times the capital turns over in a year. A distributor cannot do much about the first term: vendors and competitors set it. It can do a great deal about the second, because the capital it employs is mostly working capital, and working capital is a set of choices about how much stock to hold, how long to let customers take to pay, and how long to take to pay suppliers.

Redington, FY26: 1.8% × 9.2 turns ≈ 17%

Start with the reported numbers. Redington's FY26 revenue from operations was ₹1,19,162 crore. Its profit before tax was ₹1,697 crore and its interest bill ₹477 crore, so earnings before interest and tax (EBIT) were about ₹2,174 crore, an EBIT margin of 1.82%. At 31 March 2026 its shareholders' equity was ₹10,160 crore and its borrowings ₹2,842 crore on screener's basis (₹2,637 crore excluding ₹204 crore of lease liabilities), so capital employed was about ₹13,000 crore. Revenue divided by capital is 9.2: each rupee of capital was used 9.2 times in the year. Multiply 1.82% by 9.2 and the return on year-end capital is about 16.7%. Screener's 18.4% uses average capital; the company's 19.7% uses average capital and adds back the ₹152 crore Arena impairment. In Q1 FY27 the company reported 22.2%, and its India business 28.3%.

Notice what the arithmetic does not need. It does not need a high margin. Redington's EBIT margin of about 1.8% is close to Ingram Micro's 1.7% GAAP operating margin, and its return is respectable because its capital base is small relative to its sales. That small base comes from three facts: inventory that sells in about a month, a relatively short collection period for the region, and vendors (Apple especially) that provide generous credit.

Entero, FY26: 3.7% × 2.8 turns ≈ 10%, or 15% on tangible capital

Entero's FY26 revenue was ₹6,591 crore. Profit before tax and exceptional items was about ₹187.5 crore and finance cost ₹54.4 crore, so EBIT was about ₹242 crore, a 3.67% margin, twice Redington's. But its capital employed at March 2026 was about ₹2,366 crore (equity ₹1,689 crore plus borrowings including leases ₹677 crore), so revenue turned the capital only 2.8 times. 3.67% × 2.8 is a return of about 10%, close to screener's 10.5%. Two features explain the slow turns. First, pharma working capital is long: screener puts the year-end cycle at 74 days, the company at 59. Second, ₹749 crore of the capital is goodwill from 51 acquisitions, which earns nothing directly and never turns. Strip the goodwill out and tangible capital is about ₹1,617 crore, turning 4.1 times, for a return of about 15%. That is the basis Entero itself uses: its published ROCE of 14.60% for FY26 is EBIT over average tangible capital employed (equity minus intangibles including goodwill, plus borrowings and deferred tax). Its Q1 FY27 figure of 21.1% is on the same tangible basis.

In plain terms

Redington earns less on each sale but uses each rupee of capital about nine times a year. Entero earns twice as much per sale but uses each rupee about three times, partly because medicines sit longer on shelves and chemists pay later, and partly because it paid ₹749 crore more than book value for the businesses it bought. Ask any distributor two questions: how much it keeps per ₹100 of sales, and how many times a year its capital goes round.

Growth eats cash: the arithmetic of the 2026 boom

A distributor's working capital grows with its revenue. If revenue rises by ₹100 and the business carries 30 days of working capital, it needs about ₹8.2 of extra capital. Redington's FY26 revenue grew by ₹19,828 crore; at 30 days that required roughly ₹1,630 crore of extra working capital, an ILLUSTRATIVE estimate that matches the cash-flow statement, where receivables absorbed ₹4,478 crore, inventory ₹1,884 crore and higher payables released ₹4,625 crore. Who pays for the days: vendors, banks or shareholders

Working capital has to be funded by someone. The cheapest funder is the vendor, through payable days; Redington's 58 days of supplier credit finance most of its 59 days of receivables. Next come banks and the money market, through bank lines, commercial paper and factoring. Redington's net debt excluding leases rose from ₹1,238 crore at March 2025 to ₹1,516 crore at March 2026 (cash of ₹1,121.5 crore, mostly held by overseas subsidiaries and, as CRISIL notes, "not normally available to the Indian parent", against borrowings of ₹2,637 crore). Its June 2026 gross debt of roughly ₹4,200 crore is an INDICATIVE figure implied by the company's 0.39× debt-to-equity ratio. Shareholders fund the rest through retained profit; Redington paid ₹6 a share of dividend for FY26, about ₹469 crore, in Q2 FY27, so retained earnings cover only part of the growth.

Entero's funding mix is different. It raised about ₹1,600 crore at its IPO, used much of it to cut debt and fund acquisitions, and then drew about ₹200 crore of new term debt in early 2026 for its largest medtech deal. At March 2026 its borrowings excluding leases were ₹566.7 crore against cash, bank balances and liquid mutual funds of ₹291.7 crore: net debt of about ₹275 crore. Its finance cost rose 93% year on year in Q1 FY27. With the RBI now tightening and an A- rating, every extra day of working capital costs Entero more than it costs Redington.

In plain terms

When a distributor grows fast, it has to buy more stock and wait for more customers to pay before it sees any cash. So in a boom its profit rises and its bank balance falls. That is normal, as long as the cash comes back when the boom ends. In FY23 it came back slowly, and Redington's return on capital fell for three years. The question for FY27 is whether this boom ends the same way.

Four rules the arithmetic implies

  • Judge margins in rupees, not percentages. A falling gross-margin percentage with rising gross-profit rupees and stable days is usually fine; a stable percentage with lengthening days is not.
  • Watch payable days as closely as receivable days. Net days can improve because suppliers are temporarily generous, as at Redington in Q1 FY27; that is fragile.
  • For roll-ups, separate tangible and total capital. Entero's 14.6% tangible ROCE and 10.5% total ROCE are both true; the first tells you how well the operations run, the second how well the acquisitions were priced.
  • Measure cash conversion over three years, not one. Single-year operating cash flow swings with the cycle; a three-year average below 30% of EBITDA is a warning, and Redington's FY24–FY26 average is about 25%.

What it is. What the distributor keeps after paying the vendor. Formula. (Revenue − cost of goods) ÷ revenue; and, separately, gross profit in ₹. Worked example. Sell ₹100 crore of laptops bought for ₹95.1 crore: gross margin 4.9%, gross profit ₹4.9 crore. If prices rise 30% and the distributor keeps the same percentage, it sells the same laptops for ₹130 crore and earns ₹6.4 crore. What drives it. Vendor terms and rebates; product mix (phones and large data-centre deals carry lower margins, software and medtech higher); price protection; competition; and in pharma, purchasing scale and trade-margin regulation. Benchmark. Technology: above 6% good, 4.5–6% average, below 4.5% weak. Pharma: above 11% good, 9–11% average, below 9% weak.

How the members compare. Redington's gross margin fell from 5.47% (FY25) to 5.06% (FY26) and 4.90% (Q1 FY27), yet gross profit rose 28% in Q1. India runs lower (4.47% in FY26) than the rest of the world excluding Arena (5.77%), because India is mobility-heavy. Entero's rose every year: 9.0% (FY24), 9.5%, 10.3% (FY26), 11.4% (Q1 FY27), driven by scale purchasing, medtech and exits from low-margin accounts. Definition trap. A falling percentage is not necessarily bad: if it falls because large, low-margin, fast-paying deals were added, rupees and ROCE may both rise. Redington's CFO said exactly that of its ~₹1,000 crore of data-centre deals in Q1: "dilution in margin %, but no compromise on return on capital employed".

KPI 3 · Cost-to-serve: opex as a share of gross profit

What it is. How much of the gross margin the operation consumes. Formula. (Gross profit − EBITDA) ÷ gross profit. Worked example. Gross profit ₹50 crore, EBITDA ₹20 crore: opex is ₹30 crore, 60% of gross profit. Add ₹10 crore of gross profit with only ₹2 crore more opex and the ratio drops to 53%. What drives it. Operating leverage (warehouses and head office do not grow with price inflation), automation (Redington runs automated distribution centres in Chennai, Kolkata and Dubai), wage inflation (35–40% in Turkey) and credit losses. Benchmark. Below 50% good, 50–65% average, above 65% weak.

How the members compare. Both members ran at about 60% in FY26 and improved sharply in Q1 FY27: Redington from 66% a year earlier to 56%, Entero from 64% to 56%. That improvement is the main reason profit grew faster than revenue. Definition trap. Opex is rarely disclosed as one line; derive it as gross profit minus EBITDA, on the same basis. If gross profit is on the company basis and EBITDA on screener's, the ratio is distorted by other income.

KPI 4 · EBITDA and operating margin

What it is. Profit before depreciation, interest and tax. Formula. Screener's operating profit (OP) = revenue − all operating costs, excluding other income. Redington's company "EBITDA" = profit before tax and exceptionals + finance cost + depreciation, which includes other income. Worked example. FY26: screener OP ₹2,348 crore (1.97%); company EBITDA ₹2,414 crore (2.02%); the gap is other income. What drives it. Gross margin minus cost-to-serve. Benchmark. Technology: above 2.5% good, 1.8–2.5% average, below 1.8% weak. Pharma: above 5% good, 3–5% average, below 3% weak.

How the members compare. Redington's screener operating margin has drifted from 2.9% (FY22) to 2.0% (FY26) as mobility and large deals grew; company EBITDA margin was 2.15% in Q1 FY27. CRISIL expects it to stabilise at 2.0–2.2%. Entero's has risen in every quarter since mid-2024, from 2.8% to 5.0%, reaching management's FY27 guidance in Q1; management's medium-term aspiration is "more than 6%". MMTC's is negative (−₹181 crore in FY26). Definition trap. Rising credit-loss provisions sit inside "other expenses" and depress EBITDA without being named; Redington's receivable allowances rose 3.8× to ₹224 crore in FY26, which explains part of the margin slide management attributed to mix.

KPI 5 · Inventory days

What it is. How long stock sits before it is sold. Formula. Inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365 (or × 91 for a quarter). Worked example. ₹8,171 crore of inventory (Redington, March 2026) against about ₹1,13,000 crore of annual cost of goods is about 26 days. What drives it. Product breadth (83,400 SKUs at Entero against far fewer at a technology distributor), vendor minimum-order rules, pre-buying ahead of price increases, and the share of build-to-order enterprise deals (which carry little stock). Benchmark. Technology: below 25 days good, 25–35 average, above 35 weak. Pharma runs 40–52 days because of breadth and expiry management.

How the members compare. Redington: 26 days at year-end FY26 (screener), 31 days in Q1 FY27 on the company basis, up from 25 a year earlier. Entero: 52 days (screener year-end), about 40 on the company's basis. Definition trap. Year-end inventory understates the average in a business that stocks up through the year; and in an inflationary period, rising inventory days may reflect deliberate pre-buying that earns a gain. The question is what happens when prices stop rising: the inventory then has to be sold at the price the vendor sets, with price protection only if the vendor offers it.

Pharma inventory carries a risk technology inventory does not: expiry. A distributor holding 83,400 products will always have some slow movers approaching their expiry date, and the trade's return-and-credit arrangements with manufacturers decide who bears the loss. Breadth is also the service a chemist pays for, so a pharma distributor cannot simply cut its range to cut its days. The lever is instead better demand forecasting and more frequent replenishment from manufacturers' depots, which is what a common IT platform across acquired businesses is meant to deliver. Watch inventory days at Entero alongside its SKU count: if days fall while the range keeps widening (74,700 SKUs a year ago, 83,400 now), the platform is working.

KPI 6 · Receivable days and the cost of credit

What it is. How long customers take to pay. Formula. Trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. Redington's receivables of ₹21,569 crore at March 2026 against ₹1,19,162 crore of revenue: 66 days on screener's basis; the company's average-basis figure is 59. What drives it. Customer mix (enterprises, governments and hospitals pay slowly; retail chains faster), competition for share, use of factoring (sold receivables leave the balance sheet), and regional payment habits. Benchmark. Technology: below 50 days good, 50–65 average, above 65 weak. Credit cost: provisions below 0.15% of sales good, 0.15–0.3% average, above 0.3% weak.

How the members compare. Redington's provision for doubtful receivables was 0.39% of sales in Q1 FY27 against 0.15% a year earlier, reflecting caution in Saudi Arabia and the Gulf; for FY26 as a whole the allowance was ₹224 crore, 0.19% of revenue and 3.8 times FY25's ₹59 crore, much of it at Arena. Entero's expected-credit-loss provision and claims-receivable provision together were about ₹15 crore in FY26 (about 0.23% of revenue), rising with hospital and government medtech exposure. Definition trap. Factoring flatters receivable days: Redington cut its factoring cost to about ₹8 crore in Q1 FY27 from about ₹31 crore, so more receivables stayed on the balance sheet. Like-for-like comparisons need the factored amount, which is rarely disclosed.

KPI 7 · Payable days

What it is. How long the distributor takes to pay its suppliers. Formula. Trade payables ÷ cost of goods sold × 365. Worked example. Redington's ₹18,039 crore of trade payables at March 2026 against about ₹1,13,000 crore of annual cost of goods is about 58 days. What drives it. Vendor policy above all; also the distributor's credit standing, use of supplier finance programmes, and timing of large purchases. Benchmark. More is better for the distributor, up to the point where vendors push back. US broadliners stretch to 89–111 days; Redington's 58 is mid-range; Entero's 45 (screener) or about 26 (broker basis) is low, which is the core of pharma's capital burden.

How the members compare. Redington's payable days rose from 47 to 58 between Q1 FY26 and Q1 FY27, mainly on extra vendor credit in the Middle East. Definition trap. Payable days that rise in a boom are not proof of bargaining power; they may be a temporary vendor concession to move stock. If vendors tighten terms when demand cools, net working capital rises just as revenue falls, the worst combination.

KPI 8 · Net working-capital days and the cash-conversion cycle

What it is. The net number of days of sales tied up in the business. Formula. Cash-conversion cycle = receivable days + inventory days − payable days. Company "net working capital days" usually = (receivables + inventory − payables) ÷ revenue × 365, sometimes on average balances. Worked example. Redington Q1 FY27: 59 + 31 − 58 = 32 days. At FY26 revenue, each day is ₹326 crore of capital. What drives it. The three components above. Benchmark. Technology: below 30 good, 30–40 average, above 40 weak. Pharma: below 55 good, 55–75 average, above 75 weak.

How the members compare. Redington: 37 days in Q1 FY26, 30 at Q4 FY26 (India 24, rest of world 40), 32 in Q1 FY27 (India 31, rest of world 32). CRISIL guides 36–40 for FY26–27 on its own basis (FY26: 34). Entero: 66 (Q1 FY26), 59 (Q4 FY26), 61 (Q1 FY27); screener's year-end cycle is 74. Definition trap. Three sources give three numbers for the same company: screener (year-end balances), the company (average or quarter-end balances, sometimes on revenue including GST) and the rating agency. Use one source consistently and say which.

KPI 9 · ROCE: three bases for one ratio

What it is. Operating return on the capital the business uses. Formula. EBIT ÷ capital employed (equity + debt, sometimes net of cash, sometimes excluding goodwill). Worked example. Redington FY26: EBIT ₹2,174 crore ÷ year-end capital ₹13,000 crore = 16.7%; screener (average capital) 18.4%; company (average, ex-impairment) 19.7%. What drives it. Margin × turns (section 09). Benchmark. Above 20% good, 14–20% average, below 14% weak.

How the members compare. On screener's basis: Redington 18.4%, Entero 10.5%, MMTC 8.7% (on other income). On company bases: Redington 22.2% (Q1 FY27; India 28.3%; excluding Arena 25.4%), Entero 14.6% FY26 and 21.1% Q1 FY27 on tangible capital. Entero targets 25–30% in three to four years. Definition trap. Entero's ROCE excludes ₹749 crore of goodwill from capital; Redington's does not need to (its goodwill and intangibles are only ₹130 crore). Comparing Entero's 21.1% with Redington's 22.2% therefore flatters Entero. On total capital, the gap is wide.

KPI 10 · ROE and the two profits: reported versus attributable

What it is. Return on shareholders' equity, which requires knowing whose profit it is. Formula. Profit attributable to owners ÷ average equity attributable to owners. Worked example. Redington FY26: consolidated PAT including minority interest was ₹1,284 crore; profit attributable to owners was ₹1,490.22 crore; the company's "PAT" of ₹1,565 crore adds back its ₹75.24 crore share of the Arena impairment. Attributable profit is higher than the consolidated total because Arena's minority shareholders (50.6%) absorbed ₹206 crore of Arena's loss. Entero is the reverse: PAT ₹145.84 crore, attributable ₹115.04 crore, because the sellers of acquired businesses still own 20–49% of them (minority interest 21% of PAT in FY26, 27% in Q1 FY27). Benchmark. ROE above 18% good, 12–18% average, below 12% weak; minority share of PAT below 10% good, 10–25% average, above 25% weak.

How the members compare. Redington's ROE is about 17% (screener 16.9%; company 16.8% FY26, 18.8% Q1 FY27). Entero's screener ROE is 6.9%, depressed by IPO equity and goodwill; on the company's tangible basis it was 12.5% (FY26) and 20.4% (Q1 FY27). MMTC has no minority interests, so its reported and attributable profits are the same (₹387 crore, FY26). Definition trap. Screener's "net profit" includes minority interest, but its EPS and P/E use attributable profit. A P/E computed as market cap ÷ screener net profit is therefore on a different basis from screener's own P/E. For Entero the gap is large: 49.5× on attributable earnings, about 44× on total PAT.

KPI 11 · Cash conversion: CFO ÷ EBITDA and free cash flow

What it is. How much of operating profit turns into cash. Formula. Cash flow from operations (after working-capital changes and tax) ÷ EBITDA; free cash flow = CFO − capex (Redington's company definition also deducts interest). Worked example. Redington FY26: CFO ₹231 crore ÷ OP ₹2,348 crore = 10%; FY24–FY26 combined about 25%. Entero FY26: ₹96.2 crore ÷ ₹266 crore = 36%; guidance for FY27 is at least 50%. What drives it. Revenue growth × working-capital days (section 09), tax payments and, for roll-ups, the working capital of acquired businesses. Benchmark. Three-year average above 60% good, 30–60% average, below 30% weak.

How the members compare. Redington is weak on this measure through a growth phase; Entero is average and improving. MMTC's FY26 CFO was −₹605 crore because ₹1,000 crore of its court deposit was released to Anglo Coal. Definition trap. Broker notes sometimes misreport CFO: one widely circulated figure for Entero's FY26 CFO (₹379 crore) is wrong; the audited figure is ₹96.2 crore. Always check the cash-flow statement in the annual report.

Free cash flow has its own definitional variety. Screener's free cash flow is CFO minus capital expenditure: ₹125 crore for Redington in FY26 and ₹29 crore for Entero. Redington's own definition also deducts interest paid, which gave −₹199 crore for FY26 and −₹995 crore for Q1 FY27. For a roll-up, cash paid for acquisitions (₹342 crore at Entero in FY26) and future payments to buy out minority partners are further claims on cash that neither definition captures. The useful habit is to build a simple bridge each year: CFO, minus capex, minus interest, minus acquisitions and buy-outs, minus dividends, and see whether debt had to fill the gap.

KPI 12 · Interest cover and funding cost

What it is. How comfortably operating profit covers interest. Formula. EBIT ÷ finance cost. Worked example. Redington FY26: EBIT about ₹2,174 crore ÷ screener interest ₹477 crore = 4.6×; on company EBITDA over statutory finance cost (₹2,414 crore ÷ ₹358.66 crore) it is 6.7×. CRISIL puts it at about 4.5×. Entero: EBIT about ₹242 crore ÷ ₹54.4 crore = 4.4×. What drives it. Debt levels (which rise with working capital), interest rates (repo 5.50% since 7 October 2026), credit ratings (Redington AA+, Entero A-) and the use of cheaper funding such as commercial paper or vendor credit. Benchmark. Above 6× good, 3–6× average, below 3× weak.

How the members compare. Both are average. Redington's Q1 FY27 interest of ₹89 crore is what management calls the steady-state run-rate; Entero's rose 93% year on year. MMTC has almost no debt. Definition trap. Lease interest (Ind AS 116) and factoring discount can sit in different lines; screener's interest includes leases, the company's finance cost may not include factoring. Compare like with like.

A simple sensitivity shows why the October hike matters even though it was only 25 basis points. If Redington carries an average of about ₹4,000 crore of gross debt through FY27 (an ILLUSTRATIVE figure between its March and June balances), a 0.25-point rise in its average borrowing cost adds about ₹10 crore of interest a year, under 1% of attributable profit. But a five-day rise in working capital at FY27-type revenue adds roughly ₹1,700 crore of funding need, and at 8–9% that is ₹140–150 crore of interest: fifteen times the effect of the rate hike. For distributors the volume of borrowing matters far more than its price, which is why management teams speak about days rather than rates.

KPI 13 · Vendor concentration

What it is. Dependence on the largest suppliers. Formula. Revenue from the top vendor (or top five) ÷ total revenue. Worked example. Redington FY26: Apple 31%; top five (Apple, Lenovo, HP, Dell EMC, Samsung) 60%; top ten 75% (Q1 FY27). What drives it. Category strategy, vendor appointments, and vendor channel policy. Benchmark. Top vendor below 20% good, 20–30% average, above 30% weak; but a large share of a growing, premium vendor is less risky than a small share of a declining one. How the members compare. Redington is concentrated; Entero (3,000+ manufacturers) is not; MMTC has no vendors. Definition trap. Vendor shares are disclosed on revenue, not on gross profit or capital; Apple's low-margin, fast-turning business may be a smaller share of profit than of revenue. Redington does not disclose vendor-level margins.

The Apple relationship illustrates both sides of concentration. Apple's India value share of smartphones was 27% in Q2 CY26 on an 8.5% unit share, so a distributor carrying it moves a large rupee volume per unit handled, and premium buyers have held up better than the mass market as memory costs pushed prices up. But Apple controls the channel tightly, runs its own stores and online store in India, and could add or change distributors at will. In Q1 FY27 Apple's share of Redington fell from 34% to 31% as other brands grew faster, and the CEO's comment that Apple was the last vendor to raise prices suggests the premium segment's price tailwind is now in the base. A falling Apple share driven by faster growth elsewhere is healthy; one driven by a change in Apple's channel policy would not be.

KPI 14 · Organic versus inorganic growth

What it is. Growth from the existing business versus growth bought through acquisitions. Formula. Organic growth = growth of businesses owned in both periods; inorganic = contribution of acquisitions (including "calendarisation", the full-year effect of a deal made part-way through last year). Worked example. Entero Q1 FY27: reported growth 38.2% = 17.8% organic + 20.4% inorganic, all of the latter calendarisation because there were no new deals in the quarter. FY26: 29.3% = 13.4% organic (15.6% like-for-like) + 16.0% inorganic (5.3 calendarisation + 10.7 new deals). What drives it. Market growth plus share gains; deal flow. Benchmark. Organic growth above 1.5× the market good, 1.0–1.5× average, below 1.0× weak.

How the members compare. Entero's organic growth was 1.3× the market in Q1 FY27 (1.4× like-for-like on its own measure). Its FY27 guidance of about 23% includes 11–12 points of calendarisation, so the implied core organic rate is about 12%. Redington's growth is almost entirely organic; an Arena exit would reduce reported growth by about 6 points for a year. Definition trap. "Like-for-like" and "reported organic" differ: Entero excludes businesses it chose to exit from like-for-like, which flatters it by about 2 points. Quote both.

Entero grades well on margins and on its own tangible ROCE, average on days and cash conversion, and weak on total-capital ROCE and minority leakage, which is the profile of a roll-up whose operations have improved faster than its acquisition arithmetic. Neither member is weak on interest cover, but neither is strong, and both will be tested as the rate cycle turns and working capital grows.

Definition traps, collected

  • "Revenue" may be total income. Redington's deck revenue (₹1,19,347 crore) includes ₹185 crore of other income; revenue from operations is ₹1,19,162 crore.
  • "EBITDA" may include other income. Redington's ₹2,414 crore does; screener's OP of ₹2,348 crore does not.
  • "PAT" has up to four bases. Redington FY26: ₹1,284 crore (incl. minority), ₹1,490 crore (attributable), ₹1,565 crore (attributable ex-impairment share), ₹1,687 crore (ex-Arena). Entero FY26: ₹146 crore vs ₹115 crore attributable.
  • The gap between ₹453 crore and ₹486 crore in Redington's Q1 FY27 is minority interest, not an exceptional item, whatever some result summaries call it.
  • ROCE bases differ. Entero's company ROCE excludes goodwill; screener's includes it and IPO cash.
  • Working-capital days come from three sources (screener year-end, company average or quarter-end, rating agency) and differ by several days.
  • Agent accounting shrinks revenue. Software and cloud sold as agent appear at margin only; Redington publishes no gross billings.
  • Organic growth has two versions. Entero's like-for-like excludes deliberate exits and runs about 2 points above reported organic.
  • MMTC's profit is not trading profit. FY26 standalone PBT of ₹463.30 crore included ₹473.70 crore of exceptional income; standalone PAT was ₹212.07 crore, not ₹463.30 crore; consolidated PAT of ₹387 crore adds the MMTC-PAMP share.
  • Arena's revenue has two figures. Its own pre-elimination accounts show ₹6,781 crore for FY26; its contribution to group revenue is about ₹6,824 crore.
In plain terms

In this sub-sector the same word can mean different numbers in different documents. Before comparing two figures, check four things: is revenue gross or net, is EBITDA before or after other income, is profit before or after the minority partners' share, and is capital measured with or without goodwill.

How to read a distributor's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue from operations, and its growth against the market. Pull the statutory figure, not the deck's total income. Compare with IDC (technology) or IPM (pharma) growth for the same quarter. Faster than the market is share gain or price; ask which.
  2. Gross profit in rupees, then the percentage. Rising rupees with a falling percentage is usually mix; falling rupees is a warning.
  3. Opex ÷ gross profit. Below about 60% and falling means operating leverage is working.
  4. EBITDA on one basis. Strip other income out if the company includes it; note any jump in credit provisions inside other expenses.
  5. Working-capital days, all three components. Net days up more than three days year on year is a red flag; net days down because payables rose is a yellow one.
  6. Finance cost and debt. If finance cost rises faster than EBITDA, growth is being funded with debt; check the debt-to-equity ratio and, if disclosed, factoring.
  7. Profit attributable to owners, and the minority share. Use attributable profit for EPS and P/E; track the minority percentage over time.
  8. Segment or geography split. For Redington: India vs rest of world vs Arena; for Entero: organic vs inorganic and medtech share.
  9. Exceptional items and one-offs. Impairments, gains on sale, tax refunds (Redington's Q1 FY27 other income included interest on a ₹147 crore tax refund).
  10. Management's words on the next quarter. Price expectations, channel inventory, M&A appetite, rating actions. Red flags: "channel stocking", rising provisions, guidance quietly dropped.

Ten minutes is enough if you go in this order, because each step tells you whether the next one matters. A quarter with strong revenue, flat gross-profit rupees, rising days and rising interest is a worse quarter than the headline suggests; a quarter with modest revenue, rising rupees, stable days and falling interest is a better one.

In plain terms

Read a distributor's results backwards from cash. If profit rose but working-capital days and debt rose faster, the quarter borrowed from the future. If profit rose and days held steady, it was earned.

What sits against the cash

MMTC held ₹1,788.67 crore of cash and bank balances at 31 March 2026 (₹1,338.85 crore a year earlier), the same in its standalone and consolidated accounts. That looks like a large cushion against a market value of ₹7,586 crore, but four claims sit against it.

  • Current liabilities of ₹646 crore on the standalone balance sheet.
  • Anglo Coal. A dispute over a 2007 long-term coking-coal contract ended in an arbitration award against MMTC. MMTC deposited ₹1,088.62 crore with the Delhi High Court, of which ₹1,000 crore was released to Anglo on 17 November 2025. Management estimates the residual liability at ₹170.58 crore against a provision of ₹87.76 crore, and argues that ₹259.74 crore of interest accrued on the deposit covers the gap. The auditors disagree: the FY26 opinion is qualified for the ₹82.82 crore shortfall, as was the Q1 FY27 review. Adjusted for it, Q1 FY27 consolidated profit would fall from ₹104.24 crore to ₹21.42 crore and net worth from ₹2,266 crore to ₹2,184 crore. A hearing was listed for 22 September 2026; 36 title deeds are deposited with the court.
  • Contingent liabilities of ₹844.77 crore (claims not acknowledged as debt ₹592 crore, sales tax ₹108 crore, service tax ₹109 crore and others), plus ₹209 crore of back-to-back claims.
  • The cost of staying open: about ₹105 crore a year of overheads.

The board has stated the conclusion itself: it did not recommend a dividend because "liabilities including contingent exceed available cash balance". That sentence is management signalling that the cash is not free.

In plain terms

MMTC has about ₹1,800 crore in the bank and a quarter-share of a profitable gold refinery. Against that it owes about ₹650 crore of ordinary bills, may owe more on an old coal contract than it has set aside (its auditors say so), and faces about ₹850 crore of disputed claims. Its own board says the cash does not cover everything it might have to pay, which is why it pays no dividend.

Paths to an ending

The market price implies investors expect something to happen. There are four possibilities. The government could decide to close MMTC, in which case remaining assets would be realised, liabilities settled and any residual distributed; minority shareholders own about 10% of whatever is left. It could merge MMTC into another public-sector company, at an exchange ratio set by the government. It could sell the refinery stake, crystallising its value. Or nothing could happen, as for the past four years, while overheads consume interest income. Governance is thinning in the meantime: all independent directors' terms expired between April and June 2026, the Director (Personnel) post is vacant, and the statutory auditor's term ended at the 17 September 2026 AGM. Watch for exchange penalties on board composition, which are routine for state companies, and for the appointment of a new auditor for FY27.

The stock's behaviour reflects its float. With the President of India holding 89.93%, only about 10% trades, and the 52-week range is ₹49.4–78.4. Since the primer's June figure of ₹10,212 crore the market value has fallen about 26%. MMTC's Q2 FY27 board date had not been announced by 25 October; last year's results were filed in mid-November.

In plain terms

Buying MMTC shares is a bet on what the government decides, not on a business. On an illustrative count, the cash and the refinery stake, less what MMTC owes, are worth well under half the share price. The rest of the price is a premium for a corporate event (a closure payout, a merger, a stake sale) that has no date, plus the scarcity of a stock with only a tenth of its shares in public hands.

Start with the combined rows, because they make the bucket's lopsidedness explicit. Net debt shows the same offset: the operating distributors owe about ₹1,791 crore net, and MMTC's ₹1,787 crore of cash cancels it almost exactly, leaving the bucket with roughly zero net debt. That is an accounting curiosity, not a source of strength: the cash belongs to a government-controlled shell and cannot fund Redington's receivables.

Next, compare the two profit columns. For Redington, profit to owners (₹1,490 crore) exceeds reported profit (₹1,284 crore), and screener's P/E of 15.6× uses the higher figure, so market cap ÷ reported PAT (21.9×) overstates its multiple. Then read the native columns. Gross margin and working-capital days show the two archetypes: Redington at about 5% and 32 days, Entero at about 10% and 61 days. Finally, valuations price three different things. Redington at about 16× earnings and 12.7× EV/OP is priced as a well-run, cyclical distributor; the stock has risen about 88% from its 52-week low, which suggests the market already credits much of the Q1 FY27 boom. In plain terms

The combined numbers show a bucket that earns about two rupees of operating profit per hundred, has almost no net debt and trades at about 23 times profit. Pharma: why India's margins are four times America's

The contrast in pharma is starker. McKesson, Cencora and Cardinal Health are a three-company oligopoly with gross margins of 3.6–3.8%, operating margins of 0.8–1.5% and negative cash cycles: manufacturers effectively finance them, because the wholesalers pay later than hospitals and pharmacy chains pay them. They earn high returns on very little capital. Entero earns 10–11% gross and 4–5% operating margins, but carries 59–74 days of working capital, because India's retail pharmacy trade is made up of hundreds of thousands of small, credit-dependent shops. Over a decade, consolidation in India should push margins down toward global norms and days down with them. Entero's medium-term aspiration of more than 6% EBITDA runs the other way, relying on medtech mix and scale purchasing to offset that pressure.

Valuations follow the same logic. Global IT distributors have typically traded at around 9–13 times forward earnings and US drug wholesalers at around 17–22 times, INDICATIVE ranges from market convention rather than verified data. Redington at about 16 times trailing earnings is above the global technology range, which reflects India's growth; Entero at about 50 times is far above the US drug wholesalers, which reflects the consolidation runway. Neither comparison is decisive, but both show the market is paying an India premium for growth, and a larger one in pharma.

In plain terms

Redington runs its warehouses and offices more cheaply than the American giants, so it makes the same operating margin from a smaller gross margin. But it waits longer for its money than they do, because it cannot make its suppliers wait as long. Entero earns far more per sale than an American drug wholesaler because Indian chemists need a distributor to give them credit; American pharmacies do not.

Technology and disruption: who can cut out the middleman

The threat to a distributor is rarely a new technology; it is a supplier or customer deciding to skip it. The defences are services that are hard to replicate.

Every distributor lives with the risk of disintermediation: a brand that sells direct, a large customer that buys direct, or a platform that connects the two. The risk is real but slow, because the distributor's core service, financing and serving a long tail of small buyers, is exactly what brands and platforms do not want to do. Technology distribution: from boxes to platforms

The technology distributors' answer to disintermediation is to become platforms. Ingram Micro has built its Xvantage platform to automate ordering, pricing and provisioning for resellers. Redington's equivalents are CloudQuarks, a marketplace through which thousands of small resellers buy cloud and software subscriptions, and its logistics arm ProConnect, which handles warehousing and transport for third parties as well as for Redington. These turn the distributor from a box-mover into a service layer: the reseller relies on its systems, not just its stock. The shift also changes the economics, because software and services carry less inventory and less credit risk than hardware.

The AI boom cuts both ways. It created a new category of customer: "neocloud" operators building GPU data centres, which bought about ₹1,000 crore of servers and storage through Redington in Q1 FY27. AI PCs (with more than 40 TOPS of on-device processing) were 36% of Redington's India commercial PC revenue in the same quarter, according to the CEO, and IDC counted AI notebook shipments up 110% year on year. But Redington's own corporate deck notes that "service delivery automation through AI is reducing number of SIs", the system integrators that make up much of its reseller base. Fewer, larger resellers would mean fewer customers to finance, but also more bargaining power on the other side of the table.

Pharma distribution: platforms versus integrators

In pharma, the disruptive forces are e-pharmacies, quick commerce and the integration of retail chains with wholesalers. AIOCD's nationwide strike against e-pharmacies on 20 May 2026 shows how seriously the traditional trade takes the threat. For a scaled distributor like Entero, platforms are as much customers as competitors: a quick-commerce dark store needs a reliable source of thousands of SKUs, which is what Entero provides. The more serious threat is vertical integration. When Apollo HealthCo absorbs Keimed, Apollo's pharmacies will buy from their own wholesaler; when MedPlus and Reliance supply their own stores, those volumes leave the open market. Entero's defence is breadth and service to the independent chemist and the hospital, the segments the integrators do not serve. The e-retail report covers the quick-commerce platforms on the other side of this contest.

Entero's investments in technology are mostly invisible but central to its roll-up model. Every acquired distributor is moved onto a common platform for ordering, inventory and credit, which is what allows the gross margin to rise (through central purchasing) and working-capital days to fall (through better forecasting and collections). The 51 acquisitions are only as valuable as that integration.

In plain terms

Nobody is about to invent a technology that makes distributors unnecessary. The real risk is that a big brand, a big retailer or a big platform decides it can do the distributor's job for its own products. The defence is to do things those players do not want to do, such as financing thousands of small shops, carrying tens of thousands of products, or running a marketplace for small resellers.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the bucket

The bull case rests on India's growth, distributor discipline and consolidation; the bear case on a price boom that has peaked, rising funding costs, regulation and a shell with no date for its ending.

The bull case

  • India is the engine. Redington's India business grew 63% in Q1 FY27 with a 28% company ROCE, and India is now 60% of its revenue; Entero's organic growth runs at about 1.3× a re-accelerating pharma market.
  • Distributor returns hold up. 20%+ ROCE on 2% margins (Redington) and a tangible ROCE rising from 10.7% to 21.1% (Entero) show discipline on days and cost.
  • Capital-light layers are growing. Redington's software and cloud (+37% in FY26, ~74% recurring) and Entero's medtech (₹1,000 Cr run-rate) carry higher returns.
  • Self-help is available. An Arena exit would add about ₹125 Cr a year to Redington's attributable profit (illustrative); Entero's minority buy-outs would lift its attributable share over time.
  • Consolidation runway. Fewer than 10% of India's pharma distribution is organised, and "buyers are limited to 1 or 2 organized players".
  • Optionality at MMTC. A closure, merger or refinery-stake sale could crystallise value; the refinery earned ₹674 Cr in FY26.

The bear case

  • The price boom is peaking. IDC expects PC sell-in to dwindle from Q4 2026 and smartphone units to fall more than 15% in H2; contract DRAM increases are slowing (~+20% QoQ forecast for Q4).
  • Cash is lagging profit. Redington's operating cash flow was −₹885 Cr in Q1 FY27 and gross debt roughly ₹4,200 Cr by June (indicative); the FY23 template saw ROCE fall from 28% to 19%.
  • Money is dearer. The repo rate rose to 5.50% on 7 October 2026; Entero's finance cost was up 93% in Q1 FY27.
  • Regulation bites in pharma. The 30%-of-MRP cap on 137 cancer drugs from 9 November; a wider cap is being discussed.
  • Ownership leaks. Entero's minorities took 27% of Q1 FY27 profit; goodwill is ₹749 Cr; at 49.5× earnings the multiple leaves little slack.
  • Concentration and geography. Apple is 31% of Redington; the Gulf and Turkey remain fragile; credit provisions rose to 0.39% of sales.
  • MMTC's cash is spoken for. Its board says liabilities including contingents exceed cash; the market price is 1.6–2.3× an illustrative sum of the parts.

Weighing the two sides, the balance depends on the member. For Redington the bear points are mostly cyclical and visible: if Q2 and Q3 FY27 show working-capital days staying in the low-to-mid 30s and gross-profit rupees holding as price inflation fades, the bull case on India and software dominates; if days climb toward 40 and credit provisions keep rising, the FY23 template applies. Across the bucket, the single variable that links the bull and bear cases is working capital. A distributor that keeps its days steady through a downturn can absorb lower prices, higher rates and even some regulatory pressure, because its capital base shrinks with its revenue. One that lets days drift can see a profitable year turn into a cash drain. That is why section 20 sets thresholds on days, credit cost and interest before anything else.

In plain terms

The optimist sees India's growth, disciplined distributors and a long runway for consolidation in medicines. The pessimist sees a price boom that has peaked, borrowing that has grown and become dearer, new caps on drug margins, and a cash box whose cash is already spoken for. Both are looking at the same numbers; what will settle it is whether working-capital days hold in the next two quarters.
