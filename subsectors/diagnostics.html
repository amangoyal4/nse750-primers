Diagnostics & wellness: a ₹1 lakh crore market, 6% listed

00

The one-page view: a volume business in a market nobody owns

Four listed lab chains and one Ayurveda hospital group share a label. The labs are compounding on volume and mix; the market they serve is so fragmented that the largest of them holds under 3% of it.

On a typical day roughly three million Indians (an indicative estimate: about a billion patient visits a year) give a blood sample, sit under an X-ray tube or slide into a scanner. Most of those tests are done in a room run by one technician, a centrifuge and a second-hand analyser. A small and growing share go to a brand: a Dr. Lal PathLabs collection centre in Delhi, a Metropolis franchise in Pune, a Vijaya centre in Hyderabad, or a Thyrocare franchisee who ships the sample to a giant automated lab overnight. This report is about those brands, how they make money and why investors pay 40 to 76 times their earnings to own them.

The group in this report is unusual. Four of its five members are diagnostic chains. Together, Dr. Lal PathLabs, Metropolis Healthcare, Vijaya Diagnostic Centre and Thyrocare Technologies earned ₹6,052 crore of revenue in FY26, up 17.3%, at a 29.6% operating margin, and ₹1,037 crore of reported profit. They were worth ₹63,385 crore at the 23 October 2026 close, about 57 times their trailing twelve-month profit. The fifth member, Jeena Sikho Lifecare, is a chain of Ayurveda and naturopathy hospitals that earns more from selling its own medicines than from treating patients. It sits in the same official industry bucket ("Healthcare Service Provider") but shares almost nothing with the labs except a high margin. Because its growth (+71% in FY26) and its valuation (18 times earnings) would distort every average, this report leads with the four lab chains and treats Jeena Sikho as a separate archetype in Section 17.

Five forces define this industry

Diagnostics is often described as a defensive healthcare business, which is true of its demand and misleading about its economics. Five forces explain almost every number in this report.

  • Fragmentation is the runway. India has more than 1.3 lakh laboratories by CareEdge's count and about 3 lakh by the reckoning of Metropolis's chair, most run by technicians rather than pathologists. Organised chains hold between 10% and 25% of a roughly ₹1 lakh crore market; the four listed chains hold about 6%. Most of their 10–17% volume growth is share taken from small labs, not market growth.
  • Volume over price, with mix rescuing realisation. CareEdge's sample of seven large chains grew patients from 64 million to 99 million between FY21 and FY25 while revenue per patient slipped from ₹786 to ₹759. Then Q1 FY27 broke the pattern: Dr. Lal's revenue per patient rose 10% to ₹968 and Metropolis's 6% to ₹1,219, helped by specialty tests, wellness bundles and a government rate revision.
  • Hub-and-spoke operating leverage. A reference lab is a fixed cost. Each extra sample costs little more than reagent and courier, so incremental revenue drops through at 40% or more. That is why the four chains grew operating profit 29% on 20% more revenue in Q1 FY27. Pathology is a logistics business; radiology, where Vijaya earns 37% of its revenue, is a capex-and-location business.
  • The wellness price war. Venture-funded apps (Redcliffe, Orange Health, Healthians, Tata 1mg, PharmEasy) cut the price of a "full-body checkup" to ₹499–999 between 2021 and 2024. The incumbents absorbed the hit, matched selectively and won round one: the challengers still lose money.
  • Ownership and governance overhangs, and M&A moving from regions to capability. Thyrocare's parent, PharmEasy's API Holdings, has sold about 20% of the company in ten months. Jeena Sikho has a thin float, related parties, a new auditor and an unexplained 33% share-price fall in October 2026. Meanwhile acquirers have stopped buying regions and started buying genomics and oncology capability.

Three facts about the latest quarter set the tone for the rest of the report. First, Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) was the best quarter for growth since COVID. The four chains grew revenue 19.6% to ₹1,719 crore and operating profit 29.2% to ₹535 crore, lifting their combined margin from 28.8% to 31.1%. Every one of them expanded margins. Second, growth came mostly from volume and mix, not from list prices: Dr. Lal and Metropolis both skipped their usual annual price rises to pass on a cut in GST on reagents, and Vijaya last raised prices in June 2025 in Hyderabad only. Third, the market did not reward it. Between 1 and 23 October 2026 the lab stocks fell 3–13% and Jeena Sikho 29%, after the Reserve Bank raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October and long-duration growth stocks de-rated across the market.

In plain terms

A diagnostics chain sells a cheap product (a ₹300 blood test) in enormous numbers, mostly for cash, through a network it has spent decades building. Its costs are largely fixed, so each extra patient is very profitable. The industry's big question is not whether Indians will take more tests; they will. It is whether the price of each test, which competition and bundling have pushed down for years, can stop falling, and whether the listed brands can keep taking share from the small labs that still do most of the work.

The margin gap is about mix, not size

The four chains look alike from a distance and very different up close. Vijaya earns a 41% operating margin because more than a third of its revenue comes from radiology scans that an app cannot discount and 92% of its patients walk in and pay. Metropolis earns 25% because 43% of its revenue comes from hospitals and other labs at wholesale prices and because it is digesting a loss-making acquisition. Thyrocare runs one of the cheapest testing operations in the world at about ₹40 a test and still earns 32%, because it has almost no front-end cost: franchisees own the collection centres and the patient. Dr. Lal sits at 28%, the classic premium pathology franchise with a dense North Indian network. Jeena Sikho's 44% comes from something else entirely: low-cost leased beds and its own branded medicines.

The investment debate follows from this. The bull case is that the runway is decades long, the business throws off cash (the five members generated ₹1,725 crore of operating cash flow in FY26 and none carries meaningful bank debt), and FY27 shows the volume engine running faster with margins rising. The bear case is that the multiples already assume all of that, that part of FY27's price gain is a temporary government rate revision, and that the next funding boom for home-collection start-ups could restart the price war. Both sides are argued in Section 21. Before that, the report explains how the business works, from the needle to the report.

01

From needle to report: how a sample becomes revenue

Pathology is a logistics chain with a laboratory in the middle. Radiology is a room full of expensive machines that the patient must visit. The money is made in different places in each.

A diagnostic test looks like a single product. In fact it is five separate activities stitched together: finding the patient, taking the sample, moving it, processing it and reporting the result. Each step has its own cost, its own competitors and its own share of the profit. Understanding which step a company controls is the fastest way to understand its margin.

Value concentrates at Steps 1 and 4: the brand and doctor relationships that decide where the sample goes, and the utilisation of the lab that processes it. Steps 2 and 3 are commoditised and often handed to franchisees.

Start with demand. Most Indian diagnostic tests are ordered by a doctor, and the doctor usually tells the patient where to go, or the patient goes to whichever lab the doctor's clinic uses. That is why the old pathology brands grew out of doctor practices and why their sales teams still spend their days in clinics. A second stream comes from walk-in patients who choose a brand they trust, increasingly for preventive "wellness" packages they order without a prescription. A third comes from institutions: hospitals and nursing homes that outsource their lab work, corporates that buy annual health checks, insurers and, for a few chains, government contracts. Online aggregators such as Tata 1mg, PharmEasy, Practo and Bajaj Finserv Health sit on top of all three, taking a commission for steering the patient.

Collection is the spoke of the network. A patient service centre (PSC) is a small room with a phlebotomist, a chair and a refrigerator, costing roughly ₹5–15 lakh to open (indicative). Dr. Lal had 7,727 of them at March 2026 plus 13,935 pick-up points at clinics; Metropolis has about 5,000 service points, of which only 750–800 are company-owned. Thyrocare owns almost none: its 11,730 active franchisees own the front end. Home collection, once a niche, became standard after COVID and is the core channel of the venture-funded challengers. Because a collection centre is cheap and easy to copy, it earns little on its own. Franchisees and aggregators typically keep 20–40% of the retail price on routine work (indicative).

The lab in the middle

The sample then has to travel. Blood and urine degrade with heat and time, so samples move in cold boxes on two-wheelers to the nearest processing lab, by road to a regional hub, and by air to a national reference lab for specialised tests. Thyrocare built its model on air freight to a single central lab in Navi Mumbai; it has since moved to 44 labs so that "a Thyrocare lab is within 150 km of any pin code". Logistics is a real cost, roughly 5–8% of revenue, and one that rose with fuel and air-freight prices after the West Asia crisis began on 28 February 2026.

Processing is where the scale economics live. A modern hub runs automated chemistry and immunoassay lines from Roche, Abbott, Siemens Healthineers, Beckman Coulter or Sysmex. Most labs do not buy these machines: under reagent-rental contracts the vendor places the analyser free or cheaply and earns on the reagents consumed for every reportable test. This keeps the lab's capital spending light but makes reagents its biggest variable cost, about 20–30% of revenue for a typical pathology chain (indicative). The reference lab adds molecular platforms (PCR, next-generation sequencing), histopathology and cytology, and the specialists to run them.

Finally, a registered pathologist signs the report. Indian courts and the medical regulator have held that a lab report must carry the signature of a qualified MD or DNB pathologist, which in principle shuts out technician-only labs. In practice enforcement is patchy, which is one reason the unorganised sector survives. The digital report, delivered by app or WhatsApp, has become a marketing tool: it carries reminders for the next test.

Radiology works differently

Radiology inverts almost every feature of pathology. There is no sample to transport: the patient must come to the machine. The machine is expensive, roughly ₹10–15 crore for a 3-tesla MRI, ₹4–8 crore for a 128- or 160-slice CT and ₹12–20 crore for a PET-CT (indicative), and each installation needs an Atomic Energy Regulatory Board licence. Every ultrasound machine must be registered under the PCPNDT Act, which bans sex determination and requires a form for every pregnant patient. A radiologist must read every scan, and radiologists are scarce. In return, radiology carries tickets ten to fifty times those of a routine blood test, cannot be home-collected and so cannot be discounted away by an app. Section 9 works through the economics using Vijaya, the only listed member with a large radiology business.

In plain terms

A pathology chain is a courier company with a factory at the end: it wins by collecting more samples from more places and running them through a lab it has already paid for. A radiology chain is closer to a cinema: it wins by putting expensive machines in the right neighbourhoods and filling every slot. Vijaya does both; the other three do mostly the first.

Where the value is captured

Put the steps together and the profit map is clear. The brand and the doctor relationship decide where a patient goes and how much he pays, especially for specialised tests where trust matters more than price. The lab's utilisation decides how much of that payment becomes profit: once a reference lab exists, an extra test carries only reagent, consumable and logistics cost, so contribution margins on incremental volume run at 60–70% (indicative). Mix decides the ticket: specialty, genomics and radiology tests are worth two to twenty times a routine test at similar or better gross margins. The collection layer captures little, which is why the chains are happy to franchise it, and why venture-funded apps that own only collection and a booking screen have struggled to make money.

02

Size: two estimates, one runway

Depending on whose numbers you use, Indian diagnostics was worth ₹0.85–1.1 lakh crore in FY26 and is growing 10–12% a year. Either way it is under a tenth of health spending and a sliver of the world's.

There is no official count of what Indians spend on diagnostic tests. The best estimates come from consultants hired to write the industry chapters of IPO prospectuses and from rating agencies. The two most credible current sources disagree by about a fifth, and the gap matters for every market-share number in this report.

The higher estimate comes from CRISIL Market Intelligence & Analytics, as quoted in the December 2024 prospectus of Suraksha Diagnostic. CRISIL put the Indian diagnostics market at ₹86,000–87,000 crore in FY24 and projected ₹1,27,500–1,37,500 crore by FY28, a 10–12% annual growth rate, inside a healthcare-delivery market of ₹9.5–10.5 lakh crore. Interpolating at that growth rate puts FY26 at roughly ₹1.04–1.09 lakh crore (an indicative estimate, not a published figure). The lower estimate is implied by CareEdge Ratings, which in November 2025 forecast that Indian diagnostic services would grow about 12% a year to US$15–16 billion by FY30. Back-solving that forecast gives roughly ₹73,000–78,000 crore for FY25 and ₹82,000–87,000 crore for FY26. CareEdge's own figure is in dollars and was struck at the exchange rate of its day, so converting it at today's ₹94.9 to the dollar would overstate the gap further; this report leads with rupees.

How fast the listed chains have grown

The listed chains' history is distorted by COVID. RT-PCR and antibody testing inflated FY21 and FY22, and FY23 was the hangover year in which the industry's revenue fell for the only time in two decades. CareEdge's seven-chain sample (Dr. Lal, Thyrocare, Suraksha, Vijaya, Krsnaa, Agilus and Metropolis, about 10–12% of the industry) grew sales 32% in FY21 and 19% in FY22, fell 8% in FY23, then grew 10% and 11% in FY24 and FY25. On the five-year view from FY21, the four listed lab chains compounded revenue at about 12% a year, which understates the underlying pace because the starting year is COVID-inflated. From the FY23 trough, the four grew 13.4% a year to FY26.

Small in the world, small in health spending

Diagnostics is less than a tenth of Indian healthcare spending (8.5–9% on CRISIL's numbers, "under 10%" per CareEdge). Per head, Indians spend a small fraction of what Americans spend on tests, and Indian test prices are, in CareEdge's words, "among the most affordable globally". A single American lab company, Quest Diagnostics, reported revenue of US$11.0 billion in 2025, about ₹1.05 lakh crore at today's rupee, which is roughly the size of the entire Indian market. That comparison is the cleanest statement of the runway: a country with about 18% of the world's people buys perhaps 3% of its clinical lab services (indicative). Section 19 returns to what the global industry looks like and why Indian chains earn margins that foreign labs can only envy.

In plain terms

Nobody knows exactly how big Indian diagnostics is, but the credible estimates cluster around ₹1 lakh crore and agree it grows about twice as fast as the economy in real terms. The four listed lab chains together sell about ₹6,000 crore of that, so even a perfect market-share gain story has many years to run before size becomes a constraint.

The growth rate is the more useful number. The 10–12% the consultants forecast for the market is a blend of more people tested, more tests per person and roughly flat prices. The listed chains have grown faster than that, 17% in FY26 and nearly 20% in Q1 FY27, because they are taking share. Management teams frame it the same way: Vijaya's CFO says the industry grows 13–14% and Vijaya has historically beaten it by 4–5 points; Dr. Lal guides to 13–15% for FY27 and Metropolis to 14–15% excluding acquisitions and price rises. If the market grows 11% and the listed chains 14–15%, their share rises by about half a percentage point a year. That is slow enough to be credible and fast enough, over a decade, to double their combined share.

03

Fragmentation is the runway

More than a lakh labs, most run by technicians; an organised share that insiders put anywhere from 10% to 25%; and a leader with under 3% nationally but more than 5% of North India.

The defining structural fact of Indian diagnostics is that nobody owns it. The largest listed chain, Dr. Lal PathLabs, earns about 2.6% of the national market. The ten largest organised players together hold perhaps 12–15%. The rest belongs to tens of thousands of neighbourhood labs, hospital labs and imaging centres. For a listed chain, every one of those small labs is a potential source of growth.

How many labs are there? CareEdge counts more than 1.3 lakh laboratories. Metropolis's chair, speaking on the company's Q1 FY27 call, put the figure at about 3 lakh, of which only about 10% are run by MD pathologists and 90% by technicians. Thyrocare's management says only about 2% of Indian labs carry NABL accreditation (the ISO 15189 quality standard). The National Accreditation Board lists "14,000+ accredited and recognised labs" across all its schemes, including calibration and testing labs that have nothing to do with medicine; its medical-lab subtotal is not published. Whatever the exact count, the picture is consistent: a long tail of small, often unaccredited labs that compete on proximity, doctor commissions and price.

Why the market stayed fragmented for so long

Several features of Indian healthcare protected the small lab. Most tests are paid in cash by patients who live near the lab, so proximity mattered more than brand. Doctors steered patients and small labs paid doctors for referrals, a practice the large chains say they do not match. Equipment got cheaper: Chinese chemistry and immunoassay analysers from Mindray, Snibe and Autobio cut the cost of setting up a basic lab. Regulation was light: the Clinical Establishments Act of 2010, which sets minimum standards, has been adopted by only about a dozen states plus the Union Territories, and enforcement is weak even there. And until COVID, few patients knew or cared whether their lab was accredited.

Each of those protections is now eroding. Doctors increasingly worry about liability and prefer accredited labs for anything beyond routine tests. Insurers, corporate buyers and government schemes demand NABL accreditation; since October 2025 the central government's CGHS scheme pays non-accredited providers 15% less than accredited ones. Specialised tests, genomics and AI tools require capital and talent that only chains can afford. Home collection and digital reports make a distant brand as convenient as the lab around the corner. CareEdge's view is that these forces favour large chains because the costs of technology are largely fixed while the benefits scale with volume.

In plain terms

The listed chains do not need India to take many more tests to grow; they need the patients who already take tests to move from the lab down the street to a brand. That shift is slow, perhaps half a percentage point of market share a year for the listed group, but it has decades to run and it does not depend on the economy.

Regional density beats national reach

National shares understate how strong the leaders are at home. Imports, China and the cost of entry

Diagnostics is a domestic service and faces no import competition for the service itself. Its exposure to the outside world is on the input side. India imports most of its diagnostic analysers and reagents (industry estimates put the share at 70–80%, unverified), from the US, Germany, Switzerland and Japan and increasingly from China. The Chinese platforms matter for structure: they lower the cost of opening a small lab and of running a price-war entrant, which helps keep routine-test prices low. They matter for costs too: with the rupee at about ₹95 to the dollar, imported reagents priced in dollars and euros cost more each year. Dr. Lal said it stepped up purchases and inventory against currency and West Asia risks; most of its testing material is imported.

In plain terms

Each listed chain is a local champion with a national ambition. Dr. Lal is the default lab in Delhi, Vijaya in Hyderabad, Metropolis in Mumbai and Chennai. Away from home they are newcomers, and buying a respected local lab is usually faster than building one. Watch how much of each company's growth comes from outside its home region, and at what margin.

04

Who orders the test: the demand engine

Chronic disease, an ageing population, preventive checks and specialised medicine all push testing up. Insurance helps less than most people assume, because Indian health cover mostly pays for hospital stays.

Demand for diagnostics is unusually dependable. A diabetic needs an HbA1c test every three months whether the economy is booming or not; a pregnant woman needs her scans; a cancer patient needs his markers. That base of repeat, medically necessary testing is why the industry's revenue fell only once in twenty years. On top of it sit three growth layers: an ageing and more chronically ill population, a fast-growing habit of preventive checks, and medicine's drift towards specialised tests.

Ageing and chronic disease: the annuity

India's population aged 45 and over is projected (UN figures quoted by CareEdge) to grow about 2.3–2.4% a year between 2020 and 2050, against 0.6–0.7% for the population as a whole. People over 45 test far more often than people under 30. India also has roughly 100 million adults with diabetes (ICMR-INDIAB, 2023), and large populations with thyroid disorders, heart disease, kidney disease and cancer, all of which require monitoring tests for life. This is the "annuity" part of pathology: a patient diagnosed with diabetes at 45 is a customer for thirty years. It is also why doctor referral remains the backbone of the industry: chronic patients come back on the doctor's schedule, not the lab's.

Specialisation of medicine

The third layer is the most valuable. As Indian medicine specialises, in oncology, fertility, nephrology, rheumatology and neurology, doctors order tests that a neighbourhood lab cannot run: hormone panels, tumour markers, flow cytometry, cancer gene panels, prenatal genetic screens. These carry tickets of ₹800 to ₹60,000 and require reference labs, accredited processes and specialist pathologists. Metropolis already earns 40% of its revenue from specialty tests and targets 45%; Thyrocare launched allergy, next-generation sequencing, prenatal screening and histopathology in Q1 FY27 and wants specialty to reach 15–20% of revenue in three to five years; Dr. Lal bought genomics capability through SN Genelab in October 2026. CareEdge notes that genomics "consistently delivers double-digit revenue growth".

In plain terms

Three groups of customers drive demand. Chronic patients test because they must, on their doctor's timetable. Healthy adults test because they want to, increasingly through apps and packages. And specialists order complex tests that only big labs can run. The first group gives stability, the second gives growth but invites price competition, and the third gives the highest prices.

Insurance and government: smaller levers than they look

Health insurance in India is overwhelmingly in-patient cover: it pays for a hospital stay and the tests done during it, not for the outpatient tests that make up most of a lab chain's business. So the insurance boom mainly helps hospital labs. It still matters at the margin. Retail policies increasingly include outpatient riders and free annual health checks, and an insured population is a population that gets diagnosed. Since 22 September 2025 individual health and life insurance premiums have been exempt from GST under "GST 2.0", which lowered premiums by the 18% tax. Health insurance premiums rose 15.6% in FY26 to ₹1,37,144 crore, and retail (individual) health premiums 19.9% to ₹56,696 crore, with March 2026 alone up 26.6%. Government schemes act in two directions. Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY pays for hospital admissions (and was extended to every citizen over 70 in October 2024), so it mostly feeds hospital labs. The Central Government Health Scheme (CGHS) and the ex-servicemen's ECHS buy outpatient tests directly at fixed rates. CGHS revised its rates in October 2025 for the first time comprehensively since 2014, and Dr. Lal says the revision added 2–3% to its company-level realisation in Q1 FY27; Metropolis, where CGHS is only about 1% of revenue, gets far less. On the supply side, the PM Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission (₹64,180 crore outlay, 2021–26) is building integrated public-health labs in every district, and the Free Diagnostics Service Initiative requires public facilities to offer between 14 free tests (sub-centre) and 134 (district hospital). Public testing competes for the poorest patients and creates PPP contracts for chains such as Krsnaa; it is not a direct threat to the premium brands.

Geography and convenience

More than 70% of diagnostics revenue is urban, although more than 60% of Indians live in rural areas (CareEdge). The listed chains are pushing outwards. Dr. Lal earned ₹1,090 crore, 39% of FY26 revenue, from tier-3 and smaller towns, growing 16.7% a year, and its GraminFit rural programme tested more than 1.1 lakh patients in seven states in Q1 FY27. Metropolis's tier-3 revenue grew about 25% in Q1 FY27 and it plans 400–500 of its 500 new centres in tier-2 and tier-3 towns. Vijaya's Rajahmundry hub broke even in nine months. Convenience is the last driver: home collection and app booking expanded the market, especially for wellness, rather than only moving existing tests between labs.

In plain terms

The GST cut on health insurance and the CGHS rate revision are real but modest helps. The big demand drivers are slow and structural: more older people, more chronic disease, more specialist medicine and more people who want a check-up without waiting to fall ill. None of them depends on the business cycle, which is why diagnostics revenue barely flinches in a downturn.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes

Reagents, people and the network eat most of the rupee. The split differs sharply by model: a radiology chain spends little on materials and a lot on machines; a wholesale lab spends on reagents and almost nothing on shops.

A diagnostics chain has four big cost buckets: the materials consumed by each test, the people who collect, process and report, the network of centres and labs, and the marketing that keeps doctors and patients coming. Below the operating line sit the depreciation of machines and fit-outs and, since Ind AS 116, the cost of leases. The proportions explain the margin differences between members far better than their size does.

Reagents and consumables are the largest variable cost for a pathology chain. Dr. Lal's gross margin (revenue minus materials) is about 81%, so materials take about ₹19 of every ₹100. Thyrocare's gross margin was 74.1% in Q1 FY27, because a wholesale lab sells its test cheaply and the reagent cost per test does not fall in proportion. Vijaya's gross margin is 88–89%, because a scan uses little consumable material; the same is true of Jeena Sikho (89%), whose medicines are made cheaply. Most reagents are imported and priced in dollars or euros. Under GST 2.0 the tax on diagnostic kits and reagents (HSN 3822) fell from 12% to 5% from 22 September 2025, and because healthcare services are exempt from GST, labs cannot claim input credit: the tax on what they buy is a real cost, so the cut was a real saving. Dr. Lal and Metropolis both chose to pass it on rather than raise prices.

People take ₹14–20: pathologists, radiologists, technicians, phlebotomists, logistics runners and sales staff. Dr. Lal's employee cost was 16.6% of revenue in Q1 FY27. Radiologists are the scarce input: Vijaya employs more than 400. Doctor and professional fees matter mainly in radiology, where reporting radiologists may be paid per scan. Logistics, utilities and repairs take ₹6–10, more for a lab that moves samples long distances. Marketing and other expenses, including franchisee commissions on some channels, IT, short-term rent and administration, take the rest.

What separates the models

Four economic differences sit behind the margin range. The first is who owns the patient. Archetypes A and B own the relationship with the doctor and the patient, so they set the retail price. Archetype C sells at a fixed wholesale rate to a franchisee or platform who sets the retail price and keeps the difference: Thyrocare's management gave the example of a thyroid profile with a list price of about ₹300, which a franchisee sells for ₹150–200 and on which Thyrocare books about ₹60. The second is fixed-cost leverage: it is strongest where one asset does all the work, the single giant lab in C and the scanner in B. The third is working capital. A walk-in B2C chain is paid at the counter and runs on negative working capital (Dr. Lal's cash conversion cycle is −79 days); B2B, PPP and government-panel business can take 60–120 days to collect. Metropolis, with 43% B2B, carries 38–41 debtor days, the highest among the four lab chains; Jeena Sikho's debtor days rose to 66 at the peak of its government-panel business in FY25.

The fourth is returns on capital, which acquisitions distort. Thyrocare's asset-light model earns a 35% return on capital employed and Jeena Sikho's 64%, while Metropolis earns 18% because its balance sheet carries ₹646 crore of goodwill and brand value from the Hitech acquisition alone. Section 13 explains how to adjust ROCE for cash and goodwill so that models can be compared fairly.

In plain terms

The labs that own the patient (Dr. Lal, Metropolis, Vijaya) earn the retail price and spend money to win it. The lab that sells to middlemen (Thyrocare) earns a thin price per test but spends almost nothing on shops. The apps own the patient but not the lab and have so far spent more winning customers than those customers are worth. Each model can work; each has a different weak point.

Models are blurring

The boundaries are moving. Metropolis is adding 100 "mini-hubs" with basic radiology (X-ray, ultrasound and echocardiography), and its radiology-integrated wellness packages grew 40% in Q1 FY27. Dr. Lal plans three to four radiology centres in FY27. Thyrocare is going the other way: it is selling its radiology business, Nueclear, to concentrate on pathology, and it is launching specialty tests at less than half incumbents' prices to move up the value chain without becoming a retail brand. Vijaya is pushing wellness packages that include full-body MRI, cardiac CT and liver elastography. The pathology chains are discovering that a scan is the one product an app cannot discount, and the wholesale lab is discovering that routine tests alone do not grow fast enough.

The long slide in realisation

CareEdge's seven-chain series is the best long-run evidence on price. Between FY21 and FY25 the sample's patients grew from 64 million to 99 million (12% a year) and its tests from 214 million to 401 million (17% a year). Revenue per test fell from ₹233 to ₹187, a 20% decline, and revenue per patient from ₹786 to ₹759, a 3.5% decline. Thyrocare alone processed roughly 150–170 million tests in FY25 depending on the definition, about 40% of the sample's total, at ₹40 each. As Thyrocare grew faster than the others, it dragged the sample's average revenue per test down mechanically. Revenue per patient, which is not distorted by how many parameters each lab counts, fell only 3.5% in four years, much of it because FY21 included expensive COVID RT-PCR tests.

Why bundles lower revenue per test and raise revenue per patient

Wellness bundles explain the apparent contradiction in Thyrocare's numbers, and in the industry's. An ILLUSTRATIVE example: a patient who would have taken a ₹400 thyroid profile and a ₹350 lipid panel (two tests, ₹750) is offered a ₹1,200 package covering 60 parameters. The lab's revenue per patient rises 60%, from ₹750 to ₹1,200. Its revenue per "test", if each parameter counts, collapses from ₹375 to ₹20. The patient is happier, the lab earns more, and the reported price per test looks like a rout. Because most extra parameters run on the same sample through the same analyser, the extra cost is small: the bundle usually raises the lab's profit per patient as well.

This is why Thyrocare changed its test definition in Q1 FY27, removing calculated (non-billed) parameters: its FY26 tests were restated from 209.6 million to 188.7 million. It is also why the CareEdge "price deflation" story is largely a story about counting. The fair conclusion is that list prices for routine tests have been flat to slightly down for years, while revenue per patient has been held roughly steady by mix, and in FY27 lifted by mix and a government rate revision.

In plain terms

The FY27 price gain is real but partly borrowed. About a quarter of Dr. Lal's 10% gain is a government rate revision that should fade within a year. The rest is customers buying more expensive tests. Meanwhile the chains have quietly banked an unused lever: they have not raised list prices for one to three years, so a normal 2–3% price rise remains available for FY28.

What would break the yardstick

Revenue per patient has its own traps. It rises when a company adds radiology (Vijaya's per-footfall figure is double Dr. Lal's per-patient figure because of scans, not prices), when it acquires a specialty lab (Metropolis's Core Diagnostics lifted genomics), or when it loses low-ticket B2B volume. It falls when a company wins a large hospital contract or a wholesale partner. So the number should be read alongside its components: tests per patient, the share of specialty and wellness, and B2C versus B2B growth. Section 13 sets out the benchmark ranges. For now the essential point is that the industry's realisation trend is better than its most quoted statistic suggests, and that FY27's gain should be modelled as part mix, part temporary.

Hubs, spokes and operating leverage

A reference lab is a fixed cost and an extra sample is nearly free to process. That arithmetic explains why profit grows faster than revenue in good years, and why new regions depress margins for two or three years.

Every diagnostics chain is organised the same way: many cheap collection points (spokes) feeding a few expensive processing labs (hubs), which in turn send their hardest samples to one or more national reference labs. The design exists to fill the hubs. Once a hub's fixed costs are covered, each additional test contributes most of its price to profit.

The break-even arithmetic of a hub

An ILLUSTRATIVE regional pathology hub costs about ₹6–8 crore a year to run regardless of volume: perhaps ₹3 crore of staff, ₹1 crore of rent and utilities, ₹1–2 crore of equipment and IT, ₹0.3 crore of quality and accreditation and ₹1 crore of overhead. Each test processed costs roughly ₹70–90 in variable expense (reagent ₹45–60, logistics ₹15–20, consumables ₹10) against a typical realisation of ₹250. Each test therefore contributes ₹160–180 towards the fixed cost. At ₹7 crore of fixed cost and ₹170 of contribution, the hub breaks even at about 4.1 lakh tests a year, roughly 34,000 a month or 1,200 a day. At three times break-even it earns an EBITDA margin above 40%. Analysts' rule of thumb is that a reference lab breaks even at 30–40% of capacity and earns its peak margin above 70%; no company discloses utilisation, and Dr. Lal's management says "capacity is never a constraint in pathology".

Operating leverage in the numbers

The quickest test of operating leverage is the incremental margin: the change in operating profit divided by the change in revenue. In Q1 FY27 the four lab chains added ₹282 crore of revenue year on year and ₹121 crore of operating profit, an incremental margin of 43% against an average margin of 29%. That is how margins expand without price rises. The same mechanism runs in reverse when volume stalls: in FY23, as COVID testing disappeared, the four chains' combined revenue fell 5% and their margins fell from about 31% to 26%.

Expansion into new regions works against leverage. A new hub in an unfamiliar city starts with low volume, unfamiliar doctors and full fixed costs. Scanners and shoe-leather Deep dive

Radiology through Vijaya: why a chain that spends heavily on machines earns the highest margin in the group, why utilisation is everything, and why Thyrocare decided to sell its scanners.

Pathology chains compete with shoe-leather: runners, phlebotomists and franchisees covering a city. Radiology competes with steel and glass: a few expensive rooms in the right places, kept busy from morning to night. Vijaya Diagnostic Centre is the only listed member built on both. It earns about 37% of its revenue from radiology and 63% from pathology, and its 41–43% operating margin is the highest of the four lab chains.

Why Vijaya earns more

Three features explain the margin. First, 92% of Vijaya's revenue is B2C: patients walk in, often on a doctor's referral, and pay at the counter. Pathology peers earn 25–43% from hospitals and labs at wholesale prices. Second, radiology cannot be home-collected, so the app-led price war of 2021–24 could not touch about a third of Vijaya's business. Third, density: Vijaya is the default diagnostic brand in Hyderabad, which provides 67% of its revenue, and its management says the city's second and third players are "deploying capital outside Hyderabad" because of its share. Materials are only about 11% of revenue (a scan consumes little), so contribution margin is about 89%, and 60–70% of costs are fixed. The CFO says Vijaya does about 3.7 tests per visit against 2.5–3 at pathology chains and earns about ₹1,860 per visit against ₹900–950.

Compliance, radiologists and AI

Radiology carries a compliance load that pathology does not. Every X-ray and CT installation needs an AERB licence and a radiation safety officer. Every ultrasound machine must be registered under the PCPNDT Act, and every scan of a pregnant woman documented; violations can lead to machines being sealed. Radiologists are scarce and expensive, which is why tele-radiology and AI matter. Vijaya has more than 400 radiologists and uses validated AI tools for kidney-ureter-bladder, chest and breast imaging. AI triage and reporting assistance (from firms such as Qure.ai and 5C Network) can cut reporting time and let a hub serve towns that have no radiologist, raising utilisation. The risk is that it also commoditises reporting over time.

Thyrocare's opposite choice

Thyrocare drew the opposite conclusion from the same economics. Its radiology arm, Nueclear Healthcare (PET-CT and imaging), earned FY26 revenue of ₹44.6 crore, operating profit of only ₹1.46 crore and a 7.35% return on capital, against 34.91% for Thyrocare's standalone pathology business. Its profit after tax of ₹6.16 crore depended on ₹4.87 crore of other income. In September 2026 the board proposed selling Nueclear to Trovera Healthcare for about ₹141.4 crore (₹59.5 crore in Trovera preference shares plus about ₹81.9 crore in cash), roughly 3.2 times sales and 1.7 times book value. The postal-ballot notice states that the deal is not a related-party transaction; it needs a special resolution and a majority of public shareholders, and e-voting closes on 29 October 2026. Thyrocare is also buying from Nueclear the Gurugram and Hyderabad properties where its own labs sit, for ₹20.59 crore. Radiology without density and walk-in brand pull is a capital-hungry, low-return business; Vijaya's version works because it owns Hyderabad.

In plain terms

The same scanner can be a great business or a poor one depending on who owns it. Vijaya fills its machines because patients in its cities already trust it. Thyrocare, whose brand lives in a franchisee's shop rather than a clinic, earned a 7% return from its scanners and decided to sell them. The lesson for investors is to judge radiology by return on capital, not by EBITDA margin.

The war of the full-body checkup Deep dive

Venture capital broke the price of wellness testing between 2021 and 2024. The incumbents absorbed the blow and the challengers still lose money. Thyrocare became the war's most unusual casualty: a profitable lab owned by a cash-hungry e-pharmacy.

In 2021 the Indian start-up market was awash with money, and diagnostics looked like the perfect consumer-internet business: a recurring need, a fragmented supply base and a product that could be sold on a phone and delivered to the door. Within two years a full-body checkup that used to cost several thousand rupees was being advertised at ₹499–999. This section tells the story of that war, who won the first round, and what could restart it.

How the war started

Three kinds of money arrived at once. E-pharmacies bought labs: PharmEasy's parent, API Holdings, acquired control of Thyrocare in 2021 for about ₹4,546 crore for 66% (rising to 71.22% after the open offer), and Tata Digital bought 1mg. Pure-play home-collection start-ups raised venture rounds: Healthians, Redcliffe Labs (about US$115 million raised in total, including a US$42 million Series C in September 2024) and Orange Health, which promised 60-minute home collection in Bengaluru. And platforms such as Reliance's Netmeds, Apollo 24/7 and Practo pushed lab booking. All of them used wellness packages to acquire customers, because a healthy adult ordering a checkup has no doctor steering him to a particular lab.

Thyrocare under PharmEasy

Thyrocare is where the war's logic played out most strangely. It was founded in Mumbai in 1996 as a thyroid-testing specialist with one central lab and a franchise network, and by 2021 it was the low-cost wholesale processor of Indian pathology. PharmEasy bought it to anchor an online diagnostics business. Five years later, Thyrocare is the profitable, cash-generating asset of a parent that needed cash. Its dividend payout has exceeded 100% of profit in every year since FY23 (148%, 135%, 122% and 137%), sending cash up to a parent with 51.02% of the shares. Operationally, Thyrocare has done well under its new owner. What could restart the war

Four things would bring back price pressure. A funding rebound: if Redcliffe, Orange Health or a new entrant raises a large round, discounting returns; challengers' EBITDA margins are the best gauge of price discipline. Platform aggregation: if insurers' apps, Tata 1mg or Apollo 24/7 become the default way patients book tests, labs risk becoming sub-contractors. Organised rivals with deep pockets: Neuberg (with ₹940 crore of fresh capital and an IPO planned) or Agilus could buy share. And Thyrocare's own specialty launch: its prenatal screening went live at less than half of incumbents' prices, a deliberate volume strategy that could pressure specialty realisations. So far, none of these has broken the incumbents' pricing; all four belong on the watch list in Section 22.

In plain terms

Round one went to the incumbents because the challengers ran out of cheap money before the incumbents ran out of profit. Round two depends on whether venture capital returns and whether the apps become the place where patients choose their lab. Until then, Thyrocare is less a casualty than a cash machine for its owner, and its share price reflects the fact that the owner may sell more.

The rules of the lab

India does not control diagnostic prices. It regulates labs lightly, accredits them voluntarily, taxes their inputs and pays them through government schemes. Each rule nudges share towards the large chains.

Compared with hospitals, which face a long-running court case over price caps (covered in our Indian Hospitals report), and drugs, whose prices the NPPA controls, diagnostics is lightly regulated. There is no national price control on tests. What regulation does exist mostly concerns who may run a lab, what equipment and reagents it may use, and how scans are licensed. The cumulative effect favours organised chains, because compliance costs are fixed and accreditation increasingly earns a price premium.

Accreditation now carries a price

The most important recent change is quiet. The CGHS rate revision, issued by office memorandum on 3 October 2025 and effective from 13 October, covered about 2,000 procedures and introduced explicit differentials: providers without NABL or NABH accreditation are paid 15% less, super-speciality providers 15% more, and rates in tier-2 and tier-3 cities are lower. All existing agreements lapsed, with re-empanelment required within 90 days. For an accredited chain the revision raised realisations on government business; for an unaccredited lab it cut them. Combine that with Thyrocare's estimate that only about 2% of Indian labs are NABL-accredited and Metropolis's that 90% are run by technicians, and government-linked volume becomes a lever that moves share towards chains rather than a one-off price event.

In plain terms

The government does not tell labs what to charge, but it is now paying accredited labs more than unaccredited ones for the same test. Since almost all of the small labs that dominate the market are unaccredited, every rupee of government testing tilts a little further towards the chains.

Public labs: competitor and customer

Two public programmes expand testing directly. The PM Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission had an approved outlay of ₹64,180 crore for 2021–26; administrative approvals of ₹32,928.82 crore to states covered 744 integrated public-health labs, 2,151 block public-health units, 9,519 rural and 5,456 urban health centres and 621 critical-care blocks, with a plan for one integrated lab in each of 730 districts. The Free Diagnostics Service Initiative (National Health Mission, 2015; new operational guidelines December 2025) requires public facilities to offer a minimum menu of free tests, rising with the level of care. Public testing serves the poorest patients, who were rarely the chains' customers, and often runs through PPP contracts with companies such as Krsnaa. For the premium chains it is a slow competitor at the bottom of the market, not a threat to their core.

Price controls: the tail risk

The tail risk is price regulation. States capped prices of COVID RT-PCR tests in 2020–22, showing that governments will act in a health emergency, and some states cap PPP rates. The Supreme Court's long-running case on standardising hospital rates under the Clinical Establishments rules is a reminder that "standard rates" for medical services remain a live idea; if they ever reached outpatient diagnostics, CGHS-style rates could become a benchmark for private B2C pricing. Nothing in the dossier suggests this is imminent, and the pricing evidence runs the other way: CGHS has just raised its rates. The global comparison is instructive: in the US, Medicare's PAMA cuts from 2018 became the biggest single drag on lab margins, a reminder that the payer, not the competitor, can be the larger risk.

AYUSH rules for Jeena Sikho

Jeena Sikho operates under a different rulebook. Ayurvedic practitioners are regulated by the National Commission for Indian System of Medicine (2020); Ayurvedic medicines are licensed by state authorities under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940 and Rules 1945; and the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act 1954 prohibits advertising cures for listed diseases, including diabetes, cancer and kidney disease. The Supreme Court's 2024 proceedings in the IMA versus Patanjali case sharpened enforcement of AYUSH advertising rules. This matters for a company whose patients come largely through its founder's YouTube channel and whose own investor presentation lists "CKD, diabetes, liver disease support". The report flags the regulatory perimeter; it does not allege non-compliance. On the payer side, IRDAI rules allow in-patient cover for AYUSH treatment at qualifying hospitals, and Jeena Sikho is empanelled with CGHS, CAPF, ECHS and the employee schemes of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana and Punjab. IRDAI's consultation paper of 23 September 2026 is one of the items in Section 22.

In plain terms

Lab regulation in India is a set of fixed costs that small labs often ignore and large chains must pay. As enforcement, accreditation and government payment rules tighten, the cost of being small rises. The only rule that could hurt the chains badly, a cap on what they may charge private patients, does not exist and shows no sign of arriving.

Seventy-five years, four shocks

From a Delhi doctor's lab in 1949 to a ₹63,000 crore listed group: doctor-entrepreneurs, private equity, two listing waves, COVID, the venture-capital price war and now consolidation by capability.

Diagnostics is not cyclical in the way steel or cement is. Its demand barely moves with the economy. Its "cycles" come from two other sources: epidemics, which spike testing for a season or, in COVID's case, two years; and the availability of venture capital, which decides how aggressively newcomers can cut prices. The industry's history is best read as five eras, with four shocks between them.

Era 1: doctor-entrepreneurs (1949–2005)

Every listed chain began as a doctor's practice. Dr. Lal's first laboratory opened in Delhi in 1949. Metropolis grew out of a Mumbai pathology practice founded in 1980. Vijaya Diagnostic Centre opened in Hyderabad in 1981. Thyrocare was founded in Mumbai in 1996 by Dr A. Velumani as a thyroid-testing specialist with a single central lab and franchised collection, an unusual model that became the industry's low-cost benchmark. SRL, launched by the Ranbaxy group in 1995, became the first pan-India chain. These businesses grew on the reputation of their founders among local doctors, which is still the root of their regional dominance.

Era 2: private equity and chain-building (2005–2015)

Private equity funded the shift from practice to chain. Investors such as Westbridge, Sequoia and TA backed Dr. Lal; Warburg Pincus and later Carlyle backed Metropolis; Kedaara backed Vijaya. The money paid for hub-and-spoke roll-outs, reference labs and acquisitions of smaller labs. SRL passed to Fortis in 2011 and was renamed Agilus in 2023.

Era 3: listing waves and COVID (2015–2022)

Dr. Lal listed in December 2015, the first pure-play chain on the Indian market, followed by Thyrocare in May 2016 and Metropolis in April 2019. COVID then turned a steady business into a boom. RT-PCR and antibody testing lifted CareEdge's sample sales 32% in FY21 and 19% in FY22, with margins above 29%. A second listing wave rode the boom: Krsnaa in August 2021 and Vijaya in September 2021. Acquirers bought at peak prices: Dr. Lal agreed to buy Suburban Diagnostics in Mumbai in 2021 for an enterprise value of ₹925 crore, rising to as much as ₹1,150 crore with performance payments, about 18.5 times Suburban's FY22 EBITDA; Metropolis bought Hitech Diagnostic in Chennai for about ₹636 crore.

Era 4: the price war and the hangover (2021–2024)

Two shocks hit at once. COVID testing disappeared, and the four listed chains' revenue fell about 5% in FY23. At the same time the venture-funded challengers described in Section 10 discounted wellness packages heavily. CareEdge's sample margin fell to 24.7% in FY23 and listed multiples roughly halved from their 2021 peaks. Suburban and Hitech, bought at boom prices, weighed on returns: Metropolis's ROCE fell from 36% in FY21 to 15–18%.

Era 5: volume, mix and consolidation (FY25 onwards)

The current era began as challengers' funding dried up and incumbents matched selectively on price. The four chains grew 13% in FY25 and 17% in FY26 and reached 19.6% in Q1 FY27 with margins at 31%. Bonus issues followed at Thyrocare (2:1, December 2025), Dr. Lal (1:1, December 2025) and Metropolis (3:1, record date 20 March 2026), and Jeena Sikho split its shares 5:1 in June 2025; pre-action share prices are not comparable with today's. M&A moved from buying regions to buying capability (Section 18).

Reading a lab: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

The handful of numbers that explain a diagnostics company: what each means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, how the members compare and where the definitions trip people up.

A diagnostics chain discloses a small set of operating numbers every quarter. Read together, they tell you how many people it served, how much each spent, what it cost to serve them and how much capital it took. Read carelessly, they mislead, because every company defines them a little differently. This section works through each one in the order an analyst would.

The equation: what drives revenue and profit

All four lab chains share one revenue equation and one profit equation. Revenue is the number of patients multiplied by what each spends, and what each spends is the number of tests per patient multiplied by the price of each test. Operating profit is revenue minus variable cost (reagents, logistics, commissions) minus the fixed cost of the network.

In plain terms

A lab's revenue is how many people walk in multiplied by how much each spends. Its profit depends mostly on how full its labs are. When analysts argue about a diagnostics stock, they are usually arguing about one of two things: whether patient numbers can keep growing at 8–12% a year, and whether spending per patient can keep rising despite competition.

A radiology-heavy chain must keep buying scanners, which become obsolete in eight to ten years, so a meaningful part of its EBITDA is permanently committed to replacement capex. Vijaya's management estimates its cash profit at 26–27% of revenue, well below its 41% EBITDA margin. When comparing EV/EBITDA multiples across the group, Vijaya's multiple should be read against that lower cash conversion, and Thyrocare's against the fact that it is about to become a pure pathology business once the Nueclear sale closes.

KPI 10: incremental margin

What it is. How much of each extra rupee of revenue becomes operating profit. Formula. Change in EBITDA ÷ change in revenue, year on year. Worked example. Thyrocare Q1 FY27: (₹77 − ₹58 crore) ÷ (₹240 − ₹193 crore) = 40%. Drivers. Utilisation of existing labs (high), new labs or regions (low or negative), price and mix (very high), cost inflation (negative). Benchmarks. Above 40% good, 30–40% average, below 25% weak. Comparison. Q1 FY27: Vijaya 58%, Dr. Lal 44%, Thyrocare 40%, Metropolis 33% on screener figures (37% on company EBITDA); four chains combined 43%; Jeena Sikho 26%. Trap. One quarter can mislead: a one-off in the base quarter, an acquisition or a change in accounting (Thyrocare's reagent-rental capitalisation) can swing it. Use rolling four-quarter figures.

KPI 11: ROCE, with and without cash and goodwill

Dr. Lal's screener ROCE is 28.0%, but its balance sheet carries about ₹1,526 crore of cash and treasury investments (₹1,693 crore at June 2026) that earn interest rather than lab profit. Stripping the cash out, the company calculates ROCE of 44% for FY26 and 58% for Q1 FY27. Goodwill. An ILLUSTRATIVE adjustment for Metropolis: FY26 EBIT of about ₹288 crore (operating profit ₹406 crore − depreciation ₹134 crore + other income ₹16 crore) on capital of about ₹1,745 crore (net worth ₹1,513 crore + borrowings ₹232 crore) gives about 16.5%; removing the ₹646 crore Hitech carrying value lifts it to about 26%. What it is. Growth excluding acquisitions (and ideally excluding one-off epidemics). Formula. (Revenue − revenue from businesses acquired in the last twelve months) ÷ prior-year revenue − 1. In plain terms

A single well-run lab and its collection centres can earn back its capital in under two years. The listed chains' returns look lower than that because they carry cash, acquisitions and new labs that are not yet full. When a chain's ROCE rises, it is usually because new labs are filling up; when it falls, it is usually because the company paid a lot for an acquisition.

Reading the KPIs together: what Q1 FY27 says

Applying the KPIs to the latest quarter shows how they interlock. Dr. Lal grew patients 8.2% and revenue per patient 10%, so volume and realisation contributed roughly equally to 19.1% revenue growth; about a quarter of the realisation gain was the CGHS revision. Tests per patient rose from 3.07 to 3.14. Incremental margin was 44%, and EBITDA margin rose from 28.7% to 31.0%. Management nonetheless kept its 27–28% margin guidance for the year and said any upside would be reinvested in infrastructure, advertising and new labs. The read: strong volume, temporarily flattered price, deliberately capped margin.

Metropolis grew patients 10% and revenue per patient 6% with no price rise, so its 16.6% growth was almost entirely volume and mix. B2C grew faster than B2B, specialty and TruHealth grew faster than routine, and its margin rose 210 basis points to 25.2% as Core Diagnostics improved. The read: a cleaner quality of growth than Dr. Lal's, from a lower margin base, with an unused price lever.

Vijaya grew footfall 12.7% and revenue per test 5.5%, and tests per footfall rose. Its margin reached 42.7% despite new hubs, but lease interest rose 47% and free cash flow is thin while it builds. The read: the highest-quality revenue in the group, paid for with capital.

Thyrocare grew tests 28% and patients 17%, but revenue per test fell 2% as franchisees added discounted parameters; revenue per patient still rose 7%. Margin rose to 32.2% on a gross margin of 74.1%. The read: a volume machine whose realisation depends on channel mix rather than brand.

Jeena Sikho, on its own metrics, raised occupancy to 59% and admissions 33%, but service revenue rose only 13% because ticket sizes fell, medicine sales were flat quarter on quarter, and margin fell from 45.4% to 41.1%. About ₹7 crore of its ₹14 crore other income was one-time. The read: volume up, quality of growth down.

Put the four lab chains together and the KPIs tell a consistent story: volume growth of 8–17%, realisation up 5–10% for the B2C chains, incremental margins of 33–58% and every margin higher. That is about as good as a diagnostics quarter gets, which is why the most important question for Q2 FY27 is how much of it was weather and a government rate revision, and how much was share gain that will repeat.

Definition traps, collected

  • "Patient", "footfall", "sample" and "test" differ by company. Dr. Lal reports samples; Thyrocare counts parameters; Vijaya counts scans as tests. Never compare revenue per test across companies.
  • Thyrocare changed its test definition in Q1 FY27. FY26 is 188.7 million tests on the new basis, not the 209.6 million previously reported.
  • B2C does not mean the same thing everywhere. Thyrocare's franchise revenue is a wholesale sale to a franchisee.
  • EBITDA is post-Ind AS 116. Lease rent sits below EBITDA, flattering lease-heavy networks by an indicative 2–5 points.
  • Company EBITDA may exclude one-offs or ESOP costs. Dr. Lal's FY26: ₹752 crore reported, ₹783 crore normalised (before the ₹30.09 crore labour-code charge).
  • "Debt-free" usually means no bank debt. Screener's borrowings include lease liabilities: Vijaya's ₹423 crore is entirely leases; Metropolis's ₹232 crore is ₹18.7 crore of borrowings plus ₹213 crore of leases.
  • "Net cash" is often gross cash. Dr. Lal's ₹1,526 crore (March 2026) is cash and treasury investments before ₹199 crore of leases; net of leases it is about ₹1,327 crore.
  • Reported profit growth can be distorted at both ends. Dr. Lal's FY25 profit included a ₹41 crore tax gain and FY26 a ₹30.09 crore labour-code charge; Vijaya's FY24 profit was flattered by ₹29.1 crore from a depreciation-method change.
  • Acquisitions sit inside headline growth. Metropolis's FY26 +23.7% included Core Diagnostics and three smaller labs.
  • Bonus issues and splits change per-share history. Thyrocare 2:1, Dr. Lal 1:1, Metropolis 3:1, Jeena Sikho 5:1 split. Compare market values, not share prices, across time.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in analysing diagnostics companies come from comparing numbers that only look alike: tests that are not the same unit, debt that is really rent, cash that is not net, and growth that was bought. Before comparing any two companies on any metric, check the definition in each company's own presentation.

How to read a diagnostics company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, and how much is organic. Compare with guidance (Dr. Lal 13–15%, Metropolis 14–15%). Ask what acquisitions contributed. Red flag: growth propped up by deals while organic slows below 10%.
  2. Patients (or footfall) growth. The volume engine. Above 10% is strong. Red flag: patient growth below 5% for two quarters, outside a known base effect.
  3. Revenue per patient, and why it moved. Split mix, price and government rates. Red flag: realisation rising only because of a rate revision that is about to fade, or falling because of discounting.
  4. Tests per patient. Rising suggests bundling and specialty success. Check for definition changes.
  5. B2C vs B2B growth. B2C growing faster supports pricing. Red flag: growth coming mainly from low-price hospital contracts.
  6. Wellness and specialty share. Growing wellness is good up to about a third of revenue; beyond that, price-war exposure rises.
  7. EBITDA margin and incremental margin. Compare year on year (seasonality makes quarter-on-quarter misleading). Red flag: incremental margin below the average margin without a stated reason such as new labs.
  8. Below the line. Depreciation and lease interest (rising fast means expansion); other income (Jeena Sikho's Q1 FY27 included about ₹7 crore of one-time gains); exceptional items.
  9. Network. Labs, centres, franchisees added and closed; centres per lab. Red flag: many new labs in new regions with no plan for when they break even.
  10. Cash and governance. Cash balance, capex, acquisitions, related-party items, promoter holding and pledges, auditor remarks. Red flag: receivables rising faster than revenue, or a change of auditor without a clear reason.
In plain terms

The ten-minute read comes down to three questions. Are more patients coming? Is each one spending more, and for a reason that will last? Is the extra revenue turning into profit and cash? A quarter that answers yes to all three is a good quarter, whatever the share price does on the day.

Who is winning

Vijaya wins on quality, Thyrocare on returns, Dr. Lal on scale and cash, Metropolis on improvement from a low base. Jeena Sikho wins on every ratio and loses on trust.

"Winning" in diagnostics means three things at once: taking share from unorganised labs, holding or raising revenue per patient while doing it, and turning the extra revenue into returns. No member leads on all three. The maps below place each company on the trade-offs that matter.

The first map shows growth against margin in the latest quarter. Vijaya sits alone in the top-right among the lab chains: faster growth than Dr. Lal and Metropolis and a margin above 42%. Thyrocare grows fast at a middling margin. Dr. Lal grows at the group's pace at a solid margin. Metropolis is slowest and thinnest, but it is the most improved: its margin rose 210 basis points and its management guides to 27–28% over two years, which would add about a third to its operating profit at the same revenue.

The second map explains much of the valuation spread. A higher revenue per patient signals specialty, radiology and walk-in business, which competitors find hard to copy, and the market pays for it. The exception is instructive: Metropolis earns more per patient than Dr. Lal but trades at a slightly lower multiple, because a higher share of its revenue is wholesale B2B and its returns are diluted by acquisitions. If Metropolis reaches its 27–28% margin target, it is the member with the most room for its multiple to converge with Dr. Lal's.

The third map is the uncomfortable one for bulls. The two companies with the highest returns on capital, Thyrocare and Jeena Sikho, are the cheapest; the company with the richest multiple, Vijaya, has a below-median return. The market is not paying for returns. It is paying for durability: Vijaya's and Dr. Lal's brands are judged likely to earn their returns for decades, while Thyrocare's depend on a channel its parent partly controls and Jeena Sikho's on a founder and a regulatory perimeter. Whether that judgment is right is the core of the investment debate.

An Ayurveda hospital that sells medicine Deep dive

Jeena Sikho shares a classification with the lab chains and nothing else. Its economics are a founder-led funnel, cheap leased beds and its own branded medicines; its questions are about quality of earnings, governance and a sharp October fall that no filing explains.

Jeena Sikho Lifecare runs Ayurveda and naturopathy hospitals and clinics under the HIIMS name and sells its own Shuddhi-branded medicines, at its centres and increasingly by cash-on-delivery e-commerce. It listed on the NSE's SME platform in 2022 at ₹150 a share (an issue of about ₹55.5 crore), split its shares 5:1 in June 2025, moved to the NSE and BSE main boards in August 2025, and grew revenue from ₹147 crore in FY22 to ₹801 crore in FY26. Its 44% operating margin and 64% ROCE are the highest in the group. Its share price fell from a 52-week high of ₹840 to ₹342 on 23 October 2026.

The funnel

The business starts with media. The founder, Acharya Manish, has a large YouTube following (the company cites more than 10 million followers), and the company runs health camps that, by its own account, convert about 30% of attendees into in-patient admissions. Patients typically arrive with chronic lifestyle conditions; the company's presentation lists "CKD, diabetes, liver disease support". From the camp or clinic, the patient moves to an out-patient consultation, a day-care session or a Panchakarma in-patient stay, and leaves with a course of Shuddhi medicines, which are reordered by phone or online. A six-month television sponsorship deal (on several popular general-entertainment shows) was booked up front in Q1 FY27, raising advertising spending 25% quarter on quarter.

Why the margin is so high

Three features explain the 44% margin. The bed is cheap: in leased buildings, an Ayurveda bed costs about ₹3–4 lakh to set up, against ₹30–50 lakh or more for an allopathic hospital bed. The medicines are cheap to make and carry an 86% gross margin; services carry 91%. And patient acquisition is largely the founder's media reach rather than paid doctor referrals. An ILLUSTRATIVE bed calculation: about ₹100 crore of in-patient and day-care revenue in the quarter across about 2,400 beds is roughly ₹4.2 lakh a bed a quarter, or ₹17 lakh a year, against ₹3–4 lakh of capex: the bed pays for itself within a year before any medicine is sold. That is the economic core of the 64% ROCE. Occupancy was 59% in Q1 FY27; management targets 3,000–3,500 beds this year and 7,000–10,000 within three to five years at 75–80% occupancy, alongside an aspiration of ₹3,000 crore of revenue in about three years. In plain terms

Jeena Sikho is closer to a consumer-health brand with hospitals attached than to a hospital chain. Its founder's following brings patients in, a cheap bed treats them, and its own medicines keep them buying. That model can be very profitable, and it depends heavily on one person's reach, on the rules about what Ayurveda companies may claim, and on patients continuing to believe the treatment works.

The quality-of-earnings questions

Several features of the last three quarters raise questions that the company's own numbers prompt. Revenue has plateaued at about ₹216–224 crore a quarter since December 2025, and the December quarter's ₹101 crore operating profit has not been regained. The regulatory perimeter

Jeena Sikho's growth depends on rules the lab chains never face. The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act prohibits advertising cures for listed diseases, and the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules restrict misleading claims for Ayurvedic medicines; the Supreme Court's 2024 proceedings in the IMA versus Patanjali case showed these rules can be enforced against prominent AYUSH brands. A business whose patients arrive through a founder's videos about chronic disease operates close to that perimeter. This report makes no allegation of non-compliance; it notes that the risk is structural to the model. On the positive side, IRDAI requires insurers to cover AYUSH in-patient treatment at qualifying hospitals, 51 of the company's hospitals are NABH-accredited, and a possible inclusion of AYUSH in PM-JAY would widen its market. An analyst on the Q1 call also questioned the pricing of one product against a similar rival; management defended its formulation and costs, and this report notes the question without adjudicating it.

In plain terms

On paper, Jeena Sikho is the cheapest, fastest-growing and most profitable company in this group. The market values it at a fraction of the lab chains because its growth has stalled for three quarters, part of its recent profit was one-off, it shrank a slow-paying government business, its auditor changed on its move to the main board, and its patients depend on one founder's reach. Its 33% fall in October has no public explanation. Investors should treat it as a separate, higher-risk business, not as a cheap diagnostics stock.

Owners, sellers and buyers: the M&A map Deep dive

Who owns the listed chains, who is selling, and what acquirers now buy. Deals have moved from buying a region to buying a capability, and the price of a lab has split in two.

In a fragmented industry, ownership and acquisitions shape the investment case as much as operations do. Three questions matter: are the controlling shareholders stable, is anyone large selling, and how sensibly are the chains spending on acquisitions? The answers differ sharply across the five.

Who owns the listed chains

Three of the five have stable founding families with roughly half the shares: the Lal family at Dr. Lal (52.93% at September 2026), the Reddy family at Vijaya (52.46%) and the Shah family at Metropolis (47.82%). Why Indian margins are higher

Indian chains earn 25–43% operating margins against 15–23% for the global leaders, for four reasons. Most Indian revenue is cash paid by patients, so there is no payer negotiating prices down; in the US, insurers and Medicare set lab prices, and Medicare's PAMA cuts from 2018 became the single biggest drag on margins. Labour is far cheaper. Tests are sold in bundles, which raises revenue per patient at little extra cost. And the Indian chains are growing into fixed costs, while mature global networks have long since filled theirs. The flip side is scale: Dr. Lal's ₹2,763 crore of revenue is about a fortieth of Quest's, and the whole Indian listed group is smaller than any one of the four global leaders.

The lesson from abroad

The US and European lab markets consolidated over about thirty years, taking organised share to perhaps 60–80% (an indicative estimate from memory, not re-verified), led by serial acquirers that made hundreds of small purchases. The pattern supports the Indian runway thesis: fragmentation does yield to scale, but slowly, through acquisition as much as organic growth, and the consolidators that won were disciplined about price. The other lesson is about payers. Global lab margins are capped by whoever pays the bill; India's chains enjoy free pricing because the patient pays. If insurance ever covers outpatient diagnostics widely, insurers will negotiate, and Indian margins will drift towards global levels.

What 57 times earnings requires

The four Indian chains trade at about 57 times trailing profit and about 35 times EBITDA; global leaders trade at roughly 10–13 times EBITDA (an indicative range from memory; current multiples were not fetched). The premium pays for growth 2–4 times faster, margins 1.5–2 times higher and the scarcity of listed Indian healthcare services. An ILLUSTRATIVE scenario shows what the price assumes. Technology and disruption

AI, digital pathology and genomics favour the large chains because their costs are fixed and their benefits scale. Home testing and platform aggregation are the slow threats.

Technology in diagnostics mostly works in favour of scale. CareEdge's observation is the right starting point: the cost of AI and automation is largely fixed, while the benefit grows with volume, so the chains that process the most samples gain the most. The genuine threats are slower: tests moving out of the lab and onto the patient's finger, and platforms that own the patient relationship.

The tools

AI tools for chest X-rays, CT and MRI from firms such as Qure.ai and 5C Network can flag abnormal scans and draft reports, cutting radiologist time and letting a hub serve towns that have no radiologist of their own. Vijaya already uses validated AI for kidney, chest and breast imaging across its 400-plus radiologists. Digital pathology lets a pathologist in a reference lab read slides scanned anywhere. Genomics, where sequencing costs keep falling, opens oncology panels, prenatal screening and hereditary-risk testing, which CareEdge says "consistently delivers double-digit revenue growth" with higher margins but needs heavy capex and talent. Dr. Lal launched an AI-based wellness platform in FY26. All of these raise the fixed cost of running a credible lab and lower the variable cost of each test, the combination that favours consolidation.

The threats

Two threats deserve attention. The first is testing outside the lab. Glucometers, home HbA1c and lipid kits and, increasingly, continuous glucose monitors move routine monitoring from the lab to the patient. This is a slow erosion of the routine base rather than a cliff, and it is offset by the growth of specialty testing. A related trend is new drug classes that need testing: Metropolis notes pre-prescription panels for GLP-1 obesity drugs, though their effect is hard to isolate. The second is platform risk. If apps, insurers or e-pharmacies become the place where patients choose a lab, the lab becomes a sub-contractor and loses its B2C price. So far the brands have held: Vijaya is 92% B2C and Metropolis's B2C grows faster than its B2B. Thyrocare's model shows the other path: it sells through platforms and earns ₹40 a test. Data protection rules (the Digital Personal Data Protection Act) constrain how labs can use patient data for marketing or AI training; Vijaya says it will not monetise data until the compliance position is clear.

In plain terms

Most new diagnostics technology makes big labs more efficient and small labs less viable, which helps the listed chains. The risk to watch is not a new machine but a new middleman: if patients start choosing labs through an app or an insurer, rather than on their doctor's advice or a brand they trust, the labs lose pricing power. For Jeena Sikho the equivalent risk is a change in how video platforms treat health claims.

Bull vs bear

The structural case is strong and widely known. The debate is about price, the durability of FY27's realisation gain and what management does with cash.

Few sectors have a clearer long-run story than Indian diagnostics: a fragmented, growing, cash-paying market with high margins and no debt. The difficulty is that the story is well understood and already reflected in the share prices. Both cases below are argued at their strongest.

The bull case

  • A decades-long runway. The four listed chains hold about 6% of a ₹1 lakh crore market growing 10–12%, and organised players only 10–25%. Share gain of half a point a year compounds for a long time.
  • Operating leverage is working. Q1 FY27 incremental margins of 33–58% lifted every chain's margin; Metropolis has 250–300 bp more to come by its own guidance.
  • An unused price lever. Dr. Lal and Metropolis have not raised list prices for one to three years; a normal 2–3% increase is available once the CGHS effect laps.
  • Regulation tilts to scale. CGHS pays non-accredited labs 15% less; NABL, AI and genomics costs are fixed; GST on reagents fell to 5%.
  • Fortress balance sheets. No meaningful bank debt anywhere, ₹1,725 crore of operating cash flow in FY26 and ₹1.7k crore of cash at Dr. Lal alone.
  • The price war was won. Challengers still lose money; Thyrocare's parent has repaid its debt.

The bear case

  • The price assumes success. 57 times earnings for the four chains needs close to 20% profit growth for a decade to earn double-digit returns.
  • FY27's realisation gain is partly temporary. 2–3 points of Dr. Lal's +10% is a CGHS revision that fades within a few quarters; Q1 weather may have flattered volumes.
  • Funding can come back. One large venture round for Redcliffe, Orange Health or a new entrant, or Neuberg's IPO money, could restart discounting.
  • Acquisition risk. Dr. Lal's cash is earmarked for M&A while private buyers pay 18–19 times EBITDA; Metropolis is still digesting five deals and Hitech goodwill.
  • Concentration. Vijaya earns 67% of revenue in Hyderabad, Dr. Lal 63% in the North; costs rise with a rupee near ₹95 and West Asia freight.
  • Overhangs. Thyrocare's parent may sell more; Jeena Sikho's earnings quality, governance perception and unexplained fall.

The bull case rests on facts that are hard to dispute: the market's fragmentation, the chains' cash generation and the evidence of operating leverage. The bear case rests mostly on price and on things that have not happened yet. The resolution, for an investor, is to separate the business question from the stock question. The business question has a clear answer: the large chains will keep taking share, and their best-run units earn superb returns. The stock question depends on entry price, and on whether the next two or three quarters show realisation holding up once the CGHS effect fades.

Within the group, the debate differs by company. For Dr. Lal, the swing factor is capital allocation. For Vijaya, it is whether the new metros reach Hyderabad-like margins and whether 76 times is justified by its brand. For Metropolis, it is execution on the 27–28% margin target, which would make it the cheapest route to the sector's economics. For Thyrocare, it is the parent's intentions and whether specialty can lift realisation. For Jeena Sikho, it is whether revenue resumes growth and whether the market's doubts about earnings quality and governance prove justified.

In plain terms

The industry is very likely to keep growing and the big chains are very likely to keep winning. Whether their shares do well from here depends on how much of that is already in the price, which, at 50–76 times earnings for the lab chains, is a lot. The next two quarterly results will show whether this year's price gains were a one-off or the start of a new trend.
