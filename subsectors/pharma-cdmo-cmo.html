00

The one-page view: one label, five businesses

Six listed companies call themselves contract manufacturers. They earn between 12% and 41% on revenue, turn their plants over between 0.2 and 2.7 times a year, and trade between 22 and 73 times EBITDA. The label hides more than it explains.

A contract development and manufacturing organisation, or CDMO, makes medicines, or the chemical building blocks of medicines, that belong to someone else. The client owns the molecule, the brand or the regulatory dossier. The CDMO owns the plant, the chemists and the inspection record, and is paid to turn the client's recipe into product that passes audit in Washington, Brussels and New Delhi. A contract manufacturing organisation (CMO) does the making only; a CDMO also does the development work that gets a recipe from a laboratory flask to a 10,000-litre reactor.

Six companies in the NSE 750 sit under this label: Divi's Laboratories, Gland Pharma, Neuland Laboratories, Piramal Pharma, OneSource Specialty Pharma and Akums Drugs and Pharmaceuticals. Together they booked ₹33,664 crore of revenue in FY26, up 8.0%, and ₹3,815 crore of profit after tax. On 7 October 2026 the market valued them at ₹3.92 lakh crore, about 103 times that profit. Divi's alone is 65% of the value and 67% of the profit.

The first and most important thing to understand is that these six are not one business. "CDMO" in India covers five distinct models, each with its own customer, contract, capital need and risk:

  • Innovator small-molecule CDMO. Divi's custom synthesis, Neuland's custom manufacturing solutions (CMS) and Piramal Pharma Solutions make a patented active ingredient, or an advanced intermediate, for one innovator drug company. The plant is named in the innovator's New Drug Application. This is the highest-quality revenue in the group.
  • Sterile-injectables CMO. Gland fills vials, prefilled syringes and freeze-dried powders for US generic partners who own the product approvals, and through its French subsidiary Cenexi for European drug companies.
  • Biologics and drug-device CDMO, still ramping. OneSource fills GLP-1 cartridges and assembles them into pens, makes biologic drug substance and soft-gelatin capsules. Its plants are new and its margins are still climbing towards target.
  • Domestic formulation CMO. Akums makes about 49 billion tablets, capsules and syrups a year for more than 1,500 Indian brand owners, including 26 of the country's top 30 pharma companies.
  • Generic merchant API. Inside Divi's and Neuland sits a fifth model: generic active ingredients sold to many formulators at market prices. Divi's holds 65–85% global share in naproxen, gabapentin and dextromethorphan.

Why do the margins spread from 12% to 41%? Because value in contract manufacturing is captured by three things multiplied together: regulatory embedding (is the plant named in the client's filing?), technical scarcity (how many qualified sites in the world can do this chemistry, this sterile fill or this device assembly?) and utilisation (how full is the plant?). A company scores high on all three only on commercial molecules in hard modalities: peptides, high-potency compounds, sterile products, drug-device combinations. Everywhere else it earns a conversion fee, the spread between what the client pays and what the inputs cost.

The source of the lock-in is regulatory. When a drug is approved, its application names every site that makes the active ingredient and its key intermediates. Changing supplier means a prior-approval supplement to the regulator, new validation batches and new stability data: typically 12 to 24 months of work and risk for a product that is already selling. Innovators therefore rarely switch a commercial supplier unless something goes badly wrong. A good CDMO is paid as much for not being replaced as for making the product.

FY26 was a mixed year. Piramal's contract business shrank 10% as a large customer cut inventory; OneSource absorbed the costs of new lines; Akums carried losses in its API arm. Combined revenue rose only 8%. Then the first quarter of FY27 (April–June 2026) turned sharply: combined revenue rose 25.5% to ₹9,408 crore and combined EBITDA 69%, lifting the margin from 19.4% to 26.1%. Divi's custom synthesis grew 45%, Neuland's commercial CMS revenue more than tripled, OneSource grew 37% on semaglutide pens, and Piramal's contract arm returned to 19% growth.

Three currents explain where the business is heading. The first is the US BIOSECURE Act, signed into law on 18 December 2025, which over the next decade pushes US federal money away from Chinese biotech suppliers. It is real but slow: its effect runs through federal contracts and fully lands only in the early 2030s. The second is GLP-1 drugs for diabetes and obesity, which the six serve at four different layers of the supply chain. The third is the rebound in biotech funding, which reaches a CDMO's revenue 12–24 months after the money is raised.

In plain terms

These six companies all make medicines for other people, but they are in different trades. Divi's and Neuland make the chemical heart of patented drugs and are very hard to replace. Gland fills sterile injections. OneSource is building pen-filling and biologics plants and has not yet reached full speed. Akums makes everyday tablets and syrups for Indian brands on thin margins. The market pays the most for the businesses that are hardest to replace, which is why Divi's is worth twenty times Akums on similar-looking revenue.

The market's verdict is as varied as the businesses. On FY26 numbers and 7 October prices, Akums trades at about 22 times EBITDA (enterprise value over operating profit), Piramal at about 27 times on its own EBITDA, Gland at 30, Neuland at 48, OneSource at 61 and Divi's at 73. Those multiples are high by any global standard; Western contract manufacturers trade at a fraction of them. Section 17 takes the valuation question apart. The short answer is that investors are paying for lock-in quality and for India's share gain from China, and are assuming both arrive faster than the evidence so far suggests.

What this report covers, and what it leaves out. This report is about manufacturing for hire: the client owns the molecule, brand or dossier and a member makes the API, the intermediate, the sterile fill or the finished dose. Discovery and research services, the "R" in CRDMO, belong to the sibling CRDMO report on research-led contract companies (Syngene, Sai Life Sciences, Anthem Biosciences); they appear here only as comparators. API-first companies that also run contract arms, such as Laurus, Cohance and Aarti Pharmalabs, are covered in the API and intermediates report. Gland's US generic partners are discussed in the export generics report, and the Indian brand owners who are Akums' clients in the branded formulations report. The large integrated companies, some of which run their own captive contract arms, are in the diversified majors report.

01

How a contract maker earns: the recipe belongs to someone else

A CDMO is paid for three things: solving a chemistry or sterility problem, being named in a regulatory filing, and running a plant full. The money is in the third; the moat is in the second.

The vocabulary of this business is dense, so it is worth defining once, at the start. Each term below recurs through the report.

  • CMO, CDMO, CRDMO. A CMO makes to the client's recipe. A CDMO also develops it: process chemistry, formulation, scale-up, and the chemistry-manufacturing-and-controls (CMC) section of the regulatory filing. A CRDMO adds discovery research. The six in this report are CDMOs and CMOs.
  • Custom synthesis, CMS, CRAMS. Three names for the same thing. Divi's says custom synthesis; Neuland says custom manufacturing solutions (CMS); the older industry term is CRAMS (contract research and manufacturing services). All mean making an innovator's patented active ingredient, or an advanced intermediate, exclusively for that innovator.
  • NDA, ANDA and DMF. An innovator's New Drug Application (NDA) names every source of its active ingredient and key intermediates. A generic company files an Abbreviated New Drug Application (ANDA), which refers to the API maker's Drug Master File (DMF). Being named in either is the lock-in.
  • The phase funnel. Preclinical, Phase I, II, III, then commercial launch. A molecule's value to its CDMO rises by roughly an order of magnitude at each step towards commercial supply.
  • Validation batches (PPQ). Process performance qualification batches, run before a regulator approves a new site or process. They earn revenue but are not recurring supply. Part of Divi's 45% jump in Q1 FY27 came from them.
  • Fill-finish and DDC. Fill-finish is the aseptic filling of a sterile drug into vials, prefilled syringes or cartridges. A drug-device combination (DDC) goes further and assembles the filled cartridge into a pen or auto-injector.
  • HPAPI and ADC. A high-potency API needs special containment. An antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) links an antibody to a toxic payload and needs both chemistry and sterile capability.
  • Loan licence. In Indian domestic formulation, the brand owner sells under its own name but the manufacturing licence sits with the CMO's plant. Akums operates on this model.

The flow shows why the business is lumpy. Step 2 is where relationships are formed, but it generates little money: Neuland's development revenue in Q1 FY27 was ₹20 crore against ₹418 crore of commercial supply. Step 3 is where the money is, but it arrives only when a client's molecule succeeds in Phase III, wins approval and launches. Between the two sit years of waiting and a funnel in which most candidates fail. A CDMO with many molecules in late-stage trials has a visible pipeline of future revenue; one that depends on a few commercial molecules has concentrated revenue whose fate is tied to those drugs.

How the deal is struck differs by archetype

The contract itself changes with the archetype. An innovator CDMO signs a multi-year supply agreement for one molecule with one client, often cost-plus or with volume tiers, and sometimes the client co-funds a dedicated plant. A merchant API maker sells at market prices to many generic companies and competes with Chinese cost. A sterile CMO for US generics is paid a supply price and, often, a share of the partner's profit on the product. A drug-device CDMO signs a master services agreement, then a commercial supply agreement with per-unit fees and capacity-reservation payments. A domestic formulation CMO charges a conversion fee per unit, with raw materials passed through.

Read across the rows and the economics follow. The innovator CDMO has the best pricing and the stickiest customer, but the least control over volume: if the client's drug loses its patent, volume falls whatever the CDMO does. The merchant API maker controls its own volume but not its price. The sterile CMO sits between, protected by the scarcity of qualified sterile capacity but exposed to US generic price erosion through its partners. The drug-device CDMO has the highest capital need and the longest ramp, and the highest margin if it fills its lines. The domestic CMO has the lowest capital need and the fastest payback, and the thinnest margin, because its clients own the brand and can in-source if the price is wrong.

In plain terms

A contract maker is paid in three currencies. It earns a little for solving a client's technical problem, it earns a lot when that problem turns into a drug that sells, and it earns protection from being written into the drug's approval papers. The best ones collect all three on many drugs at once. The weaker ones collect only the manufacturing fee, which competitors can undercut.

One more mechanism matters: who pays for the plant. When Divi's builds ₹2,000 crore of dedicated capacity for three molecules under long-term contracts, the client has effectively underwritten the volume. When OneSource builds roughly $100 million of cartridge and pen lines before its customers' products are approved in most markets, it carries the utilisation risk itself, and funds part of it with debt. The same capex rupee carries very different risk depending on whether a contract stands behind it. This is why investors ask not just "how much capex?" but "whose capex?"

Finally, the client's choice to outsource is not permanent. An innovator outsources because it lacks capacity, lacks a particular capability (a peptide synthesiser, a containment suite), or wants to spend its capital on research and marketing. If a molecule becomes strategic, the client may bring it in-house: Novo Holdings' 2024 purchase of Catalent, with three fill-finish sites going to Novo Nordisk for its GLP-1 drugs, is the largest recent example. A CDMO's best protection against in-sourcing is to be cheaper, faster or technically better than the client could be on its own, and to be named in the filing before the client thinks about it.

02

A $200 billion market where India holds 2–3%

Estimates of the global contract-manufacturing market vary by $90 billion depending on scope. On the most consistent definition, India is a small player growing faster than the market, from a base one-seventh of China's.

Market-size estimates in this industry disagree because publishers draw the boundary in different places: some include finished-dose manufacturing and packaging, some exclude biologics, some count captive manufacturing that is merely billed between group entities. Grand View puts the 2025 pharma CDMO market at $166 billion, growing 7.4% a year to $235.5 billion in 2030. Mordor puts it at $258.9 billion, growing 6.4% to $353.2 billion, with APIs 55% of the total. Fortune Business Insights says $255 billion. A composite of the three lands at roughly $220 billion growing about 7.5% a year.

This report anchors on ICRA's February 2026 framing of a $180–200 billion global CRDMO market, because ICRA pairs it with an India estimate on the same definition. On that basis India holds 2–3% of the global market, China 18–21%, and the wider Asia-Pacific region 21–26%. The United States and Europe, home to Lonza, Thermo Fisher's Patheon, Catalent, Siegfried and Cambrex, hold the large remainder.

How much of the work is actually outsourced? Roughly 30–40% of small-molecule API manufacturing value, 20–30% of biologic drug substance, and more for fill-finish and drug-device assembly. The single most important structural fact is that virtual and emerging biotech companies, which own about two-thirds of the clinical pipeline, outsource almost everything: they have no plants. This is why biotech funding is the leading indicator for the whole industry, and why the 2022–25 funding winter showed up as FY26 weakness at Indian CDMOs.

In plain terms

The world spends about $200 billion a year paying other companies to make its medicines. India earns about $3.5–4 billion of that, roughly a seventh of what China earns. The forecasts say India's share doubles over a decade. The six companies in this report already do about half of India's contract work, so any share gain shows up quickly in their numbers.

The scale gap with the global leaders is stark. Lonza books about CHF 7 billion a year; WuXi AppTec guides to RMB 58.5–60.5 billion for 2026, about ₹70,000 crore; Samsung Biologics booked KRW 2,578 billion in the first half alone. Divi's, the largest Indian CDMO, has FY26 revenue of ₹10,560 crore, about $1.2 billion, of which only 55% is custom synthesis. Indian contract manufacturers are one-fifth to one-tenth the size of the global leaders. That is a source of opportunity (room to grow) and of risk (customers giving a large programme to an Indian supplier are betting on a much smaller balance sheet).

03

Who buys, and why 2026 turned

Contract revenue is the last link in a chain that starts with a venture capitalist writing a cheque. The chain has a 12–24 month lag, and in 2026 it finally pulled.

Seven forces drive demand for contract manufacturing, and they do not move together. Understanding which one is driving a given company's quarter is the core skill in reading this sub-sector.

1. Innovator outsourcing and the biotech funding cycle

Emerging biotechs own most of the clinical pipeline and outsource almost all manufacturing, so their funding is the leading indicator. The funding winter of 2022–25, caused by higher interest rates, a collapse in biotech share prices and, in 2025, cuts to US National Institutes of Health budgets, starved the pipeline of new programmes. ICRA attributes the Indian sector's modest 8–10% growth in FY26 to it. In 2026 the cycle turned. US biopharma venture funding reached $16.3 billion across 235 rounds in the first half, $9.2 billion of it in the second quarter, pacing towards about $33 billion for the year. Biotech IPOs raised $5 billion across 13 offerings in the first half, more than in any full year from 2022 to 2025. The XBI biotech index rose 23.9% in the second quarter to a five-year high.

5. Patent cliffs cut both ways

For Akums the drivers are domestic: volume growth of the Indian pharma market (about 9–11% a year), brand companies' wish to avoid capex, and the revised Schedule M good-manufacturing rules, which require small manufacturers to upgrade their plants and are squeezing the long tail of tiny contract makers. Each of these pushes volume towards large, compliant CMOs.

What drives margin within an archetype

Within the same archetype, five levers explain most of the difference between a good quarter and a bad one.

  • The commercial share of contract revenue. Neuland's commercial CMS revenue was ₹418 crore in Q1 FY27 against ₹20 crore of development revenue: 95% commercial, which is why its EBITDA margin reached 35.5%. In FY25, when commercial ordering paused, its margin fell to about 23%.
  • Utilisation. Divi's runs its facilities at about 80–85%. Neuland's margin moves from the low twenties to about 40% between half-full and full quarters, because most of a chemical plant's cost is fixed.
  • Mix of hard modalities. Peptides, high-potency compounds, ADCs, sterile fills and drug-device assembly carry higher prices because fewer sites can do them.
  • Inventory gains and raw-material pass-through. When raw-material prices rise and contracts pass them on with a lag, a company holding cheap inventory earns a temporary gain. PL Capital flags inventory gains inside Divi's 67.9% Q1 FY27 gross margin.
  • Absorbing the fixed cost of new sites. OneSource's margin fell from 32% to 21% in FY26 as new lines added cost before revenue, then recovered to 27.5% in Q1 FY27. Gland's French subsidiary Cenexi took two to three years to reach EBITDA break-even after the 2023 acquisition.
In plain terms

Some contract makers earn a fat margin on expensive plants that take years to fill; others earn a thin margin on cheap plants that fill quickly. Both can make a decent return. What hurts is the expensive plant that does not fill, or the cheap plant whose clients leave. So the right question for each company is not "is the margin high?" but "is the margin normal for this kind of business, at this point in its build?"

The archetype lens also explains why two companies in the same archetype can look so different in a given year. Divi's and Neuland are both innovator CDMOs with merchant-API arms, but Divi's has 18–20 commercial projects and a dominant generic franchise to smooth its quarters, while Neuland's contract revenue rests on a handful of products. Divi's quarterly EBITDA margin moved between 30% and 41% over the last five quarters; Neuland's moved between 12% and 40%. Same archetype, very different volatility, because of concentration, which Section 7 examines.

A final caution on the archetypes: the boundaries are blurring. Divi's is building peptide capacity that pushes it towards Neuland's territory. Gland now calls half its revenue "CDMO" and has signed a technology-transfer partnership with a global pharma company. Akums' European contract is a step from domestic loan-licence work towards regulated-market CDMO. Piramal combines innovator CDMO with a sterile anaesthesia business and Indian consumer brands. Investors should value each company's mix, segment by segment, not apply a single "CDMO multiple" to the whole.

The molecule funnel and lock-in: why contract revenue is sticky and lumpy at once Deep dive

A contract maker named in an approved drug's filing is very hard to replace. But it rises and falls with that drug. Both facts come from the same source, and reading a CDMO means holding them together.

Every innovator CDMO lives inside its clients' drug pipelines. The pipeline is a funnel: many molecules enter at Phase I, a fraction reach Phase III, and fewer still launch. The CDMO's revenue from a molecule is tiny at the top of the funnel and large at the bottom, and its lock-in is weak at the top and strong at the bottom. This section explains how a project travels down the funnel, why the end of the funnel locks a supplier in, and why the same lock-in concentrates risk.

How a project becomes commercial supply

A typical journey runs like this. A biotech with a Phase I candidate sends a request for proposal to several CDMOs for a few kilograms of the active ingredient. The winner develops a workable chemical route and supplies clinical batches; revenue is a few crore. If the molecule reaches Phase II, the route is optimised for scale and cost, and the supplier that did this work is usually kept, because changing chemistry mid-trial invites regulatory questions. At Phase III, the CDMO makes larger batches, runs process validation and is written into the drug's application as a named manufacturing site. If the drug is approved and launched, the CDMO supplies tonnes a year under a multi-year agreement, often for the life of the patent.

The value of a project jumps at each step. A commercial molecule can be worth ₹50–300 crore a year of revenue to its CDMO, against a few crore in Phase I. That is why companies talk about their funnel, and why the number of Phase III molecules is the best single predictor of contract revenue three to five years out.

Why the lock-in exists

Lock-in is a regulatory fact, not a commercial courtesy. When an innovator wants to change the manufacturer of an approved drug's active ingredient, it must file a prior-approval supplement (or, for lower-risk changes, a "changes being effected" supplement) with the US FDA and equivalent variations in Europe and elsewhere. The new site must run validation batches, generate stability data that can take months to accumulate, and may need a pre-approval inspection. The process typically takes 12–24 months and carries the risk of a supply gap for a drug that may be earning billions. Unless the existing supplier fails an inspection, cannot meet volume, or has become a political liability, the innovator has little reason to bear that cost. That is why commercial supply contracts tend to last as long as the patent.

The same mechanism explains why WuXi AppTec kept growing through 2026 despite US political pressure: its commercial customers face exactly the same switching cost. China+1 therefore shows up first in new programmes, not in the installed base (Section 8).

In plain terms

Once a factory is written into a drug's approval papers, swapping it out takes a year or two of paperwork and test batches, and risks running out of the drug. So drug companies almost never switch. That protects the factory's share. It does not protect its volume: if the drug itself stops selling, because a generic arrives or doctors move on, the factory's orders fall with it.

The other side of lock-in: concentration

Because one commercial molecule can be worth hundreds of crore a year, a CDMO's revenue often rests on a few products. Neuland is the extreme case. Its top five CMS products were 96% of CMS revenue, and CMS was 67% of the company in Q1 FY27, so five products were about 64% of Neuland's total revenue. That is the single most important risk number in the sub-sector. If one of those five clients changes its ordering calendar, Neuland's quarter moves by tens of percent.

Three kinds of lumpiness, and how to tell them apart

  • Ordering-calendar lumpiness. The client's drug is selling normally, but the client orders API in a few large campaigns a year. Revenue swings quarter to quarter but is stable year to year. Neuland's FY26 is mostly this. Read it by looking at trailing-twelve-month revenue, not quarters.
  • Destocking. The client built excess inventory (often after a supply scare or a launch that undershot) and stops ordering until it is used up. Revenue can fall for several quarters while patient demand is fine. Piramal's FY26, with one large contract going to nil sales for FY27, is the clearest current example.
  • Lifecycle loss. The client's drug loses its patent or its market. Volume falls permanently. Divi's Entresto exposure is the case to watch: the US generic arrived in July 2025, yet custom synthesis grew 45% in Q1 FY27, so new projects have more than replaced it for now. The post-generic run-rate on Entresto itself is not disclosed.

A fourth source of noise deserves its own mention: validation batches. Before a dedicated plant starts commercial supply, it runs qualification batches that are invoiced to the client. Divi's Q1 FY27 custom-synthesis surge came partly from validation batches for its three dedicated projects. These are real revenue, but they are not a run-rate. Management's guidance for FY27 EBITDA margin "in line with FY26" (about 33%) is the clue that the 41% Q1 margin will not hold all year.

A worked example: what one molecule does to Neuland

Neuland's Q1 FY27 CMS revenue was ₹418 crore commercial plus ₹20 crore development, ₹438 crore in all, or 67% of company income. Development revenue was only 4.6% of CMS, but it feeds the 99-project funnel. Suppose one Phase III molecule converts to commercial supply worth ₹150–250 crore a year. That would add 7–12% to Neuland's FY26 revenue of ₹2,023 crore. At a 40–50% incremental EBITDA margin, typical when a plant's fixed costs are already covered, it adds ₹60–125 crore of EBITDA, or 10–20% of FY26's ₹580 crore. This is ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic, but it shows why a single molecule approval can move a mid-cap CDMO's earnings by a fifth, and why a single molecule loss can do the same in reverse.

In plain terms

A mid-sized contract maker's results can jump or fall by a third in a quarter because two or three customers changed when they wanted delivery. To judge the business, add up the last four quarters, or better the last two or three years, and ask how many separate drugs the profit really depends on. One new approved drug can lift profit by a fifth; one lost drug can cut it by as much.

The practical lesson for investors is to read CDMO earnings over two to three years at once, to weight disclosures about the funnel (especially Phase III counts) more heavily than any single quarter's growth, and to treat concentration as the first question, not a footnote. A company with a hundred projects and five products paying the bills is a different risk from one with twenty commercial molecules and no single product above a fifth of revenue, even if both report the same growth rate this year.

BIOSECURE and China+1: a law with a long fuse Deep dive

The US BIOSECURE Act is real, but it works through federal money, on a statutory timetable that runs into the 2030s. China's largest CDMO grew 39% in the first half of 2026. India's opportunity is a decade-long share shift, not a windfall.

No subject generates more loose talk about Indian CDMOs than BIOSECURE. The common version runs: the US has banned Chinese contract manufacturers, so their business is moving to India. Both halves of that sentence are wrong, or at least badly premature. This section sets out what the law actually does, what happened to China's leading CDMO in 2026, and what evidence exists of business moving to the six.

What the law does

BIOSECURE was enacted as part of the National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal 2026, signed on 18 December 2025. It bars US executive agencies from procuring biotechnology equipment or services from a "biotechnology company of concern". It also bars them from contracting with, or giving grants and loans to, any entity that uses such equipment or services in performing the federal contract. That second limb is what reaches commercial pharma companies: a drug-maker with US government contracts or grants has a reason to keep Chinese suppliers out of the relevant work.

What the law does not do is ban private commercial contracts. A European or American drug company that sells its medicine commercially and holds no federal contract for it is not prohibited from using a Chinese CDMO. The effect runs through federal money and through the diligence and reputational choices of large pharma companies, which tend to over-comply.

Who counts as a company of concern? Three groups: companies on the Defense Department's Section 1260H list of "Chinese military companies" that are involved in biotechnology (the final text added this biotech link); companies on a new list the Office of Management and Budget must publish, of entities controlled by a foreign adversary (China, Russia, Iran, North Korea) that pose a national-security risk; and their affiliates. Unlike the version that passed the House in September 2024, the final law does not name WuXi AppTec, WuXi Biologics, BGI, MGI or Complete Genomics. BGI and MGI were already on the 1260H list.

Read the timeline closely. Commentators who say legacy contracts are "grandfathered to 2032" are roughly right about the end point but skip the regulatory steps in between. The earliest a prohibition bites on a new federal contract is about 2028; full wind-down of existing relationships is about 2033. Five years is roughly the life of a commercial supply agreement. A drug company with a Chinese supplier for a product launched in 2025 can keep that supplier for most of the product's patent life.

In plain terms

The US law does not stop drug companies from using Chinese contract makers. It stops the US government from paying companies that use them for government work, and it gives existing contracts years to run off. Most of its force arrives after 2028 and is complete only around 2033. It nudges new projects away from China; it does not move existing ones overnight.

What actually happened to WuXi in 2026

The sharper 2026 shock came not from BIOSECURE but from the Pentagon's 1260H list. In February 2026 the Defense Department posted, then withdrew, a list including WuXi AppTec. In June it added WuXi AppTec to the 188-company list. On 8 August 2026 Chief Judge Boasberg of the US District Court for the District of Columbia granted a preliminary injunction barring the department from enforcing the designation, finding it likely "arbitrary and capricious". The court recorded that WuXi's customers and suppliers had cancelled contracts, ended relationships and moved business to competitors in the interval.

And yet WuXi AppTec's first-half 2026 revenue was RMB 28.9 billion, up 38.9%; continuing-operations revenue rose 48%; its backlog reached RMB 66.4 billion, up 25.2%; it raised full-year guidance to RMB 58.5–60.5 billion; and its peptide and oligonucleotide (TIDES) business grew 44%. Western pharma has not abandoned WuXi. Commercial supply is too deeply embedded, for exactly the regulatory reasons that protect Divi's (Section 7).

Gland's Fosun paradox

One of the six is itself Chinese-controlled. Gland's promoter, Fosun Pharma, held 45.76% after selling 6% on 4 September 2026. Fosun is not on the 1260H list and is not named in the law. The other US lever: Section 232 tariffs

A proclamation on 2 April 2026 imposed a 100% tariff on patented drugs and their active ingredients imported into the US, with generics, their ingredients and biosimilars excluded. Companies with US onshoring commitments pay 20%; those with "most-favoured-nation" pricing deals plus onshoring pay nothing. An Indian CDMO making an innovator's patented API that is shipped to the US is in scope, but the tariff falls on the importer, the innovator, and most large innovators signed deals in 2025–26 that zero it out. The direct hit is therefore limited. The second-order effect matters more: innovators may move final manufacturing steps to the US, which favours Piramal's US sites (Lexington, Riverview, Sellersville) and Western competitors with US capacity. Divi's shares fell on the announcement; its custom synthesis ships largely to European innovators (Europe and the US together are 75% of revenue, split not disclosed).

In plain terms

India's chance comes from US drug companies wanting a second, non-Chinese supplier for new drugs, not from China losing its existing customers. That shift is happening, as inquiries at Indian firms show, but it will take most of a decade to change the market shares. Investors who price it as a sudden transfer are likely to be disappointed by the pace; those who ignore it miss a real, slow tailwind.

Regulation as the moat: inspections, observations and the cost of failure

The same inspection regime that locks a supplier in can lock it out. No member has a live warning letter or import alert in October 2026, but Piramal has the busiest inspection calendar and one open item.

Regulation in this sub-sector is both the moat and the main operational risk. A CDMO earns its lock-in by passing inspections at sites named in clients' filings; a single failed inspection can stop supply of several clients' commercial products at once.

The regimes

  • United States. The FDA inspects every site named in an NDA, ANDA or DMF. After an inspection it issues a Form 483 listing observations, if any, and classifies the outcome: NAI (no action indicated), VAI (voluntary action: observations to fix, no enforcement) or OAI (official action indicated). OAI can lead to a warning letter and, at worst, an import alert that blocks shipments. A new site needs a pre-approval inspection; a change of manufacturer needs a prior-approval supplement taking 12–24 months.
  • Europe. An EU-GMP certificate issued by a member-state inspectorate, release of each batch by a Qualified Person, and a Certificate of Suitability (CEP) for APIs.
  • India. The central drug regulator (CDSCO) and state regulators. The revised Schedule M good-manufacturing rules, with a deadline of 31 December 2025 for small manufacturers, are forcing plant upgrades across the domestic formulation tail.
  • Client audits. Innovators audit their CDMOs more often than regulators do. OneSource reported 49 regulatory inspections and customer audits in FY26 with zero critical observations. Audit outcomes are not public, which is a real disclosure gap for investors.

The case study: Divi's Unit-II, 2017

In March 2017 the FDA placed Divi's Unit-II at Visakhapatnam under an import alert, followed by a warning letter in April. Unit-II was a major site for both custom synthesis and generic APIs, so the alert threatened supply of several innovators' commercial molecules at once, and the shares fell sharply. Divi's remediated quickly; the alert was lifted within months and a later re-inspection closed. The episode is the best illustration of the two-sided nature of regulation: the same naming in clients' filings that makes Divi's hard to replace also means an inspection failure hits many clients simultaneously. That Divi's kept its customers through the episode is itself evidence of how strong the lock-in is.

In plain terms

Regulators inspect these factories regularly. A clean inspection is what lets a factory keep its place in drug approvals. A bad one can stop shipments of many customers' drugs at once. Today none of the six faces a serious sanction; Piramal has the most inspections to manage and one result still pending from its UK plant.

Two points about reading inspection news. First, the number of observations matters less than their nature: a single data-integrity finding (records altered, tests repeated until they pass) is far more serious than seven procedural points about documentation. Second, the classification takes months: the market often reacts to the 483 and then again to the classification. For Akums, regulation is a growth driver rather than a risk: Schedule M is pushing small competitors out, and its EU-GMP certificate opened the European contract.

Whose capex? The question that decides the return

The pipeline splits into two kinds. Contract-backed capex is built for named customers under long-term agreements: Divi's three dedicated projects, Neuland's peptide Module 1 with its firm commitments, Akums' European site funded by the customer's advance. Its utilisation risk is largely carried by the client. Speculative capex is built ahead of contracts, in the expectation that demand will come: much of OneSource's cartridge programme, Piramal's ADC expansion, Divi's additional peptide reactors. Its return depends on the company winning work to fill it.

A worked example shows how good contract-backed capex can be. Divi's ₹2,000 crore of dedicated capex, at its normal asset turn of 1.2–1.5 times, would support ₹2,400–3,000 crore of peak annual revenue, 23–28% on top of FY26. At a 35–40% EBITDA margin that is ₹840–1,200 crore of EBITDA, and a pre-tax return on the project of roughly 35–50% before working capital. These are ILLUSTRATIVE figures built on Divi's historical ratios; the contracts' actual terms are not disclosed, and supply depends on client and regulatory approvals in CY2027.

Four decades, three cycles: from CRAMS to COVID to the 2026 turn

Indian contract manufacturing has boomed and busted three times, each cycle driven by innovator inventories and biotech funding rather than by patients. The fourth upswing began in the April–June 2026 quarter.

The six companies have very different ages. Gland was founded in 1978 as a maker of injectables for others; Neuland in 1984; Divi's in 1990 as a research centre. Akums dates from 2004. Piramal Pharma and OneSource are, as listed entities, products of the 2020s: Piramal was demerged from Piramal Enterprises in 2022, and OneSource was carved out of Strides and listed in January 2025. The cycles they have lived through share a pattern: a period in which innovators outsource more and order ahead, followed by one in which they cut inventory and new projects dry up.

  • 1978–1995
    Founders' era. Gland (1978, P.V.N. Raju) as a parenteral contract maker; Neuland (1984); Divi's Research Centre (1990), renamed Divi's Laboratories (1994); Divi's Unit-I at Choutuppal (1995).
  • 2000s
    CRAMS boom, wave one. Neuland's first FDA approval (2000); Divi's Unit-II (2002) and IPO (2003); Piramal buys Avecia's pharma business and others to build its contract arm, including Morpeth via Pfizer (2006).
  • 2008–2012
    First CRAMS bust: big-pharma restructuring and inventory cuts. Piramal sells its domestic formulations business to Abbott for about $3.7 bn (2010), funding its contract and critical-care growth.
  • 2017
    Divi's Unit-II import alert and warning letter, lifted within months. Fosun buys ~74% of Gland for about $1.1 bn.
  • 2020–2021
    COVID boom. Divi's makes molnupiravir API for MSD; Gland IPO (Nov 2020, ~₹6,480 Cr); Carlyle invests ~$490 m for 20% of Piramal's pharma business.
  • 2022–2025
    Destocking bust: post-COVID inventory cuts, a biotech funding winter and higher rates. Divi's revenue falls from its FY22 peak; Piramal swings to a loss in FY23. Piramal Pharma lists (Oct 2022); Gland buys Cenexi (Jan 2023); Akums IPO (Aug 2024); OneSource lists (Jan 2025).
  • 2024–2025
    BIOSECURE introduced (Jan 2024), passes the House (Sep 2024), dropped from that year's NDAA; Novo Holdings closes Catalent (Dec 2024); US generic Entresto launches (Jul 2025); BIOSECURE signed in the FY26 NDAA (18 Dec 2025).
  • 2026
    Semaglutide generics in India, Brazil and Canada; Section 232 tariffs (Apr); WuXi on the 1260H list (Jun), enjoined (Aug); Q1 FY27 combined revenue +25.5% (Jul–Aug); Fosun sells 6% of Gland (Sep).

The 2020–21 COVID period was the most extreme boom. Innovators and governments ordered vaccines, antivirals and supportive-care drugs in volume, and many built safety stocks of everything else. Divi's made the active ingredient for MSD's antiviral molnupiravir; Gland listed at a premium valuation in November 2020; Carlyle bought into Piramal. The bust that followed was equally sharp. In 2022–23 innovators ran down the stocks they had built, while rising interest rates closed the funding window for biotechs. Divi's revenue fell from its FY22 peak; Piramal's contract business slowed and the group posted a loss in FY23; Neuland's revenue dipped in FY25 as commercial orders paused. In FY26 Piramal's contract revenue fell another 10% as one large customer destocked.

What do the cycles teach? First, contract revenue is more cyclical than end demand for drugs, because inventories amplify swings: when an innovator decides to hold three months less stock, its supplier loses a quarter's orders even if patients take exactly the same number of pills. Second, each bust has been followed by a stronger industry, because weaker players exit and innovators consolidate suppliers. Third, the companies that came through best were those with net cash and a broad commercial base: Divi's through 2008–12 and 2022–24, Gland through Cenexi's losses. Piramal and OneSource, the two with debt, are the ones whose recoveries matter most for their balance sheets this time.

In plain terms

Drug companies stock up when they are worried about shortages and cut back when money is tight. Their suppliers feel both swings more than the drug companies do. India's contract makers had a boom in 2020–21, a slump in 2022–25 and started recovering in 2026. Every slump so far has ended with the strongest firms bigger and the weakest gone.

Reading a CDMO: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Revenue growth is the least informative number a contract manufacturer reports. The funnel, the concentration, the commercial mix, the utilisation and the inspection record tell you what next year looks like. This section explains each metric, how to compute it, what good looks like, and where the definitions mislead.

Contract manufacturers are hard to analyse from the outside because they disclose little and what they disclose is defined differently from company to company. None of the six reports an order book. Only some report project counts, and none reports them on the same basis. Margins are struck on different definitions; "CDMO revenue" means different things at Gland and at Divi's. This masterclass works through the metrics that matter, in the order an experienced analyst would use them, with the six companies' numbers where they exist and "n/d" (not disclosed) where they do not.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). Take a mid-sized innovator CDMO supplying 10 commercial molecules, each taking 10 tonnes a year at ₹50,000 a kilogram: ₹50 crore per molecule, ₹500 crore in all. Add 40 development projects earning ₹1.25 crore each, ₹50 crore. Revenue is ₹550 crore. With a 63% gross margin, gross profit is about ₹347 crore; operating costs of 30% of revenue (₹165 crore) leave EBITDA of about ₹182 crore, a 33% margin. Now suppose one client's drug loses its patent and its volume halves: revenue falls ₹25 crore, but because most costs are fixed, EBITDA falls by roughly ₹16 crore (the gross profit lost), or 9%. Suppose instead one development project converts into an eleventh commercial molecule: revenue rises ₹50 crore and EBITDA by about ₹30 crore, 17%. The asymmetry, where a small change in the molecule count moves profit far more than revenue, is what defines the business.

The other archetypes have simpler equations. For a sterile or pen-filling CMO: revenue = lines × sterile days a year × batches a day × units a batch × yield × fee per unit, plus any capacity-reservation fees. For a domestic formulation CMO: revenue = capacity in units × utilisation × realisation per unit, and EBITDA = revenue × conversion spread. Akums' numbers make the second concrete: about 49 billion units of capacity, perhaps 65–70% utilised (an estimate; Akums does not disclose it), and Q1 FY27 annualised CDMO revenue of about ₹3,856 crore imply roughly ₹1.1–1.2 of revenue and ₹0.19–0.20 of EBITDA per unit. Each extra billion units adds about ₹115 crore of revenue and ₹20 crore of EBITDA. These are ILLUSTRATIVE calculations on disclosed totals.

In plain terms

An innovator contract maker's sales are the number of approved drugs it makes, times how much of each its clients order, times the agreed price, plus small fees for early-stage work. The company decides how hard it chases new drugs and how efficiently it makes them. It does not decide how well its clients' drugs sell. That is why one new drug, or one lost drug, swings profit much more than sales.

KPI 1 · Contract (CDMO) share of revenue

What it is. The proportion of a company's revenue that comes from making products owned by clients, as opposed to its own generic APIs, own-brand formulations or other businesses. Formula: contract segment revenue ÷ total revenue, on the company's definition. Worked example: Divi's Q1 FY27 custom synthesis of ₹1,850 crore on revenue of ₹3,080 crore is 60%.

What drives it. Wins and losses of commercial molecules; the relative growth of other segments (Divi's generics and nutraceuticals, Akums' API and trade generics, Piramal's anaesthesia and consumer brands); and, more often than investors realise, changes in definition. Benchmarks: above 60% is good for a company sold as a CDMO, 40–60% average, below 40% weak. A higher share is not automatically better: Divi's generic franchise, with 65–85% global share in three molecules, is a stabiliser, not a drag.

KPI 3 · Commercial versus development mix

What it is. The split of contract revenue between recurring commercial supply and project-based development work. Formula: commercial contract revenue ÷ total contract revenue. Worked example: Neuland Q1 FY27: ₹418 crore commercial ÷ ₹438 crore CMS = 95%; development ₹20 crore, 4.6%. A year earlier, commercial was ₹120 crore and development ₹14 crore: 90% commercial on a far smaller base.

What drives it. Commercial launches of client drugs, client ordering calendars, and, on the development side, the flow of new programmes from funding. Benchmarks: there is no universal good number. A high commercial share means current profit is strong; a rising development line means future commercial revenue is being seeded. The best signal is both rising together. A commercial share near 100% with a shrinking development line is a warning that the funnel is not being refilled.

Definition trap. Validation batches before a commercial launch can be booked in either line depending on the company. Divi's Q1 FY27 custom synthesis included validation batches for its dedicated projects; they are revenue, but not a run-rate. Only Neuland among the six gives the split routinely.

In plain terms

Development work is the seed; commercial supply is the harvest. A company living off its harvest without planting new seeds will look great this year and worse in three. A company whose development line is growing is building its future, even if this year's profit is flat. Ask for both numbers, every quarter.

KPI 4 · Customer and product concentration

What it is. How much revenue depends on the largest client or the few largest products. Formula: top client (or top-5 products) revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Neuland's top-5 CMS products were 96% of CMS, and CMS was 67% of revenue, so the five were about 64% of the company. For Divi's, an analyst estimate (not company-disclosed) puts Entresto at about 40% of custom synthesis; at a 55% CS share that is about 22% of revenue, an ILLUSTRATIVE figure.

What drives it. The stage of the company's development (young CDMOs depend on a few early wins), the size of its commercial molecules, and whether it has a diversifying non-contract business. Benchmarks: top client below 15% of revenue is good, 15–30% average, above 30% weak. On this test Neuland is weak, Divi's (on the estimate) average, Akums good (1,524 clients, none disclosed as large). Piramal, OneSource and Gland do not disclose; Piramal's admission that one large contract will have no sales in FY27 shows concentration matters there too. The chart in Section 7 lays out the disclosed figures.

Definition trap. Companies prefer to disclose product concentration within a segment ("top-5 = 96% of CMS") rather than concentration of the whole company. Always multiply through to total revenue.

KPI 5 · Order book and backlog (and why nobody gives one)

What it is. The value of signed contracts not yet delivered. Formula: backlog ÷ annual revenue, in years of cover. Worked example: WuXi AppTec reported a backlog of RMB 66.4 billion against full-year 2026 guidance of RMB 58.5–60.5 billion: about 1.1 years of cover. Among the six, the only sizeable named contract is Akums' €200 million over five years, about ₹2,000–2,200 crore at ₹100–110 per euro, or roughly half a year of Akums' FY26 revenue.

Why the six do not disclose. Innovator supply contracts usually specify prices and volume bands, not firm annual quantities; clients forecast and then order. A disclosed "order book" would be a forecast dressed as a fact. Companies therefore give proxies: funnels (Piramal, Neuland), targets (OneSource's FY28 $400 million), named contracts (Akums, Divi's three dedicated projects). Benchmarks where a backlog is disclosed: above 1.5 years good, 1–1.5 average, below 1 weak. No order book is disclosed by any of the six; this report does not estimate one.

Definition trap. "RFP funnel up four times" (OneSource) measures inquiries, not contracts. A request for proposal may be sent to five CDMOs; most do not convert.

KPI 6 · Gross margin

What it is. Revenue minus materials, as a share of revenue: the value added in the plant before people and overheads. Formula: (revenue − raw materials, KSMs, packaging, change in inventory) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Divi's Q1 FY27 company-reported gross margin was 67.9%, up 766 basis points, partly helped by inventory gains.

What drives it. The mix of contract versus merchant work, the share of hard modalities, raw-material prices and how quickly contracts pass them through, and geography (Western sites have higher gross margins, offset by labour). Benchmarks: above 65% good, 55–65% average, below 55% weak, for an export API or CDMO business. Domestic formulation runs structurally lower (Akums about 43%).

KPI 8 · Utilisation

What it is. Output as a share of capacity. Formula: actual output ÷ practical capacity, in reactor-hours, batches, sterile line-days or units. Worked example: OneSource's cartridge Line 1 is available 225 sterile days a year; the company says it was at full revenue in Q1 FY27. Line 2 adds another 225 days from Q2 FY27, and the target is 475 sterile days in FY28. If Line 2 runs 100 days in its first year, the company's cartridge utilisation is about 72% (325 of 450 days), even though Line 1 is full.

What drives it. Client orders, the funnel's conversion to commercial supply, and the pace at which new capacity comes online. Because 50–60% of a plant's operating cost is fixed, utilisation is the main driver of margin within an archetype. Benchmarks: 80–90% is good (above 90% risks missed deliveries and changeover problems), 60–80% average, below 60% weak.

Who discloses. Divi's says about 80–85% across its facilities. OneSource gives line-level sterile days. Gland, Neuland, Piramal and Akums do not disclose utilisation. Akums' capacity of 49.23 billion units dates from its 2024 offer document.

Definition trap. Utilisation of what? A multipurpose API plant can be "full" in reactor-hours while running low-value molecules. Sterile capacity is often quoted as "sterile days", which excludes maintenance and changeover; a line at 225 days is not running at 62% of the calendar, it is running at full practical capacity.

KPI 9 · Asset turnover

What it is. How much revenue each rupee of fixed assets generates. Formula: revenue ÷ (net block + capital work-in-progress); a second version excludes CWIP to show how productive the completed plants are. Worked example: Akums: ₹4,359 crore of revenue on roughly ₹1,630 crore of net block and CWIP gives 2.67×. Divi's: ₹10,560 crore on about ₹8,650 crore gives 1.22×; excluding ₹2,113 crore of CWIP, 1.62×.

What drives it. The archetype (domestic formulation plants are cheap per unit of revenue; biologics plants are expensive), utilisation, the price of the product, and the stage of the build cycle. Benchmarks: above 1.5× good, 1.0–1.5× average, below 1.0× weak, with the archetype ranges in Section 5 as the finer yardstick.

KPI 11 · ROCE, and ROCE excluding work-in-progress

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital used to earn it. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), averaged over the year. The ex-CWIP version removes plants not yet operating: ROCE × capital employed ÷ (capital employed − CWIP). Worked example: Divi's reported ROCE is 22.0%. With ₹2,113 crore of CWIP inside capital employed of roughly ₹17,000 crore (an approximation), the ex-CWIP figure is about 25%: the operating plants earn more than the headline suggests.

What drives it. Margin × asset turn, as Section 5 showed; plus, in this sub-sector, the size of idle cash and of unproductive construction. Benchmarks: above 20% good, 12–20% average, below 12% weak. Neuland (26.5%) and Divi's (22.0%) are good; Gland (15.1%) and Akums (14.9%) average; Piramal (2.5%) and OneSource (0.6%) weak on trough and ramp earnings. The ROCE chart in Section 11 shows both versions.

Definition trap. Gland's 15.1% is held down by more than ₹3,000 crore of cash in its capital base; its operating return is higher. OneSource's near-zero figure reflects carve-out intangibles and plants in ramp, not the economics of a full line. Compare ROCE across the cycle, not in one year.

KPI 15 · The lead indicator: biotech funding

What it is. The money flowing into the companies that place most new development work. Formula: US biopharma venture funding plus IPO proceeds, quarterly. Worked example: $9.2 billion of venture funding in Q2 2026 is above the $8 billion-a-quarter "good" threshold; $16.3 billion in the first half paces to about $33 billion for the year.

Benchmarks: above $8 billion a quarter, with the XBI index rising, is good; $5–8 billion average; below $5 billion weak. How to use it: funding leads RFPs by months and contract revenue by 12–24 months or more, so it says more about FY28 than FY27. Definition trap. Headline funding is concentrated in a few mega-rounds; a quarter with two $1 billion rounds can look healthy while early-stage biotechs, the ones that outsource development, are still short of money.

In plain terms

The best early warning for contract makers is how much money investors are giving young biotech companies. Those companies have no factories, so when they raise money they hire contract makers. When the funding dries up, new projects stop a year or two later. In 2026 the funding came back strongly, which should help orders in 2027 and 2028.

Definition traps, collected

  • "CDMO revenue" has no standard definition. Gland's 23% (old, base business) and 50% (new, total incl. Cenexi) cannot be chained. Divi's "generics 40%" includes nutraceuticals.
  • Screener vs company EBITDA. Piramal: ₹922 crore vs ₹1,135 crore in FY26. Ratios built on one are not comparable with ratios built on the other.
  • Revenue vs total income. Neuland's FY26 revenue from operations is ₹2,023 crore; ₹2,053 crore is total income including other income. Its Q1 FY27 figure is ₹642 crore on screener, ₹650 crore from the company.
  • Reported vs adjusted profit. OneSource reported a FY26 loss of ₹74 crore; references to a "₹74 crore profit" are wrong. Its Q1 FY27 adjusted PAT was ₹64 crore against reported ₹25 crore.
  • Validation batches look like growth. They are invoiced before commercial supply starts but do not recur at that level.
  • Proxy gross margins. 100 minus screener material cost is not the company's gross margin.
  • Net cash that is not free. Akums' cash is largely a customer advance; Divi's calculated net cash understates its treasury.
  • Funnel counts on different bases. Projects, molecules, products and "commercialised or entering commercialisation" are not the same unit.
  • RFP growth is not order growth. A four-fold rise in requests for proposal measures interest, not signed work.
  • Asset turns distorted by intangibles. OneSource's 0.22× includes carve-out intangibles.
  • Currency translation. With 85–92% of Divi's and Neuland's revenue exported, a weaker rupee lifts reported growth; constant-currency growth is lower. Gland's FY27 guidance of 12–13% is in constant currency.
  • Dollar targets converted at old rates. OneSource's FY28 $400 million is ₹3,700–3,800 crore at ₹93–95, not the ₹3,400 crore sometimes quoted at ₹85.

Not every KPI matters equally for every archetype. For an innovator CDMO (Divi's, Neuland, Piramal's contract arm), the funnel, the commercial-versus-development mix and concentration come first, because they predict revenue two to four years out; margin and utilisation follow. For a sterile CMO such as Gland, the regulatory record and US price trends carry the most weight, because a sterile site's value rests on its inspection history and its partners' pricing. For a ramping drug-device or biologics CDMO such as OneSource, utilisation in sterile days, working capital and net debt matter most, because the question is whether the plants fill before the balance sheet tires. For a domestic formulation CMO such as Akums, the conversion margin, customer diversification and asset turn are the core, and regulated-market approvals the swing factor. Reading the six with one checklist in the same order flattens exactly the differences this report is about.

How to read a CDMO's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue by segment, not in total (1 minute). Pull contract revenue separately from generics, own brands or other businesses. Growth in the contract line is the signal; growth elsewhere is context.
  2. Commercial versus development (1 minute). If disclosed, check both lines. Commercial up and development flat means current strength, future risk. Development rising means the funnel is being refilled.
  3. Look for the words "validation" and "destocking" (1 minute). Management will usually say if a jump came from validation batches or a decline from a client cutting inventory. Neither is a run-rate.
  4. Trailing four quarters, not the quarter (1 minute). Add the last four quarters' revenue and EBITDA and compare with the prior four. For Neuland especially, a single quarter is noise.
  5. Gross margin and its cause (1 minute). A gross-margin jump in a period of rising raw-material prices may be an inventory gain. Ask whether management attributes it to mix or to inventory.
  6. EBITDA on a stated basis (1 minute). Note whether the margin is company-reported, adjusted or standard. Compare with the full-year guidance; a quarter far above guidance usually has a one-off inside.
  7. Funnel and concentration updates (1 minute). New Phase III molecules, new commercial launches, new customer logos; any mention of a large product or customer changing its ordering.
  8. Capex, CWIP and debt (1 minute). How much was spent, how much is still under construction, and how it was funded. Rising debt during a ramp is the red flag.
  9. Regulatory events (1 minute). Any inspection, Form 483, classification or warning letter in the quarter, by site.
  10. Guidance and its currency (1 minute). Has full-year guidance changed? Is it in constant currency? Is a dollar target converted at today's rate? Red flags: guidance cut without explanation, a margin target pushed out, or a new definition of "CDMO revenue" introduced in a strong quarter.
In plain terms

When a contract maker reports, ignore the headline growth first. Find out how much came from contract work, whether it was repeat supply or one-off batches, how the last full year compares with the year before, and whether the company borrowed to build. Then check for any trouble with regulators. Ten minutes on these questions tells you more than an hour on the press release.

The valuation puzzle: why 22× for one and 73× for another Deep dive

The six trade at about 103 times trailing earnings combined, and Divi's is 65% of their value. The spread of multiples is rational in direction. Whether it is right in size depends on growth assumptions that this section makes explicit.

Indian contract manufacturers are among the most richly valued pharma companies in the world. On 7 October 2026 the six were worth ₹3.92 lakh crore against FY26 net profit of ₹3,815 crore and EBITDA of ₹7,400 crore: about 103 times earnings and 53 times EBITDA. Most Western contract manufacturers trade at a fraction of that. This section asks two questions: why the multiples differ so much within the group, and what growth the highest multiples assume.

Why the multiples fan out

  • Lock-in quality. Divi's contract revenue is supply named in innovators' filings, built over decades with about 12 of the top-20 pharma companies, alongside generic APIs where it holds 65–85% global share. Akums' clients own their brands and can move volume or build their own plants. The market pays for revenue that is hard to take away.
  • Margin and returns. Divi's earns 33–41% EBITDA margins and 22% ROCE with net cash. Akums earns 12–15% and about 15% ROCE.
  • Visibility of growth. Divi's has three dedicated projects due in CY2027, peptides and contrast media. PL Capital models about 25% annual growth in EBITDA and profit over FY26–28. Akums has high-teens contract volume growth and margin creeping to 14–15%.
  • Scarcity and index weight. Divi's is a Nifty 50 stock and one of very few large, high-quality Indian CDMOs available to foreign and domestic institutions (20% and 19% holders). Akums has 75% promoter ownership and little free float.
  • Earnings normalisation. For Piramal and OneSource, trailing earnings are a trough or a ramp, so trailing multiples mislead. They must be valued on forward targets.

Four lessons from the global industry apply to the six. First, scale buys capability breadth: Lonza and Thermo Fisher can take a molecule from Phase I to commercial in any modality, which makes them the default choice for large innovators. India's companies are specialists by comparison, and their path to scale is through anchor contracts like Divi's dedicated projects. Second, building ahead works if you can fund the wait: Samsung Biologics built enormous biologics capacity before it had customers and now earns about 45% operating margins, but it had a conglomerate's balance sheet behind it. OneSource is attempting a similar model with debt. Third, the mid-tier consolidates: private-equity ownership of Cambrex, Recipharm and others means Indian CDMOs could be targets as well as acquirers; Piramal's history is a string of Western acquisitions. Fourth, Chinese competition is a cost and capacity problem, not just a political one: WuXi's 39% growth through a year of US political pressure shows that customers value its scale and speed.

In plain terms

India's best contract maker is as profitable as the world's best, but far smaller. That makes Indian firms good at specific jobs rather than one-stop shops for the largest drug companies. They are growing into that role by signing long contracts and building specialised plants, while the global giants keep buying factories and China keeps growing despite US pressure.

Technology and disruption: what could change the economics

Six technology and market shifts could move value between the archetypes. Two of them, oral GLP-1 drugs and Chinese peptide scale, bear directly on the 2026 investment case.

  • Continuous manufacturing and flow chemistry. Running reactions continuously in small tubes rather than in large batch reactors cuts capex per tonne and can improve safety and yield. It could erode the advantage of large batch-reactor parks such as Divi's, or it could be adopted first by the companies with the most process-chemistry depth, which would include Divi's. So far the evidence points to the second outcome.
  • Oral and small-molecule GLP-1 drugs. Lilly's orforglipron and similar pills would shift value from peptide synthesis and pens back to conventional small-molecule API. That suits Divi's and Neuland's chemistry. It is the most important long-term risk to OneSource's pen thesis beyond 2028, and a partial risk to the peptide capacity Neuland and Divi's are adding.
  • Recombinant versus synthetic peptide routes. Novo makes semaglutide by yeast fermentation; generic makers often use chemical synthesis or hybrid routes. If fermentation costs fall faster, synthetic-route capacity loses value.
  • Innovators bringing strategic modalities in-house. Novo absorbed Catalent's fill sites; Lilly is building its own API plants in the US and Europe, partly to manage tariff exposure. The more strategic a product, the less it is outsourced.
  • AI-assisted process development. Faster route-finding and tech transfer compress the development fee pool but speed molecules through the funnel. Net effect for CDMOs: less revenue per development project, more projects reaching commercial supply sooner (an estimate, not yet measurable).
  • Chinese cost and scale. WuXi's TIDES business targeted more than 100,000 litres of solid-phase peptide capacity; India's disclosed additions are a small fraction. In conventional small molecules, Asymchem, Porton and Pharmaron compete directly with Divi's and Neuland on price.

For Akums the disruptions are different. Revised Schedule M is pushing small domestic contract makers to upgrade or close, which consolidates volume towards Akums. New ways of making drugs, and new forms of drugs such as obesity pills instead of injections, could make some of today's factories less valuable. China already has far more peptide capacity than India. Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian CDMOs

The strongest bull points are lock-in, a funding upturn and a slow but real share shift from China. The strongest bear points are price, concentration and two levered balance sheets.

The bull case

  • Lock-in compounds. Every molecule that reaches commercial supply stays for years; Divi's custom synthesis grew 45% in Q1 FY27 even after its largest molecule faced US generics.
  • The funding cycle has turned. US biotech VC of $16.3 bn in H1 2026, IPOs of $5 bn and a five-year high in the XBI feed RFPs now and revenue in FY28–29.
  • China+1 is law. BIOSECURE is signed; each percentage point of global share is worth about ₹16,500 Cr of revenue to India, and the six do half of India's contract work.
  • Contract-backed capacity is arriving. Divi's three dedicated projects (CY2027), Neuland's pre-committed peptide module, Akums' advance-funded European site.
  • GLP-1 at four layers. Fragments, API, fill and pens, with the large US/EU generic window in 2031–32.
  • Operating leverage. Q1 FY27 combined EBITDA grew 69% on 25.5% revenue growth.

The bear case

  • Valuation. About 103× combined trailing earnings; Divi's needs ~26% annual profit growth for a decade on our ILLUSTRATIVE reverse calculation, and trades above its latest broker target.
  • Concentration. Neuland's top five products are ~64% of revenue; Divi's Entresto exposure (estimated) is post-generic; Piramal has a named contract at nil for FY27.
  • China is not retreating. WuXi grew 39% in H1 2026 and won an injunction; BIOSECURE bites fully only around 2033.
  • Leverage in the ramps. Piramal at 3.6× and OneSource at 3.8× net debt to EBITDA; a slipped ramp raises both.
  • Q1 flattered. Validation batches and inventory gains inside Divi's 41% margin; management guides FY27 margin in line with FY26.
  • Policy and ownership. Gland's Fosun control and the OMB list; Section 232 tariffs nudging innovators to onshore.

Weighing the two, the bull case is stronger on the businesses and the bear case stronger on the prices. The lock-in mechanism is real, the funding cycle has clearly turned, and the share shift from China, though slow, runs in one direction. Against that, the group's valuation already assumes those things go well for a long time, and the two companies with the most to gain from new capacity, Piramal and OneSource, are the two carrying debt. The evidence that would strengthen the bull case is specific: Divi's dedicated projects supplying commercially in CY2027 on time; Neuland adding commercial products so its top five fall below half of revenue; Piramal's contract business growing in the mid-teens with Morpeth cleared; OneSource's quarterly revenue doubling towards the ₹900-plus crore a quarter its FY28 target implies. The evidence that would strengthen the bear case is equally specific: a second quarter in which Divi's margin falls back to the low thirties with custom synthesis flat, a Neuland quarter in which one large product skips an order, an OAI at Morpeth, or OneSource's Line 2 filling slowly.

In plain terms

These are good businesses at high prices. The good news (sticky customers, biotech money returning, China losing some favour in the US) is real. The bad news is that investors have already paid for much of it, a few companies depend on a few products, and two of them have borrowed to build. Watch whether the new plants fill on time; that will decide which side is right.
