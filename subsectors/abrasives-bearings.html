The one-page view: the same ~14% margin, earned two different ways

Two industries share one label, one customer list and, in four cases out of five, a foreign parent. Read them as two halves, and read the parent before the profit and loss account.

This report covers five listed companies that the NSE classifies together as "Abrasives & Bearings": Timken India, SKF India (Industrial) and SKF India (now only the automotive business) make bearings; Carborundum Universal (CUMI, the Murugappa group company) and Grindwell Norton (Saint-Gobain's Indian arm) make abrasives, technical ceramics and the mineral grains that go into both. The two trades share customers — car and truck makers, steel mills, cement plants, railways, machine builders — but almost no production technology. A bearing is hardened steel ground to a few microns; a grinding wheel is a fired lump of alumina or silicon carbide grit that does the grinding. One is a component that sits inside a machine for years; the other is a consumable that wears away on the shop floor.

Put together, the five earned ₹17,327 crore of revenue in FY26, up 9.5%, with an operating profit (EBITDA before other income) of ₹2,428 crore. That is a combined margin of about 14.0%, down from 16.4% in FY25. Reported profit after tax fell 21% to ₹1,335 crore. The interesting part is that both halves landed on the same margin for opposite reasons. In bearings, Timken's ~18% margin was offset by SKF India (Industrial)'s ~11% in its first year as a standalone company. In abrasives, Grindwell's ~19% was offset by CUMI's ~11%, which carries a sanctioned Russian subsidiary and two European and African units being shut. A sector average of 14% describes none of the five companies.

The second thing to grasp is ownership. Four of the five are subsidiaries of global groups: The Timken Company (USA) owns 51.05% of Timken India; AB SKF (Sweden) owns 52.6% of SKF India (Industrial) directly; SKF Vertevo AB — the automotive company AB SKF is spinning off in Stockholm — already holds 52.58% of SKF India; and Saint-Gobain (France) controls 58.04% of Grindwell. Only CUMI is Indian-promoted (Murugappa, 38.85%). The parent decides which products the Indian company makes and which it imports from sister plants, how much it pays for the brand and technology, how much it exports to the group, and in SKF's case even how the Indian company is carved up. Royalties and group fees run from about 2.7% of revenue at Timken to about 6.5% at SKF India; SKF India (Industrial) has shareholder approval to pay up to ₹222 crore of royalty and trademark fees in FY27 against ₹25 crore in FY26. Minority investors therefore own a stream of profit whose size is partly set in Gothenburg, Ohio and Paris.

Third, 2025–26 rearranged the bearing half. SKF India demerged its industrial business into a new listed company (effective 1 October 2025, listed 5 December 2025); one pre-demerger share worth ₹5,008 is now worth ₹2,464 + ₹1,416 = ₹3,880, a 22.5% loss. In plain terms

These are five good-quality, mostly debt-free manufacturers that grew revenue about 10% last year while their profits slipped. The bearing makers lost margin to steel costs, a slow year for railway orders and the costs of SKF's break-up. The abrasive makers were split: Grindwell kept compounding, while CUMI's Indian business did well but its foreign subsidiaries in Russia, Germany and South Africa cost it money. Four of the five belong to multinationals that charge them fees and sell them goods, so a part of the profit you are buying is decided by the parent, not by the Indian market.

Fourth, China and the US frame the market. India imported ₹13,863 crore of bearings in FY26, 49% of them from China (58% for ball bearings), against a domestic bearing market of roughly ₹23,000–28,000 crore (an indicative estimate). A good share of those "Chinese imports" are the MNCs' own purchases: Timken India alone bought about ₹480 crore from its parent's plant in Wuxi. In abrasives, imports are about half the market and coated abrasives from China rose 33% last year. On the export side, US tariffs now hit bearings twice: ball and tapered-roller bearings have been Section 232 steel derivatives since 18 August 2025, at 50% on their steel content, and Indian goods carry a 10% Section 301 duty on top of the normal tariff since 24 July 2026. Timken, the only big exporter in the group (19.9% of FY26 revenue), sold just 7.8% of its revenue to the US in FY26, down 15%.

01

Rings, rollers, grit and glue: what these companies make

A bearing lets one part of a machine turn against another with little friction; an abrasive removes metal so that parts, bearings included, fit to a few microns. The two trades meet at the grinding machine.

A rolling bearing is two hardened steel rings with balls or rollers between them, held apart by a cage. It carries a load — a truck's weight on its axle, a wind turbine's rotor on its main shaft — while letting the shaft spin. Its value lies in how precisely it is made, which sets how long it lasts and how much friction it adds, and in being designed into a customer's machine. Bearings come in families: ball bearings for fast, light-to-medium loads (electric motors, fans, two-wheeler wheels and gearboxes); tapered roller bearings (TRBs), invented by Henry Timken in 1898, for combined loads on truck and tractor axles and railway wagons; spherical and cylindrical roller bearings (SRBs, CRBs) for heavy, misaligned loads in steel mills, cement kilns, mines and wind gearboxes; needle bearings for compact transmissions; and large-diameter slewing and main-shaft bearings, most of which India still imports.

An abrasive is a hard grain — fused alumina, silicon carbide, zirconia, synthetic diamond or cubic boron nitride — held in a bond or glued onto a backing. Bonded abrasives are grinding wheels, cut-off and depressed-centre discs and honing stones; they grind crankshafts, camshafts and, fittingly, the raceways of bearings. Coated abrasives are belts, sheets and flap discs, used to finish metal, wood and car paint. Super-abrasives use diamond or CBN for carbide tools, aerospace alloys and glass. Both CUMI and Grindwell also make technical ceramics (wear liners for cement and power plants, metallised parts, armour tiles) and, in CUMI's case on a large scale, the electrominerals themselves: brown and white fused alumina, silicon carbide and fused zirconia made in electric furnaces, sold both to its own wheel plants and to other buyers worldwide. Grindwell also runs a performance-plastics business and a captive digital-services unit that writes software for the Saint-Gobain group.

The value chain, step by step

The bearing chain starts with bearing-grade steel — high-carbon chromium bar and tube (the SAE 52100 / 100Cr6 grade) or case-carburising grades for big rollers — bought from Indian special-steel mills or imported. Rings are forged or cut from tube, turned, heat-treated, then ground and super-finished: this is where a bearing maker becomes one of the abrasive makers' best customers (Timken spent ₹99.8 crore on stores and spares, grinding wheels included, in FY26). Balls and rollers are often bought in. The assembled bearing goes either to an original-equipment maker (OEM) — a truck plant, a wagon builder approved by the railways' standards body RDSO, a wind-turbine maker — or to a distributor who sells replacements. Alongside sit traded bearings imported finished from the parent's plants, which fill out the range the Indian factories do not make.

The abrasive chain is shorter but more energy-hungry. Bauxite or calcined alumina is fused into brown or white alumina; quartz sand and petroleum coke become silicon carbide in an Acheson furnace; zircon sand becomes fused zirconia. All of these run on electricity: CUMI's standalone power and fuel bill was ₹269.5 crore in FY26, about 9% of sales. The grain is crushed and graded, mixed with a vitrified frit or a phenolic resin, pressed, then fired or cured into a wheel or disc. Coated products glue grain onto paper or cloth, often imported backing. The two leaders sell directly to large accounts — auto plants, bearing makers, steel mills — with application engineers who audit grinding cycles, and through dealers for discs and coated products.

Where value is captured

Four positions earn above-average returns. The first is design-in plus replacement: once a bearing is specified on a wagon, a truck axle or a turbine, it is re-bought for the machine's life, and replacement sales earn more than OEM sales because the buyer is a maintenance manager facing downtime, not a purchasing department running a reverse auction. The second is approved-vendor status for safety-critical uses — RDSO for railway bearings, defence and wind certification, Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) licences — which keeps out the unorganised fringe. The third is engineering service: SKF's condition monitoring and the abrasive makers' application engineering are priced on what the customer saves, not on the cost of steel or grit. The fourth, specific to this bucket, is the parent's share: royalty, the transfer price on traded goods, and the price at which an Indian plant exports to the group. Section 07 measures it.

02

How big the two markets are: ~₹25,000 crore of bearings, ~₹7,500 crore of abrasives

Market-research headlines overstate both markets. Rebuilt from trade data and company revenue, India uses roughly ₹23,000–28,000 crore of bearings and ₹7,000–7,700 crore of finished abrasives a year — both indicative estimates.

Published market sizes for this sub-sector vary by a factor of two, so it pays to rebuild them. One research firm puts the Indian bearings market at US$5.2 billion in 2025 (about ₹49,000 crore at today's ₹94.9 to the dollar) and the abrasives market at US$2.3 billion. Those figures appear to count imports at end-user prices, include distributor mark-ups and, for abrasives, fold in raw grain and machinery. For an investor the useful question is narrower: how much do Indian buyers spend on bearings and finished abrasives at the manufacturer's or importer's price, and how much of that do the listed companies capture?

Bearings: rebuilt from the trade data

Start with the hard number. The Commerce Ministry's trade database records ₹13,863 crore of bearing imports (HS 8482) in FY26, up 12% in rupees and 7% in dollars (US$1,568 million). Add basic customs duty and a distributor's margin and those imports are worth roughly ₹16,000–18,000 crore by the time they reach a buyer. Domestic manufacture by the organised players — counted at sales value but net of the traded goods they import, to avoid counting the same bearing twice — is roughly ₹14,000–17,000 crore: Schaeffler's bearings ~₹6,000–7,000 crore, the SKF companies ~₹3,500 crore, Timken ~₹2,300 crore, NRB ~₹1,300 crore, the unlisted National Engineering Industries (NBC brand) ~₹2,500–3,000 crore, and others about ₹1,000 crore. Subtract ₹7,179 crore of exports and India's apparent consumption comes to about ₹23,000–28,000 crore, or US$2.4–3.0 billion. That is an indicative estimate, not a published statistic.

Abrasives: imports are about half the market

The abrasives rebuild tells a different story. Domestic sales of finished abrasives — CUMI's standalone abrasives (₹1,270 crore), Grindwell's abrasives segment (₹1,491 crore), Wendt's super-abrasives (₹234 crore) and a long tail of Sterling, Orient, local 3M and Bosch output and unorganised makers (₹800–1,500 crore) — come to about ₹3,800–4,500 crore. Apparent consumption of finished abrasives is therefore roughly ₹7,000–7,700 crore (US$0.75–0.8 billion), an indicative estimate. Imports are about half of it by value, and China supplies about 62% of the imports.

Growth was healthy everywhere at the top line. The SKF automotive business grew fastest (+15.4% to ₹2,129.6 crore, against a restated ₹1,845.3 crore), riding the September 2025 GST cut on small cars and two-wheelers. In plain terms

India buys roughly ₹25,000 crore of bearings and ₹7,500 crore of finished abrasives a year (both indicative). The five listed companies are a big part of each market, but imports are a big part too: more than half of bearing imports by value come from China, and about half of all finished abrasives used in India are imported. The headline market-research numbers you see quoted are close to double these, because they count different things.

One more sizing point matters for the deep dives. Because the multinationals import so much from their own group plants, part of what looks like "the import problem" is in fact the members' own supply chain. Timken India's purchases from the group were ₹1,191 crore in FY26; SKF India (Industrial) imported ₹974 crore of finished products in the second half of FY26 alone. Together with SKF India's ₹188 crore, at least ~₹3,300 crore — roughly a quarter of India's bearing imports, an indicative figure — is intra-group. That is why the policy debate on Chinese bearings is quieter than the size of the import bill suggests (Section 09).

03

Trucks, trains, turbines and toolrooms: who buys

Demand comes from six places that rarely move together: automobiles, railways, wind farms, process industry (steel, cement, mining), exports to the parent group, and the general fabrication that consumes discs and belts.

The best way into demand is to ask what each company's customer is doing this quarter. A bearing maker sells to people who build machines (OEMs) and to people who keep machines running (the aftermarket). An abrasive maker sells to anyone who removes metal, which ties it to production rather than to investment: a car plant grinds crankshafts every day whether or not it is building a new line. That difference makes abrasives demand steadier and bearings demand more cyclical, but both have a replacement or consumption base that never goes to zero.

Railways: big programme, slow buying

Railways are Timken India's largest single end-market and its signature franchise. Every four-axle freight wagon runs on eight cartridge tapered-roller bearings; every LHB coach carries eight axle-box bearings; locomotives add axle and traction-motor bearings. The fleet also needs replacements at each periodic overhaul. The rolling-stock programme is large: Indian Railways built 9,997 coaches in FY26 against a programme of 8,700 (7,127 in FY25), more than 6,600 of them LHB coaches, and 1,914 locomotives. Yet Timken's rail revenue showed "slight de-growth" in FY26 and grew only about 3% in Q1 FY27, to ₹200 crore. Management's explanation on the 5 August 2026 call: government buying is slow because funds are being diverted to infrastructure and defence, and tenders are running a couple of months late. Rail and PSU contracts are also annual rate contracts that reset only on renewal, so a steel-price rise cannot be passed on mid-contract.

SKF India (Industrial) is more extreme. Traded goods were 52.7% of its FY26 revenue (₹1,812 crore), materials only 16.4%, and the gross margin 32.4%. A third to a half of what Timken India and SKF India (Industrial) sell was made by someone else in their group and shipped in finished. Abrasives: minerals, furnaces and people

The abrasive makers buy far less in finished form. Grindwell's traded goods were 12.8% of FY26 revenue and its gross margin 53.6% (54.6% in FY25). CUMI's consolidated gross margin was 60.9% (63.3% in FY25) on a materials-only definition; its power and fuel, about 10% of sales in recent quarters, sits lower down in "other expenses". The difference from bearings is that most of the abrasive maker's conversion cost — furnace power, kiln fuel, labour, maintenance — is its own, so wages, energy tariffs and plant utilisation matter more. An electrominerals business is the most extreme case: a fused-minerals furnace spends an illustrative 35–40% of revenue on bauxite, quartz, pet-coke or zircon, 25–30% on electricity and 20–25% on labour, electrodes and freight, leaving a profit margin of 5–10% in an average year. CUMI's consolidated electrominerals margin was 5.6% in FY26 and 4.6% in Q1 FY27.

Who passed costs on in 2026

The 2026 cost shock was a useful test of pricing power. Steel for bearings rose about ₹5,000 a tonne between January and April. Steel is an indicative 25–35% of a manufactured bearing's cost, so a 6% steel rise means roughly 1.5–2% cost inflation — an illustrative calculation. Timken recovered it: its Q1 FY27 gross margin was 39.9%, up 100 basis points year on year, because heavy-truck, tractor and private industrial customers accepted increases. Rail and PSU customers, on annual rate contracts, did not, and by mid-May Timken had realised only about 10% of the price rise it needed, with the rest phased over two quarters. SKF India (Industrial), with its heavy traded-goods mix and the rupee falling, saw its EBITDA margin drop 230 basis points to 9.2% in the same quarter — the clearest sign in the group that the newly standalone business has less pricing power, or slower contract resets, than its larger sister had as a combined company. In abrasives, CUMI absorbed about ₹16 crore of resin and fuel inflation in Q1 FY27 with volume-led growth and little pricing, and its electrominerals furnaces took the energy hit.

An MNC oligopoly at the top, China at the gate

Five groups make about two-thirds of India's organised bearing output; two make most of its home-grown abrasives. Below them sit imports, the unorganised trade and a counterfeit fringe.

The Indian bearing industry is run by multinationals. Schaeffler India (FAG, INA and LuK brands; 74.13% owned by Schaeffler AG) is the largest, with ₹9,686 crore of revenue in calendar 2025 across bearings, auto OEM, exports and aftermarket — it is classified under Auto Components, so it is a reference peer here. Next come the two SKF companies (₹5,570 crore together), Timken India (₹3,478 crore), the unlisted National Engineering Industries (the CK Birla group's NBC brand, roughly ₹3,000 crore and big in railways), and NRB Bearings (₹1,336 crore; 65–70% of India's needle bearings according to CRISIL). Tata Steel's bearings division and a handful of small makers complete the organised domestic base. Japanese majors (NSK, NTN, JTEKT) have no Indian listed subsidiaries.

Abrasives: two leaders and a flood at the low end

Abrasives are more concentrated at home and more exposed to imports. CUMI and Grindwell are the only two Indian makers of a full range; together they hold an indicative 35–38% of finished-abrasive sales by value, with Grindwell slightly larger in India. Grindwell's FY26 annual report describes the competition plainly: Chinese imports are concentrated in power-tool accessories at the low and mid tiers, brought in by power-tool companies and traders under their own brands; paint and adhesive companies are entering select abrasive ranges "with deep distribution and aggressive pricing"; and the Chinese influx also widens the market as manual work moves to power tools. Imports are about half the market, and China supplies about 62% of them.

In plain terms

India's bearing market is mostly in the hands of four multinational groups and one large Indian private company, with Chinese imports and fakes underneath. Its abrasives market has two big domestic makers who face a growing tide of cheaper Chinese discs and belts, especially in the kind of products sold through hardware shops. The leaders protect themselves by selling engineered products to factories, where quality and service matter more than price.

Owners and shareholders

Ownership is unusually concentrated and unusually domestic among the minority holders. Parents hold 51–58% of the four MNC subsidiaries; Indian mutual funds and insurers (DIIs) hold another 20–31%; foreign portfolio investors only 5–11%. 1. Timken: the export hub that became a domestic growth story

Timken India is the parent's low-cost plant for tapered and, since the Bharuch expansion, spherical and cylindrical roller bearings, plus a distributor of the group's wider range. Exports were 29.5% of revenue in FY22 and 19.9% in FY26 — the domestic business (rail, trucks, tractors, process industry) grew faster. It carries the heaviest working capital in the bearing half (inventory 135 days) because it stocks a wide range for distributors and runs export pipelines, and it has just spent heavily on Bharuch (capex ₹297 crore in FY26, 8.7% of revenue), which is why its return on capital fell from 29.9% (EBITDA to capital employed, FY22) to 20.9% in FY26.

2. SKF India (Industrial): a distributor with a factory attached

The industrial half of the old SKF India sells bearings, seals, lubrication systems and condition-monitoring services to steel, cement, wind, rail and general-machinery customers and through a large distribution network. With 52.7% of revenue bought in finished and fixed assets of only ₹211 crore against ₹3,440 crore of sales, it earns a ~30% return on capital on an 11% margin. That is a trading return. The plan is to change the model: localise high-value components, build the Pune plant, add a new TRB line and lift the margin from about 9% (Q1 FY27) to about 12% near term and 14–16% in the long run, per management. The risk is that royalty and trademark fees, approved at up to ₹222 crore for FY27, absorb part of that improvement.

3. SKF India: the automotive pure play

What is left under the old SKF India ticker is a manufacturer of two-wheeler, car and truck bearings with three plants (Pune, Bengaluru and Haridwar), 330 distribution partners and 933 employees. It makes most of what it sells (about 60% localisation, 78% local procurement), runs its factories at 90–93% loading and plans ₹500 crore of capex to FY28. Its gross margin is the highest of the three bearing companies, but it pays the heaviest fees to the group — 6.5% of revenue — and its customers are car makers who demand annual price-downs. It is the most cyclical of the five and the most exposed to the electric-vehicle transition, with EV platforms starting around late 2028.

4. CUMI: two companies in one ticker

CUMI is an Indian materials group with three businesses — abrasives, ceramics and electrominerals — and an overseas portfolio bought over two decades: VAW in Russia, Foskor Zirconia in South Africa, Awuko and Rhodius in Germany, plus units in Australia, the Americas and India (Sterling Abrasives). Its Indian standalone business earned ₹416 crore of profit after tax in FY26, up 29%; the consolidated group earned ₹195 crore for its owners after closure charges. Inventory of 200 days, the highest in the group, reflects the furnace business and the global footprint. Its low 10.5% return on capital is the overseas portfolio's doing, and Section 10 unpicks it.

5. Grindwell: the compounder with a cash pile

Grindwell is the most stable of the five: an operating margin of 18–20% in every one of the last eight quarters, a 21% return on capital, a 51% dividend payout and no foreign subsidiaries to clean up beyond a silicon-carbide unit in Bhutan. Its edge is mix. Abrasives (₹1,491 crore, 12.9% margin) carry the brand; Ceramics & Plastics (₹1,311 crore, 17.6%) is growing faster and more profitably; Digital Services (₹222.5 crore, 26.1%) sells software work to the Saint-Gobain group on almost no capital. It also holds ₹1,489 crore of treasury — 43% of total assets — which drags the reported return on capital down; on segment assets alone it earns an indicative 26–27% pre-tax.

In plain terms

Do not compare these five on gross margin. SKF India (Industrial) has the lowest gross margin because half of what it sells is bought in, but it earns the best return on capital because it needs so few factories. CUMI has the highest gross margin because it makes its own grit, but it pays for that in electricity and overseas losses. The operating margin and return on capital are the numbers that compare like with like.

The models also explain how each will grow. Timken grows by filling Bharuch and the new Jamshedpur rail line and by winning more export allocations; SKF India (Industrial) by converting traded volume into local manufacture; SKF India by riding vehicle volumes and adding Haridwar capacity; CUMI by growing ceramics and shrinking its overseas losses; Grindwell by tilting its mix towards ceramics, plastics and digital services. Only one of these five growth paths — CUMI's ceramics — depends on a technology the company has not yet fully commercialised.

What the parent takes, and what it gives Deep dive

Royalty, trademark and IT fees, traded goods, export pricing, dividends and block sales: five channels move value from Indian minority shareholders' companies to foreign parents. The FY26 annual reports measure most of them for the first time.

Four of the five members are controlled subsidiaries of global groups, and the most important analytical question about any of them is not "how fast will Indian demand grow?" but "how much of the profit that demand creates stays in the listed company?" The parent is not a passive shareholder. It supplies the brand, the product designs, the manufacturing know-how, the IT systems and a third to a half of the goods the subsidiary sells. It buys the subsidiary's exports. It decides whether profit leaves as dividends or as fees, and whether to raise cash by selling shares. Each of those decisions is a related-party transaction, and each has a price.

Five channels from the subsidiary to the parent

  • Royalty and trademark fees — a percentage of sales for the brand and technology. Timken pays 2.47% of revenue; Grindwell 1.58%; SKF India 4.1% (royalty 2.50% plus trademark 1.61%); SKF India (Industrial) paid only from October 2025.
  • IT and service charges — group IT, shared services and technical support, charged separately. SKF India paid 2.35% of revenue for IT services alone.
  • Traded goods — finished products bought from group plants. The transfer price decides how much of the margin on those goods is earned in India and how much in Wuxi, Ohio or Gothenburg.
  • Exports to the group — the price at which the Indian plant sells to sister companies sets the export margin.
  • Dividends and share sales — the parent's share of payouts, and the cash it raises by selling down its stake.

Royalty and fees: from zero to 6.5% of revenue

The FY26 annual reports close what was the biggest data gap in this sub-sector. Timken India paid The Timken Company a royalty of ₹84.4 crore, 2.47% of revenue (₹80.8 crore and 2.57% in FY25), which the annual report describes as an effective rate of "approx. 2.5%", plus ₹9.0 crore (0.26%) of inter-company service charges — a total of about 2.7%. Grindwell paid ₹48.3 crore of royalty (1.58% of standalone revenue) to Saint-Gobain Abrasives Inc. in the US, plus ₹62.4 crore of expenses charged by group companies (double FY25's ₹31.5 crore) — about 3.6% gross. SKF India, the automotive company, paid the most: royalty ₹53.2 crore (2.50%), trademark fee ₹34.3 crore (1.61%) and IT services ₹50.1 crore (2.35%), a total of ₹137.5 crore or about 6.5% of revenue. SKF India (Industrial) paid only ₹25.5 crore of royalty and trademark fees in FY26 (0.74% of revenue), because its agreements started on 1 October 2025; on its second-half revenue the rate was about 1.4%, and with IT services of ₹48.0 crore its fee load was about 4.1% of second-half revenue. CUMI, Indian-promoted, pays no royalty.

The single largest margin risk in the bearing half is a resolution passed at SKF India (Industrial)'s AGM on 13 August 2026. Why the market tolerates the leak

If parents take 2.7–6.5% of revenue in fees, set transfer prices on a third of the goods and sell stock at the top, why do Indian investors pay 40–50 times earnings for these subsidiaries? Four reasons, tested properly in Section 17. The parent's contribution is real: a 125-year-old tapered-roller design, SKF's application engineering and Saint-Gobain's materials science are worth paying for, and an independent Indian maker would spend more than 2.5% of sales on R&D to match them. The subsidiaries earn high returns despite the fees (19–30% on capital, CUMI aside) and sit on net cash. The parent's interests and the minority's are broadly aligned on growth, because the parent owns over half the equity. And there is a long-dated option that the parent eventually buys out the minority — never promised, and in Timken's case moving the other way, but part of why these stocks rarely trade cheaply.

In plain terms

Owning a share of an Indian MNC subsidiary means sharing its profits with a parent that also sells it goods, buys its exports, charges it fees and can sell shares when prices are high. Timken's parent raised about ₹4,300 crore by selling a sixth of its Indian company in 2023–24; SKF's industrial company may pay up to ₹222 crore a year in royalty from FY27. The trade-off is access to world-class products and technology. The key is to know, company by company, which way the money flows.

In plain terms

A quick test for any of these companies: add up royalty, trademark and IT fees, and compare them with dividends to the parent and with the operating margin. At Timken the fees are about 2.7% of sales against an 18% margin; at SKF India they are 6.5% against 12%. The higher that ratio, the more the parent, rather than the minority shareholder, is the first claimant on the business.

The SKF double split: one franchise, two listcos, two parents Deep dive

SKF India split into an industrial and an automotive company in October 2025; its Swedish parent is now spinning off its own automotive arm. After 1 December 2026 the two Indian companies are expected to answer to different owners while still trading with each other.

For decades SKF India was one company selling bearings to both carmakers and factories. In September 2024 AB SKF, its Swedish parent, decided to separate its global automotive business and list it on its own. India moved first. The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) in Mumbai sanctioned a scheme of arrangement on 26 September 2025; it took effect on 1 October 2025, with a record date of 15 October; shareholders received one share of SKF India (Industrial) for every SKF India share they held; and SKF India (Industrial) listed on the NSE and BSE on 5 December 2025. The industrial business had been parked in a new wholly owned subsidiary, incorporated in December 2024, before being demerged. What remains under the SKF India name and ticker (SKFINDIA) is the automotive business.

Reported profit hides two tax quirks

Neither company's FY26 profit is a clean starting point. SKF India (Industrial) booked a ₹196.1 crore exceptional charge for the demerger and listing and paid almost no tax (₹3.6 crore, after a ₹44.1 crore deferred-tax credit), so its reported ₹217.7 crore is low on the charge and high on the tax. In plain terms

If you held one SKF India share before October 2025, you now hold one share in each of two companies. Adjusted for those quirks, the industrial company trades at about 39 times earnings and the automotive company at about 36 times, both estimates.

Third, brand and fees: SKF India will adopt the SKF Vertevo brand, and its royalty, trademark and IT agreements will presumably be re-papered with Vertevo rather than AB SKF — terms not yet disclosed.

In plain terms

The Indian SKF companies will soon belong to two different Swedish parents. The automotive one already sits inside SKF Vertevo, which is being floated separately in Stockholm; the industrial one stays with SKF itself. They still sell to and buy from each other. Watch for three things: whether Indian takeover rules require any offer to minority shareholders (nobody has said), how the two companies price their trade with each other, and what fees the new Vertevo parent charges for its brand.

The split also changes what each company is. SKF India (Industrial) is now a pure industrial-bearings, seals and services business with the SKF name, a ~30% return on capital, two mutual funds holding a fifth of its shares, ₹850–950 crore of planned capex and a new MD. SKF India is a pure automotive supplier that will carry a new brand, guides to ~20% revenue growth in FY27, plans ₹500 crore of capex to FY28 and pays a 2.8% dividend yield at today's price. Investors who owned the old SKF India for its blend of industrial and automotive exposure now have to choose.

US tariffs: two layers on bearings

The US tariff path since 2025 has been unusually complicated. There is no current anti-dumping duty on Chinese bearings, and no application was found. Related-party rules: the minority's main protection

The SEBI listing rule on related-party transactions is the only formal brake on what a parent can charge. Since 2019 royalty or brand payments above 5% of consolidated turnover have needed minority approval, and larger related-party contracts in general need approval if they exceed materiality thresholds. The rule works as a disclosure device more than a cap: it forced SKF India (Industrial) to publish its FY27 ceiling of ₹222.2 crore for royalty and trademark fees and ₹133.0 crore for IT services, which is how investors can now quantify the risk. It does not stop a parent splitting charges across royalty, trademark, IT and services so that no single line crosses 5%, and minority approval for a pre-agreed group policy is usually given. Investors should read the AGM notices of all four MNC subsidiaries every year.

Takeovers and delisting: the option nobody promises

Two other sets of SEBI rules shape the "parent option" in these stocks. The takeover regulations require an acquirer of 25% or more, or of control, to make an open offer; an indirect acquisition — where control of the listed company's parent changes — can also trigger one, unless an exemption applies. SKF Vertevo's distribution to AB SKF's shareholders is exactly such a change of ultimate control for SKF India. Neither company's annual report discusses it, and no SEBI view was found, so this report does not predict whether an offer will be made. The delisting rules govern the opposite move — a parent buying out the minority — and include a fixed-price route introduced in 2024. None of the four parents has signalled any intention to delist; Timken's parent has done the reverse, selling 16.7% of its Indian company in 2023–24.

In plain terms

Indian listing rules make parents disclose and seek approval for large fees and deals with their subsidiaries, which is how we know SKF's industrial company may pay up to ₹222 crore in royalty next year. Takeover rules might, in principle, require Vertevo to offer to buy SKF India shares from the public when it takes over as parent — but nobody has said so, and investors should not count on it.

Standards, approvals and sanctions

The quality and approval regime is the members' moat. Railway bearings need RDSO approval; wind bearings need certification by turbine makers; defence ceramics meet the NIJ and BIS level 4/5 ballistic standards (CUMI). A bearings Quality Control Order, if it is brought fully into force, would raise the bar for the unorganised trade and counterfeit imports, though it would also make the multinationals certify their own imported range. On the sanctions side, CUMI's exposure is specific and serious: an OFAC SDN listing means US persons cannot deal with VAW, banks shy away from its payments (hence the trapped cash), and counterparties face secondary-sanctions risk. CUMI's FY25 impairment included foreign-currency balances held with a clearing agency and VAW's export receivables — the practical cost of being cut off from dollar payments.

Tax, labour and contingent liabilities

Finally, tax and labour items explain several of FY26's one-offs. SKF India's Q4 FY26 PBT of ₹46 crore was hit by "non-recurring expenses related to the demerger and new regulations", meaning the new labour codes; Timken's employee costs rose 7.3%, partly for the same reason. 1898 to 2026: how the industry got here, and its cycles

The Indian industry was built by joint ventures with foreign technology owners, consolidated under MNC parents in the 1990s, and has spent 2022–26 being restructured by those parents and by geopolitics.

Every member traces its technology to a foreign inventor or partner. Henry Timken patented the tapered roller bearing in 1898; AB SKF was founded in Gothenburg in 1907 on the self-aligning ball bearing; Grindwell Abrasives was set up in 1941 and merged with Norton's Indian operations in 1967; CUMI was formed in 1954 by the Murugappa group with Carborundum Co. of the US and Universal Grinding Wheel of the UK. The 1990s brought control to the parents: Saint-Gobain bought Norton globally in 1990 and with it control of Grindwell; Timken bought out Tata's share of the Jamshedpur joint venture around 1999. CUMI, the one company that stayed Indian, went the other way and bought abroad.

The cycles: bearings swing, abrasives grind on, minerals boom and bust

The three businesses inside this bucket follow different cycles. Bearings track industrial production and vehicle output with operating leverage on margins: when volumes rise, a plant's fixed costs are spread more thinly. The last trough was FY21 (Covid); the peak was FY23, when the combined SKF India earned a 17% operating margin and Timken about 20%. Since then margins have drifted down — Timken's from 23.2% in FY22 to 17.9% in FY26 as export margins normalised and Bharuch's start-up costs landed. Abrasives follow manufacturing output, which is steadier: Grindwell's margin has stayed in an 18–20% band for four years. Electrominerals follow global silicon-carbide and alumina prices and power costs: CUMI's peak was FY23–24 and the trough is now, with consolidated margins around 5%.

In plain terms

These businesses were built by importing foreign technology, and the foreign owners took control in the 1990s. Two lessons from the history carry forward. Reading the numbers: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

The thirteen measures that explain a bearing or abrasives company — what each is, how to calculate it, what good looks like, how the five compare and where the definitions trip people up — followed by a ten-minute routine for reading any quarterly result.

Most of the mistakes made about this sub-sector come from reading a single headline number — a margin, a growth rate, a P/E — without knowing what sits inside it. A bearing subsidiary's margin depends on how much it makes versus buys, what the parent charges and whether steel prices moved; an abrasive company's depends on segment mix and power. This section builds the profit equation first, then takes each native KPI in turn.

The equation: from volume to EBITDA

For a bearing or abrasive maker the operating profit equation has six terms. Volume (bearings sold, tonnes of grain, wheels shipped) times realisation (average price, set by product mix, contract terms and how much of the cost rise was passed on) gives revenue. From that come materials (steel, grain, resins), traded goods (finished products bought from the group or others), royalty and group fees, and other costs (people, power, freight, selling, administration). What is left is EBITDA. Management controls volume through capacity and sales effort, realisation partly, and other costs largely; it does not control steel or alumina prices, the rupee, or — for a subsidiary — the transfer prices and fee rates its parent sets.

The worked example shows why small changes matter. In the illustrative ₹1,000 crore company, a 5% rise in steel (materials up ₹15 crore) with no price increase cuts EBITDA from ₹150 crore to ₹135 crore — a 10% profit fall from a 1.5% cost rise. That leverage — profit moving several times faster than revenue or costs — is why these companies' quarterly profits swing more than their sales.

In plain terms

Of every ₹1,000 a typical bearing subsidiary sells, about ₹600 goes on steel and on bearings bought from its own group, ₹30 to the parent as fees and ₹220 on running costs, leaving ₹150. Because the leftover is small relative to the big cost lines, a modest change in the steel price, the rupee or the parent's fee rate moves profit a lot.

1. Segment revenue and EBIT margins

What it is: the profit before interest and tax that a company reports for each business segment, divided by that segment's revenue. Formula: segment PBIT ÷ segment revenue. Worked example: Grindwell's Ceramics & Plastics segment earned ₹230.9 crore of PBIT on ₹1,310.6 crore of revenue in FY26 — 17.6%. What drives it: product mix within the segment, capacity utilisation, input costs and, for overseas-heavy segments, the performance of foreign subsidiaries. Benchmarks (indicative): abrasives ≥14% good, 9–14% average, below 8% weak; ceramics ≥20% good, 14–20% average; electrominerals ≥12% good, 7–12% average, below 6% weak. How the members compare: only the abrasive companies report segments; the bearing companies are single-segment and disclose end-markets instead (KPI 12). 2. Gross margin: company-reported vs proxy

What it is: revenue minus the cost of goods — materials consumed, traded goods purchased and the change in inventories — as a share of revenue. Formula: (revenue − materials − traded goods ± inventory change) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Timken FY26: ₹3,419 crore revenue − ₹1,013 crore materials − ₹1,118 crore traded goods + ~₹35 crore inventory build ≈ ₹1,323 crore, a 38.7% gross margin. What drives it: the make-versus-buy mix above all, then input prices and pass-through, then product mix. Benchmarks: meaningful only within a business model — for a bearing maker that manufactures most of what it sells, above ~45% is good; for one that trades half its range, ~30–35% can be normal.

9. Capex to sales

What it is: cash spent on fixed assets as a share of revenue. Formula: capital expenditure (cash-flow statement) ÷ revenue. 11. Price-to-steel and alumina spread, and pass-through

What it is: how much of an input-cost change a company recovers through prices, and how fast. Formula: (change in realisation × revenue) ÷ (change in input cost × input volume); in practice, track gross margin against the steel or alumina price over two to three quarters. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a ₹1,000 crore bearing maker that manufactures half its revenue spends about ₹150 crore on steel. A 6% steel rise (~₹5,000 a tonne) adds ₹9 crore of cost. With no price increase, EBITDA falls from ₹150 crore to ₹141 crore (−6%); with full pass-through after a one-quarter lag, the year's loss is about ₹2.25 crore. What drives it: contract structure (OEM commodity-index clauses pass through with a one-to-two-quarter lag; rail and PSU annual rate contracts reset only on renewal), competitive intensity, and share of aftermarket. Benchmarks: gross margin held or rising through a cost increase is good; a 100–200 basis-point dip that recovers within two quarters is average; a sustained fall is weak.

Definition traps, collected

  • SKF India's FY26 on screener is two companies. ₹3,763 crore of revenue mixes six months of the combined company with six months of the automotive business. Use ₹2,130 crore (automotive) and ₹3,440 crore (industrial).
  • Screener's SKF P/Es are wrong. SKFINDUS's printed 31.7× is distorted by a pre-listing stub; SKFINDIA's 30.2× uses mixed earnings. Compute m-cap ÷ clean profit yourself.
  • CUMI has four profit numbers. Standalone ₹416 crore, owners' ₹194.7 crore, including NCI ₹167.8 crore (screener), and pre-exceptional PBT ₹416.3 crore. Say which.
  • CUMI's "abrasives margin" is 15.3% in India and 4.3% for the group. Never quote one without the other.
  • Segment revenue is before elimination. CUMI's segments sum to ₹5,345 crore against ₹5,149 crore of sales.
  • Gross margin is a make-or-buy measure. Compare it only within a business model, and never use a "revenue minus raw materials" proxy for a trading-heavy subsidiary.
  • Royalty is not the fee load. Add trademark, IT and service charges; an AGM ceiling is a limit, not a forecast.
  • A quarter is not a year. Timken's "21% exports" was the March quarter; the FY26 figure is 19.9%.
  • Grindwell's revenue is ₹3,073 crore, not ₹3,166 crore. The larger figure includes ₹93 crore of other income.
  • Net cash on screens misses deposits. Grindwell's ₹938 crore becomes ~₹1,430 crore on the annual-report basis; CUMI's cash includes ₹297–361 crore trapped in Russia.
  • Timken has two bases. Consolidated (₹3,478 crore revenue, ₹415 crore PAT, because of Timken GGB) and standalone (₹3,419 crore, ₹398 crore). End-market splits are standalone.
  • Combined SKF revenue double-counts intra-group sales. About 10% of SKF India's sales go to SKF India (Industrial); the two companies' revenue sum overstates external sales by an indicative ~₹120 crore in H2 FY26.
In plain terms

Nearly every headline number in this sub-sector has a catch. The safest habit is to ask three questions of any figure: which company and which basis (standalone, consolidated, continuing operations); which period (quarter or year); and what has been left out (traded goods, other fees, one-off charges, trapped cash).

How to read a bearings or abrasives company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, on the right base (1 minute). Use the comparable base — SKF India's Q1 FY27 was +27.1% comparable, not −54% reported. Split into volume and price where management gives it (SKF India: volume +22%).
  2. Gross margin versus last year and last quarter (1 minute). If steel or alumina moved, did gross margin hold? Timken +100 bp in Q1 FY27 = pricing power. Check for inventory-revaluation help.
  3. Operating margin, on one definition (1 minute). Use the same definition every quarter (screener OP or company EBITDA) and note the gap — SKF India's 15% versus 17.1%.
  4. Segment or end-market margins (1 minute). For CUMI and Grindwell, the segment table; for Timken, the end-market split on the call. Ceramics and C&P drive the abrasive companies; rail and exports drive Timken.
  5. Other income and exceptionals (1 minute). Strip one-offs: CUMI's Q1 FY27 ₹25.2 crore Sterling gain, SKF India (Industrial)'s Dec-25 ₹166 crore charge, SKF India's Q4 FY26 labour-code and demerger costs.
  6. Tax rate (30 seconds). Anything far from 25% needs a reason; recompute PAT at 25%.
  7. Related-party lines (1 minute). Royalty and IT fees in the quarter, any new AGM approvals, traded-goods purchases. For SKF India (Industrial), compare the royalty run-rate with the ₹222 crore ceiling.
  8. Working capital and cash (1 minute). Inventory and receivables against sales; for CUMI, the VAW restricted-cash figure (₹360.9 crore at June 2026); capex against guidance.
  9. Capacity and orders (1 minute). Bharuch utilisation, Jamshedpur start-up, SKF Pune and Haridwar timelines, CUMI ceramics orders, railway tenders.
  10. Guidance versus delivery (1 minute). Compare with the last stated guidance (SKF India ~20% growth and ~17% EBITDA; SKF India (Industrial) margin to ~12%; CUMI ceramics +23–25%, segment margins; Timken capex 8–10%). Red flags: a margin miss explained only by "one-offs" for a second quarter, rising inventory with flat sales, a royalty step-up, a new impairment note.
In plain terms

In ten minutes you can tell whether a quarter was real: did sales grow on the right base, did the gross margin survive input costs, what was one-off, what went to the parent, and did the company do what it said it would last quarter. Everything else is detail.

Why ~49× normalised earnings: the premium, and the parents

The Indian subsidiaries trade at three to four times their parents' multiples. Returns, cash, growth, scarcity and domestic ownership explain most of it; the stress test is whether earnings growth can keep up.

The five members are valued at ₹81,193 crore, about 61 times reported and an indicative 49 times normalised FY26 profit, and 33 times EV/EBITDA. Their parents and global peers trade at roughly 10–18 times earnings (approximate ranges, not re-verified this month). Indian investors are therefore paying a large premium for the same brands and technologies in Indian-listed form. Five factors explain it.

  • Returns on capital. Four of five earn 19–30% on capital (CUMI aside), against roughly 10–14% for the parents. Higher returns justify higher multiples if they can be sustained.
  • Growth. Indian revenue grew ~10% in FY26 and ~17% in Q1 FY27; the parents' revenues are roughly flat to low single digits. India is the growth market inside each group.
  • Balance sheets. All five are close to debt-free; Grindwell alone holds ~₹1,430 crore of net cash.
  • Scarcity and ownership. There are fewer than ten listed Indian bearing and abrasive companies of scale. With parents holding 51–58% and domestic institutions another 20–31%, the tradable float is small, and Indian mutual funds compete for it.
  • The parent option. MNC subsidiaries have historically been delisted or bought out at premiums often enough that the possibility supports prices — even though none of these four parents has signalled it, and Timken's has been selling.

Technology and disruption: EVs, ceramics and fewer grinding passes

No technology threatens the bearing itself; several change what kind of bearing is bought and how much grinding a part needs. The biggest new opportunity is in advanced ceramics.

Electric vehicles are the most discussed shift and, for bearings, the least threatening. A car or two-wheeler still needs wheel-end bearings whatever drives it; what changes is the drivetrain. Engines and multi-speed gearboxes, with their many bearings, give way to e-axles and electric motors that need fewer but higher-specification bearings — high-speed, low-friction and electrically insulated to stop motor currents pitting the raceways, sometimes with hybrid ceramic balls. Content per vehicle can rise in value even as the count falls. SKF India expects EV platforms to start contributing around late calendar 2028. Schaeffler, the reference peer, has already won large e-axle work. The risk for the Indian members is that the high-value EV bearings are designed and initially made abroad and imported as traded goods, widening the traded-goods share before localisation catches up.

For abrasives the EV shift is a slow negative: crankshafts, camshafts and valves — among the heaviest users of precision bonded abrasives — disappear from electric drivetrains, partly offset by gear and e-motor shaft grinding. Over 10–15 years that could trim a meaningful slice of auto grinding demand (an indicative judgement; no data quantify it). Near-net-shape forming and additive manufacturing push the same way, leaving less metal to remove. Against that, the shift from manual work to power tools in construction and fabrication widens the market for discs and coated products — which is exactly where Chinese imports compete hardest.

Bull vs bear

The bull case rests on Indian industrial demand, localisation and margin recovery; the bear case on parent fees, Chinese imports, tariffs and a valuation that leaves little cushion if growth disappoints.

The two cases below are for the sub-sector as a whole. Each member tilts the balance differently — Grindwell and Timken sit closer to the bull column on quality, CUMI and SKF India (Industrial) carry the most specific bear risks — but the shared drivers are the same: Indian production growth, input costs, the parents' choices and what Chinese makers and US tariffs do next.

The bull case

  • Demand broadening: record wind additions, GST-boosted vehicle volumes, metals projects, a large rolling-stock programme and defence ceramics, with replacement and consumable demand as a floor.
  • Margin recovery: SKF India (Industrial) back to ~12% and then 14–16%; Timken's Bharuch at 70%+ utilisation; CUMI rid of Awuko and Foskor losses; Grindwell's C&P at 20%+.
  • Localisation: Pune, Haridwar, Jamshedpur and Bharuch lines cut traded goods and lift gross margins.
  • New engines: CUMI's power-electronics and semiconductor ceramics from FY28; Grindwell's Digital Services.
  • Balance sheets and scarcity: debt-free companies with cash, high DII ownership, small floats and parents that need India as their growth market.

The bear case

  • The parent's share rises: SKF India (Industrial)'s ₹222 Cr fee ceiling, SKF India's 6.5% fee load re-papered under Vertevo, transfer prices on a third to a half of goods.
  • China: bearing imports 49% Chinese and rising; coated-abrasive imports from China +33%; corundum grain +51%; no trade remedy in force that can be confirmed.
  • Tariffs and exports: US Sec 232 on bearings' steel content and Sec 301 on India; Timken's US revenue already down 15%.
  • CUMI's Russian asset: a carrying value that assumes a 40% chance of de-listing by January 2027, and rising trapped cash.
  • Valuation: ~49× normalised and ~61× reported earnings after a year of falling profit; a 40× multiple on 10% growth is a PEG of about four.

Where does the balance lie? The evidence of the last two quarters is mixed. Revenue accelerated across the board in Q1 FY27 (+17.5% combined), and Timken and Grindwell showed they can widen margins through an input-cost shock. Against that, SKF India (Industrial)'s margin fell, CUMI's electrominerals margin hit 4.6%, and the royalty ceiling and VAW impairment assumptions introduce event risks that are hard to price. The cleanest bull argument applies to the businesses with the fewest parent and foreign complications; the cleanest bear argument applies to multiples that already assume recovery.

In plain terms

The optimistic view is that India's factories, wind farms, railways and vehicle makers will keep buying more bearings and abrasives, and these companies will make more of them locally at better margins. The cautious view is that parents may take a bigger share, Chinese competitors keep getting better, US tariffs bite, and investors are already paying for the good outcome. Both can be partly true; the September-quarter results will show which is winning.
