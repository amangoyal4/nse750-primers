00

The one-page view: demand is policy, revenue is plumbing

India has fixed the demand side of its defence industry by law and budget; what it has not fixed is the supply of engines, seekers and test clearances that turns an order into an invoice.

Indian aerospace and defence is a business with one dominant customer, the Ministry of Defence, and a peculiar rhythm. Money is promised years before it is spent. A Defence Acquisition Council approval (an "acceptance of necessity", or AoN) says the armed forces need something; a contract follows one to three years later and usually carries an advance of 10–15%; the work is then delivered in milestones over three to ten years; and the platform that results is maintained, repaired and upgraded for another thirty or forty. Listed companies earn money at every stage of that chain, but they earn it on very different terms.

The 12 companies in this report had combined FY26 revenue of ₹72,234 crore, operating profit (EBITDA, excluding other income) of ₹20,043 crore and net profit of ₹16,620 crore. On 13 October 2026 the market valued them at ₹7,83,803 crore, or 47.2 times last year's combined profit. That headline hides a very lopsided group. Hindustan Aeronautics (HAL) and Bharat Electronics (BEL) alone produce 84% of the revenue and carry 77% of the market value. Add Bharat Dynamics (BDL) and Mishra Dhatu Nigam (MIDHANI) and the four state-owned companies produce 89% of revenue and 95% of profit. The remaining eight private companies, from radar-electronics specialists such as Data Patterns and Astra Microwave to aerospace parts exporters such as Aequs and Dynamatic, are small in rupees and large in valuation: they make 5% of the group's profit but command 17% of its market value.

Five forces shape everything else in this report, and the sections that follow are organised around them.

  • A ring-fenced customer. Since FY24, 75% of the capital acquisition budget has been reserved for domestic industry, up from 58% in FY22. In FY27 that reserve is ₹1,39,100 crore. Six Positive Indigenisation Lists now ban imports of more than 5,400 items on fixed timelines. Demand is set by policy, not by the business cycle.
  • A surge after Operation Sindoor. The four-day conflict with Pakistan in May 2025 was followed by an emergency-procurement round and by record approvals: ₹6.81 lakh crore of AoNs in FY26, 3.9 times the year before, and ₹2.28 lakh crore of capital contracts signed.
  • Execution, not demand, sets revenue. HAL's order book reached ₹2,54,538 crore in March 2026, 7.7 years of revenue, yet its sales grew 6.8% because GE had delivered only 10 of the 99 F404 engines ordered in 2021. BDL's revenue fell 27% on delayed imported radars and seekers. The bottleneck now decides the growth rate.
  • A state franchise opening to private firms. HAL was left out of the competition to build prototypes of India's fifth-generation fighter, the AMCA. The draft Defence Acquisition Procedure 2026 promises a "level playing field" in development partnerships, five years of assured orders for start-up projects and a higher indigenous-content bar. Private firms made 24% of FY26 production and 45% of exports.
  • Exports and a global aerospace supply chain. Defence exports rose from ₹1,522 crore in FY17 to ₹38,424 crore in FY26. Boeing buys more than US$1.25 billion a year from over 375 Indian suppliers. For MTAR, Aequs and Dynamatic, the relevant customer is Airbus, Boeing, Safran or a US fuel-cell maker, not the Ministry of Defence.

In plain terms

The government has promised to spend more on Indian-made weapons every year and has largely kept that promise. That makes orders unusually predictable. What is not predictable is how fast companies can turn orders into deliveries, because a fighter needs an American engine, a missile needs a foreign seeker, and every item must pass the armed forces' acceptance tests. Share prices have already rewarded the predictable part. Returns from here depend mostly on the unpredictable part.

Three conclusions run through the rest of this report. First, the order book is the wrong number to model revenue from; the binding input is the slowest imported part. Second, the PSU primes look similar to private firms on operating margin but are very different businesses, because customer advances finance their inventory and earn them treasury income, while private suppliers wait 200–290 days to be paid and fund the gap with equity. Third, at 35–175 times trailing earnings, most of the group's valuations already discount several years of successful execution, so the next leg of returns will come from earnings delivery rather than from a further rise in multiples.

01

How a defence rupee becomes revenue: from approval to a forty-year tail

The sector's economics are set by a long procurement funnel and a tiered supply chain in which the design owner, the integrator and the component maker earn very different returns.

Every rupee of Indian defence revenue traces back to a decision made inside the Ministry of Defence. The armed forces draw up capability plans; the Defence Acquisition Council, chaired by the Defence Minister, grants an AoN that confirms the need, the procurement category and a rough cost; a request for proposal follows; competing products go through field trials; a Contract Negotiation Committee fixes the price; and, for contracts above roughly ₹3,000 crore, the Cabinet Committee on Security gives the final approval. Only then is a contract signed. The ministry says the whole process takes 19–26 months, and many approvals lapse and have to be re-granted. An AoN is therefore a forecast of orders two to four years out, not an order itself.

Once a contract is signed, the money moves in a recognisable pattern. Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020 terms typically pay 10–15% up front against a bank guarantee, then stage payments as milestones are met, and hold back 10–20% until the equipment is delivered and accepted. State-owned companies on nominated contracts often receive larger advances. Revenue is recognised as the work progresses, so a company with a big order book and little production shows a large balance of "customer advances" or "contract liabilities" on its balance sheet. That pool of cash, received but not yet earned, is central to the PSU business model and is explained in section 10.

The supply chain: five tiers, five kinds of economics

Behind each platform sits a pyramid of suppliers. At the top is the customer: the Army, Navy and Air Force, the Coast Guard and paramilitary forces, DRDO as a developer, ISRO for space, and foreign governments for exports. On the civil-aerospace side the customers are Airbus, Boeing, Safran, GE and Rolls-Royce. Below them sit the platform primes, which integrate a fighter, helicopter, missile system or radar network; then the sub-system makers, which build the line-replaceable units (LRUs) such as radar processors, electronic-warfare receivers and mission computers; then the precision-component and aerostructure makers; and at the base, the materials producers of superalloys, titanium and armour steel.

Value in this chain is captured in four places. The first is design authority: whoever owns the design earns the spares, upgrades and overhaul income for the life of the fleet. For the LCA Tejas, Akash and Astra programmes that owner is DRDO or its Aeronautical Development Agency; HAL, BDL and BEL hold production rights through transfer of technology. The draft DAP 2026 moves the policy language from "Make in India" to "Owned by India" for exactly this reason. The second is integration: BEL's materials and bought-out items cost about 55% of its revenue, yet it earns an EBITDA margin of about 29%; the spread is the price of turning many parts into one working system. The third is the overhaul annuity: about two-thirds of HAL's revenue comes from repair, overhaul and spares for fleets such as the roughly 260 Su-30MKIs. The fourth is sub-system intellectual property held by private firms, which earn the highest gross margins in the chain (63% at Data Patterns in FY26) but are paid last.

In plain terms

A defence company gets paid in three ways: a slice for building something, a slice for putting many suppliers' parts together into one system, and a long stream of income for keeping that system working for decades. The companies that own the design or the integration capture most of the profit. The companies that make one box inside the system can earn excellent margins on paper but often wait the better part of a year for their cash.

Value also leaks out of the country. Engines (GE's F404 and F414, Safran's M88, the Russian AL-31FP that HAL licence-builds at Koraput), missile seekers, high-end radars, semiconductors and some titanium grades are still imported. These items are expensive, so they inflate a prime's revenue without adding much margin, and they are scarce, so they set the delivery schedule. That second point turned out to matter more than anything else in FY26, and it is the subject of section 9.

02

Size, growth and a doubling in five years

Indian defence production doubled between FY21 and FY26, the capital budget grew faster than the defence budget, and the listed private firms grew twice as fast as the state primes.

Indian defence production, which the ministry measures across public and private manufacturers, reached a record ₹1.78 lakh crore in FY26, up 15.6% on FY25 and more than double the ₹84,643 crore of FY21. Growth was slow in the late 2010s, about 8% a year between FY17 and FY24 by PRS's count, then accelerated to roughly 15–16% a year over FY24–FY26 as the indigenisation rules and the domestic budget reserve took effect. The ministry's target is ₹3 lakh crore by FY29, which would need about 19% a year from here. For context, FY14 production was ₹43,746 crore, so the industry has roughly quadrupled in twelve years.

Two caveats keep this from being a straight line. Capital outlay as a share of the defence budget has drifted down over a decade, from 32% in FY14 to 26–28% in FY26–FY27, because pensions and salaries keep growing. And budgets are not always spent: in FY25 only about 51% of the capital budget had been used by December, and the revised estimate was cut about 6% below the original. The listed group grew faster than the industry it supplies

The 12 members' combined FY26 revenue of ₹72,234 crore is equal to about 41% of national defence production, but the comparison is loose: the members also sell civil aircraft parts, fuel-cell components, electronic voting machines, consumer-electronics enclosures and exports. Over FY21–FY26, revenue for the listed group grew at about 10.8% a year on a like-for-like basis (excluding Aequs, which listed in December 2025). The sector is budget-cyclical, not economy-cyclical. Order inflow follows the calendar of DAC and CCS meetings, and revenue piles up at the end of the financial year as companies rush deliveries to meet government acceptance before 31 March. HAL booked ₹13,942 crore, 42% of its FY26 revenue, in the March quarter alone; MIDHANI booked 46%. Anyone reading quarterly results needs to judge them against the same quarter a year earlier, never against the previous quarter.

In plain terms

The industry is roughly four times larger than it was in FY14 and twice the size it was in FY21, because the government decided to buy at home what it used to buy abroad. Growth in any single year depends less on the economy than on whether the defence ministry signs contracts on time, spends its capital budget, and accepts deliveries before the financial year closes.

03

The ring-fenced customer: three-quarters of the weapons budget, reserved by rule

The single most important fact about Indian defence demand is that most of it is no longer open to foreign suppliers; the second is that the armed forces still have large equipment gaps to fill.

Until the late 2010s India bought about half of its new weapons abroad. The shift came in stages. In FY21 the government began reserving part of the capital acquisition budget for domestic industry, at 40%. The reserve rose to 58% in FY22, 68% in FY23 and 75% from FY24, where it has stayed through the FY27 budget. A quarter of the domestic portion has been earmarked for private industry since FY23. Contracts have gone further than the rule: in FY25, 177 of 193 capital contracts (92% by count, roughly 81% by value) went to Indian companies, and the ministry expects about 98% of the September 2026 approval batch to be met from Indian industry. India still spent 27–28% of its FY24 modernisation budget on foreign sources, but the direction is clear.

The import ban lists

The second policy lever is the Positive Indigenisation List, a published schedule of items the services and the defence PSUs may no longer import after a stated date. The first list of 101 items came in August 2020, and five lists issued by October 2023 cover 5,012 items, according to PRS and the ministry; the longer lists name components and sub-assemblies the DPSUs may no longer import, the shorter ones whole systems the services must buy at home. A sixth list of 405 items, mostly DPSU components and sub-systems for the Su-30, LCA, T-72/T-90 and MRSAM, was issued on 18 August 2026. The ministry says more than 15,700 items have been indigenised in five years, that about ₹9,000 crore of imports have been substituted, and that DPSUs placed roughly ₹10,000 crore of orders with domestic vendors up to March 2026. For a private sub-system supplier, these lists are the most concrete pipeline there is: a banned import is a guaranteed tender.

The equipment gaps behind the budget

Policy creates the reserve; operational need fills it. The Indian Air Force has 31 active fighter squadrons against 42 authorised, and a parliamentary committee said in 2023 that at least 180 fighters were needed. The last MiG-21s retired in September 2025. The responses so far are 180 LCA Tejas Mk1A on order from HAL, an AoN for 114 Rafale multi-role fighters, and the AMCA programme. The Navy had 65 ships and submarines under construction, which drives demand for BEL's naval radars and sonars and BDL's torpedoes. After Operation Sindoor, air defence moved to the top of the list: the Quick Reaction Surface-to-Air Missile (QRSAM) programme alone is worth about ₹30,000 crore to BEL, and the long-range Project Kusha could be worth more than ₹40,000 crore to it as a sub-system partner. BEL's electronic-fuze contract, its largest single programme, gives it about eight years of visibility.

There is a ceiling to all this, and it matters for long-run forecasts. India spends about 2.0% of GDP on defence, against 3.4% for the United States, and allocations to the services ran about 20% below their projected needs over FY16–FY25. Pensions take more than a fifth of the budget. The capital budget can keep growing at 10–15% a year in nominal terms without straining the public finances, but not at the 25% a year that the domestic reserve has delivered since FY21, because the reserve cannot rise much above 75% and has already done most of its work. From here, the domestic pool grows roughly in line with capital acquisition itself.

In plain terms

By rule, three of every four rupees the government spends on new weapons must go to companies in India, and a published list bans the import of thousands of specific parts. That is why Indian defence companies can see their demand years ahead. The jump from 40% to 75% has been a one-time boost that is now mostly complete; future growth in the reserved pool will track the capital budget, which is likely to rise at 10–15% a year rather than 25%.

04

Operation Sindoor and the order surge: what four days in May set in motion Deep dive

A short war produced a fast emergency-buying round, a record year of approvals and a share-price spike. Only part of that becomes revenue for listed Indian companies, and most of it arrives later than the market first assumed.

Operation Sindoor ran from 7 to 10 May 2025. The Indian Air Force struck targets in Pakistan with BrahMos, SCALP, Crystal Maze-2, Rampage and Spice-2000 weapons; the Army used SkyStriker loitering munitions and Excalibur precision artillery rounds; and the air-defence response against Pakistani drones and missiles relied on Barak-8, Akash and BEL's Akashteer command-and-reporting network, alongside the Russian-supplied S-400 and upgraded L-70 and Shilka guns. Two lessons dominated the after-action debate. One was the sheer number of cheap drones and loitering munitions on both sides. The other was the value of a layered, networked air defence that could see, decide and fire quickly. Both lessons point straight at products made by listed Indian companies.

The fast money: emergency procurement

Emergency procurement is India's mechanism for buying quickly what the forces need now. Earlier rounds (EP-1 to EP-4, 2016–2022, and a counter-terror round in 2024–25) had established the template; EP-6 was sanctioned within about a week of the ceasefire. Its rules favour small, short-cycle products: contracts are capped at ₹300 crore each, must be signed within 40 days and delivered within a year. That rules out fighters and warships and rules in drones, counter-drone systems, loitering munitions, short-range missiles, radars and protective equipment. Among the listed companies, the clearest beneficiaries were Zen Technologies, whose management says the government "almost placed ₹800 crore worth of anti-drone orders" with it after the operation and which describes its counter-drone system as combat-proven during Sindoor; Paras Defence, whose anti-drone subsidiary accounts for 10% of its order book; and BEL, which supplies radars and the Akashteer network.

Emergency money is real, but it is smaller and messier than the headlines suggested. The ceiling was about ₹40,000 crore spread across many vendors, Indian and foreign. No consolidated tally of EP-6 contracts signed has been published; the dossier found only the ₹1,981.9 crore June 2025 batch, and some reports label it EP-5 rather than EP-6. History also counsels caution: the Comptroller and Auditor General found that 72% of Army emergency contracts it examined in an earlier round were delivered late (search-level). For an investor, emergency procurement is best treated as a one- or two-quarter revenue bump for a handful of counter-drone and munitions suppliers, not as a re-basing of the industry.

What converts, and when

An AoN becomes revenue for a listed company only after three more steps, each with its own delay. It must become a contract (one to three years, and some approvals lapse). The contract must reach a listed company's order book, either directly or through a prime's sub-contract. And the order must be executed, which for a platform takes several years. QRSAM is the clearest live example. The AoN was granted on 3 July 2025, BEL guided for it in FY26, then in FY27, and in October 2026 it is still in commercial negotiation, 15 months later. Until it is signed, BEL's FY27 order-inflow guidance of more than ₹55,000 crore cannot be met: orders disclosed between April and mid-September 2026 total only about ₹6,500 crore.

In plain terms

The war produced two kinds of money. A small, fast pot of emergency orders, capped at ₹300 crore each and to be delivered within a year, went mostly to counter-drone and munitions suppliers. A huge, slow pot of approvals, ₹6.81 lakh crore in one year, will take years to become contracts, and much of it is for foreign aircraft and missiles. Share prices jumped on the size of the second pot in the month of the first.

Who gets what

Mapped against the 12 members, the post-Sindoor pipeline concentrates at BEL (QRSAM, Akashteer, electronic warfare programmes such as Shatrughat and Samaghat worth about ₹9,000 crore, laser directed-energy weapons, and the Arudhra radars in the September AoN), at BDL (Akash, Astra, the missiles for QRSAM, of which BDL expects about ₹10,000 crore, and short-range air-defence missiles), at HAL (the 156 LCH Prachand helicopters already signed, Su-30 sustainment and upgrades, and more ALH helicopters in the September AoN), and among the counter-drone specialists (Zen and Paras). Data Patterns and Astra Microwave sit one level down, as suppliers of radar and electronic-warfare sub-systems to the primes; both have pointed to counter-drone radars, passive sensors and "electromagnetic wall" concepts as new lines, but neither had a quantified order for them in October 2026. MIDHANI benefits indirectly through alloys for missiles and engines. The aerospace exporters, MTAR, Aequs and Dynamatic, are largely outside the Sindoor story; Dynamatic's main defence link is its seat in the L&T–BEL AMCA consortium.

The market's reaction is the final caveat. The defence index rose about 31% in May 2025, BDL's trailing P/E touched 134× by the end of that month, and the index was still up 25.7% in calendar 2026 to late August while the Nifty was down 7.6% (search-level). Yet the year that followed was weak on execution: BDL's FY26 revenue fell 27%, Zen's fell 29%, HAL delivered no LCA Mk1A, and BEL's order inflow stalled. The approvals were real. The listed equity simply priced them well before they could reach the income statement.

Three readings matter. The first is that the primes are pass-through businesses. Half or more of HAL's, BEL's and BDL's revenue is someone else's product: an imported engine kit, an Israeli seeker, a sub-assembly from Data Patterns. That is why BDL's margin collapsed to 9% in FY26: when imported radars and seekers did not arrive on time, revenue fell but the company still carried its staff, factories and provisions. It is also why BEL's gross margin can swing by several hundred basis points from one quarter to the next: its Q1 FY27 gross margin fell 775 basis points year on year to 45.5% because of product mix, and EBITDA margin dropped to 25% against a 28% full-year guide.

The second is that private sub-system makers sell engineering. Materials are only 30–45% of revenue, gross margins are 50–75% (Data Patterns 63.2% in FY26, Astra 50.9%), and the big cost is engineers: Data Patterns had 1,112 of them at June 2026, Astra 701 in R&D alone. That gives them operating margins as high as or higher than the PSUs. But a large share of their spending happens long before a sale: development contracts, prototypes, qualification. That shows up later as high inventories and long receivables, not on the income statement.

The third is that aerostructure and precision shops are capital businesses. Machines, special-process lines and qualification cost money years before parts are paid for, so depreciation and interest consume most of an aero manufacturer's operating profit until volumes rise. In FY26 Aequs earned operating EBITDA of about ₹90 crore on ₹1,230 crore of revenue, then paid ₹138 crore of depreciation and ₹94 crore of interest, and lost ₹113 crore. Its aerospace segment alone earned about 23% at the EBITDA level in Q1 FY27; the group margin is pulled down by a young consumer-products business.

A historical point explains why the PSU margins are as high as they are. Older nominated contracts were priced on a cost-plus basis, giving a fixed profit of about 10–12.5% on cost. Newer contracts are largely fixed-price, which lets a PSU keep the savings when it localises a part or improves productivity. HAL's operating margin rose from 22% in FY22 to 30–32% over FY24–FY26, and BEL's from 22% to 29%, largely for this reason. The flip side is that fixed prices also leave the PSU holding the cost of delays, through liquidated damages (typically 0.5% a week up to a 10% cap), carrying costs and penalties.

In plain terms

The big PSUs make most of their money by assembling other people's parts into systems the armed forces will accept, and they earn a healthy margin on the whole bill. Private electronics firms are more like design houses: little material, many engineers, high margins on each sale but a long wait for the cash. Aerospace parts makers are heavy factories that only become profitable once their machines are busy, which is why the youngest of them still lose money.

The rulebook: who may sell, at what content, on what terms

Procurement categories, indigenous-content thresholds, import bans, offsets and investment rules decide which company is even allowed to bid; the next version of the rulebook tilts further towards Indian-owned design.

The Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020, in force since 1 October 2020, is the operating manual for capital purchases. Its most important feature is a ranked list of procurement categories. The most preferred is Buy (Indian-IDDM): equipment Indigenously Designed, Developed and Manufactured, with at least 50% indigenous content. Then come Buy (Indian), Buy and Make (Indian), Buy and Make, Buy (Global – Manufacture in India), and last, Buy (Global). A service that wants to buy abroad must explain why each higher category cannot meet the need. A November 2023 amendment set a floor of 50% indigenous content across categories. Indigenous content is measured as the base contract price less imported content, foreign freight and foreign services, divided by the base price. The higher the bar, the more a prime must localise its sub-systems, which is what creates orders for Tier-2 suppliers.

The draft DAP 2026: from "made in India" to "owned by India"

The draft released in February 2026 is the most consequential policy change since 2020, and it has not yet been promulgated: the dossier found no evidence of a final version by 13 October 2026. Three changes matter most for listed companies. First, raising the IDDM indigenous-content bar from 50% to 60% forces primes to localise another tenth of the value of each system, which is roughly the size of a radar processor, an electronic-warfare suite or an actuation system on many platforms. Second, the "Owned by India" emphasis rewards whoever owns the intellectual property, not merely whoever manufactures under licence; that is a direct opportunity for Data Patterns and Astra and a slow threat to production-agency PSUs whose designs belong to DRDO. Third, the review of how DRDO selects Development-cum-Production Partners "to ensure a level playing field", together with five years of assured orders for Make and iDEX projects, weakens the default nomination of a DPSU for every new DRDO design. A separate aerospace procedure is to follow later; reports that offsets are dropped altogether come from an opinion piece and are unverified.

Offsets: a fading source of aerostructure work

Offsets required foreign suppliers on large contracts to spend 30% of the contract value in India. They were the original route by which Dynamatic and Aequs-type companies entered the Airbus, Boeing and Dassault supply chains. The policy has largely failed on its own terms: a parliamentary Public Accounts Committee report of July 2026 found about 45% of offset obligations unfulfilled as of December 2025, with a 5% penalty, and no new offset contract signed in five years. Government-to-government deals (such as Rafale-M) and US Foreign Military Sales are exempt. For the aerospace exporters this is now a minor factor; their growth comes from Airbus's and Boeing's commercial decisions to source from India, not from offsets.

In plain terms

The rules decide who gets to bid. Today they favour anything designed and largely made in India, and they increasingly favour companies that own their designs over those that build someone else's under licence. If the 2026 draft becomes final, a private Indian firm with its own radar or electronic-warfare design will be competing for work that used to go to a PSU by default.

Two smaller regimes matter for specific members. Space reforms since 2020 (IN-SPACe, the Indian Space Policy 2023 and a February 2024 FDI amendment allowing up to 74% automatic investment in satellites, 49% in launch vehicles and 100% in components) open work for Paras's space optics, Data Patterns' satellite electronics, MTAR's ISRO engine parts and MIDHANI's alloys; the government targets a space economy of US$44 billion by 2033 from US$8.4 billion in 2022. And the drone rules, an import ban on foreign drones from February 2022 and a small ₹120 crore production-linked incentive, support counter-drone and drone-propulsion businesses such as Zen's Vector Technics subsidiary, though the Army's cancellation of drone orders over Chinese-origin parts in 2024–25 shows how strictly content rules are enforced.

Engines, seekers and the missing revenue: why order books are not sales Deep dive

HAL's order book grew by ₹65,000 crore in a year in which its sales grew ₹2,100 crore; the gap is an American engine, and similar gaps sit inside BDL, MIDHANI and the suppliers that depend on them.

With order books this large, revenue is no longer a function of demand. It is the opening order book multiplied by an execution rate, and the execution rate is set by whatever input is scarcest. In FY26 that input was the GE F404-IN20 engine for HAL's LCA Tejas Mk1A, and imported radars and seekers for BDL's Akash and Astra missiles. Understanding these bottlenecks is the single most useful thing an investor can do in this sector, because they explain why the companies with the biggest backlogs grew the slowest.

HAL: a fighter factory waiting for engines

HAL signed the contract for 83 LCA Tejas Mk1A fighters (73 single-seat plus 10 trainers), worth about ₹48,000 crore, in February 2021. GE was contracted in 2021 to supply 99 F404-IN20 engines for them, for about US$716 million. The first aircraft was due to be delivered in February–March 2024. As of September 2026, no Mk1A has been accepted by the Air Force. GE had delivered only 10 engines by August 2026, six of them in FY26 against 11 promised, and the Defence Secretary expects about 12 more between October and December 2026. GE has committed to 20–22 in FY27. HAL says the aircraft are 90–95% complete; the Air Force also wants the radar, weapons and electronic-warfare suite validated before acceptance. Sources differ on how many airframes are waiting: one report counts nine or more completed airframes, while the HAL company pack cites about 27–30 at various stages of completion.

Two balance-sheet lines give early warning across the group: rising inventory with flat revenue means product is stuck, and falling customer advances with a rising order book means the company is working through old money faster than new contracts are replacing it.

Customer advances finance the state primes' inventory and earn them treasury income; private suppliers wait 200–290 days for cash and fund the gap with equity. That one difference explains most of the gap in returns, and it is narrowing.

On operating margin, the PSUs and the private electronics firms look like peers. HAL earned 29.6% and BEL 29.2% in FY26; Data Patterns earned 40.4%, Astra 28.7%, Apollo 24.2% and Paras 25.6%. How the float works

When the ministry signs a nominated contract with a DPSU, it typically pays an advance of 10–15%, often more, against a bank guarantee, and then pays stage payments as milestones are reached. The PSU records the money as a liability ("advances from customers" or "contract liabilities") because it has not yet been earned. Until the money is spent on materials and labour, it sits in bank deposits and earns interest, which the company reports as other income. When the contract is executed, the liability is released into revenue, the cash is spent, and the interest stops. A PSU with a growing order book and generous advances therefore has three advantages at once: its inventory is financed by the customer, its cash pile grows, and its profit is lifted by treasury income that has nothing to do with manufacturing.

A rule of thumb for growth capital

The practical consequence is that the same revenue growth costs different amounts of capital in different archetypes. The dossier's rule of thumb, which should be treated as indicative, is that each ₹100 crore of extra revenue needs about ₹30–80 crore of net working capital at a DPSU after advances, ₹70–100 crore at a private electronics firm, and ₹10–15 crore of working capital plus ₹60–100 crore of fixed capital at an aerospace precision manufacturer. A private electronics company guiding to 30–40% growth, such as Paras, therefore has to find roughly ₹100–200 crore a year of extra working capital from cash flow that has historically covered a fraction of that. That is why their growth plans keep arriving with equity raises attached.

The franchise opens: a fifth-generation fighter without HAL

For sixty years new platforms went to a state company by default. The AMCA competition, the draft DAP 2026 and a rising private share of production signal that the default is ending, slowly, at the frontier.

The defence PSUs' greatest asset has never been their factories. It has been nomination: the practice of designating a DPSU as the production agency for a DRDO design or a service requirement, so that it never has to bid. BEL wins about 80–90% of its defence orders this way, by ICRA's estimate (search-level). HAL builds the Tejas, the LCH, the Su-30MKI and the ALH because nobody else was ever asked. BDL builds Akash and Astra because DRDO designated it. Nomination does not mean easy margins, since prices are negotiated line by line, but it means no competitor, a predictable order book and large advances.

The clearest break with that model came in 2026. The Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft, India's planned fifth-generation stealth fighter, approved by the Cabinet Committee on Security in March 2024 with about ₹15,000 crore for development, will have its five prototypes built by a private-led consortium. Requests for proposal went on 27 May 2026 to three shortlisted bidders. HAL, India's only fighter manufacturer, is not among them.

The draft DAP 2026 extends the same logic

The draft procedure released in February 2026 proposes reviewing how DRDO selects Development-cum-Production Partners "to ensure a level playing field". It raises the indigenous-content bar for the top procurement category from 50% to 60% and emphasises Indian ownership of intellectual property. Together these changes push value towards whoever owns the design. Private firms with their own IP, such as Data Patterns (radar, electronic warfare, avionics) and Astra (transmit-receive modules, radar sub-systems), are the natural beneficiaries; production-agency PSUs that build DRDO designs under licence face slower erosion of the nomination rent.

A quarter of the domestic capital budget has been reserved for private industry since FY23. BDL faces private competition in missiles for the first time: Adani in the ULPGM-V3 precision-guided munition, and private bidders for very short-range air-defence and anti-tank missiles, with Solar Industries and Kalyani in the background. BEL competes in open tenders against L&T, Tata, Data Patterns and Astra, and was not chosen as system integrator for the Netra Mk2 airborne early-warning programme. HAL's ALH fleet was grounded after the January 2025 Porbandar crash and progressively cleared; reputational damage of that kind makes the services more willing to look elsewhere.

In plain terms

The government is gradually ending the habit of handing every new weapons programme to a state company. The first big sign is that private consortia, not HAL, will build the prototypes of India's next fighter. Nothing changes in HAL's or BEL's revenue for years, because their existing programmes run into the 2030s, but over a decade the most profitable new work could increasingly go to whoever owns the design, and that will more often be a private company.

Two cautions temper the shift. The largest private primes, Tata Advanced Systems and L&T's defence arm, are not separately listed, so investors cannot buy the clearest winners of private entry through this sub-sector. And the listed private firms are mostly suppliers to the PSUs; if BEL loses a radar tender to L&T, Data Patterns or Astra may still supply the sub-systems inside the winning bid. The opening shifts margin from integrators to design owners more than it shifts revenue from PSUs to listed private companies.

Exports and the global aero supply chain: the second customer

Defence exports rose 25-fold in nine years, and three members already earn most of their revenue from Airbus, Boeing, Safran and a US fuel-cell maker. Exports are the only route out of a single-customer business.

India's defence exports were ₹1,522 crore in FY17. In FY26 they were ₹38,424 crore, up 62.7% on the ₹23,622 crore of FY25. The ministry targets ₹50,000 crore by FY29–30, which needs only about 14% a year from here, though FY26 included one-off contracts that may not repeat. India's share of global arms exports remains tiny, about 0.2% over 2016–23 by SIPRI's count, so it is a niche exporter that takes prices rather than sets them. The main items are BrahMos (the Philippines, US$375 million in 2022), Akash and Pinaka (Armenia), radars, artillery shells, Dornier aircraft and ALH helicopters, and aerostructures for Boeing and Airbus where these fall under defence export authorisations. The ministry says India exports to 80–100 countries.

The commercial aerospace supply chain

For the three aero-precision members, defence is a side business. Their customers are the commercial aircraft and engine makers, whose combined backlog is about 15,000 aircraft (roughly 8,700 at Airbus and 6,000 at Boeing at end-2025, cited from memory) and who are short of supplier capacity. Indian airlines alone have more than 1,700 aircraft on order. Boeing sources more than US$1.25 billion a year from India through over 375 suppliers (verified, August 2026); Airbus sources about US$1.4 billion and has said it aims for US$2 billion by 2030 (search-level). Safran has expanded in India through engine-part forgings with HAL, maintenance for LEAP and M88 engines in Hyderabad, and supply agreements with Aequs. An interim US–India trade agreement in February 2026 removed US tariffs on some Indian aircraft parts (search-level).

The members' aero franchises are distinct. Dynamatic is the incumbent: it is the world's largest single-source maker of Airbus A330 flap-track beams, has delivered more than 9,000 A320-family shipsets of beams, builds all eight door types for the A220, and since December 2025 makes the complete rear fuselage of the Dassault Falcon 6X business jet; on the US side it builds parts for the Boeing P-8, F-15 and CH-47 Chinook. MTAR is different again: its 82% exports are mostly fuel-cell hot boxes for a US customer serving data-centre power demand, with aerospace and defence only 12% of revenue. In plain terms

Selling abroad is the only way an Indian defence company can escape having one customer who sets the price and the payment date. The PSUs have barely started; their exports are a few per cent of sales. The three aerospace-parts makers are already mostly exporters, but that means their fortunes depend on how many aircraft Airbus and Boeing build, and in MTAR's case on how many fuel cells one American company sells, rather than on the Indian defence budget.

Europe's rearmament since 2024 cuts both ways. It is competing for the same Western supply chains, forgings, energetics, electronics, that Indian primes depend on for imported parts, which can lengthen lead times. It is also an export opportunity: Indian private firms shipped shells and propellant to European buyers in 2024–25 (press reports cited from memory). For the members, the most direct export levers over the next two years are BEL's US$465 million export book, BDL's next Akash or air-defence export, Zen's simulators and counter-drone systems, and the Airbus and Safran ramps at Aequs and Dynamatic.

Five cycles in one decade: from licence factories to a 56× index

The industry's history is long and slow; the listed sector's is short and fast. Equity cycles have followed approvals and news-flow, while earnings have moved at the speed of execution.

HAL traces its roots to Hindustan Aircraft in 1940 and took its present form in 1964; BEL was set up in 1954, BDL in 1970 and MIDHANI in 1973. For four decades they were licence factories, building Soviet MiG-21s and MiG-27s and T-72 tanks under transfer-of-technology arrangements. Private companies were allowed into defence manufacturing only in 2001, with foreign investment capped at 26%. An offset policy followed in 2005. Until 2018, BEL (listed since 1992) was the only defence PSU investors could buy.

Two patterns repeat. First, the stocks move on approvals and the earnings move on deliveries, so prices lead earnings by years, and gaps between them close through de-ratings rather than through earnings catching up quickly. The July 2024 peak came after a year of record AoNs; the de-rating came when the next year's AoNs fell and the LCA slipped. Second, the multiple is the volatile part. HAL's earnings per share rose from ₹114 in FY24 to ₹136 in FY26, a steady 9% a year, while its P/E swung between 31× and 49×. For anyone holding these stocks, the question is less whether the earnings will grow than how much of that growth the current multiple already pays for, the subject of section 18.

In plain terms

Defence shares in India have gone through boom, bust and boom again in five years, while the companies' profits rose fairly steadily. The share prices react to announcements of future orders; the profits arrive when equipment is delivered, often years later. Buying after a burst of announcements has tended to mean waiting a long time for the profits to justify the price.
