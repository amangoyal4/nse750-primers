00

The one-page view: the price is set abroad, the take is set in Delhi

Two state-controlled producers pump about 86% of India's crude. Their earnings depend on one number they do not control and on a second decision they cannot predict.

Indian oil and gas exploration and production (E&P) is a two-company sub-sector. Oil and Natural Gas Corporation (ONGC) and Oil India (OIL) produced roughly 86% of the country's crude in the first half of FY27 and about 62% of its gross gas in FY26. Every barrel they lift is sold to an Indian refinery at a price linked to Brent. Every unit of their legacy gas is sold at a price the government sets and caps. Neither company decides how much of a price spike it may keep: that is settled in New Delhi through royalty, an oil-industry cess, and in the past a windfall tax or forced discounts to fuel retailers. Understanding this sub-sector means understanding three things at once: the global oil price, the domestic fiscal and pricing rules that filter it, and the slow physical battle against ageing fields.

2026 put all three under a spotlight. The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 took the Indian crude basket from $69 a barrel in February to $113–114 in March and April. It eased to $82–83 in June and July, then spiked again to $116 in September, and averaged about $120 in October to date (PPAC). For the first time in two decades of $100-plus oil, the government did not reimpose a windfall tax on domestic crude. It taxed fuel exports instead, cut excise on petrol and diesel, and leaned on refiners. The result was the best quarter in the history of both producers. In Q1 FY27 (the June 2026 quarter), ONGC's standalone profit after tax was ₹17,034 crore, up 112%, on a crude realisation of $99.45 a barrel. Oil India's was ₹2,870 crore, against ₹813 crore a year earlier, on $98.73.

This report is built around five forces:

  • Price, filtered through a weak rupee. Realisation in dollars per barrel is the master variable. Costs are mostly in rupees, so with the rupee near ₹94.9 to the dollar (against an FY26 average of ₹88.31), each dollar of crude is worth about 7% more in rupees than a year ago (sections 3 and 14).
  • The state's claw-back toolkit. Subsidy sharing in 2003–2015, the windfall tax (SAED) of 2022–24, the APM gas cap, royalty and cess, and dividends to a 57–59% government shareholder decide how much of a spike the producer keeps (sections 4 and 5).
  • The volume problem. India's crude output has fallen from 32.2 million tonnes in FY20 to 27.9 million tonnes in FY26. KG-98/2, the Mumbai High redevelopment with bp, Oil India's Assam and Rajasthan push and reserve replacement are the counter-moves (section 6).
  • Two-layer consolidation. ONGC's E&P layer sits under HPCL, MRPL, ONGC Videsh (OVL) and OPaL; Oil India's sits under NRL, which is tripling capacity from 3 to 9 million tonnes a year (section 9).
  • Policy reset and capital allocation. OALP and HELP acreage rounds, the Oilfields Amendment Act 2025, the Petroleum and Natural Gas Rules 2025, the ₹84,084 crore Samudra Manthan offshore exploration scheme, record dividends, capex and the energy transition (sections 11 and 12).

The market's verdict is a deep state-owned discount. On 22 October 2026 ONGC traded at ₹225 (market cap ₹2,83,395 crore), 6.48 times trailing attributable earnings and 0.76 times book, with a 5.89% dividend yield. Oil India traded at ₹457 (₹74,467 crore), 8.91 times earnings and 1.28 times book, with a 2.52% yield. Together they are worth ₹3.58 lakh crore, about 7.45 times their FY26 attributable profit. Those multiples sit next to other state-controlled producers such as CNOOC (6.8 times) and Petrobras (7.4 times) and far below Equinor, Saudi Aramco and ExxonMobil. The discount reflects the policy risk, the falling volumes and the refinery exposure, all of which this report examines.

In plain terms

ONGC and Oil India sell oil at world prices and pay costs in rupees, so a jump in crude and a weaker rupee both lift their profits sharply. What they are allowed to keep depends on the government, which has in the past taken most of any spike through discounts or a special tax. In 2026 it chose not to tax domestic crude, which is why both companies just had record quarters. The open questions are whether that holds, whether their ageing fields can be made to grow again, and how much their refining subsidiaries cost or add along the way.

01

How a barrel becomes profit: from licence to refinery gate

An upstream company finds hydrocarbons, lifts them and sells them at the wellhead. Almost everything that decides its margin happens before and after that sale.

An E&P company does five things. It secures acreage: a licence from the government to explore a block of land or sea. It explores, by shooting seismic surveys and drilling exploratory wells, most of which in frontier areas find nothing commercial. When it does find something, it develops the field by building platforms, pipelines, processing plants and production wells. It then produces, lifting crude and gas and separating them, and finally it sells. In India that last step is unusually constrained. Domestic crude is sold to designated public-sector refiners such as HPCL, IOC, BPCL, MRPL and CPCL under Crude Oil Sales Agreements (COSA), at a formula price based on Brent plus or minus a quality adjustment. Gas from old fields is sold into the national grid at a price set monthly by the government and allocated to city gas, fertiliser and power consumers.

The products are more varied than the label "oil company" suggests. ONGC's FY26 standalone revenue of ₹1,32,508 crore (company basis) was 63% crude and condensate (₹83,810 crore on 18.79 million tonnes sold), 27% natural gas (₹36,330 crore on 15.49 billion cubic metres sold), and 9% value-added products (₹11,879 crore): LPG, naphtha, ethane-propane (C2-C3) and kerosene stripped out of the gas stream at its Hazira, Uran and Dahej plants. Oil India is simpler: crude from Upper Assam and Arunachal, gas from Assam and Rajasthan, a little LPG and condensate, and tariff income from its crude and product pipelines in the North-East.

The legal form of the acreage matters for every later number. About 79% of India's crude still comes from nomination fields, the blocks handed to ONGC and OIL before 1999 without competitive bidding. Nomination crude pays royalty (roughly 20% of wellhead value onshore and 10% offshore) plus an Oil Industry Development Board (OIDB) cess of 20% ad valorem and a small National Calamity Contingent Duty of ₹50 a tonne. Nomination gas is sold under the Administered Pricing Mechanism (APM). Production-sharing contracts (PSCs) under the pre-NELP and NELP rounds let the operator recover costs before splitting "profit petroleum" with the government. The Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy (HELP, 2016) and its Open Acreage Licensing Policy (OALP) rounds replaced this with simpler revenue-sharing contracts and gave the operator pricing and marketing freedom.

Where is value captured? At the wellhead, when crude prices are high, but only on crude. The two producers' standalone operating margins run at 30–61% of revenue, after royalty and cess have already taken about a fifth of gross sales. Gas value is captured mostly by consumers, because the APM cap holds legacy gas below its formula price whenever oil is above roughly $70. Refining margins are captured by integrated owners only when pump prices are allowed to move: in 2026 HPCL's marketing arm bled while standalone refiners without pumps (MRPL, NRL) earned windfall margins. And the exchequer captures a large share at every step: the petroleum sector contributed ₹4,75,719 crore to the central exchequer in FY26, 14% of the Centre's revenue receipts, and ₹3,41,821 crore to the states (PPAC). ONGC alone contributed ₹55,865 crore in FY26, equal to 42.2% of its turnover.

In plain terms

The oil itself is priced off Brent, but the gas is priced by the government, and the old fields that produce most of both pay a heavy royalty and a 20% cess before the company sees a rupee of profit. A company that finds new oil under the newer contracts keeps more of each barrel; a company living off fields awarded fifty years ago keeps less. ONGC and Oil India are mostly the second kind, which is why their margins move so strongly with policy.

One more structural feature shapes the numbers: neither company competes with imports on price. Both domestic and imported crude are priced off international benchmarks, so a domestic barrel is simply an imported barrel that did not need shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. That makes domestic production strategically valuable to the state in a crisis year like 2026, which strengthens the argument for letting producers keep their windfall and fund more drilling. It also means the producers have no pricing power of their own: they are price-takers on crude and policy-takers on gas.

02

Size, decline and the import gap: a shrinking share of a growing bill

India refines more than 5 million barrels a day and produces barely 0.6 million. The gap is widening every year.

India is the world's third-largest oil consumer but a minor producer. Its refineries processed 272.1 million tonnes of crude in FY26, about 5.46 million barrels a day, while domestic fields produced 27.9 million tonnes of crude and condensate, about 0.56 million barrels a day and roughly 0.6% of world supply. Crude import dependence on a consumption basis rose from 84.4% in FY21 to 88.7% in FY26, and was 88.1% in the first half of FY27 (PPAC). Gas is less import-dependent at about 50%, but domestic gas output has also slipped: 36.4 billion cubic metres (BCM) gross in FY24, 34.8 BCM in FY26 and down 4.4% in H1 FY27.

The import bill is where the decline hurts the country. India imported 245.8 million tonnes of crude in FY26 for $123.4 billion, an average of $68.49 a barrel, and its net oil and gas import bill was $119.1 billion. In H1 FY27 the crude bill alone was $90.6 billion, up 46% on H1 FY26, despite lower volumes. Domestic crude is worth roughly a tenth of the import bill, about $14 billion at FY26 prices. At Q2 FY27 prices of around $96 a barrel and ₹95 to the dollar, every extra million tonnes of domestic crude saves roughly $0.7 billion of imports a year (indicative arithmetic). That is the national-interest case for the exploration push described in section 11.

In money terms, the sub-sector is modest by revenue and large by profit. The two producers' standalone revenue was ₹1,52,986 crore in FY26 (down 0.9%), with operating profit (screener basis) of ₹67,782 crore and PAT of ₹37,349 crore (down 10.5%). Including their refining, overseas and petrochemical subsidiaries, consolidated revenue was ₹6,41,744 crore, operating profit ₹1,13,566 crore and PAT before minorities ₹57,344 crore (up 26%), of which about ₹48,044 crore was attributable to shareholders. Overseas, ONGC Videsh produced 9.67 million tonnes of oil equivalent (MMTOE) in FY26 and Indian companies together 19.2 MMTOE abroad.

The forecast is flat. ICRA expected India's FY27 crude output at 28.0–28.5 million tonnes in March 2026; with H1 down 3.5%, a full year near 27–27.5 million tonnes is more likely (indicative). Company guidance implies ONGC standalone production rising about 1–3% a year to FY28 (from 38.87 to about 40 MMTOE) and Oil India growing faster, to a stretch target of 10–12 MMTOE by FY30 from 6.64. No large national increase is plausible before FY29 without a major deepwater discovery, which is what Samudra Manthan is designed to find.

In plain terms

India buys almost nine of every ten barrels it uses, and the share it produces itself keeps shrinking because the big old fields are running down faster than new ones replace them. For the two producers that is a volume problem, but in a year of $100 oil it also makes every domestic barrel more valuable to the country, which helps explain why the government let them keep more of the price this time.

03

The 2026 price shock: a war, a weak rupee and a record quarter

Realisation per barrel is the master variable. In 2026 it rose by half in a single quarter, and the rupee added another 7%.

The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the route for a large share of India's crude, LPG and LNG imports. A ceasefire around 7 April did not fully reopen transits. The Indian crude basket, which PPAC redefined from March 2026 to track the crude India actually imports each month, jumped from $69.01 in February to $113.49 in March and $114.48 in April. It eased to $83.22 in June and $82.04 in July, rose to $90.19 in August and spiked again to $116.09 in September. October to date (to the 21st) averaged $120.09, with a spot price of $116.73 on 21 October and an unusually sour-heavy mix. Brent, on EIA's dated series, averaged $103.28 in Q1 FY27 and $96.33 in Q2 FY27.

What the producers actually receive is not the basket. Both companies sell at formula prices linked to dated Brent, with a lag and a quality adjustment. ONGC's management describes the formula as "Brent is the base… about 5%… 4% comes to our kitty", which sounds like a premium. The data say otherwise. Against EIA's dated Brent, ONGC realised $1.6–3.8 a barrel below Brent in every quarter with a printed number since Q3 FY25, and Oil India $0.8–4.6 below. The gap was widest in Q1 FY27 ($3.8 for ONGC, $4.6 for OIL) because Brent peaked in April and fell through June, and the pricing lag works against sellers on the way down. In Q2 FY27, with Brent rising from July to September, the gap should narrow.

The rupee amplifies everything. Realisations are in dollars, while most costs (salaries, domestic contracts, depreciation on rupee assets) are in rupees. The rupee averaged ₹84.57 to the dollar in FY25 and ₹88.31 in FY26; it was ₹95.46 in September 2026 and about ₹94.9 on 19 October, near a record low of ₹96.8. A move from ₹88.3 to ₹94.9 raises the rupee value of every barrel by about 7.5%. As an illustration: ONGC's Q1 FY26 realisation of $66.13 at roughly ₹86 was worth about ₹5,690 a barrel; Q1 FY27's $99.45 at roughly ₹94.5 was worth about ₹9,400, a 65% rise in rupees against a 50% rise in dollars (indicative FX).

The shock reached beyond crude. LPG consumption fell 14.9% in H1 FY27 as Gulf supply was disrupted, while petrol rose 7.0% and diesel 4.9%. Spot gas on the Indian Gas Exchange averaged $28.4 per mmBtu in September 2026, pricing imported LNG out of power and industry. Freight and insurance costs rose. The government responded with an excise cut on petrol and diesel from 26 March (to ₹11.90 and ₹7.80 a litre), export levies on diesel and ATF from 27 March, and four pump-price increases in May. On the macro side, the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and the Nifty closed at 22,780 on 28 September.

In plain terms

When oil jumps, the producers' revenue jumps with it, but their costs barely move, so profit rises much faster than price. A weaker rupee adds a second boost because the oil is sold in dollars. In the June 2026 quarter both effects arrived together and nothing was taken away by a new tax, which is why profit doubled. The same shock hit the government-owned fuel retailers in reverse, because they could not raise pump prices fast enough.

What does this mean for the September quarter? The basket averaged $96.11 in Q2 FY27 and Brent $96.33. Given the consistent $1–4 discount to dated Brent, expect ONGC and Oil India to report realisations of roughly $93–95 a barrel, about $5 below Q1 (indicative read-across). APM gas stayed capped at $7.00 throughout the quarter. Standalone profits should therefore be strong again but below Q1, while ONGC's consolidated profit should swing up because HPCL returned to a ₹3,625 crore profit. October's $120 basket points to a stronger Q3 if it holds, and also to a higher risk that the government revisits its decision not to tax domestic crude.

04

Who keeps the windfall: three spikes, three playbooks Deep dive

In 2012–14 the state took the upside through discounts; in 2022–24 through a windfall tax; in 2026 through everything except the upstream. The producer's profit depends on which pipe the state uses.

The single most important question about an Indian upstream company is not "where is Brent?" but "at what Brent does the state reach in, and through which pipe?" The government is simultaneously the producers' regulator, their majority shareholder (58.89% of ONGC, 56.66% of OIL), the price-setter for their gas, the collector of their royalty and cess, and the owner of the fuel retailers that buy their crude. When oil spikes, it has to decide who absorbs the shock: motorists, the fuel retailers, the budget or the producers. For most of the past two decades the answer included the producers. In 2026 it largely did not.

The toolkit has five instruments. Two are permanent: royalty and cess, which take roughly a fifth of gross revenue in every year, and dividends, which return most of what is left to the government as owner. Two are episodic: subsidy sharing, under which upstream companies gave discounts on crude to fuel retailers from FY2003-04 to FY2015-16, and the Special Additional Excise Duty (SAED) on domestic crude, the windfall tax of July 2022 to December 2024. The fifth is structural and quiet: the APM gas ceiling, which caps legacy gas whenever oil is above about $70 and is the subject of section 5.

Episode one: subsidy sharing, 2003–2015

From FY2003-04, the government asked ONGC, OIL and GAIL to share the "under-recoveries" that state fuel retailers suffered by selling kerosene, domestic LPG and, until October 2014, diesel below cost. The upstream companies did this by giving the retailers a discount on every barrel of crude they sold them. PPAC's tables show total under-recoveries rising from ₹5,430 crore in FY2002-03 to ₹1,03,292 crore in FY2008-09. The upstream share was about a third in the early years and 31–39% in FY10–FY12.

The peak came in FY2013-14, when the upstream companies paid ₹67,021 crore, 47.9% of a total under-recovery of ₹1,39,870 crore. ONGC alone gave ₹56,384 crore. Its gross crude price that year was $106.72 a barrel; after a discount of $65.75 its net realisation was $40.97. The hit to its pre-tax profit was ₹47,756 crore. In FY2014-15 ONGC shared ₹36,300 crore (nil in the fourth quarter), and its net realisation was $44.87 against a gross $85.28. With petrol deregulated in 2010 and diesel in October 2014, the scheme shrank to ₹1,980 crore in April–September 2015 and ended from the third quarter of FY16, when LPG subsidy moved to direct cash transfers.

Episode two: the windfall tax, 2022–2024

When Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent the basket to $93 for FY23, the government chose a tax rather than discounts. From 1 July 2022 it levied SAED on domestic crude at ₹23,250 a tonne, about $40 a barrel at the rupee rate then. The rate was reset every fortnight off international prices, effectively taxing realisation above roughly $75. It fell to ₹1,700 by December 2022, went to nil in April and May 2023, climbed back to ₹12,100 by end-September 2023, and swung between ₹1,300 and ₹9,600 through 2024 before reaching nil on 18 September 2024. It was formally scrapped on 2 December 2024. One detail matters for any future debate: SAED applied only to crude volumes up to the previous year's output; incremental production above that level was exempt.

In plain terms

Twice before, when oil went above $100, the government took most of the extra money from ONGC and Oil India: first by making them sell crude cheaply to the fuel retailers, then by charging a special tax that rose and fell every two weeks. In 2014 ONGC kept only about $41 of a $107 barrel. In 2022–24 the tax took ₹27,618 crore from the two companies over three years. In 2026 neither tool was used on domestic crude.

Episode three: 2026, a different playbook

Faced with $114 oil in April 2026, the government spread the pain in other directions. It cut central excise on petrol and diesel from 26 March to ₹11.90 and ₹7.80 a litre, sacrificing its own revenue. From 27 March it levied SAED and additional excise on exports of diesel and ATF (petrol from 16 May), revised fortnightly; the diesel levy peaked at ₹55.50 a litre on 11 April and was ₹8.50 on 16 October. Standalone refiners such as NRL gave discounts to the fuel retailers on petrol and diesel "exactly as per SAED", which is a claw-back of refining margin, not of upstream profit. Pump prices rose about ₹7.4 a litre in four steps in May. Domestic crude production paid no SAED at any point.

Why spare the upstream this time? Three reasons stand out, though the government has not stated them. First, the 2024 abolition of SAED was explicitly linked to encouraging exploration, and the Oilfields Amendment Act 2025 promises fiscal stability to investors the government is courting for OALP and Samudra Manthan. Second, the state needs upstream cash: it collects dividends from both companies and wants them to co-fund an expensive offshore exploration drive. In plain terms

This time the government let the producers keep the high oil price and took its money from fuel exporters, from its own excise and from the retail fuel companies instead. That choice is the main reason ONGC and Oil India just earned record profits. It can be reversed by a notification on any 1st or 16th of the month, so investors should treat the windfall as a policy decision that is renewed every fortnight, not as a permanent change.

Seven dollars: the gas cap as a hidden subsidy Deep dive

Legacy gas is priced at 10% of the crude basket but capped at $7.00. In 2026 the formula said $11. The difference went to fertiliser plants, power stations and city gas users.

If crude is where the producers kept their windfall, gas is where they gave it up. About 70–80% of ONGC's and Oil India's gas comes from nomination fields and is sold under the Administered Pricing Mechanism. Since April 2023, following the Kirit Parikh committee, the APM price has been set monthly at 10% of the Indian crude basket, with a floor of $4 and a ceiling of $6.50 per mmBtu (gross calorific value) for FY24 and FY25. The ceiling rises by $0.25 a year from FY26: $6.75 in FY26 and $7.00 in FY27. On the same rule it would be $7.25 in FY28 (indicative). The ceiling is what matters: whenever the basket is above about $70, the cap binds.

The history explains why the cap was introduced. Between 2014 and March 2023 domestic gas was priced half-yearly off a basket of international gas hubs (Henry Hub, NBP, Alberta, Russia). That formula collapsed to $1.79 per mmBtu in October 2020–March 2021, below the cost of production for many fields, and then soared to $8.57 in October 2022–March 2023 as European gas prices exploded. The 2023 reform linked the price to crude, which producers liked, and capped it, which consumers needed. Fertiliser plants, gas-fired power stations and city gas networks (CNG for vehicles and piped gas for homes) receive APM allocations, so the cap protects food subsidies, power tariffs and household fuel bills.

In plain terms

The government lets the producers sell old-field gas at a tenth of the oil price, but never above $7. When oil is at $70 that makes no difference; when oil is at $110 it means the gas is sold for about two-thirds of what the formula says it is worth. The gap goes to the companies that use the gas, mostly fertiliser makers, power plants and city gas networks. It is a subsidy that never appears in the budget.

The escape routes: new-well gas and HP-HT gas

Two categories of gas escape the cap. New-well gas (NWG) from new wells or well interventions in nomination fields earns a 20% premium over the APM formula price, effectively 12% of the basket, and is not subject to the ceiling. ONGC's NWG averaged $13.31 in Q1 FY27 against $8.24 a year earlier (1.2 times the April–June formula prices of $10.76, $11.59 and $10.93), and the CEO cited $13.91 in May 2026. NWG was 19% of ONGC's gas volume, 24% of sales quantity (up from 17% a year earlier) and 38% of nomination gas revenue in Q1 FY27, bringing in about ₹4,000 crore. ONGC's NWG is allocated to GAIL for city gas and to its own Dahej C2-C3 plant.

High-pressure, high-temperature (HP-HT) and deepwater gas, such as ONGC's KG-98/2 and RIL-bp's KG-D6, has marketing freedom up to a separate ceiling set every six months from lagged fuel-oil, LNG and coal benchmarks. Because of the lag, the ceiling actually fell into the 2026 spike: from $10.04 in April–September 2025 and $9.72 in October 2025–March 2026 to $8.90 in April–September 2026, before rising to $9.89 for October 2026–March 2027. ONGC's KG-98/2 gas, at 1.5–1.7 million standard cubic metres a day (MMSCMD), is about 3% of its output.

In plain terms

The way out of the $7 cap is to produce new gas: from new wells in old fields, which earn a 20% premium with no ceiling, or from deep and high-pressure fields, which have their own higher ceiling. Every extra unit of gas the producers bring on is therefore worth roughly half as much again as the old gas. That is why both companies talk about gas as their growth engine, and why delays in KG-98/2 or in Oil India's pipelines matter more than the volume numbers alone suggest.

Running up a down escalator: decline, KG-98/2 and the reserve ledger Deep dive

Mumbai High is in its fiftieth year and declines 5–7% a year without new work. Reserves are being replaced on paper; production is not yet growing.

Every oil field declines once it passes its plateau, as reservoir pressure falls and water replaces oil in the pore space. ONGC's management puts natural decline in its offshore fields at 5–7% a year, and Mumbai High, discovered in 1974 and producing since 1976, is in its fiftieth year. Standing still therefore requires constant drilling, workovers, water injection and redevelopment. ONGC spends roughly $3.5–4 billion a year on capex largely to hold production flat. The question for investors is whether the current programme can turn a two-decade decline into growth, or merely slow it.

The scorecard so far is mixed. ONGC's group production (domestic operated, joint-venture share and ONGC Videsh) fell from 55.71 MMTOE in FY22 to 50.14 MMTOE in FY26. Its domestic operated output fell more gently, from 40.45 to 38.86 MMTOE. ONGC reports its crude in several scopes, which causes confusion: standalone crude excluding condensate was 18.355 million tonnes in FY26 (18.558 in FY25); including condensate it was 19.33 million tonnes; including its share of joint ventures 20.50 million tonnes; and the group total including OVL was 27.41 million tonnes of crude plus 22.72 BCM of gas. Q1 FY27 standalone output was 4.452 million tonnes of crude (down 4.9%) and 4.756 BCM of gas (down 1.9%), figures that come from press summaries rather than a company document and should be treated as INDICATIVE.

Mumbai High and bp: renewing the giant

The more encouraging story is in the west. In 2025 ONGC appointed bp as Technical Services Provider (TSP) for Mumbai High; the Phase-1 notice of award was issued on 26 December 2025, targeting an additional 2.699 million tonnes of oil and 2.806 BCM of gas by 2040. bp's programme is running at about 107% of the contractual baseline for oil and 113% for gas, which in absolute terms means production 1–2% higher for oil and 5–6% higher for gas against a 6–7% natural decline. The TSP model has been extended to all of Western Offshore, where more than ₹40,000 crore of projects are in execution, and the ₹1,219 crore Part Replacement Pipeline Project was completed in March 2026. ONGC also ran 12,963 well-stimulation jobs and had 17 enhanced-recovery pilots approved in FY26.

In plain terms

ONGC's fields lose a few percent of output every year unless it keeps spending to hold them up. Its big new deepwater field has produced less than half the oil once hoped for and a fraction of the gas, and the company has cut its production targets three times in fifteen months. The best news is from the oldest field, Mumbai High, where bp's help is slowing the decline. For now, ONGC is running hard just to stay roughly where it is.

The reserve ledger

Reserves are the inventory an E&P sells from. Proved reserves (1P) are volumes with at least a 90% probability of being recovered; proved plus probable (2P) are best estimates with at least a 50% probability. ONGC's group 1P fell from 845.87 MMTOE on 1 April 2022 to 775.42 in 2025, then rose to 793.31 in 2026 when OVL's Sakhalin-1 interest was restored. ONGC's own domestic 1P fell by about 42 MMTOE in FY23–FY24 and has since stabilised at 517.88. Its 2P was 709.32 MMTOE on 1 April 2026 (697.83 operated plus 11.49 joint venture). The reserve replacement ratio (RRR), 2P additions divided by production, was 1.35 in FY25 and 1.17 in FY26, the 19th consecutive year above one according to the company. At about 40.3 MMTOE of domestic output a year, 2P lasts about 17.6 years and 1P about 13.1 years (indicative arithmetic).

In plain terms

For every ₹100 of oil and gas ONGC sells, about ₹20 goes straight to the government as royalty and cess, ₹21 pays for running the fields, and ₹12 covers dry wells, provisions and currency losses, leaving ₹47. On a per-barrel basis, the state's levies cost about as much as lifting the oil. Indian producers spend roughly one and a half times what CNOOC does per barrel and twice what Petrobras spends, mostly because their fields are old and small.

The most important feature of this cost structure is that it is fixed. Royalty and cess are ad valorem and scale with price, but almost everything else (staff, contracts, vessel leases, depreciation) does not. ONGC said its opex in Q1 FY27 was "broadly at the same level" as a year earlier despite inflation and a weaker rupee. As a result, when revenue rose ₹14,457 crore year on year in Q1 FY27 (from ₹32,003 crore to ₹46,460 crore), operating profit rose ₹11,170 crore (from ₹17,185 crore to ₹28,355 crore): 77% of the incremental revenue fell straight to operating profit, after the higher royalty and cess. The operating margin went from 54% to 61%.

The same leverage works in reverse. In a $60 oil year, levies of about $10 per boe, opex of $11–13 and DD&A of about $10 leave ONGC with an all-in cost near $31 per boe against CNOOC's $27.90, so its margin compresses faster than its global peers' when prices fall (indicative comparison). Year-end quarters also look worse than the run-rate: Q4 is weak every year (ONGC's operating margin was 40% in Q4 FY25 and 35% in Q4 FY26) because dry-well write-offs, provisions and foreign-exchange losses tend to be booked then. A Q4 should never be annualised.

In plain terms

Because most costs stay the same whatever the oil price does, about three-quarters of every extra rupee of revenue in the June 2026 quarter turned into operating profit. That is the good side of high fixed costs. The bad side is that when oil falls, profit falls just as fast, and the year-end quarter usually carries a pile of write-offs that make it look worse than the rest.

ONGC and Oil India dominate because they were handed India's best acreage before 1999, not because they out-competed anyone. The private challengers are in decline.

Three-quarters of ONGC's consolidated revenue is refining and fuel retail. When crude spikes and pump prices are held, the two halves of the same company move in opposite directions.

In plain terms

Oil India's refinery gains when ONGC's refinery-and-pumps business loses, because NRL sells to the retailers instead of running pumps itself. ONGC, meanwhile, holds listed stakes worth about a third of its own market value, which means the market is paying very little for its actual oil and gas business.

Five business models under two roofs

A mature nomination field, a deepwater project, an overseas stake, a refinery with pumps and a refinery without them: each earns differently and each reacts differently to the same oil price.

The two listed companies are portfolios of five distinct business models, and their economics differ enough that blending them hides more than it shows. Reading either company well means mentally splitting it into these parts.

1. The mature nomination producer (ONGC's Western Offshore and onshore Gujarat and Assam; Oil India's Upper Assam). Low lifting cost relative to deepwater, but high royalty and cess, APM-capped gas and steep natural decline (5–7% a year offshore). Margins are fat (operating margins of 45–60% in normal years) but sustaining capex is heavy: ONGC spends roughly $3.5–4 billion a year to stay flat. Native measures: decline rate, workovers, water cut, reserve replacement and opex per boe. Policy exposure is very high.

2. The deepwater and HP-HT developer (ONGC's KG-98/2; the future Samudra Manthan finds; RIL-bp's KG-D6 outside the listed universe). High upfront capex, long lead times and reservoir surprise risk, as KG-98/2's revisions show. In return: pricing freedom up to the HP-HT ceiling and low or deferred royalty. At plateau, margins can reach 60–75%, but returns are front-loaded with capex and through-cycle ROCE is lower (8–15%, indicative). Native measures: first-oil and peak dates, plateau rate, finding and development cost per boe, uptime.

3. The overseas portfolio (ONGC Videsh's 29 projects in 14 countries; Oil India's Russian, Mozambican, Venezuelan and Libyan stakes). Market prices, but sanctions, price caps and stranded receivables. OVL produced 9.67 MMTOE in FY26, down from 12.33 in FY22, and its profit went from about ₹500–600 crore a year to about ₹1,000 crore a quarter after its 20% of Sakhalin-1 was restored in December 2025. Russian realisations were weak in Q1 FY27 because of the price cap. Mozambique LNG (OVL about 16%, OIL about 4% through BREML) resumed after force majeure was lifted in 2025, with first cargo expected around end-FY28 or early FY29. Oil India has received $997 million of cumulative dividends and capital returns from Vankor and Taas-Yuryakh.

4. Integrated refining and marketing (HPCL and, partly, MRPL inside ONGC). Earnings move inversely to crude when pump prices are sticky, as Q1 FY27 showed. Operating margins of 3–8% and returns swinging between 8% and 20%. Native measures: GRM, throughput, marketing margin per litre, under-recoveries.

5. The standalone refiner and midstream assets (NRL inside Oil India; OIL's pipelines; MRPL in part). A refiner without retail gains from high cracks; NRL also gets the North-East excise benefit. Pipelines earn regulated tariffs: OIL's 9.65 million-tonne Naharkatiya–Barauni crude line, its product line (expanded from 1.72 to 5.5 million tonnes), the DNPL gas line and a 20% stake in IGGL. Green businesses (ONGC Green's 2.853 GW of renewables, OIL's 188 MW) are small today, with renewable project returns of about 6–8%.

The policy reset: new contracts, a new law and an ₹84,084 crore bet on the deep sea

After a decade of reforms that produced little new oil, the government has rewritten the upstream rulebook and put public money into frontier exploration.

India's upstream is governed by a small set of institutions. The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas (MoPNG) sets policy and notifies prices. The Directorate General of Hydrocarbons (DGH) is the upstream regulator: it awards blocks, approves field development plans and certifies reserves. The Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell (PPAC) publishes prices and data and was designated nodal agency under the Essential Commodities Act in March 2026. The Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB) regulates pipelines and city gas (it declared Oil India's DNPL a common carrier in 2026). OISD and PESO handle safety.

Each regime still governs the blocks awarded under it, so ONGC and OIL operate under several at once. Their legacy nomination fields carry the heaviest fiscal burden.

The HELP reform of 2016 replaced cost-recovery contracts, which invited disputes over what costs could be recovered, with revenue sharing: bidders compete on the share of revenue they give the government and on their work programme, and receive marketing and pricing freedom. A 2019 change removed revenue sharing in the less-explored Category II and III basins altogether, bidding on work programme only. Through OALP-IX (2024), 177 blocks had been offered and 172 awarded. OALP-X and XI are under way, with 46 blocks on offer (28 offshore, 18 onland); OALP-X covers about 2.63 lakh square kilometres with an estimated potential the government puts at around $100 billion. ONGC says it will participate "in a big way". Ten years on, though, OALP blocks produced only about 0.02 million tonnes of crude in FY26, a reminder of how long exploration takes.

The Oilfields (Regulation and Development) Amendment Act, 2025 modernised the 1948 law. It replaces the old mining lease with a unified "petroleum lease"; widens "mineral oils" to include shale oil and gas, tight oil and gas, gas hydrates and coal-bed methane; permits integrated energy projects such as carbon capture and renewables on lease areas; allows infrastructure sharing; adds fiscal-stability language; and decriminalises many penalties. The Petroleum and Natural Gas Rules, 2025 set a single petroleum lease, a 180-day deadline for approvals and the right to seek a lease extension where a discovery runs beyond the block boundary. ONGC's annual report also notes a new uniform formula for post-wellhead cost deductions in royalty computation, which removes long-standing inconsistencies and is a mild positive for net realisation (amount not disclosed).

In plain terms

The government has spent ten years making exploration contracts simpler and more attractive, and in 2025 it rewrote the underlying law to promise investors stable fiscal terms. Very little new oil has come from the newer blocks yet. The question is whether the latest round of changes, plus public money for the riskiest wells, finally attracts the large discoveries the old reforms did not.

Samudra Manthan, the National Offshore Exploration Scheme, is the biggest change. It carries an outlay of ₹84,084 crore, with an exploration phase to 2031. The government reimburses seismic surveys and, on Oil India's reading, up to about ₹675 crore per deep or ultra-deep well, and plans about ₹10,000 crore of common infrastructure hubs. Stratigraphic wells, drilled to learn the geology rather than to find a field, are fully government-funded: Oil India has one in Mahanadi worth about ₹800 crore, and ONGC drilled AND-P-1 in the Andaman. Detailed modalities had not been notified as of August 2026, according to both companies' calls. ONGC spudded its first Samudra Manthan deepwater well in Mahanadi around 25 July 2026 and struck gas on 18 September. Oil India's deepwater rigs for KG and Mahanadi are due in June–July 2027 and March 2028.

Several legal and tax disputes hang over both companies. The largest is GST (earlier service tax) on royalty, levied at 18% since 1 July 2017 and disputed by the producers. ONGC has provided ₹19,645 crore (including ₹2,526 crore added in FY26) and deposited ₹18,647 crore under protest; the question is before a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court. A favourable ruling could return up to about ₹18,600 crore of cash, roughly 6.6% of ONGC's market cap (illustrative). Oil India paid about ₹3,304 crore in August–September 2026 under protest, funded by internal accruals and borrowing and largely provided for already; its FY26 P&L provision was ₹865 crore.

ONGC and Oil India generate a lot of cash. 135 years, six shocks: the cycles that shaped Indian upstream

The industry earns most in "$70–85 oil, no levy" windows. In every earlier $100 episode the state took the upside. 2026 is the first exception in two decades.

Indian oil began in Assam. Oil was struck at Digboi in 1889, and Oil India was incorporated on 18 February 1959 as a joint venture between Burmah Oil and the government, becoming fully state-owned in 1981. The Oil and Natural Gas Commission was set up in 1956 and found Bombay High (now Mumbai High) in 1974; the field began producing in 1976 and carried India's output to a peak of about 34 million tonnes around 1990, when Mumbai High alone produced about 400,000 barrels a day. ONGC was corporatised and partly listed in 1993–94.

The 1990s opened the sector. Pre-NELP rounds brought private joint ventures into Panna-Mukta-Tapti, Ravva and Rajasthan, and NELP from 1999 offered nine rounds of production-sharing contracts. Reliance discovered KG-D6 gas in 2002 and began producing in 2009, briefly reaching more than 60 MMSCMD before reservoir problems cut output sharply. Cairn found Mangala in Rajasthan in 2004 and began producing in 2009. ONGC Videsh went global with Sakhalin-1 in 2001, Sudan, Vietnam and the purchase of Imperial Energy in 2008. Oil India listed in September 2009.

Its FY23 record came despite ₹1,887 crore of SAED, helped by the rule that exempted output above the prior year's level. Its FY26 dip (PAT down 27%) came from a $69 realisation, the lowest in five years, plus about ₹3,000 crore of dry-well, impairment and currency charges.

The lesson of the cycles is that Indian upstream earns most in windows of $70–85 oil with no special levy, as in FY19 and FY24–FY26. In every earlier episode above $100 the state took most of the upside: in 2012–14 through subsidy sharing, in 2022–24 through SAED. 2026 is the first $100-plus episode in two decades in which crude producers kept the windfall. Whether it remains an exception or becomes a new norm is the most important open question for these stocks, and it is a political question, not an economic one.

In plain terms

Looking back over twenty years, ONGC and Oil India did best when oil was moderately priced and nobody was taxing the extra. When oil was very expensive, the government took most of the gain one way or another. The June 2026 quarter broke that pattern. History says to be careful about assuming it will last; the current policy choices say it might.

In plain terms

An oil producer's profit is how much it sells, times the price, minus the government's share, minus its own costs. Price and the government's share are decided elsewhere; volume and costs are where management earns its keep. Because the costs barely move, profit swings much more than price: a 50% rise in oil roughly doubles ONGC's profit, and a 50% fall would do the reverse.

1. Production: MMTOE, oil versus gas, and the unit conversions

What it is. The volume of oil and gas lifted in a period. Indian companies report crude in million tonnes (MMT) and gas in billion cubic metres (BCM), and add them one-for-one into million tonnes of oil equivalent (MMTOE). Formula. MMTOE = crude MMT + gas BCM. Worked example. ONGC's FY26 standalone output was 19.33 MMT of crude (including condensate) plus 19.53 BCM of gas = 38.86 MMTOE; Oil India's was 3.45 + 3.19 = 6.64 MMTOE. The conversions every reader needs: one tonne of crude is 7.33 barrels, so 1 MMT a year is about 20,000 barrels a day; 1 BCM a year is 2.74 MMSCMD; 1 BCM is about 39.7 million mmBtu at 25.2 standard cubic metres per mmBtu; 1 MMTOE is about 7.33 million boe. ONGC's crude is therefore about 388,000 barrels a day and its gas about 53.5 MMSCMD; Oil India's crude about 69,000 barrels a day on the FY26 average and about 80,700 at its August 2026 record of 11,017 tonnes a day.

Drivers. Up: new wells, workovers, redevelopment (bp at Mumbai High), new fields (KG-98/2, DUDP), evacuation capacity (OIL's pipelines). Down: natural decline (5–7% a year offshore), shut-ins for commissioning, offtake problems, blockades. Benchmark. Good: growth above 3% a year; average: −2% to +3%; weak: below −2%. ONGC was about −1% in FY26 and −3% to −5% in Q1 FY27 (weak); OIL about −1% in FY26 with Q1 FY27 crude +11%. Definition trap. ONGC reports at least four scopes: crude excluding condensate (18.355 MMT), including condensate (19.33), including joint-venture share (20.50) and group including OVL (27.41 MMT crude, 50.14 MMTOE total). The 1:1 MMTOE convention also overstates gas: on a heat basis 1 BCM is only about 0.86–0.90 million tonnes of oil equivalent. Gas produced is not gas sold: ONGC's sales-to-production ratio fell to 75–76% in Q1 FY27 and OIL sold only 83% of what it produced in FY26.

How the listed companies compare. ONGC guides standalone output of about 39 MMTOE in FY27 and about 40 in FY28, split evenly between oil and gas, after cutting from about 42.5 MMTOE in November 2025. Oil India guides at least 3.9 MMT of crude in FY27, about 4.2 MMT by FY29, gas of 3.8 BCM in FY28 and about 5 BCM in FY29, and a group stretch target of 10–12 MMTOE by FY30. The conversions put individual projects in context: KG-98/2's 21,000 barrels a day is about 1.05 MMT a year, roughly 5% of ONGC's crude; its 1.5–1.7 MMSCMD of gas is about 0.58 BCM a year, about 3% of ONGC's gas. Oil India's Q1 FY27 crude of 0.95 MMT is roughly 1 MMT a quarter, the run-rate its FY27 guidance needs. Forecast volume paths are INDICATIVE: both companies have missed production guidance in recent years.

4. Lifting and production cost per boe (ILLUSTRATIVE proxy)

What it is. The cash cost of producing one barrel of oil equivalent, excluding levies, exploration and depreciation. Formula. Operating expenses ÷ (MMTOE × 7.33 million boe). Worked example. Neither ONGC nor Oil India publishes this figure, so any number is an ILLUSTRATIVE estimate. ONGC: ₹28,209 crore ÷ 284.9 million boe = about ₹990 a boe, or about $11.2 at ₹88.31 (FY25 about $11.3). Oil India: cash opex of ₹5,432 crore (employees, stores, fuel, contracts, insurance, miscellaneous) ÷ 48.7 million boe = about ₹1,116, or $12.6.

Drivers. Field maturity (water handling rises as fields age), offshore versus onshore logistics, contract rates, staff numbers, the rupee. Oil India's contract cost rose 41% in FY26 with record drilling. ONGC's cost programme includes a Pipavav supply base and crew boats replacing helicopters. Benchmark. Good: below $8; average: $8–14; weak: above $14. Both companies are "average"; CNOOC reports $7.46 and Petrobras $6.33 in Brazil and $4.45 in the pre-salt (INDICATIVE, secondary sources). Definition trap. ONGC's operating-expense line includes gas processing and transport for value-added products, which flatters nothing but inflates the per-boe figure relative to peers that report pure lifting cost; leases under Ind AS 116 move vessel and rig hire out of opex into depreciation and interest, which deflates it. Compare only like with like.

What moves it, in practice. ONGC's proxy was almost unchanged between FY25 (about $11.3) and FY26 (about $11.2), and management said Q1 FY27 opex was flat year on year despite the weaker rupee. Quarterly figures can jump for reasons unrelated to efficiency: ONGC's opex rose 9% in Q3 FY26 (₹7,151 crore against ₹6,563 crore) because of LNG consumed at Dahej. At Oil India, contract costs rose 41% in FY26 as drilling hit a record, so its per-boe cost will look better only if the new wells lift volumes. Because production is the denominator, a falling-output company sees its unit cost rise even with flat spending, which is ONGC's structural risk. Since neither company discloses the figure, investors should track the rupee opex line against production every quarter and treat any per-boe figure as an estimate.

7. Reserve replacement ratio (RRR)

What it is. Whether a producer is finding as much as it pumps. Formula. Reserve additions in the year ÷ production in the year, usually on a 2P basis. Worked example. ONGC added 44.01 MMTOE of 2P reserves in FY26 against domestic production of about 37.6 MMTOE on its definition, an RRR of 1.17 (1.35 in FY25). Drivers. Exploration success, appraisal of existing discoveries, revisions to old fields (up or down), and price (higher prices make more volume economic). Benchmark. Good: above 1.2; average: 0.9–1.2; weak: below 0.9. Both are "average". Definition trap. 2P RRR can be flattered by reclassifying resources; 1P RRR is stricter. How they compare. ONGC has reported 2P replacement above one for 19 consecutive years, yet its production has fallen for most of that period, which shows that RRR alone cannot answer the investor's question. 15. Definition traps, collected

  • Standalone vs consolidated. ONGC's consolidated revenue is about three-quarters refining. Never compare its consolidated margin (17% FY26, 8% Q1 FY27) with a pure producer's.
  • Pre-minority vs attributable PAT. ONGC FY26: ₹49,793 crore pre-minority, ₹41,424 crore attributable. Q1 FY27: ₹6,554 crore pre-minority, about ₹11,899 crore attributable. Use attributable for P/E.
  • Revenue bases. ONGC consolidated ₹6.09 lakh crore net of excise (screener) vs ₹6.62 lakh crore gross (company). OIL ₹33,081 crore sales vs ₹38,981 crore "total income".
  • EBITDA bases. Company EBITDA includes other income; screener operating profit excludes it and is after dry-well write-offs.
  • Crude scopes. ONGC 18.36 MMT (crude excluding condensate), 19.33 (including condensate), 20.50 (including JV share), 27.41 (group including OVL).
  • Realisation denominator. Divide crude revenue by volume sold (ONGC FY26 $68.9), not volume produced (which gives a misleading ~$63).
  • APM price vs ceiling. FY26 ceiling $6.75; FY27 $7.00. The formula price can sit below the ceiling (it did in five FY26 months).
  • Windfall tax. Crude SAED was nil from 18 September 2024 and scrapped on 2 December 2024. The 2026 SAED applies to fuel exports, not domestic crude.
  • Gas produced vs sold. ONGC sold 75–76% of its gas output in Q1 FY27; OIL 83% in FY26.
  • Net debt. Screener's consolidated net debt subtracts strategic stakes (IOC, GAIL, Petronet), which are not cash. ONGC standalone is net cash (~₹20.8k crore) before ₹25.8k crore of leases.
  • MRPL ownership. Legal 88.58% (71.63% direct + ~16.96% via HPCL); economic 80.94%.
  • Lifting cost. Not disclosed by either company; every per-boe cost figure is an estimate.
In plain terms

Most mistakes with these two companies come from mixing bases: comparing a group number with a standalone one, a profit before minorities with one after, or a gross revenue with a net one. Before comparing any two figures, check that they describe the same company, the same scope and the same definition.

16. How to read an E&P company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the price deck (one minute). Note the quarter's average dated Brent and Indian basket, the rupee, and the APM cap and HP-HT ceiling in force. For Q2 FY27: basket $96.11, Brent $96.33, APM $7.00, HP-HT $8.90. This tells you what the result should look like before you open it.
  2. Pull realisations (one minute). Crude $/bbl and gas $/mmBtu, and new-well gas price and share for ONGC. Compare crude with dated Brent; a gap wider than $4 needs an explanation. Red flag: a realisation that does not move with Brent.
  3. Pull volumes, produced and sold (one minute). Crude MMT and gas BCM, year on year, with the scope stated. Check the sales-to-production ratio for gas. Red flags: another guidance cut, rising shut-ins, offtake losses.
  4. Check statutory levies (thirty seconds). Royalty, cess and any SAED as a share of revenue. A sudden jump means a new levy; any "special additional excise duty" on crude is the single biggest negative surprise possible.
  5. Read the standalone P&L (two minutes). Revenue, operating profit and margin, other income, DD&A, write-offs, PAT. Separate one-offs: dry wells, impairments, FX losses, provisions for GST on royalty. Do not annualise a Q4.
  6. Translate to per-barrel terms (one minute). Operating profit per boe and opex per boe (estimate). Has cost per boe stayed flat in rupees?
  7. Bridge to consolidated (one minute). For ONGC: HPCL, MRPL, OVL and OPaL profits, then minority interest to reach attributable PAT. For OIL: NRL's GRM, utilisation and PAT. Red flag: quoting consolidated pre-minority PAT as "earnings".
  8. Check capex and drilling (thirty seconds). Quarterly capex against the annual target, wells drilled and discoveries. Spending ahead of target without volume growth is a warning.
  9. Check the balance sheet and cash (thirty seconds). Standalone net cash or debt, lease liabilities, NRL debt, any GST-on-royalty payments or refunds.
  10. Listen for the forward signals (one minute). Production guidance, KG-98/2 and DUDP milestones, OIL's pipeline dates, interim dividend, and any comment on SAED, subsidy sharing or the APM cap. Compare with what was said last quarter.
In plain terms

Before reading a result, work out what oil and the rupee did in the quarter; that explains most of the change. Then check whether volumes rose or fell, whether the government took more through levies, and whether any one-off write-offs distort the profit. Finally, separate the oil business from the refining subsidiaries, and listen to what management says about next year's production and about tax.

On costs, Indian producers are less efficient: their ILLUSTRATIVE opex proxies of $11–13 a boe are about 1.5 times CNOOC's $7.46 and about twice Petrobras's $6.33, and Indian levies of $10–12 a boe at $69 oil push ONGC's all-in cost to about $31 against CNOOC's $27.90. On reserve life, ONGC's 13 years on proved reserves is in line with the majors' 10–12 years. Global deal values give a cross-check for overseas assets: according to industry data cited in ONGC's annual report, 2P reserves changed hands at about $7.2 a boe in Q2 2026 (down from $8.6) and producing resources at about $3.8 a boe.

In plain terms

Around the world, oil companies controlled by governments trade at low valuations, and ONGC and Oil India fit that pattern. They are mid-sized and small by global standards, they spend more per barrel than the best state producers in China and Brazil, and the Indian government's share of each barrel is high. The market prices all of that in.

Technology and transition: recovering more, emitting less

The near-term technology story is getting more out of old fields; the long-term one is whether oil and gas producers can stay relevant as India decarbonises.

The most valuable technology in Indian upstream today is mature-field recovery. bp's work at Mumbai High is the clearest example: as Technical Services Provider it has lifted output to about 107% of the contractual baseline for oil and 113% for gas, enough to offset most of a 6–7% natural decline, and the model has been extended across Western Offshore. ONGC had 17 enhanced-recovery pilots approved by DGH and ran 12,963 well-stimulation jobs in FY26, and drilled 8 basement-exploration wells. Oil India uses hydro-fracturing, radial drilling and a workover planning board, and completed a record 307 workovers in FY26. ONGC's NETRA production control centre at Panvel and digital twins of offshore assets aim to cut downtime.

Deepwater execution is the main technical risk. KG-98/2's oil reservoirs turned out to be compartmentalised, with poor connectivity between reservoir blocks, so each extra barrel needs well re-entries costing more than ₹500 crore. Frontier exploration under Samudra Manthan will push both companies into deeper and less-known waters, where partners such as bp, TotalEnergies, Petrobras and ExxonMobil bring experience they lack. The 2025 Act now covers shale, tight oil and gas and gas hydrates, but commercial production from unconventionals before 2030 is unlikely.

The energy transition cuts both ways. India's oil demand is still growing (petrol +7.0% and diesel +4.9% in H1 FY27), and gas is meant to rise from 6.13% of primary energy in 2025 to 15% by 2030. But electric two-wheelers and cars, and 20% ethanol blending in petrol (reached in the November 2025–September 2026 supply year), will slow petrol demand over time; this matters more for refiners than for producers, since domestic crude is only a tenth of refinery runs. Both companies have net-zero targets for their own operations: ONGC by 2038 (Scope 1 and 2, about ₹2 lakh crore of spending), Oil India by 2040 with zero routine flaring by 2027 and zero methane emissions by 2030. ONGC Green's renewable portfolio stood at 2.853 GW in Q1 FY27; Oil India has 188 MW and targets 5 GW by 2040 plus 25 compressed-biogas plants by 2030.

Three disruptions deserve watching. Sanctions and price caps on Russian crude cut OVL's Q1 FY27 realisations and complicate dividend repatriation from Vankor and Sakhalin. The security of Hormuz-routed imports raised the strategic value of every domestic barrel in 2026, strengthening the policy case for exploration. And ESG exclusions limit the foreign investor base: foreign institutions own only 8.55% of ONGC and 7.15% of Oil India, which keeps the valuation discount in place regardless of earnings.

In plain terms

The quickest wins come from squeezing more out of fields that have run for decades, and bp's results at Mumbai High show that works. The bigger bets, deep-sea exploration and renewables, are slower and less certain. India will need oil and gas for decades, but the companies also have to show they can cut their own emissions, which many foreign investors watch closely.

Bull vs bear

The case for and against owning Indian upstream after its best quarter on record.

The bull case

  • Uncapped crude, for now. No windfall tax in a $100–120 year; the 2025 Act's fiscal-stability promise and the exploration drive make reimposition costly for the government.
  • Operating leverage plus rupee. At $93–95 realisation and ₹95 to the dollar, standalone profit stays well above FY26 levels; Q1 FY27 alone earned 53% of FY26.
  • Gas price mix improving. New-well gas at about 12% of the basket, KG-98/2 and DUDP ramp-ups, and the $0.25 annual ceiling step lift ONGC's average gas price; OIL's new gas is all premium-priced.
  • Oil India's volume growth. Record crude, a pipeline-led path to about 5 BCM of gas, and NREP tripling refining capacity.
  • Cash and yield. ONGC's E&P parent is net cash; combined FY26 dividends of ₹18,540 Cr yield 5.18%; a GST-on-royalty win could return up to ~₹18,600 Cr to ONGC.
  • Valuation. 7.45 times attributable FY26 earnings; ONGC's E&P business implied at about 2.3 times EBITDA after listed stakes and cash (ILLUSTRATIVE).

The bear case

  • Policy reversal. A crude SAED or subsidy sharing is a fortnightly notification away; OMC debt rose by about ₹70,000 Cr in one quarter.
  • Volume credibility. ONGC's FY27 guidance cut from ~42.5 to ~39 MMTOE; KG-98/2 oil at 21,000 bpd and gas at 1.5–1.7 MMSCMD against much higher early targets.
  • The gas cap. About four-fifths of ONGC's gas still earns $7.00 against an $11 formula; any freeze of the ceiling step would hurt.
  • Refining drag. HPCL lost ₹12,265 Cr in Q1 FY27; NRL's $36 GRM will normalise to $7–8 just as OIL's debt-funded NREP starts up.
  • Price reversal. At $70 crude the leverage works in reverse; all-in costs near $31/boe at ONGC leave less cushion than CNOOC.
  • Governance. The ₹15,225 Cr PMT claim ONGC did not contest, policy-driven acquisitions and green capex at 6–8% returns.

The two cases rest on different readings of the same facts. The bull case treats 2026 as a turning point: the government has chosen to let producers keep crude windfalls in exchange for more exploration, and the volume decline is bottoming out as new gas arrives. The bear case treats 2026 as an exception that a fiscally stretched government will reverse if prices stay high, and sees the repeated guidance cuts as evidence that volume growth will keep disappointing.

Sensitivities help frame the debate (all ILLUSTRATIVE). For ONGC standalone, each $10 a barrel on crude is worth about ₹7,500 crore of annual PAT; for Oil India about ₹1,250 crore. A $20-a-barrel windfall tax equivalent on ONGC's roughly 140 million barrels of nomination crude would cost about ₹26,000 crore of revenue a year at ₹95 to the dollar. Removing the APM cap at a $10–11 formula price would add roughly ₹13,000–18,000 crore a year to ONGC's gas revenue. And each $1 of GRM at NRL after expansion is worth about ₹630 crore of EBITDA. The policy variables are as large as the price variables, which is the central lesson of this report.

In plain terms

The optimists say the government has decided to let ONGC and Oil India keep more of high oil prices so they can explore more, and that new gas will finally lift production. The pessimists say the government will change its mind if oil stays high and the fuel retailers keep losing money, and that ONGC's production promises keep slipping. Both sides agree the shares are cheap; they disagree on whether that is a bargain or a fair price for the risk.

Leading indicators with the levels that would change the story, and the calendar for the next eighteen months.

Leading indicators and thresholds

  • Crude SAED notification (1st and 16th of each month). Any line for domestic "crude oil" reappearing in the fortnightly SAED table is the single largest negative risk. Threshold: Indian basket above $100 for a full quarter with OMC losses continuing raises the probability materially.
  • Q2 FY27 realisation. INDICATIVE expectation $93–95/bbl for both. Below $92 would suggest a wider-than-usual discount to Brent; above $96 would mean the pricing lag helped in a rising quarter.
  • Indian basket and the rupee. October to date $120.09 (PPAC); a Q3 FY27 average above $110 at ₹94–95 implies another record standalone quarter if no levy arrives. The rupee near its ₹96.8 record low adds about 1.1% to realisations per rupee.
  • ONGC production. FY27 guidance ~39 MMTOE. A Q2 standalone print below ~9.5 MMTOE (crude plus gas) would put even the reduced guidance at risk. KG-98/2 gas above 3 MMSCMD by Q4 FY27 is the test of the latest target.
  • New-well gas share. ONGC's NWG at 24% of sales quantity in Q1 FY27; above 30% within a year would confirm the gas price-mix thesis.
  • Oil India crude run-rate. At least ~0.97 MMT a quarter is needed for the 3.9 MMT FY27 guidance; daily output above 11,000 tonnes sustained.
  • OIL gas evacuation. DNPL tie-in completion (expected within months of August 2026) and the IGGL feeder line (~20% done in August 2026; due around December 2027). Gas sales below 85% of production signal continued bottlenecks.

  • NRL. GRM normalisation towards $7–8, NREP unit start-ups and utilisation, and net debt above 2.5 times EBITDA as a warning level.
