Indian oil & gas E&P: the windfall the state let them keep

A complete guide to India's two listed oil and gas producers, ONGC and Oil India: how a barrel and an mmBtu turn into profit, why the 2026 West Asia shock produced record upstream quarters while the government reached for every lever except a windfall tax on crude, why legacy gas is still capped at seven dollars, how two decades of field decline are being fought, and why each company's consolidated accounts hide a refinery that runs on the opposite cycle.

00

The one-page view: the price is set abroad, the take is set in Delhi

Two state-controlled producers pump about 86% of India's crude. Their earnings depend on one number they do not control and on a second decision they cannot predict.

Indian oil and gas exploration and production (E&P) is a two-company sub-sector. Oil and Natural Gas Corporation (ONGC) and Oil India (OIL) produced roughly 86% of the country's crude in the first half of FY27 and about 62% of its gross gas in FY26. Every barrel they lift is sold to an Indian refinery at a price linked to Brent. Every unit of their legacy gas is sold at a price the government sets and caps. Neither company decides how much of a price spike it may keep: that is settled in New Delhi through royalty, an oil-industry cess, and in the past a windfall tax or forced discounts to fuel retailers. Understanding this sub-sector means understanding three things at once: the global oil price, the domestic fiscal and pricing rules that filter it, and the slow physical battle against ageing fields.

2026 put all three under a spotlight. The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 took the Indian crude basket from $69 a barrel in February to $113–114 in March and April. It eased to $82–83 in June and July, then spiked again to $116 in September, and averaged about $120 in October to date (PPAC). For the first time in two decades of $100-plus oil, the government did not reimpose a windfall tax on domestic crude. It taxed fuel exports instead, cut excise on petrol and diesel, and leaned on refiners. The result was the best quarter in the history of both producers. In Q1 FY27 (the June 2026 quarter), ONGC's standalone profit after tax was ₹17,034 crore, up 112%, on a crude realisation of $99.45 a barrel. Oil India's was ₹2,870 crore, against ₹813 crore a year earlier, on $98.73.

But a reader who looks only at the consolidated numbers would see the opposite story. ONGC owns 54.90% of Hindustan Petroleum (HPCL), a refiner and fuel retailer whose pump prices were held while its crude costs soared. HPCL lost ₹12,265 crore in Q1 FY27. ONGC's consolidated profit before minority interest therefore fell to ₹6,554 crore, even as its E&P business doubled its earnings. Oil India's refinery, Numaligarh Refinery (NRL), has no retail arm, so it gained from wide refining margins: its gross refining margin (GRM) hit $35.95 a barrel. The same oil shock helped one listed producer twice and the other only once, once the subsidiary layers are added in.

Both companies are still at Q1 FY27 as their latest print, and both FY26 annual reports are out. Neither has announced a board date for Q2 FY27 (the September 2026 quarter) as of 22 October 2026. Last year the Q2 boards met between about 10 and 17 November, and the SEBI deadline this year is 14 November 2026. Two subsidiaries have already reported Q2: MRPL on 14 October (consolidated PAT ₹1,901 crore) and HPCL on 22 October (consolidated PAT ₹3,625 crore, back in profit). Section 21 lists what to look for.

This report is built around five forces:

  • Price, filtered through a weak rupee. Realisation in dollars per barrel is the master variable. Costs are mostly in rupees, so with the rupee near ₹94.9 to the dollar (against an FY26 average of ₹88.31), each dollar of crude is worth about 7% more in rupees than a year ago (sections 3 and 14).
  • The state's claw-back toolkit. Subsidy sharing in 2003–2015, the windfall tax (SAED) of 2022–24, the APM gas cap, royalty and cess, and dividends to a 57–59% government shareholder decide how much of a spike the producer keeps (sections 4 and 5).
  • The volume problem. India's crude output has fallen from 32.2 million tonnes in FY20 to 27.9 million tonnes in FY26. KG-98/2, the Mumbai High redevelopment with bp, Oil India's Assam and Rajasthan push and reserve replacement are the counter-moves (section 6).
  • Two-layer consolidation. ONGC's E&P layer sits under HPCL, MRPL, ONGC Videsh (OVL) and OPaL; Oil India's sits under NRL, which is tripling capacity from 3 to 9 million tonnes a year (section 9).
  • Policy reset and capital allocation. OALP and HELP acreage rounds, the Oilfields Amendment Act 2025, the Petroleum and Natural Gas Rules 2025, the ₹84,084 crore Samudra Manthan offshore exploration scheme, record dividends, capex and the energy transition (sections 11 and 12).

The market's verdict is a deep state-owned discount. On 22 October 2026 ONGC traded at ₹225 (market cap ₹2,83,395 crore), 6.48 times trailing attributable earnings and 0.76 times book, with a 5.89% dividend yield. Oil India traded at ₹457 (₹74,467 crore), 8.91 times earnings and 1.28 times book, with a 2.52% yield. Together they are worth ₹3.58 lakh crore, about 7.45 times their FY26 attributable profit. Those multiples sit next to other state-controlled producers such as CNOOC (6.8 times) and Petrobras (7.4 times) and far below Equinor, Saudi Aramco and ExxonMobil. The discount reflects the policy risk, the falling volumes and the refinery exposure, all of which this report examines.

In plain terms

ONGC and Oil India sell oil at world prices and pay costs in rupees, so a jump in crude and a weaker rupee both lift their profits sharply. What they are allowed to keep depends on the government, which has in the past taken most of any spike through discounts or a special tax. In 2026 it chose not to tax domestic crude, which is why both companies just had record quarters. The open questions are whether that holds, whether their ageing fields can be made to grow again, and how much their refining subsidiaries cost or add along the way.

01

How a barrel becomes profit: from licence to refinery gate

An upstream company finds hydrocarbons, lifts them and sells them at the wellhead. Almost everything that decides its margin happens before and after that sale.

An E&P company does five things. It secures acreage: a licence from the government to explore a block of land or sea. It explores, by shooting seismic surveys and drilling exploratory wells, most of which in frontier areas find nothing commercial. When it does find something, it develops the field by building platforms, pipelines, processing plants and production wells. It then produces, lifting crude and gas and separating them, and finally it sells. In India that last step is unusually constrained. Domestic crude is sold to designated public-sector refiners such as HPCL, IOC, BPCL, MRPL and CPCL under Crude Oil Sales Agreements (COSA), at a formula price based on Brent plus or minus a quality adjustment. Gas from old fields is sold into the national grid at a price set monthly by the government and allocated to city gas, fertiliser and power consumers.

Steps 1–3 decide volume and cost. Step 4 sets the gross price, mostly abroad. Step 5 decides how much of that price stays with shareholders. Refining and marketing (HPCL, MRPL, NRL) sit downstream of this chain inside the consolidated accounts.

The products are more varied than the label "oil company" suggests. ONGC's FY26 standalone revenue of ₹1,32,508 crore (company basis) was 63% crude and condensate (₹83,810 crore on 18.79 million tonnes sold), 27% natural gas (₹36,330 crore on 15.49 billion cubic metres sold), and 9% value-added products (₹11,879 crore): LPG, naphtha, ethane-propane (C2-C3) and kerosene stripped out of the gas stream at its Hazira, Uran and Dahej plants. Oil India is simpler: crude from Upper Assam and Arunachal, gas from Assam and Rajasthan, a little LPG and condensate, and tariff income from its crude and product pipelines in the North-East.

The legal form of the acreage matters for every later number. About 79% of India's crude still comes from nomination fields, the blocks handed to ONGC and OIL before 1999 without competitive bidding. Nomination crude pays royalty (roughly 20% of wellhead value onshore and 10% offshore) plus an Oil Industry Development Board (OIDB) cess of 20% ad valorem and a small National Calamity Contingent Duty of ₹50 a tonne. Nomination gas is sold under the Administered Pricing Mechanism (APM). Production-sharing contracts (PSCs) under the pre-NELP and NELP rounds let the operator recover costs before splitting "profit petroleum" with the government. The Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy (HELP, 2016) and its Open Acreage Licensing Policy (OALP) rounds replaced this with simpler revenue-sharing contracts and gave the operator pricing and marketing freedom.

Where is value captured? At the wellhead, when crude prices are high, but only on crude. The two producers' standalone operating margins run at 30–61% of revenue, after royalty and cess have already taken about a fifth of gross sales. Gas value is captured mostly by consumers, because the APM cap holds legacy gas below its formula price whenever oil is above roughly $70. Refining margins are captured by integrated owners only when pump prices are allowed to move: in 2026 HPCL's marketing arm bled while standalone refiners without pumps (MRPL, NRL) earned windfall margins. And the exchequer captures a large share at every step: the petroleum sector contributed ₹4,75,719 crore to the central exchequer in FY26, 14% of the Centre's revenue receipts, and ₹3,41,821 crore to the states (PPAC). ONGC alone contributed ₹55,865 crore in FY26, equal to 42.2% of its turnover.

In plain terms

The oil itself is priced off Brent, but the gas is priced by the government, and the old fields that produce most of both pay a heavy royalty and a 20% cess before the company sees a rupee of profit. A company that finds new oil under the newer contracts keeps more of each barrel; a company living off fields awarded fifty years ago keeps less. ONGC and Oil India are mostly the second kind, which is why their margins move so strongly with policy.

One more structural feature shapes the numbers: neither company competes with imports on price. Both domestic and imported crude are priced off international benchmarks, so a domestic barrel is simply an imported barrel that did not need shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. That makes domestic production strategically valuable to the state in a crisis year like 2026, which strengthens the argument for letting producers keep their windfall and fund more drilling. It also means the producers have no pricing power of their own: they are price-takers on crude and policy-takers on gas.

02

Size, decline and the import gap: a shrinking share of a growing bill

India refines more than 5 million barrels a day and produces barely 0.6 million. The gap is widening every year.

India is the world's third-largest oil consumer but a minor producer. Its refineries processed 272.1 million tonnes of crude in FY26, about 5.46 million barrels a day, while domestic fields produced 27.9 million tonnes of crude and condensate, about 0.56 million barrels a day and roughly 0.6% of world supply. Crude import dependence on a consumption basis rose from 84.4% in FY21 to 88.7% in FY26, and was 88.1% in the first half of FY27 (PPAC). Gas is less import-dependent at about 50%, but domestic gas output has also slipped: 36.4 billion cubic metres (BCM) gross in FY24, 34.8 BCM in FY26 and down 4.4% in H1 FY27.

The decline is not evenly spread. Western Offshore, home to Mumbai High and the Bassein, Heera and Neelam fields, produced 11.9 million tonnes in FY26 against 14.9 million in FY20. Rajasthan, where Cairn's Mangala field once ran at 175,000–200,000 barrels a day, fell from 6.65 million tonnes to 2.95 million. The only bright spots were Eastern Offshore, which rose from 0.56 million tonnes to 2.73 million on the back of ONGC's KG-98/2 deepwater block, and Assam, where Oil India lifted output from 4.09 to 4.42 million tonnes for the region. By company, ONGC's nomination share of national crude has stayed around 60% while OIL's rose from 9.6% in FY20 to 12.2% in FY26, because OIL is the only producer whose fields have grown.

The import bill is where the decline hurts the country. India imported 245.8 million tonnes of crude in FY26 for $123.4 billion, an average of $68.49 a barrel, and its net oil and gas import bill was $119.1 billion. In H1 FY27 the crude bill alone was $90.6 billion, up 46% on H1 FY26, despite lower volumes. Domestic crude is worth roughly a tenth of the import bill, about $14 billion at FY26 prices. At Q2 FY27 prices of around $96 a barrel and ₹95 to the dollar, every extra million tonnes of domestic crude saves roughly $0.7 billion of imports a year (indicative arithmetic). That is the national-interest case for the exploration push described in section 11.

Gas tells a slightly different story. India's gross gas output rose from 28.7 BCM in FY21 to 36.4 BCM in FY24, almost entirely because RIL and bp revived the KG-D6 deepwater block with new fields. ONGC's own gas fell every year over the same period, from 21.9 BCM to 18.5 BCM, and OIL's edged up from 2.5 to 3.1 BCM. Gas consumption was 68.75 BCM in FY26, down 3.6%, and fell 6.7% year on year in September 2026 as spot LNG became unaffordable. The government's aim of raising gas to 15% of India's primary energy by 2030, from 6.13% in 2025, depends on domestic output that is currently going the wrong way.

In money terms, the sub-sector is modest by revenue and large by profit. The two producers' standalone revenue was ₹1,52,986 crore in FY26 (down 0.9%), with operating profit (screener basis) of ₹67,782 crore and PAT of ₹37,349 crore (down 10.5%). Including their refining, overseas and petrochemical subsidiaries, consolidated revenue was ₹6,41,744 crore, operating profit ₹1,13,566 crore and PAT before minorities ₹57,344 crore (up 26%), of which about ₹48,044 crore was attributable to shareholders. Overseas, ONGC Videsh produced 9.67 million tonnes of oil equivalent (MMTOE) in FY26 and Indian companies together 19.2 MMTOE abroad.

The forecast is flat. ICRA expected India's FY27 crude output at 28.0–28.5 million tonnes in March 2026; with H1 down 3.5%, a full year near 27–27.5 million tonnes is more likely (indicative). Company guidance implies ONGC standalone production rising about 1–3% a year to FY28 (from 38.87 to about 40 MMTOE) and Oil India growing faster, to a stretch target of 10–12 MMTOE by FY30 from 6.64. No large national increase is plausible before FY29 without a major deepwater discovery, which is what Samudra Manthan is designed to find.

In plain terms

India buys almost nine of every ten barrels it uses, and the share it produces itself keeps shrinking because the big old fields are running down faster than new ones replace them. For the two producers that is a volume problem, but in a year of $100 oil it also makes every domestic barrel more valuable to the country, which helps explain why the government let them keep more of the price this time.

03

The 2026 price shock: a war, a weak rupee and a record quarter

Realisation per barrel is the master variable. In 2026 it rose by half in a single quarter, and the rupee added another 7%.

The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the route for a large share of India's crude, LPG and LNG imports. A ceasefire around 7 April did not fully reopen transits. The Indian crude basket, which PPAC redefined from March 2026 to track the crude India actually imports each month, jumped from $69.01 in February to $113.49 in March and $114.48 in April. It eased to $83.22 in June and $82.04 in July, rose to $90.19 in August and spiked again to $116.09 in September. October to date (to the 21st) averaged $120.09, with a spot price of $116.73 on 21 October and an unusually sour-heavy mix. Brent, on EIA's dated series, averaged $103.28 in Q1 FY27 and $96.33 in Q2 FY27.

Put in a longer frame, 2026 is the third episode of $100-plus oil in fifteen years. The basket averaged $111.89 in FY12, $107.97 in FY13 and $105.52 in FY14; it then crashed to $46 by FY16, hit a COVID trough of $44.82 in FY21, rebounded to $93.15 in FY23 after Russia invaded Ukraine, and drifted down to $70.99 in FY26. Each spike was followed by a different policy response, and section 4 shows that those responses, not the spike itself, decided what the producers earned.

What the producers actually receive is not the basket. Both companies sell at formula prices linked to dated Brent, with a lag and a quality adjustment. ONGC's management describes the formula as "Brent is the base… about 5%… 4% comes to our kitty", which sounds like a premium. The data say otherwise. Against EIA's dated Brent, ONGC realised $1.6–3.8 a barrel below Brent in every quarter with a printed number since Q3 FY25, and Oil India $0.8–4.6 below. The gap was widest in Q1 FY27 ($3.8 for ONGC, $4.6 for OIL) because Brent peaked in April and fell through June, and the pricing lag works against sellers on the way down. In Q2 FY27, with Brent rising from July to September, the gap should narrow.

The rupee amplifies everything. Realisations are in dollars, while most costs (salaries, domestic contracts, depreciation on rupee assets) are in rupees. The rupee averaged ₹84.57 to the dollar in FY25 and ₹88.31 in FY26; it was ₹95.46 in September 2026 and about ₹94.9 on 19 October, near a record low of ₹96.8. A move from ₹88.3 to ₹94.9 raises the rupee value of every barrel by about 7.5%. As an illustration: ONGC's Q1 FY26 realisation of $66.13 at roughly ₹86 was worth about ₹5,690 a barrel; Q1 FY27's $99.45 at roughly ₹94.5 was worth about ₹9,400, a 65% rise in rupees against a 50% rise in dollars (indicative FX).

The shock reached beyond crude. LPG consumption fell 14.9% in H1 FY27 as Gulf supply was disrupted, while petrol rose 7.0% and diesel 4.9%. Spot gas on the Indian Gas Exchange averaged $28.4 per mmBtu in September 2026, pricing imported LNG out of power and industry. Freight and insurance costs rose. The government responded with an excise cut on petrol and diesel from 26 March (to ₹11.90 and ₹7.80 a litre), export levies on diesel and ATF from 27 March, and four pump-price increases in May. On the macro side, the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and the Nifty closed at 22,780 on 28 September.

In plain terms

When oil jumps, the producers' revenue jumps with it, but their costs barely move, so profit rises much faster than price. A weaker rupee adds a second boost because the oil is sold in dollars. In the June 2026 quarter both effects arrived together and nothing was taken away by a new tax, which is why profit doubled. The same shock hit the government-owned fuel retailers in reverse, because they could not raise pump prices fast enough.

What does this mean for the September quarter? The basket averaged $96.11 in Q2 FY27 and Brent $96.33. Given the consistent $1–4 discount to dated Brent, expect ONGC and Oil India to report realisations of roughly $93–95 a barrel, about $5 below Q1 (indicative read-across). APM gas stayed capped at $7.00 throughout the quarter. Standalone profits should therefore be strong again but below Q1, while ONGC's consolidated profit should swing up because HPCL returned to a ₹3,625 crore profit. October's $120 basket points to a stronger Q3 if it holds, and also to a higher risk that the government revisits its decision not to tax domestic crude.
