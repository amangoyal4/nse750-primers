A complete guide to the business of flying people around India and out of it: how an airline turns a seat-kilometre into profit, why two groups now carry more than nine in ten domestic passengers, how a war-driven fuel shock and a record-low rupee pushed the whole industry into loss in a year of record fares, and what IndiGo, the one listed airline in the NSE 750, tells us about all of it.

00

The one-page view: record fares, shrinking traffic, an industry in loss

India's airlines sold seats at the highest prices in their history in 2026 and still lost money, because fuel, the rupee and a shrinking market moved faster than fares.

The Indian airline industry in late 2026 is a near-duopoly caught in a cost shock. IndiGo carried 65.4% of domestic passengers in September 2026 and the Tata-owned Air India group 26.8%, so two groups flew 92 of every 100 people who took a domestic flight. The third player, Akasa Air, had 5.6%. SpiceJet, which held around 4% a year earlier, was down to 0.6%. Behind the two leaders lies a graveyard: Kingfisher (2012), Jet Airways (2019) and Go First (2023) all failed, and SpiceJet has not filed audited accounts for the year to March 2026.

Concentration normally means profits. Not this year. The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 closed the Strait of Hormuz, sent Singapore jet-fuel prices up roughly 120% year on year in the April–June quarter, and shut many of the Gulf corridors that carry India's migrant workers and holidaymakers. The rupee slid to record lows near ₹96.8 per dollar, which matters because an Indian airline earns rupees but owes dollars for its aircraft leases, engines, maintenance and much of its fuel. ICRA expects the industry to lose ₹36,000–38,000 crore in FY27, more than the ₹28,000–30,000 crore it lost in FY26, and has held a Negative outlook since March 2026. These are forecasts, not reported figures.

The airlines did what a concentrated industry can do: they raised prices and cut capacity. IndiGo's yield, its ticket revenue per paying passenger-kilometre, rose 34.3% to ₹6.30 in the September 2026 quarter while its capacity fell 1.0%. Passengers responded. Domestic traffic fell 6.5% year on year in September 2026 and the January–September total (1,223.53 lakh passengers) was 0.82% below 2025. Outside the COVID years, that is the first negative year-to-date print on record.

IndiGo is the lens for this report because it is the only Indian airline listed in the NSE 750 and the only one that has earned money through a full cycle. Even IndiGo reported a net loss of ₹2,394 crore in FY26, though almost all of it was currency: stripping out an ₹8,100 crore forex loss (net of hedges) gives a profit of ₹5,706 crore, and stripping out one-off charges as well gives ₹7,503 crore. In Q2 FY27 it lost ₹1,879 crore reported and ₹724 crore ex-forex. Air India's group lost ₹22,238 crore in FY26 on revenue of ₹71,870 crore. Akasa lost ₹2,716 crore on ₹6,168 crore.

Eight forces explain almost everything about this industry, and the report is built around them. First, the near-duopoly and the collapse of the weaker carriers. Second, fares and yields rising while the market stalls. Third, fuel: aviation turbine fuel (ATF) priced off Singapore benchmarks, taxed outside GST, and hit by the 2026 war. Fourth, the rupee and dollar leases: Ind AS 116 puts aircraft leases on the balance sheet as dollar debts, so every fall in the rupee shows up as a forex loss. Fifth, supply shocks: Pratt & Whitney engine groundings, damp leases and delivery delays at Airbus and Boeing. Sixth, regulation and operations: new pilot rest rules triggered IndiGo's December-2025 meltdown, a DGCA fine and a competition probe. Seventh, the international pivot against Gulf carriers, complicated by airspace closures. Eighth, airport capacity: Navi Mumbai and Noida (Jewar) opened, but slots at the big metros still ration growth.

In plain terms

An airline sells a perishable product (an empty seat is worth nothing once the door closes) and buys most of its inputs in dollars. In 2026 the cost of the biggest input, fuel, jumped by half or more, the rupee fell, and the airlines could only pass part of it on. IndiGo says it recovered about 60% of the fuel increase through fares. The rest became losses, even at the market leader.

The investor's question is whether the shock is cyclical or structural. Cyclical arguments: fuel prices and crack spreads have spiked before and come down; the rupee's slide produces mostly non-cash mark-to-market losses on leases that are paid over eight to ten years; and a market with two strong players and no third has never been in a better position to keep fares rational. Structural arguments: India's airlines have lost money in aggregate in most years since 2008; the cost base is dollar-denominated and grows with every aircraft leased; and the industry has ordered roughly 1,700 aircraft against about 850 in service, so capacity will come back faster than demand if the cycle turns. The rest of the report sets out the evidence for both views and the numbers that will settle the argument.

01

How a seat makes money: thin margins at the end of a long chain

An airline sits between a global aircraft duopoly, dollar lessors, state-priced fuel and monopoly airports. Most of the value in the chain is captured before the passenger boards.

An airline sells one thing: the right to occupy a seat between two airports at a particular time. Everything else follows from the fact that the product is perishable and the cost is mostly fixed once the schedule is published. An Airbus A321neo flying Delhi to Mumbai burns roughly the same fuel, pays the same landing fee and needs the same crew whether it carries 150 passengers or 220. The airline's job is to fill the seats at the highest average price the market will bear, then add ancillaries (bags, seats, meals, change fees) and belly cargo on top.

The economics are captured in one line: profit = capacity × (revenue per seat-kilometre − cost per seat-kilometre). Capacity is measured in available seat-kilometres (ASK), one seat flown one kilometre. Revenue per ASK (RASK) depends on how full the planes are (load factor) and how much each passenger pays per kilometre (yield). Cost per ASK (CASK) depends on fuel, aircraft ownership, maintenance, staff, airport charges and, in India, the rupee. The KPI masterclass (section 15) works through each term. Here the point is structural: a one-rupee change in the gap between RASK and CASK on IndiGo's FY26 capacity of 172.4 billion ASK is worth ₹17,240 crore a year, about twice its best-ever annual profit. Small unit changes create very large profit swings.

Steps 1–3 are concentrated suppliers with pricing power or regulated returns. Step 4, the airline, is the only link with no structural price floor, which is why it absorbs the shocks.

Each supplier has more pricing power than the airline. Airbus and Boeing are a duopoly with order books full into the next decade, so delivery delays fall on the airline. Engine makers earn much of their profit on aftermarket parts and shop visits, paid by airlines in dollars. Lessors such as AerCap earn steady returns on aircraft that have become scarcer as deliveries slip. Big airports are monopolies whose charges are set by the Airports Economic Regulatory Authority (AERA) to earn a regulated return. Fuel is sold by public-sector oil marketing companies (OMCs) at prices linked to Singapore benchmarks, then taxed by the Centre and the states. The airline sits at the end of the chain with the most competition and the least ability to pass costs back.

Out of every ₹100 IndiGo spent in FY26, ₹28 went on fuel and ₹10 was a currency loss on dollar debts. Pilots, cabin crew, engineers and office staff together cost about ₹9. Labour cost is the item airline managements can control most directly, and in India it is the smallest of the big lines. The big lines are set in Singapore (fuel), Toulouse and Seattle (aircraft), and on the foreign-exchange market (the rupee).

This is why the Indian airline business is so unforgiving. The airline controls its schedule, its pricing and its own staff productivity, but more than half of its cost is linked to the dollar and roughly a third to the price of oil products. The only defences are scale (bargaining power with Airbus and lessors), a low and simple cost base (one aircraft family, high utilisation), and a large cash buffer to survive the shocks that kill weaker rivals. IndiGo has all three. Nobody else in India has more than one.

02

168 million and stalling: the size of the market

India is the world's third-largest domestic aviation market, but it grew 1% in FY26 and is shrinking in FY27. The long-run gap is huge; the short run is a stall.

Indian airlines carried 168 million domestic passengers in FY26 (1,674.8 lakh on a reconstruction from DGCA's calendar data; ICRA puts it at 1,674.2 lakh). That makes India the world's third-largest domestic market after the United States and China. Add 35 million international passengers flown by Indian carriers and the total is 203 million. The path to get there was violent: 142 million domestic passengers in FY20, 54 million in the first COVID year, then a rebound of 57% and 61% in FY22 and FY23 as pent-up travel returned.

The long-run bull case rests on how little Indians fly. IndiGo's annual report, citing industry data, puts domestic seats per head at 0.14 a year in India against 0.45 in Vietnam and 3.1 in the United States, and international seats per head at 0.07 against 0.56 in Vietnam. Airlines have bet on that: IndiGo, Air India and Akasa together have about 1,700 aircraft on order, around twice the roughly 850 in service.

03

Who flies, and why: demand is bigger than it looks and more price-sensitive than airlines hoped

Income growth, Gulf migration, outbound tourism and new airports pull demand up; fares, rail and the monsoon push it down. 2026 showed how fast the second set can win.

Income and the middle class. Air travel in India has historically grown at about 1.5–2 times GDP growth, an industry rule of thumb rather than a measured constant. Each step up in household income moves families from long train journeys to flights for weddings, holidays and work. The base is small, so the growth runway is long.

Price elasticity is real, and 2026 measured it. IndiGo raised its yield 21.3% in Q1 FY27 and 34.3% in Q2 FY27. In Q2 its passengers fell 4.7% and the industry's domestic traffic fell 6.3% in August and 6.5% in September. Part of the drop was supply (capacity was cut), so the passenger response was smaller than textbook low-cost-carrier theory would predict: a rough arc elasticity of −0.2 to −0.3 against yield, which is an ILLUSTRATIVE estimate. But the volume loss is real, and the marginal traveller who was priced out is exactly the first-time flyer the long-run story depends on. IndiGo's CFO said in July 2026 that post-COVID yields were already 40–45% above pre-COVID levels before the war, on revenge travel and constrained capacity.

Rail is a competitor below about 800 km. Air-conditioned rail classes and Vande Bharat trains, including the new sleeper versions on overnight routes, compete for the same traveller. When fares rise sharply, the marginal passenger goes back to the train. The rail booking ecosystem is covered in the travel services report.

The Gulf corridors. About 9 million Indians live in the Gulf Cooperation Council countries. Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Telangana to the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait and Oman form one of the largest labour and family-visit markets in world aviation. This was the market hit hardest by the 2026 war: IndiGo's daily departures to the Middle East fell from about 150 before the war to about 20, then recovered to 90–95% of pre-war levels by the end of June.

Outbound tourism and students. South-East Asia (Thailand, Vietnam, Bali, Malaysia), Central Asia (Almaty, Tashkent, Baku, Tbilisi), Türkiye and Greece have become mass-market Indian holiday destinations, opened up by visa relaxation and short-haul low-cost flights. Students to the UK, Europe and Australia add long-haul demand. IndiGo serves 36–45 international destinations itself and roughly 94–98 more through codeshare partners.

Business travel is concentrated on the metro trunk routes (Delhi–Mumbai, Delhi–Bengaluru, Mumbai–Bengaluru). It pays higher fares and is the target of Air India's business class and IndiGo's Stretch product. Hotels and corporate travel move together; the hotels report covers that cycle from the room side.

New airports unlock suppressed demand. Navi Mumbai opened on 25 December 2025 and Noida (Jewar) in the first quarter of FY27, relieving the two most slot-constrained metro regions. Smaller new stations (Hindon, Purnia, Rewa, Adampur, Bhavnagar were new IndiGo stations in FY26) bring first-time flyers into the network. Section 12 covers airport capacity in detail.

Seasonality is extreme. October–December (Diwali, weddings, winter holidays) and April–June (summer) are peaks. July–September, the monsoon quarter, is the trough. IndiGo has lost money in the September quarter in every recent year except FY24, which is why a Q2 loss on its own says little; the useful question is whether the loss was larger or smaller than the year before.

04

The near-duopoly: how two groups came to carry nine in ten passengers

Every failed rival handed IndiGo share. The domestic market is now priced by IndiGo, contested at the premium end by Air India, and too small at the bottom for a third carrier to thrive.

Indian aviation was a crowded market for most of its private-sector history. In 2016 IndiGo, Jet Airways, Air India, SpiceJet, GoAir, Vistara and AirAsia India all held meaningful shares. Ten years later, the domestic table has two lines that matter. In September 2026 IndiGo carried 77.30 lakh domestic passengers (65.4%) and the Air India group 31.65 lakh (26.8%). Akasa Air carried 6.64 lakh (5.6%). SpiceJet carried about 73,000, or 0.6%.

Concentration can be measured with the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), the sum of squared market shares. On September 2026 shares the Indian domestic market scores about 5,030 (65.4² + 26.8² + 5.6² plus small carriers; an estimate). US antitrust guidance treats anything above 2,500 as highly concentrated. India's domestic aviation is twice that threshold. The practical effect is that IndiGo sets the price on most routes. Where it adds capacity, fares fall; where it holds capacity back, fares rise, and nobody else has the aircraft, slots or balance sheet to undercut it at scale.

Dearer fares, emptier planes: what pricing power looks like in a duopoly

IndiGo raised its yield 34% in a quarter while cutting capacity 1%. A fragmented market could not have done that. But the planes got emptier and the quarter still lost money.

The clearest test of market structure is what happens to prices when costs jump. In 2008, when oil hit $140 a barrel, India had half a dozen airlines fighting for share; fares could not keep up, losses ballooned, and Kingfisher (which had just bought Air Deccan) began the slide that killed it in 2012. In 2026 the response was different. With two groups controlling 92% of domestic capacity, both cut flights and raised fares. IndiGo's yield went from ₹4.69 per revenue passenger-kilometre (RPK) in the September 2025 quarter to ₹6.30 a year later.

Is the pricing sustainable? The bull reading is that the market has become rational: with SpiceJet gone and Air India focused on repairing losses, nobody has an incentive to start a fare war, and capacity discipline can persist while deliveries remain constrained. The 2026 fuel shock: how ATF is priced and how much reached the fare Deep dive

ATF is priced off Singapore, converted at the rupee rate, taxed by the Centre and the states, and kept outside GST. In 2026 every one of those layers worked against the airlines.

Aviation turbine fuel (ATF) is the single largest cost for every Indian airline, and the one it controls least. Since deregulation in 2001, ATF prices have been set monthly by the public-sector oil marketing companies (IOCL, BPCL, HPCL), with private refiners (Reliance, Nayara) following. The formula starts from an international benchmark (the Singapore jet-fuel price, or Mean of Platts Arab Gulf), adds freight, insurance and the OMC's margin, converts the result to rupees, and then adds taxes. Prices are revised on the first of each month. The refining side of this chain, including the jet-fuel "crack spread" that refiners earn over crude, is covered in the refining report.

Hedging is the other half of the story, and India has almost none. IndiGo's annual report contains no material fuel-hedging disclosure. Ryanair, by contrast, had about 80% of its FY27 fuel hedged at roughly $67 a barrel when the war began. That single policy difference explains much of why Ryanair reported a 14% net margin for its year to March 2026 while every Indian airline lost money in the following quarters. Indian airlines have historically avoided fuel hedges because a large mark-to-market loss on a hedge when prices fall is politically and commercially awkward, and because the rupee leg would need hedging too. The 2026 shock will reopen that debate.

The policy toolkit, and what to watch

Four policy levers are live. The 25% cap on domestic ATF price increases from 1 April 2026 showed the state will act as a shock absorber for domestic flying, but only temporarily. A new ATF Price Stabilisation Fund was launched (CRISIL, June 2026), and IndiGo said on its Q2 call that it would consider joining "if its terms made economic sense". The special additional excise duty on ATF exports, a windfall levy, was reintroduced in March 2026 and cut to ₹10.50 a litre from ₹15 for the fortnight to 15 October; it keeps some domestic supply at home. And the long-running demand to bring ATF under GST, or for states to cut VAT, remains the single largest structural cost relief available; the GST Council has deferred it repeatedly. In April 2026 the civil aviation ministry said it was evaluating VAT cuts by states and talking to OMCs about limiting refining margins.

In plain terms

Indian airlines buy fuel at a price set in Singapore, converted at a rupee rate set by markets, and taxed by governments that cannot give the tax back as a credit. They do not hedge. In a normal year that is a manageable risk; in a war year it decides whether the industry makes money.

The forward view is not comforting. Walsh said early indications were that fuel costs would be higher in the December 2026 quarter than in September, and that he expected the jet crack spread to average about 65% this year against a historical 21–22%. With Delhi ATF at ₹1,38,500/kl on 1 November, H2 FY27 fuel CASK is likely to stay at or above the Q1–Q2 levels of ₹2.44–2.49 unless crude or the crack spread falls quickly. CRISIL expects fuel to be 40–50% of Indian airlines' operating costs in FY27 and aggregate operating profit to fall 10–15% to ₹16,000–17,000 crore. That is a forecast on an operating-profit basis; ICRA's ₹36,000–38,000 crore net loss forecast is after interest, depreciation and forex, so the two are consistent.

The forex and lease maze: why an airline's profit is a currency print Deep dive

Ind AS 116 turns aircraft leases into dollar debts on the balance sheet. When the rupee falls, the debt is revalued and the loss lands in the P&L. Reading Indian airline results means separating three profit numbers and two cash numbers.

Until FY20, an Indian airline that leased an aircraft simply expensed the monthly rent. Under Ind AS 116, the accounting standard for leases adopted from FY20, almost every aircraft lease longer than a year is capitalised. The airline records a right-of-use (ROU) asset, which it depreciates, and a lease liability equal to the present value of the future rent, on which it charges interest. The rent disappears from the P&L and reappears as depreciation plus interest. Because Indian airlines lease 80–90% of their fleets from dollar-based lessors, the lease liability is a dollar liability. Every quarter, it is revalued at the closing rupee rate, and the change goes through the P&L as a foreign-exchange gain or loss.

The effect on IndiGo's FY26 numbers was dramatic. The closing rupee moved from ₹85.50 to ₹94.56 per dollar over the year, a fall of about 10.6%. IndiGo booked a gross forex loss of ₹8,976 crore in expenses, partly offset by an ₹876 crore gain on its currency hedges (booked in other income), for a net forex hit of about ₹8,100 crore. That one line turned an underlying profit into a reported loss.

EBITDAR, not EBITDA

Airlines report EBITDAR (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and aircraft rentals) because leasing and owning aircraft are economically similar ways of getting the same asset, and EBITDAR treats them alike. Under Ind AS 116, most rent is already gone from the P&L, so the "R" now only adds back short-term and damp-lease rentals (₹2,085 crore in FY26). IndiGo defines EBITDAR as PBT plus finance cost, minus finance income, plus depreciation, plus aircraft and engine rentals, plus exceptional items. Two traps follow. First, screener.in's "operating profit" (₹12,037 crore in FY26, a 14% margin) is neither EBITDA nor EBITDAR: it deducts the forex loss and the remaining rentals. Second, a US airline reporting under US GAAP, such as Southwest, keeps operating-lease rent in operating costs, so its EBITDA is not comparable with an Indian EBITDAR.

In plain terms

Under the current accounting rules, an airline's leased aircraft appear on its balance sheet as a dollar loan. When the rupee falls, the loan is worth more rupees and the airline books a loss, even though not a single rupee has left the bank. IndiGo's CFO put the cash part of the forex loss in Q1 FY27 at only about ₹80 crore. The rest was a revaluation of payments due over the next eight to ten years.

An owned aircraft financed with dollar debt is still a dollar exposure, but it also gives an asset that can be sold or refinanced, and it stops the operating-lease liability growing as fast.

Total cash, free cash, and the screener net-debt trap

Lease accounting also confuses the balance sheet. On screener.in, IndiGo's "borrowings" were ₹87,303 crore at September 2026, which looks alarming against equity of about ₹5,000 crore (debt to equity of 11.2× on the annual report's figure for March 2026). But ₹55,159 crore of that is the capitalised operating-lease liability: future rent, not borrowed money. Debt excluding operating leases (finance leases plus loans) was ₹32,144 crore. Against that, IndiGo held ₹52,685 crore of total cash, of which ₹39,929 crore was free cash and ₹12,756 crore restricted (margin money and deposits backing guarantees and letters of credit). Screener shows only ₹26,456 crore of "investments" against borrowings and so implies net debt of about ₹60,800 crore; that ignores roughly ₹26,000 crore of cash and bank balances held in other assets. On the company's own figures, net debt including leases was ₹34,618 crore, and excluding operating leases IndiGo had net cash of ₹20,541 crore.

Engines, leases and the cost stack: when the planes cannot fly

A defect in Pratt & Whitney's engines grounded dozens of IndiGo aircraft for three years, forced expensive damp leases and still shapes capacity. Delivery delays at Airbus and Boeing made it worse.

The second constraint on Indian aviation is physical: aircraft that exist but cannot fly. In mid-2023 Pratt & Whitney disclosed a defect in powder metal used in parts of its PW1100G geared turbofan (GTF) engine, which powers many A320neo-family aircraft. Engines had to come off wing early for inspection, and shop capacity was nowhere near enough. Go First, which flew only GTF-powered A320neos, had already collapsed in May 2023 blaming engine failures. IndiGo, the world's largest A320neo operator, had roughly 60–70 aircraft grounded (AOG, "aircraft on ground") at the 2024 peak according to industry reports, and still around 40 in January 2026, with P&W promising replacement engines by June 2026.

Grounded aircraft hurt in three ways. They still carry lease rent, depreciation and some maintenance cost. They cut capacity, so fixed costs are spread over fewer seat-kilometres and unit cost rises. And to keep the schedule flying, the airline has to rent capacity: a damp lease (also called a wet lease when it includes crew) brings in an aircraft with some of the lessor's crew and maintenance, at a much higher cost per hour. IndiGo's damp-leased fleet rose to 20 aircraft at March 2026 (6 Boeing 787s, 5 Boeing 737s, 4 A321s and 5 A320s), after the CFO said in January that the moderation in AOGs "was not to the same levels" expected and "we need to bring back damp leases".

The accounting of the groundings is easy to miss. In FY25 IndiGo agreed a "customized compensation plan" with International Aero Engines (a P&W affiliate) for aircraft on ground. In plain terms

The December meltdown: what 2,507 cancellations say about a 65%-share airline Deep dive

New pilot-rest rules took full effect on 1 November 2025. Five weeks later IndiGo's schedule collapsed. The episode cost it a CEO, a fine, a schedule cut and a competition probe, and showed that the largest airline is now a systemic risk.

The Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) revised its Flight Duty Time Limitations (FDTL) for pilots in January 2024, after pilot unions took the old rules to the Delhi High Court on fatigue grounds. The new rules gave pilots more rest and limited night flying, and were phased in: Phase I (weekly rest and related provisions) from 1 July 2025, Phase II (the night-operations provisions) from 1 November 2025. Every airline had nearly two years' notice. The rules meant more pilots were needed to fly the same schedule, especially an overnight-heavy one.

In plain terms

When an airline with two-thirds of the market gets its crew rosters wrong, the country's air travel system stops for three days. That is what happened in December 2025. What the episode says about dominance

Three lessons go beyond IndiGo. First, scale concentrates operational risk. A smaller airline's crew shortage inconveniences its own passengers; IndiGo's stranded a large part of the country's flyers and raised fares on every other airline in the same week. Regulators respond to that by demanding buffers (spare crew, spare aircraft, slack in schedules) that cost money and lower utilisation. Second, efficiency and resilience trade off. IndiGo's low unit cost comes partly from flying its aircraft and crews hard, including at night; the FDTL rules deliberately reduce how hard. Third, dominance invites scrutiny. The CCI probe, opened after the cancellations, will look at whether a dominant airline limited services or imposed unfair terms. Under the Competition Act, penalties for abuse of dominance can reach 10% of average turnover; that tail risk is new.

The recovery was nonetheless fast. Share was back above 63% by March and at a record 66.3% in June 2026. The rulebook: a regulator that sets the hours, the airports, the taxes and the airspace

Indian airlines set their own fares, but almost every input to the fare is shaped by a ministry, a regulator or a state government. 2025–26 brought new rules on crew, labour, competition and fuel.

Airline fares in India have been deregulated since the 1990s, with one exception: the fare bands the civil aviation ministry imposed from May 2020 to August 2022 during COVID. Everything else about the business is regulated. The Ministry of Civil Aviation (MoCA) sets policy and negotiates the bilateral air-services agreements that decide how many seats Indian and foreign airlines may fly between India and each country. The DGCA licenses airlines and crew, sets safety and duty-time rules, publishes market data, and can order schedule changes. BCAS handles security. AERA sets aeronautical tariffs at major airports. The petroleum ministry and the OMCs set the ATF price; state governments set VAT on it. And since February 2026 the Competition Commission has an open investigation into the market leader.

The tax treatment of fuel is the single largest policy lever on airline economics. Because ATF is outside GST, the central excise and state VAT on it are a cost an airline cannot set off against the GST it collects on tickets. Many states cut ATF VAT to 1–5% between 2022 and 2025 to attract regional flights; Delhi still levies 25%. GST 2.0, the rate rationalisation that took effect on 22 September 2025, did not bring ATF into GST. Every year the industry asks; every year the states, for whom fuel taxes are a major revenue source, resist.

In plain terms

The government does not set airfares, but it sets nearly everything that goes into them: how many hours a pilot may fly, what airports may charge, how fuel is taxed, which countries Indian airlines may fly to and over, and whether the biggest airline is behaving fairly. In 2025–26 almost every one of those rules got tighter or more expensive.

Airspace became a cost centre. Pakistan closed its airspace to Indian-registered and Indian-operated aircraft on 23–24 April 2025; India reciprocated on 30 April. The ban has been extended monthly; the latest notice (NOTAM A0590, effective 17 October 2026) runs to 04:59 Pakistan time on 24 November 2026, covering the Karachi and Lahore flight information regions. Every westbound flight from north India to the Gulf, Europe or North America must route around it, adding flying time, fuel and crew hours. Air India estimated the cost at about US$600 million a year. Gulf and European airlines that can overfly Pakistan are not affected, which hands them a structural advantage on India's busiest international routes. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 then closed parts of Gulf airspace altogether for weeks.

Safety oversight took on new weight after Air India flight AI171, a Boeing 787-8, crashed 32 seconds after take-off from Ahmedabad on 12 June 2025, killing 260 people (241 of the 242 on board and 19 on the ground). The Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau's preliminary report (July 2025) said both fuel-control switches moved to cut-off seconds after take-off. In July 2026 the ministry told Parliament that tests had found no abnormality in the switches, and the AAIB told the Supreme Court that the draft final report would be ready by October 2026 in sealed cover. No public final report had appeared by 1 November 2026. Its findings will matter for Air India's liabilities, for Boeing's 787 programme and for how DGCA oversees maintenance and training across the industry.

Finally, sustainability rules are arriving. India has set an indicative sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) blending target of 1% for international flights from 2027 and 2% from 2028, and international flights will fall under the ICAO CORSIA offsetting scheme. IOCL is building SAF plants to meet the mandate. SAF costs several times conventional jet fuel, so even a 1–2% blend adds measurably to international CASK, though far less than the 2026 crack-spread spike did.

Foreign airlines, led by the Gulf super-connectors, carry most of India's international passengers. IndiGo wants 40% of its capacity abroad by 2030. The 2026 war showed how exposed that plan is.

India's international aviation market has a structural imbalance. In FY26 about 80.6 million passengers flew between India and the rest of the world (AAI airport data). Indian airlines carried about 35 million of them, roughly 43% (an estimate that divides DGCA's Indian-carrier count by AAI's all-carrier total; the two bases differ slightly). Foreign airlines carried the rest, and the largest of them are the Gulf carriers: Emirates, Qatar Airways, Etihad, flydubai, Air Arabia, Saudia, Oman Air and Gulf Air. Their business model is to fly Indians to a Gulf hub and then onward to Europe, North America and Africa ("sixth-freedom" traffic), using wide-body fleets and hubs designed for exactly that.

In plain terms

Today, a family in Lucknow going to London most likely changes planes in Dubai or Doha. IndiGo wants them to fly IndiGo to Delhi or Mumbai and then IndiGo, or a partner such as KLM or British Airways, onward. To win that traffic it needs long-range aircraft, a product good enough to compete with Emirates, partners at the other end and open airspace. In 2026 it had the partners and some aircraft, but not the airspace.

Why the Gulf carriers are hard to beat

Emirates is the benchmark. In its year to March 2026 the airline earned revenue of AED 130.9 billion (US$35.7 billion), a pre-tax profit of AED 22.8 billion (17.4% margin) and a net profit of AED 19.7 billion (15.0%), flying 53.2 million passengers on 277 aircraft at a 78.4% seat factor. Fuel was 29% of its operating costs. It had a hub designed for connections, a fleet of large widebodies, and a government shareholder. Its India passenger numbers rose 7% in the war quarter. India protects its own airlines through bilateral seat caps (the India–UAE entitlement has been frozen for years at roughly 66,000 weekly seats each way, an approximate figure), which is why Gulf carriers lobby constantly for more seats and Indian carriers resist.

The hard question is whether a domestic low-cost carrier can become a profitable long-haul network airline. The global record is mixed: several low-cost carriers that tried long-haul (Norwegian is the best-known) failed, because widebody economics, premium cabins, connections and loyalty programmes are a different business. IndiGo has advantages they lacked: a huge domestic feeder network, a home market where it controls most connecting traffic, a cost base built on Airbus aircraft, and partners such as Delta and Air France-KLM at the other end. Its new CEO ran British Airways and IAG. It also has a disadvantage none of them had: an airspace ban that lengthens its westbound routes and a currency that makes every dollar of widebody lease more expensive.

In plain terms

The international plan is IndiGo's main growth route now that it has two-thirds of the domestic market. It is also the riskiest part of the business: higher capital cost, stronger competitors, and exposure to wars and airspace closures that a domestic airline never faces.

For the industry as a whole, the 2026 numbers are a warning. Indian carriers' international share fell rather than rose in FY26, and fell sharply in the war quarter. ICRA expects Indian carriers' international traffic to shrink 3–6% in FY27. The international growth that justified the A350 and XLR orders will arrive, if it does, from 2027 onward, when the aircraft do. The international side of the travel trade, from outbound tour operators to online agents, is covered in the travel services report.

Runways and slots: why new airports matter more than new aircraft

An aircraft can only earn if it has somewhere to land at a time people want to fly. Mumbai and Delhi were full; Navi Mumbai and Noida opened in 2025–26. Slots, not planes, still ration growth at the big metros.

An airline's growth is constrained by three physical things: aircraft, crew and airport slots. Slots, the right to take off or land at a specific time, are the scarcest at India's two largest cities. Mumbai's Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj International Airport handled around 55 million passengers a year on a single effective runway system, close to its practical limit. Delhi's Indira Gandhi International is larger but its peak hours are full. When an airline fails, its slots at these airports are often its most valuable asset, which is why Jet Airways' and Go First's slots were fought over and why SpiceJet's are now the prize for IndiGo and Akasa.

India's airport network has expanded fast. The number of operational airports went from 74 in 2014 to more than 160 by 2025, according to the civil aviation ministry, as quoted in IndiGo's annual report. The Airports Authority of India (AAI) runs about 125 of them and is also the air navigation service provider; it plans to invest ₹15,000 crore in air-traffic-control automation and navigation by 2028. The largest airports have been privatised under public-private partnerships.

For the forecast period, the airport story is mildly positive for incumbents. New metro capacity removes the hard ceiling on growth at Mumbai and Delhi; the incumbents get first pick; and the private operators have every incentive to attract airlines with incentives on new routes. The cost is that airport charges keep rising faster than capacity, and that the new airports' charges are higher than the old. Investors should treat airport fees as a cost that compounds, not one that scales down with traffic.

One is a single-family, low-cost, cash-rich airline that has earned money through a cycle. The other is a full-service and low-cost group rebuilt from four airlines under new owners. Both are investing for a market that is shrinking this year.

The two groups that run Indian domestic aviation could hardly be more different. IndiGo is a low-cost carrier built on one aircraft family (the A320neo and A321neo, with ATR turboprops for regional routes), one cabin for most of its history, very high aircraft utilisation, sale-and-leaseback financing, and a network of about 2,000–2,150 daily flights. Seventy years, five failures: the cycle that keeps consolidating Indian aviation

Cheap fuel and a stable rupee bring new airlines and fare wars; an oil, currency or demand shock breaks the weakest; the survivor takes its share. 2025–26 is the same pattern again.

Indian aviation has run through the same cycle four times since private airlines were allowed back in 1994, and the 2025–26 shock is the fifth. The pattern has four stages. Boom: fuel is cheap, the rupee is stable, demand grows in double digits, and investors fund new airlines or new aircraft. Fare war: capacity outruns demand and airlines cut fares to fill seats, so margins collapse even before costs rise. Shock: oil spikes, the rupee falls, demand dips (2008, 2012, 2019, COVID, 2026), and airlines with weak balance sheets run out of cash. Consolidation: the weakest fails, lessors take back the aircraft, and the survivors take its slots, crews and passengers. Each turn of the cycle has left the market more concentrated.

In plain terms

An airline is a machine for turning seat-kilometres into rupees. Two numbers decide everything: how many rupees each seat-kilometre earns, and how many it costs. When the second is higher than the first, every extra flight loses money, and the only fixes are to raise fares, fill more seats, or cut costs per seat.

ASK: available seat-kilometres

What it is. The airline's production: one seat flown one kilometre, whether filled or not. Formula: seats on each flight × distance flown, summed over all flights. Worked example: a 186-seat A320neo flying 1,000 km produces 1.86 lakh ASK; IndiGo produced 172.4 billion ASK in FY26, about 47 crore ASK a day. What drives it: fleet size, seats per aircraft (an A321neo has 232, an A320neo 186, an ATR 78), aircraft utilisation (hours flown per day) and average stage length (longer flights produce more ASK per departure). Benchmarks: capacity growth should track demand; growth well ahead of traffic usually precedes a fare war. IndiGo grew ASK 9.5% in FY26 (domestic +4.3%, international +22.8%), then −1.0% in Q2 FY27, with "early single digits" guided for Q3 and a target of about 300 billion ASK by FY30 (roughly 15% a year from FY26). Air India and Akasa do not publish quarterly ASK; Akasa reported +30% for FY26. The trap: ASK growth from longer international flights is not the same as growth from more departures. International ASK rises fast because the distances are long; a shift to international inflates ASK growth while the number of flights barely changes.

RPK: revenue passenger-kilometres

What it is. The airline's sales volume: one paying passenger carried one kilometre. Formula: paying passengers on each flight × distance, summed. Worked example: 195 passengers on a 1,150 km flight = 2.24 lakh RPK. IndiGo sold 145.5 billion RPK in FY26 (+7.5%) and 32.8 billion in Q2 FY27 (−3.7%). What drives it: demand, price, network coverage and the mix of short and long flights. Benchmarks: RPK growing faster than ASK means load factors rising and pricing power improving; RPK growing slower means the airline is adding seats faster than it can sell them. In FY26 IndiGo's ASK grew 9.5% and its RPK 7.5%, so load factor fell. In Q2 FY27 both fell, RPK more. How peers compare: Indian rivals do not publish RPK; DGCA publishes passengers and seat factors, not distance-weighted figures. The trap: passenger counts and RPK can move in opposite directions. In FY26 IndiGo's passengers rose 4.0% while RPK rose 7.5%, because international passengers (who fly much further) grew 14.9% and domestic only 2.5%. Growth in "passengers" understates the shift to long flights.

Load factor

What it is. How full the planes are. Formula: RPK ÷ ASK (the distance-weighted passenger load factor, PLF). Worked example: 145.5 billion RPK ÷ 172.4 billion ASK = 84.4% for IndiGo in FY26 (domestic 85.6%, international 81.7%). What drives it: the balance between seats offered and demand at the chosen price; revenue managers trade load factor against yield every day. Benchmarks: above 86% is strong for an Indian carrier, 80–86% average, below 78% weak; global low-cost leaders run in the low-to-mid 90s (Ryanair) while Southwest fell to 77.4% in 2025. IndiGo's 80.3% in Q2 FY27 was the weakest of the eight quarters to September 2026. The trap: DGCA's monthly "seat factor" is passengers ÷ seats per airline, not RPK ÷ ASK. IndiGo's September 2026 seat factor was 81.1% while its Q2 company load factor was 80.3%. Never put the two in one chart.

Yield

What it is. The average price per kilometre a paying passenger pays. Formula: passenger ticket revenue ÷ RPK. Worked example: ₹20,652 crore of Q2 FY27 ticket revenue ÷ 32.8 billion RPK = ₹6.30 per RPK. A 1,150 km trip at that yield is about ₹7,250 before ancillaries. What drives it: supply and demand on each route, fuel surcharges, competition, the share of long-haul flying (long flights have lower yields per kilometre), the share of premium cabins, and the season. Benchmarks: for a domestic-heavy Indian airline, above ₹5.5 is strong, ₹4.5–5.5 average, below ₹4.5 weak (INDICATIVE bands). IndiGo's yield ran ₹4.69–5.33 through FY26 and ₹6.04–6.30 in H1 FY27. Each 1% of yield on FY26 ticket revenue (₹73,648 crore) is worth about ₹740 crore. How peers compare: Air India and Akasa do not publish yield. The trap: yield excludes ancillaries, so an airline that unbundles (charging separately for bags) can show falling yield and rising revenue per passenger at the same time. And "fares up 30%" headlines in 2026 are yield, not fares on any particular route.

RASK and PRASK

What it is. Unit revenue: everything the airline earns, divided by capacity. Formula (IndiGo): (total revenue − finance income) ÷ ASK. PRASK = passenger revenue ÷ ASK. Worked example: Q2 FY27 total income ₹24,312 crore − finance income ₹822 crore = ₹23,490 crore ÷ 40.8 billion ASK = ₹5.76. What drives it: load factor × yield, plus ancillaries and cargo, plus other income. Benchmarks: CRISIL puts the domestic industry's RASK at about ₹4.9 in FY26, rising to ₹5.2–5.4 in FY27 on fuel surcharges (a forecast); IndiGo's FY26 RASK was ₹4.99 and Q2 FY27 ₹5.76. How peers compare: Akasa reported stage-adjusted RASK up 10% in FY26 without disclosing the level. The trap: IndiGo deducts finance income (interest on its ~₹52,700 crore cash pile: ₹3,530 crore in FY26) from both revenue and cost before dividing, so its RASK and CASK are not comparable with an airline that reports gross. Also, P&W compensation for grounded aircraft is included in revenue from operations, which lifts RASK slightly.

CASK

What it is. Unit cost: everything the airline spends, divided by capacity. Formula (IndiGo): (total cost − finance income) ÷ ASK. Worked example: FY26 CASK of ₹5.00 = fuel ₹1.47 + cost excluding fuel and forex ₹3.00 + forex ₹0.52 (rounding). In Q2 FY27 it was ₹6.22 = ₹2.44 + ₹3.45 + ₹0.33. What drives it: fuel price and burn, aircraft ownership cost, maintenance, staff, airport fees, the rupee, and above all utilisation (fixed costs spread over more or fewer ASK). Benchmarks: IndiGo has the lowest CASK in Indian aviation; Akasa reported stage-adjusted CASK down 4% in FY26 without the level. The trap: stage length. Longer flights have lower CASK because take-off, landing and airport costs are spread over more kilometres. An airline that shifts to longer routes can show falling CASK without becoming more efficient; Akasa's "stage-adjusted" figures exist to correct for this.

How does IndiGo's unit cost compare abroad? Direct comparisons are hard because of currencies, stage lengths and accounting, but on any reasonable conversion IndiGo's CASK excluding fuel is among the lowest of the world's large airlines, comparable with the leading European and Asian low-cost carriers and far below full-service airlines. Its disadvantage is the other way round: fuel in India costs more than in most markets because of the tax wedge and the absence of hedging, and the rupee adds a cost that euro- or dollar-based airlines do not bear. The low controllable cost is the moat; the high uncontrollable cost is the vulnerability.

Fuel CASK

What it is. Fuel cost per ASK. Formula: fuel expense ÷ ASK. Worked example: ₹9,967 crore ÷ 40.8 billion ASK = ₹2.44 in Q2 FY27, up 68.9% on ₹1.45. What drives it: the ATF price (Brent, crack spread, rupee, taxes) and fuel burn per seat, which depends on aircraft generation (a neo burns about 15–20% less than a ceo), seat density, stage length and operating practice. Benchmarks: fuel below 30% of total cost is comfortable, 30–38% normal, above 38% stressed; IndiGo was 28.3% in FY26 and 38.1% in Q2 FY27. CRISIL expects fuel at 40–50% of Indian airlines' operating costs in FY27; Emirates' fuel was 29% of its operating costs in FY26. The trap: fuel share of cost falls when other costs rise, so a falling fuel share is not always good news. In FY26 IndiGo's fuel share dropped from 34.2% to 28.3% partly because cheaper fuel and partly because the forex loss inflated total cost.

What it is. The controllable core of unit cost, and the KPI IndiGo's management guides on. Formula: (total cost − fuel − forex − finance income) ÷ ASK. The spread and the break-even load factor

What it is. The gap between unit revenue and unit cost, and the load factor at which the airline would break even. Formula: spread = RASK − CASK. Break-even load factor ≈ CASK ÷ (yield × (1 + ancillary-to-ticket ratio)). Worked example: Q2 FY27 break-even ≈ 6.22 ÷ (6.30 × 1.12) ≈ 88%, against an actual 80.3%, so a loss. FY25 ≈ 4.66 ÷ (5.15 × 1.11) ≈ 81.5% against an actual 86.0%, so a profit. Both are ILLUSTRATIVE approximations that ignore cargo. What drives it: everything above. Benchmarks: a spread above +₹0.40 is healthy, zero to +₹0.40 thin, negative is a loss. The trap: the spread at IndiGo is net of finance income, so its zero line is pre-tax profit before exceptionals, not operating profit. An airline with no cash pile would need a wider spread to earn the same.

EBITDAR and its margin

What it is. Operating cash earnings before the cost of owning or renting aircraft. Formula (IndiGo): PBT + finance cost − finance income + depreciation + aircraft and engine rentals + exceptional items. Worked example: FY26 EBITDAR ₹15,089 crore = 17.8% of revenue from operations; adding back the ₹8,100 crore net forex gives ₹23,189 crore ex-forex (27.3%). What drives it: the spread before ownership costs. Benchmarks: above 25% strong, 15–25% average, below 15% weak (post-Ind AS 116). IndiGo ran 25.5–26.3% in FY24–FY25, 17.8% in FY26, and 10.5% (15.5% ex-forex) in Q2 FY27. How peers compare: Akasa said its EBITDAR margin "improved by 60%" in FY26, a relative figure with no level; Air India does not publish it. The trap: EBITDAR includes the forex loss unless stated otherwise, so it can swing wildly with the rupee; and it is not comparable with a US carrier's EBITDA, which deducts operating-lease rent.

Block hours and aircraft utilisation

What it is. How many hours a day each aircraft flies, measured from pushback to arrival at the gate (block hours). Formula: total block hours ÷ average aircraft ÷ 365. Worked example: IndiGo's FY26 block hours of 1.62 million ÷ about 437 average aircraft ÷ 365 ≈ 10.2 hours a day across the whole fleet, including grounded aircraft and freighters (an ILLUSTRATIVE calculation). IndiGo historically flew its active A320s about 13 hours a day; that figure is historical, and the fleet-wide average is lower because grounded aircraft drag it down. FY26 had 7,87,741 flights, about 2,160 a day; Q2 FY27 peaked at 2,020 daily flights. What drives it: turnaround time, night flying (now limited by FDTL), grounded aircraft, and network design. Benchmarks: above 13 hours strong, 11–13 average, below 11 weak for narrowbodies. The trap: airlines often quote utilisation for "operating" aircraft only, excluding AOGs, which flatters the figure. And higher utilisation through night flights now runs into the FDTL night limits.

Fleet, grounded aircraft and damp leases

What it is. The aircraft count, how many cannot fly, and how many are rented with crew to fill the gap. Formula: fleet = owned + finance-leased + operating-leased + damp-leased; damp share = damp ÷ fleet. Worked example: 442 aircraft at September 2026 = 41 owned + 66 finance lease + 330 operating lease + 5 damp; damp share 1.1%. At March 2026 it was 20 of 441 (4.5%). What drives it: deliveries, redeliveries to lessors, engine groundings, and the need for long-haul lift. Benchmarks: damp share below 2% is healthy, 2–5% a warning, above 5% a sign of a supply problem. How peers compare: Air India about 300 aircraft; Akasa 42; SpiceJet's active fleet is not disclosed (estimates of 12–21) and it damp-leased three A320s in July 2026. The trap: the headline fleet count includes grounded aircraft (IndiGo no longer discloses the AOG number) and damp-leased ones, so "fleet growth" can overstate flying capacity. The 2025 claim that IndiGo was "adding one aircraft a week" was true of deliveries, not of the net fleet, which shrank to 432 in June 2026.

In plain terms

A fleet count tells you how many aircraft an airline is paying for, not how many are flying. The difference, grounded jets, is invisible in the headline and very visible in the cost line. When reading an airline's fleet, ask how many are owned, leased, rented with crew and parked.

Ancillary revenue

What it is. Revenue from everything except the base fare: bags, seats, meals, change and convenience fees, priority, loyalty partners. Formula: ancillary revenue ÷ revenue from operations (or per passenger). Worked example: IndiGo FY26 ₹9,092 crore ÷ ₹84,962 crore = 10.7%, or about ₹740 per passenger; Q2 FY27 ₹2,481 crore (10.6%). What drives it: unbundling (IndiGo's 2026 Lite Fare without a checked bag), digital sales, seat-selection pricing, and loyalty tie-ups (BluChip with Accor ALL). Benchmarks: above 20% strong (Ryanair 32%, about €24 per passenger), 10–20% average, below 10% weak. How peers compare: Indian rivals do not disclose. The trap: what counts as "ancillary" varies by airline; some include cargo or convenience fees, others not. IndiGo's "other revenue" (about ₹2,222 crore in FY26) also includes P&W compensation, so it should not be read as cargo alone.

Revenue per passenger and stage length

What it is. The average total revenue an airline collects for each passenger, and the average distance each passenger flies. Formula: (ticket + ancillary revenue) ÷ passengers; stage length = RPK ÷ passengers. Worked example: IndiGo FY26: (₹73,648 crore + ₹9,092 crore) ÷ 123.4 million passengers ≈ ₹6,700 per passenger, and 145.5 billion RPK ÷ 123.4 million ≈ 1,180 km per passenger (both ILLUSTRATIVE calculations). In Q2 FY27, (₹20,652 crore + ₹2,481 crore) ÷ 27.5 million ≈ ₹8,400 per passenger over about 1,190 km. What drives it: fares, ancillaries and the mix of short domestic hops and long international sectors. Benchmarks: there is no universal band, because revenue per passenger rises mechanically with distance; compare it only within the same airline over time or between airlines with similar networks. How peers compare: Ryanair's €15.54 billion of revenue over 208.4 million passengers is about €75 per passenger on short European sectors. The trap: a rising revenue per passenger can simply reflect longer flights. When IndiGo adds Athens or Manchester, its revenue per passenger rises even if every individual fare is unchanged; yield (per kilometre) is the cleaner price measure.

Total cash versus free cash

What it is. The cash buffer, and the part of it the airline can actually use. Formula: total cash = free cash + restricted cash (margin money, deposits backing guarantees and letters of credit). Worked example: IndiGo at September 2026: ₹52,685 crore total = ₹39,929 crore free + ₹12,756 crore restricted. Free cash ÷ trailing revenue (₹93,858 crore) ≈ 43%. What drives it: operating cash flow (strongly positive even in loss years because depreciation and forex are non-cash: IndiGo's FY26 cash from operations was ₹23,470 crore), lease payments, aircraft purchases and sale-and-leaseback proceeds. Benchmarks: free cash above 35% of annual revenue is strong, 15–35% average, below 15% fragile. How peers compare: Air India and Akasa depend on owner funding; SpiceJet has negative net worth. The trap: screener.in classifies much of IndiGo's cash outside "investments", so a quick net-debt calculation from screener overstates net debt by about ₹26,000 crore.

Lease liabilities, net debt and the net US-dollar liability

What it is. The airline's obligations: capitalised leases, loans and finance leases, and the dollar part of all of them. Formula: net debt including leases = total debt (incl. lease liability) − total cash; net US$ liability = US$ financial liabilities − US$ financial assets. Worked example: IndiGo September 2026: ₹87,303 crore − ₹52,685 crore = ₹34,618 crore net debt including leases. Net debt ÷ EBITDAR was 2.8× in FY26 (1.6× in FY25) on the company's figure. The net dollar liability was ₹84,296 crore (about US$8.9 billion) at March 2026, with a 1% rupee move worth ₹843 crore. What drives it: fleet growth, lease rates, the shift to ownership, and the rupee. Benchmarks: net debt incl. leases below 2× EBITDAR comfortable, 2–4× average, above 4× stretched. The trap: lease liabilities are not "borrowings" in the usual sense; they are committed future rent. And the net dollar liability includes maintenance reserves and deposits, so it differs from the lease liability alone.

PAT ex-forex

What it is. Net profit excluding the mark-to-market forex loss or gain. Formula: reported PAT + forex loss (net of hedges, after tax effects as the company computes it). Worked example: FY26: −₹2,394 crore + ₹8,100 crore = ₹5,706 crore. Q2 FY27: −₹1,879 crore reported, −₹724 crore ex-forex. What drives it: the operating spread and finance income. Benchmarks: a net margin above 8% is strong for an airline, 2–8% average, below 2% weak. How peers compare: Akasa FY26 loss ₹2,716 crore, about ₹1,724 crore ex-forex (estimate). The trap: do not confuse PAT ex-forex (₹5,706 crore) with PAT ex-forex ex-exceptional (₹7,503 crore). And remember that ex-forex PAT still includes the higher rupee cost of dollar lease rents and maintenance, so it is not a constant-currency figure.

On-time performance and cancellation rate

What it is. Reliability: the share of flights departing on time, and the share cancelled. Formula: on-time departures ÷ scheduled departures (DGCA uses six or more metro airports; IndiGo reports ten); cancellations ÷ scheduled flights. Worked example: IndiGo Q2 FY27: 91.3% on time at ten major airports, 0.35% cancelled, 99.9% technical dispatch reliability. December 2025: 9.65% cancelled. What drives it: crew and aircraft buffers, rostering software, weather (winter fog in north India), airport congestion and ATC. Benchmarks: on-time above 85% strong, 75–85% average, below 75% weak; cancellations below 0.5% strong, above 2% a red flag. How peers compare: DGCA March 2026 on-time at six metros: IndiGo 88.7%, Akasa 82.6%, Air India group 78.0%, Alliance Air 62.9%, SpiceJet 43.0%. The trap: airlines choose the airports and periods they report; compare like with like using DGCA's monthly data, and note that a low cancellation rate can coexist with long delays.

Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDAR vs EBITDA vs screener "operating profit". Under Ind AS 116 most rent is already depreciation and interest; screener's operating profit deducts forex and residual rentals and is neither measure. Use the company's EBITDAR, and state whether it is ex-forex.
  • RASK and CASK net of finance income. IndiGo deducts interest earned on its cash from both. Not comparable with airlines that report gross.
  • Three FY26 profits. Reported −₹2,394 crore; ex-forex ₹5,706 crore; ex-forex ex-exceptional ₹7,503 crore. Never call ₹7,503 crore "ex-forex".
  • Gross vs net forex. ₹8,976 crore gross in expenses; ₹8,100 crore net of the ₹876 crore hedge gain booked in other income.
  • Load factor vs seat factor. Company RPK ÷ ASK vs DGCA passengers ÷ seats. Close, not the same.
  • Calendar vs fiscal year. DGCA cumulative data run January–December; airlines and ICRA use April–March. Label every total CY or FY.
  • Three bases for domestic share. DGCA monthly (65.4% in Sep-2026), fiscal-year own count (~64% in FY26), and seat or capacity share. Name the basis.
  • International share. IndiGo carries about one in five of all India-international passengers but close to half of those flown by Indian airlines.
  • Fleet counts include grounded, damp-leased and freighter aircraft.
  • Yield is not fare. It excludes ancillaries and moves with stage length and mix.
  • OEM compensation in revenue. P&W/IAE compensation for grounded aircraft sits in IndiGo's revenue from operations.
  • Lease liability is not a loan, and screener's net debt ignores much of IndiGo's cash.
  • Global peers use different years (Southwest is calendar), currencies and standards (US GAAP keeps operating leases as rent). Convert at period-average rates and state the year.

In plain terms

Most mistakes in airline analysis come from comparing two numbers that look alike but are built differently: a DGCA seat factor against a company load factor, a calendar-year total against a fiscal-year one, an Indian EBITDAR against a US EBITDA. Before comparing, check the basis.

How to read an Indian airline's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Capacity first (ASK growth). Is the airline growing, flat or shrinking, and against what guidance? IndiGo Q2 FY27: −1.0% vs "flattish" guided. Red flag: capacity growing much faster than DGCA traffic.
  2. Load factor and passengers. Are planes fuller or emptier than a year ago? Q2 FY27: 80.3%, −2.2 points. Red flag: falling load factor while capacity grows.
  3. Yield and RASK. How much pricing power? Q2 FY27 yield +34.3%, RASK ₹5.76 (+26.4%). Check whether ancillaries kept pace.
  4. Fuel cost and fuel CASK. Compare the rise in fuel cost with the rise in revenue. Q2 FY27: fuel +₹4,005 crore vs revenue +₹4,809 crore.
  5. CASK ex-fuel ex-forex. The controllable cost trend. Q2 FY27 ₹3.45, +14.6%. Red flag: double-digit rises with flat capacity.
  6. The spread. RASK − CASK; negative means a loss before tax and exceptionals. Q2 FY27 −₹0.46.
  7. Forex line and closing rupee. Note the quarter-end USD/INR and the forex loss; divide one by the other to check the sensitivity (₹830–1,120 crore per ₹1 recently).
  8. Three profits. Reported PAT, PAT ex-forex, and any exceptional items. Q2 FY27: −₹1,879 crore and −₹724 crore; no exceptionals.
  9. Cash and debt. Free cash, restricted cash, lease liability, debt excluding leases. Q2 FY27: free cash ₹39,929 crore, up on the quarter. Red flag: free cash falling with no aircraft purchases to explain it.
  10. Fleet and guidance. Fleet by ownership type, damp leases, next-quarter capacity guidance and any change to the medium-term plan. Q2 FY27: 442 aircraft, 5 damp; Q3 guided "early single digits".

The checklist works for other Indian airlines with one adjustment: they disclose far less. Air India and Air India Express publish annual statutory accounts (filed with the registrar and summarised in the press around July) and occasional management commentary; Singapore Airlines adds a half-yearly view of Air India's losses on its own basis. Akasa publishes an annual release with growth rates and relative changes in RASK and CASK, but few absolute unit figures. SpiceJet, which is listed, has not published results since the December 2025 quarter. For the industry, DGCA's monthly traffic release (passengers, shares, seat factors, on-time performance, cancellations and complaints) is the only common, high-frequency dataset, and it should be read alongside each month's ATF revision and the rupee.

Ten numbers, in that order, explain almost every airline quarter. For IndiGo's Q2 FY27 they say: pricing power was strong, volume weak, fuel overwhelming, controllable costs rising on the rupee and low utilisation, the currency loss smaller than a year earlier, and the balance sheet still getting stronger. The one number not in the release, the call transcript's commentary on FY28 capacity, is due to be filed around early November 2026.

In plain terms

Read capacity, then price, then fuel, then the controllable costs, then the rupee, then the cash. If the spread is negative, find which of those moved; if the reported profit and the ex-forex profit point in different directions, believe the ex-forex one for the business and the reported one for the balance sheet.

Technology and disruption: engines, long-range narrowbodies and the software that runs the crews

The technologies that matter most to Indian airlines are not apps. They are engine durability, the range of a single-aisle jet, crew-rostering software and, from 2027, sustainable fuel.

Engine durability is the industry's biggest technology risk. The new-generation engines that make the A320neo family 15–20% more fuel-efficient than its predecessor have proved less durable than promised. Pratt & Whitney's GTF powder-metal defect grounded dozens of IndiGo aircraft from 2023 to 2026 and helped kill Go First; CFM's LEAP-1A, which IndiGo is now standardising on through its July 2026 memorandum for more than 1,000 engines, has its own wear issues in hot and sandy conditions like India's and the Middle East's. Engine shop capacity is a global bottleneck, so a durability problem translates into months of grounding. IndiGo's move to diversify suppliers, and to build engine maintenance capacity in India (Safran's LEAP shop in Hyderabad; IndiGo's planned MRO in Bengaluru), is as much a technology strategy as a procurement one.

Long-range narrowbodies change the map. The Airbus A321XLR can carry a two-class cabin for eight to nine hours, which puts Central Asia, Türkiye, Greece, East Africa and parts of Europe in range of Indian cities without a widebody. That allows point-to-point international routes from secondary cities that could never fill a 787, bypassing the Gulf hubs. IndiGo was the first Indian operator (January 2026) and had four by September 2026 against 69 on order. The economics are unproven on long thin routes, and the aircraft's passenger comfort on eight-hour flights is a competitive question against Gulf widebodies.

Crew-rostering and operations software moved from back office to boardroom after December 2025. DGCA's inquiry named "deficiencies in system software support" among the causes of IndiGo's meltdown. As duty-time rules tighten, the ability to build legal, resilient rosters across thousands of pilots and cabin crew becomes a core competence, and buffers that software cannot create must be bought with extra crew. Airlines are also trialling fuel-optimisation tools (IndiGo is testing SITA's OptiClimb) that tune climb profiles to save a few percent of fuel per flight.

Digital distribution and revenue management are where the largest low-cost carriers make their margins. IndiGo sells most of its tickets on its own app and website, has achieved IATA NDC (New Distribution Capability) certification for richer content through agents, and has begun unbundling with the Lite Fare. Dynamic pricing engines that adjust fares by the minute are what allowed the industry to lift yields 34% in a quarter. The online travel agents and platforms on the other side of that distribution are covered in the travel services report.

In plain terms

The technology that most affects an Indian airline's profit is the engine under its wing: whether it lasts as long as the maker promised. After that comes the range of its narrowbody jets, which decides which international routes it can fly without a widebody, and the software that schedules its crews within the new rest rules.

Sustainable aviation fuel and carbon rules are the slow-moving disruption. India's indicative SAF blending target is 1% for international flights from 2027 and 2% from 2028, and international flights will also face CORSIA offsetting. SAF costs several times conventional jet fuel, so a 2% blend could add a few percent to international fuel cost. IOCL is building SAF capacity to meet the mandate; refiners' plans are covered in the refining report.

Rail is the substitute that is improving fastest. The Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed rail line (first section expected around 2027) and the new Vande Bharat sleeper trains on overnight routes will compete with short domestic flights. On trunk routes under about 500 km, rail can win on door-to-door time; on 800–1,500 km overnight routes, it competes on price. In 2026, when airfares rose a third, rail was the obvious alternative for price-sensitive travellers. Over time, the airlines' most defensible domestic traffic is on routes longer than rail can serve in a night.

New financing structures are a quieter innovation. GIFT City's aircraft-leasing regime let IndiGo route all 13 of its Q1 FY27 inductions through an Indian entity, and its first JOLCO showed Indian airlines can tap Japanese tax-efficient financing. Over time, more Indian-owned or Indian-leased aircraft would reduce the dollar exposure that turned FY26's profit into a loss, though the aircraft themselves are still priced in dollars.

Bull vs bear: a structurally better industry, or the same cycle with fewer players?

The bull case rests on structure, under-penetration and IndiGo's balance sheet. The bear case rests on fuel, the rupee, the order book and a regulator that is paying closer attention.

The bull case

  • Structure: two groups carry 92% of domestic passengers, the No. 2 is focused on fixing losses, and SpiceJet's exit frees slots. Rational pricing let IndiGo raise yields 34% in a quarter.
  • Under-penetration: 0.14 domestic seats per head against 0.45 in Vietnam; only about 8.7% of Indians hold passports; 160+ airports and two new metro airports.
  • Balance sheet: IndiGo's ₹39,929 crore of free cash lets it keep flying, keep its orders and take share through the downturn.
  • Non-cash losses: most of the forex hit is revaluation of leases paid over eight to ten years; it reverses if the rupee steadies.
  • International runway: IndiGo is already India's largest international airline by passengers, with XLRs, A350s from 2027 and partners including Delta, Air France-KLM, Virgin, BA, KLM and Qantas.

The bear case

  • Fuel: Delhi ATF at ₹1,38,500/kl (+46% YoY), crack spreads expected near 65% vs 21–22% historically, no hedging; IndiGo recovered only ~60% of the increase.
  • The rupee: a US$8.9 billion net dollar liability; each ₹1 costs about ₹890 crore; the exposure grows with every lease.
  • Demand ceiling: domestic traffic fell in 2026 at today's fares; ICRA forecasts a ₹36,000–38,000 crore industry loss in FY27.
  • The order book: about 1,700 aircraft on order against ~850 in service; IndiGo alone has ₹7.45 lakh crore of commitments.
  • Regulation: CCI investigation, DGCA schedule intervention, FDTL crew costs, labour codes, possible fare scrutiny.

The bull case is strongest on structure. India's domestic market has never had fewer competitors or a stronger leader. In every previous cycle, recovery brought back competition: new airlines, cheap capacity and fare wars. This time the only plausible new entrant at scale is Akasa, which is still losing money; the No. 2 is owned by conglomerates more interested in repairing losses than in buying share; and the leader has the cash to outlast anyone. If fuel and the rupee merely stabilise, the industry's pricing power should let IndiGo return to FY24–FY25 levels of profit within two years.

The bear case is strongest on costs. More than half of an Indian airline's costs are linked to the dollar, a third to the price of oil products, and neither is hedged in any material way. The 2026 shock showed that even a dominant airline can pass on only about 60% of a fuel spike before demand falls. And the order book means capacity will rise sharply from 2027–28, whatever demand does; the A350s, XLRs and A320neos are already committed.

In plain terms

A 10% move in jet fuel changes IndiGo's annual profit by about as much as a 5% move in fares. That is why the fuel price, not the fare, is the first number to check each month, and why the bull case needs fuel to fall or the duopoly to keep pricing for it.

Where does that leave the balance? The structural case for Indian aviation is the best it has ever been, and IndiGo is the clearest beneficiary. The cyclical case in late 2026 is poor: fuel at record rupee prices, a weak currency, shrinking traffic and rising forecasts of industry losses. The investment question is the price paid for the first while the second plays out. At about 9.6× ex-forex EBITDAR (an estimate), the market is already looking through the cycle; the risk is that the cycle lasts longer than the market's patience.

Airlines publish quarterly, but the industry moves monthly: DGCA traffic around mid-month, ATF on the 1st, the rupee every day. These are the numbers and dates that will decide FY27.

Indian aviation is unusually well served by high-frequency data. DGCA publishes domestic traffic, market shares, seat factors, on-time performance and cancellations every month, usually around the middle of the following month. The oil companies revise ATF prices on the first of every month. The rupee trades daily, and the quarter-end closing rate decides the forex line. Three results matter most. First, the Q2 FY27 call transcript, due on BSE around early November, will confirm what IndiGo's management actually said about FY27 capacity ("low single digits" in press reports; "early single digits" for Q3 in the press release), whether the FY28 "mid-teens" plan is under review, and what happens to the damp-leased 787s. Second, DGCA's October and November traffic: the festive season is the strongest demand period of the year, and if passengers do not return at current fares, the yield strategy will need rethinking. Third, IndiGo's Q3 FY27 results in late January 2027 (expected): Walsh has already warned that fuel will be higher than in Q2, so the question is whether festive yields can offset it.

In plain terms

Before IndiGo reports its December-quarter results, investors will already know three things: how many people flew in October and November (DGCA), what fuel cost on 1 November and 1 December (the OMCs), and where the rupee closed on 31 December. Those three numbers will explain most of the result.

Beyond IndiGo, two smaller stories could change the shape of the market. SpiceJet has not filed audited FY26 accounts, a June or September 2026 shareholding pattern, or any result after the December 2025 quarter; an exchange action for non-compliance, an insolvency petition by a lessor or creditor, or a rescue would each decide who gets its Delhi and Mumbai slots. Akasa was reported in September 2026 to be negotiating an order for more than 200 additional 737 MAX aircraft; how that order is financed, and whether its backers commit fresh equity after a ₹2,716 crore FY26 loss, will show whether India keeps a credible third airline. On the Air India side, watch for Tata and Singapore Airlines capital infusions and for Singapore Airlines' half-yearly disclosure of its share of Air India's losses.

Macro context will matter as much as company news. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which lifts financing costs for aircraft purchases and may slow discretionary travel. The rupee was about ₹94.9 per dollar on 19 October after touching a record low near ₹96.8. And the West Asia situation, which since February 2026 has driven crude, freight and LPG shocks across Indian industry, remains the single largest swing factor for jet fuel and for Gulf traffic. A durable ceasefire and a fall in the jet crack spread would do more for Indian airline profits than anything the airlines themselves can do.
