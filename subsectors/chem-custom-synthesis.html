00

The one-page view: six companies that rent out difficult chemistry

The customer owns the molecule; the Indian house owns the process. Everything else in this report follows from that split.

A custom-synthesis company makes a chemical that somebody else has invented, to that somebody's specification, usually in the last few steps before it becomes a drug, a crop-protection active, a contrast agent for an MRI scan or an additive in a battery. The customer (Bayer, Syngenta, a Finnish generic-drug maker, GE HealthCare, Baker Hughes) decides what the molecule is and how much it wants. The supplier decides how to make it: which route, which reagents, which reactor, how to handle a fluorinating agent or an organolithium reagent without a fire. That know-how is what the six Indian companies in this report are selling.

The six are very different in size and shape. Jubilant Ingrevia is the largest by revenue (₹4,388 crore in FY26) and is really an integrated chemistry platform: alcohol-based acetyls at the bottom, pyridines and vitamins in the middle and a growing specialty and contract-manufacturing business on top. Anupam Rasayan (₹2,365 crore) is the classic contract house for agrochemical and specialty-chemical multinationals, now turning itself into a group through three acquisitions in 30 months. Acutaas Chemicals (₹1,339 crore, formerly Ami Organics) sells advanced pharmaceutical intermediates and has become a high-margin contract manufacturer for a European drug maker. Aether Industries (₹1,160 crore) is a young, research-led house with its own products, dedicated plants for single customers and a research-services line. Blue Jet Healthcare (₹947 crore) supplies intermediates for iodinated contrast media to three of the world's four largest makers. Neogen Chemicals (₹862 crore) is a bromine and organolithium specialist that is spending ₹1,795 crore to become India's battery-electrolyte and lithium-salt supplier.

Together they reported ₹11,061 crore of revenue in FY26, up 19% on FY25, with ₹2,369 crore of EBITDA (a 21.4% margin, or 27.0% if Jubilant Ingrevia's thin-margin acetyls business is excluded) and ₹1,352 crore of profit after tax. The stock market valued them at about ₹89,700 crore on 13 October 2026, or roughly 66 times combined trailing profit. That single number hides a range from 31 times (Jubilant Ingrevia) to 214 times (Neogen), and the spread is the most useful thing to understand about this group.

The five forces that shape this sub-sector

  • Contracts lock in the right to supply, not the timing of orders. A letter of intent (LOI) followed by audits and regulatory qualification makes a supplier hard to replace, but the customer still decides when to order. Anupam's ₹14,646 crore LOI table is about six times its revenue, yet the company sits on 490 days of inventory and earned only about ₹172 crore for its own shareholders in FY26.
  • Concentration is the other face of the moat. Blue Jet's pharmaceutical-intermediate segment went from ₹212 crore in one quarter to ₹2.4 crore three quarters later and back to ₹121 crore the quarter after, because one customer ran down stock of one cardiovascular intermediate. Aether's largest customer, Baker Hughes, was about a quarter of its revenue in Q4 FY26.
  • Chemistry depth earns the margin; working capital decides the return. The same contract can earn a 27% or a 13% pre-tax return on capital depending on whether the supplier holds two months or a year of inventory.
  • Capex runs two to four years ahead of revenue. Plants are built before molecules arrive. The stock multiples track unfinished capital work: Neogen's capital work in progress is 1.8 times its finished plant and it trades at 214 times earnings.
  • The agrochemical bust is over, the recovery is not. Global crop-protection destocking in 2023–25 cut the group's profit by a third from FY22 to FY24. PI Industries, the sector's agro bellwether, still reported revenue down 16% in FY26. Growth since then has come from new molecules, new plants and non-agro customers rather than a broad agro upswing.
In plain terms

These companies are chemical workshops for hire. Once a big customer has audited a workshop and written its name into a regulatory file, it rarely switches, which makes the business sticky. But the customer orders when it wants to, so revenue jumps around; the workshop has to build its next plant years before it knows how full it will be; and the investors buying the shares today are mostly paying for plants and contracts that have not yet produced profit.

Section 10 covers the cycle and the history, section 11 the regulation that creates the switching costs, and section 12 is the KPI masterclass: every number an analyst should pull from a quarterly result, with benchmarks. Sections 13 to 15 compare the six companies. Sections 16 and 17 are deep dives on battery chemicals and on valuation. PI Industries belongs to the agrochemical sub-sector but is used throughout as the reference point for agro custom synthesis.

01

How chemistry for hire earns: the customer owns the "what", the supplier owns the "how"

Value climbs with every step from commodity feedstock to the regulated final molecule. Indian houses make their money one or two steps from the end.

The vocabulary in this industry is a thicket of acronyms, but the underlying business has only a few moving parts. A chemical that ends up in a tablet, a pesticide or an electrolyte is built in steps. A key starting material (KSM), often a cheap commodity organic bought from China, is converted through a series of reactions into an intermediate, then an advanced intermediate, then the active ingredient (the API in a drug, the AI in a pesticide), which is finally formulated into the product a patient or a farmer buys. Each step adds value per kilogram, and each step narrows the number of companies in the world that can do it reliably.

Indian custom-synthesis houses mostly sell the advanced intermediate or the active step itself, what the trade calls "API-minus-one". They earn their margin from process chemistry: designing a route that is cheaper, safer, cleaner or higher-yielding than the customer could manage, and then running it at scale under audit. The difficult chemistries carry the best margins: fluorination with hydrogen fluoride, bromination, organolithium and Grignard reactions that are pyrophoric, nitration, high-pressure hydrogenation, reactions at minus 70 degrees, and continuous-flow processes that keep hazardous intermediates in tiny volumes.

The business models in one paragraph each

Custom synthesis or custom manufacturing (CSM) means making a molecule to one customer's specification, usually its patented active or an advanced intermediate of it. Anupam is the purest Indian example and PI Industries the largest. CRAMS (contract research and manufacturing services) is process research plus manufacture on contract; Aether reports its research-services income separately, at roughly 9–10% of FY26 revenue. CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organisation) means taking a molecule from development through scale-up to commercial supply for an innovator under a multi-year agreement: Acutaas for Fermion, Jubilant Ingrevia's new agro CDMO plant, Blue Jet's pharma intermediates. CEM (contract or exclusive manufacturing, Aether's term) is dedicated capacity for one customer's molecule, often run on an "open cost" basis where the customer sees the cost sheet and pays a margin on top.

Alongside the contract lines, most of these companies sell catalogue products: intermediates they developed themselves and sell to many customers, such as Acutaas's intermediates for generic drugs or Aether's large-scale manufacturing (LSM) products. Catalogue lines carry more price risk, because there is no contract and Chinese competitors can undercut, but they also spread the customer base.

Where the value is captured

Margins concentrate where few suppliers worldwide can run the chemistry and the molecule is written into a regulated customer file. The best evidence is inside the companies themselves, where the same balance sheet runs businesses at very different rungs of the ladder.

The commodity tail of each company (Jubilant Ingrevia's acetyls, Acutaas's "commodity specialty" products, Blue Jet's high-intensity sweeteners) earns low and volatile margins. The contract and advanced-intermediate lines earn 20–40%. A large part of analysing any of these companies is working out how much of the revenue sits on which rung and whether the mix is moving up.

In plain terms

A kilogram of a simple solvent might sell for a few hundred rupees; a kilogram of the last intermediate before a patented pesticide can sell for many thousands, because only a handful of plants in the world are approved to make it. Indian custom-synthesis houses try to live as close to that last step as possible, where the customer's regulator has written their plant into the approval and switching is slow and expensive.

Three ideas a first-time reader should hold

First, a contract sets price and the right to supply, not timing. Anupam's revenue fell to ₹1,437 crore in FY25 with every LOI intact, then rose 65% in FY26. The contracts did not change; the customers' ordering did. Second, concentration is the moat's flip side. The qualification process that keeps competitors out also means a few molecules and a few customers carry each company, so one customer's inventory decision can move a whole year. Third, capex leads revenue by two to four years. Aether's FY26 return on capital employed was 11.9% and Neogen's 6.5% while capital work in progress sat at roughly a quarter and two-fifths of their capital employed respectively. Returns follow a J-curve: they fall while plants are built and filled, then rise steeply if the molecules arrive.

These three ideas explain most of what looks odd in the numbers: strong margins with weak returns (Aether, Neogen), huge contract books with modest profit (Anupam), and a high-return company that still lost a third of a segment in a quarter (Blue Jet). They also explain why reading this sub-sector by the quarter is dangerous. A quarter tells you about a customer's warehouse; a year or two tells you about the business.

02

Size and the six-company record: a ₹11,000 crore group growing at 19%

Global market sizes are loose; the companies' own numbers are not. Strip out Jubilant Ingrevia and the group has nearly doubled in four years.

There is no reliable single number for the "custom synthesis market". The broadest estimates (ResearchAndMarkets, 2023) put global custom synthesis and manufacturing, including pharmaceuticals, at about US$271 billion in 2022 growing to about US$475 billion by 2028, roughly 10% a year. That definition is so wide it is only useful as an order of magnitude. Narrower slices are more meaningful. BCG estimated in 2022 that the outsourced agrochemical CDMO market focused on value-adding steps (actives and advanced intermediates) was about US$6 billion, that a full third of agrochemical manufacturing is outsourced, and that Asian CDMOs' share of the outsourced pool had risen to about 30%. The pharmaceutical CRDMO market is far larger, US$180–200 billion, where India holds only 2–3% against China's 18–21% (ICRA, February 2026). The contrast-media intermediates market that Blue Jet serves was about US$1.07 billion in 2024, forecast to reach about US$1.56 billion by 2029 (Frost & Sullivan, cited by Blue Jet).

Indian numbers are no better. India's chemical sector is about US$278 billion (FY24, IBEF); specialty chemicals are estimated anywhere from US$35 billion to US$67 billion depending on the definition. India exported US$20.55 billion of chemicals in FY26 and imported US$28.48 billion. No public source splits out Indian custom-synthesis exports, and no credible current estimate of India's share of agro or pharma custom synthesis exists. BCG's 2022 view was that an established Indian agro CDMO "has room to grow to about US$125m per year in sales" from a single customer relationship, which is a reminder that individual relationships, not market shares, are the unit of analysis.

In plain terms

Nobody can tell you precisely how big this market is, because every molecule is its own small market. What matters is that the customers (global agrochemical, pharmaceutical, imaging and materials companies) outsource a large and slowly rising share of their manufacturing, and Indian houses are taking share from European and Chinese suppliers one molecule at a time.

The six-company record, FY22 to FY26

The companies' own numbers tell a clearer story. Combined revenue went from ₹8,303 crore in FY22 to ₹11,061 crore in FY26, a compound annual growth rate of 7.4%. That modest figure is dragged down by Jubilant Ingrevia, whose revenue fell from ₹4,949 crore to ₹4,388 crore over the period as acetyl prices normalised after the 2021–22 spike. Excluding it, the other five grew from ₹3,354 crore to ₹6,673 crore, an 18.8% compound rate: the business nearly doubled in four years.

Two cautions on these figures. First, the group's growth in FY26 and Q1 FY27 includes acquisitions: Anupam consolidated Tanfac's growth and, from late February 2026, the US company Jayhawk Fine Chemicals, which added roughly ₹140–150 crore in Q1 FY27 alone. The organic growth is still strong, led by Acutaas's contract ramp and Aether's dedicated plants, but it is narrower than the headline.

In plain terms

The group is roughly the size of one mid-sized European contract manufacturer and has been growing about 19% a year outside Jubilant Ingrevia. The FY24 dip shows the business is not immune to cycles; the FY26 rebound shows that new contracts and plants, not just a better market, can pull it out.

03

Four buyers, four clocks: who orders these molecules and why

Agrochemical innovators swing hardest, pharma is steadier, imaging grows like a utility, and materials and batteries are the new frontier.

1. Agrochemical innovators: the biggest swing

Global crop-protection companies (Bayer, Syngenta, BASF, Corteva, FMC, Sumitomo) are the largest single source of demand for Anupam, Jubilant Ingrevia's specialty business and part of Aether. They outsource advanced intermediates and actives for patented molecules, and they order through distributors and retailers whose inventories swing with weather, crop prices and interest rates. When the channel is overstocked, orders to suppliers fall much further than actual spraying on farms. That is what happened in 2023–25: after the post-COVID scramble for supply in 2021–22, distributors held too much stock just as Chinese generic pesticide prices collapsed, and innovators cut orders to their contract manufacturers for the best part of two years.

By early 2026 the trade press was declaring the worst of the destock over, but the recovery is uneven and price deflation continues. Corteva's crop-protection volumes rose 2% in the first half of 2026 while prices fell; Syngenta's crop-protection sales rose 4% in reported dollars but fell 1% at constant exchange rates; FMC's first-quarter revenue fell 4%. China's pesticide price index fell another 4.4% month on month in July 2026, and Chinese pesticide output was up 23% year on year early in the year, which keeps generic prices low. Most tellingly, PI Industries, India's largest agro custom-synthesis house, reported FY26 revenue of ₹6,714 crore, down 16%.

So where did Anupam's 65% FY26 growth come from? Mostly not from a booming agro market. Tanfac, its fluorine subsidiary, added ₹154 crore, Jayhawk added roughly one month of revenue, and the rest came from new molecules under LOIs ramping up. Organic agro growth was not separately disclosed. The lesson for the whole group is that growth since FY25 has come from new contracts, new plants and non-agro customers. A genuine agro upswing, if it comes, would be on top.

2. Pharmaceuticals: steadier, but molecule-specific

Pharma demand is driven by patient consumption, which does not destock the way a pesticide channel does. Acutaas's intermediates are about 90% for chronic therapies (antidepressants, anticoagulants, anti-diabetics), where prescriptions grow every year. Its advanced-intermediate segment grew 77% year on year in Q1 FY27 as its contract with Fermion, the API arm of Finland's Orion group, ramped up. Pharma contract manufacturing more broadly is in an upswing: Divi's custom-synthesis revenue grew 45% in Q1 FY27.

But pharma can still destock at the level of one molecule. Blue Jet's pharma-intermediate segment (a cardiovascular intermediate for one customer) fell from ₹212 crore in Q1 FY26 to ₹2.4 crore in Q4 FY26 as the customer used up stock, then recovered to ₹121 crore in Q1 FY27. End-demand for the drug did not collapse; the customer's warehouse did. Section 8 treats this as the case study in concentration.

3. Medical imaging: the utility-like clock

Iodinated contrast media are injected for CT scans and X-ray angiography, and demand follows the number of scans, which rises with ageing populations and wider access to imaging. Frost & Sullivan figures cited by Blue Jet put the global contrast-media formulations market at US$6.7 billion in 2024, growing to US$9.5 billion by 2029 (7.2% a year, after 6.8% in 2019–24). Iodinated agents are about three-quarters of the market. The intermediates market that Blue Jet supplies was about US$1.07 billion in 2024, forecast to grow 7.8% a year.

5. Batteries: the newest and least proven buyer

Battery electrolytes need a lithium salt (usually lithium hexafluorophosphate, LiPF₆) dissolved in solvents, plus additives such as vinylene carbonate (VC) and fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC). Neogen is building 32,000 tonnes of electrolyte and 5,500 tonnes of salts and additives; Acutaas started commercial supply of VC and FEC in Q1 FY27; Anupam has a small battery-chemical contract and a non-binding LOI with the Spanish battery developer Basquevolt. The demand case rests on India's cell factories, and those are behind schedule: about 1 GWh had been commissioned by March 2026 against 40 GWh awarded under the production-linked incentive scheme. Early volumes therefore depend on exports. Section 16 covers this in depth.

In plain terms

A pesticide supplier's orders depend on how full distributors' warehouses are; a contrast-media supplier's depend on how many CT scans hospitals do; a battery-chemical supplier's depend on whether cell factories get built. The six companies have very different mixes of these clocks, which is why they rarely move together and why "the sector" is a weak guide to any one of them.

04

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: half to raw materials, a fifth to operating profit

The cost structure is simple; the spread between the best and worst company is not. Blue Jet keeps ₹26 of every ₹100 as profit, Neogen ₹3.

Put the six companies' FY26 accounts together and a typical ₹100 of revenue splits roughly as follows. About ₹48 goes on raw materials: the starting materials, solvents, reagents and catalysts. About ₹8 goes on employees, which is low for a research-heavy business because Indian scientists and plant operators cost a fraction of their European equivalents. About ₹22–23 goes on power, fuel, freight, effluent treatment, maintenance and other overheads. What remains, ₹21.4, is EBITDA. Add ₹1.6 of other income (mostly treasury income at the cash-rich companies), subtract ₹4.2 of depreciation and ₹2.7 of interest, and pre-tax profit is about ₹16. Tax and the minority share take ₹3.8, leaving ₹12.2 of profit after tax.

The inputs that matter

Each company has one or two inputs that drive its costs and, often, its competitive position. Halogens are central: Neogen is built on bromine (India's Archean Chemical is a large domestic producer) and lithium; Anupam bought Tanfac partly to secure hydrogen fluoride and potassium fluoride for fluorination, and Tanfac now makes 29,700 tonnes of HF and 1,00,000 tonnes of sulphuric acid a year. Iodine, from Chile and Japan, is the key input for Blue Jet's contrast-media intermediates. Lithium carbonate is imported by Neogen, which describes itself as India's largest importer. Molasses alcohol is the start of Jubilant Ingrevia's whole chain (alcohol to acetaldehyde to acetic acid and on to pyridines); it competes with ethanol blending for the same sugar-mill output.

Behind all of these sits China. India imported about US$11.1 billion of organic chemicals from China in 2024, and many key starting materials for both pharma and agro molecules come from Chinese plants. When Chinese prices fall, Indian input costs fall too, but so do the prices of competing Chinese intermediates. When Chinese supply is disrupted (as during the 2017–19 environmental crackdown or COVID lockdowns), Indian houses win orders but pay more for inputs.

Pass-through: who carries the raw-material risk

Contract business usually passes raw-material costs through. Long-term agreements commonly reset prices quarterly or half-yearly against an input index, and Aether's dedicated (CEM) plants run on an "open cost P&L" in which the customer sees the cost sheet and pays an agreed margin. Catalogue products do not have that protection. On Aether's Q4 FY26 call, an analyst estimated that prices in its large-scale manufacturing line had fallen 27–30%; management attributed part of it to logistics timing. Freight became a cost issue again in Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27 as Middle East disruption pushed up shipping rates, flagged by Aether, Neogen and Blue Jet.

05

Fragmented by molecule: many small monopolies, one large rival in China

No company dominates the sub-sector, but each dominates a few molecules. The structure is being reshaped by acquisitions, private equity and promoter sell-downs.

The industry is fragmented in a particular way. There is no "market share" in custom synthesis as a whole, because each molecule is its own market with two or three qualified suppliers worldwide. Within those micro-markets, shares can be very high. Acutaas is said by brokers to supply 80–90% of the world's trazodone intermediate (an antidepressant), though this is unverified. Blue Jet says it accounted for more than 75% of India's exports of a selected contrast-media intermediate in FY20–22. Jubilant Ingrevia is one of the largest pyridine and beta-picoline producers outside China and among the top three in niacinamide (vitamin B3). Concentration is therefore high by molecule and low by company.

The wider Indian field

Beyond the six NSE 750 members in this report, the listed Indian custom-synthesis cohort includes PI Industries (agro CSM, ₹6,714 crore of FY26 revenue), Navin Fluorine (CDMO plus fluorospecialties), SRF (fluorospecialty chemicals for agro innovators), Aarti Industries (pharma and agro intermediates), Divi's (pharma custom synthesis, ₹10,560 crore), Neuland (custom manufacturing for pharma) and Hikal (agro and pharma CSM). Process-led specialists such as Vinati and Clean Science compete for capital and talent but sell their own products. Several of these sit in sibling reports, notably fluorochemicals and pharma CDMO/CMO.

Important unlisted players include Gharda Chemicals (agro), Sajjan India (which has done innovator agro work) and the Indian arms of global groups. In Europe, Saltigo (part of Lanxess) and Siegfried compete for the same innovator work, with higher costs but longer track records. Japanese and Korean companies appear mainly as customers and technology partners: Morita (Japan) holds up to 20% of Neogen's LiPF₆ joint venture, and MUIS (a Mitsubishi Chemical–UBE joint venture) licenses electrolyte technology to Neogen.

The sub-sector has started to consolidate, and the most aggressive consolidator is Anupam Rasayan. Promoters selling, institutions buying

The other structural shift is in ownership. Over the past two years promoters have sold down at four of the six: Jubilant Ingrevia's promoters from 51.47% to 45.22% (in the June 2025 quarter); Aether's from 81.78% to 74.92% by offer for sale, to meet minimum public shareholding rules; Blue Jet's from 86.00% to 73.15% through sales and a ₹800 crore QIP; Acutaas's from 35.98% to 32.66%. A. The LOI-led contract house

Acutaas and Blue Jet both started by selling intermediates where they hold a large share of world supply, and both added a contract-manufacturing line that, when running, lifts margins sharply. C. The R&D-led multi-model house

Aether runs three businesses on one research base. Its own large-scale products are sold to many customers at market prices. But in FY26 the stack produced about ₹400 crore of sales, a small fraction of what the letters add up to, because most of the molecules are still being scaled up by the customers.

Worked example: what a ₹3,000 crore, five-year LOI is really worth

Take the largest row in Anupam's table, the ₹3,000 crore LOI with a Japanese multinational, due to commercialise in FY27. Read naively, it is ₹600 crore a year for five years, or 25% of Anupam's FY26 revenue. Now apply Jubilant Ingrevia's disclosed ramp curve for new CDMO projects (20–25% of peak in year one, 50–60% in year two, over 80% in year three). The ILLUSTRATIVE path is about ₹135 crore in year one, ₹330 crore in year two, ₹480 crore in year three, and ₹600 crore in years four and five. Five-year revenue is about ₹2,100–2,300 crore, not ₹3,000 crore. Unless the tenor is extended, the headline value overstates what the supplier will bill in the contract period by roughly 25–30%.

What a contract protects, and what it does not

A custom-synthesis contract does three valuable things. It sets a price framework, usually with raw-material pass-through, so the supplier is not exposed to input swings (Aether's CEM plants run on an open-cost basis). It grants the right to supply, which, once the supplier is written into the customer's regulatory file, is expensive for the customer to take away. And it creates qualification lock-in: when Blue Jet's pharma customer resumed ordering in Q1 FY27, it came back to Blue Jet for the same molecule, not to a competitor.

It does not protect three things. It does not protect timing: Anupam's FY25 revenue fell with every LOI intact, and Blue Jet's pharma segment almost vanished for a quarter. It does not protect volume if the customer's own product loses share. And it does not protect against generic erosion when the customer's patent expires; SRF noted in Q1 FY27 that generic pressure on innovator agrochemical players continues, and an innovator under price pressure squeezes its suppliers.

How much each company tells you

Disclosure quality varies widely, and an investor should weight forecasts accordingly. Ranked from most to least: Anupam publishes a full LOI table; Acutaas gives a rupee target for CDMO revenue (₹1,000 crore by FY28, "expected to beat") and per-molecule peaks (four new validated molecules from H2 FY27, each ₹50–100 crore at peak); Jubilant Ingrevia gives the value of its agro CDMO contract (about US$300 million, roughly ₹2,640 crore at ~₹88/$) and its ramp curve; Aether names customers on calls but gives no contract values; Neogen gives approvals and capacity; Blue Jet names no customers in its presentation and says only that about 70% of sales are backed by contracted volumes. More disclosure is not the same as more certainty. Anupam's table is also where the gap between promise and delivery is most visible.

In plain terms

A good way to value a contract book is to ask three questions: how much will actually be billed within the stated term, how much capital (including inventory) is needed to deliver it, and what happens if the customer pauses for a year. The headline LOI number answers none of them.

One molecule, one customer: concentration as moat and trap Deep dive

The qualification that keeps rivals out also ties each company to a few buyers. Blue Jet's FY26 shows the risk in its purest form.

Every company in this group can produce a long list of customers and products: Blue Jet has 511 commercial products, Neogen 258, Anupam 185-plus clients. Those lists are reassuring and mostly irrelevant. In custom synthesis, concentration is measured by molecule, not by company count. A supplier can have hundreds of customers and still depend on one molecule for a third of its profit, because contract molecules are large and catalogue products are small. The right question is not "how many customers?" but "how much of EBITDA would disappear if the largest molecule paused for two quarters?"

The case study: Blue Jet's cardiovascular intermediate

Blue Jet's history is the cleanest illustration. In FY24 it was mainly a contrast-media company: that segment was ₹480 crore of ₹712 crore of revenue, about two-thirds. Then a pharma-intermediate contract for one customer's cardiovascular molecule took off. In FY25 the pharma segment reached ₹458 crore, 44% of revenue, larger than contrast media, and EBITDA margin rose to 37%. In FY26 the customer ran down its inventory. Pharma revenue went from ₹212 crore in Q1 FY26 to a combined ₹84 crore or so in Q2 and Q3, then to ₹2.4 crore in Q4. In Q1 FY27 the customer was back, and the segment billed ₹121 crore.

The danger signal: a segment whose share jumps

The most useful early-warning sign is not a high concentration number but a fast-rising one. Blue Jet's pharma segment went from minor in FY24 to the largest segment in FY25 and then collapsed. A segment that doubles its share of revenue in a year is usually one customer's molecule ramping, and the same customer can slow it down just as quickly. In this group, the numbers to track in the same way are Aether's Baker Hughes share and dedicated-plant revenue, Acutaas's CDMO share (the Fermion contract), and, from FY27, Jubilant Ingrevia's agro CDMO line.

Does the market price concentration? Not consistently. It paid about 42 times earnings for Blue Jet (highly concentrated, but cash-rich and with high returns) and about 95 times for Aether (concentration rising, heavy capex). In plain terms

When a company's fastest-growing business is one customer's product, celebrate the growth and then ask what happens if that customer stops ordering for two quarters. The answer is in Blue Jet's FY26 accounts: revenue down 8%, profit down 19%, and a full recovery only when the customer came back.

The companies are trying to dilute their concentration, and their routes show what diversification looks like in this business. Blue Jet is spending its ₹800 crore QIP on a ₹1,000 crore first phase at Vizag and opened a Hyderabad research centre in August 2026 for peptides, GLP-1 intermediates, biocatalysis and flow chemistry. Aether added 19 new marquee clients in FY26 and passed more than 50 customer audits. Acutaas has four new validated CDMO molecules arriving from H2 FY27. Anupam has bought diversification outright. Each route takes years, and in the meantime the largest molecule still drives the quarter.

Working capital and the J-curve: why margins do not equal returns Deep dive

The same contract can earn 27% or 13% on capital depending on inventory. Plants built ahead of molecules depress returns for years.

The six companies' EBITDA margins range from 13% to 36%. Their returns on capital employed range from 6.5% to 31.6%. The two rankings are not the same, and the difference is almost entirely explained by two kinds of capital that earn nothing: inventory and receivables that sit in the business for months, and plants that have been paid for but are not yet producing. This section takes each in turn.

The cash conversion cycle

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) is the number of days between paying for raw materials and collecting cash from the customer: debtor days plus inventory days minus payable days. In this group it ranges from 55 days at Jubilant Ingrevia to 377 days at Anupam. A longer cycle means more of the company's capital is tied up in stock and receivables at any time, so more equity or debt is needed to support the same revenue.

Two companies win identical contracts and make identical margins. One ships its product within two months of buying the raw materials; the other holds a year's worth of stock. There is a nuance. Not all inventory is waste. Some customers pay for safety stock, some of Anupam's inventory supports molecules that are about to ramp, and an acquisition (Jayhawk) adds a full balance sheet before a full year of revenue. The improvement from 715 days in FY25 to 377 days in FY26 shows that the number can move quickly once revenue catches up. The test is direction: a cycle that falls as revenue grows is a business finding its rhythm; one that stays high while revenue grows is one that is funding its customers.

The capex J-curve

The second source of idle capital is plant under construction. In this business a supplier often builds or allocates a dedicated block before the customer's molecule ramps, and certainly before it reaches peak volumes. During construction, the money sits in capital work in progress (CWIP), earning nothing. After commissioning, the plant runs below capacity for two or three years while fixed costs are fully in place. Return on capital therefore falls during the build and recovers only as the plant fills: the J-curve.

The six companies have very different ages. Blue Jet was incorporated in 1968 as India's first saccharin maker; Jubilant Ingrevia's Gajraula complex dates from 1978; Neogen's founder started making lithium bromide in the mid-1980s; the Desai family's chemistry business behind Anupam began in Surat in the same decade; Ami Organics (now Acutaas) was founded in 2004 and Aether in 2013. The boom: China's retreat and COVID's scramble

Between 2016 and 2019 Chinese authorities closed or relocated thousands of chemical plants for environmental and safety reasons. Global agrochemical and pharma companies that had relied on a single Chinese supplier for an intermediate suddenly found themselves without one, and began qualifying second sources. Indian houses with process chemistry and clean regulatory records were the obvious candidates. COVID then added a scramble for supply: in 2021–22 customers ordered more than they needed, worried about lockdowns and shipping. Margins hit records (Blue Jet's operating margin reached 36% in FY22; Jubilant Ingrevia earned ₹477 crore) and the listing wave followed.

The bust: destocking meets Chinese deflation

From 2023 the over-ordering reversed. Distributors and innovators ran down the stock they had built, and Chinese plants that had reopened flooded the market with generic pesticide actives at falling prices. Indian suppliers were hit twice: lower volumes and pressure on the prices of their catalogue products. The damage varied with exposure.

Regulation: the customer's file is the moat, the factory licence is the risk

Most of the regulation that matters is written by the customer's regulator, not India's. It protects incumbents and punishes accidents.

Custom-synthesis companies are lightly regulated as businesses (no price controls, no licensing of who may sell what to whom) and heavily regulated as factories. The most important rules, however, are not about the supplier at all. They are about the customer's product. A drug master file (DMF) filed with the US FDA or a European authority describes how an active ingredient is made, including which supplier makes which step. An agrochemical registration with the US EPA, the EU or India's CIB&RC describes the manufacturing process and the source of the active. Change the supplier and the customer may have to file a variation, run new stability and impurity studies, and wait for approval. That is the switching cost that turns a qualified supplier into a near-permanent one.

Factory regulation: the real operating risk

The chemistries that earn the best margins are also the most dangerous. Fluorination uses hydrogen fluoride, which is acutely toxic; organolithium reagents ignite in air; bromination and nitration can run away thermally. Indian state pollution boards and the environment ministry control every new plant through consents to establish and operate, and a serious incident can bring closure orders, slower approvals for the whole industrial estate, and customer audits. Neogen's Dahej fire in March 2025 is the group's recent example: an exceptional loss of about ₹14 crore, an insurance claim of which ₹164 crore has been recovered and ₹186 crore was still receivable, and a plant out of action for more than a year, restarting only in Q2 FY27. The direct cost was manageable; the lost production and delayed customer qualifications were not.

In plain terms

The rules that protect these companies are written by the FDA, the EPA and their equivalents for the customer's product; once a supplier's plant is named in those approvals, replacing it is slow and expensive. The rules that threaten them are the ones that govern what happens inside the factory gate, because a fire or a leak can shut a plant for a year.

Policy: incentives arrive slowly

India has no production-linked incentive for custom synthesis as such. The policy most relevant to this group is the battery cell PLI, and its slow progress shows the limits of policy-led demand: of 40 GWh awarded, only about 1 GWh had been commissioned by March 2026, and a 10 GWh rebid closed on the date of this report, with technical bids opening on 14 October 2026. Abroad, rules that discourage dependence on Chinese suppliers (for US battery-tax credits, EU battery supply chains and possibly US pharma procurement) could help Indian suppliers, but their implementation is uncertain and changes with politics. We treat them as possible tailwinds, not as part of the base case.

Ownership rules and the sell-downs

One listing rule has shaped the stocks directly. Promoters of a listed Indian company must bring their holding down to 75% within a set period, which forced Aether's offer for sale in 2025 and part of Blue Jet's sell-down. Other promoters sold voluntarily. Investors should expect further supply where holdings remain high and should read promoter selling in that light: some of it is compliance, not a signal.

Reading the numbers: the KPI masterclass for custom synthesis Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, how to compute them, what good looks like, how the six compare, and the traps in each definition.

A custom-synthesis company's results can be read in ten minutes once you know which numbers matter and in what order. This section builds that toolkit. It starts with the equation that links reactors to revenue to returns, works through each native KPI with a formula, a worked example, the benchmarks and the six companies' values, then assembles the anatomy of one typical plant, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps, and a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

The equation: from reactor to return

Every rupee of revenue in this business passes through a reactor. The company decides how many kilolitres of reactor it builds and how well it runs them; the customer decides how much it orders and when; the market (or the contract) sets the price per kilogram. Profit then depends on how much of the price is kept after materials and conversion costs, and the return depends on how much capital, fixed and working, was needed to earn it.

Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE, round numbers). A company builds a 400 KL dedicated block for ₹400 crore (₹1 crore per KL). At full use, with its contracted molecule, each KL can generate ₹1.5 crore of revenue a year, so the block's full potential is ₹600 crore. In year three it runs at 80%, so revenue is ₹480 crore. Gross margin is 52% (₹250 crore); conversion costs (staff, power, effluent, maintenance, freight) take ₹140 crore, leaving EBITDA of ₹110 crore, a 23% margin. Depreciation is ₹20 crore, so EBIT is ₹90 crore. Capital employed is the ₹400 crore block plus about ₹197 crore of working capital (a 150-day cash cycle on ₹480 crore of revenue), or ₹597 crore. Pre-tax ROCE is 90 ÷ 597 ≈ 15%. Lift utilisation to 100% and margin to 25%, and ROCE reaches 20%; cut the cash cycle to 55 days at the same time, and it reaches about 27%. Every KPI below is one term of this equation.

In plain terms

A custom-synthesis plant earns money in three steps: fill the reactors (utilisation), sell what comes out at a good price per kilogram (realisation and mix), and do it without tying up too much money in stock and unfinished buildings (capital). Management has a strong grip on the first and third steps only once customers order; the second depends on the molecule.

1. Contract book and LOI cover

What it is. The disclosed value of letters of intent, long-term agreements or CDMO contracts, compared with annual revenue; or, where no value is given, the share of sales made under contract. Formula. Contract cover = disclosed book ÷ last-year revenue; or contracted sales ÷ total sales. Worked example. Anupam's ₹14,646 crore book on ₹2,384 crore of FY26 revenue is 6.1 times; Jubilant Ingrevia's US$300 million contract (about ₹2,640 crore at ~₹88/$) on ₹4,388 crore is 0.6 times; Acutaas's ₹1,000 crore FY28 CDMO target is 0.75 times its FY26 revenue. Blue Jet says about 70% of sales are backed by contracted volumes; Aether's CEM plus CRAMS were 55–60% of revenue.

What drives it. New LOIs signed, the length of each LOI (a ten-year LOI looks twice as large as a five-year one for the same annual volume), and exchange rates for dollar contracts. It falls as contracts are delivered, unless replaced. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Good: a book above three times revenue, or more than 60% of sales contracted. Average: one to three times, or 30–60%. Weak: below one time, or under 30%.

The definition trap. An LOI is usually non-binding and states intent over a tenor, not annual orders, and the ramp means the stated value is rarely billed within the stated term (Section 7's worked example: about 70–75% of headline). Compare the book with what it delivered: Anupam's ₹14,646 crore produced about ₹400 crore of revenue in FY26. A better KPI is "contract revenue recognised this year ÷ book value", which few companies disclose.

2. Customer and molecule concentration

What it is. The share of revenue (or, better, EBITDA) from the largest customer, the top ten customers, or the largest molecule. Formula. Largest customer revenue ÷ total revenue; top-ten revenue ÷ total. Worked example. Aether's Baker Hughes revenue of about ₹76 crore in Q4 FY26, divided by quarterly revenue of roughly ₹300 crore, is about 25%. Blue Jet's top ten customers were 84% of FY23 revenue.

What drives it. A new contract molecule ramping pushes concentration up; new customers and acquisitions push it down. A customer's destock pushes it down temporarily (Blue Jet's pharma segment in Q4 FY26), which can make concentration look better precisely when the risk has just materialised. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Largest customer: good below 15%, average 15–30%, weak above 30%. Top ten: good below 50%, average 50–75%, weak above 75%. How the six compare. Five of the six do not disclose a top-customer share. Blue Jet's 84% top-ten (FY23) is weak by this yardstick; Aether's ~25% largest customer is average and rising; Acutaas's statement that no product exceeds 10–15% of sales is the only explicit product cap.

The definition trap. Customer counts flatter. "185+ clients" or "511 products" says nothing about how much of profit sits with the top molecule. Revenue concentration also understates profit concentration, because contract molecules usually carry higher margins than the catalogue tail.

3. Commercial products and the molecule pipeline

What it is. The number of molecules in commercial supply, and the number in development or validation. Formula. A count; more useful as "new commercial molecules per year" and "pipeline ÷ commercial". Worked example. Acutaas expects four new validated CDMO molecules from H2 FY27, each worth ₹50–100 crore at peak; if all four reach the midpoint, that is ₹300 crore of revenue, 22% of FY26 sales.

What drives it. Research headcount, the number of customer audits passed, and the rate at which CRAMS projects convert to commercial supply. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Good above 300 commercial products, average 100–300, weak below 100, for a broad-based house; a contract specialist can be excellent with a few dozen. How the six compare. Blue Jet 511 (of which 19 contrast intermediates), Acutaas 450-plus, Neogen 258, Anupam 125-plus with 90-plus in the pipeline, Aether about 30-plus large-scale products. 4. Reactor capacity and utilisation

What it is. Installed reactor volume (in kilolitres) and the share of it in productive use. Formula. Utilisation = reactor-hours used ÷ reactor-hours available, as reported by management; or, as a proxy, revenue ÷ revenue at full capacity. Worked example. Acutaas's rebuilt 442 KL Ankleshwar plant ran at 23% in Q1 FY27. If it reached the 83% of the older Sachin site, and each KL earned the same revenue, Ankleshwar's contribution would rise about 3.6 times without new capex.

What drives it. Customer orders and campaign scheduling (customer-driven); how quickly validation batches convert to commercial campaigns; downtime for changeovers and maintenance. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Good above 75%, average 50–75%, weak below 50%. Multipurpose plants rarely exceed 80–85% because of changeovers. How the six compare. Jubilant Ingrevia's pyridine plant over 90%; Acutaas Sachin 83%, Jhagadia 55%, Ankleshwar 23%; Aether Site 2 75%, Site 3 70%, Site 4 55%; Neogen's new salts and Aether's Site 5 are just starting. Anupam, Blue Jet and Neogen's core do not report utilisation.

The definition trap. Utilisation by volume and by value can differ sharply: a plant full of low-value campaigns is "busy" but poorly used. Units differ too (Anupam reports tonnes per year, others kilolitres), and a company may quote utilisation only for the sites where it looks good.

In plain terms

An empty reactor is the most expensive thing in this industry: the depreciation, staff and power arrive whether or not the customer orders. Utilisation is the single best explanation for why two plants with the same chemistry earn very different returns, and the disclosed numbers show plenty of empty reactors across the group today.

5. Revenue per kilolitre

What it is. How much revenue each kilolitre of reactor volume produces in a year. Formula. Annual revenue ÷ installed reactor KL. Worked example. Neogen's FY26 revenue of ₹862 crore on 477 KL (438 organic plus 39 inorganic) is ₹1.81 crore per KL. Blue Jet's ₹947 crore on 1,175.6 KL is ₹0.81 crore per KL.

What drives it. The value of the molecule per kilogram, the number of batches a year (shorter cycle times mean more batches), yield per batch and utilisation. Small-batch, high-value organolithium chemistry (Neogen) earns more per KL than large-volume contrast intermediates (Blue Jet), even though Blue Jet's margins are higher. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Good above ₹1.5 crore per KL, average ₹0.8–1.5 crore, weak below ₹0.8 crore, but only within the same type of chemistry.

8. Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after raw materials. Formula. (Revenue − cost of materials consumed ± change in inventories) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Blue Jet's Q1 FY27: revenue ₹293.1 crore, materials ₹137.1 crore, gross margin 53.2%. Its Q4 FY26 gross margin was 56.4% and Q1 FY26's 48.4%: the swing reflects the mix between contrast media and pharma intermediates as much as any price change.

What drives it. Product mix (contract and advanced molecules carry higher gross margins than catalogue or commodity products), process yield and route improvements, raw-material prices (passed through on contracts with a lag, not on catalogue lines), and currency. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Good above 55%, average 45–55%, weak below 45%. How the six compare. Acutaas 57.7% (FY26; 62.0% in Q4 FY26), Blue Jet 53.2% and Aether 49.8% (Q1 FY27), Jubilant Ingrevia 52.0% (FY26), Neogen 46.8% (Q1 FY27); Anupam does not disclose a comparable figure (see the chart in Section 4).

The definition trap. Gross margin moves when raw-material prices move even if the absolute rupee margin per kilogram is unchanged, because pass-through contracts add materials to both revenue and cost. A falling gross margin percentage during a raw-material spike can be harmless. And do not compute gross margin as 100 minus screener's "material cost %", which uses a different classification; use the company's own figures.

10. Cash conversion cycle, inventory and debtor days

What it is. The days of revenue (or cost) tied up in the operating cycle. Formula. What drives it. Campaign manufacturing and multi-step processes (inventory), customer payment terms and the share of exports (debtors), supplier credit (payables), acquisitions, and stock built ahead of new molecules. In plain terms

ROCE answers the question an owner cares about most: for every ₹100 put into the business, how much operating profit comes back each year? Acutaas returns about ₹30; Neogen about ₹6. Most of the gap is not chemistry but capital: unfinished plants and slow-moving stock.

12. Free cash flow conversion

What it is. How much of operating profit turns into cash after working capital and capex. Formula. (Operating cash flow − capex) ÷ EBITDA, or ÷ PAT. Worked example. Anupam's FY26 operating cash flow was ₹334 crore on EBITDA of ₹526 crore (64% conversion before capex, a big improvement on −₹30 crore in FY25), but after capex its free cash flow was −₹220 crore. Neogen's operating cash flow was −₹231 crore on ₹137 crore of EBITDA: working capital absorbed more than all of its operating profit.

What drives it. The change in working capital (growth in inventory and debtors consumes cash), capex phase, and interest. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). In a mature year, operating cash flow should be 70–90% of EBITDA and free cash flow positive; in a build year, negative free cash flow is normal if funded and temporary. How the six compare. Acutaas (−₹36 crore) is close to self-funding; Anupam, Aether and Neogen are not (Section 9's chart). The definition trap. Operating cash flow can be flattered by stretching payables and by factoring receivables, and depressed by one-off insurance timing (Neogen's ₹186 crore fire claim receivable).

13. Export share and currency

What it is. The share of revenue sold outside India, and by extension the exposure to the rupee. Formula. Export revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example. If 60% of a ₹1,000 crore business is invoiced in dollars, a 5% depreciation of the rupee lifts revenue by about ₹30 crore; if half the raw materials are also dollar-priced imports, the net gain to EBITDA is closer to ₹15–20 crore.

What drives it. Customer base (multinationals buy offshore, but some buy through Indian subsidiaries), contract structure, and domestic substitution of imports. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). A balanced 50–80% export share with a domestic hedge is good; 30–50% average; below 30% or above 90% adds concentration of a different kind. How the six compare. Acutaas about 60–76% (quarterly FY26), Anupam 58% (Q1 FY26), Jubilant Ingrevia about 43% (FY23), Neogen 30% (Q1 FY27), Aether below its normal 50–60% because CEM customers now buy through Indian subsidiaries; Blue Jet does not disclose, though most of its customers are regulated-market multinationals.

What it is. Research spending relative to revenue, and the number and seniority of scientists. Formula. R&D expense ÷ revenue; R&D headcount and PhDs. Worked example. Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA basis. Screener's operating profit excludes other income; some companies' headline EBITDA includes it (Jubilant Ingrevia ₹607 Cr vs ₹568 Cr; Anupam ₹543 Cr vs ₹526 Cr). State which one you use.
  • PAT and minorities. Anupam's FY26 PAT of ₹222 Cr includes Tanfac's minority shareholders; owners' PAT was about ₹172 Cr (EPS ₹15.09). Bliss GVS will widen the gap. P/E must use owners' earnings.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. Blue Jet's consolidated screener page is stale (FY20–21) and produces a wrong ROCE (~57%), ROE (~52%) and P/E (~76×). Use standalone or company figures.
  • Net debt. Sources disagree: Anupam ₹1,454 Cr (screener) versus about ₹1,950 Cr (derived from a garbled company balance-sheet table; to be verified); Neogen ₹1,390 Cr versus ₹1,295 Cr; Jubilant Ingrevia ₹569 Cr versus ₹587 Cr. Post-period raises (Blue Jet, Neogen) and deals (Bliss) change all of them.
  • ROCE basis. Average capital, year-end capital and ex-CWIP can differ by several points (Neogen 6.5%, 5.2%, 8.6%).
  • Gross margin. Use the company's figure, not 100 minus a database's "material cost %".
  • LOI value. A multi-year, usually non-binding total; not an order book and not annual revenue.
  • Capacity units. KL of reactor volume, tonnes per year and "blocks" are not interchangeable.
  • Segments. Neogen's battery subsidiary (₹36 Cr in FY26) sits inside its organic and inorganic segments, not on top of them.
  • EV with mismatched dates. Market caps after a QIP include new shares, while the latest balance sheet's cash predates the raise; EV/EBITDA is then overstated (Blue Jet ~32× on March cash, ~29× pro-forma).
In plain terms

Most of the disagreements about these companies' numbers are not about what happened but about which definition was used. Before comparing two figures, check four things: standalone or consolidated, including or excluding other income, including or excluding minority shareholders, and which date the balance sheet is from.

How to read a custom-synthesis company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue by segment, year on year and quarter on quarter (1 minute). Identify which line moved. A segment that jumped or collapsed is usually one molecule; ask which customer. Red flag: a single segment more than doubling or halving.
  2. Gross margin (1 minute). Compare with the last four quarters. A rise means better mix or yields; a fall means price pressure on catalogue lines or a mix shift to lower-value products. Red flag: falling gross margin with stable raw-material prices.
  3. EBITDA margin against guidance (1 minute). Use operating EBITDA excluding other income. Red flag: margin held up only by other income or foreign-exchange gains.
  4. Depreciation and interest (30 seconds). A step-up signals a plant commissioned; that plant now needs revenue. Red flag: depreciation up, revenue flat, for two quarters.
  5. Owners' PAT (30 seconds). Strip out minority interests (Anupam, and any company with listed subsidiaries).
  6. Working capital (1 minute, at half-years). Inventory and debtors against revenue. Red flag: inventory rising faster than revenue for two half-years.
  7. Capex, CWIP and utilisation (1 minute). Commissioning dates against what was promised; utilisation of the newest site. Red flag: a new plant below 30% utilisation a year after commissioning.
  8. Contract flow (1 minute). New LOIs, conversions to commercial supply, customer audits passed, validation batches. Red flag: LOIs announced but commercialisation dates slipping.
  9. Concentration signals (1 minute). Named customers on the call; any comment about a customer's inventory. Red flag: "temporary destocking at a key customer".
  10. Net debt, cash flow and guidance (1.5 minutes). Net debt after any raise or deal; operating cash flow; whether annual guidance was raised, kept or quietly dropped. Red flag: guidance withdrawn or reframed as a multi-year aspiration.
In plain terms

If you only have two minutes, look at segment revenue (to find the molecule that moved) and inventory against revenue (to see whether growth is being funded by stock). Those two numbers explain most surprises in this sub-sector.

Putting the toolkit to work: three quick readings

Blue Jet, Q4 FY26. Step one shows the pharma segment at ₹2.4 crore against ₹212 crore three quarters earlier: one molecule. Step two shows gross margin at 56.4%, higher than a year earlier, because the remaining revenue was richer contrast media; nothing was wrong with pricing. Step nine finds the management comment about a customer's inventory. The reading: a timing problem at a qualified supplier, not a lost franchise, which Q1 FY27's ₹121 crore confirmed.

Acutaas, Q1 FY27. Revenue up 59%, but EBITDA margin down from 42% to 34% quarter on quarter. Step two shows gross margin still near 58%, so the dip came from costs below the gross line (new-plant overheads, battery start-up) rather than price. Step seven shows Ankleshwar at 23% utilisation: the plant that must fill for the margin to return. The reading: margin depends on utilisation over the next three or four quarters, not on the contract.

Neogen, Q1 FY27. Revenue up 34% and EBITDA margin up to 19.3%, but interest up 64% to ₹21 crore. Technology and disruption: hard chemistry is the moat, obsolescence the threat

Flow reactors and hazardous chemistries keep rivals few. Molecules, modalities and customer decisions can still make a plant redundant.

The technology that defines this sub-sector is process chemistry, and the frontier is moving in three directions. The first is continuous-flow chemistry, in which reactants are pumped through narrow channels rather than mixed in a large batch vessel. Flow keeps hazardous intermediates in tiny volumes, improves heat control and often raises yield. Anupam lists Corning G1 and G4 flow reactors and silicon-carbide flow systems; Acutaas runs its VC and FEC production on flow technology it developed itself; Blue Jet's new Hyderabad centre focuses on continuous manufacturing. The second is hazardous and specialised chemistries: fluorination (Anupam through Tanfac), organolithium and Grignard reactions (Neogen), high-pressure hydrogenation and cryogenic reactions. These keep competitor numbers low because few plants are licensed and equipped for them. The third is biocatalysis and new modalities: enzymes replacing chemical steps, and peptides and GLP-1 intermediates (Blue Jet's Hyderabad focus) as pharma shifts towards larger molecules.

Four disruption risks

Chinese competitors with the same tools. Flow chemistry and hazardous-reaction capability are not uniquely Indian. Chinese CDMOs have them at larger scale and often lower cost, and their backlogs are growing. India's edge is that it is not China, which matters to customers worried about supply security and to regulators, but that edge is political, not technical, and can narrow.

Molecule obsolescence. Every contract molecule has a life. Innovator agrochemicals lose patent protection and face generic competition, which squeezes the innovator and then its supplier. In imaging, a shift towards lower-dose or different contrast agents could change which intermediates are needed. In batteries, the move from nickel-rich chemistries to lithium iron phosphate, and potentially to sodium-ion cells, changes which salts and additives are used and in what quantities. A plant built for one molecule needs to be re-purposed when that molecule fades.

Customer in-sourcing or dual-sourcing. This is the biggest single risk for Blue Jet, whose contrast customers could bring intermediate steps in-house or qualify a second supplier. It applies to every dedicated plant in the group: Aether's Baker Hughes and Milliken blocks, Jubilant Ingrevia's agro CDMO plant.

The shift in pharma towards biologics and peptides. If an increasing share of new drugs are large molecules made by fermentation or peptide synthesis, the classic small-molecule intermediate business grows more slowly. Blue Jet's GLP-1 intermediate research is a hedge; the others are mostly exposed to small molecules and agro.

In plain terms

The moat in this industry is being able to run dangerous or difficult reactions safely and cheaply. That moat is real, but it protects the process, not the product: if the customer's molecule goes out of fashion, or the customer decides to make it itself, the best process in the world does not help.

The companies' defence is to keep moving up the ladder: more steps per molecule, more regulated products, more customers per plant, and research capabilities that let them pick up the next generation of molecules. Measured by that standard, the most important technology indicators to watch are the number of new molecules entering validation each year, research headcount and spending, and the share of revenue from products launched in the past three years, which none of the six yet discloses.

Bull versus bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The bull case is about structural share gain and operating leverage on new plants. The bear case is about timing, concentration, capital and price.

The bull case

  • Structural share gain: global innovators keep outsourcing (about a third of agro manufacturing already is) and keep looking for non-Chinese sources; Indian houses have signed a wave of multi-year LOIs since 2019.
  • Sticky revenue once qualified: regulatory files make suppliers hard to replace, as Blue Jet's customer showed by returning in Q1 FY27.
  • Operating leverage ahead: Ankleshwar at 23%, Aether's Site 5 at 2 of 16 blocks, Neogen's new plants and Bliss at 30% utilisation give large room to grow without new capex.
  • Capex cycle peaking: Anupam's "major capex complete", Acutaas's FY27 capex down to ~₹150 Cr; free cash flow should improve from FY27–28.
  • Growth already visible: +19% in FY26, +25% in Q1 FY27, with the agro market still soft; a real agro recovery would add to it.
  • Optionality: battery chemicals at record VC prices, semiconductor chemicals, GLP-1 intermediates.

The bear case

  • Valuation: about 66× earnings and 39× EBITDA, two to three times global peers; the dearest stocks earn the lowest returns.
  • LOIs are not revenue: Anupam's ₹14,646 Cr book delivered about ₹400 Cr in FY26; ramps take years and slip.
  • Concentration: one molecule took Blue Jet's segment from ₹212 Cr to ₹2.4 Cr; Aether's largest customer is ~25% of a quarter.
  • Capital intensity: inventory of 345–490 days at three companies; negative free cash flow at four; repeated equity raises and promoter selling.
  • China: price deflation in agro intermediates and pyridines, and Chinese control of battery-salt pricing.
  • Execution and integration: Anupam's three acquisitions in 30 months, funded partly by Bain-led instruments; Neogen's battery build after a plant fire.

The two cases are not mutually exclusive, and the balance between them differs by company. For Acutaas the bull case is largely visible in reported numbers, and the bear case is mostly valuation and one anchor customer. For Jubilant Ingrevia the bear case is mostly Chinese pricing and a commodity tail, and the bull case is a re-rating if specialty and CDMO grow fast enough to change the mix. For Blue Jet the bull case is a high-return franchise in a steadily growing market, and the bear case is concentration. For Aether and Neogen the outcome turns on whether new plants fill, and on how long investors will wait. For Anupam the question is whether a contract house turned acquisitive group can convert its LOIs and integrate its purchases before its balance sheet and minority interests absorb the gains.

At the sub-sector level, the single most important variable is the pace at which capital already committed (CWIP, inventory, acquired plants) starts to earn. If utilisation of the new capacity rises by roughly 15–20 points a year, as the J-curve in Section 9 assumes, group ROCE could move from the low teens towards 20% within three years and today's multiples would compress naturally through earnings growth. If it rises half as fast, returns stay low, more equity is raised, and the multiples have to do the adjusting.

In plain terms

The optimist sees a group of Indian chemistry companies winning global contracts, with plants half-full and ready to make much more money. The pessimist sees the same half-full plants, a lot of borrowed money and stock tied up in warehouses, a few customers with too much power, and share prices that already assume everything goes right. Both are looking at the same facts; the difference is the timetable.
