00

The one-page view: three trades, one label, two stocks that matter to the market

Fashion, eyewear and electronics share a shelf in the index but not an economic model. What unites them is the store, and in 2025–26 the store-led growth model was tested.

"Speciality retail" in the NSE 750 is a label stuck on three different businesses. Five companies sell clothes: Trent (Westside and Zudio), Aditya Birla Lifestyle Brands (Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly, Peter England), Aditya Birla Fashion and Retail (Pantaloons, OWND!, ethnic wear, luxury), Arvind Fashions (U.S. Polo Assn., Tommy Hilfiger, Calvin Klein, Arrow, Flying Machine) and Vedant Fashions (Manyavar, Mohey). One company makes and sells spectacles: Lenskart. Two are regional consumer-electronics chains selling air-conditioners, televisions, refrigerators and phones made by others: Aditya Vision in Bihar, Jharkhand and Uttar Pradesh, and Electronics Mart India (Bajaj Electronics) in Telangana and Andhra Pradesh.

Together these eight companies booked ₹62,016 crore of revenue in FY26 and ₹2,347 crore of profit after tax, and on 16 October 2026 they were worth ₹3.17 lakh crore. Two of them, Trent and Lenskart, account for 85% of that value while generating 47% of the revenue. The remaining six together are worth ₹46,731 crore. One of them, ABFRL, lost ₹830 crore last year and trades below its book value. That split is the first thing to understand about the sub-sector: the market pays very high prices for long, self-funded store roll-outs that earn high returns on capital, and very little for everything else.

The second thing to understand is that every company here runs the same basic machine. It rents or builds a shop, stocks it, and earns a margin on what passes through it. Growth comes from two places: more square feet, and more sales from each square foot already open (like-for-like growth, or LFL). New square feet cost capital. LFL growth costs almost nothing, which is why it is the number investors watch most closely. The five forces that shape this report all act on that machine.

The five forces that define Indian speciality retail

  • The value-fashion land grab is meeting its limits. Value fashion (clothes mostly under ₹1,000) was a ₹3.5 lakh crore market in FY24, heading for ₹5.0 lakh crore by FY30. Zudio went from about 352 stores in FY23 to about 1,000 by September 2026, but Trent's fashion LFL has run at "low single digits" for four quarters, and Zudio's net additions fell from 198 in FY26 to 19 and 17 in the first two quarters of FY27. Trent's share price is about 47% below its bonus-adjusted October 2024 peak. Reliance, Landmark's Max and ABFRL's OWND! are chasing the same customer.
  • Store economics are flattered by lease accounting. Since April 2019, Ind AS 116 has moved store rent out of operating costs and into depreciation and interest. Reported EBITDA is therefore 5 to 9 percentage points higher than the cash economics. Lenskart's Q1 FY27 margin was 21.7% as reported and 13.3% after rent; Trent's FY26 margin was 18.7% on screener and 13.5% on the company's own operating measure. Screener "borrowings" include lease liabilities too.
  • Demand is discretionary and policy is now shaping it. Urban spending slowed through FY25–FY26. Then the February 2025 Budget cut income tax and GST 2.0 (from 22 September 2025) created a cliff: apparel up to ₹2,500 a piece is taxed at 5%, above it at 18% (previously 12%). Value retailers gained; premium menswear and big-ticket ethnic wear, Vedant above all, did not. For the electronics chains, a hot summer 2026 and a cut in GST on air-conditioners from 28% to 18% did the work.
  • Who keeps the margin depends on who owns the brand and who pays for the store. Post-lease EBITDA margins run from 43.8% at Vedant (franchisees fund the shops) to 18.7% at Trent (own labels, owned operations), 16.3% at ABLBL (owned brands, many channels), 13.7% at Arvind (mostly licensed brands paying royalties), 8.6% at ABFRL (incubation losses) and 6–9% at the electronics chains (they resell other companies' products).
  • Valuation dispersion is the investment story. Trailing P/E runs from 171× (Lenskart) and 86× (Trent) down to 33× (Vedant), and ABFRL sits at 0.94× book. The group trades at 135× its combined FY26 profit, and at 98× once ABFRL's loss is removed from both sides.

In plain terms

These companies all run shops, but they are not the same business. A Zudio store sells its own ₹300 T-shirts and keeps the whole margin. A Manyavar store is paid for by a franchisee, so Vedant keeps a slice of a high-margin sherwani sale without spending on the shop. A Bajaj Electronics store sells Samsung and LG goods and keeps about 17 paise of every rupee before costs. Lenskart makes the lenses in its own factory and sells them in its own shops. The stock market prices them very differently because the amount of profit each extra shop adds, and how much capital it needs, is very different.

01

Three trades, one label: how a speciality retailer earns

The apparel chain, the eyewear maker and the electronics reseller each capture a different slice of the value chain, and the slice decides the margin.

A rupee spent on a shirt passes through five hands before it reaches the till: the fibre and fabric mill, the garment maker, the brand owner who designs and markets it, the shop that sells it, and the tax authority. Speciality retailers make money by owning one or more of those links. The more links a company owns, and the fewer it has to share with others, the larger its gross margin. The cost is that it must also carry the capital and the risk of each link it owns.

Steps 3 and 4 hold most of the margin. Trent owns both for ~100% of its products. Brand houses own Step 3 and share Step 4. Electronics chains own only Step 4 for goods designed and made by Samsung, LG, Voltas or Apple.

The first trade is apparel, and it comes in several shapes. A private-label retailer such as Trent designs its own clothes, has them made by contract factories, and sells them only in its own stores. It keeps the brand margin and the retail margin, and it does not advertise much because the store itself is the brand. A brand house such as ABLBL or Arvind Fashions designs and markets brands and sells them through every available door: its own exclusive brand outlets (EBOs), shop-in-shops inside department stores, thousands of independent multi-brand outlets (MBOs), and online marketplaces. Each door other than its own takes a cut, typically 25–35% at an MBO and 25–40% at a department store, and online marketplaces take commission plus discount funding. A brand house earns a higher gross margin than a private-label retailer (54–57% against roughly 40–44%) because it charges for the brand, but it spends heavily on advertising and runs more inventory. A franchise brand such as Vedant owns the brand and the design but lets franchisees fund and run the shops in return for about 30% of the retail price.

The second trade is eyewear, where Lenskart has done something unusual for Indian retail: it owns the factory. Its plant at Bhiwadi in Rajasthan can produce about 50 million pairs a year and makes roughly 70% of the prescription glasses it sells. Lenskart designs frames, grinds lenses, runs eye tests in about 3,459 stores and sells through its app. Owning the factory lets it keep a product margin of 64–70%, the highest gross margin in the group. Owning the stores and the factory also makes it the most capital-hungry business in the group.

The third trade is consumer electronics, and here the retailer owns only the shop. Samsung, LG, Voltas, Daikin, Apple and others design, make, advertise and set the maximum retail price. Aditya Vision and EMIL keep a gross margin of 14–17%, plus incentives from manufacturers for hitting volume targets and subsidies from consumer-finance companies on zero-cost EMI schemes. They compete on location, range, credit availability, installation and service. Their profits depend on volume and stock turns, not on margin per unit.

In plain terms

The more of the journey from factory to customer a company controls, the more of the final price it keeps. Vedant keeps a lot without owning shops because franchisees pay for them. Trent keeps a lot because it owns both the label and the shop. Arvind gives part of its margin away as royalties to the brands it licenses. The electronics chains keep the least because Samsung and LG own the products and set the prices; the chains make their money by selling a large volume quickly.

02

How big the wallet is: ₹9.3 lakh crore of clothes, and a small listed slice

The listed apparel companies together hold under 5% of what Indians spend on clothing. Organised retail's share gain, not market growth alone, is the opportunity.

According to CareEdge Ratings (January 2026), Indians spent an estimated ₹9.30 lakh crore on apparel in FY25. The market has grown at about 7% a year since FY18, which implies roughly ₹5.8 lakh crore in FY18 (our back-calculation). CareEdge projects about ₹16 lakh crore by FY30, an implied growth rate of about 11.5% a year. That forecast assumes faster growth than the past seven years, which included COVID; a more cautious reader would treat ₹16 lakh crore as the upper end of a plausible range.

Within organised apparel, the market is still fragmented. CareEdge estimates that the top seven apparel retailers together hold about 16% of organised sales. Multiplied by organised's 41% share, that is roughly 6.5% of the whole market. Even Trent, India's most valuable apparel retailer, books about ₹20,000 crore against a ₹9.3 lakh crore market, a share of around 2%. The five listed apparel members together book about ₹43,300 crore, roughly 4.7% of the market. For investors, that is the central fact behind the long-runway argument: the listed leaders can grow for many years by taking share from unorganised sellers, even in a slow-growing total market.

Value fashion is the fastest-moving slice. CareEdge sizes it at ₹3.5 lakh crore in FY24, rising to ₹5.0 lakh crore by FY30 (about 7% a year), and names Zudio, Max Fashion, Reliance's Yousta and ABFRL's Style Up (now OWND!) as the leading organised players. Value fashion grows more slowly than the whole market in this forecast because much of it is still unorganised and price-deflationary; the opportunity for chains is conversion from unorganised, not growth of the pool. Tier-2 and tier-3 cities account for roughly 23% of apparel demand and most of the new stores.

Eyewear is much smaller. Lenskart's offer documents, citing Redseer, size the Indian eyewear market at about US$9 billion (roughly ₹75,000 crore at an exchange rate of ₹83–84 per dollar; our conversion), of which only about 23% is organised. Organised share is projected to rise to around 31% by FY30. The demand base is wide: refractive error affected an estimated 43% of the population in FY20, about 53% in FY25 and is projected at about 62% by FY30, driven by screen time and ageing.

Consumer electronics has no reliable primary-source market size for retail. KPMG notes, seen only in summary, put consumer durables at about ₹3 lakh crore by FY29 (11% a year from FY24) and major home appliances at ₹2.5 lakh crore by 2030; the two figures cover different product scopes. Treat them as INDICATIVE. What is better documented is regional dominance: Aditya Vision holds more than half of organised electronics retail in Bihar (CRISIL, cited in the company's filings) and EMIL describes itself as India's fourth-largest consumer-durables chain and the largest in the south.

03

What makes India spend: incomes, weddings, weather and a tax reset

Five demand engines drive these companies, and in Q1 FY27 they pointed in different directions at once.

Speciality retail is the most discretionary corner of Indian consumption. A family can postpone a new shirt, a second pair of spectacles or a replacement television in a way it cannot postpone food or medicine. That makes the sub-sector sensitive to five demand engines: household income and confidence, the tax burden on the purchase, the wedding and festival calendar, the weather, and the availability of consumer credit. In Q1 FY27 (April to June 2026) these engines were pulling in different directions, which is why revenue growth ranged from 7% at Vedant to 39% at EMIL.

Engine 1: incomes, confidence and the tax cut

Through FY25 and most of FY26, urban discretionary demand was weak. CareEdge notes that apparel slowed in the July–September 2024 quarter on food inflation and soft sentiment before a festive and wedding-led recovery from October–November 2024. FY26 like-for-like growth was weak almost everywhere in apparel: Trent's fashion LFL fell to low single digits, Vedant's revenue grew 3.5% and ABLBL's 7%. Two policy changes then put money back in pockets. The Union Budget of 1 February 2025 raised the zero-tax threshold under the new regime to ₹12 lakh of income (₹12.75 lakh for salaried taxpayers) from FY26, foregoing roughly ₹1 lakh crore of revenue. And GST 2.0 from 22 September 2025 cut tax on most clothes and on air-conditioners and large televisions. The effect on apparel has so far been modest, and from October 2026 the comparison base will already include both changes, so the easy year-on-year boost ends with Q2 FY27. For the everyday-staples side of the same household budget, see our packaged foods report.

Engine 2: the GST reset

The new GST structure taxes apparel and footwear priced up to ₹2,500 a piece at 5% and anything above at 18%. Before September 2025 the rates were 5% up to ₹1,000 and 12% above. For value retailers, whose racks are mostly under ₹1,000, nothing changed except a cut on the ₹1,000–2,500 band. For premium brands and wedding wear the tax on many items rose by six percentage points. Section 7 works through who wins and who loses. For the electronics chains the reset was entirely positive: ACs and televisions above 32 inches moved from 28% to 18%, a cut of about 8% in the shelf price if passed through in full.

Engine 3: weddings and festivals

India holds roughly 10 million weddings a year, and the number of auspicious dates (muhurats) in any season moves ethnic-wear sales sharply. In 2026 an extra lunar month (Adhik Maas) in May–June removed wedding dates from the first quarter. ABFRL's ethnic portfolio grew only 4% in Q1 FY27 and Vedant's same-store growth was 3.8%; both blamed the short season. Management at both expects the second half to catch up, with ABFRL guiding about 20% ethnic growth for FY27, weighted to H2, and Vedant guiding high-single-digit same-store growth for the remaining nine months. Festivals matter for everyone: Diwali shifts between October and November and moves revenue between the second and third quarters.

Engine 4: the weather and consumer credit

For Aditya Vision and EMIL, the April–June quarter is the season. Summer 2025 was cool and wet; air-conditioner makers reported falling sales and EMIL's FY26 profit fell 33% to ₹107 crore. Summer 2026 was hot: EMIL's same-store sales grew 34.2% in Q1 FY27 and profit reached a record ₹121 crore, and Aditya Vision's revenue grew 27%. Credit amplifies the cycle. About 43% of Aditya Vision's FY25 sales were financed through EMI schemes, so any tightening of consumer lending (as with the Reserve Bank's higher risk weights on consumer credit in November 2023, partly reversed in 2025) shows up in conversion rates at the store.

Engine 5: smaller cities, online and the eye test

Tier-2 and tier-3 cities, about 23% of apparel demand, are where chains are converting unorganised spending. About 80% of Zudio's FY26 openings were in tier-2/3 and emerging micro-markets. Lenskart opened in 50 new cities in Q1 FY27 alone, and Aditya Vision plans more than 30 stores in FY27, including entry into Madhya Pradesh. Online is the other channel shift: e-commerce is about 22% of organised apparel and is projected to reach about 25% by FY30. Arvind's online sales to consumers grew 38–39% in Q1 FY27 and ABLBL's e-commerce 20–30%, while Zudio sells nothing online at all. For Lenskart the demand engine is the eye test: it ran 23.8 million tests in FY26, up 48%, and each test is the top of a sales funnel that converts into prescription glasses.

Imports and China

Apparel retail in India is largely a domestic-sourcing business. Garments are made by contract factories in Tiruppur, Bengaluru, the National Capital Region and Ludhiana, with Bangladesh and Vietnam competing for some categories. In plain terms

Every listed company in this report has Reliance as a rival somewhere. The ₹2,500 cliff and the other rules of the game

GST 2.0 drew a line through the middle of the apparel market. Lease accounting, foreign-investment rules and new labour codes shape the rest.

Since 22 September 2025, the tax on a piece of clothing or footwear depends on which side of ₹2,500 its sale value falls. At or below ₹2,500 the rate is 5%. Above ₹2,500 it is 18%. Under the old structure the split was at ₹1,000: 5% below and 12% above. The change, part of the GST Council's "GST 2.0" rate rationalisation, cut tax for the ₹1,000–2,500 band and raised it for everything more expensive. It is the most important regulatory change for this sub-sector since GST itself arrived in July 2017.

The arithmetic is simple and it matters. Take four purchases, each with a pre-tax price set by the retailer. The other rules that shape the numbers

  • Ind AS 116 (leases), effective 1 April 2019. Store leases are capitalised as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. Rent disappears from operating costs and reappears as depreciation and interest. It changes reported EBITDA, debt and return on capital, but not cash. Section 9 is devoted to it.
  • Foreign direct investment. Single-brand retail is open to 100% foreign ownership through the automatic route, with a 30% local-sourcing requirement above 51%. Multi-brand retail is capped at 51% with conditions. This is why Inditex works through a joint venture with Trent and PVH with Arvind, and why Reliance and Tata, not foreign groups, own the largest multi-brand chains.
  • Labour codes, effective 21 November 2025. A wider definition of wages raised gratuity and leave provisions. Trent took a one-off charge of about ₹20 crore (FY26 PAT ₹1,721 crore reported, ₹1,741 crore adjusted). Retailers with large store workforces face a modestly higher permanent cost.
  • Quality-control orders and MRP labelling. Bureau of Indian Standards orders on synthetic fibres and footwear (2023–25) raised input costs for imported materials; Legal Metrology rules govern the maximum retail price printed on every item.
  • Consumer credit. The Reserve Bank raised risk weights on unsecured consumer credit in November 2023 and partly reversed them in 2025. EMI availability drives conversion at Aditya Vision and EMIL.
  • Eyewear. Spectacles and lenses are not price-controlled. The National Commission for Allied and Healthcare Professions Act (2021) could formalise who may conduct eye tests, which would favour chains with trained optometrists.
  • Pending. The industry is lobbying for a lower rate above ₹2,500. Draft consumer-protection rules for e-commerce could limit deep discounting by marketplaces.
In plain terms

Since September 2025, a ₹2,400 shirt carries 5% tax and a ₹2,600 shirt carries 18%. That simple line makes cheap clothes cheaper and expensive clothes dearer. It helps Zudio and Pantaloons, and it hurts wedding outfits and premium suits, which is why Manyavar and the big menswear brands are re-pricing items to sit just under ₹2,500. Air-conditioners and big TVs got a tax cut, which helped the electronics chains have their best summer on record.

Why the model worked

Zudio's economics rest on three choices. The first is private label: Trent designs everything and has it made by contract manufacturers, so it keeps both the brand and the retail margin, and it can price a T-shirt at ₹299 while still earning a gross margin around 40%. The second is speed: Trent's inventory turns in about 74 days on screener's cost-based measure, down from 83 in FY24, and its cash conversion cycle is 36 days. Fast turns mean less discounting, less stale stock and less working capital per rupee of sales. The third is no advertising: the store is the advertisement, opened on a busy high street or in a new mall in a tier-2 town. Together these gave Trent a return on capital employed of 28% in FY26 and operating cash flow of ₹2,668 crore on ₹20,074 crore of revenue.

The financial results were spectacular. Trent's consolidated revenue rose from ₹4,498 crore in FY22 to ₹20,074 crore in FY26, 4.5 times in four years. Reported EBITDA margin rose from 13% to about 19%. Profit after tax went from ₹35 crore to ₹1,721 crore. Westside, the older and larger-format department-store brand, kept growing too, but Zudio's 10.80 million square feet now make up about 60% of Trent's 18.04 million square feet of fashion space.

The crack: like-for-like growth went to low single digits

The first sign of trouble came in like-for-like growth. Through Q4 FY26, all of FY26 and Q1 FY27, Trent described its fashion LFL as "low single digit". Analysts had pencilled in 7–9% for Q1 FY27. Trent does not give a number, and it does not split Westside and Zudio, so the market has had to infer the trend. Management offered two explanations: new stores now open from a higher revenue base, and certain micro-markets have become dense with Trent's own stores. The second explanation is the important one. When Zudio opens a second or third store in the same town, some of the customers it serves were already Zudio customers. The group still gains revenue, but each existing store's sales stop growing.

The store engine and the rent illusion: what lease accounting hides Deep dive

Every store-led retailer reports an EBITDA that leaves out its biggest fixed cost. Read the numbers after rent, and the rankings change.

Since 1 April 2019, Indian listed companies have accounted for leases under Ind AS 116, the Indian version of the international standard IFRS 16. For a retailer that rents hundreds of stores, the change was dramatic. Rent, previously the second-largest operating cost after the goods themselves, no longer appears in operating expenses at all. Instead, each long lease is recorded on the balance sheet as a "right-of-use" asset with a matching lease liability, the asset is depreciated over the lease term, and the liability accrues interest. The cash paid to landlords is the same. The reported numbers are not.

In plain terms

So EBITDA looks bigger and debt looks bigger, while the cash going to landlords is unchanged. When a retailer reports a 20% EBITDA margin, ask what it would be after paying rent. For Lenskart the answer was 13%; for Trent it was about 13.5%.

The store engine: four numbers that drive everything

Strip away the accounting and every store retailer runs the same engine. Revenue equals the number of stores, times the average area per store, times revenue per square foot. Profit equals that revenue times the gross margin, minus rent, staff and other store costs, minus head-office, logistics and marketing. Growth comes from adding space (which costs capital), and from raising revenue per square foot on existing space (which mostly does not). The two interact: if a new store takes sales from an older one nearby, total revenue still rises, but LFL falls and the return on the new store's capital is lower than it appears.

The engine runs very differently across the group. EMIL's large electronics stores average about 8,700 square feet and sell roughly ₹38,800 of goods per square foot a year (our estimate from FY26 revenue and average area), because televisions and air-conditioners are expensive. Trent's blended productivity is about ₹12,600 per square foot. Vedant's system sales, measured at the shop till, are about ₹11,450 per square foot. Pantaloons sells roughly ₹7,500–8,000 per square foot on a low-confidence broker figure. A higher number is not better in itself: what matters is revenue per square foot relative to rent per square foot and gross margin. Section 15 works through each.

Rent, payback and the price of a growth stock

Lease accounting also distorts how investors value these companies. On reported numbers Trent trades at 42.4 times FY26 EV/EBITDA, where enterprise value includes lease liabilities. On the company's rent-adjusted operating EBITDA of ₹2,702 crore, with an enterprise value that excludes leases (about ₹1,56,400 crore), it trades at about 58 times. Lenskart trades at 65.5 times reported FY26 EBITDA, and at about 115 times our estimate of its rent-deducted EBITDA. Both comparisons are internally consistent; they simply answer different questions. The rent-deducted multiple is the one that tells you what you pay for the cash a store network actually generates.

Payback is the other half of the rent question. A store that recovers its fit-out and stock in under two years can be rolled out quickly from internal cash, which is what Trent did. A store with a four-year payback ties up capital for longer and needs either debt or a franchisee. That is why Aditya Vision, at a 3–4-year payback, funds its roll-out partly with working-capital loans, and why Vedant, which lets franchisees fund the shops, needs almost no capital at all.

In plain terms

A shop chain grows in two ways: open more shops, or sell more in the shops it already has. Opening shops costs money and takes time to pay off; selling more in existing shops is almost pure profit. When most of the growth comes from new shops and existing shops are flat, as at Trent in 2025–26, the company is spending capital to grow. When existing shops are booming, as at EMIL in summer 2026, profit jumps far faster than sales.

Who keeps the margin: franchise, licence, owned brand

Vedant earns 44% EBITDA by letting others pay for the shops; Arvind earns 14% after paying royalties. Both models have a catch.

Four of the five apparel companies in this group are, at heart, brand owners rather than shopkeepers. How they own the brand, and who pays for the shop that sells it, determines how much of the shopper's rupee they keep. The extremes are Vedant Fashions, which owns its brands and lets franchisees fund almost all its stores, and Arvind Fashions, which licenses most of its brands from American owners and pays them a royalty on every sale. ABLBL sits between them: it owns its brands outright but runs or supplies thousands of doors itself.

Vedant: the franchise machine

Manyavar is India's best-known men's wedding-wear brand, and Vedant has built it almost entirely through franchise-owned, franchise-operated (FOFO) stores. The franchisee leases the shop, fits it out, hires the staff and pays the rent. Vedant designs the clothes, has them made by contract manufacturers, supplies the stock, sets the price and runs the marketing. When a customer pays ₹100 at the till, the franchisee keeps about ₹30 as its commission, and Vedant books about ₹70 as revenue. In company-owned, franchise-operated (COFO) stores the franchisee's share is lower, about 18–19%.

ABLBL: owning the brand, running the channels

ABLBL's model sits between the two. It owns Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly and Peter England, so it pays no royalty on its core. In plain terms

What 171 times earnings implies

At ₹1,12,446 crore, Lenskart trades at 171 times trailing earnings, 12.8 times book and 12.8 times FY26 revenue. On reported FY26 EBITDA, which includes rent, the enterprise value multiple is 65.5 (about 57.5 on trailing twelve months). On our estimate of rent-deducted FY26 EBITDA of about ₹975 crore, with enterprise value excluding leases, it is about 115. For comparison, EssilorLuxottica, the world's largest eyewear company, has traded at roughly 35–45 times earnings (approximate, not re-verified), so Lenskart costs about four times as much per rupee of profit. The return on capital employed is 8.1%, depressed by IPO cash and a factory and store network built ahead of demand.

A rough way to see what the price assumes: if Lenskart reached its 25% rent-deducted margin on revenue three times today's (about ₹26,000 crore), rent-deducted EBITDA would be about ₹6,600 crore, and the current enterprise value would be about 17 times that figure. That is an ILLUSTRATIVE calculation, not a forecast, but it shows the scale of growth and margin expansion already in the price. Revenue growing at 30% a year would take roughly four years to triple.

In plain terms

Lenskart is the only company in this group that owns its factory, which lets it sell glasses cheaply and still keep about 70% of the product price. It has only just started making real operating profit: after paying rent, it kept about 13 paise of every rupee of sales in the last quarter. The share price assumes it will roughly triple its sales and almost double that margin over the next several years. If it does, the price can make sense; if it grows more slowly, the stock has a lot of expectation built in.

Ownership and the share overhang

The promoter group owns 17.52% (screener, September 2026), of which Peyush Bansal holds about 10.3%; the primer's "~20%" should read 17.5%. Foreign institutions hold 18.26%, domestic institutions 26.81% and "public" 36.99%. In December 2025 the public category was 62.30%; the drop most likely reflects reclassification or sales by pre-IPO investors such as SoftBank, Temasek and Kedaara as lock-in periods expired, while institutional holdings rose. That is our interpretation, not a disclosure. Further lock-in expiries around the first anniversary of the listing (November 2026) are a potential source of share supply.

Risks specific to Lenskart

  • Valuation. 171 times trailing earnings and about 115 times rent-deducted EV/EBITDA leave the share price dependent on sustained 25–30% growth and a doubling of the rent-deducted margin.
  • International. Almost half of revenue earns single-digit margins and is exposed to the yen and South-East Asian currencies.
  • Competition. Titan EyePlus is the organised challenger; online sellers and the large unorganised optician base compete on price.
  • Smart glasses. Lenskart sells its own ("B by Lenskart"), but if Meta, Google or Apple control the category, the value may sit with the platform rather than the retailer.
  • Policy. A reported GST cut on spectacles from 12% to 5% (unverified) would help; formal regulation of optometry could raise costs for smaller rivals more than for Lenskart.

The weather trade: Aditya Vision and EMIL in a hot summer

Two regional electronics chains sell other companies' products on thin margins. A good summer can multiply their profit; a cool one can halve it.

Aditya Vision and Electronics Mart India sell televisions, air-conditioners, refrigerators, washing machines, phones and small appliances, mostly in large-format stores. They do not make anything, so they keep only 14–17% of the selling price as gross margin, and their reported EBITDA margins run between 6% and 10%. What makes them interesting is concentration: each dominates one region, and each depends heavily on one season. For the manufacturers' side of the same cycle, see our household appliances report, which covers the AC and consumer-durables makers.

Aditya Vision is based in Patna and has built what it calls a creeping cluster across Bihar, Jharkhand and eastern Uttar Pradesh. It had 214 stores on 28 September 2026, with sales split Bihar 72%, Uttar Pradesh 16% and Jharkhand 11%, and it claims more than half of organised electronics retail in Bihar (a CRISIL estimate cited in its filings). It plans more than 30 stores in FY27, including its first in Madhya Pradesh. Revenue has grown from ₹899 crore in FY22 to ₹2,672 crore in FY26, about 31% a year, and profit from ₹35 crore to ₹117 crore. From the first department stores of the late 1990s to the value boom and the 2025–26 shake-out, every phase was set off by a policy change or a shock.

Three phases stand out. The first organised wave (1998–2012) built department stores and men's brands and sold them mostly through multi-brand outlets. In plain terms

Indian chain retail is only about 25 years old. It has been shaped by big rule changes (GST in 2017, lease accounting in 2019, GST 2.0 in 2025) and by shocks (COVID, the collapse of Future Group). After a four-year boom in which Trent and Lenskart grew four to six times, 2025–26 has been the first real test, and the market is now sorting the companies by how well their shops earn money and how much debt they carry.

Reading a retailer: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about a speciality retailer. Each has a formula, a benchmark and at least one way to mislead.

A speciality retailer's results can look simple: revenue, EBITDA, profit. For each it gives the definition, the formula, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, what good and weak look like, how the eight listed companies compare, and the trap in how it is defined. It starts with the equation that ties them together.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the pieces fit. Take an ILLUSTRATIVE value-fashion chain with 500 stores of 10,000 square feet each, 5 million square feet in all, selling ₹12,000 per square foot a year. Revenue is 5 million × ₹12,000 = ₹6,000 crore. At a 40% gross margin, gross profit is ₹2,400 crore. Rent at ₹75 per square foot per month is ₹900 a year per square foot, or ₹450 crore (7.5% of sales). Staff cost another ₹450 crore, other store costs ₹700 crore and head office, logistics and marketing ₹240 crore. EBITDA after rent is ₹2,400 − 450 − 450 − 700 − 240 = ₹560 crore, a 9.3% margin. Under Ind AS 116 the company would report ₹1,010 crore (16.8%), because the ₹450 crore of rent is moved below the EBITDA line.

Now raise same-store sales by 5%. Revenue rises by ₹300 crore and gross profit by ₹120 crore. Rent and staff do not change, and other costs rise only slightly, so EBITDA after rent rises by close to ₹120 crore, about 21%, from a 5% sales gain. That four-to-one ratio is operating leverage, and it is why every analyst asks about LFL first. Run the same arithmetic backwards and a 5% LFL decline removes a fifth of the profit.

In plain terms

A shop chain's sales are just the number of shops, times their size, times how much each square foot sells. Its profit is the share of those sales it keeps after paying for the goods, minus rent, salaries and running costs, which mostly stay the same whether the shop is busy or quiet. That is why a small rise or fall in sales at existing shops causes a much bigger rise or fall in profit.

1. Revenue per square foot

What it is. Annual sales divided by the average selling area. It measures how hard the space works, and it is the single best summary of a store's health. Formula: revenue ÷ average retail area (opening plus closing, divided by two). Worked example: Trent's standalone revenue of ₹19,701 crore in FY26 on an average area of about 15.6 million square feet gives about ₹12,600 per square foot (our estimate; the March 2025 area is backed out from the disclosed 30% growth). Annualising Q1 FY27 gives the same: ₹5,666 crore × 4 ÷ 18.04 million ≈ ₹12,560.

What moves it. Footfall, conversion (the share of visitors who buy), average ticket, and the share of new stores still ramping up. Opening many stores at once pulls it down even if every mature store is healthy. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). For value and department-store formats, above ₹12,000 is good, ₹7,000–12,000 average and below ₹7,000 weak. For electronics, where ticket sizes are high, above ₹35,000 is good and below ₹25,000 weak. How they compare. EMIL sells about ₹38,800 per square foot (₹7,183 crore over about 1.85 million average square feet, our estimate; the primer's ₹55,000–60,000 used an outdated area of 1.2 million). Pantaloons sells about ₹7,500–8,000 on a low-confidence broker figure. Vedant's network sells about ₹11,450 of retail value per square foot. 2. Like-for-like growth (LFL, SSSG, LTL)

What it is. Sales growth from stores that were open in both the current and the comparison period, usually for at least twelve months. It isolates demand at existing stores from growth bought with new space. Formula: (this period's sales from the comparable store set ÷ the same stores' sales a year earlier) − 1. Worked example: if 900 stores open throughout both years sold ₹10,000 crore last year and ₹10,300 crore this year, LFL is 3%, even if total revenue rose 20% because of 200 new stores.

What moves it. Footfall and conversion (demand, competition, cannibalisation by the company's own new stores), average ticket (price increases, mix, GST changes), and calendar effects such as festival shifts and wedding dates. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Apparel: above 8% good, 3–8% average, below 3% weak. Electronics: above 12% good, 5–12% average. Eyewear: above 15% good. How they compare in Q1 FY27: EMIL 34.2%, Lenskart India 18.3%, Aditya Vision 18%, Arvind 11.6%, ABLBL Lifestyle 7%, Pantaloons 4%, Vedant 3.8% and Trent "low single digit". The electronics numbers are weather-aided and will not repeat in a normal summer.

The definition trap. There is no standard. In plain terms

3. Store count, net additions and area growth

What it is. The number of stores at period end, the net change (openings minus closures), and the change in selling area. Area is better than store count when formats differ in size. Formula: store growth = net additions ÷ opening stores; area growth = closing area ÷ opening area − 1. Worked example: Lenskart began FY26 with about 2,724 stores and added 603 net to reach 3,327, store growth of 22%. Trent's Zudio count rose from about 765 to 963 (+26%) in FY26, then by only 36 (about +4%) in the six months to September 2026.

What moves it. Capital, site availability (tier-2/3 malls and high streets are scarce), property approvals, franchisee appetite at Vedant, and management's own judgement of new-store returns. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). 15–25% a year with stable LFL is good; 8–15% average; below 5%, or above 30% with falling LFL, weak. Definition trap: net additions hide closures, so a chain closing weak stores while opening new ones can show modest net growth with healthy gross openings. "Stores" may include shop-in-shops, kiosks or franchise doors, as Vedant's 669 versus 501 shows.

4. Average store size and format mix

What it is. Total selling area divided by the number of stores, by format. It tells you what kind of retailer you are looking at and how much capital and rent each opening commits. 5. Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after the cost of the goods sold. It shows pricing power, sourcing efficiency and how much discounting was needed. Formula: (revenue − cost of goods) ÷ revenue. Worked example: a ₹999 Zudio shirt that costs ₹600 to source, freight and stock earns a gross margin of 40%; a ₹2,999 branded shirt that costs ₹1,300 earns 57%.

What moves it. Private-label share, sourcing scale, input costs (cotton, polyester, freight, the rupee), full-price sell-through versus markdowns, mix (ACs earn more than phones at electronics chains), and vendor incentives. ABFRL flagged about 4% raw-material inflation for FY27 and expects to pass on half. Arvind's 56.7% in Q1 FY27 came from selling more at full price. Aditya Vision's margin fell about a point on a heavier phone mix. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Value private label above 40% good, 35–40% average; brand houses above 55% good, 48–55% average; eyewear above 65%; electronics above 16% good, below 14% weak. How they compare: the chart in Section 1 shows Lenskart's 70.3% product margin, Vedant's ~65.7%, Arvind's 56.7%, ABFRL's 56.6%, Trent's INDICATIVE 40–44% band, EMIL's 17.2% and Aditya Vision's ~15–16%.

The definition trap. Companies include different costs in cost of goods. Some put freight, warehousing and store-level shrinkage inside; others put them in operating expenses. Lenskart's "product margin" excludes costs that a conventional retailer would include, so it is not directly comparable with Arvind's gross margin. Franchise models such as Vedant compute gross margin on booked revenue (after the franchisee's share), which inflates it relative to a retailer selling at the till price. Compare gross margin within the same archetype, and track its change over time rather than its level across trades.

In plain terms

Gross margin is what is left of each ₹100 after paying for the product. A brand can charge more for the same shirt, so its gross margin is higher, but it also spends more on advertising and expensive shops. A gross margin that suddenly rises usually means fewer discounts; one that falls usually means stock was not selling and had to be marked down.

6. EBITDA after rent and the lease effect

What it is. Operating profit after paying store rent, the measure a store owner would recognise. Since Ind AS 116, reported EBITDA excludes fixed lease rent, so this number must be rebuilt or taken from voluntary company disclosure. Formula: reported EBITDA − lease payments (principal plus interest on lease liabilities, as shown in the financing cash flow). The lease effect is the gap in percentage points. Worked example: Lenskart's Q1 FY27 reported EBITDA of ₹589 crore minus lease payments of about ₹228 crore gives ₹361 crore; on revenue of ₹2,714 crore that is 13.3% against 21.7% reported, a lease effect of 8.4 points.

What moves it. Everything that moves operating profit, plus rent per square foot and the share of stores that are leased rather than owned or franchised. A chain that owns its properties, like EMIL in part, or uses franchisees, like Vedant, has a smaller lease effect. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Apparel above 13% after rent is good, 8–13% average, below 8% weak; electronics above 6% good. A lease effect under 4 points marks an asset-light model; 4–7 points is normal; above 8 is rent-heavy. How they compare: Trent about 13.5% after rent (5.2-point effect); Lenskart 13.3% in Q1 FY27 (8.4 points) and about 11% in FY26 (estimate); ABFRL negative after rent on Motilal Oswal's analysis. ABLBL, Arvind, Vedant, Aditya Vision and EMIL do not disclose the figure.

7. Rent-to-sales

What it is. Annual store rent as a share of revenue. It measures how expensive the real estate is relative to what it produces, and it is the largest fixed cost after staff. Formula: lease rent (fixed plus variable) ÷ revenue. Worked example: a 10,000 square-foot store paying ₹75 per square foot a month pays ₹90 lakh a year; if it sells ₹12 crore, rent-to-sales is 7.5%. If sales fall to ₹9 crore, the same rent becomes 10%.

What moves it. Location (mall versus high street, metro versus tier-3), lease terms (fixed, revenue share or hybrid), the bargaining power of the tenant (anchor tenants such as Westside and Zudio get better terms), and, above all, sales per square foot, the denominator. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Below 7% good, 7–11% average, above 12% weak for apparel; electronics chains sit around 2.5–3.5% because their ticket sizes are high. How they compare: Trent's implied rent-to-sales is about 5.2% (estimate from the gap between reported and operating EBITDA). The ILLUSTRATIVE composites in Section 4 put brand houses at 8–11% and franchise models at 2–3%. No other member discloses the figure. Definition trap: under Ind AS 116 the rent number is no longer in the income statement; it must be rebuilt from lease payments in the cash-flow statement, which mix principal and interest and may include payments for warehouses and offices.

8. Inventory days, stock turns and sell-through

What it is. How long stock sits before it is sold. Lower is better: less capital tied up, less risk of markdowns. Formula: inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365 (screener's method), or inventory ÷ revenue × 365 (many companies' method). Stock turns are 365 ÷ inventory days. Full-price sell-through is the share of a season's stock sold before markdowns. Worked example: a retailer with ₹1,660 crore of inventory and ₹8,180 crore of annual cost of goods carries 74 days of stock, turning it about five times a year.

What moves it. Range breadth, number of channels (brand houses must stock department stores and MBOs), sourcing lead times, seasonality (electronics chains stock up before summer), and how much unsold stock the company takes back. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Retailers below 80 days good, 80–150 average, above 150 weak; brand houses on the company's sales-based measure below 100 good. How they compare: on screener's cost basis, Trent and EMIL carry 74 days, Vedant 131, Aditya Vision 136 (up from 108 in FY24), Lenskart 150, Arvind 211 (105 on the company's own sales basis), ABLBL 252 and ABFRL 298.

9. Store payback and four-wall return

What it is. How long a new store takes to earn back the cash invested in it, and the return that store earns on that capital. "Four-wall" means counting only costs inside the store, before head-office overheads. Formula: payback = (fit-out capex + net working capital + deposits) ÷ annual store EBITDA after rent; four-wall return = (store EBITDA − depreciation) ÷ investment. Worked example: the ILLUSTRATIVE Zudio-type store later in this section needs about ₹2.66 crore (₹1.87 crore of fit-out and ₹0.79 crore of working capital) and earns about ₹2.26 crore of four-wall EBITDA after rent: a payback of about 1.2 years, or 1.5 years after allocated overheads.

What moves it. Sales per square foot above all, then gross margin, rent and the speed of ramp-up. A store that takes three years to mature, as at Aditya Vision, has a longer payback than one that reaches full sales in its first year. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). Under two years good, two to four years average, above four years weak. How they compare: Aditya Vision discloses break-even in 6–8 months and payback in 3–4 years. 11. Channel mix and direct-channel share

What it is. For brand houses, the share of revenue sold through channels the company controls (its own stores and its own website or app) rather than through department stores, independent outlets and third-party marketplaces. Formula: (own-store + own-online revenue) ÷ total revenue. Worked example: a brand selling ₹100 of goods at retail through an MBO books perhaps ₹65–75 after the outlet's margin; through its own store it books the full ₹100 (net of GST) but pays the rent and staff. What moves it. Store openings, online investment, and the strategic choice to reduce dependence on department stores. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE). 12. System sales versus booked revenue (franchise models)

What it is. For a franchise brand, "system sales" are what shoppers pay at the till across the network; "booked revenue" is what the brand records, after the franchisee's commission. Formula: booked revenue = system sales × (1 − franchisee share). Worked example: Vedant's network sold about ₹2,050 crore at the till in FY26; after franchisees kept about ₹614 crore (30%), Vedant booked ₹1,435 crore. One average EBO sold about ₹3.1 crore a year, of which Vedant booked about ₹2.1 crore (estimates using the disputed 669-store count).

What moves it. Store openings, same-store growth and changes in the franchise mix (FOFO stores keep about 29–30%, COFO about 18–19%). Benchmark: system sales growing faster than booked revenue signals a shift toward franchisee-favourable terms; the reverse can mean channel stuffing, with stock pushed to franchisees ahead of demand. How it compares: in FY26 system sales grew 6.1% and booked revenue 3.5%; in Q1 FY27 retail sales grew 3.4% and booked revenue 7.2%. Investors should watch whether the faster revenue growth reflects real retail demand or stock moved into the channel, especially with debtor days at 166. 14. Revenue per store and the maturity curve (electronics)

Definition traps, collected

  • Reported EBITDA excludes rent. Since April 2019, store rent sits below EBITDA. Reported margins are 5–9 points higher than the cash economics for store-heavy retailers. Ask for, or rebuild, EBITDA after rent.
  • "Borrowings" include leases. Screener's borrowings for Trent (₹2,561 crore), Lenskart (₹3,097 crore), ABLBL (₹3,018 crore) and ABFRL (₹6,189 crore) are mostly or partly lease liabilities. Trent and Lenskart are net cash on financial debt.
  • Mixed-basis multiples. EV/EBITDA must use either lease-inclusive debt with reported EBITDA, or lease-free debt with rent-deducted EBITDA. Mixing them flatters the multiple.
  • Inventory days on cost versus sales. Screener divides by cost of goods, which roughly doubles the figure for brand houses relative to company disclosures (Arvind: 211 vs 105).
  • System sales versus booked revenue. Vedant's till sales (~₹2,050 crore) are 43% higher than its booked revenue (₹1,435 crore). Trent books full store revenue, so no such gap exists there.
  • Same-store growth has no standard definition. Trent gives "low single digit", Vedant excludes shop-in-shops, Lenskart reports India only, ABFRL and ABLBL use "LTL", the electronics chains use stores open over a year.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. Trent's Zara and Star joint ventures are not consolidated; Vedant and Aditya Vision are shown standalone in this report because screener's consolidated data is stale or absent.
  • Profit before or after minorities. Arvind's screener profit (₹184 crore) is before PVH's minority share; reported attributable profit was ₹124 crore.
  • Restated history after demergers. ABFRL figures before FY24 include the Madura brands now at ABLBL. Any multi-year chart that mixes the two bases is wrong.
  • Per-share history after bonuses. Trent's pre-2026 prices must be divided by 1.5 for the 1:2 bonus.
  • Electronics margins are not apparel margins. A 17% gross margin is excellent for EMIL and disastrous for a fashion brand. Compare electronics chains on turns, ROCE and EBITDA per square foot.

How to read a speciality retailer's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, then split it. Pull headline growth, then the same-store number and the store or area growth. Revenue growth minus same-store growth is roughly what new space contributed. Red flag: total growth held up only by openings while same-store growth falls for a third quarter.
  2. Read the same-store definition. Check whether it changed, whether online is in, and whether the company gave a number or a phrase. Red flag: a switch from a number to "low single digit", or a new definition.
  3. Store count and net additions against guidance. Compare with the annual target and the run-rate. Red flag: openings far behind plan with no property explanation (Trent's 36 Zudios in H1 FY27 against 200–250 a year is the live test).
  4. Gross margin and its explanation. A rise from full-price sell-through is good; a rise from a mix shift may not last; a fall from markdowns means stock was not selling.
  5. EBITDA after rent, not just reported. Use the company's pre-Ind AS figure where given (Lenskart) or subtract lease payments from the cash-flow statement. Red flag: reported EBITDA up while rent-deducted EBITDA is flat.
  6. Operating leverage. Compare EBITDA growth with revenue growth. EMIL's Q1 FY27 OP +117% on revenue +39% shows leverage working; profit growing slower than revenue at a store chain usually means new-store costs or weak LFL.
  7. Inventory and receivables. Check inventory days on the same basis as last year and debtor days for franchise and wholesale models. Red flag: inventory growing faster than revenue, or Vedant-style receivables rising with revenue that outpaces retail sales.
  8. Cash and net debt excluding leases. For loss-making or expanding companies, check gross cash against guidance (ABFRL: ~₹1,000 crore in Q1 heading to ~₹500 crore). Red flag: cash burn faster than guided, or any new borrowing to fund operations.
  9. Segment and format detail. Pantaloons versus ethnic at ABFRL, Lifestyle versus Emerging at ABLBL, India versus international at Lenskart, mature versus non-mature stores at EMIL. The consolidated number often hides one part carrying the rest.
  10. Management's next-quarter signals. Wedding dates, festive timing, summer outlook, GST or pricing actions, and opening plans. Then check the valuation against what has just been reported: at 86× or 171× earnings, a quarter that is merely good can move the stock down.
In plain terms

When a retailer reports, look first at whether existing shops sold more, then at how many shops were opened, then at the margin after rent, and finally at stock and cash. Those four checks explain most share-price reactions in this sector. Headline revenue and reported EBITDA, the numbers in the press release title, are the least informative.

Who is winning: growth, returns and what the market pays for them

Three maps place the eight companies on the dimensions that matter: where their growth comes from, whether it earns a return, and how much investors pay for each combination.

"Winning" in speciality retail means three things at once: growing, earning a good return on the capital that growth needs, and doing it from existing stores rather than only from new ones. No company in the group currently does all three. The maps below show who comes closest and where the market disagrees with the fundamentals.

The first map is the investor's basic test. Trent is the only company combining double-digit growth with a return above 20%. Aditya Vision and Arvind sit near the centre: respectable growth, mid-to-high-teens returns. Vedant has the second-best return but the slowest growth, which is why the market pays it the lowest P/E among profitable members. Lenskart is the outlier on growth and below the line on return; its valuation is a bet that it moves up the chart. EMIL's FY26 position reflects a cool summer; its Q1 FY27 trajectory would move it right and up. ABFRL is alone in the bottom-left: modest growth, negative return.

The second map shows that the market is not paying for current returns in a straight line. Lenskart, with an 8% return, carries the highest multiple. Among the other six, multiples sit between 33 and 53 times regardless of whether the return is 8% (EMIL) or 23% (Vedant). The market is paying for expected growth and penalising stalls: Vedant's high return comes with almost no growth, and Aditya Vision's lower return comes with strong growth and a favourable summer. This is a reminder that in this sub-sector, the multiple tracks the growth runway more than the current return.

The third map separates the two sources of growth. Every company sits above the diagonal because all of them opened stores, but the distance varies enormously. Trent is the furthest away: its existing stores barely grew, and new space did almost all the work. EMIL sits close to the line: a hot summer filled the stores it already had. Why 33× to 171× earnings: valuation and the global lens

Indian speciality retailers trade at two to six times the multiples of comparable global companies. The premium is a bet on store runways that 2025–26 has started to test.

On 16 October 2026 the eight companies traded at a combined 135 times FY26 profit, or 98 times with ABFRL removed. Those are extreme multiples by any standard. The world's most admired fashion retailer, Inditex, has traded at roughly 25 times earnings; Fast Retailing (Uniqlo) at 35–40 times; H&M at 20–25 times; the off-price chain TJX at 25–30 times. EssilorLuxottica has traded at 35–45 times and Best Buy at 12–15 times. What justifies a premium, and what does not

Three arguments support higher multiples in India. The first is growth: Trent grew revenue 17–23% and Lenskart 33% in the latest periods, against mid-single digits for most global peers. The second is runway: organised retail is about 41% of Indian apparel and 23% of eyewear, so the leaders can keep taking share for a decade. The third is returns: Trent's 28% ROCE is comparable with Inditex's, and its stores pay back fast enough to fund growth internally. None of these applies equally to every company. Vedant's growth has stalled, ABLBL converts little of its EBITDA into profit, ABFRL loses money, and the electronics chains earn high-single-digit margins in a cyclical trade where global peers trade in the low teens.

Profitability is also lower than headline comparisons suggest. Trent's reported EBITDA margin of 18.7% compares with Inditex's roughly 28% on the same lease-inclusive basis; its net margin of 8.6% compares with roughly 15%. Lenskart's 13.3% after-rent margin is about half its own target. So the premium is not for current profitability: it is for the expectation that growth will continue long enough, and margins will rise far enough, to bring earnings up to the price.

What the multiples imply, worked through

An ILLUSTRATIVE way to read a high P/E is to ask what earnings growth would bring it down to a "normal" level in five years, holding the share price flat. At 18% a year, earnings rise 2.29 times and the price is about 38 times. Technology and disruption: quick commerce, cheap apps and smarter stores

The threat to store-led retail comes from three directions: instant delivery of basics, ultra-cheap online fashion, and manufacturers selling direct. Technology also helps the incumbents.

For two decades the main digital threat to Indian fashion retail was the marketplace: Myntra, Ajio, Amazon and Flipkart. Online now accounts for about 22% of organised apparel and is expected to reach about 25% by FY30. The listed retailers adapted by selling through those marketplaces and building their own apps. The new threats are faster and cheaper, and they hit specific parts of the market.

Quick commerce

Myntra's M-Now (about 30-minute fashion delivery, launched December 2024), Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy Instamart now list apparel basics, innerwear, socks and accessories, and start-ups such as Slikk and KNOT have built quick-delivery fashion apps. These dates and offerings come from 2025 press coverage and were not re-verified. The threat is to impulse and replenishment purchases: the plain T-shirt, the pack of socks, the innerwear refill. Zudio's basics and ABLBL's Van Heusen innerwear are the most exposed. Occasion wear (Manyavar), prescription eyewear (which needs a test and fitting) and large appliances (which need delivery and installation) are the least exposed.

Ultra-cheap online fashion

Meesho, which listed in 2025, sells unbranded fashion at very low prices to small-town India. Shein relaunched in India with Reliance in February 2025, bringing trend-led fast fashion at ₹200–500 price points to an app. Temu-style models are not currently permitted. These players attack the same ₹200–500 band where Zudio, Max and Yousta compete, without needing a store. Zudio's defence is that its customers can touch, try and take home the same day, and that it carries no return costs; its weakness is that it has no online presence at all.

Manufacturers selling direct

In electronics, Samsung, Apple and Xiaomi run their own brand stores and online shops, Amazon and Flipkart sell phones and televisions at aggressive prices, and quick-commerce apps deliver phones within the hour. Technology as a weapon for incumbents

  • Store reconfiguration and merchandising. Trent says its reconfigured stores deliver two to three times the throughput of older layouts (as reported in a broker note), using data on what sells where to set ranges and space.
  • Site selection. Lenskart uses GeoIQ, a location-analytics tool, to choose sites, aiming for short paybacks as it enters new cities.
  • Remote optometry. Lenskart runs AI-assisted remote eye tests in more than 500 stores, letting it open small stores without a resident optometrist in each.
  • Smart glasses. Lenskart sells "B by Lenskart" smart glasses. Meta's Ray-Ban glasses show the category can scale; the risk is that the value sits with whoever controls the software platform.
  • Omnichannel inventory. Brand houses use their stores as fulfilment points for online orders, improving full-price sell-through. Arvind's direct channels reached 62% of revenue.

Disruption from within: cannibalisation

The most immediate disruption to store-led retail may come from the retailers themselves. Opening stores close together raises total revenue but can lower each store's sales, as Trent's "micro-market densification" shows. Electronics chains cluster deliberately because their stores share delivery, installation and warehousing; value-fashion stores mostly share customers. As the best sites in tier-2 and tier-3 India fill up, every store-led grower will face the same choice between slower openings and weaker like-for-like growth.

In plain terms

Cheap clothes can now arrive by phone in half an hour, or be ordered from Meesho or Shein for less than Zudio charges. That threatens plain, cheap basics most. Wedding outfits, prescription glasses and air-conditioners are much safer, because people want to try them, need them fitted or need them installed. The retailers best protected are those whose products need a shop; the most exposed are those selling simple, cheap items that travel well in a delivery bag.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian speciality retail

The structural story is strong. The prices already reflect much of it, and 2025–26 exposed the limits of store-led growth.

The bull case rests on decades of formalisation in a ₹9.3 lakh crore apparel market and an under-served eyewear market, led by companies with proven formats and clean balance sheets. The bear case rests on slowing like-for-like growth, intensifying competition from Reliance and online, policy that now penalises premium price points, and multiples that leave little cushion if growth disappoints.

The bull case

  • A long runway. Organised retail is about 41% of apparel and 23% of eyewear; the listed apparel leaders hold under 5% of the apparel market between them.
  • Proven unit economics. Trent earns a 28% ROCE and funds its roll-out from ₹2,668 crore of operating cash flow; a mature value-fashion store can pay back in under two years (illustrative).
  • Policy tailwinds. The FY26 income-tax cut, GST 2.0 on value apparel and ACs, and a reported cut on spectacles all lower prices where most of these companies sell.
  • Operating leverage on recovery. EMIL's Q1 FY27 showed 39% revenue growth becoming 117% operating-profit growth; a revival in apparel LFL would do the same for fashion chains.
  • Lenskart's margin path. After-rent EBITDA rose from 9.1% to 13.3% in a year against a 25% target, while India same-store growth runs near 18%.
  • Clean balance sheets at the leaders. Trent, Lenskart and Vedant are net cash on financial debt.

The bear case

  • Valuation. 135× combined earnings; Lenskart at 171× and Trent at 86× require years of 20–40% earnings growth.
  • LFL has stalled at the bellwether. Trent's fashion LFL has been low single digit for four quarters, and Zudio added only 36 stores in H1 FY27.
  • Competition. Reliance (Trends, Yousta, Shein India, Reliance Digital), Landmark's Max, OWND!, Meesho and quick commerce all target the same customers.
  • The ₹2,500 cliff. 18% GST on premium apparel and wedding wear pressures ABLBL, Arvind and especially Vedant.
  • Accounting flatters. Reported EBITDA overstates cash economics by 5–9 points; rent-deducted multiples are much higher than headline ones.
  • Company-specific weak spots. ABFRL's cash runway, ABLBL's thin profit conversion, Vedant's stalled growth, Arvind's licence dependence and the electronics chains' weather exposure.

Weighing the two, the structural argument is stronger than the near-term one. The formalisation of Indian retail has years to run, and the best formats in this group earn returns that justify continued investment. But the market priced Trent and Lenskart for an uninterrupted version of that story, and the past eighteen months showed that store-led growth can slow sharply even in a large, under-penetrated market. For the cheaper names, the debate is less about valuation and more about specific fixes: ABFRL's cash, ABLBL's conversion of EBITDA into profit, Vedant's return to growth, Arvind's licence terms and the electronics chains' ability to earn through a normal summer.

Three questions will decide which side is right over the next year. Will Zudio's openings recover in H2 FY27 and will Trent's LFL return to mid-single digits? Will Lenskart's after-rent margin keep rising while store growth continues? And will ABFRL reach FY27-end with the cash it has promised? Section 22 sets out thresholds for each.

In plain terms

Over ten years, the case for India's best retailers is strong: most clothes and glasses are still bought from small independent shops, and the chains are better and cheaper. Over the next year or two, the risk is that their share prices already assume everything goes right. The test is whether existing shops start selling more again, not just whether new shops open.

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

Speciality retail has its own vocabulary, and several terms are used differently by different companies. Where a definition varies, the table notes it.

Glossary of speciality-retail terms
TermMeaning
LFL / SSSG / LTL / L2LLike-for-like, same-store sales growth, like-to-like: sales growth at stores open in both periods. Definitions differ by company.
Revenue per sq ftAnnual sales divided by average selling area.
Gross marginRevenue minus cost of goods, as a share of revenue.
Ind AS 116Indian lease-accounting standard (from 1 April 2019): leases go on the balance sheet; rent is replaced by depreciation and interest.
Pre-Ind AS / after-rent EBITDAEBITDA after deducting lease rent; comparable with pre-2019 reporting and with franchise models.
Right-of-use (ROU) assetThe capitalised value of the right to use a leased store.
Lease liabilityThe present value of future lease payments, shown within borrowings on screener.
EBOExclusive brand outlet: a store selling only one brand, owned or franchised.
MBOMulti-brand outlet: an independent shop stocking many brands.
Shop-in-shopA branded space inside a department store, usually on a margin-share basis.
FOFO / COFO / FOCOFranchise-owned franchise-operated; company-owned franchise-operated; franchise-owned company-operated.
System salesTotal sales at the till across a franchise network, before the franchisee's share.
Private labelProducts designed and sold under the retailer's own brand (Zudio, Westside).
Licensed brandA brand used under licence from its owner in return for a royalty (U.S. Polo Assn., Arrow, Reebok).
Direct channelsSales through a brand's own stores and own website or app.
Full-price sell-throughShare of a season's stock sold before markdowns.
Inventory daysInventory divided by cost of goods (screener) or sales (many companies), times 365.
Four-wall EBITDAA store's profit after its own costs (including rent), before head-office overheads.
PaybackYears for a store's profit to repay its fit-out and working capital.

Demerger / RemainCoSplitting a business into a new listed company; the business left behind is the RemainCo (ABFRL after May 2025).
