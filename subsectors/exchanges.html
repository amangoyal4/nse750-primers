A complete guide to India's listed exchanges: how BSE, MCX and IEX earn a small fee on a very large flow of trading, why SEBI's expiry-day rules turned BSE from a 3% player into a third of the options market, why MCX's revenue follows option premium rather than the headline notional numbers, how a power regulator is redesigning IEX's market, who actually pays for India's derivatives boom, and what the newly listed NSE says about the 50× earnings the market pays for the group.

00

The one-page view: three tolls on India's appetite for risk

An exchange charges a tiny fee on an enormous flow, over a cost base that barely moves. In India, the regulator decides how big the flow is, who gets it and what may be charged.

An exchange charges a toll on risk-taking. Every time someone buys or sells an option, a futures contract, a share, a kilogram of silver or a megawatt-hour of electricity on its platform, it collects a fee measured in fractions of a paisa per rupee. The fee is small enough that no customer shops around to save it, and the cost of running the road is almost fixed: a matching engine, a clearing house, a disaster-recovery site and a few hundred engineers cost about the same whether ten lakh or a crore of contracts change hands. So when traffic rises, almost every extra rupee of fees becomes profit. India's three listed exchanges in the NSE 750, BSE Limited, Multi Commodity Exchange of India (MCX) and Indian Energy Exchange (IEX), earned operating margins of 68%, 71% and 84% in FY26. Few listed businesses in India come close to these margins.

Traffic did rise. The three members' combined revenue went from ₹4,862 crore in FY25 to ₹8,042 crore in FY26 (+65%), operating profit (EBITDA, screener basis) to ₹5,642 crore (a 70.2% margin) and reported profit after tax to ₹4,312 crore. BSE and MCX together earned ₹3,819 crore in FY26, more than ten times the ₹355 crore they earned in FY23. On 25 October 2026 the market valued the three at ₹2,17,818 crore, or 50.5 times FY26 profit and 44.7 times trailing-twelve-month profit. Since 24 September 2026 the group also has a listed benchmark: the National Stock Exchange (NSE), the dominant equity exchange, listed on BSE after a ₹22,561.57 crore offer for sale and is worth ₹4.13 lakh crore. NSE is not a member of this report's universe (it joined the market after the NSE 750 list was frozen), so it appears throughout as a labelled reference, never in our totals.

Behind those totals are three very different exchanges, each answering to a different regulator. BSE is Asia's oldest stock exchange (1875), and three years ago it was close to irrelevant in derivatives. It now runs a third of India's equity-options market, because SEBI's 2024–25 rules gave every exchange exactly one weekly expiry and fixed the days. MCX holds more than 98% of India's non-agricultural commodity derivatives. Its FY26 revenue doubled on a bullion-and-energy volatility shock, and in Q1 FY27 it fell 21% from the previous quarter as that volatility faded. IEX runs about 84% of India's exchange-traded electricity on a fee of 2 paise per unit from each side. Its volumes keep compounding, but the power regulator, CERC, is building a mechanism called market coupling that would pool every exchange's bids into one national order book. That would remove the liquidity advantage that has kept IEX dominant.

Five forces define this sub-sector, and the report is built around them:

  • SEBI's F&O rules turned expiry day into a regulated duopoly and rebuilt BSE. One weekly expiry per exchange (November 2024) and fixed expiry days (NSE Tuesday, BSE Thursday, from September 2025) took BSE's share of options premium from about 3% to about 31.5%. The next lever SEBI has discussed, ending or lengthening weekly expiries, is the largest single risk to BSE and to NSE (section 04).
  • The state's take keeps rising, and it rests on a retail-loss business. STT on options premium went from 0.0625% to 0.10% (October 2024) and to 0.15% (1 April 2026); on futures it rose fourfold to 0.05%. SEBI's FY26 study found 87.7% of individual traders lost money, ₹91,685 crore in total, and that STT took 27% of their trading costs against 16% for exchange fees. That is a political risk for any business whose fees depend on those traders (section 05).
  • Premium, not notional. MCX's headline turnover numbers reached records in Q1 FY27 while its revenue fell, because it is paid on option premium and the premium per rupee of bullion exposure collapsed from 1.03% to 0.35% after gold's volatility faded and the import duty rose to 15%. Energy options, not gold, now drive its fees (section 06).
  • IEX is a toll booth that the regulator is redesigning. Market coupling, a possible fee cut and a ₹10 per unit price cap that binds on most evenings mean IEX's growth now depends more on regulatory decisions than on power demand. Its price-to-earnings multiple has fallen to 17.8 times (section 07).
  • Operating leverage, cash and the valuation question. With about 70% margins, little capital and debt-free balance sheets, these businesses compound fast when volumes rise and shrink fast when they fall. At 44.7–50.5 times earnings, about double global exchanges, the market is paying for continued growth; NSE's listing at 44.9 times gives it a direct comparison (sections 08, 17 and 18).

The latest quarter already shows the cycle turning. Every member and NSE has reported only up to Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026); the September-quarter results come between 29 October and 4 November 2026. In Q1 FY27 the members' combined revenue was ₹2,426 crore, 65% higher than a year earlier but 7.7% lower than in Q4 FY26. MCX fell 21% quarter on quarter, BSE was flat and IEX slipped seasonally. In plain terms

Exchanges are very profitable because they charge a small, unnoticed fee on a huge amount of trading and their costs hardly change with volume. In India the amount of trading, and which exchange gets it, depends heavily on decisions by SEBI, the Finance Ministry and the power regulator. Over the last two years those decisions handed BSE a third of the options market, left MCX exposed to swings in commodity volatility, and put IEX's market structure up for redesign. The stocks are priced for growth to continue; the regulators are focused on protecting the people who lose money trading.

01

How an exchange earns: a slice of every trade, before the state takes a bigger one

An order passes through a broker, an exchange, a clearing corporation and a depository, and the government collects tax along the way. The exchange's slice is small per trade but it is taken on every trade.

When a trader in Pune buys a Sensex option on an app, five parties earn something from that order. The broker charges about ₹20. The exchange matches the order with a seller and charges a transaction fee on the premium, from both the buyer and the seller. The exchange's clearing corporation becomes the counterparty to both sides (this is called novation), collects margin and guarantees that the trade settles even if one side defaults. If the trade involves shares, the depository moves them between demat accounts. And the state collects securities transaction tax (STT), stamp duty, GST on every fee and SEBI's own turnover fee. On the other side of most retail options trades sit proprietary trading desks and foreign portfolio investors, which take the trading profits.

The exchange and its clearing corporation are usually one group (BSE Clearing, MCXCCL, NSE Clearing). IEX runs settlement in-house. The value concentrates at the exchange, which charges on every trade, and at the state, which charges more.

The exchange's fee is set as a rate on the right base for each product. The current schedules are: NSE equity options ₹35.03 per lakh of premium, each side (₹3,503 per crore); BSE Sensex and Bankex options ₹3,250 per crore of premium per side, and ₹500 per crore on Sensex 50 and stock options; BSE futures nil; MCX futures ₹2.10 per lakh of notional and MCX options ₹41.80 per lakh of premium; IEX up to 2 paise per unit (kWh) from each of the buyer and the seller, the cap set by CERC. Because every trade has two sides, an exchange's realised revenue per crore of turnover is about twice the published rate. Section 13 explains this trap in detail.

Clearing earns a second, quieter stream: interest on money that belongs to others. Clearing members post margins in cash and securities, and the clearing corporation keeps a settlement guarantee fund (Core SGF) of its own and members' contributions. The interest on those balances is real income, but companies book it differently. NSE reported ₹842 crore of "operating investment income" inside revenue and another ₹1,929 crore of income on its own investments as other income in FY26. BSE puts ₹171 crore of treasury income on clearing funds inside revenue from operations. MCX management said float income was about ₹30 crore in Q1 FY27, inside operating income. This is why screener's operating profit and the companies' reported EBITDA never quite match, and why we compare margins on one basis (screener) and discuss treasury income separately.

In plain terms

An Indian exchange earns a fee from both the buyer and the seller on every trade, plus smaller amounts from listing companies, selling data and earning interest on traders' margin money. For BSE and NSE, options trading is by far the biggest source. But the government earns more from the same trades than the exchange does, mainly through securities transaction tax.

IEX is the exception to almost all of this. It does not run options. Its customers are electricity distribution companies, generators, captive plants and large industrial buyers, and its fee is per unit of power, not per rupee of value. In FY26 its fee was about 4.1–4.3 paise for every unit traded (both sides together) on a day-ahead price that averaged ₹3.86, so it kept about 1.1% of the value of the electricity. In Q2 FY27, with the day-ahead price at ₹5.70 a unit, the same fee was about 0.75% of value. Higher prices do not raise IEX's revenue, and above a point they reduce it, because buyers bid for less. The rest of IEX's economics are the same as BSE's and MCX's: fixed costs, a clearing role, and interest on a large pool of settlement money (other income of ₹151 crore in FY26, a quarter of revenue).

02

Two pools: the rupees exchanges earn, and the activity they earn it on

The listed exchanges' revenue pool is small for a market of India's size. The activity pool behind it is very large, and only one measure of it, premium, is closely linked to revenue.

Measured by revenue, India's exchange industry is modest. The four listed exchanges, our three members plus NSE, earned about ₹24,643 crore of revenue in FY26, roughly $2.6 billion at ₹94.9 to the dollar. That is about 40% of CME Group's 2025 revenue of $6.5 billion. Yet NSE is the world's largest derivatives exchange by number of contracts traded (FIA, calendar 2025), and MCX the world's largest commodity-options exchange by contracts. The gap between those rankings and the revenue tells the first story of this industry: Indian contracts are small and fees are low, so a very large number of trades produces a moderate amount of revenue.

Activity is a much larger number, and the unit matters. In plain terms

Indian exchanges handle enormous amounts of trading but earn relatively little revenue from it, because each contract is small and each fee is tiny. What matters for their revenue is not the headline "notional" value of contracts, which can run to hundreds of lakh crore a day, but the money actually paid for options (premium) and the volume of power or futures traded. That premium pool kept growing even in the year SEBI tightened the rules, which is why exchange profits rose while broker profits fell.

Two cautions on growth. First, the FY25–FY26 surge combined three one-time effects: BSE's share gain (which cannot be repeated from 31.5% at the same pace), MCX's bullion and energy volatility shock in Q3–Q4 FY26, and the shift of MCX's business from futures to options, whose fees are higher per rupee of activity. Second, the pool is now growing more slowly: premium turnover rose 7% in FY26 against 73% in FY23, and the number of individual traders fell. Future growth depends on market levels, volatility and the next set of regulatory decisions more than on new traders.

03

What drives the volume: traders, volatility, gold, IPOs and power demand

Each exchange's activity depends on a different mix of participants and prices. The common factor is volatility, and the common constraint is the regulator.

Exchange volumes have six main drivers: retail participation, market level and volatility, commodity prices and policy, primary-market activity, the structure of the power market, and institutional participation. They do not move together, which is why BSE, MCX and IEX can report opposite quarters.

1. Retail participation: more accounts, fewer F&O traders

Demat accounts grew from about 4 crore in March 2020 to about 23.2 crore by June 2026; CDSL, in which BSE owns 15%, holds roughly four-fifths of them. Registered investor accounts number about 25.8 crore at BSE and 25.7 crore at NSE, and NSE counts about 13 crore unique registered investors. But the trading base is shrinking. NSE's active clients fell from 4.92 crore in March 2025 to 4.57 crore in March 2026, the first annual fall of the app era, and were 4.61 crore in September 2026. SEBI counted 106.2 lakh individual equity-derivatives traders in FY25 and 87.5 lakh in FY26 (−18%), with new entrants down from 34.3 lakh to 20.8 lakh (−39%). That is the intended effect of SEBI's curbs.

Fewer traders did not mean less premium, because the traders who left were the smallest. SEBI's study shows the remaining traders are larger and more active, and that proprietary desks and foreign investors supply a growing share of turnover. For an exchange paid on premium, the size of the pool matters more than the number of people in it. For a broker paid per order, it is the reverse, which is why exchange profits rose in FY26 while most broker profits fell (see Stockbroking).

2. Market level and volatility: the fuel

Option premium rises with implied volatility: when markets swing, options cost more, so the same number of contracts carries more premium. The February–March 2026 sell-off, which followed the outbreak of the West Asia crisis on 28 February, produced record premium in Q4 FY26: NSE ₹76,375 crore a day (+43% quarter on quarter), MCX ₹10,628 crore and BSE ₹28,921 crore. SEBI's data show industry index-options premium peaked at ₹1,18,474 crore a day in March 2026. As markets calmed in Q1 FY27, MCX's premium fell 15% and BSE's rose only 2%. The Nifty closed at 22,780 on 28 September 2026. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and the rupee was near ₹94.9 to the dollar, close to its record low of about ₹96.8. A volatile macro backdrop supports option premium; a calm one reduces it.

5. Power-market structure: demand, renewables and short-term buying

IEX's volume depends on India's electricity demand and on how much of it is bought in short-term markets rather than through long-term contracts. 6. Institutions, foreign investors and proprietary desks

Proprietary trading desks and algorithmic firms supply much of the liquidity in index options and are the largest profit-earners there. About 650 FPIs participate in BSE's derivatives (management targets 800), and MCX has about 220 FPIs, about 2.5% of its turnover. These participants depend on bank funding and guarantees, which is why the RBI's new rules for bank credit to capital-market intermediaries, effective from 1 July 2026, matter for volumes (section 11).

In plain terms

Exchange volumes rise when more people trade, when prices swing more, when commodities are in the news, when companies list, and when more power is bought on the spot market. In 2026 the number of individual derivatives traders fell, but the remaining traders were bigger and markets were volatile, so the money traded kept rising. That combination is unlikely to last indefinitely, and a calmer market would reduce option premium even if the number of traders recovered.

The expiry-day duopoly: how SEBI's rules rebuilt BSE Deep dive

For twenty years NSE ran almost all of India's equity options. Two SEBI rules, aimed at protecting retail traders, created a protected weekly slot for BSE, and BSE took a third of the market.

In FY24 NSE held 96.86% of India's equity-options premium. By Q1 FY27 it held 68.48%. Over the same period BSE's daily premium turnover went from about ₹2,000 crore to ₹29,615 crore, and its profit from ₹772 crore to an annualised run-rate of about ₹3,500 crore. Exchange market share rarely moves this much this quickly, because liquidity attracts more liquidity: traders go where the order book is deepest. It moved here because the regulator changed the rules about when contracts expire, and in doing so turned what had been a near-monopoly into a duopoly with one expiry day each.

How it happened, step by step

May 2023: the relaunch. BSE relaunched Sensex and Bankex derivatives with smaller lot sizes and a Friday weekly expiry, so that traders had something to trade on a day when NSE had no expiry. In Q1 FY24 BSE's premium turnover was ₹47 crore a day; by Q4 FY24 it was ₹4,630 crore. That first year averaged about ₹2,000 crore a day, not the "~₹50 crore" sometimes quoted, which is the first quarter's figure alone.

20 November 2024: one weekly expiry per exchange. SEBI's circular of 1 October 2024 allowed each exchange only one weekly options contract, on one benchmark index. NSE kept Nifty and lost its weekly Bank Nifty, FinNifty and Midcap contracts; BSE kept Sensex and lost Bankex weekly. NSE had built its volume on a weekly expiry almost every day of the week, and the rule removed most of them at once. Traders who wanted a second weekly expiry in the week now had to go to BSE.

June–September 2025: fixed days. SEBI then restricted expiries to Tuesday or Thursday and required its approval for any change. NSE moved Nifty's weekly expiry to Tuesday from 1 September 2025; BSE kept Thursday. On 4 September 2025, the first Thursday under the new calendar, BSE handled a record 56% of that day's options premium (₹49,265 crore). The effect was a regulated duopoly of expiry days: each exchange has one day a week on which it has the market's most actively traded contract, and neither can move onto the other's day without SEBI's consent.

Price and brand. BSE also charges less: ₹3,250 per crore of premium per side against NSE's ₹3,503. The difference is too small for traders to notice, but brokers and proprietary desks trading very large volumes do. The Sensex is a well-known benchmark, so BSE did not have to build recognition for a new index.

In plain terms

SEBI wanted to cut down on retail traders betting on options that expire within days. It allowed each exchange only one weekly contract and fixed the days they expire. That left NSE with Tuesday and BSE with Thursday, and BSE, which had almost no options business three years ago, now runs about a third of the market. BSE did not win this share by being better at running an exchange; the rules created the opening, and BSE was ready to use it.

The risks: the same regulator, the next rule

The biggest risk to BSE comes from the same source as its gains. In September 2025 SEBI's chairman said the regulator would consult on lengthening the tenure of derivatives and moving away from weekly expiries, with a "glide path". No consultation paper had been published by October 2026, but BSE's shares fell 4.5–8% on each report. If weekly options were ended, both exchanges would lose most of their options volume, but BSE would lose relatively more: NSE has a large monthly Nifty franchise built over two decades, while BSE's monthly business, though growing, is still small. BSE's non-expiry-day volume is a partial hedge against a rule aimed at expiry days; it is not a hedge against an end to weekly contracts.

The second risk is the near-term volume trend. Monthly data from secondary reports put BSE's premium turnover at about ₹26,200 crore a day in June 2026, about ₹25,400 crore in July and about ₹18,700 crore in August. If September was similar, Q2 FY27 would average about ₹21,000–23,000 crore a day, 20–30% below Q1 (INDICATIVE). That would be BSE's first material sequential fall since the curbs began, and would reduce quarterly equity-derivatives revenue by roughly ₹230–300 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE). Management named the April STT increase and the RBI's bank-guarantee rules, effective from 1 July 2026, as headwinds; it said the impact on BSE had been "minimal" so far but that it "cannot draw solace" because old bank guarantees are still running off. June 2026 also showed a smaller, structural effect: in a month with five Tuesdays and four Thursdays, NSE had one more weekly expiry, and BSE's share dipped to 32.4%.

The third is competition from a newly listed NSE. NSE now reports to public shareholders, has ₹28,930 crore of investments, and has every reason to defend its share. Management at BSE says, "We do not measure our success in terms of market share," and points instead to growth in premium, 600 active members and about 650 participating foreign investors. But share is the key measure for BSE's revenue and its valuation, and a newly listed rival under pressure to show growth is the likeliest source of pressure on it.

In plain terms

BSE's options business is large, but it rests on a rule SEBI could change. If SEBI ended weekly options, BSE would lose more than NSE because NSE has a bigger monthly business. In the meantime, BSE has spread its trading across more days of the week, which helps a little. Its September-quarter numbers, due on 4 November 2026, will probably show the first real quarterly decline in its options volume since 2024.

Who really pays: the loss studies, the tax take and the exchange's slice Deep dive

Most individuals who trade options lose money. The government taxes those trades more heavily each year, and SEBI publishes the losses. Exchanges earn a smaller slice than either, but the policy risk falls on them too.

The economics of Indian exchanges rest on a business that regulators regard as harmful to many of its customers. SEBI's studies of individual traders in equity futures and options have found the same pattern four times: in FY22, 89% of individual traders lost money; over FY22–FY24, 93% of more than a crore of individual traders lost money, with aggregate losses above ₹1.8 lakh crore and an average loss of about ₹2 lakh per trader; in FY25, 90.9% lost, ₹1.12 lakh crore in total (revised); and in FY26, 87.7% lost, a combined net loss of ₹91,685 crore, or about ₹1.17 lakh per trader. Options accounted for 92% of the FY26 losses. Any business whose fees depend on that activity carries political risk, whatever the legal position of the exchange itself.

In plain terms

Why this is a political risk, and how it might play out

Three features make the risk real. First, the losses are visible and large: ₹91,685 crore in one year is about 21 times the three members' combined FY26 profit. Second, the people losing are numerous: 87.5 lakh individuals traded in FY26, and SEBI publishes the results in a study every year or two. Third, the policy tools are cheap for the state: higher STT raises revenue while discouraging the activity, and changes to expiries or contract sizes cost the government nothing. The direction of policy has been one way since 2023.

Against that, the curbs have worked on the measure SEBI watches. The number of individual traders fell 18% in FY26, new entrants fell 39%, the share of 0DTE (same-day expiry) trading in index options fell from 70% to 59%, and the aggregate loss fell by about a fifth. SEBI can point to progress without further steps. The likeliest next moves are incremental: further tenure changes, tighter rules for proprietary desks, or a third STT step in the Budget of 1 February 2027. For exchanges, the question is not whether the tightening continues but whether it reduces premium turnover, which it has not done so far. Premium, not notional: reading MCX after the bullion shock Deep dive

MCX's activity numbers hit records in Q1 FY27 while its revenue fell by a fifth. The explanation is that it is paid on option premium, and premium is driven by volatility in energy, not by the price of gold.

MCX is India's commodity-derivatives exchange, with more than 98% of the value of non-agricultural commodity futures and, according to FIA's FY2025 rankings, the world's largest commodity-options exchange by contracts. Since 2023 it has changed from a futures exchange into an options exchange, and that change explains both its boom and its latest quarter. In FY26 its revenue doubled to ₹2,302 crore and its profit rose 138% to ₹1,332 crore. In Q1 FY27 revenue fell 21% from Q4 to ₹702 crore and profit fell 22% to ₹413 crore, even though the notional value of options traded rose 72% to ₹9.90 lakh crore a day. To understand MCX, look at premium, not notional.

The base effect: from 63 Moons to its own platform

MCX's FY24 numbers are distorted and should not be used as a base. Until October 2023 it ran on software licensed from 63 Moons, the company of its founder group, which had been forced out after the 2013 NSEL payment crisis. The licence fee escalated to about ₹125 crore a quarter before MCX moved to a TCS-built Commodity Derivatives Platform (CDP) on 16 October 2023 (not October 2024, as is sometimes stated). FY24's operating margin was 9% and profit ₹83 crore. Growth "from FY24" is therefore meaningless; FY23 (₹514 crore revenue, ₹149 crore profit) or FY25 are better bases. The platform cost is not entirely fixed even now: MCX paid ₹108 crore of product licence fees in FY26 (up 56%), a line that scales with volume.

A toll booth the regulator is redesigning: IEX and market coupling Deep dive

IEX's volumes keep compounding and its margin is 84%. Its valuation has halved because CERC is building a national order book that would make the exchange a bid-collector rather than the place where power prices are set.

IEX is the simplest business in this report. It runs auctions for electricity: a day-ahead market (DAM, including a high-price segment), a real-time market (RTM) with 48 half-hourly auctions a day, term-ahead and contingency contracts (TAM and DAC), green-power markets and renewable energy certificates (RECs). Buyers and sellers each pay up to 2 paise per unit (kWh), the cap set by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC). In FY26 IEX traded 141.1 billion units (BU), up 17%, on revenue of ₹616 crore and an 84% operating margin, and earned ₹493 crore after tax. Volume in Q2 FY27 was 39,685 million units (MU), up 12.7%, and H1 FY27 77.2 BU, up 14.2%. Yet the share is at ₹101, close to its 52-week low of ₹100, on 17.8 times trailing earnings, against about 40 times a year earlier. The reason is market coupling.

The volume engine: real-time and term markets

IEX's growth has shifted from the day-ahead market to the real-time and term markets. RTM volume rose from 24.2 BU in FY23 to 54.9 BU in FY26 (+41% in FY26), and management expects it to keep growing at 25–30% a year and to overtake the day-ahead market. In Q2 FY27, RTM traded 16,490 MU (+10.5%) and the term and contingency markets 5,477 MU (+101.5%), while the day-ahead market grew only 2.2% to 14,855 MU and green markets fell 5.8%. REC trading fell 65.5% to 15.27 lakh certificates. DAM, which was about 95% of IEX's volume six or seven years ago, was 37% of Q2 FY27 volume.

Prices are rising, and the cap is binding

For IEX, high power prices are not good news. Its fee is per unit, so the price level does not affect revenue directly, but when prices rise buyers bid for less, and when prices reach the regulatory cap of ₹10 per unit in DAM and RTM (₹20 in the high-price day-ahead segment) demand that would have cleared at a higher price goes unmet. The day-ahead price averaged ₹4.47 in FY25 and ₹3.86 in FY26, then rose to ₹5.70 in Q2 FY27 (+46%) and about ₹7.30 in September 2026 (+105%). In September, buy bids in DAM rose 281% from a year earlier but cleared volume rose only 0.5%. Management says evening prices hit the ₹10 cap on about 90–95% of days, while solar-hour prices fall below ₹2. The day-ahead market is limited by supply and by the cap, not by demand. Battery storage, which buys cheap solar power and sells it in the evening, is the change management calls a "game changer" for this pattern.

What market coupling is

Today each of India's three power exchanges (IEX, PXIL and HPX) runs its own day-ahead auction and discovers its own price. Because IEX holds 98–99% of day-ahead volume, its price is the national price and every buyer and seller wants to be where the liquidity is. Market coupling would change this. Under CERC's draft regulations, each exchange would collect bids, validate and anonymise them and send them to a single Market Coupling Operator (MCO), Grid-India (the state-owned grid operator), which would run one national auction, maximise economic surplus and publish a uniform clearing price, splitting markets only when transmission is congested. If the exchange no longer discovers the price, liquidity stops being a reason to choose IEX. The exchanges would then compete on fees, service and products, and a rival could take share without first building a deep order book.

The legal route has largely run out for now. APTEL said IEX could not be harmed by regulations that did not yet exist, and the Supreme Court let CERC proceed, calling the power-exchange segment "an unorganised space… in need of regulation". IEX argues that CERC's claimed welfare gain from coupling, about 0.3%, is insignificant, and that in Grid-India's pilot the gains went to sellers and prices rose. In court it also cited a SEBI report alleging illegal trading by CERC officials connected to the coupling decision. Separately, IEX's FY26 annual report (note 46) discloses an ongoing independent investigation by its audit committee into a whistle-blower complaint alleging conflict of interest and potential diversion of business involving certain officials; management says there is no material impact.

Practical objections may matter more than legal ones. Grid-India itself asked CERC to clarify which day-ahead segments would be coupled, to procure an industry-grade solver, to avoid creating a single point of failure (with a fallback that lets exchanges discover prices if the coupling system fails, noting that Europe's coupling is run by nine exchanges in rotation), to set up a steering committee to draft the procedure, and to put inter-exchange settlement rules in the regulation itself. Management reads these as reasons implementation "will take long time". Coupling the real-time market is harder still: CERC's separate proposal to cut RTM gate closure from 75 to 50 minutes would leave little time for an operator to run a national auction.

In plain terms

IEX is dominant because everyone trades where everyone else trades. The power regulator wants all exchanges' bids to go into one national auction run by the state grid operator. If that happens, it no longer matters which exchange a buyer uses, so IEX could lose customers to rivals that charge less. The courts have let the regulator go ahead, but the final rules have not been issued and the grid operator itself has raised practical problems. Meanwhile IEX's volumes keep growing, mostly in the real-time market, which coupling would reach later, if at all.

How much could IEX lose? Scenarios

Management's own estimate is that if day-ahead coupling happens, IEX might lose "about 20, 30, 40%" of its day-ahead volume. With DAM at about 37–39% of volume, that is roughly 8–15% of total volume (ILLUSTRATIVE). Management also points out that NSE kept its dominance in equities despite BSE's lower fees for many years, and says, "I don't see any loss in market share." The European experience supports a moderate view: after the EU allowed several nominated exchanges (NEMOs) to compete within one coupled day-ahead market from 2015, incumbents such as EPEX SPOT and Nord Pool kept most of their volume for years and competed on fees and services. The difference in India is that the operator would be a single state body, buyers (state distribution companies) are concentrated and sensitive to policy, and the fee cap is already low.

Where ₹100 goes: a fixed-cost machine with one cost that grows

Staff, technology and premises barely change with volume. Regulatory fees and clearing costs do, and at BSE they are the largest cost of all.

An exchange's costs fall into two groups. The first is the platform: matching engines, clearing systems, disaster-recovery sites, data centres, software licences and staff. These costs are planned a year or more ahead, sized for peak traffic and are largely fixed. The second is linked to activity: SEBI's regulatory fee, clearing and settlement costs paid to the clearing subsidiary or to other clearing corporations under interoperability, contributions to the settlement guarantee fund, and some licence fees. In a quiet year the first group dominates; in a boom the second grows with revenue while the first stays flat. That is why margins jumped as volumes rose.

The hidden margin driver at BSE: a fee on notional, revenue on premium

Regulated monopolies: why every market has one or two venues

Liquidity concentrates, ownership is capped and licences are scarce. Market share in Indian exchanges has moved only when the regulator moved it.

Every market in this report is a monopoly or a duopoly. NSE has 93% of equity cash turnover and nearly all equity futures; NSE and BSE split equity options about 68:32; MCX has more than 98% of non-agricultural commodity futures; IEX has about 84% of power-exchange volume and 98–99% of the day-ahead market. Concentration of this kind is normal for exchanges everywhere, because liquidity attracts liquidity: the deepest order book offers the tightest prices, which draws more traders, which deepens the book. India's fee levels make the effect stronger. At ₹3,250 per crore of premium (0.0325%), a rival cannot win business by charging less, because the saving is far smaller than the cost of a slightly worse price.

Why entrants fail, and when share moves

Entrants have repeatedly failed without regulatory help. The Metropolitan Stock Exchange (MSEI, formerly MCX-SX) has been recapitalised several times and still has negligible volume. PXIL (2008) and HPX (2022) never broke IEX's day-ahead liquidity. BSE itself spent two decades as a distant second to NSE in derivatives. Share has moved meaningfully only three times, each time because of regulation or a regulatory failure: NSE overtook BSE in cash trading within about a year of its 1994 launch, after the Harshad Mehta scam led to SEBI's reforms and screen-based trading; BSE gained options share after SEBI's 2024–25 expiry rules; and CERC's market coupling is designed to move share away from IEX.

Ownership rules shape the industry

SEBI caps ownership of market infrastructure institutions (MIIs): no single shareholder may own more than 5%, and stock exchanges, depositories, banks, insurers and public financial institutions may own up to 15%. That is why BSE owns exactly 15% of CDSL and NSE 15% of NSDL, and why Kotak Mahindra Bank holds 15% of MCX (Kotak is covered in our Private Banks report). None of the three members has a promoter. Their ownership is spread among foreign investors, domestic institutions and very large numbers of retail shareholders: BSE has 11.6 lakh shareholders, IEX 14.3 lakh and MCX 4.2 lakh. An exchange also cannot list on itself, which produces an odd symmetry: BSE is listed only on NSE, and NSE is listed only on BSE, so each now earns listing fees from its main rival.

Four business models under one licence type

An equity challenger, a commodity monopoly, a power exchange and (as reference) a dominant multi-asset exchange share one economic model: a fee on activity over a fixed cost base. Their risks are quite different.

All four exchanges in this report charge a fee on activity, but what drives that activity, which regulator sets the rules and how cyclical the result is differ a great deal. The differences explain why the market values IEX at 18 times earnings and MCX at 50.

The commodity monopoly: leverage to volatility

MCX does not need to win share; it has it. Its revenue depends on how much commodity risk Indians want to trade and how expensive that risk is. The rulebook: five regulators, one business model

SEBI sets products, expiries and fees; the Finance Ministry sets the tax; CERC sets IEX's fee and market design; the RBI sets how traders are funded. Every member's revenue is activity times a regulator-approved fee.

For most listed companies, regulation is a constraint around the edges. For an exchange it is the business model. The products an exchange may list, the days they expire, the minimum size of a contract, the fee it may charge, the tax its customers pay, the margin they post and the money they may borrow to post it are all set by a regulator. Between 2024 and 2026 Indian regulators used every one of those levers, sometimes to curb activity, sometimes to move it between exchanges and sometimes to change a market's structure.

SEBI's turnover fee and the settlement guarantee fund

SEBI charges exchanges a turnover fee. For options it is levied on notional value, which is why BSE's regulatory bill is so large relative to revenue (section 08). Contributions go through the profit and loss account and are lumpy: NSE contributed ₹234 crore in FY25 and ₹1 crore in FY26; BSE ₹90 crore and ₹77 crore; MCX's Core SGF was ₹1,444 crore at 30 June 2026. The old rule requiring exchanges to transfer a quarter of their profits to the fund no longer applies.

The RBI's bank-guarantee rules

In February 2026 the RBI tightened the rules for bank credit to capital-market intermediaries. Bank guarantees given to exchanges and clearing corporations must be at least 50% collateralised, with at least 25% in cash; guarantees for proprietary trading must be fully secured with at least 50% cash; equity collateral takes a 40% haircut; and banks may not fund brokers' own-account purchases. The rules were due on 1 April 2026 but were postponed; BSE's MD said the circular "has started taking effect from July 1st". Both BSE and MCX named it a Q2 FY27 factor. MCX said it saw "no significant detriment yet" but expects higher funding costs for members; BSE warned that existing guarantees will mature over the coming months, so the full effect will appear gradually. Because proprietary desks supply much of the liquidity in index options, these rules could affect exchange volumes more than retail sentiment does.

Power and commodity rules

CERC's Power Market Regulations of 2021 provided for coupling; the draft amendment of April 2026 would implement it (section 07). CERC also sets the ₹10 price cap in DAM and RTM and ₹20 in the high-price segment, and approves each new IEX product; Green RTM, peak contracts and longer term-ahead contracts are pending. On the commodity side, SEBI has regulated commodity derivatives since the Forward Markets Commission merged into it in September 2015; electricity futures came under SEBI after a 2021 agreement with CERC and launched in July 2025; and agricultural derivatives on key crops have been suspended since December 2021. SEBI now has dedicated commodity teams inside its market-regulation department, and open items such as position limits and co-location in commodities have no timetable.

In plain terms

Almost every number that drives an Indian exchange's revenue is set or influenced by a regulator: what products it can offer, when they expire, how big they are, what fee it charges, how much tax traders pay, and how traders fund their positions. Over the past two years regulators have used these powers to reduce retail speculation, to move business from NSE to BSE and to try to open IEX's market to competition. The next decisions to watch are SEBI's view on weekly expiries, CERC's final coupling rules and fee order, and the February 2027 Budget.

150 years, six shocks: how the industry got here

India's exchange history is a sequence of scandals, reforms and rule changes. Each one moved market share or reset profits.

BSE began in 1875 as a brokers' association in Bombay and ran an open-outcry floor for more than a century. NSE, set up by government-owned institutions after the 1992 Harshad Mehta securities scam, introduced screen-based nationwide trading in 1994 and overtook BSE in cash turnover within about a year. NSE then won index futures (2000) and options (2001), and for two decades India's derivatives market was effectively NSE's. Commodity and power exchanges arrived in the 2000s, the 2013 NSEL payment crisis reshaped their ownership, and the post-pandemic retail boom, followed by SEBI's curbs, produced the profits and the share shifts described in this report.

The six shocks

1. The 1992 scam and NSE's arrival. The Harshad Mehta scandal gave SEBI statutory powers and created the political will for a modern exchange. A payment default on the National Spot Exchange led regulators to force its parent's exit from MCX and IEX, leaving both without promoters and leaving MCX dependent on its former parent's software until 2023. 3. The NSE co-location case (2015 onwards). A whistle-blower alleged that some brokers had preferential access to NSE's servers; SEBI's orders in 2019 included a ₹625 crore disgorgement, and the case delayed NSE's IPO by nearly a decade. It ended with a ₹1,491.21 crore settlement in 2026. 4. The pandemic retail boom (2020–2024). Demat accounts rose from about 4 crore to 15 crore by March 2024, and weekly index options became India's most traded product. 5. SEBI's curbs and the share shift (2024–2025). The rule changes cut individual traders but moved options share from NSE to BSE. 6. 2026's policy cluster. A second STT increase, the RBI funding rules, CERC's coupling draft and the gold duty increase all arrived within five months, alongside a geopolitical volatility spike and NSE's listing.

Reading an exchange: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about an exchange's results. Most of them have at least one definition trap, and several are reported differently by each company.

Exchange results look simple: a few revenue lines, a short cost list and a very high margin. The difficulty is in the activity data that sits behind the revenue. Turnover can mean premium or notional, which differ by a factor of 100 or more. Fees are quoted per side but earned on both sides. "Revenue" at BSE has four different totals. Market share depends on whether it is measured by value, lots, open interest or premium. This section builds the revenue equation, then goes through each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example, what moves it, what good looks like, how the members compare, and where it misleads.

In plain terms

An exchange's revenue is how much is traded each day, multiplied by the number of trading days, multiplied by its fee, multiplied by two because both sides pay. Almost everything else is detail. If you know the daily premium turnover and the fee, you can estimate the quarter's revenue before the company reports it, usually to within a few per cent.

KPI 1 · Options premium ADTO

What it is. Average daily turnover of options measured by the premium paid, the price of the options rather than the value of what they control. It is the single most important activity number for BSE, NSE and MCX, because since October 2024 their option fees are charged on premium. Formula: total premium traded in the period ÷ trading days. Worked example: if ₹18 lakh crore of premium trades in a 62-day quarter, ADTO = ₹29,000 crore a day. What drives it: the number and size of active traders, implied volatility (higher volatility makes options more expensive), the market level, product design (expiry days, lot sizes) and taxes. Benchmark: growth above 30% a year is strong, 0–30% average, a decline weak. The trap: the same word "turnover" is used for notional, which is 80–800 times larger for the same trades; always check which one is quoted. Also, BSE and NSE report equity-options premium; MCX's covers commodity options with very different volatility.

KPI 2 · Notional ADTO and the premium-to-notional ratio

What it is. Notional turnover is the face value of the exposure traded: strike (or underlying) price × lot size × number of contracts. It drives futures fees, SEBI's regulatory fee on options, and margin requirements; it does not drive option fees. The ratio of premium to notional tells you how expensive options are, which mostly reflects volatility and time to expiry. Formula: premium ADTO ÷ notional ADTO. Worked example: BSE's Q1 FY27 premium of ₹29,615 crore ÷ notional of ₹237 lakh crore = 0.125%, or 12.5 basis points. What drives it: implied volatility, the share of very short-dated (same-day expiry) contracts, which carry little premium, and the mix of products. Benchmark: there is no good or bad level; what matters is the direction, because a falling ratio means falling revenue per unit of activity (and, at BSE, a rising regulatory-fee burden). KPI 4 · Transaction yield: the both-sides trap

What it is. Transaction revenue per unit of turnover: the effective fee the exchange collects. Formula: transaction revenue for a product ÷ (ADTO × trading days). Worked example: BSE's FY26 equity-derivatives revenue of ₹3,134 crore ÷ (₹19,523 crore × 248 days, about ₹48.4 lakh crore) = about ₹6,470 per crore of premium. The published fee is ₹3,250 per crore, so the realised yield is almost exactly twice the rate, because buyer and seller each pay. NSE's ₹9,996 crore of options revenue ÷ (₹57,662 crore × 248) = about ₹6,990 per crore, twice its ₹3,503 rate. MCX's options yield is about ₹8,600 per crore (2 × ₹4,180, plus a little), and its futures yield about ₹430 per crore (2 × ₹210). IEX's is about 4.1–4.3 paise per unit for both sides together. What drives it: the fee schedule, product mix (BSE's ₹500-per-crore products lower its average) and any rebates (now banned). Benchmark: stable or rising is good; a cut of more than 10% is weak. The trap: fee schedules are quoted per side and turnover is counted once, so dividing revenue by turnover gives twice the published fee. Always say whether a "take rate" is per side or per trade. And never compare a premium-based yield with a notional-based one: MCX's options yield is twenty times its futures yield because the bases differ, not because options are overpriced.

KPI 7 · Core SGF contribution

What it is. The exchange's and clearing corporation's payment into the Core Settlement Guarantee Fund, which protects the market if a clearing member defaults. Formula: contribution charged to the profit and loss account in the period; the fund's size is the stress-test-based minimum required corpus, split at least 50% clearing corporation, at least 25% exchange and up to 25% members. Worked example: if SEBI's monthly stress test raises the required corpus by ₹200 crore, the clearing corporation and exchange together must contribute at least ₹150 crore, and that sum is an expense. What drives it: open interest, volatility in stress scenarios and the concentration of clearing members' positions. Benchmark: small and stable is good; a large jump signals that risk in the system rose. Members: BSE ₹91.7 crore (FY24), ₹90 crore (FY25), ₹77 crore (FY26); NSE ₹234 crore (FY25) and ₹1 crore (FY26); MCX's Core SGF stood at ₹1,444 crore at 30 June 2026 (₹1,367 crore in March). The trap: the contribution is lumpy and sits in different lines; BSE reports EBITDA "including Core SGF" while others bury it in other expenses. A quarter's margin can move a few points on this alone. Do not add the SGF balance to an exchange's own cash.

KPI 8 · Employee and technology cost as a share of revenue

What it is. The fixed platform costs. Formula: employee cost ÷ revenue; technology (software, licences, data centres, IT) ÷ revenue. Worked example: MCX FY26: employees ₹180 crore ÷ ₹2,302 crore = 7.8%; software ₹105 crore = 4.6%. What drives it: headcount, annual increments and variable pay; platform upgrades, disaster-recovery requirements and volume-linked licences. Benchmark: employees below 6% good, 6–10% average, above 10% weak; technology 4–8% adequate, below 4% a possible sign of under-investment, above 12% heavy. Members: NSE employees 4.8% and technology 7.9%; MCX 7.8% and 4.6%; IEX about 9% and about 3%; BSE about 5% and 5% (ILLUSTRATIVE estimates for IEX and BSE). The trap: a falling ratio in a boom is operating leverage, not efficiency; and a low technology ratio is not automatically good, because exchanges that under-invest have outages. MCX's "software" line (₹105–108 crore) is a product licence fee that scales with volume, so it is not purely fixed.

KPI 11 · DAM and RTM clearing prices

What it is. The average price at which power clears in IEX's day-ahead and real-time auctions. Formula: volume-weighted average market-clearing price over the period. Worked example: at ₹5.70 per unit, a 1,000 MU day-ahead month is worth ₹570 crore of power, of which IEX earns about ₹4 crore (two sides at 2 paise). What drives it: demand, coal and gas availability, renewable output by hour, hydro and the price cap. Benchmark: moderate prices (₹3–5) support buy-side volume; prices near the ₹10 cap suppress it. Members: DAM ₹4.47 (FY25), ₹3.86 (FY26), ₹5.70 (Q2 FY27, +46%), about ₹7.30 in September 2026; RTM ₹5.20 in Q2 FY27. The trap: price is not revenue. IEX's fee is per unit, so higher prices do not raise revenue, and when the cap binds (on about 90–95% of evenings, according to management) cleared volume stalls even as bids rise: DAM buy bids rose 281% in September but cleared volume only 0.5%.

For the September quarter the price data already point in one direction: day-ahead prices up 46% and real-time prices up 49%, with day-ahead volume up only 2.2%. When IEX reports Q2 FY27 on 29 October, the useful questions are how often the cap bound, how much volume moved from the day-ahead to the real-time and term markets, and whether battery storage or new supply is starting to flatten the evening peak. Those determine whether the day-ahead market can grow again before coupling arrives.

KPI 12 · Traded clients and participation

What it is. The number of unique clients who traded in the period. Formula: unique client codes (UCCs) with at least one trade. Worked example: MCX's F&O traded clients rose from 7.03 lakh in Q1 FY26 to 13.72 lakh in Q1 FY27; with premium ADT of ₹9,086 crore, that is about ₹66,000 of premium a day per traded client (ILLUSTRATIVE). What drives it: new accounts, product accessibility (smaller contracts such as Silver 100 g), broker marketing and regulation. Benchmark: rising clients with rising premium per client is the healthiest pattern; falling clients with rising premium per client (the equity market in FY26) means a more professional, more concentrated base. Members: BSE equity-derivatives UCCs 7.9 million (Q1 FY26) → 11.2 million (Q1 FY27); MCX 13.72 lakh traded F&O clients (Q1 FY27); IEX has more than 8,000 participants, mostly institutional. The trap: "registered investors" (25.8 crore at BSE), "demat accounts" (23.2 crore), "active clients" (NSE 4.61 crore) and "traded clients" are all different populations. A rising registered count says little about trading.

It is ILLUSTRATIVE, not any company's accounts. Options supply 65% of revenue; activity-linked costs (SEBI fee, clearing and settlement, Core SGF) take 19%; fixed platform costs 16%. The arithmetic is internally consistent: revenue ₹1,000 crore − costs ₹350 crore = EBITDA ₹650 crore; + other income ₹50 crore − depreciation ₹35 crore = profit before tax ₹665 crore; − tax at 25% (₹166 crore) = profit after tax ₹499 crore, a 50% net margin. If activity then rose 20% across the board, revenue would rise ₹200 crore and the activity-linked costs ₹38 crore (20% of ₹190 crore), so EBITDA would rise ₹162 crore to ₹812 crore on ₹1,200 crore of revenue: a 67.7% margin and an 81% incremental margin. If activity fell 20%, EBITDA would fall to ₹488 crore (61%), a 25% fall in operating profit from a 20% fall in revenue.

Each member departs from this template in a predictable way. BSE looks most like it, but with a heavier regulatory line (19% of revenue from operations rather than 19% of all revenue) and a lighter treasury line in screener's accounts because its clearing-fund income sits in revenue. MCX has a lower regulatory line (about 6–7%), a higher staff and licence line, and options weighted towards energy; its activity swings more than the template's, so its 20% scenarios happen within a single quarter. IEX has almost no activity-linked cost, so a 20% fall in volume would cut its operating profit by only about 24%, but its fee is capped by CERC and its other income adds a quarter of revenue below the line. NSE has a larger annuity tail (data, listing, indices), a much larger own-treasury book and, in FY26, a one-off ₹1,432 crore settlement charge. The point of the template is to show which lines move with activity; the members' actual ratios are in the peer table in section 14.

Definition traps, collected

  • Premium vs notional. Option fees are on premium; SEBI's fee and futures fees on notional. The two differ by 80–800 times and move apart with volatility.
  • Per side vs per trade. Published fees are per side; realised yield is about twice the rate.
  • BSE's four revenue numbers. Screener sales ₹5,124 crore; revenue from operations ₹4,834 crore; total income ₹5,148 crore; screener other income ₹24 crore. The differences are investment income on clearing funds and own treasury, classified differently by the company and by screener. This report uses screener figures in tables and company lines in prose, and says which.
  • IEX's two revenue numbers. Revenue from operations ₹616 crore (screener); "consolidated revenue" of ₹747 crore in company materials is total income including other income.
  • Three EBITDA bases. Screener operating profit; company EBITDA including other income (MCX); company EBITDA including Core SGF (BSE).
  • Associates are not subsidiaries. BSE's 15% of CDSL and 22.62% of HPX, and IEX's 47.28% of IGX, are equity-accounted: their revenue is not in consolidated revenue; only a share of profit is.
  • Share bases. Premium, notional, lots, open interest, expiry-day only, or the wider short-term power market: always name the base and period.
  • Units for power. MU vs BU; RECs in certificates, excluded from MU; DAM includes HP-DAM; TAM often includes DAC.
  • Gross cash vs own cash. Clearing members' margins and SGF balances sit on exchange balance sheets but are not the exchange's money. MCX's ₹5,426 crore of gross cash and investments includes ₹1,367 crore of SGF and member margins; its own net cash is about ₹2,600 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE estimate).
  • Base years. MCX's FY24 is distorted by the 63 Moons licence; BSE's FY24 profit includes a ₹367.5 crore exceptional gain on CDSL shares; NSE's FY26 includes ₹1,432 crore of settlement fees.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in analysing exchanges come from mixing up two versions of the same number: premium and notional, per side and per trade, revenue with or without interest income, the exchange's own cash and traders' money. Before comparing any two figures, check that they are on the same basis.

How to read an exchange's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with activity, before revenue. Pull the quarter's premium ADTO (BSE, MCX), futures ADT (MCX) or MU volume (IEX), and the same quarter last year and last quarter. For BSE, compare with NSE's premium to get share. Red flag: premium down quarter on quarter while notional is up, which means falling volatility.
  2. Estimate transaction revenue. ADTO × trading days × twice the per-side fee. If reported transaction revenue differs from your estimate by more than 5%, find out why (product mix, fee change, rebate, accounting).
  3. Check the premium-to-notional ratio. For BSE, a falling ratio means its SEBI fee will take a larger share of revenue; for MCX, it means lower revenue per unit of activity.
  4. Read the cost lines that move with volume. Regulatory fees, clearing and settlement, Core SGF and (at MCX) product licence fees. Red flag: these growing faster than transaction revenue.
  5. Read the fixed costs. Employees and technology quarter on quarter. One-off items (BSE's ~₹40 crore debtor provision in Q4 FY26; MCX's one-time variable pay in Q1 FY27) should be separated.
  6. Compute operating margin on one basis. Use revenue from operations and operating costs, excluding other income. Compare with the same quarter last year, not just the previous quarter, because volatility is seasonal and event-driven.
  7. Separate treasury income. Note other income and any mark-to-market gains or losses on bond portfolios (BSE's investment income doubled in Q1 FY27 as an MTM loss reversed). Red flag: profit growth that comes mostly from other income.
  8. Check associates and one-offs. CDSL's profit share at BSE; IGX at IEX (a one-off gain is likely when IGX lists); legal and regulatory provisions (MCX's contingent liabilities for legal and regulatory matters rose to ₹45.79 crore).
  9. Read the management commentary for regulation. SEBI (expiries, products), Finance Ministry (STT), RBI (bank guarantees), CERC (coupling, fee cap). Note any quantified impact and any new product approvals.
  10. Finish with valuation on the right earnings. Divide market value by trailing profit and by the current quarter annualised, and ask whether the quarter was a volatility peak or trough. Red flag: a P/E that looks low only because the latest quarter was exceptional.
In plain terms

Read an exchange's results in this order: how much was traded, what fee was earned on it, which costs rose with it, and how much of the profit came from interest rather than trading. Then check what the regulators did during the quarter. That covers most of what matters, and it takes about ten minutes once the numbers are to hand.

What 50× earnings buys: valuation and the NSE benchmark

Indian exchanges trade at about twice global multiples. NSE's listing gives the market a direct comparison, and a large new supply of exchange shares.

At ₹2,17,818 crore the three members trade at 50.5 times FY26 profit and 44.7 times trailing profit. BSE trades at 46.5 times trailing (53.0 times FY26), MCX at 50.0 times (57.8 times FY26) and IEX at 17.8 times. For comparison, NSE trades at 44.9 times trailing, and the large global exchanges at 23–34 times (section 18). The premium rests on four things: growth (BSE and MCX roughly tripled revenue in two years, against mid-single-digit growth at global peers), margins (68–84%), returns on equity (39–56%), and scarcity, since until September 2026 Indian investors who wanted to own an exchange had only BSE, MCX and IEX, plus market-infrastructure stocks such as CDSL, NSDL, CAMS and KFin.

NSE's listing: a benchmark and a new supply of exchange shares

NSE's IPO ran from 17 to 21 September 2026: a pure offer for sale of 12.64 crore shares at ₹1,785, raising ₹22,561.57 crore, the second-largest Indian IPO after Hyundai Motor India. It was subscribed 5.71 times (qualified institutions 12.68 times, non-institutional investors 6.55 times, retail 1.39 times) from 38.4 lakh applications, with an anchor book of ₹6,746 crore. Sellers included SBI, CPPIB, Temasek's Aranda Investments and Morgan Stanley's MS Strategic. NSE listed on BSE on 24 September at ₹1,800, 0.84% above the issue price (two independent secondary sources), touched ₹1,878 and was ₹1,669 on 23 October, 6.5% below the issue price. Its draft prospectus was filed in June 2026 (our Stockbroking report, written before the listing, describes the IPO as pending). Anchor lock-ins expire in stages from late October to December 2026.

The listing changes the valuation question for BSE in two ways. First, it creates a direct comparison. NSE has four times BSE's profit, 68.5% of options premium, 93% of cash trading, ~100% of futures, a much larger data and index business, and ₹28,930 crore of net cash. BSE is valued at about 32% of NSE's market value on 24% of its FY26 profit. On trailing earnings the two trade at similar multiples (46.5 and 44.9 times), which means investors are paying BSE's growth premium only modestly, but they are also paying almost the same multiple for a smaller, more concentrated business. Second, NSE's ₹4.13 lakh crore of market value roughly triples the listed supply of Indian exchange equity. Institutions that held BSE as their only way to own an equity exchange can now hold NSE instead. That may have contributed to BSE's 27% fall from its 52-week high of ₹4,447 (INDICATIVE; the fall also reflects the July–August volume data).

Why Indian margins are higher, and what that implies

Indian margins are higher than all global peers except HKEX for three reasons: the Indian exchanges have no large data-and-analytics businesses attached (ICE, LSEG and Nasdaq do, and those businesses earn lower margins), labour is cheaper, and each is a monopoly or duopoly in its market, charging a regulated fee. HKEX at 79% is the closest analogue: a near-monopoly exchange-plus-clearing house without a data conglomerate. CME at about 65–69% comes next. The Indian premium in valuation is therefore about growth and market structure, not margin. Indian returns on equity (39–56%) are two to four times global levels because the businesses need little capital.

Two lessons from abroad

Retail options booms end with contract-size changes. South Korea's KOSPI 200 options were the world's most traded contract until the regulator raised the contract multiplier fivefold in 2012 (from 100,000 to 500,000 won); volumes fell sharply over the following years. Taiwan's weekly options went through similar cycles. India's November 2024 increase in minimum contract value to ₹15 lakh is the same kind of step, and so far it has cut the number of contracts and traders without cutting premium. That is the main difference from Korea, and the reason it is too early to say India has avoided Korea's outcome (based on general knowledge of the Korean episode; INDICATIVE).

Market coupling does not always destroy incumbents. The EU's day-ahead power markets have been coupled since 2014, and since 2015 rules have allowed several exchanges (NEMOs) to compete within the coupled market. Incumbents such as EPEX SPOT and Nord Pool kept most of their volume for years and competed on fees and services. Europe's coupling is run jointly by the exchanges in rotation; India's draft names a single state operator, which Grid-India itself flagged as a single point of failure. This is IEX's strongest argument and its most important uncertainty.

In plain terms

Indian exchanges earn less revenue than the big American and European exchanges but keep more of it as profit, and they have been growing much faster, which is why investors pay about twice as much for each rupee of their earnings. Two foreign examples are worth watching: Korea's options boom ended after the regulator made contracts much bigger, and Europe's power exchanges survived market coupling better than many expected.

Technology, outages and disruption

The platform is the product. Outages are the most direct operational risk, and regulators now penalise them; technology also drives the competitive changes in data, co-location and power markets.

An exchange sells reliability. If the matching engine stops, traders cannot close positions, clearing members cannot meet margin calls, and the regulator asks why. SEBI's framework for market infrastructure institutions sets business-continuity standards, recovery-time objectives and deadlines for root-cause analysis, and can impose penalties, "financial disincentives" and business restrictions. Every exchange in this report has had a serious incident at some point; the question for investors is how often, how long, and what the regulator does next.

MCX's outages

MCX had two incidents in 2025: a trading halt on 23 July and an outage of more than four hours on 28 October, when trading moved to the disaster-recovery site and resumed at 1:25 pm; the root cause was reported as a predefined limit on simultaneous users linked to a reference-data parameter. SEBI sought details. MCX's FY26 secretarial audit shows that MCX paid ₹1 crore into its Investor Protection Fund as a SEBI "financial disincentive" for one technical glitch, and its clearing corporation paid ₹50 lakh into its Core SGF for another. Separately, MCX's standalone contingent liabilities for "legal and regulatory matters" rose from ₹0.11 crore to ₹45.79 crore between March 2025 and March 2026, an item worth a question to the company. ICICI Direct lists technical breakdowns as a key risk. MCX has since added an executive director for critical operations and technology, and says its platform handles more than 3 billion messages a day with capacity for more than twice that. NSE had a four-hour outage on 24 February 2021, so the problem is not unique to MCX.

Co-location, data and fairness

Co-location, renting rack space next to the matching engine, is a large revenue stream for NSE and a growing one for BSE, and it is also where fairness concerns concentrate. NSE's co-location case (alleged preferential access for some brokers) and the related dark-fibre case ended with a ₹1,491.21 crore settlement approved by SEBI in 2026. BSE charges for co-location by order flow at about 20% of prevailing market rates, which management says will be "reconsidered at an appropriate stage", a pricing lever. Market data is the other underused stream: BSE says data is only 4–5% of revenue for Indian exchanges against 10–25% in developed markets, and from 1 January 2027 it will distribute its own data rather than through Deutsche Börse. At least 50 AMCs use MCX's bullion prices to value their funds, which MCX expects to monetise through data services.

Competition by product, and new pools

Technology also lowers the barriers between markets. NSE has entered MCX's territory with electricity futures, gold 10 g contracts, electronic gold receipts and a collaboration with S&P Platts, and it injected ₹100 crore into its commodity subsidiary. BSE has launched derivatives on new indices (Focused IT in May 2026, with Sensex Next 30 and Focused Midcap approved) and products such as StAR NPS and electronic gold receipts. MCX and IEX are both building coal exchanges, and MCX a minerals exchange. At GIFT City, NSE IX and India INX offer dollar-denominated derivatives; NSE IX earned ₹112 crore of transaction revenue in FY26. Crypto, tokenisation and 24-hour trading are not material for India's regulated exchanges in the near term.

In plain terms

Exchanges sell reliability, and when their systems fail, regulators now fine them and ask hard questions. MCX had two such failures in 2025 and paid small penalties. Technology also opens new revenue, from data and server space next to the exchange, and lets exchanges move into each other's markets: NSE into commodities and electricity, MCX and IEX into coal.

Bull vs bear

The bull case is a structural rise in Indian risk-trading with exchanges as fixed-cost toll collectors. The bear case is that 2026 was a volatility peak and the regulators are not finished.

The two cases below apply to the sub-sector as a whole. They weigh differently for each member: the bear case is strongest for BSE (regulatory concentration) and MCX (volatility), the bull case strongest for IEX if coupling proves mild.

The bull case

  • Premium has survived every curb so far. NSE + BSE options premium rose every year from FY22 to FY26 and was up a third in Q1 FY27, despite eight SEBI measures and two STT increases. Individual traders fell 18%, but premium rose.
  • Operating leverage is extreme. Incremental margins of 83–84% mean modest activity growth turns into large profit growth, and the members are debt-free with about ₹8,800 crore of investments.
  • Long runways outside options. BSE's cash share (target "meaningfully double-digit" by early 2027), data (4–5% of revenue against 10–25% abroad), co-location pricing, StAR MF; MCX's electricity, coal and mineral exchanges; IEX's RTM (+25–30% a year guided), term products, storage arbitrage and coal.
  • Structural growth in participants. 23.2 crore demat accounts, record SIP flows, a deep IPO pipeline and power demand growing 11% a year.
  • IEX looks oversold. At 17.8 times with a 3.46% yield, the market assumes heavy coupling damage; management's own estimate (8–15% of volume) and the European record suggest less, and an IGX listing gain and buy-back are possible.

The bear case

  • 2026 was a volatility peak. MCX's revenue fell 21% in Q1 FY27; BSE's premium was about ₹18,700 crore a day in August against ₹29,615 crore in Q1. Multiples of 47–50 times rest on near-peak activity.
  • The regulator has not finished. A move on weekly expiries would hit BSE hardest; a third STT step in February 2027 is possible; the RBI's funding rules are still feeding through.
  • Political exposure. 87.7% of individual traders lose money, ₹91,685 crore in FY26; the state collects more from each trade than the exchanges and has a reason to keep tightening.
  • Margins depend on volatility at BSE. The SEBI fee on notional means a calmer market compresses BSE's margin even at constant share.
  • Competition is stiffening. A listed NSE has reasons to defend share; NSE is entering MCX's electricity and gold markets; coupling and a possible fee cut threaten IEX; NSE's listing adds supply of exchange shares.

Weighing the two, the bull case is about the long-term direction of Indian risk-taking, and the bear case about the next two to four quarters and the next regulatory decision. Both can be right. The September-quarter results will show whether premium turnover is holding up under the April STT increase and the July RBI rules; SEBI's stance on weekly expiries and CERC's final coupling rules will decide the structural questions. For a long-term holder, the critical variable is whether the fee pool keeps growing as participation broadens; for a shorter-term holder, it is the gap between run-rate earnings and the earnings in the price.

Glossary of exchange terms
TermMeaning
ADTO / ADT / ADTVAverage daily turnover (the three abbreviations mean the same). Always check whether it is premium or notional.

NotionalThe face value of the exposure a derivative controls (price × lot size × contracts). Futures fees and SEBI's turnover fee on options are charged on it.

STT / CTTSecurities transaction tax / commodity transaction tax, collected on each sale and paid to the government; not exchange revenue.
SEBI turnover (regulatory) feeFee exchanges pay SEBI; on notional for options. BSE paid ₹566 Cr in FY26.
Clearing corporation; novationThe entity that becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, guaranteeing settlement (BSE Clearing, NSE Clearing, MCXCCL).
