00

The one-page view: three monopolies on the wires, one bet on the build

Distribution licences earn a regulated return that rarely moves. Everything that does move, fuel, merchant power, factories and debt, sits on top of them.

India sells roughly 1,450–1,500 billion units of electricity a year to homes, shops, farms and factories, and more than nine-tenths of that is distributed by state-owned companies. The three listed companies in this report are the large exceptions. Tata Power runs the wires in parts of Mumbai, north and west Delhi, all of Odisha and Ajmer; Torrent Power runs Ahmedabad, Gandhinagar, Surat, the Dahej and Dholera industrial zones and the union territory of Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu (DNHDD), plus franchises in Bhiwandi, Agra and Shil-Mumbra-Kalwa; CESC has run Kolkata since 1899 and now also Greater Noida, Chandigarh and four franchise towns. Together they served about 22.3 million customers in FY26, booked ₹1,09,965 crore of revenue and were worth ₹1,91,252 crore on 22 October 2026.

The word "integrated" means each of them also owns power plants: coal, gas, hydro, solar, wind and, increasingly, pumped storage and batteries. This report is about how those two halves fit together. The wires are a cost-plus business. A state electricity regulatory commission adds up the licensee's approved costs, allows a post-tax return on the equity it has invested in the network (15.5% on the wires in Mumbai, 13–15% on new assets in Gujarat), and sets a tariff that recovers the lot. The generation half is a mix of regulated plants, long-term contracts and merchant exposure. The sibling report on power generation covers the economics of pure generators in depth; here generation matters chiefly for how it changes the risk of an otherwise regulated utility.

Five forces decide how these companies make and lose money, and the report is organised around them.

  • The wires compound. Profit on a licence is roughly regulated equity × allowed return on equity (ROE). Tata Power's regulated equity rose 10.8% in a year to ₹15,178 crore (June 2026). As long as regulators approve network capex, profit grows with it, with little link to the economy's cycle.
  • Losses are the moat and the option. Torrent loses 2.33% of the power it buys in its licensed areas; Tata Power-DDL in Delhi has aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) losses of 5.42%; CESC Kolkata 6.11% against a regulatory norm of 8%. India as a whole lost 15.04% (FY25). Beating the norm earns a bonus, and every new privatised area is an opportunity to repeat the trick.
  • Regulatory assets and tariff lag decide cash. When a regulator holds tariffs down, the shortfall is booked as income and carried as a "regulatory asset" until it is billed. CESC's Kolkata tariff has been frozen for years and its last true-up order covers FY22; its regulatory balances run from ₹5,257 crore (CARE-adjusted) to ₹8,666 crore (balance sheet). That is why it trades at 1.38× book value while Torrent, with timely Gujarat tariffs, trades at 3.18×.
  • The capex super-cycle is re-levering the sector. Tata Power guides ₹25,000 crore of capex in FY27 and more than 9 GW of operating renewables by March 2027; Torrent is building ~4.6 GWp of renewables, a 3 GW pumped-storage plant and a 1,600 MW coal plant; CESC's Purvah Green has 4.8 GWp contracted. Company net debt reached ₹84,346 crore, 3.16× combined company EBITDA, and operating cash flow covers only about 60% of investment.
  • Fuel and merchant shocks hit the plants, not the wires. Tata's 4,150 MW Mundra plant burns imported coal and runs under a government Section 11 direction until 31 December 2026; Torrent owns 2,730 MW of gas capacity that the 2026 West Asia war made uneconomic (Sugen ran at a 43% plant load factor in Q1 FY27). Exchange prices fell from ₹5.94 to ₹3.86 per unit over FY23–FY26 and have jumped to ~₹5.7 in Q2 FY27.

The year to October 2026 was rough for the shares. All three peaked within a week of each other in late April and early May 2026 and then fell 23% (Tata), 32% (Torrent) and 35% (CESC). The fall coincided with an RBI rate hike to 5.50% on 7 October, the West Asia LNG shock, a Singapore court ruling against Tata in the Kleros arbitration (a US$490 million award with no provision booked), and CESC's equity-funded purchase of 1,411 MWp of ReNew solar plants. In plain terms

These are toll roads for electricity with power stations attached. The toll road part, the local wires, earns a return the regulator sets, and the best operators earn a bit more by wasting less power than the regulator assumes. The power-station part is where the surprises come from: fuel that triples in price, exchanges that swing, and factories and solar farms that need borrowed money before they earn anything. Read each company as "how big and how well-run are its toll roads" plus "how much risk has it bolted on top".

The rest of the report follows that logic. Sections 01–07 explain the machine: the value chain, the size of the money pool, demand, the regulated-return engine, the cost stack, the industry's ownership and the seven business models hiding inside these three companies. Sections 08–13 are the defining themes, four of them deep dives: why low losses are a moat, what five years of private distribution in Odisha have shown, how regulatory assets split valuations, how the 2026 fuel shock skipped the wires, the capex cycle, and the solar factories. Section 15 is the KPI masterclass; sections 16–22 compare the companies, value them and list what to watch.

01

How an integrated utility earns: from coal pile to meter

Five links in the chain, three ways of being paid, and one reason to own both ends.

Electricity is made, moved and sold in five steps, and these companies sit in all of them. Fuel (domestic coal, imported coal, gas, sunlight, wind or falling water) becomes power in a generating station. High-voltage transmission lines carry it to substations near cities. The distribution company, or discom, buys that power, steps the voltage down through its 33 kV and 11 kV feeders and low-tension lines, meters each customer, sends a bill and collects the money. Beyond the meter, a growing set of products (rooftop solar, EV charging, smart meters, home batteries) sits on the customer's side.

The money flows in the opposite direction. Take an ILLUSTRATIVE average tariff of ₹8 per unit in an Ahmedabad-type licence. Of that, around ₹5.5–6 pays for the power itself, bought from Torrent's own gas and coal plants, from renewable contracts and from the market; a little goes to the transmission company; the rest covers the discom's staff, maintenance, depreciation, interest and its regulated profit. Because the regulator passes power-purchase cost through (with a lag, via a fuel and power-purchase adjustment surcharge, FPPAS), the discom's profit depends very little on what it pays for power and a great deal on how much equity it has in the network and how efficiently it runs it.

Three ways of being paid

Inside one company, three different revenue models coexist, and each has its own risk.

  • Cost-plus regulated (Section 62 of the Electricity Act). Distribution, Mumbai transmission, plants such as Torrent's Sugen (regulated by the central commission) and Amgen (by Gujarat's), Tata's Maithon and CESC's Budge Budge. The regulator approves costs and allows a return on equity. Profit is stable; the risk is that the regulator disallows a cost or delays the true-up.
  • Competitively bid (Section 63). Renewable plants under 25-year contracts at a fixed tariff (₹2.18–4.87 per unit in these portfolios), transmission lines won in auctions, Nabha's 25-year contract with Punjab. Revenue is fixed per unit; the risks are construction cost, plant performance and buyer payment.
  • Merchant. Uncontracted capacity sold on the Indian Energy Exchange (IEX) or bilaterally: Torrent's 1,200 MW Dgen gas plant, parts of CESC's Dhariwal, and power trading. Profit is the spread between the exchange price and variable cost, which can be large or negative.

Why own both ends

Integration gives two things. The first is an assured buyer. About 32.8% of Torrent's operating thermal capacity and 44% of its operating renewables sell to its own discoms; CESC says 78% of its 2,140 MW of thermal capacity is tied to its own distribution. A plant selling to its own licensee under a regulated tariff does not need to win an auction or chase a state discom for payment. The second is compliance: discoms must buy a rising share of renewable power (the renewable purchase obligation), and an integrated group can build that capacity itself, as Torrent is doing with 450 MW at ₹3.65 per unit and 250 MW of firm-and-dispatchable renewables at ₹4.87 for its Gujarat discoms, and CESC with 300 MW of solar at ₹2.69 for Kolkata. The catch is self-dealing scrutiny: the regulator must accept that buying from a sister company was the cheapest prudent option.

One company, three profit numbers

Before going further, a warning that runs through the report. Every one of these companies has at least three defensible profit figures. Tata Power's FY26 "reported PAT" of ₹5,118 crore includes the shares of profit that belong to minority holders (non-controlling interests, NCI) in Delhi, Odisha, Maithon and its renewables arm; the profit attributable to Tata Power's own shareholders was ₹3,747 crore, down 5.6% from ₹3,971 crore in FY25, even though reported PAT rose. The company's own "PAT ₹5,212 crore" is profit before exceptional items. Its "EBITDA ₹16,090 crore" includes ₹3,433 crore of other income, while screener's operating profit is ₹13,271 crore. CESC's EBITDA of ₹4,707 crore includes ₹903 crore of regulatory income, an accounting accrual for tariff it has not yet been allowed to bill; screener's operating profit is ₹3,624 crore. This report uses screener consolidated figures for cross-company tables, names the basis whenever it quotes a company figure, and computes valuation multiples on profit to owners.

In plain terms

When a utility says "record profit", ask whose profit. If a state government owns half of the discom that grew fastest, half of that growth is not yours. And if part of the profit is "regulatory income", the regulator has promised the money but not yet let the company bill it. The headline number can rise while the cash and the shareholder's share both fall, which is exactly what happened at Tata Power in FY26.

02

Size: a ₹10 lakh crore money pool, mostly in state hands

India's grid has doubled its peak in a decade. The three listed utilities distribute about 7% of the power and own under 5% of the plants.

India had 532.7 GW of installed generating capacity on 31 March 2026, after a record net addition of 57.5 GW in FY26, and roughly 552 GW by July 2026, with non-fossil sources above half. Coal and lignite still dominate output, but "other renewables" (solar, wind, biomass, small hydro) now almost match coal in nameplate capacity. Gas, the fuel behind a third of Torrent's capacity, has shrunk to under 4% of the national fleet.

The distribution money pool

Distribution is where the money changes hands. India's energy requirement was 1,694 billion units (BU) in FY25. After transmission and distribution losses, roughly 1,450–1,500 BU reach paying customers. At an average cost of supply of about ₹7.09 per unit (FY24, PFC), retail distribution revenue including state subsidies comes to roughly ₹10–11 lakh crore a year. These pool figures are ILLUSTRATIVE estimates built from national averages, not reported totals. By comparison, the three listed companies' combined FY26 revenue of ₹1.10 lakh crore includes generation, solar manufacturing and EPC as well as distribution.

Private operators serve perhaps 30–35 million of India's 300 million-plus electricity consumers, roughly 10–12% (an INDICATIVE composite: Tata 13.1 million, CESC 4.9 million and Torrent 4.3 million, plus the unlisted Adani Electricity Mumbai and the two BSES discoms in Delhi). By volume, the three members distributed about 99 BU in FY26 (an ILLUSTRATIVE sum: Tata 47.9 BU from its annual report, CESC 21.0 BU, Torrent ~30 BU from our primer), about 7% of national retail sales.

The national picture has improved sharply. In plain terms

India spends around ₹10 lakh crore a year on electricity bills, and state companies collect nearly all of it. The three listed utilities handle about one unit in every fourteen. Their growth does not depend on winning a bigger slice of a fixed pie each year; it depends on investing more in the networks they already run, on running them more efficiently than the regulator assumes, and on the occasional state that decides to hand over its wires.

03

Demand: weather beats GDP

FY26 brought the first fall in India's peak demand in the series. FY27 has already set a record. For a licensee, volume matters less than you would expect.

For most of the last decade electricity demand rose faster than GDP: peak demand went from 148 GW in FY15 to 250 GW in FY25, and energy requirement grew 4.7% a year. Then FY26 broke the pattern. A cool, wet year cut energy growth to 0.8% and the year's peak to 245.4 GW, below FY25's 249.9 GW. A delayed monsoon and a long, hot summer reversed it within months: all-India demand rose 8.5% in Q1 FY27, the peak hit a record 270.8 GW on 21 May 2026, and national consumption in Q2 FY27 was 502 BU, up 11.5%.

Why volume matters less for a licensee

A distribution licensee's profit is set mainly by its regulated equity and its efficiency, not its sales. If Kolkata sells 5% more units, CESC buys 5% more power and passes the cost through; its allowed return does not rise in proportion. Volume does matter in three indirect ways. More load eventually requires more network capex, which grows regulated equity. A hot summer that pushes purchases onto an expensive exchange creates under-recovery that sits as a regulatory asset until the next tariff order, which is why CESC's operating margin fell to 16% in Q1 FY27 even as its revenue rose 5%. And franchisees, who keep the spread between collections and a fixed input rate, do earn more on more volume. For the generation arms, of course, a tight summer is good news: merchant prices rise and thermal plants run harder.

The demand drivers that matter for these licences

  • Cooling load. Air-conditioning now sets the national peak. In April–June 2026 the daily peak fell in solar hours on 61 of 91 days, which is why solar-plus-storage, not more coal, is the main answer to peak growth.
  • Rich, industrial service areas. The members' licences are concentrated in Mumbai, Delhi, Ahmedabad, Surat, Kolkata, Greater Noida and Chandigarh, and in industrial zones such as DNHDD (a 1,437 MW peak) and Dahej. Industrial and commercial customers pay more than their cost of supply and cross-subsidise households and farms, so they are the most valuable customers and the most likely to leave via open access.
  • Data centres. Tata Power reported a pipeline of more than 2.8 GW of data-centre demand from 18 players in Mumbai, with ₹15,000 crore of grid upgrades and a 400 kV ring planned by 2031. Greater Noida's Jewar corridor and Ahmedabad-Dholera are similar catchments.
  • Electrification. EV charging, metros, induction cooking and green hydrogen add load slowly; Tata runs 5,900+ public charging points and 2.4 lakh home chargers.

A hot summer is good for anyone selling spare power and awkward for anyone who must buy it at a regulated price. Each of these companies is both. The wires compound: regulated equity × allowed return

The earnings floor of every member is a formula the regulator writes. The formula is getting stricter even as the headline returns rise.

A distribution licence is, financially, a box of assets on which the regulator promises a return. Each year the licensee files a plan for network capex: new substations, feeders, transformers, meters, cables under city streets. If the state commission approves it as prudent and it is completed and capitalised, roughly 30% of its cost is treated as equity and 70% as debt (the normative gearing most commissions use). The equity portion joins the licensee's regulated equity, and the commission allows a post-tax return on it every year for the asset's life. Debt earns normative interest, depreciation recovers the capital, and operating costs are allowed at normative levels. The tariff is whatever recovers all of that plus the cost of power.

So the core of a licensee's profit is a multiplication: regulated equity × allowed ROE. Grow the equity by investing in approved capex and the profit grows with it, almost independently of the economy. That is why investors call distribution and regulated transmission the "earnings floor" of these companies, and why Tata Power now discloses its regulated equity licence by licence.

The returns are rising on paper and getting conditional in practice

Two of the most important regulators rewrote their rules for FY26–FY30, and both moved in the same direction: a higher headline return that must be earned. Maharashtra's commission (MERC), which regulates Tata Power's Mumbai generation, transmission and distribution, allows 15.5% on generation, transmission and the distribution wires, but only 14% of that is a base rate; the other 1.5 percentage points are performance-linked, and on the wires they are kept only if network availability is at least 99.5%. Retail supply earns 17.5% (15.5% plus 2.0% linked to performance), and storage 18%. Mumbai's transmission network ran at 99.9% availability in FY26 against MERC's 98% norm, so Tata keeps the bonus for now.

Gujarat's commission (GERC), which regulates Torrent's Ahmedabad, Surat, Dahej and Dholera licences and its Amgen plant, moved new assets capitalised from 1 April 2025 to a return-on-capital-employed (ROCE) framework, with a base ROE of 13% that rises to 15% as incentive milestones are met. Legacy assets keep their old 14% ROE on regulated equity, but incentives can lift the effective rate to 15%; Torrent's annual report describes the ceiling as "up to 15.5%". Management said on its Q4 FY26 call that the new regime gives a "probability to improve ROE by ~1% a year", while tighter normative parameters reduce the savings it can bank in licensed areas. The central commission (CERC) allows 15.5% post-tax on inter-state generation and transmission, which covers Torrent's Sugen and Unosugen, its transmission subsidiary and Tata's Maithon.

Earned returns sit above or below the allowed rate for four reasons: efficiency gains (beating the normative loss, availability or heat-rate assumptions), incentives (MERC's and GERC's performance slabs), disallowances (costs the regulator refuses, such as Torrent's ₹171 crore Unosugen true-up disallowance in Q4 FY26), and timing (true-ups that arrive years late, with or without carrying cost). Consolidated ROEs of 10.2% (Tata), 12.5% (Torrent) and 12.0% (CESC) look far below 15% because they blend regulated licences with merchant plants, factories and, above all, large amounts of capital tied up in projects still under construction that earn nothing yet.

In plain terms

The regulator lets the company earn about 15% a year after tax on the equity it has put into the wires, provided the regulator agreed the spending was needed. So a licensee grows by spending sensibly on its network and by keeping the regulator onside. The new rules add a twist: part of that 15% is now a bonus for reliability and efficiency, so a sloppy operator earns closer to 13–14%. That favours exactly the incumbents in this report, which already beat the norms.

Power purchase eats two-thirds or more of a discom's revenue. The licensee's profit is the small, regulated remainder.

What the licensee keeps is a thin slice that equals roughly its allowed return plus efficiency gains. The cost stack also explains why revenue swings so much without moving profit. When fuel or exchange prices rise, revenue rises with them, because the surcharge passes the cost on; margins as a percentage of revenue then fall even though the rupee profit is unchanged. CESC's 9.2% revenue growth in FY26 and Tata's 4.7% decline both say more about fuel and surcharges than about the businesses. Analysts in this sector therefore work in rupees per unit and in rupees of regulated return, not in margin percentages.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 a household pays, around ₹70 goes straight to whoever generated the power. The distribution company's own costs and profit come out of the remaining ₹30, and its profit is only ₹4–7 of that. Because the big cost is passed on, the profit barely moves when fuel prices jump. What can hurt is interest on borrowed money for projects that are not earning yet, and that is growing.

Private islands in a state grid: who owns India's wires

Around nine in ten electricity customers are served by state companies. The private licensees are a handful of cities, one state and two union territories.

India has roughly 72 distribution utilities; PFC's 2023-24 performance report covered 63 of them. Almost all are owned by state governments. The private exceptions fall into three groups. The old city licensees are the companies that predate the state electricity boards: Calcutta Electric Supply Corporation (1897), the Tata hydro companies that electrified Bombay, and the Ahmedabad and Surat electricity companies now inside Torrent. The joint ventures are privatised state discoms in which a private company owns 51% and the state 49%: Tata Power-DDL in Delhi (2002), the two BSES discoms in Delhi (Reliance Infrastructure), Tata's four Odisha discoms (2020–21) and Torrent's DNHDD (2022). The franchises are areas where a state discom keeps the licence and the wires but hands operation to a private company for 15–20 years.

Competition happens at privatisation auctions

Inside a licence area there is, in practice, no competition: the Electricity Act allows multiple licensees, but almost none exist. In the 2020–25 round Tata won all four Odisha discoms, Torrent won DNHDD, and RPSG's Eminent Electricity won Chandigarh with an ₹871 crore bid in November 2020, taking over only on 1 February 2025 after litigation. In plain terms

Nobody can set up a rival network in Ahmedabad or Kolkata, so the incumbents face no day-to-day competition. The franchise model: high revenue, thin margin, finite life

The franchise deserves its own note because it is how private operators demonstrate their skill to state governments. An input-based franchisee takes over billing, collection, maintenance and loss reduction in a defined area (Bhiwandi in 2007 was the first in India) and pays the state discom a fixed input rate per unit of power fed in. If it cuts losses and collects more than the input rate assumes, it keeps the spread. Torrent has cut Bhiwandi's AT&C loss from 58% at takeover to single digits; CESC's Rajasthan franchises have moved their combined loss from 12.78% to 9.21% in a year (Q1 FY27 vs Q1 FY26). Moats of 2–6%: what efficient distribution looks like Deep dive

Torrent's Gujarat licences, CESC Kolkata, Tata Mumbai and Delhi lose a third of the national rate or less. That gap is a profit stream, a regulatory currency and the members' entry ticket to new territory.

Every discom loses some electricity between the power station and the customer's meter. Part of the loss is physics: current heats conductors and transformers, more so on long rural lines and overloaded low-tension networks. The larger part, in India, is commercial: theft by hooking onto lines, tampered or faulty meters, unbilled connections, and bills that are sent but never paid. The industry measures this in two ways. Transmission and distribution (T&D) loss is the share of power fed into the network that is never billed. Aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) loss adds the effect of bills not collected. India's AT&C loss was 15.04% in FY25. The members' best licences run at 1–6%.

Why low losses are worth money

The regulator sets a normative loss for each licence and builds the tariff on it. If the licensee loses less than the norm, it has bought power for customers who are, in effect, paying for more units than the normative calculation assumed; the saving is shared or retained according to the commission's rules. Take CESC Kolkata as a worked example. Its FY26 T&D loss of 6.11% was 1.89 percentage points below WBERC's 8% norm. On roughly 12,800 MU of input, that is about 240 MU saved; at an average power cost near ₹5 a unit, about ₹120 crore a year of efficiency-type gain (an ILLUSTRATIVE calculation; sharing rules vary). Before FY24 the norm was 14.3%, so the same performance was worth roughly four times as much. WBERC then cut it to 9% (FY24), 8.5% (FY25) and 8% (FY26), which is a large part of why CESC's standalone profit growth slowed even as its network got better.

Low losses also earn regulatory goodwill, which is harder to quantify but matters as much. Ahmedabad and Surat were ranked first, with A+ grades, in the Ministry of Power's 14th integrated rating of discoms (FY25), and Torrent reports 99.9% power availability. How they do it

  • Dense, urban, metered networks. Short feeders, underground cables in city cores and few agricultural connections keep technical losses low. Kolkata packs 37 lakh customers into 567 sq km.
  • Enforcement backed by the state. Theft is a crime, and police and courts must act on it. Tata's Odisha discoms booked 145 MW (TPCODL), 137 MW (TPWODL) and 120 MW (TPNODL) of theft load in FY26 and recovered ₹71 crore, ₹48.9 crore and ₹41.8 crore. Without political backing, the same playbook stalls, as Malegaon shows.
  • Metering and IT. Smart meters reveal losses feeder by feeder and enable prepaid billing: Tata Power-DDL has 7.59 lakh, TPWODL 8.13 lakh, TPCODL 7.4 lakh.
  • Collection discipline. At Agra, Torrent collects more than 100% of current billing because it also recovers arrears, which is why Agra's AT&C loss (~5.4%) is lower than its T&D loss (8.42%).

The Odisha experiment: five years of private discoms in a poor state Deep dive

The largest privatisation of distribution in two decades has beaten its annual targets, doubled its profit and still sits at the national loss rate. It is Tata Power's biggest option and its biggest regulatory exposure.

Odisha has tried private distribution twice. The first attempt, in 1999, handed the state's discoms to AES and later Reliance Infrastructure; it ended in 2015 when the regulator revoked Reliance's licences after years of high losses and disputes. The second attempt was designed to avoid that fate. The Odisha Electricity Regulatory Commission (OERC) auctioned 51% stakes in the four discoms through competitive bids, with the state (through GRIDCO) keeping 49%, and wrote "vesting orders" that fixed year-by-year AT&C targets and capex commitments. Tata Power won all four: TP Central Odisha (TPCODL) from 1 June 2020, TP Southern Odisha (TPSODL) and TP Western Odisha (TPWODL) from 1 January 2021, and TP Northern Odisha (TPNODL) from 1 April 2021.

The result is the largest private distribution footprint in India by customers: 99.54 lakh registered consumers, 30,163 MU sold and ₹19,979 crore of revenue in FY26. It is also an entirely different business from Mumbai or Delhi. Southern Odisha (TPSODL) is among the poorest regions in the country, with tribal districts, long rural lines and low consumption per connection; western Odisha (TPWODL) carries heavy industrial load around Jharsuguda and Sambalpur. The inherited workforce is large and the network, at takeover, was in poor shape.

What five years have taught

  • Private operators can cut losses fast where enforcement is backed. Blended AT&C is down to 15.5% and TPNODL is below 10%, against a history of 30%-plus.

  • Earnings are lumpy. ECL reversals, arrears and seasonal billing move quarterly profit more than operations do.
  • It is the template for Tata's 40 million-customer goal. If Uttar Pradesh or another state privatises on similar terms, Odisha is the proof of capability.
In plain terms

Odisha shows a private company can fix a broken discom: losses fell from well over 30% to 15.5% in five years and every annual target was met. The prize is that each point of loss saved is worth about ₹250 crore a year, far more than the discoms earn today.

Regulatory assets and tariff lag: profit the regulator owes you

When tariffs do not keep up with costs, the shortfall becomes income and a receivable. Its size, and how fast it turns into cash, explains most of the P/B gap between Torrent and CESC.

A regulated utility recovers its approved costs through tariffs. When costs rise faster than the regulator allows tariffs to rise (fuel spikes, delayed tariff orders, deliberately frozen tariffs), the utility is entitled to the difference but cannot bill it yet. Under Indian accounting (Ind AS 114), it books that entitlement as "regulatory income" in the profit and loss account and carries a matching "regulatory deferral account debit balance", commonly called a regulatory asset, on the balance sheet. When the regulator later approves a true-up and lets the utility bill the amount, often with a carrying cost for the delay, the asset turns into cash. Until then, profit is running ahead of cash.

The fuel shock that skipped the wires Deep dive

The 2026 West Asia war hit imported coal, LNG and exchange prices. The distribution businesses barely noticed. Mundra was shielded by a government order; Torrent's gas fleet was not.

On 28 February 2026 war broke out in West Asia. The Strait of Hormuz closed until a ceasefire on 7 April; Petronet declared force majeure and GAIL said on 4 March that its Qatari LNG allocation had been cut to zero. India sourced 59% of its 2025 LNG imports (26 million tonnes) from Qatar and the UAE. The Platts JKM spot price hit US$25.39 per million British thermal units (MMBtu) on 3 March; Torrent described spot LNG as "near US$20" in August, and QatarEnergy's force majeure was reportedly extended to November 2026. Freight, crude and coal prices rose with it, the rupee fell to about ₹94.9 per dollar (19 October) and, at home, a long, hot summer pushed the peak to a record. For the three utilities, the shock arrived through their power plants, not their wires.

Mundra: from decade-long loss to fuel-neutral, by government order

Tata Power's 4,150 MW Mundra ultra mega power project (five 830 MW units, held in Coastal Gujarat Power) was won in 2006–07 at a largely fixed tariff and designed to burn imported Indonesian coal. Indonesia's 2011 coal-pricing rule broke its economics, the Supreme Court refused compensatory tariff relief in 2017, and Tata carried Mundra's losses for a decade. The plant was shut from July 2025 for about nine months. On 22 March 2026 the Ministry of Power invoked Section 11 of the Electricity Act, which lets the government direct a generator in extraordinary circumstances, and ordered imported-coal plants to run from 1 April 2026. The direction was extended to 30 September and then, by an order of 25 September, to 31 December 2026; Section 11 was also applied to 112 captive plants for October–December.

Under the direction and a supplementary PPA (SPPA) signed with Gujarat's GUVNL, Mundra is paid its actual coal cost at its actual heat rate plus a normative fixed cost, but no return on equity; management concedes "some under-recovery" is possible. On its 28 July call Tata said cabinet approvals from three more procuring states were expected in August and the fourth in September, that all procurers were taking full capacity, and that it was "very confident" of running the plant continuously until the PPA ends in 2038. Those further approvals were not confirmed in anything available by 22 October. The plant ran at 94% availability in Q1 FY27.

India had about 193 GW of module capacity against only about 30 GW of listed cell capacity in April 2026, so modules are plentiful and cells are scarce.

The policy that makes it work, and the risk in it

The margin rests on ALMM-II. Developers bidding for domestic-content projects need listed cells, and with only about 30 GW of listed cell capacity against far larger module demand, cells command a premium. CESC came later. RPSG Solvanta, a 1.1 GW module plant, has operated since October 2025 (rated CARE A−). 130 years, five shocks: how the sector got its shape

City licences from the 1890s, a privatisation wave in the 2000s, a decade of fuel losses, a reform push, and a war in 2026.

The three companies are survivors of a system that was otherwise nationalised. When state electricity boards took over India's power sector after independence, a handful of private city licensees kept their franchises: CESC in Calcutta, the Tata hydro companies supplying Bombay, and the Ahmedabad and Surat electricity companies. Their history explains their geography. The modern shape of the sector then came from five shocks.

  • Shock 1, privatisation (2002–2010). Delhi's distribution was privatised in 2002 into 51:49 joint ventures, one of which became Tata Power-DDL; Bhiwandi became India's first input-based franchise in 2007 and Agra followed in 2010, both with Torrent. Tata won the Mundra ultra mega power project in 2006–07 at a largely fixed tariff.
  • Shock 2, imported coal and gas (2011–2018). Indonesia's 2011 coal-pricing rule broke Mundra's economics; domestic gas collapsed just as Torrent's Dgen was finished in 2014. The Supreme Court refused compensatory tariffs in 2017. Odisha's first privatisation failed in 2015.
  • Shock 3, reform and crisis (2019–2022). Odisha's discoms went to Tata in 2020–21, RDSS replaced the failed UDAY scheme in 2021, the Late Payment Surcharge Rules arrived in 2022, and a coal crisis in October 2021 left plants with about four days of stock.
  • Shock 4, the renewables pivot (2022–2025). BlackRock and Mubadala invested in TPREL, Tata built TP Solar, Torrent raised a QIP and CESC won Chandigarh. Mundra shut for nine months and FY26 brought the cheapest exchange power in years.
  • Shock 5, the West Asia war (2026). LNG force majeure, Section 11 for imported-coal plants, a record peak, the Nabha and ReNew deals, the Kleros ruling and a rate hike.

Reading an integrated utility: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything these companies report. Each has a formula, a benchmark and at least one way to mislead.

An integrated utility's results are long, full of regulatory jargon and stuffed with numbers on different bases. The trick is to know which handful of figures drive value and to read each one with its definition in mind. This masterclass starts with the profit equation, works through a round-number example, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how it is calculated, a worked rupee example, what moves it, what good looks like, how the three companies compare and where the definition can trip you up. It ends with an illustrative licensee P&L, a scorecard, the traps collected in one place and a ten-minute routine for reading a quarterly result.

The profit equation

Every integrated utility's profit can be split into a regulated core and a set of unregulated layers. The regulated core is the return the regulator allows on the equity invested in licensed assets, plus whatever extra the company earns by beating the regulator's efficiency norms. The unregulated layers are contracted generation (renewables and bid thermal), merchant sales, manufacturing and services. From the total, subtract the interest on debt that the regulator does not cover (projects under construction, acquisitions, factories) and any disallowances. What is left is consolidated PAT; subtract the minority partners' share and you reach profit to owners, the only number a shareholder can claim.

A worked example in round numbers. A licensee with ₹3,000 crore of regulated equity and a 15% allowed return earns ₹450 crore. Suppose it buys 12,000 MU a year, its regulator assumes a 5% loss and it actually loses 3.4%: it saves 1.6% of 12,000 MU, about 192 MU, which at a ₹5.5 power cost is worth roughly ₹106 crore; if it keeps two-thirds under the sharing rules, that is about ₹69 crore. In plain terms

Start with the regulator's promise (equity × allowed return), add the bonus for running the network better than assumed, add whatever the non-regulated businesses make, subtract interest the regulator does not pay for, and then subtract the partners' share. Almost every surprise in a utility's quarterly result can be traced to one of those five steps.

1. Regulated equity

What it is. The equity portion of the asset base on which the regulator allows a return. Formula. Regulated equity ≈ normative equity share (usually 30%) × approved, capitalised capex, less any equity recovered over the asset's life under the regulator's rules. Worked example. A ₹2,000 crore substation and feeder programme, approved and commissioned, adds ₹600 crore of regulated equity; at 15% that is ₹90 crore of annual PAT. What moves it. Approved capex (up), capitalisation timing (assets earn only once commissioned), disallowances (down) and the end of an asset's regulatory life. Benchmark. Growth above 10% a year is strong, 5–10% average, below 5% weak (INDICATIVE ranges). Members. 2. Allowed ROE and efficiency gains

What it is. The post-tax return the regulator allows on regulated equity, plus the extra the licensee earns by beating normative parameters. Formula. Earned regulated return ≈ allowed ROE + (efficiency gains + incentives − disallowances) ÷ regulated equity. Worked example. On ₹3,000 crore of equity, a 15% allowed ROE is ₹450 crore; a ₹69 crore retained efficiency gain lifts the earned return to 17.3%; a ₹50 crore disallowance would cut it to 13.6%. What moves it. The control-period regulations (MERC 15.5% on wires, 17.5% on supply; GERC 13–15% on new assets; CERC 15.5%), performance slabs (MERC's 1.5 points depend on 99.5% wires availability), how tight the norms are, and how promptly true-ups arrive. Benchmark. Earning at or above the allowed rate is good; 1–2 points below is average; more than 3 points below signals tariff lag or disallowance (INDICATIVE).

3. AT&C loss, T&D loss and distribution loss

What it is. The share of power bought that is not converted into cash. Formulas. T&D (or distribution) loss = (units input − units billed) ÷ units input. Billing efficiency = units billed ÷ units input. Collection efficiency = amount collected ÷ amount billed. AT&C loss = 1 − (billing efficiency × collection efficiency). Worked example. A discom buys 100 units, bills 92 and collects 98% of what it bills: T&D loss is 8%, AT&C loss is 1 − (0.92 × 0.98) = 9.84%. If it also collects old arrears worth 3% of the current bill, collection efficiency is 101% and AT&C falls to 7.1%, below the T&D loss. What moves it. Theft enforcement, metering, network upgrades, customer mix (rural and agricultural load lose more), seasonality and collection drives. Benchmark. For an urban licensed area, below 5% is good, 5–10% average, above 10% weak; for a state-type area, AT&C below 12% is good, 12–18% average, above 18% weak (INDICATIVE).

4. Units sold and sales mix

What it is. Million units (MU, i.e. GWh) billed to customers, and the split by customer type. Formula. Units sold = units input × (1 − T&D loss). Worked example. Kolkata sold 11,990 MU in FY26 (including export and banking) on ₹9,732 crore of revenue from operations, about ₹8.12 a unit; EBITDA was ₹2,673 crore, about ₹2.23 a unit, and PAT ₹852 crore, about ₹0.71 a unit. What moves it. Weather, industrial activity, new connections, open-access migration and rooftop solar. Benchmark. Volume growth of 4–6% a year is normal for an urban licence (INDICATIVE); mix matters more than total, because industrial and commercial customers cross-subsidise households. Members. FY26 sales: Tata 47.9 BU (Odisha 30.2, Delhi 11.0, Mumbai 6.1, Ajmer 0.65), CESC ~21.0 BU, Torrent ~30 BU. Q1 FY27 growth: Odisha +10.4%, TPDDL +3.9%, Kolkata +4.9%, NPCL +10%, CPDL +16%, Torrent purchases +3.4%. Why the mix matters. High-tension industrial and commercial customers typically pay tariffs above their cost of supply, and the surplus subsidises households and farms. A licence such as Surat, Dahej or DNHDD, where industrial load dominates, is therefore richer per unit than a residential city, and more exposed if large users leave through green open access (available from 100 kW under the 2022 rules) or install captive solar. Cross-subsidy and additional surcharges blunt the incentive to leave but do not remove it. Greater Noida's "superior HT:LT mix" (CARE's phrase) is one reason NPCL earns a 7.6% PAT margin. Definition trap. Torrent reports units purchased, not sold, in its quarterly material; CESC's Kolkata figure includes export and banking; a cost-plus licensee's profit does not rise in proportion to units.

5. The ACS-ARR gap

What it is. The difference between a discom's average cost of supply (ACS) per unit and its average revenue realised (ARR) per unit. Formula. ACS-ARR gap = (total cost ÷ units input) − (total revenue including subsidy ÷ units input). Worked example. A discom that spends ₹7.09 per unit input and realises ₹6.88 has a 21-paise gap; on 100 BU that is a ₹2,100 crore annual loss. What moves it. Tariff revisions, subsidy payments, losses and power cost. Benchmark. Zero or negative is good (the RDSS target); under 25 paise average; above 50 paise weak (INDICATIVE). National series. ₹0.69 (FY21), ₹0.22 (FY22), ₹0.45 (FY23), ₹0.21 provisional (FY24) and about ₹0.06–0.07 in FY25 on PFC's measures; CESC's September 2026 presentation cites 6 paise. Members. Not reported as such by the three: for a private licensee, the equivalent is the regulatory-asset build-up, which is the next KPI. Definition trap. The national gap is quoted on several bases (subsidy received vs subsidy booked, cash-adjusted or not), which can differ by tens of paise in the same year.

6. Regulatory assets and deferral balances

What it is. The cumulative tariff shortfall the regulator owes the licensee (or, if negative, the excess it owes customers). Formula. Closing balance = opening balance + regulatory income booked (under-recovery) + carrying cost − amounts recovered through tariffs or true-ups. Worked example. A licensee whose costs rise ₹500 crore above what the frozen tariff recovers books ₹500 crore of regulatory income and a ₹500 crore asset; if the regulator allows recovery three years later with a 9% carrying cost, it collects about ₹650 crore, but in the meantime it has borrowed to fund the gap. What moves it. Tariff freezes, fuel spikes, delayed true-ups, court orders (the Supreme Court's four-year liquidation timetable) and changes in tax rules (CESC's balance was cut ₹752 crore on recomputation under the new tax regime). Benchmark. Below 5% of annual revenue is good, 5–15% average, above 15% weak (INDICATIVE).

7. Collection efficiency

What it is. Cash collected as a share of amounts billed in the period. Formula. Collection efficiency = collections ÷ billing (× 100). Worked example. Government department dues (water pumps, street lights), disconnection rules (Odisha's heatwave no-disconnection order to 15 June 2026), prepaid smart meters and arrears campaigns. Benchmark. 15. Cash conversion: operating cash flow to operating profit

What it is. How much of the operating profit a utility reports arrives as cash in the same year. Formula. Cash conversion = cash from operations (CFO) ÷ operating profit (screener basis, before other income). Worked example. Tata Power's FY26 CFO of ₹5,993 crore against ₹13,271 crore of operating profit is a 45% conversion, against about 102% in FY25 (₹12,680 crore on ₹12,450 crore); Torrent converted 98% (₹5,464 crore on ₹5,577 crore); CESC 112% (₹4,057 crore on ₹3,624 crore), up from about 91% in FY25 (all calculated from screener). What moves it. Regulatory-asset build-up (profit booked, cash deferred), working capital in EPC and manufacturing, state receivables (Odisha's panchayat dues), surcharge timing and, in the other direction, true-up collections and receivable sales. Benchmark. Above 90% over a rolling two-year period is good, 70–90% average, below 70% weak (INDICATIVE); single years swing.

Why it matters here. In a regulated business the cash should follow the profit closely, because tariffs are designed to recover costs plus a return. A persistent gap means one of three things: the regulator is deferring recovery (regulatory assets), customers or governments are paying late (receivables), or the unregulated businesses are absorbing working capital. Tata's FY26 drop coincided with rising regulatory assets (₹9,009 crore to ₹10,790 crore over the twelve months to June 2026), the TP Solar ramp-up and EPC receivables; CESC's jump reflects improving earnings quality as regulatory income fell. Definition trap. Screener's operating profit excludes other income and regulatory income, so a utility that books large regulatory income (CESC) can show conversion above 100% while its company-basis EBITDA conversion is lower (₹4,057 crore ÷ ₹4,707 crore = 86%). In plain terms

Nearly every headline number in this sector has a twin on a different basis. Before you compare two companies or two periods, check four things: whose profit (all of it or the owners' share), which loss (billing only or including collection), which period (a quarter or a year), and which debt (gross, net, or the aggregator's version).

How to read an integrated utility's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Profit to owners first (1 minute). Find PAT attributable to owners and its year-on-year change; then reported PAT and the minority share. If reported PAT grew and owners' PAT did not, find out which part-owned subsidiary drove it. Red flag: minority share rising for several quarters.
  2. One-offs (1 minute). Look for true-up income, carrying-cost orders, ECL reversals, tax gains, disallowances and exceptional items (TPDDL ₹783 crore, Torrent's ₹637 crore tax gain and ₹171 crore disallowance). Strip them before judging the trend.
  3. Regulatory income and the deferral balance (1 minute). How much of EBITDA is regulatory income, and did the balance rise? Red flag: balance growing faster than revenue with no true-up in sight.
  4. Distribution volumes and losses (1 minute). Units sold by licence and losses against the same quarter last year and against the regulatory target. Red flag: losses up year on year in a licensed area without a clear seasonal or one-off reason (Torrent Ahmedabad Q1 FY27: 10.8% vs 7.9%).
  5. Regulated equity and capitalisation (1 minute). Is regulated equity growing ~10% a year? Was CWIP capitalised? Red flag: CWIP growing while regulated equity stalls.
  6. Generation: availability, PLF and fuel (1 minute). Thermal availability and PLF; gas-plant PLF; Section 11 status for Mundra; fuel-cost commentary. Red flag: a regulated plant falling below normative availability, which cuts its capacity charge.
  7. Renewables commissioning and CUF (1 minute). MW commissioned vs guidance (Tata Q2 target 800–900 MW vs 419 MW done), curtailment, CUF. Red flag: capacity ready but stranded by transmission.
  8. Unregulated engines (1 minute). TP Solar output and margin, rooftop revenue, merchant volumes and prices, coal-mine income. Red flag: manufacturing margin falling as national cell capacity arrives.
  9. Leverage and cash (1 minute). Net debt, ND/EBITDA and ND/equity on the company's basis; capex in the quarter vs annual guidance; interest cost trend. Red flag: interest growing much faster than EBITDA (Torrent +38% in Q1 FY27).
  10. Regulatory calendar and contingencies (1 minute). Tariff orders, true-ups, court rulings, franchise renewals (Bhiwandi), arbitration (Kleros), acquisition closings (ReNew). Red flag: a material contingency with no provision and no update.

For CESC there is no earnings call: guidance comes from its quarterly investor updates and conference presentations (the latest dated 18 September 2026), so the checklist has to be run on the presentation and the notes to accounts. For Tata and Torrent, the call transcripts are filed within a few days and are where management explains seasonal losses, one-offs and commissioning slippage.

In plain terms

Read a utility's results in this order: what did shareholders actually earn, what was one-off, how much was IOU from the regulator, how did the wires perform, are the new projects arriving on time, and is the debt growing faster than the earnings. If all six look fine, the quarter was fine, whatever the headline says.

Why the premium, and why CESC misses it

Indian regulators allow far higher nominal returns than Western ones: 14–18% post-tax, against allowed returns of roughly 9.5–10.5% for US regulated utilities and a real return of around 6% in the UK (INDICATIVE, from memory-based ranges). Yet the members' earned consolidated ROEs, 10–12.5%, are not much higher than US utilities' allowed returns, because so much of their capital is unregulated or under construction. Tata and Torrent therefore trade at a 40–70% P/E premium to US regulated utilities not for their current returns but for growth: regulated equity compounding at around 10% a year, a renewables and storage build that could double EBITDA by FY30, and the option of new distribution territory. CESC trades below every global peer and below POWERGRID because the market doubts the growth (one regulator, a frozen tariff) and discounts the regulatory balances in its book value.

Scale differs too. At ₹94.9 to the dollar, Tata Power's FY26 revenue was about US$6.6 billion, Torrent's about US$3.1 billion and CESC's about US$2.0 billion, small next to NextEra or Iberdrola, which report revenues several times larger. Global investors therefore treat the members as emerging-market growth utilities rather than as defensive yield plays, which also makes the shares more sensitive to interest rates and to the rupee.

How to value an integrated utility: sum of the parts

The practical method is to value the pieces separately. The regulated licences can be valued on a multiple of regulated equity or on P/E in line with POWERGRID's 14–15× and a premium for growth; contracted renewables on EV/EBITDA or discounted cash flow at a project cost of equity; merchant and fuel-exposed plants on a cycle-average spread; manufacturing on a cyclical industrial multiple; and then subtract net debt and the minorities' share at each level. The approach shows why Tata's group multiple can look high while its regulated core is not: much of the value the market assigns is to TPREL (where 11.43% belongs to the BlackRock–Mubadala consortium), TP Solar and the pipeline. It also shows why CESC's discount is so large: its Kolkata licence is valued as if part of the ₹5,257–8,666 crore of regulatory balances will never be collected, and Purvah as if it will need dilutive equity.

Investors pay more for Indian utilities than for American ones because they expect the Indian ones to grow much faster, not because they earn more today. That makes the shares behave more like growth stocks: when interest rates rise or a big project hits trouble, they fall harder than a typical utility. CESC is the exception, priced as though its growth and its regulator's IOUs are both in doubt.

Technology and disruption: what could erode the wires

Rooftops, batteries, open access and parallel licences threaten the best customers. Storage threatens gas. Cell capacity threatens the factory margin.

A distribution monopoly looks permanent, but its economics rest on a few customers paying more than their cost of supply. Technology and policy are slowly giving those customers alternatives. Six developments matter for the members.

  • Open access and captive renewables. The Green Energy Open Access Rules of 2022 let consumers with 100 kW or more of load buy renewable power directly from generators using the discom's wires. Industrial and commercial users, who cross-subsidise households and farms, are the most likely to go. Cross-subsidy and additional surcharges blunt the incentive, and Tata and Torrent sell commercial-and-industrial solar themselves (Torrent has ~1,161 MWp of C&I projects in its pipeline), but each departing megawatt raises the tariff burden on those who stay. Licences with heavy industrial load, such as Surat, Dahej, DNHDD and Greater Noida, are most exposed.
  • Rooftop solar and home batteries. PM Surya Ghar aims at one crore rooftop installations. Every rooftop cuts the bills of a discom's best-paying residential customers. Tata Power is on both sides: its rooftop revenue reached ₹4,800 crore in FY26, it installs about 30,000 systems a month against about 1,000 two years ago, and it targets a 25% market share; Tata Power-DDL alone crossed 10,000 rooftop installations (160 MWp).
  • Smart and prepaid meters. RDSS funds smart meters across the country; the members have installed hundreds of thousands (TPWODL 8.13 lakh, Tata Power-DDL 7.59 lakh). Meters cut losses and speed collection, which is good for Odisha's AT&C, but they cost money, face consumer resistance and make billing disputes more visible.
  • Batteries against gas. Battery-storage tariffs in Indian auctions roughly halved in 18 months, from about ₹3.72 lakh to ₹1.775 lakh per MW per month. Cheaper storage competes directly with peaking gas plants like Dgen and Sugen, and with merchant evening power generally. Torrent's answer is to own storage (Raigad); Tata's is to build pumped storage and batteries at scale (131.2 MWh commissioned in Q2 FY27).
  • Parallel licensing. The draft Electricity (Amendment) Bill of November 2025 would let several licensees supply customers over the same network, with network charges set by the regulator, and push towards cost-reflective tariffs. After pushback the government said in February 2026 it was revising the draft; no evidence was found that it has been introduced. If passed, it would threaten the incumbents' retail monopolies in Mumbai, Ahmedabad and Kolkata while opening state discom areas to them; Tata says it is "well-positioned" for parallel licensing and PPP models.
  • Solar overcapacity. India had about 193 GW of module capacity against about 30 GW of listed cell capacity in April 2026. Module margins are already compressing; cell margins hold only while ALMM-II binds. TP Solar's 25% EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27 is best treated as a peak-cycle number.

In plain terms

Big customers can increasingly make or buy their own green power, and cheap batteries are making gas plants less useful. The companies that sell rooftops, batteries and solar contracts themselves, Tata most of all, turn the threat into a product. Those relying on gas plants or on a few rich industrial customers have the most to lose.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

A regulated floor with a large, debt-funded build on top. The argument is about whether the build earns its cost of capital and whether the regulators keep their side of the bargain.

The bull case

  • A compounding floor. Regulated equity grows about 10% a year (Tata +10.8%), and new MERC and GERC rules raise the ceiling on returns for efficient operators, which these three are.
  • Losses as an engine. Every point of AT&C saved in Odisha is worth about ₹250 crore a year (ILLUSTRATIVE), and FY26 targets were met in all four discoms.
  • Regulatory assets turning to cash. The Supreme Court's four-year liquidation order is already producing true-ups (TPDDL ₹783 crore PAT); CESC's balances could unlock value if WBERC complies.
  • Contracted growth. Tata's 5.3 GW, Torrent's 4.6 GW and Purvah's 3.0 GWp under construction, Raigad's ₹1,680 crore annual storage revenue and Nabha's ~₹1,000 crore EBITDA are largely contracted.
  • Privatisation optionality. UP, Maharashtra and the Amendment Bill could hand incumbents new territory; Odisha proves they can run it.
  • Valuation support at the low end. CESC at 11× owners' earnings with a 4.6% yield and all three near 52-week lows after a 23–35% fall.

The bear case

  • Leverage rising into higher rates. Net debt ₹84,346 crore (3.16× EBITDA), CFO covering ~60% of investment, and an RBI repo hike to 5.50%.
  • Earnings quality. Tata's owners' PAT fell 5.6% while reported PAT rose; true-ups, ECL reversals and regulatory income flatter the trend.
  • Regulatory risk. WBERC's freeze; Odisha's Q1 FY27 losses 4–15 points above FY27 targets; tighter norms; disallowances such as Torrent's ₹171 crore.
  • Fuel and contract cliffs. Mundra's direction expires 31 December 2026 (and earns no ROE under the SPPA); Torrent's gas fleet is uneconomic at US$20 LNG; Bhiwandi's franchise expires in January 2027.
  • Contingencies. The Kleros award (~₹6,100 crore with interest, ILLUSTRATIVE) is unprovided; CESC needs ₹3,500–4,000 crore of equity.
  • Peak-cycle manufacturing profits. TP Solar's 25% margin rests on a cell shortage that new capacity is closing.

The two cases are not mutually exclusive, and the three companies sit in different places between them. Torrent is the purest version of the bull case on the wires and the clearest version of the bear case on fuel and re-levering. Tata Power carries the most of both: the biggest regulated growth and pipeline, and the most dilution, contingencies and execution risk. CESC's bull case is almost entirely regulatory and valuation-driven: if WBERC resumes timely true-ups and Purvah is funded without heavy dilution, its multiple has the most room to move; if not, the discount persists.

What would change the balance? On the positive side, Q2 FY27 results showing Odisha losses back near target, confirmation of Mundra's remaining SPPAs, a WBERC true-up for FY23 or a base-tariff revision, and on-schedule commissioning (Tata's 2.5–2.7 GW in FY27). On the negative side, a Singapore Court of Appeal loss on Kleros, non-renewal of Bhiwandi, slower commissioning with rising interest, or another year of rising regulatory balances at CESC and TPDDL.

In plain terms

The optimist sees dependable toll roads funding a profitable clean-energy build at a moment when the shares are cheap. The pessimist sees companies borrowing heavily, booking IOUs as profit and relying on governments that are slow to pay and slow to raise prices. Both are describing the same companies; the next few quarters' results will show which description fits each one better.
