Indian copper: a miner in a country of smelters

A complete guide to India's copper chain, from a 750-metre-deep shaft in Rajasthan to the cable in a data centre: why the country refines twenty-five to thirty times more copper than it mines, why smelters are now paying miners to take their concentrate, how Hindustan Copper, the only listed copper miner, turns a record London price into a 54% margin, and whether its plan to triple ore output by FY30 can be delivered on the calendar the market is paying for.

00

The one-page view: a miner in a country of smelters

India refines twenty-five to thirty tonnes of copper for every tonne it digs. In 2026 that is exactly the wrong way round.

India is a copper processor, not a copper miner. The United States Geological Survey puts India's 2025 mine output at about 23,000 tonnes of copper, roughly 0.1% of the world's 23 million tonnes, and its reserves at 2.2 million tonnes, about 0.2% of the world's 980 million. Yet Indian refineries produced about 620,000 tonnes of cathode in calendar 2025, around 2% of world refined supply, and roughly 704,000 tonnes in FY26. The gap is filled by ships: India imported 1.57 million tonnes of copper concentrate in FY26, plus anodes, cathode, scrap and wire. The five main copper import lines cost about $16.6 billion, close to ₹1.46 lakh crore at an assumed FY26 average rupee of ₹88 to the dollar (an indicative conversion).

Inside that import-heavy chain sits one listed miner. Hindustan Copper Ltd (HCL) holds every operating copper mining lease in the country. It mined 3.67 million tonnes of ore in FY26 at three complexes, Malanjkhand in Madhya Pradesh, Khetri in Rajasthan and the Indian Copper Complex in Jharkhand, and recovered 27,421 tonnes of copper contained in concentrate. HCL no longer smelts. Its Khetri smelter was suspended in December 2008 and its Ghatsila smelter and refinery in December 2019. It now sells concentrate to the custom smelters on India's west coast, mainly Hindalco's Birla Copper at Dahej, and exports the rest.

Five forces explain nearly everything about this sub-sector in 2026.

  • The London Metal Exchange (LME) price cycle. Copper set a record cash price of $14,850 a tonne on 17 August 2026, against an average of $9,947 in calendar 2025. Grids, renewables, electric vehicles and the power needs of AI data centres are the demand story. Yet the International Copper Study Group (ICSG) forecasts a refined-copper surplus of about 104,000 tonnes in 2026 and 368,000 tonnes in 2027. The bridge between "record price" and "surplus" is the United States, where tariff fears pulled about 1.6 million tonnes of metal into warehouses, and a shortage of concentrate rather than of metal.
  • Where value sits in the chain. The fee a miner pays a smelter to process concentrate, the treatment and refining charge (TC/RC), fell to an annual benchmark of zero for 2026, and spot charges are around minus $235 a dry tonne. Smelters are paying miners. Hindalco's copper business earned a 4.0% EBITDA margin in FY26 and its profit fell 7%; Vedanta's copper business made an EBITDA loss of ₹48 crore on ₹31,069 crore of revenue. HCL's 27,000 tonnes of mined copper earned an operating profit of ₹1,463 crore, about half of Hindalco's copper EBITDA from a business eighteen times its volume.
  • Ore, grade and HCL's Vision 2030. HCL plans to lift ore output from 3.67 million tonnes to 4.71, 5.18, 5.90 and then 12.2 million tonnes between FY27 and FY30, with ₹7,189 crore of capex. More than half the increase is booked in the final year, a quarter of the target belongs economically to a JSW company under a mine-developer-operator (MDO) contract that pays HCL 12.5% of revenue, and FY26 itself came in at 84% of the ore target management set in September 2025.
  • Import dependence and policy. The Tuticorin smelter's forced closure in May 2018 turned India from a net exporter of refined copper into a net importer. Policy now pushes the other way: a ₹16,300 crore National Critical Mineral Mission, nil customs duty on concentrate and scrap, a quality-control order that keeps uncertified foreign cathode out, record mineral-block auctions, and new smelters at Mundra and Dahej. Abroad, the US imposed a 50% Section 232 tariff on the copper content of wire, cable and other semi-finished products, and has still not decided on a duty on refined copper.
  • State ownership and the stock. The government sold 6% of HCL in an offer for sale in August 2026, cutting its stake from 66.14% to 60.14%. ICRA upgraded HCL to AAA on 29 September 2026. A new chairman, Anupam Misra, a marketing and trading executive, took charge on 1 July 2026. The share price has fallen about 30% from the pre-offer close, to ₹399 on 23 October 2026, with no company-specific trigger that we could find.

In plain terms

Copper is dug up as rock that is less than 1% copper, crushed and floated into a powder that is about a quarter copper, then melted and refined into plates that are 99.99% copper. In most years the people who melt and refine it earn a steady fee. In 2026 there is not enough powder to go round, so the fee has gone below zero and the people who own the mines keep almost all the profit. India owns very few mines and a lot of smelters. Its one listed miner, Hindustan Copper, is therefore sitting on the best economics in the chain, and the open question is whether it can produce much more of the stuff before the cycle turns.

Two cautions run through everything that follows. First, HCL's results are a lever on the copper price: in FY26 revenue rose 49% and profit doubled on only 12% more copper sold, and the arithmetic works in reverse. At the October 2026 rupee of about ₹94.9, every $1,000 a tonne on the LME moves HCL's annual revenue by roughly ₹260 crore (indicative). Second, HCL's volumes have gone nowhere for a decade: it produced 31,578 tonnes of metal in concentrate in FY16 and 27,421 tonnes in FY26. The stock's valuation, about 32 times trailing earnings and over 20 times EBITDA, is a bet that the next five years will look nothing like the last ten.

01

Rock to rod: how copper moves from a mine to a cable

Seven stages, four different products called "copper", and a profit pool that moves up and down the chain with the cycle.

A tonne of refined copper starts as roughly 130 to 140 tonnes of rock. Indian copper ore is a sulphide, mostly chalcopyrite, with between 0.7% and 1.3% copper by weight. Everything that happens between the blast and the cable is about removing the other 99%, and each step has its own owner, its own unit of account and its own way of being paid.

Mining. HCL drills, blasts and hauls ore at Malanjkhand, where the open pit has been exhausted and mining is moving underground, at Khetri and Kolihan in Rajasthan, where shafts go deeper than 700 metres, and at Surda and Kendadih in Jharkhand. The unit is tonnes of ore and its grade, the percentage of copper in it. Beneficiation. HCL's concentrator plants crush and grind the ore and use froth flotation to lift the copper minerals into a concentrate of 17–26% copper, averaging around 22–23%. The unit is tonnes of metal in concentrate (MIC), the copper contained in that powder. This is where HCL's product ends.

Smelting and refining. Custom smelters buy concentrate, from HCL and, far more, from Chile, Indonesia, Peru and Australia. They melt it into matte, convert it to blister and anode copper of about 99%, then electro-refine it into cathode plates of 99.99%, the grade the LME will accept for delivery. Smelting releases sulphur dioxide, captured as sulphuric acid at roughly three tonnes per tonne of copper, and the anode slime left in the refining tanks yields gold, silver, selenium and tellurium. India's smelters are Hindalco's Birla Copper at Dahej (500,000 tonnes a year) and Adani Enterprises' Kutch Copper at Mundra (500,000 tonnes), both in Gujarat. Vedanta's Silvassa refinery (about 222,000 tonnes after debottlenecking) refines imported anode and blister because its Tuticorin smelter has been shut since 2018.

Rod. Most cathode is melted again and continuously cast into 8 mm wire rod (CCR or CCWR), the feedstock for wire drawing. Hindalco is the world's second-largest rod maker outside China; Vedanta made 283,000 tonnes of rod in FY26; HCL's Taloja plant now draws rod for third parties for a fee. Fabrication. Cable and wire makers such as Polycab, KEI, Havells, Finolex, RR Kabel and Apar draw rod into wire, insulate it and sell it as building wire, power cable and winding wire. Others make tubes for air-conditioners, strip, foil and brass. End use is buildings, the power grid, industry, consumer durables and vehicles. A large parallel loop of scrap, collected domestically and imported, is melted back into rod and alloys and supplies close to two-fifths of India's copper.

Who earns what. The miner's revenue is the LME-linked value of the copper it sells, minus the TC/RC it pays the smelter, plus whatever the smelter pays for gold and silver in the concentrate. Its costs are drilling, blasting, haulage, hoisting from depth, power, water, explosives, contractor fees and a royalty to the state that rises with the copper price. When the price is high the miner captures the metal rent, the gap between price and cost. The custom smelter earns the TC/RC, the "free metal" it recovers above the payable percentage it paid for, by-product credits, a domestic premium over LME, and a rod conversion margin. The metal itself passes through. The fabricator passes metal through again, pricing cable on LME or MCX plus a premium, and earns on volume times a conversion spread, brand and distribution.

This is why the same copper price can make one part of the chain rich and leave another breaking even. A $1,000 a tonne rise in LME adds roughly ₹240–260 crore a year to HCL's revenue, most of which reaches profit. Vedanta says the same rise adds only ₹55–70 crore a year to its copper EBITDA, from a business selling six times as much copper, because it buys the metal at the same LME-linked price it sells at.

In plain terms

A miner owns copper; a smelter rents it. The miner is paid for the metal and bears all the price risk. The smelter is paid a fee for transforming somebody else's metal, so its profit depends on the size of that fee and on what it can sell on the side, chiefly acid and precious metals. A cable maker is paid for turning metal into a branded, approved product. When you read that "copper is at a record", ask which of the three the company in front of you is.

Two cross-cutting features complete the picture. First, India's chain is broken at Step 2: one domestic miner produces about 27,000 tonnes of copper in concentrate while India's smelters need well over half a million tonnes, so the concentrate trade is overwhelmingly imported. Second, the chain runs downhill into a very large, fragmented fabrication industry that is the real customer. The cable makers covered in our cables sub-sector report are the closest real-time gauge of Indian copper demand, and the power-sector build-out in our power generation report is the largest single source of new orders for them.

02

Four numbers called "production": sizing Indian copper

Ore, metal in concentrate, refined cathode and total demand differ by a factor of a hundred. Always ask which one is meant.

"India's copper production" can mean four quite different things, and the most common error in writing about the sector is to mix them. In FY26, HCL mined 3.67 million tonnes of ore; that ore contained, after concentration, 27,421 tonnes of copper in concentrate; India's smelters and refiners produced about 704,000 tonnes of primary refined cathode, mostly from imported concentrate and anode; and Hindalco estimates total Indian copper demand, including scrap, at about 1.56 million tonnes.

History. India's primary refined output peaked at 830.5 kt in FY18, when Hindalco's Dahej (413.8 kt), Vedanta's Tuticorin smelter (217.0 kt), Vedanta's Silvassa refinery (186.4 kt) and HCL's Ghatsila plant (13.3 kt) were all running. Tuticorin was closed in May 2018 and output collapsed to 454.3 kt in FY19 and a trough of 363.6 kt in FY21, when the pandemic and HCL's smelter shutdown added to the loss. Recovery came from Hindalco's debottlenecking, Silvassa's ramp-up on imported anode and, from FY25, Adani's new Kutch smelter, which went from 22 kt in FY25 to 114.2 kt in FY26. Even so, FY26's ~704 kt is still about 15% below FY18. ICSG data show India's refined output up another 23% in January to August 2026; if that pace holds through FY27, output of roughly 860 kt would pass the FY18 peak for the first time (indicative).

In plain terms

India uses about 1.5 million tonnes of copper a year, worth very roughly ₹1.6–1.9 lakh crore at FY26 prices (an indicative estimate). About two-fifths is recycled scrap, about two-fifths is cathode made in Gujarat and Silvassa from imported raw material, and the rest is imported cathode. The copper actually mined in India would cover less than 2% of that. The per-person number says demand can grow for decades; the 2026 quarterly numbers say that at today's prices buyers are cutting back.

The market's value is easiest to frame in rupees. At FY26's average LME of $10,817 a tonne and an assumed ₹88 rupee, a tonne of refined copper cost about ₹9.5 lakh before premiums and duties, and around ₹13 lakh at October 2026's $14,215 and ₹94.9. A 1.56 million-tonne market is therefore a ₹1.6–2.0 lakh crore metal flow, larger than the combined revenue of most Indian sub-sectors, but almost all of it is pass-through: the profit pool sits in a few thin layers of fees, premiums and one thick layer of mining rent.

03

Who uses India's copper: grids, homes and a price that bites

The structural story is power, buildings and data centres. The 2026 story is buyers cutting back at $14,000 a tonne.

Copper is the default conductor of electricity, and almost every end-use in India is a way of moving power. The Ministry of Mines' Copper Vision Document, quoted in HCL's August 2026 presentation, splits Indian demand into building construction 24%, infrastructure 19%, industrial 17%, diverse uses 17%, consumer durables 12% and transport 11%.

Power is the backbone. India's target of 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by around 2030–32 is copper-intensive at every step. Thermal plants use roughly a tonne of copper per megawatt; onshore wind and solar parks, with their long cable runs, inverters and transformers, use about three to five tonnes per megawatt; offshore wind about eight to ten. These are industry rules of thumb rather than measured Indian figures, so we label them indicative. Transmission adds more: every green-energy corridor needs transformers, switchgear and substation cabling. Our power generation report covers the capacity plan.

Data centres are the newest driver. Estimates of copper per megawatt of data-centre capacity range from about 11.6 t/MW on a construction-only scope (Linesight, July 2026) to about 27 t/MW for one Microsoft facility and 20–40 t/MW in Chinese compilations, and up to 66 t/MW on a ten-year life-cycle basis (Schneider). We present a range of ~10–40 t/MW, again indicative. India's planned data-centre capacity, including AdaniConneX's 2 GW ambition, would add tens of thousands of tonnes of demand over several years: material at the margin, small next to buildings and the grid.

Vehicles and appliances. An average car uses about 23 kg of copper and a luxury or hybrid car about 40 kg (ICSG data quoted by IBM); a battery-electric car uses about three to four times an internal-combustion car, often cited at 80 kg or more. The PM E-DRIVE scheme carries a ₹10,900 crore outlay. Room air-conditioners use inner-grooved copper tube, which Hindalco now makes at Vadodara, and the production-linked incentive schemes for electronics and white goods add local demand.

A mining monopoly, a smelting oligopoly and a scrapyard

Each layer of the chain has a different structure, and the structure explains who has pricing power.

Mining is a state monopoly. HCL holds all the operating copper mining leases in India and, by its own count, access to about 45% of the country's copper ore reserves and resources. Its annual report puts its share of India's reserves higher still: "mining lease of more than 80% of country's copper reserves". The Indian Bureau of Mines counted nine copper mining leases covering 3,916.85 hectares on 31 March 2023, and five reporting mines, all in the public sector.

The monopoly is eroding at the edges. Since the 2015 amendment to the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, new leases are awarded only by auction, and copper is on India's 2023 list of 30 critical minerals. The government auctioned a record 141 mineral blocks in calendar 2025 alone. HCL itself was preferred bidder for the Baghwari-Khirkhori copper block in Madhya Pradesh in January 2026 and was allotted the Bodal block in Chhattisgarh for exploration with MECL. Hindalco told analysts in August 2026 that it is "drilling holes" on a copper exploration block, and Vedanta and JSW are both looking. More subtly, HCL's own largest expansion, the 3 MTPA Rakha and Chapri mine in Jharkhand, will be built and run by JSW's South West Mining under a 20-year mine-developer-operator contract that gives JSW the first right to buy the concentrate.

The custom smelter is a toll processor whose toll has gone negative. Hindalco, Kutch and, in normal times, Tuticorin buy concentrate at LME minus TC/RC and sell cathode and rod at LME plus a premium. A refinery without a smelter, like Silvassa, does not even collect the acid credit, which is one reason Vedanta's copper business broke even.

The recycler profits when high prices pull scrap out of homes and factories. Scrap trades at a discount to LME, and the discount widens in rupees when LME is high. Hindalco told analysts in August 2026 that recycling returns are "quite more attractive" than smelting even after counting acid, which is why it is building scrap-melting and e-waste plants. HCL is pivoting its idle Gujarat plant the same way, through Lohum.

The fabricator passes metal through and earns on the spread and the brand. Polycab and KEI buy rod at LME or MCX plus a premium, hedge their exposure, and sell cable priced off the same metal reference plus a conversion margin. Their profit is volume times spread; their returns, with Polycab's ROCE at 33%, come from distribution, approvals and brand rather than metal. Rising copper prices inflate their revenue and can cause short-term inventory gains or losses, but do not change the economics much.

Smelters paying miners: the TC/RC inversion Deep dive

The fee that once paid India's smelters has gone below zero. Profit has moved from Dahej and Mundra to Malanjkhand and Chile.

For half a century the copper concentrate trade has worked one way. A miner ships concentrate to a smelter and is paid the LME value of the copper in it, less two charges: a treatment charge (TC) in dollars per dry metric tonne of concentrate, for smelting it, and a refining charge (RC) in US cents per pound of payable copper, for refining the result. By convention the two move together, with the RC in cents equal to one-tenth of the TC in dollars: a "$80 and 8 cents" benchmark. Each year a large Chilean miner, usually Antofagasta, and a group of Chinese smelters settle a benchmark that sets the tone for long-term contracts worldwide. In 2026 that benchmark is zero.

Why it happened. Smelting capacity has grown much faster than mine supply, above all in China, which now owns about 48% of world refining and has not closed surplus plants. Mine supply has disappointed: Panama's Cobre Panama mine was shut in November 2023, Ivanhoe's Kamoa-Kakula in the Congo suffered seismic events in May 2025, Freeport's Grasberg in Indonesia had a mud-rush incident in September 2025 that left its mill at about two-thirds of normal into late 2026, Chile's output fell about 7% in January to August 2026, and Mount Isa in Australia closed in July 2025. ICSG now expects world mine output to grow just 1.3% in 2026, and its January to August data show mine output down 0.9% while refined output rose 1.7%, the gap filled by scrap and by smelters drawing down concentrate stocks. When there is more smelting capacity than concentrate, smelters bid against each other for feed, and the bidding currency is the TC.

The fall was abrupt. The 2024 benchmark was $80 and 8.0 cents. The 2025 benchmark, settled in December 2024 between Antofagasta and Jiangxi, was $21.25 and 2.125 cents. The 2026 benchmark was settled at zero. Spot charges went negative in early 2025 and were around minus $235 to minus $240 per dry tonne in October 2026. Hindalco told analysts in August 2026 that its all-in TC/RC for April to June 2026 was minus 26 to minus 30 cents per pound, roughly minus $570 to minus $660 per tonne of copper. At LME Week in London in October 2026, the industry's annual gathering, Ivanhoe's Robert Friedland warned that miners and smelters might fail to agree any 2027 benchmark at all.

In plain terms

The TC/RC is the price of a smelting service. Like any price, it falls when there is too much of the service and not enough demand for it. The world has built far more smelters than it has mines to feed them, so smelters are now competing for concentrate by offering to do the work for nothing, or even paying miners for the privilege. That is good news for anyone who owns a mine, and bad news for anyone who owns only a smelter.

What the inversion is worth to HCL

HCL sells its concentrate on spot TC/RC terms, not the annual benchmark, management told analysts in September 2025, so it captures negative spot charges in full. "Normal" deductions used to be about $100–150 per tonne of copper. If 2026 contracts run near Hindalco's minus 26–30 cents a pound, the deduction has become a credit of about $570–660 a tonne. The swing from a ~$125 deduction to a ~$600 credit is roughly $700 a tonne of copper, worth about ₹180–190 crore a year at 27,000 tonnes and ₹94 to the dollar. That is our estimate; HCL does not disclose its contract terms. It is comparable to a $700 move in the LME itself, and it explains perhaps a third of the gap between HCL's FY26 realisation and the LME average.

The global miners show the same effect. Antofagasta booked TC/RC as a credit of $18.7 million in the first half of 2026, against a $29.7 million expense a year earlier. Southern Copper's cash cost net of by-products was nine cents a pound in Q3 2026. In plain terms

In FY26 HCL's 27,000 tonnes of mined copper earned about half as much as Hindalco's 487,000 tonnes of smelted and rolled copper. Per tonne, the mine earned nine times the smelter. Hindalco is still profitable because it sells acid, gold, silver and rod, and because a war pushed acid prices up. But the business it was built to do, charging miners to process their concentrate, currently earns nothing.

Can it reverse? Three things would restore positive charges: mine supply recovering (Grasberg back to full capacity by the end of 2027, Kamoa-Kakula, Cobre Panama's stockpiles, new Andean projects), Chinese smelter discipline, or a fall in smelting demand. ICSG's forecast of 3.1% mine growth in 2027 points toward some recovery. Against that, India alone is building smelters as if TCs will normalise: Kutch Phase 2, Hindalco's FY29 expansion, JSW Odisha, a possible Tuticorin restart and the Vision Document's 5 Mtpa. Hindalco's chief executive Satish Pai has framed his expansion as a deliberately contrarian bet, building "when it's the most adverse condition". For HCL, the lesson is that part of its record margin is a cyclical gift from the smelting industry's overbuild, and that it can be taken back.

How a record price and a surplus coexist. Two bridges reconcile them. First, the shortage is in concentrate, not metal: mines are producing less while scrap and smelter stocks fill the gap, which is why TC/RC is negative even as refined metal is in surplus. Second, the refined surplus has been absorbed into US stockpiles built ahead of a possible tariff. That makes the pending US decision on a refined-copper duty, which the Section 232 proclamation of July 2025 left for later (a possible 15% from 1 January 2027 and 30% from 2028), a two-sided catalyst. Imposing it keeps metal trapped in the US and the COMEX–LME spread wide; dropping it could release a stock overhang into the world market. As of late October 2026 no decision had been published, and reports in September described the COMEX–LME arbitrage as closed, with the spread recently around $310 a tonne.

In plain terms

Copper is expensive in 2026 for two reasons that have little to do with Indian demand. Mines have had a string of accidents and closures, and the United States has been hoarding metal in case it puts a tariff on imports. The official forecasters actually expect slightly more copper to be produced than used this year and next. If the American hoard is released, or the mines recover faster than expected, the price could fall a long way even though the long-run electrification story stays intact.

What the price means for HCL. HCL's FY26 revenue rose 49% and its profit almost doubled, on 12% more copper sold. Over four years the decoupling is even starker: between FY22 and FY26 the dollar LME average rose about 12%, the rupee price of copper about 32%, HCL's revenue per tonne of MIC produced about 52% and its revenue 69%, while volume grew only about 11%. The gap between the dollar price and HCL's rupee revenue is the weaker rupee (an assumed ₹74.5 in FY22 against ₹88 in FY26), the switch of TC/RC from a deduction to a credit, gold and silver payables, and provisional-pricing gains in a rising market.

Rakha and Chapri are not HCL volume in the economic sense. HCL's tender opened at 10%; management noted that in coal the equivalent is about 4%. HCL pays and recovers the royalty, DMF and NMET. Its Director (Finance) put it plainly: "for Rakha asset there is no capex from our side. It's MDO totally." Rakha's grade per the latest geological report is about 0.70–0.74%.

The economics are good for HCL's return on capital, since a 12.5% revenue share with no capex is almost pure margin. But they change what 12.2 MTPA means. HCL's own ore at plan is about 9.2 MTPA; Rakha's 3.0 MTPA is worth one-eighth of its revenue to HCL, so its "economic" ore is about 9.6 MTPA-equivalent (our arithmetic). And Rakha's concentrate may well flow to JSW's own announced 500 ktpa smelter in Odisha: a private miner-to-smelter chain forming inside HCL's lease.

The critical-minerals push

Government policy since 2023 has pointed decisively toward more domestic copper. The National Critical Mineral Mission, approved by the Cabinet on 29 January 2025, carries a ₹16,300 crore outlay plus ₹18,000 crore expected from public-sector companies over FY25 to FY31; the Geological Survey of India ran 230 critical-mineral projects in 2025–26. The Copper Vision Document, prepared by HCL for the Ministry of Mines and released on 4 July 2025, projects demand rising about six-fold by 2047 and calls for 5 Mtpa of additional smelting and refining capacity by 2030, plus secondary refining, recycling and overseas assets. Customs duty on copper ores and concentrates and on blister copper went to nil in the July 2024 Budget, and on scrap in February 2025. The auction regime has awarded 585 blocks since 2015, a record 141 in calendar 2025.

In plain terms

India spends about $16–17 billion a year importing copper in various forms, more than HCL's entire stock-market value of about ₹38,600 crore (around $4 billion). The government's answer is to build more smelters and recycling plants at home and to auction more ground for mining. The smelters are being built fast; the mines will take a decade. Until the mines arrive, every new Indian smelter adds to the concentrate import bill rather than reducing it.

The paradox is that each new smelter increases India's import dependence for concentrate even as it reduces refined imports. A 500 ktpa smelter needs roughly 2 million tonnes of concentrate a year; HCL's entire annual output today would supply such a smelter's copper needs for less than three weeks. The country's strategic exposure is therefore shifting from cathode, which can be bought from many suppliers including Japan duty-free, to concentrate, which is controlled by a few Andean and African miners and fought over by Chinese smelters. That makes HCL's resource base, and the auction pipeline, more strategically valuable every year, and it explains why the state is unlikely to relinquish control of HCL even as it sells shares.

The rulebook: leases, clearances, duties and quality orders

The binding constraint on Indian copper is not capital or demand. It is permission: to mine, to clear forest, to run a smelter, to import.

Copper sits under several regulators at once. The Ministry of Mines is HCL's administrative ministry and oversees the Indian Bureau of Mines, the Geological Survey of India and the exploration trust. The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957, amended in 2015 to make auctions the only route to new leases, in 2023 to create exploration licences for critical and deep-seated minerals, and in 2025 to turn the National Mineral Exploration Trust into a wider development trust, sets the rules for access. The environment ministry grants environmental and forest clearances, and its timelines are the binding constraint on HCL's plans. State governments extend leases and collect royalty and District Mineral Foundation contributions. Pollution control boards license smelters. The Bureau of Indian Standards sets quality-control orders, the finance ministry and CBIC set duties, and DIPAM manages the government's shareholding.

In plain terms

To dig copper in India you need a lease from the state, an environmental clearance, a forest clearance if trees are involved, water rights and, increasingly, a winning auction bid. Six decades, five shocks: how Indian copper got here

From a state-owned mine-to-rod monopoly to a private, import-fed smelting industry with one state miner left standing.

1967–1995: the state monopoly. Hindustan Copper was incorporated in 1967, taking over copper assets from NMDC, and built an integrated mine-to-wire-rod business: the Khetri Copper Complex in Rajasthan in the 1970s, the Indian Copper Complex at Ghatsila in Jharkhand on the assets of the old Indian Copper Corporation, the Malanjkhand open pit in Madhya Pradesh from 1982, and a rod plant at Taloja. Until 1995 HCL was the sole producer of refined copper in India.

1995–2018: liberalisation and the custom smelters. Sterlite's Tuticorin smelter (1997, later expanded to 400 ktpa) and Birla Copper at Dahej (Indo Gulf, merged into Hindalco in 2002) brought world-scale custom smelting on imported concentrate. India became a net exporter of refined copper and output peaked at 830.5 kt in FY18. HCL's small, old smelters became uneconomic; Khetri's was suspended in December 2008.

2018: Tuticorin. The closure order of 28 May 2018 removed about a quarter of India's FY18 output and turned the country into a net importer, with Japan as the swing supplier. 2019–2021: HCL's crisis. The Malanjkhand open pit reached its final depth before the underground mine was ready, the Surda lease lapsed, and the Ghatsila smelter was suspended in December 2019. HCL lost ₹569 crore in FY20 on sales of ₹832 crore and its borrowings peaked at ₹1,564 crore. It pivoted to selling concentrate, signing a long-term sale agreement with Hindalco on 17 September 2020, and raised equity through a QIP in 2021.

In plain terms

HCL's profits have followed the copper price with two to three times the swing, while its volumes stayed flat. The last time the price fell, in FY20, it coincided with an operational crisis and HCL lost money. The bear case for the sub-sector is a repeat of that combination: a lower copper price arriving while the expansion is still being built and costs are rising.

The cycle lesson is about operating leverage. In plain terms

HCL controls how much copper it digs and what it costs to dig. It does not control the price of copper, the exchange rate or, much, the fee smelters charge. In a good year the uncontrolled terms can double profit; in a bad year they can halve it. That is why analysts of copper miners start with volume and unit cost, the parts management owns, and treat the price as a scenario rather than a forecast.

1. Ore mined (million tonnes)

What it is. The tonnes of rock hoisted or hauled from the mine and sent to the concentrator. It is the base of the whole revenue chain. Formula. Sum of ore from each mine, usually reported annually in lakh tonnes. Worked example. HCL mined 36.72 lakh tonnes in FY26: Malanjkhand 24.25, Khetri 10.04 and the Indian Copper Complex 2.43. What drives it. Available working faces (stopes) underground, hoisting and haulage capacity, equipment availability, contractor performance, water and power, monsoon seepage, and approvals. HCL's management says the first half of the year is development-heavy and monsoon-hit, so output is concentrated in the second half and especially in March.

Benchmarks. The relevant comparison is not other miners but HCL's own capacity and guidance: ore at or above 95% of guidance is good, 85–95% average, below 85% weak. FY26 came in at 84% of the 4.35 million tonnes guided in September 2025. Peers. No other Indian listed company mines copper; global miners report copper produced rather than ore. Definition trap. Ore mined is not ore treated (milled), and capacity figures for ore, milling and smelting are often quoted interchangeably. HCL's milling capacity is 4.7 MTPA; its ore capacity "present" is about 4.2 MTPA; its FY30 ore target is 12.2 MTPA.

2. Head grade (% copper)

What it is. The percentage of copper in the ore fed to the concentrator. It is the single most important geological variable: halving the grade halves the copper produced from the same tonnage at roughly the same cost. Formula. Copper contained in ore ÷ ore tonnes. Worked example. 1 million tonnes at 0.9% contains 9,000 tonnes of copper; at 0.75% it contains 7,500 tonnes, a 17% fall for the same mining cost. What drives it. The orebody itself, mine sequencing (which stopes are mined), dilution from waste rock, and dykes and faults. HCL's plants are designed for about 0.8% feed.

3. Concentrator recovery (%)

What it is. The share of copper in the ore that the flotation plant captures into concentrate; the rest goes to tailings. Formula. Copper in concentrate ÷ copper in ore treated. Worked example. 1 million tonnes at 0.85% contains 8,500 tonnes; at 88% recovery, 7,480 tonnes reach the concentrate. What drives it. Mineralogy, grind size, reagent regime, plant condition and feed consistency. HCL's management said the Khetri concentrator recovers poorly without Kolihan's higher-grade ore, and HCL's own risk list names "low process efficiency".

Benchmarks. Above 90% is good, 85–90% average, below 85% weak (indicative). HCL. Not disclosed. If HCL's mined grade is about 0.82–0.85% and its MIC-to-ore yield is 0.747%, recovery is implied at roughly 88–91%, an indicative back-calculation. Definition trap. A failed recovery project can be expensive: HCL's ₹158 crore plant to re-treat Malanjkhand tailings, from a sole technology provider who did not deliver, has been impaired by ₹146.47 crore.

4. Metal in concentrate and the MIC-to-ore yield

What it is. Metal in concentrate (MIC) is the copper contained in the concentrate HCL produces and sells: its true production unit. The MIC-to-ore yield divides it by ore mined and captures grade and recovery together. Formula. Yield = MIC ÷ ore mined = head grade × recovery (approximately). Worked example. FY26: 27,421 tonnes ÷ 3.672 million tonnes = 0.747%. At FY27's 4.71 million-tonne ore target the same yield gives about 35,000 tonnes; ICRA's expectation of ~30,000 tonnes of MIC sales implies a haircut.

5. Realisation versus LME

What it is. The average rupee or dollar price HCL receives per tonne of copper sold, compared with the average LME price over the same period. Formula. Sales ÷ MIC sold, converted at the period-average rupee, ÷ average LME cash. Worked example. FY26 sales of ₹3,054.40 crore ÷ 27,369 tonnes = ₹11.16 lakh a tonne; at an assumed ₹88 that is about $12,680, or 117% of the FY26 LME average of $10,817. FY25 was about 106% and FY24 about 95%.

What drives it. Four things pushed FY26 above LME. Concentrate is sold on provisional pricing, with final settlement on assay and the quotational-period price; in a sharply rising year the "differential sales value" is booked upward, a point the auditor highlighted. Gold and silver payables rode a doubling of precious-metal prices. TC/RC fell to zero and then became a credit. And the rupee average itself is uncertain. Definition trap. Do not write "HCL realises the LME price". It realises an LME-linked price, net of TC/RC, plus payable precious metals, with a lag; in a falling year the provisional-pricing effect reverses and realisation can drop below LME.

Payables and the quotational period. A smelter does not pay for every pound of copper in the concentrate. The payable percentage, typically about 96–96.5% for a 25% concentrate and lower for poorer grades, is the share it pays for; the rest is the smelter's "free metal" if it recovers more. HCL's management put its payables at about 95–96%. The quotational period is the month or months whose average LME sets the final price, often the month after delivery. In a rising market, concentrate invoiced provisionally at an earlier, lower price is later settled higher, and the gain shows up as a positive "differential sales value" in the following quarter. That is why a miner's reported realisation in a sharp rally runs ahead of the period-average LME, and why the reverse happens in a sell-off. Analysts who build HCL's quarterly revenue from the quarter's LME average alone will be wrong by the size of that lag.

6. TC/RC: dollars per tonne and cents per pound

What it is. The treatment charge (US$ per dry tonne of concentrate) and refining charge (US cents per pound of payable copper) that a miner pays a smelter. Formula. All-in charge per pound ≈ TC ÷ (pounds of payable copper per dry tonne) + RC. At 25% copper and ~96.5% payable, one dry tonne contains about 532 pounds of payable copper, so $1 of TC is about 0.18–0.19 cents a pound. Worked example. The 2024 benchmark of $80 and 8.0 cents is about 14.4 + 8.0 = 22.4 cents a pound, or about $494 per tonne of copper. The 2026 benchmark of zero is zero. A −$235 spot TC alone on 27% concentrate is roughly −$870 per tonne of copper.

What drives it. The balance between mine supply and smelting capacity, above all in China. Benchmarks (smelter view). Above $80 and 8 cents is good for smelters, $50–80 average, below $25 weak; for miners the scale is reversed. Peers. Hindalco: −26 to −30 cents a pound all-in for April–June 2026. Antofagasta: TC/RC a $18.7 million credit in H1 2026. HCL: not disclosed, but sold on spot terms. Definition trap. TC/RC is paid by the miner to the smelter. Low or negative TC/RC is good for miners and bad for custom smelters; the direction is the most common error in copper writing.

In plain terms

When you sell concentrate you are really selling the copper inside it, minus a processing fee. That fee is quoted in two pieces, a charge per tonne of concentrate and a charge per pound of copper, which makes it look complicated. Convert both into cents per pound of copper and compare that with the copper price itself, around 645 cents a pound in October 2026. In 2024 the fee was about 22 cents, roughly 3–4% of the price at that time; in 2026 it is around zero or below.

7. Cash cost per tonne of ore

What it is. Operating cost, excluding depreciation and interest, divided by ore mined. It measures mining and processing efficiency independent of grade. Formula. (Total expenses ex-D&A ex-finance) ÷ ore mined. Worked example. FY26: ₹1,615.84 crore ÷ 3.672 million tonnes ≈ ₹4,400 a tonne, against about ₹3,838 in FY25, up 15% (our arithmetic). What drives it. Depth and haul distance, the shift from open pit to underground, contractor rates, power and diesel prices, wage settlements, explosives, and royalty, which is inside HCL's cost of production and rises with the copper price.

Benchmarks. Below ₹2,500 a tonne is good for an open pit, ₹2,500–4,500 average for underground mining, above ₹4,500 weak (indicative). HCL at ₹4,400 is at the upper end of average. Definition trap. Because royalty is linked to the LME price, cost per tonne rises automatically in a strong copper year; strip royalty out (it was about ₹750 a tonne of ore in FY26) to see the underlying operating trend.

How the peers compare. No Indian peer mines copper, and global miners report cost per pound of copper rather than per tonne of ore, so this KPI is mainly a test of HCL against its own history. The useful decomposition is by complex: Malanjkhand, with the largest tonnage and the lowest all-in cost ($5,000 a tonne of copper on management's FY25 figure), against Khetri and Jharkhand at about $6,000. A shift in mix toward the smaller, deeper Rajasthan and Jharkhand mines, which is what happened in FY26 as Malanjkhand fell 11% and Khetri rose 40%, raises the company average even if every mine holds its own cost.

8. Cost per tonne of copper (C1-type and all-in)

What it is. Cost divided by copper produced, the metric that places a mine on the global cost curve. The industry standard is the C1 cash cost, net of by-product credits; "all-in" or "AISC" measures add sustaining capex and overheads. Formula. (Cash cost − by-product credits) ÷ pounds of payable copper. Worked example. HCL FY26: ₹1,615.84 crore ÷ 27,421 tonnes = ₹5.89 lakh a tonne, about $6,700 or 304 cents a pound at ₹88, gross of by-products and including royalty (255 cents ex-royalty). FY25 was ₹5.28 lakh, about 283 cents. Management told analysts in September 2025 that all-in cost of production, including employee cost, D&A and overheads, was about $5,000 a tonne at Malanjkhand and $6,000 at Khetri and in Jharkhand on an FY25 basis.

9. EBITDA margin and EBITDA per tonne of copper

EBITDA ÷ revenue; EBITDA ÷ copper tonnes sold. Worked example. Per tonne sold, that is ₹5.35–5.6 lakh, roughly $6,100–6,400 at ₹88.

10. Reserves, resources and mine life

What it is. Reserves are the tonnes of ore that feasibility studies show can be mined economically; resources are the wider mineralised inventory. Mine life is reserves divided by annual extraction. Formula. Life (years) = reserves (Mt) ÷ ore mined per year. Worked example. HCL's reserves of 156.97 million tonnes at 1.31% (about 2.05 million tonnes of copper) last about 42 years at 3.7 MTPA but only about 13 years at 12.2 MTPA. Its total reserves and resources, 767.37 million tonnes at 0.94%, would last about 63 years at 12.2 MTPA if all of it were converted.

11. Stripping ratio and the underground transition

What it is. In an open pit, the stripping ratio is tonnes of waste removed per tonne of ore; underground there is little waste stripping but each tonne costs more to develop, hoist and backfill. Formula. Waste ÷ ore (open pit). Worked example. A pit at a 4:1 ratio moves 5 tonnes of rock for each tonne of ore; as it deepens the ratio rises until underground mining becomes cheaper. Malanjkhand reached that point around 2019–2020. What drives it. Pit geometry, orebody dip and depth. HCL does not disclose stripping ratios.

Underground, the key variables are development metres (the tunnels and declines needed to reach new stopes), stope availability, hoisting capacity and backfill. Malanjkhand runs a 3.0 MTPA paste-fill plant and is moving to sub-level and blast-hole stoping; HCL's FY26 problems there were "geotechnical issues in the north section and limited stope availability in the south". Khetri and Kolihan already operate beyond 700 metres, with exploration to about 750 metres. Mine-development expenditure is capitalised and amortised on ore extracted (₹157.55 crore in FY26), so D&A rises with volume. Benchmark and trap. There is no simple good/average/weak band; the trap is to treat the end of an open pit as a one-off. HCL's ten-year delay at Malanjkhand shows the transition is the riskiest phase in a copper mine's life.

12. Capex per tonne of capacity

What it is. Capital spent per tonne of annual copper capacity added. Formula. Project capex ÷ annual tonnes of copper the project adds. Worked example. HCL's ₹7,189 crore Vision-2030 plan adds roughly 60,000 tonnes a year of MIC on HCL's own mines (our estimate), about ₹12 lakh per annual tonne or $12,600 at ₹94.9. The Rakha MDO's ~₹2,700 crore for about 19,000 tonnes a year is about $14,700 a tonne. A greenfield copper mine elsewhere typically costs $20,000–35,000 a tonne of annual capacity (indicative).

What drives it. Brownfield versus greenfield, depth, the need for new concentrators, infrastructure (power, water, roads) and contractor costs. Benchmarks. Below $20,000 good, $20,000–35,000 average, above $40,000 weak (indicative). HCL's plan is cheap per tonne because it reuses existing sites and shafts; the risk is in timing, not cost. Definition trap. Do not compare a smelter's capex (Kutch Phase 1 at about $2,400 per tonne of annual capacity) with a mine's; they buy different things.

In plain terms

A copper mine is a long series of physical steps: rock, grade, recovery, concentrate, price, fee, rupees, cost, royalty, depreciation, tax. HCL is average or a little below at most of the physical steps (grade, recovery, cost per tonne of rock) and very well placed on the market steps in 2026 (price, fee, rupee). Its expansion plan is cheap per tonne by world standards. The numbers to watch are the ones that move first: ore mined, MIC, cost per tonne of ore and the realisation premium.

13. Smelter utilisation

What it is. Cathode output divided by nameplate capacity: the key operating variable for a custom smelter, because its costs are largely fixed. Formula. Output ÷ capacity for the period. Worked example. Kutch Copper's Q1 FY27: 65.4 kt ÷ (500 ktpa ÷ 4) = 52%; FY26 114.2 ÷ 500 = 23%. Hindalco FY26: about 422 ÷ 500 = 84%. Silvassa: 170 ÷ 222 = 77%. India: ~704 ÷ 1,216 = 58%. What drives it. Concentrate availability, planned shutdowns (Hindalco took a major one in Q1 FY27), ramp-up learning, acid off-take and power. Benchmarks. Above 90% good, 75–90% average, below 60% weak (indicative). Definition trap. Annual and quarterly bases differ; Adani's 52% is quarterly output against a quarter of annual capacity. And "metal sales" (Hindalco 487 kt) can exceed own-smelter output because rod is also made from purchased cathode.

14. Sulphuric acid and by-product credits

What it is. The revenue a smelter earns from sulphuric acid, phosphoric acid and fertiliser, and precious metals recovered from anode slime. Formula. Acid tonnes × acid price + precious metals + other credits, per tonne of copper. Worked example (illustrative). About 3.2 tonnes of acid per tonne of copper at ₹9,000–11,000 a tonne, war-spike prices, is ₹29,000–35,000 per tonne of copper, roughly $300–400. That is a third or more of Hindalco's ~$930 per tonne in Q1 FY27. What drives it. Sulphur prices (which spiked with the West Asia war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz), fertiliser demand, logistics to acid customers, and gold and silver prices. Benchmarks. No standard band; the share of EBITDA from by-products is the thing to track. Definition trap. Acid is a smelter by-product, not HCL's. A refinery without a smelter (Vedanta Silvassa) earns none. And a by-product windfall is cyclical: it is not a recovery in the core smelting toll.

15. LME and rupee sensitivity

What it is. The change in revenue and profit for a given change in copper price, exchange rate or volume. Formula. ΔRevenue ≈ MIC × ΔLME × ₹/$; ΔRevenue ≈ MIC × LME × Δ₹/$. Worked example. At 27,400 tonnes and ₹94.9, ±$1,000 a tonne is about ±₹260 crore of revenue and, after royalty and 25% tax, about ±₹175–195 crore of PAT. ±₹1 to the dollar at $14,200 is about ±₹39 crore of revenue, roughly 2.5% of FY26 EBITDA. +1,000 tonnes of MIC at $14,200 and ₹94.9 adds about ₹135 crore of revenue, with 60–70% incremental EBITDA.

In plain terms

18. Definition traps, collected

  • MIC is not cathode. HCL's 27,421 tonnes is copper in concentrate. It must never be added to India's ~704 kt of refined output.
  • Four "production" numbers. Ore (3.67 Mt), MIC (27.4 kt), primary refined (~704 kt) and total demand including scrap (~1.56 Mt) are different things; always say which.
  • Ore mined is not ore treated, and reserve grade is not mined grade. Do not quote IBM's misprinted 2.35% treated grade for FY23.
  • Three EBITDA bases for HCL. Company ₹1,533.83 crore (50.2%, incl. other income), screener ₹1,463 crore (47.5%), ICRA ₹1,495 crore (48.6%). Pick one and say so.
  • Sales are not revenue from operations. HCL's FY26 sales were ₹3,054.40 crore; revenue from operations ₹3,077.92 crore adds services and other operating income.
  • Realisation is not LME. It is LME-linked, net of TC/RC, plus precious metals, with provisional-pricing lag. FY26's 117% depends on an assumed ₹88 rupee.
  • TC/RC direction. Paid by the miner to the smelter; low or negative is good for miners and bad for smelters.
  • Capacity means four things. Ore mining (4.2 → 12.2 MTPA), milling (4.7 MTPA), smelting and refining (HCL's 68.5 kt is idle) and rod (Taloja 60 ktpa, tolling).
  • Smelter "metal sales" are not smelter output. Hindalco sold 487 kt of metal but refined about 422 kt; rod is also made from purchased cathode.
  • Trade data are gross tonnes. HS 2603 counts concentrate weight, not copper content; 7403 includes alloys; 7408 includes wire rod.
  • Standalone equals consolidated for HCL. The subsidiaries are immaterial; use standalone, as the company and ICRA do.
  • Headline ore is not economic ore. Rakha/Chapri's 3.0 MTPA earns HCL a 12.5% revenue share, not full mining margin.

19. How to read a copper company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Pull the quarter's LME average and rupee first (Q1 FY27 $13,329 and about ₹94; Q2 FY27 $14,104 and ₹94–95). Most of the revenue change is explained before you open the results.
  2. Revenue against the price. Divide revenue by the LME average to get a volume-and-premium proxy (HCL Q1 FY27: about ₹70 crore per $1,000 of LME, against ₹54 a year earlier). A rise well above price and rupee means more volume or a bigger premium; a fall means volume slipped. HCL publishes no quarterly MIC, so this proxy (about 7,100 tonnes implied for Q1 FY27, indicative and low-confidence) is the best available.
  3. Operating margin against last year's same quarter, not the previous quarter. HCL is seasonal: Q1 is the monsoon and maintenance trough, Q4 the March push.
  4. Other income and exceptional items. Strip out one-offs (the ₹95.75 crore PRMS provision in Q3 FY26) before comparing PAT.
  5. Cost per tonne trend. Annually, divide expenses ex-D&A by ore mined. A rise above about 10% a year without a price explanation is a red flag.
  6. Royalty line. It should move with price; if it rises faster, check for back-dated or decretal amounts.
  7. Capex run-rate against plan. FY27's plan is ₹1,421.7 crore, about three times FY26's ₹464.5 crore. A first half far below a quarter of that is an early warning on Vision 2030.
  8. Project milestones. Malanjkhand crushing and pumping award, shaft equipping, Kolihan apron feeder, Surda Stage-II forest clearance, Rakha first ore, Lohum start-up at the Gujarat plant.
  9. Balance sheet and contingencies. Debt (nil since September 2026), liquid investments (over ₹1,000 crore), and any movement in the ₹1,262 crore of contingent liabilities, above all the IVRCL award.
  10. For smelters, read per tonne, not per cent. EBITDA per tonne of metal, TC/RC commentary, acid realisations, utilisation and working-capital debt. A record EBITDA from acid is not a recovery in TC/RC.
In plain terms

To read HCL's quarter quickly: look up what copper and the rupee did, check whether revenue rose more or less than that, compare the margin with the same quarter last year, take out one-off items, and then check whether the expansion spending is on track. For Hindalco or Adani, ignore the percentage margin and ask how much they earned per tonne and how much of it came from acid.

One last caution applies across all sixteen measures: in a one-company sub-sector, every benchmark is partly a comparison of HCL with itself over time. Global miners provide the cost and margin yardsticks, the smelters provide the value-chain contrast, and HCL's own guidance provides the execution yardstick. Of the three, the last has been the least reliable.

How to read the table

Start with the per-tonne column. It is the cleanest single view of where value sits in the copper chain. HCL earned about ₹5.35 lakh of operating profit per tonne of copper it sold in FY26. Adani's Kutch Copper earned about ₹1.16 lakh in a single, unusually favourable quarter; Hindalco about ₹57,700 for the full year; Vedanta slightly below zero. Southern Copper and Antofagasta earned the dollar equivalent of roughly ₹9–12 lakh a tonne, but much of that is by-product revenue from molybdenum, gold and silver that HCL's concentrate does not carry. The mine keeps the rent, and the richer mine keeps more of it.

Then compare margins with care. HCL's 47.5% and the global miners' 63–65% are like-for-like operating margins on mining revenue. The smelters' 4.0% and −0.2% are not comparable: their revenue includes the full value of the metal they buy, so their percentage margin falls as copper rises even if their profit per tonne is unchanged. The cable makers' 10.5–13.9% margins are conversion margins on metal-inclusive revenue and are remarkably stable through the cycle.

Valuation tells you what the market believes. HCL trades at about 32 times trailing earnings and 21.5 times trailing EBITDA. The Indian groups that own smelters trade far lower (Hindalco about 7 times EBITDA, Vedanta under 5), because copper is a small and thin-margin part of much larger aluminium, zinc and oil businesses. Adani Enterprises' 31 times reflects airports, data centres and new energy, not copper. The global miners give the most useful comparison: Southern Copper, the lowest-cost and longest-life producer, at about 15–17 times EBITDA; Freeport at about 9–10 times. HCL's premium is therefore about 1.3–1.6 times Southern Copper's and about 2–2.5 times Freeport's (indicative), not "double the global average" across the board. The market is paying for scarcity, as India's only listed copper miner, and for Vision 2030.

The sensitivity column explains the multiple's fragility. A $1,000 move in copper changes HCL's revenue by about ₹260 crore, most of which falls to profit: after royalty and tax, roughly a sixth of TTM PAT. The same move changes Vedanta's copper EBITDA by ₹55–70 crore and barely touches the cable makers. An investor who owns HCL owns the copper price, in rupees, with an expansion attached.

In plain terms

Only one row in this table is the sub-sector; the rest are mirrors. Hindalco, Vedanta and Adani show what the copper business looks like further down the chain, where the margins are thin. Polycab and KEI show the customers. Southern Copper, Antofagasta and Freeport show what world-class mines earn and what investors pay for them. HCL makes more per tonne than any Indian company in the chain, less than the best global miners, and is valued more richly than any of them.

Basis differences to remember. HCL reports standalone figures that equal its consolidated numbers for practical purposes. Hindalco's copper segment revenue is not comparable with its group revenue; Vedanta's group figures have been restated after the June 2026 demerger, so the primer's pre-demerger ₹1,74,075 crore revenue is not comparable with the ₹78,437 crore continuing-operations figure. Adani does not disclose Kutch Copper revenue. The global miners report in dollars on calendar years (BHP on a June year). And the quarter matters: HCL's latest reported quarter is Q1 FY27 (June 2026), Polycab and KEI have already reported Q2 FY27 (September 2026), and every other company here reports between 27 October and 6 November 2026.

Who is winning: rent, scale and the price of scarcity

Three maps place HCL against the domestic smelters and the global miners on profit per tonne, valuation and cost.

In a single-member sub-sector "winning" means two things: which part of the chain is capturing value in this cycle, and how HCL compares with the companies that do the same job elsewhere. The first map answers the first question; the second and third answer the second.

The first map is the TC/RC story in one picture. Hindalco, Kutch and Vedanta process ten to twenty times HCL's tonnage and earn less, in total and far less per tonne. Southern Copper and Antofagasta sit top right because they have scale and rich, by-product-heavy orebodies. HCL sits in the "small, rich" corner, which is a good place to be in a concentrate-short market, and its route to the top-right corner runs through Vision 2030.

The second map shows the scarcity premium. HCL earns a margin about 14 points below Southern Copper's and trades at a higher multiple of EBITDA. The cable makers trade at similar multiples on a quarter of the margin, because investors expect their volumes to compound for a decade; HCL's multiple embeds a similar expectation of volume growth, which is precisely what its history has not delivered.

In plain terms

In 2026 the mines are winning and the smelters are not. Among mines, HCL is small and its costs are higher than the world's best once their by-products are counted. It looks like a winner because copper is expensive and because it is the only listed way to own an Indian copper mine. If copper got cheaper, the third map would matter more than the first.

Put together, the maps say that HCL is winning the cycle but not the structure. Its position in the value chain is ideal for a concentrate-short market. Its position on the cost curve is mediocre and its scale is small, so its profits depend more on the price than those of the miners it is valued against. The only lever that improves both, cost per tonne and scale, is volume growth from Vision 2030. That is why the plan's calendar matters more than any other variable in the sub-sector.

The global lens: a 0.1% miner in a 23-million-tonne world

Chile, the Congo and Peru dig the world's copper; China refines half of it; India is a mid-sized refiner with almost no mines.

World copper mine output was about 23 million tonnes in 2025 and refinery output about 29 million tonnes, the gap filled by secondary (scrap) refining; world reserves are about 980 million tonnes, according to the US Geological Survey's 2026 Mineral Commodity Summaries. Chile (5.3 Mt), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (3.2 Mt) and Peru (2.7 Mt) mine almost half of it. China mines only 1.8 Mt but refines about 14 Mt, 48% of the world. India mines about 23 kt and refines about 620 kt (calendar basis), with 2.2 Mt of reserves.

Scale. HCL's annual copper output is about 12% of what Southern Copper mines in a single quarter, and about 4% of Antofagasta's 2026 guidance. The benefit of negative charges is global: Antofagasta's TC/RC turned into an $18.7 million credit in H1 2026, and HCL's spot sales capture the same effect.

The global supply story in 2026 is one of disruption. ICSG's January to August data show Chile down 7% (El Teniente, Escondida, Spence), Indonesia's concentrate output down 29% after the Grasberg mud-rush, the Congo's concentrate down 26% on Kamoa-Kakula, and Australia down 10% after Mount Isa closed. Freeport expects Grasberg's mill to reach about 80% of capacity by mid-2027 and full capacity by the end of 2027; its smelter in Indonesia restarted in late August 2026. Cobre Panama remains shut, processing only stockpiles. On the demand side, China is 59% of world usage and grew 2.8% in the first eight months of 2026; the rest of the world fell 0.6%.

In plain terms

The world's copper comes from a handful of countries, mostly in South America and Africa, and most of it is melted in China. India is a mid-sized melter that buys its raw material from the same mines as China and competes with Chinese smelters for it. Its own mine output is a rounding error on the world scale. That is why the global price and the global smelting fee, not Indian demand, set the profits of Indian copper companies.

The valuation read-across. Global miners at peak prices typically trade at roughly 6–10 times EBITDA, with the highest-quality, longest-life assets such as Southern Copper at 15–17 times (indicative, from data-site estimates rather than filings). HCL at about 21.5 times trailing EBITDA trades above all of them. Some of the premium is structural to Indian markets, where scarce listed commodity producers and government-owned companies included in PSU indices attract persistent domestic flows; some is the Vision-2030 option. The comparison is a reminder that the market is valuing HCL more as a growth stock than as a miner. Technology and disruption: depth, recovery and aluminium

The technical risks in Indian copper sit underground and in the concentrator; the commercial risk is a cheaper conductor.

Underground mass mining at depth is the core technology risk. Malanjkhand's underground mine will use sub-level and blast-hole stoping with paste backfill from a 3.0 MTPA plant; Khetri and Kolihan already work beyond 700 metres, with exploration to about 750 metres. The hazards are geotechnical failure (Malanjkhand's north section in FY26), ventilation and heat, water ingress in the monsoon, hoisting bottlenecks, and above all a thin domestic base of underground metal-mining contractors, which HCL itself names as a risk. The 2011–2021 IVRCL episode shows what happens when a contractor fails: a decade's delay and a disputed ₹320 crore award.

The MDO model is HCL's answer to the contractor problem. By handing Rakha and Chapri to JSW's South West Mining for 20 years plus 10, HCL transfers execution and capital to a private operator with a 20–25% return target, and keeps a 12.5% revenue share. HCL already outsources most development and ore production at Khetri, Malanjkhand and Kendadih ("we give a wholesome contract", in the words of its finance team), so the MDO is an extension rather than a break. If Rakha delivers first ore by the end of FY27 as ICRA expects, the model could be repeated elsewhere; if it slips, the 12.2 MTPA target slips with it.

Grade and recovery. HCL's reserves and resources average 0.94% copper and Rakha's ore about 0.70–0.74%, and its plants were designed for about 0.8%. Every tenth of a percentage point of grade or point of recovery changes output materially. The failed tailings re-treatment plant at Malanjkhand (₹146 crore impaired) is a warning about beneficiation technology bought from a single vendor. Leaching of low-grade ore, which Freeport promotes as its "leach innovation" programme, could matter for Indian deposits in the long run, but HCL has not announced any leaching project.

Smelting technology and social licence. Dahej and Mundra use flash-smelting technology with acid plants to capture sulphur dioxide. Emissions control, acid handling and community relations are the risks; the Tuticorin closure after the May 2018 shootings shows that a smelter's licence to operate can be withdrawn regardless of its engineering. Kutch Copper says it integrates about 115 MW of renewable power, and Vedanta's Indian copper runs on 64% renewable power, which matters for future carbon-border rules in export markets (copper is not in the first phase of the EU's carbon border mechanism).

Substitution and thrifting are the commercial disruption. At $14,000 a tonne, aluminium substitution in power cables, overhead conductors, radiators and HVAC coils becomes compelling; optical fibre has long replaced copper in telecom; and manufacturers use thinner walls, copper-clad aluminium and smaller conductors. India's Q1 FY27 demand fall of 9% is partly this. Recycling is the other cap on primary demand: scrap supplies about 39% of Indian copper and rises with price, helped by nil scrap duty and the ₹1,500 crore recycling scheme.

In plain terms

The technology that matters most for Indian copper is not exotic. It is the ability to dig safely and cheaply half a mile underground, to recover as much copper as possible from low-grade rock, and to run a smelter without poisoning the neighbours. The biggest threat to copper demand is an old, cheaper metal, aluminium, which looks more attractive every time copper sets a record.

Bull versus bear: the sub-sector in two columns

For HCL, and for Indian copper as a whole, the arguments on each side are strong. Here they are side by side.

The bull case

  • The right place in the chain. In a concentrate-short world with TC/RC at zero or below, the mine keeps the rent. HCL earned about ₹5.35 lakh a tonne in FY26 against Hindalco's ₹57,700.
  • Structural demand. India uses 0.6 kg of copper per person against a world 3.2 kg; 500 GW of non-fossil power, transmission, data centres, EVs and housing all need it.
  • A funded, cheap expansion. Vision 2030 costs about $12,600 per annual tonne, below global greenfield norms, and HCL is debt-free and AAA-rated with over ₹1,000 crore of liquidity.
  • Policy tailwinds. The critical-mineral mission, nil duties on concentrate and scrap, auctions, the cathode QCO and the Copper Vision Document all favour domestic supply.
  • Capex-free growth at Rakha. A 12.5% revenue share with no HCL capex is high-return volume.
  • Rupee and price. At ₹94.9 and $14,000+ copper, FY27 should be another record year (ICRA: margin above 50%, ~30,000 t MIC).

The bear case

  • Price mean-reversion. ICSG sees refined surpluses of 104 kt in 2026 and 368 kt in 2027; a release of US tariff stockpiles could push LME back toward $10,000, roughly halving HCL's PAT.
  • A decade without volume growth. MIC was lower in FY26 than in FY16; FY26 ore came in at 84% of guidance; the 12.2 MTPA target dates from FY2010-11.
  • A back-loaded plan. 6.3 Mt of the 8.5 Mt increase is booked in FY30; management's own Malanjkhand timetable ends in 2031; a quarter of the target belongs economically to JSW.
  • Cost inflation and grade. Cost per tonne of ore rose about 15% in FY26; grades are below 1%; net costs are third-to-fourth quartile globally.
  • TC/RC can reverse. A return to positive charges would cost HCL roughly ₹180–190 crore a year (indicative) even at an unchanged LME.
  • Valuation and supply. About 32 times trailing earnings and 21.5 times EBITDA, above the best global miners; up to ~10 points of further government selling before ICRA's trigger; ₹1,262 crore of contingent liabilities.

How to weigh them. The bull case is mostly about the cycle and the chain: copper is scarce in the form that matters, and HCL owns the only Indian supply of it. The bear case is mostly about execution and price: the volume that would make HCL a genuinely large miner has been promised for fifteen years, and the price that makes it so profitable today rests partly on a tariff-driven stockpile and a smelting glut that can both unwind. In plain terms

If copper stays expensive and HCL delivers even half of its expansion on time, today's share price could look reasonable in hindsight. If copper falls back toward its recent average and the expansion slips again, profits could halve while the share still trades at a premium to the world's best miners. Both outcomes are plausible; the evidence that decides between them arrives quarter by quarter, in ore tonnes, capex spent and the LME.

For the reference companies the balance is different. Hindalco's copper business is a steady, thin-margin franchise with an acid windfall; Vedanta's is a break-even refinery with a contested restart option; Adani's is a ramp-up whose returns depend on concentrate access and TC/RC normalising; the cable makers are volume compounders largely indifferent to the copper price. None of them is a substitute for owning a copper mine, which is why HCL's scarcity premium has persisted.

Glossary

The copper terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • Anode / blister. Partly refined copper (~98–99%) from the smelter, cast into slabs for electro-refining.
  • Backwardation. When the cash price is above the three-month price, a sign of near-term tightness ($119 on 22 Oct 2026).
  • Benchmark TC/RC. The annual treatment and refining charge agreed between a major miner and Chinese smelters; $0 for 2026.
  • BIS QCO. A Bureau of Indian Standards quality-control order; for copper cathode, in force since 1 Dec 2024.
  • C1 cash cost. Mine-site cash cost per pound of payable copper, net of by-product credits.
  • Cathode. Refined copper plate of 99.99% purity, LME-deliverable.
  • CCR / CCWR. Continuous-cast copper wire rod, usually 8 mm, the feedstock for wire drawing.
  • CEPA. The India–Japan trade agreement that lets Japanese cathode in duty-free.
  • Concentrate. The powder (17–26% Cu for HCL) produced by flotation from crushed ore.
  • COMEX. The US copper futures market; its premium over LME reflects US tariff expectations.
  • Custom smelter. A smelter that buys concentrate from third-party miners rather than its own mines.
  • DMF / NMET. District Mineral Foundation and exploration-trust levies paid alongside royalty.
  • Free metal. Copper a smelter recovers above the payable percentage it paid for.
  • Head grade. Copper content of ore fed to the concentrator, %.
  • HS codes. 2603 ores and concentrates; 7402 anodes; 7403 refined copper; 7404 scrap; 7408 wire.
  • ICSG. International Copper Study Group, the authority on the global copper balance.
  • LME. London Metal Exchange, whose cash price is the reference for copper contracts worldwide.
  • MDO. Mine developer-operator: a contractor that funds, builds and runs a mine for a share of revenue (Rakha/Chapri: 12.5% to HCL).
  • MIC. Metal in concentrate: tonnes of copper contained in concentrate, HCL's production unit.
  • MMDR Act. Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957, which governs leases and auctions.
  • NCMM. National Critical Mineral Mission (Jan 2025, ₹16,300 Cr outlay).
  • OFS. Offer for sale: a promoter's sale of shares through the exchange (HCL, Aug 2026).
  • Payable. The share of copper in concentrate that the smelter pays for, ~95–96.5%.
  • Provisional pricing / quotational period. Concentrate invoiced at an early price and settled later at the average over an agreed period.
  • Recovery. Share of copper in ore captured into concentrate.
  • R&R. Reserves and resources: the full mineral inventory; reserves are the economically proven part.
  • Section 232. US national-security tariff law; 50% on copper content of semis and derivatives since 1 Aug 2025.
  • Stope. An underground opening from which ore is extracted.
  • Tolling. Processing someone else's metal for a fee (HCL's Taloja rod plant).
  • TradeStat. The commerce ministry's DGCI&S trade database, source of India's import and export figures.
  • Vision 2030. HCL's plan to lift ore capacity from ~4.2 to 12.2 MTPA by FY30 with ~₹7,189 Cr of capex.
