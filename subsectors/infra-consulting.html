00

The one-page view: two brains-for-hire, one mix equation

Two state-owned engineering houses, each tied to one ministry's spending, whose profits are set less by how much work they win than by what kind of work it is.

India has two listed engineering and infrastructure consultants. Engineers India Ltd (EIL) designs and supervises refineries, petrochemical complexes, pipelines, gas plants, fertiliser units and underground oil caverns, mostly for the oil public-sector companies and, increasingly, for overseas clients such as the Dangote group in Africa. RITES Ltd does feasibility studies, design, project management and quality inspection for Indian Railways, metro corporations, state governments, ports and airports; it also builds small projects on a turnkey basis, leases locomotives and exports Indian-built trains. Together they reported ₹6,343 crore of revenue and ₹1,146 crore of profit in FY26 and are worth ₹24,007 crore on the stock market. That is small: the combined value is less than a quarter of one large private construction company's.

They matter because they sit at the front of two of the largest capital programmes in the country. Indian Railways plans to spend ₹2,93,030 crore in FY27, and India's refining capacity is meant to rise from about 267 million tonnes a year to about 310 million tonnes by 2028–30, with a ₹1.45 lakh crore BPCL refinery in Andhra Pradesh queued behind that. Before a rupee of that money is poured into concrete, someone has to decide what to build, draw it, write the tenders and police the contractors. These two companies do a large share of that work for the government.

The single most useful fact about them is that their revenue is two different businesses wearing one label. Fee consultancy (studies, design, project management, inspection) earned a segment margin of about 30% in FY26. Turnkey work, where the consultant buys equipment and hires builders and books the whole project cost as its own revenue, earned about 6%. Consultancy was 52% of the two companies' segment revenue but about 78% of their segment profit. Almost every move in their margins over the last five years is explained by the share of turnkey in the month's billing, not by fee rates or cost control.

The second fact is that FY26 looks better than it was. EIL's headline profit rose 19% (37% standalone), but its annual report shows that ₹226.5 crore of revenue and ₹213.6 crore of profit came from one turnkey contract: EIL had cut the contract price in earlier years for expected delay, and on mechanical completion the price was restored. The prior year carried its own boost, ₹128.9 crore of consultancy "variable consideration", including an ₹86.3 crore change-order claim on HPCL's Rajasthan refinery that the client has not approved and that the auditor has flagged. Strip both out and EIL's underlying consolidated profit grew about 7%. RITES' consolidated consultancy margin of 39.5% includes about ₹125 crore of high-margin power-procurement fees earned by REMCL, a subsidiary in which the Ministry of Railways owns 49%; on a standalone basis its consultancy margin has fallen from 36.9% to 33.7% to 31.4% in three periods.

The third fact is about cash. Both companies are debt-free and look cash-rich, but much of the cash on their balance sheets belongs to clients, who pay in advance for work and for projects the consultants manage on their behalf. RITES held ₹2,946 crore of cash and bank balances at March 2026, yet its own annual report for FY25 split its cash into ₹861 crore of owned funds and ₹2,475 crore of ring-fenced client funds. Once that is recognised, RITES' price-to-earnings multiple excluding its own cash is about 28 times, not the 21 times that the gross cash figure suggests. EIL's cash pile is more its own, and it also owns 4.37% of Numaligarh Refinery (fair value ₹1,024 crore) and 26% of the Ramagundam fertiliser joint venture (carried at ₹498 crore).

The fourth fact is the owner. The Government of India holds 51.32% of EIL and 72.20% of RITES. It is also, directly or through its companies, nearly all of their domestic clients, and it sets the rules under which they are hired, the dividends they must pay and the salaries of their regular staff. Over the past decade the government has steadily replaced nomination (awarding a consultancy job to a state-owned firm without a tender) with competitive bidding. RITES says about 70% of its new orders and 62% of its order book are now won in open competition, and EIL's management told analysts in August 2026 that competition is "very high in all the segments", including public-sector work.

The fifth fact is that the two stocks have gone separate ways. EIL trades at ₹258, close to its all-time high, after winning the largest single order in its history: a project-management and engineering-procurement-construction-management contract for Dangote's planned 700,000-barrel-a-day refinery in Kenya, "in excess of US$450 million" (at least ₹4,270 crore at ₹94.9 to the dollar). RITES trades at about ₹198, about half its July 2024 peak, after six years in which its revenue did not grow (₹2,474 crore in FY20, ₹2,415 crore in FY26). On headline numbers EIL looks cheaper (18.5 times trailing earnings, 1.95% dividend yield) than RITES (22.8 times, 4.05% yield). After adjusting for one-offs, cash and investments, the gap narrows sharply.

01

How an engineering consultant earns: selling hours, booking steel

The work runs from a feasibility study to a commissioned plant; the money depends on which of those steps the consultant is paid for, and whether it books other people's costs as its own revenue.

Every large project in India passes through the same sequence. An owner, such as an oil company, a railway zone, a metro corporation or a state government, decides it may want to build something. To get budget or board approval it needs a feasibility report or a detailed project report (DPR), which sets out what to build, where, with what technology and at what cost. A consultant is hired to write it. If the project goes ahead, the consultant (often the same one) prepares the design, the specifications and the tender documents, evaluates the bids from construction contractors, and then sits on the owner's side through construction as project management consultant (PMC), checking quality, certifying bills and policing the schedule. After commissioning, the plant needs revamps, safety studies and debottlenecking, and those jobs tend to go to whoever drew the original plant, because they hold the drawings and the institutional memory.

The consultant earns a fee at each step. For studies the fee is usually a lump sum. For design and PMC it is either a man-hour rate or a percentage of project cost: industry practice puts rail and metro PMC fees at roughly 2–3% of project cost and refinery engineering-procurement-construction-management (EPCM) fees at roughly 3–8% (INDICATIVE ranges from typical public-sector tender norms; neither company publishes its fee rates). Inspection is charged per inspection or as a share of the value inspected.

Steps 2, 3 and 5 earn 25–40% margins on almost no capital. Step 4 can earn the same fee as PMC or, if taken as turnkey, multiply revenue by ten to twenty times for a thin margin and real risk.

Step 4 is where the two business models split. Under PMC or EPCM the owner buys the equipment and signs the construction contracts, and the consultant earns a fee for managing them. Under lump-sum turnkey (LSTK) or "turnkey" execution, the consultant itself signs a fixed-price contract with the owner, then buys the equipment and hires the construction subcontractors. Its revenue is now the whole contract value. RITES gave its own example on the August 2026 earnings call: a ₹100 crore project carrying a ₹5 crore fee appears as ₹5 crore of revenue if RITES is the PMC, and as ₹105 crore of order book and revenue if RITES takes it as turnkey. The fee is the same; the denominator is twenty-one times larger.

Turnkey earns three to five times the profit of PMC, but on twelve to seventeen times the revenue, with working capital, bank guarantees, liquidated damages and cost-overrun risk attached. That is why brokers value EIL's turnkey profit at about 10 times and its consultancy profit at about 24 times.

Two further revenue streams exist only at RITES. It exports rolling stock: it buys locomotives and coaches from Indian Railways' factories and private builders and ships them, with spares and support, to Bangladesh, Mozambique, Sri Lanka and others, often under an Indian government line of credit. It books the full contract value as revenue, earning about 16% in FY26. It also leases locomotives, 91 of them at the last count (FY25 annual report), on wet lease (with crew and maintenance) to power plants, steel mills and ports at 30-plus sites, earning about 35%. Both companies also hold equity in client projects: EIL in Numaligarh Refinery and the Ramagundam fertiliser joint venture, RITES in REMCL (51%), which buys power for the Railways, and in a wagon-making joint venture with SAIL.

Where is value captured? At the front of the chain and in the inspection business at the back: studies, basic engineering, PMC and inspection earn 25–40% margins on near-zero capital, because the clients pay in advance and the only asset is people. The turnkey step captures revenue but little value. Value also comes from two less obvious places: equity stakes in the projects the consultants helped build (EIL's Numaligarh dividend was ₹43.8 crore in FY26), and the float on client money, which shows up in "other income".

In plain terms

A consultant that takes a project on turnkey becomes, for that project, a small contractor: it buys the pumps and pays the builders, and it books all of that as its own sales. A rupee of turnkey revenue and a rupee of consultancy revenue are not the same rupee. One carries 2 to 8 paise of profit, the other 25 to 40.

Clients like turnkey for practical reasons. It gives them a single point of responsibility, and RITES says state clients also prefer it for GST compliance. For the consultant it fills the order book and keeps engineers busy when pure fee work is scarce. The cost is that it drags the reported margin down, raises working capital and exposes the consultant to cost overruns, liquidated damages and claims. EIL says it takes most new turnkey work on an "open-book estimate" basis to limit that risk; RITES says turnkey will not exceed about half its order book.

02

Small, old and static: six years without growth

India's infrastructure spending roughly doubled after FY20; the two listed consultants' combined revenue rose 1.8% a year and their profit 1.4% a year.

The two companies are old. EIL was incorporated in 1965 so that India would not need foreign firms to design each new refinery; RITES was set up in 1974 to sell Indian railway expertise abroad. Both have been listed for years (EIL since the 1990s, RITES since its June 2018 IPO). Yet by revenue they remain small: EIL's FY26 consolidated revenue of ₹3,928 crore is about US$414 million, and RITES' ₹2,415 crore about US$254 million at ₹94.9 to the dollar. EIL is roughly one-seventeenth of France's Technip Energies by revenue, and RITES about one-forty-fifth of the US design firm Jacobs.

More striking is that they have barely grown. Between FY20 and FY26, the combined revenue went from ₹5,711 crore to ₹6,343 crore, a compound rate of 1.8% a year, and combined profit from ₹1,057 crore to ₹1,146 crore, 1.4% a year. Over the same period Indian Railways' capital spending rose from about ₹1.5 lakh crore to ₹2.65 lakh crore, and the refinery expansion programme moved from the drawing board into construction. The consulting wallet grew; these two did not take a growing share of it.

How big is the market they serve? No government statistic measures "engineering consultancy" revenue, so any figure is a triangulation. Building up from capital spending, the hydrocarbon consulting wallet (studies, design, PMC and EPCM on refinery, petrochemical, pipeline and gas projects) is perhaps ₹6,000–12,000 crore a year, and the transport consulting wallet (railways, metros, highways, ports) perhaps ₹5,000–9,000 crore. Adding the listed pair's consultancy revenue to the known unlisted players (Tata Consulting Engineers about ₹2,036 crore in FY25; WAPCOS ₹1,680 crore in FY25; MECON ₹1,515 crore in FY26) and the Indian arms of global firms gives an organised Indian engineering and infrastructure consultancy market of roughly ₹15,000–25,000 crore. On that basis EIL and RITES together (₹3,170 crore of consultancy revenue) hold perhaps 13–21% (all INDICATIVE).

Why have they not grown with the capex boom? Three reasons recur throughout this report. First, clients built in-house capability: the oil companies strengthened their own project departments, and the railway execution arm RVNL, the high-speed rail corporation and the metro corporations now do much of their own project management. Second, the government shifted from nomination to competitive tendering, which brought in private Indian firms and the Indian arms of global consultancies, especially on metro and high-speed rail general consultancy packages funded by multilateral lenders. Third, both companies filled the gap with turnkey work, which adds revenue but little profit; and RITES' export business, which peaked in FY22, went almost silent for two years.

In plain terms

The country's infrastructure spending roughly doubled; the two companies whose job is to design and supervise that spending did not grow at all. The money went to their clients' in-house teams, to private and foreign rivals, and to contractors. What changed in FY25–FY26 is that both now hold record order books at the same time, for the first time.

The forecasts point to growth again. EIL guides FY27 revenue at least 10% above FY26's standalone ₹3,850 crore (about ₹4,200 crore) and has a ₹5,000 crore target for FY28. RITES guides "double-digit" growth in FY27. One broker (Prabhudas Lilladher) forecasts combined revenue growth of about 15% a year from FY26 to FY28. The order books support that arithmetic: at June 2026 the two held ₹23,869 crore of unexecuted work, about 3.8 times trailing revenue, before EIL adds the Kenya order. The question for investors is less whether revenue grows than how much of the growth is consultancy, which carries profit, and how much is turnkey, which mostly does not.

The one-off that made EIL's FY26

EIL's December 2025 quarter stood out: consolidated revenue of ₹1,210 crore (up 58%), turnkey revenue of ₹720 crore (up 110%) and a turnkey segment margin of 38%, against 5–8% in every other recent quarter. The FY26 annual report, note 48(a), explains it. On one ongoing turnkey project (the client is not named), EIL had in earlier periods reduced the contract price it recognised, because it expected the project to be delayed and liquidated damages to be deducted. Under the revenue standard Ind AS 115 that expected deduction is a reduction of the transaction price, not a cost. When the project reached mechanical completion, the price was restored: ₹226.52 crore of revenue and ₹213.58 crore of profit, all in turnkey, all in FY26. It is the reversal of a revenue deduction, not a provision written back and not a claim won in arbitration, and it will not recur. Management said on the August 2026 call that it does not expect any provision writeback in FY27.

In plain terms

For both companies the most important number in a quarterly release is not revenue growth but the share of revenue that came from consultancy. One extra percentage point of consultancy in EIL's mix moves its operating profit by about ₹7–8 crore a year on FY27's revenue base; a quarter in which turnkey bills heavily will look weak on margins even when it is perfectly healthy. And any segment margin far outside the usual band (consultancy above 30% at EIL, turnkey above 10%) is almost certainly a one-off; read the notes before extrapolating it.

The forward arithmetic follows from guidance. EIL expects consultancy to be at least 55% of FY27 revenue (₹2,300–2,400 crore) at 24–25%, and turnkey to recover in the second half at around 7–7.5%. RITES expects exports of at least ₹300 crore at mid-teens margins and wants turnkey to stay at or below half its order book. If both deliver, the combined consultancy share of segment profit stays near 75–80% and combined margins hold. The risk at EIL is that a turnkey surge in the second half dilutes the margin even as revenue grows; the risk at RITES is that turnkey keeps rising as a share of the book while standalone consultancy margins keep falling. Refineries, pipelines and caverns: the capex EIL lives on

A 42-million-tonne refinery build-out that is nearly finished, a ₹1.45 lakh crore project still to be tendered, and an energy-security push sharpened by the 2026 West Asia war.

EIL's domestic business depends on one ministry's companies: the oil refiners and pipeline operators owned by the state (IOC, BPCL, HPCL and its Rajasthan refinery joint venture HRRL, Numaligarh Refinery, MRPL, GAIL and ONGC). The Kenya order

On 22 September 2026 EIL filed a stock-exchange disclosure that it had signed a contract "in excess of US$450 Million" with the Dangote group to act as project management consultant and EPCM consultant for a 700,000-barrel-a-day greenfield refinery and petrochemical plant in Kenya. Press reports place the project at Mokowe in Lamu County and cite a total investment of about US$16 billion, with a groundbreaking on 30 September. At ₹94.9 to the dollar, US$450 million is about ₹4,270 crore, which should be read as a floor (INDICATIVE conversion). It is the largest single order in EIL's history, nearly equal to the whole domestic consultancy book, and it is not in the June 2026 order book.

It follows the Lekki expansion order in Nigeria (650,000 barrels a day towards 1.4 million, "in excess of US$350 million", about ₹3,300 crore), booked in the March 2026 quarter. Together the two Dangote mandates are worth roughly US$800 million, or about ₹7,600 crore (INDICATIVE), of PMC and EPCM fees from one privately owned group. Execution rate and the revenue it implies

Dividing a year's revenue by the opening order book gives the execution (or burn) rate. EIL's FY26 standalone revenue was 32.9% of the March 2025 book (30.9% excluding note 48(a)). Applied to the March 2026 book of ₹15,109 crore, a 28% rate gives about ₹4,230 crore for FY27, consistent with the guidance of about ₹4,200 crore. In plain terms

EIL has swapped one dependency for another. Five years ago almost all its work came from India's state oil companies. RITES' six flat years: exports, turnkey and competitive bidding Deep dive

A record order book that is half turnkey, a fee business losing margin to open tenders, and an export arm that books revenue only when a whole train leaves the factory.

Management's defence is that turnkey is an accounting choice rather than a strategic drift. On the August call the CMD repeated the ₹100-crore example: the same scope earns ₹5 crore as PMC or ₹105 crore as turnkey, with the same fee. It does not change the fact that the consultancy part of the book, ₹2,597 crore, is smaller than in March 2026 (₹2,754 crore), and that consultancy inflow in Q1 FY27 was ₹279 crore against turnkey's ₹306 crore.

In plain terms

RITES' order book is at a record mainly because it is taking on more construction-style jobs for state clients. Those jobs bring big revenue numbers and small profits. The part of the book that makes the money, consultancy and inspection, is not growing.

Nomination is giving way to bidding

The second force is the procurement mode. RITES now reports that about 62% of its order book and about 70% of new orders are won in competition (63% of the book at March 2026), the rest by nomination. A decade ago most of its domestic work came by nomination from ministries and railway bodies. Competitive tenders, often decided on lowest price after a technical qualification, bring tougher margins; management names competitive orders, rising travel costs and the coming pay revision as the three pressures on profitability. The standalone consultancy margin trend, 36.9% in FY25, 33.7% in FY26 and 31.4% in Q1 FY27, is the cleanest evidence that the pressure is real.

RITES has not lost its volume: it booked 128 orders worth ₹670–674 crore in Q1 FY27, about 1.4 orders a day. What it has lost is pricing power on part of the work. Its inspection franchise is the exception: QA revenue reached about ₹70 crore in Q1 FY27, back above its level before the Railways opened part of the work to competition, and management guides double-digit growth.

People are the plant: where ₹100 of revenue goes

A consultant has no factory; its capacity is engineers, its main cost is payroll, and the government sets the pay scale for its permanent staff.

An engineering consultancy's capacity is the number of billable engineer-hours it can sell. There is no physical plant to expand; capital spending is office buildings, software and, at RITES, locomotives for leasing. EIL's net fixed assets were ₹338 crore at March 2026 and its FY26 capex about ₹67 crore. RITES' fixed assets of ₹867 crore are mostly its leased locomotive fleet. Neither company discloses the measure that would matter most, billable utilisation (the share of available engineer-hours charged to projects), so investors have to infer it from revenue per employee and from margins.

EIL had 2,747 permanent employees at 31 March 2026 (2,650 a year earlier), plus 17 non-permanent staff, according to the business-responsibility section of its annual report. Who else draws India's plants: competitors, clients and in-house teams

Two listed companies, a handful of unlisted state consultancies, Tata's engineering arm, global design houses and the clients' own project departments.

The listed sub-sector is just two companies, but the market they compete in is crowded. In hydrocarbons, EIL is the default engineer for public-sector refinery and petrochemical work, with rivals at both ends: global licensors and front-end engineering houses (Technip Energies, KBR, Fluor, Worley, Toyo, McDermott) that control process technology and win much of the private refiners' work, and construction groups such as L&T's energy-hydrocarbon business that take whole projects as EPC contractors. In plain terms

EIL is close to a default choice for India's state oil companies, but it is one bidder among many for private and foreign work. RITES is one of many consultants in transport, strong in inspection and in government-to-government exports, weaker in the large metro and high-speed packages. Both face clients who have learned to do much of the work themselves.

Other rules that shape the numbers

Both companies are Navratnas (EIL since 2014, RITES since October 2023), which gives their boards more freedom to invest in joint ventures and subsidiaries without ministry approval. Their statutory auditors are appointed by the Comptroller and Auditor General. Their regular staff are paid on Department of Public Enterprises scales, revised about once a decade. Both comply with the 25% minimum public shareholding rule. And because they are majority state-owned, their boards are partly appointed by their ministries: EIL added two independent directors in August 2026, and RITES' chairman's tenure was reportedly extended to June 2027.

The net effect of the state's three roles is a business with protected demand and constrained upside. Demand is protected because the state's own capex is large and growing. Upside is constrained because the state, as client, increasingly makes its own companies compete for that capex; as owner, it takes most of the cash out; and as employer, it sets a cost base the companies cannot easily flex.

Sixty years, five phases: from national engineers to divergent stocks

Built to replace foreign designers, listed to raise money for the state, re-rated in the 2023–24 PSU rally, and now moving in opposite directions.

Phase 1: national capability (1965–1990). EIL was incorporated in 1965, with Bechtel as an early collaborator, so that India would not need foreign firms to design each new refinery. RITES was set up in 1974 under the Ministry of Railways to export Indian railway expertise, and from the start it shipped hardware alongside advice. Both grew as near-monopoly in-house engineers to their ministries.

Phase 2: listing and diversification (1990–2014). EIL listed in the 1990s and engineered successive refinery waves (Panipat, Mathura, Bina, Bathinda, Paradip among them). It added lump-sum turnkey work so it could take whole jobs. RITES diversified into highways, ports, airports and urban transport, built its inspection franchise and added turnkey works and locomotive leasing. The government's 2010 and 2014 sales took its EIL stake towards the 51% floor, and EIL became a Navratna in 2014.

Phase 3: cash piles and capital return (2015–2021). Refinery capex slowed after 2015, and DIPAM pushed cash-rich state companies to return capital. EIL bought back shares in 2017 and again in 2020–21 at ₹84. RITES listed in June 2018 and the government sold about 15% more through two offers in 2019–20. RITES' revenue reached its high, ₹2,662 crore, in FY22 on exports to Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and Mozambique.

Phase 4: the PSU re-rating (2023–July 2024). Railway, defence and public-sector stocks multiplied as government capex hit records. EIL peaked at about ₹304 in July 2024 and RITES at about ₹826 before its 1:1 bonus (about ₹413 adjusted). Fundamentals lagged: RITES' revenue fell 7% in FY24 and another 10% in FY25 as exports collapsed, and EIL's FY24 revenue was flat.

Phase 5: de-rating and divergence (August 2024–2026). RITES fell back to ₹175–260 as revenue stalled and margins slipped. EIL went the other way: a record order book, 27% revenue growth (flattered by the note 48(a) item), the Lekki expansion order and, in September 2026, the Kenya order. The West Asia war briefly pushed EIL to a 52-week low of ₹164 in early 2026 before energy-security spending and the Kenya win lifted it to a new high of ₹321.

Reading an engineering consultant: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, two equations and a ten-minute checklist: how to tell a good quarter from a lucky one at a business that sells engineers' time and sometimes books other people's steel.

Every engineering consultant's results can be reduced to two equations. The first turns the order book into revenue: order book × execution rate = revenue. The second turns revenue into profit: revenue × segment mix × segment margin, less unallocated corporate cost, plus other income = profit before tax. Management controls some of the terms (which orders to bid for, whether to take work as PMC or turnkey, cost discipline on site) and not others (when clients award, how fast they build, interest rates on the cash pile, the government pay scale).

A worked example in round numbers

Take an ILLUSTRATIVE consultant with an opening order book of ₹10,000 crore and an execution rate of 30%. Revenue is ₹3,000 crore. If 55% is consultancy at a 25% segment margin, consultancy earns ₹412.5 crore; the other 45% is turnkey at 7%, earning ₹94.5 crore. Segment profit is ₹507 crore. Subtract ₹150 crore of unallocated corporate cost to get operating profit of ₹357 crore, add ₹150 crore of other income to get profit before tax of ₹507 crore, and at 25% tax, profit after tax is about ₹380 crore. Now change only the mix: at 45% consultancy and 55% turnkey, segment profit falls to ₹453 crore and profit after tax to about ₹340 crore, a 10.6% drop with identical revenue. Change only the execution rate to 25%: revenue falls to ₹2,500 crore and profit after tax to about ₹316 crore. The numbers resemble EIL's FY27 guidance on purpose.

In plain terms

Ten points of mix change profit by about a tenth; five points of execution rate change it by about a sixth. Neither shows up in "orders won" headlines. That is why the KPIs below are mostly about what kind of work is in the book and how fast it is being billed.

1. Order book and order inflow, by bucket

What it is. The order book is the unexecuted value of signed contracts at the period end; inflow is the value of new contracts signed during the period. Formula: closing book = opening book + inflow − revenue recognised ± scope changes and cancellations. Worked example: EIL opened FY26 with ₹11,717 crore, won ₹7,978 crore and recognised ₹3,850 crore of standalone revenue; 11,717 + 7,978 − 3,850 = ₹15,845 crore, against a reported ₹15,109 crore, implying about ₹736 crore of de-scoping, exchange-rate and other adjustments. Drivers: client capex cycles, award timing, win rate in competitive tenders, and the choice between PMC and turnkey on the same project (which changes the order value twenty-fold).

Benchmark: what matters is not the size but the composition. A book that is over 60% consultancy is good, 40–60% average, under 40% weak. EIL's June 2026 book was ₹14,424 crore, 73% consultancy (domestic ₹4,046 crore, overseas ₹6,452 crore), turnkey ₹3,926 crore. Definition trap: RITES' REMCL figure is an annualised amount rather than signed contract value, and broker notes round the June book to ₹9,450 crore while the company deck prints ₹9,445 crore. EIL's book will jump in Q2 FY27 by at least ₹4,270 crore for Kenya, a US-dollar contract converted at a rate the company has not disclosed.

5. One-off and variable-consideration normalisation

What it is. Adjusting reported profit for items that will not recur: price restorations, change-order claims booked before approval, provisions written back on settlement. Formula: normalised profit = reported profit − one-off gains + one-off losses (each after tax). Worked example: EIL's FY26 consolidated profit of ₹692 crore less ₹213.58 crore of note 48(a) profit taxed at 25% (about ₹160 crore) = about ₹532 crore; FY25's ₹580 crore less ₹112.27 crore of variable-consideration profit after tax (about ₹84 crore) = about ₹496 crore. Underlying growth was about 7%, not 19% (INDICATIVE). Benchmark: good is one-offs below 5% of profit before tax, average 5–15%, weak above 15%. 7. Billable utilisation: the number nobody discloses

What it is. The share of engineers' available hours that are charged to client projects. It is the single most important operating KPI of any consultancy, and neither EIL nor RITES discloses it. Formula: billed hours ÷ available hours. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): 2,000 billable engineers × 1,800 available hours a year × 75% utilisation × ₹5,000 an hour = ₹1,350 crore of fee revenue. At 65% utilisation the same team produces ₹1,170 crore, ₹180 crore less, while the payroll is unchanged; at a 25% margin that drop wipes out more than half of segment profit. Drivers: the timing of awards (engineers sit on the bench between projects), the share of long PMC jobs (high, steady utilisation) vs studies (lumpy), and whether the firm can redeploy engineers across sectors.

Benchmark: global design firms typically target 70–80% (INDICATIVE). How they compare: not disclosed by either, so the proxies are consultancy revenue per employee (KPI 6) and the consultancy margin (KPI 4): when EIL's consultancy margin fell to 16.7% in the June 2025 quarter and recovered to 24–28% in later quarters, utilisation was almost certainly the main moving part. RITES' hiring of contract staff ahead of its book (KPI 6) is a bet that utilisation will rise. Definition trap: management commentary about a "bench build-up" is a utilisation statement in disguise: it means more hours are being paid for than billed, for now.

In plain terms

If you could know one number about a consultant, it would be how busy its engineers are. These two companies do not publish it. The best substitutes are the consultancy margin and consultancy revenue per employee, read over several quarters; a sudden margin drop with no one-off in the notes usually means engineers waiting for work.

8. Other income as a share of profit before tax

What it is. Income that does not come from operations: interest on cash and deposits, dividends from strategic stakes, rentals, exchange gains, mutual-fund gains. Formula: other income ÷ profit before tax. Worked example: EIL's FY26 consolidated other income of ₹242 crore ÷ profit before tax of ₹894 crore = 27%; RITES' ₹123 crore ÷ ₹608 crore = 20%. EIL's standalone other income of ₹209 crore breaks down into about ₹94.8 crore of interest, ₹43.8 crore of Numaligarh dividend, ₹24.2 crore of rent, ₹19.1 crore of dividend from its inspection subsidiary CEIL, ₹13.8 crore of exchange gains and ₹9.4 crore of mutual-fund gains. Drivers: the size of the cash pile, deposit rates (the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which lifts treasury yields at the margin) and the dividend policies of the companies they hold stakes in. A 50-basis-point change in yield on EIL's ~₹1,450 crore of treasury cash is about ₹7 crore of pre-tax income a year.

Benchmark: below 15% good (profit is mostly operating), 15–30% average, above 30% weak. Both sit in the average band in FY26. EIL's share has been as high as 46% (FY24), when its operating margin was 9%; RITES' was 11–14% in FY22–FY24 and has risen to 20–21% as operating profit fell. At RITES, whether interest earned on ring-fenced client funds is kept or passed to clients has not been confirmed from the accounts.

9. Own cash vs client money

What it is. The part of the cash on the balance sheet that the company could actually distribute, as opposed to money held for clients against work not yet done. Formula: own cash = gross cash and bank − client funds held (ring-fenced deposits and advances that must be spent on clients' projects). Worked example: RITES' FY25 annual report splits standalone cash and bank into ₹860.8 crore of owned funds and ₹2,474.9 crore of clients' funds, with an equivalent liability to clients (March 2024: ₹698.5 crore and ₹2,372.6 crore). Its March 2026 consolidated cash and bank was ₹2,946.1 crore (₹557.0 crore cash and equivalents plus ₹2,389.1 crore of other bank balances); the March 2026 split has not been published in a document available for this report, so own funds are estimated at about ₹600–900 crore (INDICATIVE). EIL holds ₹1,363.9 crore of cash and bank plus ₹84 crore of current investments, against ₹662.4 crore of income received in advance and ₹151.7 crore of client advances.

Drivers: the volume of deposit-work and turnkey projects (clients pay ahead), advance terms on consultancy, and how fast the company spends clients' money. Benchmark: own cash covering at least a year of dividends is good. How they compare: the two companies' screener "other liabilities", mostly client advances and payables, total ₹5,949 crore (EIL ₹2,707 crore, RITES ₹3,242 crore), more than their combined gross cash of ₹4,394 crore. 10. Receivable days, unbilled revenue and cash conversion

What it is. How long clients take to pay, and how much work has been done but not yet billed. Formula: receivable (debtor) days = trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365; cash conversion = operating cash flow ÷ profit after tax. Worked example: RITES' trade receivables of ₹931.2 crore ÷ revenue of ₹2,415 crore × 365 = 141 days. EIL's standalone trade receivables were ₹451.4 crore, but its unbilled revenue (work done, not yet invoiced) rose to ₹1,096.7 crore from ₹801.2 crore, while income received in advance fell to ₹662.4 crore from ₹848.9 crore. Netting receivables, unbilled revenue, advances and billing in excess, EIL moved from roughly balanced to being owed about ₹734 crore, which is why its operating cash flow was only ₹319 crore against profit of ₹692 crore (0.46×).

Benchmark: receivable days below 60 good, 60–120 average, above 120 weak; cash conversion above 0.9× good, 0.6–0.9× average, below 0.6× weak. EIL's debtor days are good (45 in FY26) but its cash conversion is weak (0.46×, 0.20× in FY25) because the cash is tied up in unbilled work and claims. 11. Contingent liabilities and claims

What it is. Possible obligations that are not on the balance sheet because the company judges an outflow less than probable: disputed taxes, claims by contractors and clients, guarantees. Formula: contingent liabilities ÷ net worth, after removing items the client must reimburse. Worked example: EIL's contingent liabilities were ₹742.1 crore at March 2026 (₹758.6 crore a year earlier): ₹196.6 crore of commercial claims in courts or arbitration, ₹507.8 crore of Karnataka VAT for FY2009-10 to FY2016-17 (under appeal in the Supreme Court and reimbursable by the client under the contract) and ₹35.5 crore of GST. On EIL's ₹3,146 crore net worth that is 24%, or about 7% excluding the client-reimbursable VAT.

Separately, note 49 describes a contract terminated in April 2016 after an investigating agency found the contractor's qualifying certificate to be bogus; the contractor claims ₹409.6 crore against EIL's counter-claim of ₹129.1 crore, pending in the Supreme Court. In another dispute, EIL deposited ₹164.8 crore with the Delhi High Court registrar in May 2022 against an arbitral award of US$12.65 million plus interest while it challenges the award (₹96.3 crore is provided and ₹68.5 crore shown as contingent). Benchmark: net contingent liabilities below 10% of net worth good, 10–25% average, above 25% weak. RITES' FY26 contingent liabilities could not be obtained because its FY26 annual report was not accessible. Definition trap: large headline contingent figures can be mostly pass-through (EIL's VAT), while an item outside the contingent note, like the ₹409.6 crore claim, can matter more.

12. Return on equity, with and without the cash

What it is. Profit as a return on shareholders' equity; and the same return on the equity actually used in the business, once surplus cash and strategic stakes are removed. Formula: ROE = PAT ÷ average equity; ROE ex-cash = (PAT − post-tax other income − JV share) ÷ (equity − own cash − strategic investments). Worked example: EIL's FY26 ROE is about 23.8% (₹692 crore ÷ average equity of about ₹2,908 crore). Ex-cash: core profit of about ₹463 crore on equity net of ₹1,448 crore of treasury and ₹1,522 crore of stakes, about ₹176 crore, is not meaningful (above 100%). RITES' ROE is 15.4% (screener) or 15.6% on owners' profit; on owned cash of about ₹861 crore, core profit of about ₹304 crore on about ₹1,820 crore of equity gives roughly 17% (INDICATIVE).

Drivers: margins and mix (ROE), payout (a high payout keeps equity small) and the cash pile (which earns only treasury yields). Benchmark: ROE above 22% good, 15–22% average, below 15% weak. EIL is good and rising; RITES is average and has fallen from 24.4% in FY20. Definition trap: a capital-light consultancy's ROE ex-cash is often infinite, because client advances fund the working capital; the useful number is the trend in reported ROE and the share of it that comes from other income.

  • Exports book on despatch. A rake that leaves on 1 October rather than 30 September moves about ₹92 crore of revenue into the next quarter.
  • Gross cash vs own cash. RITES' gross cash is about three-quarters clients' money (FY25 split). "Cash per share" without that note overstates the cushion.
  • Price deductions and variable consideration. Under Ind AS 115 an expected delay penalty reduces revenue and can later reverse (EIL note 48(a)); an unapproved change order can be booked as revenue if judged highly probable (the HRRL ₹86 crore).
  • Order-book conventions. RITES' REMCL bucket is annualised; brokers round to ₹9,450 crore against the deck's ₹9,445 crore; EIL's Kenya order will enter at an undisclosed exchange rate.
  • Quarterly "other income". At EIL, screener's quarterly line appears to include the RFCL share, so it is not comparable with the annual figure.
  • Headcount bases. EIL 2,747 (annual report) vs 2,735 (manpower deck); RITES 3,124 incl. 1,379 contract staff (Q1 deck) vs 3,169 on a wider basis (Q4 deck).
  • "Book-to-bill" means two things. Some brokers use it for order-book ÷ revenue (cover), others for inflow ÷ revenue. RITES' "3.8× book-to-bill" in broker notes is cover.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number for these two companies has a second version on a different basis. Before comparing two figures, check that both are standalone or both consolidated, both include or exclude other income, and both include or exclude the one-off.

How to read an engineering consultant's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Open the segment table first (2 minutes). Note consultancy revenue and margin, and turnkey revenue and margin. Normal ranges: EIL consultancy 22–28%, turnkey 5–8%; RITES standalone consultancy 31–37%, turnkey 1.5–2%, leasing 34–38%. Anything outside the range is a one-off until proven otherwise.
  2. Compute the mix. Consultancy ÷ total revenue (EIL guides at least 55% for FY27; Q1 was 62% standalone). A falling consultancy share explains a falling margin before any cost story does.
  3. Read the notes to the results for exceptional, variable-consideration and writeback items. Look for words such as "change order", "price variation", "liquidated damages", "provision no longer required". Restate profit without them.
  4. Check the order book by bucket, not just the total. For EIL: domestic consultancy, overseas consultancy, turnkey; for RITES: consultancy, turnkey, exports. Red flag: a rising total with a falling consultancy bucket.
  5. Check inflow and book-to-bill for the quarter and the year to date, against guidance (EIL ₹8,000 crore; RITES an order book of ₹10,000 crore by March 2027). Note which bucket the inflow went into.
  6. RITES only: count the rakes and locomotives despatched. Export revenue is binary by quarter. Compare it with the ₹300 crore FY27 floor and the stated delivery schedule (Bangladesh, Mozambique, South Africa).
  7. Compare operating margin with guidance on the right basis. EIL: 16% (its definition) and 24–25% consultancy segment margin; RITES: red lines of 20% EBITDA and 15% PAT on an annual consolidated basis.
  8. Separate other income and the JV share. At EIL, the RFCL contribution can swing quarterly profit by ₹40–50 crore; at both, other income is a fifth to a quarter of profit before tax.
  9. At half-year, check the balance sheet: unbilled revenue, receivables, client advances, operating cash flow. Red flag: profit rising while unbilled revenue rises faster.
  10. Listen to the call for four things: Middle East awards (EIL), Kenya phasing and Dangote financing, the BPCL Andhra tender timetable, and the nomination vs competition split of new orders (RITES).
In plain terms

The fastest way to misread these companies is to start with revenue growth and the headline margin. Start with the segment table, then the notes, then the order book by bucket. If those three agree, the quarter is real; if they do not, the headline is telling you something else.

Cash that isn't all theirs: client money, stakes and the core P/E Deep dive

Strip out the treasury, the strategic stakes, the clients' money and the other income, and the two stocks cost about the same: 25–28 times core earnings.

Both companies are debt-free and both carry large cash balances, which makes their headline price-to-earnings multiples look modest for capital-light businesses with returns on equity of 15–24%. The cash needs careful handling. Some of it is the companies' own surplus. Some of it is clients' money, held against work not yet done. Some of the "investments" are strategic stakes in clients' projects, which earn dividends and equity-accounted profit and are worth something in their own right. And a fifth to a quarter of profit before tax comes from this pile rather than from engineering. The right comparison strips all of it out on both sides: value of the operating business ÷ profit of the operating business.

EIL: treasury plus two strategic stakes

At March 2026 EIL held ₹1,363.9 crore of cash and bank balances (₹87.8 crore of cash and equivalents and ₹1,276.1 crore of other bank balances) and ₹84 crore of current investments: about ₹1,448 crore of treasury. On top of that, it owns 4.37% of Numaligarh Refinery (7.69 crore shares, carried at fair value of ₹1,024.1 crore, up from ₹938.3 crore a year earlier) and 26% of RFCL, the Ramagundam urea plant it helped build with NFL and FCIL (equity-method carrying value ₹497.9 crore). Together the stakes are on the books at about ₹1,522 crore. The Numaligarh dividend was ₹43.8 crore in FY26; RFCL contributed ₹47.4 crore of equity-accounted profit, and management expects RFCL's first dividend in FY27. Against all of this, EIL holds client money in the form of ₹662.4 crore of income received in advance and ₹151.7 crore of client advances, and its unbilled revenue of ₹1,096.7 crore is cash it is owed.

RITES: mostly clients' money

RITES' consolidated cash and bank at March 2026 was ₹2,946.1 crore. Its FY25 annual report, the latest available split, divided standalone cash into ₹860.8 crore of owned funds and ₹2,474.9 crore of clients' funds held for deposit works and turnkey projects, with an equal liability. Using ₹2,946 crore as "RITES' cash" (as one broker's net-debt figure of −₹2,504 crore effectively does) overstates the cushion by about ₹2,000 crore. RITES' other financial liabilities at March 2026, ₹1,991.5 crore current and ₹446.9 crore non-current, are largely those client deposits. The owned-fund figure moves with dividends: at a 95% payout, RITES retains little of each year's profit.

In plain terms

RITES looks cheaper than it is because most of its cash belongs to clients; EIL looks cheaper than it is because FY26 profit contains a contract-price restoration that will not recur. Forward view: the multiples converge

One broker's FY27 estimates put EIL's consolidated profit at about ₹707 crore (with ₹189 crore of other income and ₹115 crore of JV profit) and RITES' owners' profit at about ₹449 crore (₹106 crore of other income, ₹14 crore of JV share). On the same method, core profit would be about ₹450 crore at EIL and ₹355 crore at RITES, giving core multiples of about 26× and 24× (INDICATIVE). Annualising EIL's Q1 FY27 core profit (₹158 crore less ₹42.5 crore of RFCL share and about ₹28.5 crore of post-tax other income, about ₹87 crore) gives about 33×, but the June quarter is seasonally weak at both companies. On combined numbers, the two operating businesses are valued at about ₹20,176 crore against core FY26 profit of about ₹767 crore: about 26× (INDICATIVE).

Three things would change the picture. A higher yield on cash (the RBI's October rate rise) lifts other income, which flatters headline earnings but not core earnings. A large return of capital (another EIL buyback, or a special dividend) would shrink the cash and equity and raise reported ROE without changing core value. And the Kenya order, if it executes on schedule, would raise EIL's core profit by more than any of the adjustments above, which is what the market is pricing.

Why the cash matters for the government too

For the majority owner, these companies are also a source of cash. Of the roughly ₹663 crore of FY26 dividends, about ₹420 crore went to the government. DIPAM's guidelines push cash-rich public-sector companies towards buybacks when reserves are large, and EIL has done two. With the government at 51.32% of EIL, a buyback in which the government tenders would push its stake closer to the 51% line; with RITES, the government's 72.20% leaves room for an offer for sale instead. Either route returns cash to the state; minority shareholders' share depends on the route.

Approximate market ranges (INDICATIVE, not from company documents) put Technip Energies at roughly 10–13 times earnings, Worley 16–20 times, Jacobs and AECOM about 20–26 times forward earnings, and WSP 25–30 times. In plain terms

By world standards the Indian consultants are small, cheap to run and very profitable on fee work, but they take much longer to turn orders into revenue and depend on far fewer clients. EIL's African wins show it can compete abroad on price; the global firms' 2026 problems show that the same Gulf exposure EIL wants carries real risk.

Technology and disruption: fewer hours, new fuels, electric sidings

Software that cuts engineering hours, an energy transition that changes what gets built, and clients who keep doing more themselves.

Model-based and AI-assisted engineering. 3D plant models, building-information modelling, digital twins and generative design for piping and layout reduce the engineer-hours needed per project. For a consultancy paid by the hour, that is a threat: clients will eventually re-price fees to reflect it. For one paid a lump sum or a percentage of project cost, it is an opportunity: the same fee with fewer hours means higher margins. EIL's 2,346 engineering-qualified staff (manpower-deck basis) are its capacity; if productivity tools raise output per engineer, the company can execute a larger book (Kenya plus domestic work) without proportionate hiring, but only if it prices work by outcome rather than by hour.

The energy transition. Global refining capacity will eventually decline, but Indian and African demand growth extends the refinery-building runway into the 2030s, which is why both of EIL's largest recent orders are refineries. EIL's adjacent bets (coal gasification, green hydrogen, compressed biogas and biorefineries, nuclear balance of plant, carbon-capture studies) are real but small; no carbon-capture order has been quantified. Coal gasification and nuclear are the two areas where policy support could make the new verticals large within five years.

Electrification and the leasing fleet. RITES' 91 leased locomotives serve industrial sidings at power plants, steel mills and ports. Much of that fleet is diesel. As sidings electrify and battery or hybrid shunters become viable, the leasing business, which earns about 35% margins, faces a slow erosion over 10–15 years unless RITES re-fleets.

Clients doing it themselves. The oil companies have strengthened their in-house project departments, and RVNL, the high-speed rail corporation and the metro corporations do much of their own general consultancy. Each year of record capex makes clients more capable. Licensor-led contracting (process licensors bundling front-end design with proprietary equipment) and modular construction squeeze the independent engineer's role in petrochemicals. And Chinese engineering and finance compete directly with EIL in Africa and with RITES' credit-line-backed rolling-stock exports.

The biggest technology risk is not that software replaces engineers but that clients use it to pay for fewer engineer-hours. Companies that sell outcomes (a design, a supervised project) at a fixed price gain; companies that sell hours lose. Both members are moving, slowly, towards the first model.

There is an opposite force too. Digital project management, drone and sensor-based inspection, and digital twins of rail and refinery assets create new fee lines for a consultant that already owns the client relationship. RITES' inspection franchise in particular could become a data business if it digitises how it certifies rolling stock and components. None of this is visible in the numbers yet.

Bull vs bear: order books against one-offs, yield against drift

The case for and against the sub-sector, set out straight, with the numbers each side leans on.

The bull case

  • Record books, rich in consultancy at EIL: ₹23,869 crore combined at June 2026 (3.8× revenue), EIL 73% consultancy, before at least ₹4,270 crore for Kenya.
  • A capex runway at home: railway capex of ₹2,93,030 crore in FY27 (+10.5%); BPCL's ₹1.45 lakh crore Andhra refinery, SPR Phase II and coal gasification behind the current refinery wave.
  • High fee margins on cheap engineers: consultancy segment margins of 24–34%, about 1.5–2.5 times global design firms, with almost no capital employed.
  • Proven overseas franchise: two Dangote mandates worth about US$800 million show EIL can win large international PMC work against Chinese and Western rivals.
  • Exports back: RITES' rolling-stock book (₹1,775 crore) is the largest in four years, with a quarterly order cadence promised.
  • Cash returns: about ₹663 crore of dividends in FY26; RITES yields 4.05% with a 95% payout; both debt-free.

The bear case

  • Flattered earnings: EIL's FY26 profit growth was about 7% underlying, not 19%; an unapproved ₹86 crore HRRL claim sits in unbilled revenue.
  • Concentration: Dangote is the majority of EIL's future overseas fees; two clients are 95% of EIL's turnkey revenue; the Middle East has stalled.
  • Turnkey drift at RITES: half the book earns 1.5–2%; standalone consultancy margin down from 36.9% to 31.4% in five quarters.
  • Six flat years: combined revenue grew 1.8% a year from FY20 to FY26 while India's infrastructure capex nearly doubled.
  • Cash is less than it looks: about three-quarters of RITES' cash is clients' money; core P/E is about 25× (EIL) and 28× (RITES), not 18–23×.
  • The owner: a possible RITES share sale (about 21 points saleable), competitive bidding in place of nomination, and a pay revision of unconfirmed timing.

The bull case rests on conversion: that the record order books, especially EIL's consultancy-heavy book, turn into profit at today's segment margins over FY27–FY30. On EIL's guidance (revenue of about ₹4,200 crore in FY27 and ₹5,000 crore in FY28, at least 55% consultancy at 24–25%), plus the Kenya contract executing on a normal curve, EIL's consultancy profit could roughly double from FY26's underlying level by FY29 (ILLUSTRATIVE). RITES' bull case is narrower: exports of ₹300–450 crore a year at mid-teens margins, inspection growing double digits and the consultancy margin stabilising above 31%, with the 95% payout turning modest growth into a total return.

The bear case rests on quality and dependence. Strip out the one-offs and the client money, and investors are already paying growth multiples for businesses that did not grow for six years. EIL's growth is concentrated in one private African sponsor and in an Indian refinery tender it may not win; its cash conversion has been weak for two years. RITES' book is growing in the part that earns least, its fee margins are falling as more work is tendered, and its owner may sell more stock. Neither company controls the pace at which its clients build.

In plain terms

The optimist sees two firms with the best order books in their history at the start of a decade of state and African capex. The sceptic sees one firm whose best year was partly a contract-price reversal and one whose record book is mostly low-margin construction, both priced at about 25–28 times core earnings. The next four quarters of segment margins and order composition will show which reading is closer.

What would settle the argument? For EIL: the Q2 FY27 order book with Kenya, a consultancy margin holding at 24–25% while turnkey recovers in the second half, an improvement in cash conversion, approval or reversal of the HRRL change order, and a BPCL Andhra award. For RITES: the Bangladesh rakes arriving on a regular cadence, standalone consultancy margins holding above 31%, consultancy's share of new orders rising rather than falling, and clarity on whether the government plans to sell more shares.

The two results days will answer most near-term questions: how big EIL's book becomes with Kenya, whether its margins hold without one-offs, and how far RITES' export revenue has slipped.
