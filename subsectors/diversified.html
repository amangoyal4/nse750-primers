00

The one-page view: seven companies, one question — what is the structure worth?

This is not an industry. It is a valuation problem that seven very different companies happen to share.

The seven companies in this report have no customer, raw material or regulator in common. Adani Enterprises runs eight airports, makes solar modules and wind turbines, smelts copper and mines coal under contract. Grasim makes viscose fibre, caustic soda and paint, and controls India's largest cement company and a large financial-services group. Godrej Industries makes fatty alcohols and owns big stakes in a soap-and-hair-dye company, a property developer and an animal-feed business. 3M India sells tapes, abrasives and medical consumables under licence from its American parent. DCM Shriram makes caustic soda, PVC, sugar, ethanol, urea and uPVC windows inside one legal entity. Swan Corp trades polymers, owns most of a listed shipyard and an LNG terminal that has never operated. RattanIndia Enterprises sells goods on Amazon and builds electric motorcycles.

What unites them is that the usual tool of equity analysis, a single price-to-earnings multiple applied to group profit, gives a misleading answer for every one of them. Grasim's consolidated profit includes a lender's earnings and the minority shareholders' share of UltraTech. Adani Enterprises reported a record operating profit in Q1 FY27 and a net loss in the same quarter. DCM Shriram's Q1 FY27 profit was about 80% one-off items. Swan Corp's profit came entirely from "other income" while its operations lost money. RattanIndia's reported result moves with the market price of a stake it holds in another listed company. To understand these businesses, the reader has to value the parts, work out who owns what, check how cash moves between the parent and its subsidiaries, and judge whether the people in control will ever hand that value to minority shareholders.

Three findings frame everything that follows.

  • Two names are the sub-sector. Adani Enterprises (₹3.64 lakh crore) and Grasim (₹1.99 lakh crore) are 85% of the seven's combined ₹6.60 lakh crore market value. Any aggregate is really "Adani plus Birla plus five others", so this report shows every total with and without Adani.
  • Two opposite valuation regimes sit side by side. On our illustrative sum-of-the-parts, Adani Enterprises trades about ₹95,000 crore (roughly 26% of its market value) above what its existing businesses would be worth at mature multiples. Grasim and Godrej Industries trade below the market value of their listed stakes alone: Grasim's stakes in UltraTech and Aditya Birla Capital are worth 1.18 times Grasim's own market value, and Godrej Industries' three listed stakes are worth 1.43 times its market value.

The five forces that define this sub-sector

  • Structure beats operations in valuation. For six of the seven, the share price is driven more by structure (stake prices, holdco debt, demerger plans) than by the parent's own profit and loss account. Grasim's price follows UltraTech; Godrej Industries follows Godrej Consumer and Godrej Properties; Adani Enterprises follows the market's belief in its next listings.
  • Distribute or be discounted. Adani Enterprises demerged its matured businesses to shareholders in 2015 and 2018 and is priced at a premium. Grasim has held UltraTech since the 2000s and Godrej Industries pays no dividend; both trade at discounts of roughly 28% and 36% on our illustrative workings.
  • Old engines fund new bets. Every holdco here is funding a loss-making or pre-profit engine from its own balance sheet: airports, solar manufacturing, copper and data centres at Adani; Birla Opus paints and Birla Pivot at Grasim; Godrej Capital at Godrej Industries; a shipyard at Swan; electric motorcycles and drones at RattanIndia. The discount widens or narrows with disclosure and the break-even path of these bets.
  • Governance is priced. Promoter pledges (Swan 40.7%), related-party intensity (3M India about 31% of revenue), dependence on other income (Swan, Godrej Industries) and Adani's US legal arc move multiples more than any segment margin.
  • Commodity cores pay the bills. Viscose fibre, caustic soda, oleochemicals, PVC, sugar, coal trading and mining services are cyclical. When they turn up, as viscose and caustic did in Q1 FY27, holdco earnings jump; when they turn down, the holdco leans on dividends from its listed subsidiaries and on borrowing.

In plain terms

Buying a share of a conglomerate is buying a basket that someone else controls. The market charges a fee for that control when the controller keeps the basket closed, borrows against it and spends the cash on new ventures without saying how they are doing. It pays a premium when the controller has a record of opening the basket and handing shareholders the contents. That single idea explains most of the gap between Adani Enterprises at a premium and Grasim and Godrej Industries at a discount.

The rest of the report is organised around that idea. Sections 01–05 explain the five business models, the size of the group and the basis problems in its numbers, where revenue goes, the operating engines underneath and the wider universe of Indian conglomerates. Sections 06, 07, 09 and 10 are the deep dives: how to value a conglomerate in rupees, why Adani Enterprises earns a premium and how it funds itself, the governance signals that matter, and demergers as the main way Indian groups have closed the discount since 2023. Section 13 is the KPI masterclass. The peer table, positioning maps and seven company profiles follow, then the global lens, technology risks, the bull and bear cases and a watch-list with thresholds.

01

Five ways to own a business, and why each is priced differently

Incubator, operating holdco, diversified operator, MNC subsidiary and small-cap venture holdco: same label, different machines.

"Conglomerate" covers at least five different business models in this group. They differ in where the profit is made, how it reaches the listed company's shareholders, and what can stop it on the way. Getting the archetype right is the first step, because each one calls for a different valuation tool and a different KPI.

Each step is a leak. The holdco discount is the market's estimate of how much is lost between Step 1 and Step 5, plus a charge for the risk that Step 4 consumes the rest.

Archetype 1: the incubator (Adani Enterprises)

Adani Enterprises seeds national-scale infrastructure businesses inside the flagship, builds them with a mix of debt and equity raised at the parent, and, once they are mature, demerges or lists them. It has done this before: Adani Ports, Adani Power and Adani Transmission were demerged in 2015, and Adani Green Energy and Adani Total Gas in 2018. Shareholders of Adani Enterprises at those dates received shares in the new companies directly. Today the incubator holds airports, solar and wind manufacturing (Adani New Industries), mining services, coal trading, roads, a copper smelter, a 50:50 data-centre venture and a PVC plant under construction. It is valued on a sum-of-the-parts of the maturing assets plus an option value on future listings, less net debt. The KPIs that settle the argument are contracted EBITDA growth, capex relative to operating cash flow, net external debt to EBITDA and the timing and terms of each demerger.

Archetype 2: the operating holdco (Grasim, Godrej Industries)

An operating holdco controls listed crown jewels and also runs businesses of its own. Grasim controls UltraTech Cement (56.11%) and Aditya Birla Capital (about 52.3%) and runs viscose and cellulosic fibre, chlor-alkali chemicals, Birla Opus paints, the Birla Pivot business-to-business building-materials platform, renewables and textiles. Godrej Industries holds about 23.7% of Godrej Consumer Products, 44.8% of Godrej Properties and 64.9% of Godrej Agrovet, runs an oleochemicals business and owns Godrej Capital, a housing-finance and lending business. The valuation is a sum of the listed stakes at market, the holdco's own businesses on multiples and holdco net debt, minus a holdco discount. The KPIs are the discount itself, the ratio of listed stakes to the holdco's own market value, holdco net debt and the break-even path of the new engines.

Archetype 3: the diversified operator (DCM Shriram)

DCM Shriram runs five or six uncorrelated businesses inside one legal entity: chloro-vinyl (caustic soda, chlorine derivatives, PVC), sugar and ethanol, urea, farm inputs, hybrid seeds and Fenesta windows. Everything is wholly owned, so there are no listed stakes and no minority interests to strip out, and one balance sheet funds all the capital spending. The idea is that the cycles offset: in FY25–26 chloro-vinyl profits carried the company while sugar was squeezed by cane costs. It is valued on consolidated EV/EBITDA and P/E at a discount to pure-play peers, with a segment sum-of-the-parts as a cross-check. The KPIs are segment profit mix, return on capital through the capex cycle and the caustic and PVC spreads.

Archetype 4: the MNC subsidiary (3M India)

3M India is 75% owned by 3M Company of the US. It licenses the parent's technology platforms and brands into India across four segments (transportation and electronics, safety and industrial, health care, consumer) and pays royalty and management fees to the parent. It is valued as a scarce, asset-light, high-return compounder, on a P/E in the 65–80 times range. The KPIs are return on capital, royalty and related-party payments as a share of sales, the dividend payout ratio and the parent's own restructuring: the 2024 spin-off of its health-care business as Solventum and its exit from PFAS chemicals.

Archetype 5: the small-cap turnaround or venture holdco (Swan Corp, RattanIndia Enterprises)

These two collect assets that are distressed or early-stage and ask the market to value them before they earn. Swan holds a polymer-trading business, an LNG import terminal project at Jafrabad in Gujarat that is not earning, most of a revived shipyard that is separately listed, and textiles and Mumbai real estate. RattanIndia owns Cocoblu, an Amazon marketplace seller that produces about 98% of revenue, plus Revolt electric motorcycles, NeoSky drones and a fintech venture. A P/E is meaningless for both, because reported profit is driven by other income or by fair-value marks. The useful measures are price to book, the look-through value of any listed stakes, operating profit excluding other income, the promoter pledge and dilution.

In plain terms

Before looking at any number, ask one question: where does the money actually get made, and does it ever come back to me? At DCM Shriram the answer is "in the company I own, all of it". At Grasim it is "mostly at UltraTech, which pays Grasim a dividend that Grasim then spends". At 3M India part of it goes to the US parent as fees before any profit is struck. At Swan and RattanIndia the answer today is "not yet". Each answer needs a different yardstick.

The archetype also tells the reader which risk dominates. For the incubator it is funding: the build runs ahead of operating cash flow for years, so the question is whether lenders and equity investors keep supplying capital on acceptable terms. For the operating holdco it is capital allocation: whether cash from the crown jewels is reinvested well or poured into sub-scale ventures. For the diversified operator it is the cycle, because offsetting cycles do not always offset. For the MNC subsidiary it is the parent, which sets royalties, product scope and dividend policy. For the venture holdco it is survival and dilution.

Warning two: most of the borrowing belongs to lenders and subsidiaries

The seven's combined borrowings of ₹3.92 lakh crore look alarming next to ₹54,728 crore of operating profit. About ₹2.79 lakh crore of that sits in Grasim's and Godrej Industries' consolidated balance sheets, and most of it is the funding of lending subsidiaries (Aditya Birla Capital's ₹2.19 lakh crore loan book; Godrej Capital) and of Godrej Properties' real-estate inventory. Borrowing to fund a loan book is the raw material of a lender, not leverage in the industrial sense. The holdco-level debt of the two, which is what a Grasim or Godrej Industries shareholder actually stands behind, is ₹12,490 crore and ₹11,063 crore respectively.

Warning three: profit "including minority interest" overstates what shareholders own

Screener's net profit line includes the share of profit belonging to minority shareholders of subsidiaries. For Grasim that makes a large difference: FY26 consolidated profit of ₹10,300 crore includes UltraTech's other 44% of owners and ABCL's other 48%; profit attributable to Grasim's own shareholders was ₹4,966 crore. At Godrej Industries the gap is similar in proportion (₹2,412 crore versus about ₹1,241 crore attributable to owners). On the owners' basis, the seven earned about ₹17,436 crore in FY26, and the aggregate P/E rises from 27.3 times to about 38 times. Adani Enterprises has its own three-way version of the problem: its FY26 profit is ₹9,951 crore on screener's basis, ₹9,746 crore attributable to owners on the company's restated basis, and ₹9,339 crore in our earlier primer. This report uses the company's ₹9,746 crore and states the basis.

In plain terms

Totals for this group are like adding a family's household budget to the budgets of the companies where family members work. You get a big number, but most of it is not the family's money. For any conglomerate, first separate what the listed parent owns outright, what it controls but shares with minority owners, and what is really a lender's balance sheet. Only then compare.

Three conventions are used from here on. Market values are at the 16 October 2026 close. Profit is shown on the owners' basis where the company reports it, with screener's figure including minorities alongside. Operating profit is the company's own EBITDA where it reports one; screener's "operating profit" is shown where it is the only consistent figure, with a note when the two differ. The differences are not small: screener puts Grasim's FY26 operating profit at ₹36,296 crore because it treats Aditya Birla Capital's lending interest as revenue and its interest cost as a financing item, while Grasim's own consolidated EBITDA is ₹25,872 crore.

Third, 3M India's cost line hides a second owner's claim. About ₹27 of every ₹100 of revenue is spent on goods bought from other 3M group companies, and a further ₹4.20 goes to the parent as royalty (₹87 crore) and corporate management fees (₹129 crore). These are normal for an MNC subsidiary and below the thresholds at which Indian rules require minority approval, but they mean the parent's economic take from India is larger than its 75% share of profit. Fourth, RattanIndia is a reseller with a narrative attached. About ₹95 of every ₹100 is the cost of goods Cocoblu sells on Amazon. The remaining few rupees cannot carry the costs of building electric motorcycles and drones, which is why the operating line is negative. DCM Shriram, the one archetype where all the businesses are wholly owned and operating, sits in the middle: ₹11 of EBITDA, ₹4 of depreciation and ₹1 of interest leave about ₹6 of profit, and even that figure is lifted by a one-time deferred-tax credit of ₹239 crore in FY26.

In plain terms

The cost structure tells you what kind of risk you are buying. An incubator spends heavily now and earns later, so near-term profit is low and depends on one-off gains. A holdco's own businesses can be barely profitable while its listed stakes are worth a fortune. An MNC subsidiary is very profitable but shares part of its economics with the parent before shareholders see a rupee. A reseller lives on a few rupees of margin, so any new venture it funds shows up immediately as a loss.

Supply-side forces differ just as much by archetype. Adani Enterprises' cost base is set by Chinese solar-cell and polysilicon prices, copper concentrate treatment charges, coal contracts and construction costs for airports. Grasim's fibre and chemicals costs depend on wood pulp, sulphur, salt and power; its paint costs on titanium dioxide and monomers. DCM Shriram's depend on power for its electrolysers, on sugarcane prices set by the Uttar Pradesh government and on gas for urea. 3M India's depend on what its parent charges for imported products. None of these cost drivers is common to the group, which is why the report focuses on structure and valuation rather than a single industry cycle.

The engines underneath: airports, cement, paint, caustic and a reseller on Amazon

The group has no single demand driver. It has about fifteen, and each moves a different share price.

Because the seven share no end-market, "demand" for this sub-sector means the demand facing each operating engine. The useful question is which engines are big enough to move each parent's value, and which way they are moving. This section walks through them in order of size, using Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) as the latest read.

Imports, China and foreign owners

Competition from imports operates at the level of individual engines, not the group. Chinese solar modules and cells set the price that Adani New Industries can charge, and India's Approved List of Models and Manufacturers and import duties are the main protection. In plain terms

Every company here has a controller who can decide, largely alone, whether to distribute, demerge, raise money or invest in something new. Minority shareholders are passengers. That is not unusual in India, but it is why the identity, record and incentives of the controller matter more in this sub-sector than in almost any other.

The structure also explains the size of each company's index weight and liquidity. Grasim, with the largest free float and the most diversified institutional ownership, is the only member where minorities collectively hold more than the promoter. At Godrej Industries, 3M India and RattanIndia, about three-quarters of the shares never trade. Management controls the middle three terms (what it builds, holds in cash and borrows); it does not control the first, which moves with the subsidiaries' share prices every day.

Two derived numbers matter more than the NAV itself. The implied discount is one minus the market value divided by gross NAV. The implied value of everything else is the holdco's market value minus the market value of its listed stakes. When that second number is negative, the market is saying that the holdco's own businesses, its cash and its future decisions are together worth less than nothing to a minority shareholder. That is the position of both Grasim and Godrej Industries today.

Analysts usually explain the discount as a bundle of four costs. The first is tax leakage: if the holdco ever sold a listed stake it would pay capital-gains tax, now 12.5% on long-term gains on listed equity since July 2024, and dividends passing through a holdco can be taxed again unless the Section 80M pass-through applies. The second is illiquidity and control: a 56% stake in UltraTech cannot be sold quickly without a large price concession, and a minority in the holdco cannot force a sale. The third is the agency or capital-allocation cost: the controller may use the cash in ways minority holders would not choose. The fourth is complexity and disclosure: when segment losses are not reported, investors assume the worst.

The cross-check: look-through earnings

An SOTP based on market prices inherits whatever the market thinks of UltraTech or Godrej Consumer. A look-through earnings calculation is an independent check. For Grasim, 56.11% of UltraTech's FY26 profit of ₹8,188 crore is ₹4,594 crore; 52.3% of ABCL's ₹3,864 crore is ₹2,021 crore; the standalone business excluding dividends lost roughly ₹700 crore on our estimate. Look-through profit is about ₹5,900 crore and the look-through P/E is about 34 times, close to the blended P/E of the two stakes. On that measure Grasim is not cheap: its discount on assets is offset by the drag of loss-making new businesses on earnings.

What the discount is really measuring

The evidence across Indian and global holdcos is consistent. Pure investment holdcos with no operating business and no distribution policy tend to trade at discounts of 50% or more; an often-cited 2012 Business Standard study put the average for India's 15 largest listed holdcos at 54.5%, with a range from 30% (Maharashtra Scooters) to 83.6% (Bombay Burmah). Operating holdcos with a visible growth engine tend to trade at 25–40%. Holdcos where the market expects a distribution or listing can trade at a premium, as Tata Investment Corporation did during periods of debate about a Tata Sons listing. These historical averages are INDICATIVE anchors, not current measurements. On that spectrum Grasim (about 28%) sits where an operating holdco with a credible growth engine should, while Godrej Industries (about 36%) sits nearer the pure-holdco end because its holdco debt and lack of dividends make it behave like one.

Two practical lessons follow for anyone using these numbers. First, the stake percentages and the holdco's cash are the inputs most often wrong in published SOTPs: our Grasim figures use 56.11% of UltraTech at March 2026, and a lower post-dilution figure of 55.3% would cut NAV by about ₹2,600 crore and the discount by about 0.7 percentage points. Second, the discount is not a fixed number to be "closed"; it is a running price of the controller's behaviour. Section 10 shows what has actually closed it in India since 2023: demergers.

Adani Enterprises: why the market pays a premium for an incubator Deep dive

Record operating profit, a reported loss, ₹39,930 crore of new equity and a US legal arc that has largely, but not entirely, closed.

Adani Enterprises is the opposite case to Grasim and Godrej Industries. "Other income" can be perfectly legitimate: interest on cash, dividends from subsidiaries, a gain on selling an asset. But it does not repeat in the way that selling products does. When it is larger than the profit itself, the company is not yet earning its keep from operations, and the P/E multiple on reported profit tells you nothing. Swan's 37 times P/E is a good example of a number to ignore.

Signal four: dilution as a funding tool

Two members have funded themselves partly by issuing shares. Adani Enterprises issued ₹39,930 crore of new equity in nine months, cutting the promoter stake by about three percentage points. That is a transparent, priced choice to reduce leverage, and the rights issue was offered to all shareholders pro rata. Swan's promoter stake has fallen from 64.09% in September 2023 to 53.96%, about ten points, while a rising share of the remainder is pledged. Dilution is not a red flag in itself; the questions are whether it is offered to all holders on equal terms and whether the capital raised earns more than its cost.

Why demergers work in India

Four mechanisms explain why demergers have narrowed discounts. The first is tax. A demerger that meets the conditions of Section 2(19AA) of the Income Tax Act (all assets and liabilities of the undertaking transferred, shareholders of the parent receiving shares of the child in proportion, at least three-quarters of the parent's shareholders by value becoming shareholders of the child) is tax-neutral for the company and the shareholder under Section 47. A stake sale, by contrast, triggers capital-gains tax at 12.5% on long-term gains. That asymmetry is why Indian groups prefer demergers to selling down stakes. The second is price discovery: once listed separately, each business is valued against its own peers, so a high-multiple business is no longer averaged down with a low-multiple one. The third is capital allocation: cash generated by one business can no longer be redirected to another without a transaction that minorities can see and vote on. The fourth is index and fund eligibility, which can bring new buyers to the separated companies.

The evidence is consistent, if not uniform. Tata Motors' commercial-vehicle business listed 28.5% above its discovered price. Jio Financial Services became a large-cap on listing. The record of Adani Enterprises' own demergers in 2015 and 2018 is the foundation of its present premium, as Section 07 explained. Demergers do not always lift combined value, however: if the separated company inherits too much debt, if the entitlement ratio favours one set of holders, or if index funds are forced to sell a small child company, the first months can be weak. The structure of each scheme matters as much as the decision to demerge.

In plain terms

A demerger is a way of handing shareholders the contents of the basket without anyone paying tax or selling anything. Instead of owning a share of a company that owns a shipyard or an airport business, you own a share of the shipyard or the airport business itself. That removes the layer the market was charging a discount for, which is why groups that have done it have generally seen the combined value rise.

The notable non-demerger: Grasim and UltraTech

Grasim has used the demerger route before: Aditya Birla Capital was listed in 2017 through a demerger from Grasim. But UltraTech, built from Grasim's own cement business and the 2004 acquisition of L&T's cement unit, has been held as a subsidiary since then, and the group has said UltraTech will not be merged with Grasim; no distribution of UltraTech shares has been proposed. That is a rational choice for the group (UltraTech's dividends fund Grasim's new businesses, and control of India's largest cement company is a strategic asset), but it makes Grasim's discount structural rather than temporary. The market cannot expect the UltraTech value to be handed over, so it prices Grasim on what Grasim does with UltraTech's dividends, which today means paints and Pivot.

The Godrej split: simplification without distribution

The April 2024 family realignment untangled one of India's oldest cross-holding structures, in which listed and unlisted Godrej companies held stakes in each other and shared the brand and the Vikhroli land bank. It created a clearer listed group, but it did not distribute anything to Godrej Industries' minority shareholders. Godrej Industries remains a holdco with three listed stakes, a chemicals business, a growing lender and rising holdco debt. Its discount is the wider of the two operating holdcos for the reasons set out in Section 06. The obvious structural catalyst, a listing of Godrej Capital, is about five years away on the timetable cited in our primer.

The rules that set the discount: SEBI, tax law and US enforcement

No regulator oversees "conglomerates". A handful of securities, tax and sanctions rules nonetheless shape how much of the value reaches minorities.

Because the seven operate in different industries, they answer to many sector regulators: the Airports Economic Regulatory Authority for Adani's airports, the solar manufacturing list for its modules, state cane-price orders and fertiliser subsidies for DCM Shriram, the Reserve Bank for Aditya Birla Capital and Godrej Capital. What they have in common are the rules governing how a controlled company treats its minority shareholders and how value moves through a group. Those rules set the floor under the holdco discount.

Related parties and royalties

SEBI tightened its related-party rules in 2021–22, lowering the materiality threshold to ₹1,000 crore or 10% of consolidated turnover, whichever is lower, and requiring that material transactions be approved by shareholders with related parties barred from voting in favour. For MNC subsidiaries, a separate rule treats royalty or brand payments above 5% of turnover as material. 3M India's royalty of about 1.7% of sales, or about 4.2% including management fees, sits below that line. The rule matters less for what it prevents than for what it reveals: every year, the annual report lists related-party transactions in detail, and that note is the best single source for judging how a group treats its listed companies.

Pledges, float and creeping acquisitions

The takeover regulations require promoters to disclose pledges and, beyond certain thresholds, the reasons for them. Swan's 40.7% pledge of promoter shares equals roughly 22% of the company's total equity (40.7% of a 53.96% promoter stake), above the 20%-of-capital trigger, which is why its promoter group filed a statement of reasons on 16 October 2026. The minimum public shareholding rule caps promoters at 75%. That cap binds tightly for 3M India (75.00%), RattanIndia (74.87%) and Godrej Industries (74.64%), which cannot raise their stakes further even if they consider their shares cheap. Grasim's promoters, at 45.20%, have plenty of room and have been using the creeping-acquisition allowance of up to 5% a year.

Tax: the measurable part of the discount

Tax law explains a measurable slice of every holdco discount. If Grasim sold its UltraTech stake, it would pay long-term capital-gains tax, now 12.5% on listed equity. Dividends that UltraTech pays to Grasim are taxable for Grasim, but Section 80M lets Grasim deduct them to the extent it pays dividends to its own shareholders, so the leakage depends on the holdco's own payout. Demergers that meet Section 2(19AA) avoid tax entirely, which is why they are the preferred route to unlock value. On our Grasim workings, a pure tax haircut on the listed stakes (about ₹29,300 crore) would explain only about two-fifths of the ₹75,754 crore gap between gross NAV and market value; the rest is the market's charge for control, opacity and the absence of distribution.

US enforcement: a new kind of risk for an Indian group

Adani Enterprises is the first member of this group to face US sanctions and securities enforcement at scale. The OFAC settlement, the dismissal of the criminal counts against three individuals and the SEC civil settlement are described in Section 07. The broader lesson for valuation is that groups raising dollar debt and transacting in US dollars are within reach of US sanctions and anti-bribery law even for business done outside the US, and that compliance failures can cost both money (₹2,644 crore in this case) and access to markets while matters are pending.

In plain terms

Indian rules do not stop a group from being complicated, but they force it to show its workings: related-party deals above a size need minority approval, pledges must be disclosed with reasons, and promoters cannot own more than 75%. For an investor, those disclosures are the raw material. The rules that cost money, such as capital-gains tax on stake sales, explain part of the holdco discount; the rest is a judgement about the people in control.

Sector regulation still matters inside each engine. The airport regulator's tariff orders set aeronautical charges for Adani's airports; the solar manufacturing list and import duties protect Adani New Industries from cheaper imports; the Uttar Pradesh cane price, the government's ethanol procurement prices and the urea subsidy formula set much of DCM Shriram's earnings; pollution rules govern chlor-alkali plants at Grasim and DCM Shriram. Those rules are covered in the relevant sub-sector reports. For this report the point is that a single policy change can move one engine sharply without affecting the others, which is the diversification argument working as intended.

From Licence Raj diversification to the break-up wave

A century of Indian conglomerates in eight phases, and why the premium and the discount have swung the way they have.

India's conglomerates were not built by a theory of portfolio diversification. They were built by the licence system. Before 1991 industrial capacity needed a government licence, so a business house with capital and political access spread across whatever industries it could get licences for. The result was a generation of groups making textiles, cement, chemicals, fertilisers and consumer goods under one family. Every member of this report except Adani Enterprises and RattanIndia traces its roots to that era.

  • Origins (1889–1990). Delhi Cloth Mills was founded in 1889 and grew into the DCM group; the Godrej business began in 1897; Swan Mills was incorporated in Bombay in 1909; Grasim (Gwalior Rayon Silk Manufacturing) was founded in 1947. Diversification was the default form, because capacity licences, not markets, decided what a group could make.
  • The focus wave (1990s–2000s). After liberalisation, groups began separating and listing businesses. The DCM group was split among family branches, creating DCM Shriram among others. Godrej Consumer Products listed in 2001 and Godrej Properties in 2010. Grasim bought L&T's cement business in 2004 and folded its own cement into UltraTech in 2010.
  • Discounts entrench (2010s). Holdcos that held listed stakes and rarely distributed came to trade at deep discounts: a 2012 Business Standard survey put the average for the 15 largest at 54.5%. Grasim listed Aditya Birla Capital by demerger in 2017, and Godrej Agrovet listed the same year.
  • The incubator proves itself (2015–2018). Adani Enterprises demerged Adani Ports, Adani Power and Adani Transmission in 2015, and Adani Green Energy and Adani Total Gas in 2018, showing that a holdco that hands over its children can be valued at a premium.
  • Shock (2023). The Hindenburg report in January 2023 led Adani Enterprises to withdraw its ₹20,000 crore follow-on offer; group market values fell sharply and later partly recovered. The same month Swan bought Veritas, and in December 2022 the tribunal had approved its shipyard bid.
  • The break-up wave (2023–26). Jio Financial, ITC Hotels, Siemens Energy, Tata Motors and Vedanta separated businesses; the Godrej family split its structure. Section 10 covers the evidence.
  • The Adani legal arc (2024–26). A US indictment in November 2024 was followed by an OFAC settlement in May 2026 and the dismissal with prejudice of the securities and wire-fraud counts against three individuals in August 2026, with judgment reserved on other counts; meanwhile Adani Enterprises raised about ₹40,000 crore of equity.
  • The holdco as builder (2024–26). Grasim's Birla Opus, launched in early 2024 with a ₹4,000 crore rights issue behind it, tests whether a holdco can create a new crown jewel rather than just hold old ones.

The long view yields three lessons. First, the discount has never been a constant. It widens when holdcos hoard cash and borrow, and narrows when they distribute, list or merge. Second, the market's tolerance for complexity has fallen. In the 1980s a diversified group was a sign of strength; since about 2015, investors have rewarded simplification so consistently that most large groups have announced at least one separation. The Hindenburg report in 2023 cut Adani group market values sharply within days, a bigger move than any quarterly result has produced, and the dismissal in August 2026 is part of why the stock trades at a premium again.

In plain terms

Indian conglomerates were born because the government decided what could be made, and a group with licences made everything. Now that markets decide, investors reward groups that separate their businesses and hand the pieces to shareholders. The seven companies in this report are at different points on that journey: Adani has done it before and is expected to again, Grasim and Godrej have not, and the small caps are still collecting pieces.

The cycle also has a funding dimension. Groups expand when capital is cheap and their own share prices are high, as Adani Enterprises did before 2023 and again in 2025–26, and contract or simplify when capital is scarce or their governance is questioned. In late 2026, with Adani's legal overhang reduced, Grasim's paint business scaling and Godrej's new leadership in place, all three large members are in expansion mode. The next downturn in their commodity engines will test whether the holdcos can keep funding their bets without either more borrowing or more dilution.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the pieces fit. Suppose a holdco owns 50% of a listed company worth ₹1,000 crore, so its stake is worth ₹500 crore. In plain terms

A holdco's share price is the value of what it owns, minus what it owes, minus a charge for the fact that you do not control it. The KPIs below measure each piece: how big the charge is, how much the holdco owes, how much its own businesses earn, how much cash leaks out before reaching you, and how trustworthy the people in charge have been.

1. Holdco discount

What it is. The percentage by which a holdco's market value falls short of the value of its parts. Formula: 1 − market cap ÷ gross NAV, where gross NAV = listed stakes at market + own businesses at a multiple + holdco cash − holdco gross debt. Worked example: Grasim's gross NAV on our ILLUSTRATIVE workings is ₹2,74,470 crore against a market value of ₹1,98,716 crore, so the discount is 1 − 0.724 = 27.6%. For Godrej Industries, ₹54,776 crore of NAV against ₹35,174 crore of market value gives 35.8%.

What drives it. It widens with holdco debt, cash burned in undisclosed ventures, low payouts, thin free float and the absence of any catalyst; it narrows with demergers, listings of subsidiaries, buybacks, special dividends and clearer disclosure. Benchmark: below 20% is good, 20–40% average, above 40% weak, with Indian pure investment holdcos historically around 50% or more. How the members compare: Godrej Industries about 36%, Grasim about 28%; Adani Enterprises shows a premium, not a discount. The definition trap: published discounts are rarely comparable, because analysts differ on which assets they count, which multiples they use for unlisted parts and whether they deduct holdco debt. A "30% discount" from one broker and a "20% discount" from another can describe the same share price. Always rebuild the NAV yourself from stake percentages and today's prices.

2. Listed stakes ÷ market cap

What it is. The market value of a holdco's listed holdings divided by its own market value. It needs no estimates, which makes it the most robust holdco KPI. Formula: Σ (stake % × subsidiary market cap) ÷ holdco market cap. Worked example: Godrej Industries holds about 23.7% of Godrej Consumer (₹87,493 crore), 44.8% of Godrej Properties (₹48,296 crore) and 64.9% of Godrej Agrovet (₹12,177 crore). The subsidiaries' share prices (numerator) and the holdco's own price (denominator); because the holdco tends to move less than its subsidiaries, the ratio rises when subsidiaries rally. 3. Look-through P/E

What it is. The holdco's market value divided by its proportionate share of the profits of everything it owns. Formula: market cap ÷ (Σ stake % × investee PAT + own PAT excluding dividends from those investees − holdco interest not already deducted). Worked example: Godrej Industries' share of FY26 profits: 23.7% of Godrej Consumer's ₹1,861 crore (₹441 crore), 44.8% of Godrej Properties' ₹1,841 crore (₹825 crore), 64.9% of Agrovet's ₹445 crore (₹289 crore), plus ₹61 crore of standalone profit. Total about ₹1,616 crore; ₹35,174 crore ÷ ₹1,616 crore = 21.8 times.

What drives it. Investee profit growth and the holdco's own losses or profits. Benchmark: there is no absolute range; compare it with the weighted P/E of the investees. A look-through P/E well below the investees' indicates a genuine discount on earnings, not just on assets. Members: Grasim about 34 times against UltraTech at 37.3 and ABCL at 25.8; Godrej Industries about 21.8 times against 42.1, 29.8 and 26.0. The trap: counting dividends twice. A holdco's standalone profit includes dividends from the subsidiaries whose profits you have already counted, so they must be removed. For Grasim, standalone profit of ₹348 crore included more than ₹1,000 crore of such dividends; excluding them, the own businesses lost about ₹700 crore on our estimate.

6. Return on capital employed, consolidated and by segment

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a percentage of the capital tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (equity + debt − cash), averaged over the year; by segment, segment EBIT ÷ segment capital employed. Worked example: a business with ₹1,000 crore of capital earning ₹150 crore of EBIT has a ROCE of 15%. 3M India's 50% means it earns half its capital back in operating profit each year, because its parent's technology and brands are not on its balance sheet and it has paid out more than its profits for three years.

What drives it. Margins and asset turnover, and for conglomerates the timing of new assets: capital is counted from the day it is spent, profit only after ramp-up. Benchmark: above 15% good, 10–15% average, below 10% weak; for segments, above 20% good. What it is. How many years of operating profit it would take to repay net debt. Formula: (borrowings − cash − liquid investments) ÷ EBITDA. What drives it. Capex ahead of earnings raises it; equity raising and new assets reaching full earnings lower it. 8. Capex ÷ operating cash flow

What it is. How many rupees a company invests for every rupee its operations generate. It shows whether growth is self-funded. Formula: capital expenditure (or cash used in investing) ÷ cash from operations. Worked example: Adani Enterprises spent ₹27,680 crore on investing in FY26 against ₹2,357 crore of operating cash flow: 11.7 times. What drives it. The stage of the capex cycle. DCM Shriram, with its build largely finished, is at about 1.0 times; Adani, mid-build, is far above. 10. Promoter pledge

What it is. The share of the controlling shareholder's stake that has been pledged to lenders as collateral. Formula: pledged promoter shares ÷ total promoter shares; also compute pledged shares ÷ total shares outstanding. Worked example: Swan's promoters hold 53.96% of the company and have pledged 40.7% of that holding. Pledged shares therefore equal about 22% of Swan's total equity, an amount large enough that a forced sale would swamp normal trading volumes.

What drives it. Promoter borrowing needs, often to fund group companies or their own contributions to capital raises. Benchmark: zero is good, below 10% average, above 25% weak. Members: Swan 40.7% (plus a further 6.86% of total equity pledged on 13 October 2026), RattanIndia "mid-teens" per our primer (unverified), others nil or not shown. The trap: pledges by promoter-group entities that are not the main holder, and "non-disposal undertakings" that work like pledges but may be reported differently. A low headline pledge can hide encumbrance elsewhere in the group; read the full disclosure under the takeover regulations.

What it is. The value of transactions with group companies relative to revenue; for MNC subsidiaries, the specific payments to the parent for technology, brand and services. 12. Payout ratio

What it is. The share of profit paid out as dividends. For a holdco, it is the clearest measure of whether value flows to minority shareholders. Formula: dividends declared ÷ profit attributable to owners. Worked example: Grasim paid ₹10 a share on about 68 crore shares in FY26, about ₹680 crore, against owners' profit of ₹4,966 crore: about 14%. 3M India paid ₹506 a share including a ₹346 special dividend, about 109% of profit.

What drives it. Investment needs, holdco debt, and the controller's own need for cash. Benchmark: 30–60% for mature businesses; below 10% weak for a holdco sitting on listed stakes. 13. Normalised (core) profit

What it is. Reported profit with one-off items removed: exceptional gains and charges, tax write-backs and one-time deferred-tax credits, fair-value marks. Formula: reported PAT − one-off gains + one-off charges, each net of tax. Worked example: DCM Shriram's Q1 FY27 profit of ₹693 crore included ₹474 crore of tax write-backs and ₹79 crore of exceptional gains; the company and press put normalised profit at ₹147 crore (simple subtraction gives about ₹139 crore; the difference is presumably tax on the exceptional gains). Its FY26 profit of ₹856 crore included a ₹239 crore deferred-tax credit, leaving core profit of about ₹617 crore.

What drives it. Operations only, which is the point. Benchmark: compare growth in core profit with growth in reported profit; a persistent gap is a warning. Members: DCM Shriram's reported P/E is 11.5 times but about 26 times on core profit; Adani Enterprises' FY26 P/E is 37.4 times on owners' profit but about 117 times on core earnings. The trap: deciding what counts as one-off. Adani Enterprises has reported a large exceptional item in several recent periods (stake-sale gains, then the OFAC settlement). The same anatomy shows why dividend policy at the subsidiaries matters so much to the parent. In plain terms

A typical holdco makes a modest living from its own factory, collects dividends from its famous subsidiaries and uses them to pay its bankers and fund its next idea. As long as the dividends keep coming, it looks fine. If it borrows more, or the next idea loses more, the profit disappears quickly even though the value of its stakes has not changed at all.

Definition traps, collected

  • Consolidated versus standalone. Grasim's consolidated revenue (₹1,75,431 crore) is four times its own businesses' (₹41,039 crore). Never compare a holdco's consolidated margin with an operating company's.
  • Profit including minorities. Screener's net profit for Grasim (₹10,300 crore) is more than double profit attributable to owners (₹4,966 crore). Use owners' profit for any P/E.
  • Two EBITDAs. Screener's operating profit for Grasim (₹36,296 crore) treats ABCL's lending interest as revenue; Grasim's own EBITDA is ₹25,872 crore. Grasim's standalone EBITDA of ₹3,558 crore includes other income and dividends; screener's standalone operating profit is ₹1,781 crore.
  • Three Adani profits. FY26: ₹9,951 crore (screener, including minorities), ₹9,746 crore (company, owners, restated), ₹9,339 crore (our earlier primer). Q4 FY26: −₹167 crore (screener) or −₹221 crore (primer).
  • Two Adani leverage ratios. 3.3 times "net external debt" (company) and 5.4 times total net debt (Motilal Oswal) are both defensible and not comparable.
  • Holdco debt versus group debt. Only standalone debt belongs in a holdco NAV; consolidated debt is already in the subsidiaries' market values.
  • Associates are invisible in revenue. Godrej Consumer is an associate of Godrej Industries, so its revenue is not consolidated, though it is the second-largest asset.
  • Double-counted dividends. In look-through earnings, strip subsidiary dividends out of the holdco's own profit.
  • Other income that is not "other". Fair-value marks (RattanIndia) and settlements (Adani's OFAC charge on screener) can sit on different lines in different presentations.
  • Units. RattanIndia's FY26 loss was ₹1,663 million (₹166 crore), not ₹1,663 crore; check every striking figure against the balance sheet.
  • Stale stakes. Stake percentages and subsidiary market values change; recompute them on the day.
  • Revenue that is not disclosed. Birla Opus's full-year revenue and losses are not reported; every figure for them in this report is an estimate or a quarterly disclosure.

Most of these traps have the same root: a conglomerate reports one set of accounts for a structure that contains several businesses with different owners, and the data providers that standardise those accounts make reasonable but different choices about where each line goes. The practical defence is to choose one basis per question and stick to it. For "how is the holdco itself doing?", use standalone figures, owners' profit and holdco debt. For "how are the businesses doing?", use segment disclosures and the subsidiaries' own results. For "what is the share worth?", use the SOTP with today's stake values. Mixing bases, for example comparing a consolidated margin with a standalone debt figure, is how most published errors about these companies arise.

In plain terms

Nearly every headline number for a conglomerate has a twin that tells a different story: consolidated and standalone, including and excluding minorities, company EBITDA and data-provider EBITDA, net external debt and total net debt. None of them is wrong. The mistake is mixing them, or comparing one company's version with another company's different version.

How to read a conglomerate's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Pull both statements (1 minute). Download the consolidated and standalone results. For Grasim and Godrej Industries, the standalone page tells you how the holdco itself is doing; the consolidated page mostly tells you about UltraTech, ABCL and Godrej Properties.
  2. Find profit attributable to owners (1 minute). Ignore "net profit" if it includes minorities. Red flag: a large or growing gap between the two without a matching change in ownership.
  3. Strip the one-offs (1 minute). Read the exceptional-items line and the tax line. A tax rate far below 25% (DCM Shriram's Q1 FY27 write-back) or far above it (3M India's 42% in FY26) needs an explanation before the profit is used.
  4. Compare other income with profit before tax (1 minute). If other income is more than 40% of PBT, the operating business is not carrying the company. Red flag: other income rising while operating profit falls (Swan's pattern).
  5. Read segment EBITDA for the two or three engines that matter (1 minute). Airports and New Energy for Adani; fibre, chemicals and Opus revenue for Grasim; chemicals for Godrej Industries; chloro-vinyl and sugar for DCM Shriram. Red flag: a segment's revenue reported without its profit for several quarters.
  6. Check holdco debt and interest (1 minute). Standalone borrowings (reported half-yearly) and standalone interest. Red flag: holdco interest above holdco operating profit (Godrej Industries in FY26).
  7. Look for money moving between parent and subsidiaries (1 minute). Capital infusions into lenders or new ventures, loans to group companies, dividends received. Red flag: rising infusions into a business whose results are not disclosed.
  8. Recompute stakes ÷ market cap and the discount (1 minute). Multiply each stake by today's subsidiary market value and divide by the holdco's market value. A ratio rising above 1.0 means the market is valuing everything else below zero.
  9. Read the shareholding pattern (1 minute). Promoter holding, pledges and changes. Red flags: a rising pledge (Swan in October 2026), or promoter selling while the company raises capital.
  10. Note the related-party and capital-raising items (1 minute). Royalty or fee changes for MNC subsidiaries; rights issues, placements and warrant conversions for incubators. Red flag: capital raised on terms not offered to all shareholders.
In plain terms

The ten-minute routine replaces "did profit go up?" with three better questions. Is the operating business earning its keep without help from one-offs or other income? Is the holdco borrowing more than its own business can support? And is value moving towards minority shareholders (dividends, demergers, disclosure) or away from them (pledges, related-party payments, undisclosed ventures)?

Taken together, the twelve KPIs sort the seven into the archetypes of Section 01 almost automatically. The incubator shows high capex to cash flow, leverage near its tolerance and a premium. The operating holdcos show stakes worth more than their market value, dependence on dividends and discounts that track holdco debt. The diversified operator shows middling, clean numbers with tax noise. The MNC subsidiary shows exceptional returns and a high related-party share. The small caps show negative operating returns, dependence on other income and pledges. That is the practical value of the masterclass: once the right numbers are on the page, the structure explains the price.

Who is winning: return, value and governance on one page

Three maps and a scorecard. Winning here means turning structure into value for minority shareholders, not just growing.

"Winning" for a conglomerate is not the same as growing fastest. It means earning a good return on the capital it controls, converting that into value for minority shareholders, and doing so in a way the market trusts. The three maps below show each member on those dimensions; the scorecard combines them.

The first map shows the core tension of the sub-sector. Price-to-book is the natural multiple for holdcos and asset collectors, and ROCE is the natural measure of whether the capital is working. 3M India sits alone in the top right: an asset-light subsidiary whose high book multiple is earned by a 50% return. Adani Enterprises and RattanIndia sit in the top left, valued at four to five times book on returns below 6%, which is what a market paying for future businesses looks like; the difference is that Adani has a record of creating listed companies and RattanIndia does not. The first lesson is that a discount can close, but rarely by itself. Investor AB's discount narrowed over many years as it built a record of buying well, selling well and returning cash, and at times it has traded at a premium. Jardine Matheson's structure was simplified by collapsing layers of cross-holdings, which removed one source of its discount. Both required a deliberate choice by the controlling family. In India the equivalent tool has been the demerger, which is tax-efficient and has a strong recent record.

The second lesson is that buybacks alone do not close a discount when the market doubts capital allocation. Exor has bought back shares and still trades at a wide discount, because investors are unsure what it will buy next. Godrej Industries does not pay a dividend or buy back shares, and its holdco debt is rising; even a buyback would face the question of how it was funded.

The third lesson concerns incubators. SoftBank Group shows what happens to a venture holdco when listings stall and asset values fall: the discount can widen to 50% or more even with a famous founder in charge. Reliance, India's other incubator, has been debated at a modest discount because investors expect Jio and Retail to be listed eventually. Adani Enterprises' premium is unusual by global standards, and it depends on the incubator loop continuing to produce listings. If it stopped, the global evidence suggests the market would move it from premium to discount quickly.

In plain terms

Around the world, owning a company through a holding company usually costs investors 20–50% of the underlying value. India is not unusual. The holdcos that escape the discount are the ones that consistently return value to shareholders or simplify their structures. The incubators that earn a premium are the ones that keep turning out listed companies; when they stop, the premium tends to become a discount.

One structural difference makes India's discounts slightly easier to close than some others. Demergers that meet the Income Tax Act's conditions are tax-neutral, and SEBI's rules require minority approval of material related-party deals. Korea and Japan have had to use governance codes and activist pressure to achieve similar ends. The tools exist in India; what varies is whether controlling families choose to use them.

No single technology threatens "conglomerates". Specific shifts threaten specific engines, and the holdco carries the consequences.

Engine by engine

  • Solar manufacturing (Adani). Chinese module and cell price deflation compressed New Energy EBITDA by 20% in Q1 FY27. Protection comes from the government's approved-manufacturer list and import duties. The long-term bet is integration back to polysilicon by 2027–28, which would lower costs but requires heavy capital in a market where global capacity far exceeds demand.
  • Green hydrogen and data centres (Adani). The cost curve for electrolysers and green hydrogen remains uncertain. Data centres face rapid obsolescence as AI workloads raise power density per rack; AdaniConneX's 960 MW pipeline and the Jabil hardware venture are positioned for that shift but must keep up with it.
  • Copper (Adani). Treatment and refining charges for custom smelters collapsed globally in 2024–25 as smelting capacity outran mine supply. Kutch Copper's economics depend on by-product sulphuric acid and on domestic premiums, which is one reason its reported EBITDA per tonne needs checking.
  • Paints (Grasim). Birla Opus faces responses from incumbents (Asian Paints, JSW Paints, Indigo and others) on price, dealer incentives and tinting technology. Digital dealer platforms and tinting systems lower entry barriers but also raise the cost of competing for painters and dealers.
  • Fibre and chlor-alkali (Grasim, DCM Shriram). Lyocell and recycled fibres compete with conventional viscose. In chlor-alkali, membrane-cell efficiency and access to cheap renewable power decide cost position; both Grasim (1.96 GWp of renewables) and DCM Shriram are investing in captive power. Chinese PVC and caustic exports are a recurring threat, managed through anti-dumping duties.
  • Oleochemicals and lending (Godrej Industries). Shifts between palm-derived and petroleum-derived surfactants move margins. Godrej Capital competes with digital lenders and bank-backed housing financiers on cost of funds and technology-led underwriting.
  • Industrial consumables (3M India). The parent's exit from PFAS chemicals narrows the product range; local competitors in adhesives and abrasives (Pidilite, Grindwell Norton, Carborundum Universal) offer substitutes at lower cost.
  • LNG and shipbuilding (Swan). India's LNG import terminals run well below nameplate capacity, which weakens the case for a new terminal. Shipbuilding is working-capital intensive and depends on government support schemes for Indian yards.
  • E-commerce and EVs (RattanIndia). Amazon's seller policies decide Cocoblu's economics. Large two-wheeler incumbents (Bajaj, Hero and others) have entered electric motorcycles; drone procurement by government buyers is lumpy.
In plain terms

The diversification of a conglomerate works in both directions with technology. A shock in one business, such as cheap Chinese solar modules, does not sink the whole group. But the holdco is also exposed to more shocks at once, and its share price reacts most to the shocks in the one or two businesses that make up most of its value.

The structural effect of technology on the holdco question is subtler. Faster technological change shortens the time a holdco can hold a business before it needs fresh capital, which increases the cash flowing from the holdco into its subsidiaries and new ventures. For Grasim, that means paints and Pivot need continued investment; for Adani, that data centres and manufacturing need refreshes on a shorter cycle than airports or roads. Each additional rupee of reinvestment is a rupee not distributed, which, on the logic of Section 06, tends to widen the discount unless the reinvestment is clearly disclosed and clearly profitable.

Bull vs bear for India's conglomerates

The arguments on both sides, and how much a shift in the discount or premium would move each large member.

The case for and against this sub-sector is really a case about structure: whether the discounts at the holdcos will narrow, whether the premium at the incubator will hold, and whether governance will keep improving. The operating engines matter, but mostly through their effect on those three questions.

The bull case

  • Assets bought below value: Grasim's and Godrej Industries' listed stakes are worth 1.18 and 1.43 times their market values; investors get the holdcos' own businesses for less than nothing.
  • A demerger playbook that works: the 2023–26 wave (Jio Financial, ITC Hotels, Tata Motors, Vedanta) shows Indian groups can and do unlock value, tax-free.
  • The incubator's record: Adani Enterprises has created five listed companies before; airports, copper and data centres could follow, and its funding is now equity-led.
  • Legal overhang reduced: the OFAC matter is settled and the criminal counts against three individuals are dismissed with prejudice; CARE upgraded Adani Enterprises to AA in October 2026.
  • Operating leverage arriving: Grasim's standalone EBITDA doubled in Q1 FY27; Godrej's chemicals profit rose 86%; DCM Shriram's capex cycle is done.
  • Insiders buying: Grasim's promoters are using creeping acquisitions to buy a discounted holdco.

The bear case

  • Discounts are structural: Grasim has said UltraTech will not be merged, Godrej Industries pays no dividend, and Godrej Capital's listing is about five years away.
  • Undisclosed bets: Birla Opus's losses and Godrej Capital's capital needs are not fully visible; new-venture losses can absorb the holdcos' own earnings.
  • Holdco leverage: Godrej Industries' interest exceeds its own operating profit and its debt is rising.
  • Premium at risk: Adani Enterprises spends nearly ₹12 for every ₹1 of operating cash flow; a missed ramp-up or a delayed demerger would attack a premium worth about a quarter of its value.
  • Residual legal and compliance risk: judgment was reserved on other counts, the dismissal was not a verdict, and OFAC found the violations egregious.
  • Small-cap governance: Swan's rising pledge and other-income profits, and RattanIndia's thin disclosure, mean reported numbers there cannot be taken at face value.

Weighing the two sides, the balance differs sharply by member. The holdcos offer a margin of safety in assets but need a trigger to unlock it; without one, the discount can persist for years. Adani Enterprises offers growth that is partly priced in; if the next listing slips, the premium is what is lost. Neither is a bet on an industry cycle. Both are bets on what the people in control will do next.
