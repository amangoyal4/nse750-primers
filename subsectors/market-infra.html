Depositories & registrars: volume compounds, the toll gets cut

A complete guide to the companies that keep India's investment records: how a ₹3.50 charge on a share sale and about two hundredths of a per cent on a mutual-fund rupee add up to a ₹57,000 crore group of listed companies, why their volumes grow at 15–25% a year while their revenue grows at half that, how CDSL, CAMS and KFin Technologies differ, and what has to go right for valuations of 34–54 times earnings.

00

The one-page view: three toll booths on India's savings boom

The volumes these companies process compound at 15–25% a year. The price they are allowed to charge goes the other way. That gap explains most of what follows.

Every time an Indian investor sells a share, starts a SIP, opens a demat account or completes a KYC form, a small fee is collected by a company most investors have never heard of. This report covers the three listed companies in the NSE 750 that collect those fees. Central Depository Services (India) (CDSL) is the depository that holds roughly four of every five demat accounts in the country. Computer Age Management Services (CAMS) and KFin Technologies are the two registrars and transfer agents (RTAs) that keep the books for the entire mutual-fund industry: CAMS for 67.2% of its average assets and KFin for the other 32.8% in Q1 FY27 (Apr–Jun 2026). A fourth company, NSDL, the older and more institutional depository, listed in August 2025. It is not a member of this sub-sector list, so it appears throughout as context and never inside member totals.

Together the three members earned ₹3,962 crore of revenue in FY26 (up 10.2%), an operating margin of 45.3% and ₹1,271 crore of reported profit after tax (down 4.0%). On 22 October 2026 the market valued them at ₹56,986 crore, or 44.8 times FY26 profit. These are tiny businesses by revenue: the three together earn less in a year than a mid-sized Indian bank earns in a quarter. The market values them on what they control: the official record of who owns what in India's capital market, a role that is mandated, licensed and very expensive for anyone else to copy.

Four forces define the sub-sector, and a fifth sets the share prices:

  • Volume compounds; price is the battleground. Demat accounts went from 9 crore to 23.2 crore in four years, mutual-fund assets doubled, and monthly SIP inflows reached a record ₹32,490 crore in September 2026. But every fee has been cut. The depository debit charge was flattened to ₹3.50 in October 2024, RTA fees fall automatically as each fund house grows (the "telescopic" slab), and SEBI cut KYC registration fees from 1 April 2026. In FY26, CDSL's accounts grew 18% and its revenue 6%.
  • Regulated duopolies with switching costs. There are two depositories, by licence, and two mutual-fund registrars of scale, by history. No third player has broken in. But the depositories are Market Infrastructure Institutions that SEBI treats as near-utilities, so the limit on their returns is set by the regulator, not by competition.
  • Activity versus stock. CDSL earns heavily from activity: IPO credits, sell-side debits, new KYC records and treasury gains. Its FY26 profit fell 13.5%. The RTAs earn from the stock of assets, so CAMS and KFin both grew profit in the same year despite a sharp market fall.
  • Two ways to diversify. CAMS builds adjacent businesses itself and has pushed its margin back to 46.4%. KFin buys: it took 51% of Singapore's Ascent Fund Services for ₹307.7 crore with an obligation to buy the rest, and its margin fell to 34.2% in Q1 FY27.
  • Valuation. At 34–54 times trailing earnings, and 20–40% below their 52-week highs, the stocks need volume growth to resume and price cuts to slow at the same time.

The single most useful fact about this group is how differently the market prices its parts. CDSL produces 28.9% of the members' revenue and 35.8% of their profit, but it makes up 44.9% of their market value. The two registrars produce 71% of revenue and earn 55% of the value. Investors pay most for the retail depository: it has the highest margin and the cleanest monopoly position (four of every five new investors arrive through it), and it is the company most exposed to a pickup in market activity. They pay less for CAMS, which has the most predictable earnings but a fee that falls a little each year, and something in between for KFin, which is growing fastest by acquisition.

The latest quarter shows the toll booths re-accelerating. In Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) the members' revenue rose 17.8% to ₹1,045 crore and profit rose 11.5% to ₹320 crore, after the 4% fall in FY26. The mix of that growth matters. Strip out KFin's newly acquired Ascent business and the registrars grew about 11% organically. CDSL's profit rose 15.7% while its operating profit rose only 6.2%, because ₹43.8 crore of the quarter's income was mark-to-market and accrued income on its investment portfolio. The headline recovery is real, but it is partly bought (Ascent) and partly treasury.

The next data points arrive within ten days of this report: KFin reports Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) on 26 October with a call on 27 October, CAMS on 29 October, and CDSL and NSDL hold board meetings on 31 October. They are the first results since mutual-fund assets peaked at ₹87.08 lakh crore in August 2026 and fell 5.1% in September, and since the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October.

In plain terms

These companies own the official ledgers of Indian investing. The ledgers grow every year because more people invest, so the companies' workload grows fast. But the people who set their prices (SEBI for the depositories and KYC, the fund houses for the registrars) cut those prices a little each year. Owning the shares is a bet that the workload keeps outrunning the price cuts, at a valuation that already assumes it will.

01

How the plumbing earns: from a trade or a SIP to a line in a ledger

The investor deals with a broker, a platform or a fund house. Behind each of them is a registry that is paid by the entry, by the folio or by the basis point.

India's investment infrastructure has three layers. The front end is the broker, bank, fintech app or distributor that owns the customer relationship: Zerodha, Groww, Angel One, HDFC Securities and the rest of the businesses covered in our stockbroking report, and the fund houses in our asset managers report. The middle is the exchanges and clearing corporations that match and settle trades. The back end, the subject of this report, is the registries that record who owns what. There are three kinds. A depository holds shares, bonds and other securities in electronic (demat) form and moves them between accounts. A mutual-fund registrar (RTA) keeps the master list of every investor in every scheme it services. A KYC registration agency (KRA) holds one copy of each investor's identity check so that other intermediaries can fetch it instead of repeating it.

Each layer is paid differently. The front end earns brokerage, distribution commission, account fees and interest on client money. The back end earns small, standardised fees on a very large number of entries. The equity investor's path runs through a depository; the mutual-fund investor's path usually does not, because most MF units are held in "statement of account" form directly on the registrar's books.

The first path shows why CDSL's fortunes are tied to the discount brokers. The investor never picks a depository; the broker does. Zerodha, Groww, Upstox and Angel One built their businesses on CDSL, and that is how CDSL went from second place to holding 80% of all demat accounts. The flip side is that a broker can open new accounts at the other depository if it offers a better deal or a smoother technical integration. NSDL says fintech brokers now bring about 20% of its new accounts, up from about 2% a few quarters earlier.

The second path explains why the registrars behave like a slow-moving share of the fund industry's assets. The fund house never touches the investor record; the registrar does all of it, from the SIP instalment to the redemption payout to the tax statement. The fund house pays for this out of its own management fee, at a few basis points (hundredths of a per cent) of the assets serviced, plus per-transaction charges for some services. Since April 2026, under the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations 2026, the registrar fee is explicitly one of the expenses inside the "base expense ratio" that SEBI caps. When a fund house is squeezed, the registrar's fee is one of the costs it looks at.

Value in this chain collects at the record, not at the customer. Brokers and platforms compete fiercely for customers and have pushed brokerage towards zero. The registries sit behind them with roles that are mandated by regulation and very hard to replicate: a new depository needs a SEBI licence plus integration with every exchange, clearing corporation, broker and issuer; a new mutual-fund registrar needs to migrate a fund house's entire investor history without a single misallocated unit. That is why margins at the back end run at 40–51%, while listed brokers' margins swing with every market cycle.

The weakness of the position is equally clear. The registries do not own the customer, so they cannot raise prices by marketing or product design. Their prices are either set by SEBI (depository and KYC tariffs) or negotiated with a concentrated set of fund houses (registrar fees). 02

Size and growth: a ₹4,000 crore industry on an ₹83 lakh crore base

The fee pools are small, but they have nearly doubled in four years, and the members' revenue has compounded at 17% a year.

Measured by revenue, India's investment registries are small. The three members earned ₹3,962 crore in FY26, roughly US$420 million at ₹94.9 to the dollar, less than a tenth of Broadridge's revenue in the United States. Add NSDL's standalone depository business (₹705 crore) and the unlisted issuer registrars, and the whole sub-sector is perhaps ₹5,500–6,000 crore of annual revenue (indicative). What is large is the base it services: about 23.2 crore demat accounts holding securities worth more than ₹620 lakh crore across both depositories, and a mutual-fund industry with average assets of about ₹83 lakh crore in Q1 FY27.

The members' revenue grew from ₹2,101 crore in FY22 to ₹3,962 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 17.2% a year. Profit grew more slowly, from ₹748 crore to ₹1,271 crore (14.2% a year), and FY26 was the first year of decline since FY23. The pattern of the five years is worth stating because it repeats: a strong market (FY22) is followed by a soft one (FY23, when the three grew revenue only 7% and profit barely at all), a boom (FY24–FY25, when revenue grew 24% and 29%), and a squeeze (FY26).

What of the future? None of the companies gives a multi-year industry forecast and no rating-agency projection was available for this report. The managements' own guidance is a useful anchor. CAMS guides FY27 revenue growth of about 13% and operating EBITDA growth of about 16%. KFin guides 18–20% revenue growth a year over the medium term and an EBITDA margin of 40–45%. CDSL gives no guidance as a matter of policy. A neutral base case, which we label indicative, has industry MF assets growing 13–16% a year to FY30 (SIP inflows of about ₹3.9–4.0 lakh crore a year plus market gains of 8–10%) and demat accounts growing 12–15% a year on net additions of 2.5–3 crore a year. On those assumptions, and with yields falling 3–4% a year, the registrars' MF revenue would grow at roughly 10–12% a year, and the depository's revenue would grow with issuer folios and activity rather than with accounts.

The dollar comparison is a reminder of scale. India services 23.2 crore demat accounts and 28.6 crore mutual-fund folios, yet the fee pool is a few hundred million dollars because each record earns a few rupees. Indian registries are priced per basis point and per entry, not per account per year as in the US. That is the paise economy, explored in section 04.

In plain terms

The whole Indian business of keeping investment records earns roughly ₹5,500–6,000 crore a year, less than one large bank's quarterly profit. It is valuable because it grows with every new investor and every rupee invested, and because nobody can easily take the job away. It is small because each entry earns very little, and the regulator intends to keep it that way.

03

Volume compounds: accounts, SIPs and assets

India's household savings are moving from deposits and gold into markets. Each new investor adds an account, a KYC record and a folio; each SIP rupee adds assets on a registrar's book.

The demand driver for all three companies is the financialisation of Indian household savings. Mutual-fund assets were ₹82.64 lakh crore at the end of September 2026, still small next to bank deposits of more than ₹230 lakh crore. Every rupee that moves from a deposit into a fund adds to a registrar's fee base, and every first-time equity investor opens at least one demat account (often several, one per broker) and generates a KYC fetch or creation.

Demat accounts are the clearest measure. There were 5.5 crore in March 2021 and 23.2 crore by June 2026. The pandemic-era wave of discount-broker sign-ups did most of it: 3.5 crore accounts were added in FY22 alone. Growth is now slowing. CDSL's management discussion records that industry new-account additions fell from 4.6 crore in calendar 2024 to 3.06 crore in calendar 2025, down a third. CDSL's quarterly net additions went 57, 65, 76 and 74 lakh through FY26 and then 58 lakh in Q1 FY27. One person can hold several demat accounts, so these are accounts, not investors. NSE's count of active trading clients is a fraction of the account total.

The asset mix matters as much as the total, because registrar fees are tiered by asset class. Equity funds pay several times the basis points of liquid and debt funds. In September 2026, equity was 45.4% of industry assets, passive funds (index funds and ETFs) 17.8%, debt 21.3% and hybrid 13.9%. Debt-fund flows swing the total by ₹2–3 lakh crore in a single month as companies park and withdraw cash; those swings inflate or deflate assets without changing registrar revenue much. KFin's chief executive said a shift from debt to liquid funds explained about 30% of the roughly 2% quarter-on-quarter fall in its yield in Q1 FY27.

Trading activity feeds the depository more than the registrars, and it has cooled. SEBI's measures on equity derivatives, phased in from 20 November 2024 (larger index contract sizes, one weekly expiry per exchange, upfront collection of option premium), were followed by the first fall in participation since FY16. SEBI's August 2026 study counts 106.2 lakh active individual derivatives traders in FY25 and 87.5 lakh in FY26, down 18%; new entrants fell from 34.3 lakh to 20.8 lakh. Derivatives trades do not create depository debits (those come from delivery sales), but derivatives enthusiasm brings new accounts, margin pledges and KYC records. CDSL's transaction-charge line has been flat at ₹59–66 crore a quarter since FY26 began.

The paise economy: how ₹3.50 and two basis points become ₹57,000 crore Deep dive

Every revenue line in this sub-sector is a very small price multiplied by a very large number. Knowing which number moves, and who sets the price, is most of the analysis.

The best way to understand these companies is to write down their meters. A meter is a count of something (debits, folios, KYC records, rupees of assets) multiplied by a price per unit. Each of the three members has four or five meters that explain almost all of its revenue. The prices are tiny: ₹3.50, ₹5, ₹28, two hundredths of one per cent. The counts are enormous: 68 crore debits, 38.7 crore folios, ₹57 lakh crore of assets. This section sets out the meters, shows what one investor is worth to each company, and then traces how those paise build up to a ₹56,986 crore market value.

What one investor is worth

The meters turn into strikingly small numbers per investor. Take a ₹5,000 monthly SIP into an equity fund (illustrative). From paise to market value

The arithmetic chain from tariff to market value runs like this. ₹3,962 crore of revenue at a blended 45.3% operating margin gives ₹1,795 crore of operating profit; after depreciation (₹251 crore), other income (₹174 crore), interest and tax, it leaves ₹1,271 crore of profit. The market pays 44.8 times that profit, or 14.4 times revenue. Each rupee of fee revenue is therefore valued at about ₹14. A one-paisa change in a fee that is charged 68 crore times a year moves revenue by ₹68 lakh and, at CDSL's multiple of about 22 times revenue, market value by about ₹15 crore (indicative). The sensitivity is larger for the registrars' basis-point fee: one hundredth of a basis point on CAMS's ₹57 lakh crore is ₹5.7 crore of revenue a year.

In plain terms

Because the fees are so small and the volumes so large, a cut of a few paise or a fraction of a basis point flows straight into profit. That is why one SEBI circular or one large renewal can matter more to these companies than a year of account growth.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: ledgers versus call centres

The depository is a software ledger with almost no investor-facing labour; the registrars are part software, part service operation. That gap is worth about ten points of margin.

None of these companies has a factory, an inventory or meaningful debt. The costs are people, technology, outsourced operations and, at the depository, regulatory levies. How those costs split explains the margin gap between the depository and the registrars, and why the registrars are now pushing artificial intelligence into their operations.

CDSL spends about ₹14 of every ₹100 of revenue on employees (₹156.5 crore in FY26, up 28%) and another ₹14 on computer technology (₹162.2 crore, up 43%). The remaining ₹21 of costs covers SEBI fees and the Investor Protection Fund contribution (set at 5% of profit from depository operations, ₹23.8 crore in FY26), charges CVL pays other KYC agencies when its clients fetch their records (₹25.4 crore), investor education, marketing, CSR and administration. That leaves ₹51 of operating profit. Management describes employee and technology costs as "largely fixed in nature" and puts total technology investment at about ₹240–250 crore a year: roughly ₹160 crore of running costs plus ₹80–100 crore of capital spending.

CAMS spends ₹33 per ₹100 on employees (₹497 crore), ₹14 on operating expenses such as printing, courier, call centres and bank charges for its payments arm (₹210 crore), and ₹8 on other costs, leaving ₹45. KFin spends ₹40 per ₹100 on employees (₹525 crore including Ascent; ₹38 excluding it) and ₹19 on everything else, leaving ₹41. The registrars employ thousands of people in processing centres and service branches (CAMS has 285+ service centres) because mutual-fund investors still submit physical forms, call helplines and need their records corrected.

Duopolies by licence and by history, and who else is in the room

Two depositories, two mutual-fund registrars of scale, one dominant KYC agency. Market share depends on what you count.

Each layer of the plumbing is concentrated, but the reason differs. The depositories are a duopoly by law: only CDSL and NSDL hold depository licences under the SEBI (Depositories and Participants) Regulations, 2018, and SEBI has shown no interest in licensing a third. The mutual-fund registrars are a duopoly by history and switching cost: anyone could register as an RTA, but no third registrar of scale has emerged in two decades because moving a fund house's investor records is slow, risky and visible to the regulator. The KYC agencies are an oligopoly of four now that CAMS has bought NSE's KRA business, with one dominant player.

The depositories: three ways to measure share

"CDSL has 80% share" is true for one measure only. By accounts, CDSL held 18.59 crore of 23.15 crore at June 2026, or 80.3%. By new accounts in Q1 FY27, CDSL added 58 lakh net against NSDL's 12.4 lakh, about 82% (an analyst on CDSL's August call put the figure at 81.4% in June, down 420 basis points since March, and management did not dispute it; NSDL puts its own net incremental share at 17.6%). By custody value, NSDL holds 85.8%, about ₹535 lakh crore against CDSL's ₹88 lakh crore, because institutional investors, foreign portfolio investors and almost all listed debt sit at NSDL. By total income (standalone), the split is CDSL 56.8% and NSDL 43.2%. By depository operating revenue, CDSL's consolidated ₹1,145 crore against NSDL's standalone ₹705 crore gives CDSL 61.9%.

The regulator is the ceiling: MII rules, tariffs and the 2026 fund rules

Competition does not cap these companies' returns. SEBI does, through tariff oversight, profit levies, governance rules and, indirectly, the expense caps on their customers.

SEBI regulates every business in this report: depositories under the SEBI (Depositories and Participants) Regulations, 2018; registrars under the Registrars to an Issue and Share Transfer Agents Regulations, 1993 (being rewritten); KYC agencies under the KRA Regulations, 2011; and the registrars' customers, the fund houses, under the new SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026. Other regulators touch the adjacencies: IRDAI for insurance repositories, PFRDA for NPS record-keeping, the RBI for account aggregators and NSDL's payments bank, the WDRA for CDSL's commodity repository, IFSCA in GIFT City, and the Ministry of Corporate Affairs for the rule that private companies must dematerialise their shares.

The depositories carry the heaviest obligations because they are Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs), alongside stock exchanges and clearing corporations. That status brings five constraints. Ownership is capped (15% for an exchange or approved institution, 5% for anyone else), which is why BSE holds exactly 15% of CDSL and NSE exactly 15% of NSDL. Boards must include public-interest directors, and SEBI approves key appointments: CDSL's two new executive directors were approved by SEBI before shareholders voted in Q1 FY27. A share of profit goes to the Investor Protection Fund: CDSL's contribution is set at 5% of profit from depository operations (₹23.8 crore provisioned in FY26). SEBI can penalise operational failures: it levied a ₹3 crore "financial disincentive" on CDSL in April 2025 for technical glitches between 2021 and 2024. And SEBI oversees the charges.

How depository prices are set, and why they rarely rise

Depository tariffs are published and overseen by SEBI, and the direction of policy since 2020 has been lower and flatter charges. In July 2024 SEBI's "true-to-label" circular required MIIs to charge uniform, published fees rather than volume-based slabs that rebated money to large brokers. CDSL first cut its slab rates by 50 paise on 1 June 2024 and then moved to a flat ₹3.50 per debit from 1 October 2024, with ₹0.25 off for women first-holders and for MF and bond ISINs. Basic services demat accounts for small investors carry nil or low annual charges, and depositories must pay DPs ₹100 for each new basic account opened outside the top 15 cities, funded by increments to issuer charges. Issuer charges themselves are formula-driven; in management's words, "it is what it is". The only levers left to the depository are volume and new services.

The 2026 fund rules: a second-order squeeze on the registrars

The most important rule change for the registrars does not regulate them at all. The SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026 (gazette dated 14 January 2026, in force from 1 April) replace the old total-expense-ratio caps with a base expense ratio (BER) that excludes statutory levies (securities transaction tax, stamp duty, GST and exchange fees, now disclosed separately). Brokerage that funds can charge on their own trades is capped at 6 basis points in the cash market (from about 8.59 net) and 2 basis points in derivatives (from about 3.89), and the extra 5 basis points that schemes with exit loads could charge is removed. The BER caps per slab are 10–15 basis points lower than the old caps, but SEBI says the slabs above ₹2,000 crore were "revised upwards to limit the impact on cost structure of AMCs broadly to the extent of exclusion of statutory levies". The economic squeeze on fund houses is therefore much smaller than the table suggests; the real loss is the brokerage cut and the 5 basis points.

Activity versus stock: thirty years, four booms and a squeeze

CDSL's profit swings with IPOs, trading and new accounts; the registrars' profit follows average assets. The history shows which booms each one rides.

The sub-sector was created by law in 1996. The Depositories Act of that year allowed securities to be held electronically, and NSDL was set up the same year by IDBI, UTI and NSE. CDSL followed in February 1999, promoted by BSE with banks. The mutual-fund registrars are older: Karvy's registry began in 1985 and CAMS was founded in Chennai in 1988. Through the 2000s CAMS became the largest MF registrar, Karvy Computershare (a joint venture with Australia's Computershare) the second, and in 2006 CVL launched the KYC registration system with the fund industry.

For most of its first two decades CDSL was the smaller depository. It became the larger by accounts in 2019, as the discount brokers it served (Zerodha, Upstox, Groww, Angel One) signed up the first-time investors of the pandemic years. eDIS (2021), DDPI (2022) and T+1 settlement (2022–23) were built on its rails. The registrars went through their own change: General Atlantic bought Karvy's registry business in 2018 and renamed it KFin Technologies, months before Karvy Stock Broking's 2019 misuse of client securities led SEBI to tighten pledge rules. CDSL listed in June 2017, India's and Asia-Pacific's first listed depository by its own description; CAMS listed in October 2020, KFin in December 2022 and NSDL in August 2025, at ₹800 a share in a ₹4,011 crore offer for sale.

The squeeze of FY26 had several causes acting together. Markets topped in September 2024 and were choppy through FY26 before the February–March 2026 crash. The telescoping fee: why the registrars earn less per rupee every year Deep dive

Both registrars took yield resets of about 10% in FY26. CAMS has since held flat; KFin is still sliding. The contract design makes some erosion automatic.

A registrar's fee is not a single rate. Each fund house negotiates a schedule of basis-point rates by asset class (equity, debt, liquid, index and so on) and by size band. As the fund house's assets move into higher bands, the rate on each additional rupee falls. This "telescopic" design means the registrar's average yield falls even if no contract is renegotiated, simply because its clients grow. On top of that, contracts are periodically renewed, and at renewal the large fund houses push for lower rates. And the mix of assets changes with markets and investor preference. KFin's chief executive describes yield as "a factor of three distinct factors": price, mix, and which fund houses grow.

The fight-back, and the new pressure

The registrars have four responses. Mix: equity assets pay the most, so a market that favours equity schemes (CAMS's equity share rose to 54.3% in Q1 FY27) helps yield. New asset classes: SIFs, GIFT City funds, MF-only PMS and new fund houses all start on the steep end of the telescope. Productivity: if the fee per rupee falls 3–4% a year, cost per transaction must fall faster; CAMS's AI-led headcount reduction is the clearest example. Diversification: businesses whose price is not tied to Indian MF assets, examined in the next section.

The new pressure is the 2026 fund rules. Because the registrar fee now sits explicitly inside the capped base expense ratio, a fund house whose budget is squeezed has an obvious place to look at renewal. The direct effect on fund houses is milder than headlines suggested (most of the slab cut removes levies from the cap), so the likely outcome is steady pressure rather than another sharp reset. But the timing matters: large contracts reset in FY26 before the rules took effect, and the next renewal round will be negotiated under them. Investors should watch whether CAMS's yield stays flat through FY27 and whether KFin's erosion slows to its guided 3% or so.

A final point on measurement. Both companies' yields are our calculations, not company disclosures, and the denominator matters: CAMS reports its market share excluding fund-of-funds but bills on assets including them; quarterly average assets differ from month-end assets by several lakh crore when markets move sharply (March 2026 closed at ₹73.7 lakh crore against a Q4 average of about ₹81.6 lakh crore). Comparing a quarterly revenue figure with a month-end asset figure can make yields appear to jump or collapse when nothing has changed.

In plain terms

The registrars' contracts are built so that the bigger a fund house gets, the less it pays on each extra rupee. That alone shaves a few per cent off their average price each year. In FY26 both also gave larger clients one-off price cuts of around 10%. CAMS's price has been stable since; KFin's is still falling.

In plain terms

A registrar does well when the assets it services grow much faster than its price falls. Assets growing 20% and prices falling 4% is a good year; assets growing 10% and prices falling 10% is a flat one.

Build versus buy: two ways to escape the basis point Deep dive

CAMS grows its own adjacent businesses and has restored its margin. KFin bought 51% of a Singapore fund administrator, with a deferred bill of about ₹600 crore for the rest.

Both registrars have reached the same conclusion: a business whose price falls 3–4% a year needs revenue that is not priced in basis points of Indian mutual-fund assets. They have chosen opposite routes. CAMS has built a portfolio of small adjacent businesses, mostly from scratch or through small acquisitions, keeping its margin intact. KFin has bought scale abroad, accepting a lower margin for several years in exchange for faster growth and an international platform.

The KYC toll: one investor check, fetched many times

India's KYC agencies earn per record created and fetched. SEBI cut both prices from April 2026, and the sector's largest KYC business belongs to CDSL.

Before 2006 every fund house and broker verified an investor's identity separately. The KYC registration agency (KRA) system replaced that with "verify once, fetch everywhere": the first intermediary to onboard an investor uploads the KYC record to one KRA (a creation), and every later intermediary fetches it (a fetch), paying a fee each time. CDSL's subsidiary CVL launched the system with the mutual-fund industry in 2006 and remains by far the largest KRA, with 10.62 crore records in June 2026 and a claimed share of more than 70%. CAMS KRA became number two (2.07 crore records) after buying NSE's KRA business during FY26; NSDL's NDML has 2.02 crore records and 1,995 intermediaries; KFin also runs a KRA.

1. Demat accounts and market share: stock versus incremental

What it is. The number of beneficial-owner (BO) accounts held at the depository at period end, and the depository's share of all accounts. Formula. Share = own accounts ÷ (CDSL + NSDL accounts); incremental share = own net additions ÷ total net additions in the period. Worked example. In Q1 FY27 CDSL added 58 lakh net accounts to reach 18.59 crore; NSDL added 12.4 lakh to reach 4.56 crore. CDSL's stock share is 18.59 ÷ 23.15 = 80.3%; its incremental share is 58 ÷ 70.4 = 82.4%. What drives it. Broker choice: the discount brokers that open most new accounts pick the depository, so integration quality (eKYC, eDIS, DDPI, pledge APIs) and the economics offered to DPs decide share. Benchmarks. For CDSL, incremental share above 85% is good, 78–85% average, below 75% weak; net additions above 70 lakh a quarter good, 50–70 lakh average, below 40 lakh weak. How the members compare. Only CDSL has this KPI; it was 81.4% incremental in June 2026 on an analyst's figure, down from the "85% to 90%" management cited for FY26. The trap. Accounts are not investors (one person can hold accounts at several brokers), and stock share lags incremental share by years. 3. Transaction charge per debit, and debits

What it is. The depository's fee each time securities leave a demat account (a sale or transfer), and the number of such debits. Formula. Transaction revenue = debits × tariff; implied debits = transaction revenue ÷ ₹3.50. Worked example. CDSL's ₹239.5 crore of FY26 transaction charges ÷ ₹3.50 implies about 68 crore debits (indicative; the ₹0.25 discount for women first-holders and MF and bond ISINs means the true count is a little higher). A debit is charged per ISIN per day, so an investor who sells three stocks in one day generates three debits. Drivers. Delivery selling by retail investors (not derivatives), the number of active accounts, and the tariff. Benchmarks. Transaction revenue growth above 15% good, 0–15% average, negative weak. Comparison. CDSL's line fell 10% in FY26 and has been flat at ₹59–66 crore a quarter since; NSDL's grew 17% (context). The trap. The investor's "DP charge" of ₹13–30 per sell-day is mostly the broker's fee; the depository's share is ₹3.50. A broker cutting its DP charge does not change CDSL's revenue.

4. Issuer charges per folio

What it is. The annual fee companies pay for their securities to be held at the depository, divided by issuer folios (the count of holdings across all issuers). Formula. Annualised issuer revenue ÷ issuer folios. Worked example. CDSL's Q1 FY27 issuer income of ₹128 crore, annualised to ₹512 crore, over 38.73 crore folios is about ₹13.2 per folio a year; a year earlier, ₹114 crore × 4 ÷ ~33.2 crore folios was about ₹13.7 (both indicative). Drivers. Folio growth (more holdings per account, more accounts holding shares), new issuers (including unlisted companies dematerialising), and the fee formula, which SEBI oversees and which has been increased to fund payments to DPs for basic accounts. Benchmarks. Issuer income growth above 15% good, 8–15% average, below 8% weak. Comparison. CDSL +40% in FY26 and +12.3% in Q1 FY27; NSDL's custody and annual fees +37% in FY26 (context). The trap. The FY26 surge included one-off admission fees from unlisted companies, which fell from ₹3.5 crore in Q4 FY26 to ₹0.3 crore in Q1 FY27 after the "small company" definition was narrowed. Growth will slow toward folio growth, and per-folio revenue is drifting down.

5. KYC records and the fetch/create tariff

What it is. The stock of investor KYC records a KRA holds, and the per-event fees it earns when records are created, fetched, modified or searched. Formula. KYC revenue ≈ creations × ₹5 + fetches × ₹28 + searches × ₹0.25 + modifications (tariffs from 1 April 2026). Worked example. If 80% of a KRA's KYC revenue is fetches and 20% creations, the 2026 reset (fetch −20%, create −75%) cuts revenue by 0.8 × 20% + 0.2 × 75% = 31% at constant volume (indicative). Drivers. New investors (creations), investors joining additional intermediaries (fetches), and SEBI's tariff. Benchmarks. KRA revenue growth above 15% good, 0–15% average, negative weak; record share above 65% dominant. Comparison. CVL 10.62 crore records and ₹45 crore of Q1 FY27 revenue (+22%); CAMS KRA 2.07 crore records and 1.9% of CAMS's revenue (−2.6%); KFin does not disclose. The trap. "Market share" in KYC has no common basis: CVL's ">70%" is a share of records, CAMS's "~20%" is probably a share of new KYCs. Do not compare them.

6. MF average AUM serviced and market share

What it is. The quarterly average assets of the fund houses whose investor records the registrar keeps, and that figure's share of the industry total. Formula. Share = registrar's quarterly average AUM ÷ industry quarterly average AUM. Worked example. In Q1 FY27 CAMS serviced ₹55.9 lakh crore (excluding fund-of-funds) of an industry ₹83.1 lakh crore: 67.2%. KFin serviced ₹27.3 lakh crore: 32.8%. Drivers. Industry growth (flows plus market movement), the growth of each registrar's client fund houses, new fund-house wins and rare switches. Benchmarks. Serviced AUM growth 2 points above industry growth is good, within 1 point average, 2 points below weak. Comparison. CAMS +14.8% year on year against industry +15.3% (slightly below); KFin +16.4% (above). On equity assets the picture reverses: KFin's equity AUM grew 14.1% against the industry's 16.3%, and its equity share fell to 32.4%. The trap. Share of total AUM flatters a registrar whose clients have large liquid and debt books; equity share is what drives revenue.

7. Yield in basis points: asset-based versus all-MF

What it is. Revenue earned per rupee of average assets serviced, expressed in basis points a year. Formula. Asset-based yield = asset-based MF revenue (annualised) ÷ average AUM (on the billing basis); all-MF yield uses all MF revenue. Worked example. CAMS's Q1 FY27 asset-based revenue was 74.1% of ₹395 crore = ₹292.7 crore; × 4 = ₹1,171 crore; ÷ ₹57.33 lakh crore (including fund-of-funds) = 2.04 basis points (indicative). Drivers. Contract price (renewals), telescoping (clients growing into cheaper slabs), mix (equity pays more than debt, liquid and index), and which fund houses grow. Benchmarks. Yield erosion below 2% a year good, 2–4% average, above 5% weak (reset years). Comparison. CAMS asset-based 2.33 → 2.08 → 2.04 basis points (FY25, FY26, Q1 FY27); KFin all-domestic-MF 3.64 → 3.37 → 3.15 (all indicative). The trap. Three denominators are in circulation: quarterly average AUM (what registrars bill on), month-end AUM (what AMFI headlines report) and AUM excluding fund-of-funds (what CAMS uses for market share). In March 2026, month-end AUM was ₹73.7 lakh crore and the quarter's average about ₹81.6 lakh crore; using the wrong one moves a yield by 10%.

8. MF versus non-MF revenue share

What it is. The share of revenue that does not depend on Indian mutual-fund assets. Formula. Non-MF revenue ÷ total revenue (CAMS); non-domestic-MF revenue ÷ total revenue (KFin). Worked example. KFin's Q1 FY27 domestic MF revenue was ₹215.2 crore of ₹356.5 crore, so non-domestic-MF was 39.6%. Drivers. Growth in payments, alternatives, KRA, insurance and analytics (CAMS); international, issuer and alternatives (KFin); acquisitions. Benchmarks. For CAMS, above 20% good, 12–20% average, below 10% weak; for KFin, above 40% good, 30–40% average, below 25% weak. Comparison. CAMS 14.9%; KFin 39.6%. The trap. Non-MF revenue is not automatically better revenue. CAMSPay's margins are thin because of bank charges, and Ascent's EBITDA margin is about 8%. A rising non-MF share that lowers the group margin is a trade, not a free win.

9. Live SIPs, folios and unique investors

What it is. Counts of the investor relationships a registrar services. Formula. Live SIPs = registered SIPs not ceased; live folios = folios with a non-zero balance; unique investors = distinct PANs. Worked example. CAMS serviced 6.72 crore live SIPs (63.9% share), 116.2 million live folios and 4.85 crore unique investors in Q1 FY27; KFin's 37.9 million is SIP live folios, and its average live MF folios were 118.1 million. Drivers. New investors, SIP registrations and cancellations, clean-ups of dormant records. Benchmarks. Live SIP share above 65% good, 60–65% average, below 58% weak (CAMS); live folio growth above industry good. Comparison. CAMS live SIP share rose from 61.5% to 63.9%; KFin's SIP live folios fell from 43.4 million (Q4 FY25) to 37.5 million (Q4 FY26) even as its SIP inflows rose 16.5%, which suggests a clean-up of dormant registrations or a definition change. The trap. KFin's deck also shows "199 million" and "373+ million" investor folios; these are headline totals across all registers (MF and issuer), not investors. AMFI's 28.62 crore folios and 10.06 crore contributing SIP accounts are industry counts; one investor has many of each.

In plain terms

Never call a folio, an account or a SIP an "investor". One person can have a dozen of each. The only count of distinct people is by PAN, and only CAMS publishes it for its own book (4.85 crore).

10. EBITDA margin: screener operating profit versus company EBITDA

What it is. Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation as a share of revenue. Formula. Screener "operating profit" = revenue − operating expenses (excluding other income). Companies' own "EBITDA" sometimes includes other income. Worked example. CAMS: screener ₹683 crore (45.1%) against company operating EBITDA ₹686 crore (45.2%), immaterial. KFin: screener ₹530 crore = company ₹529.7 crore (40.7%). CDSL: screener ₹582 crore (50.8%) excludes other income; NSDL's company "EBITDA" of 57.8% in Q1 FY27 includes other income, while its operating margin was 43.8%. Drivers. Yield, volume, productivity, mix of lower-margin businesses. Benchmarks. Depository above 55% good, 45–55% average, below 45% weak; registrar above 45% good, 38–45% average, below 35% weak. Comparison. FY26: CDSL 50.8%, CAMS 45.1%, KFin 40.7%; Q1 FY27: 47.1%, 46.4% (company), 34.2% (39.4% excluding Ascent). The trap. NSDL's consolidated margin (about 28%) includes a payments bank; compare depositories on standalone operating margins only.

11. Employee cost per rupee of revenue, and headcount

What it is. The registrars' largest cost and their main productivity lever. Formula. Employee cost ÷ revenue; also revenue or transactions per employee where headcount is disclosed. Worked example. CAMS's FY26 employee cost of ₹497 crore on ₹1,516 crore of revenue is 32.8%; if revenue grows 13% and average headcount falls 4–5% with modest wage increases, the ratio drops by about 2 points in a year (indicative). Drivers. Wage inflation, appraisal timing (CAMS deferred appraisals to 1 July, so Q2 carries a step-up), automation, acquisitions (Ascent lifted KFin's ratio). Benchmarks. Registrar below 32% good, 32–38% average, above 40% weak; depository below 15% good. Comparison. CDSL 13.7%; CAMS 32.8%; KFin 40.3% (37.9% excluding Ascent). The trap. Some outsourced processing sits in "operating expenses" (CAMS ₹210 crore), so a company that outsources more looks leaner on employee cost alone. Compare total cash costs too.

13. Payout and dividends

What it is. The share of profit paid out as dividends. Formula. Dividend per share ÷ earnings per share. Worked example. CAMS paid ₹12.50 a share for FY26 (post-split basis: interims of ₹2.20 and ₹2.80 adjusted, ₹3.50, and a ₹4 final) on EPS of ₹19.20, a payout of about 65%. Drivers. Cash generation (all three convert 90–110% of EBITDA into operating cash flow), capital needs (platform capex, acquisitions such as Ascent's put), and policy. Benchmarks. Above 60% good for an asset-light business, 40–60% average, below 35% weak. Comparison. CDSL 58% (₹12.75 final), CAMS 65% (₹12.50), KFin 60% (₹12 final, from ₹7.50 for FY25). Dividend yields at 22 October 2026: CAMS 1.87%, KFin 1.41%, CDSL 1.02%. The trap. Per-share history must be adjusted for CDSL's 1:1 bonus (record date 24-Aug-2024) and CAMS's 1:5 split (effective 5-Dec-2025); unadjusted figures make dividends look like they fell.

15. Operating leverage: the incremental margin

What it is. How much of each extra rupee of revenue turns into extra operating profit. It is the cleanest test of whether a platform business is scaling. Formula. Change in EBITDA ÷ change in revenue, year on year. Worked example. CAMS's Q1 FY27 revenue rose ₹40.8 crore (₹354.2 → ₹395.0 crore) and its operating EBITDA ₹28.4 crore (₹154.8 → ₹183.2 crore), an incremental margin of about 70% against an average margin of 46%. KFin's revenue rose ₹82.4 crore but its EBITDA only ₹8.1 crore, an incremental margin of about 10%, because most of the added revenue was Ascent's at an 8% margin. CDSL's FY26 revenue rose ₹63 crore while its operating profit fell ₹43 crore: negative operating leverage. Drivers. Fixed platform costs, price changes (a yield cut has a 100% incremental margin in reverse), acquisitions and investment cycles. Benchmarks. Above 60% good, 40–60% average, below 40% weak for a mature registry. The trap. Acquired revenue and one-off items distort the measure; compute it on organic figures where the company provides them (KFin excluding Ascent: revenue +₹26.3 crore, EBITDA +₹4.5 crore, still only about 17%).

16. Cash conversion and free cash flow

What it is. How much of reported operating profit arrives as cash. Formula. Operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA; free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditure. Worked example. In FY26 CDSL converted 107% of EBITDA into operating cash flow (₹467 crore) and generated ₹346 crore of free cash flow after heavy technology and premises capex; CAMS converted 110% (₹584 crore; free cash flow ₹444 crore); KFin 90% (₹370 crore; ₹259 crore), with management putting free cash flow at about 51% of EBITDA. Together the three generated ₹1,049 crore of free cash flow, a yield of about 1.8% on their combined market value. Drivers. Receivable days (KFin's 76 days against CAMS's 14, because issuer and international clients pay more slowly), capex cycles (CDSL's fixed assets rose from ₹106 crore to ₹500 crore in four years) and tax. Benchmarks. Above 100% good, 85–100% average, below 80% weak. The trap. Free cash flow before acquisitions overstates what is available to shareholders at KFin, which owes about ₹600 crore for the rest of Ascent between 2028 and 2030.

In plain terms

These businesses turn almost all their profit into cash and need little capital, which is why they pay out 58–65% of profit as dividends. The exception to watch is KFin, where slower-paying clients and the Ascent bill absorb more of the cash.

Anatomy of one typical unit: a ₹1 lakh crore fund house's registrar mandate (illustrative)

To see how the KPIs fit together, take one illustrative mandate: a fund house with ₹1 lakh crore of average assets, 55% equity, 30% debt and liquid, 15% index and other. Assume a blended schedule of 2.6 basis points on equity, 1.2 on debt and liquid and 1.0 on index and other. Asset-based revenue is ₹14.3 + ₹3.6 + ₹1.5 = ₹19.4 crore (1.94 basis points). Transaction and other MF fees add ₹2.9 crore, for revenue of ₹22.3 crore. The registrar spends ₹7.3 crore on staff, ₹3.1 crore on printing, courier, call-centre and bank costs, and ₹1.9 crore on technology and administration, leaving EBITDA of ₹10.0 crore (44.8%). After ₹1.4 crore of depreciation and 25% tax, profit is about ₹6.5 crore. Now run year two: assets grow 15%, the blended yield falls 3% (telescoping), transaction fees rise 10% and costs rise 7%. Revenue rises 11.3% to ₹24.8 crore, EBITDA 16.7% to ₹11.7 crore (47.0%) and profit 18% to ₹7.6 crore. That is the registrar model working as designed: assets growing well ahead of yield erosion, with costs held below revenue growth.

Definition traps, collected

The traps below are the ones that most often produce wrong conclusions in broker notes, press coverage and even company presentations. Each has appeared in at least one public description of these companies in the past year, including our own earlier primers, which used a CAMS yield of "about 3 basis points" (true of FY24 only) and a CDSL cash figure larger than its balance sheet.

  • Three AUMs. Quarterly average AUM (billing basis), month-end AUM (AMFI headline) and AUM excluding fund-of-funds (CAMS's share basis). In a falling quarter the average sits well above the close.
  • Accounts are not investors. 23.2 crore demat accounts and 28.62 crore MF folios belong to far fewer people. Write "18.6 crore demat accounts at CDSL", never "18.6 crore investors".
  • Three depository shares. CDSL has ~80% of accounts, ~57% of total income and ~14% of custody value. Say which.
  • Two registrar yields. CAMS's asset-based yield (~2.04 bps) and its all-MF yield (~2.35 bps) differ by a sixth; KFin's 3.15 bps is all-MF. Compare like with like.
  • Revenue versus total income at CDSL. ₹1,145 crore is revenue; ₹1,238 crore is total income including ₹93.6 crore of other income. CDSL's deck "other" also mixes operating lines (e-CAS, e-voting) with treasury.
  • Standalone versus consolidated at CDSL. Standalone profit includes dividends from CVL (₹62 crore in FY26), which distort standalone year-on-year growth. Use consolidated.
  • Reported versus attributable PAT at CAMS. ₹472 crore reported, ₹476 crore attributable to owners (loss-making subsidiaries carry negative minority interests).
  • Including versus excluding Ascent at KFin. FY26 EBITDA margin 40.7% versus 43.5%; Q1 FY27 34.2% versus 39.4%. And Ascent is only 51% owned.
  • Company EBITDA versus operating profit. NSDL's "EBITDA" includes other income; screener's operating profit does not.
  • Cash. Use company-reported cash and investments, not screener's "investments" line; deduct earmarked balances and add debt-like obligations such as KFin's Ascent put.

How to read a depository or registrar's quarterly results in ten minutes

All three companies publish an investor presentation with the results and hold a call within a few days; CDSL and CAMS also publish monthly or quarterly operating data. The checklist below assumes the presentation and the AMFI data for the quarter are open side by side. The order matters: volume and price first, then the lines that swing, then costs, then capital.

  1. Revenue growth, then the volume behind it (1 minute). For CDSL, compare revenue growth with growth in issuer folios, debits implied by transaction charges and net new accounts. For CAMS and KFin, compare MF revenue growth with growth in quarterly average AUM. A gap wider than 5 points means price is falling faster than normal.
  2. The yield (1 minute). Compute asset-based (CAMS) or domestic-MF (KFin) revenue × 4 ÷ quarterly average AUM. Flat quarter on quarter is good; a fall of more than 2% in a quarter is a red flag unless explained by mix.
  3. The activity lines at CDSL (1 minute). IPO and corporate-action fees and KYC/online data. Check them against the quarter's IPO calendar and new-account count.
  4. Other income (1 minute). For CDSL, ask how much was MTM and accrual on investments (₹43.8 crore in Q1 FY27). Subtract it before judging profit growth.
  5. Operating margin, then the cost lines (1 minute). Employee cost growth versus revenue growth; technology cost at CDSL; appraisal timing at CAMS (Q2 step-up).
  6. Non-MF growth and margin (1 minute). CAMS: CAMSPay, Alternatives, KRA. KFin: international (and Ascent's EBITDA margin, about 8.4% in Q1 FY27), issuer, alternatives.
  7. Market share (1 minute). CAMS and KFin QAAUM share and equity share; CDSL incremental account share against NSDL's.
  8. Regulatory notes (1 minute). Any change to tariffs, KYC fees, IPF levies or SEBI penalties; any renewal commentary from the registrars.
  9. Cash and payout (1 minute). Cash build, dividends declared, and for KFin any change in the Ascent liability (it moves through equity, not profit).
  10. Guidance and red flags (1 minute). CAMS's FY27 guide (revenue ~13%, EBITDA ~16%); KFin's (18–20% revenue, margin ≥40% incl. Ascent by end-FY27); CDSL gives none. Red flags: yield down more than 2% in a quarter, margin down with no one-off, incremental share below 80%, treasury income above 15% of pre-tax profit.
In plain terms

On results day, start with volume and price, not profit. For the registrars that means average assets and yield; for the depository, holdings, sales, listings and new accounts. Then strip out treasury gains and acquisitions to see what the core business did.

The global lens: double the margins, a fraction of the revenue

Global registries and fund administrators earn 18–38% margins on billions of dollars of revenue. India's members earn 41–51% on a few hundred million. That gap is the opportunity and the risk.

India's market plumbing is small in dollar terms. The three members' FY26 revenue of ₹3,962 crore is about US$418 million at ₹94.9 to the dollar. Broadridge, the US investor-communications and post-trade technology company that is the closest analogue to KFin's issuer business and CDSL's e-voting and e-CAS, reported revenue of US$7,476.8 million for its year to June 2026, of which US$4,878.0 million was recurring fee revenue. SS&C Technologies, which owns DST, the largest US mutual-fund transfer agent, reported US$6,272.2 million for calendar 2025. Computershare, Karvy's former joint-venture partner and a global share registrar, reported management revenue of US$3,212.5 million for its year to June 2026, of which US$748.7 million was "margin income" (interest earned on client balances).

The margins run the other way. Broadridge's adjusted operating margin was 20.5% (GAAP 17.4%), guided to about 21% for FY27 with recurring revenue growth of 6–8% in constant currency. SS&C's adjusted operating margin was 38.2% with organic growth of 4.8% (from its own release, via a search summary). Computershare's EBIT excluding margin income was US$447.5 million, an 18.2% margin on revenue excluding margin income (also via a summary of its own documents). Three lessons follow. First, pricing models differ. US transfer agency has historically been priced per account per year, at tens of dollars an account; India's registrars are paid in basis points of assets, about ₹114 per live folio a year at CAMS (indicative). Per-account pricing in India would be commercially and politically impossible at current account sizes, which is why assets, not folios, drive Indian registrar revenue. Second, the depository model is unusual. In most large markets the central securities depository is a utility: DTCC is owned by its users and returns surplus to them. India's depositories are listed, profit-seeking MIIs whose regulator diverts part of their profit to investor protection. A 50% margin is a standing invitation for a regulator to cut tariffs. Third, the valuation premium is large: the members trade at roughly 1.5–3 times the earnings multiples of global peers, justified (if at all) by unit growth of 15–20% a year against low single digits abroad.

The global comparison also clarifies KFin's strategy. By buying Ascent, KFin entered a market where Apex, Citco, SS&C and the custody banks compete on price per fund, and where margins are closer to global norms than to Indian registrar margins. Ascent's 8% EBITDA margin is not an aberration in that market; KFin's bet is that Indian engineering costs and its mPower platform can lift it towards KFin-level margins over three to five years. For CAMS, the global lesson is about payments and data: SS&C and Broadridge grew by layering software and data services on top of their registries, which is what CAMSPay, Think360 and CAMS's account-aggregator business attempt on a much smaller scale.

In plain terms

Indian record-keepers keep about twice as much of each rupee of revenue as their global counterparts, and are valued at two to three times the earnings multiple. Abroad, the same jobs are either run as utilities or priced competitively. That comparison is the strongest argument that Indian margins are more likely to fall than rise over time.

Technology and disruption: AI helps costs, migrations and cyber risk threaten them

The main technology risks here are operational: platform rebuilds, cyber incidents and rule changes that move the "golden record" from one registry to another. Being displaced by a new competitor is a smaller risk.

Artificial intelligence is a cost tailwind more than a threat. The registrars' largest cost is people processing transactions, answering queries and correcting records. CAMS already uses AI to accept about 10% of physical transactions at the first data-entry step, with all eight transaction types due by November 2026, and guides a 4–5% fall in average headcount in FY27. KFin has launched AI tools for investor relations (Aegix), SIP automation (Finex) and a cloud pension-administration platform (SupremaPlus). The risk is that fund houses ask for the savings at renewal: if registrars' costs fall 5% a year, their largest clients will expect a share, especially under the 2026 expense caps.

Platform migrations are the largest operational risk. CAMS is moving its mutual-fund processing to a new platform, Fundsnet, with go-live planned for September 2026 and the old platform to be retired during FY27. In a registry, an error is not a bad quarter; it can be a misallocated unit, a wrong NAV applied or a failed payout, each a regulatory event. CDSL has spent heavily on new data centres and systems (depreciation up sixfold in four years) and was penalised ₹3 crore for technical glitches between 2021 and 2024. Execution quality on these rebuilds will matter more to the next three years' earnings than any change in competitive position.

Cyber security is a regulatory as well as a business risk. The three members hold identity and holdings data for 15–25 crore people. SEBI's Cyber Security and Cyber Resilience Framework (August 2024) applies to depositories, KYC agencies and qualified registrars, and requires governance, testing and incident reporting. KFin appointed a group chief information security officer in October 2026 and cites an external security rating of 810. A breach at an MII would bring penalties, remediation costs and possibly tariff concessions.

Rule changes could move value between registries. Three are worth watching. If mutual-fund units were required to be held only in demat form (a long-running debate), part of the record-keeping role would move from the registrars to the depositories, although the registrar would still run the fund's register. If CKYC became the single KYC source, KRA fetch revenue would compress further. And the platforms that already sit between investors and registrars, MF Central (a 50:50 joint venture between CAMS and KFin), the exchanges' MF platforms and MF Utility, could take on more of the investor-service layer. So far the registrar's register remains the legal "golden copy" in every case.

Competition for broker integrations is technological. An analyst on CDSL's August call said a rival had "reduced onboarding friction" for fintech brokers, and CDSL's incremental account share fell about 420 basis points in a quarter. Depository choice is made by brokers' engineering teams, so API quality, onboarding speed and support for new products (direct pay-out, pledges, unified investor apps) decide share at the margin. CDSL has launched a unified investor app, embedded proxy-advisor recommendations in e-voting and uses distributed-ledger technology to record secured-bond covenants. Tokenised securities could in theory bypass a depository, but Indian law requires securities to be held in a depository, so this is a distant risk.

New regulated data businesses are the opportunity side: account aggregators (CAMSfinserv with about 9% share; NSDL's NADL), consent management under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (CAMS's ConsenPro), insurance repositories (CAMSRep's 10.7 million e-insurance accounts; CDSL's CIRL with 24.42 lakh) and NPS record-keeping (KFin's 12.2% share and 22% of new subscribers). Each is small today; together they are the registrars' route to revenue that is not priced in basis points.

In plain terms

Technology is mostly helping these companies cut costs. The danger is not a start-up replacing them; it is a botched system upgrade, a data breach, or a rule change that moves part of their job to someone else.

Bull versus bear

The bull case is volume and scarcity. The bear case is price, regulation and a valuation that already assumes the bull case.

The bull case

  • Structural volume: 23.2 crore demat accounts, 10.06 crore contributing SIP accounts and ₹32,490 crore a month of SIP inflows, still small against ₹230 lakh crore of bank deposits; SIPs held through the 2026 crash.
  • Unbreakable structure: two depositories by licence, two MF registrars by switching cost, one dominant KRA; no credible new entrant in two decades.
  • Annuity shift at CDSL: issuer charges now 40% of revenue and "over time" revenue 41%; earnings should become steadier as activity lines shrink in the mix.
  • Pricing reset behind CAMS: yield flat for three quarters, margin back to 46.4%, FY27 guidance of ~13% revenue and ~16% EBITDA growth.
  • Operating leverage and AI: CAMS's incremental margin of ~70% in Q1 FY27; headcount falling while transactions grow ~20%.
  • Cash machines: no debt, ₹3,214 crore of net cash, cash conversion above 100% at CDSL and CAMS, payouts of 58–65%.
  • New pools: SIFs, GIFT City, MF-only PMS, AIFs, NPS, payments, international fund administration.

The bear case

  • Price only goes one way: debit tariff flattened, KYC fees cut ~30%, registrar yields down 7–11% in FY26, and the 2026 expense caps put the registrar fee inside a capped budget.
  • Regulator as ceiling: MII status, IPF levy, a ₹3 crore penalty precedent, and 40–51% margins against 18–38% globally invite further intervention.
  • Activity has cooled: new demat additions down a third in 2025, active derivatives traders down 18%, SIP growth slowing to ~11%, MF assets off their August peak.
  • Share leakage: CDSL's incremental share down ~420 bps in a quarter; KFin losing equity-asset share.
  • KFin's margin and Ascent bill: margin 34.2%, down even ex-Ascent; ~₹600 crore still owed for the remaining 49%; General Atlantic's 14% overhang.
  • Treasury dependence: about a quarter of CDSL's Q1 FY27 profit came from investment gains; the 7-Oct rate rise points the other way.
  • Valuation: 34–54× earnings; CDSL needs ~17% a year EPS growth for five years just to reach 25×.

Weighing the two, the bull case is about volume and the bear case is about price, and both are true. The swing factor is the gap between them. In a normal year (assets up mid-teens, yield erosion of 3–4%, activity stable) the registrars should grow profit in the low-to-mid teens and CDSL in line with issuer folios plus whatever activity adds. In a year like FY26, with a pricing reset and an activity slump at once, profit can fall even as volumes grow 18–20%. The valuations sit closer to the first scenario than the second.

The members differ in which side of the argument they are most exposed to. CAMS has absorbed its reset and needs the least growth to justify its price, but carries the most exposure to renewal pressure from large fund houses. KFin has the fastest growth and the most diversification, but also the most execution risk and the heaviest deferred obligation. CDSL has the strongest franchise and the highest margin, but the most regulatory exposure and the highest bar set by its valuation. A portfolio of all three is a bet on Indian financialisation with the price risk spread across three different price-setters: SEBI, the fund houses and the activity cycle.

What would change the balance? On the bull side, a sustained primary-market cycle with several very large issues, a clear end to registrar yield erosion through FY27 renewals, and KFin's margin returning to 40% would all argue that FY26 was the trough. On the bear side, another SEBI tariff action at the depositories, a second registrar reset under the 2026 expense rules, or a prolonged fall in MF assets from the August peak would argue that FY26 was a preview rather than an exception.

In plain terms

Everyone agrees more Indians will invest. The argument is whether these companies will be allowed to keep enough of the extra business. Investors buying at today's prices are betting that they will.
