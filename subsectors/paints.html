00

The one-page view: a distribution moat, repriced by a cheque book

Six listed companies, two of them worth four-fifths of the group, in an industry that spent two years learning whether money can buy what took Asian Paints eighty years to build.

Indian paint was, for most of two decades, one of the best businesses on the stock exchange. A product that is chemically ordinary (white pigment, a plastic binder, water, chalk and a few additives in a tin) was sold through a distribution system that was anything but ordinary: more than a lakh and a half of dealer counters, each with a tinting machine that mixes thousands of shades on demand, replenished within hours from a network of depots, and recommended to the homeowner by a painter who had been trained, rewarded and courted by the brand for years. That system let three incumbents (Asian Paints, Berger and Kansai Nerolac) earn gross margins of 40–45%, returns on capital of 25–38% and stock-market multiples of 50–80 times earnings.

Then the Aditya Birla group decided the moat could be bought. Grasim announced a paints business in 2021, raised the budget from ₹5,000 crore to ₹10,000 crore, built six plants with 1,332 million litres of annual capacity and launched Birla Opus on 22 February 2024. It gave dealers free tinting machines, paid them margins of 12–16% where the leader paid about 4%, threw in 10% extra paint free and offered 20 days of credit instead of five. Eighteen months later the JSW group bought the Indian arm of AkzoNobel (now JSW Dulux) in a deal of roughly ₹13,000 crore. A market that behaved like a two-and-a-half-player oligopoly became a five-to-six-player contest almost overnight.

This report covers the six companies the NSE 750 classifies as paints and adhesives: Asian Paints, Pidilite Industries, Berger Paints India, JSW Dulux, Kansai Nerolac Paints and Indigo Paints. Together they earned ₹75,121 crore of revenue in FY26 (+4.6%), ₹13,793 crore of EBITDA (an 18.4% margin) and ₹10,692 crore of reported profit, which falls to about ₹9,100 crore once a one-time ₹1,846 crore gain at JSW Dulux is removed. They were worth ₹4.62 lakh crore on 19 October 2026. Birla Opus, the disruptor, is not among them: it sits inside Grasim, covered in our diversified holding companies report.

The five forces that define this sub-sector

  • The entrant war, now fought with trade spend rather than price. Opus and JSW turned a dealer-led oligopoly into a contest. By 2026 the weapon had shifted from list-price cuts to rebates, free material and credit: Berger reports a challenger "aligned" its dealer prices with the industry, but large-dealer rebates and 10% free paint continue.
  • Raw materials decide the year; competition decides who keeps the margin. FY26's soft crude and titanium dioxide (TiO₂) funded the price war. The 2026 West Asia war reversed it: crude spiked above $109 a barrel, Asian Paints saw 25% raw-material inflation in Q1 FY27 and took about 14% of price. With Brent back near $110 in mid-October, a second round of 3–4% begins on 1 November.
  • A capacity glut. Opus alone has 1,332 million litres of capacity running at an indicative quarter of its potential; Asian Paints has 1,850 million litres; Berger, Kansai and JSW are all adding. Every new litre must be pushed through a dealer, which keeps schemes elevated for years.
  • The adhesives moat versus the paint moat. Pidilite passed through about 12% of price in Q1 FY27 with volumes still growing 11.3%, kept a 52.5% gross margin and earns a 31% return on capital. The paint moat proved replicable with capital; the glue moat did not. That is why Pidilite trades at 57 times earnings and Asian Paints at 45.7.
  • A valuation reset, and adjusted profits that need reading carefully. Asian Paints traded at 60–80 times earnings for most of its listed life; it is now at 45.7. JSW Dulux's FY26 profit contains a ₹1,846 crore disposal gain; Kansai Nerolac's FY24–25 profits contained roughly ₹750 crore a year of one-off other income. Reported growth rates for both mislead.

Q1 FY27 (April to June 2026) was the best quarter for the group in three years: revenue up 15.6% to ₹22,387 crore, operating profit up about 25% and profit after tax up about 31%. Read it carefully. Two things produced it, and only one of them lasts. The first is real: volumes held up well (Asian Paints' decorative volume +9%, Berger +8.4%, Pidilite's underlying volume +11.3%, JSW Dulux +25% on a small base) while prices were raised to pass on the oil shock. The second is temporary: companies sold paint made from raw materials bought before the shock, so the cost of what they sold lagged the price they charged. Asian Paints and Pidilite both told investors that the benefit reverses in Q2. Results for Q2 FY27 arrive between 28 October (Pidilite) and 5 November (Berger), and they are the first clean read on margins under the shock.

In plain terms

Paint companies make money not because paint is hard to make, but because getting the right shade into a painter's hands, in the right town, the same afternoon, was hard to copy. Birla Opus copied it with money: free machines, fatter dealer margins, longer credit. The incumbents kept most of their customers, but at a price: lower prices per litre, bigger rebates and more advertising. The oil shock of 2026 has temporarily hidden that cost by forcing everyone to raise prices together. The real test comes when oil falls and someone is tempted to cut again.

01

From tin to wall: where a rupee of paint is earned

Paint chemistry is cheap and widely known. The money is made at the dealer counter, in the tinting machine and in the painter's recommendation.

A tin of decorative paint is a dispersion of a white pigment (titanium dioxide, or TiO₂) and cheaper fillers (calcite, china clay, talc) in a binder, usually a water-based acrylic or vinyl emulsion, with thickeners, biocides and dispersants added. Enamels and wood finishes use solvent-based alkyd or polyurethane resins instead. Almost every input is a petrochemical or a mined mineral, bought at prices the paint company does not control. None of the formulations is secret in any useful sense: a competent chemist can match an Asian Paints emulsion's performance in a few months.

What a competitor cannot easily copy is everything that happens after the paint leaves the factory. The industry sells mostly through independent dealers, who keep a small range of white "base" paints and a set of concentrated colourants. When a customer chooses a shade, the dealer's tinting machine squirts precise doses of colourant into the base and shakes it, producing any one of 2,000 to 5,000 shades in minutes. The dealer does not need to stock thousands of finished colours; the manufacturer does not need to forecast them. The system works only if the dealer is restocked quickly (Asian Paints is known for same-day or next-day replenishment), if the machine is maintained and calibrated, and if the colour the painter shows the customer on a card is exactly what comes out of the tin.

The margin concentrates at Step 3–5, where the brand owner controls the colour system and the dealer relationship. Raw-material makers capture value only in price spikes; dealers earn thin percentages on fast turnover.

The last link in the chain matters most. Indian homeowners rarely choose a paint brand: they choose a colour and a budget, and the painter or contractor decides which brand to buy and from which dealer. Painters are paid by the job, not by the brand, so loyalty programmes, training, insurance schemes and reward points have become a large, mostly hidden cost. Pidilite runs the same playbook with carpenters, plumbers and masons: about 1.5 million influencers are engaged with its programmes, and they decide which adhesive or waterproofing compound goes into a job far more than the householder does.

Industrial coatings sit on a different economic footing. A car maker approves a paint system only after a year or more of testing, and once approved the supplier is rarely changed mid-model. That stickiness is valuable, but the customer is a large, sophisticated buyer who prices supply against its own cost sheet and resets prices with a lag. Industrial coatings therefore earn lower margins than decorative paint (Kansai's 12–14% EBITDA margin is the clearest illustration) but are untouched by the dealer war. The more industrial a company's mix, the lower its margin and the lower its multiple, but the steadier its volumes.

In plain terms

Raw-material makers sell chemistry, dealers sell convenience, painters sell labour, and the paint brand sells trust plus logistics. The brand captures the biggest slice because it owns the colour system and the relationships at both ends of the chain. Anyone who wants that slice must either build those relationships for decades or pay dealers and painters enough to switch, which is exactly what Birla Opus did.

02

A ₹80,000 crore market at four kilograms a head

India paints its walls a fraction as often, and with a fraction as much paint, as richer countries. That gap is the long-run growth case, and the reason entrants were willing to spend ₹10,000 crore.

The Indian paint market was worth roughly ₹80,000 crore in 2025 on the most widely cited estimate (Outlook Business, December 2025), up from about ₹43,500 crore in 2015–16. That is a nominal growth rate of about 6–7% a year over nine years: slower than the "double-digit" growth the industry likes to describe, because the period includes demonetisation, the GST transition, the pandemic and the FY25–26 price war. Other estimates range from about ₹72,000 crore to research-house figures equivalent to more than ₹95,000 crore that probably include more categories. We use ₹72,000–80,000 crore as an INDICATIVE range; no single series is authoritative.

A bottom-up check supports the upper end. The five listed paint members sold about ₹55,000–57,000 crore of paint in India in FY26 (their ₹60,520 crore of revenue minus Asian Paints' roughly ₹3,100 crore of international sales and its home-décor businesses). Birla Opus adds an INDICATIVE ₹4,800–5,000 crore. Nippon Paint India, JSW Paints, Shalimar, the Indian arms of global industrial specialists and a long tail of regional and unorganised makers plausibly add ₹15,000–18,000 crore. The total lands at ₹78,000–82,000 crore.

The structural argument rests on consumption per person. India uses roughly 4–5 kg of paint per person a year, against 15–25 kg in developed markets. The gap reflects lower incomes, smaller homes, a large stock of unpainted or lime-washed rural dwellings, and long repainting cycles. As incomes rise, three things happen together: more homes are painted at all, they are repainted more often (the typical cycle is five to seven years and has been shortening), and households trade up from distemper to emulsion and from economy to premium emulsions. Each of these lifts volume or value. Historically, decorative volume grew at roughly 1.5–2 times real GDP; that rule of thumb is approximate rather than a measured series.

Closing even a third of the gap would roughly double Indian volumes. That is why two conglomerates spent heavily to enter, and why the incumbents' multiples stayed high for so long.

India's mix is also unusual. Decorative paint is about 70–75% of the Indian market, against roughly 40% globally, where industrial and automotive coatings dominate. That matters for profitability: decorative paint, sold through brands and dealers, carries higher margins than industrial coatings sold to purchasing departments. It also explains why the Indian listed paint companies, which are overwhelmingly decorative, earn EBITDA margins comparable to Sherwin-Williams, the US store-led decorative leader, and well above most global coatings groups. India is a small part of the global coatings market (an INDICATIVE ~5% of a roughly ₹17–19 lakh crore global industry), but it is one of the few large markets still growing volume at high single digits.

Adhesives and construction chemicals are a separate, smaller market whose total size we could not source reliably. Pidilite alone had ₹14,601 crore of FY26 revenue, and the category's growth is visible in one statistic the company disclosed in its Q1 FY27 call: tile adhesives are used in only 25–30% of tile installations in India, with the rest still laid on cement mortar. Waterproofing is similarly under-penetrated. These are the adjacencies every paint company now wants, which makes Pidilite's market both a growth runway and a new front line.

Forecasts deserve caution. A reasonable INDICATIVE range for industry value growth to FY30 is 8–10% a year, made up of high-single-digit volume growth and low-single-digit price and mix. Berger's own target of ₹20,000 crore of revenue by 2030 implies about 11% a year. ICICI Direct models Asian Paints' revenue growing about 14% a year from FY26 to FY28, helped by the 2026 price increases. None of these allows for a renewed price war when raw-material costs fall, which is the main downside scenario.

In plain terms

The Indian paint market is not large by global standards, and over the last decade it grew at roughly the pace of nominal GDP. The case for owning it was never its size today. It was that an Indian uses perhaps a quarter of the paint a person in a rich country does, so volume should keep compounding for decades. Entrants believed that too, which is why the runway is now shared among more players.

03

What makes India paint: repaints, new homes, villages and Diwali

Three-quarters of decorative demand is repainting, which makes paint more defensive than housing, more seasonal than most consumer goods and more rural than its urban image suggests.

The single most important fact about decorative demand is that roughly 70–75% of it is repainting of existing homes (the Asian Paints primer's estimate; the JSW Dulux primer says "about two-thirds"). Only a quarter to a third comes from new construction. Repainting is driven by the age of the last coat, household income, weddings and festivals, and aspiration. It can be deferred in a bad year, but walls keep ageing, so deferred demand comes back. That makes paint far less cyclical than cement or steel, which depend directly on construction.

New housing: the lagging second engine

Painting is the last trade on a building site, so paint demand from new construction lags the construction cycle by 12 to 24 months. The current housing cycle, discussed in our real estate report, has been strong in launches and sales since 2022, which means a pipeline of completions needing paint through FY27–28. Two policy changes help at the margin. The GST 2.0 reform of 22 September 2025 cut the tax on cement from 28% to 18% (see our cement report), trimming roughly ₹25–30 a bag and modestly cheapening self-built homes. And the Reserve Bank's policy repo rate stands at 5.50% after its 7 October 2026 meeting, keeping home-loan rates moderate. Paint itself got no GST cut in 2025: paints, varnishes and putty remain at 18%, the rate they moved to in November 2017. A few tax portals claim a 2025 cut for paint; the current 18% rate is not in doubt.

Construction-linked products respond faster than paint. Tile adhesives, waterproofing and construction chemicals go in during building, not at the end, so Pidilite's Dr. Fixit and Roff, Berger's STP and Indigo's Apple Chemie track construction activity more directly than emulsions do.

Rural ahead of urban

Paint has a reputation as an urban, aspirational product, but rural and small-town India has been the stronger market recently. Asian Paints reported double-digit demand growth in all three months of Q1 FY27, with rural ahead of urban; Pidilite described demand as resilient across urban and "rurban" markets. That matters for strategy: the leader's density of dealers in small towns is a defensive asset, and Indigo Paints was built specifically on small-town dealer counters. It also contrasts with the wider consumer economy: NielsenIQ data for April–June 2026 showed home and personal care volumes down 4.3%, while paint volumes rose high single digits. Paint was one of the stronger consumer categories of the first half of 2026.

Weather, festivals and the shape of the year

Paint is seasonal. The monsoon (July to September, the second fiscal quarter) suppresses exterior painting and slows site work, so Q2 is normally the weakest quarter. The festive season, especially the weeks before Diwali, and the dry months before summer (the third and fourth quarters) are the peaks. Diwali falls around 8 November in 2026, nearly three weeks later than in 2025 (20 October), so less of the pre-festive painting rush lands in September, the last month of Q2, and more of it in October and early November (Q3). That makes Q2 comparisons noisier than usual: Asian Paints expects a pick-up "at the fag end of Q2". Local weather matters too; floods in Assam hurt Berger, which is strong in the east, in Q1 FY27.

Premiumisation, adjacencies and the industrial half

Within decorative paint, mix improvement is a steady tailwind: households move from distemper to emulsion and from economy to premium emulsions, and they buy more waterproofing, textures and wood finishes. In Q1 FY27 mix contributed about 3 percentage points of Asian Paints' roughly 7% realisation growth. New launches made up about 17% of Asian Paints' sales. Faster-growing adjacencies sit at the edges: Berger's construction chemicals have grown about 25% a year, Indigo's Apple Chemie grew about 40% in Q1 FY27.

Industrial demand follows different drivers entirely. Car and two-wheeler production drives Kansai's auto coatings and Asian Paints' PPG joint venture for auto OEM and refinish; electric vehicles need the same body paint as petrol cars, so the transition does not threaten volume. Government and private infrastructure capex drives protective coatings, where Asian Paints' APPPG joint venture grew 21.3% in Q1 FY27. International demand adds a third leg for Asian Paints, whose overseas revenue rose 27.2% in Q1 FY27, with the Middle East up 34% despite the regional war.

Finally, cost spikes consolidate the market. When raw materials jump, small and unorganised paint makers struggle to buy inputs and to raise prices quickly, and the large brands take share. Asian Paints said exactly that in July 2026: its volume growth was "ahead of small players". The same pattern is visible in packaged foods and personal care, where big brands gained share from regional ones in the same shock.

In plain terms

Most paint is bought because a wall got old, not because a building got finished. That is why paint demand is steadier than housing, why villages and small towns matter as much as cities, and why a late Diwali can move a whole quarter's numbers. When you read a quarterly result, check the festival calendar and the monsoon before concluding anything about demand.

04

Where ₹100 goes: a petrochemical cost base under a consumer brand

More than half of every rupee of paint revenue is spent on oil-linked and mined inputs the company cannot price. Everything else is the cost of the brand and the distribution machine.

A paint company's income statement looks like a consumer-goods company's on top and a chemical company's underneath. Revenue is set by brand power and competition at the dealer counter. Costs are dominated by raw and packaging materials whose prices are set in global commodity markets: crude oil and its derivatives (monomers, solvents), ethylene and propylene, titanium ore, tinplate and plastic resin. For Asian Paints in Q1 FY27, raw and packaging materials consumed ₹56.4 of every ₹100 of revenue; for the challengers it is typically ₹58–62; for Pidilite, whose branded adhesives command a larger premium over their chemistry, it was ₹45.3 in FY26.

The input basket

Inside the raw-material bill, two families dominate. Titanium dioxide, the white pigment that gives paint its opacity, is the single most expensive input by weight, and an INDICATIVE 20–30% of a decorative raw-material basket. (A figure of "55–60%" is sometimes quoted for TiO₂; that is closer to raw materials' share of total costs, not TiO₂'s share of materials.) TiO₂ is made by a global oligopoly (Chemours, Tronox, Kronos) and by a large and growing Chinese industry using the cheaper sulphate process, and India imports much of its need. Monomers and binders, the vinyl acetate monomer (VAM), butyl acrylate, styrene and 2-ethylhexyl acrylate that are polymerised into the emulsion holding paint together, are another INDICATIVE 25–35%. They are oil and gas derivatives, and India makes almost no VAM, which is why Asian Paints' plan to build a VAM plant at Dahej is strategically important. For Pidilite, VAM alone is 35–40% of the raw-material bill: white wood glue is essentially polyvinyl acetate.

Below the gross margin: the cost of the brand

Once materials are paid for, Asian Paints keeps about ₹43.6 of every ₹100. Employees cost about ₹7.5. Other expenses take about ₹15.5: advertising and promotion (an INDICATIVE ₹4–5), freight and distribution (an INDICATIVE ₹5), power, the depreciation-free running costs of tinting machines, royalties, rent and IT. Working capital: big stocks, paid for by suppliers

Paint companies hold large inventories: Asian Paints carried 118 days of inventory at FY26, Berger 144 and Kansai 119 (screener.in definitions, which measure inventory against cost of goods). Much of that is raw material, bought ahead to secure supply of imported pigment and monomers, plus finished bases spread across hundreds of depots. Two things keep working capital manageable. Dealers pay fast at the incumbents (Asian Paints' standard window is about five days, which is why Opus's 20-day credit was such an effective weapon), and suppliers are paid slowly (payable days of 79–106 across the group). The result is net working capital of just 18 days at Pidilite, 29 at Indigo, 38 at Asian Paints, 49 at Berger and 69 at Kansai, whose auto customers pay on industrial terms. Low working capital plus inexpensive plants is the reason Indian paint companies earn returns on capital far above their global peers even at similar margins.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 a paint company bills, more than half goes straight to oil-linked and mined inputs it cannot control. The brand's job is to keep the price per litre high enough that about ₹20 is left as operating profit after paying for people, advertising and trucks. When oil jumps, the materials slice swells within a quarter; the question is always whether competition lets the company raise prices fast enough to protect the ₹20.

From oligopoly to crowd: who owns the Indian wall

Three incumbents held about three-quarters of organised decorative paint in FY25. Nobody can tell you precisely what Asian Paints' share is today, and that uncertainty is itself informative.

For decades Indian decorative paint was a stable hierarchy: Asian Paints far ahead, Berger a clear second, Kansai Nerolac and the ICI/Akzo Nobel India business (Dulux) competing for third, and a long tail of regional brands and unorganised makers. Asian Paints became the largest paint company in India in 1967 and never gave up the position. Its lead was built on distribution rather than chemistry: it sold directly to dealers from the 1970s instead of through wholesalers, used computers for demand forecasting long before its rivals and rolled out tinting from the 1990s.

Imports and China: an input story, not a product story

Finished paint is barely traded across borders. It is bulky, low in value per kilogram and tinted locally, so imports of finished decorative paint are negligible. China matters as a supplier of inputs, above all TiO₂. The 2025 anti-dumping episode (section 8) showed how much: a duty of $460–681 a tonne on Chinese TiO₂ was imposed in May 2025 and quashed by the Calcutta High Court in September after the paint industry challenged it. Monomers come from the Middle East, East Asia and China, and Pidilite largely imports its VAM. That import dependence is why the 2026 West Asia war, which disrupted shipping and pushed up crude, hit Indian paint costs so quickly.

Control is not in question anywhere, which makes the entrant war a contest between family and corporate balance sheets rather than a takeover game.

Birla Opus did not win dealers with better paint. It won them by paying three to four times the margin, lending them machines for free and giving them more time to pay. Here is how that worked, what it cost the incumbents and where the fight stands in late 2026.

What it costs Grasim

Opus loses money, though Grasim does not say how much. A simple calculation shows why. A ₹6,600 crore run-rate at an INDICATIVE ₹200 a litre implies about 300–350 million litres of sales against 1,332 million litres of capacity: roughly 25% utilisation. The plants, depots, sales force and machines are largely fixed costs, so at a quarter of capacity they are spread thinly. Each litre also carries the entrant's trade terms: a 15% dealer margin and 10% free paint mean the same 20-litre tin that earns an incumbent a net ₹3,000 might earn Opus an ILLUSTRATIVE ₹2,600–2,700, with higher raw-material costs per litre because it buys at smaller scale. The second entrant: JSW

The JSW group took a different route. It launched JSW Paints in 2019, which reached only about 6,000 retailers by 2024, and then bought a ready-made premium brand: in June 2025 JSW Paints agreed to acquire up to 74.76% of Akzo Nobel India in a deal of about ₹13,000 crore including the open offer. No scheme to merge the unlisted JSW Paints into the listed company has been filed; how the two brands and dealer networks are combined is the key pending event.

The incumbents' responses, and a legal front

  • Volume over value. Asian Paints chose to defend volume: about 9% decorative volume growth in FY26 against 4.3% value growth, accepting lower revenue per litre to keep counters.
  • More advertising and trade spend. A&P up 15–20% at leading players in FY25; rebates and free material on incumbent packs.
  • A lower margin target. Asian Paints reset its EBITDA band to 18–20%, below the 21%+ it earned in FY24.
  • Machines and counters. Berger is installing about 10,000 tinting machines a year (2,100+ in Q1 FY27). Indigo kept adding dealers through the downturn.
  • Backward integration. Asian Paints started captive VAE production at Dahej on 17 September 2026 and is building a VAM plant, lowering its cost per litre.
  • The competition case. Grasim complained to the Competition Commission of India in late 2024. On 1 July 2025 the CCI found a prima facie case that Asian Paints had abused its dominance by pressuring dealers to avoid Opus, cutting their incentives or credit, discouraging Opus tinting machines and pressing suppliers, C&F agents and transporters not to work with the entrant. It ordered an investigation by its Director General. Asian Paints challenged the order in the Bombay High Court. We found no public outcome as of October 2026. Whatever the result, the case limits how hard the leader can lean on dealer exclusivity.

2026: from price cuts to trade spend

The character of the war changed in 2026. On Berger's Q1 FY27 call, management said that "a challenger has aligned its dealer prices closer to the industry", meaning the most visible discounting had eased. In the same breath it said that rebates to large dealers had risen and that 10% free material continued on many packs. The war moved from list prices, which everyone can see, to trade spend, which is harder to track and harder to reverse. The 2026 cost shock then pushed everyone, Opus included, to raise prices together, which temporarily made the price war look like price discipline. Section 7 explains why that reading is premature.

In plain terms

Opus did not need better paint; it needed dealers to say yes. Paying a small shop three or four times the usual margin, lending it a free machine and giving it three weeks to pay made the shop's owner two or three times richer, so tens of thousands said yes. The incumbents kept most of their counters, but only by sharing more of each sale with the dealer. That cost does not show up as an expense; it shows up as slower growth in revenue per litre.

In plain terms

The entrant war is now a test of patience. Grasim and JSW can fund losses for years, and the incumbents can afford rebates for years. Nobody gives up a dealer counter cheaply, so the extra margin paid to dealers is unlikely to come back to the brands soon, even if list prices look calm.

For two years Asian Paints sold more litres while earning less per litre. In Q1 FY27 the sign reversed. The reversal came from oil, not from a truce.

The most useful single statistic in Indian paint is the gap between volume growth and value growth in decorative paint. Volume growth measures how many more litres (or kilograms) a company sold than a year earlier; value growth measures how much more revenue those sales brought in. If a company sells 9% more litres and earns 4% more revenue, each litre is fetching about 5% less than a year ago: through price cuts, bigger dealer schemes, more free paint, or a shift toward cheaper products. We define the gap as volume growth minus value growth, so a positive gap means value is lagging volume.

Price, mix or both? Reading a positive gap

A positive gap is not purely a price cut. Asian Paints' volume figure counts wall putty, primers and economy emulsions alongside premium paint. Putty in particular is heavy and cheap: a company that sells more putty and economy products, or grows faster in small towns where customers buy cheaper ranges, will show volume growing faster than value even without cutting a single price. In FY25–26 all three forces were at work: list-price holds and selective cuts, bigger schemes and free material to match Opus, and a mix shift toward cheaper products as households traded down and rural demand outpaced urban. The company does not split the gap into these parts; anyone claiming a precise split is guessing.

The useful distinction is between mix that reflects customer choice and price that reflects competitive pressure. Mix effects reverse when incomes recover. Price effects reverse only if competitors stop paying for counters. In FY26 the evidence (A&P up 15–20% at the leaders, rebates up, the leader's margin band cut to 18–20%) points to a large price component.

Why the Q1 FY27 flip is cost-push, not a truce

Three facts show that the Q1 FY27 reversal was forced by costs rather than chosen from strength. First, Asian Paints' raw-material inflation was 25% in the quarter, and it took about 14% of cumulative price increases (6–8% in April, 3–5% from 5 May) to cover it: price followed cost. Second, margins held only because the company was still selling paint made from cheaper inventory: consolidated gross margin rose 91 basis points to 43.6% "led by low-cost raw material inventory", and management guided Q2 margins lower as the full cost arrives. Third, the price war's trade spend did not stop: Berger reported continuing rebates and free material from a challenger in the same quarter. When every player faces the same cost shock, everyone raises prices together and the volume–value gap flips without anyone becoming more disciplined. The test comes when crude falls: if prices are rolled back or schemes are expanded, the gap will turn positive again.

In plain terms

A truce would show up as prices holding after oil falls. That has not been tested yet.

In plain terms

When you see a "price-mix gap" number, ask two questions: what counts as volume (putty and primers inflate it), and did costs move? A negative gap during a cost spike is pass-through; a negative gap when costs are flat is pricing power. Only the second earns a higher valuation.

Crude, TiO₂ and the 2026 shock: when costs decide the year Deep dive

A year of cheap inputs funded the price war. A war in West Asia ended it. Who passed on costs, who could not, and who is building their way out of the problem.

Paint companies' profits swing with raw-material prices more than with demand. When monomers and pigments are cheap, gross margins widen and companies can fund advertising, schemes and price holds; when they spike, margins compress until price increases catch up, usually one or two quarters later. FY26 was a deflation year: Chinese oversupply kept TiO₂ soft, crude stayed below $80 a barrel and monomer prices were benign. Asian Paints' consolidated gross margin rose to 44.4% in Q3 FY26 and 44.8% in Q4. That cushion paid for the volume-over-value strategy.

The 2026 sequence

The war in West Asia in March–April 2026 reversed the cycle. Brent crude moved from below $80 to above $109 a barrel (about $107 on 2 April), shipping through the region was disrupted and petrochemical prices followed. Plastic packaging resins rose 30–50%. Pidilite reported 40–50% replacement-cost inflation in its raw materials at the start of FY27. Asian Paints measured 25% raw-material inflation in Q1 FY27. Crude eased to around $90 by early July, then rose again: Brent was back above $100 in October and around $110 in mid-October 2026. That second rise triggers a second round of price increases from 1 November.

Building out of the problem: backward integration

The structural response to volatile inputs is to make them. Asian Paints is furthest along. On 17 September 2026 it began commercial production of vinyl acetate ethylene (VAE) emulsion at Dahej in Gujarat, with capacity of 150,000 tonnes a year for captive use. Alongside, it is building a VAM plant of about 100,000 tonnes a year plus ethylene storage, still under construction. India makes almost no VAM, and VAM is the input that sets the cost of both emulsion paint and white glue; our chemical intermediates report covers the monomer chain. Asian Paints also has a white-cement plant at Fujairah in the UAE, reported to be commissioned, supplying putty. Grasim brings its own integration to Opus: it owns Birla White (white cement and putty) and a large chlor-alkali and chemicals business.

Integration changes the competitive equation in two ways. It lowers the integrated company's cost per litre in normal times, and it reduces how much of a spike it suffers in bad times. Both widen the gap between the leader and the smaller challengers, who buy every input at market prices. The benefit is not yet visible in Asian Paints' margins; the VAE plant started late in Q2 FY27 and VAM is due later.

In plain terms

A paint company is partly an oil-price trade. When oil and pigments are cheap, margins swell and companies can afford a price war; when oil spikes, margins shrink until prices catch up. In 2026 the big brands raised prices fast and kept margins (helped by cheap stock bought earlier); the slower movers, Berger and Kansai, took the hit first. The next quarter will show what margins look like when the cheap stock is gone.

The capacity glut: a quarter-full factory caps everyone's price

Opus built capacity for a market share it does not yet have. Until those plants fill, every litre of spare capacity is an incentive to keep paying dealers.

Asian Paints, the leader with roughly half of organised decorative paint, has 1,850 million litres across its plants. Why idle capacity keeps the war going

A factory running at a quarter of capacity behaves very differently from one running at three-quarters. Its fixed costs (plant depreciation, salaried staff, depots, sales force, the tinting machines already placed) are already committed, so every additional litre sold contributes almost its whole gross margin to covering them. The rational choice for an owner with idle capacity and a patient balance sheet is to keep buying volume (through dealer margins, free paint and credit) for as long as each extra litre covers its variable cost. That is the economic logic behind the persistence of trade spend in 2026, even after list prices converged.

The scale of the spare capacity matters. Organised decorative paint is an INDICATIVE 2,400–2,900 million litres a year (Asian Paints' estimated volume divided by its 47–56% share range). At 8–10% volume growth, the whole market adds roughly 200–290 million litres a year. Opus's idle capacity of about 1,000 million litres is therefore about four years of the entire industry's volume growth, before counting anyone else's new plants. Even if Opus took every incremental litre in India, it would need until around FY30 to fill its factories. It will not take every litre, so the overhang lasts longer.

Capacity cost also explains why entry was possible at all. On Opus's numbers, about ₹8,000 crore of plant spending bought 1,332 million litres: an INDICATIVE ₹6 crore per million litres of annual capacity. In plain terms

A glut like this ends slowly, through market growth, not suddenly.

Five business models under one label

"Paints and adhesives" groups a scale distribution machine, three kinds of challenger, a glue franchise and, outside the index, a conglomerate-funded entrant. Each makes money differently.

The six listed companies share a classification but not a business model. The differences matter more than the similarities, because each model responds differently to the same shock. A raw-material spike hurts a hybrid with auto customers (who reset prices slowly) more than a decorative brand that can reprice its dealers within weeks. An entrant war hurts decorative challengers more than the leader, and barely touches the glue maker. Five archetypes cover the sub-sector, including one, the conglomerate-funded entrant, that has no listed pure-play.

2. The decorative challengers

Three challengers each chose a different flank. Berger built regional strongholds in the east and north and a sizeable industrial and protective tail. Kansai Nerolac is two businesses. 4. The adhesives franchise

Pidilite makes glue, sealants, waterproofing and construction chemicals whose chemistry is generic (white glue is polyvinyl acetate), but whose brands (Fevicol, M-Seal, Fevikwik, Dr. Fixit, Roff) are close to category names. Its customers are tradesmen who use a few rupees' worth of product on a job worth thousands, where failure is costly and blame lands on them. That makes them loyal and price-insensitive. 5. The funded entrant

The fifth model is the one reshaping the others. A conglomerate with a strong balance sheet builds capacity ahead of demand, accepts years of losses and buys distribution with trade terms. It does not need to earn a return quickly; it needs to reach a scale at which the incumbents' advantages no longer apply. Eight decades, five turns: from a garage in Mumbai to a crowded counter

The industry's history is a long, profitable climb followed by two years of upheaval. The upheaval is the part that sets today's valuations.

Asian Paints began in 1942 as the Asian Oil & Paint Company in a Mumbai garage, founded by four friends (from the Choksey, Choksi, Dani and Vakil families) during wartime import restrictions. By 1967 it was India's largest paint company, and it has stayed there. Its distinctive choices came early: selling directly to dealers rather than through wholesalers, investing in computerised forecasting in the 1970s, and building a tinting-based colour system from the 1990s. Pidilite's story started in 1959, when Balvant Parekh launched Fevicol to replace the animal glues carpenters used; it listed in 1993. Berger, a brand of British origin, was bought from Vijay Mallya's UB group by the Dhingra brothers in 1991. Nerolac came under Japanese control and became Kansai Nerolac. ICI's Indian paint business became Akzo Nobel India when AkzoNobel bought ICI globally in 2008.

From roughly 2000 to 2020 the industry enjoyed an unusually profitable stability. Decorative volume grew at about 1.5–2 times real GDP, households traded up steadily, gross margins sat at 40–45%, and Asian Paints' return on capital ranged from the mid-20s to the high-30s (screener.in shows 38% as late as FY24). The market paid 60–80 times earnings for Asian Paints and 50 times or more for Berger and Pidilite. Disruption came only from the edges: JSW Paints launched in 2019 without denting anyone; Berger bought STP in construction chemicals the same year; Indigo listed in February 2021.

The five turns

Five turns define the modern history. The first, the 2017 GST cut, made branded paint cheaper relative to unorganised alternatives and accelerated formalisation. The second, the 2021 entry announcements, began a slow derating as investors priced the risk of competition. The third, FY25, was the shock year: Opus's launch and the incumbents' defensive spending took Asian Paints' revenue down 4.5% and its profit down by about a third. The fourth, FY26, was the volume-over-value year: soft input costs let the incumbents grow litres at high single digits while giving back price, and JSW bought Dulux. The fifth, from March 2026, is the cost shock that turned a price war into a pricing round. Whether that pricing survives the next fall in crude is the question that will define FY28.

Reading a paint company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything that happens to an Indian paint or adhesives company. What each one means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, how the six compare and where the definitions trip people up.

Paint companies publish less operating detail than banks or hospitals. There is no regulatory template, so each company chooses what to disclose, and the same word ("volume", "share", "dealers") can mean different things at different companies. The way to read the sector is to start from the equation that produces profit, identify which terms management controls, and then track the handful of indicators that reveal each term. This section does that in order: the equation, a worked example, one sub-section per KPI, an anatomy of one typical plant, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps and a ten-minute checklist for quarterly results.

The equation: what management controls and what it does not

For a decorative paint company, operating profit is the number of litres sold, multiplied by the net price received per litre, minus the cost of the materials in each litre, minus the fixed and semi-fixed costs of running the brand and the distribution system. Each term has its own drivers, and only some are in management's hands.

Dealer incentives sit inside "net realisation", not in costs, because most are netted from revenue. That is why a price war shows up first in value growth lagging volume growth. For adhesives, replace litres with tonnes and add influencer programmes; for industrial coatings, litres are set by the customer's production and realisation by contracts reset with a lag.

Two terms are largely outside management's control: the demand component of litres sold (repainting cycles, housing completions, the monsoon) and the cost of materials. The entrant war moved net realisation from the controlled column toward the contested one: each company still sets its list price, but what it actually receives per litre now depends on what rivals pay dealers. The 2026 oil shock showed the materials term's power: a 25% rise in input costs required roughly 14% of price just to stand still.

KPI 1: Decorative volume growth

What it is. The year-on-year change in the quantity of decorative paint sold, in litres or kilograms. It is the cleanest signal of demand and market share, because it strips out price changes. Formula: volume growth = (litres or kg sold this period ÷ litres or kg sold in the same period last year) − 1. Worked example: a company that sold 300 million litres in Q1 FY26 and 327 million in Q1 FY27 grew volume 9.0%.

What drives it. Up: repainting demand, housing completions, rural income, good weather, share gains, new dealers and machines, cheaper products that bring in new buyers. Down: a weak monsoon or floods, a late festival, share losses to entrants, high prices after a cost spike, channel destocking. Benchmarks: above 10% is good; 6–10% average; below 5% weak. Asian Paints guides 8–10% for FY27.

KPI 2: Value growth and the price-mix gap

What it is. Value growth is the change in decorative revenue; the price-mix gap is volume growth minus value growth. Formula: gap (percentage points) = volume growth − value growth. Positive means value lags volume (price cuts, schemes, downtrading, cheaper mix); negative means value is ahead (price increases, premiumisation). Worked example: volume +9%, value +4.3%: gap +4.7 points (Asian Paints FY26). Volume +9%, value +16.6%: gap −7.6 points (Q1 FY27).

What drives it. Up (wider positive gap): price cuts, rising dealer schemes and free material, growth in putty and economy ranges, faster rural growth. Down (toward negative): price increases, premium launches, a shift to emulsions from distemper. Benchmarks: zero or negative with stable share is good; 0–4 points average; above 5 points weak, signalling a price war or downtrading. Before the oil shock, ICICI Direct expected Asian Paints' gap to stay at 4–5 points.

KPI 3: Realisation growth (price plus mix)

What it is. The change in average revenue per litre, which splits into a price effect (what the company charges for the same product, net of discounts) and a mix effect (selling more expensive or cheaper products). Formula: realisation growth ≈ (1 + value growth) ÷ (1 + volume growth) − 1; price and mix are estimated by analysts from management commentary. Worked example: average realisation of ₹200 a litre rising 7% to ₹214: about ₹8 from price increases (4%) and ₹6 from mix (3%). For Asian Paints in Q1 FY27, 1.166 ÷ 1.090 − 1 = 7.0%, which ICICI Direct split into 4% price and 3% mix.

Drivers and benchmarks. Price rises with input-cost pass-through and falls with competition; mix rises with premiumisation and falls with putty, economy ranges and rural growth. Good: above 5% with volume above 8% (pricing without losing customers). Average: 0–5%. Weak: negative (price war or downtrading). Only Asian Paints gives enough data to calculate this regularly. The trap: companies quote "weighted price increase" (Asian Paints about 6.8–7% in Q1) that differs from the headline hikes announced (about 14% cumulatively), because hikes apply to only part of the range and part of the quarter.

KPI 4: Gross margin

What it is. The share of revenue left after raw materials, packaging and traded goods. It measures the brand's premium over its chemistry and the timing of price increases against costs. Formula: gross margin = 1 − (materials consumed + purchases of traded goods ± change in inventories) ÷ revenue from operations. Worked example: revenue ₹10,000 crore, materials ₹5,640 crore: gross margin 43.6%.

What drives it. Up: falling TiO₂ and monomer prices, price increases, premium mix, backward integration, low-cost inventory in the first quarter of a spike. Down: input inflation, delayed price increases, more free material (which raises materials cost without revenue), cheaper mix. Benchmarks: decorative paint ≥43% good, 38–43% average, below 38% weak; branded adhesives ≥53% good, 48–53% average, below 48% weak.

KPI 5: Advertising, promotion and trade spend

What it is. Advertising and promotion (A&P) is spending on media, sponsorship and brand campaigns; trade spend is money paid to dealers and painters through discounts, rebates, free material and loyalty schemes. Formula: A&P % = A&P expense ÷ revenue. Trade spend is rarely disclosed and is mostly deducted from revenue. Worked example: ₹4,500 crore of A&P on ₹1 lakh crore of revenue = 4.5%.

Drivers and benchmarks. A&P rises with launches, new entrants and festive campaigns; trade spend rises with dealer competition. An INDICATIVE benchmark: 3.5–5% of revenue with share gains is good; 5–6% average; above 7% without volume gains is weak. How the six compare: not disclosed quarterly by any member (n/a); the only sourced data point is that leading players raised A&P by 15–20% in absolute terms in FY25. The definition trap: trade spend, the real weapon of the entrant war, appears in revenue (as lower realisation), not in A&P. A company that cuts advertising but raises dealer schemes shows a better expense ratio and a worse volume–value gap.

KPI 6: EBITDA margin

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, as a share of revenue: the summary of everything above. Formula: EBITDA margin = (revenue − materials − employee costs − other expenses) ÷ revenue; other income is excluded. Worked example: ₹35,584 crore revenue and ₹6,700 crore EBITDA = 18.8% (Asian Paints FY26).

What drives it. Gross margin, operating leverage on fixed costs, A&P intensity and mix of industrial against decorative. Benchmarks: 20% or above is good; 15–20% average; below 13% weak. Guidance bands: Asian Paints 18–20% (reset from 21%+ in FY24), Berger 15–17%, Kansai 13–14%, Pidilite 20–24%.

KPI 7: Return on capital employed (ROCE)

What it is. Operating profit as a share of the capital (equity plus debt) tied up in the business. It is the best single measure of moat strength. Formula: ROCE = EBIT ÷ average (equity + borrowings). Worked example: EBIT of ₹5,500 crore on ₹21,000 crore of capital employed = 26.2%.

What drives it. Margin and capital turnover. Paint plants are cheap relative to sales (a full plant turns its capital roughly three times a year; see section 9) and working capital is low, so a good paint company earns high returns at moderate margins. Returns fall when margins compress, when new capacity sits idle and when cash piles up on the balance sheet. Benchmarks: above 25% good; 15–25% average; below 15% weak.

KPI 8: Working-capital days and the cash cycle

What it is. How many days of sales are tied up in inventory and receivables, net of what suppliers finance. Formula: cash conversion cycle = debtor days + inventory days − payable days; screener's "working-capital days" is (current assets − current liabilities) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: debtors 46 days + inventory 118 − payables 79 = a cash cycle of 85 days (Asian Paints FY26; screener shows 84 after rounding). Inventory days here are measured against cost of goods, so they look high.

What drives it. Dealer credit terms (five days at the leader; 20 days at Opus), raw-material stocking ahead of price increases, depot count, and the share of industrial customers (who pay on 60–90-day terms). Benchmarks: working-capital days below 30 good; 30–60 average; above 60 weak. Cash cycle below 70 days good; 70–90 average; above 90 weak.

KPI 11: Capacity utilisation

What it is. Litres produced as a share of installed capacity. It drives fixed-cost absorption and, at the industry level, pricing discipline. Formula: utilisation = litres produced (or sold) ÷ installed capacity. Worked example: 1,350 million litres sold ÷ 1,850 million litres of capacity ≈ 73% (Asian Paints, INDICATIVE).

Drivers and benchmarks. Utilisation rises with volume growth and falls with new plants. Above 75% is good; 55–75% average; below 50% weak (fixed costs uncovered). How the six compare: no member discloses it. Indicative estimates: Asian Paints ~70–75%; Opus ~25%; others n/a. The trap: capacity is quoted in litres, but sales include putty and other products measured in kilograms; capacity figures also differ between "installed" and "effective" (shift-adjusted) bases.

KPI 12: Underlying volume growth (Pidilite)

What it is. Pidilite's measure of tonnage growth in its adhesives, sealants and construction chemicals, reported separately for the consumer and bazaar (C&B) segment and for business-to-business (B2B). KPI 13: Industrial share of sales

What it is. The share of revenue from industrial coatings (auto OEM, refinish, protective, general industrial, coil, marine). It is a strategy choice, not a quality measure: industrial business lowers margins but adds stability and immunity from the dealer war. Formula: industrial revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: Asian Paints' two PPG joint ventures sold ₹1,025 crore in Q1 FY27 (₹651.9 crore PPGAP plus ₹372.9 crore APPPG), about a tenth of its ₹10,521 crore consolidated sales.

Drivers and benchmarks. Auto production, infrastructure and industrial capex drive growth. There is no "good" level: Kansai at ~45% has the steadiest volumes and the lowest margins; Asian Paints at ~15% and Berger at ~20% use industrial as ballast. Industrial growth outpaced decorative in Q1 FY27 at Kansai (double-digit), Asian Paints (APPPG +21.3%, PPGAP +13.5%) and JSW Dulux (double-digit). The trap: Asian Paints' JVs are consolidated, but Berger's auto joint venture with Nippon is not, so Berger's reported industrial share understates its exposure.

In plain terms

Selling paint to car factories is a steadier but thinner business than selling it to homeowners. The car maker tests your paint for a year, rarely switches supplier, and negotiates hard on price. A paint company with lots of industrial business will grow more evenly and earn less per rupee. That is the trade Kansai Nerolac has made, and why its valuation is half that of its decorative peers.

KPI 14: Net cash and its quality

What it is. Cash and liquid investments minus borrowings. Paint companies are almost all net-cash, so the question is how much and whether it is visible. Formula: net cash = cash + current and non-current investments − borrowings (including lease liabilities if the source includes them). Worked example: Asian Paints had about ₹7,087 crore of cash and ₹3,929 crore of borrowings at FY26 (ICICI Direct), net cash about ₹3,160 crore. Benchmarks: net cash is good; net debt above one year of EBITDA would be weak for this sector. How the six compare: Pidilite ~₹3,933 crore (screener proxy); Asian Paints ~₹3,160 crore; Kansai ~₹2,062 crore (proxy); Berger ₹1,198 crore at March 2026 and ₹1,424 crore at June (company figure; the screener proxy shows only ₹554 crore); Indigo ~₹279 crore; JSW Dulux n/a. The trap: screener's proxy of investments minus borrowings ignores bank balances; JSW Dulux shows zero investments, so the proceeds from its ₹2,143 crore sale (net of distributions) are invisible there. Borrowings include lease liabilities and subsidiary debt.

Anatomy of one typical decorative plant (ILLUSTRATIVE)

To see why utilisation and trade terms matter so much, consider one ILLUSTRATIVE decorative paint plant with 100 million litres a year of capacity, serving its region through depots and dealers. Plant capital is about ₹600 crore (₹6 crore per million litres, from Opus's numbers). We run it two ways. As an incumbent at 75% utilisation, it sells 75 million litres at ₹200 a litre net: ₹1,500 crore of revenue. Materials at 57% leave ₹645 crore of gross profit; staff, advertising, freight and overheads of ₹345 crore leave ₹300 crore of EBITDA, a 20% margin. Depreciation of about ₹40 crore leaves ₹260 crore of EBIT on about ₹756 crore of capital (the plant plus 38 days of working capital), a pre-tax return of about 34%.

As an entrant at 25% utilisation, the same plant sells 25 million litres at only ₹170 a litre net, because it pays dealers 15% and gives away 10% free paint: ₹425 crore of revenue. Its materials cost a higher 60% of revenue (smaller purchasing scale, and the free paint) and gross profit is ₹170 crore. But most of its overheads are fixed: staff, launch-level advertising, depots and machine servicing still cost about ₹275 crore. EBITDA is a loss of about ₹105 crore, and after depreciation a loss of ₹145 crore. Break-even EBITDA needs revenue of about ₹690 crore (₹275 crore of fixed costs ÷ a 40% gross margin), or about 40 million litres: 40% utilisation at entrant prices.

Definition traps, collected

  • "Volume" is not one thing. Asian Paints counts putty and low-priced items in decorative volume; Berger's volume is standalone and includes industrial; Pidilite's UVG is adhesives tonnage; Indigo gives category volumes only; JSW Dulux gives one figure on the retained business; Kansai gives none.
  • A negative price-mix gap in a cost spike is pass-through. It signals pricing power only if costs are flat or falling.
  • The first quarter of a cost spike flatters margins. Low-cost inventory is sold at new prices; the full cost arrives a quarter later (Q2 FY27 for this cycle).
  • Trade spend hides in revenue. Dealer rebates and free paint lower realisation, not expenses. A falling A&P ratio can coexist with a hotter price war.
  • Market share has no common basis. Total paints, organised decorative and "decorative incl. putty" give Asian Paints anywhere from 39% to 56%. Use one basis, and prefer direction to level.
  • JSW Dulux's FY26 is distorted twice. Revenue excludes powder coatings (sold), so it is −11.6% reported but about −0.5% like-for-like; profit of ₹1,974 crore includes a ₹1,846 crore exceptional gain, so underlying profit is about ₹390 crore.
  • Kansai's FY26 profit fall is not an operating collapse. FY24–25 profits included about ₹750 crore a year of one-off other income; underlying profit was roughly flat.
  • Standalone versus consolidated. Kansai's primer headline is standalone (₹7,739 crore); consolidated is ₹8,052 crore. Asian Paints' gross margin differs by up to a point between bases. Indigo's margin differs by about a point.
  • Pidilite's share count doubled. A 1:1 bonus in FY26 took shares to about 101.9 crore; all earlier per-share figures must be halved.
  • Net cash on screener understates cash where bank balances sit outside investments (Berger, JSW Dulux).
  • Headline price hikes overstate realised price. Asian Paints' ~14% cumulative hikes produced about 4% of price in Q1 FY27's realisation because of timing and product coverage.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading paint companies come from comparing numbers that look alike but are not: one company's "volume" with another's, one quarter's margin with a quarter that had cheaper stock, one source's market share with another's. Before comparing, ask what is counted, on which basis and in which part of the cost cycle.

How to read a paint company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, then volume growth (minute 1). Pull consolidated revenue growth and the company's own volume figure. Check what volume includes. Revenue far above volume in a cost spike means price; far below means schemes, cuts or cheaper mix.
  2. Compute the price-mix gap (minute 2). Volume minus value for decorative. Compare with the previous four quarters. A gap turning positive again after Q1 FY27 would signal the war resuming.
  3. Gross margin, year on year and quarter on quarter (minute 3). Note whether management cites low-cost inventory. A gross-margin fall of more than 200 basis points in a quarter means price increases lagged costs.
  4. Price increases taken and planned (minute 4). Headline hikes, the weighted realised effect and the next round (1 November 2026 for this cycle). Compare with raw-material inflation quoted on the call.
  5. EBITDA margin against the guided band (minute 5). Asian Paints 18–20%, Berger 15–17%, Kansai 13–14%, Pidilite 20–24%. A beat driven by inventory is not a beat.
  6. Other income and exceptional items (minute 6). Strip out land sales, disposal gains, impairments and labour-code charges before calculating profit growth. Red flag: profit growth well above EBITDA growth.
  7. Competition commentary (minute 7). Listen for dealer rebates, free material, "aligned" prices and share comments. Cross-check against Grasim's Opus revenue and share-gain disclosure in the same season.
  8. Distribution and capacity (minute 8). Dealer and machine additions, new plants, capex guidance. Rising debtor days alongside dealer additions means credit is being used to win counters.
  9. Segments and adjacencies (minute 9). Industrial, international, waterproofing, home décor, Pidilite's C&B against B2B. Which part grew, and at what margin?
  10. Guidance and the next quarter (minute 10). Volume and margin guidance, raw-material outlook (crude, TiO₂, VAM), festive timing. Red flags: a guidance cut paired with higher A&P, or "investing for growth" language without volume to show for it.

Who is winning: the leader regains ground, the glue maker never lost it

Three maps and a scorecard. The surprise of 2026 is not that the entrants grew, but that the largest incumbent is now taking share from the smaller ones.

The narrative of 2024–25 was simple: a deep-pocketed entrant was taking share from a complacent leader. The numbers for FY26 and Q1 FY27 complicate it. Opus kept growing, but Asian Paints grew faster than the other four listed paint companies combined in both periods (+4.9% against +0.6% in FY26; +17.9% against +9.4% in Q1 FY27). Asian Paints itself says its volume grew "ahead of small players". The cost shock helped the leader: when inputs spike, scale buyers secure supply, integrated producers suffer less and small makers struggle to reprice. The squeeze from Opus is now landing more on the second tier than on the first.

The leader: regaining relative ground

Asian Paints' Q1 FY27 combined 9% decorative volume growth, 16.6% value growth, a 20.6% EBITDA margin and a 40% rise in profit. It is the only paint company in the top-right quadrant of the growth–margin map. Its advantages in a cost shock are structural: scale purchasing, the fastest price pass-through in the industry, captive VAE since September and the deepest cash pile among the paint companies. Its weaknesses are also clear: share is still drifting to Opus (Opus gained 30 basis points in Q1 FY27), the competition investigation is unresolved and its multiple has de-rated from 60–80 times to 45.7. Section 16 examines its strategy in detail.

The glue maker: never in the fight

Pidilite sits furthest top-right on every map. It grew revenue 21.3% in Q1 FY27 with volume up 11.3%, held a 26.3% EBITDA margin and earns a 31% return on capital. Its main risk is not competition but valuation: at 57 times earnings and about 42 times EV/EBITDA, it is priced for the franchise to keep compounding at double digits. Section 17 explains why its moat survived the entrant war intact.

The second tier: squeezed between the leader and the entrants

Berger is holding share better than the leader did in FY25, but its FY26 revenue grew only 2.9%, its gross margin fell 300 basis points in Q1 FY27 and its EBITDA margin (15.4% in FY26) is three to five points below Asian Paints'. It is adding capacity (Panagar, Odisha) into the glut. Kansai Nerolac has the weakest decorative position and the lowest returns, but its auto business grew double digits and its valuation (22.8× P/E, about 13× EV/EBITDA) is the lowest in the group. JSW Dulux is growing volume 25% but at the lowest margin in the group (11.9% in Q1 FY27): a premium brand being used to buy share by a group with deep pockets. Asian Paints' counter-attack: volume, chemistry and a lower margin target Deep dive

The leader did not try to out-spend the entrant on every counter. It defended volume, accepted a lower margin band, built its own raw materials and leaned on businesses the war cannot reach.

When Opus launched, Asian Paints had three broad options: cut prices across the board to make entry uneconomic, defend only its most valuable counters and concede the rest, or defend volume everywhere while accepting a permanently lower margin. It chose a version of the third. FY26's numbers show the cost: decorative volume up about 9%, value up about 4.3%, an EBITDA margin of 18.8% against 21.4% in FY24, and a return on capital down from 38% to 26%. They also show the benefit: the leader grew faster than its four listed paint rivals combined, kept its counters and, by Q1 FY27, was outgrowing small players in the cost shock.

The new operating frame: 8–10% volume, 18–20% margin

Asian Paints' FY27 guidance is decorative volume growth of 8–10% and an EBITDA margin of 18–20%. The margin band is the important number: it is the leader's own estimate of what the new competitive structure allows, about two points below the 21%+ it earned in FY24. The Q1 FY27 margin of 20.6% sat above the band only because of low-cost inventory, and the company guided Q2 lower. ICICI Direct models an FY27 EBITDA margin of 18.3% on revenue of ₹41,534 crore, giving EBITDA of ₹7,616 crore and profit of ₹5,111 crore. Every point of margin on that revenue is worth about ₹415 crore of EBITDA; at roughly 30 times EV/EBITDA, about ₹12,500 crore of market value, or 5–6% of the company. That is why the market watches the band more closely than the growth guidance.

Four levers beyond the price list

  • Premiumisation and launches. New products contributed about 17% of sales. Mix added about 3 points to realisation in Q1 FY27.
  • Backward integration. The Dahej VAE plant (150,000 tonnes a year, captive) started on 17 September 2026; the VAM plant (about 100,000 tonnes a year) and ethylene storage are under construction. A white-cement plant at Fujairah in the UAE, reported commissioned, supplies putty. Together these lower cost per litre in a way no challenger can match.
  • Industrial coatings through PPG. PPGAP (auto OEM and refinish) sold ₹651.9 crore in Q1 FY27 (+13.5%, PBT ₹102.7 crore); APPPG (protective and industrial) ₹372.9 crore (+21.3%, PBT ₹25.8 crore). Industrial value growth exceeded 16%.
  • International. Overseas revenue was ₹936.5 crore in Q1 FY27 (+27.2%; +20.3% in constant currency), with profit before tax up 94.9% to ₹74.1 crore. Asia grew 24%, the Middle East 34%, Africa 33% and the South Pacific 17%.

Pidilite: the moat the war missed

Why a company that sells white glue earns more per rupee than the paint leader, passed a 12% price increase through a cost shock without losing volume, and trades at 57 times earnings.

Pidilite Industries is classified with the paint companies, but it lives in a different competitive world. Its flagship, Fevicol, holds about 70% of branded white-glue and adhesives sales in India (company and primer estimate); Dr. Fixit has about 35% or more of retail waterproofing; M-Seal, Fevikwik and Roff dominate their niches. The chemistry is generic (Fevicol is polyvinyl acetate; much of its raw-material bill is VAM), yet Pidilite earned a gross margin of 54.7% in FY26, an EBITDA margin of 24.1% and a return on capital of 31%, all the highest in the group.

Why the moat holds

Three features make adhesives harder to attack than paint. The first is the economics of the buyer. A carpenter uses perhaps a few hundred rupees of glue to build furniture worth tens of thousands; if a joint fails, his reputation suffers. The glue's price is trivial relative to the risk of switching, so the carpenter sticks with the brand he trusts. Paint, by contrast, is a large share of a painting job's cost, so dealers and painters are more responsive to margin and price. The second is the influencer network: Pidilite engages about 1.5 million carpenters, plumbers, masons and contractors through training and loyalty programmes built over decades. The third is distribution breadth without dependence: Fevicol and Fevikwik sell through more than 4 lakh outlets (hardware, stationery, general stores), no single one of which matters, so there is no equivalent of the tinting-machine counter for an entrant to buy.

Where the moat is being tested

Pidilite's frontier is construction chemicals, where the moat is thinner. Tile adhesives are used in only 25–30% of Indian tile installations, a large runway, but cement companies and tile makers are entering with their own products and distribution. Waterproofing, where Dr. Fixit leads, is now a priority for every paint company: Asian Paints (SmartCare), Berger (Home Shield and STP), Indigo (Apple Chemie) and JSW Dulux (Aquatech). The paint companies have the same painters and contractors on site and can bundle waterproofing with paint. Pidilite's own move into paint, Haisha (launched 2023 for rural and semi-urban markets), remains small and undisclosed; it should not be treated as a meaningful paint competitor.

Two housekeeping points matter for anyone using per-share data. Pidilite issued a 1:1 bonus in FY26, doubling its equity capital from ₹51 crore to ₹102 crore and its share count to about 101.9 crore; FY26 earnings of ₹2,471 crore therefore equal about ₹24.3 a share, and any per-share figure before FY26 must be halved for comparison. And the managing director changed in April 2025, when Sudhanshu Vats took over.

The risk to the shareholder is the price. At 57 times trailing earnings, about 42 times FY26 EV/EBITDA and 14 times book, the market assumes double-digit compounding for many years. Its market value fell only about 7% between the primer refresh in mid-2026 and 19 October, the smallest decline in the group, which says the market already treats it as the sub-sector's safe harbour.

In plain terms

A carpenter will not risk a ruined cupboard to save ₹20 on glue, so Fevicol can raise its price and keep its customers. A painter choosing between two similar emulsions, on the other hand, can be persuaded by a dealer offering a better margin. That difference in buyer psychology is why Birla Opus could attack paint but nobody has managed to attack Fevicol, and why investors pay more for each rupee of Pidilite's profit than for Asian Paints'.

The premium rests on two things global groups lack: a long volume runway (Indians use 4–5 kg of paint a year against 15–25 kg in rich countries) and much higher returns on capital at similar margins, thanks to cheap plants and low working capital. In plain terms

Investors still pay far more for Indian paint and glue companies than for their global equivalents, because Indian volumes have decades of growth left and Indian companies earn much higher returns on the money they invest. The Opus war cut into those returns, and the market has cut the premium. Pidilite, whose returns were untouched, kept most of its premium; Kansai, whose returns are lowest, trades close to a global coatings multiple.

Technology and disruption: capital, chemistry and the painter's phone

The disruption that actually happened was financial, not technological. The next ones may come from integration, digital painter networks and new product formats.

Paint is a mature technology, and none of the disruptions reshaping the Indian industry involves a breakthrough in chemistry. The live disruption is capital: an entrant used a conglomerate balance sheet to replicate a distribution moat. The next ones are more incremental but cumulatively important: who owns the raw materials, who owns the painter relationship, and which product formats grow fastest.

Capital as the disruptive technology

Birla Opus's machines are about 40% smaller than rivals', but their real innovation is commercial: they are free. Section 6 described how fat dealer margins, free paint and long credit replicated what took the incumbents decades. The lesson for the industry is uncomfortable: a distribution moat built on dealer economics can be bought by anyone willing to lose money for long enough. That is why the incumbents are now looking for moats that are harder to buy.

Integration as the counter

Making your own binders and pigments is the most durable cost advantage available. Asian Paints' VAE plant at Dahej started on 17 September 2026 and its VAM plant is under construction; its white-cement plant at Fujairah supplies putty. Grasim is integrated in chemicals and white cement through Birla White. Smaller challengers buy everything at market prices. Over time, integrated players can price lower and still earn more, which may decide the long-run shape of the industry more than dealer terms do. Our chemical intermediates report covers the monomers at stake.

Owning the painter

The painter, not the homeowner, usually chooses the brand. Every major company runs painter loyalty programmes, training and insurance schemes, increasingly through apps that track purchases and pay rewards directly. Several have gone further and sell painting as a service directly to homeowners (Asian Paints' safe-painting services and Beautiful Homes stores, Berger's express painting), using colour visualisers and online booking. A painter network that is digital is easier to switch than one built on personal relationships with a dealer, which could lower switching costs for entrants; but a company that owns the service relationship with the homeowner bypasses the dealer altogether. Neither effect is yet measurable in the results.

Products and formats

  • Waterproofing and construction chemicals are the fastest-growing adjacencies and the front line between Pidilite and the paint companies (Dr. Fixit against SmartCare, Home Shield, Apple Chemie, Aquatech).
  • Putty and textures grow faster than emulsions in volume and dilute the value-to-volume ratio, one of the reasons the price-mix gap is hard to read.
  • Water-based and low-VOC coatings are replacing solvent-based enamels and wood finishes slowly (e.g., JSW Dulux's Sadolin Hydro PU), driven by regulation and health awareness.
  • Powder coatings keep replacing liquid paint in industrial uses; JSW Dulux exited the segment when Akzo Nobel India sold its powder business.
  • Electric vehicles need the same body paint as petrol cars, so Kansai's auto business is unaffected; the bigger industrial risk is consolidation among global suppliers (AkzoNobel's planned combination with Axalta) reshaping who supplies Indian OEMs.
  • E-commerce and quick commerce matter for adhesives (Fevikwik), small packs and DIY, but tinting, bulk and painter-led buying keep decorative paint largely offline.

Bull vs bear: a truce or a pause?

The bull case says the cost shock forced a discipline that will outlast it. The bear case says the war has only changed weapons, and the glut guarantees it resumes.

Both cases start from the same facts: two years of entrant spending, a cost shock that let everyone raise prices together, a Q1 FY27 that looked like the old industry, and valuations well below their history. They differ on what happens when crude falls and on whether returns on capital settle in the mid-20s or keep sliding.

The bull case

  • The runway is intact. India uses 4–5 kg of paint a head against 15–25 kg in rich countries; repainting is 70–75% of demand and keeps compounding; paint outgrew everyday consumer goods in 2026.
  • Discipline is returning. A challenger "aligned" dealer prices in 2026; everyone, Opus included, raised prices in the shock; a second round is due on 1 November.
  • The leader is regaining ground. Asian Paints outgrew the other four paint members in FY26 and Q1 FY27, and cost spikes favour scale.
  • Integration lowers costs. Captive VAE is live and VAM is coming; Asian Paints' cost per litre should fall relative to every challenger's.
  • Valuations have already reset. Asian Paints at 45.7× against a historic 60–80×; Kansai at about 13× EV/EBITDA; the group 13–31% below 52-week highs.
  • Pidilite's moat is proven. Full pass-through of a 40–50% cost shock with double-digit volume growth.

The bear case

  • The war has changed weapons, not ended. Rebates to large dealers rose and 10% free paint continues; trade spend is harder to see and harder to reverse than list prices.
  • The glut guarantees pressure. Opus runs at an indicative quarter of capacity; filling it takes about four years of the whole industry's growth, while Berger and others add plants.
  • Q1 FY27 was flattered. Low-cost inventory lifted margins; Q2 is guided lower; when crude falls, price rollbacks and schemes can turn the volume–value gap positive again.
  • Returns are structurally lower. Asian Paints' ROCE fell from 38% to 26%, Berger's from 28% to 22%, and the leader's margin band was cut to 18–20%.
  • Regulatory and integration overhangs. The CCI investigation into Asian Paints is open; JSW's two paint businesses have not been combined; the TiO₂ duty could return.
  • Premiums still need growth. 45–57× for the two largest members assumes double-digit earnings growth for years.

What would settle the argument

Three observations over the next four to six quarters would decide it. First, the volume–value gap after crude eases: if Asian Paints' gap stays near zero while raw-material costs fall, the bull case is right that discipline returned; if it turns positive by several points again, the bear case is right. Second, Opus's trajectory: Grasim's quarterly share gains (90 basis points in Q4 FY26, 30 in Q1 FY27) and any first disclosure of losses or utilisation. Slowing gains with narrowing losses would point to a settling market; accelerating gains with more trade spend would point to a longer war. Third, the incumbents' returns on capital: the FY27 annual reports will show whether Asian Paints' ROCE stabilises in the mid-20s or keeps falling.

For the sub-sector as a whole, the cost shock has bought time rather than resolved anything. It is entirely possible for both cases to be partly right: decorative paint could settle as a five-player market with permanently lower returns than the old oligopoly, while still earning more than most consumer businesses, and while Pidilite continues to compound in its separate world.

In plain terms

The optimists think the oil shock taught every paint company, including the newcomers, that price wars are expensive, and that the industry will stay disciplined once costs fall. The pessimists think the newcomers still have empty factories to fill and will go back to buying dealers as soon as they can. Watch what happens to prices when oil falls: that is the moment that decides which side is right.
