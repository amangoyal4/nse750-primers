00

The one-page view: two companies, one label, five forces

India's listed "animal feed" universe is a shrimp-feed toll-keeper and a Godrej agri conglomerate. They share a raw-material cycle, little else.

Only two companies in the NSE 750 carry the FMCG · Animal Feed label, and they could hardly be more different. Avanti Feeds is a ₹6,066 crore shrimp business from Andhra Pradesh: it sells about half of all the shrimp feed used in India, processes and exports shrimp through a subsidiary it owns 60:40 with Thailand's Thai Union, and sits on roughly ₹2,450 crore of net cash. Godrej Agrovet is a ₹10,233 crore Godrej-group conglomerate that makes compound feed for cattle, poultry and fish, runs India's largest oil-palm milling business, sells branded crop-protection chemicals, owns the Jersey dairy brand through Creamline Dairy, and sells Real Good Chicken and Yummiez frozen foods. Only about 46% of Godrej Agrovet's revenue is feed, and in FY26 its oil-palm mills earned more segment profit than its feed mills.

Together the two booked revenue of ₹16,299 crore in FY26 (+8.8%), operating profit of ₹1,614 crore (a 9.9% margin) and consolidated profit after tax of ₹1,102 crore including minority interests. They were worth ₹19,618 crore on 30 October 2026, about 18 times attributable earnings. Those combined numbers are real sums of two company rows, but they hide the more useful fact: the two companies respond to different forces. Avanti is a near-pure bet on the economics of one crop (farmed whiteleg shrimp, Litopenaeus vannamei) in one state, sold largely into one export market. Godrej Agrovet is a portfolio of six agricultural businesses whose profits partly offset each other.

The five forces that shape this report

  • The protein-cost cycle. Feed is a spread business: a mill buys fishmeal, soybean meal, maize, wheat flour and rice bran, and sells a mixed pellet. When fishmeal doubled from about ₹100 to ₹230 a kilo in May–June 2026 and soymeal rose about 45%, Avanti's feed EBITDA per tonne halved year on year, from ₹13,987 to ₹6,094, in the very quarter its volume rose 17%. Sections 04, 10 and 13.
  • The US duty stack on Indian shrimp. An anti-dumping duty (5.53% for Avanti's export arm since September 2026), a countervailing duty (5.77% since December 2024) and a run of tariffs (IEEPA 50% at the peak, then Section 122, now Section 301 at 10%) are layered on top of each other. They decide what an American importer will pay for Indian shrimp, which decides what an Andhra farmer is paid, which decides how many ponds are stocked and how much feed is bought. Section 08.
  • Ecuador as the global price-setter. Ecuador ships more shrimp than India, at lower cost, and in January–August 2026 overtook India as the largest supplier to the United States (196,171 tonnes against 180,019, NOAA). Section 09.
  • Contested pricing power in shrimp feed. Avanti's share is the moat, but Andhra Pradesh has two 2020 statutes on feed and has set up a committee, with a Big-4 consultant, to design a feed-price formula indexed to fishmeal, soymeal and wheat. Section 11.
  • For Godrej Agrovet, portfolio mix and the Kataria reset. A new chief executive, Sunil Kataria, is investing in cattle feed, oil palm, crop care and branded foods, rebooting dairy, the Bangladesh joint venture and Astec LifeSciences, and putting shrimp feed under strategic review. Oil palm, now 40% of segment profit, is exposed to the 24 September 2026 cut in India's import duty on crude palm oil. Section 16.

Avanti earns 60% of the combined profit on 37% of the revenue, but is valued at less than half of the combined market capitalisation, because investors treat its FY26 profit as a cyclical peak and because about ₹2,450 crore of its value is simply cash. Q1 FY27, the April–June 2026 quarter reported in August, is the latest set of numbers for both companies. Combined revenue rose to ₹4,755 crore, operating profit fell to ₹346 crore (a 7.3% margin, from 11.5%) and profit after tax fell to ₹244 crore. The next results are close: Godrej Agrovet's board meets on 6 November 2026 and Avanti's on 10 November 2026, and Avanti's management has already said the September quarter would be "again a larger pain point". Astec LifeSciences, Godrej Agrovet's 67%-owned listed subsidiary, has reported its September quarter already: a loss of ₹37.4 crore on revenue of ₹72.6 crore.

In plain terms

A feed company is a kitchen that buys groceries and sells meals. It earns the gap between the price of the meal and the cost of the groceries, a few rupees on each kilo. When the groceries (fishmeal and soymeal) suddenly double in price, the kitchen can only raise its menu price as fast as its customers, shrimp and dairy farmers, can afford, and their ability to pay depends on what the world pays for their shrimp or milk. In 2026 the groceries got dearer at the same time as the United States made Indian shrimp harder to sell. Avanti felt both. Godrej Agrovet, with its oil-palm mills and crop-chemical business, felt them less.

The rest of the report follows the money. Sections 01–07 explain how the industry is built: the value chains, the size of the market, demand, costs, structure, the business models and the rules. Sections 08–12 are the forces: the US duty stack, Ecuador, the fishmeal shock, the contest over feed prices and the history of the cycle. Section 13 is the KPI masterclass, the toolkit for reading any feed or aquaculture company's numbers. Sections 14–18 are the companies: the peer table, who is winning, a deep dive on Godrej Agrovet's reset, a deep dive on Avanti's adjacencies, and a profile of each. The report closes with the global lens, technology, the bull and bear cases, what to watch and a glossary. Cross-references point to the sibling reports on agri products, sugar and ethanol, agrochemicals, packaged foods and edible oil, which cover the inputs and adjacent businesses in more depth.

Three conventions matter throughout. First, the period: every figure is labelled FY26 (April 2025 to March 2026), Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) or Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026, not yet reported by either member). Second, the profit basis: Avanti's ₹657 crore of FY26 profit includes the 40% of its export arm that belongs to Thai Union; the share that belongs to Avanti's own shareholders is ₹606.06 crore. Godrej Agrovet is the reverse: its attributable profit (₹472.78 crore) is higher than its consolidated profit (₹445 crore) because outside shareholders absorb part of Astec's losses. Third, "EBITDA": Avanti's own EBITDA figure includes about ₹200 crore of treasury income, so this report uses screener's operating profit, which excludes other income, in every table that adds or compares companies.

01

How feed makes money: four chains, one spread

Every rupee in this sub-sector starts with a farmer converting feed into protein. The listed companies sit at different points on four separate chains.

Animal feed is the largest single cost in producing any farmed protein. For an Andhra Pradesh shrimp farmer feed is 50–60% of the cost of a harvest; for a broiler chicken it is roughly 65–70%; for a crossbred dairy cow, compound feed is a smaller share because the animal also eats fodder and crop residue, but it is the part that most directly lifts milk yield. A feed mill buys agricultural by-products and protein meals, grinds, mixes, cooks and pellets them to a nutritional recipe, bags the result and sells it through dealers on credit. The mill does not take the farmer's biological risk (disease, weather, mortality) or the farmer's price risk (what the shrimp, milk or chicken will fetch), but both of those risks come back to it through the farmer's ability to pay.

The listed members sit on four chains. Avanti is on the shrimp chain at three points (seed, feed and processing). Godrej Agrovet is on the cattle-feed and poultry chains (feed, and in poultry also processed chicken), on the oil-palm chain (it buys fruit and mills it into crude palm oil), and on the crop-protection chain, which is not a feed business at all but sits inside the same company.

The shrimp chain is short in time and long in geography. A pond is stocked with post-larvae, fed for 100–150 days and harvested at 30–100 count (30 to 100 whole shrimp per kilo; lower count means bigger, more valuable shrimp). A 10-tonne harvest at a feed conversion ratio of 1.3–1.5 consumes 13–15 tonnes of feed, worth about ₹10–12 lakh at ₹78–80 a kilo (an illustrative calculation). The processor buys the harvest, peels, grades, cooks and freezes it, and ships it in reefer containers to an importer in New Jersey, a wholesaler in Shanghai or a retailer in Rotterdam. Avanti earns on the seed, the feed and the processing, but the farmer in between carries the risk that a white-spot outbreak wipes out the pond or that the export price falls before harvest.

The cattle and poultry chain is the opposite of shrimp in almost every respect. The feed is cheap (about ₹30 a kilo against ₹78 for shrimp feed) because it is made of bran, cakes and grain rather than fishmeal. The market is huge and fragmented, with cooperatives such as Amul's federations making large volumes alongside private compounders. Most broiler feed is made by the integrators who own the birds (Suguna, Venky's, IB Group, CP Foods), so a merchant like Godrej sells mainly to layer farms, independent growers and dairy farmers. And the farmer's decision to buy compound feed is discretionary: a dairy farmer can feed crop residue and green fodder and only buys concentrate when milk prices make the extra litres worth it. That is why milk procurement prices are the best single lead indicator of cattle-feed volume, and why Godrej Agrovet's cattle-feed volume rose 18% in FY26 and 15% in Q1 FY27 while milk prices were high.

Two points from the table drive the rest of the report. First, the profit pool in Indian aquaculture is feed, not processing: shrimp feed earns a higher margin on a larger revenue base, and over FY18–FY26 feed supplied 81–90% of Avanti's company EBITDA. Processing is the volume pool, and its margin depends on export prices, duties and the rupee. Second, the "animal feed" label understates how much of Godrej Agrovet is something else. Feed is its volume engine; oil palm and crop care are its profit engines in a good year; dairy and branded chicken are its brand-building, low-margin consumer arms. The segment profit of feed (₹347 crore) was below that of oil palm (₹384 crore) in FY26.

In plain terms

Avanti sells the shovels in a gold rush that happens in shrimp ponds: it supplies the feed, sometimes the baby shrimp, and then buys back some of the harvest to process and export. Godrej Agrovet sells shovels to dairy, egg and chicken farmers, mills palm fruit into oil, sells weedkillers, and owns a milk brand and a frozen-chicken brand. The word "feed" covers both companies, but the shrimp shovel is three times as expensive per kilo and, in a good year, five times as profitable per tonne.

02

Size and growth: 57.7 million tonnes, and one very profitable pond

India makes more compound feed than any country except China, the US and Brazil, but the listed money is concentrated in the smallest slice, shrimp feed.

The best available industry total comes from Alltech's annual global feed survey: India produced 57.73 million tonnes of compound feed in 2025, up 4.5% from 55.24 million tonnes in 2024. That ranks India second in Asia and fourth in the world, behind China (330.1 million tonnes), the United States (267.4 million) and Brazil (89.9 million); the world total was about 1.44 billion tonnes. "Compound feed" means industrially mixed feed. It counts the broiler feed that integrators make in their own mills, the cattle feed that Amul's federations make in theirs and the merchant feed that Godrej sells, but not the crop residue, green fodder and home-mixed concentrate that most Indian cattle still eat.

Growth is steady rather than spectacular. Compound feed grows about 4–6% a year in India, with dairy feed fastest (+6.8% in 2025) because crossbred cows, which respond most to concentrate, are the fastest-growing part of the herd. Shrimp feed grows with shrimp output, which has been roughly flat-to-up mid-single digits since the vannamei boom of the 2010s matured. Avanti's feed volume grew only 3.4% a year between FY18 and FY26 (4.30 to 5.62 lakh tonnes), which tells you that Avanti is a holder of share in a slow-growing pond rather than a growth company. Godrej Agrovet's animal-nutrition volume grew 11.6% in FY26, two to three times the market, which implies share gains in cattle feed.

Read together, the two charts show the shape of the cycle. Revenue grew at about 5% a year, roughly in line with nominal agricultural output. Profit grew at 13.5% a year, but not because the business got structurally better: Avanti's profit nearly tripled from the FY22 trough, when soymeal hit records and its feed margin fell to 7.0%, to the FY26 peak, when cheap Peruvian fishmeal and soymeal at a two-year low lifted it to 16.8%. The export side gives the second sense of scale. India exported 7,92,647 tonnes of frozen shrimp in FY26, worth US$5.62 billion, the largest single item in a seafood export basket that reached a record US$8.55 billion. That is about 2.7 times the 2,93,053 tonnes of fresh and frozen shrimp that the United States imported from India in calendar 2025 (NOAA), which is why the US matters so much but is not the whole story: in FY26 the US took 32% of India's shrimp by volume, China 21% and the EU 17%. Section 03 covers where those tonnes went and why; section 09 compares India with Ecuador, which exports even more.

In plain terms

India's feed industry is big by tonnes, about 58 million tonnes a year, but most of it is cheap cattle and chicken feed made by integrators and cooperatives that are not listed. The listed money sits in the small, expensive corner, shrimp feed, which is worth roughly ₹9,000 crore a year and where one company has about half the market. That is why a ₹6,000 crore company is a pure shrimp story and a ₹10,000 crore company needs oil palm and crop chemicals to round out its profit.

03

What pulls demand: shrimp prices abroad, milk prices at home

Feed demand is derived demand. Farmers buy more when what they sell is worth more, so the lead indicators are export shrimp prices, milk procurement prices and the monsoon.

Nobody buys feed for its own sake. A shrimp farmer stocks a pond when the expected farm-gate price at harvest covers seed, feed, power and labour with a margin for disease risk; a dairy farmer buys compound feed when the extra milk it produces is worth more than the feed. Demand for each species' feed therefore follows the price of its output, with a lag of one crop cycle for shrimp (three to five months) and of a few weeks for cattle feed. This section takes each end-market in turn.

Shrimp: an export crop priced in dollars

India eats very little of the shrimp it farms; the large majority is exported. The farm-gate price in Andhra Pradesh therefore follows export realisations, net of the duties that the importing country charges and the processor's margin. In FY26 India's frozen-shrimp exports went to the United States (32.3% of volume), China (21.4%), the EU (17.1%), South-East Asia (10.6%), Japan (5.1%) and the Middle East (3.8%), with the rest scattered. The direction of change was more telling than the shares: US volume fell 17.9% after the 50% tariff of August 2025, while China rose 24% and the EU 36%. India's exporters diverted product rather than stopping production, which is why total export volume still rose 6.9%.

Milk, eggs and chicken: domestic protein

For Godrej Agrovet's feed, the end-markets are domestic. The government's Basic Animal Husbandry Statistics 2025 put India's milk output at 247.87 million tonnes in 2024-25, up 3.58%, with per-capita availability of 485 grams a day. Milk from exotic and crossbred cattle grew fastest (+4.97%) against indigenous cattle (+3.51%) and buffalo (+2.45%); crossbred cows are the most feed-intensive, so that mix shift supports compound cattle feed. Egg output reached 149.11 billion (+4.44%), with Andhra Pradesh (18.37%), Tamil Nadu (15.63%) and Telangana (12.98%) the top producers; meat output was 10.50 million tonnes, of which poultry meat 5.18 million.

Three smaller pulls: pet food, edible oil and the monsoon

Pet food is the newest end-market. Avanti sold 300 tonnes of Avant Furst cat and dog food in FY26 (₹4.21 crore) and targets 800 tonnes in FY27; the urban Indian pet-food market is growing fast, though the market-size estimates in circulation are not well sourced. Edible-oil import substitution drives Godrej's oil palm: India imports more than half of its edible oil, and the National Mission on Edible Oils – Oil Palm (NMEO-OP) aims to raise domestic crude palm oil output, which was only 3.80 lakh tonnes in 2024-25. Finally, the monsoon drives crop protection and, indirectly, everything else: the 2026 monsoon ended 12.6% below normal after one of the driest Junes in more than a decade, and Godrej's Crop Care revenue fell 17% in Q1 FY27.

In plain terms

To forecast feed demand, look at what the farmer sells. If American and Chinese buyers pay more for Indian shrimp, Andhra farmers stock more ponds and buy more of Avanti's feed. If dairies pay more for milk, dairy farmers buy more of Godrej's cattle feed. The catch is that Godrej also owns a dairy, so the same high milk price that sells more feed squeezes its milk business.

One demand feature is specific to shrimp and matters for reading quarterly results: seasonality. Andhra Pradesh's first crop is stocked in January–March and harvested in April–July; the second is stocked in July–August and harvested in October–December. The April–June quarter is therefore Avanti's peak feed quarter, and October–March is the trough. Comparing Q1 FY27 with Q4 FY26 will always flatter volume; comparisons must be year on year. Godrej's cattle feed has milder seasonality, with demand typically softer in the flush season when green fodder is abundant.

Where ₹100 goes: a raw-material business with a thin rind of profit

Seventy to eighty rupees of every hundred a feed mill earns go straight back out to buy ingredients. Everything else in the business is a fight over the remaining twenty.

The cost structure of a feed mill is simple and unforgiving. Raw materials are 70–80% of revenue; conversion (power, labour, maintenance), packing, freight and dealer margins take most of the rest; and the operating profit is what is left, typically 5–8% for cattle and poultry feed and 7–17% for shrimp feed across the cycle. Depreciation is small because a feed mill is cheap to build relative to its revenue: an extrusion line for shrimp feed costs perhaps ₹10,000–15,000 per tonne of annual capacity (INDICATIVE), and a tonne of capacity can generate ₹50,000–60,000 of revenue a year at typical utilisation. The business is therefore not capital-intensive; it is raw-material-intensive and working-capital-intensive, because ingredients must be bought ahead of the season and dealers expect credit.

Six business models under one label

The two members combine six distinct archetypes, each with its own revenue driver, margin driver and capital need. Avanti is two of them; Godrej Agrovet is four.

Grouping the businesses by how they make money is the fastest way to understand why the two companies behave so differently in the same quarter. A specialist aqua-feed maker earns on tonnes times spread, in a concentrated market; a merchant multi-species feed maker earns the same way in a fragmented one; a shrimp processor earns on an export price net of duties and currency; an oil-palm mill earns on extraction efficiency inside a policy formula; a branded protein or dairy business earns on brand and mix; and an agrochemical brand earns on the monsoon and product pipeline.

The archetypes also explain how risk is correlated inside each company. Inside Avanti the correlation is positive in the tail: a US duty shock hits the export arm directly and, through lower farm-gate prices and delayed stocking, the feed arm a crop later. Both arms share the same farmers and the same state. In Q1 FY27 the two arms moved in opposite directions only because the shock was an input shock (fishmeal) rather than a demand shock: processing margins rose on better prices and currency while feed margins halved. Inside Godrej Agrovet the correlation is negative by design. High milk prices lift cattle feed and squeeze dairy; a dry monsoon hurts crop care but does little to oil palm in the short run; a high CPO price helps oil palm and does not hurt feed. In Q1 FY27 feed and oil palm grew profits while crop care and dairy fell, and the group's underlying EBITDA fell only 8%.

Capital intensity varies more than margins. Feed mills are cheap; an oil-palm business needs nurseries, extension staff and mills, and plantations take four years to first harvest and eight to peak yield, so returns on new zones lag investment by years. Processing needs plants and cold chain and its working capital swells in harvest peaks. Branded dairy and chicken need cold chain and advertising. That is why Godrej guides capex of ₹300–350 crore a year, mostly for oil palm, while Avanti says it foresees "no big capex in the next two years" apart from a ₹175 crore pet-food plant.

In plain terms

Avanti is two businesses that rise and fall together because they share the same shrimp farmers. Godrej Agrovet is four or five businesses chosen, partly by accident, so that when one has a bad year another often has a good one. Investors pay for that smoothing, but they also discount it, because the parts that earn the least (dairy, chicken, Astec) dilute the parts that earn the most (oil palm, crop care).

Oil palm is the policy file that matters for Godrej. NMEO-OP, launched in August 2021 with an outlay of ₹11,040 crore, allots factory zones to processors, subsidises planting (support raised from ₹12,000 to ₹29,000 a hectare) and obliges the processor to buy every bunch of fruit grown in its zone at a formula price linked to the international CPO price, with a viability price as a floor for growers. The mission targeted 6.5 lakh hectares of new planting and 11.2 lakh tonnes of domestic CPO by 2025-26; about 6.20 lakh hectares had been planted by November 2025, but CPO output was only 3.80 lakh tonnes in 2024-25 because young palms take years to yield. The edible oil report covers the duty and the domestic refining industry in more depth.

The remaining domestic rules are background rather than drivers. The Coastal Aquaculture Authority registers farms and hatcheries and, since its 2023 amendment, regulates broodstock and nucleus breeding centres. MPEDA registers exporters and runs residue monitoring, which matters for the EU, where India's shrimp exports grew 36% in FY26, because an EU residue finding can shut an exporter out. The Insecticides Act governs Crop Care's product registrations, and molecule bans can remove a product overnight; Godrej cited "regulatory disruptions" in Crop Care in FY26. A national feed act has been discussed for years but not enacted, so feed quality is governed by voluntary BIS standards and state acts such as Andhra Pradesh's.

In plain terms

Three governments shape this industry. Washington decides how much extra an American buyer pays for Indian shrimp. Andhra Pradesh, which hosts most of India's shrimp ponds, has laws that let it intervene in feed prices and is now drawing up a formula. New Delhi sets the duties that decide the price of palm oil, maize and soymeal, and the tax that pushes Indian fishmeal abroad. A feed company's profit can change because of any of the three.

Four duties, one shrimp Deep dive

An anti-dumping duty, a countervailing duty and two generations of tariffs sit on top of each other. Their sum fell from about 57% to about 21% in a year, but the part that never goes away keeps ratcheting up.

A kilo of frozen Indian shrimp entering the United States in October 2026 pays four separate charges that were set by three different processes, at different times, for different reasons. Understanding them one by one is the only way to read the export leg of Avanti, and through it the farm-gate price that decides how much shrimp feed Andhra Pradesh buys. The headline tariff gets the press coverage; the anti-dumping and countervailing duties do more lasting damage, because they never expire and are reset upward by annual reviews.

The anti-dumping duty is the oldest layer. The United States has had an anti-dumping order on Indian frozen warmwater shrimp since February 2005, and every year US Commerce can review the previous twelve months of entries and reset the cash deposit that importers must pay on future shipments. For two decades the rate for most Indian exporters was low. It has now risen in two consecutive reviews: from about 1.35% to 3.76% in February 2026 and to 5.53% in September 2026 for the "non-examined" exporters, a group that includes Avanti Frozen Foods. The rate for the two mandatory respondents, Devi Sea Foods and Sandhya Aqua, was 4.04% and 7.01%. The cash deposit is a deposit, not a final duty: the final amount for each year's entries is set only when that year's review ends, which is why Avanti carries ₹17.70 crore of contingent liability for US anti-dumping duty on entries from February 2023 to January 2024.

The countervailing duty is new, and the first point to fix in the record. A 2013 US countervailing-duty case against Indian shrimp ended with a negative injury finding by the International Trade Commission and no order. The current CVD dates from the October 2023 petitions against Ecuador, India, Indonesia and Vietnam, a final determination on 28 October 2024 and an order on 26 December 2024; the "all others" rate that applies to Avanti is 5.77%. Commerce found countervailable subsidies including India's duty-drawback scheme. Unlike a tariff, a CVD order lasts until it is revoked in a sunset review, normally after at least five years.

The tariffs are the volatile layer. In 2025 the United States imposed IEEPA "reciprocal" tariffs (10% from April, 25% on India from 7 August) and then a 25% penalty for India's purchases of Russian oil from 27 August 2025, taking the tariff to 50%. In February 2026 India and the US announced a deal at an 18% reciprocal rate, but no Federal Register document implements an 18% rate on India; the Russia penalty was removed from 7 February and, after the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs 6–3 on 20 February, collection stopped on 24 February. A 10% Section 122 surcharge applied from that day for 150 days, and from 24 July 2026 a 10% Section 301 tariff, imposed after a forced-labour investigation, replaced it. Ecuador, Indonesia, Mexico and Canada sit at the same 10%, so for the first time since early 2025 the main shrimp suppliers face tariff parity.

How much of Avanti is exposed?

The direct exposure is smaller than the headlines imply. Avanti's processed-shrimp segment was ₹1,689.5 crore in FY26, 28% of consolidated revenue. The annual report's geographic note puts US revenue at ₹1,050.7 crore, 17.3% of group revenue and 66.5% of non-India revenue, up 42% from ₹738.1 crore in FY25. Only 60% of that belongs to Avanti's shareholders, because Thai Union owns 40% of Avanti Frozen Foods. The indirect exposure, through farm-gate prices and feed demand, is larger and harder to measure: Avanti's feed business sells to the same farmers who supply every Indian exporter, and the United States took 32% of India's frozen-shrimp volume in FY26.

The rise in US revenue in a year of 50% tariffs looks paradoxical until the Incoterm is understood. Avanti Frozen Foods is the US importer of record on its American sales and sells duty-paid (DDP): the price it charges its US customer includes the duty, which it pays to US Customs and then recovers in the price. In FY26 it front-loaded shipments before and after the tariff and absorbed part of it; when IEEPA collection ended in February 2026, its duty-paid selling price came down, which is why management said processing ASP fell "following the withdrawal of reciprocal tariffs effective from 24 February" while processing profit rose. Most Indian exporters sell FOB instead, leaving the importer to pay the duty and bid a lower price.

The refund that is real but frozen

Because Avanti Frozen Foods was the importer of record, it paid IEEPA tariffs itself, and it has claimed refunds it estimates at US$15–20 million, about ₹142–190 crore at ₹94.9 to the dollar, of which 60% (₹85–114 crore) would belong to Avanti's shareholders. US Customs opened a refund portal on 20 April 2026, but the entries also sit inside AD and CVD reviews whose liquidation is suspended. In the words of the subsidiary's CFO on 27 August 2026: "Unless the suspension is lifted, the CBP will not process the reciprocal tariffs." Management expects the money "over the next 12–18 months". The refund is not recognised in the FY26 accounts. When it comes, it is likely to arrive alongside the final anti-dumping true-up for the same entries, part of which is the ₹17.70 crore contingent liability, so the two will partly net.

In plain terms

American charges on Indian shrimp come in two kinds. Tariffs are political and can vanish: the 50% of late 2025 is gone, and today all major suppliers pay the same 10%. Anti-dumping and anti-subsidy duties are legal and stick around: India's add up to about 11%, they go up a little every year, and Ecuador's main exporters pay much less. Those sticky duties, not the tariff headlines, are what keep Indian shrimp slightly more expensive than Ecuadorian shrimp in the US, and that difference flows back to the price an Andhra farmer gets and the feed he buys.

In plain terms

Avanti's export arm acted as its own US importer, so it paid the big 2025 tariffs itself and is now owed roughly ₹140–190 crore back. That money is real but stuck in a US customs queue behind the same anti-dumping reviews that could also send Avanti a bill. Treat it as a possible one-off in FY28, not as earnings.

The price-setter on the equator: Ecuador

Ecuador farms more shrimp than India, more cheaply, and sells most of it head-on to China. In 2026 it also became America's largest supplier. Indian farm-gate prices are set in its shadow.

Global shrimp prices are set by the largest low-cost supplier, and that is Ecuador. Its exports in 2025 were about 1.39 million tonnes worth about US$7.47 billion (INDICATIVE: trade-press figures, not verified from a primary source), up 15% in volume and 23% in value, roughly 1.75 times India's 7.93 lakh tonnes of frozen-shrimp exports. Ecuador's farms are mostly extensive and semi-intensive ponds with low stocking density, domesticated and disease-tolerant genetic lines, and a short supply chain to its Pacific ports. About half of its exports by value go to China, mostly as head-on shell-on shrimp; the US and Europe take most of the rest. India, by contrast, farms intensively at higher densities, relies on imported broodstock, and sells mostly peeled and value-added shrimp, above all to the United States.

The two countries compete head-to-head in the US market for peeled and raw frozen shrimp, and that competition is where Indian prices are decided. When the US raised the tariff on Indian goods to 50% in August 2025 while Ecuador paid 15%, American buyers switched. NOAA data show Ecuador's shipments to the US rising 23.3% in calendar 2025 to 230,448 tonnes while India's were flat at 293,053 tonnes. In January–August 2026 the gap closed and then reversed: Ecuador shipped 196,171 tonnes (+33.1%) and India 180,019 tonnes (−17.6%), and Ecuador overtook India as the largest supplier of shrimp to the United States.

Why Ecuador keeps winning even at tariff parity

Since 24 July 2026 both countries pay the same 10% Section 301 tariff, yet the duty gap has not closed. The 2024 trade cases produced countervailing-duty orders on Ecuador, India and Vietnam but no anti-dumping order on Ecuador. Ecuador's CVD rates from an expedited review finalised in August and amended in September 2026 are 2.23% for Nirsa/Procesadora Posorja, 2.25% for Propemar and 15.18% for Empacadora del Pacífico. That ~9-point structural gap was hidden while the IEEPA tariffs dominated; it is now the main duty difference between the two suppliers, and it can only be narrowed through the AD and CVD reviews.

Ecuador's lead is not purely about duties. Its cost advantage rests on cheap land, extensive farming, domesticated genetics and scale, and on government support that the US CVD case examined. A cost spike therefore shows up in two or three quarters of compressed margin; price increases recover part of it, and a fall in input prices then widens the margin again, as in FY24–FY26.

The asymmetry between Avanti's pricing in 2025 and 2026 shows how the frictions work. In April 2025, with fishmeal and soymeal falling, Avanti cut its feed price by ₹4 a kilo to defend share, and still earned record margins because input costs fell further. In February 2026, as costs started to rise, it took a first increase that had little effect in the March quarter. It raised prices again by about 10% on 19 June 2026, after fishmeal had already doubled. A 10% rise on ₹80 is about ₹8 a kilo; the cost increase at spot was several times that. A farmer whose shrimp sells at a price set by Ecuador and the US duty stack cannot absorb a 30% feed increase in one season, and the Andhra Pradesh government, with its 2020 feed statutes and its new price committee, would not let him be asked to. Section 11 covers that contest.

Formulation is the other defence. Avanti says it is "working on the quality aspect along with the formulation" to "mitigate the usage of the raw material" without hurting the feed conversion ratio. Alternatives to fishmeal include poultry by-product meal, krill and squid meal, insect meal, single-cell protein and fermented soy; global leaders such as Skretting and BioMar already run low-fishmeal shrimp diets. Every substitution has to be proven in the pond, because a worse FCR costs the farmer more than the feed saves. The industry's other lever is policy: Avanti, the Feed Manufacturers Association and CLFMA have asked the Centre to regulate fishmeal exports and allow limited soymeal imports. Management expects relief "upon arrival of fresh crops", meaning the October–December soybean and kharif maize harvests, and from a monsoon that, though 12.6% below normal overall, eased fears of the worst El Niño effects.

Godrej Agrovet's experience in the same quarter is a useful contrast. Its animal-nutrition result rose 30% in Q1 FY27 and its result per tonne was ₹2,088 (₹2,536 on the company's underlying basis), within its own guidance of ₹2,050–2,200. The difference is the recipe. Cattle feed uses no fishmeal and relatively little soymeal; its main inputs are rice bran, oilcakes and maize, and management passed on about 60–70% of the maize increase. Cheaper feed also means the farmer's affordability ceiling is further away: a ₹2 a kilo increase on ₹30 cattle feed is a smaller decision for a dairy farmer with high milk prices than a ₹16 a kilo increase on ₹80 shrimp feed is for a shrimp farmer facing American duties.

In plain terms

In mid-2026 the two main proteins in shrimp feed, fishmeal and soymeal, roughly doubled and rose by half. Avanti had cheaper stock in its warehouses and raised its price by about a tenth, which kept it profitable in the June quarter but at half the usual margin. With spot prices higher still and farmers unable to pay much more, the September quarter will probably be weaker before new soybean crops and a better fishmeal season bring costs down.

In plain terms

The right way to value Avanti's feed business is not on its best year or its worst quarter but on the average profit it makes per tonne through a full cycle, about ₹9,500. FY26 was roughly 40% above that; the June 2026 quarter about 35% below. The stock price fell from ₹1,594 to ₹659 as the market moved from pricing the peak to pricing the trough.

Who sets the price of shrimp feed?

Avanti's half of the market is the moat. Andhra Pradesh's 2020 feed statutes and a new price-formula committee are the drawbridge.

In most concentrated markets the leader sets the price and the followers match it. Shrimp feed in India works that way in practice: Avanti's price list moves first, and CP Aquaculture, Growel and the multinationals follow within weeks. But the leader's pricing power is bounded on two sides. Below, it cannot let prices fall so far that rivals with deeper multinational parents take share at the margin, as they tried to in 2024–25. Above, it cannot push prices so high that farmers stop stocking ponds, because feed is 50–60% of the farmer's cost of production and his selling price is set by Ecuador and the US duty stack. In 2026 a third boundary became explicit: the state.

A formula linking feed prices to fishmeal, soymeal and wheat would cut both ways. Avanti's response so far has been conciliatory. Its joint managing director said the industry must balance "the affordability and sustainability of the farmer" and that stakeholders were "acceptable to that kind of a formulation". That is the right public posture for a company whose customers are also voters in the state that hosts its plants, but it also signals that management expects some form of indexation to happen. ICRA, in its July 2026 rationale on Godrej Agrovet, separately named "price capping mechanisms" as a regulatory risk for animal nutrition, which suggests the rating agencies see the AP approach as a template that could travel.

For Godrej Agrovet the formula matters much less. Its shrimp feed is small and under strategic review, and its cattle feed, though also covered by the AP Animal Feed Act, is sold across fourteen states in a fragmented market where cooperatives set reference prices. If anything, a formula that lifts shrimp-feed pricing discipline could make a sale of Godrej's shrimp business easier. For the rest of the shrimp-feed industry, a formula would probably entrench the leader: a regulated spread rewards the lowest-cost, highest-volume producer and removes price as a weapon for challengers.

In plain terms

Avanti can usually choose its price because it sells half of India's shrimp feed. But its customers are Andhra Pradesh farmers, and the state government has laws on feed and is designing a formula that would tie feed prices to the cost of fishmeal, soymeal and wheat. If the formula lets prices rise quickly when ingredients spike, it helps in years like 2026. If it also forces prices down when ingredients are cheap, it takes away the bumper profits of years like FY26. Which way it lands depends on numbers that have not been published.

Thirty years, five cycles

From monodon and white spot, through the vannamei boom, two input squeezes and a tariff war, to the protein shock of 2026. Each cycle was driven by inputs or trade, never by demand alone.

The history of Indian shrimp feed is a history of three variables: what the farmer grows, what the world pays for it and what the feed costs to make. Godrej Agrovet's history is a history of portfolio building, then pruning. Laying the two side by side shows how rarely the sub-sector's profits have been set by domestic demand, and how often by Peru, Washington and the soybean crop.

The fifth cycle is the present one, and it has two halves. FY25–FY26 was the margin peak: fishmeal was cheap after a good Peruvian catch, soymeal fell for two and a half years, and Avanti earned 15.3% and then 16.8% in feed despite cutting its price. Then came a trade shock and an input shock in quick succession: the US tariff on India hit 50% in August 2025, and fishmeal and soymeal spiked in May–June 2026. The cycle can be written in one line. Indian shrimp-feed margins equal the cost of fishmeal and soymeal subtracted from what Andhra farmers can bear, and what they can bear is set by US and Chinese shrimp prices net of duties. The FY22 trough and the FY26 peak were both input-driven. FY27 combines an input spike with a US duty regime that has only just settled at 21%, and a state government that wants a say in the price. For Godrej Agrovet the line is different: its returns are set by the mix of its businesses, and the history shows that its feed spread is thin and steady, its oil palm is lumpy and policy-dependent, and its acquisitions of consumer businesses (dairy, chicken) and an agrochemical company have so far earned less than its cost of capital.

In plain terms

Every big swing in Avanti's profits in thirty years came from either the cost of feed ingredients or the price of shrimp abroad, never from Indian farmers suddenly wanting more feed. The boom years followed a new shrimp species and cheap inputs; the bad years followed disease, oversupply, expensive soymeal or American tariffs. FY26 was a top-of-cycle year. History says the next few quarters look more like FY22 than FY26, until ingredient prices turn.

The other businesses in the sub-sector have equations of the same shape. 5. Raw-material cost per kilo: the ingredient basket

What it is. The cost of the ingredients in a kilo of feed, and the prices of the main ingredients. Formula. Σ (inclusion rate × ingredient price), or, from the accounts, cost of materials consumed ÷ tonnes produced. Godrej does not disclose an ingredient basket; maize was ₹24–28 a kilo in mid-2026 (Agmarknet and trade sources). Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE). With 25% fishmeal, 30% soymeal, 20% wheat flour and 25% other ingredients, the basket cost about ₹55,900 a tonne in FY26 (72% of the ₹77,900 realisation) and ₹72,250 in Q1 FY27 (89% of ₹80,800), using the prices in section 10.

World fishmeal (Peru), Indian soybean crops and import rules, wheat policy, maize demand from ethanol, the rupee, and buying skill: a mill that bought forward before a spike, as Avanti did, consumes cheaper stock for a quarter or two. 6. Capacity utilisation

What it is. How much of a plant's rated capacity is used. Formula. Production ÷ installed capacity (annualise quarterly figures). Worked example. Avanti produced 5,67,986 tonnes of feed in FY26 against capacity of 7,75,000 tonnes at five sites: 73%. In Q1 FY27 it produced 1,76,316 tonnes, 91% on an annualised basis, because April–June is the peak season. Its processing plants sold 16,976 tonnes of shrimp against a stated 26,000–28,000 tonnes of capacity, about 61% (management says the stated figure overstates usable capacity because processing is seasonal). Godrej does not disclose feed capacity; it has 14 animal-nutrition plants and 2 aqua-feed plants, and the reset has identified "select plants" for closure.

What drives it. Demand, seasonality and capacity additions. Benchmark. Good above 80%, average 65–80%, weak below 60%. Why it matters. At 73% Avanti can absorb about 2 lakh tonnes of extra demand without capital spending, which is why it put feed expansion on hold in FY26 and guides "no big capex in the next two years". Low utilisation is not a crisis in a business where plants are cheap, but it caps return on capital. The trap. Seasonal businesses cannot run at 100% annual utilisation; a shrimp-feed plant that runs flat out in April–June may sit at 50% in January–March. Compare utilisation with the company's own history, not with an abstract ceiling.

7. Market share, and its denominator

What it is. The company's tonnes as a share of the market's. Formula. Own tonnes ÷ market tonnes. Worked example. Avanti's 5.62 lakh tonnes ÷ 11–12 lakh tonnes of shrimp-feed consumption (management's 2026 estimate) = 47–51%; the annual report's own estimate is 51–53%. Godrej's 16.47 lakh tonnes ÷ 577 lakh tonnes of Indian compound feed (Alltech) = about 2.9% (INDICATIVE). What drives it. Dealer reach, product performance in the pond (feed conversion), credit terms and price. Benchmark. In a concentrated market, a share above 40% usually confers price leadership; below 10% in a fragmented market means price-taking. The trap. The denominator is an estimate. India's shrimp output is 8–9 lakh tonnes on Avanti's basis but about 10.8 lakh tonnes in MPEDA's broader 2023-24 series (snippet-sourced), which would imply a larger feed market and a lower Avanti share. Always state the basis, and watch the direction of change rather than the level.

8. Shrimp processing volume and export realisation

What it is. Tonnes of processed shrimp sold, and the average rupee and dollar price per kilo. Formula. Processing segment revenue ÷ tonnes. Worked example. Avanti Frozen Foods sold 16,976 tonnes in FY26 (+20%) for ₹1,689.5 crore: ₹995 a kilo, about US$11.3 at ₹88 to the dollar. In Q1 FY27 it sold 3,520 tonnes (−16.6%) for ₹333.7 crore: ₹948 a kilo. Management targets about 19,000 tonnes in FY27. Processing EBITDA was 12.5% in FY26 and 16.0% in Q1 FY27, helped by better prices, currency and other income.

What drives it. The US and Chinese import price for the relevant count and product form, the duty stack and who pays it (DDP or FOB), the rupee (₹94.9 to the dollar in October 2026 against an FY26 average of ₹87–88), raw-shrimp cost and processing yield. Benchmark. Processing EBITDA: good above 14%, average 9–14%, weak below 8%. The trap. Realisation per kilo mixes price, product (cooked and value-added sell for more) and Incoterm: a DDP price includes US duty, so when IEEPA ended in February 2026, Avanti's duty-paid selling price fell even though its net economics improved. Read processing realisation together with the duty regime and the margin, never alone.

9. The US duty stack, as a KPI

What it is. The total of US anti-dumping duty, countervailing duty and tariffs payable on the company's shrimp, as a percentage of customs value. Formula. AD cash-deposit rate + CVD rate + applicable tariff. Worked example. Avanti Frozen Foods since 4 September 2026: 5.53% + 5.77% + 10% = 21.3%; at the August 2025 peak, about 57% (INDICATIVE: the pre-2026 AD rate is snippet-sourced). A low-rate Ecuadorian exporter pays about 12.2%. What drives it. US annual reviews (AD), sunset and changed-circumstance reviews (CVD), trade policy (tariffs). Benchmark. Good below 10%, average 10–25%, weak above 25%. The trap. Cash-deposit rates are not final duties; the final liability for each year's entries is set when that year's review ends, sometimes years later, and can be higher or lower. And who bears the duty depends on the Incoterm: on FOB sales the US importer pays and bids less, on DDP sales the exporter pays and, if the tariff is later struck down, may be owed a refund, as Avanti is.

12. Net working-capital days

What it is. How many days of sales are tied up in inventory and receivables, net of payables. Formula. Average (inventory + receivables − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. Godrej's average net working capital was ₹686 crore in FY26, 25 days of sales, down from ₹988 crore (39 days) in FY25 and ₹1,172 crore (45 days) in FY24; in Q1 FY27 it was ₹665 crore, 21 days (54 days in Q1 FY25). Avanti's screener working-capital days were 38 in FY26 (57 in FY22); its inventory alone was 76–79 days, with receivables of 8–9 days and payables of 19–20.

What drives it. Ingredient buying (feed makers stock up before price rises and seasons), dealer credit, supplier terms. Benchmark. For an agri conglomerate: good below 30 days, average 30–50, weak above 50; for feed inventory: good below 60 days, average 60–80, weak above 80. The trap, and it is a big one here. Godrej's trade payables rose 87% in FY26 to ₹1,651 crore, and ICRA attributes the fall in working-capital intensity to "a growing prevalence of acceptances", that is, supplier finance, in which a bank pays the supplier early and Godrej pays the bank later. Godrej's own "underlying" animal-nutrition result excludes the cost of that financing. Part of the celebrated fall from 45 to 21 days, and part of the ₹1,281 crore of FY26 operating cash flow, is therefore financing rather than a structural improvement. If the acceptances were treated as debt, net debt would be higher.

13. Return on capital employed

What it is. Operating profit as a percentage of the capital used to earn it. Formula. EBIT ÷ average capital employed (equity + debt, or net fixed assets + working capital at unit level). Worked example. Avanti: 24.4% on screener's basis and 22.3% on the company's for FY26. Godrej: 19.2% on screener's basis and 20% on the company's (EBIT ÷ average equity plus borrowings), up from 16% in FY25 and 13% in FY24. What drives it. Margin and asset turnover. Feed mills turn their assets quickly, so a modest margin can produce a high ROCE; oil palm needs years of nursery and plantation investment before mature returns; consumer and agrochemical acquisitions add goodwill. Benchmark. Good above 20%, average 14–20%, weak below 14%.

The trap. Company-level ROCE hides very different unit-level returns. Avanti's ~22–24% is diluted by about ₹2,450 crore of cash; the feed business on its own earns far more on its operating capital (the anatomy below suggests 40%+ in a good year). Godrej's 20% is flattered in a different way: buying out minority shareholders at Creamline and Godrej Foods reduced its equity by ₹616 crore and ₹371 crore through the minority-interest reserve, so equity-based return measures (ROE 20.9%) look higher than the underlying businesses justify. A normalised Godrej ROE, adding back those write-downs, would be closer to 16–17% (INDICATIVE).

14. Net cash or net debt

What it is. Borrowings minus cash and liquid investments. Formula. Borrowings (state whether lease liabilities are included) − (cash + bank balances + liquid investments + term deposits). Worked example. Avanti, March 2026: treasury of ₹2,460 crore (current investments ₹1,251.8 crore, cash ₹42.2 crore, other bank balances ₹395.7 crore and ₹770.5 crore of term deposits with LIC, Bajaj Finance and M&M Finance) less borrowings of ₹10.5 crore = net cash of about ₹2,450 crore, about 27% of its market value. 15. Attributable versus consolidated profit

What it is. Consolidated profit after tax includes the share of subsidiaries' profits that belongs to outside (non-controlling) shareholders; attributable profit is what belongs to the listed company's own shareholders. Formula. Attributable PAT = consolidated PAT − profit attributable to non-controlling interests (NCI). Worked example. Avanti FY26: consolidated PAT ₹656.8 crore, of which ₹50.9 crore belongs to Thai Union's 40% of Avanti Frozen Foods; attributable ₹606.06 crore (FY25 ₹528.82 crore). Godrej FY26: consolidated PAT ₹445 crore, but attributable ₹472.78 crore (FY25 ₹429.72 crore), higher, because outside shareholders absorb about a third of Astec's ₹81 crore loss. Combined: ₹1,102 crore consolidated, ₹1,078.84 crore attributable.

Why it matters. P/E ratios should use attributable profit, because the market value is the value of the listed company's own shares. Avanti's market value of ₹8,974 crore is 14.8 times attributable FY26 profit but 13.7 times consolidated profit. Benchmark. Not a good-or-bad number, but a gap larger than 10% of PAT means the subsidiary structure matters to valuation. The trap. Avanti's own results slide labels ₹656.8 crore as "PAT (after minority interest)"; the label is wrong, as its EPS (₹44.48 on 13.62 crore shares) and the annual report's MD&A ("Net Profit attributable to the owners… ₹606.06 crore") show. Definition traps, collected

  • "EBITDA" with and without other income. Avanti's company EBITDA (₹947.6 crore, 15.6%) includes about ₹200 crore of treasury income; screener's operating profit (₹750 crore, 12.4%) does not.   • Consolidated versus attributable profit. Avanti's ₹657 crore includes Thai Union's share of the export arm; attributable is ₹606.06 crore. Godrej's attributable (₹472.78 crore) exceeds its consolidated (₹445 crore).
  • "Feed volume" means different things. Avanti: shrimp feed only. Godrej: six species. ₹/kg differs 2.6-fold; the Bangladesh JV is excluded from Godrej's tonnes.
  • Per-tonne profit on different measures. Avanti EBITDA per tonne (₹13,118) is not comparable with Godrej EBIT per tonne (₹2,107); use segment result for both (₹9,937 vs ₹2,107).

  • Market-share denominators. 51–53% (company estimate) vs 47–51% (management's consumption estimate); MPEDA's broader production series would imply less.
  • Revenue split. Avanti's FY26 revenue was about 72% feed and 28% processing, not the 79/21 seen in older material; it swung back to 82/18 in Q1 FY27.
  • Oil-palm revenue excludes trading. Godrej's ₹1,908 crore segment revenue excludes ₹90 crore of oil trading; CRISIL's "edible oil" figures include it.

In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading these two companies come from comparing numbers that look alike but are measured differently: EBITDA with or without interest income, profit with or without the partner's share, tonnes of shrimp feed against tonnes of cattle feed. Before comparing any two figures, check that they are on the same basis.

How to read a feed company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Tonnes, year on year (minute 1). Pull feed volume and compare with the same quarter last year, never the previous quarter (shrimp feed is seasonal: April–June peak, October–March trough). Red flag: volume down while the industry is stocking.
  2. Realisation per kilo (minute 2). Divide feed revenue by tonnes. Compare with the price actions management has announced. Red flag: realisation falling while ingredient costs rise (share defence).
  3. Profit per tonne (minute 3). Divide feed EBITDA or segment result by tonnes; compare with last year and with the through-cycle average (Avanti ~₹9,500 EBITDA; Godrej guide ₹2,050–2,200 EBIT). This is the most important number in the release.
  4. The ingredient basket (minute 4). From the call or presentation, note fishmeal, soymeal, wheat and maize consumption costs and the spot prices. Red flag: spot well above consumption cost, which means next quarter's margin is already lower.
  5. Price actions and the regulator (minute 5). Note any price change, its date, and anything said about the AP feed-price committee or fishmeal export curbs.
  6. The export leg (minute 6). For Avanti: processing tonnes, realisation, margin, the US share of sales and any change in the AD, CVD or tariff layers. Note whether processing margin was helped by currency or other income.
  7. The other segments (minute 7). For Godrej: oil palm (CPO realisation, margin, any formula change), crop care (monsoon), dairy (milk procurement prices), Godrej Foods and Astec (already reported separately; Q2 FY27 PAT −₹37.4 crore).
  8. Other income and treasury (minute 8). Split Avanti's reported EBITDA into operating profit and other income. Red flag: a "beat" driven by treasury yield or currency.
  9. Working capital and debt (minute 9). Inventory and payables; for Godrej, net debt and whether payables are still rising (supplier finance).
  10. Attributable profit and guidance (minute 10). Use attributable EPS for the P/E; note any change to volume guidance (Avanti ~5,85,000 t for FY27) and to Godrej's "mid-teens PBT growth" target, which was put under review after Q1.

The same ten-minute routine works for the unlisted and foreign peers whose numbers appear in this report: tonnes, price, profit per tonne, the ingredient basket and the policy or trade overlay are the five things that decide every feed company's quarter. The order matters. A reader who starts with revenue growth, as most result headlines do, will conclude that Q1 FY27 was a strong quarter for Avanti (+18% revenue); a reader who starts with profit per tonne will see that feed earned less per tonne than in any full year since FY22.

In plain terms

Skip the revenue headline. Find how many tonnes were sold, how much was earned on each tonne, and what the ingredients cost now compared with what was used. Those three numbers tell you whether the quarter was good and what the next one will look like.

The global lens: small in tonnes, rich in margin

India's two listed feed companies are minnows next to China's and Thailand's giants, but Avanti's shrimp-feed spread is among the widest in the world in a good year.

The global feed industry is dominated by a few integrated groups. Technology and disruption: cheaper protein, smarter ponds

The technologies that matter most would lower fishmeal use, raise feed efficiency and cut disease. Each helps the industry; some shrink the feed tonnes a kilo of shrimp needs.

Disruption in feed is slow and incremental, but the direction is clear. Every major technology in the pipeline either substitutes for the most volatile ingredient, fishmeal, or raises the efficiency with which feed becomes shrimp, milk or meat. The first defends margins; the second, over time, reduces the tonnes of feed needed per kilo of output.

Alternative proteins are the most important technology for Avanti, because fishmeal is both its largest cost and its most volatile one. Global leaders already run shrimp diets with much lower fishmeal inclusion than India's, and every point of fishmeal replaced lowers the sensitivity shown in section 10. The constraint is pond performance: a substitute that worsens the feed conversion ratio costs the farmer more than the feed saves, and Indian farmers have long memories of feeds that did not perform. Formulation know-how is therefore a competitive asset, which is one reason the Thai Union collaboration is worth more than its ₹7.87 crore royalty suggests. A successful low-fishmeal formula would, however, also lower the barrier to entry for rivals, who could buy the same substitutes.

Feed efficiency is the slow-moving threat. Automated feeders, better genetics and biosecure ponds all raise the amount of shrimp harvested per kilo of feed. That is good for farmers and for India's competitiveness against Ecuador, and good for feed demand if it leads to more ponds and more crops, but over a decade it means fewer tonnes of feed per tonne of shrimp. Climate is the exogenous variable that overrides all of these: El Niño affects fishmeal through Peru's anchovy catch and Indian crops through the monsoon, and it was the single most important external factor for both companies in FY27.

In plain terms

The biggest technology shift for shrimp feed would be recipes that need much less fishmeal without hurting the shrimp. That would make companies like Avanti less exposed to fishmeal price spikes. The slow threat is the opposite: smarter feeding machines and better shrimp genetics mean each kilo of shrimp needs less feed, which over many years means fewer tonnes sold per pond.

Bull vs bear

The bull case is a cyclical recovery in spreads and a cleaner Godrej. The bear case is a regulated spread, a sticky US duty gap and an oil-palm price cut.

Both cases start from the same facts: a sub-sector whose combined profit fell 27% in the June 2026 quarter, valued at about 18 times FY26 attributable earnings and 11.4 times FY26 operating profit, with one member at a 52-week low and the other in a strategic reset. The disagreement is about whether FY26 or Q1 FY27 is the better guide to normal earnings, and whether policy (in Andhra Pradesh, Washington and New Delhi) will help or hurt.

The bull case

  • Input costs mean-revert. New soybean and kharif maize crops and a better Peruvian season bring fishmeal and soymeal down; Avanti's spread returns towards its ~₹9,500 a tonne through-cycle level, nearly double Q1 FY27's ₹6,094.
  • The duty peak is behind. The stack has fallen from ~57% to 21.3%, India and Ecuador now pay the same 10% tariff, and India's US shipments hit a 19-month high in August 2026 (+25% YoY).
  • An AP formula speeds pass-through. Indexation could let feed prices follow ingredients without a political fight, cutting the lag that hurt Q1 FY27.
  • Cash and refunds. Avanti's ~₹2,450 Cr of net cash is 27% of its value; an IEEPA refund of US$15–20 mn is still to come.
  • Godrej's reset works. Oil palm grows towards 1,50,000 ha; cattle feed keeps taking share at ₹2,050–2,200 a tonne; dairy and Astec stop losing money; shrimp feed and live birds exit.

The bear case

  • FY26 was the peak, not the base. It was earned at the bottom of a two-and-a-half-year soymeal decline; spot fishmeal of ₹225 a kilo points to a weaker Q2 FY27.
  • A regulated spread. If AP's formula sets the allowance near a trough margin, Avanti loses its peak-year upside permanently; ICRA already flags "price capping".
  • The sticky duty gap. AD has ratcheted 1.35% → 3.76% → 5.53%; India pays ~9 points more than Ecuador's big exporters, and Ecuador now leads the US market.
  • Concentration. Avanti's feed and export arms share one state, one set of farmers and one main export market; disease or a policy shock hits both.
  • Godrej's footnotes. CPO duty cut to ~11% lowers oil-palm realisation; Astec lost ₹37.4 Cr in Q2 FY27; working capital leans on supplier finance; ₹620 Cr of debt falls due in FY28.

The balance of evidence favours a middle path. Ingredient spikes have always reversed, and Avanti's history says the spread returns to its average within two to three years; but the AP formula and the anti-dumping ratchet make it less likely that the next peak looks like FY26. For Godrej, the reset is credible and the oil-palm runway real, but the duty cut and Astec mean FY27 earnings are more likely to disappoint than surprise. The two members' risks are now less correlated than at any time in the past decade: Avanti's turn on ingredients and Andhra Pradesh, Godrej's on palm oil and execution.

In plain terms

Optimists say the expensive ingredients of 2026 will get cheaper as they always have, American duties have come down a long way, and Godrej is cleaning up. Pessimists say last year's profits were a one-off, Andhra Pradesh may cap feed prices, India still pays more in US duties than Ecuador, and palm oil just lost some protection. Both have a point; the September-quarter results in November will show which is winning.
