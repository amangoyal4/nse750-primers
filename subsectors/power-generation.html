00

The one-page view: two businesses sharing one grid

An annuity that pays a fixed return on equity, and a cyclical trade that pays whatever the evening market will bear. Every listed generator is some mix of the two.

Indian power generation in October 2026 is really two businesses running on the same wires. The first is an annuity. A coal, hydro or nuclear plant built under Section 62 of the Electricity Act is paid a capacity charge that recovers a 15.5% post-tax return on its regulated equity as long as the plant is available 85% of the time, plus an energy charge that passes the fuel bill straight through to the buyer. A 25-year solar or wind contract at a fixed tariff is the same kind of business wearing different clothes: the price is locked, and the investor's job is to build cheaply and keep the plant running. NTPC, NHPC, SJVN and NLC India are the regulated archetypes. Adani Green, NTPC Green, ACME Solar and KPI Green are the contracted-renewable archetypes.

The second business is a trade. A plant with uncontracted capacity sells into the power exchanges, where the day-ahead price on IEX averaged ₹3.8 per kWh in FY26, a year in which national demand grew just 0.8%, and then ₹7.3 per kWh in September 2026, after demand grew 9–11% and peak demand hit a record 270.8 GW on 21 May 2026. Plants that sell even a fifth of their output this way see their profits swing far more than their volumes. Adani Power, JP Power, JSW Energy's thermal fleet and, at the margin, RattanIndia and GMR live partly in this world.

Capacity is growing faster than at any point in the country's history. India had 475 GW installed in March 2025 and 549 GW by June 2026, after adding a record 61.6 GW gross in FY26 alone. Yet the binding constraints are no longer money or equipment. They are transmission (FY26 line additions were 79% of target, and solar curtailment is rising), offtake (44.8 GW of awarded renewable capacity has no signed sale agreement with a distribution company) and the evening peak (solar produces nothing after sunset, which is why discoms are once again signing 25-year coal contracts at around ₹6 per kWh). This report is organised around five forces that follow from those constraints.

The five forces that shape the sub-sector

  • 1. Daytime energy is abundant; firm evening power is scarce. Solar is 30% of capacity but only 10.9% of energy. It drives the midday price toward zero and contributes nothing after sunset. Value is moving to anything that can deliver at 8 pm: coal with contracts, pumped hydro, batteries, and "firm and dispatchable" renewable bundles. Sections 08 and 10.
  • 2. Coal's second act. The government wants at least 80 GW of new coal capacity by FY32, 42.7 GW is under construction and Crisil expects thermal capex to double to ₹2.3 lakh crore over FY26–28. NTPC's regulated equity compounds at about 9% a year, and Adani Power has 23.7 GW locked in. Section 09.
  • 3. Storage becomes an asset class of its own. Around 272 GWh of storage has been tendered, battery tariffs have halved in eighteen months and 15.9 GW of pumped hydro is being built. The next decade's growth capex is measured in GWh as much as GW. Section 11.
  • 4. The customer has been fixed, mostly. The Late Payment Surcharge Rules of 2022 cut legacy dues owed to generators by 98%, from ₹1.40 lakh crore to ₹3,330 crore. Lower receivables mean less working capital, better ratings and cheaper debt. Section 07.
  • 5. Weather-driven merchant cycles on top of a structural uptrend. The same economy produced 0.8% demand growth in FY26 and about 9% in the first four months of FY27. Because the exchange price is set at the margin, a few percent of demand moves it by 50–100%. Section 12.

In plain terms

Most of the profit in Indian power still comes from coal plants that sell under long contracts at regulated returns. Most of the stock-market value sits in companies promising to build the next generation of plants. The forces in this report decide whether those promises turn into profit: whether the new solar parks can get their power onto the grid and sold, whether batteries become cheap enough to carry solar into the evening, and whether the coal revival lasts long enough to justify the plants now being ordered.

Three cross-currents make the next two years unusual. First, the grid is short of firm power in the evening while it is long of energy at noon; that split, rather than a single "shortage" or "glut", is now the defining feature of the market. Second, every listed generator is in a heavy capex cycle at once: NTPC has 35.7 GW under construction, Adani Power 23.7 GW locked in, JSW Energy 13.6 GW, NTPC Green 16.0 GW and NHPC 9.2 GW. Depreciation and interest on newly commissioned assets are already compressing profits at NHPC, JSW Energy and SJVN. Third, the stocks have de-rated since mid-2026. Every member's market value except ACME's and two of the small caps' is below its June–July level, and several (NTPC, NHPC, SJVN, Reliance Power, RattanIndia) trade near their 52-week lows despite record operating quarters.

The rest of the report explains each mechanism from first principles. Sections 01 to 07 cover the plumbing: the value chain, the size of the market, demand, supply and costs, ownership, business models and regulation. Sections 08 to 13 are the four deep dives and the history of the cycle. Section 14 is a long masterclass on the numbers that matter when you read a generator's results. Sections 15 to 17 compare the fourteen companies, and the final sections cover the global lens, technology, the bull and bear cases and what to watch.

01

How a kilowatt-hour becomes profit

Fuel, plant, wires, distributor, consumer: generators sit in the middle of a chain whose most profitable link is now the mine and whose scarcest is storage.

Electricity has a peculiar supply chain because the product cannot be stored cheaply and must be produced in the same second it is consumed. At the start of the chain is fuel: coal from Coal India and captive mines, moved by rail to the plant; imported coal through ports at Mundra and Udupi; gas and LNG; uranium for nuclear; and water, sunlight and wind, which are free. The generator turns that fuel into electricity at a "bus-bar", the point where the plant meets the grid. From there, inter-state transmission lines (owned by POWERGRID and by private licensees who win build-own-operate tenders) and state transmission utilities carry it to around 70 distribution companies, or discoms. Most discoms are owned by state governments; a handful are private, such as those run by Tata Power, Torrent, CESC and Adani Electricity. The discom bills the consumer.

The commercial chain is different from the physical one. About 87% of electricity is sold on long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs). These come in two flavours. Under Section 62 of the Electricity Act, a regulator (the CERC for central plants, a state commission for state plants) sets the tariff on a cost-plus basis: it adds up the plant's capital cost, fuel cost and operating costs and allows a fixed return on equity. Under Section 63, the tariff is discovered by competitive bidding and then simply adopted by the regulator; the winning bidder keeps any efficiency gains and bears any cost overruns. Renewable power is usually bought through an intermediary, a "renewable energy implementing agency" (REIA) such as SECI, NTPC, NHPC or SJVN. The REIA signs a PPA with the generator and a back-to-back power sale agreement (PSA) with a discom, earning a trading margin of about 7 paise per kWh. The other 13% of power moves on short-term markets: the exchanges' day-ahead, real-time and term-ahead segments, bilateral trades between traders, and the deviation settlement mechanism that charges generators and buyers for straying from their schedules.

Where the value is captured

Value has moved upstream and sideways. Coal mining is the most profitable layer: Coal India earned ₹31,071 crore of profit on ₹1.68 lakh crore of revenue in FY26, an 18% net margin, and generators that own captive mines (NTPC meets 19% of its coal needs from its own mines; NLC, Adani, JSW and Reliance Power's Sasan also have mines) cut their fuel cost below the linkage price. Regulated generation earns its 15.5% return on a 30% equity slice of capital cost, plus incentives for running above normative availability and for beating normative efficiency. Competitive thermal plants keep the gap between their bid tariff and their actual costs, plus whatever the exchange pays for uncontracted power. Renewable IPPs earn a spread between a project return of about 9–10% and a borrowing cost of about 8.4–9%, levered three to four times on equity. Transmission earns a regulated 15% return. The exchange takes about 2 paise per kWh from each side of a trade at an EBITDA margin near 80%. The state discoms, which collect the consumer's money, are close to break-even: in FY24 their average cost of supply was ₹7.09 per kWh against average revenue (excluding state subsidy) of ₹5.54.

Two thirds of what a consumer pays is the generator's bill. That is why every rupee of generation cost matters to state politics, and why the cheapness of solar (₹2.5–2.7 per kWh) has been so attractive to discoms whose tariffs barely cover costs. It is also why the shift to firm, evening power at ₹5–6 per kWh is a real burden for a discom that sells to farmers at subsidised rates. A generator's pricing power is ultimately capped by what a cash-strapped state discom can afford to pay, and by the speed at which it pays.

In plain terms

A power generator sells a product its customer cannot store and must have every second of the day. Most of the time the price is fixed for 25 years in advance, by a regulator or by an auction. The money is made by building the plant cheaply, keeping it running, buying fuel well, and getting paid on time. Only a small slice of power is sold at whatever the market will pay that day, but in a tight year that slice is where the surprises, good and bad, come from.

The three business types introduced here each have their own revenue equation. For a regulated plant, profit is roughly the 15.5% allowed return multiplied by regulated equity, plus efficiency gains and incentives, less any costs the regulator disallows. For a renewable IPP, revenue is megawatts × 8,760 hours × capacity utilisation × (1 − curtailment) × tariff, and profit is what remains after interest and depreciation consume most of a 90% EBITDA margin. For a merchant thermal plant, the contribution is the gap between the exchange price and the plant's variable fuel cost, multiplied by the units sold. Section 14 works through each equation with rupee examples. The point for now is that the three equations respond to completely different variables: the size of the equity base, the cost of debt and the grid's ability to take power, and the price set by the marginal evening megawatt.

02

From 275 GW to 549 GW: size, growth and the money pool

India doubled its generating capacity in eleven years, and the mix flipped from two-thirds thermal to majority non-fossil. The revenue pool is roughly ₹8.5–9 lakh crore a year.

India's installed generating capacity was 274.9 GW in March 2015. It reached 475.2 GW in March 2025, 532.7 GW in March 2026 and 548.9 GW at the end of June 2026, the latest date for which the Central Electricity Authority's figures could be verified. That is a compound growth rate of 5.6% a year from FY15 to FY25, and a 13% jump in the twelve months to June 2026. The acceleration is recent: gross additions were about 24 GW in FY24, 34.1 GW in FY25 and a record 61.6 GW in FY26, driven by solar developers rushing to commission before the waiver on inter-state transmission charges began to phase down in June 2025. ICRA expects around 50 GW in FY27; 16.3 GW was added in April–June 2026 alone.

The money pool

Translating energy into money requires an average price, and there is no single published one. A reasonable composite is about ₹4.8 per kWh at the bus-bar: NTPC's regulated two-part tariff averaged ₹4.46 in Q1 FY27 (₹1.78 fixed plus ₹2.68 variable), renewable contracts range from ₹2.5 to ₹4, merchant power averaged ₹3.8–5 over the past two years, and new coal contracts are pricing around ₹6. On roughly 1,840 BU, that gives a generation revenue pool of about ₹8.5–9 lakh crore a year, an indicative estimate. The fourteen listed companies in this report booked ₹3.38 lakh crore of revenue in FY26 (₹3.35 lakh crore once NTPC Green's figures, which sit inside NTPC's consolidated accounts, are counted once). They therefore capture about 38–40% of the pool. The rest belongs to state generation companies (MAHAGENCO, RVUNL, UPRVUNL, TANGEDCO and others), the Damodar Valley Corporation, NPCIL's nuclear fleet, Tata Power, Torrent, CESC, privately held renewable platforms such as Greenko, Avaada and ReNew, and industrial captive plants.

Other measures of size give a sense of scale. The short-term power market (exchanges plus traders) turned over ₹89,681 crore in FY25. Inter-state transmission charges were ₹46,219 crore. The fourteen listed generators carried gross borrowings of ₹7.09 lakh crore and net fixed assets plus capital work-in-progress of ₹10.4 lakh crore at March 2026, and their combined market value was ₹12.3 lakh crore on 13 October 2026. The investment pipeline is even larger: Crisil expects ₹2.3 lakh crore of thermal capex over FY26–28; renewable additions of 45–50 GW a year at ₹4.25–6 crore per MW imply roughly ₹2.5–3 lakh crore a year of renewable investment (an estimate); and the CEA's transmission plan to FY36 calls for ₹7.93 lakh crore.

In plain terms

India's power system is twice the size it was in 2015 and is still growing at 5–7% a year in energy terms. The listed companies own about a third of the capacity and earn about two-fifths of the revenue. Because they are building faster than the rest of the industry, their share is likely to rise, but the bills for that construction arrive first.

Two caveats matter for anyone using these numbers. First, capacity is not output. Solar's 30% share of installed capacity produced 10.9% of energy in April–June 2026, while coal's 42% share produced about 70%; the gap is utilisation, which is the subject of Section 04. Second, India's consumption per head is still low. It was 1,395 kWh in FY24 against a world average of about 3,474 kWh, so even after a decade of 5–7% growth the country would remain well below global norms. The structural demand story is not in doubt. The questions are how fast it arrives, what time of day it arrives, and who is paid to meet it.

03

Demand: the weather now beats GDP

The same economy produced 0.8% demand growth in FY26 and about 9% in early FY27. Heat, rain and air-conditioners now explain more of the swing than industry does.

Analysts used to model electricity demand as GDP growth multiplied by an elasticity of about 0.8–1.0. That still works over a decade: demand grew at 7.35% a year over FY21–FY25, roughly in line with nominal activity and electrification. It does not work year to year any more. In FY26 India's demand grew just 0.8%, because a long and unusually wet monsoon suppressed cooling load and agricultural pumping; generation in April–June 2025 actually fell 2%. In the first four months of FY27 demand rose 9.4%, as a delayed monsoon and heatwaves pushed air-conditioning to new highs. All-India consumption grew 11.5% in July–September 2026 and 11% in September alone. The economy did not change speed by that much. The weather did.

Weather matters this much because cooling is now the swing load. In April–June 2026, the daily peak occurred during solar hours on 61 of 91 days, which tells you that afternoon air-conditioning, not evening lighting, now sets the record. On 21 May 2026, the fourth record day in a row, peak demand reached 270.8 GW; thermal plants supplied 62.8% of that peak and renewables including hydro about 34%. The ministry had projected 278.8 GW for 2026, and the minister has said the peak could approach 300 GW next year, though that statement could not be independently verified.

The five drivers, ranked by how much they move the numbers

  • Weather and cooling load. The dominant swing factor year to year. A late or weak monsoon adds several percentage points of demand; an early, wet one subtracts them. Its effect on the exchange price is amplified because the marginal megawatt is expensive.
  • Structural growth and electrification. About 5–7% a year over a cycle. Crisil expects demand to reach about 2,000 BU by FY28, a 5.5% growth rate. Per-capita use (1,395 kWh in FY24) is about 40% of the world average.
  • Industry and commerce. Industry is about 41–42% of consumption, domestic users about 25% and agriculture 17–18% (CERC; FY24 split, indicative because the published table was truncated). New loads include green hydrogen, steel and aluminium expansions and data centres; the ministry's projection of about 26 GW of data-centre demand by FY32 is a headline figure only.
  • Electrification of transport and cooking. Electric vehicles, metro systems and railway traction are small today but compounding.
  • The push for 24×7 supply. The Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme has sanctioned ₹2.83 lakh crore of works, including smart meters. Aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) losses fell to 15.04% in FY25. Lower theft dampens billed demand growth slightly, but improves collections, which matters more to generators.

In plain terms

Over ten years, India's electricity demand will roughly double: that part is not controversial. What has changed is the size of the year-to-year wobble. One hot summer adds 9% to demand; one wet one takes it to zero. Generators with fixed contracts barely notice. Generators that sell some power on the exchange see their profits move two or three times as much as demand does.

For investors, the important implication is about forecasting. Earnings at regulated companies depend on how much equity they commission, not on whether demand grew 1% or 9%. Earnings at merchant-exposed thermal IPPs depend heavily on the weather in a particular quarter, which is why Adani Power's April–June 2026 profit rose 47% while its FY26 revenue had fallen 3.5%. The ICRA forecast for FY27 demand growth was raised from about 5% in May to about 6.5% in August. A year from now, the strength or weakness of the 2027 monsoon will matter as much to the merchant names as any capex plan.

There is also a structural change in when demand arrives. Because solar output peaks at midday, a midday demand peak is easy to meet; the hard hours are the early evening, when solar fades and air-conditioning, lighting and cooking load overlap. The grid's reliability is now set not by its total capacity but by how much dispatchable power it has between roughly 6 pm and 11 pm. That single fact drives the first force in this report and is the subject of the first deep dive in Section 08.

Supply: coal still makes 70% of the power, and where ₹100 goes

Capacity and energy are two different maps of the same grid. Utilisation explains the gap, and the cost structure explains why a coal plant and a solar park are completely different businesses.

At 30 June 2026 India had 548.9 GW of capacity: coal and lignite 230.8 GW, solar 162.2 GW, wind 57.4 GW, large hydro 52.1 GW, gas 20.1 GW, biomass 10.9 GW, nuclear 8.8 GW and small hydro 5.2 GW, with a sliver of waste-to-energy and diesel. Non-fossil capacity was 297.4 GW, or 54.2%. The energy picture is very different. In April–June 2026 the grid produced a record 524 BU. Coal and lignite made 369 BU of it, about 70%. Solar made 57 BU (10.9%, the first time it has crossed 10% in a first quarter), large hydro 39 BU, wind 31 BU, nuclear 16 BU and gas 7 BU. Non-fossil sources are 54% of the capacity but about 29% of the energy.

In plain terms

A coal plant is a fuel-processing business: most of its revenue is the coal bill, which the buyer reimburses, and the profit is a modest slice on top. A solar park is a financing business: it has almost no running costs, so nearly all its revenue goes to repay the loans and the equity that built it. One is sensitive to fuel logistics and plant availability; the other to interest rates and whether the grid can take its power.

China: low fuel dependence, high equipment dependence

India's generators import little fuel: imported coal is 5–7% of the power sector's coal supply, mostly for coastal plants built to burn it (Adani Power's Mundra and Udupi, Tata's Mundra, JSW's Ratnagiri and Vijayanagar). Equipment is a different story. China makes about 85% of the world's solar manufacturing capacity and about 95% of its wafers, most of the lithium-iron-phosphate battery cells used in storage, and a large share of wind components. India has responded with a layered set of protections: a 40% basic customs duty on imported modules and 25% on cells since April 2022; an Approved List of Models and Manufacturers (ALMM) that utility projects must buy from, with about 193 GW of module capacity on List-I; and, from 1 June 2026, a List-II for solar cells, which had 30 GW enlisted across 12 makers in April 2026. Chinese boiler-turbine-generator sets have been effectively barred since 2020, leaving BHEL as the dominant supplier of main-plant thermal equipment and a bottleneck for the coal revival.

Five ways to own a power plant

Regulated PSU, competitive thermal IPP, utility-scale renewable IPP, C&I developer and storage operator: each earns a different kind of return and deserves a different multiple.

The fourteen listed generators can be sorted into five business models. The sorting matters because the same headline number, a 30% operating margin or a 10% ROE, means something completely different in each. A regulated PSU with a 10% ROE may be perfectly healthy and simply carrying a lot of unfinished construction on its balance sheet; a merchant IPP with a 10% ROE might be in a bad year; a renewable IPP with a 10% ROE in its early years may be on track for a mid-teens equity return over its life.

07

The rulebook: Section 62, Section 63 and the rules that fixed payments

The regulator sets the return on half the industry, auctions set the price on the rest, and a 2022 rule on late payments did more for generator balance sheets than any tariff order.

The Electricity Act of 2003 is the foundation. It removed the need for a licence to generate power, allowed large consumers to buy directly from generators through "open access", and created the two tariff routes described in Section 01: Section 62, where a regulator sets a cost-plus tariff, and Section 63, where the tariff is discovered by competitive bidding. It also gave the central government a reserve power under Section 11 to direct generators in extraordinary circumstances, which has been used repeatedly since 2022 to force imported-coal plants to run during shortages. An amendment bill has been pending for years, and a Draft National Electricity Policy was released in January 2026, which mentions resource adequacy and capacity markets.

Regulated returns: what the CERC allows

The CERC's Tariff Regulations for 2024–29 took effect on 1 April 2024. As reported from the draft, they allow a post-tax return on equity of 15.5% for thermal plants and run-of-river hydro, 16.5% for existing storage hydro and pumped storage (the draft proposed 17% for new pumped storage; the final figure could not be verified), and 15% for new transmission. Normative equity is 30% of admitted capital cost. A thermal plant recovers its full fixed charge only if it is available at least 85% of the time, and earns an incentive of 75 paise per kWh at peak and 50 paise off-peak for generation above an 85% PLF. Additional capital spending outside the original scope, such as flue-gas desulphurisation equipment required by a change in law, earns only the State Bank of India's one-year lending rate plus 3.5 percentage points, capped at 14%. In March 2026 the CERC added regulations for batteries co-located at thermal plants, whose December 2025 draft proposed a 14% ROE, a 12-year life and 85% round-trip efficiency.

These returns are post-tax and on equity, which makes them generous by global standards for a low-risk asset: a 15.5% post-tax return equates to about 20.7% pre-tax at a 25.17% corporate tax rate, and it is earned on equity that is never depreciated. The catch is that the return is earned only on capital the regulator admits. Cost overruns and interest during construction delays can be disallowed, which is the main reason hydro companies such as NHPC and SJVN earn well below the headline 16.5% on their books.

The late-payment rules that changed the counterparty

For two decades the biggest risk in Indian power generation was not the regulator or the fuel; it was getting paid. State discoms ran persistent losses and paid generators late, sometimes years late. The Electricity (Late Payment Surcharge and Related Matters) Rules, notified on 3 June 2022, changed that by giving the rules teeth. Overdue bills attract a surcharge at a base rate of the SBI lending rate plus 5%, rising by 0.5 percentage points for every month of delay up to 3 points above base. Legacy dues outstanding at notification could be rescheduled into up to 48 monthly instalments. Firm power is the new scarcity Deep dive

Solar has made noon power almost free and done nothing for 8 pm. Who supplies the evening peak, at what price, is now the most valuable question in the sector.

Every grid operator's day now has the same shape. In the morning, demand ramps up while solar output is still low, and coal and hydro carry the load. From about 10 am to 3 pm, solar floods in: on a sunny day in Rajasthan or Gujarat there is more cheap solar power than the local grid can absorb, so some of it is curtailed and the exchange's green day-ahead price collapses. Then, between about 5 pm and 7 pm, solar output falls to zero within an hour or two just as households switch on lights, fans, air-conditioners and cooking appliances. The grid needs tens of gigawatts of other capacity to ramp up within that window and stay on through the night. Engineers call the resulting net-demand profile a "duck curve". The economics follow directly from its shape: energy is abundant and cheap at midday, and firm capacity is scarce and expensive in the evening.

The question: what is the cheapest firm kilowatt-hour?

The central planning question for the next six years is how India meets an evening peak that rises from 271 GW to perhaps 366 GW by FY32 (an estimate interpolated from the CEA's 459 GW for FY36). Two things stand out. First, more than half of a new coal tariff is the fixed charge, which is why a new coal plant only makes sense with a long contract that pays that charge whether or not the plant is dispatched. In plain terms

Solar power is now the cheapest electricity India can buy, but only at lunchtime. Power that is guaranteed to be there at 8 pm costs two to two-and-a-half times as much, whether it comes from a new coal plant, a battery, a pumped-hydro reservoir or a bundle of all three. The companies that can deliver evening power reliably are selling the scarce product; the ones that sell only daytime solar are selling the abundant one.

The answer: a portfolio, not a winner

On headline tariff, FDRE has already undercut new coal: ₹5.25 for round-the-clock delivery against about ₹6.0. Coal still wins on two counts that the tariff comparison misses. It provides seasonal firmness, covering the weeks in the monsoon and winter when solar and wind output both dip, which a two- or four-hour battery cannot. And it is deliverable: a pit-head coal plant does not face curtailment or depend on a new 765 kV line being finished. The likely outcome is a portfolio. Coal provides baseload and seasonal cover; batteries handle daily shifting of two to four hours; pumped storage handles six to eight hours; and FDRE contracts bundle renewables with storage to deliver a defined shape of power.

The CEA's own long-term adequacy plan says the same thing in numbers. It projects storage needs of 87 GWh by FY28, 236 GWh by FY32 and 888 GWh by FY36, while the government simultaneously targets at least 80 GW of new coal by FY32. Neither is a substitute for the other in the planners' view. Crisil expects about 70% of incremental demand to FY28 to be met by renewables, with coal covering the remaining 30% and the reliability margin.

Who wins the evening peak among the fourteen

  • NTPC has all three tools: 15.7 GW of coal under construction, an 18 GW pumped-storage portfolio (Tehri PSP is fully commercial), and 5 GWh of batteries being installed at 15 thermal stations under the new CERC rules, which earn a regulated return on a co-located asset.
  • Adani Power is the purest private bet on firm thermal capacity: 23.7 GW locked in, of which more than half has 25-year contracts under the SHAKTI B(iv) coal-linkage scheme.
  • JSW Energy combines thermal (5.7 GW operating, 3.8 GW under construction), 24 GWh of pumped storage under construction with two 12 GWh contracts with MSEDCL and UPPCL, and in-house battery assembly.
  • ACME Solar and Adani Green are the FDRE and battery specialists: ACME signed a 300 MW FDRE contract at ₹6.28 and has 3.6 GWh of batteries running; Adani Green has 6.6 GWh of batteries at Khavda.
  • NHPC and SJVN hold the pumped-storage pipelines of the public sector: NHPC has 15 projects totalling 17.9 GW under survey.

The losers in this framing are the owners of daytime-only solar without storage or a contracted buyer, and the developers whose awards are among the 44.8 GW waiting for sale agreements. Coal's second act: from stranded asset to scarce asset Deep dive

In 2018 a sixth of India's private coal fleet was bankrupt. In 2026 discoms are signing 25-year coal contracts again and the plants bought cheaply in the bust are earning 15%-plus returns.

Few sectors have swung as violently as Indian thermal power. In the private-thermal boom of 2005–2012, roughly 50 GW of private coal capacity was planned, the Ultra Mega Power Projects were awarded (Reliance Power's Sasan at a levelised ₹1.196 per kWh, Tata's Mundra), and Reliance Power's January 2008 IPO raised ₹11,563 crore. Then came a fuel shock. Indonesia's 2011 coal-pricing rules broke the economics of plants built to burn cheap imported coal at fixed tariffs; domestic coal shortages and the Supreme Court's cancellation of 204 coal blocks in September 2014 starved others. By 2018 a parliamentary committee counted 34 stressed thermal projects of about 40 GW, and the all-India coal PLF had fallen to about 61%. The Reserve Bank's February 2018 circular forced lenders to resolve the bad loans.

The resolution created today's industry. Adani Power acquired Korba West, Raipur, Raigarh, Lanco Amarkantak (now Korba Power), Coastal Energen (via Mutiara), Vidarbha Industries (Butibori) and others, building an inorganic fleet of 7.45 GW. RattanIndia's Amravati plant was restructured, with its debt assigned to an asset reconstruction company in 2019. JP Power sold its hydro plants to JSW Energy in 2015–16 and restructured. JSW bought KSK Mahanadi's 1.8 GW in 2025. NTPC and MAHAGENCO bought the 1,350 MW Sinnar plant out of insolvency in February 2026 for a reported ₹3,800 crore, about ₹2.8 crore per MW, an unverified figure.

In plain terms

Ten years ago, India had too many coal plants and not enough buyers, and many private plants went bust. The survivors were bought cheaply, often for a third of what they cost to build. Now demand has caught up, coal is scarce again in the evenings, and those plants are running nearly flat out. The question for investors is how long the window lasts before the new plants and batteries being built today arrive.

The risks to the coal revival

  • Pass-through disputes. Change-in-law claims for coal cost, ash transport and taxes take years to settle. RattanIndia carries ₹2,757 crore of receivables, 336 debtor days, largely MSEDCL change-in-law claims now before the Supreme Court.
  • Equipment and execution. BHEL is the dominant supplier of boilers and turbines, and 89% of the coal under construction is less than half built. Delays raise capex and cut regulated returns when interest during construction is disallowed.
  • Coal logistics. Stocks fell to about 11 days in August 2026; rail rakes and e-auction premiums can squeeze plants without captive mines or firm linkages. JP Power surrendered its captive Amelia North mine and will move Nigrie to linkage or auction coal.
  • Policy caps. The ₹10 per kWh exchange cap and Section 11 orders add supply exactly when merchant prices spike.
  • Environmental capex. Flue-gas desulphurisation deadlines have been relaxed for many plants (JP Power's are now exempt as Category C), but the obligation has not disappeared everywhere.
  • The long tail. A coal unit ordered in 2026 runs into the 2050s. If storage and FDRE costs keep falling, the last decade of its life could be at low PLF, and the plant's value then depends entirely on how its contract treats capacity that is available but not dispatched.

For now, the regulated and contracted structure protects most of the new build: a Section 62 plant earns its return at 85% availability regardless of dispatch, and SHAKTI B(iv) contracts pay a capacity charge. The plants most exposed to a reversal are the merchant-heavy ones, which brings us to the cycle in Section 12. India can build solar faster than it can sell it or carry it. 44.8 GW of awards wait for a buyer, curtailment has quadrupled and transmission is running at a quarter of its annual target.

For a decade the renewable story was a capacity race: who could win the most auctions at the lowest tariff and build the fastest. That race is over. India added 61.6 GW in FY26, mostly solar. The binding constraints are now two things the developer does not control: whether a discom will sign a sale agreement for the power, and whether the grid can carry it from the desert to the load centres. Both have become visible in company results.

The offtake squeeze

As of 30 April 2026, 44.8 GW of renewable capacity held letters of award from the implementing agencies with the matching power purchase and sale agreements unsigned. Earlier snapshots put the figure at 43.9 GW in September 2025 and about 42 GW in October 2025, so the backlog is not clearing on its own. Discoms do not want more daytime-only solar: they already have surplus at noon, the inter-state transmission waiver is phasing out, and many would rather contract firm power. On 25 May 2026 the MNRE proposed a one-time relief package. Discoms that sign within three months would get a full inter-state transmission waiver, deemed compliance with renewable obligations, and the option to add a two-hour battery to vanilla solar, with the non-solar-hour tariff set under Section 62. Developers would get three exits: keep their connectivity and move the project to a new buyer, swap the award for another contract, or leave without penalty and recover their bank guarantees. The package is still a proposal.

The listed exposure is mostly indirect but material. SJVN, as an implementing agency, has awarded about 15–16 GW but signed sale agreements for only about 6 GW, leaving about 9 GW unsigned. ACME has 1,200 MW of awards without a PPA out of 5,080 MW under construction. Reliance Power's 930 MW SECI solar-plus-storage project has its PPA but was granted final connectivity at Kurnool only from 30 May 2029, which pushes the effective commissioning date out by years. Adani Green converted 974 MW from merchant sale to a 25-year PPA during H1 FY27, and about a quarter of its capacity sells to commercial and industrial buyers rather than through the implementing agencies.

The wires squeeze

Transmission has not kept pace. India added 12,139 circuit-km of lines in FY26 against a target of 15,382, 79% of plan, and only 4,081 circuit-km by 31 July 2026 against an FY27 target of 16,554. Right-of-way disputes, land and monsoon disruptions are the usual causes. Renewable projects that finish before their lines are ready can connect on "temporary general network access", but ICRA reports (in a search snippet that could not be fetched in full) that about a third of the 54.8 GW of recently commissioned renewables evacuate this way and face 50–60% curtailment during solar hours. In Rajasthan, a new 765 kV line commissioned in December 2025 added only about 600 MW of usable margin.

Why a few percent of lost output matters so much

Because a solar park has almost no running costs and most of its revenue goes to interest and depreciation, every unit it cannot sell comes straight out of the equity return. A worked example (detailed in Section 14) of a 300 MW solar project with a ₹2.60 tariff, ₹4.25 crore per MW capex, a 30% CUF and 75% debt at 8.5% gives a 16.2% post-tax equity IRR. Cut the CUF to 27%, roughly what 10% curtailment does, and the equity IRR falls to 12.3%. Raise the capex to ₹4.75 crore per MW, roughly the premium for domestic cells, and it falls to 12.6%. Lower the tariff to ₹2.45 and it falls to 13.9%. Raise the borrowing rate to 9.5% and it falls to 14.9%.

In plain terms

Building a solar park is now the easy part. The hard parts are finding a utility that will sign a 25-year contract to buy its output, and getting a power line built to carry that output to the cities. If a project loses one unit in ten to curtailment, its owner's return falls by about a quarter. That is why developers are moving from plain solar to hybrid and storage-backed projects, and to industrial customers who buy directly.

How the listed developers are adapting

  • Connectivity as an asset. ACME holds about 10 GW of connectivity inventory and declines to commission on temporary access in Rajasthan; its curtailment cost was under 1% of revenue in Q1 FY27. Its FY27 commissioning (about 1.5 GW) is explicitly "subject to substation and transmission availability" at Fatehgarh, Bikaner and Neemuch.
  • Moving up the product ladder. Hybrid (₹3.25), FDRE (₹5.25–6.28) and battery-backed contracts command higher tariffs and fit what discoms want. ACME's under-construction fleet carries a weighted tariff of ₹4.4 per kWh against ₹3.4 for its operating fleet.
  • Going around the discom. KPI Green sells to industrial buyers under group-captive arrangements in Gujarat and holds 5.1 GW of evacuation approvals. About 25% of Adani Green's capacity sells to commercial and industrial customers.
  • Shifting mix toward wind. NTPC Green's operating fleet is 93% solar, but 6.4 GW of its 16.0 GW under construction is wind, which runs at night and lifts blended CUF. Adani Green's wind CUF was 44.4% in Q1 FY27 against 25.3% for solar.
  • Absorbing it. SJVN has about 120 MW curtailed at Bikaner until a shunt reactor arrives and about 300 MW at Khavda because of connectivity delays. Adani Green reports lower solar grid availability at Khavda because of daytime transmission curtailment.

Transmission construction itself is a separate investable theme, dominated by POWERGRID and private licensees and by engineering contractors, which are covered in our civil construction sub-sector report. For generators, the lesson of 2026 is that a gigawatt with a signed contract, a finished line and a storage option is worth far more than a gigawatt with only an award letter. That is the right lens for the under-construction numbers in the peer table: quality of the pipeline matters as much as its size.

Storage becomes an asset class Deep dive

Battery tariffs halved in eighteen months, 272 GWh has been tendered and the CEA wants ten times today's tenders by FY36. The question is who keeps the returns once everyone has a battery.

Storage is the technology that turns cheap noon solar into evening power, and it has gone from pilot to mainstream in about two years. Around 272 GWh of storage had been tendered in India by May 2026: about 142 GWh of pumped-storage hydro (PSP) and about 133 GWh of battery energy storage systems (BESS). About 7.5 GWh of batteries were operating, with another 3 GWh due by December 2026; 15.8 GW of BESS and 15.9 GW of PSP were under construction. The CEA's long-term resource adequacy plan says India needs 87 GWh of storage by FY28, 236 GWh by FY32 and 888 GWh by FY36. Even after the recent rush, tenders cover less than a third of the FY36 need.

Batteries: from ₹3.72 lakh to ₹1.775 lakh per MW per month

Standalone batteries in India are usually procured as a capacity service. The buyer, typically a state utility, pays a fixed monthly charge per MW of battery for 12–15 years and uses the battery to shift energy, normally with two hours of storage per MW of power and one or two cycles a day. The government supports the early projects with viability gap funding: a first tranche covering 13.2 GWh (up to ₹46 lakh per MWh or 30% of capex) and a second tranche, approved on 10 June 2025, covering 30 GWh at ₹18 lakh per MWh with a ₹5,400 crore outlay. The second tranche's ₹33,000 crore of mobilised investment implies a capital cost of about ₹1.1 crore per MWh.

Competition has driven tariffs down at remarkable speed. Gujarat's early tenders cleared at ₹3.72 lakh per MW per month; its Phase VI round in April 2025, without subsidy, at ₹2.80 lakh. Andhra Pradesh cleared at ₹2.08 lakh with 30% subsidy. Then in October 2025 Rajasthan's 1 GW/2 GWh tender set a record low of ₹1.775 lakh, about 15% below the previous low, with eleven winners, most of them new entrants. Prices have since edged back up: Gujarat cleared ₹2.10 lakh in February 2026 and ₹2.32 lakh in August 2026 (won by NLC India Renewables), and NLC's subsidiary won a Haryana tender at ₹2.35 lakh in September 2026.

What does ₹1.775 lakh per MW per month mean in energy terms? Twelve months gives ₹21.3 lakh per MW per year for a two-hour (2 MWh) system. The answer is: barely. At the record-low tariff, a contracted standalone battery earns an unlevered return of roughly 4–8% over its contract, below the 8.4–9% cost of project debt, before allowing for capacity fade and the cost of adding cells to maintain the contracted capacity. Bidders at that tariff are betting on further falls in cell prices, on selling spare cycles in the market, or on a residual life beyond the contract. That is the "winner's curse" risk in the tender data, and it is why the record low has not held. Compare ACME's experience in its merchant phase: in Q1 FY27 its batteries earned ₹226 crore of revenue and ₹189 crore of EBITDA from short-term contracts and exchange sales, an 84% margin and an annualised EBITDA yield of more than 20% on a capitalised cost of about ₹93 lakh per MWh. Merchant spreads are where the excess returns sit today, and they will narrow as 30+ GWh of new batteries come online.

In plain terms

A battery charged with cheap noon solar and discharged at 8 pm is worth a lot when few batteries exist, because evening power is scarce. Once every utility has batteries, the difference between noon and evening prices shrinks, and a battery on a fixed contract becomes a low-margin utility asset. The early movers, ACME in particular, are earning the scarcity premium now; the late bidders at record-low tariffs may struggle to earn their cost of capital.

Pumped hydro: the regulated alternative

Pumped-storage hydro uses surplus power to pump water uphill into a reservoir and releases it through turbines when power is needed, typically storing six to eight hours of output. It is slow and expensive to build but lasts 40 years or more and earns a regulated return under Section 62: 16.5% post-tax ROE for existing storage hydro and a proposed 17% for new projects. Projects awarded by 30 June 2028 also receive a 25-year waiver of inter-state transmission charges. Eleven PSPs totalling 15,870 MW are under construction, including Saidongar-I (3,000 MW) in Maharashtra, Sharavathy (2,000 MW) in Karnataka, and Greenko's Gandhi Sagar and Saundatti; another 8,140 MW has CEA concurrence. India has about 7 GW operating; Greenko's 1,680 MW Pinnapuram project was fully commissioned in FY26 and NTPC's 1,000 MW Tehri PSP is now fully commercial.

Among the listed generators, JSW Energy has the most concrete PSP book: 24 GWh under construction for two 12 GWh contracts, with MSEDCL at ₹84.6 lakh per MW per year (due FY30) and UPPCL at ₹77.2 lakh per MW per year (due FY31), plus 2.4 GWh for PCKL. NTPC has an 18 GW PSP portfolio, NHPC 17.9 GW across 15 projects under survey, and Adani Green more than 5 GW of PSP sites. NTPC is also installing 5 GWh of batteries at 15 coal stations under the CERC's March 2026 regulations, which treat co-located storage as a regulated asset with a draft 14% ROE. That is a third route to storage returns: a lower headline return than merchant batteries, but regulated and therefore bankable at low cost.

The strategic conclusion is that storage returns will split three ways. Contracted standalone batteries at today's tariffs look like low-return infrastructure, attractive only to bidders with very cheap capital or very cheap cells. The merchant cycle: IEX prices and the weather

Only 13% of power changes hands in short-term markets, but that 13% sets a large share of private generators' profit, and its price doubled in a year.

The exchange price is the most volatile number in Indian power and one of the most important for private generators. On IEX, which handles about 85% of exchange volume, the day-ahead market clears a single price for each 15-minute block of the next day, set by the most expensive generator needed to meet demand. When demand is weak and coal plentiful, the marginal megawatt is a cheap pit-head coal unit or surplus solar, and prices sit around ₹3–4 per kWh. When demand surges and coal stocks are tight, the marginal megawatt in the evening is an expensive imported-coal or gas unit, or simply nobody, and prices hit the ₹10 cap.

The listed companies show this leverage in their results. Adani Power realised ₹7.04 per kWh on merchant and short-term sales in Q1 FY27, up 13% year on year and well above the IEX average of ₹5.1 for the period; its merchant realisation had fallen from ₹6.92 in FY24 to ₹5.30 in FY26 before recovering. In plain terms

Most power is sold under 25-year contracts, but the leftover is sold every day on an exchange, and its price can double in a year if the summer is hot and coal is short. Because a plant's costs barely change, almost all of that extra price drops into profit. That is why a few merchant-heavy companies can report profit jumps of 40–70% in a good quarter, and why their share prices move with the weather forecast.

What dampens the cycle

  • The price cap. Day-ahead and real-time prices have been capped at ₹10 per kWh since April 2023, with a separate high-price segment for gas, imported coal and batteries. The cap clips the very top of a scarcity spike.
  • Section 11 supply. When prices rose in 2026, the government ordered Tata's 4,000 MW imported-coal plant and 112 captive plants to run flat out and sell surplus on the exchanges from October to December 2026, adding supply exactly when merchant sellers would profit most.
  • New capacity. About 50 GW a year of additions, 2.3 GW of new coal in Q1 FY27 alone, and 10+ GWh of new batteries by early 2027 all compete for the evening blocks.
  • Market coupling. The CERC's July 2025 order to couple day-ahead markets across exchanges is under appeal and not expected before December 2027. Grid-India's study suggested negligible price impact; the main effect would be on IEX's market share, not on generators.
  • Demand mean-reversion. A normal monsoon in 2027 would bring demand growth back toward 5–6% and prices toward ₹4–5. FY26 showed how fast the market can loosen.

The practical lesson for reading merchant-exposed generators is to separate the contracted base from the merchant kicker, value the base on a utility multiple and the kicker on a normalised price, not on the peak. A ₹7.3 month does not make a ₹7.3 year: FY27's full-year average is more likely to land in the ₹5–6 range, an estimate. The same discipline applies in reverse: FY26's ₹3.8 was a weather trough, not a new normal, because the structural shortage of evening capacity described in Section 08 is still there.

Shortage, glut, stress, repeat: seven decades in one timeline

Indian power runs in cycles of roughly eight to ten years. The 2026 version is unusual: the grid is short of evening capacity and long of noon energy at the same time.

The history of Indian power generation is a sequence of shortages followed by over-building, financial stress and consolidation. The state electricity board era (1948 to the 1990s) produced chronic shortages, with peak deficits of 12–17%; NTPC and NHPC were set up in 1975 to build central capacity. The Electricity Act of 2003 opened generation to private investors. That started a private thermal boom, which ended in the fuel shocks and stressed assets of 2011–2018. The resolution of those assets, the discom reforms of UDAY and the LPS Rules, and the solar boom shaped the industry this report describes.

In plain terms

The next downturn, when it comes, is more likely to hit owners of daytime-only solar first and evening-capable capacity last.

The history also carries a warning about the current thermal revival. The last private coal boom was justified by forecasts of persistent shortage and financed with high leverage; it ended when fuel costs and demand diverged from those forecasts. Today's build is different in important ways (contracts with fuel pass-through, linkage coal under SHAKTI, healthier discoms, lower leverage at the leading IPPs), but the timing risk is the same. Units ordered in 2026 arrive in 2030–2032, when storage may be far cheaper. The contracts, not the forecasts, will decide who is protected.

Reading a power generator: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Three revenue equations, a dozen operating numbers and the definition traps hidden in each. Master these and a generator's quarterly results take ten minutes to read.

Power generation has more native metrics than most industries, because the product is physically measurable to the kilowatt-hour and heavily regulated. That is a gift to the analyst: if you know a plant's capacity, how hard it ran, what it was paid per unit and what its fuel cost, you can rebuild most of its revenue and a good part of its profit. It is also a trap, because companies define the same term differently (gross or net capacity, AC or DC, PLF or availability, company EBITDA or reported operating profit) and the differences can be larger than the trends you are trying to spot. This section works through each metric in turn: what it is, how it is calculated, a worked rupee example, what drives it, what good and weak look like, how the fourteen companies compare, and the trap to watch for.

Start with the equations: three ways a generator earns

Every generator's profit can be written as one of three equations, or a blend of them. The regulated equation applies to Section 62 plants: NTPC's coal and hydro fleets, NHPC and SJVN's dams, NLC's lignite plants, Rosa at Reliance Power, and parts of others. Revenue has two parts. The capacity charge recovers the annual fixed cost (return on equity grossed up for tax, interest on the loan, depreciation, normative O&M and interest on working capital) in proportion to how available the plant was against the 85% norm. The energy charge recovers the fuel cost per unit at a normative heat rate and auxiliary consumption, multiplied by the units the buyer actually scheduled. Profit, to a good first approximation, is the allowed return on regulated equity plus whatever the plant earns by beating the norms, less what the regulator disallows.

In plain terms

A regulated generator's profit is mostly "how much equity has the regulator let it put to work, times 15.5%". A renewable generator's profit is "how many units did it sell at its fixed price, minus a large interest bill". A merchant plant's profit is "how far was the market price above its fuel cost". Before reading any result, decide which equation the company mostly runs on; that tells you which numbers to look at first.

1. Installed and under-construction capacity (GW)

Definition. Installed capacity is the nameplate rating of plants that have reached commercial operation (COD), in megawatts or gigawatts. Under-construction capacity is what has been awarded or ordered but not commissioned. Formula. Sum of unit ratings; growth = (capacity this year ÷ last year) − 1; pipeline intensity = under construction ÷ installed. Worked example. NTPC's group capacity rose from 82,646 MW in June 2025 to 90,904 MW in June 2026, up 10.0%; with 35,702 MW under construction its pipeline intensity is 0.39x. ACME has 2,990 MW operating and 5,080 MW under construction, an intensity of 1.70x: its capacity could nearly treble if everything is built.

Drivers. Capital, equipment (BHEL for coal, listed cell makers for solar), land, connectivity, and for renewables a signed sale agreement. Good, average, weak. Good is a pipeline that is fully tied up: fuel, land, PPA and connectivity in hand, and a high share more than half built. Weak is a pipeline of award letters without buyers or lines. Coal under construction nationally is 89% less than half complete, so even a "good" pipeline is years from revenue.

2. Plant load factor (PLF)

Definition. The energy a thermal plant actually generated as a share of what it could have generated running at full capacity all year. Formula. PLF = gross generation (MWh) ÷ (capacity MW × hours in the period). Worked example. An 800 MW unit that generates 5,256 GWh in a 365-day year has a PLF of 5,256,000 ÷ (800 × 8,760) = 75%. If it were scheduled for 6,307 GWh it would hit 90% and, under CERC rules, earn incentive payments on the units above 85%. Drivers. Demand (and whether discoms schedule the plant), fuel availability, merit order (cheaper plants are dispatched first), outages and solar-hour back-down.

Good, average, weak. Above 80% is good for coal; 65–75% average; below 60% weak. All-India coal ran at 65.2% in FY26 and 73% in Q1 FY27. For a merchant plant, PLF is a direct measure of how much it sold; for a regulated plant, PLF matters mainly above 85%, because the fixed charge depends on availability, not dispatch.

4. Capacity utilisation factor (CUF) for renewables

Definition. The renewable equivalent of PLF: energy delivered as a share of what the plant would produce at full output all year. Formula. CUF = energy (MWh) ÷ (AC capacity MW × 8,760). Worked example. ACME generated 2,020 MU in Q1 FY27 from 2,990 MW; over the quarter's 2,184 hours that is 2,020,000 ÷ (2,990 × 2,184) ≈ 30.9%, its highest ever. At the 23% national solar average the same fleet would have produced about 1,500 MU, roughly a quarter less revenue. Drivers. Irradiation and wind resource (Rajasthan and Gujarat are best for solar; Gujarat, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu for wind), single-axis trackers, a high DC-to-AC ratio (more panels per inverter), technology, cleaning, and the season. Good, average, weak. Solar above 28% good (trackers and over-built DC), 22–27% average, below 20% weak; wind above 35% good, 28–33% average, below 25% weak.

5. Curtailment

Definition. Energy a renewable plant could have produced but was instructed not to deliver because the grid could not absorb or carry it. Formula. Curtailment % = energy backed down ÷ energy available. Worked example. The 300 MW solar plant above produces 788 MU at 30% CUF. The equity IRR falls from 16.2% to 12.3%. Drivers. Transmission readiness, temporary connectivity, local surplus in solar hours, and grid stability limits in weak networks. Good, average, weak. Below 1% good; 1–5% average; above 5% weak. Projects on temporary access have seen 50–60% curtailment during solar hours.

How they compare. Few companies quantify it. ACME says curtailment cost less than 1% of revenue in Q1 FY27. SJVN cited about 120 MW curtailed at Bikaner and about 300 MW at Khavda. Adani Green discloses lower solar grid availability at Khavda. NTPC Green, Adani Green and JSW do not give a percentage, so no company chart is possible: n/a for most of the fourteen. The definition trap. Whether a PPA compensates for curtailment ("deemed generation") varies. Compensated curtailment still earns revenue; uncompensated curtailment does not. National curtailment figures differ by a factor of four between MNRE (8.1 TWh in April–June 2026) and CREA (about 2.1 TWh) because of definitions.

6. Station heat rate and auxiliary consumption

Definition. Heat rate is the fuel energy a thermal plant needs to produce one kWh, in kcal/kWh; lower is better. Auxiliary consumption is the power the station uses itself (pumps, fans, mills), as a percentage of gross generation. Formula. Heat rate = fuel heat input ÷ gross kWh; auxiliary % = station consumption ÷ gross generation. The energy charge rate (ECR) combines them: ECR = heat rate × landed fuel price ÷ fuel calorific value ÷ (1 − aux%). Worked example. At a heat rate of 2,250 kcal/kWh, coal at 3,600 kcal/kg and ₹4,500 per tonne, and 5.75% auxiliary use, ECR = 2,250 ÷ 3,600 × ₹4.5 ÷ 0.9425 = ₹2.98 per kWh. If the plant actually achieves 2,200 kcal/kWh while being paid on a normative 2,250, it saves about ₹0.07 per kWh; on 5,614 MU that is about ₹37 crore a year of profit above the regulated return.

Good, average, weak. Heat rate below 2,250 kcal/kWh is good (supercritical units); 2,300–2,450 average; above 2,500 weak (old subcritical). Auxiliary consumption below 5.5% good, 5.75–7% average, above 8% weak. How they compare. None of the fourteen discloses a fleet heat rate or auxiliary percentage in a comparable way, so this is n/a for all; the clues are indirect, such as NTPC's incentive income and Adani Power's margin over its PPA tariffs. The definition trap. Gross versus net heat rate, and coal calorific value "as received" versus "as billed", can each shift the number by several percent; disputes over coal grading are a long-running source of litigation with Coal India.

In plain terms

A coal plant is paid for fuel as if it burned coal at a standard efficiency. If it is more efficient than the standard, it keeps the difference. These gains are small per unit, a few paise, but on billions of units they add up to hundreds of crores, which is a large part of why NTPC earns more than its headline 15.5% return.

7. Regulated equity and the earned ROE

Definition. The equity base on which a regulator allows a return: normally 30% of the admitted capital cost of commissioned plants (or the actual equity, if lower). Formula. Regulated equity = 30% × admitted capital cost; core regulated profit ≈ allowed ROE × regulated equity. Worked example. SJVN's Buxar coal plant (1,320 MW) has a capitalised cost of about ₹7,000 crore per 660 MW unit, implying regulated equity of about ₹2,100 crore per unit and about ₹4,000 crore for the station. At 15.5%, that is about ₹620 crore a year of allowed return once both units are commercial and running at or above normative availability. Drivers. Commissioning: regulated equity grows only when a project reaches COD and the regulator admits its cost. That makes the commissioning calendar the single most important forecasting input for a PSU.

8. Tariff and realisation (₹/kWh)

Definition. The price a generator is paid per unit. Regulated and many bid thermal contracts are two-part: a fixed (capacity) charge per kWh of availability and a variable (energy) charge per kWh generated. Renewable contracts are usually a single fixed tariff for 25 years. Realisation is the average actually received, including merchant sales and pass-throughs. Formula. Realisation = revenue from power sales ÷ units sold. Worked example. NTPC's average tariff in Q1 FY27 was ₹1.78 fixed plus ₹2.68 variable, ₹4.46 per kWh. For thermal, fuel cost (passed through) and the vintage of the plant (older plants have lower fixed charges); for renewables, the auction year and product (vanilla solar, hybrid, FDRE); for merchant sales, the exchange price and the time of day sold.

15. Definition traps, collected

  • Operating profit is not EBITDA. Screener's operating profit excludes other income; NTPC, NHPC, SJVN, NLC, GMR and RattanIndia book large other income (late-payment surcharges, regulatory income, JV dividends, carrying cost on claims). Company "EBITDA" often includes it. NLC's company EBITDA of ₹7,475 crore equals screener operating profit plus other income exactly.
  • Renewable and thermal margins are not comparable. An 88% margin at ACME and a 30% margin at NTPC say nothing about relative profitability, because a coal plant's revenue includes the fuel it passes through.
  • Capacity comes in many flavours. Installed, operational, commercial, effective, locked-in, portfolio; AC versus DC; consolidated versus group including JVs.
  • PLF versus deemed PLF versus availability. JSW's deemed PLF counts capacity paid for but not scheduled; availability, not PLF, drives a regulated plant's fixed charge.
  • CUF denominators and adjustments. AC or DC; before or after curtailment; "grid-adjusted" or actual.
  • Book ROE versus regulated ROE. Unfinished construction on the balance sheet depresses book ROE at NHPC, SJVN and NTPC Green without any loss of regulated return.
  • Realisation includes pass-throughs. Falling ₹/kWh at a coal generator can mean cheaper fuel, not weaker pricing.
  • Net debt and leverage bases. Gross or net, with or without leases, on actual, run-rate or operational EBITDA.
  • One-offs are frequent. Prior-period income (Adani Power ₹1,386 crore in Q1 FY27), true-ups (SJVN ₹679 crore in FY26), tax credits (NTPC, NLC, JSW and JP Power all had unusual tax lines in FY26 or Q1 FY27), impairments (Reliance Power, SJVN).
  • Double counting in group totals. NTPC consolidates NTPC Green; adding the two overstates revenue, debt and capacity. Adani Power's 24% of JP Power is equity-accounted, so revenue is not double counted.
In plain terms

Most of the mistakes people make with power companies come from comparing numbers that look the same but are defined differently: one company's EBITDA includes interest income and another's does not, one quotes solar capacity in panel watts and another in grid watts. Before comparing two companies on any metric, check that the definition matches.

16. How to read a power generator's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Identify the equation. Regulated, contracted renewable, merchant, or a blend? This decides which of the next steps matter most.
  2. Capacity bridge. Operating capacity at the start and end of the quarter, what was commissioned and on what date. A unit commissioned late in the quarter contributes little revenue but its full interest and depreciation from COD.
  3. Utilisation. PLF and availability for thermal; CUF and grid availability for renewables. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter, because of seasonality.
  4. Units sold and realisation. Units × ₹/kWh should roughly reconcile to revenue; split contracted and merchant units and their prices.
  5. Separate fuel from margin. For thermal, look at fuel cost per unit and EBITDA per unit, not revenue growth.
  6. Strip the one-offs. Prior-period income, true-ups, regulatory deferrals, impairments, exceptional items and unusual tax lines. Recompute "continuing" EBITDA and PAT.
  7. Interest and depreciation. Rising sharply? Check whether it reflects newly capitalised projects (expected, temporary drag) or rising leverage on stalled assets (worse).
  8. Leverage and cash. Net debt, net debt ÷ EBITDA on a stated basis, capex for the quarter against guidance, and any equity raised.
  9. Receivables. Debtor days and overdue dues; any jump in unbilled revenue or change-in-law claims.
  10. Pipeline quality. Under-construction capacity with PPA, land, connectivity and financial closure; commissioning dates moved or held; storage and FDRE contracts signed.

Done in that order, the ten steps take about ten minutes for most companies and catch the issues that move the stock: a merchant price tailwind mistaken for structural improvement, a one-off true-up mistaken for growth, a capitalisation drag mistaken for deterioration, or a pipeline that looks large but lacks buyers or wires. The single most useful habit is to rebuild revenue from units and price before reading the management commentary.

Next, compare growth with the pipeline. The global lens: China's scale, India's prices, the world's multiples

India builds a tenth of what China builds, pays among the world's lowest prices for solar and batteries, still burns far more coal than most, and values its renewable developers at multiples no global peer commands.

India is the world's third-largest electricity producer, at about 1,830 TWh a year, behind China (about 9,500 TWh) and the United States (about 4,300 TWh), and about 6% of global generation; those global figures are from Ember and are memory-based. It is also the third-largest solar market. But the gap with China is enormous. China added 315 GW of solar and 119 GW of wind in calendar 2025 alone, according to its National Energy Administration, taking its solar fleet to about 1.2 TW. India's net additions of solar and wind in FY26 were about 51 GW, the best year in its history and roughly an eighth of China's.

The consequence for investors is that Indian renewable returns are thin per unit, and depend heavily on low financing costs and high utilisation.

On valuation, the comparison is stark. Indian regulated generators trade roughly in line with, or below, global regulated utilities, which typically trade at 15–20 times earnings. Indian renewable developers trade at 49–129 times, against 15–25 times for global leaders such as NextEra and Iberdrola. One global trend is now arriving in India: data-centre demand. In the United States, data centres have become the main driver of electricity demand growth and of long-term contracts for firm power, including nuclear. India's ministry projects about 26 GW of data-centre demand by FY32 (a headline figure only), and round-the-clock renewable contracts with large corporate buyers are the natural product for it. Green hydrogen, if it scales, would add a second large round-the-clock industrial buyer. Both favour developers who can deliver firm power, not just daytime energy.

In plain terms

India buys solar power and batteries more cheaply than almost any country, builds about an eighth as much as China each year, and still relies on coal for seven-tenths of its electricity. Its state-owned generators are priced like utilities anywhere; its private renewable developers are priced far above their global peers, which means investors are paying in advance for growth that still has to clear India's grid and contract bottlenecks.

Technology and disruption: cheaper batteries, flexible coal, small reactors

The technologies that will reshape the sub-sector are mostly already here. What changes over the next decade is their cost, and the market rules that let them earn.

Storage cost deflation is the biggest disruptor. Lithium-iron-phosphate cell prices fell from about US$156 per kWh in 2022 to under US$100 in 2024 (memory-based), and Indian battery tariffs followed: from ₹3.72 lakh per MW per month to a record ₹1.775 lakh within about eighteen months. Batteries are already operating at the margin of the grid, supplying up to 4.1 GW at the night peak from about 7.5 GWh installed. If cell costs keep falling, storage threatens the evening premium that peaking gas and merchant coal earn today. In the near term it complements coal rather than replacing it: NTPC is installing batteries at its own coal stations so that it can shift output and earn a regulated return on the batteries, and the CERC now has a tariff framework for exactly that.

Firm renewable bundles are converging with new coal. FDRE contracts, which combine solar, wind, batteries and over-built capacity to guarantee a delivery shape, cleared at ₹5.25 per kWh round-the-clock in August 2026, below the roughly ₹6.0 of recent coal contracts. Peak-only versions cleared at ₹5.99–6.28. Convergence of firm renewable and new coal costs by about FY28–30 is a reasonable estimate. It does not make coal obsolete, because coal covers seasonal gaps that a four-hour battery cannot, but it caps how much a discom will pay for new thermal capacity after the current wave.

Manufacturing localisation is raising costs now to reduce risk later. From June 2026, projects must use solar cells from the approved List-II, which had about 30 GW enlisted against about 193 GW of module capacity. New cell and wafer plants (ReNew's 6 GW ingot and wafer line, Navitas's 3.6 GW of cells, and the large integrated makers) will ease the bottleneck over two to three years. Until then, domestic-content modules cost 60–70% more than imported-cell ones, which is why the capex sensitivity in Section 10 matters.

The grid itself is changing. The CEA now requires grid-forming capability for batteries above 50 MW, aimed at the weak-grid problems seen at Bhadla and Khavda, where large solar clusters strain voltage stability. Better forecasting and scheduling reduce deviation charges for renewables. Coal plants are being made more flexible: running down to 55% of capacity in solar hours, ramping faster, and co-firing biomass (NTPC's group co-fired 5.7 lakh tonnes in Q1 FY27). Flue-gas desulphurisation retrofits continue, though deadlines have been relaxed for many plants.

Nuclear has reopened to private capital. The SHANTI Act, passed in December 2025, repealed the Atomic Energy Act of 1962 and the 2010 nuclear liability law and allows Indian private companies to own and operate nuclear plants. The national target is 100 GW of nuclear by 2047, against 8.8 GW today. NTPC is building a 2.8 GW plant at Mahi Banswara with NPCIL and has identified 30 sites for small modular reactors through a dedicated subsidiary; Adani, JSW, Reliance and Tata have expressed interest. It is a 2035-plus earnings story, but it is the only zero-carbon source of firm baseload other than hydro.

Market design is the quiet disruptor. Day-ahead market coupling across exchanges, capacity or resource-adequacy markets (mentioned in the January 2026 draft National Electricity Policy), electricity futures on MCX and NSE (launched in 2025, a memory-based date) and virtual PPAs would all change how generators are paid for flexibility and firmness. A capacity market in particular would turn today's informal evening-peak premium into an explicit payment, which would favour anyone with dispatchable capacity.

Bull versus bear

The structural case is strong and widely known. The arguments are about timing, delivery and price.

The bull case

  • Demand doubles in a decade. The CEA projects 459 GW of peak and 3,365 BU of energy by FY36; per-capita use is 40% of the world average; data centres, hydrogen and electrification add new firm loads.
  • Firm power is scarce. Evening peaks, record exchange prices and 22–23 GW of new coal contracts at about ₹6 show that dispatchable capacity commands a premium, and the leaders (NTPC, Adani Power, JSW, ACME) own it.
  • The counterparty is fixed. Legacy dues fell 98% under the LPS Rules; discoms are close to break-even; rating upgrades outnumber downgrades four to one; debt is cheaper.
  • Regulated compounding. NTPC's regulated equity grows about 9% a year at a 15.5% allowed return, and NHPC's could rise two-thirds in FY27 as dams are commissioned.
  • Cheap assets. Most operating coal trades at less than half its ₹12 crore per MW replacement cost, and the regulated PSUs trade at 10–20 times earnings, near global utilities.
  • Storage is a new growth market. 272 GWh tendered against a CEA need of 888 GWh by FY36; early movers are earning more than 20% EBITDA on capex.

The bear case

  • Delivery, not demand, is the bottleneck. 44.8 GW of renewables lack buyers; transmission is at a quarter of its FY27 target; curtailment has quadrupled; projects are slipping (Arun-3, Reliance Power's 2029 connectivity).
  • Capex before earnings. Interest and depreciation on new projects are already compressing profit at NHPC, JSW and SJVN; free cash flow is negative at Adani Power, Adani Green, NTPC Green, ACME, KPI and SJVN.
  • Renewable valuations assume perfect execution. 49–129 times earnings against 15–25 times for global peers; a two-point fall in equity IRRs from curtailment or cell costs would erase much of the value.
  • Merchant peaks do not last. FY26's ₹3.8 exchange price came only a year before FY27's spike; caps, Section 11 orders, new coal and batteries all lean against high prices.
  • Battery tariff compression. Record-low bids imply unlevered returns of 4–8%; the scarcity premium will shrink as 30+ GWh of new batteries arrive.
  • Governance and legal overhangs. Criminal and regulatory proceedings at Reliance Power, pledged promoter shares at KPI, GMR and JP Power, contingent lender claims at JP Power, promoter-funded debt at RattanIndia.

Weighing the two, the bull case on the industry is stronger than the bull case on many of its stocks. The structural need for firm capacity, storage and transmission is not in doubt, and the payment discipline imposed in 2022 has made the business far safer than it was. What is in doubt is who captures the value. Regulated generators will earn their allowed return on whatever they commission; the risk there is construction delay, not demand. Private thermal IPPs with contracted pipelines and low leverage, Adani Power above all, are capturing the coal revival now, and their main risk is a faster-than-expected arrival of cheap storage after 2030. Renewable developers face the widest range of outcomes: those with storage, firm contracts, connectivity and cheap capital will become strong businesses, while those holding plain solar awards without buyers or lines may see returns well below their cost of capital.

Two events would most change the balance. On the bull side, the signing of most of the 44.8 GW of stranded renewables under the relief package, together with a step-up in transmission additions, would show that the delivery race can be won and would support the renewable valuations. On the bear side, a cool, wet summer in 2027 that sends exchange prices back toward ₹4, combined with a further fall in battery tariffs, would expose both the merchant-heavy coal names and the highest-valued developers at the same time.

In plain terms

Almost everyone agrees India needs much more electricity and much more of it in the evening. The disagreement is about price: whether today's share prices already assume that every plant will be built on time, connected to the grid and paid for. The cheapest stocks are the slow, regulated ones; the most expensive are the ones whose plans are furthest from being finished.

The Q2 FY27 results season is the first test of the thesis in this report. July–September is seasonally weak for coal (the monsoon lifts hydro and wind and cuts cooling demand) and strong for hydro, so comparisons should be with Q2 FY26, not with the record first quarter. For the merchant-exposed names, the question is how much of September's ₹7.3 exchange price they captured and whether coal costs rose with it. For NHPC, SJVN, JSW and NTPC Green, the question is how large the interest and depreciation drag from newly commissioned assets has become relative to the new revenue. In plain terms

If you watch only three things, watch the monthly exchange price through the winter, the number of stranded solar projects that sign buyers, and the price of the next big battery tender. Together they tell you whether the evening shortage is lasting, whether the renewable pipeline can be delivered, and how much of the storage premium will survive.

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

Glossary of power-sector terms
TermMeaning
ALMMApproved List of Models and Manufacturers: the government list of solar modules (List-I) and, from 1 Jun 2026, solar cells (List-II) that utility projects must use.
AT&C lossesAggregate technical and commercial losses: the share of power a discom buys but never gets paid for, through line losses, theft or non-collection.
Annual fixed cost (AFC)The yearly fixed charges a regulated plant recovers: return on equity, interest, depreciation, O&M and working-capital interest.
Auxiliary consumptionPower a station uses itself, as a share of its gross output.
BESSBattery energy storage system. Sized in MW (power) and MWh (energy); a 2-hour system has twice as many MWh as MW.
BU, MU, GW, MWBillion units (kWh), million units, gigawatts, megawatts. 1 BU = 1,000 MU = 1 TWh.
Capacity charge / energy chargeThe two parts of a regulated tariff: a fixed payment for being available, and a variable payment for fuel per unit generated.
CERC / SERCCentral and state electricity regulatory commissions, which set tariffs and market rules.
CODCommercial operation date, from which a plant earns its tariff and starts depreciation and interest in the P&L.
CUFCapacity utilisation factor: a renewable plant's actual output as a share of its maximum possible output.
CurtailmentOutput a renewable plant is instructed to cut because the grid cannot take it.
Day-ahead market (DAM) / real-time market (RTM)Exchange segments where power for the next day, or for the next hour, is traded in 15-minute blocks.
DiscomDistribution company: the utility that buys power and sells it to consumers.
DSMDeviation settlement mechanism: penalties and payments for deviating from scheduled generation or drawal.
FDREFirm and dispatchable renewable energy: contracts that bundle solar, wind and storage to guarantee delivery in defined hours.
FGDFlue-gas desulphurisation: equipment to cut sulphur emissions from coal plants.
GNA / T-GNAGeneral network access to the inter-state grid; temporary access for projects whose permanent lines are not ready.
Heat rateFuel energy needed to generate one kWh, in kcal/kWh; lower is more efficient.
IPPIndependent power producer: a private generator selling to discoms or other buyers.
ISTSInter-state transmission system; ISTS charges are the fees for using it, waived for renewables commissioned by mid-2025 and phasing back in.
LoALetter of award from an auctioneer; a project is bankable only when the PPA and PSA follow.
LPS RulesLate Payment Surcharge Rules 2022, which penalise late payment by discoms and rescheduled legacy dues.
Merchant powerPower sold without a long-term contract, on exchanges or short-term deals.
PAFPlant availability factor: share of time a plant was declared available; drives fixed-charge recovery for regulated plants.
PLFPlant load factor: a thermal plant's actual output as a share of its maximum possible output.
PPA / PSAPower purchase agreement (generator to buyer) and power sale agreement (intermediary to discom).
PSPPumped-storage project: hydro storage that pumps water uphill when power is cheap and releases it when needed.
REIARenewable energy implementing agency (SECI, NTPC, NHPC, SJVN) that runs auctions and stands between generator and discom.
Regulated equityThe equity base, normally 30% of admitted capital cost, on which the regulator allows a return.
RTCRound-the-clock supply.
Section 11 / 62 / 63Sections of the Electricity Act 2003: government directions in emergencies; cost-plus regulated tariffs; tariffs discovered by competitive bidding.
SHAKTI B(iv)A coal-linkage scheme under which plants that win state supply contracts receive coal allocations.
SHANTI ActThe 2025 law that opened nuclear power to Indian private companies and repealed the old nuclear liability regime.
VGFViability gap funding: a government capital subsidy, used for early battery projects.
