00

The one-page view: India sold more cars than ever and its carmakers earned less on each

A tax cut lit up demand, a war in West Asia lit up costs, and the four companies in this bucket turned out to be three very different businesses.

India's passenger-vehicle (PV) industry has just had the strangest eighteen months in its history. Domestic wholesales reached a record 46.43 lakh units in FY26 (+7.9%), then grew another 28.3% in the first half of FY27, the fastest pace since the post-COVID rebound. Exports hit a record 9.05 lakh. Small cars, which the industry had written off as a shrinking relic, came back to life after the GST rate on them was cut from 29–31% to 18% on 22 September 2025. And yet the listed carmakers made less money. In the June 2026 quarter the four members of this sub-sector grew combined revenue 15.8% while their operating profit fell 17.1%. Maruti Suzuki's EBITDA margin dropped from 12.6% to 8.6% of net sales; Hyundai's from 13.3% to 9.3%.

The reason is the second shock of 2026. The West Asia war that began on 28 February pushed Brent above $110 a barrel, raised the price of steel, aluminium, copper, polymers, rubber and precious metals, and helped drive the rupee to around ₹95 a dollar. Material costs at Maruti rose from 74.5% to 80.5% of net sales in a single year. Petrol in Delhi went from ₹94.77 to ₹102.12 a litre in four hikes in May, and on 7 October the Reserve Bank raised the repo rate to 5.50%, its first increase since February 2023. Demand has held so far; costs have not.

This bucket contains four listed companies, and they are not alike. Maruti Suzuki (58.72% owned by Suzuki Motor) and Hyundai Motor India (82.5% owned by Hyundai Motor Company) are licensed subsidiaries of foreign carmakers, selling mass-market cars at home and exporting through their parents' networks. Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles (TMPV), the old Tata Motors listing, is mostly Jaguar Land Rover (81% of FY26 revenue), plus Tata's own Indian car and electric-vehicle business. The fourth, Tata Motors Ltd (TMCV), is a truck and bus maker. It sits here because the library's sub-sector map inherited the industry tag of the pre-demerger Tata Motors; NSE and screener now classify it as Capital Goods › Commercial Vehicles, and its natural home is the commercial vehicles report. We keep it as a member, give it its own peer-table row and profile, and keep its trucks out of every passenger-vehicle volume number in this report.

Five forces explain almost everything that happens to these stocks, and the rest of this report is organised around them:

  • Tax and fuel set the affordability line. GST 2.0 cut small-car prices by about 8.5% and revived the entry segment that Maruti dominates (its mini cars grew 124% in Q1 FY27). The 2026 fuel shock and the October rate hike push the line back. FY27–FY28 volume is a race between the two (Section 04).
  • Commodity and currency inflation is now the margin variable, not volume. Volumes rose 25–30% while margins fell 3–4 points. Price increases lag costs by a quarter or more (Section 05).
  • The SUV-led share war. Behind Maruti's ~39% sit four near-equal challengers: Mahindra & Mahindra (14.2% of FY26 wholesales, now #2), Tata (13.6%) and Hyundai (12.6%, down from #2 to #4), with Toyota a step behind. Utility vehicles are 68% of the market (Section 07).
  • JLR is the profit pool's swing factor, and it is structurally weaker than it was. A five-week cyberattack shutdown, US tariffs, a collapsing China business, rising discounts and an expensive electric transition took JLR's EBIT margin from 8.5% to 0.7% in FY26 (Section 12).
  • India as an export hub, and the parent's share. Suzuki and Hyundai Motor Group are making India their small-car and emerging-market base. Royalties alone take about 37% of Maruti's profit and 36% of Hyundai's (Section 09).

In plain terms

Indian car buyers have never bought more, and the companies have never been busier, but the cost of building each car has risen faster than its price. Maruti made about ₹63,000 of operating profit per car in the June 2026 quarter against about ₹87,500 a year before. The question for the next year is whether price increases catch up with costs before higher fuel prices and interest rates cool demand.

01

How a car earns: from Suzuki's licence to the dealer's forecourt

An Indian carmaker is an assembler of bought-in parts. Around 80% of every rupee goes to suppliers, so a few points of cost decide the year.

A car is built from about 25,000 to 30,000 parts. The carmaker (in industry language, the original equipment manufacturer or OEM) designs or licenses the vehicle, buys most of those parts from component suppliers, presses, welds and paints the body, assembles the car, and sells it wholesale to a franchised dealer. The dealer sells it to the customer, usually on a loan, and earns more later from servicing, spare parts, insurance and the eventual used-car trade-in. Every step leaves something on the table, but the largest slice by far goes to suppliers of steel, aluminium, plastics, electronics and, for electric cars, batteries.

The OEM keeps what is left between Step 2 and Step 4: about 9–13% of net sales as EBITDA at the mass-market makers in a normal year, much less when commodities spike.

Technology and platform. Maruti builds Suzuki-designed cars under licence and paid Suzuki a royalty of ₹5,392 crore in FY26, 3.09% of net sales. Hyundai Motor India paid its parent ₹1,975 crore, 2.79% of revenue. Tata develops its own platforms (ALFA, OMEGA-ARC and the acti.ev electric architecture) and pays no royalty to a carmaker parent, though Tata group companies pay a brand fee to Tata Sons. JLR owns its brands and engineering outright. This single difference, rented technology versus owned technology, explains much of how the four companies behave: the licensed subsidiaries spend little on research and earn steady margins; the owners spend heavily and live with more volatile returns.

Inputs. Raw materials and bought-out components are 75–80% of net sales at a mass-market OEM. Steel is the largest single material by weight, followed by aluminium, copper (in wiring harnesses and motors), plastics and rubber, glass, and precious metals in catalytic converters. An electric car adds a battery pack that is 35–45% of its cost and rare-earth magnets in its motor. The other side of this ledger is the auto components industry, whose content per vehicle has been growing faster than vehicle production as cars add airbags, screens, sunroofs and driver-assistance sensors. Tyres alone are a meaningful cost item; the tyre makers raised prices 11–15% in 2026.

The plant. Car assembly is capital-intensive but not labour-intensive: Maruti's employee cost was 4.9% of net sales in Q1 FY27. That is why scale matters so much. A plant costs the same to staff and depreciate whether it runs at 70% or 100% of capacity, and every car above break-even carries only its materials and variable costs.

The channel. Indian carmakers sell to dealers, not consumers. The dealer pays for the car before dispatch (Maruti runs a negative cash conversion cycle of about −19 days: dealers pay upfront, suppliers are paid in about 61 days), finances its stock through banks, and earns a gross margin of roughly 3–5% plus incentives. Most cars, around 75–80%, are bought on credit, so interest rates and bank appetite matter directly.

After the sale. Spares and servicing, extended warranties, insurance commissions and used-car platforms (Maruti True Value, Hyundai H-Promise) are the steadiest and highest-margin slice of the chain, though Maruti and Hyundai do not disclose them separately. Exports run through the parent: Maruti's sales to Suzuki rose 74% to ₹25,467 crore in FY26 as Suzuki distributed Indian-built cars, including the electric e-Vitara, in its overseas markets.

JLR's structure is the outlier: materials are a smaller share because a Range Rover sells for far more than its parts cost, but staff, marketing, dealer incentives, warranty and tariffs absorb much more, and its depreciation and amortisation (about 8–9% of revenue, largely capitalised engineering) sits below EBITDA. That makes JLR a high-fixed-cost business whose profit swings violently with volume. TMCV's truck business carries a lower material ratio than Maruti and a higher margin, which is one reason its margin held at 11.7% in Q2 FY27 despite a 5.9-point hit from variable costs.

In plain terms

An Indian carmaker buys about four-fifths of what it sells. If steel, aluminium and plastics rise 8% and it cannot raise prices, its operating profit can fall by a third. That is why the commodity cycle, not the number of cars sold, has decided FY27 so far.

Who captures the rest. The parent's royalty (2.8–3.1% of sales) is taken before the listed company's margin, so it is equivalent to about a third of the listed company's profit. Dealers keep 3–5% of the price. Banks and finance companies earn a spread on roughly four in five cars. The government takes the largest single slice of all: 18% GST on a small car and 40% on everything else, plus road tax and registration that vary by state. On a ₹8 lakh ex-showroom small car, GST is about ₹1.22 lakh (ILLUSTRATIVE, at 18% on a pre-tax price of ₹6.78 lakh), more than the OEM's entire operating profit on the car.

02

46 lakh cars a year: the size of the market and the shape of its record

India is the world's third-largest car market, but it still has only about 30 cars per thousand people. FY26 broke the record; FY27 is breaking it again.

Domestic passenger-vehicle wholesales (dispatches from factories to dealers, the figure SIAM reports and the one that drives company revenue) were 46,43,439 units in FY26, up 7.9% and a record. Production was 55,39,115 units and exports another record of 9,05,200 (+17.5%). Over FY21–FY26 domestic sales compounded at 11.3% a year, exports at 17.5% and production at 12.6%, but that period starts from the COVID trough. From the previous peak in FY19 (33.77 lakh), growth was only about 4.6% a year: the industry lost three years to the NBFC credit crisis, the BS-VI emissions transition and the pandemic, then two more to a semiconductor shortage that capped supply.

The shape of FY26 matters as much as its total. The first half fell 1.4% as buyers postponed purchases ahead of the expected tax cut; the second half rose 16.7% after GST 2.0 took effect on 22 September 2025. That pattern then carried into FY27: Q4 FY26 grew 13.2%, Q1 FY27 grew 25.9% (12,73,811 units) and Q2 FY27 grew 30.7% (13,60,687 units). Exports are the quieter half of the story. India shipped 9.05 lakh passenger vehicles in FY26, more than double the 4.04 lakh of FY21. Maruti accounted for 4,47,774 of them (49.5%) and Hyundai for 1,90,125 (21.0%), so the two Asian-owned subsidiaries ship seven in ten Indian-made export cars. The destinations are Latin America (Mexico and Chile), the Middle East, Africa (South Africa above all), South-East Asia and, increasingly, Japan and Europe, where Maruti now sends the Fronx, Jimny and the electric e-Vitara. Two of those markets turned hostile in 2026: Mexico raised duties on cars from countries without a trade agreement, and the West Asia war disrupted shipping to the Gulf. PV exports grew 8.8% in Q1 FY27, a record first quarter, then fell 5.2% in Q2 to 2.29 lakh.

The value of the market. At an industry average realisation of roughly ₹9 lakh per car before GST, domestic sales were worth about ₹4.2 lakh crore in FY26 and exports (at about ₹6.8 lakh a car) another ₹0.6 lakh crore, a total of around ₹4.8 lakh crore at factory-gate prices (INDICATIVE estimate). At retail prices including GST, consumers spent something like ₹5.5–6 lakh crore. The three Indian brands in this bucket (Maruti, Hyundai and Tata's Indian PV business) had FY26 revenue of about ₹3.13 lakh crore, roughly two-thirds of the factory-gate pool.

India in the world. India is the world's third-largest market for passenger and light vehicles after China (about 27–30 million a year) and the United States (about 16 million), and accounts for roughly 5–6% of global light-vehicle sales of about 90 million (approximate figures from industry trackers). The more important number is ownership. India has roughly 30–35 cars per thousand people, against more than 200 in China and about 800 in the United States. That gap is the foundation of every long-term bull case for the sector, and the reason Indian carmakers trade at multiples their own parents can only envy. Maruti's chairman now expects the industry to reach 6.1–6.3 million units by FY31, with small cars growing "significantly faster" than in the past five years; that forecast was raised because of GST 2.0.

In plain terms

India buys about 46 lakh new cars a year and makes 55 lakh, sending the difference abroad. That makes it a big market by global standards but an under-penetrated one: fewer than one Indian in thirty owns a car. Each record year still leaves most of the long-run growth ahead, which is why investors pay up for these companies even when a single year disappoints.

03

Why Indians buy cars: affordability, the SUV habit, fuel and credit

Demand is a ratio of price to income, financed. Each of the four moving parts shifted sharply between 2024 and 2026.

Affordability. The problem the industry faced in FY23–FY25 was simple. Entry-level car prices had risen by roughly 35–45% since FY19, pushed up by the BS-VI emission rules, mandatory safety equipment (airbags, ABS, electronic stability control) and commodity inflation, while the incomes of the lower-middle-class households who buy those cars had not kept pace (approximate industry estimates). First-time buyers drifted to used cars or stayed on two-wheelers, and FY25 domestic sales grew only 2.0%. GST 2.0 changed the arithmetic overnight: the rate on small cars fell from 29–31% to 18%, cutting on-road prices by about 8.5%. Maruti's mini segment (Alto, S-Presso) grew 124% in Q1 FY27, and first-time buyers rose to 54% of its sales from 51% the quarter before. Section 04 examines that experiment in detail.

Premiumisation and the SUV habit. Indian buyers trade up rather than in. Utility vehicles, SIAM's term for SUVs, crossovers and people-carriers (MPVs), were roughly 28% of domestic PV sales in FY19, about half by FY23, more than 60% by FY24 and 68% in Q2 FY27. Maruti's internal estimate of the FY26 market puts SUVs at 56.8% and MPVs at 10.3%, against 21.0% for hatchbacks and 8.5% for sedans. About half of first-time buyers now choose a compact SUV. The shift explains the re-ordering of the league table (Section 07): Mahindra, which sells only SUVs, and Tata, whose Nexon and Punch are compact SUVs, gained share, while Maruti's share of utility vehicles remains below its share of the overall market.

Note that the GST line does not follow body style. It follows length and engine size: a car under four metres with a petrol engine of up to 1,200 cc (or a diesel of up to 1,500 cc) pays 18%, whatever it looks like. So the Nexon, Venue, Brezza, Punch, Exter and Fronx, all compact SUVs, now pay the same 18% as a hatchback, while a Creta pays 40%. The SUV trend and the small-car revival are therefore not opposites: a large part of the "small car" revival is small SUVs.

Fuel economics. Running costs became a purchase criterion again in 2026. After four increases in May, petrol cost ₹102.12 a litre in Delhi, diesel ₹95.20 and compressed natural gas (CNG) ₹83.09 a kilogram. For a compact car, that works out to roughly ₹6.0 per kilometre on petrol, ₹3.3 on CNG and ₹1.1–1.3 on home-charged electricity (ILLUSTRATIVE, assuming 17 km a litre, 25 km a kilogram and about 140 Wh per kilometre at ₹8 a unit). CNG was already 21.98% of PV retail sales in FY26, about 40% of Maruti's sales, 27% of Tata's and 18% of Hyundai's. Tata's electric-car bookings ran at about 3.5 times the pre-war average after the fuel hikes.

Credit. Roughly 75–80% of new cars are financed. The RBI cut the repo rate from 6.50% to 5.25% between February and December 2025 (125 basis points), and auto-loan rates followed, which SIAM credited for part of FY26's strength. On 7 October 2026 the RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% and described its stance as "calibrated tightening". For a ₹8 lakh, five-year loan, a 50-basis-point rise in the lending rate adds about ₹200 to the monthly instalment, roughly 1.2% (ILLUSTRATIVE: ₹16,606 a month at 9.0% against ₹16,802 at 9.5%). The direct effect is small; the signal, that the cutting cycle is over, matters more.

Rural and replacement demand. Rural India is about 45% of Maruti's domestic sales, and Hyundai's rural share reached a record 25.9% in Q1 FY27 (rural growth +23.2% against urban +2.8%). Good monsoons in 2025 and 2026 supported farm incomes, as the tractors and two-wheelers reports also show. The FY26 income-tax cut, which raised the zero-tax threshold to ₹12 lakh, and the constitution of the 8th Pay Commission are the two policy supports OEMs cite for urban salaried buyers.

Supply, not demand, set several quarters. It is easy to forget that the industry's volume is not always a demand number. China's licensing of rare-earth magnet exports from April 2025 left Indian OEMs with four to six weeks of inventory and forced Maruti to cut its April–September 2025 e-Vitara production plan from 26,500 to 8,200 units. A fire at Mobis India, Hyundai's in-house component supplier, cost Hyundai about 13,900 cars (mostly Cretas) in June 2026. Tata's Sanand plant lost five days of production in July 2026, its Sierra is constrained by engine castings and sheet metal, and Maruti says it ran at "nearly full capacity utilisation" in the second half of FY26 with 1.9 lakh pending bookings at the end of March. When a company's volume lags the market, check the supply side before concluding that its products are losing.

In plain terms

A car purchase in India is decided by four things: the sticker price after tax, the monthly loan instalment, the running cost, and whether the buyer feels richer. In 2025 all four improved together. In 2026 the tax cut stayed, but fuel became dearer and loans stopped getting cheaper. So far buyers have absorbed it; the festive season of October–November 2026 is the first real test.

04

The GST experiment: what an 8.5% price cut did to India's small car Deep dive

The biggest tax change for cars since the 2008–09 excise cuts revived a segment the industry had written off. A fuel shock and a rate hike are now testing how much of it lasts.

On 22 September 2025, the GST Council's "GST 2.0" reform collapsed the old structure of 28% GST plus a compensation cess of 1–22% into two rates for cars. A small car (petrol, CNG or LPG engine of up to 1,200 cc, or diesel up to 1,500 cc, and no longer than 4,000 mm) now pays 18%. Every other car and utility vehicle pays a flat 40%, with no cess. Electric vehicles stay at 5%. The Ministry of Finance's own FAQ on the reform describes the old regime for larger vehicles as "28% GST and compensation cess ranging from 17–22% with overall incidence 45–50%". The compensation cess, introduced in 2017 to repay states for revenue lost in the GST transition, was abolished.

The government made small cars about 8.5% cheaper and people bought them again: Maruti sold more than twice as many Altos and S-Pressos as a year earlier. How much of the boom is real

Three distortions make the post-reform numbers look stronger than the underlying trend. First, the base. Buyers postponed purchases in August and September 2025, waiting for the cut, then rushed in October and November. What could undo it

The tax cut is permanent; the other parts of the affordability equation are not. Since the reform: petrol in Delhi has risen from ₹94.77 to ₹102.12 a litre and CNG from ₹77.09 to ₹83.09 a kilogram (four hikes each in May 2026); the RBI has reversed 25 of its 125 basis points of cuts; and the rupee has fallen to about ₹95 a dollar, which forced every OEM to raise prices. Maruti raised prices in June and August 2026, Hyundai three times in calendar 2026 (about 100 basis points in total), Tata by about 1% (April and July). On a ₹7 lakh car, ₹7 more per litre of petrol over 12,000 km a year at 17 km a litre adds about ₹5,000 to the annual running cost (ILLUSTRATIVE), small next to the ₹60,000 saved on the purchase price by the tax cut, but recurring. The 57th GST Council on 8 October 2026 made no change to vehicle rates.

The cut also changed the competitive maths of electric cars. Before the reform an EV paid 5% against 29% for a small petrol car and up to 50% for a large SUV; now it pays 5% against 18% and 40%. The advantage over a large SUV is still large; against a small car it shrank by about half. That matters more for Tata's Tiago.ev and Punch.ev than for its Harrier.ev.

The two-wheelers report describes the same pattern in that market: a two-speed recovery in which the entry segment rebounded hard on the tax cut and fuel prices then tested it. In cars, the first clean read will be the October–November 2026 festive season (Navratri ran roughly 11–20 October and Diwali falls around 8 November), measured against the very strong October–November 2025 rush. Hyundai's management expects industry growth in the second half of FY27 to be in the "lower single digits". If retail holds above that while dealers carry 43–45 days of stock, the tax cut will have created a durably larger entry market; if not, the industry will face a stock correction in Q4 FY27.

In plain terms

The tax cut made a small car about ₹60,000 cheaper. The 2026 fuel hikes cost a typical owner about ₹5,000 a year more, and loans have stopped getting cheaper. Buyers have so far decided the deal is still good. The risk is not that the boom reverses, but that next year's growth looks weak against this year's inflated base and that dealers are carrying more cars than they can sell.

The materials squeeze: commodities and the rupee now set the margin

In FY27 the volume boom and the margin collapse arrived together. Material costs, the currency and the timing of price increases explain the gap.

The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 hit carmakers through every input at once. Brent crude averaged about $117 a barrel in April and about $126 across early October, with a high of $135.51 on 2 October. Polymer prices rose 43% in March after the Strait of Hormuz closed; natural rubber reached about ₹285 a kilogram; copper and aluminium traded near records, supported by the United States' 50% Section 232 tariffs on their derivatives; domestic steel rose with coking-coal prices and the safeguard duty on imports; and precious metals used in catalytic converters climbed. The rupee fell from an FY25 average of ₹84.8 a dollar to about ₹95 now (a record low near ₹96.8 in early October), raising the cost of imported electronics, battery cells and every dollar-linked commodity. Tyre makers alone raised prices 11–15% in 2026.

Capacity decisions in this industry are made five years ahead and paid for over fifteen. A carmaker that adds a 2.5-lakh line when the market is about to slow ends up with a plant running at 70%, as Hyundai found in Chennai and JLR in the UK. A carmaker that under-builds loses share it may never regain, which is Maruti's fear after FY26. Investors should watch the ratio of a company's capacity additions to its realistic share of the market's growth: Maruti adding 5 lakh units in a year when the market might grow 3–5 lakh is a bet that it will take more than its share, partly through exports.

In plain terms

Maruti sold every car it could build in late FY26 and is adding two new production lines this year. Whoever has spare capacity when demand is strong wins share; whoever has spare capacity when demand slows loses margin.

The parent as customer: export allocation

The parent's role in exports is growing faster than its role as licensor. Maruti's sales to Suzuki rose 74% in FY26 to ₹25,467 crore, as Suzuki took Indian-built cars, including the e-Vitara, into its network of about 120 markets. Suzuki is now Maruti's licensor, its export distributor and its largest single customer, and the price at which those cars are sold to Suzuki is a related-party transfer price. Maruti's royalty rate fell from 3.47% to 3.09% of net sales in FY26, partly because exports through Suzuki and lower-royalty models grew; investors should read that drop together with the export price, not on its own.

The same logic applies at Hyundai. India is becoming Hyundai Motor Group's emerging-markets base: the new Venue is built only at Pune and shipped to 29 markets, heading for 35; the Exter in left-hand drive goes to 13 markets and the Verna to more than 25. Hyundai targets exports at about 30% of volume by 2030 (25.6% of revenue in FY26). But SUVs are only 13–14% of its exports against 70% of domestic sales, and the parent decides which markets the Indian plant serves and which go to Korea, Indonesia, Türkiye or the Czech Republic. Hyundai's FY26 related-party purchases show the web: ₹8,733 crore from Mobis India (the supplier whose plant fire hit Q1), ₹1,943 crore from Hyundai Transys Lear, ₹1,736 crore from Kia India, ₹1,479 crore from HMC itself and ₹1,085 crore from Hyundai's Indonesian plant. The material related-party resolutions passed with about 97.9% of votes, essentially the parent's 82.5% plus institutions.

Incentives: who is paid to build what

India's support for electric cars runs through supply-side incentives, not consumer subsidies. PM E-DRIVE, the ₹10,900 crore scheme that replaced FAME, pays demand incentives for electric two- and three-wheelers, buses, trucks and ambulances and funds charging (₹2,000 crore), but it gives nothing to private electric cars; it has been extended to March 2028 for some components. PLI-Auto, with an outlay of ₹25,938 crore over FY23–FY28, pays a percentage of incremental sales of advanced-technology vehicles with at least 50% domestic value addition. By December 2025 only ₹2,321.94 crore had been disbursed, Tata Motors being the largest recipient (₹403.18 crore) ahead of Mahindra (₹283.82 crore). For Tata PV only the Nexon.ev and Harrier.ev qualified in Q1 FY27; the Tiago.ev, Punch.ev, Sierra.ev and Curvv start accruing from Q3 and the company expects the whole range to be certified by Q4. Hyundai's first dedicated mass-market EV, due in the second half of FY27, is designed for PLI eligibility from day one. Maruti and Hyundai are not significant PLI-Auto beneficiaries for their current ranges. The PLI for battery cells (₹18,100 crore) has moved slowly; Tata's Agratas cell plant is outside it.

The SPMEPCI import scheme lets a carmaker that commits at least ₹4,150 crore to Indian manufacturing import up to 8,000 electric cars a year at 15% duty instead of 70–110%. It is the route by which Tesla (selling imported cars from Mumbai and Delhi stores since July 2025) and VinFast entered. Chinese makers face tighter scrutiny of investment under India's Press Note 3 rules, which has slowed BYD's plans for local manufacturing.

Trade: the rules that decide export markets

For JLR the critical rule is the UK–US Economic Prosperity Deal, effective 30 June 2025: UK-built cars entering the US pay 10% within a quota of 100,000 cars a year (administered quarterly), against 27.5% (2.5% MFN plus 25% Section 232) before. India–UK trade agreement (CETA, signed July 2025) will cut Indian duties on UK cars to 10% within a quota over time, a marginal help for JLR's Indian imports. For Maruti and Hyundai the problem is Mexico, which raised duties on cars from countries without a trade agreement (India and China among them) to as much as 50% from 2026; SIAM flagged it as a drag on exports. India's own exports to the US face a 10% Section 301 tariff since July 2026 (after IEEPA tariffs were struck down by the US Supreme Court in February), which is negligible for passenger vehicles.

In plain terms

In India the government, not the market, sets much of a car's price and specification. A tax cut can lift sales by a quarter in a year, a fuel-economy rule can force a carmaker to sell electric cars it would rather not, and a tariff in Mexico or Washington can close an export market overnight. Reading policy is part of reading these stocks.

The Corporate Average Fuel Efficiency (CAFE) rules set a limit on the average CO₂ emissions of all the cars an OEM sells in a year, adjusted for the fleet's average weight. CAFE III, covering FY28–FY32 (1 April 2027 to 31 March 2032), was notified by the Ministry of Power on 30 September 2026, according to trade-press reports (we have not reviewed the gazette text). Its main features, which follow the July 2026 draft:

  • Targets of about 94.8 g/km in FY28, tightening to about 78.9 g/km by FY32: roughly 16% tighter than CAFE II in the first year and about 30% by the last.
  • Weight adjustment with a reference weight raised to 1,229 kg (from 1,170 kg in the September 2025 draft) and a flatter slope, which eases targets for lighter fleets and tightens them for SUV-heavy ones.
  • The ≤909 kg small-car relief was dropped. The September 2025 draft had offered about 3 g/km of relief for very light, small-engined cars, a clause Maruti lobbied for and Tata and Mahindra opposed. As reported by trade press, the final rule leaves it out.
  • Super-credits: each battery-electric or range-extender car counts as 3.0 vehicles, plug-in hybrids and flex-fuel strong hybrids 2.5, strong hybrids 1.6 and flex-fuel cars 1.1.
  • Carbon-neutrality factors that discount the emissions of cars running on E20-plus petrol (8%), flex fuel (22.3%) and CNG (5%).
  • Compliance in blocks (FY28–FY30, then FY31–FY32), with credits that can be banked or traded between OEMs, and any shortfall bought from the Bureau of Energy Efficiency at ₹2,500 per g/km per vehicle in FY28, rising to ₹4,500 by FY32.

CAFE arithmetic: how many EVs does an OEM need?

The super-credits make the arithmetic forgiving. Take an ILLUSTRATIVE fleet of 10 lakh petrol cars averaging 105 g/km against a target of about 95. With no electric cars it misses by 10 g/km, and buying the shortfall at ₹2,500 per g/km per vehicle would cost ₹25,000 a car, or ₹2,500 crore a year. If 4% of its sales are electric, and each counts as three zero-emission cars, its fleet average falls to about 93.3 g/km (9.6 lakh × 105 ÷ [9.6 lakh + 3 × 0.4 lakh]) and it complies. At 2% electric it reaches about 98.9 g/km and would owe about ₹975 crore. CNG cars with their 5% neutrality factor and strong hybrids with their 1.6 multiplier provide cheaper partial routes. These numbers are simplified (they ignore the weight adjustment and blocks) and are for illustration only.

The economics of an Indian electric car

Electric cars in India sell on running costs and tax, not subsidies. They pay 5% GST against 18% or 40% for petrol cars, enjoy road-tax and registration waivers in many states, and cost about ₹1.1–1.3 per kilometre to run against about ₹6 on petrol (ILLUSTRATIVE). Against that, the battery pack is 35–45% of the cost of the car, India imports nearly all its cells (LFP cells fell to about $50–60 per kWh in China in 2025, an approximate figure), and the motor needs rare-earth magnets that China licensed for export from April 2025. Tata's margin on EVs depends heavily on PLI: management says BEVs are priced "at least margin-neutral" at JLR, and Tata PV's whole range is expected to be PLI-certified by Q4 FY27. For an OEM, the question is whether EVs can earn the same profit per car as petrol models once PLI ends; no member has shown that yet.

In plain terms

The new fuel-economy law does not force Maruti to sell many electric cars: because each EV counts three times and CNG cars get a discount, a few per cent of electric sales plus its large CNG range should be enough. Mahindra, with heavy SUVs, needs far more. Tata is already well ahead. The rule makes electric cars an option for the market leader and a necessity for the SUV specialists.

Jaguar Land Rover is the biggest business in this report by revenue and the one that moves the sector's profit pool most. Five blows in one year

1. Tariffs. In April 2025 the United States imposed a 25% Section 232 tariff on imported cars on top of the 2.5% MFN duty, and JLR paused shipments to the US, its largest profit market. The UK–US deal cut the rate to 10% within a 100,000-car quota from 30 June 2025, but the damage to FY26 volume was done: North American wholesales fell 32.7% to 86,855, while North American retail fell only 16.9%. JLR was running down its US dealer stock, which means some of the FY26 fall was a one-off destocking that should not repeat.

2. The cyberattack. From 31 August–1 September 2025 a cyberattack shut JLR's production worldwide. Plants were paused for about five weeks and restarted on 8 October 2025 (the ER&D services report describes the knock-on effect on Tata's engineering suppliers). TMPV booked ₹2,786 crore of supplier claims and cyber-incident expenses as exceptional items, about £220 million at roughly ₹126 to the pound (INDICATIVE conversion; the annual report gives no sterling figure). The UK government backed a £1.5 billion loan guarantee (drawn as a UKEF-backed facility on 2 October 2025), and Moody's (Ba1) and S&P (BBB−) moved JLR's outlook to negative.

3. China. China wholesales fell 26.8% to 34,533 in FY26 and retail 25.4%. Chinese luxury buyers are switching to local electric brands, and a luxury-car tax and retrospective tax issues hit imports. In June 2026 JLR's Chinese joint venture with Chery (CJLR) stopped building JLR-badged cars; from 30 July it began producing a CJLR-branded Freelander instead. China is still 13% of JLR's revenue, and management expects it to get "a little bit worse before it stabilises".

4. The Jaguar gap. JLR stopped selling the old Jaguar range ahead of the brand's relaunch as an electric-only luxury maker. Jaguar wholesales fell from 26,900 in FY25 to 6,500 in FY26 and 800 in Q1 FY27. The first new model, the Type 01 four-door GT, was revealed in New York on 6 October 2026, with production due in early 2027 and no material volume until FY28.

5. Discounts. Retail variable marketing expense (VME, the discounts and incentives JLR funds at the dealer) rose to 7.1% of revenue in Q1 FY27 from 4.1% a year earlier, as weak demand in China and Europe and the run-out of old models forced higher incentives.

The way back: what management has promised

At its June 2026 Investor Day JLR guided FY27 revenue of about £26 billion (+13%), an EBIT margin of about 4%, operating cash flow at about breakeven and investment of £3.7 billion. The bear case is that JLR's FY25 margin was a peak built on a post-pandemic order backlog and a strong China, and that the new normal is mid-single-digit EBIT with heavy investment: China permanently smaller, US tariffs permanently at least 10%, discounts higher, and £18 billion of planned electric spending over five years arriving into a softening global luxury-EV market. The bull case is that FY26 was an accident (cyber plus tariff pause plus Jaguar gap) on top of a franchise whose three core nameplates still sell at very high prices, and that North American destocking and the cyber shutdown will not recur. JLR sells fewer than one car for every eight Maruti sells, but each is worth about twelve times as much. Its costs are mostly fixed, so when it sold 23% fewer cars in FY26 its profit nearly vanished. Four decades of cycles: a monopoly, a hatchback era, three shocks and an SUV boom

India's car industry has had long booms and sudden stops. The listed companies' returns show how violent the swings can be even for the leader.

1983–2002: the Maruti monopoly. The Maruti 800, launched in December 1983 by a joint venture between the Government of India and Suzuki, created the Indian car market. For fifteen years there was little competition. Hyundai (with the Santro, 1998) and Tata (with the Indica, 1998) were the first serious challengers, and Hyundai began exporting from Chennai in 1999.

2002–2008: Suzuki takes control and Tata goes global. The government sold out of Maruti between 2002 and 2007, leaving Suzuki in control; Maruti listed in 2003. In June 2008 Tata Motors bought Jaguar Land Rover from Ford for about $2.3 billion, months before the global financial crisis.

2008–2018: the hatchback decade. Small cars were more than 70% of the market and Maruti's share exceeded 50% by FY19. Diesel cars boomed on subsidised fuel and then faded. Hyundai's Creta (2015) and Maruti's premium NEXA channel (2015) foreshadowed the SUV shift.

FY19–FY22: three shocks. The IL&FS default in September 2018 froze the NBFCs that financed many car purchases. The leap to BS-VI emission norms in April 2020 added cost. COVID shut factories and showrooms, and then the global semiconductor shortage capped production in FY22 even as demand returned. Domestic sales fell from 33.8 lakh in FY19 to 27.1 lakh in FY21. Maruti's ROCE fell to 6% in FY22; JLR lost money in FY19–FY21 after a £3.1 billion impairment in FY19 driven by China, Brexit and the collapse of diesel.

FY23–FY24: the SUV boom. Pent-up demand and a wave of new SUVs (Nexon, Punch, Brezza, Grand Vitara, XUV700, Scorpio-N, the new Creta) lifted sales to 38.9 lakh and then 42.2 lakh; utility vehicles passed half and then 60% of the market. JLR's "Refocus" programme and Range Rover and Defender order books lifted its EBIT to 8.5% by FY25 and turned it to net cash.

FY25: the stall. Domestic sales grew only 2.0%. Small cars kept shrinking, dealers carried more than 50 days of stock and discounts rose. Hyundai Motor India listed in October 2024 in a ₹27,870 crore offer for sale, India's largest IPO at the time.

FY26–FY27: shocks and a tax cut. US tariffs, China's rare-earth licensing, JLR's cyberattack, GST 2.0, the Tata demerger, a record 46.4 lakh year, then the West Asia war, a commodity spike, fuel hikes and a rate hike. The next cycle is already visible: CAFE III from FY28, electric penetration heading for double digits, and the post-GST base effect in H2 FY27.

Reading a carmaker: the KPIs that matter and the trap in each Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain a car company's quarter. Each has a formula, a benchmark and at least one way to mislead you.

A carmaker's revenue is units multiplied by price, plus some spares and other operating income. Its profit is what remains after paying for materials (most of the cost), the parent's royalty where there is one, staff, freight, advertising, warranty and dealer support. A worked example: one Maruti car, a year apart

Divide Maruti's company-basis figures by the number of cars it sold and the equation becomes concrete. In Q1 FY26 Maruti sold 5,27,861 vehicles and booked net sales of about ₹36,620 crore, or about ₹6.94 lakh a car. In Q1 FY27 it sold 6,82,724 and booked ₹49,959 crore, about ₹7.32 lakh a car: realisation rose 5.5%. But material cost per car rose from about ₹5.17 lakh (74.5%) to about ₹5.89 lakh (80.5%), up 14%. Employee and other costs per car, net of other operating income, fell because volume spread the fixed part thinner. Operating EBITDA per car fell from about ₹87,400 to about ₹62,900, a 28% drop, and EBIT per car from about ₹58,300 to about ₹37,100. Volume growth of 29% offset much of the per-car loss, so total EBITDA fell only 6.7%.

In plain terms

1. Volumes: domestic and export

What it is. Units sold in the period, split into domestic wholesales (dispatches to Indian dealers) and exports. Formula. Reported directly in monthly sales releases. Worked example. Maruti's FY26 total was 24,22,713: domestic 19,74,939 (including light commercial vehicles and supplies to other OEMs) and exports 4,47,774. Exports were therefore 18.5% of volume. What drives it. Demand, capacity, supply-chain disruptions (the Mobis fire, the rare-earth magnet squeeze) and dealer stock decisions; for exports, the parent's allocation and conditions in destination markets. Benchmark. Good: domestic growth more than five points above the industry; weak: below the industry. Exports above 20% of volume are good for a mass-market OEM (diversification and better realisation in rupees when the currency weakens). How the members compare. In Q1 FY27 Tata PV grew wholesales 46.1% and Maruti 29.3% against an industry up 25.9%; Hyundai fell 1.3%; JLR fell 9.2%. Hyundai's export share was 24.5% in FY26 and Maruti's 18.5%; Tata's was under 2%. The trap. Maruti's "domestic sales" include LCVs (9,579 in Q1 FY27) and sales to other OEMs (23,181, mainly Toyota-badged Maruti cars). Those Toyota cars also appear in Toyota's own figures, so summing company-reported volumes double-counts them. And TMCV's trucks are never passenger vehicles.

2. Market share: wholesale versus Vahan retail

What it is. A company's units as a share of the industry's. Formula. Company domestic PV units ÷ industry domestic PV units, on the same basis. Worked example. Maruti's H1 FY27 domestic PV sales of 10,80,240 ÷ SIAM's 26,37,067 = 41.0%. Drivers. Product freshness, segment mix (SUVs and EVs gained), supply, pricing and dealer reach. Benchmark. A gain of a point a year is strong; a loss of a point a year is weak. Comparison. On FY26 wholesales: Maruti ~39.3%, M&M 14.2%, Tata 13.6%, Hyundai 12.6%. On FADA retail: Maruti 39.71%, M&M 13.42%, Tata 13.04%, Hyundai 12.29%. Hyundai lost about 1.2 points of retail share in FY26, the weakest showing. The trap. There are at least four bases: SIAM wholesale, FADA or Vahan retail, a company's own "Vahan share" (Tata's 14.3% in Q1 FY27) and company-defined segment shares (Hyundai's "mid-SUV share"). SIAM's monthly figures sometimes exclude Tata. Wholesale share moves with dealer stocking, so a company pushing stock into dealers looks as if it is gaining share. Always name the basis, and prefer retail over several quarters.

3. UV mix

What it is. Utility vehicles (SUVs, crossovers and MPVs) as a share of a company's domestic sales. Formula. UV units ÷ total domestic PV units. Worked example. Tata's Q2 FY27 dispatches of 1,19,324 Nexon/Punch, 25,365 Curvv/Sierra and 15,411 Harrier/Safari total 1,60,100 of 1,96,674, a UV mix of about 81% (INDICATIVE). Drivers. Product portfolio and buyer preference; a higher UV mix usually means a higher realisation and margin per car. Benchmark. At or above the market's 68% is good for a full-line maker; well below it means the company depends on a shrinking or tax-favoured segment. Comparison. Maruti about 39% (Q1 FY27); Hyundai 70% of domestic (Q1 FY27); Tata about 81%; Mahindra essentially 100%. The trap. SIAM's "UV" includes MPVs (Ertiga, Innova) and sub-four-metre crossovers (Fronx, Punch), so "68% SUVs" overstates the share of true SUVs. And the GST line is length and engine size, not body style: many "UVs" pay the small-car 18%. Hyundai's exports are only 13–14% SUVs, so its total UV mix is lower than its domestic one.

4. ASP: realisation per vehicle

What it is. Average revenue per vehicle sold. Formula. Net revenue ÷ units. Worked example. Tata PV's Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹17,930 crore ÷ about 1,82,300 wholesales = about ₹9.84 lakh, up from about ₹9.1 lakh in FY26. Drivers. List prices, discounts, mix (SUVs, EVs, top variants), exports and taxes (GST is excluded from revenue, so a tax cut does not reduce ASP directly but changes the mix). Benchmark. ASP growth above inflation plus three points is good; below inflation weak. Comparison. Maruti about ₹7.6 lakh (FY26, screener revenue basis) and ₹7.32 lakh on net sales in Q1 FY27; Hyundai about ₹9.1 lakh; Tata PV about ₹9.1 lakh (FY26) and ₹9.8 lakh (Q1 FY27); JLR about £74,400 (roughly ₹93 lakh); TMCV's trucks about ₹18 lakh. The trap. A rising ASP can mean price increases (good), richer mix (good), or a fall in cheap models' volume (not good). Maruti's Q1 FY27 ASP rose 5.5% even though mini cars more than doubled, because exports and UVs also grew and price increases began. Always split the change into volume, price and mix (below).

6. Material cost and commodity sensitivity

What it is. Raw materials and bought-out components as a share of net sales. Formula. (Materials consumed + purchases of traded goods ± inventory change) ÷ net sales. Worked example. Maruti's material ratio rose from 74.5% to 80.5% in a year; at Q1 FY27 net sales of ₹49,959 crore, those six points cost about ₹3,000 crore in the quarter. Drivers. Steel, aluminium, copper, polymers, rubber and precious-metal prices, with a lag set by supplier contracts; the rupee; and mix (EVs carry a much higher material ratio because of the battery). Benchmark. Below 72% is good for a mass-market OEM, 72–77% average and above 77% weak. Comparison. Maruti 80.5% (Q1 FY27); TMPV consolidated 63.8% (FY26, a JLR-weighted figure); TMCV consolidated 67.7% (FY26) and standalone 72.4% (Q2 FY27). Hyundai does not give a comparable split in our sources. Sensitivity. One point of material ratio is worth about ₹1,900 crore of annual EBITDA at Maruti, about 9% of FY26 operating profit (INDICATIVE). The trap. Inventory changes and supplier settlement timing move the ratio: Maruti says about 110 basis points of its Q1 commodity hit came from switching some suppliers to monthly settlement and should reverse. A sudden improvement can likewise be timing rather than lower costs.

7. Discounts and VME

What it is. The price reductions an OEM funds at the point of sale: cash offers, exchange bonuses, free accessories, finance subsidies and dealer incentives. At JLR it is called variable marketing expense (VME). Formula. Discounts ÷ revenue, or rupees per vehicle. Worked example. If a ₹10 lakh car carries a ₹30,000 offer, the discount is 3% (ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers. Dealer stock, model age, competitive launches and demand. Discounts rise when inventory exceeds about 40 days or a model is near replacement. Benchmark. For mass-market OEMs, below about 3% of revenue is good (INDICATIVE); for JLR, VME below 4% good, 4–6% average and above 6% weak. Comparison. Hyundai 2.8% in Q1 FY27 against 3.4% a year earlier, "below the industry average"; Maruti said discounts improved by 50 basis points in Q4 FY26 and were flat in Q1 FY27; JLR's VME rose to 7.1% from 4.1%. Tata does not disclose a figure. The trap. Companies rarely disclose discounts, define them differently, and some (dealer-funded or bundled accessories) never appear in OEM accounts. Industry-wide rupee-per-car figures come from paywalled broker trackers; any figure beyond the company disclosures above is an estimate.

9. Royalty as a share of sales

What it is. The fee paid to the technology owner, usually the parent. Formula. Royalty expense ÷ net sales; also royalty ÷ PAT. Worked example. Maruti's ₹5,392 crore ÷ ₹1,74,370 crore net sales = 3.09%; ÷ ₹14,445 crore standalone PAT = 37.3%. Drivers. Below 2% of sales is good for minorities, 2–4% average and above 4% weak (INDICATIVE). Comparison. Maruti 3.09% (3.47% in FY25); Hyundai 2.79% (2.71%); Tata and JLR pay none to a carmaker parent. The trap. The rate can fall while the parent's total take rises, through capitalised lump-sum fees (₹294 crore at Maruti), services, components and export transfer prices. 10. R&D and capex intensity

What it is. Spending on product development and capacity relative to revenue. Formula. Capex ÷ revenue; R&D (expensed plus capitalised) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Maruti guides FY27 capex of about ₹14,000 crore against revenue run-rate of roughly ₹2 lakh crore, about 7%. Drivers. New plants, new platforms, the EV transition and regulation (CAFE, safety). Benchmark. Mass-market licensed OEMs typically spend 5–8% of sales on capex and little on R&D; own-IP makers 4–6% of revenue on R&D (approximate); luxury makers 12–16% on total investment. Comparison. Maruti about 7% capex; Hyundai's Pune expansion is up to ₹11,000 crore in phases to 2030; TMPV's product-development spend was ₹36,504 crore in FY26 (about 11% of revenue, 68% capitalised), mostly JLR's £3.6 billion; JLR plans £3.7 billion in FY27; TMCV targets 2–4% (2.7% in Q1 FY27). The trap. Capitalised engineering flatters EBITDA: 74% of JLR's engineering spend was capitalised in Q1 FY27, so it shows up later as amortisation, below EBITDA. Comparing EBITDA margins of a heavy capitaliser (JLR) and a low spender (Maruti) without looking at depreciation and amortisation misleads.

In plain terms

Some carmakers record development spending as an expense right away; others record it as an asset and write it off over years. The second approach makes today's profit look higher. JLR capitalises about three-quarters of its engineering, so its EBITDA looks healthier than its cash flow.

11. EV penetration and CAFE compliance

What it is. Electric vehicles as a share of a company's sales, and its fleet-average CO₂ against the CAFE target. Formula. BEV units ÷ total units; fleet CO₂ = sales-weighted average emissions with super-credit multipliers and neutrality factors, compared with the weight-adjusted target. Worked example. Tata's Q1 FY27 EVs of more than 34,000 ÷ about 1,82,300 wholesales = about 19%. Drivers. Product range, battery cost, PLI, fuel prices and charging. Benchmark. Above 15% of own sales good, 5–15% average, below 5% weak; for CAFE, a projected surplus is good and a shortfall that must be bought at ₹2,500–4,500 per g/km per car is weak. Comparison. Tata about 19% in Q1 FY27 and about a quarter in September; Maruti and Hyundai about 1–2%. Nomura estimates the FY28 EV share needed for compliance at 1–3% for Maruti and 4–7% for Hyundai and TMPV. The trap. "EV share" can mean share of the EV market (Tata 39.9%) or share of the company's own sales (about 19–26%). CAFE compliance is not public quarterly, and the super-credits mean a small EV share can neutralise a large petrol fleet. No company yet discloses its projected CAFE III position; all compliance arithmetic here is ILLUSTRATIVE.

12. Dealer inventory days

What it is. How many days of retail sales sit in dealer stock. Formula. Dealer stock ÷ average daily retail sales. Worked example. A dealer network holding 4,50,000 cars against retail of 10,000 a day carries 45 days (ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers. The gap between wholesale dispatches and retail registrations; festive-season stocking. Benchmark. 21–30 days is healthy, 30–40 average, above 45 weak (discounts and dealer stress follow). Comparison. FADA's industry figure: about 52 days (March 2025), about 28 (March 2026), 43–45 (September 2026). Maruti says about 13 days (Q1 FY27); Tata about 30. The trap. OEM and FADA figures are on different bases and dates; a company can flatter quarterly wholesales by pushing stock to dealers, which is why wholesale growth well ahead of retail is a warning sign.

13. Net cash or debt and free cash flow

What it is. The balance-sheet cushion and the cash the business generates after investment. Formula. Net debt = borrowings − cash and liquid investments; FCF = cash from operations − capex (company definitions vary). Worked example. Hyundai had ₹10,552 crore of cash and bank balances against ₹1,098 crore of borrowings at March 2026: net cash of about ₹9,450 crore. Drivers. Profitability, working capital (Indian OEMs are paid upfront by dealers), capex and dividends. Benchmark. Net cash above 20% of market value is strong; net debt above one times EBITDA weak. Comparison. Maruti holds ₹76,838 crore of investments against ₹102 crore of borrowings (net cash about ₹76,700 crore, about 22% of its market value) and generated ₹8,754 crore of FCF in FY26. TMPV's net debt rose from ₹30,710 crore (March 2026) to ₹42,200 crore (June 2026) after FY26 FCF of −₹26,823 crore. TMCV had ₹15,900 crore of net cash in September 2026, before paying for Iveco. The trap. Screener's "investments" miss Hyundai's cash (which sits in other assets), include TMCV's strategic Tata Capital stake (marked to market each quarter) and include TMPV's equity-accounted holdings. A screener-basis group "net cash" is therefore misleading; use company net-debt definitions and state the date.

What it is. JLR's adjusted EBIT margin (its headline profitability measure) and its free cash flow in pounds. Formula. In plain terms

Two sensitivities show why the industry behaves as it does. Commodities: if materials rise by three points of net realisation (₹19,500) and prices do not move, EBITDA falls to ₹42,500 (6.5%) and EBIT to ₹19,500; profit after tax roughly halves. Utilisation: treat employees and half of other costs (about ₹51,750 a car at full output) as fixed. The same anatomy looks different for a larger car. That is why Tata's PV margin depends so much on PLI, and why Maruti and Hyundai have been slow to push EVs at home.

Definition traps, collected

  • "Tata Motors" is two companies. TMPV (BSE 500570) is JLR plus Tata cars and EVs; TMCV, legally "Tata Motors Ltd" (BSE 544569), makes trucks and buses.
  • TMPV's ₹82,645 crore FY26 PAT is not earnings. It contains an ₹82,616 crore non-cash demerger gain; profit from continuing operations was −₹1,377 crore.
  • TMPV's history mixes bases. FY25 revenue is restated for PV only, but FY25 and Q1 FY26 PAT are old whole-company figures.
  • TMCV has three revenue numbers and a short base year. Standalone ₹77,399 crore, consolidated ₹83,855 crore, and a ~9.3-month FY25; its screener operating profit (9.1%) sits below company EBITDA (12.3%) because of a ₹2,418 crore Tata Capital fair-value loss.
  • Company margins and screener margins differ. Maruti's company EBITDA is on net sales (8.6% in Q1 FY27); screener's is on revenue including other operating income (8.2%).
  • Wholesale is not retail. Dealer stock changes separate them, and the GST deferral and rush distorted both in 2025–26.
  • Market share has four bases. SIAM wholesale, FADA/Vahan retail, company Vahan claims and company-defined segments; SIAM sometimes excludes Tata.
  • Maruti's "domestic" includes non-cars. LCVs and supplies to Toyota are inside it; Toyota's badge-engineered cars are counted twice if OEM figures are summed.
  • Hyundai is India's largest cumulative PV exporter, not the largest annual one. In FY26 Maruti exported 4.48 lakh to Hyundai's 1.90 lakh.
  • JLR comes in four flavours. Pounds or rupees, wholesale ex-China JV or retail including it, adjusted EBIT or segment EBIT, FCF or operating cash flow.
  • "UV" is broad. SIAM's utility vehicles include MPVs and sub-four-metre crossovers that pay small-car GST.
  • "EV share" has two meanings. Share of the EV market versus share of the company's own sales.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading this sector come from comparing numbers on different bases: wholesale with retail, company EBITDA with screener operating profit, TMPV's old whole-company profit with its new car-only profit. Before comparing any two figures, check that they measure the same thing over the same period.

How to read a carmaker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volumes first (1 minute). Pull domestic and export units from the monthly releases, and compare growth with SIAM's industry figure for the quarter. A company growing well below the industry is losing share; check whether supply (a fire, a plant changeover) explains it.
  2. Realisation (1 minute). Divide revenue by units. Split the revenue change into volume and price-plus-mix. A rising ASP with falling volume can mean the cheap models stopped selling.
  3. Material ratio (1 minute). Raw materials as a share of net sales, against last quarter and last year. More than one point of deterioration needs an explanation (commodities, FX, mix, settlement timing).
  4. EBITDA margin and EBITDA per vehicle (1 minute). On the company's basis, against its guidance (Hyundai 11–14% for FY27) and against the other members. Note the basis: net sales or revenue.
  5. The margin bridge (1 minute). Read the company's explanation of the change: commodities, pricing, discounts, FX, operating leverage, one-offs. Price increases announced but not yet in the quarter are next quarter's help.
  6. Other income and treasury (1 minute). At Maruti, treasury income can be over 40% of pre-tax profit, and bond mark-to-market moves it. Separate operating EBIT from PAT.
  7. Inventory and orders (1 minute). Dealer stock (company and FADA), pending bookings and waiting periods. Wholesale growth far above retail growth is a red flag.
  8. Cash and debt (1 minute). FCF and net debt or cash on the company's definition; at TMPV, JLR's FCF and net debt in pounds; at TMCV, the effect of Iveco from Q3 FY27.
  9. The segments that matter (1 minute). At TMPV, read JLR (EBIT, VME, China, wholesale and retail by region) and Tata PV (EBITDA, EBIT, EV share, PLI) separately; never use consolidated margins alone.
  10. Guidance and policy (1 minute). Management's view of industry growth, commodity outlook, launches, capacity and CAFE plans; and any change to royalty, related-party transactions or export allocation.

For TMPV, add one more step: reconcile the consolidated figure to its parts. JLR's revenue in pounds times the average exchange rate should land close to its share of TMPV revenue (about 81%), and the remainder is Tata PV and EV plus eliminations. If consolidated EBITDA moves very differently from the sum of JLR and Tata PV, look for hedging gains or losses, PLI timing or exceptional items. For TMCV from Q3 FY27, separate Iveco's contribution from the Indian business before comparing with history, because the consolidated figures will roughly triple.

Red flags to look for: wholesale growth well ahead of retail; a rising material ratio with no price action; discounts or VME rising for two quarters; EBITDA held up by PLI or other income; a royalty or related-party change approved with the parent's votes; capitalised development spend rising faster than revenue; and, at JLR, negative free cash flow before working capital.

In plain terms

The ten-minute routine is: how many cars, at what price, at what material cost, for how much profit per car, and with how much cash left over. Everything else in a results presentation explains one of those five.

The global lens: India's carmakers are valued like consumer companies, their parents like carmakers

Toyota, Suzuki and Hyundai trade at 8–10 times earnings. Their Indian subsidiaries trade at 25–30 times. The gap is about growth, scarcity and who captures it.

Globally, car manufacturing is a mature, cyclical, low-multiple business. In 2025 and 2026 the industry absorbed US tariffs (25% Section 232 on imported cars, eased by bilateral deals), a price war in China led by BYD and dozens of local EV makers, slowing electric-car demand in Europe and the US, and the cost of developing two powertrain families at once. Operating margins at the large incumbents compressed and their valuations stayed low.

Why the India premium exists. Three reasons. First, growth: India has 30–35 cars per thousand people against 200-plus in China, and Maruti expects the market to grow from 4.6 million to 6.1–6.3 million units by FY31, while Japan, Europe and the US are flat at best. Second, returns: Maruti and Hyundai earn 12–13% operating margins in a normal year with net cash, comparable to Toyota and Suzuki and well above Volkswagen or Hyundai Motor Co. Third, scarcity and ownership: there are few listed carmakers in India, domestic institutions (25.3% of Maruti, 11.9% of HMIL) hold them as core consumer stocks, and the parents' large stakes leave small free floats.

China as competitor. Chinese brands do not yet sell many cars in India: imports carry 70–110% duty, and India's Press Note 3 restricts Chinese investment. BYD sold 831 electric cars in India in September 2026. But Chinese makers dominate the export markets where Indian-built Suzukis and Hyundais compete (Latin America, the Middle East, Africa and South-East Asia) and supply about 36% of India's imported auto components, as the auto components report notes. JSW MG, India's second-largest EV seller, uses SAIC technology. And in China itself, local EV brands have taken share from foreign luxury makers, which is why JLR's Chinese joint venture stopped building JLR-badged cars.

In plain terms

Investors pay about three times as much for each rupee of profit at Maruti or Hyundai India as they pay for each yen or won of profit at Suzuki, Toyota or Hyundai's Korean parent. They are paying for India's growth. That premium is the reason the parents keep investing here, and the reason a slowdown in Indian growth would hurt these stocks more than it would hurt their parents.

Technology and disruption: electrons, ethanol and software

Electric cars are the obvious disruption. Hybrids, flex-fuel, Chinese platforms, software and supply chains for magnets and cells matter as much.

Electrification pace. Electric cars reached 8.3% of PV registrations in September 2026, from about 2.6% in FY25. If petrol stays above ₹100 a litre and cell prices keep falling, a share of 15–20% by FY30 is plausible (INDICATIVE). That is a threat to the two MNC subsidiaries, which sell about 1–2% of their volume as EVs, and an opportunity for Tata, although Tata's share of the EV market has fallen from about 70% in FY23 to about 40% as Mahindra, MG and VinFast arrived. Maruti's answer is the Hansalpur-built e-Vitara (mainly exported) and a fourth production line "largely dedicated to EV production"; Hyundai's is the Creta Electric and a dedicated mass-market EV in the second half of FY27.

Hybrids and flex-fuel as the alternative route. CAFE III rewards strong hybrids (1.6 times), flex-fuel strong hybrids (2.5 times) and E20-plus and flex-fuel vehicles through neutrality factors of 8% and 22.3%. That makes a CNG, hybrid and ethanol path a cheaper way to comply for Maruti and Toyota than a rapid shift to battery cars. Maruti already sells strong hybrids (Grand Vitara, Victoris, the Toyota-built Invicto) and aims for a powertrain mix by FY31 of about 35% CNG and compressed biogas, 25% hybrids and 15–20% EVs (company aspiration). Toyota leads hybrids in India. The risk is that consumers, pushed by fuel prices, move to EVs faster than the hybrid path anticipates.

Chinese platforms and entrants. BYD imports fully built EVs; JSW MG builds SAIC-designed cars locally; VinFast has a plant in Tamil Nadu. India's restrictions on Chinese investment slow full local manufacturing by Chinese brands, but Chinese technology is already inside Indian cars through components, battery cells and, in MG's case, whole platforms. Tata's decision to move its Avinya EV to the CJLR Freelander platform (launch 2027) shows how far Chinese engineering now reaches into even a home-grown OEM's plans.

Software and cyber risk. JLR's 2025 cyberattack showed the operating risk of connected manufacturing: a five-week global shutdown, ₹2,786 crore of supplier and cyber costs, and a ratings outlook cut. At the same time TMPV's agreement to license vehicle architecture to Stellantis shows that software-heavy platforms can be monetised. Advanced driver-assistance features (ADAS), connected-car services and over-the-air updates are now standard on mid-size SUVs and raise the content per vehicle captured by suppliers (see the ER&D services and auto components reports).

Magnets, cells and materials. China's licensing of rare-earth magnet exports from April 2025 forced Maruti to cut its early e-Vitara plan from 26,500 to 8,200 units for April–September 2025 and left OEMs with four to six weeks of stock. Motors without rare-earth magnets (ferrite or wound-rotor designs) are in development. India imports nearly all its battery cells; Tata's Agratas plant at Sanand and the PLI-ACC scheme are meant to change that, slowly. Materials for internal-combustion cars, steel and aluminium from the iron and steel industry and engine castings and forgings (a constraint on Tata's Sierra, see castings and forgings), remain the bottleneck for petrol cars.

Bull vs bear: a structural growth market in a margin squeeze

The long-run case rests on India's low car ownership. The near-term case rests on costs, the post-GST base and JLR.

The bull case

  • Structural demand. 30–35 cars per thousand people, a tax cut that reopened the entry market, rising incomes and Maruti's forecast of 6.1–6.3 million units by FY31 (from 4.6 million).
  • Margins are cyclical, not broken. FY22 showed margins can halve in a commodity spike and double within two years. Price increases since June, the reversal of Maruti's settlement-timing effect and easing one-offs should lift H2 FY27.
  • Export hubs. Suzuki and Hyundai Motor Group are making India their small-car and emerging-market base (4.48 lakh Maruti exports, US$15.5 billion HMG plan, Pune to 3.2 lakh).
  • Balance sheets. Maruti holds ₹76,838 crore of investments and Hyundai ₹10,552 crore of cash; TMCV generates strong free cash flow.
  • Valuations have reset. Maruti at 24.6× is near its 52-week low; TMPV trades below book; a JLR recovery to its ~4% FY27 guidance would be a large earnings swing.
  • EV leadership at Tata. Four in ten Indian EVs, an IP licence to Stellantis and a growing PLI stream.

The bear case

  • Peak volume growth. H2 FY27 compares with the post-GST surge; Hyundai expects "lower single digits"; dealers hold 43–45 days of stock.
  • Costs may stay high. Brent near $126 in October, a rupee near ₹95 and metals near records; price increases risk choking the entry-level demand the tax cut revived.
  • Rates and fuel. The RBI has started raising rates and pump prices are up ₹7 a litre; credit-financed buyers are sensitive to both.
  • JLR's structural damage. China permanently smaller, US tariffs at least 10%, VME at 7.1%, an £18 billion EV bet into a soft luxury-EV market, and ₹42,200 crore of group net debt.
  • The parent's share. Royalty takes 36–37% of profit at the MNC subsidiaries, exports are transfer-priced, and parents control allocation.
  • Share loss to SUV and EV specialists. Mahindra (now worth more than Maruti) and Tata are winning the SUV and EV segments where the next decade's growth lies.

Weighing them. The bull case is stronger on the long run: the ownership gap, the parents' commitment and the balance sheets of Maruti and Hyundai are hard to argue with, and the industry has recovered from every previous margin squeeze within two years. The bear case is stronger on the next two or three quarters: the base effect, dealer stock and commodity prices all point to slower growth and only partial margin recovery in H2 FY27. JLR is the genuine fork in the road. If it delivers its FY27 guidance, TMPV's valuation at 0.87 times book looks too low; if FY27 is another year of negative free cash flow, the debt and the investment programme will collide.

What would change our view. Maruti and Hyundai margins back above 11% by Q4 FY27 with retail growth in high single digits would confirm the bull case for the India pure-plays. A second quarter of JLR EBIT below 3% with VME above 7% would confirm the bear case for TMPV. For TMCV, the test is whether Iveco can be integrated without diluting the returns that justify its premium.

In plain terms

Over five years, India will almost certainly buy many more cars and these companies will sell most of them. Over the next six months, the companies face a high comparison base, expensive raw materials and dearer loans. Patient investors are being offered lower prices than a year ago because of short-term pain; the open question is how long the pain lasts, especially at JLR.
