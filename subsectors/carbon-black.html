A complete guide to the three NSE 750 companies classified as carbon black: PCBL Chemical, India's largest producer; Himadri Speciality Chemical, a coal-tar refinery that feeds its own carbon black reactors; and Rain Industries, a global calcined-coke and pitch maker that sells carbon black oil. How a tonne of heavy oil becomes a tonne of black, why the profit is a spread that Chinese and Russian surplus can squeeze, how the 2026 oil shock turned into a windfall quarter and a cash drain at the same time, and what the battery-materials bets are worth.

00

The one-page view: three carbon companies, one spread

The tonnes barely move from year to year. The rupees earned on each tonne swing by half, and that swing is the whole investment case.

Carbon black is the fine black powder that makes up roughly a quarter to a third of the weight of a tyre. It is made by burning a heavy aromatic oil in too little air, so that the carbon falls out as soot of a controlled size and shape instead of burning to carbon dioxide. Nobody buys it for its own sake. Tyre makers buy it because it turns soft rubber into a tread that lasts 50,000 kilometres; pipe, cable and ink makers buy smaller amounts of finer grades for colour, ultraviolet protection or electrical conductivity. The product is old, the chemistry is well understood, and in India there is now more capacity than domestic demand.

That last fact shapes everything. India had about 2.0 million tonnes a year of carbon black capacity in early 2024 (CRISIL) and, after PCBL's, BKT Carbon's and Birla Carbon's expansions, roughly 2.2–2.4 million tonnes in late 2026 (an indicative estimate). Domestic consumption is about 1.3–1.45 million tonnes, an indicative figure extrapolated from the only regulator-published demand series. The gap leaves the country. India turned net exporter of carbon black in FY22 and in FY26 shipped 443.9 thousand tonnes (kt) worth ₹4,376 crore while importing 130.2 kt. An industry that exports a third of its output cannot set its own price: it takes the price that clears in Thailand, Poland and the United States, where Chinese and Russian black compete.

So the number that matters is not revenue and not even volume, but EBITDA per tonne: the rupees of operating profit left on each tonne after paying for the oil, the gas, the plant and the freight, plus whatever the plant earns by turning its waste gas into electricity. PCBL, the largest producer, earned about ₹19,350 a tonne in the first half of FY25, about ₹15,500 in the first half of FY26 and about ₹14,900 for FY26 as a whole, while selling a record 618,956 tonnes. Volume rose 3.8%; EBITDA fell 22% and profit after tax fell 54%. Then the West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 pushed Brent crude from the mid-$60s to $117 a barrel in April, and PCBL's Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) EBITDA per tonne jumped to ₹22,900, including about ₹70 crore of gains on cheap inventory. Management still guides FY27 at only ₹16,500–17,000 a tonne. Same plants, same customers, same tonnes: three very different years.

The three listed companies in this bucket are very different businesses, and the report treats them that way. PCBL Chemical (900 ktpa, about 40% of Indian capacity) is the purest carbon black play, with tail-gas power plants and, since January 2024, a debt-funded phosphonates business called Aquapharm. Himadri Speciality Chemical is India's largest coal-tar distiller: it turns coke-oven tar into pitch for aluminium smelters, naphthalene for chemicals and heavy oils that feed its own 250 ktpa of carbon black reactors, and it is spending on lithium-iron-phosphate cathode, anode material and carbon nanotubes. Rain Industries makes no furnace carbon black at all: it is the world's largest coal tar pitch maker and a top calcined petroleum coke producer, sitting here because its distillation units sell carbon black oil to carbon black makers.

The market prices them on anything but tonnes. On an aligned twelve-month basis to June 2026, Rain earned 58% of the group's revenue and 57% of its operating profit but carries only 15% of its market value. Himadri earned 16% of revenue and 20% of operating profit but carries 59% of the value. The difference is leverage, growth and optionality. Rain's enterprise value is more than half debt, and minority shareholders in its subsidiaries took 69% of its CY25 profit. Himadri had net cash until March 2026 and is the only one of the three with a credible claim to be building a battery-materials business at commercial scale.

Five forces define this sub-sector, and the rest of the report is built around them:

  • The spread cycle. Tyre contracts pass feedstock costs through with a quarter's lag, about a third of PCBL's volume is spot, and inventory is bought months ahead. Rising crude produces windfalls; falling crude, or a flood of cheap Russian and Chinese black, produces squeezes. EBITDA per tonne is the number to track (deep dive, section 08).
  • Tyres as the anchor customer. About 60–65% of Indian carbon black goes into tyres, 55–65% of tyre sales are replacement, and Indian tyre exports reached ₹27,312 crore in FY26. Since the EU banned Russian carbon black in July 2024, India's exports to the EU-27 rose from 5.3 kt (FY22) to 130.3 kt (FY26), but the squeeze arrived through prices, not import volumes, and there is no anti-dumping shield (deep dive, section 09).
  • The 2026 oil shock and working capital. Higher oil prices inflate receivables and inventory. Himadri's H1 FY27 operating cash flow was −₹154 crore; Rain built safety stocks with US$257 million of working-capital debt (section 10).
  • Leverage and capital allocation. PCBL carries ₹4,536 crore of net borrowings and Negative outlooks at CRISIL and CARE; Himadri swung from net cash to ₹519.8 crore of net debt in six months; Rain owes US$892 million net (deep dive, section 17).
  • Specialty, conductive and battery bets. Specialty grades earn a multiple of rubber black per tonne; Himadri's LFP, anode and nanotube lines and PCBL's Nanovace silicon-anode joint venture are what the market is paying for, but they are still pilot-scale (deep dive, section 16).

In plain terms

A carbon black plant earns a fee for converting oil into a tyre ingredient. The fee is not written in any contract: it is whatever is left after the customer pays a price linked to last quarter's oil and the company pays this quarter's. When oil rises fast, the company wins for a quarter; when oil falls fast, or when cheaper imports appear, it loses. In 2026 all three companies reported their best margins in years, and all three spent cash to carry more expensive oil and tar through their warehouses and receivables.

In plain terms

This is not one industry. PCBL is a carbon black maker with a chemicals bolt-on, Himadri is a coal-tar refinery that happens to make carbon black, and Rain is an aluminium-supply company that sells carbon black oil as a by-product. They share feedstocks, customers in the carbon value chain and exposure to the same oil, freight and rupee shocks, which is why they are worth reading together, but no single "carbon black market share" can be computed across them.

01

Soot by design: how a tonne of oil becomes a tonne of black

Carbon black is a conversion business with two feedstock routes, a power plant bolted on, and a customer list dominated by tyre makers.

Furnace carbon black, which is more than 95% of world output, is made in a refractory-lined reactor. A heavy, highly aromatic oil is sprayed into a flame of natural gas and air at 1,300–1,800 °C. Because there is not enough oxygen to burn it fully, the oil cracks into carbon particles a few tens of nanometres across that fuse into grape-like aggregates. Water sprays quench the reaction at a precisely chosen point; the smoke passes through bag filters; the collected black is wetted, pelletised, dried and shipped in bulk trucks or bags. The grade is set by two properties, particle size (surface area) and "structure" (how branched the aggregates are). Tyre treads need small, high-surface-area particles (the N100–N300 series); sidewalls and carcasses use larger particles (N500–N700). The volume grades are N330, N550 and N660.

Yield is the first piece of economics. Depending on the grade and the oil's aromaticity, a plant needs about 1.6–1.9 tonnes of oil for each tonne of carbon black; the rest leaves as "tail gas", a low-calorific mixture rich in carbon monoxide and hydrogen. Modern plants burn that gas in boilers and turbines. PCBL generated about 839 million units (MU) of electricity in FY26 against 618,956 tonnes of carbon black, roughly 1.36 megawatt-hours per tonne, and sold about 511 MU to the grid and to group companies. The power plant turns a waste stream into a second revenue line worth ₹1,500–2,500 a tonne in a normal year (an indicative estimate) and much more when grid prices spike, as they did in Q1 FY27 at ₹5.39 per unit.

The feedstock comes by two routes, and the choice matters. Carbon black feedstock (CBFS) is a petroleum residue: the decant or clarified slurry oil from a refinery's fluid catalytic cracker, or the tar left by an ethylene cracker. It is priced off crude and high-sulphur fuel oil, plus freight. Carbon black oil (CBO) is a coal-tar fraction produced by tar distillers such as Himadri and Rain. In mid-2026 PCBL said CBO was about US$150 a tonne dearer than CBFS. Indian makers mainly use imported CBFS, which ICRA judged in 2023 to be cheaper than China's coal-tar route. Himadri is different: its own distillation produces the heavy oils its reactors burn, so part of its feedstock is captive and does not have to be bought at a market price on the US Gulf Coast.

A distiller earns the "barrel value" of everything that comes out of a tonne of tar. Himadri's edge is that its oils go straight into its own carbon black reactors; Rain's link to carbon black is the CBO it sells to producers.

Customers sort into three groups. Tyre makers (MRF, Apollo, JK Tyre, CEAT, Bridgestone India, Balkrishna and the global majors) buy most of the volume on annual volume agreements with quarterly formula pricing; Birla Carbon's five largest customers took 63% of its India sales (CRISIL). Non-tyre rubber goods makers (belts, hoses, seals, footwear, moulded parts) buy similar grades, which PCBL reports as "performance chemicals". Specialty users (plastics masterbatch, pressure pipes, semi-conductive cable compounds, printing inks, automotive coatings, and lithium-ion electrode makers) buy in small lots with tight specifications and pay much more per tonne. Approval for a tyre grade takes 6–18 months, which makes customers sticky once a supplier is in.

Where is value captured along this chain? First at feedstock: buying oil cheaply, in the right aromatic quality, with freight under control, decides cost; the CBFS-versus-CBO choice alone is worth about US$150 a tonne of oil. Second at yield: a point of yield on 1.75 tonnes of oil per tonne is real money at oil prices of ₹40,000–50,000 a tonne. Third at grade mix: specialty black earns perhaps two to four times rubber black per tonne (the industry rule of thumb; Orion, the US-listed producer, earned about three times as much per tonne in specialty as in rubber in Q4 2025, a single-source indicative figure). Fourth at location: port-side plants at Mundra, Kochi and Chennai cut freight on imported oil and on exports. Last, the power plant converts waste into cash.

In plain terms

A carbon black plant is a small refinery in reverse: it buys a cheap, dirty oil, throws away about 40% of its mass as gas, and sells the rest as a carefully engineered powder. The gas is burned for electricity, which the plant sells. Its profit depends on how cheaply it buys oil, how much black it gets from each tonne, how many specialty grades it can sell, and how much power it can export.

02

Size: a surplus country in a 14-million-tonne world

The world uses about 14 million tonnes a year and has rated capacity for nearly 20 million. India has built faster than it consumes.

Global carbon black demand was 14,301 kt in 2024 (estimate) against rated capacity of 19,621 kt, according to the consultancy Notch, as cited in PCBL's FY25 annual report. That is nominal utilisation of about 73%, although Notch says achievable capacity is 20–22% below the rated figure, so real plants run nearer 92–94% of what they can actually make. Notch expects demand to reach 17,050 kt by 2030 (about 3% a year), against rated capacity of 22,606 kt. Specialty black is a small, faster-growing slice: about 1.06 million tonnes in 2025, growing about 4% a year (Notch, via Himadri's FY26 annual report). The global value of the market is commonly put at US$18–22 billion, but that is an indicative industry range we could not tie to a primary source.

India's own numbers are harder to pin down because no regulator publishes national capacity. CRISIL put it at about 2,000 ktpa in February 2024, up 36% in two fiscal years. Since then PCBL has added 110 ktpa (790 to 900), BKT Carbon, the carbon black arm of tyre maker Balkrishna Industries, has added 92 ktpa (to 360 ktpa by April 2026), and Birla Carbon is starting a 120 ktpa plant at Naidupeta in Andhra Pradesh "before year-end 2026". That puts current capacity at roughly 2.2–2.4 million tonnes, an indicative estimate. On demand, the only regulator-published series is from DGTR's 2020 anti-dumping review: rubber-grade demand of 806 kt (FY17), 865 kt (FY18), 949 kt (FY19) and 824 kt (FY20, a Covid-hit year). Growing FY19 at about 6% a year and adding specialty gives FY26 consumption of about 1.3–1.45 million tonnes; a bottom-up check of producers' domestic sales plus imports lands in the same range. In plain terms

India now has roughly 900,000 tonnes a year more carbon black capacity than it uses, and another half a million tonnes is being built. That surplus must be exported, which means Indian prices are set by what buyers in Asia, Europe and the US will pay, not by Indian tyre makers alone. Combined revenue of the three listed companies barely moved for four years; most of the recent rise is higher oil and tar prices passed through to customers.

03

Tyres pull the demand, and tyre exports pull the growth

Six of every ten tonnes go into tyres; most tyres are replacements; and every tyre India exports carries Indian carbon black with it.

Tyres are the anchor. CARE Ratings (July 2026) says more than 60% of Indian carbon black goes into tyre manufacturing, CRISIL says nearly 65%, and PCBL's own FY26 tyre share was 57%. Globally the convention is about 70% tyres, 20% other rubber goods and 10% specialty. A passenger-car tyre is roughly a quarter to a third carbon black by weight, and a truck tyre uses more in absolute terms. Within tyres, replacement demand is the steady part: CARE puts replacement at about 55% of tyre-industry sales and CRISIL at 60–70%. Replacement depends on the kilometres the existing vehicle fleet drives, not on new-vehicle sales, so it holds up in an auto downturn. GST 2.0, from 22 September 2025, cut the tax on new tyres from 28% to 18% (a snippet-grade source: Apollo Tyres' release), which supported replacement demand in the second half of FY26.

The geography is shifting toward Europe. Exports to the United States, India's largest tyre market, fell 5.9% in FY26 (the year of the 50% US tariff), while Germany rose 37% and Italy 40%. Europe takes roughly a third of India's tyre exports, and the India–EU free trade agreement, concluded in January 2026 and awaiting ratification, would remove the EU's 4.5% duty on Indian tyres. The EU charges no duty on carbon black itself, so the agreement helps Indian carbon black mostly indirectly, through more Indian tyres sold in Europe. In the US, Indian goods now face a 10% Section 301 tariff on top of the normal duty, while China and several other origins face more, which PCBL calls a "materially lower tariff" than competing Asian and Middle Eastern carbon black. US sales are only about 5% of PCBL's revenue.

Tyre technology is also moving in carbon black's favour on balance. Radial truck tyres, larger SUV tyres and heavier EVs need more reinforcement and longer-wearing treads, raising carbon black per tyre and the share of premium high-dispersion grades. The offset is silica: "green" low-rolling-resistance treads replace part of the tread black with precipitated silica. For Indian volumes, the net effect so far has been positive, and the tyre industry's own capex plans are the clearest leading indicator of carbon black demand two to three years out.

Outside tyres, demand is a mix of industrial and infrastructure drivers. Non-tyre rubber, roughly a fifth to a quarter of demand, tracks industrial production and auto components (see the auto components report). Plastics masterbatch, pipes and power cables tie specialty black to construction and electrification. Inks and coatings are small. Battery conductive additives are under 1% of demand today, but they are the fastest-growing slice: Himadri's investor deck puts global lithium-ion consumption at about 1.6 TWh in 2025 rising to about 6.8 TWh by 2035, and conductive carbons are 1–2% of electrode mass. For Rain and for Himadri's pitch business the relevant demand is aluminium: about 0.4 tonnes of carbon anode per tonne of aluminium, with China's 45 million-tonne output cap pushing new smelters to the Middle East, India and Indonesia.

In plain terms

If you want to know how much carbon black India will sell next year, look at the tyre industry: how many tyres it makes, how many it exports, and how much new capacity it is building. Replacement tyres keep the floor steady; tyre exports, especially to Europe, supply the growth. A trade deal that removes the EU's tyre duty matters more to carbon black makers than any change in duty on carbon black itself.

In plain terms

The two companies outside the furnace-black business answer to different customers. Himadri's pitch and Rain's calcined coke go into aluminium smelters, so their volumes follow aluminium output outside China. Rain's carbon black oil goes to carbon black makers, which is the only literal carbon black link in its business.

Where ₹100 goes: two-thirds oil, a seventh profit

Feedstock is the bill, freight and the rupee sit inside it, and the margin is whatever survives the lag between buying oil and billing the tyre maker.

The cost structure of a furnace carbon black plant is brutally simple. Oil is about two-thirds of the selling price. In PCBL's own description, carbon black feedstock is 90–95% of material cost and about 90% of it is imported, against exports of about 35% of sales, which makes PCBL a net payer of foreign currency (it hedges most of the net exposure). About 75% of PCBL's oil comes from the US Gulf Coast. That means three prices sit inside the feedstock bill: the oil itself (which tracks Brent and high-sulphur fuel oil), ocean freight from Texas and Louisiana to Gujarat or Tamil Nadu, and the rupee-dollar rate. A plant cannot control any of them. What it controls is yield, the grade mix, fixed costs, and how fast its own selling prices catch up.

How much does a crude move matter per tonne of carbon black? A tonne of heavy residual oil is roughly 6.3–6.7 barrels, so a $10 a barrel move in crude shifts the oil price by about $63–67 a tonne. At 1.75 tonnes of oil per tonne of black and ₹94.9 to the dollar, that is roughly ₹10,500–11,000 of cost per tonne of carbon black, about a tenth of the selling price. This is an illustrative calculation that assumes residue prices move one-for-one with crude, which they do only loosely. The pass-through mechanics then decide who bears the move. About two-thirds of PCBL's volume is sold to tyre majors on formulas that reset prices each quarter using the previous quarter's feedstock index; about a third is spot, which reprices at once. When crude rises fast, spot prices rise immediately and inventory bought at lower prices is sold at new prices, so the plant earns a windfall for a quarter; when crude falls fast, the formula keeps prices high for a quarter but spot buyers walk away and inventory loses value. In Q1 FY27 PCBL's realisation rose about ₹27,000 a tonne quarter on quarter, of which ₹11,000–12,000 was margin and the rest cost pass-through.

Freight and gas are the other variables the 2026 crisis exposed. The Red Sea and Hormuz disruptions rerouted tankers around the Cape of Good Hope, adding about 14 days and pushing container and tanker rates sharply higher. PCBL deliberately held back export orders in Q1 FY27 where freight "was eating away" the margin. Natural gas, used as reactor fuel, became scarce enough that PCBL's 1 ktpa super-conductive line at Palej was delayed by "gas shortage", and Aquapharm cited LPG availability problems. For Rain, European energy prices and Asian imports drove losses at its hydrogenated hydrocarbon resin (HHCR) plant, which management says were "substantially reduced" from Q2 2026.

In plain terms

For every ₹100 a carbon black maker bills, about ₹65 goes straight to the oil supplier and about ₹14 is left as operating profit. A ₹10 a barrel move in crude changes the cost of each tonne of black by roughly ₹10,000–11,000, nearly as much as the whole profit on that tonne. The business survives only because most of that cost is passed to customers, with a delay of about a quarter.

In plain terms

The profit line below EBITDA is where the three companies really differ. A carbon black plant with no debt keeps about half of its EBITDA as profit. PCBL, carrying acquisition debt, kept less than a fifth of its FY26 operating profit; Rain, carrying legacy dollar and euro debt, kept about a sixteenth of its CY25 operating profit before paying minority shareholders.

Who makes it: one giant, an MNC, a tyre maker and a tar refiner

Four producers hold about four-fifths of Indian capacity. Two of them are unlisted or captive, and the newest big entrant is a customer.

India's carbon black industry is a tight oligopoly with a fringe. PCBL, with 900 ktpa (768 ktpa rubber black and 132 ktpa specialty after the 20 ktpa Mundra specialty line commissioned in Q1 FY27), has about 39–41% of national capacity. Birla Carbon India, owned by the Aditya Birla group's global carbon black holding SKI Carbon Black (Mauritius), had about 386 ktpa according to DGTR's 2020 findings (382 ktpa per CRISIL in 2025) and is adding 120 ktpa at Naidupeta, Andhra Pradesh. BKT Carbon, the captive arm of the off-highway tyre maker Balkrishna Industries, reached 360 ktpa (35 ktpa specialty) by April 2026 after a 92 ktpa expansion at Bhuj, and plans about 300 ktpa more within four years; the trade press calls it India's third-largest producer. Himadri, at about 250 ktpa, is therefore number four, not number three as our older primer said. Continental Carbon India (Ghaziabad), Epsilon Carbon (Ballari, integrated with coal-tar distillation) and Cabot India make up most of the rest; none of them publishes capacity.

In plain terms

Four firms own about four-fifths of Indian capacity, which would normally mean pricing power. Four ways to earn from carbon, and why Rain is in this bucket

A converter, a tar refinery, a global aluminium-carbon supplier and a set of bolt-on bets: the same chemistry, four different profit engines.

The first archetype is the scale furnace-black converter with power: PCBL's core and Birla Carbon. Revenue is tonnes times a selling price that passes through feedstock cost plus a conversion spread; profit is the spread per tonne plus power income, improved by specialty mix and yield. A greenfield plant costs about ₹1.2 lakh per tonne of annual capacity at Birla's Naidupeta (₹1,400–1,500 crore for 120 ktpa), while PCBL's Naidupeta Phase I is budgeted at about ₹73,000 a tonne (₹1,100 crore for 150 kt) and its Chennai brownfield cost about ₹50,000 a tonne (₹450 crore for 90 ktpa). At a realisation of ₹1.0–1.3 lakh a tonne, a greenfield plant turns over its capital about once a year; at ₹14,000–17,000 of EBITDA a tonne its pre-tax cash payback is five to nine years depending on cost, which is why brownfield debottlenecks earn far better returns than new sites (indicative arithmetic). Typical EBITDA margins are 13–18% and through-cycle ROCE 12–20%.

The second is the integrated coal-tar refinery: Himadri. Revenue is the barrel value of everything a tonne of tar yields: pitch for smelters, naphthalene for chemicals and the SNF/PCE concrete admixtures, and heavy oils, much of which Himadri burns in its own carbon black reactors instead of buying imported CBFS. Its carbon materials and chemicals segment, which holds all of these, earned a 19.0% segment margin in FY26 (15.2% in FY25) on ₹4,366.9 crore of external revenue, even though that revenue fell 4.8%. Integration lowers feedstock cost, capital intensity is lower because growth has come from brownfield additions on owned sites, and the company's own ROCE (excluding investments and capital work in progress) was 32% in FY26. Its weakness is disclosure: Himadri does not split revenue between carbon black, pitch and naphthalene, so any estimate of carbon black's share (we use 40–50% of revenue) is indicative.

The third is the global carbon-materials supplier: Rain. Its Carbon segment calcines green petroleum coke into anode-grade calcined coke (2.4 mtpa) and distils coal tar into pitch and oils (1.3 mtpa), both sold mainly to aluminium smelters; Advanced Materials (0.5 mtpa, the old Rütgers chemicals business) makes resins and aromatic intermediates; Cement (4.0 mtpa) sells in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana; and the group co-generates 187 MW. Rütgers is a German coal-tar chemicals group based in Castrop-Rauxel, acquired in 2013 through a Belgian holding company. Rain makes no furnace carbon black. It sits in this NSE classification because its distillation units sell carbon black oil to carbon black producers: in Q2 CY26, "lower calcination volumes were partly offset by higher carbon black oil sales in Carbon distillation".

No shield: anti-dumping history, trade deals and the tariff maze

India protected rubber-grade carbon black for about eleven years and then let the duty lapse. Today the industry's protection is cost, freight and trade agreements.

The trade-remedy history is longer than most summaries say. DGTR, India's trade-remedies authority, opened an anti-dumping investigation into carbon black used in rubber applications from Australia, China, Iran, Malaysia, Russia and Thailand on 26 December 2008, issued final findings on 24 December 2009, ran a mid-term review in 2013 and a first sunset review whose findings on 1 October 2015 continued duties on China, Russia and Thailand. A separate case against Korea was opened in February 2015 and terminated in November 2015. The second sunset review, filed by PCBL, Himadri and Continental Carbon India, was initiated on 20 May 2020; on 22 December 2020 DGTR recommended duties of US$494 a tonne on China and US$36.17 a tonne on Russia. On 5 January 2021 the Finance Ministry, by Office Memorandum F.No.354/148/2020-TRU, recorded the government's decision not to impose them. Duties had been in force from about 2010 to the end of 2020.

Why did the government say no? DGTR's own 2020 data help explain it. Imports from the subject countries were 8.1% of consumption in FY17, 9.2% in FY18, 14.2% in FY19 and 9.0% in FY20, and opposing parties pointed to Birla Carbon, which was not an applicant, earning a 20.8% operating margin in FY20. A duty would have raised costs for tyre makers, a much larger and more export-oriented industry. Since then India has become a net exporter (FY22) and its net exports have tripled, so a fresh case would have to argue injury from imports that are now under a tenth of demand. That is not impossible: Russian and Chinese price undercutting in 2024–25 was the kind of evidence trade cases are built on. But as of October 2026 DGTR's site shows no new case, and without a DGTR recommendation there can be no duty notification.

For carbon black the US is a modest market: about 5% of PCBL's revenue, and India's FY26 carbon black exports to the US were 46.7 kt, down 5.9%. CRISIL estimated the 50% peak cost PCBL 0.5–1.0 percentage points of margin, softened because the duty was computed net of the US-origin oil inside the product. The new regime is better for India relatively, since competing origins face higher effective duties, and PCBL talks of an "upcoming presence in Texas", where Aquapharm already has a plant. For Rain, Section 232 was the larger risk; it says calcined coke and pitch are on the exempt list and that US smelter restarts help it indirectly.

Environmental policy shapes costs at the margin. Carbon black plants are "red category" industries under the Central Pollution Control Board, and PCBL and Himadri run zero-liquid-discharge plants. PCBL reports greenhouse-gas intensity of 1.91 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per tonne of carbon black (Scope 1 and 2, FY25). The EU's carbon border tax (CBAM) does not yet cover carbon black or tyres, but customer Scope 3 targets increasingly reward tail-gas power and certified circular feedstock (Himadri's Mahistikry site holds ISCC PLUS certification). India's restrictions on petcoke imports, in place since 2018, are Rain's main domestic policy exposure: its Indian calciners ran at about 50% until 2024 and about 90% in 2025 after allocations improved. India Ratings reports a DTA allocation of 598,589 tonnes of green petcoke for FY26, while the SEZ unit (370,000 tonnes) may import without a cap for export production. Finally, the production-linked incentive for advanced chemistry cells (50 GWh) and the US push for non-Chinese battery supply chains underpin the battery-materials plans.

In plain terms

Indian carbon black makers once had a decade of anti-dumping protection. The government ended it in January 2021 because the tyre industry, the bigger customer, would have paid the cost, and because imports were already small. There has been no new case since. A company that tells you a duty is coming is guessing; one that tells you its edge is cost, freight and trade deals is describing the real world.

In plain terms

The US tariff on Indian goods has been 50%, then a promised 18% that never took effect, then 10% under one law and now 10% under another, which is being challenged in court. For carbon black the US matters less than Europe; for tyres it matters a lot. What counts is not the headline rate but the gap between India's rate and its competitors'.

The cleanest way to see how carbon black makes and loses money is to hold volume constant and watch the spread. PCBL is the right subject: it is the largest producer, discloses tonnes every quarter, and its management gives an EBITDA-per-tonne figure for the carbon black and power business. Why did the spread fall in FY25–FY26? Three things happened together. First, crude softened: Brent averaged about $63–64 in the December 2025 quarter. Under formula pricing a falling oil price is good for a quarter (selling prices lag down) and then neutral, but it also lowers the absolute rupee value of the margin the market will allow and weakens spot prices. Russia's lost market: EU sanctions and India's export arbitrage Deep dive

Europe shut out Russian carbon black in July 2024. Indian exporters filled part of the gap and tripled net exports, but paid for it in price.

Before 2023 Russia was Europe's carbon black supplier. CRISIL estimated that Russia exported about 1,000 kt in FY23, roughly 70% of it (about 700 kt) to the EU; PCBL puts Western Europe's import need at about 500 kt a year, and its management cites an EU-wide market of about 1.8 million tonnes. The EU's tenth sanctions package changed that. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/427 set a quota of 752,475 tonnes for Russian carbon black (CN 2803) from 26 February 2023 to 30 June 2024, about 560 kt a year, and from 1 July 2024 imports were banned. European tyre makers had to find hundreds of thousands of tonnes elsewhere, and Russian producers had to find new buyers for the same tonnes.

The Indian customs data show who won the European business. India's carbon black exports to the EU-27 were 4.4 kt in FY21 and 5.3 kt in FY22, then 43.0 kt in FY23, 48.4 kt in FY24, 99.7 kt in FY25 and 130.3 kt in FY26, when the EU took 29% of India's carbon black exports. The beachheads are Poland (25.2 kt in FY26, up 53%), Germany (20.6 kt), Italy (18.1 kt), the Netherlands (13.4 kt) and Belgium (12.9 kt, up 130%), the countries with large tyre plants. In Q1 FY27 PCBL shipped about 16,000 tonnes to Europe. China won a similar share of the European gap.

The windfall that ate the cash: the 2026 oil shock and working capital

Higher oil prices lifted margins and, in the same quarters, tied up hundreds of crores in inventory and receivables.

A pass-through business has a cash problem that its profit and loss account hides. When feedstock prices rise 30–50%, the company pays more for every tonne of oil or tar in its tanks and in transit, and it waits 60 days or more for customers to pay invoices that are now 30–50% larger. Supplier credit grows too, but usually by less, and some suppliers shorten it in a crisis. The result is that a year of record margins can also be a year of negative operating cash flow. The West Asia war produced exactly that in 2026, and because Brent re-spiked to $114 in September and about $125 in mid-October, the second half of FY27 will repeat it unless prices fall.

Himadri shows it most clearly, because it filed a full half-year balance sheet and cash flow on 17 October 2026. Its Q2 FY27 consolidated revenue rose 58% to ₹1,691 crore while all-product standalone volume rose only 8.3% (146,806 tonnes against 135,513): about a third of the revenue growth was price and pass-through. Gross profit was ₹520 crore in Q2, the same as in Q1, on revenue 18% higher. Gross margin fell from 38.2% a year earlier to 36.3% in Q1 FY27 and 30.8% in Q2 FY27, and company EBITDA margin from 22.2% to 17.9%, even though absolute EBITDA rose 27% to ₹303 crore.

PCBL's exposure is similar in kind. Its screener working-capital metrics for FY26 were 62 debtor days, 65 inventory days and 92 payable days, a 35-day cash conversion cycle; management warned in April of "significant pressure on working capital and hence on the net debt", and CARE reports it was using about 59% of its working-capital limits. The lesson for investors is to read the cash flow statement in the same breath as the margin. In an oil spike, EBITDA per tonne rises, reported profit rises, and debt rises too. In an oil collapse, margins fall for a quarter but cash floods back. The two effects partly offset over a full cycle, but they rarely land in the same fiscal year, and the companies with the most leverage, PCBL and Rain, have the least room to carry an extra ₹500 crore of working capital at the wrong moment. The RBI's 25-basis-point repo hike to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 makes that carry slightly more expensive.

In plain terms

When oil prices jump, a carbon black company's profit rises but its bank balance falls, because every tonne of oil in the tank and every unpaid customer invoice is now worth more. Himadri made ₹608 crore before tax in the first half of FY27 and still ended up ₹154 crore short on operating cash. If oil stays above $110 into early 2027, expect the same pattern again.

In plain terms

Strong profits in an oil spike and strong cash in an oil collapse are two halves of the same cycle. Judge a carbon company across a full cycle, and be wary of any balance sheet that cannot absorb a sudden ₹500 crore increase in working capital.

Six decades, five cycles: how the industry got here

A national champion, a protected decade, a Russian boom for exporters, a diversification wave and an oil-shock windfall.

Indian carbon black began as an import-substitution industry. Phillips Carbon Black was incorporated in 1960 and built its first plant at Durgapur; Birla's Hi-Tech Carbon started at Renukoot in 1988. Himadri was incorporated in 1987 in Kolkata and moved from coal-tar distillation into carbon black much later. Rain, incorporated in Hyderabad in 1974, began in cement and petroleum coke calcining before borrowing to globalise. The first cycle that matters for today's investor began in 2007–2013, when Rain bought CII Carbon in the US and then Rütgers in Germany, creating the dollar and euro debt that still dominates its balance sheet.

The profit history shows how differently the three rode those cycles. PCBL's profit after tax was a steady ₹426–491 crore from FY22 to FY24, then fell to ₹435 crore (FY25) and ₹198 crore (FY26) as interest on the Aquapharm debt met a falling spread. Himadri went the other way: from ₹39 crore in FY22, when its pitch and carbon black margins collapsed, to ₹755 crore in FY26, a nineteen-fold rise driven by mix, integration and a net-cash balance sheet. Rain swung from a record ₹1,577 crore (CY22, before minorities) to losses of ₹796 crore and ₹450 crore in CY23 and CY24, then a small profit in CY25.

The ROCE table is the most compressed history of the industry. PCBL earned 25% in FY19, before Aquapharm, when its borrowings were only ₹793 crore; by FY26 it earned 8%, half because of the spread and half because of the acquisition's goodwill and interest. Himadri fell to 4–5% in FY21–FY22 and climbed to 22% (32% on the company's own basis, excluding investments and capital work in progress). Rain's returns have stayed in single digits in five of eight years.

In plain terms

Ten years ago PCBL was the high-return company in this group and Himadri the struggling one. Today it is the reverse. The difference was not carbon black: it was what each company did with its balance sheet. PCBL borrowed to buy a chemicals business just before its core spread fell; Himadri paid down debt and invested in the products it already understood.
