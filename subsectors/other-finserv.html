Wealth & other financial services: four businesses, one ladder of multiples

A complete guide to the most mixed bucket in Indian finance: a family holding company that trades for less than one of its stakes, three wealth managers that live on fees from the country's richest families, a mutual-fund distribution platform that earns a toll on every SIP in small-town India, and two old financial groups still cleaning up after the 2018 credit bust. How each one makes money, why the market pays anywhere from 10 to 90 times their earnings, and what will move those numbers next.

00

The one-page view: four businesses filed under one label

"Other financial services" is a residual bucket. What ties its seven members together is not a shared business but a shared rule: the market pays for income that comes back every year, and discounts everything else.

Index providers put a company in "Other Financial Services" when it is clearly financial but is not a bank, a lender, an insurer or a stock exchange. The seven NSE 750 members that land here are therefore four different businesses with four different profit-and-loss accounts, regulators and valuation tools. Reading them as one industry is the first mistake an investor can make. Reading them as four separate industries is the second, because they share a single customer base (Indian savers moving money out of gold, property and deposits into financial assets) and a single pricing logic (the more of a company's income recurs, the higher its multiple).

The four archetypes are simple to name. Bajaj Finserv is a financial holding company: it owns 51.3% of Bajaj Finance, India's most valuable non-bank lender, and 77.3% each of Bajaj Life and Bajaj General, the two insurers it bought out from Allianz in January 2026. 360 ONE WAM, Nuvama Wealth Management and Anand Rathi Wealth are wealth managers that advise rich families for a fee and, in 360 ONE's case, also manufacture the funds they recommend. Prudent Corporate Advisory is a mutual-fund distribution platform that equips about 37,000 independent distributors, mostly in smaller towns, and keeps a slice of the commission they earn. JM Financial and Edelweiss Financial Services are older diversified groups that grew into wholesale and real-estate lending before 2018, were hurt when the IL&FS default froze that market, drew regulatory action in 2024, and are now turning themselves back into fee businesses.

The five forces that decide this bucket

  • Financialisation and the wealth boom (the volume engine). Indians put ₹3.40 lakh crore into mutual funds through SIPs in FY26, 19% more than a year earlier, and the number of ultra-high-net-worth individuals is forecast by Knight Frank to rise from 19,877 in 2026 to 25,217 by 2031. That is why wealth managers grew client assets 16–26% while the Nifty fell 3.3% in FY26.
  • Recurring versus transactional income (the multiple engine). Trail, advisory and management fees earned on assets come back every year; brokerage, deal fees and upfront commissions do not. FY26 and the first half of FY27 proved the difference: Nuvama's capital-markets revenue fell 19% and JM's advisory profit fell 59% while recurring income held.
  • Regulated prices (the yield engine). SEBI decides how much a mutual fund can charge and how distributors are paid. A new expense regime began on 1 April 2026, and a May 2026 consultation on commissions is still open. For a distributor that keeps 31 basis points of an 88 basis-point trail, a small rule change is a large profit change.
  • People and operating leverage (the margin engine). A wealth firm's factory is its relationship managers. New ones lose money for two to three years; once a book is mature, most extra revenue falls to profit. Buying growth (360 ONE's three acquisitions) pushes the cost-to-income ratio up; slowing hiring pulls it down.
  • Structure and legacy (the discount engine). For Bajaj Finserv, JM and Edelweiss the question is not whether the businesses are good but how much of their value reaches the listed shareholder. Bajaj Finserv trades below the market value of its Bajaj Finance stake alone; JM and Edelweiss trade at 1.1 and 2.8 times book against 4 to 28 times for the wealth managers.
In plain terms

These seven companies all live off Indians getting richer and putting more of their savings into markets. The market rewards the ones whose income arrives automatically every year, such as a commission on money that stays in a fund, and pays far less for income that depends on a good month for deals or trading, or that comes with a loan book attached. Most of what follows explains why that rule holds, where it breaks, and which numbers tell you which side of it a company is on.

Three recent events frame the rest of the report. First, the drawdown: a US–Iran conflict from late February 2026 pushed Brent crude above $100 a barrel, foreign investors sold a record ₹1.14 lakh crore of Indian equities in March, and the Nifty fell roughly 9–11% that month and 13.5% from its 26,373 peak. That tested whether wealth-firm profits are as sticky as their multiples assume; the answer so far is "mostly", with 360 ONE's Q2 FY27 profit down 4% and Anand Rathi's reported profit down 11% (but up 22% on its own adjusted measure). Second, Bajaj's purchase of Allianz's 26% stakes in the two insurers for ₹24,180 crore, completed in January 2026, which put a fresh private price on businesses the stock market now seems to value at less than nothing. Third, SEBI's new mutual-fund regulations, effective 1 April 2026, which rewrote how fund expenses are capped and disclosed and left the question of distributor commissions to a consultation that has not concluded.

The report is organised around these forces. Sections 1 to 6 explain the businesses, the market size, the cost structure and the four archetypes. Sections 7 to 10 are deep dives into the four themes that most define the bucket: the two speeds of fee income, the Bajaj Finserv holdco discount, the economics and regulation of mutual-fund distribution, and the legacy groups' turn from lender to fee house. Section 13 is the KPI masterclass, the toolkit for reading any of these companies' results. 01

From savings to fee: how a rupee of wealth becomes revenue

Money moves from households through an advice layer and a manufacturing layer into markets. Each layer takes a fee, and the layer that owns the client relationship decides who gets paid.

The wealth and distribution business is a chain. At one end sit Indian savers, from a schoolteacher in Rajkot with a ₹2,000 monthly SIP to a promoter family that has just sold a stake for ₹500 crore. At the other end sit the markets their money ends up in. In between are two layers that charge for their services: an advice and distribution layer, which owns the client and recommends what to buy, and a manufacturing layer, which builds and runs the products (mutual funds, portfolio-management services, alternative investment funds, insurance policies and structured notes). Every listed member of this bucket sits in one or both of those layers, except Bajaj Finserv, whose subsidiaries are mostly lenders and insurers.

Fees flow back up the chain: the investor pays the fund an expense ratio, the fund pays the distributor a trail, the adviser may also charge an advisory fee, and the plumbing earns basis points on custody and transactions. The richest margin goes to whoever owns the client and the product together.

The pricing at each step is distinctive. A mutual fund charges its investors a total expense ratio, a percentage of assets deducted daily from the fund's value. In a "regular" plan, sold through a distributor, part of that ratio is paid on to the distributor as a trail commission for as long as the money stays invested; in a "direct" plan, bought without a distributor, there is no trail and the ratio is lower. Since April 2019 SEBI has banned upfront commissions on mutual funds, so distribution income is now almost entirely trail. Wealth managers charge in more ways: an advisory fee as a percentage of assets, management fees and carried interest on the alternative funds they run, upfront fees on structured products and bonds, brokerage on trades, and interest on loans against clients' securities. Platforms like Prudent receive trail from fund houses and pass most of it to the independent distributors who brought the client.

Who keeps how much of the fee depends on where the client relationship sits. Take an illustrative equity fund sold through a distributor with a total expense ratio of 1.6%. The investor pays ₹1.60 a year for every ₹100 invested. Roughly ₹0.88 of that goes to the distribution chain as trail; if the distributor is an independent working through Prudent, about ₹0.57 reaches the distributor and ₹0.31 stays with the platform. The remaining ₹0.72 covers the fund house's management fee and scheme costs. The split explains a great deal of what follows: the fund house keeps the largest single slice, the person who meets the client keeps the next largest, and the platform in between keeps the thinnest, which is why its profit is so sensitive to regulation.

Integration changes the arithmetic. A firm that both advises the client and manufactures the product the client buys earns two layers of fee on the same rupee. 360 ONE is the clearest example: its wealth arm advises about 4,000 families with more than ₹10 crore each, and its asset-management arm runs alternative funds and portfolio services that many of those families hold. That is a large part of why it converts about 39% of its company-basis net revenue into profit, against 32% at Anand Rathi, which distributes other firms' funds and arranges structured products but manufactures little, and 17% at Prudent, which passes most of its gross commission to the distributors on its platform.

In plain terms

When you buy a mutual fund through an adviser, a small percentage of your money is taken every year and split between the fund company and the people who sold it to you. Whoever controls your relationship gets to decide what you buy, and therefore who gets paid. The firms that both advise you and run the fund you end up in collect twice, which is why they make the fattest margins.

The scarcest asset in the chain is the relationship with a very rich family. Knight Frank counts 19,877 Indians with net worth above US$30 million (about ₹264 crore at ₹88 to the dollar) in 2026, and Mumbai and Delhi together hold almost three-fifths of them. 360 ONE alone serves more than 4,000 families with over ₹10 crore invested, and those families account for about 95% of its wealth assets. Such relationships are slow to build, sticky once built, and expensive to lose, because a senior relationship manager who leaves can take a large part of a book with him. That is the core tension of the business: the asset that earns the fee is a client, but the person who holds the client is an employee.

Finally, the plumbing. Exchanges, depositories, the registrars CAMS and KFin, and custodians earn small fees on very large volumes. Only one member has a meaningful plumbing business: Nuvama's asset-services arm, which holds and clears securities for foreign portfolio investors and alternative funds, and which grew revenue 34% in Q1 FY27 to about ₹260 crore. It is a useful diversifier, because custody fees depend on assets held rather than on market direction, but it also exposed Nuvama to the SEBI–Jane Street episode in July 2025, when the stock fell about 11% in a day because Nuvama was a custodian and clearing member for the group.

The pools and the members' slice

Mutual-fund assets and SIP flows are measured precisely every month. "The wealth market" is not, and every estimate of it uses a different definition.

Two numbers anchor any discussion of this bucket because they are counted monthly by AMFI, the fund industry's association, and can be checked. Mutual-fund assets under management stood at ₹82.64 lakh crore at the end of September 2026, up from ₹73.73 lakh crore in March 2026 and ₹53.40 lakh crore in March 2024. Monthly SIP contributions reached a record ₹32,490 crore in September 2026. Everything else about the size of Indian wealth management is an estimate, and the estimates disagree because they measure different things.

AUM moves for two reasons, and investors should keep them apart. Flows are new money in minus money out; mark-to-market is the change in the value of what is already invested. FY26 shows the difference clearly. Industry AUM rose 12.2% in a year when the Nifty fell 3.3%, so the growth came overwhelmingly from flows. That matters for the distributors and wealth managers in this bucket because flows are, to a large extent, the result of their own work, while mark-to-market is not. A distributor whose AUM grows in a falling market is winning clients; one whose AUM grows only when markets rise is riding the tide.

In plain terms

"AUM" at these companies means five different things: assets that pay a recurring fee, all assets a client has parked including those in custody, mutual-fund money a distributor brought in, a loan book, or policyholder money at an insurer. Adding them up across companies produces a meaningless number. Compare each company with itself over time, and compare fees earned, not assets claimed.

Against the hard pools, the listed members are small. Prudent's ₹1.39 lakh crore of mutual-fund assets is about 1.7% of the industry, and its SIP book is about 3.7% of industry SIP flows, a higher share because its distributors focus on small, regular, equity investors. The three wealth managers' fee-earning pools (360 ONE's ARR assets, Anand Rathi's AUM and roughly ₹4 lakh crore of Nuvama's managed and advised assets excluding custody) add up to about ₹8.6 lakh crore, around a tenth of PwC's ₹85 lakh crore pool; this sum is an INDICATIVE estimate because it blends definitions. Their wealth revenue of roughly ₹6,500 crore is about 5–6% of Equirus's ₹1.2 lakh crore revenue pool, also INDICATIVE. Banks' private-banking arms, the unlisted NJ India, boutiques such as Waterfield, ASK and Sanctum, and a long tail of individual advisers hold the rest.

Two growth consequences follow from those small shares. First, the listed firms can keep compounding faster than their markets for years by taking share, which is what 360 ONE (ARR assets +26% in FY26), Anand Rathi (AUM +18% to September 2026) and Prudent (closing AUM +17.6% to June 2026) have been doing. Second, their growth is not capped by the size of the pool but by their ability to hire, keep and make productive the people who win the clients. The constraint is supply, not demand, which is why the cost and people sections of this report matter as much as the market-size section.

One definitional caution belongs here. 360 ONE's headline "total AUM" rose from ₹6.74 lakh crore in March 2026 (as stated in our primer) to ₹8.99 lakh crore in September 2026 according to its Q2 FY27 release, a 33% jump in six months that markets and flows cannot explain. The likely cause is a change in what is counted, as custody, broking and transactional assets from the B&K and UBS acquisitions were brought in. This report treats the ₹8.99 lakh crore as REPORTED but uses ARR AUM (₹3.12 lakh crore in March 2026, ₹3.53 lakh crore in September 2026) for every analytical comparison.

03

Where new wealth comes from

More rich families, a steady shift from informal to advised money, monthly SIP habits, a new shelf of products for the rich, and market levels that amplify all of it in both directions.

Demand for wealth advice and fund distribution is driven by five forces. Four are structural and move slowly; the fifth, market levels, is cyclical and moves fast. The listed firms' growth in FY26, when client assets rose 16–26% at the wealth managers while the Nifty fell, shows the structural forces outrunning a bad market. The fall in their transactional income in the same year shows the cyclical force still bites.

1. More rich families

The supply of wealth-management clients is growing quickly at the top. Knight Frank's 2026 Wealth Report counts 19,877 Indians with net worth above US$30 million and forecasts 25,217 by 2031, with billionaires rising from 207 to 313 over the same period. The Hurun India Wealth Report 2025 counted about 8.7 lakh dollar-millionaire households, roughly 90% more than in 2021 (a recalled figure, shown as reported). The wealth is concentrated: Mumbai has about 35% of India's ultra-rich and Delhi about 23%, which is why every wealth firm's largest offices are in those two cities and why competition for relationship managers there is most intense.

2. Liquidity events

Wealth managers grow fastest when clients receive cash. IPOs, promoter stake sales, private-equity exits and start-up ESOP sales all convert paper wealth into investable money, and a family that has just sold a stake needs advice on what to do with it. JM Financial's investment-banking pipeline gives a sense of the flow: about 60 filed IPOs worth roughly ₹1.5 lakh crore at the end of Q1 FY27. A busy primary market therefore helps the wealth managers twice, first through deal and distribution fees and then through the new money that lands in client accounts. A frozen primary market, as in March 2026, hurts both.

3. The shift from informal to advised money

A large share of Indian wealth is still managed by its owners or by informal advisers. Deloitte estimates that about US$0.4 trillion of the US$1.1 trillion of wealth-management demand in FY24 was self-managed or informally managed. As wealth passes to a younger generation, as regulation tightens around informal advice, and as products such as portfolio-management services and alternative funds become familiar, more of that money moves to firms that charge for advice. This shift is why the listed firms can grow faster than the stock of wealth itself. It is also hard to measure precisely, and a commonly cited figure that only 15% of Indian wealth is professionally managed has no traceable source.

4. SIPs and household financialisation

The mass-market version of the same shift is the SIP. Monthly contributions of ₹32,490 crore in September 2026, folios at 28.62 crore and equity inflows positive for more than five years in a row show that Indian households have adopted regular market investing as a habit. FY26 SIP contributions of ₹3.40 lakh crore were 19% higher than in FY25 despite a falling market. This is Prudent's core demand driver, and it supports the mutual-fund books of Anand Rathi and the fund houses inside Edelweiss, Bajaj Finserv and 360 ONE.

In plain terms

Indians are getting richer, and they are slowly moving their savings from gold, land and bank deposits into funds and shares. The very rich need advice on large sums; ordinary families set up monthly investments that run for years. Both trends create fee income for the firms in this report, and both kept going even when the stock market fell in early 2026.

5. Market levels: the amplifier

Markets do not create demand for advice, but they change its value. Rising markets lift AUM through mark-to-market and lift transactional income through more trading, more IPOs and more appetite for structured products. Falling markets do the reverse. FY26 was the test case: the Nifty fell 3.27% for the year and 13.5% from its 26,373 peak to about 22,820 at the end of March 2026, then rose 7.5% in April and closed at 22,997 on 15 October 2026. No clean index-level figure for calendar 2026 to date was found for this study; the only year-to-date measure available is a dollar-denominated India ETF proxy at roughly −11%, which mixes the rupee's move with the index's and is shown only as a proxy.

Operating leverage: why net flows matter more than markets

Once a wealth firm has covered its fixed costs, additional revenue is highly profitable. A rough rule from the dossier is that 60–70% of incremental revenue on a mature book falls to pre-tax profit, because the RM's fixed pay, the office and the technology are already paid for and only the variable bonus rises. This is the source of the sector's operating leverage, and it works in both directions: a 10% rise in assets through markets lifts profit by more than 10%, and a 10% fall cuts it by more. 360 ONE guides its cost-to-income ratio down by 100–150 basis points in FY27, to 49–50%, as the costs of its B&K Securities acquisition are absorbed; an earlier analyst expectation of 45–46% has slipped.

Capital: only where there is a balance sheet

Pure advice and distribution need almost no capital. Anand Rathi had ₹83 crore of borrowings against ₹999 crore of net worth in March 2026, and Prudent ₹34 crore against ₹883 crore. Capital enters where a member lends or insures. 360 ONE's ₹15,931 crore of borrowings fund the loans-against-securities book of 360 ONE Prime, which lends to wealth clients against their portfolios; Nuvama's ₹11,544 crore fund similar loans, margin trading and clearing. JM's ₹11,635 crore and Edelweiss's ₹18,595 crore fund what remains of their wholesale credit plus their housing-finance, ARC and private-credit businesses. Bajaj Finserv's ₹4.30 lakh crore are almost entirely Bajaj Finance's and Bajaj Housing Finance's lending books. Because these borrowings fund client or lending assets rather than corporate leverage, net debt and EV/EBITDA are not meaningful for most of the bucket, a point that matters when reading the peer table.

In plain terms

A mature wealth book is very profitable because the adviser's salary and the office are already paid for; extra money from the same clients is almost pure profit. New advisers are the opposite: they cost money for two or three years before their client books pay for them. When a firm hires heavily or buys another firm, its costs jump first and the payoff comes later.

Two capacity measures summarise this section and reappear in the KPI masterclass. For an advice firm, capacity is the number of RMs multiplied by the assets each can manage; Anand Rathi's ₹251 crore per RM and ₹7.6 crore per family (₹1,08,377 crore across 14,309 families) are the clearest public examples. For a platform, capacity is the number of active distributors multiplied by the assets each brings; Prudent's roughly 36,900 distributors and ₹1.39 lakh crore imply an average of about ₹3.8 crore of fund assets per distributor, an INDICATIVE figure that hides a long tail of very small agents. Neither business is limited by physical capacity; both are limited by the supply of trusted people.

Two speeds of fee income Deep dive

The 2026 drawdown ran a live experiment: recurring fees on assets held up, fees that depend on activity fell, and the profit lines of every member split along that seam.

Every firm in this bucket earns two kinds of fee. Recurring income (annual recurring revenue, or ARR, at the wealth managers; trail at the distributors) is a percentage of assets that clients keep invested, charged every year without a new decision by the client. Transactional income (TBR, transactional or brokerage revenue) comes from activity: brokerage on trades, upfront fees on structured products and bonds, investment-banking deal fees, carried interest when a fund sells an asset well. Recurring income moves with assets; transactional income moves with markets, sentiment and deal flow. The market values the two very differently, and FY26 to Q2 FY27 showed why.

Why the market pays more for recurring income

The reason is durability. A rupee of ARR earned this year is likely to be earned again next year, and the year after, as long as the client stays and the assets are not withdrawn; with typical client retention it behaves like an annuity that also grows with markets and new flows. A rupee of brokerage or deal fees earned this year says little about next year. An investor valuing the first is valuing a stream; an investor valuing the second is valuing a single payment and guessing at the next. That is why analysts value ARR-heavy firms partly on price-to-ARR-assets (360 ONE's market cap is about 12.4% of its ₹3.53 lakh crore ARR assets, an INDICATIVE calculation) and capital-markets firms on through-the-cycle earnings at lower multiples.

A simple ILLUSTRATIVE example shows the size of the effect. Take two firms each earning ₹100 crore of profit. Firm A earns ₹80 crore from recurring fees and ₹20 crore from transactions; Firm B the reverse. If the market pays 40 times recurring profit and 12 times transactional profit (round numbers within the ranges seen in this bucket), Firm A is worth ₹3,440 crore (80 × 40 + 20 × 12) and Firm B ₹1,760 crore (20 × 40 + 80 × 12): the same profit, valued at 34 times and 18 times. The numbers are invented; the mechanism is not, and it explains most of the ladder in Section 0.

In plain terms

A fee you collect every year on money that stays with you is worth far more than a one-off commission on a trade or a deal, even if this year's cheque is the same size. The first comes back next year without anyone lifting a finger; the second has to be won again. Investors pay up for the first and keep a wary eye on the second.

The holdco discount, decoded Deep dive

Bajaj Finserv's 51.3% stake in Bajaj Finance is worth more than Bajaj Finserv itself. The market is, in effect, assigning a negative value to two insurers that Bajaj paid ₹24,180 crore to own outright.

Bajaj Finserv is the largest company in this bucket by every measure, and the one whose share price is least explained by its own operations. It was created in 2007 when Bajaj Auto was split into three companies: Bajaj Auto (motorcycles), Bajaj Holdings & Investment (a portfolio company) and Bajaj Finserv (the financial businesses). Why the discount is so wide

Holding-company discounts exist everywhere, typically 10–40% for listed holdcos globally, because shareholders face an extra layer of costs and taxes, cannot choose which businesses they own, and depend on the holdco's capital-allocation choices. Bajaj Finserv's discount has widened for reasons specific to it. First, Bajaj Finance has done well and the insurers have not. Bajaj Finance's Q1 FY27 profit rose about 28% to ₹6,081 crore on assets up 24%. The MF toll road and SEBI's hand on the gate Deep dive

A distribution platform keeps 31 basis points of an 88 basis-point trail on money that keeps arriving every month. The regulator decides how big the trail is, and it has rewritten the rules twice in eight years.

Mutual-fund distribution looks like the dullest business in this bucket and is in some ways the most elegant. Prudent Corporate Advisory runs this business at scale through a platform: about 37,000 independent mutual-fund distributors (MFDs), most in smaller towns, use Prudent's technology, back office, research and product access, and Prudent passes on most of the commission it collects. Eighteen years of fee rules

SEBI has reshaped distribution economics repeatedly. The timeline below lists the main changes. The pattern is consistent: each round has cut or capped what investors pay and changed how distributors are paid, and each round has pushed the industry towards scale, trail income and platforms.

  • Aug 2009
    SEBI bans entry loads on mutual funds. Distributors must now be paid by fund houses out of scheme expenses, not by investors at the point of sale.
  • 2012
    An additional expense allowance of up to 30 bps for flows from beyond the top 30 cities (B30) encourages small-town distribution. It was later suspended (2023) and redesigned; the exact current form is approximate.
  • Oct 2018 → Apr 2019
    SEBI cuts expense-ratio slabs (equity maximum 2.25% on the first ₹500 Cr, falling to about 1.05% above ₹50,000 Cr), bans upfront commissions (trail only) and cuts the extra exit-load allowance to 5 bps.
  • Jan 2026
    SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026 notified, replacing the 1996 regulations.
  • 1 Apr 2026
    New expense regime takes effect: the Base Expense Ratio (BER) becomes the fund house's fee, with brokerage, levies and taxes disclosed separately; performance-linked fees are allowed; costs above the caps fall on the AMC, trustee or sponsor.
  • May 2026
    SEBI consultation on mutual-fund commission payments. Outcome pending at October 2026; content not retrieved for this report.
  • Q1 FY27
    A GST-related change lowers Prudent's payout yield by 3 bps to 57 bps; retained yield rises to 31 bps; GST-unregistered distributors move onto platforms (additions ~600 a month vs a FY26 run-rate of 413).

The 2026 regime: what is known and what is not

The SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026 replaced the old total-expense-ratio framework with a Base Expense Ratio that represents the fund house's management fee, with other costs (brokerage, regulatory levies, statutory taxes) disclosed and capped separately. The detailed numbers that matter most to distributors come only from secondary sources and have not been verified against the gazette for this report: BER ceilings reported at 2.10% for equity funds and 1.85% for other funds on the first ₹500 crore of assets, stepping down with size, and brokerage caps reported as cut from 12 to 6 basis points for cash-market trades and from 5 to 2 basis points for derivatives. On the evidence of Q1 FY27 results, the 2026 regulations did not introduce direct caps on distributor commissions. Prudent's gross yield held at 88 basis points, and the company describes it as a new steady state.

The live risk is the May 2026 consultation on commission payments. Its content was not retrieved for this study, so its proposals cannot be described with confidence; what matters is that SEBI has opened the question of how distributors are paid, and that the outcome could change the gross yield, the split between platform and distributor, or the structure of payments. For Prudent, which earns about 85% of revenue from mutual-fund trail, and for Anand Rathi, which earns about 52%, this is the single most important regulatory event on the calendar. The cautionary example from abroad is St. James's Place in the UK, whose fee structure was overhauled after the Financial Conduct Authority's Consumer Duty rules in 2024, with a large hit to its valuation.

In plain terms

The government's market regulator decides how much a mutual fund can charge and has changed the rules several times, always in the investor's favour. The newest rules took effect in April 2026 and so far have not cut what distributors earn; in fact a tax-related change helped Prudent slightly. But SEBI is now consulting on how distributors should be paid, and the answer could matter more to Prudent's profit than any change in the stock market.

Direct plans versus the small-town distributor

The other threat is direct plans. Since 2013 every fund has offered a direct plan with no distributor commission and a lower expense ratio, and zero-commission apps such as Groww, Zerodha Coin and Paytm Money have made direct investing easy for urban, self-directed investors. Prudent's counter-bet is that most Indians, especially outside the largest cities, want a person to explain, reassure and follow up, and that this human advice is worth the trail. Its network skews to smaller towns across 20-plus states and about 140 locations, and it serves about 20 lakh retail investors. Its SIP book of ₹1,203 crore a month in June 2026 (up 21% from ₹996 crore) is about 3.7% of industry SIP flows, while its assets are about 1.7% of the industry's, which shows how strongly its distributors focus on regular, small-ticket equity investing.

From wholesale lender to fee house Deep dive

JM Financial and Edelweiss grew big lending to developers and companies, were caught by the 2018 credit freeze and by regulators in 2024, and are now trying to become what the market pays for: capital-light fee businesses.

The capital-migration equation

The investment case for a legacy group is an equation about where its capital sits. Group return on equity is the weighted average of the returns earned on each pool of capital. JM's ₹10,658 crore of net worth can be split, ILLUSTRATIVELY, into about ₹2,500 crore in investment banking, wealth and asset management earning about 25%, about ₹5,000 crore in private markets earning 10–12%, and about ₹3,100 crore in lending earning 3–4%. That gives about ₹1,285 crore of profit on ₹10,600 crore, roughly 12%, close to the reported 11.9%. Moving ₹2,000 crore from the lending pool into fee businesses earning 25% would add about ₹430 crore of profit and lift return on equity to about 16%. That is the re-rating case: not faster growth, but a different mix.

Who sets the price of advice

Three regulators, a dozen rulebooks and a tax code decide what these firms may sell, to whom, and for how much. The rules move often, and usually in the investor's favour.

Regulation is not a background condition in this bucket; it is a pricing input. SEBI sets the maximum expense a mutual fund may charge and how distributors may be paid, the minimum ticket for portfolio-management services and alternative funds, and the rules separating advice from selling. The RBI regulates every lending arm, from Bajaj Finance to 360 ONE Prime's loans against securities and the ARCs at JM and Edelweiss. IRDAI regulates the insurers. Tax policy, set by the government, has twice in three years changed the economics of a product line overnight. A member's exposure to each of these is a large part of its risk profile.

Products for the rich

Three rule changes shaped the wealth managers' product shelves. In April 2023 market-linked debentures (MLDs), a form of structured note, lost their long-term capital-gains tax treatment, so gains are now taxed at the investor's slab rate. Many expected that to end the product; instead, issuers and distributors re-engineered it, and Anand Rathi's structured-product share of revenue rose from about 24% to about 27%. Since 2023–25 SEBI and the RBI have tightened alternative funds: the RBI's December 2023 circular restricted banks and NBFCs from investing in AIFs that lend to their own borrowers (a way of disguising stressed loans), and SEBI introduced pro-rata investor rights and dematerialised units. And SEBI's Specialised Investment Fund category (framework 2025, ₹10 lakh minimum) created a new mid-ticket product between mutual funds and PMS, giving wealth firms another shelf to sell from.

Market rules that hit the transactional lines

SEBI's circular of 1 October 2024 on equity derivatives, phased in from 20 November 2024, allowed only one weekly expiry per exchange, raised the minimum contract value to ₹15 lakh, required upfront premium collection and introduced intraday position monitoring. Derivatives volumes fell sharply in FY26, which explains much of Nuvama's 19% fall in capital-markets revenue and the weakness in JM's and B&K's broking. On 3 July 2025 SEBI's interim order against the Jane Street group added an event risk for custodians: Nuvama, as custodian and clearing member for the group, saw its stock fall about 11% the next day, and the episode slowed PAG's sale of its stake.

Insurance and the holdco

Three changes affect Bajaj Finserv's insurers. IRDAI's expense-of-management and surrender-value rules from October 2024 pressure life-insurer margins, because they limit costs and raise payouts to policyholders who exit early. From 22 September 2025 individual life and health premiums became exempt from GST; that helps sales but insurers lost input-tax credit on their costs, which ICICI Direct estimates cost Bajaj Life about 290 basis points of new-business margin. And the 2025 decision to allow 100% foreign ownership of insurers coincided with Allianz's exit from Bajaj and its move into a partnership with Jio Financial, so the policy that might have attracted foreign capital in fact preceded the departure of the one foreign partner in this bucket.

Thirty years, five cycles

A founding generation of broker-bankers, a decade of building financial supermarkets, the 2018 credit bust, a listing wave of pure-plays, and a drawdown that tested them all.

The history of this bucket is a history of business models falling in and out of favour. The diversified financial group, which combined investment banking, broking, lending and insurance under one roof, was the dominant model of the 2000s and early 2010s. The 2018 credit bust discredited it. Pure-play wealth and distribution businesses, spun out of or founded alongside those groups, became the favoured model of the 2020s. The firms in this report are, in effect, survivors and offspring of that cycle.

1995–2007: the founding generation

Edelweiss was founded in 1995 by Rashesh Shah and Venkat Ramaswamy as an investment bank. JM Financial, the Kampani family's banking house, ran a joint venture with Morgan Stanley from 1999 to 2007. Prudent was founded in Ahmedabad in 2000 by Sanjay Shah. Bajaj entered insurance in 2001 through joint ventures with Allianz (74:26), and in 2007 Bajaj Auto was demerged into Bajaj Auto, Bajaj Holdings & Investment and Bajaj Finserv, which took the financial businesses. Rakesh Rawal became chief executive of Anand Rathi's wealth business in the same year.

2008–2017: the financial supermarket

With cheap wholesale funding and a property boom, Edelweiss and JM built large wholesale and real-estate lending books, ARCs and, in Edelweiss's case, insurance, on top of their banking and broking franchises. IIFL Wealth, the business that became 360 ONE, was incorporated in 2008. The model looked powerful: a group that advised promoters on deals could also lend to them and distribute their paper to wealthy clients.

2018–2021: the bust

The September 2018 default of IL&FS froze wholesale funding for non-bank lenders. Edelweiss and JM were left holding developer loans they could not easily refinance, and Edelweiss's gearing and losses became an existential problem. Both began selling assets: Edelweiss agreed in 2020–21 to sell control of its wealth business to PAG; JM sold minority stakes in some businesses. SEBI's 2018–19 cut in fund expenses and its move to trail-only commissions reshaped distribution in the same period.

2019–2023: the pure-plays list

IIFL Wealth was demerged and listed in September 2019; Bain Capital bought about 25% in 2022 for about ₹3,680 crore; and it was renamed 360 ONE in November 2022. Anand Rathi Wealth listed on 14 December 2021 after an IPO at ₹550 a share. Prudent listed in May 2022, with its private-equity investor TA Associates (in since 2018) later exiting. Nuvama was demerged from Edelweiss with a record date of 2 June 2023 and listed in September 2023 with PAG as promoter.

2023–2026: boom, enforcement, buyout, drawdown

Monthly SIP inflows roughly doubled from ₹13,000–14,000 crore in FY23 to about ₹26,000 crore by March 2025 (recalled AMFI history), and wealth multiples expanded to 40–90 times earnings. 360 ONE made three acquisitions. In 2024 the RBI and SEBI acted against JM (March) and the RBI against Edelweiss (May), with all restrictions lifted within seven months. In 2025–26 Bajaj bought out Allianz for ₹24,180 crore and Edelweiss continued to monetise its parts. Then came the drawdown: a US–Iran conflict from late February 2026, Brent above $100 a barrel, record foreign selling, and an FY26 Nifty return of −3.27%.

Three lessons from the cycle carry into the analysis that follows. Balance sheets attached to fee businesses have been a source of value destruction more often than value creation in this industry, which is why the market discounts them. Regulatory change has repeatedly reset fee levels, always downward or sideways, and firms that depended on a single fee stream were hit hardest. And each period of strong markets has produced acquisitions and expansion whose cost shows up in the next downturn, as 360 ONE's cost-to-income ratio did in 2026.

Reading the numbers: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number that matters for a wealth manager, a distributor, a holdco or a legacy group: what it means, how it is calculated, what good looks like, how the seven compare, and where the definitions mislead.

The firms in this bucket publish a great many numbers, and most of them are defined by the company itself. "AUM", "revenue", "yield" and even "profit" mean different things at different firms, and sometimes at the same firm in different quarters. This section builds the toolkit from the bottom up. It starts with the equation that links the operating numbers to profit, then takes each native KPI in turn, then assembles a typical unit's economics, a benchmark scorecard, a list of definition traps and a ten-minute routine for reading a quarterly result. The wealth-manager equation comes first because it is the template; the distributor, holdco and legacy-group variants are covered as they arise.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the equation behaves. ILLUSTRATIVELY, take a wealth firm with average fee-earning assets of ₹1,00,000 crore earning a yield of 0.80% (80 basis points). Its recurring revenue is ₹800 crore. Add ₹250 crore of transactional revenue and net revenue is ₹1,050 crore. At a cost-to-income ratio of 50%, operating cost is ₹525 crore and pre-tax profit is ₹525 crore; after tax at 24%, profit is about ₹399 crore. Now change one input at a time. A 10% rise in markets lifts average assets to ₹1,10,000 crore and recurring revenue by ₹80 crore; with costs largely fixed in the short run, most of that reaches pre-tax profit, which rises by up to about 15%. A 5 basis-point fall in yield cuts revenue by ₹50 crore and pre-tax profit by nearly 10%. A one-point rise in the cost-to-income ratio costs ₹10.5 crore. The firm's profit is most sensitive to the two numbers it controls least, markets and yield, and the cost line is where management shows its discipline.

In plain terms

A wealth firm's profit is basically: the money it looks after, times the small percentage it charges each year, plus any one-off fees, minus what it pays its people and its offices. Most of what you need to know about a quarter is which of those four parts moved, and whether the firm caused the move or the market did.

1. Which AUM? Fee-earning assets versus the headline

What it is. Assets under management is the stock of client money a firm looks after, but firms count it in very different ways. The number that matters for revenue is fee-earning assets, the part on which the firm earns a recurring percentage. Formula. Fee-earning AUM = headline AUM − custody-only assets − transactional or broking assets − assets on which no recurring fee is charged. Worked example. 360 ONE reported total AUM of ₹8,99,481 crore at September 2026 but ARR AUM of ₹3,53,382 crore; only the second earns recurring fees, so a 10% move in the headline says little about revenue unless the ARR figure moves too. Drivers. Flows, markets and, for the headline, acquisitions and changes in what is counted. Benchmark. There is no good or bad level; what matters is that fee-earning AUM grows faster than the market and that the firm reports it consistently. How the members compare. 360 ONE reports ARR AUM; Nuvama reports client assets including about ₹1.3 lakh crore of custody that earns a few basis points; Anand Rathi and Prudent report client or fund assets on which they earn trail; Edelweiss reports fee-paying AUM for EAAA; Bajaj Finance's "AUM" is a loan book.

2. ARR AUM and the recurring share of revenue

What it is. ARR AUM is the part of client assets that earns annual recurring revenue: advisory fees, discretionary PMS fees, management fees on the firm's own funds and trail on distributed funds. The ARR share is recurring revenue as a percentage of net revenue. Formula. ARR share = ARR revenue ÷ net revenue. Worked example. 360 ONE's FY26 ARR revenue was ₹2,289 crore on net revenue of ₹3,144 crore, a share of about 73% on these figures; the company and our primer describe it as about 75%, the difference reflecting other income and definitions. Drivers. Up when clients move from transactional products into advisory and discretionary mandates, when the firm's own funds grow, and when markets are weak (because transactional income falls faster). Down in strong markets with heavy trading and deal activity. Benchmark. Good ≥70%; average 50–70%; weak <50%. How they compare. 360 ONE about 75%, Nuvama about 59%, Prudent about 85% (trail) plus insurance, Anand Rathi about 52% mutual-fund trail plus about 27% structured products, JM and Edelweiss not disclosed on a comparable basis.

3. ARR retention: the yield in basis points

What it is. The annual percentage the firm earns on its recurring assets. Formula. ARR retention = annualised ARR revenue ÷ average ARR AUM. Worked example. 360 ONE: FY26 ARR revenue of ₹2,289 crore on average ARR assets of about ₹2,80,000 crore (₹2.48 lakh crore opening, ₹3.12 lakh crore closing) gives about 82 basis points (INDICATIVE); the company reported 74 basis points in Q1 FY27 and 79 in Q2 FY27. 4. Transactional revenue (TBR)

What it is. Revenue that depends on activity rather than on assets held: brokerage, upfront distribution fees on bonds and structured products, investment-banking fees, carried interest. Formula. TBR = net revenue − ARR revenue (for firms that split them). Worked example. 360 ONE's FY26 TBR was ₹777 crore, about a quarter of net revenue. After the B&K Securities acquisition, management guided quarterly TBR of ₹175–180 crore, up from ₹125–130 crore, because B&K's institutional broking is transactional by nature. Drivers. Market levels and volatility, primary-market activity, derivatives rules, and client risk appetite. Benchmark. There is no "good" level; the question is how large a share of profit depends on it. Below about 30% of revenue, a firm can absorb a bad year; above 40%, its profit is effectively cyclical.

6. Cost-to-income ratio

What it is. Operating costs as a percentage of net revenue. Formula. C/I = (employee cost + other operating cost) ÷ net revenue. Worked example. 360 ONE's FY26 C/I of 49.9% on ₹3,144 crore of net revenue implies about ₹1,569 crore of costs; a one-point rise would cost about ₹31 crore of pre-tax profit. Drivers. Up with RM hiring (new RMs are unprofitable for two to three years), acquisitions (B&K lifted 360 ONE's ratio to 51.3% in Q1 FY27), ESOP charges and weak markets (revenue falls, costs do not). Down with maturing RM books, slower hiring and rising markets. Benchmark. For wealth firms: good ≤45%, average 45–55%, weak >55%. Global private banks run at 65–80% (recalled, INDICATIVE).

9. Distributor yields: gross, payout and net

What it is. For a distribution platform, three yields matter: the gross commission received from fund houses, the payout passed to sub-distributors, and the net retained. Formulas. 13. Insurance KPIs: new-business margin, combined ratio and solvency

14. Adjusted versus reported profit, and whose profit it is

What it is. Firms increasingly publish their own profit measures alongside the statutory figure, and holdcos report profit before and after minority interests. Formulas. Adjusted PAT = reported PAT − fair-value gains on own investments + ESOP cost ± tax effects (Anand Rathi's definition); attributable PAT = consolidated PAT − minority interest. Worked example. Anand Rathi's Q1 FY27 reported profit of ₹163 crore included about ₹110 crore of other income and fair-value gains; its adjusted figure was about ₹116 crore. In Q2 the gap reversed: reported ₹89.3 crore, adjusted ₹121.9 crore. Bajaj Finserv's FY26 consolidated profit of ₹19,669 crore is before minority interest; only ₹9,801 crore belongs to its shareholders. Drivers. Definition traps, collected

  • AUM means fee-earning assets at 360 ONE (ARR AUM), client assets including custody at Nuvama, client wealth at Anand Rathi, distributed mutual-fund assets at Prudent, a loan book at Bajaj Finance, policyholder funds at Bajaj Life and security receipts at an ARC. Never add them.
  • 360 ONE's total AUM went from ₹6.74 lakh crore (March 2026) to ₹8.99 lakh crore (September 2026) on an apparent change of definition. Use ARR AUM.
  • Revenue is gross total income on screener (₹4,470 crore for 360 ONE, ₹4,638 crore for Nuvama, ₹4,223 crore for JM) but net revenue in company reports (₹3,144 crore, ₹3,122 crore and ₹2,749 crore), because the gross figure includes interest on loan books. Use net for margins and yields; gross only for like-for-like screens.
  • Profit is pre-minority on screener for the holdcos (Bajaj Finserv ₹19,669 crore; Edelweiss ₹680 crore) but attributable in this report (₹9,801 crore; ₹547 crore). JM's Q1 FY27 profit has three definitions. Anand Rathi now leads with adjusted profit.
  • Yield is ARR retention at 360 ONE, all-in revenue over assets at Anand Rathi, and gross or net trail at Prudent. They are not comparable.
  • Net flows may include acquired books (360 ONE's FY26 figure includes the UBS book). Ask for organic flows.
  • Operating margin and cost-to-income are often quoted from different bases and do not sum to 100%.
  • ROE includes goodwill at acquisitive firms (360 ONE) and leverage at firms with lending arms (Nuvama, Bajaj Finserv). Check tangible ROE and capital intensity.
  • Per-share data at Nuvama before its 5-for-1 split (face value now ₹2) are not comparable with current figures; the ₹14 dividend in our primer does not reconcile with a 49% payout on ₹57 EPS.

  • Insurance quarters quoted as years: a single-quarter combined ratio is not an annual result.

How to read a wealth or financial-services company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find the archetype first (30 seconds). Holdco, wealth manager, distributor or legacy group? It decides which numbers below matter.
  2. Pull fee-earning assets, not headline AUM (1 minute). ARR AUM, distributed MF AUM or fee-paying AUM, closing and average. Check for a definition change if the headline grew much faster.
  3. Split growth into flows and markets (1 minute). Net flows as a percentage of opening assets, organic only. Over 12% annualised is strong; under 6% is a warning whatever the market did.
  4. Compute the yield (1 minute). Recurring revenue ÷ average fee-earning assets, annualised. A drop of more than 3–5 basis points in a quarter needs an explanation (mix, regulation or pricing).
  5. Separate recurring from transactional revenue (1 minute). Recurring growth is the quality signal; treat transactional revenue as a cyclical bonus.
  6. Check cost-to-income and RM count together (1 minute). Rising costs with rising RM count is investment; rising costs with flat RM count is a margin problem.
  7. Reconcile the profit lines (1.5 minutes). Reported, adjusted and attributable. Identify fair-value gains, ESOP charges, one-offs and minority interests. Use attributable for valuation.
  8. For holdcos, update the sum of the parts (1.5 minutes). Mark listed stakes to market, note any new transaction prices, recompute the discount. For insurers, read combined ratio, VNB margin and solvency, not quarterly PAT.
  9. For legacy groups, read the balance sheet (1 minute). Size of the run-down book, provisions, capital moved into fee businesses, segment returns, borrowings.
  10. Scan for red flags (30 seconds). A changed KPI definition, a new "adjusted" metric, a guidance cut, rising pledges or promoter selling, regulatory notices, a fall in SIP book or distributor count, or a combined ratio above 105%.
In plain terms

When results come out, ignore the headline profit first. Look at whether clients added money, what percentage the firm earned on it, whether costs grew faster than revenue, and which profit figure the company is showing you. For a holding company, check what its stakes are worth. That takes ten minutes and tells you more than the press release.

Four maps show the same pattern from different angles: recurrence and capital-lightness earn the multiple; growth alone does not.

Operationally, a winner grows fee-earning assets faster than the market, holds its yield, converts revenue into profit and earns a high return on the capital it uses. In the stock market, a winner is one whose profits investors trust enough to pay a high multiple. The four maps below place the members on both dimensions. Technology and disruption

Apps take the self-directed investor, banks bundle advice with loans, AI lowers the cost of serving the mass-affluent, and conduct failures remain the oldest risk of all.

Technology threatens this bucket unevenly. It has already taken a large share of urban, self-directed investors from distributors through direct plans and zero-commission apps. It is lowering the cost of serving the mass-affluent through robo-advice and AI-assisted tools. It threatens the senior relationship manager serving a ₹50 crore family much less, because that relationship is built on trust, complexity and access to products that no app can replicate. The members' exposure therefore runs roughly in reverse order of client wealth: Prudent and the mutual-fund side of Anand Rathi most, 360 ONE's UHNI franchise least.

Direct plans and zero-commission apps

Every Indian mutual fund has offered a direct plan without distributor commission since 2013, and apps such as Groww, Zerodha Coin and Paytm Money have made it easy to buy them. They have taken a large share of new urban investors who are comfortable choosing funds themselves. The distributors' defence is that most Indian households, especially outside the largest cities, want a person to explain products, reassure them in a downturn and handle paperwork. Prudent's network is skewed to smaller towns for exactly this reason, and its SIP book's growth (21% in the year to June 2026) suggests the defence is holding. Anand Rathi has its own digital channel, Digital Wealth, with ₹2,218 crore of assets (+22%), about 2% of its total; 360 ONE bought ET Money in 2024 for about ₹370 crore to own a direct-investing platform.

AI and robo-advice

AI tools lower the cost of research, portfolio construction, client reporting and compliance. For the mass-affluent, they make low-cost automated advice viable; for platforms, they make each distributor more productive. Prudent has announced an AI-powered distributor platform. Bajaj Finance, inside Bajaj Finserv, has guided to a 25–40 basis-point improvement in its operating cost relative to net total income from AI. For the wealth managers, AI is more likely to raise RM productivity (more families per RM, faster reporting) than to replace RMs, which would support margins. The risk is to firms whose value lies in standardised advice to mid-sized clients, where software can substitute for people.

Banks, GIFT City and new competitors

Bank private banks remain the main share threat to the listed specialists: they already know their clients' finances and can bundle wealth advice with lending and deposits at low marginal cost. GIFT City, India's offshore financial centre, is creating outbound funds and new structures that bring global managers closer to Indian clients. And SEBI's new Specialised Investment Fund category creates room for new mid-ticket products and managers.

Conduct and operational risk

The oldest disruption in wealth management is self-inflicted. Structured products sold to HNI clients can produce complaints and regulatory scrutiny if they perform badly in a drawdown, which is a specific risk for Anand Rathi. Underperformance in PMS and alternative funds can trigger outflows, as 360 ONE experienced in parts of its PMS book; it also carries a ₹336 crore tax demand under appeal. Custody and clearing businesses carry operational and counterparty risk, as Nuvama's exposure to the Jane Street episode showed. And the legacy groups' 2024 regulatory actions are a reminder that regulators now scrutinise structures that blur lending, distribution and investment.

In plain terms

Apps have already won the young city investor who is happy to pick funds alone. They have not won the family with ₹50 crore, or the small-town saver who wants someone to call. The firms in this report are betting on those two groups. Their bigger risks are a bank offering the same service more cheaply, or a mis-sold product that turns into a scandal.

Bull vs bear

The structural case is strong and widely shared. The risks are concentrated in valuation, regulation and the gap between record client flows and flat profits.

The bull and bear cases for this bucket are both easy to state, and both are supported by the same data. The bull points to 19% SIP growth, client assets compounding at 16–26% through a falling market, and capital-light businesses earning 25–47% returns on equity. The bear points to multiples of 30–90 times earnings, a regulator that has cut fees every few years and has another consultation open, and Q2 FY27 profits that fell at two of the highest-quality firms despite record flows.

The bull case

  • Structural demand: more ultra-rich families (19,877 → 25,217 by 2031, Knight Frank), a steady shift from informal to advised money, and ₹3.40 lakh crore a year of SIPs that kept arriving through the March 2026 sell-off.
  • Recurring income proved itself: ARR and trail held up in FY26 while transactional income fell; 360 ONE posted record ARR flows and retention rose to 79 bps.
  • Capital-light compounding: Anand Rathi, Prudent and Nuvama earn 27–47% ROE and can grow without raising equity.
  • Operating leverage ahead: 360 ONE guides cost-to-income down 100–150 bps as B&K is absorbed; RM hires from FY25–26 mature into profit.
  • Unlock catalysts: the EAAA IPO at Edelweiss, JM's capital migration and any insurer listing or simplification at Bajaj Finserv could narrow discounts that are at 1.1–3.6 times book.
  • Regulation so far benign: the 2026 MF regulations did not cut distributor yield; a GST change actually raised Prudent's retained yield.

The bear case

  • Valuation: Anand Rathi at ~90× and Prudent at ~57× FY26 earnings need roughly 22–27% annual earnings growth for a decade to justify a 12% return (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Commission risk: SEBI's May 2026 consultation is open; a 5 bp cut absorbed by Prudent would remove about 23% of its profit (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Flows are not profits: 360 ONE's Q2 PAT fell 4% and Anand Rathi's reported PAT 11% despite record flows; costs and transactional lines matter.
  • Market beta: a further 10% fall in equities would hit transactional income first and AUM-linked fees second; Prudent's book is 97% equity.
  • Overhangs: PAG's exit and a 33.9% pledge at Nuvama; Bain's stake at 360 ONE; a widening holdco discount at Bajaj Finserv.
  • Legacy risk relabelled: JM's profits now lean on "private markets", which contain credit risk; Edelweiss carries ₹18,595 Cr of borrowings and insurance losses.

The balance between the two depends on which member one holds. For the wealth managers and Prudent, the bull case is largely about the next five to ten years of Indian financialisation, and the bear case is about the price already paid for it plus one regulatory decision. For Bajaj Finserv, the bull case is that the insurers recover and the discount narrows from an unusually wide level; the bear case is that the discount persists as long as the young businesses lose money. For JM and Edelweiss, the bull case is that cheap valuations already discount the legacy, and the bear case is that the legacy is not finished.

In plain terms

Almost everyone agrees that Indian wealth management will grow fast for many years. The argument is about price. The best businesses already trade at prices that assume excellent growth for a decade, while the cheap ones are cheap because they still carry risks from the past. A single SEBI decision on commissions could matter more than a year of market moves for the distributors.

HNI / UHNIHigh-net-worth individual / ultra-high-net-worth individual; UHNI usually means net worth above US$30 million (about ₹264 Cr).
Holdco discountThe percentage by which a holding company's market value falls short of its GAV.
LAS / MTFLoans against securities / margin trading facility: lending to clients secured on their portfolios.
MFDMutual-fund distributor, registered with AMFI and paid trail commission by fund houses.
MLDMarket-linked debenture: a structured note whose return depends on a market index; lost its capital-gains tax advantage in April 2023.
Net flowsNew client money minus withdrawals in a period, excluding market movements.
NBM / VNBNew-business margin / value of new business: the economic value of new life policies written, and that value as a percentage of premium.
PMSPortfolio management service: a managed account in the client's name; ₹50 lakh minimum.
RMRelationship manager: the adviser who owns the client relationship in a wealth firm.
SIFSpecialised Investment Fund: a SEBI category (2025) between mutual funds and PMS, ₹10 lakh minimum.
SIPSystematic investment plan: a standing instruction to invest a fixed amount in a mutual fund every month.
Solvency ratioAn insurer's available capital as a multiple of the regulatory requirement; IRDAI's minimum is 150%.
SOTPSum of the parts: valuing a group by valuing each business separately and adding them up.
Structured productA note or debenture whose payoff is linked to a market index, often with capital protection; sold to HNI clients for an upfront fee.
TBRTransactional or brokerage revenue: income from trades, upfront fees, deals and carry, not from assets held.
TERTotal expense ratio: the annual percentage of a fund's assets charged to investors.
Trail commissionThe annual commission a fund house pays a distributor as a percentage of the assets that remain invested.
