00

The one-page view: a utility hiding inside a chemical company

Air costs nothing, power costs a great deal, and the money is made by whoever signs the longest contract closest to the customer.

Industrial gas companies take the air around us, cool it until it turns liquid at about −183 °C, and boil it apart into oxygen, nitrogen and argon. The feedstock is free. The process needs a great deal of electricity and a plant that costs several hundred crore rupees. What comes out is sold three ways: down a pipeline to a steel mill next door on a contract that runs for 15 to 20 years, in cryogenic road tankers to factories and hospitals within a few hundred kilometres, or in steel cylinders to welders, laboratories and clinics. Each route has its own economics, and the mix between them decides almost everything about a gas company's margins, growth and valuation.

India's outsourced industrial gas market was worth roughly ₹13,500 crore in FY26. Two independent methods land within 1–2% of each other: CRISIL credits INOX Air Products' ₹3,034 crore with a 22.4% share (implying about ₹13,545 crore), and Ken Research put 2025 at US$1.41 billion (about ₹13,380 crore at ₹94.9 to the dollar). That is small by global standards — around 1.3–1.5% of a world market worth about $100–110 billion — for the world's second-largest steel producer. The gap is the growth story: per-capita gas use in India is low, and every new blast furnace, semiconductor fab, solar-cell line and coal-gasification plant needs gas on tap.

The listed slice is thin. Of the three companies filed under "Industrial Gases" in the NSE 750, only two make gas. Linde India (75% owned by Linde plc) is the second-largest single gas company in the country, with FY26 revenue of ₹2,531 crore, of which the gases segment was ₹2,128 crore gross and the rest project engineering. Ellenbarrie Industrial Gases is a Kolkata family company that listed in July 2025, with ₹342 crore of revenue from a dense network of plants in eastern and southern India. Refex Industries started life as a refrigerant-gas trader, but in FY26 its refrigerant revenue was ₹34.6 crore against ₹2,012.7 crore from handling coal and fly ash for thermal power stations, and it shut the refrigerant business in January 2026. Refex is in this report because of its label, and it serves as a case study in why sector codes lag reality.

The five forces that define the sub-sector

  • The tonnage-contract annuity. Large customers — overwhelmingly steel mills — outsource their oxygen and nitrogen to a gas company that builds an air separation unit (ASU) inside their fence and bills a fixed facility fee plus a power-linked variable charge for 15–20 years. India's steel capacity is 222 Mtpa against a 300 Mtpa target for 2030. Linde India's acquisition of two 1,800 tonnes-per-day ASUs at Tata Steel Kalinganagar, completed on 30 September 2026, is the largest single addition in its history.
  • Electricity is the raw material. Power is 50–70% of the cash cost of making gas. Linde India spent 16.5% of FY26 revenue on power and fuel; Ellenbarrie 21.6%, down from 36.1% in FY23. Contract design decides who carries that cost: on-site contracts pass it through, merchant liquid prices reset only with a lag.
  • The parent and the minority. Linde plc owns 75% of the listed Linde India and 100% of the unlisted Praxair India, which operate in the same country under a 2020 territory-sharing agreement. Minority shareholders rejected related-party transactions with Praxair India in 2021 and again, 89% against, in March 2026. The case is now in the Supreme Court.
  • A market that is mostly unlisted. INOX Air Products (₹3,034 crore, 22.4% share), Praxair India, Air Water India and Air Liquide India are all private. INOX Air Products filed a draft prospectus for a pure offer-for-sale in September 2026; once it lists, investors will have a larger, more profitable comparable.
  • The scarcity premium. Linde India trades at about 89× FY26 earnings and Ellenbarrie at 35× trailing earnings, against roughly 25–30× for global gas majors. Scarcity of listed pure-plays explains much of that gap, and the INOX listing tests it.

Three facts temper the margin story. First, Linde India's reported 36% operating margin for FY26 includes a ₹90 crore reversal of liabilities booked against expenses; strip it out and the underlying margin is about 32%, and underlying pre-tax profit actually fell year on year once a ₹55.5 crore one-time share of joint-venture profit is removed too. Third, the near-term steel signal has cooled: crude steel output grew 3.0% in Q1 FY27 and hot metal, the most oxygen-hungry step, only 1.4%, against 10.7% growth for FY26 as a whole. Contracted tonnage still adds revenue, but merchant volumes depend on a softer industrial backdrop.

In plain terms

A good industrial gas company is closer to a toll road than a chemical maker. It builds a plant next to a big customer, signs a contract that guarantees a minimum payment for two decades, passes the electricity bill through, and keeps a steady return on the money it spent. Around that core it sells surplus liquid by tanker at whatever the local market will pay. In India only two listed companies do this, they earn margins that rival anyone in the world, and investors have paid very high multiples for the privilege. The questions now are whether earnings keep up, whether Linde India's minority shareholders get a fair deal from its parent, and what happens to those multiples when India's biggest gas company lists.

01

From air to invoice: how a molecule becomes money

One plant, three products, three delivery routes — and a hard limit on how far a tanker can travel before the margin evaporates.

Dry air is 78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen and 0.93% argon, with traces of carbon dioxide, neon, krypton, xenon and helium. A cryogenic air separation unit filters and compresses air to about 5–6 bar, strips out water and carbon dioxide on molecular sieves, cools it in a heat exchanger and an expansion turbine until it liquefies, and then distils it in a double column. Oxygen collects at the bottom, nitrogen at the top, and argon is drawn from a side column. The products leave either as gas (cheap to make, sent by pipeline) or as liquid (two to three times more power per tonne, but storable and transportable).

The three routes to market

On-site or tonnage supply is the anchor. A steel mill, refinery or fab needs hundreds or thousands of tonnes of gas a day, continuously. Rather than build and run an ASU itself, it signs a build-own-operate contract: the gas company builds the plant inside or next to the customer's site, owns it, runs it, and is paid a fixed monthly facility fee (which recovers the capital and a return) plus a variable charge that passes through the electricity cost, with escalation clauses linked to inflation indices. A take-or-pay clause guarantees payment for a minimum volume — usually 70–90% of contracted capacity — whether the customer draws it or not. The economics resemble a regulated utility with growth.

Merchant or bulk liquid is the profit kicker. Liquid oxygen, nitrogen and argon are trucked in insulated tankers to customer storage tanks at hospitals, glass plants, pharma units, food processors and fabricators. Prices are set per tonne delivered, often on annual contracts, sometimes spot. Margins per tonne are the highest in the business, but volumes and prices move with industrial activity, power cost and the amount of spare liquid capacity in the region.

Packaged gases — cylinders, cylinder quads, dewars and specialty mixtures — carry the highest price per unit and the highest handling cost. Margins depend on distribution density, safety record and cylinder rental. This is the most fragmented end of the market, with hundreds of small fillers.

A fourth activity sits beside these: engineering, building ASUs for oneself or for others. Linde India is the Linde group's project engineering hub for India, and its project engineering division (PED) had gross revenue of ₹980.6 crore in FY26. Ellenbarrie won its first large third-party order — a ₹480.73 crore turnkey 1,200 TPD ASU for BHEL — in September 2026. Engineering revenue is lumpy and earns far lower margins than gas, which matters when reading quarterly numbers.

Where the value is captured: density beats size

The single most important physical fact about this industry is that liquid gas is cheap per kilogram and expensive to move. A tonne of liquid oxygen sells for roughly ₹8–15 a kilogram; running a cryogenic tanker costs something like ₹50–80 a kilometre. Beyond about 200–300 km from the plant, the freight eats the margin. Each ASU therefore commands a local catchment, and most industrial clusters in India have only two or three credible bulk suppliers. Ellenbarrie calls this the "molecule-mile". It is why a ₹342 crore regional company earns an operating margin within two points of Linde India's, and why the national market share of "others" (about 30–40%) overstates how fragmented any single region really is.

Within that local structure, value sits in three places. On-site tonnage captures the most durable value: a utility-like return of perhaps 12–16% on project capital for two decades. Merchant liquid captures the highest margin per tonne, especially in argon and medical oxygen, but is cyclical. Engineering captures little margin directly but lowers the cost of a company's own plants and wins strategic relationships. What does not capture value is the feedstock (free) and the trading of imported commodity gases, which is exactly the business Refex exited. For how refrigerant gases are actually manufactured and why the producers rather than the traders hold the economics, see our fluorochemicals report.

In plain terms

Imagine a factory whose raw material is free, whose biggest bill is electricity, and whose product cannot travel far. It wins by putting plants exactly where the customers are, locking the biggest customers into decades-long contracts, and selling the leftovers locally. That is the whole industrial gas business model.

02

A ₹13,500 crore market, and why the estimates disagree

Two independent methods agree on the size; the growth forecasts range from 7.5% to 12% a year depending on who is selling the shares.

Market sizes for industrial gases in India vary by a factor of almost three depending on scope. The broad numbers — IMARC's $3.81 billion, TechSci's $3.06 billion — appear to include captive hydrogen, carbon dioxide and specialty gases that never pass through a gas company's books, and their scope is unclear. The useful number is the outsourced market: gas bought by customers from gas companies. On that basis, the evidence converges on about ₹13,500 crore for FY26.

03

Steel writes the order book; fabs and gasifiers add pages

Every million tonnes of blast-furnace steel needs roughly 350–450 tonnes of oxygen a day, and India plans to add almost 80 million tonnes by 2030.

Gas demand is derived demand: no one buys oxygen for its own sake. The customer that matters most is the integrated steel mill, which blows oxygen through molten iron in a basic oxygen furnace to burn off carbon, enriches its blast-furnace air with oxygen to raise output, and uses nitrogen and argon in casting, purging and stainless refining. Gas is typically under 2–3% of a steel mill's cost, so demand is insensitive to price and tracks steel output and capacity almost one for one. For the steel industry itself, see our iron and steel report.

The near-term signal has cooled

FY26 was a strong year for steel, with crude output up 10.7% to 168.4 million tonnes. Q1 FY27 was weaker. PIB's July 2026 bulletin showed crude steel up only 3.0% to 42.1 million tonnes and hot metal — iron from blast furnaces, the most oxygen-intensive step — up just 1.4%. Finished-steel consumption rose 8.3%, but imports jumped 49% to 2.06 million tonnes, with China supplying 32%. In other words, Indian demand is fine but domestic mills ceded share to imports, and oxygen demand from existing mills grew in low single digits. Stainless consumption rose 27.5%, which is good for argon demand. For contracted tonnage the slowdown matters less, because take-or-pay floors protect revenue; for merchant liquid it matters more.

The other demand pools

Chemicals, fertiliser and coal gasification. Chemical plants use nitrogen for inerting and oxygen for oxidation; refineries and fertiliser plants use hydrogen. Coal gasification is the newest oxygen-hungry segment: the government targets 100 million tonnes of coal gasification by 2030, backed by an ₹8,500 crore incentive scheme, and each gasifier needs a large ASU. BHEL and Coal India's 2,000 TPD coal-to-ammonium-nitrate project at Bandhabal, Jharsuguda (Odisha) is the first visible example — its 1,200 TPD ASU is the order Ellenbarrie won. For the downstream businesses, see our fertilisers and commodity chemicals reports.

Electronics and solar. Semiconductor fabs consume huge volumes of ultra-high-purity nitrogen — a mid-size fab might need 10,000–30,000 Nm³ an hour (INDICATIVE) — plus ultra-pure oxygen, argon, hydrogen, helium and electronic specialty gases. India had approved 12 semiconductor projects worth about ₹1.64 lakh crore by May 2026, including the Tata–PSMC fab at Dholera (₹91,526 crore), and a Semicon 2.0 outlay of ₹1,27,500 crore was announced in July 2026. INOX Air Products bought land for a ₹500 crore electronic-gases hub at Dholera and signed a long-term ultra-high-purity nitrogen contract with ReNew's solar-cell plant there. A Linde ASU for the Tata fab is due in the second half of 2027, but which Linde entity owns it is unconfirmed — under the 2020 territory rules either could — so we keep it out of Linde India's numbers.

Healthcare. Medical oxygen, nitrous oxide and medical air are about 9.8% of market value. The COVID second wave in 2021 briefly pushed medical oxygen demand from under 1,000 TPD to nearly 9,000 TPD and left behind 4,135 PSA plants in public hospitals. Section 17 covers what that legacy means today; for hospital economics see our hospitals report.

Glass, food, pharma, auto and fabrication make up the long tail: oxy-fuel glass furnaces, carbon dioxide for beverages, nitrogen for food packaging and freezing, argon-CO₂ welding mixes, nitrogen blanketing in pharma reactors. This tail is where packaged and small-bulk gas is sold, and it tracks general manufacturing activity.

Hydrogen and the energy transition are an option rather than a driver for the listed companies. The National Green Hydrogen Mission has a ₹19,744 crore outlay; by February 2026 it had awarded production incentives for 862,000 tonnes a year of green hydrogen to 18 companies and electrolyser incentives for 3,000 MW a year to 15. Electrolysis also releases about 8 tonnes of oxygen per tonne of hydrogen, which could one day become a source of cheap merchant oxygen near electrolyser clusters (Section 19).

In plain terms

Gas companies grow when steel mills build new blast furnaces and sign them up for 20 years of oxygen. India plans to add roughly a third more steel capacity by 2030, which could mean ₹9,000–14,000 crore of new gas plants. Semiconductor fabs, coal gasifiers and solar-cell plants add to that. The catch is timing: steel output from existing mills slowed sharply in Q1 FY27, and the new contracts take years to build.

04

Electricity is the raw material

Air is free; the power bill is half to two-thirds of the cash cost of every tonne. Contract design decides who pays it.

A gas plant is, in economic terms, a machine for turning electricity into molecules. The main air compressor runs around the clock; liquefying the product for tankers needs a second, even more power-hungry stage. On industry rules of thumb, a large tonnage ASU making gaseous oxygen uses about 0.38–0.45 kWh per Nm³, or roughly 270–320 kWh per tonne. Making liquid oxygen or nitrogen takes about 600–1,000 kWh per tonne depending on plant size and liquefier efficiency. At Indian high-tension industrial tariffs of about ₹6.5–9.0 per kWh (including duties and cross-subsidy surcharges, INDICATIVE), the power bill for a tonne of liquid can be ₹4,000–9,000, against a delivered selling price of perhaps ₹10,000–15,000.

Who bears that cost depends on the contract. In an on-site contract the variable charge is indexed to the customer's power tariff, or the customer buys and pays for the electricity directly; either way the gas company is protected. In merchant and packaged sales the gas company buys the power and recovers it through price, with a lag of a quarter or more and only if the regional market is tight enough. That is why merchant-heavy players' margins swing with state electricity tariffs (usually reset each April by state regulators) and why the cheapest lever for a merchant producer is renewable power bought through open access.

The members' power bills

Two of the three members now disclose their power cost, and the comparison is instructive. Linde India spent ₹417.4 crore on power and fuel in FY26, 16.5% of revenue (21.3% in the March 2026 quarter). Its BRSR shows 6,678 terajoules of electricity, about 1,855 GWh, across 24 plants, with renewables at 12.7% of energy — 22.0% if power paid for by on-site customers is excluded. Dividing the power bill by the energy gives about ₹2.25 per kWh, far below any grid tariff. That is not a cheap tariff: it is evidence that a large share of the electricity Linde India's plants consume is paid for directly by its on-site customers and never touches its P&L. Ratio comparisons across companies therefore depend heavily on contract mix.

Management attributes the fall to newer, more power-efficient plants (Uluberia-II and Kurnool), a long-term renewable power purchase agreement, exchange purchases, and a shift in mix towards on-site plants where the customer pays for power. Ownership history: how the map was drawn

The current structure is the product of global deals. Linde AG bought BOC in 2006, bringing BOC India (founded in 1935 as Indian Oxygen & Acetylene) into the group; it was renamed Linde India in 2013. When Praxair and Linde merged in October 2018, the Competition Commission of India cleared the deal with an India carve-up: Linde India sold its South India business to Air Water for about US$194 million in 2019 (consistent with the ₹862 crore of other income it booked in calendar 2019, though the attribution is our inference), and Air Water also bought Praxair India's East India oxygen, nitrogen and argon business. That is how Air Water became India's third-largest gas company almost overnight, and why Linde India's footprint today is concentrated in the East, North and West.

Capacity mix is not revenue mix

The most common mistake in reading gas companies is to treat tonnes-per-day as if every tonne earned the same. It does not. An on-site tonne is usually billed as a facility fee, often with the customer paying the power bill directly, so it brings in far less revenue than a merchant tonne sold delivered in a tanker. Ellenbarrie shows the gap starkly: its capacity is 47% bulk (915 TPD) and 53% on-site (1,018 TPD), yet on-site plants contributed only 2.7% of gas revenue in FY23, 4.4% in FY24 and 15.6% in FY25, and about ₹14 crore of roughly ₹84 crore (around 17–20%) in Q1 FY27. Its on-site contracts at Kharagpur (Tata Steel, a 15-year lease-cum-O&M from February 2019) and Kurnool (Jairaj Ispat, 15 years from June 2021) are structured so that the customer pays for power and Ellenbarrie earns a fee.

Why the mix decides the multiple

A company that is 60% on-site behaves like a regulated utility with growth: revenue is contracted, power is passed through, and returns are steady at the project level. A company that is 60% merchant behaves more like a regional cement maker: local pricing power, but exposed to capacity additions by rivals, power tariffs and the industrial cycle. Engineering adds lumpiness and dilutes margins in the quarters it is heavy. Linde India is a hybrid of all three, with the tonnage share rising as Tata Steel's ASUs come on stream. INOX Air Products is the most tonnage-heavy by capacity and the most profitable. Refex is none of these.

In plain terms

A tonne of on-site capacity earns a modest, contracted fee; a tonne of merchant capacity earns a much bigger but less certain price. A company's tonnes-per-day tell you how big it is, not how much it earns. Ask how the tonnes are split and how each kind is paid for.

How a tonnage contract is priced

The economics of a tonnage contract are designed so that the gas company is indifferent to the customer's power price and to how much gas the customer actually draws, within limits. Revenue has three parts. A facility fee, fixed per month, recovers the capital cost and a target return over the contract life — typically a 12–16% pre-tax project IRR. A variable charge per tonne drawn passes through the electricity (or the customer supplies the power itself). And escalation clauses index the non-power parts to wholesale or consumer price inflation. A take-or-pay clause guarantees the fee and a minimum volume charge, typically 70–90% of contracted capacity.

Two Lindes, one parent: the Praxair India saga Deep dive

A 75% parent owns a 100% sister company in the same market. Minority shareholders have voted no twice, the regulator and the appeals tribunal have sided with them, and the case now sits in the Supreme Court.

Linde plc owns two gas companies in India. It holds 75% of the listed Linde India Ltd through The BOC Group, and 100% of the unlisted Praxair India Pvt Ltd, inherited from the 2018 Praxair–Linde merger. The two compete for the same customers in the same country, and the parent decides which of them gets each new piece of business. For the 25% of Linde India held by outside shareholders, every rupee of business steered to Praxair India is a rupee in which they have no share. That conflict is the defining governance issue in the listed sub-sector, and in 2025–26 it became one of the most important related-party cases in Indian market regulation.

The map: who gets what

After the merger, the Competition Commission's clearance and the asset sales to Air Water reshaped both companies. Then, on 24 March 2020, Linde India and Praxair India signed a joint-venture and shareholders' agreement that divides new business between them. The two positions

Linde India's position, as set out in its results note, is that it complied with the listing rules because it tested materiality contract by contract, and no single contract exceeded 10% of turnover. It sought minority approval in March 2026 only "as a matter of abundant caution" under SEBI's aggregation reading, and stopped transacting after the defeat. SEBI's position, upheld by SAT, is that materiality must be tested on the aggregate of all transactions with one related party in a year, that the 2020 agreement itself is a related-party transaction, and that business allocated to Praxair India under it is a transfer of value that must be valued independently. SEBI has also said that the roles of directors and officers "will be addressed separately", so a personal-liability phase may still come.

The auditors have taken note. Price Waterhouse qualified its FY25 opinion on Linde India's standalone accounts over exactly this materiality question and added an emphasis of matter on the NSE valuation; a secondary summary suggests the FY26 opinion is also modified. Expect a qualification every year until the Supreme Court decides.

In plain terms

The parent company owns all of one Indian gas business and three-quarters of another, and it decides which one gets new customers. Outside shareholders in the listed one say they have been short-changed, the market regulator and the appeals tribunal agree that the deals needed their approval, and an independent valuer has now put a number on what was allegedly given away. The Supreme Court will decide whether that number leads anywhere.

What could happen next

There are four broad outcomes, and only the first has already happened. (a) Stop or restructure the flows. Linde India stopped transacting with Praxair India after 5 March 2026. That removes the governance problem going forward but creates a revenue gap: the ₹265 crore of FY25 revenue from Praxair India (about 11% of revenue) was mostly engineering work and gas sales to the sister company. Q1 FY27's jump in engineering share to 22% of revenue suggests third-party engineering orders are filling some of the gap for now. (b) Compensation. If the Supreme Court upholds SEBI, the valuation report could become the basis for compensating Linde India for business it gave up — the "bull catalyst" some investors have long hoped for. Its size is unknown. (c) A merger or exit offer. Linde plc could resolve the conflict by merging Praxair India into Linde India or by offering to buy out minorities. Nothing has been filed, and this remains speculation. (d) Further regulatory action, including penalties or proceedings against directors.

Three facts help weigh these outcomes. First, the evidence does not suggest Linde India is over-earning at Praxair India's expense today: INOX Air Products earns a 51% EBITDA margin against Linde India's 32–36%, and Linde India's gases segment margin (about 30% at the PBIT level) is in line with global norms. Second, the fact that the parent abstained in March 2026 rather than lobbying shows it accepts the minority's right to decide. Third, institutions have quietly left: domestic institutions held 13.8% of Linde India in March 2019 and 6.74% in September 2026; foreign portfolio investors held 5.8% in 2017 and 2.12% now. The 16% held by retail and other public shareholders is a large, dispersed group that has voted decisively twice.

Why it matters for valuation

The saga sits on both sides of Linde India's valuation. On one side is a catalyst premium: the possibility of compensation or a buyout offer, either of which could crystallise value above the current share price. On the other is a leakage discount: the possibility that the best new business in the South, in hydrogen and in renewals keeps going to the 100%-owned sister, and that the listed company's growth is structurally capped. The stock's fall of about 29% from its 52-week high of ₹8,049 began before the March vote and owes more to earnings quality (Section 11) than to the vote itself, but the overhang will not lift until the Supreme Court rules.

In plain terms

For an outside shareholder in Linde India, the question is not whether the company is well run day to day — its plants and margins are good — but whether the parent will share the Indian growth fairly between the company it fully owns and the one it owns three-quarters of. Until the courts settle it, that uncertainty is part of the share price, in both directions.

Medical oxygen is treated as a drug under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, which means a manufacturing licence from the state drug controller, pharmacopoeia purity standards and batch records. Shock one: the steel bust (2014–2018)

When Indian steel hit overcapacity and cheap Chinese imports in 2014–16, merchant oxygen prices fell and gas companies with spare liquid capacity could not pass on costs. Linde India's operating margin sat at 15–16%, its borrowings peaked at ₹1,445 crore in December 2016, and it earned only ₹16–26 crore of PAT a year in 2016–18. Ellenbarrie's margin fell to 8%. The lesson of that period — that merchant economics are cyclical and debt-funded expansion is dangerous — still shapes the industry's preference for contracted tonnage.

Shock two: the global merger (2018–2020)

The Praxair–Linde merger transformed Linde India's balance sheet. Business and asset transfers, including the sale of the South India business to Air Water, produced ₹862 crore of other income in calendar 2019 and ₹335 crore in 2021, and borrowings fell from ₹1,199 crore to ₹108 crore in 2019 and to almost nothing by 2020. It also created the Praxair India conflict that dominates the governance story today.

Shock three: COVID and the oxygen crisis (2020–2021)

In April–May 2021 India's medical oxygen demand rose from under 1,000 TPD to nearly 9,000 TPD in a few weeks. From 2021 margins reset upward as the steel cycle turned, merchant pricing stayed disciplined and power-cost pass-through improved. Reading a gas company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen numbers that tell you how a gas company really earns, what each should look like, where the three members stand, and the traps hidden in each definition.

A gas company's reported revenue is a poor guide to its health. Revenue can jump because an engineering project was billed, because power started running through the books, or because a contracting subsidiary was consolidated — and fall for the opposite reasons — while the underlying gas business is unchanged. The analyst's job is to separate the parts: contracted tonnage fees, merchant volume times spread, packaged margin, engineering margin, and the overheads and treasury income around them. This section sets out the equation, then walks through each native KPI.

The profit equation

Every gas company's operating profit can be written as the sum of four business lines, less overheads. The terms differ in who controls them. Management controls plant efficiency, the bid price on tonnage contracts, where it builds merchant capacity, and how much engineering it takes on. It does not control the customer's draw on a tonnage plant (beyond the take-or-pay floor), merchant prices in a region where a rival adds capacity, electricity tariffs, or the steel cycle.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE)

Take a hypothetical regional gas company with 1,000 TPD of on-site capacity, 500 TPD of merchant liquid capacity, a cylinder business and a small engineering arm. The on-site plants cost ₹400 crore (₹0.40 crore per TPD); at a 15% pre-tax IRR over 20 years they need ₹64 crore a year of capital recovery, plus ₹6 crore of O&M, so the customers pay a facility fee of ₹70 crore and pay their own power. On-site EBITDA is ₹64 crore. The merchant plant runs at 80% for 350 days, selling 140,000 tonnes at an average ₹12,000 a tonne: ₹168 crore of revenue. Power at 750 kWh per tonne and ₹6.5 per kWh costs ₹68 crore, freight at ₹1,500 a tonne costs ₹21 crore, and plant O&M ₹15 crore, leaving ₹64 crore of EBITDA. Cylinders bring in ₹40 crore at a 20% margin (₹8 crore) and engineering ₹50 crore at 12% (₹6 crore). Overheads are ₹12 crore.

The company reports revenue of ₹328 crore and EBITDA of ₹130 crore, a 39.6% margin. But the shape underneath is what matters. The on-site plants are only 21% of revenue yet 45% of segment EBITDA (₹64 crore of ₹142 crore), and they carry no power or price risk. The merchant plant is half the revenue and the other 45% of segment EBITDA, and every ₹1 per kWh rise in tariff costs it ₹10.5 crore — 8% of group EBITDA. A 10% merchant price cut costs ₹16.8 crore, or 13%. Engineering is 15% of revenue and under 5% of profit. If next year the engineering arm doubles, revenue rises 15% and the margin falls to about 35% with no change in the gas business at all.

In plain terms

Split every gas company into its contracted part, its market-exposed part and its lumpy part before reading its margin. The contracted part is small in revenue but large in profit and very safe; the market part is big and swings with power prices and local competition; the engineering part can make revenue jump while profit barely moves.

1. Installed capacity (TPD) and utilisation

What it is: the tonnes of product a company's plants can make each day, and the share actually made. Formula: utilisation = tonnes produced ÷ (TPD × operating days). Worked example: a 220 TPD liquid plant producing 61,600 tonnes in a 350-day year runs at 61,600 ÷ (220 × 350) = 80%. What drives it: for merchant plants, local demand, rival capacity and the ramp-up period (Ellenbarrie's management describes 18–24 months to reach the 80–90% "sweet spot"); for on-site plants, the customer's draw — but the take-or-pay floor means revenue does not fall below the contracted minimum. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): merchant utilisation above 85% is good, 70–85% average, below 70% weak.

How the members compare: Ellenbarrie disclosed 1,933 TPD at Q1 FY27 — 915 TPD bulk across six plants and 1,018 TPD on-site across four — and says its legacy plants are fully utilised, with spare capacity only at the plants commissioned in Q4 FY26. Linde India does not disclose total TPD; it has 24 plants and its BRSR implies about 5.7 million tonnes of gas sold a year. Refex has none. INOX Air Products, the benchmark, has 21,180 TPD. The trap: companies quote TPD of oxygen for tonnage plants and TPD of liquid product for merchant plants, and some quote Nm³ per hour (1,000 TPD of oxygen ≈ 29,200 Nm³/h). Capacity under an O&M contract — Ellenbarrie's 2 × 1,250 TPD at NMDC Nagarnar — belongs to the customer and should not be counted. And an ASU built for a third party (Ellenbarrie's 1,200 TPD for BHEL) is engineering revenue, not capacity.

4. EBITDA margin, and the gas-segment margin

What it is: operating profit before depreciation, interest, tax and other income, as a share of revenue. Formula: (revenue − operating costs) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Linde India FY26: ₹909 crore ÷ ₹2,531 crore = 35.9%; strip the ₹90 crore liability reversal and it is ₹819 crore ÷ ₹2,531 crore = 32.4%. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE, gas business): above 38% good, 28–38% average, below 28% weak.

How the members compare: INOX's 51.3% (on total income) sets the ceiling. 5. Power cost as a share of revenue, and per tonne

Worked example: a liquid plant using 750 kWh per tonne at ₹6.5 per kWh spends ₹4,875 a tonne on power; at a ₹14,300 average realisation that is 34% of revenue. What drives it: plant vintage and size (new large ASUs use 10–20% less power per tonne), tariffs and open-access renewable purchases, the share of liquid versus gaseous product, and — crucially — whether on-site customers pay their own power. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): below 20% of revenue good, 20–30% average, above 30% weak; for liquid product, below 650 kWh per tonne good, 650–900 average, above 900 weak; for tonnage gaseous oxygen, below 0.38 kWh per Nm³ good.

6. Product mix and the argon kicker

What it is: the split of gas revenue between oxygen, nitrogen, argon and specialty products. Formula: argon revenue ÷ gas revenue. Worked example: in the 220 TPD plant example, argon is 4% of tonnes (2,500 of 61,600) but 13% of revenue (₹11.3 crore of ₹88 crore), because it sells at about ₹45,000 a tonne against ₹13,000. What drives it: plant design (argon side columns), stainless-steel and welding demand, and regional argon supply — which rises every time someone builds an ASU for oxygen. Benchmarks: argon and specialty at 10%+ of merchant revenue is healthy; anything heavily dependent on argon spot prices is volatile.

How the members compare: Ellenbarrie's gas revenue in Q1 FY27 was 50% oxygen, 40% nitrogen and 10% argon and mixtures. Its management said argon prices spiked in Q2 FY26, bottomed in Q3 FY26 and had partly recovered, still below H1 FY26 levels, and that it is moving more argon onto long-term contracts. India's argon imports collapsed 83% in FY26 as domestic capacity grew — a sign of a regional surplus. Linde India and Refex do not disclose a mix. The trap: a quarter with a high margin may simply be a quarter with high argon prices. Ellenbarrie's Q2 FY26 (38% margin) and Q3 FY26 (31%) show the swing.

7. Capex intensity, capex/sales and CWIP

What it is: how much capital a company is investing relative to its size, and how much of its asset base is still under construction. Formula: capex ÷ revenue; ₹ crore per TPD; CWIP ÷ capital employed. Worked example: Linde India's FY26 investing outflow of ₹705 crore ÷ ₹2,531 crore revenue = 28%; FY25's ₹1,305 crore = 53%. What drives it: the tonnage contract cycle; large on-site wins arrive in lumps. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): 15–30% of revenue in a growth phase is good; 30–50% average; above 60% (or below 8%, which signals under-investment) weak. For capex per TPD, tonnage below ₹0.40 crore is good, ₹0.40–0.55 average; merchant plants run ₹0.5–1.0 crore.

How the members compare: Linde India 28% in FY26 (53% in FY25), with CWIP of ₹1,343 crore — about 30% of capital employed — at March 2026. INOX's FY26 investing outflow was ₹878 crore, 29% of revenue. Refex ~12%. The trap: screener's "cash from investing" mixes capex with treasury purchases and sales. Definition traps, collected

  • Segment revenue is gross. Linde India's gases (₹2,128.2 crore) and PED (₹980.6 crore) segments sum to ₹3,108.8 crore; ₹578.2 crore of PED work on its own plants is eliminated to reach ₹2,530.6 crore consolidated revenue.
  • Reported margin includes one-offs. Linde India's FY26 opex is net of a ₹90 crore liability reversal; its "other income" includes a ₹55.5 crore JV share. Strip both and FY26 PBT (~₹588 crore) is below FY25 (₹614 crore).
  • TPD is not one unit. Oxygen TPD (tonnage), liquid TPD (merchant) and Nm³/h are different measures; O&M-only plants and plants built for third parties are not capacity.
  • On-site TPD is not revenue. Ellenbarrie is 53% on-site by capacity but ~17–20% by revenue; never multiply on-site TPD by a merchant price.
  • Power ratios depend on who pays. Linde India's BRSR energy includes power its on-site customers pay for directly; its implied ₹2.25 per kWh is not a tariff.
  • Total income versus revenue. Ellenbarrie's FY25 revenue was ₹312.5 crore; ₹348.4 crore was total income. INOX's 51.3% margin is on total income.
  • PAT has three bases at Refex. Consolidated total ₹203.7 crore (used in this report's tables), consolidated continuing ₹242.4 crore, standalone continuing ₹247.2 crore.
  • "Linde in India" is two companies. Market-share figures and hydrogen projects attributed to "Linde" often belong to Praxair India.
  • Linde India's history mixes calendar and fiscal years, with a 15-month period to March 2023. Any five-year growth rate needs care.
  • Engineering orders are not gas contracts. Ellenbarrie's BHEL order is EPC revenue at EPC margins, not owned capacity.
In plain terms

Most of the confusing numbers in this sector come from four sources: engineering revenue mixed with gas revenue, electricity that may or may not be in the revenue line, one-off gains inside "other income", and capacity figures in different units. Fix those four and the companies become easy to compare.

How to read a gas company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its split (1 minute). Pull total revenue and, if given, the gases versus engineering split (Linde India's segment note; Ellenbarrie's segment table). If revenue grew much faster than gas revenue, engineering or a newly consolidated business is the cause.
  2. Operating margin against last year's same quarter (1 minute). Compare like with like; check whether materials bought rose as a share of revenue (Linde India's went from 19.6% to 27.1% in Q1 FY27), a sign of engineering mix.
  3. Power cost as a share of revenue (1 minute). A jump with flat prices means tariffs rose or merchant volumes fell; a fall may be mix rather than efficiency.
  4. One-offs (1 minute). Scan the notes for reversals, impairments, reclassifications and JV gains. Linde India's FY26 had two.
  5. Other income as a share of PBT (1 minute). Above 20% means you are partly buying a treasury; ask what the treasury holds.
  6. Depreciation and CWIP (1 minute). A step-up in depreciation means plants were capitalised; profit from them should follow. Falling CWIP with no depreciation step-up is a question.
  7. New contracts and commissioning (1 minute). Note any tonnage plant started (Tata at Linde India from 30 September 2026; Jai Balaji at Ellenbarrie from 25 September 2026) and model its first full quarter.
  8. Engineering order inflow and execution (1 minute). BHEL execution at Ellenbarrie; PED share at Linde India; wind execution at Refex. Expect margin dilution.
  9. Governance items (1 minute). RPT disclosures, auditor modifications, court updates (Linde India's Supreme Court appeal), promoter holding and pledges (Ellenbarrie 77.15%; Refex pledge 47.4%).
  10. Cash and working capital (1 minute, half-yearly). Debtor days, operating cash flow against EBITDA, capex against guidance (Ellenbarrie ₹250 crore FY27). Red flags: debtor days rising above 90, operating cash flow below half of EBITDA, or capex running ahead of contracted demand.
In plain terms

When a gas company reports, look first at what share of revenue was engineering, then at margins against the same quarter last year, then for one-offs and treasury income. Then check what new plants started and whether the cash came in. Ten minutes of that tells you more than the headline profit figure.

New plants (Kurnool, Uluberia-II) were still ramping in Q1 FY27, which is where the next leg of growth comes from: management's Q1 FY27 call described an 18-month build, an 18–24-month ramp to 80–90% utilisation and roughly a 3-year payback after ramp, with merchant contracts signed only 3–4 months before commissioning (none pre-sold). The gases segment earned a 38.4% segment result in FY26, up from 33.7%, and 38% in Q1 FY27. Ellenbarrie's gas revenue was 50% oxygen, 40% nitrogen and 10% argon and mixtures; steel was 35% of revenue in Q1 FY27, up from 29% a year earlier; and 86–92% of revenue came from repeat customers.

Kigali: the regulatory clock

Under the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol (adopted October 2016, ratified by India in 2021), India is in Article 5 Group 2. Its baseline is average HFC consumption in 2024–2026 plus 65% of its HCFC baseline; consumption freezes in 2028 and must fall by 10% by 2032, 20% by 2037, 30% by 2042 and 85% by 2047. Quotas from 2028 are likely to be allocated on baseline-period consumption or imports. TradeStat data show importers building that history: FY26 imports of HFC-134a (HS 2903.45) rose 56% to ₹385 crore and of HFC blends (HS 3827) 79% to ₹296 crore, while HFC-32 imports fell 54% to ₹94 crore as domestic production grew. Refex left during the baseline window, forfeiting any future quota position but also removing regulatory and working-capital risk.

Technology and disruption

Cheap on-site generators nibble at the small end, green hydrogen could flood oxygen markets near electrolysers, and steel's decarbonisation changes how much oxygen a tonne needs.

The core technology of industrial gas — cryogenic distillation of air — is a century old and still improving at the margin: new large ASUs use 10–20% less power per tonne than older ones, and remote operating centres and telemetry on customer tanks cut operating and logistics costs. The disruptions that matter come from the edges of the market and from changes in customers' own processes.

The six disruptions to watch

  • Non-cryogenic generation. PSA, VPSA and membrane units make lower-purity oxygen and nitrogen on site at small scale. They have already taken share in hospitals (4,135 public PSA plants) and small fabricators, eroding cylinder and small-bulk demand. The majors mitigate this by selling and servicing such units themselves.
  • Green-hydrogen by-product oxygen. Electrolysis releases about 8 tonnes of oxygen per tonne of hydrogen. The 862,000 tonnes a year of green hydrogen awarded incentives by February 2026 would release about 6.9 million tonnes of oxygen a year — three times India's 2.3 million tonne oxygen market — if all of it were built and the oxygen captured. The full 5 million tonne target would imply about 40 million tonnes. Near electrolyser clusters (Kandla, Paradip, Tuticorin), cheap by-product oxygen could undercut merchant prices. Given the slow pace of green-hydrogen projects and the cost of capturing and liquefying by-product oxygen, this is a low-probability threat before 2030 but the most important long-run one for merchant players.
  • Steel decarbonisation. Hydrogen-based direct reduction plus electric arc furnaces cut oxygen per tonne of steel from about 100 Nm³ to 30–40 Nm³, but raise hydrogen and nitrogen demand. Oxygen-enriched blast furnaces, top-gas recycling and carbon capture raise oxygen demand. The net effect for ASU owners is uncertain; existing tonnage contracts protect the current book.
  • Power-market change. Time-of-day tariffs and renewable open access reward plants that can liquefy at off-peak hours and store the product, effectively turning liquid nitrogen and oxygen into energy storage. Flexible, newer plants gain an edge.
  • Semiconductor-grade purity. Fabs need ultra-high-purity gases and electronic specialty gases with exacting supply-chain standards. The global majors and INOX (with its ₹500 crore Dholera hub) hold the edge; Ellenbarrie is unproven here and says specialty gases would be a lower-margin, trading-like business.
  • Refrigerant and coal transitions (Refex). HFCs are giving way to HFOs and natural refrigerants (R-290, CO₂), but Refex has exited. Its ash business depends on coal power, which faces long-run decline, though India's thermal fleet is still growing (about 80 GW of new coal planned by 2032) and the fly-ash utilisation mandate underpins demand for now.

The economics of green hydrogen are improving but remain far from competitive with grey hydrogen in most uses. SECI's auctions discovered green ammonia prices of ₹55.75 per kg (August 2025, 75,000 tonnes a year), ₹50.75 per kg for a later award and ₹51.80 per kg for an NTPC Renewable Energy contract signed in March 2026; by February 2026 price discovery covered 724,000 tonnes a year for 13 fertiliser units. For Linde India specifically, hydrogen supply is Praxair India's franchise under the 2020 agreement, so green hydrogen is an engineering opportunity (building electrolyser-linked plants) rather than a gas-sales one. For the members as a whole, the most practical technology lever in the next three years is the least glamorous: cheaper, renewable power through open-access agreements, which Linde India (12.7% renewable share) and Ellenbarrie (one long-term PPA, more sought) are both pursuing.

In plain terms

The gas business is not about to be disrupted by a new invention. The real changes are gradual: small oxygen generators taking the low end, steel mills changing how they make steel, and — further off — hydrogen plants producing oxygen as a waste product. Over the next few years, buying cheaper green electricity matters more than any of these.

Bull vs bear

The contracted growth is real and visible; so are the earnings-quality questions, the parent conflict and the coming supply of a cheaper comparable.

The case for and against Indian industrial gases as an investment theme comes down to whether contracted tonnage growth and structural demand can outrun three pressures: valuations that already assume strong growth, earnings that flattered FY26, and the arrival of INOX Air Products on the exchange.

The bull case

  • Visible step-up in contracted earnings: Linde India's 2 × 1,800 TPD Tata Steel ASUs (from 30 September 2026) and Ellenbarrie's 325 TPD Jai Balaji plant (from 25 September 2026) add 15–20-year take-or-pay income from Q3 FY27.
  • Structural demand: 78 Mtpa of steel capacity still to build, 12 approved semiconductor projects, coal gasification and solar-cell plants; India is only 1.3–1.5% of world gas value.
  • Local oligopolies: physics limits competition to two or three suppliers per cluster; INOX's 51% margin shows how profitable the structure can be.
  • Strong balance sheets: Linde India and Ellenbarrie are net cash; INOX's ₹850 crore net cash shows the industry funds growth internally.
  • Catalysts at Linde India: a Supreme Court ruling could lead to compensation for business transferred to Praxair India, or to a restructuring by the parent; Linde India has already stopped the disputed transactions.
  • Margin levers at Ellenbarrie: new plants ramping, renewable power and a ≥40% EBITDA target.

The bear case

  • Valuation: the pure-play gas makers trade at 81× FY26 earnings and 51× EBITDA against 20–32× for global majors; Linde India's ROCE (18.2%) is below its parent's.
  • Earnings quality: Linde India's FY26 included a ₹90 crore reversal and a ₹55.5 crore JV gain (underlying PBT fell); 37% of Ellenbarrie's pre-tax profit is treasury income, partly in Level-3 AIFs and loans against shares.
  • Supply of a comparable: INOX Air Products' IPO will offer a bigger, higher-margin, net-cash company; scarcity premiums compress.
  • The parent conflict: Praxair India keeps the South, Central India and hydrogen; any Supreme Court outcome is uncertain, and auditors are qualifying the accounts.
  • Near-term demand: hot metal grew just 1.4% in Q1 FY27; argon prices are below H1 FY26 and imports collapsed as domestic capacity rose.
  • Margin dilution and supply overhangs: engineering (Linde India PED; Ellenbarrie's BHEL order) depresses reported margins; Ellenbarrie's promoters must sell down to 75% by about July 2028.

Weighing the two, the operating case for Indian industrial gas is strong and the contracts that will lift FY27–FY28 earnings are already signed and running. The investment case depends on price. At 81× earnings for the pure-play gas makers, the market is already paying for several years of that growth, while FY26's reported earnings overstated the underlying trend at both companies. The two dated tests are clear: the Q3 FY27 results (around February 2027), which will be the first full quarter of the Tata Steel and Jai Balaji contracts, and INOX Air Products' IPO pricing, which will set a market benchmark for what a large, profitable Indian gas company is worth. For Linde India there is a third, undated test in the Supreme Court. Refex sits outside this debate: its investment case rests on ash-handling growth, wind-turbine execution, collections from state-linked customers and governance, not on gas.

In plain terms

The business is good and growing, and new contracts are about to lift profits. The worry is that investors have already paid for much of that growth, last year's profits were flattered, and a bigger, cheaper-looking rival is about to list. The next two results seasons and the INOX IPO will show which side is right.

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

Glossary of industrial gas terms
TermMeaning
ASUAir separation unit: a cryogenic plant that liquefies air and distils it into oxygen, nitrogen and argon.
TPDTonnes per day of product; tonnage plants quote oxygen TPD, merchant plants liquid TPD.
Nm³Normal cubic metre: gas volume at 0 °C and 1 atmosphere. 1 tonne of O₂ ≈ 700 Nm³; 1,000 TPD O₂ ≈ 29,200 Nm³/h.
LOX / LIN / LARLiquid oxygen, liquid nitrogen, liquid argon. GOX and GAN are the gaseous forms.
On-site / tonnageGas supplied by pipeline from a dedicated plant at the customer's site, usually on a 15–20-year contract.
BOOBuild-own-operate: the gas company builds, owns and runs the plant and sells gas to the customer.
Take-or-payA clause requiring the customer to pay for a minimum volume whether or not it uses it.
Facility feeThe fixed monthly charge in a tonnage contract that recovers the plant's capital cost and return.
Merchant / bulkLiquid gas delivered by cryogenic tanker to customer storage tanks, priced per tonne.
PackagedGas sold in high-pressure cylinders, cylinder quads or dewars.
PSA / VPSA(Vacuum) pressure-swing adsorption: a non-cryogenic way to make lower-purity oxygen or nitrogen at small scale.
BF-BOF / EAF / AODBlast furnace–basic oxygen furnace steelmaking; electric arc furnace; argon-oxygen decarburisation (stainless refining).
HyCOHydrogen and carbon monoxide; in India, Praxair India's franchise under the 2020 Linde agreement.
ESG (in this report)Electronic specialty gases used in semiconductor manufacturing (NF₃, WF₆, SiH₄ and others).
PEDProject engineering division: Linde India's plant-building business.
CWIPCapital work-in-progress: plant under construction, not yet earning or depreciating.
RPTRelated-party transaction: a deal with a group company or promoter; material RPTs need minority approval under SEBI LODR Regulation 23.
MPSMinimum public shareholding: listed companies must have at least 25% public ownership.
OFSOffer for sale: existing shareholders sell shares in an IPO; the company receives no money.
AIF / Level-3Alternative investment fund; Level-3 assets are valued using unobservable inputs.
Kigali AmendmentThe 2016 amendment to the Montreal Protocol phasing down HFC refrigerants; India freezes in 2028.
NPPA / NLEMNational Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority; National List of Essential Medicines (medical oxygen is on it).
PESOPetroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation: licenses gas cylinders, storage and filling plants.
OPM / EBITDA marginOperating profit (before other income, depreciation, interest and tax) ÷ revenue.
ROCEReturn on capital employed: EBIT ÷ (net worth + debt).
