00

The one-page view: a shrinking market, growing brands

Seven companies, four business models, one puzzle: the shelf sold fewer units in 2026, and the biggest brand owners sold more.

Indian personal care is the business of selling small, cheap, habitual things to a very large number of people: a sachet of shampoo, a bar of soap, a bottle of coconut oil, a tube of toothpaste, a razor blade, a pain balm. The ticket size is tiny, the purchase is repeated every few weeks, and the product inside the pack usually costs a fraction of the price on it. The money is made by owning the brand that a household reaches for without thinking, by putting that brand within arm's reach in twelve to thirteen million shops, and by keeping the cost of the oil, chemicals and plastic inside the pack under control.

Seven companies in the NSE 750 make up this sub-sector. Three are large Indian houses with big overseas arms: Marico (Parachute, Saffola, value-added hair oils), Godrej Consumer Products (Good Knight, Cinthol, Godrej Expert hair colour) and Dabur India (Chyawanprash, Red toothpaste, Vatika). Two are subsidiaries of American multinationals that own near-monopolies in their categories: Colgate-Palmolive (India) and Gillette India. One is a house of small niche monopolies: Emami (Navratna, BoroPlus, Zandu, Kesh King). And one is a digital-first newcomer: Honasa Consumer (Mamaearth, The Derma Co). Together they earned ₹57,289 crore of revenue and ₹8,497 crore of profit after tax in FY26, and were worth ₹3.61 lakh crore on 17 October 2026.

The puzzle that defines 2026

NielsenIQ, which tracks what shops sell, reported that home and personal care volumes fell 4.3% in April–June 2026 while prices rose 5.4%, and that rural FMCG volumes fell 5%. That is a contraction. In the same quarter the listed brand owners reported their strongest domestic volume growth in years: Marico 11% (a twenty-quarter high), Colgate's toothpaste about 8.5%, Emami 8% on a like-for-like basis, Godrej Consumer's India business 7% and Dabur's India FMCG business 5%. Revenue for the seven rose 15.8% year on year and operating profit 16.0%. Section 03 explains how both statements can be true. The short answer is that the large brands took share from small, regional and unbranded players, sold more through modern trade and online channels that the shop panel under-counts, and lapped a weak base left by the GST changeover of September 2025.

The five forces in one paragraph each

1. Share from a shrinking market. The defining fact of 2026. Large brands with deep distribution and the cash to keep advertising took share while the overall tracked market contracted. Section 03.

2. Input-cost cycles that differ by category. Marico lives with copra (dried coconut kernel), Godrej Consumer with palm oil and crude derivatives, and Colgate, Emami and Honasa with chemicals and packaging. In 2025 copra spiked and Marico's EBITDA margin fell from 19.7% to 17.1%; copra is now about 35% below its peak and Marico's margin was back to 20.7% in Q1 FY27. In 2026 the West Asia war tripled Godrej Consumer's costs for LPG, kerosene and LABSA and cut Emami's gross margin by 360 basis points. Section 04.

3. A tax cut that reached the consumer. GST 2.0 on 22 September 2025 cut hair oil, soap, shampoo, toothpaste and shaving products from 18% to 5%. Unlike packaged foods, where brands mostly kept the ₹5 and ₹10 price points and added grams, personal-care brands cut the printed price. Section 05.

4. Digital-first and legacy, converging. Online was 20% of beauty and personal care spending in FY25 and quick commerce is expected to become the largest online format by 2030. The incumbents are buying digital brands; the digital native is building shop distribution. Section 08.

5. Quality now trades at a discount to momentum. Colgate and Gillette earn about 31% EBITDA margins and around 100% return on capital but trade at 35 times earnings, 24–26% below their 52-week highs. The market pays more for volume momentum: Marico at 55 times and Honasa at 58 times. Sections 09 and 17.

In plain terms

These companies sell habits. Most Indians oil their hair, brush their teeth and bathe every day, so demand never collapses; what moves is which brand gets bought and what the oil, chemicals and plastic inside it cost. In 2026 fewer packs were sold overall, but more of them carried a Parachute, Colgate or Navratna label, because big brands could afford to keep prices sensible and advertising loud while small rivals could not.

The rest of the report follows that logic. Sections 01 and 02 explain how the money is made and how big the market is. Sections 06 to 12 cover structure, business models, digital disruption, the multinational subsidiaries, overseas exposure, regulation and history. 01

From copra to kirana: how ₹100 of personal care is made and split

A brand owner keeps half to seventy per cent of what it sells for after paying for the oil, chemicals and pack, then spends a large slice of that on advertising to keep it.

Personal care in the Indian listed-company sense is broader than cosmetics. It runs from coconut oil and toothpaste, which almost every household buys, to serums and sunscreens bought by a small urban minority, and it includes adjacent "home and personal care" lines such as mosquito repellents, air fresheners and Ayurvedic health products that the same companies make in the same plants and sell through the same distributors. Several members also sell food: Marico's Saffola edible oils and oats, Dabur's Real juices and Badshah spices. Those food lines are covered in the Packaged Foods report; here they appear only as part of each company's mix. The practical consequence is that no listed company's revenue is pure personal care, and the sub-sector's ₹57,289 crore cannot be cleanly carved into personal-care and non-personal-care pieces.

The value chain

The chain from farm and refinery to bathroom shelf has seven links, and the brand owner sits in the middle of it, controlling formulation, brand and distribution while buying almost everything physical. Agricultural inputs come first: copra from Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka; herbs such as amla, neem and meswak; honey; menthol from the mint farms of Uttar Pradesh. Petro- and oleo-chemical inputs come next: imported palm oil and soap noodles from Indonesia and Malaysia, linear alkyl benzene sulphonic acid (LABSA) for detergents and surfactants, light liquid paraffin as a hair-oil base, sorbitol and silica for toothpaste, LPG as an aerosol propellant. Then packaging: HDPE and PET bottles, laminate tubes, sachets and cartons, all priced off crude oil.

Manufacturing is mostly in-house, with clusters in the former tax-holiday states (Himachal Pradesh around Baddi, Uttarakhand, the North-East, Jammu and Kashmir), which explains why Emami, Dabur, Marico and Godrej Consumer have plants in places far from both their raw materials and their consumers. Honasa is the exception: it makes nothing itself and relies entirely on contract manufacturers. Distribution runs from the company to a carrying-and-forwarding agent, a super-stockist or distributor, a wholesaler and finally the retailer, reaching roughly twelve to thirteen million outlets. Traditional trade (kiranas, chemists and wholesalers) still accounts for about 82% of FMCG sales. Online marketplaces, beauty specialists such as Nykaa, and quick-commerce apps such as Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart are the fast-growing alternative.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes

The single most important structural fact about personal care is the size of its gross margin. After paying for raw materials and packaging, a personal-care company keeps roughly ₹46 to ₹70 of every ₹100 of net sales, against ₹35 to ₹55 in packaged foods. Colgate keeps about ₹70, Emami and Honasa about the same, Marico about ₹47. The difference between those numbers is the difference between selling a branded commodity (coconut oil, where the oil is most of the cost) and selling a formulation (toothpaste, cream), where the ingredients are cheap and the brand is almost everything.

The second fact is where that gross margin goes. Advertising and promotion (A&P) is not a rounding item: Emami spends 17–20% of sales, Colgate 13–14%, Marico about 10%, Dabur 7–8% and Honasa about a third. Employees take 7–9%, freight and distribution 5–7%, conversion and overheads 6–8%. The two multinationals also pay their parents a royalty and technical fee whose rate has not been verified from the annual reports for this report; the figure in the chart below is an assumption, labelled as such. What is left is EBITDA of about ₹19–22 for a typical Indian house, ₹30–31 for the multinationals, ₹25 for Emami and ₹10–15 for Honasa.

Two further slices sit outside the company's P&L but shape it. The trade takes about 15% of the GST-exclusive retail price in general trade (roughly 10% for the retailer and 5% for the distributor and super-stockist), an indicative figure used in the worked examples later. Online and quick-commerce platforms take more, perhaps 15–25% or above once commissions, advertising and fees are counted, though they remove the wholesaler layer and favour premium packs. And the state takes GST, now mostly 5% on mass personal care after the September 2025 cut, against 18% before it.

In plain terms

A tube of toothpaste that sells for ₹100 contains perhaps ₹25 of paste and packaging. The rest pays the shop, the distributor, the tax collector, the advertising, the salesmen and the parent company, and still leaves about ₹22 of profit after tax. A bottle of coconut oil at the same price contains closer to ₹44 of oil and plastic, so it leaves much less room, and its profit moves every time the price of coconuts moves.

Capital is the last piece. Personal care is not capacity-constrained the way cement or steel is, and companies rarely disclose plant utilisation. Capex is small relative to sales: Colgate carries ₹718 crore of fixed assets against ₹6,035 crore of revenue, Gillette ₹304 crore against ₹3,100 crore. Marico's ₹3,633 crore and Godrej Consumer's ₹12,556 crore are larger mainly because they include acquired brands and goodwill, not factories. That is why the organic businesses earn returns on capital that look implausible to anyone used to industrial companies, and why acquisitions, which put the purchase price on the balance sheet, drag those returns down. Section 13 works through that arithmetic.

02

A ₹2 lakh crore habit, moving online

India's beauty and personal care market is roughly ₹2 lakh crore and growing about 12% a year; the bigger change is where it is bought.

Estimates of the size of India's beauty and personal care (BPC) market vary by source and definition, so every figure here is INDICATIVE. Redseer's February 2026 study, the most detailed recent source, puts the market at about US$23 billion in FY25, up from about $15 billion in FY20, and expects about $40 billion by FY30, a compound growth rate of about 12%. At Redseer's own conversion rate of ₹85 to the dollar that is about ₹1.96 lakh crore in FY25 and ₹3.4 lakh crore in FY30; at today's rate of about ₹95.5 it would be roughly ₹2.2 lakh crore. Syndicated research houses such as Ken Research and IMARC put the market nearer $28 billion (about ₹2.4 lakh crore at ₹85), largely because they draw the category boundary more widely. The sources differ; the direction does not.

Where it is bought: the format shift

The more important change is in how Indians buy these products. In FY20, 72% of BPC spending went through unorganised retail (kiranas, chemists, local cosmetics stores), 20% through organised stores and 8% online. By FY25 the unorganised share had fallen to 52%, organised stores had risen to 28% and online to 20%. Redseer projects that by FY30 online will be 34–38%, organised stores 28–32% and unorganised 32–36%. Online BPC was about $4.8 billion in FY25 (about ₹40,800 crore at ₹85) and is projected at $13–15 billion by FY30. Within online, the format that matters most is quick commerce, which was about 15% of online BPC in FY25 and is expected to reach 30–40% by FY30, overtaking the horizontal marketplaces.

What drives demand

Five structural forces push demand up over years rather than quarters. The first is habit deepening. The average Indian brushes about five times a week, rural Indians two to three times, and fewer than 15% brush twice a day, according to Colgate's primer data. Every increase in frequency is volume growth that no competitor has to lose. Hair oiling is close to universal, so the growth there comes from converting loose oil to branded packs and from moving consumers up to value-added oils. The second is premiumisation and category creation: body wash in place of soap, serums and sunscreens, men's grooming. Liquid soaps were one of the few categories NielsenIQ called an "island of growth" in April–June 2026, and Colgate says its premium portfolio grows about six times as fast as the category.

The third force is digital discovery. Online's share rose from 8% to 20% in five years, and Redseer expects Gen Z and Gen Alpha to account for about half of BPC spending by 2030, a cohort that discovers products through 500 million social-media users and some three million influencers. The fourth is "naturals" and Ayurveda, which took toothpaste share from Colgate over the last decade (its value share slipped from the mid-to-high 40s to about 43%) and built Patanjali and Mamaearth. The fifth is formalisation: loose coconut oil, unbranded soap and illegal incense-stick mosquito repellents lose share to brands, and they lose it fastest when input prices spike, because small players buy at spot and cannot absorb a cost shock.

In plain terms

The market grows for three reasons that compound: more people use these products more often, they trade up to better versions, and they switch from loose or unbranded products to brands. In 2025–26 the first reason stalled in rural India, but the third accelerated, which is why the big brands still grew.

Cyclical forces sit on top. Rural demand led urban through calendar 2024 and the first half of 2025, then turned negative: NielsenIQ rural FMCG volume fell 0.8% in January–March 2026 and 5% in April–June. Weather matters for specific brands: a mild 2025 summer and unseasonal March 2026 rain hurt Emami's cooling oils, talc and Dermicool and Dabur's beverages, and April–June 2026 had a split summer, hot in the south and west and mild in the north and east. Inflation pushed home and personal care prices up 5.4% in April–June 2026 even though GST had been cut nine months earlier. And geopolitics hit export markets: the West Asia war from about March 2026 cut Emami's international sales by 12% in Q1 FY27 and hit Dabur's Middle East business.

Channel data shows where growth is coming from. Traditional trade is about 82% of FMCG sales and NielsenIQ described it as stalling in both urban and rural markets in April–June 2026. Modern trade volume grew 17.5% and e-commerce volume 34.8% in the same quarter, with non-metro e-commerce growing three times as fast as the metros. E-commerce is 21% of FMCG in the top eight metros and 25% in the southern metros. For personal care specifically, the online share is far higher than for FMCG as a whole, and quick commerce is now the dominant online channel for several incumbents: about 75% of Dabur's online sales and 35% of Emami's e-commerce sales.

The first two reasons are the investment case. Input inflation in 2025–26 hit everyone, but it hit small players hardest. A regional soap maker or a loose coconut-oil seller buys raw material at the spot price, has little working capital, cannot hedge and cannot fund a television campaign to explain a price rise. A national brand can absorb part of a cost spike in its margin for a few quarters, can cut pack sizes or launch a ₹1 sachet to hold a price point, and can keep advertising. When input prices then fall, as copra did in 2026, the national brand holds part of its price and takes the margin back, while the shopper who switched during the spike often stays. Marico's Parachute is the cleanest example: the brand's volume grew 10% in Q1 FY27 and in the early teens in Q2 FY27 according to the company's 5 October update.

The second reason is that NielsenIQ's panel sees less and less of personal care. Personal care is bought online far more than food: 20% of BPC spending was online in FY25 against roughly 7% of urban FMCG. Modern trade and e-commerce volumes grew 17.5% and 34.8% in the quarter. Emami's organised channels (modern trade and e-commerce) grew 19% like-for-like and are now 32% of its domestic sales. As this share grows, a shop panel built on kiranas becomes a progressively worse guide to the companies' sales, and investors should expect the gap to persist.

In plain terms

Imagine a town where total soap sales fell 4%, but the two big brands each sold 8% more, because the three small local brands lost half their customers and a new online shop opened that the market survey does not visit. That is roughly what happened to Indian personal care in 2026.

The rural contradiction

The sharpest disagreement is about rural India. NielsenIQ says rural FMCG volume fell 5% in April–June 2026, after falling 0.8% in January–March. Dabur, the member most dependent on rural sales, said in its Q1 FY27 commentary that rural demand "remains strong", and in Q4 FY26 it reported rural growth running about 350 basis points ahead of urban. Three explanations fit. Dabur's rural sales are measured as primary shipments, not consumer offtake. Dabur gained share in specific categories (its toothpaste grew near double digits and its hair oils grew 18% in value on 8% volume). And the panel's rural sample is weighted towards small traditional stores, where the decline was concentrated. None of these settles the question. If the panel is right, rural-heavy brands will see it in their own numbers within two or three quarters; that makes Dabur's and Emami's Q2 FY27 volume prints, due in early November, the key test.

Will the gap persist?

Three things would close the gap between the brands and the market. A rural recovery would lift the market toward the brands. A fall in input prices would let small players come back on price, as they did in 2023–24 when commodity costs were benign and regional brands regained share in soaps and hair oils. And a slowdown in the shift to modern trade and online would remove one source of share gain. The first is expected; the second is not visible yet (palm and crude derivatives are still elevated, copra is off its peak but supported by a higher minimum support price); the third shows no sign of happening. On balance the listed leaders should keep outgrowing the panel, but by less than the 9–15 percentage points of Q1 FY27, which was flattered by the weak base. The price of a dried coconut: input cycles by category Deep dive

Each company has its own commodity. Gross margin moves with it; the strategic choice is whether to protect volume or margin when it spikes.

Personal-care companies are often described as defensive because demand for soap and toothpaste barely moves with the economy. Their margins are a different matter. Every company in this group buys a basket of agricultural and petrochemical inputs whose prices can double in a year, and each basket is different. Marico's is dominated by copra. Godrej Consumer's is palm oil and crude derivatives. Colgate, Emami and Honasa sell formulations where the ingredients are a small share of the price, so packaging and chemicals matter more than any single crop. That is why the same year can be good for one member and bad for another, and why 2025 and 2026 produced two different shocks with two different sets of losers.

Copra: how a crop sets Parachute's margin

Copra is dried coconut kernel. It is pressed into coconut oil, yielding roughly 62–65% oil by weight, and that oil is most of what is inside a bottle of Parachute. Marico buys copra from farmers and traders in Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, and its cost moves with the coconut harvest, which is itself driven by rainfall two to three years earlier, pest damage and the price of competing uses (tender coconut, desiccated coconut, oil for cooking). The government sets a minimum support price (MSP) for milling copra, at which state agencies stand ready to buy; for the 2026 season it is ₹12,027 a quintal, up ₹445 from ₹11,582. The MSP is a floor, not a ceiling. In 2025 market prices went far above it.

The level of the 2025 peak is not reliably sourced. Marico's statement in October 2026 that copra is "about 35% below peak" implies that the peak was roughly 1.5 times today's price, and industry reports suggest Kerala milling copra roughly doubled to more than ₹20,000 a quintal in mid-2025; both figures are INDICATIVE. What is documented is what Marico did about it. It raised Parachute's price, cut the millilitres in each pack (legal since standard pack sizes were deregulated in 2017), and accepted a lower margin rather than lose volume. Parachute's revenue rose 29% in Q4 FY26 on flat to low volume. Marico's FY26 EBITDA margin fell from 19.7% to 17.1%.

The competitive logic is what makes copra unusual. Parachute's main rival is not another brand but loose coconut oil sold from tins in local shops. Loose sellers buy at spot. Palm oil: Godrej Consumer chose volume

Soap is, chemically, a perfumed salt of fatty acids, and in India most of those fatty acids come from imported palm oil, palm fatty acid distillate (PFAD) or ready-made soap noodles. When palm rises, soap makers face the same choice Marico faced with copra, with one difference: soap is a crowded category led by Hindustan Unilever, with Wipro's Santoor, ITC, Reckitt's Dettol and Godrej Consumer competing hard, so price increases are harder to make stick. Godrej Consumer cited a roughly 40% surge in palm prices as the main squeeze on FY26. Under chief executive Sudhir Sitapati, whose stated philosophy is volume first, it absorbed much of the increase. Its consolidated EBITDA margin slipped only from 21.5% to 20.9%, because soaps are a minority of its India profit; household insecticides and hair colour carry most of it.

Palm is a dating problem as well as a price problem. The 40% surge refers to Godrej Consumer's FY26 cost experience, while Britannia, in the packaged-foods group, reported palm down 12% year on year at Q4 FY26, comparing against a different base and possibly a different product (crude palm oil against palm derivatives). Both can be right for their own windows; neither should be quoted without its date. In September–October 2026 Malaysian palm futures traded around MYR 4,500–4,800 a tonne, above Malaysian forecasts of MYR 3,900–4,100, supported by Indonesia's B50 biodiesel mandate, which diverts palm into fuel. Godrej Consumer's 5 October note for Q2 FY27 flagged "renewed inflation" in palm and crude derivatives, to be met with "calibrated pricing".

Crude and the 2026 West Asia shock

The second shock arrived in 2026, and it hit a different set of companies. The West Asia war from about March 2026 pushed crude from below $80 to above $109 a barrel and disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandab. Crude feeds personal care through many channels: LABSA in detergents and soaps, light liquid paraffin in hair oils, LPG as the propellant in aerosol insecticides and air fresheners, kerosene in mosquito-repellent formulations, and polyethylene and PET in every bottle. Godrej Consumer reported that its costs for LPG, kerosene and LABSA roughly tripled in Q1 FY27; its India EBITDA margin fell to 21.6% against 24–25% in FY26, and its India gross margin fell sharply (the exact figure was not available for this report). PET resin prices rose 30–50% in March–April 2026. Emami, whose products are mostly formulations in bottles and tubes, saw its gross margin fall 360 basis points to 65.8% on crude-linked and packaging inflation, and called the period "one of the sharpest inflationary environments seen by the sector in recent years".

Each company has a different commodity problem. Coconuts got cheaper in 2026 and oil-based chemicals got much more expensive, so Marico's margins went up while Godrej Consumer's and Emami's went down. Investors who treat "FMCG input costs" as one number miss this completely.

The lessons of two shocks

Three lessons carry forward. First, gross margin in a branded-commodity business is cyclical and should be read across a full cycle, not a single year; Marico's FY26 margin and its Q1 FY27 margin are both true and neither is normal. Second, volume is the strategic choice. Marico and Godrej Consumer both chose to protect volume through their respective spikes, accepting lower margins, and both are now reporting their best volume growth in years. Third, advertising is the shock absorber. When gross margin falls, the temptation is to cut A&P to protect EBITDA. That flatters one quarter and costs share later; Marico raised its advertising by 25% in Q1 FY27 even as its margin recovered, which is the opposite of what a company harvesting a relief year would do.

The structural answer is to own fewer commodity-linked products. Marico's stated plan ("Marico 2.0") is to cut the share of revenue linked to commodities from about 70% to about 50% by FY30, by growing foods (now past ₹1,000 crore) and digital-first brands (past ₹1,100 crore of annual run-rate). Godrej Consumer wants about 40% of revenue from new categories by FY30. Both are long-dated aspirations, and both depend on the newer businesses becoming profitable at scale, which section 08 examines.

GST 2.0: the tax cut that reached the shelf Deep dive

On 22 September 2025 hair oil, soap, shampoo and toothpaste went from 18% to 5% GST. Personal-care brands cut their printed prices; food brands mostly added grams. The side effects lasted longer than the cut.

GST 2.0, which took effect on 22 September 2025, collapsed most goods into two main slabs of 5% and 18%, with a 40% rate for "sin" goods. For personal care it was the largest tax change since GST began in July 2017. Hair oil, shampoo, toilet soap, toothpaste, toothbrushes, shaving cream, talcum powder and shaving gear moved from 18% to 5%. Skin creams, colour cosmetics, deodorants and perfumes and hair colour are believed to have stayed at 18%; that list could not be verified against the rate notification for this report and should be treated as provisional. The cut covered most of what the seven listed companies sell in India by value, with the main exceptions being Honasa's skin-care range, Godrej Consumer's hair colour and household insecticides, and parts of Emami's cream portfolio.

The arithmetic on a ₹100 pack

The mechanics are simple once the price chain is laid out. Indian retail prices are printed on the pack as an MRP that includes GST. The company receives what is left after GST and the trade's margins are removed. At 5% GST the same ₹100 pack is worth ₹95.24 before tax and the company realises ₹81.43, ₹8.97 or 12.4% more. A company that kept its MRP unchanged would therefore have received a 12.4% price increase from the government.

Why personal care cut price while food added grams

The contrast with packaged foods is instructive. As the Packaged Foods report describes, biscuit, noodle and snack makers mostly kept their ₹5 and ₹10 price points and put more grams in the pack, because those coin-sized prices are what the shopper recognises and what the kirana's change drawer is built around. Personal care has fewer such anchors. A ₹135 tube of toothpaste or a ₹435 bottle of shampoo is not bought with a single coin, the shopper compares prices across brands on the same shelf, and a visibly lower MRP is a stronger message than a slightly bigger tube. Sachets are the exception: ₹1, ₹2 and ₹5 shampoo and hair-oil sachets mostly kept their price and gained a little content, behaving like food. There was also public pressure from the government for companies to show consumers the benefit, which made a printed price cut the safest course for large brands.

In plain terms

When the tax on toothpaste fell, Dabur could have kept the ₹153 price and pocketed the difference. Instead it printed ₹135 on the tube. The company earns about the same per tube as before; the shopper pays about 12% less. The bet is that cheaper tubes mean more tubes, and more brushing.

The short-term damage: a destock

A price cut on a pack that is already in the shop creates a problem. Wholesalers and retailers held stock bought at the old, higher price, which they would have to sell at the new, lower MRP. They responded by running down that stock before ordering more, and in some cases by waiting for companies to compensate them. For companies selling through wholesalers, which carry weeks of inventory, the result was a sharp fall in shipments around September and October 2025. Emami, with the most wholesale-heavy and rural distribution in the group, reported domestic volume down about 16% in Q2 FY26, and its quarterly revenue fell from ₹904 crore in June 2025 to ₹799 crore in September 2025. That weak quarter is now the base against which Q2 FY27 will be compared, so Emami's and Dabur's growth rates in the results due in November 2026 will be flattered.

The hidden cost: inverted duty

The longer-lasting side effect is an accounting and cash problem called an inverted duty structure. GST works as a chain of credits: a company pays GST on its inputs, collects GST on its sales, and remits the difference. When the tax on the output (now 5%) is lower than the tax on the inputs (18% on most chemicals, laminates, cartons and many services), the company pays more GST on its purchases than it collects on its sales, and the surplus credit piles up. The law allows a refund of accumulated credit arising from inputs, but under a restrictive formula, and does not allow a refund of the part arising from input services or capital goods. Unrefundable credit eventually has to be written off as a cost.

An ILLUSTRATIVE toothpaste tube shows the scale. Suppose a company buys about ₹24.67 of materials and packaging and about ₹8 of taxable services (freight, advertising production, contract services) per ₹81.43 of sales. At 18% it pays about ₹5.88 of input GST and collects only ₹4.07 of output GST (5% of ₹81.43), leaving about ₹1.81 of excess credit per tube, roughly 2.2% of realisation. Of that, the share relating to services, about ₹1.44, cannot be refunded at all. Colgate booked a one-time inverted-duty charge in FY26, which, together with a high FY25 base that included interest on a tax refund, explains why its FY26 profit fell 7.8% on flat revenue. It excluded another such charge from its Q1 FY27 underlying profit growth of 11%. Other members have said less, but every company selling mainly 5% goods made from 18% inputs faces the same arithmetic, and investors should expect similar charges or footnotes until the GST Council changes the refund rules.

In plain terms

The government cut the tax on the toothpaste but not on the tube, the chemicals or the advertising. Companies now pay more tax on what they buy than they collect on what they sell, and only part of the difference comes back. That leftover is a small but permanent cost unless the rules change.

The verdict

GST 2.0 did three things. It made the big brands' price-value equation better in categories where they had room to grow consumption, and that showed up in toothpaste and hair-oil volumes within two to three quarters. It caused a one-quarter distortion (the September 2025 destock) that now flatters year-on-year comparisons for wholesale-heavy companies. And it created a lasting inverted-duty cost that is easy to miss because it appears as an "exceptional" or "one-time" item. It did not rescue mass soaps or rural HPC once the 2026 crude shock arrived. For an analyst, the clean test is to compare the categories that were cut to 5% with those that stayed at 18%; on the limited evidence available, the cut categories grew faster, but input inflation, base effects and share gains all point the same way, so the tax effect cannot be isolated with confidence.

In plain terms

Most of these companies lead one or two small categories: a cooling oil, an antiseptic cream, a toothpaste, a razor. A. The multinational near-monopolies

Colgate and Gillette are Indian subsidiaries of global companies that own the brands, the research and the formulations. India pays for access to them through a royalty and service fees, and in return holds very little capital: no brand on the balance sheet, a small factory base, and negative working capital because distributors pay faster than suppliers are paid. The result is a business that earns 30–31% EBITDA margins on very little capital, so its return on capital employed (ROCE) is 90–108%, and pays out more than all of its profit as dividends (payout above 100% is possible when a company distributes accumulated reserves or its cash from negative working capital). The risks are maturity (toothpaste and razors grow with population and frequency, not much faster), leakage to the parent through royalty, and format or ingredient disruption: naturals in toothpaste, trimmers in shaving. Section 09 goes deeper.

B. The Indian houses with overseas arms

Godrej Consumer, Dabur and Marico carry commodity and currency risk by design. Their biggest products (coconut oil, soaps, hair oils) are branded commodities whose gross margins swing three to five points with copra, palm or crude, and their overseas arms (24–40% of revenue) earn in Bangladeshi taka, Indonesian rupiah, Nigerian naira, Turkish lira, Egyptian pounds and Argentine pesos. Acquisitions add goodwill to the balance sheet, which depresses ROCE: Godrej Consumer's 18.8% reflects years of buying in Indonesia, Africa and Latin America and the Raymond consumer business, while Marico, which has grown more organically and holds less goodwill relative to profit, earns 47%. These companies trade on volume momentum and on how well they navigate the commodity cycle; payout policies vary widely (Godrej Consumer 110%, Dabur 77%, Marico 30% in FY26, though 83% in FY25 because of the timing of interim dividends).

C. The house of niche monopolies

Emami runs a portfolio of category leaders in small niches: cooling oils, antiseptic creams, pain balms, prickly-heat powder, Ayurvedic hair oil, men's fairness cream (now Smart & Handsome). Gross margin is about 70% in a normal year because the products are formulations, and A&P at 17–20% of sales is the highest of any established member because niche brands need constant reminding. The business is seasonal (summer for Navratna and Dermicool, winter for BoroPlus and Zandu), rural and wholesale-heavy, which makes quarters lumpy, and its tax rate is unusually low (about 8% of profit before tax in FY26) because of tax-holiday units and accumulated credits that will not last forever. The stock is the cheapest in the group at 21.5 times earnings, which reflects slow growth in the core niches, the West Asia hit to exports and fresh promoter share pledges reported in May 2026.

D. The digital-first house of brands

Honasa is asset-light: all manufacturing is contracted out, working capital is negative (cash conversion cycle of −71 days on screener data), and gross margin is about 70%. But it spends about a third of its revenue on advertising and promotion, because a digital-first brand has to buy its customers on Instagram, YouTube, Amazon and quick-commerce apps every month. EBITDA is what remains after customer acquisition, which is why it was 3.4% in FY25, 9.9% in FY26 and 14.6% in Q1 FY27. Every percentage point of A&P that falls as a share of sales adds a point of margin, about ₹24 crore at FY26 revenue. That operating leverage is the investment thesis; the risk is that acquisition costs rise faster than revenue.

Digital-first meets the kirana Deep dive

Online is a fifth of beauty and personal care spending and quick commerce is becoming its biggest channel. Incumbents are buying digital brands; the digital native is building shop distribution. The two models are converging.

The online shift in personal care is faster and deeper than in any other FMCG category. Redseer estimates that online sales of beauty and personal care were about $4.8 billion in FY25, roughly ₹40,800 crore at its ₹85 conversion rate, and projects $13–15 billion (about ₹1.1–1.3 lakh crore) by FY30, taking online from 20% to 34–38% of the market. Another series, based on Redseer data quoted in Nykaa's disclosures and used in the E-Retail report, puts online BPC at about ₹52,000 crore in calendar 2025, growing about 35% a year; the definitions and periods differ, so this report uses the FY25 figure. All of these are INDICATIVE estimates.

Within online, the format mix is changing. Horizontal marketplaces (Amazon, Flipkart) had about 40% of online BPC in FY25 and are expected to fall to 20–30%. Vertical specialists such as Nykaa had about 27%, heading to 15–25%. Quick commerce (Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart) had about 15% and is expected to reach 30–40%, becoming the largest single online format. Brand websites hold steady at about 10%, and value commerce (Meesho-type platforms) rises from about 8% to 10–15%.

The new-age brand wave

The online channel created a new class of brands. Redseer counts about 70 new-age BPC brands with more than ₹100 crore of revenue in FY26, expected to exceed 150 by FY31, and five with more than ₹1,000 crore. Yet scale remains rare: only about 20 BPC brands of any kind in India have revenue above ₹1,500 crore. The weapons of these brands are speed (a new serum can go from idea to shelf in weeks), influencer-driven discovery, ingredient-led positioning ("actives", "toxin-free") and a direct relationship with the customer. Their weaknesses are rising customer-acquisition costs on digital platforms, thin offline distribution, and brand fade once the novelty passes.

The digital native's arc: Honasa

Honasa Consumer, founded in 2016 with Mamaearth, is the largest listed digital-first BPC company and the clearest test of whether the model scales. Its history since listing has been a full cycle in under three years. It listed in November 2023. In Q1 FY27 revenue grew 27% (32% like-for-like), EBITDA margin reached 14.6% and profit more than doubled to ₹90 crore. Quick commerce: the new shelf

Quick commerce has moved from a novelty to the main online channel for several incumbents in two years. About 75% of Dabur's online sales now come through quick-commerce apps, and its quick-commerce sales grew about 54% in FY26. Quick commerce is 35% of Emami's e-commerce sales. Colgate's modern trade plus e-commerce and quick commerce is about 10% of its business, skewed to premium products and accretive to margin. For brand owners the channel has clear attractions: it removes the wholesaler layer, sells premium and impulse products well, carries little inventory and gives data on what sells where within hours. Its costs are platform commissions, paid placement and ad fees that together can reach 15–25% or more of the selling price (INDICATIVE), the threat of platforms' own private labels, and friction with traditional distributors, whose federation (AICPDF) has protested against quick-commerce pricing; the Competition Commission sought evidence in April 2025 but no order had been found by October 2026.

In plain terms

Ten-minute delivery apps now sell a large and growing share of urban India's shampoo and face wash. They suit brands well because they sell expensive variants and need no wholesaler, but the apps charge for every bit of visibility and could one day sell their own cheaper versions.

Who has the economics right?

The economic contest is between two cost structures. A digital-first brand has a gross margin of about 70% but spends about a third of sales on advertising and pays platform fees for fulfilment. A legacy brand has a lower gross margin in commodity categories but spends 7–20% on A&P and reaches millions of shops through a distribution system already paid for by its existing brands. Legacy wins on the cost of reaching a customer once the brand is known; digital wins on speed and premium positioning. The likely end state, already visible, is convergence: Marico and Emami run digital brands inside legacy distribution systems, and Honasa builds a general-trade network around brands made famous online. The winners will be the companies that have both brand-building speed and physical availability. For now the evidence favours the incumbents slightly: their acquired digital portfolios are growing fast (Emami's at 61%, Marico's targeted at 2.5 times by FY27), while Honasa has had to rebuild its own offline system once already.

The rented-brand monopolies: Colgate and Gillette

About 31% EBITDA margins, returns on capital near 100% and payouts above 100%, all built on brands the Indian companies do not own. The market has stopped paying a premium for it.

Colgate-Palmolive (India) and Gillette India are the two purest examples in Indian equities of a subsidiary that rents its most valuable asset from its parent. Colgate-Palmolive Company of the US owns 51.00% of Colgate India; Procter & Gamble owns 75.00% of Gillette India, exactly at the maximum promoter holding permitted under minimum public shareholding rules. Both have dominated their categories for decades. Colgate's name is used as a generic word for toothpaste across much of India, and its value share is about 43%. Gillette holds about 45–50% of organised razors and blades and 60–70% of cartridges by value.

Colgate: a near-monopoly that has restarted volume

Colgate's FY26 was flat: revenue of ₹6,035 crore against ₹6,040 crore, and profit down 7.8% to ₹1,325 crore, partly because of a one-time GST inverted-duty charge and partly because FY25 had been flattered by interest on a tax refund. Its gross margin is about 70% (69.7% in Q1 FY27, up about 110 basis points), its EBITDA margin 31.0%, its ROCE 108% and its payout 119%; it paid ₹48 a share for FY26 in two interim dividends of ₹24. The more important change came in Q1 FY27: revenue rose 11.8%, and toothpaste volume rose about 8–8.5%, the first high-single-digit volume growth in years. Two things drove it: the GST cut made toothpaste about 11% cheaper, and Colgate chose to spend on advertising (13–14% of sales, raised in Q1 FY27) rather than take price. Its premium portfolio grows about six times as fast as the category. The cost was a slightly lower EBITDA margin (30.1% in Q1 FY27), and profit rose 6.9% reported (11% excluding inverted-duty and restructuring charges).

Gillette: the razor-and-blades annuity, and a changed calendar

Gillette India's numbers need one correction before use. The company changed its financial year from July–June to April–March. Its reported accounts therefore show a twelve-month year to June 2024, then a nine-month "year" from July 2024 to March 2025 (revenue ₹2,235 crore), then a full twelve-month FY26 from April 2025 to March 2026 (revenue ₹3,100 crore, profit ₹654 crore). The nine-month stub cannot be compared with a twelve-month year; on a like-for-like twelve-month basis taken from the primer, FY26 revenue grew about 8% and profit about 23%, implying a base of roughly ₹2,870 crore of revenue and ₹532 crore of profit for April 2024 to March 2025 (INDICATIVE). The company itself calls April–June 2026 "Q1 FY2027".

The business model is the textbook razor-and-blades annuity. Gillette Guard, launched for India in 2010, sells a handle for about ₹15 and blades for about ₹5. A man who shaves with it buys ten to twelve refills a year for perhaps thirty years, so the handle is a customer-acquisition cost for 300 or more blade sales. Double-edge blades are 60–70% of the units sold in India but cartridges are 60–70% of the value, so every man who moves from a ₹2 double-edge blade to a ₹5 Guard blade to a ₹30 Mach3 cartridge multiplies the revenue per shave. In Q1 FY27 grooming revenue grew 9% to ₹628.65 crore with a segment margin of about 27.6%, and oral care (Oral-B brushes) grew 19% to ₹154.37 crore at about 24.4%. The structural threat is trimmers: the stubble look and electric trimmers substitute for wet shaving, and Bombay Shaving Company, with about ₹200 crore of revenue, earns about 45% of it from trimmers.

Why the returns look impossible

A 108% return on capital means Colgate's operating profit exceeds the capital employed in the business. The explanation is that very little capital is employed. The brands, research and formulations sit with the parent. Fixed assets are ₹718 crore against ₹1,870 crore of EBITDA. Working capital is negative: on screener's data Colgate's cash conversion cycle is −188 days, because it collects from distributors in about 13 days, holds about 75 days of inventory and pays suppliers much later. Book equity is only about ₹1,584 crore because the company pays out more than it earns. The same logic gives Gillette 90.7%. These returns are real, but they cannot be redeployed: there is nowhere in the Indian business to invest more capital at 100%, which is why the cash goes out as dividends.

The royalty question

Both companies pay royalties and technical or service fees to their parents for the use of brands and know-how. Neither the rate nor the rupee amount was verified from the FY26 annual reports for this report; the Gillette primer itself says the figure must be read from the related-party note. What can be shown is the sensitivity. On an ASSUMED royalty of 5% of sales, a figure commonly cited for Colgate but unverified, Colgate's FY26 royalty would be about ₹302 crore, equivalent to about 16% of its reported EBITDA and about 14% of EBITDA before the royalty. Each percentage point of royalty would be worth about ₹60 crore a year to the parent and cost minority shareholders the same.

The premium has gone

For most of the last decade the two subsidiaries traded at 40–60 times earnings because investors valued their stability, payout and near-zero capital needs. In 2026 that premium eroded. Marico and Emami: concentrated exposures

Marico's international business grew 20% in constant currency in FY26, its best in fourteen years, and 15% in Q1 FY27, led by Vietnam (+27%) and the Middle East (+24%). Emami's international business, about 17–18% of FY26 sales and concentrated in the Gulf, the CIS and SAARC countries, fell 12% in Q1 FY27 because the West Asia war "constrained its ability to execute orders". When comparing growth across members, the safest approach is to compare India volume with India volume and to treat overseas growth separately, in rupees.

The rulebook: tax, claims, chemicals and the parent's fee

Personal care is lightly regulated on price and heavily regulated on what a pack may claim and contain. Four rules move the numbers.

Unlike hospitals or pharmaceuticals, Indian personal care has no price control. Companies set their own MRPs and change them at will, subject only to the rule that the printed MRP is the maximum. What regulation governs is tax (GST), what may be made and sold (the cosmetics and Ayurvedic licensing regimes), what may be claimed (advertising law and industry codes), how the multinational subsidiaries may pay their parents (listing rules), and a scattering of rules on packaging waste, pack sizes and commodities. Most are background. Four move the numbers: GST rates and the inverted-duty refund rules, the related-party rules on royalties, Ind AS 29 for overseas subsidiaries, and the copra minimum support price.

Tax is the biggest lever

GST is the only regulation that has moved personal-care prices across the board in the last decade, twice. In July 2017 GST 1.0 put most personal care at 18%, broadly in line with the old excise-plus-VAT burden but ending the excise exemptions that had pulled factories to Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand and the North-East. In September 2025 GST 2.0 cut most of it to 5%. The difference matters because personal care straddles the two slabs. A company whose portfolio is mostly in 5% categories (Marico, Colgate, Dabur, Gillette) has gained a price advantage over products in the 18% slab, and faces the inverted-duty problem described in section 05. A company with a large 18% portfolio (Honasa's skin care, Godrej Consumer's hair colour and insecticides) has neither the benefit nor the problem. Future GST Council meetings that change refund rules or move items between slabs are therefore events worth tracking.

Claims, licences and the Ayurvedic grey zone

Personal-care companies live close to the line between cosmetics and medicines. A cosmetic may claim to cleanse, beautify or protect; a drug may claim to treat. Ayurvedic proprietary medicines such as Dabur's Chyawanprash and Hajmola or Emami's Zandu balm sit in a third category, licensed under the Ayush system and allowed some health claims within limits. Patanjali's 2024 run-in with the Supreme Court over misleading advertising made regulators and courts more alert to Ayurvedic claims, which matters for Dabur and Emami because their marketing relies on traditional efficacy. Separately, advertising codes have pushed the industry away from fairness positioning: Hindustan Unilever renamed Fair & Lovely as Glow & Lovely in 2020, and Emami renamed Fair & Handsome as Smart & Handsome in January 2025, now aimed at a broader men's-grooming market with a target of ₹1,000 crore in three to four years.

In plain terms

Nobody tells these companies what to charge. The state shapes their business through tax, through what they are allowed to say on the pack and in advertisements, and, for the two multinationals, through rules that let ordinary shareholders veto a big increase in the fee paid to the parent.

The rules that touch only one or two companies

Several regimes matter intensely to one member. The Insecticides Act decides whether Godrej Consumer competes against registered products or against illegal incense sticks using unregistered molecules; enforcement is the variable. Minimum public shareholding fixes P&G's holding in Gillette at 75%, so it cannot increase its stake without an open offer and a delisting route. The Legal Metrology deregulation of standard pack sizes in 2017 is what allowed Marico to cut the millilitres in Parachute packs during the copra spike. Extended producer responsibility for plastic packaging is a slowly rising cost for every member, heaviest for sachet-intensive mass brands. Ind AS 29 determines how Dabur's Turkish and Godrej Consumer's Argentine results are reported. And the copra MSP, set each December by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, is the closest thing Marico has to a floor price for its main input. None of these is likely to change abruptly, but each should be on the watch list for the company it affects.

The industry was built by Ayurvedic and oil-milling families and multinational subsidiaries, reshaped by an acquisition decade and a naturals wave, and tested by six shocks since 2014.

The seven companies span almost a century and a half. Dabur began as a Kolkata Ayurvedic dispensary in 1884. Godrej was founded in 1897 and pioneered vegetable-oil soap. Emami was founded in 1974 by R. S. Agarwal and R. S. Goenka. Gillette India became a P&G subsidiary when P&G bought The Gillette Company in 2005. Honasa, founded in 2016, is the only member born online.

Reading a personal-care company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number that matters, what it means, how to compute it, what good looks like, how the seven compare, and where the definitions mislead.

A personal-care company's results look simple: revenue, a gross margin, an advertising line, EBITDA, profit. The difficulty is that each of those numbers is the product of several forces, some of which management controls (volume, mix, advertising, pricing) and some of which it does not (copra, palm, crude, currencies, tax). Reading the sector well means separating the two. This section starts with the equation that links them, works it through two illustrative ₹100 packs, then takes each native KPI in turn, builds an illustrative ₹1,000 crore brand, sets out a benchmark scorecard, collects the definition traps and ends with a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

The equation: from households to profit

Revenue in personal care is the number of households a brand reaches, multiplied by how often each uses the product, multiplied by what the company receives per use. In practice analysts work with a simpler version: revenue equals volume multiplied by realisation per unit, where realisation reflects both price and mix (the shift between cheap and premium variants), plus international revenue converted at the prevailing exchange rate. Profit follows from revenue through the gross margin, which is where commodity costs bite, and then through the spending lines that management chooses: advertising and promotion, people, distribution and overheads, and for the multinationals a royalty to the parent.

In plain terms

Two products on the same shelf at the same price can be completely different businesses. The bottle of oil is mostly oil; the tube of toothpaste is mostly brand. That single difference explains why Colgate earns 31% margins, why Marico's margins swing with the coconut harvest, and why a 10% jump in raw-material costs is a crisis for one and a nuisance for the other.

KPI 1: Underlying volume growth (UVG)

What it is. The growth in the quantity of product sold, excluding the effect of price changes, in the domestic business. It is the single best measure of whether more consumers are choosing a company's brands, and it compounds: volume gained this year is the base for next year. Formula. Company-defined; usually the growth in tonnes, litres or standardised units at constant prices, sometimes value growth deflated by a price index. Worked example. If a company sold 100 million standard units last year and 108 million this year, UVG is 8%; if revenue rose 12%, the remaining 4 points (approximately) are price and mix.

What drives it. Penetration (new households), frequency (more brushing, more oiling), share gains from small and unbranded rivals, distribution reach, and price: a price rise usually costs some volume, a GST-led price cut can add some. Benchmarks. Good is 8% or more, average 4–8%, weak below 4%, judged against the market, which NielsenIQ put at −4.3% for HPC in April–June 2026. How the seven compare. Q1 FY27: Marico India 11%, Colgate toothpaste about 8–8.5%, Emami domestic 8% like-for-like, Godrej Consumer India 7% (9% consolidated), Dabur India FMCG 5.0%. Gillette and Honasa do not disclose a volume figure.

KPI 2: Price/mix, and the growth decomposition

What it is. The part of revenue growth that does not come from volume: list-price changes, changes in pack sizes, shifts between cheaper and more premium products, and changes in trade terms. Formula. Approximately, value growth minus volume growth. Worked example. Dabur India FMCG in Q1 FY27: 9.5% value growth minus 5.0% volume growth gives about 4.5 points of price and mix. Good is 2–5 points driven by mix; average 0–2 points; weak is negative, or above 10 points, because double-digit price growth in this sector is almost always commodity pass-through that will reverse, not pricing power.

KPI 3: Gross margin

What it is. The share of net sales left after raw materials and packaging. It is the measure of how much the brand is worth over the physical product. Formula. (Net sales − materials consumed − packaging − change in inventories) ÷ net sales. Use the company's reported figure where available; a proxy of 100% minus screener's material-cost percentage can differ because of how packaging, stock changes and traded goods are classified. Worked example. In the hair-oil bottle above, ₹37.43 of gross profit on ₹81.43 of realisation is 46.0%. What drives it. Commodity prices, pricing actions, mix, and procurement efficiency. Benchmarks. For formulation businesses (MNCs, Emami, Honasa), good is 65% or more, average 60–65%, weak below 60%. For branded-commodity houses, good is 50% or more, average 45–50%, weak below 45%.

The gross margin under stress. The two packs above can be used to test any input shock. Take the hair-oil bottle through the 2025 copra spike: if today's oil cost of ₹37.50 is 35% below the peak, the peak cost was about ₹57.70. With no price action, gross profit falls from ₹37.43 to ₹17.23 a bottle, a gross margin of about 21%, and EBITDA turns into a loss of about ₹3.94. To hold the rupee gross profit, realisation has to rise by about 25% to roughly ₹101.60 (a printed price near ₹125), and the EBITDA margin still falls from 20% to about 16% because the same rupees of profit are now earned on a larger revenue base. Holding the percentage gross margin would need a price rise of about 46%, which no mass brand can take. That is why the real-world response, as at Marico in FY26, combines a partial price rise, smaller packs and a lower margin.

The GST test. The same chain shows what GST 2.0 did. At 18% GST, a ₹100 MRP pack yields a company realisation of ₹72.46; at 5% it yields ₹81.43, 12.4% more. A brand that cut its MRP to about ₹89 kept its realisation where it was and passed the whole benefit to the shopper; one that kept the ₹100 MRP took a 12.4% price increase in its revenue line without any change in volume. When reading results for the two quarters either side of September 2025, it is worth asking which of the two a company chose, because the second shows up as price/mix and flatters revenue growth.

KPI 4: Advertising and promotion intensity

What it is. Advertising and promotional spending as a share of sales: the cost of keeping a brand chosen. Formula. (Advertising + sales promotion + trade-promotion schemes) ÷ net sales. Worked example. On the toothpaste tube, ₹10.99 of A&P on ₹81.43 is 13.5%. What drives it. Competitive intensity, new launches, digital customer-acquisition costs, and management's choice between margin and share. Benchmarks. There is no single good number; what matters is the direction relative to the margin. Good is stable or rising A&P while margin holds; average is A&P cut to defend margin (which flatters one quarter and costs share later); weak is A&P above 30% of sales with no operating leverage, a sign that a brand is being bought rather than built.

KPI 6: Return on capital, and the goodwill trap

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital tied up in the business. Return on equity (ROE) is the shareholder version, using profit after tax and book equity. Formula. ROCE = EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), or ÷ (fixed assets + working capital); ROE = PAT ÷ shareholders' equity. Worked example. A brand earning ₹170 crore of EBIT on ₹200 crore of capital earns 85% ROCE; the same brand bought for ₹8,500 crore earns 2% (worked fully in the anatomy below). What drives it. Margin, asset turnover, negative working capital, and above all whether brands were built (no balance-sheet cost) or bought (full price on the balance sheet). Benchmarks. Good above 40%, average 20–40%, weak below 20%.

KPI 8: International share and constant-currency growth

Local demand, local inflation (which inflates constant-currency growth) and currency moves. Benchmarks. Good is under 30% of revenue with rupee growth above 10%; average 30–40%, or rupee growth below 5%; weak is concentration in one frontier currency with falling rupee revenue.

Definition traps, collected

  • Gillette's calendar. Gillette India moved from a July–June year to April–March. Its "Mar-2025" column is a nine-month stub (₹2,235 crore of revenue); never compare it with a twelve-month year. FY26 now means April 2025 to March 2026, like the others.
  • Colgate's consolidated page. Screener's consolidated page for Colgate stops in 2010 and shows a stale P/E of 111×; use the standalone figures, which are what this report uses (the subsidiary is immaterial).
  • Honasa's revenue basis. Reported FY26 revenue is ₹2,392 crore (+15.7%); "like-for-like" is about ₹2,479 crore (+20%). Q1 FY27 is ₹756 crore reported (+27%) and ₹785 crore like-for-like (+32%). State which one is used.
  • Volume definitions. Every company defines volume differently; pack-size cuts can hide in or out of volume; Emami quotes like-for-like; Godrej Consumer India and consolidated differ.
  • Acquisitions. Marico's FY26 growth includes four acquired brands; Emami's Q1 FY27 domestic growth is 20% reported and 12% like-for-like.
  • The Q2 FY26 base. July–September 2025 was distorted by the GST changeover destock; Q2 FY27 growth rates for wholesale-heavy companies will be flattered.
  • Constant currency. In hyperinflationary markets constant-currency growth overstates real growth; use rupee and volume growth.
  • Screener's operating margin. It excludes other income, which is material for Dabur (4.4% of sales); company "EBITDA" may include or exclude it.
  • Net cash. Screener's summary balance sheet has no cash line, so its investments-minus-borrowings proxy understates the cash of Colgate, Gillette and Honasa; borrowings include lease liabilities.
  • Gross margin proxies. One minus the material-cost ratio is not always the company's gross margin; packaging, stock changes and traded goods are classified differently.
  • Quarter-on-quarter comparisons. Emami and Dabur are seasonal; always compare with the same quarter a year earlier.
  • Press versus filings. Godrej Consumer's Q1 FY27 revenue growth is 15.4% on screener's figures but "+19%" in several press reports (possibly a restated base or a different revenue line); Marico's Q1 profit appears as ₹652 crore and ₹630 crore, Dabur's as ₹586 crore and ₹591 crore, depending on whether minority interests are deducted.

How to read a personal-care company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Domestic volume growth first (one minute). Find the company's underlying volume number and compare it with last quarter, the same quarter last year and NielsenIQ's market figure. Red flag: volume below 4% while revenue grows in double digits.
  2. Decompose revenue growth (one minute). Split it into volume, price/mix, acquisitions and currency. Red flag: more than 10 points of price, which usually reverses.
  3. Gross margin and its cause (one minute). Note the change in basis points and what management blames: copra, palm, crude, packaging, mix. Red flag: falling gross margin with no pricing action planned.
  4. A&P (one minute). Did advertising rise or fall as a share of sales? Red flag: EBITDA margin up only because A&P was cut.
  5. EBITDA margin against the same quarter last year (one minute). Seasonality makes sequential comparisons misleading for Emami and Dabur.
  6. International in rupees (one minute). Check rupee growth and volume, not only constant currency; note any hyperinflation restatement.
  7. Exceptional items (one minute). Look for GST inverted-duty charges, labour-code provisions, impairments and restructuring. Red flag: "one-time" items that recur.
  8. Channel and portfolio signals (one minute). Organised, e-commerce and quick-commerce growth; new-age brand growth; distribution reach.
  9. Cash and working capital (one minute). Any rise in inventory or receivable days; for half-year results, operating cash flow against profit.
  10. Guidance and the next base (one minute). What management says about the next quarter's volume and margin, and whether the year-ago quarter was weak or strong. Red flag: guidance that relies on a commodity falling further.
In plain terms

Read a personal-care result in this order: did they sell more units, did they keep more of each rupee after paying for the oil and the bottle, did they keep advertising, and is anything unusual hiding in the "one-time" line? If those four answers are good, the headline profit is probably telling the truth.

One last point on using these KPIs. No single measure is sufficient in this sector, because each company's numbers are bent by a different force: copra at Marico, palm and crude at Godrej Consumer, hyperinflation and the Middle East at Dabur, seasonality and wholesale stocking at Emami, the parent at Colgate and Gillette, and customer-acquisition costs at Honasa. The most reliable reading comes from combining volume, gross margin and A&P for the domestic business, measured against the company's own history and against the market, and treating everything else (overseas growth, acquisitions, price) as context that can reverse.

What a multiple implies

For a business that distributes almost all its profit, a simple dividend-growth relationship links the price-to-earnings ratio to the growth the market expects: P/E is roughly the payout ratio multiplied by (1 + growth), divided by (cost of equity − growth). It is crude, because it assumes growth continues for ever, but it shows how sensitive valuations in this sector are to small changes in expected growth. For a company paying out 100% of earnings with an ILLUSTRATIVE cost of equity of 11%, perpetual growth of 6% justifies about 21 times earnings, 7% about 27 times, 8% about 36 times and 9% about 55 times. Colgate at 35 times is therefore pricing roughly 8% perpetual earnings growth; a year in which its profit fell 7.8% was enough to push the market to doubt that.

In plain terms

At these prices, investors are betting on decades of steady growth. If they decide a company will grow 7% a year instead of 8%, a fair price falls by about a quarter, even though nothing visible has changed. That is why these stocks can fall 25–30% in a year in which their businesses barely move.

Why the market now prefers momentum

The valuation map in section 15 showed the paradox: the two companies with the highest returns on capital (Colgate, Gillette) trade at 35 times, while Godrej Consumer (19% ROCE) trades at 42 times and Honasa (19%) at 58 times. The multinationals combined trade at 36.1 times earnings against 44.4 times for the five Indian-promoted companies, reversing the historical premium. Three things explain it. First, the multinationals' growth stalled in FY26 (Colgate's profit fell), and because their returns cannot be reinvested, their value is almost entirely a function of growth. Second, the volume revival in 2026 was strongest at Marico and Honasa, and markets reward acceleration more than level. Third, royalty and payout policies set by a foreign parent create a governance discount that is easy to ignore in good years and harder to ignore in flat ones.

The individual multiples follow the same logic. Marico at 54.8 times earnings (about 44 times FY26 EBITDA) prices a long runway of double-digit volume growth, a margin recovery from the copra trough and the Marico 2.0 mix shift away from commodities. Emami at 21.5 times prices mature niches, export disruption, a tax rate that must rise and the promoter-pledge overhang.

Technology and disruption: shelves, formats and formulas

The disruptions that matter are in where products are bought, how brands are built, what men shave with and what consumers believe works.

Personal care is not a technology-intensive business in the way electronics or pharmaceuticals are, but it is being reshaped by technology from several directions at once. Each disruption has a different set of winners and losers among the seven.

Eight disruptions, ranked by impact

  • Quick commerce and the online shift. Quick commerce is expected to become the largest online BPC format by 2030 (30–40% of online). It favours premium, impulse and new-age brands and squeezes traditional distributors. Platform private labels and rising advertising take-rates are the margin risk. Winners so far: Dabur, Emami, Honasa, Marico's premium lines. Covered in section 08 and in the E-Retail report.
  • D2C and new-age brands. About 70 new-age brands exceed ₹100 crore of revenue and Redseer expects more than 150 by FY31, taking a quarter to nearly a third of spending. Their edge is speed, influencers and ingredient-led positioning; their weakness is customer-acquisition cost and offline scale.
  • Buy-the-brand consolidation. HUL–Minimalist, Marico's six digital brands, Emami's IncNut and Axiom. The risk is overpaying: the Africa write-down at Godrej Consumer is the cautionary precedent.
  • Trimmers versus blades. A format shift, not a brand shift, and the main structural threat to Gillette's blade annuity. P&G's answer sits largely in its Braun brand rather than in Gillette India's listed business.
  • Naturals and actives. Ayurvedic and "toxin-free" positioning took toothpaste share from Colgate over a decade; science-led actives (The Derma Co, Minimalist) now challenge mass skin care. Winners: Dabur, Honasa; pressured: Colgate, mass-cream brands.
  • Regulation of claims and packaging. Tighter scrutiny of fairness, Ayurvedic and "toxin-free" claims, and extended producer responsibility for multilayer sachets, raise compliance costs and limit marketing.
  • Climate and seasonality. Milder summers and erratic rain shift Emami's and Dabur's seasonal sales; hotter summers help cooling products. The variance is rising.
  • AI and digital distribution. Distributor-management systems, retailer-ordering apps and AI-led beat planning improve fill rates and assortment; Emami calls digital and AI "core enablers". The advantage goes to scale players that can afford the systems.

The pattern across these disruptions is that technology lowers the cost of launching a brand and raises the cost of scaling one. Anyone with a contract manufacturer and an Instagram budget can reach ₹100 crore; only about twenty BPC brands in India exceed ₹1,500 crore. The incumbents' advantage lies precisely in the scaling stage: distribution to millions of outlets, media-buying power and procurement. Their vulnerability lies in the launch stage, where they are slower. The equilibrium that is emerging, with incumbents buying successful digital brands and digital houses building offline networks, reflects that division.

In plain terms

The internet made it easy to start a beauty brand and hard to make it big. The old companies are good at making brands big but slow at starting them, so they buy the young brands that work. The young companies that stay independent have to learn to do what the old ones do: reach shops in small towns.

Bull vs bear

The case for and against owning Indian personal care in late 2026.

The sub-sector's investment case rests on a simple proposition: that the large brands will keep taking share from a market whose volumes are weak, while input costs and the GST distortion wash out. The bear case is that 2026's share gains were partly a one-off and that valuations, even after the de-rating, still assume decades of steady growth. Both sides are summarised below.

The bull case

  • Share gains in a weak market: 5–11% domestic volume growth against a NielsenIQ market down 4.3% shows the large brands' advantage in distribution, advertising and procurement.
  • Structural demand: brushing frequency, conversion from loose oil and unbranded soap, premiumisation and a market growing about 12% a year to about ₹3.4 lakh crore by FY30 (indicative).
  • Margin recovery ahead: copra 35% below its peak, a GST-depressed base for Q2 FY27, and Godrej Consumer's India margin expected to recover to 22–26% if commodities stabilise.
  • Exceptional economics: 46–70% gross margins, negative working capital, returns on capital of 19–108% and payouts that let shareholders collect cash while waiting.
  • Valuations reset: five of seven are 24–33% below their highs; the multinationals are at a decade-low 35× with 2.5–2.7% yields.

The bear case

  • A shrinking market: HPC volume −4.3% and rural −5% are not a base for long-term growth; if the slowdown reaches company numbers, multiples have further to fall.
  • One-off flattery: Q1 and Q2 FY27 growth rates are inflated by the GST destock base, acquisitions and inflation-driven overseas growth.
  • Input risk: crude derivatives and palm are still high; copra could re-inflate; packaging is near record levels.
  • Digital disruption: online and quick commerce favour new brands and platforms, raise advertising costs and threaten traditional distribution moats.
  • Still expensive: about 42× earnings combined; Honasa and Marico at 55–58× require years of double-digit growth; royalty risk at the multinationals; governance overhangs (Emami pledges, thin Gillette float).

On balance, the evidence of 2026 supports the bull case on business quality and the bear case on valuation. The listed leaders have shown they can grow volume when the market does not, and the input-cost cycle is turning in Marico's favour and could turn in Godrej Consumer's and Emami's if crude eases. But valuations still assume that growth persists for a long time, the quarters immediately ahead are flattered by weak bases, and the rural data are poor. The most useful stance is company-specific: the case is strongest where volume momentum and margin recovery coincide (Marico), most contested where growth must justify a premium multiple (Honasa), and most interesting where quality has been de-rated (Colgate, Gillette) or where a margin recovery is not yet priced (Godrej Consumer, Emami).

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • A&P: advertising and promotion spending, including media, sampling and trade promotions; shown as a share of sales.
  • BPC: beauty and personal care, the market definition used by Redseer (bath and body, skin, hair, oral, wellness, make-up, deodorants and fragrance).
  • Cash conversion cycle (CCC): debtor days plus inventory days minus payable days; negative means suppliers fund the business.
  • Constant currency (CC): growth measured at last year's exchange rates; inflated by local inflation in hyperinflationary markets.
  • Copra: dried coconut kernel, pressed to make coconut oil; Parachute's main raw material.
  • D2C: direct-to-consumer; brands born and sold mainly online, often through their own websites and marketplaces.
  • Destock: when distributors and retailers run down inventory instead of reordering, cutting a company's shipments for a period.
  • EBITDA margin: operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, as a share of sales; screener's "OPM" excludes other income.
  • EV/EBITDA: enterprise value (market value plus net debt) divided by EBITDA.
  • GAUM: Godrej Consumer's Africa, USA and Middle East segment.
  • GST 2.0: the September 2025 restructuring of India's goods and services tax into two main slabs of 5% and 18%.
  • Gross margin: sales minus raw materials and packaging, as a share of sales.
  • HPC: home and personal care, NielsenIQ's basket covering soaps, detergents, hair care, oral care and similar products.
  • Hyperinflation accounting (Ind AS 29): restatement of results of subsidiaries in very high-inflation economies such as Turkey and Argentina.
  • Inverted duty structure: when GST on inputs exceeds GST on outputs, so input credit accumulates and is only partly refundable.
  • LABSA: linear alkyl benzene sulphonic acid, a crude-derived surfactant used in detergents and soaps.
  • Like-for-like (LFL): growth excluding acquisitions or other changes in scope.
  • LODR Regulation 23(1A): SEBI rule making royalty payments above 5% of consolidated turnover a material related-party transaction needing minority approval.
  • Modern trade (MT): supermarkets, hypermarkets and organised chains.
  • MRP: maximum retail price printed on the pack, inclusive of GST.
  • MSP: minimum support price, the government's floor price for a crop such as copra.
  • NIQ: NielsenIQ, which tracks retail sales through a panel of shops.
  • PFAD / soap noodles: palm fatty acid distillate and semi-finished soap pellets, the main soap raw materials.
  • Price/mix: the part of revenue growth from price changes and shifts towards more or less expensive products; value growth minus volume growth.
  • Quick commerce (QC): delivery apps (Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart) promising delivery in minutes from local dark stores.
  • Royalty: a fee paid by a subsidiary to its parent for the use of brands and technology.
  • ROCE / ROE: return on capital employed (EBIT ÷ capital employed) and return on equity (profit after tax ÷ shareholders' equity).
  • Traditional trade (TT): kiranas, chemists, wholesalers and other independent shops.
  • UVG: underlying volume growth, the company-defined growth in quantity sold in the domestic business.
  • VAHO: value-added hair oils, such as perfumed, almond, amla or cooling oils, as opposed to plain coconut oil.
