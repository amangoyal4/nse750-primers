00

The one-page view: a franchise business where the doctor is the customer

Off-patent medicines, sold under brand names, chosen by a doctor, paid for by the patient. Price control touches a fifth of it; the rest compounds.

India's branded-formulations business sells medicines whose patents expired long ago, under brand names that doctors have written for years. A cardiologist in Indore does not prescribe "telmisartan"; she prescribes a named brand of it, and the chemist downstairs dispenses that brand. The patient pays, mostly out of pocket. No insurer, formulary or pharmacy-benefit manager stands between the brand owner and the prescriber. That single fact explains why this is one of the most profitable businesses in Indian listed markets, and why it is regulated the way it is.

The market is large and steady. The Indian Pharma Market (IPM), measured at retail value, was ₹2,58,977 crore for the twelve months to August 2026 (PharmaTrac), up 10.4% in value but only about 1% in units. Thirteen companies in the NSE 750 make their money mainly from this kind of brand, in India and in branded emerging markets such as Africa, South-East Asia and Brazil. One of them, J. B. Chemicals, merged into Torrent Pharmaceuticals on 8 July 2026 and is now delisted; it is profiled here because its sale is the defining deal of the cycle, but it is kept out of every total. The other twelve reported ₹85,907 crore of revenue in FY26 at a blended 25.9% EBITDA margin, earned ₹13,991 crore of profit and were worth ₹6.33 lakh crore on 5 October 2026, about 45 times their combined profit.

Three facts define the economics. First, the market grows on price and new launches, not on more pills: in the quarter to August 2026, IPM growth of 11.9% split into 6.0 points of price, 3.6 points of new products and only 2.3 points of volume. Second, pricing power is real but rationed. Only about 18% of the market sits under direct government ceiling prices; the other 82% may raise prices by up to 10% a year, and in practice raised them by 5.6% a year on average between April 2020 and March 2025 (Parliamentary Standing Committee, August 2026). Third, the productive asset is a doctor's habit, not a factory. Companies that do not carry acquisition goodwill earn very high returns on capital: Abbott India 44.8%, GSK 61.4%, Sanofi Consumer Healthcare 89.9%.

Between 2024 and 2026 the rules of the game shifted. A marketing code (UCPMP 2024) restricted gifts and paid travel for doctors. A stricter manufacturing standard (revised Schedule M) began to squeeze thousands of small contract makers. GST on most medicines fell from 12% to 5% in September 2025. Consolidation accelerated: Torrent bought J. B. Chemicals for an equity value of ₹25,689 crore, Mankind bought Bharat Serums and Vaccines for an enterprise value of ₹13,630 crore, and Eris kept assembling a diabetes and injectables franchise. And India acquired its first new blockbuster drug class in a decade: Eli Lilly's Mounjaro became the country's best-selling brand, while generic semaglutide arrived in March 2026 with 25 brands and a price war.

Who is winning? In the latest quarter (Q1 FY27, April–June 2026) Torrent's India base business grew 19% against an IPM growing about 12%, Corona's revenue grew 22%, Ajanta's India secondary sales 15% and Eris's domestic branded business 14%. At the other end, Abbott India grew only 4.3%, Pfizer 8.3% and Emcure's organic India business 6–7%. The four MNC subsidiaries (Abbott India, GSK, Pfizer, Sanofi Consumer) are cash machines whose growth is set by a parent's portfolio decisions. The acquirers (Torrent, Mankind, Eris) carry debt and goodwill that pull their reported return on capital down to 13–15%, even though the brands underneath earn far more.

The risks are as specific as the strengths. Price control could widen: a parliamentary committee asked in August 2026 for a review of how new non-scheduled drugs get their launch prices. Acquirers must pay down 1.3–2.1 times EBITDA of net debt. The GLP-1 class is turning into a price war in which the innovator itself cut prices by up to 48%. And the stocks are expensive: Torrent trades at 81 times trailing earnings, Corona at 59, Mankind at 48.

In plain terms

A branded medicine company in India is paid for a habit: a doctor writing the same brand name for years, and a patient refilling it every month. The company's job is to win that habit (through its sales representatives and its range), keep it (through quality and supply), and raise the price a little every year within the government's rules. Do that well and the business earns a third of its revenue as operating profit with very little capital. Investors pay consumer-staples multiples for it, and the arguments are about how far the government will let prices rise and how much the acquirers have overpaid.

The report runs in four parts. Sections 1–7 explain the business: what counts as branded, how big and how fast, the shift to chronic therapies, the prescription machine, the cost structure, the market structure and the five business models. Sections 8–14 cover the forces: price control, the marketing and quality rules, brand M&A, the MNC subsidiaries, GLP-1, the emerging-markets half and the history. Section 15 is the KPI masterclass. Sections 16–23 compare the companies, profile each one, set out valuation, disruption, the bull and bear cases, what to watch and a glossary.

01

What counts as a branded formulator, and what does not

Thirteen companies, one business model: finished medicines sold under their own names, prescribed by doctors, bought at a chemist.

This report covers the companies whose revenue is dominated by finished medicines sold under their own brand names, prescribed by doctors and sold through chemists, mainly in India and in branded emerging markets (Asia, Africa, Latin America and Brazil). The medicine is almost always off-patent: the molecule could be made by anyone, but the brand carries the doctor's trust and the company's quality reputation. It is one of seven pharma sub-sectors in the NSE 750, each covered in its own report; the export, API and contract-manufacturing models are covered in theirs (see the sub-sector reports).

Four neighbouring business models look similar from a distance but run on different economics, and they are not in this report.

  • API makers sell the drug chemical itself, by the kilogram, to other drug makers. Their customer is a purchasing manager comparing quotes, not a doctor, so price follows the cost of solvents and Chinese competition.
  • Export generic formulators sell tablets and injectables into the US and European generic markets, where a handful of buying groups and tenders set the price and every product erodes as more competitors file. Their franchise is regulatory approval, not a brand.
  • CDMOs (contract development and manufacturing organisations) make molecules or finished doses for innovator companies under long contracts. Their moat is being written into a client's regulatory filing.
  • Diversified majors, namely Sun, Cipla, Dr Reddy's, Zydus, Lupin and Glenmark, also own very large India brand franchises (Sun is the IPM's number one), but their profits are a mix of US generics, specialty products and India. They are classified as Diversified Majors and covered in the sector overview; they appear here only as competitors.

Several of the thirteen sit near a border, and it matters for how their numbers read. Ajanta now earns about 29% of revenue from US generics, Alkem about 20%, Ipca runs an API arm and a US business through Unichem, and Emcure's Canadian and European subsidiaries sell mostly unbranded generics. Where a figure blends the two, the text says so. At the other extreme, Sanofi Consumer Healthcare is an over-the-counter brand company: its products (Allegra, Combiflam) are bought by patients on a chemist's or a doctor's suggestion rather than prescribed in the usual sense, but the brand-led, asset-light economics are the same.

Two consequences follow. A company's reported revenue is about two-thirds of what the same sales are worth in IPM data, so a company's share of the IPM cannot be read straight off its income statement. And the channel's slice is fixed in percentage terms, so every price increase the brand owner takes also raises the chemist's and stockist's earnings. That is one reason the trade has supported regular price rises, and one reason the government keeps returning to the idea of capping trade margins.

In plain terms

If a strip of tablets costs a patient ₹100, the company that owns the brand receives about ₹69, the chemist keeps about ₹19, the stockist about ₹8 and the government about ₹5. The factory that made the tablets might have been paid ₹15–20 of the company's ₹69. Everything else the company spends goes on persuading doctors and running the business, and what is left over is profit.

02

A ₹2.6 lakh crore market that grows on price and launches, not pills

Ten per cent a year in value, one to two per cent in volume. The difference is the franchise.

The Indian Pharma Market is the retail value of medicines sold through chemists, as measured by two data vendors. On the PharmaTrac series (Pharmarack/AWACS, the one the trade press quotes monthly and that Torrent, Alkem and Ajanta cite) it was ₹2,58,977 crore for the twelve months to August 2026, up 10.4% in value and about 1.0% in units. The single month of August 2026 was ₹23,272 crore, up 10.7%. Twelve months earlier the market had been growing more slowly: the moving annual total to February 2026 was ₹2.44 lakh crore, up 8.4%, and Pharmarack itself guided 7.8–8.1% growth for calendar 2026 before the run-rate came in at 10–11%.

Two vendors, two numbers. IQVIA publishes a competing audit. The two differ by roughly 5–10% in absolute market size and by 0.5–1 percentage point in a company's share. Mankind, for example, cites an IQVIA share of 4.7%, while its August 2026 PharmaTrac monthly sales imply about 5.6%. This report uses PharmaTrac unless a figure is marked IQVIA, and never mixes the two in one chart.

The growth equation: price, launches and very little volume

The most important chart for understanding the business is the split of growth. In the quarter to August 2026 the IPM grew 11.9%: 6.0 points came from price increases on existing products, 3.6 points from products launched in the previous year or so, and just 2.3 points from more units of existing products. A single-month cut from February 2026 tells the same story. Over the longer run (FY15–25) the composite is similar: value growth of 9–10% made up of roughly 4–5 points of price, 2–3 of volume and 2–3 of launches (indicative composite). Between FY18 and FY22 the IPM compounded at about 11% in value but only 2.9% in volume (IIFL broker comparison).

Why is volume so weak in a country where a tenth of adults are diabetic? Partly because acute therapies, especially anti-infectives and respiratory drugs, are shrinking in units after the COVID-era boom and as weaker cough-and-cold seasons and FDC bans take products off shelves. Partly because chronic patients, once on therapy, take a fixed dose: the market grows in patients slowly, and in pills per patient hardly at all. The result is that a company's "volume" growth comes mostly from taking prescriptions away from weaker brands, not from the market getting bigger. That is why the industry's arguments are about prescription share, and why companies obsess over growth relative to the IPM rather than absolute growth.

Therapy by therapy

Growth is very uneven across therapies. Cardiac (₹36,506 crore over twelve months, 14.1% of the IPM) and anti-diabetic medicines both grew about 15% in August 2026, with units up 2–3%. Vitamins and nutrients grew 16%. Anti-infectives, the biggest acute category, grew under 3% in value while units fell 5.5%; respiratory was similar. A company's therapy mix therefore sets a ceiling or a floor on its growth before its own execution counts at all.

Looking ahead, a reasonable base case is IPM value growth of 9–11% a year over FY26–30: 4–6 points of price, 2–3 of volume and 3–4 of launches, with the GLP-1 class adding perhaps half a point to a point a year (indicative estimate). Pharmarack's own 7.8–8.1% guide for 2026 proved too conservative, which is a reminder that the industry's growth has surprised on the upside more often than the downside over the past decade. The listed companies' growth will differ from the market by their therapy mix, their launch engines and their acquisitions, which is the subject of the rest of this report.

In plain terms

Indians are not buying many more pills each year; they are paying a bit more for the same pills and switching to newer combinations and brands. A company that wants to grow faster than the market has to take prescriptions from someone else, launch products that doctors adopt, or buy brands. All three cost money, which is why growth and margin trade off against each other in this industry.

03

The chronic shift: from five-day courses to lifelong refills

Chronic and sub-chronic medicines are now 56% of the market. Each one is an annuity, and the franchises built on them earn the highest multiples.

Twenty years ago more than 60% of the Indian market was acute medicine: antibiotics, cough syrups, painkillers, antacids, bought for a few days and then forgotten. By the moving annual total to February 2026, acute had shrunk to 44% of the IPM, with chronic therapies (cardiac, diabetes, thyroid, neuro-psychiatry) at 35% and sub-chronic (vitamins, gastro maintenance, pain management, some dermatology) at 21% (Pharmarack, via BioSpectrum). Four years earlier, in February 2022, acute had been 47%. Another cut of the data puts chronic alone at 40.6% of the IPM (May 2026), because it counts parts of sub-chronic as chronic. The definitions differ by vendor and by company, so any chronic-share figure needs its definition stated alongside it.

Why investors pay more for chronic revenue

  • It is an annuity. A hypertensive or diabetic patient refills twelve times a year, for years. An acute brand sells one five-day course and must be re-prescribed from scratch next time.
  • It is sticky. Doctors rarely switch a stable patient's brand, because a change risks a bad reading at the next visit. Once a brand is in a patient's routine, it tends to stay.
  • It grows faster. Chronic therapies compounded at about 12.3% between FY18 and FY22 against 11% for the IPM (IIFL), and cardiac and anti-diabetic are growing about 15% in 2026 against about 10% for the market.
  • It pays the field force better. Specialist-heavy chronic portfolios generated ₹9.5–10 lakh of revenue per MR per month in 2022, against about ₹4 lakh for acute-heavy companies (IIFL). The same salary buys more revenue.
  • It is less seasonal. Acute sales swing with the monsoon and the flu season; chronic sales barely notice.

The demand behind the shift is demographic and medical. The ICMR-INDIAB study (2023) counted about 101 million Indians with diabetes, 136 million with pre-diabetes and about 315 million with hypertension. Obesity, ageing, the spread of specialists into smaller towns and wider insurance cover all push more people into diagnosis and long-term treatment. Hospital admissions add demand for injectables and critical-care products, the segment Mankind (through Bharat Serums) and Eris (through Swiss Parenterals) bought into.

Chronic has its own risks, and they are not small. The cardiac and diabetes staples are disproportionately on the National List of Essential Medicines, which puts them under ceiling prices (section 8). And chronic molecules are the ones whose patents expire into price wars: sitagliptin in 2022, dapagliflozin in 2025 and semaglutide in March 2026 each drew dozens of Indian generic brands within weeks, at 50–90% below the innovator. A chronic franchise built on one molecule can see its price fall by half in a year. The best franchises therefore spread across many molecules and therapies, and launch into each patent cliff early rather than defending against it.

The best-selling brands

The top of the brand league shows the mix of old and new. The number one brand in India for the twelve months to August 2026 was Mounjaro, Eli Lilly's tirzepatide, at ₹1,256 crore and up 502%, less than eighteen months after launch. Behind it were GSK's Augmentin (₹957 crore), a decades-old antibiotic, and USV's Glycomet GP (₹950 crore), an unlisted company's diabetes combination. Positions four to ten, by name only, were Cipla's Foracort, Alkem's Pan, Ipca's Zerodol SP, Abbott's Mixtard insulin, Alkem's Clavam, Himalaya's Liv.52 and Alkem's Pan D. Alkem therefore owns three of the top ten brands; GSK, Ipca and the Abbott group one each.

The company-level picture follows the therapy mix. Torrent (about 75% chronic and sub-chronic), Corona (73.4%) and Eris (about two-thirds) are the chronic specialists, and they are the companies the market values most richly per rupee of earnings. In plain terms

An antibiotic brand has to win the doctor again every time a patient falls ill. A blood-pressure brand wins the doctor once and then collects a refill every month for years. That is why the market pays more for companies whose sales are mostly chronic medicines, and why acute-heavy companies spend their money buying or launching chronic brands. The catch is that chronic molecules are where patents expire into price wars and where the government is most likely to fix prices.

04

The prescription machine: doctors, reps and the PCPM arithmetic Deep dive

A branded franchise is a sales organisation that happens to own medicines. Its output is prescriptions, and its productivity is revenue per representative.

Every branded-formulations company is built around the same loop. It segments India's doctors by specialty and prescribing potential, sends medical representatives to see them repeatedly, persuades them to write the company's brand, and then measures whether the prescriptions turned into sales at the chemist. The loop is old, but its economics decide almost everything about a company's growth and margin, and it is the part of the business that changed most under the 2024 marketing code.

The field force: how many reps, and what each one costs

Nobody publishes an authoritative count of India's medical representatives. Industry sources put the number at "2 lakh+" or "about 5 lakh", depending on whether the representatives of small franchise companies are included; a sensible working range is 2–3 lakh promotional MRs at organised companies and up to about 5 lakh including the franchise tail (indicative). Among the listed companies, Mankind runs the largest army, at 16,500+ according to its Q1 FY27 investor slides. Its primer cites 18,500+, which probably counts first-line managers; this report uses the deck figure. PCPM: the single most useful number in the business

Productivity is measured as PCPM, revenue per representative per month: India revenue divided by the number of MRs, divided by twelve, in ₹ lakh. It folds together everything the field force does well or badly: which doctors it sees, which therapies it carries, how strong its brands are, how long its people have stayed. In 2022, a broker comparison (via IIFL) put chronic-heavy companies such as Torrent and Sanofi at ₹9.5–10 lakh, the industry average at ₹5.5–6 lakh, and acute-heavy companies such as Alkem and Ipca at about ₹4 lakh. In its Q1 FY27 call Torrent said the combined Torrent and JB field force was running at "just over ₹10 lakh". JB alone was at ₹6.87 lakh in FY24 (Kotak Institutional Equities). Every other company figure in this report is a calculation from company revenue and headcount, and is labelled as such.

Why does PCPM matter so much? Because the field force is mostly a fixed cost. If a representative costs ₹8 lakh a year (about ₹0.67 lakh a month), the field force consumes about 13% of revenue at a PCPM of ₹5 lakh but only about 6.7% at ₹10 lakh. Doubling productivity is worth six to seven points of EBITDA margin before anything else changes. That arithmetic is the core of Torrent's margin lead, and the reason acquirers can pay high prices for brands: put an under-promoted brand into a more productive field force and its margin jumps.

In plain terms

A medical representative is paid roughly the same whether the doctors he visits prescribe ₹4 lakh or ₹10 lakh of the company's medicines a month. So a company whose representatives each generate ₹10 lakh keeps far more of every rupee than one whose representatives generate ₹4 lakh. Most of the gap between a 20% margin and a 33% margin in this industry is explained by that one ratio.

Three sales numbers, and why they disagree

A company's income statement shows primary sales: what it sold to stockists. Market data vendors track secondary sales: what stockists sold on to chemists, valued at retail. Prescription audits by IQVIA and SMSRC track how often doctors write each brand. In a steady state the three move together. When they diverge, the gap is usually channel inventory. A company that pushes stock into the channel before a price rise, or a distributor that destocks after a currency scare, can make the reported quarter look much better or worse than the underlying demand. Torrent's Brazil business is a clean example: reported revenue grew only 3% in Q1 FY27 while IQVIA's in-market data showed 19% growth, because distributors cut their inventory. Companies that report their secondary growth against the IPM (Alkem, Ajanta) give investors a cleaner read.

The launch engine

New products added 3.5–3.6 points to IPM growth in 2026. For Indian companies, a "new product" is rarely a new molecule. It is a new fixed-dose combination, a new strength, a new pack or a new brand of a molecule coming off patent. Companies with the best launch habits get to market first: more than half of Ajanta's 350+ launches were first-to-market in India. The output is a portfolio of large brands. Mankind has 22 brands above ₹100 crore, Torrent had 18 before JB, Alkem about 12, and Corona generates about 70% of its sales from 27 "engine brands". A brand above ₹100 crore usually means hundreds of thousands of patients on therapy and a doctor base that defends it.

Prescriptions versus value: Mankind's breadth

Mankind has been number one in prescriptions in India for nine years running, with a 15.2% share of all prescriptions (IQVIA, Q1 FY27), yet it is only third or fourth by value. That gap is its model: its 16,500 representatives cover more than five lakh doctors, including general practitioners in small towns, writing large numbers of lower-priced prescriptions. Corona sits at the opposite pole, with about 76% of its prescriptions coming from specialists, and Eris says its representatives cover 80,000+ specialists. Breadth gives resilience and volume; specialist depth gives price and PCPM. Both can work, but they produce different margins.

Trade generics and the franchise tail

Not every medicine sold in India is promoted to a doctor. Trade generics are products sold under a brand or a generic name without detailing, pushed through stockists and chemists with channel margins of 50–80% or more, so that the chemist has a strong reason to recommend or substitute them. One estimate puts them at about ₹24,000 crore, around 10% of the IPM (a substack source of low reliability; indicative only). Mankind and Alkem both run trade-generics divisions, as do Cipla and Lupin. The business is capital-light but lower-margin for the company, and it is the first target of any trade-margin cap. Below it sits the PCD or franchise tail: thousands of small marketers who buy medicines from third-party plants in Baddi, Sikkim or Gujarat and appoint franchisees to promote them district by district, with claimed margins of 20–50% (industry blogs, low reliability). That tail is where revised Schedule M and the marketing code bite hardest (section 9).

In plain terms

There are two ways to get a medicine into a patient's hands in India. The branded way persuades the doctor to write your name, which is expensive but durable. The trade way pays the chemist a fat margin to sell your product over the counter, which is cheap to start but easy for a competitor to copy with a fatter margin. The large listed companies do mostly the first and some of the second; the small unorganised players live on the second, and new rules are making that harder.

What the 2024 marketing code changed

Before March 2024, promotion in India's tail often meant gifts, hospitality and conference travel. The Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical Marketing Practices (UCPMP 2024) banned gifts and personal benefits to doctors and their families, restricted conference travel and hospitality to speakers, required continuing medical education to run through recognised institutions in India with disclosed spending, and restricted free samples. The Supreme Court had already ruled in 2022 (Apex Laboratories) that freebies to doctors are not tax-deductible. The effect is to favour companies whose prescriptions rest on brand equity, scientific detailing and field-force quality: large chronic franchises and MNCs gain, inducement-led small marketers lose (indicative assessment). So far there is no visible damage to market growth; the IPM accelerated to 10–11% after the code.

05

Where ₹100 of revenue goes, and why the field force is the factory

Materials are a quarter of sales. People and promotion are a third. What remains is one of the richest margins in listed India.

The cost structure of a branded franchise looks nothing like a manufacturer's. For Indian franchise companies, materials and purchased finished goods are only 22–32% of revenue: the molecule is cheap, and the brand owner keeps 65–80% of revenue as gross margin. Company-reported gross margins in the sample range from about 80.5% at Corona (FY25) and about 77% at Ajanta to 65.4% at Alkem (Q4 FY26). The big costs sit below the gross line: employees (16–20% of revenue, of which the field force is perhaps 55–65%), and promotion, samples, medical education, freight and distribution (another 15–20%). MNC subsidiaries look different again: they buy much of their portfolio as finished goods from the parent or contract makers, so materials run 35–55% of revenue, but they spend less on people and promotion and end up at similar margins.

Below EBITDA, the archetypes diverge again. Depreciation is 3–6% of revenue for Indian franchises (more for acquirers, which amortise purchased brands) and 1–2% for asset-light MNCs. Inputs: why API prices matter less here

India depends on China for about 74% of its imports of drug chemicals and intermediates by value (FY25). For an export generics company, where materials are 40–48% of revenue and the selling price is set by a US buyer, a jump in Chinese API prices goes straight to the margin. For a branded franchise, materials are a quarter of revenue and the company can pass some cost through in its annual price increase, so API inflation is a nuisance rather than a threat. The exception is the NLEM basket, where ceiling prices move with the wholesale price index rather than the company's costs.

Manufacturing: own plants, contract makers and the asset-light MNCs

Most Indian franchises run a mix of their own plants and contract makers. Corona's main tablet plant runs above 90% utilisation, and it has just opened an EU-certified hormone plant. Sanofi Consumer Healthcare owns no plant at all; its products are made at Sanofi India's Goa site and in group plants. Abbott India is described as "largely third-party manufacturing", and Pfizer imports about half its portfolio as finished goods. Akums, the largest contract formulator, holds about 30% of India's domestic contract-manufacturing market, makes about 49 billion units a year and serves 26 of the top 30 Indian pharma companies, at a margin of 12–15%. That margin, against 30%+ for the brand owners it serves, is the clearest evidence of where value sits in the chain.

Working capital: light in India, heavy abroad

Stockists in India pay against short credit periods, so receivables for a domestic franchise run about 20–45 days of sales, and channel inventory 30–45 days. MNC subsidiaries often run near-zero or negative working capital. Emerging-market businesses are the opposite: distributors in Africa and Asia take 90–180 days, currencies devalue, and tenders pay late (indicative ranges). Ajanta's FY26 operating cash flow of ₹529 crore against EBITDA of ₹1,500 crore shows what an EM working-capital build can do to cash conversion in a single year.

In plain terms

In most industries the factory is the expensive part. In branded pharma the expensive part is the sales force and the promotion budget, and the factory is cheap enough to rent. That is why an Indian brand company with ₹100 of sales spends only about ₹28 on making the product and keeps ₹30 or more as operating profit, and why a company that makes medicines for others on contract keeps only ₹12–15.

Who holds the market: fragmented at the top, crowded at the tail

No company has more than 9% of the IPM. The top ten hold about half; thousands of small marketers share the rest.

India's branded market is unusually fragmented for an industry with such high margins. Sun Pharma, the largest, holds about 8.4% of the IPM. The top ten companies hold about 49% (PharmaTrac, August 2026, computed), and the top twenty perhaps 65–70% (indicative). Below them sit hundreds of mid-sized companies and thousands of small trade-generic and franchise marketers. The fragmentation persists because a doctor's habit is local and personal: a company that is number one in one city's cardiology can be invisible in the next state.

Five ways to run a brand business

The same asset (a doctor's habit) supports five different business models, with different growth, capital needs and risks.

All thirteen companies earn high returns because their real asset is the brand and the prescriber relationship rather than a plant. But they combine that asset with different therapy choices, geographies and ownership structures, and the result is five distinct archetypes. Knowing which archetype a company belongs to tells you what to expect from its growth, its margin, its balance sheet and its main risk before you open its results.

Companies are moving between archetypes

The archetypes are not fixed, and several companies are deliberately migrating. Mankind is moving from the mass-market model toward the chronic-specialist one: its chronic share has risen from about 36% to 41%, and Bharat Serums added specialist fertility and critical-care brands. Alkem is attempting the same shift from a 22% chronic base, more slowly. Ajanta and Ipca are drifting the other way, toward US generics, which raises growth but brings the commodity economics this sub-sector otherwise avoids. The MNC subsidiaries are shrinking toward fewer, larger brands as legacy products are handed to Indian partners. And Eris and Torrent show how an acquirer can step from one archetype to a larger version of itself through a single deal. When a company's archetype is changing, its historical margins and multiples are a poor guide to its future ones, which is why the transition itself is often where share prices move most.

In plain terms

A company that built its brands decades ago, like GSK or Abbott India, shows enormous returns because the brands cost almost nothing on its books. A company that bought its brands last year, like Torrent, shows ordinary returns because it paid full price for them. Both brands may earn the same profit per rupee of sales. The useful question is how fast each company's profit can grow from here and what it costs to make it grow.

The price-control map: NLEM, the 10% rule, GST and the trade-margin fight Deep dive

Eighteen per cent of the market follows a government formula. The other eighty-two per cent sets its own price, inside a 10% annual cap, and Parliament has started to ask questions.

Indian drug pricing has two tiers, and every company sits somewhere on the line between them. Medicines on the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM) are "scheduled": the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) fixes a ceiling price for them under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order 2013, and adjusts it once a year in line with the wholesale price index. Everything else is "non-scheduled": the company sets its own launch price and may raise the maximum retail price by up to 10% a year. According to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Chemicals and Fertilizers (33rd Report, tabled August 2026), scheduled medicines are about 18% of the market and non-scheduled about 82%. An older broker cut (March 2023) put NLEM exposure at 14% of IPM value and 23% of volume; the difference reflects date and method.

How a ceiling price is set

For a scheduled formulation, NPPA takes the simple average of the price to retailer of every brand with at least 1% market share, and adds a 16% retailer margin. Brands priced above the ceiling must cut to it; brands below it may not rise above it. The ceiling then moves every April by the change in the wholesale price index. A new combination or a new strength of a scheduled molecule counts as a "new drug", and NPPA fixes its retail price too, so launches built on NLEM molecules are price-controlled from day one. NLEM 2022 lists 384 medicines; a parliamentary reply in July 2026 cited 388 medicines in 29 categories, with ceiling prices in force for 935 scheduled formulations.

The annual revision has swung hard with inflation, and the swing matters more than the level. In April 2022 scheduled drugs were allowed a 10.76% increase and in April 2023 12.12%, because wholesale prices had surged. In April 2024 the allowed increase was about zero, in April 2025 1.74% and in April 2026 0.65%. Companies heavy in NLEM products enjoyed an unusual price tailwind in FY23–24 and then lost it entirely in FY25–27.

The 82%: freedom with a cap

Non-scheduled prices are the industry's profit engine. The law allows a 10% annual increase; companies actually took 5.6% a year on average between April 2020 and March 2025, according to the parliamentary committee. Restraint has reasons: doctors notice, competitors undercut, and very large increases draw NPPA's attention. But the launch price of a new non-scheduled drug is not regulated at all, which is how companies reset prices upward through new combinations and strengths. That is exactly the gap the committee wants closed: it asked for "a comprehensive review" of how new non-scheduled drugs get their initial prices. It also reported that between December 2022 and November 2025 NPPA released 1,426 draft price working sheets for comment, received about 30 representations, and changed only six prices as a result.

NLEM exposure is not always a penalty, and the arithmetic shows why. Take two companies, one with 22% of sales scheduled and one with 7%, both raising non-scheduled prices by the 5.6% average. In plain terms

For about a fifth of the medicines sold in India, the government decides the maximum price and lets it rise each April only as much as wholesale inflation. Lately that has been almost nothing. For the other four-fifths, companies choose their own price and nudge it up five or six per cent a year. A company whose biggest brands are on the government's list therefore grows more slowly in a low-inflation world, however well it sells.

Extraordinary powers and the trade-margin question

Paragraph 19 of the price order lets the government fix prices in "extraordinary circumstances" for any drug. It was used to cap coronary stent prices in 2017, knee implants afterwards, and in February 2019 to cap trade margins at 30% on 42 non-scheduled cancer drugs. A wider trade-margin rationalisation, which would cap how much the stockist and chemist may earn between the company's price and the MRP, has been floated repeatedly since 2015–18. It has not been enacted beyond the 2019 oncology pilot. If it were, trade generics, with their 50–80% channel margins, would be hit hardest, and branded prescription products, with conventional 25–30% channel margins, much less.

The GST cut: lower MRPs, unchanged realisations

At its 56th meeting on 3 September 2025, the GST Council cut the tax on most medicines from 12% to 5%, effective 22 September 2025; 33 life-saving drugs went from 12% to nil and three cancer and rare-disease drugs from 5% to nil. The arithmetic cuts the maximum retail price by about 6.25% (1.05 ÷ 1.12). NPPA ordered MRPs revised but did not require existing stock to be relabelled. Because GST is a pass-through, company realisations were unaffected; the change is mildly positive for affordability and volume. One open question for anyone reading market data: whether PharmaTrac's retail-value series includes GST. If it does, IPM value growth from September 2025 to September 2026 is understated by the base effect, and should step up afterwards. The vendor's treatment has not been verified.

How brands may be sold: the marketing code, the quality squeeze and banned combinations

Since 2016 regulation has removed whole categories of products and practices. Each round has helped the large, organised brand owners.

Price control decides what a brand may charge. A second set of rules decides how it may be promoted, where it may be made and which products may exist at all. Over the past decade those rules have tightened in four steps, and each step has shifted business from the unorganised tail toward the listed companies.

The marketing code, and how it is enforced

The Department of Pharmaceuticals notified UCPMP 2024 on 12 March 2024. Its main provisions are set out in section 4: no gifts, no paid travel or hospitality except for speakers, medical education only through recognised Indian institutions with spending disclosed, restricted samples and capped brand reminders. Enforcement runs through ethics committees at the industry associations (ECPMP), and companies file annual self-declarations hosted by the Indian Pharmaceutical Alliance. The first visible test case involved AbbVie: the department forwarded a complaint that it had spent ₹1.91 crore taking 30 doctors to Monaco and Paris. The association's committee found no irregularities, but a special audit committee found breaches. Commentators have called for the code to be given statutory force; as of October 2026 it remains voluntary in law, with growing practical teeth.

Revised Schedule M: the quality squeeze

Schedule M sets good manufacturing practice for Indian plants. The revised version, aligned with WHO and PIC/S standards, was notified on 28 December 2023. Large firms had to comply from January 2025; small and medium firms that applied for an extension had until 31 December 2025. Only about 1,600–1,700 MSMEs applied; the rest faced inspection at once, and CDSCO told state regulators to inspect and send monthly action reports (Business Standard, November 2025). India has roughly 10,500 drug manufacturing units, about 8,500 of them MSMEs. The upgrade costs are beyond many of them. The beneficiaries are organised contract makers such as Akums and brand owners with compliant plants of their own; the losers are the franchise marketers that relied on cheap third-party production.

Fixed-dose combinations: products banned outright

India's market was built partly on fixed-dose combinations, two or more molecules in one tablet or syrup, many of them never properly evaluated. In March 2016 the Health Ministry banned 344 FDCs. After litigation, the Supreme Court sent the question to the Drugs Technical Advisory Board in December 2017, and 328 were finally banned in September 2018, including cough and cold combinations such as Pfizer's Corex and Abbott's Phensedyl. In August 2024 a further 156 FDCs were banned, mainly antibiotic, painkiller and cold combinations. Each round removed brands that some companies depended on, and pushed prescribing toward simpler, better-evidenced products.

Quality shocks and the generic-prescribing threat

Contaminated cough syrups made by small Indian firms killed children in The Gambia and Uzbekistan in 2022, and Coldrif syrup was linked to more than 20 child deaths in Madhya Pradesh in October 2025. Each episode tightened testing for diethylene and ethylene glycol and strengthened the case for Schedule M. CDSCO also publishes monthly lists of samples found "not of standard quality"; big brands sometimes appear, usually disputing the sample as spurious. Reputational damage from a recall travels across a company's portfolio, as Sanofi Consumer Healthcare found when Combiflam and Allegra suspensions were recalled in 2024.

The latent policy risk to the whole model is generic-name prescribing. In August 2023 the National Medical Commission issued a regulation requiring doctors to prescribe by generic name; it was put in abeyance after protests from doctors. If a version were enforced, the prescription would no longer name the brand, and the decision would move to the chemist, whose incentive is the trade margin. The draft e-pharmacy rules of 2018 also remain unfinalised. Neither has moved, but neither has been withdrawn.

Strategic buyers pay six to nine times sales for Indian brands because their field forces can raise the brands' margins. The cost is debt, goodwill and a long payback.

Building a ₹100 crore brand from scratch takes years of detailing, launches and luck. Buying one takes a cheque. Over the past fifteen years acquisition has become the main route to scale for India's branded franchises, and since 2022 it has accelerated. Torrent's purchase of J. B. Mankind's purchase of Bharat Serums and Vaccines (enterprise value ₹13,630 crore) is the second-largest of the cycle. Eris has assembled a diabetes and injectables franchise through four deals in three years. The prices are high, and the logic behind them is the PCPM arithmetic from section 4.

Why a buyer can afford 7× sales

An acquired brand usually arrives under-promoted. It sits in a smaller field force with lower productivity, carries a separate head office, and has a portfolio gap that the buyer can fill. Margins rise, sometimes sharply. JB is the clearest current case. As a standalone listed company in FY26 it earned a 26.7% EBITDA margin (28.4% adjusted). In Q1 FY27, its first full quarter inside Torrent, the JB business earned 35.3% on revenue of ₹1,201 crore (up 10%), contributing ₹424 crore of EBITDA. Field attrition fell from about 30% before the deal to 16%. Torrent's synergy target of ₹90 crore is tracking above ₹100 crore.

In plain terms

Torrent paid about six and a half times JB's annual sales. That only makes sense if Torrent can sell JB's brands more efficiently than JB could on its own. The payback arithmetic

Even with synergies, brand acquisitions repay slowly. Take Curatio, which Torrent bought at an enterprise value of ₹1,885 crore. Assume revenue of ₹275 crore at a 40% contribution margin, or ₹110 crore of EBITDA (about 17 times), growing 15% a year. Cumulative pre-tax EBITDA repays the purchase price in about ten years. Lift the margin to 48% through synergies and payback shortens to eight or nine years (illustrative calculation; section 15 works it through year by year). JB's numbers look better on the run-rate: the equity value of ₹25,689 crore is about 23 times JB's FY26 EBITDA of ₹1,107 crore, but about 15 times its Q1 FY27 EBITDA annualised (₹424 crore × 4; calculated). The difference between those two multiples is the synergy the market is paying for.

Private equity as the brand aggregator

The deal flow follows a cycle. Private equity buys an under-managed franchise, often from a family or an MNC, professionalises its field force and portfolio, adds brands, and sells to a strategic buyer that pays the top multiple. KKR did this at JB between 2020 and 2025 before selling to Torrent at ₹1,600 a share. Advent did it at Bharat Serums before selling to Mankind. TA Associates did it at Curatio. ChrysCapital (Mankind, Corona) and Bain (Emcure) exited through listings instead. La Renon and other PE-backed franchises are the next candidates (indicative). The cycle means that the listed strategics are usually buying assets that have already been improved once, so the second round of synergies has to come from scale rather than from fixing an obvious problem.

The balance-sheet cost

Acquisitions change how the acquirers look. Reported return on capital falls because goodwill and intangibles sit in the denominator: Torrent's FY26 ROCE was 15.2%, Mankind's 13.5% and Eris's 14.1%, against 40%+ for the brands underneath (section 15 explains how to adjust). Leverage rises. Torrent funded its JB stake with about ₹14,000 crore of debt (₹12,500 crore of bonds plus commercial paper); its borrowings rose from ₹3,202 crore to ₹15,026 crore, net debt was about 2.1 times EBITDA in Q1 FY27, with a peak of about 2.5 times expected in FY27, and the target is below 0.5 times within about two and a half years. Eris is at about 2.0 times and targets below 1.5 times by December 2026. Mankind's net debt has fallen to ₹4,294 crore (1.3 times), with about ₹2,500 crore of bonds due for repayment in FY27. Emcure guides its net debt up to ₹1,450–1,560 crore in Q2 FY27 on acquisition payments, with net cash targeted by FY28. And the share count can grow: Torrent's promoter holding fell from 68.31% to 60.78% after shares were issued to JB's minority holders at 51 Torrent shares per 100 JB.

In plain terms

Buying brands gets a company bigger fast, but it is paid for with borrowed money and the brands take eight to ten years to earn back their price. In the meantime the company's reported returns look ordinary and its debt looks heavy. Investors are betting that the buyer can run the brands better than the seller did. The first quarters of evidence from Torrent and JB are encouraging; the debt repayment schedule over the next two years is the real test.

MNC subsidiaries: cash machines with a parent's hand on the tap

Abbott India, GSK, Pfizer and Sanofi Consumer own some of India's best-known brands, pay out most of their profit, and grow only as fast as their parents allow.

Four of the thirteen companies are listed Indian subsidiaries of multinational parents: Abbott India (Abbott Laboratories holds 74.99%), GSK Pharmaceuticals (GSK plc, 75.00%), Pfizer Limited (Pfizer Inc., 63.92%) and Sanofi Consumer Healthcare India (Opella, controlled by the buyout firm CD&R, 71.27%). They market brands built over decades: Thyronorm, Duphaston and Digene at Abbott; Augmentin, Calpol, Betnovate and Ceftum at GSK; Becosules, Gelusil and the Prevenar vaccine at Pfizer; Allegra and Combiflam at Sanofi Consumer. Manufacturing is largely outsourced or imported: Pfizer buys about half its portfolio as finished goods from the parent network, Sanofi Consumer owns no plant, and Abbott India relies largely on third-party makers.

Three things the minority shareholder does not control

Royalties and related-party flows. Each subsidiary pays trademark, technical or licence fees to its group and buys goods from group companies. None of the four discloses a headline royalty rate; the detail sits in annual-report related-party notes, and this report does not estimate it. The nearest thing to a number is Sanofi Consumer's shareholder-approved ceiling on related-party transactions of about ₹461 crore a year, which is a limit, not actual spend.

Portfolio routing. The parent decides whether a new molecule goes to the listed subsidiary, an unlisted Indian entity or an Indian partner. Abbott's nutrition, diagnostics and devices businesses, and Abbott Healthcare Pvt Ltd (the ex-Piramal brands), sit outside the listed company. Lilly co-markets tirzepatide with Cipla under the Yurpeak brand; Novo Nordisk gave Emcure a second Wegovy brand, Poviztra. None of the new obesity drugs runs through a listed MNC subsidiary.

Brand exits. Since about 2018 MNCs have shrunk, licensed out or handed over legacy brands to Indian companies with bigger field forces. The pattern is now well established.

The GLP-1 land-grab: India's first new blockbuster class in a decade Deep dive

An innovator brand became India's best-seller in eighteen months. Then 25 generic brands arrived, and the innovator cut its price by up to 48%.

GLP-1 drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide and their relatives) treat type 2 diabetes and obesity by mimicking a gut hormone that controls blood sugar and appetite. In India they went from nothing to a ₹2,333 crore class in about eighteen months. They also became the clearest live test of the branded model: what happens when a high-value chronic molecule goes generic, and which franchises can take share when it does.

How the class arrived

Eli Lilly launched Mounjaro (tirzepatide) in India in March 2025 and later added a co-marketing deal with Cipla (brand Yurpeak). Novo Nordisk sold Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus (semaglutide), and in 2025 gave Emcure a second Wegovy brand, Poviztra. Novo's core Indian semaglutide patent expired on 20 March 2026, and generic semaglutide launched the next day. Within a month, 25 competitors were selling it. On 1 April 2026 Novo cut the price of starting doses of Ozempic and Wegovy by 36% and 48% respectively, to ₹5,660, with average cuts across doses of about 24% and 27% (ThePrint, Business Standard and Reuters via search excerpts).

What GLP-1 does to the anti-diabetic franchises

The positive case is straightforward. GLP-1 has created a large, high-value category inside the anti-diabetic therapy area, which grew 15% in August 2026, and it draws new patients and specialists into treatment. Companies with chronic reach, supply security and speed can take share. The negative case is more subtle. The innovator's price cuts plus 25+ generics make semaglutide a price war, and generic semaglutide value is still small relative to the innovator brands. Companies that buy API or finished product dilute their gross margin: Eris's fell about 300 basis points in Q1 FY27 on insulin and GLP-1 mix. The class leader, tirzepatide, remains outside the generic fight. Oral GLP-1s (oral Wegovy, orforglipron) could change the device and price economics again. Over the longer term, effective weight-loss drugs may reduce demand for some anti-diabetic, blood-pressure and lipid medicines (indicative assessment).

In plain terms

The obesity and diabetes injections are the biggest new thing in Indian medicine in years, and the number one brand in the country is now an American company's injection. When one of these molecules lost its patent in March, more than two dozen Indian companies launched copies within a month, and the original maker slashed its price. Torrent grabbed the largest slice; Eris went cheap to win volume. The prize so far is a few hundred crore a year, much smaller than the headlines suggest, but it shows which franchises can move quickly when a big chronic molecule opens up.

Branded abroad: the emerging-markets half and its currency bill

The same playbook (doctors, reps, brands) works in Africa, Asia and Brazil. It comes with devaluations, tenders and distributors who pay late.

Several of the thirteen run India's branded model in other countries. The risks to stress

  • Currency translation: a devaluation cuts rupee revenue overnight and can produce forex losses on receivables (Ajanta's ₹103 crore in FY26).
  • Distributor concentration and channel inventory: one distributor's destocking can wipe out a quarter's growth (Torrent Brazil, Q1 FY27).
  • Tenders and donor funding: institutional anti-malarial and HIV volumes depend on Global Fund and US budgets (Ipca's 24–33% fall).
  • Registration lag and sanctions: dossiers take one to three years per country; Russia carries sanctions risk.
  • Drift into US generics: Ajanta, Alkem and Ipca have all grown their US businesses faster than their branded ones, which raises growth but brings FDA and price-erosion risk the branded model otherwise avoids.
In plain terms

Selling branded medicines in Nigeria or the Philippines works much like selling them in India: you persuade doctors and pharmacists to trust your name. But you are paid in a currency that can lose a third of its value in a year, by distributors who may take six months to pay, and sometimes by aid agencies whose budgets get cut. So the same brand earns a lower and less reliable margin abroad, and investors value it accordingly.

Five decades, six shocks: how price control and patents shaped the industry

From a market owned by multinationals to one owned by Indian franchises, and from detailing with gifts to detailing with science.

Today's structure is the product of policy. In 1970 multinationals held about 70% of the Indian market. The Patents Act of that year recognised only process patents for medicines, so Indian companies could legally copy any molecule by a different route, and successive price-control orders (1970, 1979, 1987, 1995) kept prices low enough that MNCs had little incentive to invest. By the 2000s Indian companies held 75–80% of the market. The rest of the history is a series of shocks, each of which redistributed share and profit.

  • 2005–2012: product patents and the MNC return. India adopted product patents from 1 January 2005, with Section 3(d) blocking minor "evergreening" changes. MNCs came back by buying Indian franchises: Daiichi bought Ranbaxy in 2008, and Abbott bought Piramal's domestic business in 2010 for $3.72 billion, about nine times sales, the deal that set the template for India-franchise valuations. NLEM 2011 and the 2012 pricing policy moved ceiling prices to a market-based formula.
  • 2013–2016: DPCO 2013 and the NLEM shock. The 2013 price order put scheduled drugs under market-average ceilings, and the 10% annual cap on non-scheduled drugs took effect. In March 2016, 344 FDCs were banned, hitting cough and cold brands such as Corex and Phensedyl.
  • 2017–2019: GST and channel disruption. GST arrived at 12% on most medicines in July 2017, months after demonetisation, and both disrupted trade-generic channels and stockist inventories. Stent prices were capped in 2017; trade margins on 42 cancer drugs in 2019. Torrent bought Unichem's India business (₹3,600 crore).
  • 2020–2022: COVID's boom and bust in acute. Anti-infectives, vitamins and fever medicines boomed (Dolo-650 became a household name), then normalised against high bases. NLEM 2022 added 34 medicines and dropped 26, and WPI-linked hikes of 10.8% and 12.1% followed in April 2022 and 2023. Sitagliptin's patent expiry in 2022 started the first big chronic price war.
  • 2023–2024: listings and the rule book. Mankind listed in May 2023, Emcure in July 2024, Sanofi Consumer after its 2024 demerger. The generic-prescribing regulation came and was suspended (2023); UCPMP 2024 and revised Schedule M arrived; 156 FDCs were banned; Eris bought Biocon's India brands and Mankind bought Bharat Serums.
  • 2025–2026: consolidation and GLP-1. Mounjaro launched; Torrent agreed to buy JB; GST fell to 5%; Corona listed; Pfizer handed brands to Cipla; Ajanta licensed semaglutide for emerging markets; generic semaglutide launched; the April 2026 NLEM revision was 0.65%; JB merged into Torrent; and a parliamentary committee questioned launch pricing.

In plain terms

The question for the next decade is whether that drift continues or whether price regulation finally catches up.

The equation, worked in rupees

Take a mid-sized chronic franchise with 5,000 representatives at a PCPM of ₹8 lakh. Its India revenue is 5,000 × ₹8 lakh × 12 = ₹4,800 crore a year. At a 72% gross margin it keeps ₹3,456 crore after materials. Its representatives cost about ₹8.5 lakh each fully loaded, or ₹425 crore (8.9% of sales). Managers, head-office marketing, medical education, samples, distribution and other selling costs take about 24% of sales (₹1,152 crore), and other employees and overheads about 6% (₹288 crore). EBITDA is about ₹1,591 crore, a 33% margin. All of this is illustrative, built on industry conventions rather than any one company.

Next year's price growth depends on the NLEM mix. If 12% of sales is scheduled and gets the April 2026 revision of 0.65%, and the other 88% rises by the 5.6% average, blended price growth is about 5.0%. Add 3 points of volume and share gain and 3.5 points from new launches (close to the IPM's 3.6), and revenue grows about 11.5%, to ₹5,352 crore. Now hold headcount flat and raise PCPM from ₹8 lakh to ₹10 lakh: revenue rises by ₹1,200 crore; at a 72% gross margin, less about 10% of the increment in extra promotion, EBITDA rises by roughly ₹744 crore and the margin moves from 33% to about 39%. That is the core of the Torrent model, and the reason PCPM is the KPI analysts watch most closely.

Adding people works differently. Suppose the company hires 500 more representatives (a 10% expansion). Their salaries cost ₹42.5 crore a year, and samples, launch promotion and new first-line managers perhaps another ₹20 crore. Much of the revenue a new representative "holds" is carved out of existing territories, so the incremental revenue starts small: say ₹1 lakh a month each in year one (₹60 crore, or ₹43 crore of gross profit, a loss of about ₹19 crore), ₹2 lakh in year two (₹120 crore, roughly ₹20 crore of contribution) and ₹3.5 lakh by year three (₹210 crore, about ₹80 crore of contribution). The figures are illustrative, but the shape is real: field-force expansions depress margins for four to six quarters before they pay, which is why companies announce them alongside cautious margin guidance.

The equation reconciles with real companies. Torrent and JB together have 9,400 representatives at "just over ₹10 lakh": 9,400 × ₹10 lakh × 12 ≈ ₹11,300 crore a year. Torrent's Q1 FY27 India base business was ₹2,157 crore and JB's domestic business roughly ₹700 crore a quarter, together about ₹11,400 crore annualised (calculated). Mankind's 16,500 representatives at about ₹5.7 lakh give ₹11,300 crore, close to its ₹11,338 crore of prescription-domestic revenue.

In plain terms

Count the sales representatives, work out how much each one sells a month, and multiply by twelve: that is roughly the company's Indian revenue. Most of the strategic choices in this industry are about one of those two numbers. Hiring more representatives raises revenue slowly and costs money upfront. Making each representative more productive, through better brands, better doctors and lower turnover, raises revenue and margin at the same time.

MR attrition

What it is. Annual exits ÷ average headcount. Example: a 10,000-strong field force losing 2,000 people a year has 20% attrition. Drivers: pay and incentive design, the quality of first-line managers, the strength of the portfolio a representative carries (it is easier to sell winning brands), and integration events. Benchmarks: below 15% is good (JB under Torrent reached 16% by June 2026, from about 30%); 15–25% is average (Alkem 18–19%); above 30% is weak, though older industry surveys put the field norm at 30–40%. Why it matters: every departure resets a doctor relationship and a new hire takes two to three years to reach full PCPM. Trap: few companies disclose it regularly, and definitions vary (voluntary exits only, or all exits; MRs only, or all field staff).

New-launch contribution

What it is. Revenue from products launched in the past two or three years as a share of sales, or the points of growth they add. Example: a company with ₹5,000 crore of India sales that grows 12%, of which ₹200 crore comes from launches of the past year, gets 4 points of growth from launches. Drivers: speed to patent expiries, a pipeline of combinations and strengths, in-licensing. Benchmarks: above 8–10% of sales or 4+ points of growth is strong; 4–8% or 2–4 points is average; below 3% or under 2 points is weak. The IPM average is 3.5–3.6 points (2026). GSK's "freshness index" targets 10–15% of sales from new products near term and 20–25% in five to seven years; more than half of Ajanta's 350+ launches were first-to-market. Trap: few companies report it, and "new" may mean a new molecule, a new strength of an old one or a new pack. A franchise that grows only through launches of new strengths of existing brands is extending, not renewing, its portfolio.

Definition traps, collected

  • Two vendors. PharmaTrac and IQVIA differ by 5–10% in market size and 0.5–1 point in share (Mankind 5.6% vs 4.7%). Never mix them in one comparison.
  • Retail value versus company revenue. IPM and brand figures are at retail value; company revenue is at stockist prices about 30% lower. Brand share of company revenue and company share of IPM both need adjusting.
  • Primary versus secondary. Reported revenue includes channel inventory moves; secondary sales do not. Torrent Brazil: +3% reported, +19% in-market.
  • Chronic share. With or without sub-chronic; with or without acquisitions. Compare like with like (IPM 35% chronic, 56% with sub-chronic).
  • PCPM. Company or secondary revenue; MRs with or without managers; whether trade generics and consumer products are in the numerator. Most published company figures are calculations.
  • MR count. Mankind 16,500+ (deck) vs 18,500+ (primer): the difference is probably managers.
  • India growth. Organic or including in-licensed and acquired brands (Emcure 10.2% headline, 6–7% organic).
  • Gross margin. Company definitions differ; "100 minus material cost" proxies are not comparable, especially for MNCs buying finished goods.
  • EBITDA. Reported or adjusted (forex, ESOPs, deal costs); with or without other income.
  • ROCE. With or without acquisition goodwill and idle cash.
  • Net debt. Company figures versus data-service calculations that treat strategic stakes as cash or miss bank deposits (Alkem).
  • Profit. One-offs: Eris's FY26 PAT of ₹648 crore includes a ~₹150 crore deferred-tax gain (underlying ~₹500 crore); Torrent's Q4 FY26 carried JB-related exceptional items.
  • Year-ends and bases. Sanofi Consumer reports calendar years; Abbott India and Pfizer are standalone; Torrent's FY26 contains about two months of JB.

How to read a branded pharma company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. India growth against the IPM (minute 1). Find the domestic growth figure and the market growth management compares it with. Note the vendor and whether it is secondary or primary. Red flag: a company that stops disclosing the comparison after a weak quarter.
  2. Growth split by therapy, chronic versus acute (minute 2). Chronic should grow faster than acute. Red flag: chronic growing below the IPM, which suggests share loss in the business that matters most.
  3. Organic versus acquired (minute 3). Strip out acquired and in-licensed brands. Red flag: headline growth well above organic for several quarters (Emcure's 10% vs 6–7%).
  4. Price, volume and launches (minute 4). If management splits growth, check that volume is positive. Red flag: growth that is all price in a year when the NLEM revision was near zero, which implies aggressive non-scheduled hikes the government may notice.
  5. Gross margin (minute 5). Compare with the same quarter last year. Red flag: a fall of 200 basis points or more without a clear mix explanation (Eris's −300 bps was explained by insulin and GLP-1).
  6. EBITDA margin and its drivers (minute 6). Look for field-force additions, launch spending and one-offs. Red flag: margin up while India growth slows, which can mean promotion was cut to protect the quarter.
  7. Field-force data (minute 7). MR count, PCPM, attrition where disclosed. Red flag: rising attrition, or a headcount jump with no margin guidance.
  8. Exports and currency (minute 8). For EM-heavy companies, check constant-currency growth and forex losses. Red flag: in-market growth far above reported growth for more than two quarters (persistent destocking), or rising receivable days.
  9. Balance sheet (minute 9). Net debt and its trend against stated targets; for MNCs, cash and dividends. Red flag: an acquirer's leverage rising when it should be falling.
  10. Policy and pipeline notes (minute 10). NLEM additions affecting the portfolio, FDC bans, UCPMP issues, recalls, patent-expiry launches (GLP-1), parent decisions for MNCs. Red flag: a "regulatory" one-off that recurs.
In plain terms

The quickest test of a branded pharma quarter is three questions. Did the company grow faster than the Indian market, and was that growth its own rather than bought? Did it keep its gross margin while doing it? And is its debt (or, for an MNC, its cash and dividend) heading where management said it would? If all three answers are yes, the rest of the detail usually confirms the picture.

How to read the table

Start with the combined row. Twelve companies with ₹85,907 crore of revenue, a 25.9% blended EBITDA margin and ₹13,991 crore of profit are valued at ₹6.33 lakh crore, about 45 times earnings and 28 times EBITDA. Revenue grew 14% in FY26, flattered by Torrent's acquisition of JB and Mankind's first full year of Bharat Serums; organic growth for the group was closer to 10–12% (indicative).

Then read across the growth and margin columns together. Torrent is the only company that combines scale, a 32.6% margin, a chronic-heavy portfolio and well-above-market growth, which is why it is the most valuable company in the group by a wide margin and trades at 42.7 times EBITDA. Corona has the growth (Q1 FY27 +21.9%) and the chronic mix but not yet the margin or scale; the market prices it at the same EV/EBITDA as Torrent in anticipation. Eris has Torrent's margin but trades at 16.7 times EBITDA because of its leverage, its recent de-rating and investor doubts about GLP-1 and insulin margins. The four MNCs have the highest margins and returns but mostly the slowest growth, and their multiples span Pfizer's 16.1 times to GSK's 33.2 times, roughly in proportion to the credibility of their growth plans.

The export-heavy companies (Ipca, Ajanta, Emcure, and to a degree Alkem) need a second look. Their consolidated margins are lower because US or European generics, API and contract manufacturing dilute the branded franchise, and their India share of revenue is 30–67%. Eris's P/E of 25 times becomes about 33 times on underlying profit; Torrent's 81 times is inflated by JB-related one-offs (its primer frames forward earnings in the low 40s); and every net-debt figure depends on whether the source subtracts investments.

Valuation and the global lens: why 16–43× EBITDA

Indian branded franchises trade at two to four times the multiples of branded-generic peers elsewhere. The premium rests on a few specific features, each of which can erode.

On 5 October 2026 the twelve listed companies traded at between 16.1 times EBITDA (Pfizer) and 42.7 times (Torrent), with a median of about 27.5 times, and at between 22.9 and 81.4 times trailing earnings. Those are consumer-staples multiples, not pharmaceutical ones. Branded-generic and emerging-market pharma companies elsewhere trade far lower: Hikma (Middle East and North Africa branded plus US generics) at roughly 9–10 times EBITDA, Brazil's Hypera at 8–10 times, South Africa's Aspen and Adcock Ingram at 6–9 times (Natco owns about 49% of Adcock). Why the premium exists

  • A growing market with real pricing power. The IPM grows about 10% a year in nominal terms, with 5–6 points from price on the 82% of products outside direct control.
  • A brand moat without a payer. The doctor chooses the brand and the patient pays; there is no insurer, formulary or pharmacy-benefit manager to squeeze price.
  • High returns, low capital needs. Factories can be rented; MNC subsidiaries earn 45–90% on capital and pay most of it out.
  • Domestic-investor scarcity. Indian mutual funds and pension money must own healthcare, and few large, clean, domestic franchises are listed, especially after JB was taken private by merger.
  • No US exposure. Pure-India franchises carry no FDA inspection risk, no US price erosion and no exposure to the 2026 US tariff on patented drugs.

Scale explains some of the gap with global peers in the other direction. The entire Indian Pharma Market, about ₹2.6 lakh crore, is smaller than the annual sales of a single global pharma major, and Sun's India business (₹19,290 crore in FY26), the country's largest, would be a mid-sized division elsewhere. The listed Indian subsidiaries also trade well above their own parents: Abbott India at 34.5 times earnings against roughly 25 times for Abbott Laboratories, GSK India at 43.9 times against roughly 10 times for GSK plc, Pfizer India at 22.9 times against roughly 8–10 times for Pfizer Inc. (parent multiples approximate). Thin float, net cash and the Indian growth premium explain the gap.

What the multiple assumes

A simple test shows how much growth today's prices assume. At the group's combined 45 times earnings, the earnings yield is about 2.2%. If profits grow 13% a year for ten years they rise 3.4-fold, and today's price would be about 13 times year-ten earnings, a normal multiple for a mature, high-quality franchise. If profits grow 8% a year, they rise 2.2-fold, and today's price would still be about 21 times year-ten earnings (illustrative arithmetic, before dividends). In other words, the current valuation assumes that the best franchises keep compounding well above the market for a decade. For Torrent, at 81 times trailing (low 40s on forward earnings per its primer) and with debt to repay, and for Corona at 59 times, that assumption carries most of the weight; for Pfizer and Eris at 16–17 times EBITDA, much less is assumed.

Technology and disruption: what could loosen the doctor's grip

The branded model depends on the doctor naming the brand and the chemist dispensing it. Most disruption risks attack one of those two links.

The business has been remarkably stable for decades, and the main threats to it are not technologies in the usual sense but shifts in who decides which pack a patient takes home. Eight risks are worth tracking. Some are slow, some are policy switches that could flip at short notice.

  1. Policy against the brand itself. Mandatory generic-name prescribing (the NMC's August 2023 rule, in abeyance), a trade-margin cap, NLEM expansion into chronic staples, and the parliamentary push (August 2026) to review how non-scheduled launch prices are set. Any one would move value from brand owners to chemists, payers or patients.
  2. Channel disintermediation. Organised chains (Apollo, MedPlus, Wellness Forever) and e-pharmacies (Tata 1mg, PharmEasy, Netmeds) push private labels and trade generics, demand better margins and substitute at the counter; quick-commerce medicine delivery adds pressure. Their share is still small: a 2021 estimate put chains at about 8.5% and online at about 2.8%, with unorganised chemists at about 88.7% (Ken Research, lower credibility; indicative). E-pharmacy was about $3.5 billion in 2025 on the same source. The e-pharmacy rules remain in draft.
  3. Jan Aushadhi. More than 20,000 government generic outlets, targeted at 25,000 by 2027; small in sales but a constant public price comparison.
  4. Doctor consolidation into hospital chains. As more doctors work in corporate hospitals, prescribing shifts toward formularies, tenders and in-house pharmacies, where the representative's call matters less. No sourced data exist on the hospital channel's share of the IPM (perhaps 15–20%, estimate). Specialist-heavy and injectable franchises (Corona, Eris, Mankind's BSV) are partial hedges.
  5. Digital and AI-assisted detailing. Remote e-detailing, CRM analytics and AI-assisted targeting became more valuable after the marketing code limited old-style promotion. They could raise PCPM for leaders and widen the gap with laggards (indicative assessment).
  6. Patent-cliff price wars in chronic categories. Gliptins (2022), gliflozins (2025) and semaglutide (2026) each drew dozens of generic brands within weeks. Every big chronic expiry is both a launch opportunity and a threat to incumbent brands.
  7. GLP-1 cannibalisation. Effective weight-loss drugs may, over years, reduce demand for some diabetes, blood-pressure and lipid medicines; oral GLP-1s would change the device and price economics (long-term, indicative).
  8. Quality shocks and parent decisions. Recalls and "not of standard quality" alerts can damage a whole brand family (Sanofi Consumer, 2024); MNC parents can hand brands away (Pfizer to Cipla) or route new molecules elsewhere.

Bull vs bear

The case for a decade of compounding, and the case that price regulation and valuation finally catch up.

Both cases start from the same facts: a market growing about 10% a year on price and launches, a fragmented field where the best franchises keep taking share, a government that has so far left 82% of prices alone, and valuations that already assume much of this continues. They differ on whether the rules and the multiples hold.

The bull case

  • Structural chronic demand. About 101 million diabetics and 315 million hypertensives, with chronic and sub-chronic already 56% of the IPM and growing faster than acute.
  • Durable pricing. 82% of the market sets its own price, and actual increases (5.6% a year) sit well inside the 10% cap; the NLEM revision has been near zero but cannot go negative on WPI alone.
  • Consolidation runway. The top ten hold about 49%; UCPMP and Schedule M are squeezing the unorganised tail; PE keeps preparing franchises for strategic buyers.
  • Proven M&A playbook. JB's margin rose from 26.7% to 35.3% inside Torrent in one quarter; Eris tripled its insulin share after the Biocon deal.
  • New categories. GLP-1, adult vaccines, injectables and critical care add high-value demand; MNC brand handoffs give Indian companies cheap access to established brands.
  • No US risk. Pure-India franchises avoid FDA inspections, US price erosion and US tariffs.

The bear case

  • Valuation. 45 times combined earnings, 40–80 times for chronic leaders, with little allowance for a slowdown or a policy shock.
  • Price-control creep. The August 2026 parliamentary review of launch pricing, a ten-year-old trade-margin proposal, the next NLEM revision and the shelved generic-prescribing rule all point the same way.
  • Weak volume. Market volume grows only 1–2% a year; growth that depends on price is exactly what regulators target.
  • Leverage at acquirers. Torrent (~2.1×, peaking ~2.5×), Eris (~2.0×) and Mankind (1.3×) must repay debt while integrating brands.
  • GLP-1 economics. 25+ generics and innovator price cuts of up to 48% make semaglutide a price war; the class leader, tirzepatide, stays with Lilly.
  • MNC drift and EM risk. The subsidiaries keep losing share; Ajanta, Ipca and Emcure carry currency, donor and US-generic risk.

What would settle the argument? On the bull side, three things: the next two years of IPM growth staying at 10% or more with positive volume; Torrent and Eris cutting leverage on schedule while JB's margin holds near 35%; and no formal change to non-scheduled launch pricing after the parliamentary report. On the bear side, any one of three would be enough to compress multiples: a notified trade-margin cap, a revived generic-prescribing rule, or a broad NLEM revision that adds chronic staples. A quieter bear signal would be a run of quarters in which the leaders' growth premium over the IPM narrows, since that premium is what the valuations pay for.

How to use the indicators

No single month or quarter settles anything in this sub-sector, because monthly market data are noisy (the acute season, the timing of price increases and the GST base effect all move them) and quarterly company figures include channel inventory. The useful discipline is to look for three consecutive readings in the same direction before changing a view, and to check whether market data and company data agree. If PharmaTrac shows a company gaining share while its reported India growth slows, the gap is probably inventory and should close. If both slow together, the franchise is losing ground. On policy, the signal is the document, not the headline: a committee report or a consultation paper is a warning, a draft notification is a risk, and a gazette notification is a change to the numbers.

In plain terms

Watch three things each month: whether the Indian market is still growing about 10% with at least some volume, whether the leading companies are still growing faster than it, and whether the government has moved on prices. Each quarter, check whether Torrent and Eris are paying down debt as promised and whether JB's brands are holding their new margins. Those few numbers will tell you more than any forecast.
