00

The one-page view: owners up, builders down

India's roads are being built with public money and then sold to private owners. The money in this industry has moved from pouring concrete to collecting tolls.

Ten years ago an investor in Indian roads bought a construction company and hoped it won contracts. Today the money sits somewhere else. In FY26 Indian drivers and truckers paid ₹70,278 crore in tolls on national highways and expressways, more than twice the ₹33,929 crore of FY22. That stream grows every year because traffic grows and because tariffs rise by formula every 1 April. Meanwhile the award of new road contracts has fallen for three years in a row: the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH) awarded about 7,000 km in FY26 against 12,731 km in FY22, and the National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) awarded only 107 km in the June 2026 quarter. Builders are short of work. Owners of finished roads have never had more cash coming in.

This report is about that split. Its subject is the whole Indian road-asset industry: the government agencies that plan and pay for highways (MoRTH and NHAI); the four ways a road is procured (engineering-procurement-construction or EPC, the hybrid annuity model or HAM, build-operate-transfer or BOT, and toll-operate-transfer or TOT); the tolling system (FASTag, the new annual pass, barrier-free tolling); the government's programme of selling toll rights to finished roads; and the infrastructure investment trusts (InvITs) that buy them. One company in the NSE 750 is a pure road-asset owner: IRB Infrastructure Developers. It is the lens, not the whole picture. Where a comparison helps, the report uses a clearly marked reference set: six EPC and HAM builders (Ashoka, PNC, KNR, H.G. Infra, G R Infra and Dilip Buildcon, which are members of the civil construction report), the listed road InvITs, and four global toll companies.

Five forces shape the industry, and the report is built around them.

  • The toll pool compounds, and inflation indexation is turning from headwind to tailwind. Tolls on post-2008 concessions rise each April by 3% plus 40% of the change in the wholesale price index (WPI). With WPI near zero in December 2025, the April 2026 revision was only about 2.5% after rounding. With WPI at 11.39% in September 2026, the April 2027 revision could be roughly 6.6–7.8% (indicative), the largest since FY23.
  • The award cycle is broken for builders but irrelevant, even helpful, for owners. Fewer new contracts hurt contractors' order books. They do not touch the toll on roads already open, and they push NHAI to raise money by selling finished roads instead.
  • Asset monetisation and InvIT capital recycling are the business model. NHAI and MoRTH have raised ₹1,71,065 crore by selling toll rights (TOT), selling roads to InvITs and securitising project cash flows. That money cut NHAI's debt from ₹3.48 lakh crore (FY22) to ₹2.40 lakh crore (Nov-2025). IRB bids for roads at a 15–16% equity return, runs them for three or four years, and sells them to its own listed trust at 12–12.5%.
  • Interest rates are the valuation lever. A toll road is a long, leveraged, inflation-linked cash stream. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, and the 10-year government bond yields 7.2–7.4%. Road InvITs yield 8.5–10.7%, but roughly 38% of a typical distribution is return of capital, not income.
  • Policy is the tail risk. One NHAI circular in September 2025 cut every base toll by 3–5%; it was reversed in July 2026. The annual pass, barrier-free tolling, satellite-based tolling and plaza-spacing rules can each move revenue on thousands of kilometres overnight.

The margin ladder tells the same story from a different side. A toll road that is open and stabilised converts about 85–91% of its revenue into EBITDA, because the road is already built and the main running costs are toll staff, patrolling and periodic resurfacing. A construction contractor keeps 15–21%. IRB sits between the two because it is both: its toll segment earned 91% in Q1 FY27, its InvIT segment 94% and its construction segment 15%, for a consolidated 52.4% in FY26 (screener basis). The investor's question in this industry is which part of the ladder a company stands on, and for how many more years.

The bad news is just as concentrated. IRB's market value was ₹19,615 crore on 23 October 2026, with the share at ₹16.2, its 52-week low, and below book value (0.94×). The six reference builders traded at 0.66× their combined book. The listed road InvITs, by contrast, traded at about 1.6× book and 13.7× EV to operating profit. The market is paying for finished roads in trusts and refusing to pay for the companies that build them or recycle them. Three reasons recur in this report: interest rates went up in October 2026; IRB's largest single asset, the Mumbai–Pune Expressway, stops paying in April 2030; and much of IRB's income comes from trusts it controls, which raises governance questions that the market prices in.

In plain terms

India now builds most new highways with taxpayers' money and then sells the right to collect tolls on them to private investors, who pay upfront. The people who own those toll rights are doing very well, because traffic and tariffs keep rising. The people who build roads are struggling, because the government is awarding far fewer new contracts. IRB is a toll owner with a construction arm attached, and its shares are cheap because investors worry about interest rates, its biggest road's 2030 end date and the deals it does with its own trusts.

01

How a road earns money: four contracts, one asphalt strip

The same 100 km of highway can be an EPC contract, a hybrid annuity, a BOT toll concession or a TOT bundle. Who carries the traffic risk decides who earns what.

A highway does not sell a product in the usual sense. It sells a right: the right to charge vehicles for using it, or the right to be paid by the government for having built and maintained it. Everything in this industry follows from who holds that right, for how long, and who carries the risk that fewer vehicles turn up than forecast. India uses four main contract types, and they distribute risk very differently.

EPC (engineering, procurement, construction) is the simplest. NHAI pays 100% of the cost; the contractor builds the road for a fixed price and guarantees it for a defect-liability period of four to ten years. There is no asset, only a contract. EPC has been NHAI's dominant mode in recent years, at about 65–70% of awards (ICRA). HAM (hybrid annuity), introduced in 2016, is a half-way house. NHAI pays 40% of the bid project cost during construction, adjusted by a price index, in five to ten instalments. The developer funds the other 60% with debt and equity and is repaid over 15 years in 30 half-yearly instalments, plus interest on the reducing balance at the bank rate or the average one-year MCLR of the top five banks plus 1.25–3%, plus inflation-indexed maintenance payments. NHAI collects the toll. The developer carries construction and interest-rate risk but no traffic risk.

BOT-toll (build, operate, transfer) hands the developer everything: it builds, finances, operates and collects tolls for a concession of typically 20–30 years, then returns the road to NHAI. It carries construction, financing and traffic risk. India's BOT boom of 2005–2012 ended in a bust of terminated projects and is only now being cautiously revived. TOT (toll, operate, transfer) is BOT without the building. NHAI or a state agency auctions the right to toll an already-built, publicly funded road for 15–30 years in exchange for a single upfront payment. The buyer carries traffic risk on a road with a known traffic history, plus maintenance. Newer TOT bundles also include mandated initial works.

Value is created at three points: when the bid sets the return, when a road is de-risked from construction to steady tolling (financing costs fall 200–300 bp, per IRB), and as traffic and tariffs compound over the concession. The developer captures the second point when it sells.

Where is value captured? The bid sets the return at the start: IRB targets a 15–16% equity return on TOT bids, and HAM equity returns typically sit around 12–16% depending on how aggressively the developer bid. The second jump comes from de-risking. A road under construction is a risky loan; a road with three years of toll history is close to an inflation-linked bond, and lenders and buyers price it accordingly. IRB says its cost of debt and equity falls by roughly 200–300 basis points between construction and stabilisation. That spread is what the developer pockets when it sells a road to an InvIT that accepts 12–12.5%. The third source is time: traffic growth plus the April tariff revision plus scheduled debt repayment. IRB says a 25-year concession keeps gaining value until about year 18 or 19.

The accounting follows the risk, and it confuses many first-time readers. A BOT or TOT concession is booked as an intangible asset, the right to charge users, and amortised over the concession, usually in proportion to expected toll. During construction, the concessionaire books "construction revenue" equal to its cost plus a margin, against that intangible, so a developer's revenue in build years is inflated by what is really capital spending. A HAM project is booked as a financial receivable that earns interest. That is why an InvIT can earn ₹3,117 crore of EBITDA and only ₹217 crore of profit (Cube Highways, FY26): amortisation of the upfront fee, not weak traffic, eats the profit line. The cash still flows.

In plain terms

With EPC the government buys a road from a builder. With HAM the government pays for a road over 15 years, so the builder becomes a lender for that time. With BOT a private company builds the road itself and earns tolls for decades. With TOT the government sells the tolls on a road it already built, for cash today. An InvIT is a trust that buys finished roads and pays most of the toll income out to its investors.

02

The toll pool compounds: ₹17,942 crore to ₹70,278 crore in nine years

India's highway toll has grown about 16% a year since FY17. FASTag, new plazas and traffic did the work; from April 2027 inflation should add more.

The prize is large and growing. National highway and expressway toll receipts reached ₹70,278 crore in FY26, made up of ₹68,483 crore of user fees and ₹1,795 crore from the new FASTag annual pass, according to a Rajya Sabha reply by the road transport minister. That compares with ₹61,408 crore in FY25, ₹33,929 crore in FY22 and ₹17,942 crore at NHAI fee plazas in FY17. The FY17–FY26 compound growth rate is about 16.4% a year; FY22–FY26 is about 20%. The number of fee plazas rose from 733 to 1,209 over FY22–FY26. At ₹94.9 to the dollar the FY26 pool is roughly US$7.4 billion, about three times Transurban's entire revenue.

Three things drove that growth, and only one of them repeats forever. The first was leakage control. FASTag, the RFID tag on the windscreen, became mandatory on 15–16 February 2021. Cash lanes had leaked revenue through exemptions, misclassified vehicles and plain theft; electronic collection closed most of that gap, and toll jumped 41.6% in FY23. Today more than 98% of national highway toll is collected by FASTag, and about 97% across IRB's roads, so that one-off is spent. The second was new roads: plazas opened on the expressways and four-lane upgrades completed under Bharatmala, which raised the network's tolled length. That continues, though more slowly as construction slows. The third is traffic times tariff, the compounding engine: more vehicles each year multiplied by a tariff that rises by formula each April. That one repeats.

Where is the money collected? Four states dominate. In FY26 Uttar Pradesh collected ₹8,862 crore, Rajasthan ₹7,317 crore, Maharashtra ₹7,053 crore and Gujarat ₹6,352 crore, together about 42% of the national pool. They are India's freight corridors: the Delhi–Mumbai and Delhi–Kolkata spines, the western ports' hinterland and the Rajasthan crossing that links them. IRB's portfolio overlaps them closely. By lane-kilometre its largest states are Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. That is not an accident: a toll owner wants the corridors where trucks have no alternative.

What next? ICRA expects national toll growth of 7–9% in FY27, made up of 4.5–5.5% traffic growth and a 3.4–4% rate effect, slowing from about 10% in FY26 because the April 2026 tariff revision was small. For FY28 it expects 10–12%, because the April 2027 revision should jump to 6.2–6.4% for newer concessions (linked to December WPI) and 4.5–5.5% for older ones (linked to March WPI). ICRA's view assumed December 2026 WPI of 8–8.5%. Wholesale inflation has since run hotter: 9.92% in August and 11.39% in September 2026, with fuel and power up 25.9% after the West Asia shock. If December WPI lands at 9–12%, the formula gives a revision of roughly 6.6–7.8% before rounding (indicative), and FY28 toll growth could reach 12–14% (indicative) rather than 10–12%.

Why does inflation help a toll owner more than it hurts? On the revenue side, every percentage point of WPI adds about 0.4 point to the tariff of a post-2008 concession. On the cost side, a toll road's cash costs (toll staff, patrolling, routine maintenance) are only 7–15% of revenue, and resurfacing, the main bitumen-linked cost, comes every five to seven years. So a WPI spike lifts revenue on almost the whole revenue base and costs on a small slice. The losers from the same spike are fixed-price EPC contractors, whose bitumen, steel and diesel bills rise immediately. The formula also cushions deflation: because of the fixed 3%, WPI can fall by up to 7.5% before the revision turns negative.

Two qualifications. First, the toll pool and the investable pool are not the same. About half of national highway toll, ₹31,810 crore in FY25 or roughly 52%, is still collected at publicly funded plazas and paid into the Consolidated Fund of India (MoRTH revenue head 1054). That half is the stock NHAI can still sell through TOT bundles or InvIT rounds. Second, the annual pass has changed what "toll" means. Pass trips are now about 20% of all FASTag transactions and pay a flat ₹15 per crossing. Concessionaires with bids before June 2025 are compensated by NHAI, so their revenue is protected, but part of it now arrives as a receivable from the government instead of from the windscreen.

In plain terms

India collects about ₹70,000 crore a year in highway tolls, four times what it did nine years ago. Electronic tags stopped the leaks, new expressways opened, and more trucks and cars use the roads each year. Tariffs rise every April by a formula linked to wholesale prices. Inflation was almost zero last year, so tolls rose only about 2.5%; it is now above 11%, so next April's increase could be the biggest in four years.

03

What moves traffic: trucks pay, cars grow, courts and new roads divert

Freight vehicles earn most of the toll on a typical corridor. Traffic on any single road depends as much on what is built around it as on GDP.

Toll revenue is traffic times tariff, and traffic is not one thing. A commercial vehicle typically pays two to six times the car tariff, depending on axles, and on freight corridors trucks, buses and multi-axle vehicles (MAVs) can be about 60–75% of toll revenue (indicative; it varies widely by road). Roads carry about 70% of India's domestic freight, and NITI Aayog projects the truck fleet growing from about 4 million in 2022 to about 17 million by 2050. That makes highway toll a leveraged claim on goods movement. The best high-frequency proxy is the e-way bill count under GST; FASTag transaction growth (+14.8% in FY26) is another.

Cars are the growth leader on expressways. On the Mumbai–Pune Expressway and the Ahmedabad–Vadodara Expressway, both owned by IRB outright, car traffic has driven double-digit growth. Cars also respond to fuel prices and, since August 2025, to the annual pass. A private car owner can now pay ₹3,000 (₹3,075 from April 2026) for up to 200 crossings a year at national highway plazas, about ₹15 a crossing against a typical single-trip car fee of ₹60–350. About a third of car electronic-toll transactions are now pass-based. For pre-June-2025 concessions NHAI pays compensation, so the pass mostly changes who pays, not how much. For new bids it is disclosed and not compensated.

Traffic on a single road depends on its network. A new feeder or a completed widening can transform it: IRB's Palsit–Dankuni road in West Bengal grew toll 47.7% in Q1 FY27 after its completion certificate in July 2025 allowed a tariff step-up. A new parallel road can drain it: NHAI's own InvIT reports traffic diverted at two sites "due to the alternate road being made free by MoRTH". Courts can intervene directly. Since August 2023, a Bombay High Court (Aurangabad bench) restriction diverted multi-axle trucks away from IRB's Yedeshi–Aurangabad and Solapur–Yedeshi roads, and toll on both fell in FY24–FY25 until it recovered in FY26; the concessionaires have claimed force majeure. Overloading enforcement can add revenue too: fines collected at a weigh-bridge-equipped plaza are a material swing item on MAV-heavy roads.

The spread on that chart is the main lesson of this section. Within one portfolio, in one quarter, toll growth ranged from −9% to +48%. Network events (a completion certificate, a court order lifted, a new expressway feeding the road) mattered more than the economy. An analyst should therefore read a toll portfolio road by road, not as an average. The national figures help as a baseline: ICRA puts national traffic growth at about 6% in FY26 and 4.5–5.5% in FY27. Cube Highways reported traffic growth of 9.3% in Q1 FY27 (12.7% weighted by asset value). IRB InvIT's like-for-like toll grew 8%.

Shocks test the model. Covid in FY21 held national toll growth to 1.5%, and IRB's revenue fell to ₹5,299 crore, but concession agreements gave a 100% extension of the concession for the force-majeure period plus 50% reimbursement of O&M costs. That clause is why toll roads came through the pandemic with balance sheets intact. The West Asia crisis from 28 February 2026 raised crude and freight costs, and the four pump-price increases of May 2026 raised truck operating costs. Toll is roughly 10–15% of a long-haul truck trip's cost (indicative), so higher diesel prices slow marginal freight without stopping it. NHAI's InvIT saw multi-axle disruption on one corridor; IRB told investors in July 2026 that it was "not seeing any meaningful impact", with growth "driven primarily by domestic consumption".

The longer-run threats to traffic are structural, and section 20 covers them: dedicated freight corridors that could take long-haul bulk off parallel highways, new greenfield expressways that pull traffic from older tolled roads, and distance-based satellite tolling that would charge for kilometres driven rather than plazas crossed. None is visible in today's numbers. All three matter more for a 20-year concession than any single quarter's growth.

In plain terms

Trucks pay most of the toll on a typical highway, so tolls follow the movement of goods; cars matter most on expressways. But what happens around one road, a new link road, a court order, a free alternative, can move its toll far more than the economy does. That is why a toll portfolio has to be read road by road.

04

Where ₹100 of toll goes: a road is a debt-service machine

Once a road is built, running it is cheap. The money goes to lenders and to writing off the upfront price, which is why InvIT cash far exceeds InvIT profit.

The cost structure of a finished toll road is the single most important fact in this industry, and it looks nothing like a construction company's. The road itself is a sunk cost. What remains are toll-plaza staff and systems, highway patrol and ambulances, routine repairs, insurance, and every five to seven years a major resurfacing ("periodic overlay") that can cost a quarter of a year's toll. Together these typically take ₹10–18 of every ₹100 collected. Everything else belongs to the people who financed the road: interest to lenders, repayment of principal, and the return of and on the equity that paid the upfront fee or the construction.

Capacity, the classic supply-side question, is not the constraint in this industry. India's contractors can execute roughly 10,000 km a year; MoRTH built 10,660 km in FY25. 05

The award drought: from 12,731 km to 107 km a quarter

New road contracts have fallen for three years. For builders that is an existential problem; for owners of operating roads it changes almost nothing.

Between FY21 and FY23 India awarded highways at a record pace. MoRTH, which counts awards by NHAI, NHIDCL, state public works departments and the Border Roads Organisation, awarded 12,731 km in FY22 and 12,376 km in FY23. NHAI alone awarded 3,124 km in FY26 against a target of about 4,500 km (Nuvama), and just 107 km in the June 2026 quarter: about 102 km in May and 5 km in June, according to PNC and Ashoka management.

For owners the drought is close to irrelevant, and in one respect helpful. A toll road that is already open earns the same toll whether NHAI awards 107 km or 12,000 km in a quarter. And a government that is awarding less, borrowing nothing and repaying debt has every reason to sell more finished roads, which is the owners' pipeline. The pipeline that matters to owners is changing shape. NHAI has put out for bids 54 projects worth about ₹1.8 lakh crore (26 EPC, 21 HAM and 7 BOT-toll) for the second half of FY27. It plans about 1,735 km of BOT-toll with ₹57,573 crore of capital outlay in FY27, up from 1,388 km and ₹34,482 crore on its previous slide, and is designing a "toll-cum-annuity" hybrid: 10–25% upfront government support, a fixed 20-year concession, and the concessionaire keeping the toll. G R Infra's management says the revamped framework could bring more than 10,000 km into private bidding. Net-worth eligibility for BOT was relaxed to 25% of project cost (50% for private-equity bidders). The caution is recent history: in 2025 several large BOT packages of more than 900 km and about ₹18,900 crore in Maharashtra and Gujarat drew no bids at all. IRB called them "one-off cases" where toll did not justify the cost, and said a typical BOT tender draws five to seven bidders.

In plain terms

The government has been handing out far fewer road-building contracts for three years, and in April–June 2026 NHAI awarded almost nothing. Builders' revenues are falling and they are bidding a third below the official cost estimate to win work. Owners of roads that are already open do not care: their tolls keep coming. The next test is whether a new private toll-road model attracts bidders after a batch of 2025 tenders drew none.

Selling roads has become a debt-reduction tool as much as a capex funder.

Why does the government sell, and why does it matter which way it prices? Selling a TOT bundle swaps a 20-year toll stream for cash today. The government's implicit cost of capital is its borrowing rate, about 7.2–7.4% on the 10-year bond; the buyer wants 12–16%. Monetisation therefore only makes sense for the state when cash today is worth more than the spread, for example to retire NHAI debt and fund new roads without new borrowing, or when private operators run the road better. Price too low and the sale transfers value to private owners; price too high, with aggressive reserve prices and added obligations such as initial capex or barrier-free tolling readiness, and bids dry up. NHAI raised the initial-capex requirement on TOT-20/21/22 in Bihar and postponed the bids to late August 2026. How NHAI resolves that tension each year decides the size of the owners' opportunity.

In plain terms

The government built thousands of kilometres of highway with borrowed money. Now it sells the right to collect tolls on finished roads to investors who pay upfront, and uses the cash to repay debt and build more. It has raised about ₹1.7 lakh crore so far. It wants to raise far more, but so far it has offered only a fraction of what it promised, and that, not demand from investors, limits how much private owners can buy.

Two things follow for an investor. 08

The rulebook: a formula, a circular and a pass

Toll roads are contracts with the state. The contract protects the owner well against most things, and only partly against the state itself.

Four institutions write the rules. MoRTH sets policy, the National Highways Fee Rules and the budget. NHAI, which spends about 60% of the ministry's budget, is the concession authority and a AAA-rated quasi-sovereign counterparty. IHMCL, NHAI's tolling company, runs the FASTag ecosystem with the National Payments Corporation of India's NETC switch. SEBI regulates InvITs under the 2014 InvIT Regulations. States matter too: the Mumbai–Pune Expressway belongs to the Maharashtra State Road Development Corporation (MSRDC) and the Hyderabad Outer Ring Road to the Hyderabad Metropolitan Development Authority (HMDA), so the two of IRB's three largest roads that are not national highways sit under state contracts and state rules.

The tariff formula

Under the National Highways Fee (Determination of Rates and Collection) Rules, 2008, base rates per kilometre for each vehicle class (set in 2007-08) are revised every 1 April by a fixed 3% a year, compounding, plus 40% of the change in WPI, and rounded to the nearest ₹5. ICRA says newer concessions use December WPI and older ones March WPI. Concessions signed before 2008 run under the 1997 rules or project-specific formulas, some with fuller WPI pass-through. The formula has two features that matter. The fixed 3% means tariffs rise even in deflation, until WPI falls by 7.5%. And the 40% weight means tolls lag inflation whenever WPI is above 5%. Rounding to ₹5 bites hardest on low tariffs: IRB said the April 2026 revision was "roughly 2.5%, largely due to rounding", against a formula result of about 3.3%.

The linking-factor episode: policy risk and contractual protection in one year

On 13 September 2025 an NHAI circular changed the factor used to convert the old WPI series (base 2004-05) to the current one (base 2011-12) from 1.641 to 1.561. Because tariffs are computed from a base year, that single number cut base toll rates by about 3–5% across every affected concession. CRISIL estimated a median cut of about 100 basis points in equity returns across 64 toll roads representing about ₹95,000 crore of investment. The Highway Operators' Association of India and Roadstar Infra InvIT challenged it. On 8 July 2026 NHAI reversed the change and restored 1.641. The episode cuts both ways: it showed how much value a technical circular can move, and it showed that the industry's contractual and legal defences work when they are used. Investors who priced the cut as permanent in late 2025 were wrong; investors who priced policy risk at zero were also wrong.

The annual pass

The FASTag Annual Pass, notified on 17 June 2025 by amending the Fee Rules and launched on 15 August 2025, lets a private non-commercial car, jeep or van make up to 200 crossings at national highway plazas for ₹3,000 a year (₹3,075 from 1 April 2026). By July 2026, 83.3 lakh passes had been sold and 71.1 crore pass trips made, about 20% of all FASTag transactions and about a third of car transactions. FY26 pass receipts were ₹1,795 crore. For concessions whose bid date preceded the notification, the pass is a Change in Law: NHAI's 1 September 2025 circular set a daily compensation formula (pass trips × single-trip car fare × a plaza constant), and a 17 October 2025 operating procedure set up a portal linked to NPCI. Cube Highways booked ₹108.1 crore of compensation in Q1 FY27, 11.8% of its toll revenue, with receivable days of 42. For new bids the pass is disclosed and not compensated, so car-traffic risk shifts to the bidder. IRB has not disclosed its compensation. Its two state concessions, Mumbai–Pune and Hyderabad ORR, appear to be outside the national highway pass because the pass applies at NH and national-expressway plazas; this is our reading and is flagged for confirmation with the company.

SEBI's InvIT rules

The InvIT Regulations, as amended to October 2026, cap borrowings at 70% of the value of InvIT assets, counting net consolidated debt and deferred payments at the trust, holdco and SPV level. The rest of the rulebook is a list of latent changes. Plazas must normally be at least 60 km apart on the same section in the same direction, with local-resident concessions within 20 km; ministers have promised for years to remove plazas inside 60 km. The Parliamentary Committee on Estimates recommended in 2025 a revision of the toll framework, including the base rate and inflation indexing, a refund mechanism for incomplete highways and better FASTag scanning. Satellite-based tolling rules were notified in September 2024, with a 20 km-a-day free allowance for GNSS-equipped private vehicles. Barrier-free multi-lane free-flow tolling is live at six plazas. None of these is a crisis. Each could move revenue on a large part of the network, and only some would count as a compensable change in law.

In plain terms

Tolls rise every April by a fixed formula, and the contracts protect owners against most shocks, including Covid. The weak spot is the government itself: in 2025 one technical circular cut tolls by 3–5% overnight, and only a legal challenge got it reversed ten months later. The new annual pass for cars is compensated for old contracts, but the cash now comes from NHAI rather than from drivers.

Four eras of Indian road PPP: boom, bust, the state, the sale

Private builders took the traffic risk and lost; the government took it back; now it sells finished roads to private owners who never touch a shovel.

Era one, 1995–2004: the pioneers. The NHAI Act of 1988 became operational in 1995. The Mhaiskar family's Ideal Road Builders built the Thane–Bhiwandi bypass, among India's first BOT toll roads, in 1997–98, and IRB Infrastructure Developers was incorporated in 1998. The Golden Quadrilateral, launched around 1998–2001, was built mostly on EPC and annuity contracts. MSRDC opened the Mumbai–Pune Expressway in 2002, and in August 2004 IRB won the right to toll it for 15 years for a reported ₹918 crore upfront (not verified in primary documents). The template for the next twenty years, a private company paying for the right to toll a state-built road, was set here.

Era two, 2005–2012: the BOT boom. The 2009 model concession agreement and viability-gap funding brought a rush of private bidders, including GMR, GVK, Reliance Infra, IRB, IL&FS, Ashoka, Sadbhav and Essel, many of them bidding premiums to NHAI on optimistic traffic forecasts. IRB listed in February 2008. Awards peaked in 2011–12 on BOT-premium terms. Then came the bust of 2012–14: land acquisition stalled after the 2013 land law raised costs (from about ₹0.8 crore to about ₹3.6 crore per hectare), GDP slowed, traffic missed forecasts, premium-deferment schemes were needed and many projects were terminated. IL&FS, partly a road group, collapsed in September 2018.

Era three, 2015–2023: the state takes the risk back. The hybrid annuity model arrived in 2016 and EPC scaled up. The private share of national-highway financing fell from 51% in FY15 to 15% by FY24. Bharatmala was approved in October 2017. In May 2017 IRB InvIT Fund became India's first InvIT, raising about ₹5,035 crore at ₹102 a unit. Monetisation began with TOT-1 in FY19 (Macquarie, ₹9,681.5 crore). NHAI borrowed heavily and its debt peaked at about ₹3.48 lakh crore in FY22. FASTag became mandatory in February 2021. IRB won "Mumbai–Pune 2.0" from MSRDC in 2020 for ₹8,262 crore. In 2021 Ferrovial (Cintra) and GIC invested about ₹5,300 crore in IRB, GIC put about ₹4,000 crore into the new Private InvIT, and NHAI launched its own InvIT, NHIT.

The cycle pattern is the key to the present. Private construction risk boomed and busted in 2005–2014 and has stayed low since. Private ownership of operating roads has boomed since 2018 through monetisation. Today's debate is whether India can bring private construction risk back through the BOT revival and the toll-cum-annuity hybrid when a batch of 2025 BOT packages drew no bids, and whether the ownership boom survives higher interest rates. History says that private capital takes traffic risk happily on roads that already have traffic, and reluctantly on roads that do not.

One more lesson from the BOT bust is relevant to any investor. The failures of 2012–14 came less from tolling than from everything before it: land that was not available on the appointed date, approvals that took years, and premium bids that assumed traffic growth far above what materialised. TOT and InvIT buyers today avoid all three because they buy roads that exist and have a traffic record. Their risks are of a different kind: overpaying at auction, interest rates, and rule changes. The industry has not removed risk; it has moved it from construction sites to auction rooms and the bond market.

In plain terms

Private companies built India's first toll roads in the late 1990s and early 2000s, rushed in during a boom, and lost heavily when land, traffic and the economy disappointed after 2012. The government then took over building roads itself. Since 2018 it has been selling the finished roads to investors. The question now is whether private builders will take risks again on new roads, and whether the investors who buy finished roads keep buying when interest rates rise.

Reading a road asset: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen numbers explain almost everything about a toll road, a HAM project, an InvIT or a sponsor platform. Each has a formula, a benchmark and a trap.

A road asset is simple to describe and easy to misread. Revenue is traffic times tariff; costs are small; most of the value goes to lenders and to repaying the upfront price; and the asset dies on a known date. The KPIs below follow that logic, from the toll booth to the unitholder. For each, the section gives the definition, the formula, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, a benchmark, how IRB and the reference set compare, and the most common way the number misleads.

The equation, and a worked example

Every toll road reduces to one chain. Toll revenue equals vehicles per day, by class, times the tariff for each class, times 365, adjusted for exemptions, annual-pass compensation and overloading fees. EBITDA is toll minus operating and maintenance costs. Cash to equity is EBITDA minus interest, scheduled principal, tax and resurfacing capex. Value is the present value of that cash until the concession ends, with no terminal value, because the road goes back to the government. Management controls costs, financing and, at the bid, the price paid. It does not control traffic, which follows the economy and the network, or the tariff, which follows the formula and the regulator.

A worked example in round numbers (illustrative). Take a 100 km four-lane national highway: 400 lane-km. It carries 20,000 vehicles a day at an average toll of ₹275 across cars, buses and trucks. That operating leverage, on a cash stream with a known end date, is what every KPI below measures from a different angle.

In plain terms

A toll road earns money from the number of vehicles times what each pays. Costs are small, so most of each extra rupee of toll drops to profit. The owner's job is to pay the right price at the start, borrow cheaply, and keep costs down. Traffic and tariffs, the two things that matter most, are outside its control.

KPI 1 · Toll collection growth: traffic versus rate

What it is. Year-on-year growth in gross toll collected, which should always be split into three parts: like-for-like traffic growth, the April tariff revision, and new or departed assets. Formula. Toll growth ≈ (1 + traffic growth) × (1 + rate revision) − 1, plus or minus perimeter changes. Worked example. If a road's toll grows 11.3% in a year with a 6% tariff revision, traffic and mix grew about 5% (1.113 ÷ 1.06 − 1). Drivers. Freight, car ownership, network effects (new feeders, free alternatives), court orders, overloading enforcement, and the annual pass. Benchmark. Like-for-like toll growth above 12% is good, 7–12% average, below 7% weak. National toll grew about 11.5% in FY26 on user fees.

How IRB compares. In Q2 FY27 (Jul–Sep 2026) IRB's group toll rose 25.0% to ₹2,377.6 crore, but that blends very different things. The wholly owned pair (Mumbai–Pune and Ahmedabad–Vadodara) grew 14.6%: Mumbai–Pune 13.9%, Ahmedabad–Vadodara 16.2%. The Private InvIT grew 41.9%, mostly because TOT-17, TOT-18 and the Ganga Expressway were new. The public IRB InvIT grew only 6.7% on a perimeter that lost the Omallur–Salem–Namakkal concession in August 2026. In plain terms

IRB's margin went up from 45% to 54% in a year without any road becoming more profitable. It went up because the low-margin construction business shrank and the high-margin trust income grew. KPI 8 · HAM annuity and interest

What it is. The cash a HAM concessionaire receives from NHAI: construction-period support, then semi-annual annuities, interest and maintenance payments. Formula. Annuity = 60% of bid project cost (price-index adjusted) ÷ 30 half-years; interest = (bank rate or average top-5-bank one-year MCLR + 1.25–3%) × reducing balance; plus O&M payments repriced half-yearly. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE). A ₹1,000 crore HAM: NHAI pays ₹400 crore during construction; the developer funds ₹600 crore with ₹450 crore of debt and ₹150 crore of equity. After completion it receives 30 half-yearly annuities of ₹20 crore plus interest at, say, 9.5% on the reducing balance (about ₹56 crore in the first year), plus O&M payments. With debt at 8.5% amortised over 15 years, the equity IRR is roughly 15%, before the 12–18% EPC margin the developer also earns on the ₹1,000 crore build. Selling the SPV to an InvIT a year after completion at roughly 1.2–1.4× equity book realises the gain.

KPI 14 · Debt-service cover: DSCR and PLCR

What it is. Lenders' measures of how safely a road's cash covers its debt. The debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR) compares one period's cash flow available for debt service (CFADS) with that period's interest and principal; the project-life coverage ratio (PLCR) compares the present value of all CFADS to the end of the concession with the debt outstanding. Formula. DSCR = CFADS ÷ (interest + principal due); PLCR = PV(CFADS to concession end) ÷ debt. Worked example. In the anatomy below, a road with ₹160 crore of CFADS (EBITDA less tax) and ₹96 crore of interest and principal has a DSCR of about 1.7×. Drivers. Toll growth, interest rates, the repayment profile (back-ended loans lift early DSCR), and resurfacing years. Benchmark. 1.8× or more comfortable; 1.3–1.8× average; below about 1.3× most loan agreements trap cash, so distributions stop.

How IRB and the reference set compare. NHIT reported a DSCR of 2.49× in Q1 FY27; Cube 2.0×; CRISIL puts the IRB Private InvIT's average DSCR at 1.7–1.9×, with cash-trap covenants at 1.30× (1.20× on the TOT-17 and TOT-18 loans). At the holdco, IRB's dollar-bond covenants show a PLCR of 2.8× against a 1.8× minimum on the "Segment I" group (present value of CFADS ₹67,100 crore against ₹23,900 crore of gross debt) and gross leverage of 2.8× against a 4.0× cap on "Segment II" (IRB standalone, Mumbai–Pune, Ahmedabad–Vadodara and two HAMs: about ₹6,900 crore of debt on ₹2,500 crore of EBITDA). Definition trap. A comfortable DSCR today can coexist with a short PLCR if the concession is near its end. For Mumbai–Pune, with three and a half years left, the relevant test is whether cash to April 2030 covers the ₹3,654 crore refinanced in Q1 FY27, not this year's cover ratio.

  • Consolidated is not the portfolio. IRB's consolidated revenue (₹7,648 crore in FY26) contains the toll of two roads, construction and O&M billed to trust SPVs, and trust-related income. Group toll of all 24 tolling SPVs is about four times the toll inside consolidated revenue.

  • Three EBITDAs. Screener operating profit, company EBITDA and segment EBITDA differ by tens of crores a quarter. State the basis.
  • Net debt that nets strategic stakes. Screener's ₹699 crore is wrong; the company KPI is ₹11,057 crore; Ind AS gives ₹19,784 crore; the Ahmedabad–Vadodara concession-fee liability of ₹14,881 crore sits outside all of them.
  • Construction revenue that is really capex. Under concession accounting, a developer books construction revenue on its own BOT/TOT/HAM projects. Much of IRB's construction segment is work for its own trusts; about 36% of FY26 consolidated revenue (₹2,736 crore) was billed to Private InvIT SPVs.
  • Amortisation, not traffic, explains low InvIT profit. Cube earned ₹217 crore of PAT on ₹3,117 crore of operating profit in FY26.
  • HAM is a receivable. No traffic risk; booked under Ind AS 109; reported revenue and margin understate cash receipts.
  • Five definitions of toll. Plaza gross toll; toll of consolidated entities; economic share; NHAI user fee; user fee plus annual-pass receipts. FASTag totals include state roads.

How to read a road-asset company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the monthly toll releases, not the results. IRB publishes group toll around the 8th of each month. By results day you already know the quarter's toll by vehicle: in Q2 FY27, ₹2,378 crore (+25.0%), wholly owned +14.6%.
  2. Split toll growth into rate, traffic and perimeter. Subtract the April revision (about 2.5% this year) and strip out new or ended concessions. Like-for-like traffic below 4% for two quarters is a warning.
  3. Read segment revenue and margin. BOT/TOT should be 89–91%, InvIT 90–94%, construction about 15%. A falling toll margin usually means a resurfacing year; a falling InvIT margin means fees or distributions fell.
  4. Check distributions received from the trusts. In Q1 FY27 IRB received ₹136 crore (₹102 crore from the Private InvIT, ₹35 crore from IRB InvIT) against ₹46 crore a year earlier. This is the cash that services the holdco.
  5. Look at finance cost, net of one-offs. Q1 FY27 finance cost was ₹438 crore including a ₹37 crore one-off; underlying ₹401 crore against ₹462 crore. Refinancing savings should show here.
  6. Separate exceptional items. Use pre-exceptional PAT for growth and reported PAT for the peer table. Ask whether any gain on a trust transfer is booked at IRB or only at the trust.
  7. Track company net debt and the dollar. Net debt/equity should keep falling towards the FY30 zero target (0.51× in June 2026). Mark the US$740 million notes to the current rupee rate.
  8. Read the order book and new orders. O&M additions of ₹4,000–5,000 crore a year are the guide; EPC is a small residual.
  9. Note related-party flows and receivables. Revenue billed to the trusts (about 36% in FY26) and the ₹3,853 crore receivable from the Private InvIT flagged by CARE. Rising receivables with flat cash is a red flag.
  10. Finish with the pipeline and policy. TOT wins, recycling transactions, any change to the fee rules, annual-pass compensation and, for IRB specifically, any news on Mumbai–Pune after April 2030.
In plain terms

Ten minutes is enough if you read in the right order: how much toll came in and why, how much profit each business made, how much cash the trusts sent up to the parent, what the interest bill did, and whether debt is falling. Then check for one-off items, money owed by the company's own trusts, and any rule changes from the government.

Build, stabilise, sell to yourself: IRB's two-trust machine Deep dive

IRB bids for roads through a private trust it controls, de-risks them, and sells them to a public trust it sponsors. The spread funds the next bid. The governance question is built in.

IRB calls its strategy B.E.S.T. The mechanics are simpler than the acronym. New toll roads, mostly TOT bundles, are bid for and held inside IRB Infrastructure Trust, the privately placed InvIT that IRB owns 51% of alongside GIC (25%) and Ferrovial's Cintra (24%). The private trust targets an equity return of 15–16% at the bid. After three or four years of operation, when traffic has a record and the debt has been refinanced more cheaply, selected roads are sold to the listed IRB InvIT Fund, in which IRB holds 16.12%, at a price that gives the buyer 12–12.5%. The gap between the two returns is crystallised as cash in the private trust, which uses it to bid for the next roads without IRB having to put in fresh equity. IRB earns at every step: distributions from both trusts, project-management fees, fixed-price O&M contracts for the life of each road, and EPC work on initial capex.

The governance debate

The machine works, and it creates a structural conflict that investors price. IRB is the majority owner of the seller, the sponsor and a 16% holder of the buyer, and the operator and project manager of both. Transfer prices are set between related parties, supported by independent valuations and unitholder approvals. About 36% of IRB's FY26 consolidated revenue, roughly ₹2,736 crore (₹2,009 crore of EPC and ₹727 crore of O&M), was billed to SPVs of the private trust; billing to IRB InvIT's SPVs comes on top. In May 2026 IRB approved O&M agreements with three IRB InvIT SPVs running from April 2030 to the end of their concessions. CARE flags about ₹3,853 crore owed to IRB by the private trust, whose recovery depends largely on arbitration claims for cost overruns; that is roughly a fifth of IRB's market value. The case for the structure is that it is transparent, approved and aligned: GIC and Cintra sit on both IRB's board and the private trust's, and outside unitholders must approve each transfer. The case against is that every price in the loop is set by parties on more than one side of it. This report does not take a side; it notes that the discount in IRB's valuation is consistent with the market charging for the uncertainty.

One alignment signal is easy to miss. SEBI requires IRB to hold only 3% of IRB InvIT at its listing age. IRB holds 16.12% and put ₹351 crore into the September 2026 issue to keep it there. The 13 points above the floor are worth about ₹1,300 crore at market (indicative): capital IRB could release, and a reason it bears part of any overpayment by the buyer.

TOT: NHAI's ATM and the private sector's 20-year bet Deep dive

A TOT buyer pays today for 20 years of tolls on a road it did not build. The price only works if India's traffic and inflation together compound at 9% or more for two decades.

Toll-operate-transfer is the cleanest form of the monetisation trade. NHAI (or a state agency) auctions the right to collect tolls on a bundle of operating roads for a fixed period, typically 20 years on recent NHAI bundles with a "revenue-linked" adjustment to the period, against a single upfront fee. The buyer takes on traffic risk and maintenance, increasingly with mandated initial works and readiness for barrier-free tolling. There is no construction risk, no land risk and a known traffic history. For NHAI it is cash today without new debt. For a buyer, it is a 20-year, inflation-linked toll stream bought at auction.

The terms are also getting tougher. TOT-20, 21 and 22 in Bihar (145.6, 139.4 and 90.7 km on NH-27) added initial-capex obligations, which NHAI then raised further before postponing the bids to late August 2026; the treatment of barrier-free tolling still needs clarification, according to IRB. Why does IRB win so often? Its advantages are scale in O&M, which lowers cost per lane-km; a tolling and traffic database across 27 assets; access to GIC's and Ferrovial's equity through the private trust; and the ability to recycle stabilised assets into its own listed trust at a lower cost of capital, which lets it bid a higher price than a developer that must hold to maturity. A TOT buyer pays the government a lump sum today, about 11–13 times the first year's tolls on recent deals, for the right to collect tolls for about 20 years. Also, the government has been selling far fewer of these deals than IRB hopes, and the terms of new ones are getting stricter.

IRB has collected tolls on it since 2004. Its current contract, "Mumbai–Pune 2.0", was won from MSRDC in 2020 for ₹8,262 crore, paid as ₹6,500 crore upfront plus ₹850 crore in each of years two and three and ₹62 crore in year four. A Lok Sabha answer (search snippet only) indicates the end date was fixed with reference to the ₹704.45 crore capitalised cost of the common expressway and NH4 stretch.

It collected ₹1,844 crore in FY26: about 68% of the toll on IRB's two wholly owned roads, about 29% of the toll of the wholly owned roads plus the private trust, and about 22% of group toll. Rates are the valuation lever: a road is a long bond with a toll booth

The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and the 10-year yield touched 7.36%. For leveraged, long-dated toll assets that is the single biggest price input.

A toll road's cash flows are long, contracted and loosely inflation-linked, and they are usually funded 40–55% with debt. That makes road assets behave like long bonds in two ways at once. Their value is the present value of 15–30 years of cash, so a higher discount rate lowers it. And their equity cash depends on interest costs, most of which are on floating-rate bank loans, so a higher policy rate raises costs with a lag. Both effects turned negative in October 2026. The RBI raised the repo rate by 25 bp to 5.50% on 7 October; the 10-year government bond yield reached about 7.23% that day, a two-year high, and opened at 7.355% on 12 October after a ₹25,000 crore open-market sale; one bank expects one or two more hikes to 5.75–6.0%. CPI inflation was 4.8% in August, while WPI ran at 11.39% in September.

The timing matters. Most of the industry refinanced just before the turn. IRB refinanced ₹3,654 crore of Mumbai–Pune debt at 65 bp lower and moved about ₹11,000 crore of private-trust debt across six SPVs to the AAA trust level at 160 bp lower, saving about ₹205 crore a year between them. Cube's cost of debt is 7.49%, with 28.5% fixed at the trust level, and it issued a ₹637 crore NCD at 7.39%. CRISIL notes that IRB's private trust carries floating-rate exposure and expects sector gross debt to EV to rise 100–150 bp by March 2027 as trusts buy more. Debt costs will drift up from here rather than jump.

The equity effect has already shown up in prices. On 23 October 2026 IRB traded at ₹16.2, its 52-week low (range ₹16.1–24.0), and every reference builder sat near its low. The trusts held up better: IRB InvIT at ₹61 (₹57.0–65.4) and Cube at ₹153 (₹131–165). That pattern fits the logic of this section. Trust units are priced off their yield spread and their contracted distributions, which rose; IRB is priced as a leveraged equity whose upside depends on the recycling machine's pace, and the machine slows when buyers' cost of capital rises. The spread between IRB's 15–16% bid IRR and the trust's 12–12.5% transfer IRR only works while trust investors accept 12%. If the 10-year bond rises to 7.5–8%, they may not.

In plain terms

Toll roads behave like long-term bonds: when interest rates go up, what investors will pay for their future tolls goes down, and their loan bills rise. Rates went up in October 2026. Road trusts still pay well above a government bond, but if rates keep rising, buyers will demand higher returns, and that squeezes the profit IRB makes by selling roads to its trust.

The global lens: why Indian toll roads trade at half the global multiple

Ferrovial and Transurban trade at 25–28× EBITDA; Indian road trusts at 12–14× and IRB at 7×. Duration, indexation and policy explain most of the gap.

The four global reference companies show what long-duration toll assets are worth when investors trust them. Ferrovial (Spain), which owns 19.86% of IRB and 24% of its private trust, has a market value of about US$35.5 billion (about ₹3.37 lakh crore) and trades at about 25.5× EV/EBITDA; its crown asset, Toronto's 407 ETR, has a concession to 2098, and it runs dynamically priced managed lanes in Texas and Virginia. Transurban (Australia) is worth about A$44.2 billion (about ₹2.77 lakh crore) and trades at about 28.3× EV/EBITDA; its revenue of about A$3.9 billion (about ₹24,400 crore) from roughly 22 roads is about a third of India's entire national highway toll pool. Vinci (France) is worth about €58.5 billion (about ₹6.44 lakh crore) but is dominated by construction, hence 6.3×. Atlas Arteria (Australia) holds a stake in France's APRR motorways and yields 10.8%. Four differences explain most of the gap. Duration: Indian national-highway concessions run 15–30 years (TOT about 20, BOT 20–30, HAM 15 years of annuity), against 30–99 years for the global majors' flagship assets, and there is no terminal value at the end. A 20-year asset is worth far less per rupee of EBITDA than a 70-year one at any discount rate. Indexation: India's formula passes through 40% of WPI plus a fixed 3%; Transurban's tolls typically rise at CPI or a 4% minimum, and French autoroutes at about 70% of CPI plus works-linked add-ons. Pricing freedom: India has no congestion or dynamic pricing, which caps upside on dense corridors such as Mumbai–Pune; US managed lanes reprice in real time. Policy and rates: Indian tariffs can move by circular, as September 2025 showed, and India's risk-free rate is 7.2–7.4% against 4–5% in Australia or Europe.

Why, then, do Ferrovial and GIC own a fifth and a sixth of IRB? Because India is the world's largest road build-out and its monetisation programme is the largest pipeline of operating toll roads for sale anywhere. Foreign toll companies are valued at about twice the price of Indian road trusts for each rupee of operating profit, mostly because their roads have concessions lasting 30–99 years and tolls that track consumer inflation, while Indian concessions end after 15–30 years and follow a government formula. Technology and policy risk: from plazas to satellites

FASTag closed the leaks. The next changes, barrier-free tolling, satellite tolling and passes, change who pays and how much. Only some of them are compensated.

FASTag is saturated. More than 98% of national highway toll is collected electronically, and about 97% on IRB's roads. Cash leakage is largely gone; what remains is exemptions, vehicle-class misclassification and blacklisted tags, and cash users pay double. The growth from adoption is over: FASTag collections grew 13.7% in FY26 and the first half of FY27 annualises to about ₹86,000 crore, up roughly 5% (indicative), partly because annual-pass trips carry no per-trip value.

The annual pass is the biggest change since FASTag. With 83.3 lakh passes sold and pass trips at about 20% of all FASTag transactions by July 2026, a slice of car toll has turned into a receivable from NHAI for pre-June-2025 concessions. At Cube, compensation was 11.8% of toll in Q1 FY27, and toll excluding compensation fell about 1.6% (indicative). The risk for owners has changed from traffic to counterparty and formula: will NHAI pay on time (Cube's receivable days were 42), and will the plaza constant in the compensation formula hold? For new bids the pass is uncompensated, so it lowers what bidders will pay. A future pass for trucks, or a broader "toll-free local" scheme, would be a material change in law.

Barrier-free tolling (MLFF). Multi-lane free-flow tolling uses gantries with RFID readers and number-plate cameras instead of booths. Six plazas were live by 1 August 2026, starting with Choryasi near Surat on 1 May 2026, followed by Mundka on Delhi's UER-II and Daulatpura on the Delhi–Jaipur NH-48; bids have been invited for 104 more. MLFF removes queues and plaza costs but removes the barrier too, so revenue depends on recovering unpaid tolls from violators through vehicle-registration-linked e-challans. The questions for concessionaires are who bears revenue lost in the transition, who pays for the equipment (newer TOT bundles require MLFF readiness) and how enforcement works. IRB says "clarifications are still required".

Satellite (GNSS) tolling. Rules for satellite-based electronic toll collection were notified in September 2024, with a planned hybrid inside FASTag, dedicated GNSS lanes, an IHMCL tender for "toll charger" software and a reported pilot on Bengaluru–Mysuru. GNSS-equipped private vehicles get 20 km a day free. The deep question is whether distance-based charging, paying for kilometres actually driven, replaces section-based plaza tolling, which charges the full section even for partial trips. Distance tolling would cut revenue on sections with heavy local or short-trip traffic and could raise it where plazas are sparse (indicative); the free allowance is a direct haircut on peri-urban roads.

Other structural risks. Electric vehicles pay tolls nationally, but they erode the fuel cess (₹46,930 crore budgeted in FY27), which raises the state's reliance on tolls and monetisation. Dedicated freight corridors could take long-haul bulk freight off parallel highways, including several Gujarat, Rajasthan and Maharashtra corridors where IRB operates (indicative). New greenfield expressways divert traffic from older tolled roads, and MoRTH has made alternate roads free at some sites. Plaza rationalisation (the 60 km rule) and the Committee on Estimates' call to revisit the toll framework are latent political risks.

Bull vs bear: the case for owning Indian roads, and against

The sector's long-run cash is not in much doubt. The argument is about price, policy and who captures the spread.

Both cases start from the same facts: a toll pool compounding at mid-teens rates over a decade, a government that needs to keep selling roads, and an investor base of long-term global capital. They diverge on three questions. Will the tariff formula and the monetisation pipeline deliver the 9–10% annual toll growth that TOT pricing assumes? Will interest rates allow trusts to keep buying at 12% returns? And, for IRB specifically, does the recycling machine create value for IRB's shareholders, or mainly move it between related parties?

The bull case

  • The toll pool compounds: ₹33,929 crore (FY22) to ₹70,278 crore (FY26), with FY28 growth of 10–14% (indicative) as the April 2027 revision jumps on 11% WPI.
  • Inflation is a friend: 40% of WPI passes into tariffs while cash costs are only 10–18% of toll; the fixed 3% protects against deflation.
  • Supply of finished roads is large: about half of NH toll is still collected at public plazas, and NHAI must cut debt; NMP 2.0 targets ₹4.42 lakh crore from roads.
  • IRB's mix is improving: Q1 FY27 PAT +51%, distributions to IRB tripled, net debt/equity 0.51× and falling to a zero target by FY30, CARE AA assigned in October 2026.
  • Contracts work: Covid extensions, annual-pass compensation and the reversal of the linking-factor cut show the protections hold.
  • Valuation: IRB at 0.94× book and 7.3× EV/EBITDA against an illustrative sum of the parts 27–39% higher; global toll assets at 25–28×.

The bear case

  • The 2030 cliff: Mumbai–Pune, about 21% of group toll and the densest road IRB owns, ends on 30 April 2030 with no successor contract.
  • Related-party economics: about 36% of IRB's FY26 revenue came from its own private trust's SPVs, and ₹3,853 crore is owed by that trust, recoverable mainly through arbitration.
  • TOT pricing and supply: ~13× first-year toll needs 9–10% annual growth for 20 years; TOT supply has been ₹1,000–16,000 crore a year, far below the ₹40,000–50,000 crore IRB expects.
  • Rates: repo up to 5.50% and the 10-year at 7.2–7.4%; the 12% transfer return that powers recycling depends on trust buyers' cost of capital.
  • Policy: one circular cut base tolls 3–5% in 2025; MLFF, GNSS distance tolling and future passes could move revenue, and new bids are not compensated.
  • Balance-sheet extras: the ₹14,881 crore AV concession-fee liability sits outside net debt; US$740 million of dollar notes add about ₹74 crore of rupee debt per ₹1 move.

The two cases are less opposed than they look. The bull case is mainly about the sector: the pool, the formula and the pipeline. The bear case is mainly about IRB's structure and timing: one big road ending, one big counterparty that is its own trust, and a business model that depends on a spread between two costs of capital. An investor can hold both views at once, constructive on toll assets and wary of a holding company that recycles them. The evidence that would settle it is concrete: what MSRDC decides on Mumbai–Pune, whether the ₹3,853 crore receivable is collected, how much TOT NHAI actually sells in FY27, and whether the April 2027 revision lands near 7%.
