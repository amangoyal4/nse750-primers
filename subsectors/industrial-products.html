Industrial products: fifteen companies, five different businesses and a valuation running ahead of its cash

A complete guide to the NSE's "Industrial Products" bucket, which is really five businesses under one label: contract electronics makers, engineered-to-order process and cryogenic equipment shops, precision-component and machine-tool makers, automation and mining-consumables annuities, and heavy steel fabricators for the power grid. How each one turns an order into cash, why the bought-in material inside ₹100 of revenue decides most of what follows, who is winning, and why a multiple of about 73 times earnings leaves the group exposed to its weakest number: cash conversion.

00

The one-page view: fifteen companies, one label, five economic engines

The bucket mixes circuit-board assemblers with turbine-blade machinists. The useful question is not what they make but how much of each rupee they actually add.

The NSE classification "Capital Goods — Industrial Products" sounds like a catalogue of pipes, bearings, valves and castings. The fifteen members inside the NSE 750 universe are something else. Three of them assemble printed circuit boards for carmakers, railways, smart-meter programmes and US equipment makers. Five build pressure vessels, cryogenic tanks, glass-lined reactors, ethanol plants and air-conditioner coils to order. Three machine turbine blades, build CNC machine tools or cut industrial gears. Two sell control systems and mill liners into installed bases that need replacing whatever the economy does. Two fabricate steel: transmission towers for the grid, and plant equipment for a steel group. Pipes appear only as a small polymer line at Skipper, and steel matters mainly as a raw material.

Together these fifteen companies reported ₹38,039 crore of revenue in FY26, ₹5,373 crore of EBITDA (a 14.1% margin) and ₹3,233 crore of net profit. At the 13 October 2026 close the market valued them at ₹2,29,204 crore, which is 70.9 times FY26 profit or about 73 times trailing-twelve-month profit. Put differently, investors are paying about six rupees of market value for every rupee of revenue in a group whose average member keeps 14 paise of operating profit from each rupee of sales. No member consolidates another, so these totals are clean sums; but Tega's FY26 numbers exclude Molycop, the grinding-media giant it bought on 1 June 2026, and Lloyds Engineering's exclude the associate and subsidiaries it is merging with. Both companies are about to look very different.

The report is built around one idea: the economics of these companies differ more by where value is added than by what metal they cut. The cleanest test is how much of ₹100 of revenue goes on bought-in material. For a contract electronics maker it is ₹65–78. For a transmission-tower fabricator it is ₹60–68. For a process-equipment shop it is ₹50–60. For a precision-parts machinist making turbine airfoils it is ₹25–35. That one ratio sets the gross margin, the damage done when an input price rises before it can be passed on, and how much working capital each extra rupee of revenue absorbs. So the fifteen are grouped into five archetypes, A1 to A5, and compared within an archetype before they are compared across the bucket.

Five forces explain most of what happens to these companies, and the rest of the report is organised around them:

  • Value-add share decides margins, working capital and pass-through risk. Bought-in material ranges from about a quarter of revenue at Azad to three-quarters at Syrma. A two-point un-passed rise in component prices costs an EMS company about 14% of its EBITDA; the same rise barely registers at an airfoil machinist.
  • Policy-made demand overshoots. Ethanol blending, the electronics component scheme (ECMS), the semiconductor mission, defence indigenisation and the transmission plan all create demand that does not follow the private capex cycle. Ethanol shows how these booms end: distillery capacity reached about 1,970 crore litres against roughly 1,100 crore litres of E20 fuel demand, and Praj's return on capital fell from 31% to 6% in three years.
  • Global energy and aerospace demand is supply-constrained. GE Vernova holds 116 GW of gas-turbine backlog and slot reservations; GE Aerospace's backlog exceeds US$210 billion; grid investment worldwide is heading for about US$3 trillion over CY24–29. These markets pay a premium for qualified capacity, and Indian suppliers such as Azad, INOX India, Skipper and Tega are exporting into them.
  • Indian private capex is broadening, not booming. RBI's seasonally adjusted capacity utilisation has sat between 74.9% and 75.8% for five quarters, right at the level where companies start adding capacity but short of a general boom. Power is 40% of bank-sanctioned project cost; chemicals is under 6%.
  • Valuations are far ahead of cash conversion. The group trades at about 73 times trailing earnings, yet five of the fastest growers (Kaynes, Azad, Lloyds, KRN, Jyoti) converted between −133% and +10% of FY26 EBITDA into operating cash and funded the gap with equity.

Three conclusions run through the report. First, the precision-parts makers are the only archetype whose margin structurally exceeds 20%, because they sell qualified capacity in a market short of it; the market already prices them accordingly, with Azad at 139 times earnings. Second, the electronics makers are growing at 27–46% but keep 11–16% of revenue as EBITDA and convert little of it into cash, so their equity story depends on climbing into components, chip packaging and design. Third, the steadiest earnings in the bucket, Skipper's transmission towers and Elecon's gears, trade at the lowest multiples (28× and 33×), while the most fragile cash profiles carry the highest. That inversion is the sub-sector's main fragility, and the 2025–26 falls in Kaynes (down 52% in a year), Praj and Anup show what happens when it is tested.

In plain terms

These fifteen companies share an exchange label, not a business model. An electronics assembler that buys ₹70 of components for every ₹100 it sells lives on volume and supplier credit. A turbine-blade machinist that buys ₹25 of metal lives on the scarcity of its qualified machines. A tower maker lives on steel tonnes and government contracts that adjust for steel prices. Judge each against its own kind first; only then ask which kind deserves the higher price.

01

Five businesses under one label: the archetype map

Sort the fifteen by bought-in material per ₹100 of revenue, and the order books, margins and cash cycles fall into place.

An archetype here is a group of companies that make money the same way: similar cost structure, similar customers, similar meaning of "order book", similar cycle. The five below are defined by economic signature, not product. Two companies that both bend steel plate (Anup's heat exchangers and Skipper's towers) end up in different archetypes, because one sells engineering and metallurgy to refineries while the other sells galvanised tonnes to a regulated grid. Two companies that look unrelated (Honeywell Automation's control systems and Tega's mill liners) share an archetype, because both earn a large part of their revenue from an installed base that must be serviced or replaced.

Read the chart from top to bottom and the margin ranking almost writes itself. Syrma, with about ₹76 of components inside every ₹100, earned an 11.3% EBITDA margin in FY26. Kaynes and Avalon, at about ₹65, earned 16% and 11% (Avalon still carries a loss-making US plant). INOX India and Anup, at ₹45–60 of material, earned about 22%. Azad, with the smallest bought-in share, earned 37%. The exceptions are informative. GMM Pfaudler adds plenty of value (material about ₹40 of ₹100) but earns only 11%, because its European workforce costs 28–31% of revenue. Praj's FY26 margin of 4.9% reflects a collapse in domestic ethanol orders rather than its cost structure. Honeywell Automation buys much of its hardware from its parent and pays royalties to it, so part of its value-add leaves the Indian entity before it reaches EBITDA.

The edge cases

Five members do not sit neatly in their boxes, and each edge case matters for how the numbers should be read.

  • KRN Heat Exchanger (A2) makes fin-and-tube heat exchangers and coils for air-conditioner makers such as Daikin. It behaves partly like a component supplier: short purchase-order cycles, no reported order book, room-AC seasonality and copper and aluminium making up most of its material. It is the A2 member most exposed to the copper price, now above US$14,000 a tonne.
  • Praj Industries (A2) is part technology licensor, part EPC contractor and part fabricator. Its cycle is set by ethanol, sustainable aviation fuel and compressed-biogas policy rather than by chemical capex, which is why it gets its own deep dive.
  • Elecon (A3) makes precision-machined, heat-treated gears with OEM-like qualification, but its material-handling segment (about 28% of FY26 revenue) behaves like an A5 project business.
  • Lloyds Engineering Works (A5) is becoming a group EPC platform. Its pending merger with Lloyds Infrastructure & Construction (LICL), whose revenue is about 1.5 times its own, will make its largest customer its own promoter group's Gadchiroli steel and mining build-out.
  • Honeywell Automation India (A4) is the only multinational subsidiary: 75% owned by the parent, net cash of about ₹3,700 crore, and since 29 June 2026 part of the continuing automation company, Honeywell Technologies, after the parent spun off its aerospace arm.
In plain terms

A company that buys ₹75 of parts to sell ₹100 of product has ₹25 to pay its people, run its factory and earn a profit. If parts cost 2% more and it cannot charge more for a quarter, it loses two of those 25 rupees, about a twelfth of everything it adds. A company that buys ₹25 of metal has ₹75 to play with, and the same 2% rise costs it half a rupee. That arithmetic explains more about the fifteen than any product description.

02

From order to cash: how each archetype actually makes money

All five run the same loop of win, buy, convert, deliver and collect. They differ in which step holds the risk and who funds the wait.

Every company in the bucket earns money in the same broad sequence. It wins an order, or a slot in a customer's production schedule. It buys material. It converts that material by assembling, fabricating, machining or galvanising it. It delivers and bills. Then it waits to be paid. Profit is made in the conversion step. Risk sits wherever the company has committed money before the customer has: material bought before a price is fixed, a factory built before a part is qualified, a receivable outstanding to a state electricity distributor. Cash is made or lost between the second step and the last.

The five versions of the loop

A1 EMS. An electronics manufacturer builds to its customer's design. Its "order book" is a rolling schedule of purchase orders and forecasts covering 6–18 months, and much of it can be rescheduled. It buys components, many imported from China, Taiwan and Korea, often 6–8 months ahead in 2026 because of West Asia disruption, and passes component price changes through with a lag of one or two quarters. Profit is the value-add on each board times volume. Cash depends on how long components sit in inventory and how long customers take to pay; smart-meter programmes, where the counterparty is an infrastructure provider waiting on a state distributor, are the slowest payers of all.

A2 process equipment. A heat-exchanger, reactor or cryogenic-tank shop sells engineered-to-order (ETO) equipment against a firm order with milestone billing. It usually books material against each order, so a price spike after the order is won hurts only on long, fixed-price jobs; Anup says most of its contracts are fixed-price with no pass-through. Customer advances can fund much of the working capital, which is why Praj, with ₹899 crore of advances on its balance sheet, runs on negative working capital. The risk is utilisation: welders, engineers and heavy bays are a fixed cost of about 20–25% of revenue, so a gap in orders shows up a year later as a collapse in margin.

A3 precision parts and machines. Azad cannot sell a single blade until the customer has approved that part number at that plant, which takes 12–36 months. In return it gets long-term contracts (Azad calls them LTCPAs) and high switching costs. Jyoti builds machine tools against firm orders from machine shops, many of them small firms that pay slowly or through financing partners. Elecon sells catalogue and engineered gearboxes plus aftermarket spares. All three carry heavy inventory: superalloy forgings bought a year ahead, imported CNC controls, finished machines held for demonstration.

A4 automation and consumables. Honeywell Automation wins a control-system project, then earns lifecycle services, spares, software and migration revenue on that installed base for years. Tega's liners are replaced every 3–12 months while a mine is running, whatever the copper price, so revenue tracks tonnes of ore milled. Molycop's grinding balls are consumed the same way, under contracts mostly tied to steel indices.

A5 heavy fabrication. Skipper rolls its own steel angles, fabricates and galvanises towers and increasingly erects the lines itself. Domestic transmission contracts carry price-variation clauses indexed to IEEMA formulas for steel and zinc, so the margin per tonne holds while the rupee revenue moves with steel. Lloyds fabricates steel-plant and naval equipment against EPC-style contracts, a third of them from its own promoter group.

In plain terms

Every company here makes money in the factory and loses cash in the waiting. The question for each one is who pays for the wait. At Praj, the customer does, through advances. At Honeywell Automation, suppliers do, because it pays them later than its customers pay it. At Kaynes, Azad and Lloyds, shareholders do, through share issues, because the cash tied up in stock and receivables grew faster than profit.

03

Size, growth and the markets behind the label

There is no single "industrial products market". There are five, each measured differently, and the listed companies are growing far faster than all of them.

The sub-sector has no agreed market size because its members sell into unrelated markets. The workable approach is to size each archetype's market on its own terms and then compare the listed companies' growth with it. Each market is measured in its own unit: electronics in rupees of production, machine tools in rupees of consumption, process equipment in capex sanctioned by banks, transmission in circuit-kilometres and utility capex, and precision parts in the backlogs of a handful of global OEMs.

Electronics (A1). India produced ₹13.11 lakh crore of electronics in FY26, up 15.8%, and exported more than ₹4 lakh crore. But most of that is mobile-phone assembly by Foxconn, Tata Electronics and Dixon, with domestic value addition historically around 15–20%. The listed members work in low-volume, high-mix (LVHM) industrial, automotive, railway, medical and defence electronics, where gross margins are two to three times those of mobile assembly. Estimates of India's pure EMS market range from about US$25 billion (New Venture Research, 2023) to more than US$100 billion (industry interviews that conflate production with EMS). The narrower measure is the more credible one. On it, the non-mobile slice is perhaps US$8–12 billion, and Kaynes, Syrma and Avalon together (₹10,048 crore of FY26 revenue) hold roughly a tenth of it, an INDICATIVE estimate. More than 400 EMS firms operate in India, so the market is fragmented and the runway long.

Precision parts and machines (A3). Machine-tool consumption grew from ₹12,036 crore in FY21 to ₹31,781 crore in FY25, but domestic makers supplied less than half of it; Japan alone sent about 47% of imported machining centres in 2024. That import share of about 59–65% is Jyoti's opportunity. Azad's market is not Indian at all. It is the order book of the world's gas-turbine and aero-engine makers: GE Vernova plans to lift gas-turbine output from about 20 GW a year in Q3 2026 to 24 GW in 2028 and 30 GW by 2030, and GE Aerospace's LEAP engine deliveries rose 41% in H1 2026. Elecon's gears serve an Indian industrial-gearbox market estimated at ₹6,000–8,000 crore, a figure from older company material that this report could not re-verify.

Process equipment (A2). The best domestic proxy is the capex that banks and financial institutions sanction. In FY26 that was ₹4.35 lakh crore across 1,032 projects, of which chemicals and pesticides took 5.9%, or about ₹25,700 crore. CRISIL expects specialty-chemical companies to pare capex to about ₹16,500 crore in FY27 as their margins fall to 14–14.5%. Against that, the export markets are larger and stronger: a global LNG liquefaction build-out described by the IEA as the largest ever (figures not verified this session), US pharma reshoring, and fusion and space projects that buy INOX India's cryogenic vessels.

Automation and consumables (A4). India's process-automation market (control systems, safety, SCADA and services) is put at US$3–4 billion and is dominated by five or six multinationals. Tega's market is global: mill liners worth about US$1.3–1.6 billion, where it is second to Weir, and grinding media worth US$5–6 billion, where Molycop leads.

Transmission (A5). The National Electricity Plan for transmission commits ₹9.15 lakh crore to 2032, adding about 1.91 lakh circuit-kilometres of lines at 220 kV and above. Power Grid's annual capex is rising from about ₹9,400 crore in FY22 to about ₹25,000 crore in FY26 (estimate).

04

Five demand engines, and why they rarely fire together

Each archetype answers to a different customer: an electronics policy, a refinery budget, a turbine maker's slot book, a miner's throughput, a grid plan.

Most of the bucket's demand comes from five sources, and they move on separate clocks. That is useful for diversification at the level of the bucket and dangerous for anyone who treats "Indian capex" as the single driver. In FY26 the transmission and aerospace clocks were running fast, the chemical-capex clock was slow and the ethanol clock had stopped.

1. Electronics content and policy (A1)

Electronics content per product is rising in cars and two-wheelers (battery management, motor controllers, chargers), railways (Kaynes' railway revenue rose 81% in Q1 FY27 on Kavach signalling work), defence, medical devices, smart meters and data-centre hardware. Policy multiplies it: production-linked incentives (PLI) for phones and IT hardware, the Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (ECMS, ₹69,548 crore of approved investment by August 2026), the semiconductor mission and preference for local content in public procurement. On top sits China+1, meaning Western equipment makers dual-sourcing out of China. Syrma's exports were 24% of Q1 FY27 revenue and are guided to grow 30–40% in FY27; Avalon earns 59–62% of its revenue from US-based customers. Domestic consumption matters too: consumer electronics was about a third of Syrma's Q1 revenue.

2. Process-industry and energy-transition capex (A2)

Refinery and petrochemical expansions, fertiliser plants, thermal-power and nuclear projects feed Anup's heat exchangers and vessels (oil and gas was 40% of its Q1 FY27 revenue; power and energy 22%). Pharma and contract-manufacturing capex feeds GMM Pfaudler's glass-lined reactors; its managing director called glass-lined demand the best "for quite some time", led by Indian CDMOs, Hyderabad API makers and US pharma reshoring (for the customer side, see the pharma CDMO report). Industrial gases, LNG, space and fusion science feed INOX India, whose largest new customer is a US private space company with more than ₹1,000 crore of orders. Ethanol, compressed biogas and aviation-fuel policy feed Praj. Room air-conditioners, buses, railways and data-centre cooling feed KRN; ICRA expects Indian room-AC volumes to recover 12–14% in FY27 after a rain-hit FY26.

3. Global gas turbines, aero engines and defence (A3)

Data-centre power demand, coal retirements and grid firming have filled gas-turbine order books. GE Vernova held 116 GW of gas backlog and slot reservations at Q2 2026, up from 100 GW a quarter earlier, and targets at least 125 GW by year-end. Commercial aerospace is short of engines: LEAP deliveries rose 41% in H1 2026, and Pratt & Whitney's geared-turbofan shortages are expected to last to the end of the decade. Azad earns about 81% of its revenue from energy and oil-and-gas parts and 17% from aerospace and defence. Domestically, defence indigenisation (₹1.78 lakh crore of defence production in FY26, 24% private) and auto, electronics and die-mould machine shops drive Jyoti, where aerospace and defence are 38% of the order book. Steel, cement, power and sugar plants drive Elecon's gears.

4. Installed bases that keep consuming (A4)

Honeywell Automation's revenue depends on process-industry projects (refining, chemicals, pharma, power) and building and data-centre construction, plus migrations and cybersecurity upgrades of control systems already installed. Its growth has been muted: revenue rose 3% in FY25 and 12% in FY26, and 1.8% in Q1 FY27. Tega follows tonnes of ore milled rather than mining capex. With copper above US$14,000 a tonne and gold near US$4,400 an ounce, miners run mills harder, process lower-grade ore and replace conventional liners with premium hybrid ones. Legacy consumables revenue grew 36% in Q1 FY27.

5. The grid (A5)

Transmission is the steadiest engine in the bucket. India plans 1.91 lakh circuit-km of high-voltage lines and 33 GW of HVDC links by 2032; power alone took 39.6% of bank-sanctioned project cost in FY26. Skipper's order book reached a record ₹9,217 crore in June 2026, 80% of it domestic transmission, with a bid pipeline above ₹35,000 crore. Lloyds' demand, by contrast, is tied to one group's steel and power build-out at Gadchiroli.

Pass-through means the customer eventually pays for a rise in raw-material prices. "Eventually" is the catch. A capex cycle that broadens but does not boom

Indian factories are running at the level where expansion starts to make sense, not much above it. Power is pulling; chemicals is not.

The Reserve Bank's survey of manufacturers (OBICUS) is the best single gauge of whether Indian companies need new capacity. Its 73rd round, released on 5 August 2026 and covering the March 2026 quarter, put capacity utilisation at 77.4%, or 75.2% after seasonal adjustment. Seasonally adjusted utilisation has now sat between 74.9% and 75.8% for five straight quarters. The RBI and most economists treat a sustained level above about 75% as the point at which brownfield additions become necessary. India is at that threshold, not decisively above it. Utilisation peaked at about 83% in 2007-08 and fell to about 63% in the first COVID quarter.

Who is actually exposed. Direct US exposure is modest for most members. Avalon is the clearest case: its US plant and US-based customers make up most of revenue, and it reports that tariff pass-through costs it about 0.9 percentage points of EBITDA margin. Azad's customers are American, but aero parts largely enjoy duty-free treatment under the WTO civil-aircraft agreement and many energy parts ship to OEM plants in Europe and Japan. Elecon says Indian-origin gearboxes into the US face a 50% tariff, which management attributes to Section 232, and has set up a Mexican entity to serve the Americas. INOX India is barred from US manufacturing or sales until 2028 by a non-compete. Skipper's US exposure is only 3% of its export book. The binding constraint for steel-intensive exports (towers, tanks, vessels, coils, gearboxes) is now Section 232, charged on full customs value. The 15% rate for grid and industrial equipment runs to the end of 2027 and steps to 25% on 1 January 2028.

Relative, not absolute, rates decide orders. Under Section 232 India is treated like its competitors, and Indian steel exports to the US have held up because rivals pay the same duty. The damage in 2025–26 came from the six months when India faced 50% against roughly 15–20% for Vietnam, Mexico and the EU; EMS and machined-parts exporters lost price competitiveness, and orders slipped rather than vanished. After February 2026 that gap closed.

The 2026 Hormuz shock was a bigger operating event than tariffs. The effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz from about March to July 2026 pushed Brent crude to US$100–110 a barrel in May–July and the rupee past ₹95 to the dollar. Container freight to Europe roughly doubled; INOX India held back ₹32–35 crore of finished equipment in Q1 FY27; Skipper's exports halved year on year in the quarter; KRN's UAE business fell as freight rose fourfold. EMS makers built "strategic inventory" and paid 30–35% more for components. The weak rupee helped exporters' rupee revenues (Azad, INOX, Tega) and hurt importers of controls and chips (Jyoti, the EMS three).

In plain terms

A Western turbine maker will pay a little more for a blade from Hyderabad than from China if the Hyderabad plant is qualified, reliable and not subject to sanctions risk. A Western utility buying steel towers mostly wants the cheapest tonne after tariffs. So China+1 is a strong tailwind for the bucket's qualified, high-value exporters and a weak one for its steel fabricators, whose export fortunes depend on US tariff rules that changed six times in a year.

Niche oligopolies and crowded middles: how the industry is structured

Several members lead small global niches with two or three rivals. Others compete in fragmented markets of hundreds. Which side of that line a company sits on explains its pricing power.

Industrial products is not one market with a concentration ratio. It is a patchwork of niches. In some, a listed member is the leader and its rivals can be counted on one hand: glass-lined reactors in India, cryogenic tanks, ethanol process technology, mill liners, grinding media, industrial gearboxes. In others, the member is one of hundreds: contract electronics, heat exchangers and pressure vessels, transmission towers. The first group earns pricing power from switching costs, certifications and know-how. The second competes on execution, cost and working-capital tolerance.

Defence and transmission policy are steadier. In plain terms

When the government pays half the cost of a factory, more factories get built. That is good for the companies that build or equip them, until enough have been built. The question to ask of every policy-driven order book is what happens to the supplier the year after the target is met. Ethanol answered it for Praj: orders stop, and margins halve.

Can Indian EMS earn more than a pass-through margin? Deep dive

Kaynes, Syrma and Avalon grow at 27–46% a year on 11–16% margins and weak cash conversion. Their valuations assume they climb into components, chip packaging and design. The evidence so far is mixed.

An EMS company's problem is visible in its cost structure. Between ₹65 and ₹78 of every ₹100 of revenue is components it buys and passes through. The value it adds, ₹22–35 of gross margin, has to cover its people, factories and profit. Globally, the companies that capture most of an electronic product's value are the chip, component and brand owners, not the assemblers. India's domestic value addition in electronics has historically been about 15–20%. The bull case for Indian EMS is that the listed companies can move up the stack. The bear case is that each step up costs a lot of capital, takes years to fill, and in the meantime makes the returns worse.

The world's gas-turbine and jet-engine makers are short of qualified parts suppliers. Azad sells that scarcity at a 37% margin, and the market prices it at 139 times earnings.

For most of the past decade the constraint in aerospace and power generation was demand. Since 2024 it has been supply: castings, forgings and machined airfoils. Airlines have more aircraft on order than engine makers can deliver; utilities and data-centre developers want more gas turbines than turbine makers can build. When an industry is short of parts, the suppliers that already hold qualifications are in an unusually strong position. The working-capital problem

Azad's cash profile is the weakest part of its case. On a sales basis its FY26 inventory was ₹329 crore (199 days of revenue) and receivables ₹312 crore (189 days), against payables of ₹85 crore (51 days): about 337 days of net working capital. Screener's own cash-conversion-cycle figure of 1,720 days is an artefact of the company's tiny material-cost base, which inflates inventory and payable days measured against material cost; working-capital days of about 304 are the usable screener measure. Superalloy forgings are stocked six to twelve months ahead under the long-term contracts, and qualification parts sit as work in progress. FY26 operating cash flow was −₹119 crore, free cash flow −₹691 crore, and the company moved from net cash of ₹434 crore in FY25 to net debt of ₹272 crore at March 2026, after a ₹700 crore share placement. The CFO targets about 200 days of working capital in H1 FY27 and 160–180 days by H2, mainly by cutting debtor days from 170–180 to about 90 through bill discounting. That moves receivables off the balance sheet in exchange for a financing charge; it does not change how long the customer takes to pay.

Who else in the bucket sells into the bottleneck

Azad is the purest play, but not the only one. Jyoti CNC's 5-axis and turn-mill machines, including those from its French subsidiary Huron Graffenstaden, sell to aerospace and defence machine shops; that segment is 38% of its ₹4,848 crore order book, though export-control licences on Huron machines have slowed shipments. INOX India holds AS9100D aerospace certification and builds very large cryogenic tanks for a US private space company. Anup has begun nuclear and thermal-power heat exchangers. All three benefit from the same shortage of qualified Western capacity, without Azad's single-sector concentration.

The bull and bear case for Azad can be stated with numbers. For the bull: an ₹6,500 crore contract book, four plants built and four more planned, customers that control about 75% of the global gas-turbine market, a 37% margin and a move into complete engines (the DRDO turbojet; a GTRE turbo-gas-generator contract). For the bear: a few customers dominate revenue; FY26 cash flow was negative; return on equity is about 9% after the placement; the stock trades at 139 times trailing earnings and about 81 times EV/EBITDA; and on the reverse-DCF arithmetic used in this report, earnings would need to compound at about 33% a year for ten years just to earn a 12% return at a 25× exit multiple. Additive manufacturing could, over ten to fifteen years, replace some machined parts. The scarcity is real, and the price already reflects a lot of it.

In plain terms

Turbine makers are sold out for years and cannot build faster than their suppliers. A blade maker that already holds the approvals is hard to replace, so it earns a fat margin. But every new plant takes two or three years to qualify, during which it costs money and earns little, and the metal has to be bought a year in advance. Azad's profits are excellent; its cash flow is negative; its share price assumes both the growth and the cash arrive on time.
