Industrial products: fifteen companies, five different businesses and a valuation running ahead of its cash

A complete guide to the NSE's "Industrial Products" bucket, which is really five businesses under one label: contract electronics makers, engineered-to-order process and cryogenic equipment shops, precision-component and machine-tool makers, automation and mining-consumables annuities, and heavy steel fabricators for the power grid. How each one turns an order into cash, why the bought-in material inside ₹100 of revenue decides most of what follows, who is winning, and why a multiple of about 73 times earnings leaves the group exposed to its weakest number: cash conversion.

00

The one-page view: fifteen companies, one label, five economic engines

The bucket mixes circuit-board assemblers with turbine-blade machinists. The useful question is not what they make but how much of each rupee they actually add.

The NSE classification "Capital Goods — Industrial Products" sounds like a catalogue of pipes, bearings, valves and castings. The fifteen members inside the NSE 750 universe are something else. Three of them assemble printed circuit boards for carmakers, railways, smart-meter programmes and US equipment makers. Five build pressure vessels, cryogenic tanks, glass-lined reactors, ethanol plants and air-conditioner coils to order. Three machine turbine blades, build CNC machine tools or cut industrial gears. Two sell control systems and mill liners into installed bases that need replacing whatever the economy does. Two fabricate steel: transmission towers for the grid, and plant equipment for a steel group. Pipes appear only as a small polymer line at Skipper, and steel matters mainly as a raw material.

Together these fifteen companies reported ₹38,039 crore of revenue in FY26, ₹5,373 crore of EBITDA (a 14.1% margin) and ₹3,233 crore of net profit. At the 13 October 2026 close the market valued them at ₹2,29,204 crore, which is 70.9 times FY26 profit or about 73 times trailing-twelve-month profit. Put differently, investors are paying about six rupees of market value for every rupee of revenue in a group whose average member keeps 14 paise of operating profit from each rupee of sales. No member consolidates another, so these totals are clean sums; but Tega's FY26 numbers exclude Molycop, the grinding-media giant it bought on 1 June 2026, and Lloyds Engineering's exclude the associate and subsidiaries it is merging with. Both companies are about to look very different.

The report is built around one idea: the economics of these companies differ more by where value is added than by what metal they cut. The cleanest test is how much of ₹100 of revenue goes on bought-in material. For a contract electronics maker it is ₹65–78. For a transmission-tower fabricator it is ₹60–68. For a process-equipment shop it is ₹50–60. For a precision-parts machinist making turbine airfoils it is ₹25–35. That one ratio sets the gross margin, the damage done when an input price rises before it can be passed on, and how much working capital each extra rupee of revenue absorbs. So the fifteen are grouped into five archetypes, A1 to A5, and compared within an archetype before they are compared across the bucket.

Five forces explain most of what happens to these companies, and the rest of the report is organised around them:

  • Value-add share decides margins, working capital and pass-through risk. Bought-in material ranges from about a quarter of revenue at Azad to three-quarters at Syrma. A two-point un-passed rise in component prices costs an EMS company about 14% of its EBITDA; the same rise barely registers at an airfoil machinist.
  • Policy-made demand overshoots. Ethanol blending, the electronics component scheme (ECMS), the semiconductor mission, defence indigenisation and the transmission plan all create demand that does not follow the private capex cycle. Ethanol shows how these booms end: distillery capacity reached about 1,970 crore litres against roughly 1,100 crore litres of E20 fuel demand, and Praj's return on capital fell from 31% to 6% in three years.
  • Global energy and aerospace demand is supply-constrained. GE Vernova holds 116 GW of gas-turbine backlog and slot reservations; GE Aerospace's backlog exceeds US$210 billion; grid investment worldwide is heading for about US$3 trillion over CY24–29. These markets pay a premium for qualified capacity, and Indian suppliers such as Azad, INOX India, Skipper and Tega are exporting into them.
  • Indian private capex is broadening, not booming. RBI's seasonally adjusted capacity utilisation has sat between 74.9% and 75.8% for five quarters, right at the level where companies start adding capacity but short of a general boom. Power is 40% of bank-sanctioned project cost; chemicals is under 6%.
  • Valuations are far ahead of cash conversion. The group trades at about 73 times trailing earnings, yet five of the fastest growers (Kaynes, Azad, Lloyds, KRN, Jyoti) converted between −133% and +10% of FY26 EBITDA into operating cash and funded the gap with equity.

Three conclusions run through the report. First, the precision-parts makers are the only archetype whose margin structurally exceeds 20%, because they sell qualified capacity in a market short of it; the market already prices them accordingly, with Azad at 139 times earnings. Second, the electronics makers are growing at 27–46% but keep 11–16% of revenue as EBITDA and convert little of it into cash, so their equity story depends on climbing into components, chip packaging and design. Third, the steadiest earnings in the bucket, Skipper's transmission towers and Elecon's gears, trade at the lowest multiples (28× and 33×), while the most fragile cash profiles carry the highest. That inversion is the sub-sector's main fragility, and the 2025–26 falls in Kaynes (down 52% in a year), Praj and Anup show what happens when it is tested.

In plain terms

These fifteen companies share an exchange label, not a business model. An electronics assembler that buys ₹70 of components for every ₹100 it sells lives on volume and supplier credit. A turbine-blade machinist that buys ₹25 of metal lives on the scarcity of its qualified machines. A tower maker lives on steel tonnes and government contracts that adjust for steel prices. Judge each against its own kind first; only then ask which kind deserves the higher price.

01

Five businesses under one label: the archetype map

Sort the fifteen by bought-in material per ₹100 of revenue, and the order books, margins and cash cycles fall into place.

An archetype here is a group of companies that make money the same way: similar cost structure, similar customers, similar meaning of "order book", similar cycle. The five below are defined by economic signature, not product. Two companies that both bend steel plate (Anup's heat exchangers and Skipper's towers) end up in different archetypes, because one sells engineering and metallurgy to refineries while the other sells galvanised tonnes to a regulated grid. Two companies that look unrelated (Honeywell Automation's control systems and Tega's mill liners) share an archetype, because both earn a large part of their revenue from an installed base that must be serviced or replaced.

Read the chart from top to bottom and the margin ranking almost writes itself. Syrma, with about ₹76 of components inside every ₹100, earned an 11.3% EBITDA margin in FY26. Kaynes and Avalon, at about ₹65, earned 16% and 11% (Avalon still carries a loss-making US plant). INOX India and Anup, at ₹45–60 of material, earned about 22%. Azad, with the smallest bought-in share, earned 37%. The exceptions are informative. GMM Pfaudler adds plenty of value (material about ₹40 of ₹100) but earns only 11%, because its European workforce costs 28–31% of revenue. Praj's FY26 margin of 4.9% reflects a collapse in domestic ethanol orders rather than its cost structure. Honeywell Automation buys much of its hardware from its parent and pays royalties to it, so part of its value-add leaves the Indian entity before it reaches EBITDA.

The edge cases

Five members do not sit neatly in their boxes, and each edge case matters for how the numbers should be read.

  • KRN Heat Exchanger (A2) makes fin-and-tube heat exchangers and coils for air-conditioner makers such as Daikin. It behaves partly like a component supplier: short purchase-order cycles, no reported order book, room-AC seasonality and copper and aluminium making up most of its material. It is the A2 member most exposed to the copper price, now above US$14,000 a tonne.
  • Praj Industries (A2) is part technology licensor, part EPC contractor and part fabricator. Its cycle is set by ethanol, sustainable aviation fuel and compressed-biogas policy rather than by chemical capex, which is why it gets its own deep dive.
  • Elecon (A3) makes precision-machined, heat-treated gears with OEM-like qualification, but its material-handling segment (about 28% of FY26 revenue) behaves like an A5 project business.
  • Lloyds Engineering Works (A5) is becoming a group EPC platform. Its pending merger with Lloyds Infrastructure & Construction (LICL), whose revenue is about 1.5 times its own, will make its largest customer its own promoter group's Gadchiroli steel and mining build-out.
  • Honeywell Automation India (A4) is the only multinational subsidiary: 75% owned by the parent, net cash of about ₹3,700 crore, and since 29 June 2026 part of the continuing automation company, Honeywell Technologies, after the parent spun off its aerospace arm.
In plain terms

A company that buys ₹75 of parts to sell ₹100 of product has ₹25 to pay its people, run its factory and earn a profit. If parts cost 2% more and it cannot charge more for a quarter, it loses two of those 25 rupees, about a twelfth of everything it adds. A company that buys ₹25 of metal has ₹75 to play with, and the same 2% rise costs it half a rupee. That arithmetic explains more about the fifteen than any product description.

02

From order to cash: how each archetype actually makes money

All five run the same loop of win, buy, convert, deliver and collect. They differ in which step holds the risk and who funds the wait.

Every company in the bucket earns money in the same broad sequence. It wins an order, or a slot in a customer's production schedule. It buys material. It converts that material by assembling, fabricating, machining or galvanising it. It delivers and bills. Then it waits to be paid. Profit is made in the conversion step. Risk sits wherever the company has committed money before the customer has: material bought before a price is fixed, a factory built before a part is qualified, a receivable outstanding to a state electricity distributor. Cash is made or lost between the second step and the last.

The five versions of the loop

A1 EMS. An electronics manufacturer builds to its customer's design. Its "order book" is a rolling schedule of purchase orders and forecasts covering 6–18 months, and much of it can be rescheduled. It buys components, many imported from China, Taiwan and Korea, often 6–8 months ahead in 2026 because of West Asia disruption, and passes component price changes through with a lag of one or two quarters. Profit is the value-add on each board times volume. Cash depends on how long components sit in inventory and how long customers take to pay; smart-meter programmes, where the counterparty is an infrastructure provider waiting on a state distributor, are the slowest payers of all.

A2 process equipment. A heat-exchanger, reactor or cryogenic-tank shop sells engineered-to-order (ETO) equipment against a firm order with milestone billing. It usually books material against each order, so a price spike after the order is won hurts only on long, fixed-price jobs; Anup says most of its contracts are fixed-price with no pass-through. Customer advances can fund much of the working capital, which is why Praj, with ₹899 crore of advances on its balance sheet, runs on negative working capital. The risk is utilisation: welders, engineers and heavy bays are a fixed cost of about 20–25% of revenue, so a gap in orders shows up a year later as a collapse in margin.

A3 precision parts and machines. Azad cannot sell a single blade until the customer has approved that part number at that plant, which takes 12–36 months. In return it gets long-term contracts (Azad calls them LTCPAs) and high switching costs. Jyoti builds machine tools against firm orders from machine shops, many of them small firms that pay slowly or through financing partners. Elecon sells catalogue and engineered gearboxes plus aftermarket spares. All three carry heavy inventory: superalloy forgings bought a year ahead, imported CNC controls, finished machines held for demonstration.

A4 automation and consumables. Honeywell Automation wins a control-system project, then earns lifecycle services, spares, software and migration revenue on that installed base for years. Tega's liners are replaced every 3–12 months while a mine is running, whatever the copper price, so revenue tracks tonnes of ore milled. Molycop's grinding balls are consumed the same way, under contracts mostly tied to steel indices.

A5 heavy fabrication. Skipper rolls its own steel angles, fabricates and galvanises towers and increasingly erects the lines itself. Domestic transmission contracts carry price-variation clauses indexed to IEEMA formulas for steel and zinc, so the margin per tonne holds while the rupee revenue moves with steel. Lloyds fabricates steel-plant and naval equipment against EPC-style contracts, a third of them from its own promoter group.

In plain terms

Every company here makes money in the factory and loses cash in the waiting. The question for each one is who pays for the wait. At Praj, the customer does, through advances. At Honeywell Automation, suppliers do, because it pays them later than its customers pay it. At Kaynes, Azad and Lloyds, shareholders do, through share issues, because the cash tied up in stock and receivables grew faster than profit.

03

Size, growth and the markets behind the label

There is no single "industrial products market". There are five, each measured differently, and the listed companies are growing far faster than all of them.

The sub-sector has no agreed market size because its members sell into unrelated markets. The workable approach is to size each archetype's market on its own terms and then compare the listed companies' growth with it. Each market is measured in its own unit: electronics in rupees of production, machine tools in rupees of consumption, process equipment in capex sanctioned by banks, transmission in circuit-kilometres and utility capex, and precision parts in the backlogs of a handful of global OEMs.

Electronics (A1). India produced ₹13.11 lakh crore of electronics in FY26, up 15.8%, and exported more than ₹4 lakh crore. But most of that is mobile-phone assembly by Foxconn, Tata Electronics and Dixon, with domestic value addition historically around 15–20%. The listed members work in low-volume, high-mix (LVHM) industrial, automotive, railway, medical and defence electronics, where gross margins are two to three times those of mobile assembly. Estimates of India's pure EMS market range from about US$25 billion (New Venture Research, 2023) to more than US$100 billion (industry interviews that conflate production with EMS). The narrower measure is the more credible one. On it, the non-mobile slice is perhaps US$8–12 billion, and Kaynes, Syrma and Avalon together (₹10,048 crore of FY26 revenue) hold roughly a tenth of it, an INDICATIVE estimate. More than 400 EMS firms operate in India, so the market is fragmented and the runway long.

Precision parts and machines (A3). Machine-tool consumption grew from ₹12,036 crore in FY21 to ₹31,781 crore in FY25, but domestic makers supplied less than half of it; Japan alone sent about 47% of imported machining centres in 2024. That import share of about 59–65% is Jyoti's opportunity. Azad's market is not Indian at all. It is the order book of the world's gas-turbine and aero-engine makers: GE Vernova plans to lift gas-turbine output from about 20 GW a year in Q3 2026 to 24 GW in 2028 and 30 GW by 2030, and GE Aerospace's LEAP engine deliveries rose 41% in H1 2026. Elecon's gears serve an Indian industrial-gearbox market estimated at ₹6,000–8,000 crore, a figure from older company material that this report could not re-verify.

Process equipment (A2). The best domestic proxy is the capex that banks and financial institutions sanction. In FY26 that was ₹4.35 lakh crore across 1,032 projects, of which chemicals and pesticides took 5.9%, or about ₹25,700 crore. CRISIL expects specialty-chemical companies to pare capex to about ₹16,500 crore in FY27 as their margins fall to 14–14.5%. Against that, the export markets are larger and stronger: a global LNG liquefaction build-out described by the IEA as the largest ever (figures not verified this session), US pharma reshoring, and fusion and space projects that buy INOX India's cryogenic vessels.

Automation and consumables (A4). India's process-automation market (control systems, safety, SCADA and services) is put at US$3–4 billion and is dominated by five or six multinationals. Tega's market is global: mill liners worth about US$1.3–1.6 billion, where it is second to Weir, and grinding media worth US$5–6 billion, where Molycop leads.

Transmission (A5). The National Electricity Plan for transmission commits ₹9.15 lakh crore to 2032, adding about 1.91 lakh circuit-kilometres of lines at 220 kV and above. Power Grid's annual capex is rising from about ₹9,400 crore in FY22 to about ₹25,000 crore in FY26 (estimate).

04

Five demand engines, and why they rarely fire together

Each archetype answers to a different customer: an electronics policy, a refinery budget, a turbine maker's slot book, a miner's throughput, a grid plan.

Most of the bucket's demand comes from five sources, and they move on separate clocks. That is useful for diversification at the level of the bucket and dangerous for anyone who treats "Indian capex" as the single driver. In FY26 the transmission and aerospace clocks were running fast, the chemical-capex clock was slow and the ethanol clock had stopped.

1. Electronics content and policy (A1)

Electronics content per product is rising in cars and two-wheelers (battery management, motor controllers, chargers; see the auto components report for the customers' side), railways (Kaynes' railway revenue rose 81% in Q1 FY27 on Kavach signalling work), defence, medical devices, smart meters and data-centre hardware. Policy multiplies it: production-linked incentives (PLI) for phones and IT hardware, the Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (ECMS, ₹69,548 crore of approved investment by August 2026), the semiconductor mission and preference for local content in public procurement. On top sits China+1, meaning Western equipment makers dual-sourcing out of China. Syrma's exports were 24% of Q1 FY27 revenue and are guided to grow 30–40% in FY27; Avalon earns 59–62% of its revenue from US-based customers. Domestic consumption matters too: consumer electronics was about a third of Syrma's Q1 revenue.

2. Process-industry and energy-transition capex (A2)

Refinery and petrochemical expansions, fertiliser plants, thermal-power and nuclear projects feed Anup's heat exchangers and vessels (oil and gas was 40% of its Q1 FY27 revenue; power and energy 22%). Pharma and contract-manufacturing capex feeds GMM Pfaudler's glass-lined reactors; its managing director called glass-lined demand the best "for quite some time", led by Indian CDMOs, Hyderabad API makers and US pharma reshoring (for the customer side, see the pharma CDMO report). Industrial gases, LNG, space and fusion science feed INOX India, whose largest new customer is a US private space company with more than ₹1,000 crore of orders. Ethanol, compressed biogas and aviation-fuel policy feed Praj. Room air-conditioners, buses, railways and data-centre cooling feed KRN; ICRA expects Indian room-AC volumes to recover 12–14% in FY27 after a rain-hit FY26.

3. Global gas turbines, aero engines and defence (A3)

Data-centre power demand, coal retirements and grid firming have filled gas-turbine order books. GE Vernova held 116 GW of gas backlog and slot reservations at Q2 2026, up from 100 GW a quarter earlier, and targets at least 125 GW by year-end. Commercial aerospace is short of engines: LEAP deliveries rose 41% in H1 2026, and Pratt & Whitney's geared-turbofan shortages are expected to last to the end of the decade. Azad earns about 81% of its revenue from energy and oil-and-gas parts and 17% from aerospace and defence. Domestically, defence indigenisation (₹1.78 lakh crore of defence production in FY26, 24% private) and auto, electronics and die-mould machine shops drive Jyoti, where aerospace and defence are 38% of the order book. Steel, cement, power and sugar plants drive Elecon's gears.

4. Installed bases that keep consuming (A4)

Honeywell Automation's revenue depends on process-industry projects (refining, chemicals, pharma, power) and building and data-centre construction, plus migrations and cybersecurity upgrades of control systems already installed. Its growth has been muted: revenue rose 3% in FY25 and 12% in FY26, and 1.8% in Q1 FY27. Tega follows tonnes of ore milled rather than mining capex. With copper above US$14,000 a tonne and gold near US$4,400 an ounce, miners run mills harder, process lower-grade ore and replace conventional liners with premium hybrid ones. Legacy consumables revenue grew 36% in Q1 FY27.

5. The grid (A5)

Transmission is the steadiest engine in the bucket. India plans 1.91 lakh circuit-km of high-voltage lines and 33 GW of HVDC links by 2032; power alone took 39.6% of bank-sanctioned project cost in FY26. Skipper's order book reached a record ₹9,217 crore in June 2026, 80% of it domestic transmission, with a bid pipeline above ₹35,000 crore. Lloyds' demand, by contrast, is tied to one group's steel and power build-out at Gadchiroli, and its pending merger turns it into an EPC contractor (for the wider EPC landscape, see the civil construction report).

Pass-through means the customer eventually pays for a rise in raw-material prices. "Eventually" is the catch. A capex cycle that broadens but does not boom

Indian factories are running at the level where expansion starts to make sense, not much above it. Power is pulling; chemicals is not.

The Reserve Bank's survey of manufacturers (OBICUS) is the best single gauge of whether Indian companies need new capacity. Its 73rd round, released on 5 August 2026 and covering the March 2026 quarter, put capacity utilisation at 77.4%, or 75.2% after seasonal adjustment. Seasonally adjusted utilisation has now sat between 74.9% and 75.8% for five straight quarters. The RBI and most economists treat a sustained level above about 75% as the point at which brownfield additions become necessary. India is at that threshold, not decisively above it. Utilisation peaked at about 83% in 2007-08 and fell to about 63% in the first COVID quarter.

Who is actually exposed. Direct US exposure is modest for most members. Avalon is the clearest case: its US plant and US-based customers make up most of revenue, and it reports that tariff pass-through costs it about 0.9 percentage points of EBITDA margin. Azad's customers are American, but aero parts largely enjoy duty-free treatment under the WTO civil-aircraft agreement and many energy parts ship to OEM plants in Europe and Japan. Elecon says Indian-origin gearboxes into the US face a 50% tariff, which management attributes to Section 232, and has set up a Mexican entity to serve the Americas. INOX India is barred from US manufacturing or sales until 2028 by a non-compete. Skipper's US exposure is only 3% of its export book. The binding constraint for steel-intensive exports (towers, tanks, vessels, coils, gearboxes) is now Section 232, charged on full customs value. The 15% rate for grid and industrial equipment runs to the end of 2027 and steps to 25% on 1 January 2028.

Relative, not absolute, rates decide orders. Under Section 232 India is treated like its competitors, and Indian steel exports to the US have held up because rivals pay the same duty. The damage in 2025–26 came from the six months when India faced 50% against roughly 15–20% for Vietnam, Mexico and the EU; EMS and machined-parts exporters lost price competitiveness, and orders slipped rather than vanished. After February 2026 that gap closed.

The 2026 Hormuz shock was a bigger operating event than tariffs. The effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz from about March to July 2026 pushed Brent crude to US$100–110 a barrel in May–July and the rupee past ₹95 to the dollar. Container freight to Europe roughly doubled; INOX India held back ₹32–35 crore of finished equipment in Q1 FY27; Skipper's exports halved year on year in the quarter; KRN's UAE business fell as freight rose fourfold. EMS makers built "strategic inventory" and paid 30–35% more for components. The weak rupee helped exporters' rupee revenues (Azad, INOX, Tega) and hurt importers of controls and chips (Jyoti, the EMS three).

In plain terms

A Western turbine maker will pay a little more for a blade from Hyderabad than from China if the Hyderabad plant is qualified, reliable and not subject to sanctions risk. A Western utility buying steel towers mostly wants the cheapest tonne after tariffs. So China+1 is a strong tailwind for the bucket's qualified, high-value exporters and a weak one for its steel fabricators, whose export fortunes depend on US tariff rules that changed six times in a year.

Niche oligopolies and crowded middles: how the industry is structured

Several members lead small global niches with two or three rivals. Others compete in fragmented markets of hundreds. Which side of that line a company sits on explains its pricing power.

Industrial products is not one market with a concentration ratio. It is a patchwork of niches. In some, a listed member is the leader and its rivals can be counted on one hand: glass-lined reactors in India, cryogenic tanks, ethanol process technology, mill liners, grinding media, industrial gearboxes. In others, the member is one of hundreds: contract electronics, heat exchangers and pressure vessels, transmission towers. The first group earns pricing power from switching costs, certifications and know-how. The second competes on execution, cost and working-capital tolerance.

In plain terms

When the government pays half the cost of a factory, more factories get built. That is good for the companies that build or equip them, until enough have been built. The question to ask of every policy-driven order book is what happens to the supplier the year after the target is met. Ethanol answered it for Praj: orders stop, and margins halve.

Can Indian EMS earn more than a pass-through margin? Deep dive

Kaynes, Syrma and Avalon grow at 27–46% a year on 11–16% margins and weak cash conversion. Their valuations assume they climb into components, chip packaging and design. The evidence so far is mixed.

An EMS company's problem is visible in its cost structure. Between ₹65 and ₹78 of every ₹100 of revenue is components it buys and passes through. The value it adds, ₹22–35 of gross margin, has to cover its people, factories and profit. Globally, the companies that capture most of an electronic product's value are the chip, component and brand owners, not the assemblers. India's domestic value addition in electronics has historically been about 15–20%. The bull case for Indian EMS is that the listed companies can move up the stack. The bear case is that each step up costs a lot of capital, takes years to fill, and in the meantime makes the returns worse.

When an industry is short of parts, the suppliers that already hold qualifications are in an unusually strong position. In plain terms

Ethanol's boom and bust: a template for every policy-made equipment cycle Deep dive

India hit 20% ethanol blending five years early. The equipment supplier that built most of the capacity saw its profit fall 90% in two years. Hydrogen, biogas and aviation fuel are next.

Ethanol blending was the cleanest policy-made capex boom of the decade. In the 2013-14 ethanol supply year India blended 1.53% ethanol into petrol. The government then set rising targets, guaranteed offtake by oil-marketing companies at administered prices, opened grain as a feedstock and subsidised distillery loans. Blending reached about 10% in 2021-22, 14.6% in 2023-24, 19.2% in 2024-25 and 20% in December 2025, five years ahead of the original 2030 target. Distillery capacity rose from about 680 crore litres in 2018-19 to about 1,970 crore litres in 2025-26, with roughly 400 crore litres more due by FY27.

Four decades of cycles: what each boom and bust did to these businesses

Indian capital goods have lived through one super-boom, a decade-long slump and a policy-led revival. Most of today's members listed in the last five years and have never seen a full down-cycle as public companies.

The history of Indian capital goods since 2003 explains both why investors pay high multiples today and why they should be careful doing so. From 2003 to 2008 a general capex super-boom pushed factory utilisation to about 83% and capital-goods stocks such as L&T, BHEL and Suzlon to 40–60 times earnings; heat-exchanger shops and tower fabricators ran full. The global financial crisis ended it. Public spending held order books up for a while, but from 2011 to 2020 Indian private capex was stuck in a long slump caused by over-leveraged corporates and bad bank loans, with utilisation of about 70–74%. Process-equipment and transmission margins compressed and Indian EMS was a sub-scale, low-margin business.

The revival came from policy and specific booms rather than a general recovery. Production-linked incentives were launched in April 2020, in the same year utilisation hit its COVID trough of about 63%. From FY22 to FY24 a specialty-chemicals and pharma capex boom, the first-generation ethanol boom and an IPO wave arrived together: Tega listed in December 2021, Syrma in August 2022, Kaynes in November 2022, Avalon in April 2023, INOX India and Azad in December 2023, Jyoti CNC in January 2024 and KRN in October 2024. Eight of the fifteen members have listed since late 2021.

Reading the numbers: the industrial-products KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twelve metrics explain most of what these companies do. Each one has a definition trap, and most companies define at least one of them their own way.

Industrial-products companies publish a great deal of data and almost none of it is standardised. One company's order book includes letters of intent; another's is the lifetime value of contracts that fix prices but not dates. One company's EBITDA includes treasury income; another's excludes one-off acquisition costs. This section takes the twelve metrics that matter, defines each, shows the formula and a worked example in rupees, explains what drives it, sets out what good, average and weak look like by archetype, compares the fifteen companies and names the trap. It then builds an ILLUSTRATIVE profit and loss account for one typical plant, collects the benchmarks and traps in two tables, and ends with a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

1. The equation: how an order becomes revenue, profit and cash

Every company in the bucket can be described by three linked equations. The first turns orders into revenue. For order-driven businesses (A2, A3 machines and gears, A5), revenue in a period is the opening order book multiplied by the share of it executed in the period, plus "book-and-bill" orders won and delivered within the period. For volume businesses (A1 EMS, A4 consumables), revenue is volume times realisation. The second turns revenue into operating profit: revenue times the value-add percentage, minus the fixed conversion cost of people, factories and overheads. The third turns profit into cash: EBITDA minus tax, minus capital expenditure, minus the increase in net working capital, where that increase is roughly the working-capital days divided by 365, multiplied by the increase in revenue.

In plain terms

Three questions summarise any company in this bucket. How many orders does it have and how fast can it turn them into sales? How much of each sale is its own work rather than bought-in parts? 2. Order book and order-book cover

Definition. The order book (or backlog) is the unexecuted value of firm orders at a date. Order-book cover is that value divided by trailing-twelve-month (TTM) revenue, which tells you how many years of current revenue are already booked. Formula: cover = order book ÷ TTM revenue; months of cover = cover × 12. Worked example: Skipper's order book was ₹9,217 crore at June 2026 against TTM revenue of about ₹5,609 crore, so cover is 9,217 ÷ 5,609 = 1.64 times, or about 20 months.

Drivers. Cover rises when order inflow outpaces execution and falls when execution accelerates or orders dry up. It depends on contract length (transmission lines take 12–36 months; glass-lined reactors 10–12) and on how much of the business is short-cycle and never enters the book (spares, aftermarket, catalogue gearboxes). Good, average, weak. For engineered-to-order equipment, 1.2–2.0 times is healthy, 0.9–1.2 is average and below 0.9 risks under-utilised workshops a year later. For EPC and transmission, 2–3 times is good and below 1.5 thin. For EMS, whose books are rolling schedules, above 2 times is strong.

3. Order inflow and book-to-bill

Definition. Order inflow is the value of new firm orders booked in a period. Book-to-bill is inflow divided by revenue (or by the value executed) in the same period; above 1 means the order book is growing. Formula: book-to-bill = order inflow ÷ revenue. Worked example: in Q1 FY27 Jyoti booked ₹601 crore of orders and executed ₹485 crore, a book-to-bill of 601 ÷ 485 = 1.24. INOX India booked a record ₹532 crore against ₹371 crore of revenue, 1.43.

Drivers. End-market capex, bid pricing discipline (Anup deliberately bid less in FY26 to protect margins), large one-off orders and the timing of customer approvals. Good, average, weak: above 1.2 is good, 0.9–1.2 average, below 0.9 weak, but only over at least four quarters. A single quarter can swing wildly on one order.

4. Value-add, or gross margin, and the proxy trap

Definition. The share of revenue left after paying for bought-in materials and components: the company's own value-add. Formula: gross margin = (revenue − cost of materials consumed, including change in inventories) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Kaynes' Q1 FY27 revenue was ₹946.0 crore and raw-material cost ₹620.5 crore, so gross margin = (946.0 − 620.5) ÷ 946.0 = 34.4%, down from 41.3% a year earlier.

Drivers. Product mix (high-mix industrial versus consumer EMS; exotic alloys versus carbon steel), raw-material prices and the lag in passing them on, and how much work is done in-house versus bought out. Good, average, weak: for high-mix EMS above 33% is good, 22–33% average and below 22% weak (mobile assembly runs at 8–15%). For process equipment 40–50% is normal; for precision parts above 60%.

6. Net working-capital days: inventory, receivables and payables

Definition. Net working capital (NWC) is the cash tied up in running the business: inventory plus receivables (and contract assets), minus payables and customer advances. Expressed in days of revenue, it tells you how long each rupee of sales is locked up. Formulas: NWC days = (inventory + receivables − payables − customer advances) ÷ revenue × 365. The components: inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods × 365; receivable days = receivables ÷ revenue × 365; payable days = payables ÷ cost of goods × 365. The cash-conversion cycle (CCC) is inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days. Worked example: Azad's FY26 inventory was ₹329 crore, receivables ₹312 crore and payables ₹85 crore on ₹603 crore of revenue. On a sales basis that is (329 + 312 − 85) ÷ 603 × 365 = 337 days, nearly a year of revenue tied up.

Drivers. Material lead-times (6–8 months for electronics components in 2026; a year for superalloy forgings), customer type (state distributors and small machine shops pay slowly; refiners and OEMs pay faster), advance terms, supplier credit, and growth itself, since a fast-growing company carries inventory for next quarter's larger sales. Good, average, weak: under 60 days is good, 60–120 average and above 120 weak; for receivables alone, under 90 days is good and above 150 weak.

9. Return on capital employed (ROCE)

Definition. Operating profit after depreciation (EBIT) as a share of the capital that funds the business: equity plus debt (some definitions net off cash). Formula: ROCE = EBIT ÷ average capital employed. Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE): a company with ₹200 crore of EBIT and ₹800 crore of average equity plus debt earns 200 ÷ 800 = 25%. If it raises ₹400 crore of new equity and leaves it in the bank for a year, capital employed becomes ₹1,200 crore and ROCE falls to 16.7% even though the business has not changed.

Drivers. EBITDA margin × asset turnover, minus depreciation, all divided by the working capital and fixed assets the business needs, plus any idle cash. Good, average, weak: above 25% good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak.

17. How to read an industrial-products company's quarterly results in ten minutes

A ten-minute routine for any of the fifteen, in order of importance:

  1. Identify the archetype first. It tells you which three numbers matter most: for EMS, gross margin, working capital and cash flow; for process equipment, order inflow, order book and EBITDA margin; for precision parts, utilisation of new plants, working capital and margin; for annuities, volume and recurring share; for transmission, order book, margin per tonne and interest cost.
  2. Read revenue growth against the order book. Did revenue grow faster or slower than last year's order book implied? A shortfall with a full book means an execution or dispatch problem (INOX's Gulf shipping holds); a shortfall with a thin book means a demand problem (Anup's weak FY26 bookings).
  3. Check order inflow and book-to-bill. Look at the last four quarters, not one. Is the book growing, and at what margin was it booked? Anup's management said its new orders were at margins "very close to historic".
  4. Check gross margin against input prices. If components, steel, copper or alloys moved, did gross margin move with them? A fall that management attributes to a pass-through lag should reverse within two quarters.
  5. Check EBITDA margin on a consistent basis. Strip out other income, exceptional items and foreign-exchange gains or losses; compare with the same quarter last year, since many of these businesses are seasonal.
  6. Read below EBITDA. Depreciation and interest rise when new plants are commissioned (Kaynes, Azad); tax-rate swings can manufacture profit growth (Azad's 9.3% quarter). Compare profit before tax with last year before reading net profit.
  7. Find working capital. Many companies disclose NWC days in the presentation; otherwise use the half-yearly balance sheet. Rising inventory and receivables faster than revenue is the earliest sign of cash strain.
  8. Find cash flow. At the half-year and year-end, compute operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA. Below 40% for a second year needs an explanation; negative needs a funding plan.
  9. Check the balance sheet and funding. Net debt, any new equity raise, preference shares, pledges and the share count. Growth funded by repeated equity issues dilutes per-share value even when profit rises.
  10. Listen for three phrases on the call. "Timing slippage" (a plant or order is late), "strategic inventory" (cash is going into stock) and "customer funding issues" (receivables are at risk). Each has preceded a weak quarter in this bucket in 2026.
In plain terms

Start with what kind of company it is, then check whether orders are coming in, whether the company kept its margin on them, and whether the profit turned into cash. If the answers are yes, yes and yes, the quarter was good whatever the headline growth. If the third answer is no for the second year running, the headline growth does not matter much.

Valuation versus cash: what a price of about 73 times earnings assumes Deep dive

The bucket trades at six times revenue and about 73 times trailing profit. Run the arithmetic backwards and it requires a decade of earnings growth in the mid-twenties.

At ₹2,29,204 crore, the fifteen companies trade at 70.9 times FY26 profit and about 73 times trailing-twelve-month profit (₹3,122 crore). The median company is also at about 73 times. For context, a company growing earnings at 15% a year and paying out little would usually be valued at 25–35 times. Four explanations account for most of the premium. First, scarcity: domestic mid- and small-cap funds, fed by SIP inflows of roughly ₹25,000–28,000 crore a month, chase a small set of listed companies exposed to electronics, aerospace, defence, the energy transition and China+1, and thirteen of the fifteen have free floats of 25–60%. Second, growth duration: FY23–26 revenue CAGRs of 30–50% at several members, and FY27 guidance of 25–30% or more at Jyoti, Azad and Avalon. Third, policy optionality from ECMS, the semiconductor mission, defence, the transmission plan and green fuels. Fourth, a perception of low capital intensity that is partly wrong, because the fastest growers are working-capital heavy and equity-funded.

The reverse DCF

A reverse discounted-cash-flow calculation asks what growth today's price implies. The ILLUSTRATIVE version used here assumes a 12% cost of equity, ignores dividends (yields are under 1%) and assumes that in ten years the stock trades at a "mature" 25 times earnings. To earn exactly 12% a year, the share price must rise by 1.12 to the power ten, or 3.106 times. At about 73 times today, the future price is 73 × 3.106 = 226.7 times today's earnings; divided by the exit multiple of 25, earnings must rise 9.07 times, which is 24.7% a year compounded for ten years. On FY26 profit (70.9×) the answer is about 24.3%.

When a share price assumes long-duration compounding, a slowdown or a cash-conversion disappointment changes the expected duration, not just next year's earnings, and the de-rating is large. The 2025–26 record shows the pattern. Kaynes fell 54% from its 52-week high as cash flow and governance questions surfaced, even while revenue grew 40% in Q1 FY27. Anup fell 39% after its weak quarter; Praj 33% as ethanol orders stalled; Elecon 24% after two guidance cuts; Honeywell Automation 22% on slow growth. The stocks near their highs (Azad, Syrma, Tega, Jyoti) are those whose latest quarters met or beat expectations.

Other multiples tell the same story

EV/EBITDA on trailing operating profit (dossier calculations) runs from about 12 times at Skipper and Tega (pro forma) and 16 times at GMM to 54–64 times at INOX India and KRN, 61 times at Lloyds, 76 times at Avalon and 81 times at Azad. On every measure, the ordering is roughly the inverse of the cash-conversion ordering in the KPI section.

That inversion is a fragility, not a forecast. It can persist while growth stays high, and some of the expensive companies may convert their working capital into cash as plants mature, which would make today's multiples look less extreme in hindsight. But the bucket has already shown, in 2025–26, what happens when growth and cash part company in a stock priced at 60–90 times: a fall of a third to a half, regardless of how good the long-term story is. The most useful single question for each holding is therefore not "how fast will it grow?" but "when will its growth start paying for itself?"

In plain terms

The market is paying the most for the companies that turn the least of their profit into cash, on the assumption that the cash will come later. When a company's numbers have cast doubt on that assumption, its shares have fallen by a third to a half within a year. That is the main risk in owning this bucket at today's prices.

Three readings follow. First, the gap is largest where the Indian business is least differentiated from its global peers. Build-to-print EMS is a mature, competitive business abroad, valued at 12–25 times; Indian EMS trades at three to six times that, on the expectation that it will grow faster for longer and climb into components. Second, the gap is smallest where the scarcity is global: Howmet, the leading Western airfoil and aero-structures supplier, trades at roughly 40–50 times because the same aero-engine shortage that benefits Azad benefits it. Azad's premium over Howmet is a premium for faster growth from a smaller base, and Howmet's long record of converting profit into cash is something Azad does not yet have. Third, Indian members' cost advantage is real. Indian engineering hours are roughly 30–40% cheaper than Western ones, and the CII estimates India's cost gap against China in electronics components at 10–20%; Western OEMs accept that gap for supply security. That is a durable reason for Indian suppliers to win share, not a reason for their earnings to be worth three times as much as their peers'.

The global demand picture also explains why some Indian archetypes are doing better than others. Global grid investment is expected to nearly double from US$1,644 billion over CY19–23 to US$3,053 billion over CY24–29, with India about 4% of the total, so Skipper's export ambitions are aimed at a market twenty-five times its home one. The global LNG liquefaction wave and US pharma reshoring underpin INOX India and GMM Pfaudler's overseas order books. And GE Aerospace's backlog above US$210 billion, with commercial services of about US$170 billion, means the aero-parts shortage is measured in years.

In plain terms

Abroad, a company that assembles circuit boards for other firms is valued at about 18 times its profit, a heat-exchanger maker at about 27 and a top airfoil supplier at about 45. In India, the equivalents trade at 64 to 139. Some of that gap is earned by faster growth. But growth eventually slows, and when it does, Indian multiples will be measured against these global ones.

Technology and disruption: what could move value between the archetypes

The near-term technology shifts mostly favour the members: advanced packaging, liquid hydrogen, hybrid liners, HVDC. The long-term risks are additive manufacturing and automation that erodes India's labour-cost edge.

Technology change in this bucket works on two timescales. Over three to five years, most of the visible shifts create demand for what the members make or are building: chip packaging, multilayer boards, liquid-hydrogen tanks, modular process plants, hybrid mill liners, HVDC lines and grid-scale gas turbines. Over ten to fifteen years, a few shifts could take value away: 3D-printed engine parts, lights-out electronics assembly that narrows India's labour-cost advantage, and transmission upgrades that need fewer new towers.

EMS has the most at stake. The global value of electronics sits in chips and components; India imports most of them, including about 90% of bare boards. Advanced packaging, multilayer and high-density boards and power modules for electric vehicles are the technologies that would let Kaynes and Syrma capture more of each product's value. They are also the most capital-intensive and fastest-moving technologies in the bucket: a packaging line qualified today may need upgrading within a few years, and customers qualify each new product separately. Meanwhile, automated assembly lines reduce the labour content of contract electronics, which narrows one of India's advantages over China and Vietnam.

Process equipment's technology bets are policy bets. Liquid hydrogen needs tanks that hold −253 °C with minimal boil-off, which is INOX India's core vacuum-insulation know-how; small-scale LNG, carbon capture and zero-liquid-discharge plants all use the welding, metallurgy and certification skills of A2 shops. But the demand for each depends on mandates and subsidies (Section 12's cycle) rather than on technology alone.

Precision parts face the slowest and biggest threat. Additive manufacturing already prints some jet-engine fuel nozzles. If it spreads to blades and vanes over the next ten to fifteen years, some machining content would move from suppliers such as Azad to printer operators, possibly the OEMs themselves. The offset is that printed parts still need finishing, inspection and qualification, and that Azad's dedicated-plant contracts lock in current designs for many years.

Transmission technology changes the tonnage, not the need. HVDC links need fewer but heavier towers; compact and monopole designs suit urban corridors; re-conductoring existing lines with high-temperature conductors can add capacity without new towers. Skipper's monopoles and heavier 765 kV towers are positioned for the first two; the third is a modest long-term substitute.

In plain terms

For the next few years, new technology mostly means new orders for these companies: chip-packaging plants, hydrogen tanks, heavier transmission towers, smarter mill liners. The longer-term risks are 3D printing of turbine parts and robots that make cheap labour matter less. Neither is likely to show up in results before the end of the decade.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the bucket at 73 times earnings

The bull case rests on supply-constrained global demand, policy support and share gains. The bear case rests on cash conversion, policy overshoot and price.

The two cases are not mirror images. The bull case is mostly about demand and position: the members sell into markets that are short of qualified suppliers or are being built by policy, and they are taking share. The bear case is mostly about money: how much of the growth turns into cash, how much new capacity policy is creating, and how much growth today's prices already assume. Both cases are supported by the numbers in this report; which one dominates will differ by company and archetype.

The bull case

  • Supply-constrained global demand. GE Vernova's 116 GW of gas backlog and slots, GE Aerospace's US$210 billion-plus backlog and ~US$3 trillion of global grid spending reward qualified capacity; Azad, INOX India, Skipper and Tega export into these markets.
  • China+1 favours qualified Indian suppliers. Western OEMs pay for supply security on airfoils, cryogenic vessels, heat exchangers and high-mix electronics; exports grew 61% at Syrma and 177% at KRN in Q1 FY27.
  • A regulated domestic order stream. ₹9.15 lakh crore of transmission to 2032, with prices adjusted by formula for steel and zinc; Skipper has held a 10% margin for three years.
  • Policy support for the value climb. ECMS (₹69,548 crore approved), the semiconductor mission and defence indigenisation help EMS and precision makers move up the stack.
  • Record order books almost everywhere. Skipper ₹9,217 crore, Kaynes ₹8,904 crore, Syrma ₹6,770 crore, Jyoti ₹4,848 crore, Praj ₹4,589 crore, GMM and INOX India at records.
  • Domestic capex is broadening. Bank-sanctioned project cost +18% in FY26; capital-goods output +14.6% in March 2026; utilisation at the expansion threshold.

The bear case

  • Profit is not becoming cash. Kaynes, Lloyds, KRN and Azad had negative operating cash flow in FY26; Jyoti's three-year total is negative; growth is funded by equity and debt.
  • Valuation assumes a decade of ~25% compounding. About 73× trailing earnings and six times revenue; the reverse DCF needs 24–33% a year at the most expensive names.
  • Policy overshoot. Ethanol showed the pattern (Praj profit −90%); smart meters became a receivables problem; ECMS has approved more investment than targeted.
  • Operating leverage cuts both ways. Anup's −28.5% revenue quarter wiped out its profit; new plants at Kaynes, Azad and KRN add depreciation and interest before revenue.
  • Concentration and governance. One customer is ~24% of INOX India's book; ~32% of Lloyds' own book is related-party; Kaynes' disclosures were questioned; Tega carries ~₹11,200 crore of group debt.
  • Trade and geopolitics. Section 232 tariffs on full customs value rise to 25% for grid equipment in 2028; the Hormuz shock showed how fast freight and components can disrupt exports.

Weighing them by archetype gives a more useful answer than weighing them for the bucket. The bull case is strongest for A3 precision parts and A5 transmission, where demand is supply-constrained or regulated; the question there is price (Azad) or execution and balance sheet (Skipper). The bear case is strongest for A1 EMS and the fast-ramping A2 names, where cash conversion is weakest and valuations highest. A4 has become two separate debates: Honeywell Automation's slow growth against its cash pile, and Tega's leverage against its consumables franchise. For the bucket as a whole, the single variable that would most improve the bull case is cash conversion: if the four cash-negative growers turned operating cash flow positive at 50% or more of EBITDA in FY27, the valuation argument would change. If they did not, the 2025–26 de-ratings suggest what would follow.
