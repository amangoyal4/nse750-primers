00

The one-page view: five businesses, one free rail

Every listed Indian fintech is trying to answer one question: how do you earn money next to payment rails the state has decided should cost the user nothing?

India built the world's largest real-time payments system and then made it free. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) handled 24,162 crore transactions worth ₹314 lakh crore in FY26, about 86% of all retail digital payments by count, and since 1 January 2020 a merchant accepting a UPI payment has paid no merchant discount rate (MDR). That one policy choice shapes everything in this report. The companies that move the money cannot charge for the movement itself, so they earn their living from what sits around it: renting a soundbox to a kirana store, arranging an EMI on a television, selling a gift card to a brand, finding a borrower for a bank, or selling a health policy for an insurer.

The five companies in the NSE 750 fintech sub-sector are therefore not one business but four or five. PB Fintech (Policybazaar and Paisabazaar) is an insurance and credit marketplace paid by insurers and lenders. Paytm (One97 Communications) is a merchant payments network that monetises through device subscriptions and, increasingly, loan distribution. Pine Labs is merchant-acquiring and card-issuing infrastructure, strongest in large-format retail, EMI and gift cards. AvenuesAI (the renamed Infibeam Avenues, owner of CCAvenue) is an online payment gateway that books its revenue gross. Zaggle sells corporate spend-management software, rewards and co-branded cards issued through partner banks. What unites them is that each earns a thin slice of a very large flow, and that the size of each slice is set, in the end, by a regulator.

The autumn of 2026 then delivered two regulatory shocks in eight days, pointing in opposite directions. On 15 September the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) circulated a framework to reintroduce an MDR of 0.4% on merchant UPI payments above ₹2,000, to start on 15 October; on 13 October it deferred the start to "after Diwali, on a date to be notified". On 23 September the insurance regulator, IRDAI, published a consultation paper proposing product-wise caps on what insurers may pay distributors, including 15% on a new individual health policy sold by an intermediary such as Policybazaar. PB Fintech's share price fell 36% the next day. Since the July primers, PB Fintech has lost about ₹26,700 crore of market value and Paytm has gained about ₹25,800 crore.

Five forces define the sub-sector, and the report is organised around them. First, the state made the rail free: zero MDR on UPI and RuPay debit since 2020, a government incentive scheme that still pays about ₹2,000 crore a year, and an MDR that very nearly came back this October. Second, the regulator sets the price: RBI's payment-aggregator directions, its digital-lending rules and the 5% cap on default loss guarantees, NPCI's market-share cap and MDR circular, and IRDAI's commission paper together decide what each company may charge. Third, profitability arrived through cost cuts, mix and treasury income, not through take rates, and the companies' "adjusted EBITDA" excludes stock-based pay that runs to 2–6% of revenue. Fourth, credit distribution is the profit engine, and it is pro-cyclical: it rides on lenders' appetite for small unsecured loans. Fifth, promoter-less shareholder registers and departing foreign investors set the supply of shares, which explains much of the price action.

In plain terms

These five companies sit next to enormous rivers of money and are allowed to scoop only a cupful each. The government decided that UPI payments should be free to merchants, so the firms earn from rent on devices, fees for arranging loans and commissions for selling insurance. In FY26 they finally made money, but two-thirds of the combined profit came from interest on cash in the bank rather than from the businesses themselves. And in September 2026 two regulators showed how quickly the size of the cupful can change.

Valuations price these forces very differently. Paytm, at about 160 times trailing reported earnings (128 times on screener.in's ex-exceptional basis), is 59% of the group's market value on 30% of its revenue. PB Fintech, repriced by IRDAI, now trades at 62.8 times. Pine Labs, still 30% below its November 2025 IPO price, is at about 140 times a profit that is almost entirely interest income. AvenuesAI (0.85 times book) and Zaggle (15.6 times earnings) are priced as low-quality businesses rather than as growth stocks. 01

How the money moves: a payment, a policy and a loan

Each member sits at a different point on three value chains. Knowing which point tells you who pays it, and who can stop paying it.

A digital payment in India passes through five or six hands in under two seconds. A customer opens an app (PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm) or taps a card; the app's sponsor bank or the card network routes the instruction; the customer's own bank (the "remitter" or "issuer") debits the account; the merchant's acquiring bank or payment aggregator (PA) receives the funds and settles them to the merchant, sometimes the same day and sometimes after a day or two. On a card payment the merchant pays an MDR, most of which goes to the bank that issued the card as "interchange". On a UPI payment nobody pays anything. The fintechs in this report make money at the edges of this chain: by owning the device on the counter, by being the aggregator that settles the funds, by being the app that the customer opens, and by selling things to the merchant or the customer once the relationship exists.

The difference between a card and a UPI payment is the whole economics of the sector in miniature. On a ₹1,000 credit-card purchase at a large merchant paying an MDR of about 1.9%, the merchant gives up roughly ₹19. Around ₹16 of that goes to the bank that issued the card, which uses it to fund reward points and EMI offers; about ₹1 goes to the card network; and the acquirer or aggregator keeps roughly ₹2, out of which it must pay for the terminal, connectivity, settlement and its sales force. The acquirer's share, around 15–30 basis points (bps, hundredths of a percent) of the transaction, is a thin slice even on a card. On a ₹1,000 UPI payment the slice is zero, because the MDR is zero.

The insurance chain is simpler and far richer per rupee of flow. A customer compares policies on Policybazaar or buys through one of PB Partners' point-of-sale agents (POSPs) in a small town; the insurer issues the policy and pays PB a commission plus, until now, various "rewards" and marketing payments; when the policy renews the following year, PB earns again, at a lower rate. PB kept about ₹19–21 of every ₹100 of premium it sold in FY26 as insurance revenue, and about ₹2.2 as profit (INDICATIVE derivations). The lending chain is similar: Paytm or Paisabazaar finds a borrower, a bank or NBFC makes the loan from its own balance sheet, and the platform earns a sourcing fee and sometimes a collection fee. Paytm's financial-services revenue was ₹2,594 crore in FY26.

In plain terms

Moving money is the cheap part; owning the customer is the valuable part. A payments company that only routes UPI earns nothing. One that also rents the merchant a speaker, lends him working capital and sells his family a health policy can earn a great deal. Every listed fintech is trying to climb from the bottom of that ladder to the top, and every rung it climbs brings it under another regulator.

Where the value lands is therefore predictable. Insurance distribution captures the most per rupee of flow: about 20% of premium as revenue. Lending distribution captures a few percent of the loan amount. Gift cards sit in between: Pine Labs' issuing business earned ₹874 crore on ₹64,000 crore of gift-card value in FY26, about 1.4%. Payments capture slivers: Pine Labs earned 15.8 bps of its gross transaction value (GTV) as revenue and well under 1 bp as profit; AvenuesAI's payments net take rate was 7 bps in FY26 and 5.3 bps in Q1 FY27. This ordering explains why Paytm's strategy since 2024 has been to push loan distribution, why Pine Labs leans on EMI and gift cards, and why IRDAI's paper hit PB Fintech's share price harder than anything that happened to the payments companies.

02

The flows, sized: ₹314 lakh crore of UPI and a shrinking card terminal

UPI grew at 61% a year by count for five years. Card-acceptance hardware has started to shrink. The fintechs' revenue pools are tiny fractions of these flows.

The base rail is UPI, and its growth has no precedent. In FY21 it carried 22.3 billion transactions worth ₹41 lakh crore; in FY26 it carried 241.6 billion worth ₹314 lakh crore, according to RBI's Payment System Indicators. That is a compound annual growth rate of 61% in volume and 50% in value over five years. In September 2026 UPI processed about 24.1 billion transactions worth ₹29.4 lakh crore in a single month, more than 800 million a day. Growth is slowing in percentage terms, from +106% in FY22 to +30% in FY26 by count, but the absolute additions are still enormous: FY26 added 56 billion transactions, more than the whole of FY22.

03

Free UPI and the MDR that almost came back Deep dive

The state made the biggest payment rail free, pays about ₹2,000 crore a year to keep it running, and in 2026 built the legal route to charge for it again. Then it waited until after Diwali.

Zero MDR is a policy, not a law of nature. In the July 2019 Budget the government announced that businesses with turnover above ₹50 crore must offer "low-cost" digital payment modes, and the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2019 inserted s.269SU into the Income-tax Act and s.10A into the Payment and Settlement Systems Act to bar any charge on the prescribed modes: BHIM-UPI, UPI QR codes and RuPay debit cards. From 1 January 2020, a merchant accepting UPI or RuPay debit paid nothing, and nobody else in the chain was allowed to charge the merchant either. The intent was to drive cash out of retail. It worked: UPI volume grew more than tenfold between FY21 and FY26.

But a payment costs money to process. The customer's bank runs its core banking system, the app's sponsor bank runs the UPI switch interface, the app runs servers and customer support, and the merchant's aggregator runs settlement and fraud checks. Industry estimates put the cost to payment service provider banks alone at roughly ₹0.25–0.50 a transaction (INDICATIVE). At 24,162 crore transactions a year, even ₹0.25 each would be ₹6,000 crore of cost. Under zero MDR, that cost is paid from three sources: the banks' and apps' own pockets, cross-subsidy from other products (devices, loans, insurance, advertising) and a government incentive scheme.

The incentive scheme did not collapse

The government has paid an annual incentive for low-value BHIM-UPI P2M transactions (up to ₹2,000, to small merchants) and RuPay debit since FY22. The rate has been around 0.15% of eligible value, with 80% paid unconditionally and 20% linked to banks' technical uptime and decline rates. Some commentary read the FY26 budget line of ₹437 crore as a collapse in support and a hidden motive for bringing back the MDR. The Union Budget 2026-27 documents say otherwise. FY25 actual spending was ₹1,922.77 crore. FY26 was budgeted at ₹437 crore but the revised estimate was ₹2,196.21 crore, five times the budget line. FY27 is budgeted at ₹2,000 crore. The state is still paying about ₹2,000 crore a year while it builds a route to charge.

The law moved in August 2026

The decisive change came quietly. In August 2026 Parliament amended s.10A of the PSS Act through the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 (Lok Sabha on 6 August, Rajya Sabha around 10–11 August, both by voice vote). The amendment replaces the hard-coded reference to s.269SU with a government notification power: the government will now decide by notification which payment modes stay charge-free. The Finance Minister described it as "enabling only" and left the decision to NPCI's UPI Steering Committee. Presidential assent and notification were not confirmed in the sources available for this report, so the precise legal status should be treated as unconfirmed. AvenuesAI's management, on its 11 August call, already described the Act as passed by the Lok Sabha and awaiting the Rajya Sabha.

Who gains? The biggest winners in rupees would be the customer's banks, which receive 40% for doing nothing new. Among the listed fintechs the picture differs by role. The regulator sets the price

In fintech, "pricing power" is mostly regulatory forbearance. Five rule-makers decide what these companies may charge, hold and promise.

A consumer company sets its own prices and lives with the demand that follows. A fintech negotiates with its customers inside a box drawn by a regulator, and the box moves. RBI decides who may act as a payment aggregator, how much capital it needs, where merchant money must sit and whether it may earn interest on it. RBI also decides how digital loans are sourced and how much of the lender's loss a platform may guarantee. NPCI sets UPI's operating rules, the MDR and the share any one app may hold. IRDAI sets how much insurers may spend on distribution. SEBI governs listing and stock-based pay. Behind them sit the Finance Ministry, which can change the zero-MDR law, and the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade, whose Press Note 3 controls investment from China.

Payment aggregators: a licence is now the capacity constraint

RBI's Regulation of Payment Aggregators Directions, 2025, issued on 15 September 2025, consolidated earlier guidelines and created three categories: PA-P for physical (in-store) acquiring, PA-O for online, and PA-CB for cross-border, with a ₹25 lakh per-transaction limit on cross-border. Banks need no separate authorisation; non-banks do. A non-bank needs ₹15 crore of net worth when it applies and ₹25 crore by the end of its third financial year. Existing physical-only aggregators had to apply by 31 December 2025 or wind up by 28 February 2026. Merchant money must sit in an escrow account with a scheduled commercial bank and may earn interest only on its "core portion", which caps the float income an aggregator can earn on settlement balances. For the listed companies, licences are now in hand: Pine Labs received final PA-O authorisation on 9 May 2025 and a revised certificate on 10 November 2025 covering PA-O, PA-P and PA-CB under a single authorisation, plus a PPI licence. Paytm Payments Services (PPSL) received in-principle online authorisation in August 2025, after government approval of One97's downstream investment under Press Note 3, and its final certificate in November 2025; Paytm then slump-sold its offline merchant business to PPSL for ₹975 crore with effect from 30 November 2025 to comply. AvenuesAI holds online and offline PA authorisation and received a PPI authorisation in Q1 FY27. Zaggle holds no PA or PPI licence of its own; its cards are issued by partner banks.

Digital lending: the 5% guarantee cap shapes the model

Credit distribution is regulated through the lender. RBI's September 2022 digital-lending guidelines required loans to flow directly between lender and borrower, made the lender (not the borrower) pay the platform's fee, and required a key fact statement with the all-in annual percentage rate. In June 2023 RBI capped default loss guarantees (DLG, earlier called first-loss default guarantees or FLDG) at 5% of the portfolio, to be backed by cash, a lien-marked deposit or a bank guarantee. The Digital Lending Directions, 2025 (8 May 2025) consolidated both, extended the rules to multi-lender marketplaces such as Paisabazaar (which must show all matching offers), required digital lending apps to be reported on RBI's CIMS portal and barred DLG on revolving credit and credit cards. The 5% cap is a price rule in disguise: a platform that cannot guarantee more than 5% of a book cannot take more of the credit risk, and therefore cannot charge for it. Paytm says it now distributes "mostly without any FLDG". RBI's November 2023 increase in risk weights on unsecured consumer credit and on bank lending to NBFCs (by 25 percentage points) shows the other lever: by raising lenders' capital cost, RBI shrank Paytm's small-ticket postpaid and personal-loan distribution almost overnight.

NPCI: the market-share cap that never arrives

NPCI first announced a 30% cap on any single app's share of UPI volume in November 2020. It has been deferred repeatedly; the current deadline is 31 December 2026. PhonePe (45.6%) and Google Pay (32.3%) together held about 78% of August 2026 volume. Enforcement would force both to shed share, which in principle helps Paytm (8.0%) and smaller apps; repeated deferrals suggest NPCI does not want to break consumer habits. Investors should treat the cap as a recurring headline rather than a base case.

PPIs, gift cards and the breakage question

Gift cards and prepaid cards are PPIs under RBI rules. RBI's draft PPI master direction of April 2026 touched the treatment of unused balances (breakage); Pine Labs publicly called a report on its breakage exposure "speculative, incorrect and misleading", arguing that under its co-branded model the balances belong to the partner brands. RBI fined Pine Labs ₹3.1 lakh in March 2026 for KYC lapses in full-KYC PPIs, a trivial sum but a reminder of supervision. On tax, the GST Council clarified in December 2024 that unredeemed vouchers are not a "supply", which removed a ₹13.4 crore service-tax demand on breakage from Pine Labs' contingent liabilities; a separate ₹214 crore GST demand on input credit for gift-card co-branding and advertising costs is stayed by the Karnataka High Court. The final PPI master direction had not been confirmed as issued.

In plain terms

Almost every important price in this business is written by someone other than the company: the fee on a UPI payment, the commission on a health policy, the share of a loan's losses a platform may cover, even whether a payments company may earn interest on the money passing through it. That is why these stocks move on circulars rather than on quarterly results, and why a "moat" in fintech often turns out to be a licence that others do not yet have.

The pattern across regulators is consistent. When a fintech's revenue line grows much faster than the underlying activity, as broker health commissions did after 2023 or as small-ticket consumer loans did before November 2023, a regulator eventually steps in. The two exceptions run the other way: the regulator can also create revenue, as NPCI tried to do with the MDR. For an investor the practical rule is to ask, for each revenue line, who could change its price by circular, and how much that line has outgrown the economy around it.

The commission cliff: IRDAI's paper and the insurance marketplace Deep dive

IRDAI freed commissions in 2023, watched broker payouts on retail health triple, and on 23 September 2026 proposed to cap them product by product. PB Fintech lost 36% of its value in a day.

PB Fintech's business is simple to describe. Policybazaar shows a customer competing policies, the customer buys, and the insurer pays Policybazaar a commission on the premium plus, until now, various rewards and marketing payments; when the policy renews, PB earns again. PB Partners does the same through more than 5 lakh registered point-of-sale agents, mostly in smaller towns. In FY26 the group sold ₹29,934 crore of premium (+42%) and earned ₹6,794 crore of revenue, of which roughly 92% came from insurance. The economics therefore rest on one rate: how much an insurer pays a distributor for each rupee of premium. Since April 2023 that rate has been set by each insurer's board within an overall limit on its expenses of management (EoM). IRDAI now proposes to set it again, product by product.

Why IRDAI moved: the evidence in its own paper

The consultation paper, titled "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution", is unusually direct about its motive. Between FY23 and FY25, premium written through general-insurance brokers rose 37% while commissions paid to them rose 173%. The average broker commission doubled from 8.5% to 17% of premium. On retail health it went from about 10% to about 30%; on motor from about 9% to about 25% (motor premium +34%, motor commissions +259%). Retail lines were 54% of broker premium but about 78% of broker commissions. On the life side, IRDAI found ULIP payouts that had risen from about 5% to about 40% of first-year premium, other savings products paying 29–60% (above 65% in some cases), NBFC corporate agencies around 42% and group credit-life around 45%. Removing the caps in 2023 was meant to let insurers innovate within an overall budget; instead, distributors captured the budget.

Much of what insurers pay web aggregators has historically come as rewards, marketing support and fees for technology or lead services; counting all of it as commission means PB cannot simply relabel payments.

Most of that annuity is health, where the proposed IDE renewal cap is 5%. Insurers have their own incentive to squeeze: with EoM ceilings falling from FY2027-28, they will cut distribution spending wherever they can, so PB faces pressure from both the cap and its customers. The mitigants are real but partial: life revenue is broadly unaffected, the paper is a draft, PB's brand and traffic give it bargaining power with insurers that want young health customers, health insurers' combined operating ratio of about 93% gives them little room to absorb higher payouts anyway, and FY27 earnings are not affected on management's reading.

In plain terms

Insurers were paying online and broker channels up to a third of a new health policy's premium to win customers. The regulator thinks that is too much and wants to cap it at 15%, and at 5% when the policy renews. Policybazaar's own estimate is that its general-insurance revenue could fall to about a third of today's level. The rules are not final and would not bite until April 2027 at the earliest, but the market immediately marked down the value of PB's future profits by more than a third.

Global precedent is not encouraging for distributors. In the US, Medicare insurance brokers such as eHealth de-rated to below one times sales after insurers cut commissions and regulators tightened marketing rules; in the UK, price-comparison sites adjusted to a ban on "price walking" in home and motor insurance by shifting mix and cutting costs, and trade on low-teens earnings multiples (INDICATIVE, secondary data). Regulators that have cut distributor pay rarely reverse it. The open question for PB is whether it can convert traffic and data into other revenue (manufacturing, services to insurers that do not count as remuneration, healthcare through its 28.46% associate PB Healthcare) faster than the commission line shrinks. See also the general insurance and life insurance reports for the insurers' side of the same paper.

Profit without a take rate: cost cuts, mix and the interest on IPO cash

All five made money in FY26, but not because anyone charged more. Operating leverage, stock-pay roll-off and treasury income did the work, and the headline "adjusted EBITDA" leaves a lot out.

The usual path to profit for a platform is to raise its take rate once customers depend on it. Indian fintech took the other path. Take rates fell or stayed flat almost everywhere: AvenuesAI's payments net take rate dropped from 11.7 bps in FY25 to 7 bps in FY26 and 5.3 bps in Q1 FY27; Paytm's net payment revenue including devices slipped from 8.8 to 8.4 bps of GMV in the year to Q1 FY27; Pine Labs' contribution margin fell from 78% to 72% over the same five quarters. Profits came instead from three other places: cutting and freezing costs while volumes grew, shifting the mix toward higher-margin lines such as loan distribution and gift-card processing, and earning interest on the cash raised in IPOs and placements.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes

The cost structures differ sharply by model. At Paytm, 45% of revenue goes straight out again as direct costs (payment-processing charges paid to banks, cashback, device and other direct costs), leaving a contribution margin of 55%; employees, including stock-based pay, take another 30%. At Pine Labs only about 28% goes in direct costs, but people take a third. PB Fintech's biggest lines are employees (36%, much of it the contact centre), payouts to POSP agents and B2B partners (28%) and advertising (18%). Each structure tells you where the lever is: Paytm earns more when processing costs fall or lending grows, Pine Labs when its salespeople become productive, and PB when advertising buys more premium per rupee.

Treasury income is real money, but it has three weaknesses as a source of profit. It does not grow with the business; it shrinks as cash is spent on acquisitions, buybacks or losses; and it moves with interest rates. In plain terms

The fintechs did not become profitable by charging customers more. They stopped hiring, cut thousands of jobs, saw their stock-option bills shrink and earned interest on the cash they raised from investors. For the group, about two rupees of every three of pre-tax profit in FY26 came from that interest. The businesses themselves earned only about ₹650 crore before tax on ₹28,000 crore of revenue. That is improving fast, but it is the number to watch, not the headline profit.

Mix is the fourth lever, and it shows up in each company differently. Paytm's revenue has shifted toward financial services (31% of FY26 revenue, 33% in Q1 FY27), which carry no processing cost. Pine Labs' issuing and acquiring platform (IAP), whose gift-card processing earns close to 100% contribution margin, grew 31% in Q1 FY27 against 15% for its terminal business, though international expansion through lower-margin distribution is diluting it. PB's core online insurance business earned a 21% adjusted EBITDA margin over the twelve months to June 2026, while its New Initiatives (PB Partners, UAE, PB for Business) lost 4%. Zaggle moved the other way, deliberately slowing its program-fee business to about 10% growth to protect cash. The quality of earnings across the group is improving, quarter by quarter, but it is not yet clean, and the next two years will show how much of FY26's profit was structural.

Credit: the pro-cyclical profit engine

Loan distribution is the fastest-growing, highest-margin line at Paytm and the recovering engine at Paisabazaar. It rides on lenders' appetite for small unsecured loans, which RBI has throttled before and can throttle again.

A payments relationship gives a platform something no bank has: daily data on a small merchant's sales. That data makes it possible to offer a ₹1–3 lakh working-capital loan, repaid automatically from the merchant's next weeks of collections, to a shopkeeper a bank would never visit. The platform does not lend its own money; it sources the borrower, runs the checks and collects, and a bank or NBFC books the loan and pays a fee. This is the most profitable thing a payments company can sell, because the fee is a percentage of the loan rather than a fraction of a basis point of a payment, and there is no processing cost against it.

Paytm: a third of revenue, and rising

Paytm's financial-services revenue (loan distribution plus smaller insurance and wealth lines) grew from ₹1,703 crore in FY25 to ₹2,594 crore in FY26 (+52%) and to ₹814 crore in Q1 FY27 (+45% YoY). It was 33% of Q1 revenue, up from 20% in FY24. Management describes the book as roughly 80% merchant loans and 20% consumer, distributed "mostly without any FLDG" for a double-digit number of lenders, including large banks, which together have "four to six times more capital than we are disbursing". More than half of merchant-loan disbursals go to repeat borrowers, and Paytm had 7.6 lakh key financial-services customers in June 2026 (+34%). It no longer discloses the value of loans distributed, so the "distribution take" (revenue as a share of disbursal) cannot be computed; the dossier's INDICATIVE estimate of 3.5–4.5% is unverified.

Why this engine is pro-cyclical

Credit distribution depends on three things outside the platform's control: lenders' cost of funds, their capital rules and borrowers' repayment behaviour. All three turn together. The November 2023 risk-weight increase raised banks' and NBFCs' capital cost on exactly these loans; Paytm responded within weeks by cutting small-ticket postpaid and personal loans, and its financial-services revenue fell 15% in FY25. RBI's hike in the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 raises lenders' funding costs again. Unsecured small-business and consumer loans are also the first to show stress in a slowdown. The DLG cap limits how much risk a platform can take to keep lenders interested, which protects the platform's balance sheet but also means it cannot buy volume with guarantees when lenders retreat.

In plain terms

Arranging loans is where a payments app earns real money, because it gets a percentage of the loan rather than a sliver of a payment. But the platform only earns if a bank wants to lend. When RBI makes such loans more expensive for banks, or when borrowers start missing payments, the banks pull back and the platform's best income line shrinks quickly, even if nothing has changed in its payments business.

For investors the practical tests are straightforward. Watch financial-services revenue growth against GMV growth (if it slows while GMV accelerates, lenders are pulling back); watch the expected-credit-loss range Paytm publishes; watch whether Paytm starts using DLG again to sustain volumes; and at PB watch whether core Paisabazaar disbursal growth holds above 25–30% now that the PB Connect book has shrunk. The lenders' side of this business is covered in the NBFC, private banks and small finance banks reports; their appetite for unsecured small-ticket loans is the single biggest external input to this engine.

The largest Indian fintechs are not listed. PhonePe and Google Pay hold about 78% of UPI; Razorpay and PayU lead online gateways. The listed five are leaders only in niches.

The listed companies are a small and unrepresentative slice of Indian fintech. The single largest player, PhonePe, is majority-owned by Walmart and has filed for an IPO but not listed. Among insurance distributors, InsuranceDekho and Acko are private and Turtlemint listed only in June 2026. Foreign capital and Press Note 3

Foreign ownership shapes the market in two ways. In plain terms

The companies you can buy on the stock exchange are not the biggest in Indian fintech. Two foreign-owned apps handle almost four of every five UPI payments, and the strongest online payment gateways are private. The listed firms have to win in corners of the market: the device on a shop counter, the EMI on a big purchase, the gift card, the health policy. When PhonePe, Razorpay or Jio list, investors will have alternatives, and the scarcity premium the listed five enjoy may shrink.

The new entrants matter most for valuation. Jio Platforms, listing around 28 October 2026, brings a deep-pocketed group with an existing telecom base into UPI, aggregation, lending and insurance broking. These are five different kinds of company that happen to share a label. One sells insurance on commission, one rents payment machines and runs gift cards, one runs a website checkout, one is a phone app with a lending business attached, and one manages corporate cards and rewards. Comparing their revenue directly is like comparing a shop's sales with a landlord's rent: the numbers mean different things.

Four features separate the better businesses from the average, whatever the archetype. First, a monetisation layer that does not depend on UPI: devices, EMI, gift cards, credit or insurance. Second, a contribution margin above about 55% for a payments platform and above 70% for infrastructure, which shows that direct costs are under control. Third, other income that is a declining share of profit before tax. Fourth, operating cash flow that is positive once merchant-settlement float movements are stripped out. On those tests in FY26, Pine Labs passes the first, second and fourth but fails the third; Paytm passes the first and second, is improving on the third and fails the fourth on reported numbers (its operating cash flow was −₹743 crore, driven by settlement timing); PB passes the first and, before the IRDAI paper, looked secure on the others except cash conversion; AvenuesAI passes the third and fourth but its take rate is falling; Zaggle passes the third but fails the fourth.

Paytm's two-year round trip Deep dive

From a regulator's order that shut its bank in January 2024 to the first annual profit in its history in FY26: what was lost, what was rebuilt, and what is still open.

On 31 January 2024 RBI ordered Paytm Payments Bank (PPBL), in which One97 Communications holds 49%, to stop accepting deposits, top-ups and credits to accounts, wallets and FASTags after 29 February, later extended to 15 March 2024. PPBL had been barred from onboarding new customers since March 2022. Because the "@paytm" UPI handles and much of Paytm's merchant settlement ran through PPBL, the order threatened the core of the business, not just an associate. Twenty-five years, four cycles: build, list, crash, re-price

The industry grew up around demonetisation and UPI, listed at the 2021 peak, was humbled by regulators in 2022–24, turned profitable in 2025–26 and was re-priced by two regulators in September 2026.

Indian fintech's history is a sequence of policy events more than business cycles. The companies were founded in a pre-UPI world: One97 Communications in 2000, CCAvenue in 2001, Pine Labs in 1998 as a petrol-pump loyalty-card business, Policybazaar in 2008, Paytm's wallet in 2010 and Zaggle in 2011. Two events in 2016 defined the next decade: UPI's launch in August and demonetisation in November, which turned the Paytm wallet into a household name almost overnight. In 2019–20 the state made UPI and RuPay free, RBI wrote its first aggregator rules and Press Note 3 closed the door on Chinese capital; COVID then accelerated digital adoption.

The listing cycle followed the money. PB Fintech (issue price ₹980) and Paytm (₹2,150, about ₹18,300 crore raised, then India's largest IPO) listed within days of each other in November 2021, near the top of the global fintech boom. Both fell 60–75% by 2022 as rates rose and investors demanded profits. The funding winter coincided with a regulatory tightening: RBI barred PPBL from adding customers (March 2022), wrote digital-lending rules (September 2022), capped DLG at 5% (June 2023) and raised risk weights on unsecured loans (November 2023). The 2024 PPBL order was the low point. From 2025 the cycle turned: Paytm's EBITDA went positive, Ant exited, RBI consolidated the aggregator rules, GST on individual health and life policies was removed (22 September 2025), and Pine Labs listed in November 2025 at about half its last private valuation.

Every big turn in this industry's history was triggered by a government or regulator: demonetisation made Paytm famous, free UPI changed how everyone earns, RBI's order nearly broke Paytm, and two circulars in September 2026 moved billions of rupees of market value in a week. The companies' own execution matters, but investors who ignore the policy calendar are reading half the story.

Two lessons from the cycle carry forward. First, the regulators have tended to act after a period of very fast growth in a specific revenue line, whether wallet balances at PPBL, small-ticket consumer loans in 2023 or broker commissions in 2023–25. Second, each shock has been followed by a period in which the stronger companies took share from weaker ones: Paytm's merchant base kept growing through 2024 even as its UPI share fell, and PB's brand advantage over smaller distributors may matter more, not less, under capped commissions.

Thin slices of huge flows: reading a fintech without being fooled Deep dive

The KPI masterclass. Volumes that are not revenue, revenue that is not income, EBITDA that is not profit, profit that is not operations, and cash that belongs to someone else: twelve measures and how to read each one.

Fintech disclosures are built for a reader who looks at the biggest number first. The biggest number is always a volume (₹17 lakh crore of GTV, ₹7 lakh crore of GMV in a quarter, ₹30,000 crore of premium), and it is never the company's revenue. Below it sits a revenue figure that may be gross or net, an "adjusted EBITDA" that excludes the cost of paying staff in shares, and a PAT that may be mostly interest on cash. Each layer is legitimate and each is disclosed, but the layers are defined differently at every company. This section starts from the equation that links them, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to compute it, a worked example, what moves it, a benchmark, how the five compare, and the trap in its definition.

The equation: what management controls and what it does not

For a payments platform the revenue equation has four terms. Volume multiplied by a net take rate gives transaction revenue; the device base multiplied by a monthly rent gives subscription revenue; loans distributed multiplied by a fee gives credit revenue; and there is a residual of advertising, gift cards and services. Profit then follows from the contribution margin (what is left after direct costs), indirect costs (people, technology, marketing), depreciation on devices, and other income on cash. Management controls the device rent, the sales effort, the cost base and, within limits, the mix. It does not control the biggest levers: the MDR on UPI (zero, set by law and NPCI), the interchange on cards (set by networks and banks), the lenders' appetite (set by RBI's capital rules and the credit cycle), and the interest rate on its cash (set by RBI's monetary policy).

Pine Labs' "Flow, Affordability & Transaction Processing" GTV (₹2.75 lakh crore in FY26) is a subset of its terminal GTV and must never be added to it. 2. Take rate (bps)

What it is. The share of each rupee of volume a company keeps as revenue. Formula. Revenue ÷ volume, in basis points (1 bp = 0.01%). Worked example. Pine Labs: ₹2,711 crore of FY26 revenue ÷ ₹17,15,000 crore of GTV = 0.0158%, or 15.8 bps; in Q1 FY27, ₹737 crore ÷ ₹4,22,000 crore = 17.5 bps. What drives it. Instrument mix (UPI earns zero, credit cards most), merchant size (large merchants negotiate lower rates), product mix (EMI and gift cards earn more than plain acquiring), and competition for volume. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). For payments revenue on GTV: above 20 bps good, 10–20 bps average, below 10 bps weak. How the five compare. Pine Labs 15.8–17.5 bps; Paytm's gross payments revenue about 19.5 bps of GMV in Q1 FY27, but its payment-processing margin is only "comfortably above 4 bps" and its net payment revenue including devices 8.4 bps; AvenuesAI's payments net take rate 7 bps in FY26 and 5.3 bps in Q1 FY27, down from 11.7 bps in FY25. For PB Fintech, the equivalent is insurance revenue as a share of premium, about 21% in FY26 (INDICATIVE, insurance being ~92% of revenue).

3. Net revenue versus gross revenue

What it is. Gross revenue includes money the company collects and passes on; net revenue is what is left after those pass-through costs. Formula. Net revenue = gross revenue − pass-through costs (interchange, bank and network fees, voucher face value, cashback). Worked example. AvenuesAI's FY26 consolidated revenue was ₹8,115.85 crore, and its operating expenses ₹7,512.71 crore, 92.6% of revenue, were largely the pass-through. The company's own net revenue was ₹603.1 crore (+15%). In Q1 FY27, gross revenue was ₹2,680 crore (+109%) but net revenue ₹147.5 crore (−3%). What drives it. Accounting choices (principal or agent), and mix: large low-margin volume deals inflate gross revenue without adding net revenue. Benchmark. For a gateway, net revenue growth should track or exceed TPV growth less take-rate compression; a net-to-gross ratio falling fast is a warning. How the five compare. AvenuesAI's net revenue fell from 16.7% of gross in FY23 to 7.4% in FY26. Zaggle's company-defined net revenue (after the cost of redeemed points and gift cards) was ₹857 crore in FY26, 45% of its ₹1,908 crore reported revenue. Paytm, Pine Labs and PB report revenue that is largely net already.

4. Contribution margin

What it is. What is left of revenue after costs that vary directly with each transaction or sale. Formula. (Revenue − direct costs) ÷ revenue. 5. Adjusted EBITDA versus reported PAT

What it is. Adjusted EBITDA is the company's preferred operating profit, before stock-based pay and other items it treats as non-operating; reported PAT is the audited bottom line. Formula. Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Adjusted EBITDA margin above 25% good, 10–20% average, below 10% weak; reported operating margin (after ESOP) above 15% good, 5–15% average, below 5% weak.

What it is. The accounting charge for employee stock options and share-linked pay. It is non-cash, but it is a real cost to shareholders because it dilutes them. Formula. Share-based payment expense ÷ revenue. Worked example. Pine Labs FY26: ₹152.43 crore ÷ ₹2,711 crore = 5.6%. What drives it. 9. Loans distributed and disbursals

What it is. The value of loans a platform sources for partner lenders, and the revenue it earns per rupee sourced. Formula. Distribution take = credit revenue ÷ disbursals. Worked example. If a platform sources ₹20,000 crore of loans and earns ₹600 crore of sourcing and collection fees, its take is 3%. Paytm earned ₹814 crore of financial-services revenue in Q1 FY27 from 7.6 lakh key financial-services customers, about ₹10,700 each in the quarter (INDICATIVE; the revenue includes some insurance and wealth). What drives it. Lender appetite (capital rules, cost of funds, credit losses), the share of repeat borrowers, ticket size, and whether the platform shares losses through DLG (more DLG, higher fees, more risk). Benchmark (INDICATIVE). Distribution take above 4% good, 2.5–4% average, below 2.5% weak; repeat-borrower share above 50% good. How the five compare. Paytm: >50% repeat borrowers, ECL on partners' books 4.5–5.0%, disbursal value no longer disclosed (take n/a; the dossier's 3.5–4.5% is an unverified estimate). PB: total disbursals ₹30,740 crore in FY26, ₹4,366 crore in Q1 FY27 (−38%; core +33%); core credit revenue ₹127 crore in the quarter. Pine Labs: affordability GTV ₹91,000 crore in Q1 FY27 (+54%), fees not separately disclosed. AvenuesAI and Zaggle: n/a.

The definition trap. "Disbursals" can mix channels with very different economics. PB's FY26 total included PB Connect's B2B2C book, which has since shrunk; quoting the core growth (+33%) without the total (−38%) misleads. Credit revenue also depends on accounting for DLG: a platform that guarantees part of the loss may book higher fees but carries a contingent liability that shows up only when loans go bad.

What it is. The premium a marketplace sells (new and renewal) and the revenue it earns on each. Formula. Insurance revenue = new premium × first-year take + renewal premium × renewal take + insurer-paid rewards and services. Take = insurance revenue ÷ premium. Worked example. Insurance take above 20% good, 15–20% average; protection (health + term) growth above 40% good. How the five compare. 15. Definition traps, collected

  • Volume is not revenue. GMV, GTV, TPV and premium are flows; each company defines them differently and they are not additive (Pine's affordability GTV is a subset of its terminal GTV; Paytm's consumer UPI GTV overlaps its merchant GMV).
  • Quarterly versus annual volume. Paytm reports GMV per quarter (₹7.1 lakh crore in Q1 FY27); annualising or confusing a quarter with a year changes take rates fourfold.
  • Gross versus net revenue. AvenuesAI's and Zaggle's revenue is gross; their margins on reported revenue are not comparable with Paytm's or Pine Labs'.
  • Adjusted EBITDA excludes ESOP at PB and Pine Labs (and includes an associate's EBITDA at Zaggle); Paytm's EBITDA includes ESOP. Use screener operating profit for comparisons.
  • "Adjusted PAT" is not PAT. AvenuesAI's ₹332 crore excludes fair-value movements; reported PAT is ₹294.9 crore and owners' share ₹279.0 crore.
  • Other income may include exceptional items on screener's basis (Paytm FY25), and liabilities written back (Pine Labs FY26: ₹133 crore screener vs ₹84 crore company).
  • One-offs drive P/E. Paytm's 128× (ex-exceptional) and ~160× (reported) are both "right".
  • Per-share history changes with consolidation. AvenuesAI's 10:1 consolidation (record date 13 Oct 2026) multiplies every earlier per-share figure by ten; its FY26 EPS is ₹9.42 on the company's weighted ₹10 basis and ₹8.00 on screener's year-end share count.
  • Renewal revenue has several definitions at PB (rolling twelve months, quarterly annualised, exit rate).
  • Disbursals mix channels (PB core +33% vs total −38% in Q1 FY27); Paytm no longer discloses disbursal value at all.
  • Cash is not all the company's. Deduct escrow, nodal and client balances; do not use screener "investments" for payment companies.
  • Receivable days can rise for benign reasons (PB's shift to instalment commission) and fall for alarming ones (early-settlement products).

16. How to read a fintech company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volume first, but only for direction (1 minute). Pull GMV, GTV, TPV or premium growth year on year and compare with UPI's growth for the same quarter (about +18–23% in Sep-2026). Red flag: volume growth far above revenue growth.
  2. Revenue, and whether it is gross or net (1 minute). For AvenuesAI and Zaggle, go straight to net revenue. Red flag: gross up, net flat or down (AvenuesAI Q1 FY27: +109% vs −3%).
  3. Take rate (1 minute). Revenue ÷ volume, compared with the same quarter last year. Red flag: a falling take rate that management attributes to "competitive" volume.
  4. Contribution margin (1 minute). Is it within guidance (Paytm mid-50s; Pine Labs 73–74% for FY27)? Ask why it moved: incentives, hardware sales, mix.
  5. Reported operating profit after ESOP (1 minute). Ignore adjusted EBITDA at first. Note the ESOP line and how much of any improvement is ESOP roll-off.
  6. Operating PBT (1 minute). Operating profit − D&A − finance cost. This is the business's own profit. Red flag: still negative while PAT is positive.
  7. Other income and one-offs (1 minute). Other income ÷ PBT; list exceptional items, impairments and tax-rate swings (AvenuesAI's tax rate fell to near zero in some FY26 quarters).
  8. The native engine (1 minute). Devices added (Paytm), DCPs and revenue per DCP (Pine), financial-services revenue and customers (Paytm), core disbursals (PB), protection growth and renewal revenue (PB), cashback ratio (Zaggle).
  9. Cash (1 minute). Operating cash flow with and without settlement float; own cash after escrow; unused IPO or QIP proceeds. Red flag: profits with persistently negative OCF (Zaggle).
  10. Regulatory paragraph (1 minute). Read the management commentary on MDR, IRDAI, DLG and licences; note anything that changes a price in the equation.
In plain terms

The fastest way to read these results is backwards from the press release: start with what the business earned before interest income and stock-option add-backs, then check whether the cash came in, and only then look at the impressive volume numbers. If the operating profit is growing and the cash flow agrees, the story is real. If only the volumes and the "adjusted" numbers are growing, it is not yet.

Technology and disruption: UPI eats the terminal, AI eats the cost base

UPI is becoming the universal acceptance layer, credit is moving onto UPI, AI agents are starting to pay, and big conglomerates are arriving. Each threatens an existing slice and opens a new one.

The biggest technology force is UPI itself. Pine Labs says about 70% of transactions on its offline POS terminals are now UPI, with an average UPI ticket above ₹1,400, and its UPI GTV grew 80% in Q1 FY27; card transactions are growing only 10–15%. Every new UPI feature erodes a card-based revenue line. RuPay credit cards can now be used on UPI; banks offer pre-approved "credit lines on UPI"; Paytm's Postpaid is a credit line on UPI; Pine Labs launched credit on UPI with J&K Bank. For acquirers, a UPI QR on every counter makes a dedicated card terminal less necessary, which is one reason India's PoS count fell by about 17 lakh in 2026. Pine Labs' response has been to make its Android terminals do more (UPI, EMI, offers, loyalty) so that the merchant pays for software, not for card acceptance.

Card-not-present payments are shifting too. Tokenisation and "click-to-pay" move control of online checkout toward card networks and large apps; RBI's alternative-authentication rules for card-not-present payments (from April 2026, not re-verified for this report) change the friction gateways manage. On the agentic frontier, Pine Labs has launched P3P, a protocol for AI agents to pay on UPI on a user's behalf, live with Vijay Sales and Gullak; AvenuesAI markets PhroneticAI, PrivateGPT and RediffOne; Zaggle has "Zaggle Brain". None of these is yet material to revenue.

AI is already material to costs. Paytm credits AI with a 3% fall in the cost of building its platform while revenue rose 28%; Zaggle says it optimised about 12.5% of its expense base in FY26 with AI; Pine Labs says about 89% of its code changes are AI-assisted. In a business where people are the largest indirect cost (30–36% of revenue at Paytm and PB), the ability to hold headcount flat while volume grows is a large part of the operating-leverage story. The counter-risk is that AI also lowers the cost of entry for competitors and of fraud for criminals; RBI's MuleHunter.AI, beneficiary-name display and device binding all raise compliance costs for aggregators and apps.

Public digital infrastructure is a two-edged sword. The Account Aggregator framework and RBI's Unified Lending Interface (ULI) make a borrower's financial data portable, which commoditises the underwriting advantage Paytm and Pine Labs claim from seeing merchants' cash flows (Pine's Setu builds Account Aggregator rails, so it earns on both sides). Bima Sugam, IRDAI's planned digital insurance marketplace, is a direct structural threat to aggregator economics if insurers can reach customers through a public platform at low cost. NPCI's relaunch of the BHIM app adds a state-backed competitor in UPI.

Then there are the conglomerates. Jio Platforms, listing around 28 October 2026, will give JioFinance and Jio Payment Solutions a public currency and a vast telecom customer base in UPI, aggregation, lending and insurance broking. Flipkart's super.money, Amazon Pay and Google's merchant tools are already present. For e-commerce and quick-commerce payments, where many large UPI tickets originate, the platforms increasingly run their own payment stacks; see the e-retail report. For software-led monetisation, Zaggle's SaaS line and AvenuesAI's AI products compete with the companies in the software products report.

In plain terms

Technology keeps making payments cheaper and easier, which is good for shoppers and hard on companies that charge for each payment. The winners will be those that use the payment to sell something else, and that use AI to keep their own costs down. The threats are free public platforms that do what the fintechs do, and giant groups such as Jio that can afford to give the service away.

Bull vs bear: operating leverage against the regulator's pen

The bull case is that operating profit is compounding fast enough to make treasury income and regulatory risk secondary. The bear case is that the regulator owns the price and has just shown it will use it.

The bull case

  • Operating leverage is visible: group operating profit rose 2.2× in Q1 FY27 on revenue up 30% ex-AvenuesAI; Paytm's indirect costs grew 6% against revenue up 28%.
  • Treasury dependence is falling: Paytm's operating PBT turned positive in Q1 FY27; Pine Labs' ESOP roll-off is nearly complete.
  • The flows keep compounding: UPI +23% by count in September 2026; Paytm GMV +31%; PB premium +41%.
  • Credit distribution has room: Paytm's lenders have four to six times more capital than it disburses; repeat borrowers exceed half.
  • Regulatory upside exists: the s.10A amendment gives the government a route to UPI MDR; a post-Diwali start would add a new revenue line.
  • Balance sheets are strong: about ₹13,500 Cr of cash at Paytm and ₹2,100 Cr net at Pine Labs; little debt anywhere.
  • Domestic funds are buying: DII holdings rose at PB, Paytm and Pine Labs in the September quarter.

The bear case

  • The regulator sets the price: IRDAI's caps could cut PB's general-insurance revenue to a third or 40% of today from FY28; NPCI can defer the MDR indefinitely.
  • Profit is mostly treasury: other income was 67% of group PBT in FY26 and 115% at Paytm.
  • Credit is pro-cyclical: a repo hike to 5.50%, tighter capital rules or rising delinquencies would hit the best revenue line first.
  • Take rates are falling: AvenuesAI's payments take rate halved in a year; Pine's contribution margin fell 5.6 points.
  • Valuations are stretched: about 160× for Paytm and 140× for Pine Labs on reported trailing earnings.
  • Share supply is heavy: no promoters at three of five; foreign holders and PE funds are sellers.
  • Competition is arriving: Jio's listing, PhonePe's IPO and big-tech wallets; two foreign apps already hold 78% of UPI.

The bull case rests on a simple arithmetic claim: that operating profit will grow fast enough, for long enough, to make the current multiples look reasonable and treasury income irrelevant. The Q1 FY27 numbers support the direction. Group operating profit more than doubled; Paytm's EBITDA margin rose from 4% to 8%; PB's operating margin from 2.5% to 7.3%. If Paytm reaches the bottom of its 15–20% long-term EBITDA margin range on revenue growing 20–25% a year, its operating profit would be several times today's within three to four years, and the multiple on that profit would look very different. The MDR is a free option on top.

The bear case rests on the observation that almost every important price in these businesses is written by someone else, and that the writers moved in September 2026. IRDAI's paper is not final, but the regulator's own evidence (commissions up 173% against premium up 37%) makes a reversal unlikely; the question is the size of the cut, not its direction. The credit engine depends on lenders who are themselves regulated and whose appetite RBI has throttled before. And the valuation leaves the market exposed to disappointment: Paytm's own management guides other income "broadly steady" and treasury was 74% of its Q1 PBT.

In plain terms

Optimists see companies that have just learned to make money and whose profits are now rising much faster than their sales. Pessimists see companies whose fees can be cut by a regulator's circular, whose best income comes from arranging loans in a credit cycle that may be turning, and whose share prices already assume years of success. Both views fit the facts; the next four quarters will show which one the operating numbers support.

On balance the evidence differs by company. The operating improvement is clearest at Paytm and the regulatory risk clearest at PB; Pine Labs' case depends on adjusted margins reaching reported profit; AvenuesAI and Zaggle are cheap for reasons visible in their own disclosures (falling take rate and flat EPS guidance; negative operating cash flow and a capitalisation review), so their re-rating depends on fixing those, not on sector sentiment.
