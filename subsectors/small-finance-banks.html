00

The one-page view: four banks, two business models, one awkward licence

A licence designed for microfinance lenders now houses one near-universal bank and three microfinance survivors on a forced march into secured credit.

India's small finance banks (SFBs) were invented in 2014 to solve one problem: microfinance companies and local lenders were good at reaching poor borrowers but had no cheap, stable money of their own. The RBI's answer was a restricted bank licence. Take deposits like any bank, it said, but lend most of the money to small borrowers, keep more capital than a normal bank and give up the large-ticket business. Eleven years later the four SFBs in the NSE 750 hold ₹2.81 lakh crore of deposits, earned ₹3,763 crore of profit in FY26 and are worth ₹94,445 crore on the market. They are still a rounding error in Indian banking: together they hold 1.20% of all scheduled-bank credit and 1.07% of deposits.

The four look alike on a licence list and are very different businesses. AU Small Finance Bank is a Jaipur vehicle financier that became a bank in 2017, absorbed Fincare SFB in April 2024 and now runs a ₹1.44 lakh crore loan book that is about 92% secured. It is 53% of the group's loans, 54% of its deposits, 70% of its FY26 profit and 75% of its market value. The other three, Ujjivan, Equitas and Jana, were born as microfinance lenders making ₹50,000 group loans to women in villages. All three are part-way through moving into loans backed by houses, vehicles and gold: Ujjivan is 52% secured (Sep-26), Jana 72.8% (Jun-26) and Equitas about 85%. Any "sector" average is mostly AU, so this report shows the three MFI-heritage banks separately wherever it matters.

Five forces define the sub-sector in October 2026, and the report is built around them.

  • The microfinance cycle has turned. Industry microfinance loans shrank for seven straight quarters, from a peak of ₹4,33,697 crore in March 2024 to ₹3,14,728 crore in December 2025, as over-borrowed households stopped paying. Loans 31–180 days overdue (PAR 31–180) went from about 2.2% to 6.3% (March 2025) and are back to 1.6% (June 2026). The four members' Q1 FY27 profit was ₹1,452 crore, 2.6 times the ₹562 crore of a year earlier.
  • Money costs too much. The four pay 6.48–7.4% for their funds against roughly 5–5.5% at large private banks, because their current-and-savings (CASA) share is only 19–29% and savers need about 110–125 basis points (bp) of extra fixed-deposit interest to choose an unfamiliar bank. Loans are also growing faster than deposits.
  • The secured-mix migration. Moving from 22% microfinance loans to 12% housing loans cuts yield but cuts losses and capital use by more. It is the single most-watched number in the sub-sector and the key to a universal licence.
  • The universal-bank gate. AU has in-principle approval (7-Aug-2025) and a final application on file since March 2026. Ujjivan's and Jana's applications came back; Equitas qualifies on paper and is in no hurry.
  • The rate cycle. Five cuts took the repo rate from 6.50% to a 5.25% trough (5-Dec-2025), which lowered SFB deposit costs by 45–75 bp with a lag. On 7-Oct-2026 the RBI raised it to 5.50%, on deposit books that had already started to reprice upward.

The scorecard is easy to state. The storm cost the MFI-heritage banks dearly: their combined profit fell from ₹2,750 crore in FY24 to ₹1,122 crore in FY26, a 59% drop, and Equitas lost ₹224 crore in a single quarter (Q1 FY26). None of the four came close to failing. Their gross bad loans (GNPA) peaked at 2.5–3.0% while the unlisted-quality tail of the sector, ESAF, Utkarsh and Suryoday, saw GNPA of 8.5–12.4% and two of them lost money. That contrast is the first lesson of this report: in microfinance the cycle is guaranteed, and what separates lenders is how much of the book is exposed when it arrives and how much capital and guarantee cover sit behind it.

The second lesson is about price. The market pays 3.54 times book value for AU, which earns a return on equity (RoE) of about 15–16%, and between 0.97 and 1.73 times book for the other three, even though Ujjivan earned a 19.7% RoE in Q2 FY27. Investors are paying for predictability, not for the latest quarter. They will pay up only when a bank shows it can earn a decent return through a microfinance bust, and so far only AU has.

In plain terms

A small finance bank is a bank with a restricted licence: it may take deposits from anyone, but must lend most of them to small borrowers and hold extra capital. The four listed ones are AU, which already behaves like a mid-sized private bank, and three former microfinance companies that are turning themselves into housing, vehicle and small-business lenders. Their profits have just recovered from a two-year microfinance bust. Their remaining handicap is that savers make them pay about 1.5–2 percentage points more for money than a big bank pays.

For a reader arriving from our private banks or NBFC reports, the shortest description is this: an SFB earns NBFC-like yields (14–17%) on bank-like funding (deposits, refinance, very little market borrowing) and pays bank-like regulatory costs (cash reserve ratio, statutory liquidity ratio, a 15% minimum capital ratio, 60% priority-sector lending). Its edge is reach: branches in towns where big banks do not lend and a field force that collects weekly. Its weak spots are a funding cost that only scale and brand will fix, and a microfinance tail that blows up every four to six years.

01

The licence bargain: a deposit franchise in exchange for a down-market mandate

What an SFB may do, what it must do, and where a rupee of margin is earned between the saver and the borrower.

The RBI issued its SFB licensing guidelines on 27-Nov-2014 and gave ten in-principle licences in September 2015: AU Financiers, Capital Local Area Bank, Disha Microfin (later Fincare), Equitas, ESAF, Janalakshmi, RGVN (later North East SFB), Suryoday, Ujjivan and Utkarsh. Eight of the ten were microfinance companies. "On-tap" guidelines of 5-Dec-2019 allowed applications at any time; Shivalik (a converted urban co-operative bank) and Unity (Centrum and BharatPe, which absorbed the failed PMC Bank) came in that way in 2021. Equitas opened as a bank in September 2016, Ujjivan in February 2017, AU in April 2017 and Jana in March 2018.

The bargain is simple to state and expensive to live with. An SFB gets the two things a non-bank lender cannot have: public deposits insured by DICGC up to ₹5 lakh per depositor, and full banking powers (payments, current accounts, third-party distribution). In return it accepts rules that tie it to small customers and hold more of its capital idle.

Three of these rules do most of the economic work. The 15% capital floor means an SFB must hold about ₹15 of capital for every ₹100 of risk-weighted assets where a universal bank needs ₹11.50; at the same return on assets, the SFB's return on equity is mechanically lower. The 60% priority-sector rule (cut from 75% by the RBI circular of 20-Jun-2025) forces lending into agriculture, micro and small enterprises and "weaker sections", which happens to be where SFBs want to lend anyway; the cut matters mainly because it frees space for mortgages and mid-sized business loans. The small-ticket rule stops an SFB from building a corporate book. Our AU primer still quotes the old 75% target; the correct figure from FY26 is 60%.

Margin is earned in the gap between Step 1 (a 6.5–7.4% cost of funds) and Step 3 (a 13.7–17.4% portfolio yield). It is lost in Step 3–4 operating cost (4–6.6% of assets, about twice a universal bank's) and in Step 5 when a cycle turns.

The money flows as follows. A saver puts a two-year fixed deposit into an SFB at about 7.65% (Ujjivan's and Jana's card rate in October 2026, against 6.40–6.45% at SBI and HDFC Bank). The bank parks about 3% of it as cash reserve with the RBI, earning nothing, and about 18% in government bonds for the statutory liquidity ratio. It lends the rest: perhaps ₹65,000 to a woman in a joint-liability group in rural Tamil Nadu at 22–25%, perhaps ₹12 lakh against a self-built house in a Gujarat town at 12–13%, perhaps ₹8 lakh against a used truck in Rajasthan at 15–17%. Field officers meet the group weekly; the housing borrower pays by monthly mandate. The spread between the 7% money and the 16% average loan is the gross margin; the cost of running about 880–2,900 branches and tens of thousands of staff takes 4–6.6% of assets; credit losses take 0.7–1.4% in a normal year and far more in a bad one.

Value is captured in two very different ways. The microfinance book earns the highest gross margin of any retail loan in India and gives back 3–8% of the portfolio in a bad cycle; Equitas's microfinance net slippage ran at 20% annualised in Q1 FY26. Secured retail (vehicles, property-backed small-business loans, housing, gold) earns less but loses far less, so it is where through-cycle return on assets (RoA) comes from. That arithmetic explains both why every MFI-heritage SFB is migrating into secured credit and why the market values AU, the bank that made the journey first, at more than three times book.

In plain terms

The licence is a trade. The RBI gave microfinance lenders access to cheap, insured public deposits, which they could never get as finance companies. In exchange they must keep 60% of their lending in priority sectors, half their loans under ₹25 lakh, and 15% capital instead of 11.5%. The deposits turned out to be less cheap than hoped, and the rules turned out to be more binding than expected, which is why the most successful SFB now wants to stop being one.

02

Size and share: 1.2% of Indian credit, growing twice as fast as the system

The SFB sector is small in system terms and large in its niches; the four listed members are four-fifths of it.

At 31 March 2026 scheduled commercial banks held ₹218.85 lakh crore of credit and ₹262.30 lakh crore of deposits. The four members held ₹2.63 lakh crore of loans (on each bank's own headline definition) and ₹2.81 lakh crore of deposits: 1.20% and 1.07% of the system. AU alone is about 0.64% of system credit and 0.58% of deposits; AU's own investor deck rounds this to "~0.7% of loans and 0.6% of deposits". All eight listed SFBs together held ₹3.52 lakh crore of deposits, 1.34% of the system. There is no published RBI table of SFB-group advances that we could fetch. Working back from CareEdge's statement that the June-2025 PSL cut freed ₹41,000 crore, or 15% of SFB advances, gives about ₹2.73 lakh crore at March 2025 and, at roughly 20% growth, an indicative ₹3.2–3.3 lakh crore at March 2026, or about 1.5% of system credit.

In plain terms

If every Indian bank loan were ₹100, the four listed SFBs would hold about ₹1.20 of it. They are growing about twice as fast as the banking system, so the share creeps up, but none of them is large enough to matter for the system's stability. They matter in their niches: microfinance, used vehicles, small-town housing and loans to shopkeepers against their homes.

03

Who borrows, and for what: ten markets that big banks reach badly

SFB demand comes from thin-file households, self-employed property owners, small fleet operators and gold-owning families, and on the other side of the balance sheet from savers chasing an extra percentage point.

The SFB customer is defined less by product than by paperwork. Most do not file income-tax returns, many are paid in cash, and many own property without a clean title chain. A large bank's credit model rejects them or prices them as if they were risky; an SFB's branch-based underwriting, which visits the shop, counts the cattle and checks the neighbours, can lend to them at a price that covers the cost of finding out. The demand drivers, roughly in order of importance to the four members' books:

  • Women in joint-liability groups. The microfinance borrower: household income ≤ ₹3 lakh a year (the RBI definition since 2022), loan size ₹50,000–70,000, repaid weekly or fortnightly. Ujjivan's average JLG ticket is ₹0.65 lakh. Microfinance reaches 718 districts across 36 states and union territories, with Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu the largest states; Bihar alone had about 1 crore clients and ₹48,569 crore outstanding with RBI-regulated lenders in February 2026.
  • Graduated micro borrowers. Individual loans of ₹1–1.5 lakh to borrowers who have repaid group loans: Ujjivan ₹6,380 crore (average ₹1.41 lakh), Jana ₹6,699 crore.
  • Self-employed property owners. Shopkeepers and workshop owners borrowing ₹3–15 lakh against a self-occupied house: Equitas's small business loans of ₹19,249 crore (40% of its book), Jana's micro-LAP of ₹6,196 crore at a 35% loan-to-value ratio, Ujjivan's micro-mortgage of ₹2,031 crore.
  • Small fleet owners and used-vehicle buyers. AU's original "Wheels" franchise (₹48,600 crore including tractors, +28%) and Equitas's vehicle book (₹10,947 crore). Equitas has deliberately shifted to used vehicles: used cars +30% and used CVs +25% while new CVs fell 28%.
  • First-time home buyers in small towns. Affordable housing of ₹10–20 lakh: Ujjivan ₹10,056 crore (+31%, average ticket ₹17.9 lakh), Jana ₹8,436 crore (LTV 50%), Equitas ₹6,044 crore.
  • Gold-owning households. The 2025–26 growth engine, helped by a gold-price boom: AU ₹4,547 crore (+130%), Jana ₹2,678 crore (+113%), Ujjivan ₹1,275 crore (+210%), Equitas ₹979 crore (+179%).
  • Micro and small enterprises. Working capital and term loans of ₹10 lakh–₹1 crore: AU business banking ₹18,727 crore (+20%), Jana ₹5,386 crore (+27%), Ujjivan ₹3,871 crore (+51%).
  • NBFCs and financial institutions. Wholesale loans that help meet priority-sector targets: Ujjivan ₹3,615 crore, Jana ₹1,913 crore, Equitas ₹1,778 crore, AU ₹5,777 crore.
  • Rural income and the monsoon. MFIN credited a better-than-expected 2026 monsoon (only Rajasthan deteriorated among the top ten states) with supporting repayment.
  • Savers. The other half of the franchise: retail depositors, especially senior citizens, who will move ₹5 lakh for an extra percentage point because DICGC insures it. New tools are ASBA accounts for IPO money (Jana had ₹947 crore blocked in Q1 FY27) and foreign-currency deposits (Equitas passed $42 million of FCNR(B) deposits).

Two of these drivers deserve a caution. Gold-loan growth of 110–210% a year is a sign of how hard it is to grow anything else safely, not only of demand. It is secured, short-tenor and cheap to underwrite, so it helps the "secured share" number quickly, but it is also concentrated on one asset price. Jana's gold book runs at a 64% loan-to-value ratio and Ujjivan's at 56% on the book (75% at origination); a 20% fall in the gold price would push the newest loans close to the RBI's maximum. Second, lending to NBFCs is a way of meeting targets with someone else's customers. Ujjivan's FIG book grew 45%; Jana has kept its NBFC lending flat on purpose.

The products also explain the geography. Microfinance is concentrated in the East and North-East (36.6% of industry loans) and in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu; the top ten states hold about 83% of the market. Secured SFB lending is more western and southern: AU in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh; Equitas in Tamil Nadu; Jana with Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Odisha and Madhya Pradesh each at 11–12% of its outlets. Ujjivan's book is spread across Karnataka (12.7%), Tamil Nadu (12.5%), West Bengal (11.1%) and Maharashtra (9.9%). Demand follows the bank's history: a lender born in microfinance in Bengaluru carries Karnataka risk, and one born in Jaipur vehicle finance carries diesel-price and freight-cycle risk.

Finally, demand for priority-sector certificates is a quiet driver. Universal banks short of their 40% PSL target buy PSL certificates from lenders with a surplus. With the SFB target cut to 60%, an SFB that keeps lending 70–80% to priority sectors has a tradable surplus; Jana earned ₹19 crore from PSLCs in Q1 FY27 and Ujjivan ₹36 crore in FY26. It is small, but it is close to pure profit.

In plain terms

SFB borrowers are people a big bank's computer cannot score: a woman running a tailoring business with a group of neighbours as her guarantors, a shopkeeper whose house is his only collateral, a truck owner with three used vehicles. Lending to them is profitable because few others do it well, and expensive because someone has to visit them. The current growth spurt in gold loans says more about how cautious the banks are being elsewhere than about a new market.

04

Where ₹100 of interest income goes: half to savers, most of the rest to running branches

The supply side of a lender is money, people and branches; an SFB spends twice what a universal bank does on the last two.

A lender's "raw material" is money, and its "factory" is a branch network and a field force. The cleanest way to see an SFB's cost structure is to follow ₹100 of interest income through the profit and loss account. The four members, on their latest full figures, look like this.

The fastest way to raise profit is not to lend at higher rates but to collect more cheaply and fund more cheaply.

The microfinance storm of 2024–26, and why it has passed Deep dive

Seven quarters of shrinking loans, a tripling of overdue loans, new guardrails, state laws and government guarantees: how one cycle reset the economics of lending to the poor.

Indian microfinance blows up every four to six years: Andhra Pradesh in 2010, demonetisation in 2016, COVID in 2020–21 and over-borrowing in 2024–25. Each time the cause looks different and the mechanism is the same. Credit to poor households grows faster than their ability to repay, someone or something interrupts the weekly collection rhythm, and borrowers who see neighbours stop paying decide they can stop too. The 2024–26 episode is the one that shaped every number in this report.

How it happened

The boom came first. After COVID, microfinance lending grew fast: industry gross loan portfolio (GLP) reached ₹4,33,697 crore at March 2024, up 24.5% in a year, spread across 7.8 crore borrowers and 14.9 crore loan accounts. Average ticket sizes rose and, crucially, so did the number of lenders per borrower. A woman with three or four microfinance loans from different lenders, plus a consumer loan, could be paying well over half her household income in instalments while each lender saw only its own loan.

Several shocks then arrived together in 2024: a heatwave and the general election, which disrupted field collections, local campaigns urging borrowers not to repay, and then, in early 2025, a wave of reports of coercive recovery and suicides in Karnataka. Overdue loans rose sharply. PAR 30–180 was 2.69% in June 2024; PAR 31–180 reached 6.4% in December 2024 and 6.3% in March 2025. Lenders stopped disbursing to over-leveraged borrowers, which in turn made it harder for those borrowers to roll over loans, so more of them defaulted. Industry GLP fell for seven straight quarters, to ₹3,14,728 crore in December 2025, down 27% from the peak. Around 50 lakh borrowers left formal microfinance by September 2025.

The fix that also shrank credit: guardrails, laws and guarantees

The industry's self-regulatory body, MFIN, imposed "guardrails" on its members (NBFC-MFIs, banks and SFBs). Guardrails 1.0, instituted in July 2024, capped lenders per borrower at four and total microfinance debt at ₹2 lakh. Guardrails 2.0, announced in November 2024, widened the ₹2 lakh cap to include unsecured retail loans and barred lending to anyone more than 60 days overdue elsewhere from 1-Jan-2025, and cut the cap to three lenders from 1-Apr-2025. These rules worked: by September 2025, 98% of clients were within the guardrails. Their side-effect was to cut off credit to exactly the over-borrowed customers who most needed a rollover, which deepened the shrink before it ended it. Ujjivan's lender overlap now shows the result: 57.7% of its group-loan customers borrow from no other microfinance lender (51.0% a year earlier), and only 0.4% have four or more.

The third fix was a guarantee. The government's Credit Guarantee Fund for Micro Units (CGFMU), run by NCGTC, covers micro loans up to ₹20 lakh, including joint-liability loans. How the four came through it

The cycle hit the four members in proportion to how much microfinance they held when it arrived, and how quickly they recognised the losses.

  • Equitas had already cut microfinance from about 25% to 12% of its book, and still suffered most in profit terms. Microfinance net slippage, annualised, reached 20.3% in Q1 FY26. It took a one-time ₹185 crore stress provision on standard microfinance loans plus about ₹145 crore from tighter provisioning norms (unsecured sub-standard loans now provided at 96%), and lost ₹224 crore in the quarter. By Q1 FY27 the microfinance 1–90 days-past-due share had fallen from 8.05% to 1.10% and net slippage to 2.76%; ₹44 crore of the stress provision remains and could be written back.
  • Ujjivan, the most exposed, took its pain earlier and spread it: FY25 profit fell 43% and FY26 was flat at ₹693 crore. Its portfolio at risk fell from 4.81% (June 2025) to 3.57% (September 2026), its provision coverage rose from 73% to 85%, and its current-bucket collection efficiency is 99.71%.

  • AU inherited a ₹7,000–7,500 crore microfinance book with Fincare just as the cycle turned. It is 5% of the bank, 96% CGFMU-covered, and capped at about 10% of loans. Slippages fell 22% year-on-year in Q1 FY27 and credit cost fell from 1.4% to 0.8% of average assets.

Why the turn is real, and what could reverse it

Three signals say the cycle has turned rather than paused. Disbursements are back: ₹77,524 crore in Q4 FY26, the highest in seven quarters, and ₹61,718 crore in Q1 FY27, up 8.9% year-on-year. Overdues are below pre-stress levels, at 1.6% PAR 31–180. And the borrower base is growing again: Ujjivan added 1.72 lakh new customers in Q1 FY27 after seven quarters of decline. On the members' books, the Q1 FY27 profit jump of 158% came almost entirely from lower credit cost, not from higher income: the three MFI-heritage banks went from a combined ₹19 crore loss to a ₹656 crore profit.

What could reverse it? A repeat of the lender-overlap problem if competition for growth loosens the guardrails; another state-level political shock; a weak monsoon (MFIN flagged only Rajasthan in 2026); and an oil-driven inflation squeeze on rural households after the West Asia crisis that began on 28-Feb-2026. The new caps the banks have set themselves (AU about 10% of loans, Equitas about 10%, Jana 10–12% growth) limit how much damage the next cycle can do. They also limit how much the most profitable product can contribute to growth. MFIN is lobbying the RBI to raise the ₹3 lakh household-income ceiling that defines a microfinance borrower; a higher ceiling would widen the market and also invite the over-lending that caused the last bust.

In plain terms

Microfinance is a business that works brilliantly until too many lenders chase the same borrowers. In 2024 that happened, and repayments collapsed in several states. The industry responded with lending caps, the government's guarantee scheme absorbed much of the loss, and the four listed SFBs, which had less microfinance than the worst-hit lenders, recovered within about eighteen months. The next cycle will come; the guardrails and guarantees mean it should cost less.

Closing that gap, more than any lending decision, decides whether an SFB can ever earn a large private bank's return on equity.

Branches: AU added 334 net touchpoints in FY26 (78 of them liability branches) and plans 100-plus more deposit branches in FY27; Ujjivan opened 104 branches in H1 FY27 and plans 144 for the year, focused on its top 8 and top 30 deposit markets; Jana is doing 78 "branch actions" (8 new, 30 splits, 40 relocations). Brand: Ujjivan runs a national campaign; Jana sponsors the RCB cricket team and credited it with a 7% QoQ rise in CASA. Affluent customers: Equitas's "Elite" book holds ₹18,475 crore, about 40% of deposits, and it has launched "Artha" for HNIs; it targets ₹200 crore a month of new-to-bank savings. New products: ASBA for IPO money, FCNR(B) deposits for non-residents (Equitas over $42 million, Ujjivan ₹60 crore in Q1 FY27), and a "zero-forex-margin" remittance product at AU.

The constraint is that each of these costs money now and pays back slowly. A new branch takes three to five years to gather a mature deposit base, so opex rises before CoF falls. The universal-bank licence is the one step that might move funding costs quickly, through brand alone; Jana's stated reason for wanting it is cheaper deposits once the words "small finance" leave its name. Section 09 shows why that door is mostly closed for now.

In plain terms

A saver choosing between SBI and an SFB with the same ₹5 lakh insurance cover will pick SBI unless the SFB pays about 1.1–1.25 percentage points more. SFBs also have fewer of the near-free current and savings accounts that big banks collect from salaried customers and businesses. Together, these make SFB money about 1.5–2 points dearer. Building branches and brand narrows the gap slowly; a universal-bank licence might narrow it faster.

In plain terms

When a bank lends out more than ₹90 of every ₹100 it has in deposits, it is running hot. To lend more, it must either raise deposit rates (which squeezes its margin) or borrow from refinance agencies and the money market (which is less stable). Equitas is the clearest example in 2026: loans up 27%, deposits up 10%, borrowings almost tripled. Watch whether its deposits catch up in Q2.

The rate cycle: why a repo cut helps an SFB slowly and a hike hurts it slowly

Fixed-rate loans on one side, term deposits that reprice over 12–24 months on the other: the lag structure that turned the 2025 cuts into a 2026 margin peak.

The RBI cut its repo rate five times between February and December 2025: from 6.50% to 6.25% (7-Feb-2025), 6.00% (9-Apr-2025), 5.50% (6-Jun-2025, a 50 bp cut accompanied by a 100 bp cut in the cash reserve ratio to 3%, in tranches) and 5.25% (5-Dec-2025). It held at 5.25% through February, April, June and August 2026. On 7-Oct-2026, at its 63rd meeting, the Monetary Policy Committee raised the repo rate by 25 bp to 5.50%, its first hike since February 2023. The rate decision was unanimous; the change of stance from "neutral" to "calibrated tightening" was carried 4–2. The drivers were crude-driven inflation after the West Asia crisis that began on 28-Feb-2026 and pressure on the rupee, which traded near ₹94.9 to the dollar on 19-Oct-2026. The next meeting is on 2–4 December 2026, and a Reuters poll found 29 of 53 economists expecting another hike.

To see why this matters, look at both sides of an SFB balance sheet. On the liability side, about 70% of funding is term deposits with residual maturities of roughly 12 months; when the repo moves, only new and renewing deposits get the new rate, so the blended cost of funds moves by perhaps a quarter of the change each quarter for three or four quarters. Savings rates are sticky. On the asset side, most of an MFI-heritage SFB's loans are fixed-rate (microfinance, vehicles, gold), so yields move only as the book turns over. The exception is loans linked to an external benchmark (EBLR) such as the repo rate: MSME and home loans that reprice within weeks. AU has many of these; the MFI-heritage banks have fewer.

An illustrative sensitivity

Take an illustrative MFI-heritage SFB with ₹1,000 crore of loans and ₹1,125 crore of interest-bearing liabilities, 70% of them term deposits with a one-year average residual maturity. Suppose the October hike, plus competitive pressure, lifts marginal term-deposit rates by 20 bp. After four quarters roughly all term deposits have repriced, so the cost of term deposits is up about 20 bp and the blended cost of funds about 14 bp (70% × 20 bp), or ₹1.6 crore a year on ₹1,125 crore. If 80% of the loans are fixed-rate and the other 20% reprice by the full 25 bp, yield rises by about 5 bp, or ₹0.5 crore. Net interest income falls by about ₹1.1 crore a year on a base of roughly ₹92 crore, about 1.2%, and NIM by about 9 bp. The same hike on an AU-type book with a larger floating share is close to neutral in the first two quarters. These numbers are indicative, but they explain why Equitas expects ~15 bp of NIM compression and why the market reads the hike as a modest negative for MFI-heritage SFBs, not a crisis.

In plain terms

Most SFB loans are fixed-rate and most SFB deposits are one- or two-year fixed deposits. When the RBI cuts rates, the bank's loans keep earning the old rate while its deposits slowly renew at lower rates, so margins widen for a year or so. When the RBI raises rates the reverse happens. The cuts of 2025 gave the four SFBs their best margins in two years in early 2026; the deposit-rate rises of mid-2026 and the October hike are now slowly taking some of that back.

Two caveats. First, the 2025 cuts also came with a cut in the cash reserve ratio from 4% to 3%, which released about 1% of deposits to earn a return; that benefit stays even as the repo rises. Second, the deposit "war" of 2026 is not only about the RBI. Savers have been moving money into mutual funds and equities, and liquidity was tight in April and May 2026, so deposit rates rose before policy did. An SFB that has to grow deposits at 25% a year, as all four do, pays the marginal rate on a much larger share of its book than a slower-growing bank, which means rate rises hurt it more than its maturity profile alone suggests.

The secured-mix migration: trading 22% loans for 12% loans, and why it can pay

Every MFI-heritage SFB is moving into housing, property-backed business loans, vehicles and gold. The arithmetic says yield falls, losses fall faster, capital use halves, and the result depends on what happens to operating cost.

The single number investors watch most closely in this sub-sector is the share of the loan book that is secured. It has been moving fast. Jana's secured share rose from 55.8% in FY23 to 72.8% in June 2026, with a target above 80%. Ujjivan's rose from about 30% in March 2024 to 52% in September 2026, and it targets 65–70%. Equitas, the early mover, cut microfinance from about 25% of its book to 13% (including ₹837 crore of microfinance loans bought by assignment) and is about 85% secured; it plans to hold microfinance "around 10%". AU, at about 92% secured, made the journey long ago.

The lesson from Equitas is that migration is not automatically profitable. Moving from a 22% product to a 15% product saves credit cost, but if the branch network and staff built for weekly collections stay the same size, operating cost per rupee of loan does not fall and RoA can drop. In plain terms

Switching from microfinance to housing loans is like switching from a high-paying but dangerous job to a steadier one with lower pay: the average year looks similar, but the bad years disappear. For an SFB it also halves the capital the bank has to hold. The catch is that the switch works only if the bank's costs shrink with its yields, and that requires fewer branches and staff per rupee lent, not just a new product menu.

The universal-bank gate: open for AU, returned to Ujjivan and Jana, ignored by Equitas Deep dive

RBI's April-2024 framework created an exit from the SFB rulebook. Two and a half years on, one bank has an in-principle approval and nobody has a final licence.

On 26-Apr-2024 the RBI published a framework for SFBs to convert voluntarily into universal banks. For an SFB the prize is large: priority-sector lending falls from 60% to 40%, the capital floor from 15% to 11.5%, the ₹25 lakh small-ticket rule and the tight exposure limits disappear, and the words "small finance" leave the brand, which the banks believe would lower their deposit costs. For the RBI the risk is also large: a bank that was a microfinance company a decade ago would get a licence to lend to anyone, in any size.

Why Ujjivan and Jana were sent back

Both met the numbers. What they did not meet, in the RBI's view, was the diversification test. The rulebook: PSL at 60%, promoter dilution, microfinance directions and the ECL clock

The regulations that shape an SFB's book, its owners and its next three years of provisioning.

Priority-sector lending. The RBI's circular of 20-Jun-2025 cut the SFB PSL target from 75% to 60% of adjusted net bank credit (ANBC) or credit-equivalent off-balance-sheet exposure, whichever is higher, from FY26. The mandatory 40% allocated to standard sub-targets (agriculture, small and marginal farmers, micro enterprises, weaker sections) is unchanged; the flexible portion falls from 35% to 20% and can go to any PSL category where the bank has an advantage. CareEdge read the change as support for diversification during the microfinance stress, and SFB shares rose up to 4% on 23-Jun-2025. In practice it frees room for mortgages above priority-sector limits, mid-sized business loans and wholesale lending, and increases the surplus that SFBs can sell as PSL certificates. All four of our primers still quote the old 75%; the correct figure is 60%.

Ownership and promoter dilution. The SFB guidelines require promoters to hold at least 40% for five years, then dilute to 30% within ten years and 15% within fifteen years, and require listing within three years of reaching ₹500 crore of net worth. Two members met these rules by folding their listed holding companies into the bank: Equitas Holdings merged into Equitas SFB in 2023, and Ujjivan Financial Services reverse-merged into Ujjivan SFB in 2024. Both now report no promoter. AU's founder family holds 22.66%, down from 25.5% in December 2023 through ESOP dilution and the Fincare share swap; AU has said it has no plan to raise it. Jana's promoters, Janalakshmi Holdings and Janalakshmi Capital, hold 16.87% and are selling down (section 17). Today domestic institutions are the largest holders: 52.25% of Equitas, 36.0% of Ujjivan and 32.84% of AU.

AU and Fincare: the sector's one large merger

AU's all-stock purchase of Fincare SFB took effect on 1-Apr-2024, the moment the microfinance cycle turned. Fincare's promoter put in about ₹700 crore before the deal. It added roughly ₹16,000–17,000 crore of loans (indicative), heavily microfinance, micro-LAP and gold, and about 1,200 touchpoints; AU's deposit touchpoints went from 727 to 1,909 and it gained 176 microfinance branches. The deal made AU "largely a pan-India franchise", in management's words, with a real presence in the East, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. It also gave AU most of its current microfinance book (₹7,488 crore, 5% of loans), which AU has since covered almost entirely with CGFMU and capped at about 10% of the book. Fincare's core banking system was migrated into AU's in a single cut-over in Q4 FY26. One consequence for analysis: AU's FY24-to-FY25 growth (interest income from ₹10,555 to ₹16,064 crore; deposits from ₹87,182 to ₹1,24,269 crore) is about 40% merger, so never quote it without "including Fincare".

Who the SFBs really compete with

  • For microfinance borrowers: NBFC-MFIs (44.3% of the market in June 2026, led by CreditAccess Grameen, Spandana, Fusion, Satin, Muthoot Microfin and Asirvad) and banks lending through business-correspondent partnerships (25.3%: IndusInd through Bharat Financial, IDFC First, Kotak, RBL). See our NBFC report.
  • For vehicles and property-backed small-business loans: Shriram Finance, Cholamandalam, Mahindra Finance, Bajaj Finance and Five-Star Business Finance (micro-LAP), all of which fund at NBFC rates but face no PSL or CRR burden.
  • For affordable housing: Aavas, Aptus and Home First, covered in our housing finance report.
  • For deposits: every bank, but especially IDFC First, RBL and Bandhan, which pay above-market rates too, plus deposit-taking NBFCs such as Bajaj Finance and Shriram. PSU banks, covered in our PSU banks report, set the floor for rates and the ceiling for trust.
  • For the next customer: fintechs and digital lenders (BharatPe through Unity, slice, Kreditbee, Navi). slice's acquisition of North East SFB shows that fintechs want the deposit licence as well.

Ownership has changed too. Foreign private equity was once central (Temasek, ChrysCapital and Kedaara at AU; TPG and Morgan Stanley PE at Jana before its IPO). Today domestic mutual funds and insurers are the anchor holders, and IFC is coming back into Ujjivan at 4.99%. The holding pattern matters because SFBs need equity every few years to grow at 20–25% while holding 15% capital, and domestic institutions have been the reliable buyers.

In plain terms

There are two kinds of listed SFB. The four in this report survived the microfinance bust with bad loans of about 2–2.5% and are profitable. The other four listed ones were hit much harder: two lost money in FY26 and three still have bad loans of 5.4–6.6%. AU alone is worth about 70% of all listed SFBs combined. The real competition comes not from other SFBs but from microfinance NBFCs, vehicle and housing finance companies, and every bank chasing the same depositors.

Four crises in sixteen years: from Andhra Pradesh to the guardrails

Every SFB on this list was shaped by at least two microfinance busts; Jana nearly died in one.

The Andhra Pradesh collapse of 2010 created the case for giving microfinance lenders deposits. Demonetisation in 2016 nearly destroyed Janalakshmi, then India's largest microfinance company. The pattern across these crises is consistent. A boom in unsecured credit to poor households, often with several lenders lending to the same borrower, meets a disruption to the collection rhythm (a political act, a cash shock, a pandemic, a heatwave and an election). Defaults spread socially, faster than any model predicts. Regulators and the industry respond with borrower protection, which permanently changes the product. And the lenders that survive diversify. Each cycle has lasted roughly 18–30 months from first stress to recovery; each has left the industry with stricter rules and a more secured mix.

Two lessons matter for investors now. The first is that survival correlates with three things: the share of the book in microfinance when the cycle starts, the capital buffer, and the willingness to recognise losses quickly. AU, with 5% microfinance and 18–20% capital, grew profit through the cycle. Equitas recognised its losses in one quarter and recovered fast; Jana, by its own account, under-judged the speed of the deterioration and took three quarters to clear it. The second is that the next cycle will not look like this one. Guardrails, CGFMU and lower microfinance shares make an over-borrowing bust less damaging. The risks that remain are political (a state-level shock that the legal exclusions do not fully stop), macro (an oil and inflation shock to rural incomes) and competitive (lenders loosening standards to regain growth).

In plain terms

Indian microfinance has had a major crisis roughly every five years since 2010, and each one has followed the same script: too much lending, a shock, a wave of defaults that spreads from village to village, and then new rules. The banks that survive the best are the ones with the least microfinance, the most capital and the fastest recognition of losses. This cycle's survivors are now mostly secured lenders; the next cycle should cost them less.

Reading an SFB: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

The profit equation, every number that matters, what good looks like, how the four compare, and the definition traps that make naive comparisons wrong.

A bank's results look intimidating because they contain dozens of ratios. For an SFB, almost all of them hang off one equation, and once the equation is clear each ratio becomes a question about one of its terms. This section starts with the equation, works through it with round numbers, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how it is calculated, a worked example, what moves it, what good, average and weak look like, how the four members compare, and the trap in its definition. It ends with an illustrative branch-cluster P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the traps collected in one place, and a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

Gross loans and AUM growth

What it is. The size of the loan book and how fast it is growing. Formula: growth = (loan book this period ÷ loan book a year ago) − 1. Worked example: AU's gross loan portfolio rose from about ₹1,17,600 crore (June 2025) to ₹1,44,250 crore (June 2026), growth of 23%. What drives it: demand, disbursement appetite, branch expansion, and the deposit growth that funds it; for microfinance, the guardrails cap how fast a book can grow. Benchmarks (indicative): good is 20–30% with stable asset quality; average 15–20%; weak is below 12% (no growth) or above 35% (growth that usually comes back as bad loans two years later). The four: Ujjivan +32.1% (Sep-26), Equitas +27%, Jana +25.7%, AU +23% (Jun-26); all within the "good" band but at the top of it.

The trap. Every bank measures its book differently. AU's "gross loan portfolio" includes ₹3,789 crore of assigned (off-book) loans and excludes ₹1,010 crore of credit substitutes held as investments. Equitas's "gross advances" include IBPC (inter-bank participation certificates) sold, securitised and assigned loans; excluding the ₹837 crore of microfinance loans it bought by assignment in Q3 FY26, its growth is 24%, not 27%. Jana's ₹37,612 crore "advances" include IBPC and securitisation; its balance-sheet advances are ₹34,699 crore. Ujjivan's ₹45,699 crore includes ₹130 crore of securitisation. IBPC and securitisation let a bank sell part of a loan to another bank while still reporting it as "AUM"; the risk may or may not have left. Compare growth on the same basis, and use balance-sheet advances for leverage and RoA arithmetic.

Deposits, CASA ratio and retail-deposit share

What it is. The funding base, its cheapest part (current and savings accounts, CASA), and its stickiest part (retail). Formula: CASA ratio = (current + savings deposits) ÷ total deposits. Worked example: Jana had ₹6,871 crore of CASA on ₹35,756 crore of deposits in June 2026: 19%. What drives it: branch age (CASA builds over years), salary and business-account relationships, savings rates offered, brand and digital. Benchmarks: CASA above 30% good, 22–30% average, below 20% weak; retail/stable deposits above 75% good, 60–75% average, below 60% weak. The four: CASA AU 29.5% (Sep-26), Ujjivan 26.0%, Equitas 25%, Jana 19%.

Cost of funds

What it is. The average rate paid on all interest-bearing money. Formula: interest expense ÷ average interest-bearing liabilities (deposits plus borrowings). Worked example: Jana paid ₹732 crore of interest in Q1 FY27; annualised (₹2,928 crore) on an indicative ₹39,600 crore of average deposits and borrowings, that is about 7.4%. What drives it: the repo rate with a lag of 2–4 quarters, CASA share, deposit growth (fast growers pay the marginal rate on more money), refinance share, and brand. Benchmarks: below 6.5% good, 6.5–7.5% average, above 7.5% weak. The four: AU 6.48%, Ujjivan 6.9%, Equitas 7.05%, Jana 7.4% (Q1 FY27), against an indicative deposit-weighted 6.76% for the group and roughly 5.0–5.5% for a large private bank.

The trap. Jana quotes both "cost of funds" (7.4%) and "cost of deposits" (7.44%); others mix them. Equitas computes on daily averages; others on monthly or opening-closing averages. Refinance from NABARD or SIDBI may look slightly dearer than deposits but carries no CRR or SLR, so Equitas calls it "marginally lower" in all-in cost. Net interest margin (NIM)

What it is. Net interest income as a share of the assets that earn interest. Formula: NII ÷ average interest-earning assets (IEA). Worked example: Ujjivan earned ₹1,248 crore of NII in Q2 FY27; annualised that is about ₹4,990 crore, on about ₹59,400 crore of average earning assets, giving 8.4%. What drives it: yield minus CoF, the share of assets held as low-yield liquidity, and the share of the book funded by equity (which costs no interest). Benchmarks (indicative): 6–8% is good for an SFB; 5–6% average; below 5% weak for an SFB; above 9% usually signals concentration risk. The four are compared below.

And never rank SFBs on NIM without the mix: the MFI-heritage banks earn 7–8.5% because 25–50% of their books yield 20–25%, while AU's 5.9% reflects a 92% secured book.

In plain terms

A high margin is not automatically good. Ujjivan's 8.4% margin comes from lending half its book to microfinance and small individual borrowers at 22%; AU's 5.9% comes from lending against vehicles and property at 13–14%. The higher margin pays for higher running costs and higher losses in a bad year. Compare margins only between banks with similar loan books, or compare what is left after costs and losses (RoA).

Opex to assets and cost-to-income

What it is. The cost of running the bank, measured against its size (opex ÷ ATA) and against its income (cost-to-income, C/I). Formulas: opex ÷ average total assets; opex ÷ (NII + other income). Worked example: Equitas spent ₹875 crore in Q1 FY27 against net total income of about ₹1,280 crore (₹1,029 crore NII plus ₹255 crore other income): C/I 68.4%. What drives it: ticket size and collection method (weekly cash versus monthly mandate), branch vintage, rural share, staff productivity, technology.
