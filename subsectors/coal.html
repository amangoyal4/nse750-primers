Indian coal: a policy price in a market squeeze

A complete guide to the business of digging India's coal and lignite: why Coal India sells nine tonnes in ten at a price the state effectively sets, why its profit still swings with the weather, how a record glut in FY26 became a power-plant shortage by September 2026, what wages, state levies and private miners are doing to the economics, and how the two listed members, Coal India and GMDC, compare with the rest of the industry and the world's coal miners.

00

The one-page view: a state-set price, a weather-set profit

India digs about a billion tonnes of coal a year, three-quarters of it through one company, and the money is made in the narrow slice that is sold at a market price.

The listed Indian coal sub-sector has two members, and one of them is almost all of it. Coal India Limited (CIL) produced 768.19 million tonnes (mt) in FY26, about 74% of the country's coal, and earned ₹1,68,400 crore of revenue from operations. Gujarat Mineral Development Corporation (GMDC), the second member, sold 7.88 mt of lignite and earned ₹2,653 crore. On the members' combined FY26 revenue of ₹1,71,053 crore, Coal India is 98.5%; it is 98.8% of operating profit, 97% of reported profit and 94.8% of the combined market value of ₹2,57,262 crore (23 October 2026). So this report reads the whole Indian coal and lignite industry through Coal India, and treats GMDC as the window onto a different trade: merchant lignite in Gujarat, a coal block in Odisha and a rare-earth deposit that has not yet earned a rupee.

Five forces explain almost everything that happens to the industry's profit. The first is weather transmitted through inventory. Coal is the residual fuel in Indian electricity: it supplies whatever demand is left after solar, wind, hydro and nuclear have done their share. A wet year with strong hydro, like FY26, left coal-fired generation 3% lower, pushed Coal India's pithead stock to a record 129.96 mt and cut its profit after tax (PAT) by 12% to ₹31,071 crore. A hot, dry El Niño year with an LNG shortage, like the first half of FY27, did the opposite: by 30 September 2026 coal stocks at power plants were down to about 21 mt, 36% of the normative level, and Coal India's September e-auction premium hit 94%.

The second force is that the price is set by policy, not by the market. About 87% of Coal India's volume goes to long-term linkage customers under fuel supply agreements (FSAs) at a notified price that the company fixes with the government looking over its shoulder. In FY26 an FSA tonne fetched ₹1,444 before levies; an e-auction tonne fetched ₹2,260. E-auction was only 11.3% of volume, but it is the part of the business that moves with the market, so it explains most of the change in profit from one year to the next.

The third force is the slow erosion of the monopoly. Captive and commercial mines, owned by power utilities, metal companies and private bidders, produced 210.46 mt in FY26, up 10.2%, while Coal India fell 1.6% and the Telangana-owned Singareni Collieries (SCCL) fell 15.9%. Coal India's share of national output slid from 77.5% in FY24 to about 74% in FY26. It still plans to reach about one billion tonnes by FY2029-30.

The fourth force is cost and the state. Wages are a third of Coal India's cash cost and the five-year wage agreement for non-executive workers (NCWA-XI) expired on 30 June 2026, with no successor yet and no provision booked. The Supreme Court's July 2024 ruling that states may tax mineral-bearing land let Jharkhand raise its cess from ₹250 to ₹450 a tonne, adding ₹3,635 crore to Coal India's FY26 rates and taxes. Overburden per tonne of coal has risen from about 1.9 to 2.58 cubic metres, and diesel and explosives followed oil prices up after the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026.

The fifth force is imports and the energy transition. India still imported 246.4 mt of coal in FY26, more than the year before, because coking coal for steel rose 16% to 66.1 mt even as steam-coal imports fell. Non-fossil sources are already 54% of installed power capacity, yet about 44 GW of new coal plants are under construction. Coal demand is still growing, and the question for investors is how long that lasts and what Coal India does with its cash in the meantime.

In plain terms

Coal India sells most of its coal at a fixed, low price to power stations and a small slice at whatever buyers will pay in online auctions. When the country is short of coal, the auction price jumps and profits rise; when coal piles up, the auction price slips and profits fall. Wages, state taxes and the cost of moving more earth per tonne creep up every year regardless. That is the whole business in three sentences, and it is why a company with the world's lowest mining costs trades at eight times earnings.

Valuation reflects that mix of strength and constraint. At ₹396 a share Coal India trades at 7.85 times FY26 reported earnings, about 5.4 times EV/EBITDA on its company-basis net cash of roughly ₹42,941 crore, and yields 6.7% on its ₹26.50 dividend. GMDC, at a 52-week low of ₹419, trades at 13.9 times FY26 reported earnings, which flatter it because they include a ₹522.65 crore GST credit; excluding that one-off, the multiple is about 23.6 times on an indicative ₹565 crore. The market is paying very little for Coal India's cash flows because it doubts their durability, and still paying something for GMDC's future mines and rare earths. The rest of this report explains both judgements, and what would change them.

01

From pit to power plant: where a tonne earns its money

Value sits in cheap opencast geology, is shared with the state through levies, and is decided at the moment of sale by which channel the tonne goes through.

Indian coal is mostly Gondwana bituminous and sub-bituminous coal: high in ash (30–45%), modest in heat value (about 3,000–5,000 kilocalories per kilogram for typical grades), and lying in thick seams near the surface in Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand and Maharashtra. The country's geological coal resources stood at 4,00,715 mt on 1 April 2025, led by Odisha (1,00,976 mt), Jharkhand (93,254 mt) and Chhattisgarh (85,263 mt). Only 37,373 mt, about 9%, is coking coal, the kind steelmakers need, which is why India is self-sufficient in power coal on paper and import-dependent for steel. Lignite, or brown coal, is a separate resource of 47,371 mt, mostly in Tamil Nadu (37,524 mt), Rajasthan and Gujarat (2,722 mt). Lignite has even less heat (about 2,200–3,500 kcal/kg) and so much moisture that it cannot economically be railed far; it is burnt at or near the mine.

The rent is earned in Step 3, where the cheapest opencast mines in Odisha and Madhya Pradesh dig coal for a fraction of its value. Step 5 decides how much of that rent the miner keeps.

The physical work is simple to describe. In an opencast mine the miner strips away the rock and soil above the seam (the overburden, or OB), loads the exposed coal into dumpers, and moves it to a siding. The ratio of overburden removed to coal won, the stripping ratio, is the single biggest determinant of mining cost. Coal India's stripping ratio was 2.58 cubic metres per tonne in FY26, so for every tonne of coal it moved about two and a half cubic metres of earth. Underground mining is now a sideline (25.56 mt of Coal India's 768.19 mt in FY26) and almost always loses money, which is why the old underground-heavy subsidiaries in the Raniganj and Jharia coalfields are the weakest parts of the group.

Evacuation is the hidden bottleneck. In Q1 FY27 about 62 mt of Coal India's 198 mt offtake still left the mines by road. The industry is replacing trucks with first-mile connectivity (FMC): conveyor belts, silos and rapid-loading systems that fill a railway rake in about an hour. Nationally 139 FMC projects worth about ₹45,000 crore and 1,319 mtpa of capacity are planned or built, 65 of them commissioned (552 mtpa). Coal India alone expects 994 mtpa of mechanised loading by FY29. In a shortage, though, the binding constraint becomes railway rakes: loading rose from 426 rakes a day in September 2026 to 469 a day in early October as the system scrambled to refill power plants.

Where the value is captured

The value chain has three claimants: the mine, the state and the railway. The mine earns a rent on geology. Coal India's best subsidiaries, Northern Coalfields (NCL) in the Singrauli field of Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh and Mahanadi Coalfields (MCL) in Odisha's Talcher and Ib Valley fields, earned about ₹777 and ₹506 of PAT per tonne of offtake in FY26. Together they made ₹21,355 crore of profit, about 69% of the group's, on 47% of its offtake. At the other end Eastern Coalfields (ECL), with old underground mines in West Bengal, lost about ₹200 a tonne. The rent is a property of the rock, not of management skill, which is one reason the stake sales in MCL and other subsidiaries matter so much to Coal India's shareholders (see section 09).

The state takes roughly as much as the shareholders. Coal India paid ₹52,299 crore to the exchequer in FY26 as royalty, District Mineral Foundation (DMF) and National Mineral Exploration Trust (NMET) contributions, GST, cess and other levies, plus ₹10,272 crore of dividend to the Government of India. Royalty is 14% of the run-of-mine price; DMF adds 30% of royalty for older leases; NMET adds 2%. These levies are billed to customers as "other charges", which is why Coal India's revenue of ₹2,221 a tonne including charges falls to ₹1,572 a tonne on a basic basis. The railway takes the third slice: coal is Indian Railways' largest freight commodity, and for a distant power plant the rail freight can equal or exceed the pithead price. That is why the decision between domestic and imported coal is made on landed cost at the plant gate, not on Coal India's price list.

In plain terms

Out of every rupee a power station pays for Coal India coal, roughly a third is taxes and levies passed straight to the government, and the railway often charges as much again to haul it. Coal India's real business is the remaining two-thirds of the pithead bill, and inside that it makes its money from a handful of huge, shallow opencast mines in Odisha and Madhya Pradesh.

Lignite works on different economics. GMDC sells to about 2,880 Gujarat customers at a price reset every fortnight against imported coal and gas, a rent on geography rather than geology. Its indicative ₹2,967 a tonne in FY26 looks high next to Coal India's ₹1,444, but per unit of heat it sits close to imported coal (section 17).

02

A billion tonnes, three producers: size and growth

National output crossed one billion tonnes in FY25 and stayed there in FY26; the growth now comes from private and captive mines, not from Coal India.

India produced 1,047.52 mt of coal in FY25, the first year above a billion tonnes: Coal India 781.06 mt, SCCL 69.01 mt and captive and other mines 197.46 mt. The Ministry of Coal has not published a final FY26 total that we could find. Adding Coal India's 768.19 mt, SCCL's 58.00 mt and the 210.46 mt reported for captive and commercial mines gives about 1,037 mt, roughly 1% lower. That figure is an indicative sum, not an official total, but the Ministry did confirm that the billion-tonne mark was crossed for the second year running on 20 March 2026. The FY26 national target had been 1,157 mt, so the year fell well short, for the simple reason that power stations did not need the coal.

The longer history shows two phases. From FY15 to FY21 national output crept from about 609 mt to about 716 mt, and Coal India missed its old target of one billion tonnes by 2020 by a wide margin. Then the shortages of October 2021 and April 2022, when power plants ran down to a few days of stock, forced a catch-up. National output jumped by about 110 mt a year between FY22 and FY24, Coal India's own production rose from 622.63 mt in FY22 to 773.65 mt in FY24, and the captive and commercial mines opened up by the 2020 reforms began to deliver. In plain terms

India now digs a little over a billion tonnes of coal a year. Coal India still digs three tonnes in every four, but almost all the growth in the last two years came from mines owned by power companies, metal companies and private bidders. The country also buys about a quarter of a billion tonnes abroad, mainly for steelmaking and coastal power stations.

Targets have a habit of running ahead of reality in this industry, and FY27 is no exception. Coal India's annual report sets a coal supply (offtake) target of 850.24 mt for FY27; its production target is reported at about 815 mt. In the first half it supplied 384.2 mt (+7.6%) and produced 321.0 mt (−2.5%). To hit the supply target it must ship about 466 mt in the second half, 20% more than the 387.8 mt of H2 FY26; to hit 815 mt of production it needs about 494 mt, 12.5% more than a year earlier. Both are stretch targets. A supply outcome of roughly 800–820 mt looks more realistic (an illustrative range). The company's longer-term goal, restated by its chairman at the August 2026 annual meeting, is about one billion tonnes by FY2029-30, backed by 124 ongoing projects with about 1,045 mtpa of capacity and ₹1,69,452 crore of sanctioned capital.

03

The residual fuel: what drives demand

Coal fills whatever electricity demand solar, wind, hydro and nuclear leave behind, so weather and gas prices move it more than the economy does.

About four-fifths of Coal India's volume ends up in power stations, so coal demand is mostly a question about electricity. Coal and lignite supplied 69.54% of India's electricity in April–June 2026 and about 66% in July–September 2026. Installed coal capacity was 2,24,158 MW on 30 June 2026, out of 5,48,858 MW in total, while non-fossil sources made up 54.18% of capacity, beating the 2030 target of 50% four years early. Capacity, though, is not generation. Solar works for part of the day, wind is seasonal and hydro depends on the monsoon. In non-solar peak hours thermal plants still supply roughly 75% of demand (188.8 GW of 251.4 GW in one recent reading).

That makes coal the residual fuel. Each year the grid takes whatever solar, wind, hydro and nuclear produce, and coal plants fill the gap. A rule of thumb from the Central Electricity Authority's numbers: each 1% of national generation is about 18–19 billion kWh, which needs about 12–13 mt of coal at roughly 0.68 kg per kWh (an indicative conversion). So a swing of three or four percentage points in coal-fired generation, which is what weather alone can cause, moves demand by 40–50 mt, more than the whole of SCCL's output.

FY26 showed what a good-monsoon year does. Hydro output rose 13%, renewable generation rose 21%, power consumption grew only about 1%, and coal-fired generation fell 3% to 1.28 trillion kWh. Pithead stocks filled, power plants had plenty, and the industrial buyers in e-auctions had no reason to bid up. FY27 has been the mirror image. A strong El Niño weakened hydro (hydro plant load factor fell from 61% to 50% in Q2), a hot summer pushed peak demand to a record 270 GW on 16 July 2026, and the West Asia war cut LNG supplies from early March, pushing gas-fired generation and industrial gas users towards coal. In Q2 FY27 total generation rose 11% to 536 billion units, coal and lignite generation rose to 351 billion units, and the coal plant load factor rose from 62% to 68%. September shortages reached 560 million units, the highest since August 2023.

The other demand levers

  • Fuel switching. Gas is under 4% of Indian power generation, so the LNG shortage mattered most for industrial heat and city-gas users. But across Asia gas-to-coal switching lifted 2026 coal demand, and the IEA expects Indian coal demand to grow 4.2% to 1.353 billion tonnes in 2026.
  • Industry (the non-regulated sector). Sponge iron output rose 8% to about 60 mt in FY26; cement, aluminium smelters with captive power, brick kilns and MSME boilers all burn coal. These buyers set the e-auction premium, and their demand is more sensitive to imported-coal prices than to the monsoon.
  • Steel and coking coal. India's steel capacity ambition of about 300 mtpa by 2030 means more blast furnaces and more coking coal. Domestic washed coking coal is a small share, so this demand flows mostly to imports (see section 18).
  • New uses: gasification. In May 2026 the Cabinet approved a ₹37,500 crore surface coal and lignite gasification scheme targeting about 75 mt, with incentives of up to 20% of plant and machinery, capped at ₹5,000 crore per project, and linkages of up to 30 years. Coal India and BHEL laid the foundation stone of a ₹25,000 crore coal-to-ammonium-nitrate plant (6.6 lakh tonnes a year) on 20 June 2026. The national target is 100 mt of coal gasified by 2030.
  • Exports. The Coal SETU window, approved on 12 December 2025, allows linkage coal to be used for any industrial purpose and for export. Volumes are trivial for now.

Over the medium term, the thermal pipeline is the anchor. India commissioned 21,080 MW of coal capacity between April 2023 and June 2026, and has about 47,545 MW under construction plus 16,000 MW awarded, roughly 63.5 GW in all. CREA counts about 44 GW of coal under construction, 90% of it less than half complete. Planners expect a thermal requirement of about 3,15,000 MW by 2035-36. Each 10 GW of new coal capacity running at a 65% plant load factor burns roughly 40 mt of coal a year (indicative arithmetic: 10 GW × 8,760 hours × 65% × 0.68 kg/kWh ≈ 39 mt), so the pipeline alone implies another 200–250 mt of demand by the early 2030s if it is built and run.

In plain terms

Coal is what India burns when the sun has set, the wind is still and the dams are low. Every new solar panel takes a little away from coal at midday, but every hot evening and every weak monsoon hands it back. As long as electricity demand grows faster than clean power can cover the evening, coal demand keeps rising, slowly and unevenly.

Two forecasts frame the debate. The old national ambition of 1.5 billion tonnes by 2029-30 has not been restated in any 2026 source we found and looks out of reach before the early 2030s. Coal India's own goal of about one billion tonnes by FY2029-30 implies about 7% a year of growth from FY26, faster than at any time except the FY22–FY24 catch-up. The more useful number for investors is the year-to-year swing: plus or minus 3–4% on weather, plus 2–3% of underlying growth while the thermal fleet expands, minus whatever renewables and batteries take from the evening peak.

04

Glut to squeeze in six months Deep dive

FY26 ended with the biggest coal pile in Coal India's history; by September 2026 a third of India's power plants were below a quarter of their normal stock. Earnings followed the inventory, not the mines.

The most useful thing an investor can know about Indian coal in any given quarter is not how much was dug, but where the stock is sitting and how fast it is moving. FY26 and the first half of FY27 are a textbook case. In twelve months the industry went from a glut that cut Coal India's profit by 12% to a shortage that pushed the auction premium to 94% and had the power ministry deferring plant maintenance. Neither extreme was caused by the mines. Both were caused by weather, gas and the inventory that sits between the pit and the boiler.

Act one: the FY26 glut

FY26 began with Coal India holding 107.16 mt of coal at its pitheads on 1 April 2025, already a comfortable cushion. Then the monsoon arrived early and stayed late. Heavy rain in the July–September quarter flooded opencast pits, slowed loading and, more importantly, filled the dams. Hydro generation rose 13% over the year and renewable generation 21%. Power consumption grew only about 1%. Coal-fired generation fell 3% to 1.28 trillion kWh, the first decline in years. Power stations, already well stocked, cut their offtake. Coal India's offtake fell 2.4% to 744.83 mt while it kept producing 768.19 mt, so about 23 mt was added to the pile. Pithead stock touched a record 129.96 mt on 31 March 2026, and total coal stocks across the system were about 224 mt in early April.

The financial damage came through price, not volume. With power plants full and industrial buyers in no hurry, the e-auction premium over the notified price fell to 46% for the year, from 48% in FY25 and 72% in FY24. In the monsoon quarter the damage was concentrated: Q2 FY26 operating profit was ₹6,716 crore, a 22% margin, against ₹12,588 crore and 29% in Q1. Two cost shocks then landed in the March quarter. Jharkhand's mineral-bearing land cess rose from ₹250 to ₹450 a tonne, lifting rates and taxes by ₹3,635 crore for the year, and a one-time executive pay-scale upgrade added ₹1,458 crore of employee cost. FY26 PAT ended at ₹31,071 crore, down 12.4%.

Act two: the FY27 squeeze

The turn came from outside the coal industry. The West Asia war that began on 28 February 2026 closed the Strait of Hormuz for weeks, took Brent to about $118 a barrel and cut LNG supplies to India from early March. Very little coal moves through Hormuz, so seaborne coal supply was barely touched, but coal demand jumped as gas users across Asia switched to coal. The Newcastle benchmark rose about 40% during 2026 to $151.15 a tonne by 22 October. At home a strong El Niño weakened the monsoon and the hydro reservoirs, and a hot summer drove peak electricity demand to a record 270 GW in July.

Coal India's response was to sell from stock. In Q1 FY27 (April–June) production fell 7% to 169.63 mt because the company was deliberately running down its pile, while offtake rose 4% to 197.86 mt, including a record June of 65.95 mt. July supplies rose 18.4% to 64.19 mt, a record for the month. August supplies rose 5.5% to 60.60 mt, and September supplies 12.5% to 61.20 mt. Q2 offtake was 186.04 mt, up 12.0%, against production of 151.37 mt, up 3.8%. Across the first half Coal India supplied 384.2 mt, 7.6% more than a year earlier, while producing 321.0 mt, 2.5% less. About 63 mt of pithead stock was liquidated. The company said in early September that its remaining stock of about 76 mt was "sufficient inventory".

Power plants did not agree. Even with Coal India shipping hard, plant stocks fell faster than they could be refilled because plants were burning about 79 mt a month between June and September. The Ministry of Coal's series for plants that run on domestic coal showed 42.8 mt on 12 July 2026, enough for about 14 days at an 85% load factor, then 18.9 mt on 29 September and 18.2 mt on 10 October. CREA's series covering all coal plants showed 21 mt on 30 September, only 36% of the 58 mt normative stock. The number of plants below 25% of their normative stock, the official "critical" line, went from 32 on 22 August to 61 on 8 September, 84 on 30 September and 85 on 1 October. At about 2.63 mt of daily consumption that is roughly 7–8 days of cover (an indicative estimate, since official days-of-cover figures were not published on a consistent basis).

In plain terms

Coal India's coal pile works like a shock absorber between the mines and the power stations. In FY26 the pile grew so large that the company had to discount its auction coal to move it. Why Q1 FY27 still showed lower profit

Investors who expected the squeeze to show up at once were disappointed by Q1 FY27. Revenue from operations rose 8% to ₹46,255 crore and PAT rose only 0.7% to ₹8,850 crore; screener's operating profit fell 4.1% to ₹12,069 crore. Three things held it back. First, the premium in April–July was only 43%, close to the previous year's level; the spike came in September. Second, diesel and explosives followed oil up after the West Asia war: materials cost rose sharply, with diesel up 27%, explosives up ₹244 crore and diesel ₹435 crore, and contractual expenses rose 11% (₹784 crore) as more outsourced overburden was removed. Third, the Jharkhand cess was in the base for the full quarter. Q1 was a volume quarter; the price effect belongs to Q2 and Q3.

The lesson: watch two dials

The episode teaches a simple discipline. Coal India's earnings are an inventory-cycle trade dressed as a utility, and two published series tell you where the cycle is. The first is plant coal stock against the normative level, published by the Ministry of Coal and CEA: below about 40% of norm, auction buyers panic; above about 80%, they relax. The second is Coal India's monthly e-auction premium, published with its monthly production release: it turned from 43% to 94% within two months. Production, the number most headlines lead with, is the least informative of the three. In Q1 FY27 a "production down 7%" headline came with revenue up 8%.

There is a sting in the tail. Coal India's FY27 targets assume continued destocking: supplying 850 mt from about 815 mt of production would draw a further 35 mt from the pile. Since 63 mt has already gone, hitting the supply target would leave pithead stock at perhaps 30–40 mt by March 2027 (indicative). That would make FY28 a production catch-up year, with more overburden and contractor spending and less help to revenue from stock sales. The glut-to-squeeze swing will not repeat in the same direction next year.

In plain terms

When you read "Coal India production falls", check whether despatches rose. If they did, the company is selling its stockpile, which is usually good for profit in the short run. The danger comes later, when the pile is gone and the company has to dig harder and pay contractors more just to keep up.

05

One price for the nation, another for the market

Nine-tenths of Coal India's coal sells at a notified price that rarely changes; the rest is auctioned. The mix, more than the tonnes, decides the year.

Coal India does not sell coal at a market price. It publishes a grade-wise notified price for non-coking coal, revises it every few years after consulting the government, and sells most of its output under long-term fuel supply agreements (FSAs) at or near that price. FSA customers are power utilities (central, state and independent), captive power plants and industrial users who won linkages in auctions. The notified price is set well below import parity, which has been the point of the system since nationalisation in the 1970s: coal is an input to electricity, electricity tariffs are regulated, and the state wants cheap power more than it wants a rich coal company. No general non-coking notified price increase was found in 2026; the last revisions were years apart. Basic FSA realisation rose only 0.8% in FY26 to ₹1,444 a tonne and was about 0.6% lower in Q1 FY27 at ₹1,435.

Three prices therefore matter, and they are easily confused. The notified price is the list price per grade and the base for both FSA billing and the e-auction floor. The FSA realisation is what linkage customers actually pay after grade, quality and size adjustments. The e-auction price is set by bidding and quoted as a percentage premium over the notified price. On top of that Coal India reports realisation in two ways: including "other charges" (royalty-linked levies, evacuation, sizing and similar recoveries) and "basic", excluding them. The difference is about ₹650 a tonne and is largely pass-through money that goes to the government.

The linkage machinery

The FSA system has been reworked several times, and each change shifts tonnes between the regulated and market tiers. The current framework is the revised SHAKTI policy approved by the Cabinet on 7 May 2025. Window I gives central and state power utilities coal at the notified price (55.2 mt was earmarked). Window II auctions linkages at a premium to anyone, including power plants designed for imported coal, for terms of up to 25 years and without the need for a power purchase agreement. Coal India's first half-yearly Window II auction began on 27 January 2026. For industry there are tranche-based non-regulated sector (NRS) linkage auctions for steel, cement and sponge iron, and since December 2025 the Coal SETU window, which allows linkage coal to be used for any industrial purpose and for export, but not resold in India.

The market tier is the single-window, mode-agnostic (SWMA) e-auction, where any buyer can bid for coal from any subsidiary, to be lifted by rail or road. In the first half of FY27 Coal India offered 142.2 mt through SWMA and allocated 57.1 mt (40%) at an average premium of 54%. Management says it offers at least 10% of production at auction and will not let the channel exceed about 20%, which at 850 mt would be roughly 170 mt. A regulated coal exchange is the next step: draft rules appeared in December 2025, and the MMDR Amendment Bill passed by Parliament in August 2026 provides for regulating mineral exchanges. Final rules were not found as of October 2026.

Why does the state keep the regulated tier so large? Partly because cheap coal is a quiet subsidy to state power utilities, whose finances are fragile and whose tariffs are politically set. Partly because a coal price at import parity would raise electricity costs for every consumer. And partly because Coal India is a monopoly under competition law: the Competition Commission has examined its FSA terms and e-auction practices in several abuse-of-dominance cases, some still pending. For an investor the implication is plain. The single largest lever on Coal India's profit is the notified price itself; an illustrative 5% increase on about 650 mt of FSA coal at ₹1,444 would add about ₹4,700 crore of EBITDA, roughly 11% of FY26's company-style EBITDA, more than any plausible swing in the auction premium. It is also the lever the company pulls least often, because it is political.

Coking coal is priced differently. Bharat Coking Coal (BCCL), the listed subsidiary in the Jharia field, links washed coking coal prices to the wholesale price index (+0.24% from 1 April 2026), and approved raw coking coal increases of 0.1–14% on 13 October 2026. Its washed prime coking coal base price was ₹13,403 a tonne in Q1 FY27, against imported coking coal averaging about $178 a tonne in FY26. Even so, BCCL lost ₹493 crore at the operating level in FY26, because its old, partly underground mines are expensive.

The e-auction premium: anatomy of the swing factor Deep dive

A tenth of the volume, a sixth of the basic revenue and most of the change in profit: how the premium is set, why it swings from 30% to 300%, and how much it is worth.

If you could know only one number about Coal India before its results, it should be the e-auction premium. It is the price at which the marginal buyer, usually a factory without a linkage, decides that Coal India coal is cheaper than the alternatives: imported coal, coal from a commercial miner, or doing without. The premium is published monthly, it is measured against the notified price, and it has ranged from about 30% in quiet years to roughly 280% at the peak of the 2022 shortage.

How the premium is set

Each month Coal India's subsidiaries offer quantities of specified grades at specified mines through the single-window e-auction. The reserve price is the notified price, or a floor above it. Buyers bid; the clearing bids, weighted by quantity, give the premium over notified price. The company's annual report gives the full-year figure: 46% in FY26, 48% in FY25 and 72% in FY24. Coal India's chairman told the August 2026 annual meeting that the FY26 average premium "over floor price" moderated to 38% from 48%. That is a different base (the floor price, not the notified price), and the statutory annual report figure of 46% is the one used in this report.

What a point of premium is worth

The sensitivity is easy to compute and easy to overstate. Take an illustrative FY27 e-auction volume of about 90 mt and a notified-equivalent price of about ₹1,550 a tonne. Each 10 points of premium adds about ₹155 a tonne, or about ₹1,400 crore of EBITDA a year, roughly 3.4% of FY26 company-style EBITDA of ₹41,242 crore. Per quarter, on 20–25 mt of auction coal, each 10 points is worth ₹300–400 crore. So September's jump from about 43% to 94%, if it held for a full quarter on 25 mt, would add roughly ₹1,900–2,000 crore of quarterly EBITDA (illustrative). That is large relative to one quarter, but small relative to the ₹4,700 crore that a 5% FSA price increase would bring in a year, or the ₹3,000–3,200 crore a year that a 10% wage increase would cost.

A big jump in the auction premium makes headlines, but Coal India only auctions about a tenth of its coal, and it will not auction more than a fifth. So even a near-doubling of the auction price adds a few thousand crore a year, while a small change in the list price that power stations pay, or in the wages of more than two lakh workers, moves profit by more.

Who wins and who loses when the premium moves

The premium matters beyond Coal India. Commercial-block miners who won blocks in auctions sell at market prices, so a premium collapse hits them first and a spike rewards them first; their revenue-share bids to state governments were set assuming some premium. Sponge-iron, brick and MSME buyers, who lack linkages, pay it directly. Power stations with linkages are insulated, but a high premium raises the value of Window II linkages and pushes coastal plants towards imported coal. GMDC's lignite price, reset fortnightly against imported coal and gas, tends to move in the same direction for the same reasons. A high premium is therefore good news for every listed coal and lignite seller and bad news for every unlinked industrial user, which is one reason the government keeps the channel small.

Three things to watch on the premium. First, the monthly SWMA release: the September figure of 94% will not hold once plant stocks rebuild, and a return to the 30–40% management calls normal is the base case for FY28. Second, the booking ratio: above 70% of offered quantity signals real scarcity; below 50% signals a glut. Third, policy: a coal exchange, the MMDR Amendment's removal of the 50% sale cap on captive mines, and more Window II and Coal SETU auctions all add supply to the market tier and should damp the premium over time.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes

Wages, contractors and levies take most of Coal India's revenue; interest on its cash pile is a fifth of its pre-tax profit.

Coal India's cost structure is unusual for a miner. Its single largest cost is people: FY26 employee benefits were ₹46,425 crore, 27.6% of revenue from operations. Next come "other expenses" (₹44,264 crore, 26.3%), which include royalty-type levies that are not recovered in the price, the Jharkhand cess, power, repairs, CSR and provisions. Third are contractual expenses (₹32,197 crore, 19.1%): payments to contractors who remove overburden, mine coal under mine developer-cum-operator (MDO) contracts and transport it. Materials, mainly explosives, diesel and spares, cost ₹11,171 crore (6.6%). Depreciation and amortisation were ₹10,137 crore (6.0%) and finance cost ₹1,216 crore (0.7%), mostly the unwinding of mine-closure provisions.

Wages, cess and the state: the cost stack nobody controls Deep dive

A wage agreement that has expired without a successor, a Supreme Court ruling that let states tax coal land, a ₹31,000 crore contingent claim and a new law that may defuse it.

Coal India's management controls productivity, outsourcing and capital spending. It does not control the three largest moving parts of its cost base: what it pays its workers under industry-wide agreements, what states and the Centre levy on each tonne, and how deep the remaining coal lies. This section takes the first two in turn, because both reached a turning point in 2026.

The state's share comes first

In FY26 Coal India paid ₹52,299 crore to central and state governments as royalty, DMF and NMET contributions, GST, cesses and other levies. It also paid ₹10,272 crore of dividend to the Government of India, which owns 61.13% of the company. Put together, the state received about ₹62,571 crore from Coal India in a year when the company's own profit after tax was ₹31,071 crore. Most of the levy money is collected from customers and passed through, so it does not reduce Coal India's profit directly. But it raises the delivered cost of coal, and any levy that cannot be passed on, such as Jharkhand's land cess, comes straight out of the margin.

The 2024 ruling and the 2026 law

On 25 July 2024 a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court ruled, eight to one, in Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India that royalty is not a tax and that states may tax mineral rights and mineral-bearing land. A follow-up order on 14 August 2024 let states recover arrears from 1 April 2005, in instalments over twelve years from April 2026, without interest or penalty. States took notice. Jharkhand raised its mineral-bearing land cess from ₹250 to ₹450 a tonne, the main reason Coal India's FY26 rates and taxes rose ₹3,635 crore.

The larger risk sat in the footnotes. Coal India's FY26 annual report carries comments from the Comptroller and Auditor General (C&AG) on two items. First, Central Coalfields' ₹364.44 crore Jharkhand MADA fee (1% of sale value from 2005-06 to 2022-23) was disclosed as a contingent liability rather than provided for; the C&AG said profit was overstated by that amount. Second, and far bigger, Coal India's own legal department had estimated Mahanadi Coalfields' exposure under Odisha's Rural Infrastructure and Socio-Economic Development (ORISED) Act 2004, a levy of 15% of annual coal value, at ₹31,227.80 crore, but the accounts did not quantify it. Management's reply: the Orissa High Court struck the Act down on 5 December 2005, the appeal is pending before a three-judge Supreme Court bench, Odisha has neither re-enacted it nor raised a demand, and disclosure would be "purely speculative". That ₹31,228 crore is roughly one year of Coal India's profit.

Then Parliament stepped in. The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Bill 2026 passed the Lok Sabha on 12 August and the Rajya Sabha on 13 August 2026. In plain terms

In 2024 the Supreme Court said states may tax land that has minerals under it. That opened the door to new state levies and to claims going back to 2005, the largest of which, an Odisha levy on Coal India's biggest subsidiary, its own lawyers put at about a year's profit. In August 2026 Parliament passed a law meant to shut that door again. Until it is notified and tested, the risk has shrunk but not vanished.

GST 2.0: a quiet cash gain

From 22 September 2025 coal and lignite moved from 5% GST plus a ₹400 a tonne compensation cess to a flat 18% with no cess. For buyers who can claim input credit the change is broadly neutral. For miners it ended an inverted duty structure in which they paid 18% GST on inputs such as explosives, equipment and services but charged only 5% on output, leaving unusable credit piling up. Coal India said the change let it use ₹5,985 crore of accumulated input tax credit, and expects the benefit to continue. GMDC booked a FY26 exceptional gain of ₹522.65 crore: ₹492.63 crore of previously reversed input tax credit (₹474.43 crore in Q2 and ₹18.20 crore in Q3) plus ₹30.02 crore of litigated credit restored in Q4. That one-off is why GMDC's reported FY26 PAT of ₹957 crore overstates its run-rate; excluding it after tax, PAT was about ₹565 crore (an indicative estimate).

The wage clock

Coal India's non-executive workers are paid under National Coal Wage Agreements negotiated every five years by a Joint Bipartite Committee for the Coal Industry (JBCCI) of management and unions. NCWA-XI covered July 2021 to June 2026, was signed about two years late in 2023, and gave a 19% minimum guaranteed benefit plus higher allowances. When it was signed Coal India booked a catch-up provision of ₹5,870.16 crore in Q4 FY23 alone, after only ₹475 crore in Q4 FY22. NCWA-XI expired on 30 June 2026. As of early October 2026 the JBCCI-XII committee had not even been constituted; unions want an exemption from section 14 of the Industrial Relations Code 2020, and a High Court petition by executives over pay anomalies may add delay. Executive pay revision falls due in January 2027. Q1 FY27 employee cost was up only 0.4%, so no provision has been booked.

The arithmetic matters. FY26 salary and wages were ₹35,991 crore, provident fund and other funds ₹9,006 crore and other employee costs ₹1,427 crore. If non-executive pay is about ₹30,000–32,000 crore of the bill, an illustrative 10% increase costs ₹3,000–3,200 crore a year, about 7–8% of company-style EBITDA, plus arrears from July 2026 of roughly ₹750–800 crore for each quarter of delay. The precedent suggests a lumpy catch-up provision, perhaps in FY28, rather than a smooth accrual; any FY27 accrual would be a policy choice by management. Headcount attrition of about 4% a year offsets part of it.

Who mines India's coal next Deep dive

Captive and commercial miners are taking the growth, Singareni is shrinking, and Coal India is selling stakes in its own subsidiaries. The monopoly is ending slowly, from the edges.

For forty years after nationalisation Coal India was Indian coal. The 2014 Supreme Court cancellation of 204 captive blocks, the 2015 Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act that re-auctioned them, and the 2020 reforms that opened commercial mining to anyone with no end-use restriction and 100% foreign investment on the automatic route changed that. Five years later the results are visible in the numbers.

The captive and commercial wave

Captive and commercial mines produced 210.46 mt in FY26, up 10.22%, and dispatched 204.61 mt. That is up from about 62 mt in FY15 and 154.2 mt in FY24. Twelve blocks were operationalised in FY26, adding 86 mtpa of capacity, and seven started production. Of 139 blocks allocated under the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act, 70 are operational and 60 producing. The Ministry of Coal expects more than 228 mt from these mines in FY27, about 8% more, with nine new mines adding 20.67 mtpa. Commercial auctions have now covered 147 mines over fifteen rounds, bringing in 44 new entrants and an expected ~₹47,500 crore a year of revenue share to states at peak. Round 15 closed on 4 August 2026 with six blocks (geological reserves about 1,504 mt, peak capacity 11.62 mtpa); Round 16, launched on 17 September 2026, offers 25 blocks in nine states.

Singareni: a warning about outsourcing

The Singareni Collieries Company (SCCL), founded in 1920 and owned 51.1% by Telangana and 48.9% by the Government of India, is the largest unlisted coal miner. It runs 37–38 mines in the Godavari valley, about 16–17 opencast and 21 underground, plus a 1,200 MW power plant and the Naini block in Odisha. Its geology is harder than Coal India's: a stripping ratio of about 6.3 cubic metres per tonne and an overall output per man-shift of 7.55 tonnes against Coal India's 12.65. In FY26 SCCL produced 58.004 mt against a 72 mt target and 69.006 mt the year before, its second straight decline. Its overburden removal reached only 365.26 million cubic metres against a target of 502 (72.8%), and the shortfall was concentrated in contracted work: contractors achieved 70.8% of their target against 89.4% for departmental crews. SCCL's own March 2026 report attributed 67% of the production loss to the poor performance of some overburden contractors and delays in awarding new contracts, 20.7% to delays in new projects and 11.9% to geo-mining conditions. Its FY27 target was cut to 62 mt, and April–July output was down another 16.3% to 16.90 mt.

The first archetype earns a rent on geology and gives part of it back by policy. Coal India's best opencast mines dig coal at a cash cost of perhaps ₹600–900 a tonne before levies (an indicative estimate), and the notified price is set well below import parity, so the company earns a steady but capped margin on most of its volume. The second archetype, captive mining, earns nothing on paper: the coal is transferred to the owner's power plant or smelter at cost. Its value is that the owner avoids paying Coal India's price plus rail freight, or the import price. That is why captive miners keep growing even when Coal India has coal to spare.

The third archetype, commercial mining, is the newest and most market-exposed. Bidders compete on the percentage of a representative price they will share with the state, with bids ranging from single digits to over 40%. They sell at whatever the market will pay, so they live and die by the same e-auction premium that drives Coal India's marginal profit. The fourth archetype, the contractor or MDO, earns a fee per tonne or per cubic metre and carries operational risk but not price risk. As Coal India and the captive miners outsource more, this is where more of the industry's cost, and some of its margin, now sits.

Rules of the pit: regulation and policy

The state owns the coal, the companies that mine most of it, the railway that moves it and the utilities that burn it. Policy is the industry's operating system.

Indian coal is regulated at every step, from who may mine to what the coal may be sold for. The Ministry of Coal sets policy, allocates blocks and administers Coal India, SCCL (jointly with Telangana) and NLC India. The Coal Controller's Organisation grades coal, compiles statistics and used to approve the opening of mines and seams; a 2025 amendment to the Colliery Control Rules handed those approvals to company boards. The Nominated Authority runs auctions under the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act. The Central Electricity Authority and the Ministry of Power set coal-stock norms for power plants, issue blending advisories and can direct imported-coal plants to run under Section 11 of the Electricity Act. The Competition Commission polices Coal India's conduct as a dominant firm; several cases over FSA terms and e-auction practices are pending before appellate bodies.

Price regulation is informal but real. Coal India fixes notified prices itself, but revisions happen after consulting the government and historically come every two to four years. In plain terms

In most countries a coal miner worries about the coal price. In India it also has to worry about whether the government will let it raise prices, how many tonnes it must reserve for power stations, which state will invent a new levy, and how long it will take to get permission to cut down a forest. Those questions are answered in Delhi and the state capitals, not in the market.

Environmental and transition policy is gentler than in most coal countries. India has no binding coal phase-out date. Its nationally determined contribution targets 50% non-fossil capacity by 2030, already exceeded at 54.18% in June 2026, and net zero by 2070. The Central Electricity Authority's 2023 rule requiring coal units to run down to 40% of capacity makes coal plants cycle more and burn less per megawatt as solar grows. The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme is intensity-based for industry. The European Union's carbon border adjustment mechanism, definitive from 1 January 2026, hits Indian steel and aluminium exporters and therefore, indirectly, the coal-fired captive power behind them. Mine-closure rules issued in 2025 require escrowed funds; 32 Coal India mines received final closure approval in FY26, 40 in all.

Fifty years, seven cycles: how the industry got here

Nationalisation, a scandal, a reform, two shortages and a glut: each shock left a rule behind, and the rules still shape the profit.

Commercial coal mining in India began at Raniganj in 1774 under the East India Company, and for two centuries it was a private, fragmented and often dangerous business. The modern industry starts with nationalisation: coking-coal mines were taken over in 1971–72, non-coking mines in 1973, and Coal India was formed as the holding company in 1975. Singareni, set up in 1920, stayed separate under the Andhra Pradesh (now Telangana) and central governments; Neyveli Lignite (now NLC India) was set up in 1956 and GMDC by the Government of Gujarat in 1963. The aim of nationalisation was security of supply and cheap fuel for the power sector, and the notified-price system that still governs most of Coal India's sales is its direct descendant.

The first cracks in the monopoly came in 1993, when captive mining was allowed for power, steel and cement companies, and in 2007, when the e-auction was introduced as a market channel alongside linkages. In November 2010 the government sold 10% of Coal India in an initial public offering at ₹245 a share, then India's largest. Since then the company has paid ₹302.25 a share in dividends, more than the IPO price, and the share has traded at ₹396 in October 2026. The second shock was "Coalgate": in September 2014 the Supreme Court cancelled 204 captive blocks allocated without auction, which led to the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act of 2015 and transparent auctions. Between 2015 and 2019 the government set and missed a target of one billion tonnes from Coal India by 2020; production rose from 494 mt in FY15 to 607 mt in FY19.

The third shock was reform. In 2020 commercial mining opened to any bidder, with no end-use restriction and full foreign ownership allowed, and the first auction took place in June. The fourth was shortage. In October 2021 and again in April 2022 a post-Covid demand rebound and weak imports left power plants with only days of coal; the government imposed blending mandates and directed imported-coal plants to run. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 took Newcastle coal above $400 a tonne, and Coal India's e-auction prices ran at roughly three times the FSA price. Coal India's PAT nearly doubled from ₹17,378 crore in FY22 to ₹31,723 crore in FY23, then reached a record ₹37,369 crore in FY24.

Reading a coal miner: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

The few numbers that explain a coal company's quarter, how to calculate them, what good looks like, and the definition traps that catch most readers.

A coal miner's results are simple once you know which numbers to read and in what order. Volume is offtake, not production. Price is a mix of a regulated tier and a market tier, and you must know which basis, with or without levies, you are looking at. Cost is mostly people, contractors and the state. Margin is best read per tonne. The balance sheet hides a large cash pile that some data services miss. This section builds each of those ideas into a key performance indicator (KPI), shows how to compute it with a worked example, sets a benchmark and compares the members, and ends with a ten-minute checklist for any quarterly result.

The profit equation

Everything a coal miner does can be written as one line. Basic revenue (excluding levies passed through to the state) equals offtake in tonnes multiplied by the weighted average price across sale channels. Subtract cash cost per tonne multiplied by tonnes and you have EBITDA. For Coal India the channels are FSA (about 87% of volume), e-auction (about 11%) and washed and other coal (about 2%).

1. Production versus offtake (and why sales differ again)

What it is. Production is coal mined; offtake (or despatch, or supply) is raw coal that leaves the mine for customers; sales quantity is what is billed. Formula. Closing stock = opening stock + production − offtake. Worked example. Coal India in Q1 FY27 produced 169.63 mt and despatched 197.86 mt, so stock fell by about 28 mt, from 129.96 mt to 101.35 mt. Revenue rose 8% even though production fell 7%. What drives it. Power demand and rail capacity drive offtake; geology, monsoon, clearances and contractors drive production. Benchmark. Good: offtake at or above production while stock is high (destocking into demand). Average: the two roughly equal. Weak: offtake persistently below production (stock building into a glut, as in FY26 when production of 768.19 mt exceeded offtake of 744.83 mt). Members. GMDC reports despatch, which is effectively sales (7.884 mt in FY26). The trap. Headlines lead with production; revenue follows offtake. In H1 FY27 Coal India's production fell 2.5% while offtake rose 7.6%.

13. Capex intensity

What it is. How much a miner reinvests relative to its depreciation and revenue. Formula. Capex ÷ D&A; capex ÷ revenue; capex per tonne. Worked example. Most mistakes in reading Indian coal companies come from mixing bases: production with offtake, prices with and without taxes, one data service's profit with another's. Pick one basis, say which it is, and stick to it.

How to read a coal company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Offtake, not production (1 minute). Pull despatch in mt and its change year on year. Compare with production: if offtake exceeds production, the quarter is helped by destocking. Red flag: offtake falling while production rises (stock building into a glut).
  2. Realisation by channel (1 minute). Note FSA and e-auction basic realisations and the e-auction share. Red flag: e-auction share below 10% or basic realisation falling while costs rise.
  3. The premium (30 seconds). Check the quarter's e-auction premium against 40% and 75%. Coal India publishes it monthly, so you should know it before the result.
  4. Employee cost (1 minute). Compare with the previous year's quarter. A jump means a wage or pay-scale provision; a flat number while a wage agreement is overdue means the provision is still to come. Red flag: a catch-up provision larger than ₹2,000 crore in one quarter.
  5. Contractual and materials (1 minute). These carry diesel and explosives. Red flag: both rising faster than volume.
  6. Other expenses and rates & taxes (1 minute). Look for new state levies, cess increases or provisions for state claims.
  7. EBITDA per tonne (1 minute). Compute it on one basis (screener OP ÷ offtake, or company EBITDA excluding other income ÷ sales tonnes). Compare with ₹450 and ₹650.
  8. Other income and the stripping adjustment (1 minute). Strip out treasury income and note the stripping credit; both flatter the reported margin.
  9. Stocks (1 minute). Closing pithead stock and its days of offtake; add the latest plant-stock reading. This is the best guide to the next two quarters.
  10. Cash and dividends (1.5 minutes). Note any interim dividend, the cash and deposits line (not just "investments"), capex against the annual target, and any listing or stake-sale news. For GMDC add lignite volume, the power segment result and any exceptional items, and check whether annual or quarterly revenue bases are being compared.
In plain terms

If you only have ten minutes, read four numbers: tonnes shipped, the auction premium, the employee cost line and profit per tonne. Together they tell you whether the quarter was driven by the weather, by the market, by the wage cycle or by the mines themselves.

One closing caution on the masterclass. Every benchmark above is indicative: they are ranges drawn from Coal India's own history and from global miners, not industry standards. Coal India has spent most of the past five years in the "average" band on most measures and in the "good" band on cash, payout and opencast productivity. GMDC sits in the "average" band on margin per tonne and the "weak" band on returns, because its capital is tied up in mines that are not yet producing and a power plant that loses money. The numbers that will move the most in FY27 are the e-auction premium, the GMDC lignite price and, sooner or later, the employee cost line.

How to read the table

Start with the combined row, then remember that it is Coal India with a rounding error. The members earned ₹1,71,053 crore of revenue in FY26, 0.6% less than the year before, and ₹37,615 crore of operating profit at a 22.0% margin. Reported PAT was ₹32,028 crore, or ₹31,636 crore excluding GMDC's GST one-off. At ₹2,57,262 crore of combined market value, the members trade at 8.0 times FY26 reported earnings and 5.7 times EV/EBITDA after deducting ₹44,341 crore of company-basis net cash. They paid ₹16,633 crore of dividends, a 51.9% payout and a 6.5% yield. Q1 FY27 combined revenue rose 8.0% to ₹47,162 crore while PAT rose 0.7% to ₹9,013 crore.

Coal India is the cheapest large coal miner in the table on earnings. Its 7.85× FY26 P/E compares with 12.6× for China Shenhua, 15.8× for Glencore and 16.6× for Whitehaven; only AlamTri (6.9×, not shown) is in the same range. On EV/EBITDA, at about 5.4×, it sits with Peabody (5.8× on FY2025) and below Whitehaven (about 6.5×) and Shenhua (11.4×). It is also the only large miner in the set with net cash worth about 18% of its market value. Why so cheap? Because the market treats its profits as capped on the upside by policy (the notified price), exposed on the downside to wages and state levies, and destined to decline slowly as captive mines and renewables erode its volume. The 6.7% yield is the market's way of saying it values the cash flow more than the growth.

GMDC is the opposite case. On reported FY26 earnings it looks moderately priced at 13.9×, but those earnings include the ₹522.65 crore GST credit; excluding it, the multiple is about 23.6× on an indicative ₹565 crore, and EV/EBITDA is about 27× FY26 operating profit. The market is paying for FY27's lignite price recovery, the Baitarani West coal block and the rare-earth option. The stock fell from an all-time high of ₹771.90 to a 52-week low of ₹419, roughly halving its market value from ₹24,546 crore to ₹13,310 crore, even as lignite prices rose 50–60% between March and September 2026. On an illustrative FY27 mining result of ₹1,250–1,350 crore, the EV/EBITDA multiple would fall to about 9×, which is the bull's arithmetic.

In plain terms

Coal India is priced like a bond with a coal kicker: about 8 times earnings, a 6.7% dividend yield and a lot of cash. GMDC is priced like a growth story: its earnings are small today, but buyers are paying for new mines, a coal block in Odisha and rare earths that may or may not arrive. The global miners sit in between, and none of them has Coal India's combination of low cost, net cash and high payout.

One caution on comparability. Coal India's margins look lower than Shenhua's or NLC's because about ₹48,000 crore of its revenue is pass-through levies; on basic revenue its FY26 EBITDA margin was about 34%. Who is winning: scale, price and the rent per tonne

Three maps put the members against India's listed coal and lignite companies and the world's miners. Coal India wins on scale and cash; GMDC on price per tonne; neither on growth.

With only two listed members, "who is winning" is best answered by placing them among the wider field. The three maps below plot Coal India and GMDC (gold dots, labelled as members) against reference companies (red dots): NLC India and Coal India's listed subsidiaries at home, and global coal miners abroad. Each map answers one question: who has scale and margin, who is cheap for what yield, and where inside Coal India the money is actually made.

The first map shows that Coal India's competitive position rests on scale and cost, not price. It produces more than any other coal company in the world, about 2.3 times China Shenhua's output and six times Peabody's, at a cash cost of roughly ₹1,000–1,100 a tonne before levies, about $11 (indicative), against Glencore's reported energy-coal free-on-board cash cost of $65.3 a tonne in 2025. That cost gap is the deepest moat in the sub-sector. Yet Coal India's margin is only middling because its price is capped by policy, and the gap between its cost and the seaborne price is shared with Indian electricity consumers rather than kept.

The second map is the valuation picture in one image. The market pays least for the most cash: Coal India, with a 6.7% yield and net cash, trades at under 8 times earnings, while GMDC, with a 2.3% yield and a much smaller profit, trades at nearly 14 times reported and about 24 times underlying earnings. The difference is the market's view of growth. Coal India's volumes are expected to grow slowly and its share to fall; GMDC is expected to roughly double its mining output over five years if its new lignite mines and the 15 mtpa Baitarani West coal block deliver. GMDC's record of missing volume guidance is the main reason its multiple has already halved.

The third map looks inside the giant. Coal India's group average of about ₹418 of PAT per tonne hides a spread from about ₹777 at NCL to a loss of about ₹200 at ECL. The winners are the large, shallow opencast operations in Singrauli and Odisha; the losers are the old underground-heavy fields of Raniganj and Jharia. That matters for two reasons. First, Coal India's future volume growth comes mostly from MCL, SECL and NCL, so its average profit per tonne should improve as the mix shifts, unless wages and levies overtake it. Second, the listing programme sells minority stakes in exactly the best units, so Coal India's shareholders give up a share of the richest rent in return for cash.

In plain terms

Coal India is winning on cost and size, and losing slowly on market share and growth. GMDC is winning on price per tonne in its home market, but it is small and has repeatedly failed to grow its volumes as promised. Inside Coal India, two subsidiaries make two-thirds of the profit, and one of them is about to be partly sold to the public.

GMDC: lignite, Odisha coal and rare earths Deep dive

A regional toll on Gujarat's industrial heat, a coal block that could double the company, and a rare-earth deposit that has not yet produced a rupee.

GMDC is 1.5% of the members' revenue and 5.2% of their market value, but it is the only listed Indian company that sells lignite into an open market, and it carries three different kinds of exposure in one small stock: a cyclical merchant-fuel business, a large greenfield coal project and a rare-earth option. Each needs to be valued differently.

The lignite toll booth

GMDC mined and sold 7.884 mt of lignite in FY26 from four producing mines in Kutch, Bhavnagar and south Gujarat: Mata-no-Madh (3.27 mt), Bhavnagar (2.55 mt, a record, with about 15 years of life left), Umarsar (1.52 mt) and Tadkeshwar (0.54 mt). A fifth, Rajpardi, closed after a safety incident. GMDC meets more than a quarter of Gujarat's lignite demand. Its customers are about 2,880 textile, ceramic, brick and chemical units plus the state power utilities; the top ten take about 20% of sales, and most pay in advance or by letter of credit. In March 2026 it made its first coastal shipments, about 39,000 tonnes from Bhavnagar to Maharashtra.

The Odisha coal block

GMDC was allotted two Odisha coal blocks in March 2023 and a third in July 2024. The largest, Baitarani West, holds about 1.097 billion tonnes of geological reserves and 468 mt mineable, with a planned peak output of 15 mtpa of G-11 grade coal, twice GMDC's entire lignite output. It received environmental and Stage-I forest clearance and awarded its mine developer-cum-operator contract on 11 December 2025, and CareEdge now expects commercial production in Q4 FY27. Burapahar in the Ib Valley is a year or two behind, and Kudanali-Lubri is under exploration. GMDC has issued about ₹1,748 crore of bank guarantees to the Ministry of Coal for these blocks, which is why ₹430.75 crore of its fixed deposits are lien-marked.

The commercial model has shifted. In May 2025 management said it would not sell at Coal India's "fairly low" notified price and would be "mindful of international price". In February 2026 it signed a memorandum with NTPC to supply G-11 coal on cost-plus terms to NTPC plants serving Gujarat's distribution companies, alongside NTPC and GNFC memoranda on gasification. Cost-plus supply lowers the risk of the project and its upside. An illustrative sketch: at 15 mtpa and a margin of ₹300–400 a tonne, Baitarani West would add ₹450–600 crore of EBITDA a year at peak, roughly as much as GMDC's whole FY26 operating profit, but peak is guided for around FY30 and the first year will be small.

The rare-earth option

GMDC's Ambadungar deposit in Chhota Udepur holds about 1.29 mt of in-situ rare-earth oxides, according to its annual report. In January 2026 BARC transferred its process for producing a mixed rare-earth concentrate from Ambadungar's ore, to be tested at pilot scale first at GMDC's own centre. The board approved a memorandum with IREL, the state rare-earths company, at its Q1 FY27 meeting on 31 July 2026, and an earlier memorandum with NMDC was reported in March 2026. No company document gives a commercial output volume or date; the target of about 12,000 tonnes a year of oxides by FY28 that has circulated is not sourced in any GMDC filing we read. Separation, not mining, is the bottleneck, and it depends on partners. Any revenue is years away, which makes it an option to value with a probability, not a forecast to model.

Imports and the global lens

India buys a quarter of a billion tonnes of coal abroad. Steam-coal imports are falling; coking-coal imports for steel are rising, and total imports rose in FY26.

India is the world's second-largest coal producer and second-largest importer, after China in both. Official trade data (DGCI&S, HS 2701) show imports of 246.4 mt in FY26, 1.1% more than FY25's 243.6 mt, worth US$26.7 billion or ₹2,35,954 crore. The headline "import substitution" story is half true. Steam (thermal) coal imports peaked at 183.5 mt in FY24 and fell to 156.1 mt in FY26, down 15% in two years, as domestic output rose and the government urged power plants to "substitute the substitutable". Coking coal imports, by contrast, rose 15.8% in FY26 alone to 66.1 mt, because India's steel industry is building blast furnaces and domestic coking coal is scarce and high in ash. India is 85–90% import-dependent for coking coal, mostly from Australia, then the US, Russia and Mozambique.

The import bill fell for a different reason than volume. Steam coal cost an average of $144 a tonne in FY23, the year after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, and $75 in FY26; coking coal fell from $344 to $178. So India's coal import bill dropped from $48 billion in FY23 to $26.7 billion in FY26 on price, not tonnes. Non-coking imports in FY26 came mostly from Indonesia (94.6 mt), South Africa (33.7 mt) and the United States (13.7 mt), per BigMint. Imported coal goes mainly to coastal power plants designed for it (Adani's and Tata's at Mundra, JSW, Essar), to blending at inland plants, and to Gujarat and Maharashtra industry. That makes import parity the ceiling for Coal India's e-auction price and the reference for GMDC's lignite price.

Seaborne prices rose sharply in 2026. The Newcastle 6,000 kcal benchmark was $151.15 a tonne on 22 October 2026, up 40.6% for the year and 45.8% on a year earlier. Indonesia's October reference prices (HBA, period II) were $126.81 for 6,322 kcal coal and $66.10 for 4,100 kcal coal. A weaker rupee, at about ₹94.9 to the dollar against about ₹86 in FY25, adds roughly 8–10% to landed cost on its own (indicative). The West Asia war barely touched coal shipping, since very little coal passes through Hormuz, but it raised coal demand through gas-to-coal switching and raised freight and insurance costs (see shipping). Imported coal therefore became more expensive just as Indian power plants ran short, widening the gap that Coal India's auctions and GMDC's lignite could price into.

In plain terms

India is buying less foreign coal for its power stations and more for its steel mills. The steel coal cannot easily be replaced from Indian mines, so import dependence for steelmaking is rising even as the power sector becomes more self-reliant. When world coal prices and the dollar rise together, as in 2026, domestic coal looks cheap and Indian miners can charge more at auction.

Coal India against the world's miners

Global coal demand was about 8.94 billion tonnes in 2026, by the IEA's estimate, of which China burned about 5.0 billion tonnes (56%) and India about 1.353 billion (15%). China is not an exporter; it is the world's largest importer, and its domestic prices and import appetite set the Asian seaborne balance. Indonesia sells about 45–50% of its exports to China and about a quarter to India. Against that backdrop Coal India is the world's largest coal miner by volume, but not by value.

Peak coal and the hedges

Coal demand is still growing, but solar plus batteries are coming for the evening peak. The miners are hedging with solar, gasification and minerals, at a scale that is still small.

The long-run threat to Indian coal is not a ban; India has no coal phase-out date. It is the economics of renewables plus storage. Solar capacity reached 1,62,152 MW in June 2026, another 1,47,720 MW of renewable capacity was under construction, and 15,870 MW of pumped storage and 15,754 MW (42,530 MWh) of battery storage were in development. Solar already takes much of the midday load. Coal's stronghold is the evening and night, when it still supplies about three-quarters of demand. Batteries that shift cheap noon solar into the evening peak are the real threat to that share. FY26 showed what a good-hydro, high-renewables year does to coal: generation fell 3%. The Central Electricity Authority's rule that coal units must be able to run down to 40% of capacity means coal plants will increasingly cycle up and down, burning less coal per megawatt installed.

Against that, India is still building about 44–48 GW of coal capacity, power demand is growing, and the IEA expects Indian coal demand to grow 4.2% in 2026. The reasonable expectation, an indicative view rather than a forecast, is that Indian coal demand plateaus some time in the 2030s rather than the 2020s, with year-to-year swings driven by the monsoon and gas prices. For Coal India that means another decade of volume, slowly growing or flat, followed by a long decline, with the price of each tonne still set largely in Delhi.

The miners are hedging. Coal India targets 9.5 GW of renewable capacity by FY2029-30: its first 100 MW solar plant was commissioned at Patan, Gujarat on 4 May 2026, 200 MW of a 300 MW project at Khavda followed on 8 July 2026, it has a 600 MW order at Jalaun in Uttar Pradesh, and it has formed joint ventures with Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh utilities. Its first energy revenue was ₹5.68 crore in Q1 FY27. With BHEL it is building a ₹25,000 crore coal-to-ammonium-nitrate plant (6.6 lakh tonnes a year) through Bharat Coal Gasification and Chemicals, whose foundation stone was laid on 20 June 2026; with GAIL it has a coal-gas venture; and its HURL fertiliser joint venture paid a maiden dividend of ₹404.37 crore (see fertilizers). It holds five critical-mineral blocks, including a rare-earth licence at Kawalapur in Maharashtra, won its first iron-ore block (Gadadharpur, Odisha, 288 mt) in August 2026, set up CIL Global in Singapore on 24 August 2026 for overseas mineral acquisitions, and is planning 1,600 MW of thermal capacity with DVC at Chandrapura (see heavy electrical).

In plain terms

India will burn more coal for some years yet, but solar panels with batteries will eventually take the evening hours that coal still owns. Coal India and GMDC are putting money into solar, coal-to-chemicals and minerals, but these bets are small next to their coal businesses and slow to pay off. For now the hedge that matters most is the cash on their balance sheets.

Bull vs bear

The case for and against Indian coal and lignite equities, on the evidence available in October 2026.

The bull case

  • The squeeze is real. Plant stocks at 36% of norm, a 94% September premium and Q2 FY27 offtake up 12% point to the strongest Q2–Q3 in two years, with GMDC's lignite prices 50–60% higher.
  • Lowest cost on earth. Coal India's ex-levy cash cost of about $11 a tonne (indicative) protects margins in any price environment.
  • Cash and yield. About ₹42,900 crore of net cash, a 6.7% yield covered nearly twice, and a P/E under 8.
  • Tail risk defused. The MMDR Amendment 2026, if notified as passed, caps state levies and legacy claims such as the ₹31,228 crore Odisha exposure.
  • Value unlock. Listed BCCL and CMPDI stakes are worth about ₹22,900 crore; MCL's listing will put a market price on the group's best asset. GMDC's Baitarani West could double its mining scale.

The bear case

  • The wage bill is coming. NCWA-XI expired in June 2026 with no provision; the last catch-up was ₹5,870 crore in one quarter, and executive revision follows in January 2027.
  • Share erosion. Captive and commercial mines grew 10% in FY26 and may now sell freely; Coal India's share fell from 77.5% to about 74% in two years.
  • Price is capped. No general notified-price increase in 2026, and management caps auctions at about 20% of volume.
  • The squeeze unwinds. Destocking cannot continue; FY28 may need more overburden, more contractor spend and less help from stock sales.
  • Transition and execution. Batteries will take the evening peak in time; GMDC has missed volume guidance and its rare earths have no output target.

How might the next eighteen months play out? Three illustrative scenarios for Coal India, built from the sensitivities in section 06 and not forecasts. In a bull case the e-auction premium averages about 70% through FY27, offtake reaches about 820 mt and the wage provision slips to FY28, which could lift EBITDA by roughly 15–20% from FY26. In a base case the premium averages 50–55%, offtake is 800–810 mt, and a partial wage accrual begins in H2, leaving EBITDA up perhaps 5–10%. In a bear case the premium falls back to 30–35% as plant stocks rebuild, a 15% wage increase plus arrears is provided in FY27, and a new state levy appears, cutting EBITDA by 10–15%. In all three, the dividend is likely to stay close to ₹26.50, because the government needs it, which is why the stock's downside has historically been cushioned by its yield.
