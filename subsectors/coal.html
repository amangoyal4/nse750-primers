Indian coal: a policy price in a market squeeze

A complete guide to the business of digging India's coal and lignite: why Coal India sells nine tonnes in ten at a price the state effectively sets, why its profit still swings with the weather, how a record glut in FY26 became a power-plant shortage by September 2026, what wages, state levies and private miners are doing to the economics, and how the two listed members, Coal India and GMDC, compare with the rest of the industry and the world's coal miners.

00

The one-page view: a state-set price, a weather-set profit

India digs about a billion tonnes of coal a year, three-quarters of it through one company, and the money is made in the narrow slice that is sold at a market price.

The listed Indian coal sub-sector has two members, and one of them is almost all of it. Coal India Limited (CIL) produced 768.19 million tonnes (mt) in FY26, about 74% of the country's coal, and earned ₹1,68,400 crore of revenue from operations. Gujarat Mineral Development Corporation (GMDC), the second member, sold 7.88 mt of lignite and earned ₹2,653 crore. On the members' combined FY26 revenue of ₹1,71,053 crore, Coal India is 98.5%; it is 98.8% of operating profit, 97% of reported profit and 94.8% of the combined market value of ₹2,57,262 crore (23 October 2026). So this report reads the whole Indian coal and lignite industry through Coal India, and treats GMDC as the window onto a different trade: merchant lignite in Gujarat, a coal block in Odisha and a rare-earth deposit that has not yet earned a rupee.

Five forces explain almost everything that happens to the industry's profit. The first is weather transmitted through inventory. Coal is the residual fuel in Indian electricity: it supplies whatever demand is left after solar, wind, hydro and nuclear have done their share. A wet year with strong hydro, like FY26, left coal-fired generation 3% lower, pushed Coal India's pithead stock to a record 129.96 mt and cut its profit after tax (PAT) by 12% to ₹31,071 crore. A hot, dry El Niño year with an LNG shortage, like the first half of FY27, did the opposite: by 30 September 2026 coal stocks at power plants were down to about 21 mt, 36% of the normative level, and Coal India's September e-auction premium hit 94%.

The second force is that the price is set by policy, not by the market. About 87% of Coal India's volume goes to long-term linkage customers under fuel supply agreements (FSAs) at a notified price that the company fixes with the government looking over its shoulder. In FY26 an FSA tonne fetched ₹1,444 before levies; an e-auction tonne fetched ₹2,260. E-auction was only 11.3% of volume, but it is the part of the business that moves with the market, so it explains most of the change in profit from one year to the next.

The third force is the slow erosion of the monopoly. Captive and commercial mines, owned by power utilities, metal companies and private bidders, produced 210.46 mt in FY26, up 10.2%, while Coal India fell 1.6% and the Telangana-owned Singareni Collieries (SCCL) fell 15.9%. Coal India's share of national output slid from 77.5% in FY24 to about 74% in FY26. It still plans to reach about one billion tonnes by FY2029-30.

The fourth force is cost and the state. Wages are a third of Coal India's cash cost and the five-year wage agreement for non-executive workers (NCWA-XI) expired on 30 June 2026, with no successor yet and no provision booked. The Supreme Court's July 2024 ruling that states may tax mineral-bearing land let Jharkhand raise its cess from ₹250 to ₹450 a tonne, adding ₹3,635 crore to Coal India's FY26 rates and taxes. Overburden per tonne of coal has risen from about 1.9 to 2.58 cubic metres, and diesel and explosives followed oil prices up after the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026.

The fifth force is imports and the energy transition. India still imported 246.4 mt of coal in FY26, more than the year before, because coking coal for steel rose 16% to 66.1 mt even as steam-coal imports fell. Non-fossil sources are already 54% of installed power capacity, yet about 44 GW of new coal plants are under construction. Coal demand is still growing, and the question for investors is how long that lasts and what Coal India does with its cash in the meantime.

In plain terms

Coal India sells most of its coal at a fixed, low price to power stations and a small slice at whatever buyers will pay in online auctions. When the country is short of coal, the auction price jumps and profits rise; when coal piles up, the auction price slips and profits fall. Wages, state taxes and the cost of moving more earth per tonne creep up every year regardless. That is the whole business in three sentences, and it is why a company with the world's lowest mining costs trades at eight times earnings.

Valuation reflects that mix of strength and constraint. At ₹396 a share Coal India trades at 7.85 times FY26 reported earnings, about 5.4 times EV/EBITDA on its company-basis net cash of roughly ₹42,941 crore, and yields 6.7% on its ₹26.50 dividend. GMDC, at a 52-week low of ₹419, trades at 13.9 times FY26 reported earnings, which flatter it because they include a ₹522.65 crore GST credit; excluding that one-off, the multiple is about 23.6 times on an indicative ₹565 crore. The market is paying very little for Coal India's cash flows because it doubts their durability, and still paying something for GMDC's future mines and rare earths. The rest of this report explains both judgements, and what would change them.

01

From pit to power plant: where a tonne earns its money

Value sits in cheap opencast geology, is shared with the state through levies, and is decided at the moment of sale by which channel the tonne goes through.

Indian coal is mostly Gondwana bituminous and sub-bituminous coal: high in ash (30–45%), modest in heat value (about 3,000–5,000 kilocalories per kilogram for typical grades), and lying in thick seams near the surface in Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand and Maharashtra. The country's geological coal resources stood at 4,00,715 mt on 1 April 2025, led by Odisha (1,00,976 mt), Jharkhand (93,254 mt) and Chhattisgarh (85,263 mt). Only 37,373 mt, about 9%, is coking coal, the kind steelmakers need, which is why India is self-sufficient in power coal on paper and import-dependent for steel. Lignite, or brown coal, is a separate resource of 47,371 mt, mostly in Tamil Nadu (37,524 mt), Rajasthan and Gujarat (2,722 mt). Lignite has even less heat (about 2,200–3,500 kcal/kg) and so much moisture that it cannot economically be railed far; it is burnt at or near the mine.

The rent is earned in Step 3, where the cheapest opencast mines in Odisha and Madhya Pradesh dig coal for a fraction of its value. Step 5 decides how much of that rent the miner keeps.

The physical work is simple to describe. In an opencast mine the miner strips away the rock and soil above the seam (the overburden, or OB), loads the exposed coal into dumpers, and moves it to a siding. The ratio of overburden removed to coal won, the stripping ratio, is the single biggest determinant of mining cost. Coal India's stripping ratio was 2.58 cubic metres per tonne in FY26, so for every tonne of coal it moved about two and a half cubic metres of earth. Underground mining is now a sideline (25.56 mt of Coal India's 768.19 mt in FY26) and almost always loses money, which is why the old underground-heavy subsidiaries in the Raniganj and Jharia coalfields are the weakest parts of the group.

Evacuation is the hidden bottleneck. In Q1 FY27 about 62 mt of Coal India's 198 mt offtake still left the mines by road. The industry is replacing trucks with first-mile connectivity (FMC): conveyor belts, silos and rapid-loading systems that fill a railway rake in about an hour. Nationally 139 FMC projects worth about ₹45,000 crore and 1,319 mtpa of capacity are planned or built, 65 of them commissioned (552 mtpa). Coal India alone expects 994 mtpa of mechanised loading by FY29. In a shortage, though, the binding constraint becomes railway rakes: loading rose from 426 rakes a day in September 2026 to 469 a day in early October as the system scrambled to refill power plants.

Customers are concentrated. Between April and December 2025 Coal India dispatched 545.7 mt, of which power utilities took 434.1 mt (79.5%), captive power plants 43.3 mt, cement 5.5 mt, sponge iron 4.3 mt, steel 2.6 mt and "others" 56.0 mt. The remaining industrial buyers, collectively called the non-regulated sector (NRS), matter far more to profit than their tonnage suggests, because they are the marginal bidders in the e-auctions that set the premium. Coal India's NRS supplies rose 19.4% year on year in September 2026, one sign that industry was scrambling for coal as gas became scarce and dear.

Where the value is captured

The value chain has three claimants: the mine, the state and the railway. The mine earns a rent on geology. Coal India's best subsidiaries, Northern Coalfields (NCL) in the Singrauli field of Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh and Mahanadi Coalfields (MCL) in Odisha's Talcher and Ib Valley fields, earned about ₹777 and ₹506 of PAT per tonne of offtake in FY26. Together they made ₹21,355 crore of profit, about 69% of the group's, on 47% of its offtake. At the other end Eastern Coalfields (ECL), with old underground mines in West Bengal, lost about ₹200 a tonne. The rent is a property of the rock, not of management skill, which is one reason the stake sales in MCL and other subsidiaries matter so much to Coal India's shareholders (see section 09).

The state takes roughly as much as the shareholders. Coal India paid ₹52,299 crore to the exchequer in FY26 as royalty, District Mineral Foundation (DMF) and National Mineral Exploration Trust (NMET) contributions, GST, cess and other levies, plus ₹10,272 crore of dividend to the Government of India. Royalty is 14% of the run-of-mine price; DMF adds 30% of royalty for older leases; NMET adds 2%. These levies are billed to customers as "other charges", which is why Coal India's revenue of ₹2,221 a tonne including charges falls to ₹1,572 a tonne on a basic basis. The railway takes the third slice: coal is Indian Railways' largest freight commodity, and for a distant power plant the rail freight can equal or exceed the pithead price. That is why the decision between domestic and imported coal is made on landed cost at the plant gate, not on Coal India's price list.

In plain terms

Out of every rupee a power station pays for Coal India coal, roughly a third is taxes and levies passed straight to the government, and the railway often charges as much again to haul it. Coal India's real business is the remaining two-thirds of the pithead bill, and inside that it makes its money from a handful of huge, shallow opencast mines in Odisha and Madhya Pradesh.

Lignite works on different economics. GMDC's lignite is sold to roughly 2,880 customers in Gujarat, mostly small and medium textile, ceramic, brick and chemical units, at a price reset every fortnight against the cost of imported coal and gas. There is no rail-linked black coal close to Kutch and Saurashtra, and gas is expensive, so GMDC captures a rent on geography rather than geology. Its realisation, about ₹2,967 a tonne in FY26 (an indicative figure derived from lignite revenue and tonnes), looks higher than Coal India's ₹1,444 FSA price, but per unit of heat it is far closer to the price of imported coal. Section 17 covers GMDC in detail.

02

A billion tonnes, three producers: size and growth

National output crossed one billion tonnes in FY25 and stayed there in FY26; the growth now comes from private and captive mines, not from Coal India.

India produced 1,047.52 mt of coal in FY25, the first year above a billion tonnes: Coal India 781.06 mt, SCCL 69.01 mt and captive and other mines 197.46 mt. The Ministry of Coal has not published a final FY26 total that we could find. Adding Coal India's 768.19 mt, SCCL's 58.00 mt and the 210.46 mt reported for captive and commercial mines gives about 1,037 mt, roughly 1% lower. That figure is an indicative sum, not an official total, but the Ministry did confirm that the billion-tonne mark was crossed for the second year running on 20 March 2026. The FY26 national target had been 1,157 mt, so the year fell well short, for the simple reason that power stations did not need the coal.

The longer history shows two phases. From FY15 to FY21 national output crept from about 609 mt to about 716 mt, and Coal India missed its old target of one billion tonnes by 2020 by a wide margin. Then the shortages of October 2021 and April 2022, when power plants ran down to a few days of stock, forced a catch-up. National output jumped by about 110 mt a year between FY22 and FY24, Coal India's own production rose from 622.63 mt in FY22 to 773.65 mt in FY24, and the captive and commercial mines opened up by the 2020 reforms began to deliver. Across the decade from FY15 to FY25 national production grew about 5.6% a year; Coal India's own production grew 3.7% a year from FY17 to FY26.

In plain terms

India now digs a little over a billion tonnes of coal a year. Coal India still digs three tonnes in every four, but almost all the growth in the last two years came from mines owned by power companies, metal companies and private bidders. The country also buys about a quarter of a billion tonnes abroad, mainly for steelmaking and coastal power stations.

Targets have a habit of running ahead of reality in this industry, and FY27 is no exception. Coal India's annual report sets a coal supply (offtake) target of 850.24 mt for FY27; its production target is reported at about 815 mt. In the first half it supplied 384.2 mt (+7.6%) and produced 321.0 mt (−2.5%). To hit the supply target it must ship about 466 mt in the second half, 20% more than the 387.8 mt of H2 FY26; to hit 815 mt of production it needs about 494 mt, 12.5% more than a year earlier. Both are stretch targets. A supply outcome of roughly 800–820 mt looks more realistic (an illustrative range). The company's longer-term goal, restated by its chairman at the August 2026 annual meeting, is about one billion tonnes by FY2029-30, backed by 124 ongoing projects with about 1,045 mtpa of capacity and ₹1,69,452 crore of sanctioned capital.

03

The residual fuel: what drives demand

Coal fills whatever electricity demand solar, wind, hydro and nuclear leave behind, so weather and gas prices move it more than the economy does.

About four-fifths of Coal India's volume ends up in power stations, so coal demand is mostly a question about electricity. Coal and lignite supplied 69.54% of India's electricity in April–June 2026 and about 66% in July–September 2026. Installed coal capacity was 2,24,158 MW on 30 June 2026, out of 5,48,858 MW in total, while non-fossil sources made up 54.18% of capacity, beating the 2030 target of 50% four years early. Capacity, though, is not generation. Solar works for part of the day, wind is seasonal and hydro depends on the monsoon. In non-solar peak hours thermal plants still supply roughly 75% of demand (188.8 GW of 251.4 GW in one recent reading).

That makes coal the residual fuel. Each year the grid takes whatever solar, wind, hydro and nuclear produce, and coal plants fill the gap. A rule of thumb from the Central Electricity Authority's numbers: each 1% of national generation is about 18–19 billion kWh, which needs about 12–13 mt of coal at roughly 0.68 kg per kWh (an indicative conversion). So a swing of three or four percentage points in coal-fired generation, which is what weather alone can cause, moves demand by 40–50 mt, more than the whole of SCCL's output.

FY26 showed what a good-monsoon year does. Hydro output rose 13%, renewable generation rose 21%, power consumption grew only about 1%, and coal-fired generation fell 3% to 1.28 trillion kWh. Pithead stocks filled, power plants had plenty, and the industrial buyers in e-auctions had no reason to bid up. FY27 has been the mirror image. A strong El Niño weakened hydro (hydro plant load factor fell from 61% to 50% in Q2), a hot summer pushed peak demand to a record 270 GW on 16 July 2026, and the West Asia war cut LNG supplies from early March, pushing gas-fired generation and industrial gas users towards coal. In Q2 FY27 total generation rose 11% to 536 billion units, coal and lignite generation rose to 351 billion units, and the coal plant load factor rose from 62% to 68%. September shortages reached 560 million units, the highest since August 2023.

The other demand levers

  • Fuel switching. Gas is under 4% of Indian power generation, so the LNG shortage mattered most for industrial heat and city-gas users. But across Asia gas-to-coal switching lifted 2026 coal demand, and the IEA expects Indian coal demand to grow 4.2% to 1.353 billion tonnes in 2026.
  • Industry (the non-regulated sector). Sponge iron output rose 8% to about 60 mt in FY26; cement, aluminium smelters with captive power, brick kilns and MSME boilers all burn coal. These buyers set the e-auction premium, and their demand is more sensitive to imported-coal prices than to the monsoon.
  • Steel and coking coal. India's steel capacity ambition of about 300 mtpa by 2030 means more blast furnaces and more coking coal. Domestic washed coking coal is a small share, so this demand flows mostly to imports (see section 18).
  • New uses: gasification. In May 2026 the Cabinet approved a ₹37,500 crore surface coal and lignite gasification scheme targeting about 75 mt, with incentives of up to 20% of plant and machinery, capped at ₹5,000 crore per project, and linkages of up to 30 years. Coal India and BHEL laid the foundation stone of a ₹25,000 crore coal-to-ammonium-nitrate plant (6.6 lakh tonnes a year) on 20 June 2026. The national target is 100 mt of coal gasified by 2030.
  • Exports. The Coal SETU window, approved on 12 December 2025, allows linkage coal to be used for any industrial purpose and for export. Volumes are trivial for now.

Over the medium term, the thermal pipeline is the anchor. India commissioned 21,080 MW of coal capacity between April 2023 and June 2026, and has about 47,545 MW under construction plus 16,000 MW awarded, roughly 63.5 GW in all. CREA counts about 44 GW of coal under construction, 90% of it less than half complete. Planners expect a thermal requirement of about 3,15,000 MW by 2035-36. Each 10 GW of new coal capacity running at a 65% plant load factor burns roughly 40 mt of coal a year (indicative arithmetic: 10 GW × 8,760 hours × 65% × 0.68 kg/kWh ≈ 39 mt), so the pipeline alone implies another 200–250 mt of demand by the early 2030s if it is built and run.

In plain terms

Coal is what India burns when the sun has set, the wind is still and the dams are low. Every new solar panel takes a little away from coal at midday, but every hot evening and every weak monsoon hands it back. As long as electricity demand grows faster than clean power can cover the evening, coal demand keeps rising, slowly and unevenly.

Two forecasts frame the debate. The old national ambition of 1.5 billion tonnes by 2029-30 has not been restated in any 2026 source we found, and with output at about 1.04 billion tonnes in FY26 it looks out of reach before the early 2030s. Coal India's own goal of about one billion tonnes by FY2029-30 implies its output growing about 7% a year from FY26, faster than at any time except the FY22–FY24 catch-up. The more useful number for investors is the year-to-year swing: plus or minus 3–4% on weather, plus 2–3% a year of underlying growth while the thermal fleet expands, minus whatever renewables and batteries take from the evening peak.
