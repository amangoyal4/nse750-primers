00

The one-page view: selling engineers' hours to a car industry that stopped buying

Six mid-sized firms, one dominant customer industry, a profit fall in FY26 and a recovery that has reached some of them and not others.

Engineering research and development (ER&D) services firms design other companies' products. Their engineers write the software that runs a car's brakes and dashboard, model the stresses in an aircraft wing, design a chip's circuits, lay out a chemical plant or certify a heart monitor for the US regulator. The client is a chief engineer or chief technology officer, not a chief information officer, and the money comes out of an R&D budget, not an IT budget. That one difference explains most of what follows: ER&D firms ride their clients' product cycles, which are lumpier and more cyclical than the steady IT spending that funds Infosys or TCS (see the companion IT Services report).

Six such firms sit in the NSE 750: L&T Technology Services (LTTS), Tata Technologies, Tata Elxsi, KPIT Technologies, Cyient and AXISCADES. Together they earned ₹35,141 crore of revenue in FY26 and ₹3,628 crore of profit after tax, and on 19 October 2026 they were worth ₹1.27 lakh crore. They are small next to the pool they fish in. Nasscom puts India's total ER&D revenue at $63 billion for FY26, and that figure includes the in-house engineering centres of foreign companies (global capability centres, or GCCs) and the engineering arms of the IT majors. The six listed specialists account for roughly 6% of it (INDICATIVE: ₹35,141 crore converted at the FY26 average rate).

FY26 was the first year in which the group's combined profit fell. Revenue rose 7.2% in rupees, but only about 1.5% in dollars (an INDICATIVE conversion) once the rupee's slide from ₹84.8 to ₹89.6 per dollar is stripped out. Profit after tax fell 15.4%. The damage was concentrated where the car industry is: the three auto-heavy firms (Tata Technologies, KPIT and Tata Elxsi) saw profit fall 21%, the three diversified firms (LTTS, Cyient and AXISCADES) 9%. About 42% of the six firms' combined revenue is linked to vehicles, an indicative estimate built from each company's vertical disclosure.

The cause was a synchronised cut in car-makers' discretionary engineering spend. European and Japanese manufacturers facing cheaper Chinese rivals, US tariffs and disappointing electric-vehicle demand announced job cuts and write-offs and pushed software programmes to the right. In September 2025 a cyberattack shut Jaguar Land Rover's UK plants for about five weeks, a direct hit to the Tata group's own engineering suppliers. KPIT, the purest listed bet on vehicle software, saw constant-currency growth fall from 18.7% in FY25 to 1.3% in FY26 and then to a 3.6% sequential decline in Q1 FY27.

The first half of FY27 shows a split recovery. LTTS lifted its EBIT margin to 15.7% in Q1 FY27 and its mobility segment returned to growth. Tata Elxsi grew 15% year on year in rupees in Q2 FY27, with an EBITDA margin of 21.9% of which 60–70 basis points came from the weaker rupee. Tata Technologies grew 28% in Q2 FY27, helped by its German acquisition ES-Tec. KPIT and Cyient did not recover: Cyient's core services business is still shrinking in constant currency, and it has pushed its 15% EBIT margin target from FY27 to the first half of FY28. Cyient reports Q2 on 22 October; KPIT's date has not been announced.

Five forces define the sub-sector, and the report is organised around them. First, the auto down-cycle and the Tata-group dependence (Section 09): how a cost crisis at European car-makers became a profit fall in Pune and Bengaluru. Second, AI and pricing (Section 10): engineering is sold by the hour, AI shrinks the hours, and who keeps the saving depends on the contract. Third, GCCs: the 2,117 captive centres in India are both the vendors' biggest rival for work and talent and, increasingly, a customer. Fourth, portfolio surgery (Section 15): AXISCADES is selling the services business it is named after, Cyient has split into three parts, and four of the six bought companies in eighteen months. Fifth, the rupee and the de-rating (Section 16): a weak rupee is propping up margins while the old valuation order has flipped, with KPIT now the cheapest of the six at 23.6 times trailing earnings and Tata Technologies the richest at 43.6 times.

In plain terms

These companies rent out skilled engineers to companies that make cars, planes, chips and machines. When those clients are confident they launch new products and hire more outside engineers. When they are squeezed, outside engineering is the easiest cost to cut, and the vendor still has to pay the engineers it hired. In FY26 the car industry was squeezed, so the vendors most dependent on it saw profits fall by a fifth.

Two things make this sub-sector harder to read than it looks. Growth figures are flattered by the rupee (FY27 rupee growth is overstated by roughly 6–8 percentage points) and by acquisitions (four of the six bought businesses in FY26–27), so the only clean measure is organic constant-currency growth, which few firms disclose cleanly. And the six are not one business: Cyient consolidates an electronics manufacturer, Tata Technologies resells design software, and AXISCADES is turning itself into a defence-electronics products company. The peer table (Section 12) and the KPI masterclass (Section 11) show how to adjust for each.

01

What ER&D actually sells: an engineer's hour, a certified deliverable, sometimes a product

The client's product is engineered, not its business systems. The buyer is the chief engineer, and the budget is R&D.

An IT services firm runs a bank's payment system or a retailer's inventory software. An ER&D firm works on the thing the client sells. When a German car-maker needs the software that lets a car's central computer talk to its battery, its cameras and its brakes, it can write it in-house, hire a European engineering firm, or hand a package of that work to KPIT or Tata Elxsi engineers in Pune and Bengaluru. When an aircraft-engine maker needs to re-certify a component after a design change, it may send the stress analysis and the certification paperwork to Cyient in Hyderabad. The output is a drawing, a simulation model, a block of tested code, a chip layout or a regulatory dossier.

Three features distinguish this work from IT services. The budget is programme-linked: it exists while a vehicle, aircraft or device is being developed and shrinks after launch. The work is often safety-critical and certified: car software must meet ISO 26262 and ASPICE, aircraft software DO-178C, rail signalling EN 50128, medical-device software IEC 62304. Certification creates switching costs because a programme rarely changes vendor halfway. And the spending is more cyclical: when a car-maker's margins fall, outsourced engineering is the most flexible line in the R&D budget, so it is cut first.

How the money is made: offshore scale, not onsite revenue

Most ER&D revenue is still billed by the hour or by the engineer-month, even when the contract is called fixed-price. A firm earns money when an engineer is busy on billable work at a good rate, and it loses money when the engineer is on the bench. The rate depends heavily on where the engineer sits. An embedded-software engineer in Pune might bill about $24 an hour; the same skill in Stuttgart or Detroit about $95. The onsite engineer brings in far more revenue, but costs almost as much as he or she earns.

The arithmetic matters for three reasons. First, the onsite share is not optional: clients want engineers in their labs and at their test tracks, and LTTS still earns 46.1% of its revenue onsite. Onsite is a relationship cost. Second, profit comes from offshore scale and utilisation: a firm that can move work from Stuttgart to Pune without losing the client earns more on less revenue. Third, because offshore salaries are paid in rupees and revenue is billed in dollars, euros and yen, a weaker rupee raises offshore contribution almost one for one. That is the mechanism behind the 60–70 basis points of currency benefit in Tata Elxsi's Q2 FY27 margin.

In plain terms

An ER&D firm makes its money from large teams of engineers in India who cost far less than they bill. The engineers it keeps near the client in Germany or the US are needed to win and run the work but barely break even. So the firm's profit depends on keeping the Indian teams busy and on the rupee staying weak.

Not all of the six sell hours. KPIT runs 67.7% of its revenue on fixed-price contracts, where it is paid for a deliverable and keeps any efficiency it finds. Tata Technologies resells Dassault and Siemens design software. Cyient owns 52.11% of Cyient DLM, a listed contract electronics manufacturer, and runs a semiconductor venture. AXISCADES is selling its engineering services business to the French staffing group Adecco's engineering arm, Akkodis, and will be left with defence electronics, semiconductor-equipment hardware (XiDA) and aircraft-parts manufacturing. The further a firm moves from hours to deliverables and products, the higher its potential margin and the higher its execution risk. Section 06 lays out the six resulting business models.

02

A $63 billion pool, and six listed firms holding about 6% of it

Market-size numbers in this industry differ by an order of magnitude. Here is which one to use, and what the listed firms' own history says.

The first definitional trap is the size of the market. Three numbers circulate, and they measure different things. Nasscom's India ER&D revenue was $63 billion in FY26 (estimated), up 6.8% from a revised $59 billion in FY25. It counts all ER&D work done from India: the listed specialists, the engineering arms of the IT majors and the in-house centres of multinational companies. Nasscom projects it to exceed $100 billion by 2030, about 12% a year. The India-outsourced ER&D spend estimated by Nasscom and BCG ($44–45 billion in 2023, rising to $130–170 billion by 2030) is a different, forward-looking measure. And global ER&D spend, everything the world's companies spend on engineering and R&D, was about $1.5–1.8 trillion in 2023 and is forecast at $2.48–2.50 trillion by 2030. Some primers quote "well over $100 billion" or "$140 billion" for the Indian market; those are not the Nasscom services number, and this report uses $63 billion.

In plain terms

The Indian engineering-services pool is about $63 billion a year, and the six listed firms take roughly one dollar in sixteen of it. They grew fast from 2021 to 2024, then stalled. In dollar terms the group barely grew in FY26; the rupee's fall made the numbers look better than the demand was.

Who buys engineering, and why: one big customer industry and five smaller ones

Vehicles are about 42% of the listed six's revenue (indicative). The other end-markets are on different cycles, which is why diversification paid in FY26.

Demand for outsourced engineering is a derivative of client product spending. When a client is launching new products, entering new markets or rebuilding an architecture, it buys engineering hours; when it is cutting costs, it stops. Automotive: the software rebuild, the EV reset and the Chinese challenge

Three forces drive car-makers' engineering budgets. The first is the software-defined vehicle. A modern car has more than 100 small electronic control units, each with its own software. Car-makers are consolidating them into a handful of central computers running a vehicle operating system that can be updated over the air. That is a multi-year rewrite of most of a car's software, and a premium car already carries well over 100 million lines of code. KPIT, Tata Elxsi and parts of LTTS were built for this work. The second is the electric-vehicle reset: Western car-makers delayed or cancelled EV programmes in 2024–26 as demand disappointed, and with them the engineering budgets. KPIT's own Q1 FY27 presentation lists car-makers in Germany, France, the UK and Japan announcing job cuts, profit warnings, pay cuts and large write-offs, driven by Chinese competition, US tariffs and falling profitability.

The third force is China. Chinese car-makers develop a new vehicle in about 24 months against about 48 for Western incumbents (industry commentary). That pressure cuts both ways: incumbents need to move faster, which favours outsourcing, but their budgets are squeezed, which cuts it. Chinese car-makers write most of their software in-house in China; the opening for Indian vendors is their expansion abroad, where they need help with European homologation and localisation. KPIT has a China chief executive focused on exactly that. Trucks and off-highway vehicles are a counter-cycle: KPIT's commercial-vehicle revenue grew 29.1% year on year in Q1 FY27 while passenger cars fell 5.1%. The full story of the down-cycle is in Section 09, and the companion Auto Components and Two & Three Wheelers reports cover the Indian side of the vehicle industry.

Aerospace and defence: sold-out backlogs, slow production

Civil aircraft makers have order backlogs that run to about 2032, but production keeps slipping. Boeing's target of 47 737s a month is now a 2027 goal (it was at 42 at end-2025), Airbus's A320 rate of 75 a month has slipped from 2026 to 2027, and Aviation Week forecasts about 1,686 deliveries in 2026, still below 2018's 1,753. For engineering vendors this means demand is about production support, sustaining engineering and new variants rather than clean-sheet aircraft. Cyient and, until its sale, AXISCADES are the main listed aero names. Indian defence is a separate, faster-growing market: AXISCADES's defence revenue more than doubled (+111%) in Q1 FY27 on an assured order visibility of ₹4,557 crore. The Aerospace & Defence report covers that market in depth.

Semiconductors and AI infrastructure

Global spending on AI data centres drives demand for chip design, verification and the hardware around the chips (cooling, power). India's Semiconductor Mission has approved 12 manufacturing units with more than ₹1.64 lakh crore of investment, a second phase with an outlay of about ₹1.27 lakh crore has been notified, and the Design-Linked Incentive scheme supports 24 chip-design projects (sources conflict on some dates). Listed exposure: LTTS's "next-generation compute and AI infrastructure" bet, Cyient Semiconductors (valued at about ₹4,650 crore in a May 2026 fundraise), and AXISCADES's XiDA, which counts three of the world's five largest technology and hyperscale companies and the largest semiconductor-equipment maker as customers. The Consumer Electronics & EMS report covers the manufacturing side.

Medical devices, industrial plants, media and telecom

Regulated, software-heavy medical devices are a steady niche: about 16% of Tata Elxsi's revenue, one of LTTS's six strategic bets, and part of Cyient's growth areas. LTTS reported an ophthalmology and microsurgery leader setting up an offshore engineering centre with it in Q1 FY27. Industrial and energy-transition work is the strongest current segment: LTTS's Sustainability segment grew 11.3% year on year in Q1 FY27 and Emerson picked LTTS as a global systems integrator. Media and telecom are mixed: telecom operators have cut capital spending, dragging Cyient's Connectivity business, while Tata Elxsi's media vertical is lumpy but won an AI-led deal in Q2 FY27.

In plain terms

About 55 rupees of every 100 the firm earns goes to its engineers, roughly half to people in India and half to the smaller, far more expensive teams abroad. What is left after offices, software and selling costs is a profit of 14–20 rupees. The biggest swing factor is whether the Indian engineers are busy on paid work or waiting for the next project.

Because the cost base is people, the industry's margin levers are few and well known: keep engineers billable, move work offshore, hire juniors rather than seniors, price well, and hope the rupee weakens. The only thing that has changed recently is that a sixth lever has appeared: deliver the same work with fewer engineer-hours, through reused platforms or AI tools, and keep the saving rather than pass it to the client. Competing locations, not imports

There is no import competition in the trade sense; services cross borders without tariffs. The competition is from other delivery locations. Eastern Europe (Romania, Poland, Czechia) is the near-shore choice for German car-makers; Mexico for US clients; Tunisia and Morocco for French ones; Vietnam is rising. The Indian firms have responded by opening there themselves: KPIT has centres in Hanoi and Tunisia and a China unit. China's own car-makers mostly develop software in-house, and are a market for Indian vendors only when they expand abroad. VinFast of Vietnam is a Tata Technologies client.

In plain terms

The six listed firms are specialists in a market where most engineering is still done by the clients themselves, either at home or in their own Indian centres. LTTS, the largest of the six, earns about a third of what each of Europe's big engineering firms earns. Size matters less here than being the specialist a client cannot easily replace.

The structure explains why pricing power is limited even for the best firms: a client can always threaten to take work in-house, move it to its own Indian centre, or give it to a larger IT firm. It also explains why the listed firms have chased certification-heavy niches (aircraft, rail, medical devices), cross-client platforms (KPIT's middleware) and products (AXISCADES's defence electronics), where a GCC is slower to replicate them.

07

Standards, visas and policy: certification is the moat, US politics the tail risk

Safety standards make the work sticky; India's labour codes and US visa rules move the cost base; services are not tariffed.

ER&D is lightly regulated as a business but heavily regulated as an activity. The software that brakes a car, flies a plane or runs a pacemaker must be developed under process standards that are audited, and the vendor that knows the process and has the audit history is hard to replace mid-programme. These standards are the closest thing the industry has to a moat. Around them sit four layers of policy that move costs and demand: Indian labour and industrial policy, US immigration and trade policy, European vehicle regulation, and India's semiconductor and defence programmes.

United States: visas and a dormant services tax

Two US measures matter. The first is the September 2025 proclamation requiring a $100,000 payment for new H-1B visa petitions, extended to September 2027. A court has blocked collection since 24 July 2026, but the uncertainty has already changed behaviour: large outsourcers' H-1B registrations fell 92%. ER&D firms keep a larger share of staff onsite than IT firms do, so they are exposed: the answer is more local hiring and more subcontracting, which raises cost. The second is the HIRE Act (S.2976), which would impose a 25% excise tax on payments to foreign service providers and deny the deduction, covering GCCs as well as vendors. It has been stalled in the Senate Finance Committee with no co-sponsors or hearings as of April 2026, GovTrack gives it a 0% prognosis, and no later action was found. It is a tail risk, not a base case.

Tariffs are an indirect channel. The US struck down its IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026 and imposed a 10% Section 301 duty from 24 July 2026 (12.5% on China and Vietnam), which is under legal challenge; a February 2026 US–India joint statement had cut the reciprocal rate on India to 18%. Services are not tariffed. The damage reaches ER&D through clients' profits: tariffs hit JLR's FY26 results and the European car-makers' margins, and those clients then cut engineering spend.

Europe: regulation that creates engineering work

European rules mostly create demand. The UNECE R155 and R156 regulations require car-makers to manage cybersecurity and software updates across a vehicle's life, which makes over-the-air update and cyber engineering mandatory work. The EU relaxed and averaged its 2025–27 CO₂ fleet targets, which reduced the urgency of some EV programmes. Rail's migration to the European signalling system (ERTMS) supports Cyient's rail work, and the EU AI Act adds compliance work for safety-critical AI.

In plain terms

Safety rules for cars, planes, trains and medical devices make it hard for a client to switch engineering vendors halfway through a project, which protects the incumbents. The policy risks are mostly American: visa costs for engineers working in the US, and a proposed tax on outsourcing that has not moved in Congress. Tariffs do not apply to engineering services directly, but they hurt the car-makers who buy them.

India's semiconductor and defence policies are the main domestic tailwinds. The Semiconductor Mission's first phase approved 12 units; Micron, CG Semi and Kaynes have begun commercial production; the second phase has been notified (the approval date is reported inconsistently). Defence indigenisation drives AXISCADES's orders for radar, missile sub-systems and mission computers, including on the Tejas Mk1A, Su-30MKI upgrade, Ashwini radar and Uttam radar programmes. Neither market is yet large enough to offset a downturn in foreign auto spend, but both are growing fast. On 7 October 2026 the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% to defend a rupee that had hit a record low near ₹96.8; it was ₹94.9 on 19 October.

08

Four cycles in thirty years: from drawing offices to the auto down-cycle

ER&D is more cyclical than IT services. Up-cycles have lasted four to eight years; the current down-cycle is in its second year.

The industry began as India's drawing office. In the 1990s Infotech Enterprises (now Cyient), founded in Hyderabad in 1991, digitised maps and engineering drawings for Western utilities and aircraft makers. Tata Elxsi grew out of a 1989 joint venture between the Tata group and the US computer maker Elxsi, and ran the visual-effects studio VCL from 1995. Tata Technologies was set up in 1994 to support Tata Motors and bought the UK engineering firm INCAT in 2005, giving it a foothold with Western car-makers. The work was low-value, priced by the hour and sold on cost.

The second phase, from about 2006 to 2016, was specialisation. Larsen & Toubro created L&T Integrated Engineering Services in 2006, which became LTTS and listed in September 2016. KPIT Cummins built an auto-electronics franchise. Infotech Enterprises renamed itself Cyient in 2014. The third phase, 2017–2019, was focus: the KPIT–Birlasoft demerger in 2019 produced a pure auto-software KPIT; TPG invested in Tata Technologies in 2017; AXISCADES began buying the defence-electronics design house Mistral in stages from 2017.

The fourth phase was the boom of FY21–FY24. Clients caught up on R&D deferred during Covid, launched EV and software-defined-vehicle programmes, and digitised engineering. The six firms' combined revenue grew about 24% a year in rupees. KPIT's constant-currency growth reached about 27% in FY24; Tata Elxsi's operating margin hit 31%; KPIT and Tata Elxsi traded at about 60 times earnings. Cyient listed its electronics subsidiary Cyient DLM in June 2023, and Tata Technologies' November 2023 IPO, a ₹3,042 crore offer for sale, was 69 times subscribed and listed about 140% above its issue price.

In plain terms

This industry has had a downturn roughly once a decade: the telecom bust of 2001–03, the financial crisis of 2008–10, the auto slowdown and Covid of 2019–20, and now the car industry's EV and cost reset of 2025–26. Each time, the up-cycle that followed lasted several years. The open question is whether this down-cycle is a deferral that ends in 2027 or a permanent shift of work to the clients' own centres and to AI.

The cycle has a feature that makes downturns hurt more than they look on the revenue line. When a client defers a programme, the vendor's engineers stay on its payroll while it looks for new work. In the short run, a deferral costs the vendor as much as a cancellation. Section 11 works through the numbers: benching 300 offshore engineers for two quarters takes about 90 basis points off a typical firm's annual EBIT margin.

The mechanism: outsourced engineering is the first line cut

A car-maker typically spends about 5–7% of revenue on R&D (INDICATIVE). Most of that is in-house engineers and test facilities that are hard to cut quickly. KPIT's Q1 FY27 presentation listed car-makers in Germany, France, the UK and Japan announcing job cuts, profit warnings, pay cuts and large write-offs.

Who keeps the AI gains: pricing, fixed-price contracts and the GCC rival Deep dive

ER&D sells engineering hours. AI shrinks the hours. Whether that is a margin gain or a revenue loss depends on the contract, and the clients' own centres are bidding for the same work.

Generative AI changes the unit the industry sells. Engineering tools can now draft embedded code from requirements, generate test cases, build simulation surrogates that replace slow physics models, and assemble regulatory documentation. Tata Elxsi has sold a GenAI regulatory-workflow deal; KPIT has built Beacon, an AI product trained on more than 2,000 production vehicle programmes; LTTS has partnered with Anthropic and Databricks and holds 244 AI patents among its 1,757. If an engineering task that took 100 hours now takes 80, someone gains 20 hours of value. The question for investors is who.

The answer depends almost entirely on the contract. Under time-and-material (T&M) billing, the client pays for hours worked; if AI cuts hours, the client buys fewer of them, and the vendor's revenue falls. Under fixed-price (FP) billing, the client pays for a deliverable; if AI cuts hours, the vendor delivers the same thing at lower cost and keeps the saving, at least until the contract is renewed and the client asks for a lower price. The IT-services experience is a guide: Kotak's base case is 3.5% pricing erosion on renewals, and HCLTech has said cost take-out deals now dominate.

A worked example: one fixed-price programme

Take a two-year software-integration package for a European car-maker, priced at a fixed ₹72.0 crore (about $7.6 million). The vendor plans 80 offshore engineers and six onsite engineers for two years: 160 offshore engineer-years and 12 onsite. Using the unit economics from Section 01 (offshore cost ₹12.0 lakh a year, onsite ₹130.6 lakh), direct staff cost is ₹19.2 crore offshore and ₹15.7 crore onsite. Test rigs, tool licences and test vehicles add ₹4.0 crore, and delivery overhead (programme management, quality assurance, a bench buffer) ₹7.2 crore. Contribution is ₹25.9 crore, 36.0% of the price. After an allocation of sales, administration and depreciation at 14% of price (₹10.1 crore), programme EBIT is ₹15.8 crore, a 21.9% margin, well above the firm average of about 15%. Fixed-price work earns more because the vendor is paid for the outcome, and carries the risk of overrunning.

In plain terms

If a vendor is paid by the hour and AI lets it do the job in fewer hours, it simply earns less. If it is paid a fixed price for the finished job, it keeps the saving, until the client renegotiates. That is why every ER&D firm is trying to move to fixed-price contracts, and why the ones that get there first will look more profitable for a while.

At company level: the AI sensitivity

The same logic applies to a whole firm. Take an ILLUSTRATIVE 10,000-person ER&D firm with 8,500 engineers offshore and 1,500 onsite, revenue of ₹4,913 crore and EBIT of ₹729 crore (14.8%, close to LTTS's FY26 14.5%; the full model is in Section 11). If the firm uses AI to do that half of the work with 3% fewer people, it saves about ₹45 crore of salaries and the EBIT loss shrinks to about ₹29 crore, a margin of 14.5%, roughly 40 basis points lower. The test: is AI a lever or a leak?

The cleanest test is to compare revenue growth with headcount growth at stable pricing. If constant-currency revenue grows faster than headcount and fixed-price margins hold, AI is a lever: the firm is producing more per engineer and keeping the gain. If revenue falls with headcount and margins fall too, AI is a leak: the gains are going to the client. The current evidence is mixed. LTTS's headcount rose 0.5% year on year against constant-currency revenue growth of 1.9%, and its EBIT margin rose 200 basis points: a modest lever. KPIT's headcount fell 1.9% against flat constant-currency revenue, but its revenue per development employee is falling and its margin fell 380 basis points: so far, a leak, though the auto cycle explains most of it. AXISCADES's employee cost fell from 53.0% to 44.1% of revenue, but that reflects its move into products, not AI.

One policy link connects the GCC and AI debates: the US HIRE Act. If enacted, its 25% tax on payments to foreign service providers would hit GCCs and vendors alike, so it would not shift work between them, but it would raise the cost of offshore engineering for American clients. It is dormant. The more immediate pricing pressure is commercial: as renewals come up in FY27–28, clients will ask for a share of the productivity they know AI is creating. The firms with the highest fixed-price share and the most proprietary platforms are best placed to resist; the firms selling commodity hours are not.

Three features of the worked firm are worth holding on to through the rest of this section. First, the two halves of the business behave differently: the offshore half has a 62.5% contribution margin and the onsite half about 11%, so any shift in mix moves profit far more than revenue. Second, almost every cost line is semi-fixed in the short run. Salaries are paid whether or not an engineer is billed, office leases run for years, and sales and administration staff do not shrink when one client defers a programme. That operating leverage is what turns a 2% revenue shortfall into a 10–15% profit shortfall. Third, the exchange rate enters twice, once in revenue (every foreign-currency rupee rises when the rupee falls) and once in cost (the onsite salaries and some subcontracting are also in foreign currency), and the net effect is positive because the rupee cost base is larger than the foreign-currency cost base. The KPIs below map onto these three features: growth and mix KPIs tell you about the top line, people and contract KPIs about the operating leverage, and currency and cash KPIs about the quality of the reported profit.

In plain terms

Count the engineers, multiply by how much of their time is billed and at what hourly rate, convert to rupees, and you have the revenue. Subtract their salaries and the costs of running the firm, and you have the profit. In this example the onsite engineers bring in almost half the revenue but almost all of it goes back out as their pay; the profit is made on the Indian teams.

1. Constant-currency revenue growth

What it is. Revenue growth measured at the previous period's exchange rates, so currency moves are stripped out. It is the only like-for-like measure of demand. Formula: (this period's revenue in each currency converted at last period's rates ÷ last period's revenue) − 1. Worked example: a firm earns $100 million in a quarter at ₹85, or ₹850 crore. A year later it earns $102 million at ₹95, or ₹969 crore. Rupee growth is 14%; dollar growth 2%. If part of the dollar revenue was billed in euros that strengthened against the dollar, constant-currency growth could be lower still, say 1.5%. What drives it: client budgets, deal ramp-ups and ramp-downs, pricing and acquisitions. Benchmarks (year on year, INDICATIVE for the current environment): good above 12%, average 5–12%, weak below 5%. Quarter on quarter: good above 3%, average 1–3%, weak below 1%.

How the six compare. On the latest sequential (quarter-on-quarter) figure each firm gives, Tata Technologies' services grew 4.3% in Q2 FY27, Tata Elxsi's transportation business 2.0% in Q2 FY27, LTTS 1.5% in Q1 FY27, Cyient's engineering business (DET) fell 0.5% in Q1 FY27 and KPIT fell 3.6% in Q1 FY27. Year on year, LTTS grew 1.9%, KPIT 0.1% and Cyient DET fell 0.9%, all in Q1 FY27. None is in the "good" band. Note the bases: Tata Elxsi's figure is one segment, Tata Technologies' is services only, and AXISCADES does not report constant-currency growth. The trap: companies choose which constant-currency figure to headline. A firm with a strong segment will quote it; read the total.

2. Organic versus acquired growth

What it is. Growth from the existing business, excluding revenue from companies bought in the past twelve months. Formula: (reported revenue − acquired revenue) ÷ prior-period revenue − 1. Worked example: a firm reports ₹1,700 crore of revenue, up 28% from ₹1,328 crore. If an acquisition contributed ₹230 crore, organic revenue is ₹1,470 crore and organic growth about 11%, before currency. Drivers: deal timing and size. Benchmark: organic constant-currency growth is the number that sets valuation; anything acquired should be judged on the price paid. How the six compare: FY26–27 growth is unusually inorganic. Tata Technologies' Q2 FY27 growth of 28% includes ES-Tec (consolidated from November 2025). KPIT's FY26 rupee growth includes the first phase of Caresoft. 3. Vertical mix and auto share

What it is. The share of revenue from each client industry. It tells you which cycle a firm rides. Formula: vertical revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: a firm with ₹6,000 crore of revenue, 80% from cars, loses 10% of its car revenue: ₹480 crore, or 8% of the total. A firm with 15% from cars loses ₹90 crore, 1.5%. Drivers: client cycles, the firm's own diversification. Benchmark: no single vertical above about 40% is a reasonable diversification test (INDICATIVE). How the six compare: KPIT about 97% vehicles, Tata Technologies about 86%, Tata Elxsi about 53% transportation, LTTS about 15% pure auto (Mobility 32.3%), Cyient about 5%, AXISCADES about 0–3%; the weighted six about 42%, all INDICATIVE (chart in Section 03). The trap: labels differ. LTTS's "Mobility" includes aircraft, rail and trucks; Tata Elxsi's "Transportation" includes some non-auto work; KPIT's split is passenger versus commercial vehicles.

4. Client concentration

What it is. The share of revenue from the largest clients, and the number of clients above revenue thresholds. Formula: top-N client revenue (trailing twelve months) ÷ total revenue. Worked example: if a firm's top five clients give 16% of ₹11,000 crore, they give ₹1,760 crore, about ₹350 crore each on average; losing one costs about 3% of revenue. If the top client alone gives 25%, losing it costs a quarter. Drivers: history (anchor clients), strategy (mining large accounts). Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): top-5 below 15% good, 15–30% average, above 30% a concentration risk; largest client below 8% good, 8–15% average, above 15% weak. How the six compare: LTTS is the only firm with a full disclosure: top-5 16.0%, top-10 26.8%, top-20 40.7%, across 423 active clients. KPIT's 25 strategic clients give 87.3% of revenue. AXISCADES's top customers gave about three-quarters of revenue before its sale. Tata Technologies' and Tata Elxsi's anchor-client shares (Tata Motors and JLR) are not sourced; both are likely in the "weak" band on largest-client dependency. The trap: "strategic client" and "top client" definitions differ; KPIT's T25 is a programme, not a ranking.

6. Headcount, utilisation and revenue per employee

What it is. Revenue per employee is annual revenue divided by headcount; utilisation is billed hours divided by available hours. Together they measure productivity. Formula: revenue ÷ average (or period-end) headcount. Worked example: KPIT's FY26 revenue of ₹6,455 crore over about 12,520 employees is ₹51.6 lakh each (about $58,000). If utilisation rises two points on the offshore base of the worked firm above, revenue rises ₹70 crore with no added cost, and EBIT margin rises about 125 basis points. Drivers: utilisation, onsite mix (onsite engineers bill far more), pricing, pass-through revenue, and, increasingly, AI productivity. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): above ₹50 lakh good, ₹38–50 lakh average, below ₹38 lakh weak; utilisation above 82% good, 76–82% average, below 76% weak. Cyient and AXISCADES n/a. No firm discloses utilisation. The trap: a high number can mean high onsite mix or resale revenue, not productivity. Tata Elxsi has the lowest revenue per head and the highest margin, because its pyramid is young and India-heavy.

Revenue per employee is also the metric to watch for the AI debate. If AI tools genuinely raise engineering productivity and the firm keeps the gain, revenue per employee should rise even with flat pricing, because the same revenue is delivered by fewer people. If clients capture the gain through lower prices or fewer billed hours, revenue per employee will be flat or falling while headcount shrinks. KPIT discloses revenue per development employee in dollars (about $61,000 a year, falling quarter on quarter; column order in the presentation INDICATIVE), which so far points to the second pattern, though the auto downturn explains much of it. Watch the direction over FY27–28 rather than the level, and compare it with the fixed-price share: a rising fixed-price share with rising revenue per head is the pattern of a firm keeping its productivity.

7. Attrition and wage inflation

What it is. Voluntary attrition is the share of employees who leave by choice in a twelve-month period; wage inflation is the average annual pay rise. Formula: leavers in the last twelve months ÷ average headcount. Worked example: a 12,000-person firm with 15% attrition must replace 1,800 people a year; at a recruitment and training cost of, say, ₹2 lakh each (ILLUSTRATIVE), that is ₹36 crore, plus lost productivity while new hires ramp up. A 9% offshore wage rise on the worked firm's ₹1,020 crore offshore payroll costs ₹92 crore, about 185 basis points of EBIT before any price increase. Drivers: the job market, GCC hiring (which pays 20–40% more), the firm's growth and culture. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): below 12% good, 12–17% average, above 17% weak. How the six compare: Tata Technologies 15.6% (Q2 FY27), LTTS 14.7% (Q1 FY27); the others did not disclose in the sources used. The trap: some firms report total attrition, others voluntary only, and some exclude trainees. Low attrition in a downturn can simply mean the job market is weak.

8. Offshore share of revenue

What it is. The share of revenue from work delivered in India (or another low-cost centre) rather than at the client's site. Formula: offshore revenue ÷ total revenue (some firms report effort share instead). Worked example: in the worked firm, replacing 150 onsite engineers with 225 offshore engineers (for example in response to visa costs) reduces revenue by ₹147 crore but raises EBIT by ₹22 crore, lifting the margin about 100 basis points. Drivers: client preference, visa rules, the type of work (testing on physical vehicles needs people on site). Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): above 60% of revenue offshore good, 50–60% average, below 50% weak. How the six compare: only LTTS discloses it: 53.9% offshore, 46.1% onsite in Q1 FY27 (53.5/46.5 in Q4 FY26). The others n/a. The trap: revenue share and effort share differ by a factor of about four, because an onsite hour bills about four times an offshore hour. LTTS's 54% offshore revenue implies roughly 80% offshore effort.

In plain terms

Moving a job from Germany to Pune makes the vendor's revenue smaller but its profit larger, because the Indian engineer costs a tenth as much and bills a quarter as much. So a falling onsite share is usually good news for margins, even though it looks like slower growth.

9. Fixed-price share

What it is. The share of revenue from contracts priced per deliverable rather than per hour. Formula: fixed-price revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: the fixed-price programme in Section 10 earns 21.9% EBIT as planned, 27.3% if AI and reuse cut effort by a fifth, and 11.7% if effort overruns by a quarter; the same work on hourly billing earns 18.9%, falling to 13.8% if the client buys a fifth fewer hours (all ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers: client willingness, the vendor's estimating skill and platform reuse. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE): above 55% good, 35–55% average, below 35% weak (for margin potential, not for risk). How the six compare: KPIT 67.7% (up from 62.5% a year earlier; mapping INDICATIVE), LTTS 35.1% (up from 32.4%); the others do not disclose. The trap: some "fixed-price" contracts are capacity contracts priced per engineer-month, which behave like hourly billing. A rising share is good for margins only if the firm estimates well.

10. EBITDA, EBIT and the margin bridge

What it is. EBITDA margin is operating profit before depreciation and amortisation as a share of revenue; EBIT margin is after them. The margin bridge explains the change from one period to the next, lever by lever. Formula: EBIT ÷ revenue; bridge = sum of the basis-point effects of each lever. Worked example: starting from the worked firm's 14.8% EBIT, two points of offshore utilisation add about 125 basis points, a 1% weaker rupee about 47, and replacing 150 onsite engineers with 225 offshore about 100; a 9% offshore wage rise takes away about 185, a 3% price cut on half the revenue about 130, and a client benching 300 offshore engineers for two quarters about 85 (all ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers: the ones in the chart, plus acquisition amortisation and investment spending. Benchmarks (INDICATIVE, current environment): EBITDA above 22% good, 16–22% average, below 16% weak; EBIT above 18% good, 13–18% average, below 13% weak.

Two lessons follow from the unit. First, the onsite team of 60 costs more than the offshore team of 600, and earns back barely more than its own pay; the account's profit is made in India. Second, the bench line is the shock absorber. If the client defers a programme and 100 offshore engineers lose their work for six months, the account loses ₹16 crore of revenue (100 × ₹32.0 lakh × half a year) while its salary bill is unchanged, and its EBIT falls by about a third, from ₹49.5 crore to about ₹33.5 crore. The unit also shows how a firm grows its margin without growing revenue. Shift ten onsite roles offshore and the account's EBIT rises by several crore. Convert the account from hourly billing to a fixed-price managed service and the firm keeps any efficiency it finds. Each of these moves is a negotiation with the client, which is why account managers in this industry are judged on margin as much as on revenue. It also explains why acquisitions often depress margins for a year or two: a bought business arrives with its own onsite-heavy, unoptimised accounts, and it takes several renewal cycles to bring them to the acquirer's delivery model. Tata Technologies' ES-Tec, KPIT's Caresoft and LTTS's Intelliswift are all in that phase now.

In plain terms

A well-run account for one big client can earn close to 18 rupees of operating profit on every 100 of revenue. But if the client pauses a project and a sixth of the Indian team has nothing to bill for half a year, a third of that profit disappears, because the engineers still have to be paid.

17. Definition traps, collected

  • ER&D is not IT services. The buyer is the chief engineer and the budget is R&D, so growth follows product cycles. HCLTech's ER&D unit and Coforge–Encora are ER&D exposures outside this sub-sector.
  • Market size. Nasscom's India ER&D revenue ($63 billion, FY26E) is not the Nasscom–BCG outsourced-spend estimate and not global ER&D spend ($2.5 trillion by 2030). Primers quoting $100–140 billion are using a different measure.
  • Rupee versus constant currency. FY26 rupee growth overstates demand by about 5–6 points; FY27 by about 6–8. KPIT grew 10.5% in rupees but 1.3% in constant currency in FY26.
  • Organic versus acquired. ES-Tec, Caresoft, Intelliswift and TAO Digital all sit inside FY26–27 growth figures.
  • Continuing versus discontinued. LTTS restated FY25–26 without SWC; AXISCADES's quarterlies mix total and continuing-only revenue (₹94–183 crore versus ₹300+ crore). Never compute growth across mixed bases.
  • Consolidated groups that are not pure ER&D. Cyient includes Cyient DLM (electronics manufacturing) and Cyient Semiconductors; Tata Technologies includes software resale; AXISCADES's retained business is products.
  • EBITDA versus EBIT versus "operating profit". Different firms report different measures, and screener's operating profit excludes other income, which is large for these cash-rich firms.
  • Segment mappings. LTTS's Q1 FY27 segment shares and KPIT's contract-type split were read from scrambled PDF tables and are INDICATIVE.
  • "Billable" is not utilisation. LTTS's 93% billable share is a role split.
  • PAT bases. Cyient's FY26 PAT is ₹463 crore on screener (attributable) and ₹534 crore as company "group PAT"; LTTS's FY26 PAT growth is +1.3% on screener and +7.4% on the company's continuing basis.

18. How to read an ER&D company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Dollar revenue and constant-currency growth, quarter on quarter and year on year. Ignore rupee growth. Ask whether the figure is for the whole firm or one segment, and whether it includes an acquisition. Red flag: a second consecutive sequential decline.
  2. Organic growth. Find the acquired contribution (ES-Tec, Caresoft, TAO, the AXISCADES aero buy) and take it out.
  3. Vertical and geography mix. Which segment drove the quarter? For the auto-heavy names, compare passenger cars with trucks and Europe with Japan/Korea/China. Red flag: the largest vertical shrinking.
  4. Margin and its bridge. EBIT or EBITDA on the company's own basis, and how much came from currency. Red flag: margin up only because of the rupee.
  5. Other income and exceptional items. LTTS's Q2 FY27 profit of ₹549 crore included about ₹200 crore of other income of unconfirmed cause; strip such items out before computing profit growth.
  6. Headcount change against revenue change. Revenue rising faster than people with stable margins means productivity is being kept; both falling means demand is weak.
  7. Attrition and wage-hike timing. A wage cycle usually costs 100–200 basis points in the quarter it lands.
  8. Deal wins and book-to-bill. TCV against revenue, and whether wins are new logos or renewals. Red flag: book-to-bill below 0.9 for two quarters.
  9. Client metrics. Top-5 and top-10 share, the number of $10 million-plus clients, and any comment on the largest client.
  10. Cash and guidance. DSO direction, free cash flow against profit, and whether guidance was raised, kept or quietly dropped (Cyient's 15% EBIT target moved from FY27 to H1 FY28; KPIT's 20.5–21.2% EBITDA guide is at risk).
In plain terms

When an ER&D result comes out, look first at growth in dollars at constant exchange rates, without acquisitions. Then check whether the margin rose because the business improved or because the rupee fell, strip out one-off income, and compare how fast revenue grew with how fast the workforce grew. Those four checks catch most of the flattering headlines.

One closing point on method. The KPIs in this section are most useful in combination rather than alone. Constant-currency growth without the acquisition adjustment overstates demand; a margin without its currency component overstates management's achievement; revenue per employee without the onsite mix and resale content misleads on productivity; and a fixed-price share without the margin trend says nothing about whether the firm is estimating well. The firms that will come out of this cycle strongest are the ones whose numbers improve on all four at once: organic constant-currency growth turning positive, margins rising faster than the rupee alone explains, revenue per engineer rising, and fixed-price margins holding as that share grows.

Who is winning: diversification paid, margin did not protect the multiple

Four maps and a scorecard show which models held up in the down-cycle, which are recovering, and what the market is paying for.

The first map plots each firm's auto exposure against its FY26 profit change. The pattern is clear at the edges and noisy in the middle. LTTS, with about 15% pure auto exposure, was the only firm whose profit rose; KPIT and Tata Technologies, the two most auto-dependent, saw profit fall about a fifth or more. But Cyient, with almost no car exposure, had the worst profit fall of all, because its problems were its own: a shrinking connectivity business, weaker "strategic units", a manufacturing arm whose revenue fell 17%, and acquisition due-diligence costs it wrote off when it paused Project Astro. Auto exposure explains most of the FY26 damage, but not all of it.

The second map asks what the market is paying for. It plots FY26 EBIT margin against trailing P/E. If the market paid for quality, the dots would run from bottom left to top right. They do not. Tata Elxsi, with the highest margin, trades at 32.8 times; Tata Technologies, with the lowest margin of the large services firms, at 43.6; KPIT, with a margin almost identical to LTTS's, at 23.6 against LTTS's 27.5. The market is paying for visible near-term recovery (Tata Technologies' guidance of "strong double-digit" growth, its $138 million deal and JLR's normalisation) and for optionality (Cyient's semiconductor venture and electronics stake, AXISCADES's defence business), not for historic margins.

The third map looks at productivity: revenue per employee against EBIT margin, for the four firms that disclose headcount. It separates two ways of making money. Tata Elxsi earns the least revenue per person and the highest margin, the signature of a young, India-heavy team doing design-led work at a premium. KPIT and LTTS earn more per person through more onsite and fixed-price work, at mid-range margins. Tata Technologies' revenue per person is flattered by software resale, which carries a low margin.

The fourth map takes a longer view: five-year dollar growth against return on capital. KPIT is the only firm in the top-right corner: the fastest grower of the boom (21% a year in dollars) with a high return. Tata Elxsi and LTTS combine high returns with slower growth. The firms that avoided relying on car-makers came through FY26 best. What the surgery does to the numbers

Portfolio surgery makes the reported numbers noisier at exactly the moment investors most need clean ones. Restatements change the base: LTTS's FY25 revenue was restated from about ₹10,670 crore to ₹9,642 crore to remove SWC, and AXISCADES's quarterly revenue on screener mixes continuing-only figures (₹94–183 crore) with totals (₹300 crore and more). Acquisitions inflate growth, as with Tata Technologies' 28%. Exceptional items cloud profit, as with Tata Technologies' Q3 FY26 and AXISCADES's ₹21.81 crore of deal costs in Q1 FY27. Debt and amortisation rise. The investor's task is to rebuild organic constant-currency growth and underlying margin for each firm, which is what Section 11's checklist is for.

The market has rewarded the surgery unevenly. Cyient is up 39% and AXISCADES 31% since July, both on the value of what they own or will own. KPIT has fallen despite Caresoft, because the acquisition does not fix the core. The INDICATIVE lesson: the market pays for surgery that reveals hidden value or changes the business model, not for acquisitions that add revenue to a shrinking core.

The rupee: a cushion that can be withdrawn

The rupee has done much of the work of holding margins up. It averaged ₹84.8 per dollar in FY25, ₹89.6 in FY26 and about ₹95.5 so far in FY27, touched a record low near ₹96.8 in early October 2026 and stood at ₹94.9 on 19 October, after the RBI raised its repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October. On the worked firm in Section 11, each 1% of depreciation adds about 47 basis points of EBIT margin; Tata Elxsi attributed 60–70 basis points of its Q2 FY27 margin gain to currency; LTTS realised ₹94.86 per dollar in Q1 FY27 against ₹85.44 a year earlier. FY27 rupee revenue growth is overstated by roughly 6–8 points relative to constant-currency growth. If the rupee stabilises, the year-on-year tailwind disappears from late FY27; if it strengthens, it reverses. The underlying constant-currency margin bridge (wage rises, AI investments, pricing pressure) will then show through, as it already has in IT services.

In plain terms

The weaker rupee has been quietly adding to these firms' profits: each dollar of revenue is worth about ten more rupees than eighteen months ago, while Indian salaries are unchanged. That support will fade once the rupee stops falling. Investors pricing a recovery need to check that it shows up in dollar growth, not just in rupee margins.

What would re-rate the group, and what would de-rate it further

A sustained return to positive sequential constant-currency growth at KPIT and Cyient, alongside continued growth at the Tata pair and LTTS, would argue that the auto down-cycle was a deferral; that combination has historically re-rated the group, because ER&D up-cycles last several years. Evidence that AI is a lever (rising revenue per engineer with stable or rising fixed-price margins) would support the premium over IT services. The opposite case is a second leg down in European car-maker budgets in 2027, clients demanding AI savings at renewal, a stronger rupee and more work moving to GCCs. On that path the ER&D premium over IT services, now roughly 1.5–2 times, would compress further. The valuation is not cheap enough to ignore the bear case: even after the de-rating, the group trades at 35 times FY26 profit, which assumes profit growth returns to double digits.

Within the group, the dispersion itself is the opportunity and the risk. The spread of trailing P/Es from 23.6 (KPIT) to 43.6 (Tata Technologies) is the widest in years among the services names, and it rests on the market's view of which recovery comes first. If KPIT's recovery arrives in the second half of FY27 as guided, the gap should narrow; if Tata Technologies' organic growth disappoints once ES-Tec anniversaries in Q3 FY27, it could narrow from the other side.

The software-defined vehicle: a rebuild that has paused, not ended

The biggest single technology shift in the listed set's market is the move from a car with 100-plus separate control units to a car with a few powerful central computers. The new architecture needs a vehicle operating system, middleware that lets software run on any hardware, new safety cases, cybersecurity and over-the-air update systems, and the migration of decades of legacy features. That is years of engineering work for every car-maker, and it is the work KPIT, Tata Elxsi and parts of LTTS were built for. Volkswagen's Cariad and Mercedes' MB.OS showed that building it all in-house is costly and slow, which pushed car-makers into partnerships (Cariad's joint venture with Rivian is the best-known). KPIT calls these captive software units the "swing factor" for its growth.

Tool and platform capture

A quieter risk is that the value of AI in engineering accrues to the companies that own the design tools rather than to the services firms that use them. Siemens, Dassault Systèmes, Synopsys (now with Ansys), Cadence and NVIDIA (through Omniverse) are building AI into simulation, design and verification software. If a car-maker's own engineers can do with an AI-enabled tool what once needed an outsourced team, the tool vendor captures the saving. Tata Technologies, which resells Dassault and Siemens software, earns a thin margin on that resale and does not own the platform. The defence for the services firms is to build their own platforms and reusable assets: KPIT's middleware and Beacon, LTTS's 1,757 patents and its Engineering Intelligence centre in Munich, Tata Elxsi's media platforms.

Semiconductors and AI hardware

AI data centres are creating engineering demand of a different kind: chip design and verification, power and thermal hardware, and test equipment. AXISCADES's XiDA business, growing 62% with three of the five largest technology and hyperscale companies among its customers, and Cyient Semiconductors are the listed exposures, along with LTTS's next-generation compute bet. India's Semiconductor Mission and the Design-Linked Incentive add domestic demand. These are small today relative to auto, but they are the fastest-growing pools the listed firms can reach.

Cyber risk at clients

The JLR attack showed a new risk: a single client's IT event can shut its plants and freeze its engineering programmes, hitting a vendor's quarter without anything going wrong at the vendor. The client-level impact on Tata Technologies and Tata Elxsi was not quantified in their disclosures. As vehicles and factories become more software-defined, the attack surface grows; so does the demand for cybersecurity engineering, which UNECE R155 makes mandatory for new vehicles.

In plain terms

AI threatens to shrink the number of engineering hours clients buy, but it is also being built into cars, robots, factories and data centres, all of which need engineering. The firms that own reusable software, platforms or products will capture more of that new work than those selling hours on someone else's tools.

Bull vs bear: a deferral that ends in 2027, or a permanent loss of the hour

The case for and against the sub-sector, and what the current valuation already assumes.

The disagreement about Indian ER&D comes down to one question: is FY26 a cyclical deferral of car-makers' engineering spend, after which the long-term drivers (software-defined vehicles, physical AI, offshoring by squeezed Western clients) resume, or is it the first sign of a structural shift in which clients do more themselves, in their own Indian centres and with AI tools, and buy fewer outsourced hours? Both cases have evidence behind them.

The bull case

  • Deferral, not cancellation: no car-maker can skip the software-defined-vehicle rebuild; Honda and Renault ramps are dated, and trucks are already growing about 30% at KPIT.
  • Recovery is visible: Tata Elxsi +15% and Tata Technologies +28% in Q2 FY27; LTTS's EBIT up 200 basis points to 15.7%.
  • Offshoring wins in downturns: Western majors earn 3–9% margins; squeezed clients move work to India, as Akkodis's AXISCADES purchase shows.
  • New pools: physical AI, semiconductors, AI data-centre hardware and Indian defence are growing fast; Nasscom sees India ER&D above $100 bn by 2030.
  • Fixed-price shift: KPIT at 67.7% and LTTS rising to 35.1% let vendors keep AI productivity until renewal.
  • Balance sheets and valuation: KPIT trades at about 12 times EV/EBITDA with net cash; free cash flow exceeds profit at five of the six.

The bear case

  • Structural insourcing: 2,117 GCCs, more than 100 new a year, paying 20–40% more for the same engineers.
  • AI deflation: on hourly contracts a 20% productivity gain can cut programme profit by about 38% (ILLUSTRATIVE); renewals will reprice fixed-price work too.
  • Concentration: about 42% of revenue is auto (INDICATIVE); KPIT's top 25 clients are 87% of revenue; the Tata pair depends on JLR and Tata Motors.
  • Rupee-flattered margins: FY27 rupee growth is overstated by 6–8 points; 60–70 basis points of Tata Elxsi's margin gain was currency.
  • Noisy numbers: acquisitions, disposals, restatements and exceptional items obscure organic growth at all six.
  • Valuation: the group still trades at 35 times FY26 profit, about 1.5–2 times the tier-one IT firms, after a year of falling profit.

The current valuation embeds a recovery. At ₹1.27 lakh crore, the group trades at 35.1 times FY26 profit. In plain terms

The optimists think car-makers have only paused their software projects and will restart them in 2027, while new work in AI, chips and defence adds to growth. The pessimists think clients have learned to do more of this work themselves and with AI. Share prices already assume a fair amount of recovery, so the burden of proof is on the optimists.

The split within the group matters as much as the group call. In the bull case KPIT, the most beaten-down and most auto-exposed, has the most to gain, and the diversified LTTS the least. In the bear case the firms with products and certified niches (AXISCADES's defence business, Cyient's aerospace and rail, Tata Elxsi's media and design) are the most defensible, and the firms selling commodity engineering hours are the most exposed. Tata Technologies sits awkwardly in both: its recovery is visible, but its valuation is the highest and its client concentration the greatest.
