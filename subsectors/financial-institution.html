Development financial institutions: a lender with no loans

A complete guide to India's development financial institutions, the state-built lenders meant to fund what banks will not. It covers why the first generation failed, why the government built a new one instead of fixing the old, how the sovereign lenders that worked earn their money, and why the only listed member, IFCI, now trades on a stake in the National Stock Exchange rather than on a single performing loan.

00

The one-page view: the state still does development finance, just not through IFCI

Two systems run side by side: a legacy lender being wound down, sold or merged, and a sovereign-funded set of new and refinance institutions that is growing fast.

A development financial institution (DFI) is a lender the state sets up to provide long-dated money for things commercial banks fund badly: factories in 1948, power plants in 1990, highways and transmission lines today. Banks borrow short (deposits that can leave tomorrow) and are wary of lending for fifteen years. A DFI is supposed to bridge that gap, with cheap long-term funding from the state and a mandate to take project risk. India has run this experiment twice, and the two runs are now visible side by side in the same set of public filings.

The first generation was IFCI (1948), ICICI (1955) and IDBI (1964), plus the state financial corporations. They worked while the state fed them concessional money. After the 1991 reforms withdrew that money, they had to borrow at market rates and lend to riskier borrowers to cover the cost. ICICI and IDBI escaped by becoming banks (2002 and 2004). IFCI stayed a non-deposit lender, resumed lending into the 2008–2012 infrastructure boom, and was crushed by the bad-loan cycle that followed. It has made no new loan since FY22. At March 2026, 95.79% of its gross advances were non-performing on the RBI basis, its capital adequacy ratio was −18.78%, and its rating sat at CARE BB and ICRA B+, both well below investment grade.

The second generation is the set of statutory "all-India financial institutions" (AIFIs) regulated by the RBI: NABARD for agriculture and rural credit, EXIM Bank for exports, NHB for housing refinance, SIDBI for small business, and since 2021 the National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development (NaBFID). Alongside them sit the listed government lenders that fund one sovereign-linked sector each: PFC and REC (power), IRFC (railways), HUDCO (housing and urban bodies) and IREDA (renewable energy). This second system is large, profitable and growing. NaBFID's loan book roughly doubled in FY26 to ₹1,16,950 crore with no bad loans; SIDBI's grew 16.8% to ₹5.80 lakh crore; PFC and REC are merging into a power-finance group with a loan book of about ₹11.64 lakh crore.

The investment question in this sub-sector is unusual because the only listed member, IFCI, is not really a lender any more. It is a holding company with a lending licence attached. What it owns that matters is 52.86% of Stock Holding Corporation of India (SHCIL, "StockHolding"), India's largest custodian and the agency that runs e-stamping in 25 states and union territories. SHCIL in turn held 11 crore shares of the National Stock Exchange going into NSE's listing on 24 September 2026; it sold only 61.9 lakh of them in the IPO and still holds 10.38 crore (4.19% of NSE). At NSE's ₹1,670 share price on 23 October 2026, IFCI's slice of that stake, plus its share of the IPO cash, is worth about ₹9,750 crore before tax (INDICATIVE). That single number explains more of IFCI's ₹15,945 crore market value than every loan it has ever made.

Five forces shape everything in this report, and the sections that follow are built around them. First, run-off: IFCI's legacy book recovers about ₹540 crore a year, enough to service debt until 2030, when ₹1,500 crore of bonds fall due inside six months. Second, the sum of the parts: the equity trades on SHCIL and NSE, not on lending. Third, the state's playbook: old DFIs are sold (IDBI to Fairfax for ₹52,878 crore, at ₹81 a share), merged (REC into PFC at 88 PFC shares per 100 REC shares; IFCI with its own subsidiaries) or left to run off, but never recapitalised to grow again. Fourth, the model reborn: NaBFID was designed with what IFCI never had after 1991, namely ₹25,000 crore of free capital, a sovereign guarantee on foreign borrowing and a ten-year tax holiday. Fifth, funding: with no deposits, a DFI's rating decides its cost of money, and its cost of money decides whether it can lend safely at all.

In plain terms

IFCI is a 78-year-old lender that stopped lending four years ago. What keeps the share price up is that it owns just over half of a company (SHCIL) that owns a slice of the stock exchange. Shareholders are effectively betting on two things: that NSE's value can be pulled up to the parent through a government-approved merger, and that the parent can meet its debts until then. Most of the "profit" you see in the consolidated accounts belongs to SHCIL's other shareholders, not to IFCI's.

For readers coming from the listed government lenders, the contrast is the lesson. PFC, REC, IRFC, HUDCO and IREDA fund one sovereign-linked sector each, borrow at AAA rates near 7–7.5%, carry gross NPAs of 0–4%, and earn returns on equity of 13–21% while trading at 0.7–2.0 times book. Their combined FY26 profit with IDBI Bank was ₹56,214 crore on a combined market value of ₹3.93 lakh crore, about seven times earnings (INDICATIVE set aggregate). IFCI is about 4% of that set's market value and around 1% of its assets and revenue, and it trades at 88.9 times owners' earnings. Those numbers only reconcile once you stop treating IFCI as a lender, which is exactly what this report does from section 9 onward.

The timing matters. IFCI's latest reported quarter is Q1 FY27 (June 2026), approved by the board on 11 August 2026; its Q2 FY27 board date had not been announced by 23 October 2026. Q2 is when NSE's annual dividend reaches SHCIL, and it is also the first quarter in which SHCIL's NSE stake is marked to a quoted price. The jump will show up in book value rather than profit, which is why section 10 warns that IFCI's reported price-to-book could fall mechanically from 1.78× to around 1.5× without anything changing in the underlying economics (ILLUSTRATIVE).

01

How a DFI earns a rupee, and how IFCI earns one now

A healthy DFI makes a spread on long loans funded by cheap, patient money. IFCI's rupees now come from recoveries, dividends, rent and government fees.

The textbook DFI business is simple to describe and hard to run. The institution raises long-term money (equity and grants from the state, long-dated bonds, foreign borrowing, lines from multilateral lenders, and, for some, deposits that banks are forced to place with it), and lends it for ten to twenty years to projects that banks consider too long or too risky. Its profit is the gap between what it earns on loans and what it pays on funds, minus running costs and loan losses. Because the loans are large and few, one bad project can wipe out years of spread. Because the funding is mostly borrowed, the lender's credit rating sets its funding cost, and that cost sets the type of borrower it can afford to lend to.

The money is made in the spread between Step 2 and Step 3 and lost in Step 4. Step 1 is the hidden subsidy: equity and grants carry no interest, so a well-capitalised DFI earns a large margin even on a thin lending spread.

Each step has a different owner of economics. In Step 1, the state decides how much free capital to put in. NaBFID received ₹20,000 crore of equity and a ₹5,000 crore grant, which together were about a quarter of its funding at March 2026 and cost it nothing. In Step 2, the rating agencies effectively set the price: AAA-rated PFC paid about 7.50% on its borrowings in FY26, HUDCO 6.95% in Q1 FY27, NaBFID 6.56% net. In Step 3, competition sets the yield: banks, the power financiers and NaBFID all bid for the same highway and renewable projects, and NaBFID's yield on advances slid from 8.59% to 8.07% in a year. Step 4 is where management skill and sector choice show up: lenders to state utilities, Indian Railways and urban bodies have collected reliably; lenders to private industrial projects mostly did not. Step 5 is small in rupees but strategically useful, because it keeps an institution embedded in government programmes.

IFCI has lost Steps 1 to 3 entirely. It receives no new government capital (₹2,000 crore came in over FY20–FY25; nothing in FY26, and none is earmarked for FY27). It has not raised a rupee of fresh borrowing since FY22; its ₹3,591.6 crore of principal at March 2026 is all old rupee bonds, carrying a weighted coupon of 9.91%. It has not sanctioned or disbursed a loan in FY22, FY23, FY24, FY25, FY26 or Q1 FY27. What is left is Step 4 (recovering the legacy book) and Step 5 (administering government schemes), plus an investment portfolio led by SHCIL. That gives IFCI a value chain of a completely different shape.

The income statement reflects this. IFCI's FY26 standalone total income of ₹924 crore looks like a lender's revenue at first glance, but the "interest" line of ₹341 crore is largely income recognised on stage-3 (credit-impaired) assets as recoveries arrive, plus treasury income; the auditors note that ₹93 crore of interest recognised on stage-3 assets was written off as bad debt in the same year. Another ₹238 crore was fair-value gains on investments, ₹186 crore dividends, ₹79 crore fees and ₹54 crore rent. On the cost side, finance costs of ₹416 crore exceeded interest income, so net interest income is negative on a cash basis. The rating agencies say this plainly: ICRA describes IFCI's earning assets as far below its debt, and CARE says IFCI would need "substantial equity infusion" to meet regulatory minimums and restart lending.

Where is value captured in this sub-sector? For the second-generation institutions, in the funding advantage: free capital, quasi-statutory deposits and a sovereign-linked rating let them lend to low-risk borrowers at a margin that still earns 11–21% on equity. For IFCI, almost entirely in SHCIL, a market-infrastructure business (custody of ₹69.27 lakh crore of assets at March 2025, depository services, e-stamping, document management) that also happens to hold the NSE stake. The lending licence itself currently destroys value: it carries ₹4,185 crore of debt including accrued interest and generates no spread. That is why the government's own plan for IFCI is a consolidation that folds SHCIL into the parent, rather than a capital injection to restart lending.

In plain terms

A working DFI is a spread business with a state subsidy built into its capital. IFCI no longer has the subsidy, the funding or the loans, so its income is what it collects from old borrowers, what its subsidiaries pay it, and fees for running government schemes. Treat its "revenue" line as a list of cash sources, not as the top line of a lending business.

02

Two DFI systems, one owner: size and growth

The AIFIs and sovereign lenders hold tens of lakh crore of assets and grow at double digits; IFCI's book is under half a per cent of the DFI set and shrinking.

Measured by loans, India's development-finance estate is now enormous, and IFCI is a rounding error inside it. NABARD's balance sheet was about ₹10.96 lakh crore at March 2026 (press figure). SIDBI's loans were ₹5,79,755 crore. EXIM Bank's net loans were ₹2,07,779 crore. NaBFID, which made its first disbursement only in 2023, reached ₹1,16,950 crore. Against these, IFCI's gross loans were ₹4,169 crore at March 2026 (₹3,981 crore at June 2026), and almost all of that is non-performing. Its standard net loan book was about ₹70 crore at June 2026, less than a single mid-sized NaBFID road loan.

The state's lending machine has not shrunk; it has moved. Money that IFCI and IDBI once lent to industry is now lent by SIDBI, NaBFID, the power financiers and the banks. 03

The ₹136 lakh crore question: why India still needs development lenders

Infrastructure needs long money that deposit-funded banks find uncomfortable; refinance demand follows MSME and rural credit; and government schemes need administrators.

The case for development finance in India rests on a mismatch that has not gone away. Infrastructure assets earn cash over twenty to thirty years; bank deposits can leave in weeks. Banks can and do lend to infrastructure, but they prefer operating assets with proven cash flows and shorter tenors, and the 2012–2018 bad-loan cycle left them wary of greenfield project risk. NaBFID's own estimate of India's infrastructure requirement is about ₹136 lakh crore over FY26–FY30 and ₹680–770 lakh crore over FY26–FY47. Even if banks, bond markets, infrastructure investment trusts and the government budget cover most of that, a residual need for long, fixed-rate, construction-stage money remains. That residual is the space the new DFI model was built for.

Refinance is the second source. SIDBI, NABARD and NHB mostly lend to other lenders: banks, small finance banks, NBFCs and microfinance institutions that on-lend to small businesses, farmers and home buyers. Their volumes follow credit growth in those segments. Bank credit to the commercial sector grew 15.9% in 2025-26 against 10.9% a year earlier, and non-bank credit grew 13.3% (RBI Annual Report 2025-26, quoted in IFCI's FY26 MD&A). SIDBI's 16.8% loan growth fits that pattern. NaBFID itself lends about 11% of its book (₹12,662 crore at March 2026) to NBFCs, a quiet sign that the new DFI is also acting as a wholesale lender to infrastructure-focused finance companies.

Government scheme administration is the third, and the only one that still feeds IFCI's operating business. The production-linked incentive (PLI) programme, launched from 2020, needs agencies to process applications, verify investment and production claims, and route incentive payments. IFCI is the project management agency for 10 of the 14 PLI schemes, including large-scale electronics, IT hardware, bulk drugs, medical devices, food processing, white goods, auto and auto components, textiles, advanced chemistry cells and specialty steel, plus related programmes: the India Semiconductor Mission (fabs, display fabs, assembly and test), PM E-DRIVE, the electric-car scheme, FAME-II, M-SIPS, SPECS, the bulk drug and medical device parks, the Sugar Development Fund (as nodal agency) and, from FY26, the rare-earth permanent magnet scheme and portal work for the electronics components scheme. Budget 2026-27 named seven frontier sectors (electronics, semiconductors, biopharma, rare earths, chemicals, textiles and capital goods), so more scheme work is plausible.

For SHCIL, IFCI's main subsidiary, demand comes from yet another place: the depth of India's capital markets and the volume of property and contract registrations. Custody assets track institutional assets under management (mutual funds, insurers, foreign portfolio investors). Depository participant revenue tracks demat accounts and trading. E-stamping, where SHCIL is the central record-keeping agency in 25 states and union territories, tracks the number of stamp-duty transactions and is largely independent of the stock market: SHCIL issued 18.09 crore e-stamp certificates in FY25, up from 15.66 crore in FY24, about 4.96 lakh a day. That annuity is one reason SHCIL's core business has value separate from its NSE stake.

In plain terms

India's need for long-term lenders is real and growing, but it is being met by institutions with cheap money and clean books: NaBFID for greenfield infrastructure, the power and rail financiers for their captive sectors, SIDBI and NABARD for refinance. IFCI's only share of the demand is administrative: it runs the paperwork and payments for the government's factory incentive schemes, and it owns a stake in a business that benefits from India's growing markets.

The drivers for each part of the sub-sector are worth keeping separate, because they move at different speeds. Infrastructure demand responds to the government's capital budget and to state-utility finances; refinance demand responds to bank and NBFC credit cycles and to how strictly banks are pushed to meet priority-sector targets; scheme work responds to policy announcements and their implementation timelines. None of these is a driver of IFCI's share price in the short run. That role belongs to NSE's share price and to news about the government's consolidation decision, which sections 10 and 11 take apart in detail.

04

Funding is fate: why the rating decides who survives Deep dive

With no deposits, a development lender's cost of money is set by its rating; its cost of money then decides what kind of borrower it can afford. IFCI lost that contest in 1991 and never won it back.

Every institution in this sub-sector shares one structural fact: none of them takes retail deposits. A bank can fund itself at 4–6% from savings and current accounts that are sticky even when they are technically repayable on demand. A DFI has to buy every rupee in the wholesale market, from bond investors, banks and foreign lenders who price it according to the credit rating. That makes the rating the single most important input in the business model. It decides the funding cost; the funding cost decides the minimum yield the lender must earn; and the minimum yield decides which borrowers it can lend to without taking more risk than its spread can absorb.

The sovereign-linked lenders that worked all sit at the top of the domestic scale. CRISIL reaffirmed AAA/Stable ratings for PFC (26 March 2026), REC (27 March 2026) and IRFC (1 April 2026); SIDBI's AAA was reaffirmed in January 2026; NaBFID is AAA domestically and rated at the sovereign level (BBB- / Baa3) internationally. These ratings rest on government ownership, strategic importance and, in NaBFID's case, explicit support written into its Act. IFCI sits at the other end: CARE BB and ICRA B+ on its bonds and bank lines, ICRA A4 on commercial paper, all on "Rating Watch with Developing Implications" pending the government's decision on its group consolidation. The same owner, the Government of India, produces AAA pricing for one institution and sub-investment-grade pricing for another. The difference is not who owns them; it is whether the owner has committed capital and support to the balance sheet.

The arithmetic of adverse selection

The funding gap looks modest in percentage points, but it is decisive once leverage is applied. A AAA lender funding at 7.0–7.5% can lend to a state transmission utility or a railway project at 9–10% and earn a 2–3 point spread with credit costs of 0.1–1%. It can afford to be choosy because its money is cheap. A lender funding at 10–11%, which is roughly where IFCI's money cost in the 2000s and 2010s, has to lend at 12–14% just to stand still after operating costs. The borrowers willing to pay 12–14% for long-term project money are, on average, the ones that could not get cheaper money elsewhere: highly geared developers, unproven promoters, projects with weak cash-flow cover. That is adverse selection, and it is the core of IFCI's story. Its expensive funding steered it towards the riskiest corner of the market, and the 2012–2018 bad-loan cycle (power, roads, telecom, real estate, airports, manufacturing) did the rest.

IFCI's legacy debt still carries the mark of that era. Its ₹3,591.6 crore of principal at March 2026 is entirely rupee bonds: no bank borrowings at all, and only ₹126 crore of bank lines secured against fixed deposits and mutual funds. The weighted average contractual coupon is 9.91% (9.79% after the 1 August 2026 redemption of a Tier II bond). The accounting finance cost divided by average principal comes to about 11.3%, because it includes accrual and effective-interest effects on zero-coupon and cumulative structures; that is the figure some sources round to "about 11%". Either way it is the most expensive money in the peer set, and since IFCI has almost no earning assets left, it is pure negative carry.

What a rate hike does

The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, citing the inflation risk from the West Asia conflict that began at the end of February 2026. For the floating-rate part of the system (bank lines, commercial paper, new bonds), that lifts funding costs within months. The pass-through models absorb it differently: IRFC passes its incremental cost to Railways under the lease formula; PFC and REC reprice part of their loan books and have guided stable spreads; NaBFID, which is growing its borrowings fast and lends a lot at fixed rates, is the most exposed to a squeeze, which compounds the margin compression already visible in Q1 FY27. IFCI is the odd case: its legacy bonds are fixed-rate, so a hike does not raise its interest bill, but it does lower the value of any fixed-income assets it holds and makes any future refinancing of the FY31 bonds more expensive.

For a state lender, the most valuable thing the government can give is not a single cheque but a credible promise to stand behind the balance sheet. Who owns what: an all-state industry with one listed remnant

Every institution here is controlled by the government; concentration is extreme in refinance and spread across several players in infrastructure; and the only private DFI successor is a bank.

Industry structure in development finance is unusual because there is no private competition in the core business. Every institution discussed in this report is owned and controlled by the Government of India, directly or through other state entities. IDBI Bank is 94.72% owned by the government and LIC together; after the Fairfax deal closes, the government will keep 15% and LIC 19%.

The business models differ by funding edge and asset edge. The ones that work pair cheap money with a low-risk borrower; the one that failed paired market-rate money with private project risk.

It helps to sort the sub-sector into archetypes, because the same word, "development finance", covers businesses with completely different economics. The useful questions are: where does the cheap money come from (the funding edge), who is the borrower (the asset edge), and what typically goes wrong (the failure mode)? IDBI: the counterfactual that eventually worked

IDBI is the useful control experiment for IFCI. Both were general-industrial DFIs that lost their concessional funding in the 1990s. IDBI converted into a bank in 2004 and gained a deposit franchise; IFCI did not. Conversion did not save IDBI from the bad-loan cycle: it lost about ₹44,476 crore across FY16–FY20 (INDICATIVE sum of annual reported losses: ₹3,574, 4,997, 8,116, 14,970 and 12,819 crore), was placed under the RBI's prompt corrective action framework from May 2017 to March 2021, and was rescued by LIC (which bought 51% in 2019) and the government. But because it had deposits, it could rebuild. By FY26 it earned ₹9,237 crore with a return on equity of 14.1%, and on 14 October 2026 the government approved the sale of 60.72% to Fairfax for ₹52,878 crore. IFCI, without deposits, could only shrink. The lesson for this sub-sector is that the funding base, not the original mandate, decided which of the old DFIs had a second life.

The archetypes also explain the government's different treatment of each institution, which section 11 develops. Refinance AIFIs and NaBFID get capital when they grow (SIDBI in Q4 FY26). Captive-sector NBFCs are consolidated for scale (PFC–REC) or partially sold to the market (IRFC's offer for sale, IREDA's placement). The converted bank is privatised (IDBI). The legacy industrial DFI is merged with its own subsidiaries and left to run off. Each treatment fits the archetype's economics: the state puts money where the funding edge already exists and takes money out where it does not.

In plain terms

Development finance works when the lender has cheap money and a safe borrower, such as a state utility, Indian Railways or another bank. It fails when the lender pays market rates and lends to risky private projects, because it ends up with the borrowers nobody else wanted. IFCI is the only Indian DFI in that second box, and the only one that collapsed.

NBFC or AIFI: the rulebook, the capital floor and the ECL question

IFCI is regulated as a government NBFC and has breached its capital floor every quarter since December 2020; the AIFIs run under a separate set of RBI directions issued on 28 November 2025.

The legal categories matter here because they decide which capital rules apply and what happens when a lender breaches them. "DFI" is a policy idea, "AIFI" is an RBI legal category, and IFCI is neither in law. IFCI is a government company registered as a non-banking financial company (NBFC-ND-SI since FY08, now under the RBI's scale-based regulation). The five AIFIs are EXIM Bank, NABARD, NHB, SIDBI and NaBFID, each created by its own Act of Parliament and regulated by the RBI under a dedicated set of directions. PFC, REC, IRFC, IREDA and HUDCO are government NBFCs, most classified as infrastructure finance companies. IDBI is a bank. Calling IFCI "an AIFI" or NaBFID "an NBFC" is a common error in coverage.

IFCI and the capital floor it cannot meet

Government-owned NBFCs were brought under the full NBFC prudential framework in stages from 2022, with minimums of 10% Tier 1 capital and 15% total capital adequacy. IFCI's capital adequacy has been negative every quarter since December 2020: −23.04% at March 2025, −21.85% at June 2025, −18.78% at March 2026 and −17.58% at June 2026. This looks paradoxical because IFCI's reported standalone net worth is positive (₹1,785.5 crore at March 2026, ₹1,797 crore at June 2026). The explanation is that regulatory capital deducts investments in subsidiaries (above all SHCIL) and net deferred tax assets, which RBI treats as capital that cannot absorb losses at the lender. CARE's adjusted tangible net worth is ₹809 crore; after the deductions for "high group exposures", regulatory capital is negative. The auditors flag the −18.78% figure as an emphasis of matter, citing the RBI notification of 28 November 2025.

The practical consequence is that IFCI cannot lend. CARE states that it "would need substantial equity infusion to be able to meet the regulatory minimum requirements… and to restart the lending business." The improvement from −23% to −18% did not come from new equity; it came from shrinking risk assets and small retained profits. Note the twist the consolidation adds: if SHCIL is merged into IFCI rather than held as a subsidiary, the deduction for the subsidiary investment disappears and SHCIL's assets (led by the NSE shares) sit directly on IFCI's balance sheet. A merged IFCI could therefore report positive capital adequacy without a rupee of new government money, even though nothing about the underlying assets would have changed. That is one reason the consolidation, rather than recapitalisation, is the state's preferred route.

The AIFI rulebook after 28 November 2025

The AIFIs are now governed by a consolidated set of RBI directions organised by entity class: the RBI (AIFIs – Prudential Norms on Capital Adequacy) Directions, 2025, dated 28 November 2025; the AIFI income recognition, asset classification and provisioning directions (updated to 1 October 2026); and companion directions on the transfer and distribution of credit risk and on financial-statement disclosures, including project-finance disclosures. NaBFID's Q1 FY27 results notes cite all of them; its regulatory minimum capital adequacy is 9% and its minimum leverage ratio 4%. NaBFID used the credit-risk transfer directions in FY26 to down-sell ₹2,205.70 crore of loans, an early sign that the new DFI intends to originate and distribute, not just hold.

The ECL question. The RBI's final expected-credit-loss directions, issued on 27 April 2026 and effective 1 April 2027, apply to commercial banks (excluding small finance banks, payments banks and local area banks). The October 2025 draft and the Governor's policy statement had mentioned AIFIs too, with a glide path for existing books to 31 March 2031, but no AIFI ECL direction had been found by 23 October 2026. For IFCI and the PSU NBFCs this is moot: they have reported under Ind AS 109, an expected-loss standard, since NBFCs moved to Ind AS around FY19–FY20. For SIDBI, NABARD, EXIM and NaBFID, which report on RBI formats under the older incurred-loss approach, an eventual ECL regime would raise provisioning on stage-2 assets and on long-tenor project loans. NaBFID's nil NPA ratio would not change, but its standard-asset provisions could.

Listing rules, governance and the auditors' caveats

As a listed company IFCI is also subject to SEBI's listing regulations, and its governance record is weak. The auditors' report carries four emphases of matter: ₹93 crore of interest recognised on stage-3 assets and written off in the same year; the negative capital adequacy; and the fact that the nodal ministries instructed IFCI not to share Sugar Development Fund and PLI case files with the auditors. A petition by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office (in the name of the Union of India) is pending before the National Company Law Tribunal in New Delhi against IFCI and 91 other respondents under the Companies Act's oppression, mismanagement and investigation provisions; notice has been served and there are no interim orders. There are no insolvency proceedings against IFCI itself.

On minimum public shareholding, IFCI is compliant (public 27.43%). IDBI at 94.72% promoter holding was not, which the Fairfax transaction and its mandatory open offer will reshape. The broader policy direction is visible in the Budget line on PFC and REC: fewer, larger public-sector financiers, each with a clearer mandate and stronger capital. IFCI's consolidation fits the same logic, and so does the RBI's choice to write a dedicated rulebook for AIFIs rather than treat them as NBFCs. The state is drawing a line between institutions it intends to grow (AIFIs, the power and rail financiers) and institutions it intends to restructure or exit (IFCI, IDBI).

In plain terms

IFCI shows positive net worth in its accounts but negative capital in the regulator's eyes, because the regulator does not count its stake in SHCIL as capital that can absorb lending losses. That rules out lending. Merging SHCIL into IFCI would remove the deduction and could make the capital ratio positive overnight, which is a big part of why the government prefers a merger to writing a cheque.

One more distinction matters for anyone comparing ratios. AIFIs and banks classify assets under RBI's income-recognition rules (NPA at 90 days overdue), while IFCI and the listed NBFCs also report Ind AS stage classifications. For IFCI the two give different headline numbers: gross NPA of 95.79% at March 2026 on the RBI basis, but gross stage 3 of 98.28% on the Ind AS basis, because stage 3 also includes reclassified security receipts and some accounts that are standard under RBI rules. Neither number is wrong; they measure different things. The KPI section (section 14) shows how to keep the bases straight.

Seventy-eight years, four lives: from nation-builder to holding company

IFCI's history is the history of Indian development finance: built on cheap state money, broken when that money was withdrawn, revived twice on borrowed funds, and finally replaced.

IFCI was India's first DFI, set up on 1 July 1948 under the Industrial Finance Corporation Act to provide long-term finance to industry in a newly independent country with almost no capital market. ICICI followed in 1955 with World Bank support, and IDBI in 1964 as a subsidiary of the RBI (separated in 1976). For four decades these institutions, together with the state financial corporations, supplied most of India's long-term project finance. Their funding came from government loans, the RBI's long-term credit funds and bonds that banks were effectively required to buy as part of their statutory liquidity holdings. The system worked because the money was cheap and guaranteed, even though credit discipline was uneven.

1991–2004: the funding rug is pulled. The reforms after 1991 withdrew concessional funding. The Narasimham committees cut the eligibility of DFI bonds for banks' statutory liquidity, and DFIs had to borrow at market rates. In 1998 the second Narasimham committee and the Khan working group recommended that DFIs convert into banks or NBFCs. ICICI reverse-merged into ICICI Bank in 2002; IDBI became IDBI Bank in 2004. IFCI converted from a statutory corporation into a public limited company in 1993, listed, and was renamed IFCI Limited in October 1999, but it never acquired deposits. Bad loans mounted through the late 1990s.

2002–2018: two rescues and a second bust. A government-led restructuring of IFCI's liabilities in 2002-03 kept it alive. It was classified as a systemically important non-deposit NBFC in FY08 and resumed lending in 2008, straight into the infrastructure and real-estate boom. Consolidated revenue peaked at about ₹4,465 crore in FY16. The loans it wrote in that period (airports, roads, telecom, power, real estate, manufacturing) went bad in the twin-balance-sheet crisis that the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016 was designed to resolve. IFCI posted losses in FY17 and then every year from FY19 to FY23. In April 2015 it became a government company, and the government's stake rose steadily as it injected equity.

A lender with no loans: the run-off book and the FY31 wall Deep dive

Ninety-six per cent of IFCI's book is bad, recoveries of about ₹540 Cr a year service the debt, capital has been negative for six years, and ₹1,500 Cr of bonds fall due in six months of 2030.

IFCI's legacy book is not a turnaround story. It is a run-off: a closed portfolio being collected, with no new business to offset the decline. Understanding it requires three numbers that do not appear in a normal lender analysis: how much is still recoverable, how fast the recoveries arrive, and whether they arrive faster than the debt falls due.

Anatomy of a 96% bad book

At March 2026 IFCI's gross loans were ₹4,168.99 crore. None of it was in stage 1 (performing with no significant increase in credit risk); ₹71.60 crore was stage 2; and ₹4,097.39 crore was stage 3, credit-impaired. Against that sat expected-credit-loss provisions of ₹2,998.81 crore, leaving net loans of ₹1,170.19 crore. On the RBI basis, gross NPAs were ₹3,589.98 crore (95.79% of advances) and net NPAs ₹573.76 crore (78.44%). Of the NPA stock, ₹3,888.96 crore of gross carrying amount was classified "doubtful for more than three years" and ₹121.58 crore "loss". Every rupee of NPA is a corporate loan; there is no retail, MSME or farm book. The standard net loan book, the part that still pays as agreed, was about ₹70 crore at June 2026, down from ₹78 crore a year earlier.

Second, the mark-up of the residual 10.38 crore shares from about ₹1,268 to the 30 September price flows through other comprehensive income: at ₹1,670, roughly ₹4,180 crore pre-tax at SHCIL, or about ₹1,900 crore after deferred tax attributable to IFCI's owners (INDICATIVE).

In plain terms

IFCI owns about half of a company that owns about 4% of the stock exchange. At today's NSE price, IFCI's share of that stake is worth roughly ₹8,300–9,750 crore, and a generous valuation of everything else IFCI owns, minus its debts, gets the total to about ₹9,000–12,200 crore. The shares are valued at ₹15,945 crore. Buyers are paying extra for the hope that a merger will unlock the value; they are not getting NSE at a discount.

In plain terms

When IFCI reports its September-quarter numbers, profit will look strong because NSE's yearly dividend lands then, and book value per share may jump towards ₹40 because NSE is now marked at its listed price. Neither is new money. The price-to-book ratio will drop on paper without the shares being any cheaper.

Sell, merge, never regrow: the state's playbook for old DFIs Deep dive

The government sold IDBI, is folding REC into PFC and wants to merge IFCI with its own subsidiaries. In none of the three did it put in capital to grow the old balance sheet.

Read together, the government's recent decisions on its financial companies reveal a consistent rule. Where an institution has a funding edge and a mandate the state still wants, it gets capital (SIDBI in the March 2026 quarter, NaBFID at birth). Where an institution is a legacy balance sheet, the state sells it, merges it for scale, or merges it with its own assets to make it self-funding. It does not recapitalise an old DFI so that it can lend again. IFCI's capital history and its pending consolidation are best understood inside that rule.

IFCI's capital history: money to survive, not to grow

The government put ₹2,000 crore of equity into IFCI over FY20–FY25: ₹200 crore in FY20, ₹200 crore in FY21, ₹100 crore in FY22 and ₹500 crore a year in FY23, FY24 and FY25 (year mapping from cash-flow statements). The last tranche was approved by IFCI's board on 29 January 2025 as a preferential issue of up to ₹500 crore (8,07,23,280 shares to the government, about ₹61.94 a share) and passed at an extraordinary general meeting on 28 February 2025; during FY25, ₹1,000 crore of share-application money was converted into shares, lifting the government's stake from 70.32% to 72.57%. Nothing came in FY26 and nothing is earmarked for FY27 (ICRA). Every rupee went to servicing debt. Reported standalone net worth rose four-fold from ₹445 crore (FY22) to ₹1,785 crore (FY26), but cumulative standalone profit over those five years was −₹2,055 crore (INDICATIVE sum): the equity rebuilt the hole the losses dug.

The DFI reborn: NaBFID's design, and the refinance AIFIs Deep dive

Same owner, opposite outcomes: NaBFID was built with free capital, a sovereign guarantee and a tax holiday, and earned 11.5% on equity with no bad loans. Its margins are now normalising fast.

In 2021 the government decided India needed an infrastructure DFI again. It did not revive IFCI. It passed the National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development Act, created a new statutory institution, and gave it every structural advantage IFCI lacked. Comparing the two is the most useful single device for understanding development finance in India: the state did not abandon the DFI idea; it abandoned the legacy balance sheet.

The design

NaBFID is 100% owned by the government (its Act allows dilution to 26%), with authorised capital of ₹1 lakh crore and paid-up equity of ₹20,000 crore. It also received a ₹5,000 crore grant specifically to lower its cost of funds. The government guarantees its foreign-currency borrowing at a concessional fee and reimburses its currency-hedging cost, and its income is tax-exempt for its first ten years (from FY22). RBI granted it AIFI status in 2022. It made its first disbursement and issued its first bond in 2023. Its domestic rating is AAA; internationally it is rated at the sovereign level. Its mandate covers greenfield and brownfield infrastructure, and from July 2025 partial credit enhancement, which lets it guarantee part of a project bond so that pension and insurance money can buy it. The managing director is Rajkiran Rai G.

Where does this end? As the loan book moves towards the roadmap target of 0.9% of GDP (around ₹4–5 lakh crore by FY30 on our assumption), the ₹25,000 crore of free capital becomes a much smaller share of funding, and the tax holiday expires after FY31. A normalised NaBFID is likely to look more like a well-rated refinance AIFI or a power financier, with a return on assets of perhaps 1.5–2% and a return on equity supported by leverage (ILLUSTRATIVE). That makes the IFCI-versus-NaBFID contrast a lesson about design rather than about permanent outperformance: the institution that works is the one with free capital, a guarantee and a safe funding base, even after the early super-normal margins fade.

Funding is where the design shows most clearly. NaBFID raised ₹59,849 crore of rupee bonds between FY24 and Q1 FY27 and, on 30 September 2026, priced its debut US$750 million ten-year bond at a 6.122% coupon, 105 basis points over US Treasuries, under a US$4 billion programme listed on India INX and NSE IX. US investors took 55% of the book, EMEA 30% and Asia 15%. IFCI has not borrowed since FY22 and could not access dollar markets at all. NaBFID has also started selling down loans (₹2,205.70 crore in FY26 under the RBI's credit-risk transfer directions), which recycles capital and signals an originate-and-distribute model rather than a pure hold-to-maturity one.

In plain terms

NaBFID is what IFCI could have been with the right funding. It started with ₹25,000 crore of capital that costs nothing, a government guarantee on its foreign borrowing and no tax for ten years, so it can lend to roads and renewables at a healthy margin with no bad loans so far. But that margin is shrinking as it borrows more to grow, and in a few years it will look like an ordinary well-run state lender rather than an unusually profitable one.

The refinance AIFIs share three traits. Their borrowers are mostly other lenders, so credit risk is low. Fees for the state: IFCI's advisory franchise and the scheme money it holds

IFCI runs the back office for 10 of the 14 PLI schemes and about 20 government mandates. It earns about ₹80 Cr a year from it and holds ₹3,259 Cr of scheme money on its balance sheet.

If IFCI has an operating business left, it is government scheme administration. Since the production-linked incentive programme began, IFCI has become the project management agency (PMA) for 10 of the 14 PLI schemes. FY26 wins included the rare-earth permanent magnet scheme and portal work for the electronics components scheme. The drone PLI ended in FY25. IFCI built and runs the scheme portals itself, moved its systems to Oracle Cloud and added CRM and ESG modules.

Reading a DFI: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain any development lender, from a AAA power financier to a run-off book. For IFCI, half of the standard lender ratios are meaningless, and three non-standard ones matter more than all the rest.

A development lender is a spread business with a capital cushion. Every useful metric either measures the spread (how much it earns over what it pays), the cost of running and protecting it (operating cost and credit losses), the cushion (capital), or the timing (whether money comes in before it must go out). A worked example makes the mechanics concrete. In plain terms

A development lender makes money on the gap between what it charges borrowers and what it pays for money, after costs and bad loans. 1. Loan book (AUM) and its growth

What it is: the stock of loans outstanding, the base on which every margin is earned. Formula: gross loans (or net of provisions; say which), sometimes including bonds held as credit substitutes and non-fund exposure. Worked example: a book of ₹59,840 crore growing to ₹1,16,950 crore is growth of 95% (NaBFID, FY26). Drivers: sanctions, the rate at which sanctions are drawn (infrastructure loans disburse over construction), repayments and sell-downs. Benchmark: for a mature sovereign lender 8–15% a year is healthy; above 30% demands scrutiny of underwriting and funding; shrinkage means run-off. Comparison: PFC group about ₹11.64 lakh crore, REC ₹5.81 lakh crore, SIDBI ₹5.80 lakh crore, IRFC ₹4.79 lakh crore, EXIM ₹2.08 lakh crore, HUDCO ₹1.73 lakh crore (+28.8%), NaBFID ₹1.17 lakh crore (+95%), IREDA about ₹0.95 lakh crore, IFCI ₹0.04 lakh crore (−4.5%). Definition trap: NaBFID quotes at least five different numbers: annual sanctions (₹1,33,020 crore in FY26), cumulative sanctions (₹3,59,357 crore by June 2026), loan book including NCDs and non-fund exposure (₹1,16,950 crore), credit outstanding (₹1,15,424 crore) and total credit exposure (₹1,28,185 crore at June 2026). IFCI's "AUM" in CARE's tables (₹3,981 crore at June 2026) is the gross book, 96% of it bad; its performing book is ₹70 crore. Always ask which number you are looking at.

Book growth also has to be funded with capital, which is why the fastest-growing institutions are the ones the state recapitalises. A lender that grows its book 30% a year at a constant risk weight needs its capital to grow roughly 30% a year too, or its capital ratio falls. NaBFID started with so much capital (73.93% capital adequacy at March 2025) that it could double its book and still report 44.22% a year later. SIDBI, growing 16.8% from a 19.62% starting ratio, needed fresh equity. IFCI, with negative capital, cannot grow at all, and its "growth" is simply the pace of run-off.

2. Net interest margin and spread

What it is: how much the lender earns on its assets after paying for its funding. Formulas: NIM = (interest income − interest expense) ÷ average interest-earning assets; spread = yield on advances − cost of borrowings. Worked example: NaBFID FY26 net interest income ₹3,268 crore on average earning assets of roughly ₹88,000 crore gives about 3.7%; its spread was 8.49% − 6.63% = 1.86 points. The gap between NIM and spread is the earning power of free capital. Drivers: up with more equity funding, a better rating, pricing power and lending in riskier segments; down with competition, rising leverage and rate hikes on floating liabilities. Benchmark: above 3% good, 2–3% average, below 2% weak, except for contractually thin pass-through models like IRFC. Definition trap: some companies calculate NIM on total assets, others on earning assets or on the loan book; the same institution can show 3.3% or 3.7% depending on the denominator.

The direction of NIM matters more than its level. NaBFID's quarterly margin fell from 3.91% to 2.75% in four quarters even as its profit rose, because the book doubled: absolute net interest income kept growing while the margin on each rupee shrank. PFC, by contrast, guides a stable spread of 2.40–2.50% for FY27 because its book grows slowly and its funding mix is mature. A falling NIM at a fast-growing lender is normal; a falling NIM at a slow-growing one is a warning about pricing or funding.

3. Cost of funds

What it is: the average rate paid on borrowed money, and the clearest single read of a non-deposit lender's competitive position. Formula: interest expense ÷ average borrowings. Worked example: SIDBI's interest expended of ₹31,781 crore on about ₹5.4 lakh crore of average interest-bearing liabilities gives roughly 5.9% (INDICATIVE). Drivers: rating, the mix of bonds versus bank lines, access to priority-sector deposits, tax-free or 54EC bonds, sovereign guarantees on foreign borrowing, and the policy rate. Benchmark: below 7% good, 7–8.5% average, above 9% weak. Comparison: NaBFID 6.56% (Q1 FY27), HUDCO 6.95%, PFC 7.50%, SIDBI about 5.9%, IFCI 9.91% coupon and about 11.3% on an accounting basis. Definition trap: accounting interest expense includes accruals, effective-interest adjustments and, for lessors like IRFC, capitalised pre-commencement interest; dividing it by average borrowings can produce nonsense (screener data imply ~4.7% for IRFC, which is not its true cost). Use company-stated figures where they exist, and state whether a number is contractual coupon or accounting cost.

The gap between the cheapest and most expensive funders in this set is about 4 percentage points. On a ₹10,000 crore book that is ₹400 crore a year, more than twice the entire profit of the illustrative book later in this section. That single number explains why a BB-rated lender cannot compete for the same borrowers as a AAA one, and why NaBFID's sovereign guarantee on foreign borrowing (which let it price a ten-year dollar bond at 105 basis points over US Treasuries) is worth more than any amount of operational efficiency.

4. Gross and net NPA, and stage 3

What it is: the share of the book that has stopped paying. Formulas: GNPA % = gross NPAs ÷ gross advances (RBI basis, 90 days overdue); NNPA % = (GNPA − NPA provisions) ÷ net advances; gross stage 3 % = credit-impaired assets ÷ gross loans (Ind AS 109). Worked example: IFCI's ₹3,589.98 crore of GNPA on gross advances of about ₹3,748 crore (RBI basis) is 95.79%; its net NPA of ₹573.76 crore is 78.44% of net advances. Drivers: sector choice and underwriting years earlier; recoveries and write-offs reduce the stock. Benchmark: below 1% good, 1–4% average, above 6% weak. Comparison: NaBFID nil, IRFC nil, SIDBI 0.12%, EXIM 0.57%, REC 0.88%, HUDCO 0.96%, PFC 1.09% (standalone), IREDA 3.75%, IFCI 95.68%. Definition trap: IFCI's Ind AS gross stage 3 was 98.28% at March 2026, wider than its RBI GNPA, because stage 3 includes reclassified security receipts and some accounts standard under RBI rules. PFC's 0.66% on aggregator sites is a different basis from its own 1.09%. Use one basis per chart.

For a fast-growing lender, also watch the absolute amount and the "slippage" (new NPAs added in the period), because a growing denominator can hide a rising numerator. IREDA shows why: its GNPA ratio improved from 4.13% to 3.75% over the year to June 2026, yet, according to consistent press reports, it declared two Gensol group accounts worth ₹672.74 crore (Gensol Engineering ₹453.77 crore and Gensol EV Lease ₹218.97 crore) as fraud and reported them to the RBI on 10 July 2026, having provided 85% at March 2026. For a run-off book like IFCI's the ratio is close to meaningless, because recoveries shrink the numerator and denominator together; the absolute net NPA (₹574 crore) is the number to track.

5. Provision coverage ratio, on three bases

What it is: how much of the bad book has already been written down. Formula: provisions ÷ bad loans; the numerator and denominator vary. Worked example (IFCI, March 2026): stage-3 ECL ₹2,997.83 crore ÷ gross stage 3 ₹4,097.39 crore = 73.16% (annual report); total ECL ₹2,998.81 crore ÷ gross loans ₹4,168.99 crore = 71.93% (CARE); NPA provisions ₹3,016.21 crore ÷ GNPA ₹3,589.98 crore = 84.0% (RBI basis, INDICATIVE arithmetic). Drivers: ageing of bad loans (RBI rules force higher provisions as NPAs age), write-backs on recovery, and management judgement under Ind AS. Benchmark: above 75% good, 60–75% average, below 50% weak; but a high PCR on a 96% NPA book still leaves a large net NPA. Comparison: EXIM 99.85%, PFC about 86% on its stage-3 book. Definition trap: some quote coverage including technical write-offs (which flatters it), some exclude it; and Ind AS provisions can be below the RBI requirement (IFCI's Ind AS provisions were ₹26.4 crore lower than IRACP-required at March 2026).

6. Capital adequacy (CRAR) and Tier 1

What it is: the loss-absorbing cushion relative to risk. Formula: regulatory capital ÷ risk-weighted assets; Tier 1 counts only core equity after deductions. Worked example: a lender with ₹1,500 crore of Tier 1 capital and ₹10,000 crore of loans at 100% risk weight has 15% Tier 1. Drivers: retained profit and new equity raise it; book growth, losses and deductions (investments in subsidiaries, deferred tax assets, intangibles) lower it. Benchmark: above 20% good, 15–20% average, below 15% weak for government NBFCs (minimum 15% CRAR, 10% Tier 1); AIFIs have lower minimums (NaBFID 9%). Comparison: NaBFID 45.13%, EXIM 26.39%, REC 24.3%, PFC 23.44%, SIDBI 20.07%, IFCI −17.58%. Definition trap: IFCI's book net worth is positive (₹1,797 crore at June 2026) while its CRAR is negative, because the regulator deducts its investment in SHCIL and its deferred tax asset. Net worth and regulatory capital are different things.

Capital is also the measure of a DFI's spare lending capacity. NaBFID's 45% ratio against a 9% minimum means it could support roughly 3.5–4 times today's risk assets before falling to a working level of 12–15% (INDICATIVE), which is why it can plan a four-fold larger book by FY30 without immediate new equity. SIDBI at 20% had no such room. For investors in listed lenders, the capital ratio is the clearest signal of whether growth will need a dilutive equity raise; for IFCI, it is the reason lending stays shut.

7. Credit cost

What it is: the annual charge for expected and actual loan losses. Formula: impairment (provisions) in the P&L ÷ average total assets or average loans. Worked example: SIDBI's FY26 provisions of ₹1,350 crore on about ₹6.0 lakh crore of average assets give about 0.22% (INDICATIVE). Drivers: new defaults, ageing of existing NPAs, write-backs on recoveries and model changes under ECL. Benchmark: below 0.5% good, 0.5–1.5% average, above 2% weak. Definition trap: for a run-off book, credit cost swings between large charges and large write-backs; IFCI's was −2.62% of average assets in FY25, +3.19% in FY26 and negative again in Q1 FY27. A negative credit cost is not a sign of quality; it is old provisions coming back as recoveries arrive.

Credit cost is also where the sovereign lenders' fates intertwine. IFCI holds 28.43% of Shiga Energy, an associate classified as held for sale; Shiga's ₹522 crore loan is one of PFC's few remaining stage-3 assets, with resolution "awaiting co-lender approval". A resolution would release provisions at PFC and possibly cash at IFCI. It is small in both institutions' accounts, but it is a concrete example of how a single stressed project can sit on several state balance sheets at once.

10. Return on assets and return on equity

What it is: the bottom-line productivity of the balance sheet (ROA) and of shareholders' money (ROE). Formulas: ROA = PAT ÷ average total assets; ROE = PAT ÷ average equity = ROA × average assets ÷ average equity. Worked example: PFC's FY26 consolidated PAT of ₹33,625 crore on average assets of about ₹12.1 lakh crore is about 2.8%; at leverage of roughly 7–8 times, that becomes a 20.7% ROE (INDICATIVE). Drivers: everything above, plus leverage. Benchmark: ROA above 2% (NBFC) or 0.8% (refinance AIFI) good; ROE above 15% good, 10–15% average, below 8% weak. The same 11–21% range of returns is produced by very different combinations, and each combination carries a different risk: NaBFID's depends on free capital, PFC's on leverage and state-utility payment, SIDBI's on very high leverage against low-risk borrowers.

11. Asset-liability maturity gap and liquidity cover

Definition trap: IFCI's ALM table shows ₹3,657 crore of its ₹3,763 crore contractual loan inflows in the "over five years" bucket. What it is: non-interest income from services: loan syndication, credit enhancement, advisory and scheme administration. Formula: fees and commission ÷ total income. Worked example: IFCI's standalone fees of ₹79.1 crore are 8.6% of its ₹924 crore total income; the group's ₹632 crore (mostly SHCIL) is 29.6% of ₹2,134 crore. Drivers: new government schemes and mandates (for IFCI), market activity and custody assets (for SHCIL), and down-selling and guarantee products (for NaBFID). Benchmark: for wholesale lenders fees are usually under 5% of income; higher shares signal a different business. Definition trap: IFCI's "fees" exclude government advisory receipts meant for advisory applicants, which are adjusted out of its ratios; and scheme money held (₹3,259 crore) is not income at all.

13. Price to book and price to adjusted book

What it is: the market's verdict on whether a lender's equity earns more than its cost. Formulas: P/B = market cap ÷ book equity; P/ABV = market cap ÷ (book equity − net NPAs, after tax where relevant). Worked example: IFCI's ₹15,945 crore market cap ÷ ₹8,944 crore parent equity = 1.78×; on standalone book of ₹6.63 a share (₹1,785 crore) it is about 8.9×; on total equity including minorities (₹15,504 crore) it is 1.03×. Subtracting IFCI's ₹574 crore of net NPA from standalone book gives adjusted book of about ₹1,211 crore and a P/ABV of about 13× (INDICATIVE). Drivers: ROE relative to cost of equity, growth, asset-quality confidence and policy. Benchmark: PSU lenders typically trade at 0.7–1.5× book. Definition trap: IFCI's P/B is a proxy for the sum of the parts, not a lender multiple; and a merger or an NSE mark-up can move book value by thousands of crore without changing the economics.

The Q2 FY27 accounts are the live example. If SHCIL marks its residual NSE stake from about ₹1,268 to the 30 September price, IFCI's parent equity could rise by about ₹1,900 crore after deferred tax and book value per share from ₹33.2 to around ₹40, cutting the P/B at today's price to about 1.5× (ILLUSTRATIVE). Nothing about the business would have changed: the market already values NSE at its quoted price. When a lender's P/B moves without any change in its ROE, look for the accounting event behind it.

14. Sum of the parts and the holding-company discount

What it is: for a lender that is really a holding company, the value of what it owns less what it owes, compared with its market value. Formula: discount = 1 − (market cap ÷ gross SOTP); a negative discount is a premium. Worked example: IFCI's indicative gross SOTP of ₹9,000–12,200 crore against a ₹15,945 crore market cap is a premium of 31–77% (INDICATIVE). Definition traps, collected

  • DFI, AIFI, NBFC: IFCI is a government NBFC, not an AIFI; NaBFID is an AIFI, not an NBFC. Capital rules differ (15% versus 9% minimum).
  • Two GNPA numbers: IFCI's RBI-basis GNPA (95.79%) and Ind AS gross stage 3 (98.28%) are both right; never mix them in one chart.
  • "Loan book" is not "performing book": IFCI's gross loans of ₹3,981 crore include only ₹70 crore of standard net loans.
  • Net worth versus regulatory capital: positive book equity coexists with negative CRAR because subsidiaries and deferred tax are deducted.
  • Consolidated profit is not owners' profit: ₹435 crore group PAT, ₹181 crore to IFCI shareholders.
  • Book value excludes minorities: screener's ₹33.2 book per share is parent equity (₹8,944 crore); total equity is ₹15,504 crore.
  • OCI is not profit: IFCI's FY25 total comprehensive income of ₹7,011 crore was mostly a ₹6,662 crore NSE mark-up.
  • SHCIL's profit contains the NSE dividend: about ₹385 crore pre-tax in FY26; do not value it twice.
  • Recoveries have scopes: ₹537 crore (annual report), ₹536 crore (ICRA), ₹576 crore (CARE); GNPA recoveries only ₹80.66 crore.
  • Three PCRs: 73.16%, 71.93% and ~84.0% for the same book.
  • Coupon versus accounting cost: 9.91% contractual, ~11.3% accounting, for the same debt.
  • Sanctions are not loans: NaBFID's cumulative sanctions (₹3.59 lakh crore) are three times its loan book.
  • Pass-through money inflates assets: ₹3,259 crore of scheme funds sit in IFCI's ₹10,064 crore balance sheet.
  • PFC's GNPA has bases: 1.09% standalone credit-impaired (company) versus 0.66% (aggregator, unlabelled).

How to read a DFI's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the date and the basis (1 minute). Which quarter (Q1 FY27 = June 2026; Q2 FY27 = September 2026), standalone or consolidated, and whether the profit line is total or owners'. For IFCI, look for "attributable to owners" first.
  2. Loan book and sanctions (1 minute). For a growing DFI, compare book growth with sanctions and disbursements; for IFCI, confirm that sanctions are still nil and note the standard net book (₹70 crore in June 2026).
  3. Spread or recoveries (1 minute). Growing lender: NIM, yield and cost of funds, and the direction of each. IFCI: recoveries in the quarter (run-rate ₹130–200 crore); below ₹100 crore is a warning.
  4. Asset quality (1 minute). GNPA and NNPA on the RBI basis, stage 3 on Ind AS, and the provision coverage basis. For IFCI, watch the absolute net NPA rather than the ratio.
  5. Credit cost (1 minute). Is the impairment line a charge or a write-back? A write-back flatters profit; separate it before judging the quarter.
  6. Capital (1 minute). CRAR and Tier 1 against the minimum. NaBFID: is capital adequacy falling towards 30%? IFCI: is the negative CRAR still improving (−17.58% in June 2026)?
  7. Liquidity and maturities (1 minute). Cash and liquid investments against the next twelve months of debt service. IFCI: liquidity after the ₹102.49 crore Tier II repayment on 31 October 2026, and progress towards the FY31 wall.
  8. Other comprehensive income and book value (1 minute). For IFCI in Q2 FY27, expect a large OCI gain from marking NSE to market; recompute book per share and P/B, and do not mistake it for earnings.
  9. Subsidiaries and dividends (1 minute). SHCIL's contribution, the NSE dividend (Q2 every year), and any special dividend that moves cash to the parent.
  10. Policy notes and red flags (1 minute). Any line on consolidation approval, GoI equity, rating actions, auditor qualifications, fraud reporting or regulatory fines. A change in any of these matters more than the quarter's profit.
In plain terms

For a growing state lender, read the spread, the bad-loan trend and the capital ratio. For IFCI, skip the margin and the profit headline; read recoveries, cash against upcoming repayments, what happened to the NSE stake, and whether there is any news from the government on the merger.

In plain terms

Most confusion about IFCI comes from using the wrong definition: total profit instead of owners' profit, gross loans instead of performing loans, net worth instead of regulatory capital, comprehensive income instead of earnings. Get the basis right and the numbers stop contradicting each other.

Who is winning: cheap money, clean books and the one outlier

Plotting IFCI against the reference peers on returns, funding and capital shows three clusters: AAA captive lenders, capital-rich new DFIs, and one run-off book priced on something else.

The winners in development finance are easy to identify once the right axes are chosen. They combine cheap funding with a low-risk borrower base and enough capital to grow. The three maps below place IFCI, the only member of this sub-sector, against the reference peers. In each, the member dot is drawn larger and labelled; gold dots are unlisted AIFIs or alternative bases.

Taken together, the maps sort the system into three groups. The AAA captive lenders (PFC, REC, IRFC, HUDCO, IREDA) earn the highest returns and are the clearest winners as businesses, though not as stocks: the market keeps them at low multiples because of concentration and ownership. The capital-rich AIFIs (NaBFID above all, then EXIM and SIDBI) are the winners in growth and balance-sheet strength, but their equity is not listed. IFCI is the outlier on every axis. As a business it is the clear loser; as a stock it has been one of the stronger performers of the past two years, because its value has migrated to SHCIL and NSE.

What would move IFCI on these maps? Only the merger. A completed consolidation that brings SHCIL's assets onto the balance sheet and retires the legacy bonds would lift IFCI's capital ratio above zero and cut its funding cost to nil on the retired debt, shifting it up and left on the second and third maps. Its return on equity would still be low, because NSE dividends and custody fees on a much larger equity base produce modest percentage returns; on the first map it would move right only slightly. In other words, even a successful merger turns IFCI into a market-infrastructure holding company, not a competitive lender.

The global lens: guarantees and captive funding make DFIs last

KfW, China Development Bank and BNDES dwarf India's DFIs, and each rests on an explicit state guarantee or a captive source of cheap money. NaBFID copied the template; IFCI after 1991 had neither.

The development banks that have lasted for decades abroad share one feature: the state either guarantees their debt explicitly or gives them a captive pool of cheap funding. KfW, Germany's promotional bank, carries an explicit federal guarantee and borrows at spreads close to German government bonds. It reported total assets of €540.7 billion for 2025, promotional volume of €98.0 billion, equity of €40.6 billion, a consolidated profit of about €1.0 billion and a CET1 ratio of 27.7%. It lends mostly through commercial banks rather than directly, which keeps its own credit risk low. China Development Bank, the world's largest DFI, reported total assets of about RMB 19.55 trillion and net profit of about RMB 91.5 billion for 2025 (press-based); it funds itself with policy-bank bonds bought largely by Chinese banks. BNDES in Brazil reported total assets of about R$962.5 billion, a credit portfolio of R$663.6 billion and net income of about R$26.8 billion (R$15.2 billion recurring) for 2025 (press-based); its funding comes from the FAT workers' fund and Treasury loans, and its subsidised lending rate was replaced by a market-linked benchmark (TLP) after 2017 reforms. Japan's JBIC, an export-finance analogue to EXIM, was not sourced.

The comparison clarifies NaBFID's design choices. Its sovereign guarantee covers foreign-currency borrowing only, not its rupee bonds; its free capital is large relative to today's book but will shrink relative to a ₹4–5 lakh crore target book; and its tax holiday ends after ten years. The World Bank group, ADB, AIIB, the New Development Bank, JICA, KfW and Proparco provide long-dated lines of credit to SIDBI, EXIM and NaBFID and co-finance Indian infrastructure projects. In plain terms

Around the world, development banks survive when the state either guarantees their borrowing outright (Germany's KfW) or hands them a captive source of cheap money (China's and Brazil's banks). India copied that idea when it built NaBFID. IFCI lost its cheap money in 1991 and never got a guarantee, which is the whole difference.

Technology and disruption: what erodes the franchises that are left

For DFIs the threat is capital markets doing their job; for IFCI's surviving businesses it is fee compression in custody, digital stamp-duty portals and low-moat scheme work.

Development finance is not usually thought of as a technology-exposed industry, but each part of this sub-sector faces a specific form of disintermediation. For the infrastructure DFIs, the competitor is a deeper capital market. Infrastructure investment trusts now buy operating roads and transmission lines; green and sustainability bonds fund renewable projects directly; partial credit enhancement (which NaBFID launched in July 2025) lets pension funds and insurers buy project bonds they would otherwise avoid. Each of these reduces the need for a DFI to hold operating assets on its balance sheet. NaBFID's response is visible in its mix: about 41–44% of its cumulative sanctions are greenfield, the construction-stage risk that markets still cannot price, and it has begun selling down loans (₹2,205.70 crore in FY26). The DFI of the future is a construction-phase lender and risk distributor, not a hold-to-maturity balance sheet.

For SHCIL, the risks are more direct. Custody and depository fees face compression from discount brokers and from bank custodians; SHCIL's strategic alliances with SBI and IDFC First Bank for custody banking are a defensive response. E-stamping, the most annuity-like business (18.09 crore certificates in FY25), could be disintermediated if states move to fully digital registration with stamp duty paid directly into treasury portals, as several already do for some transactions through government receipt accounting systems. SHCIL's mandates in seven states were renewed in FY25, which buys time but not immunity. Its broking arm launched a new trading app and a tie-up with the Department of Posts. Operational and cyber risk is real but so far small: SHCIL reported a ₹55 lakh fraud through linked bank accounts on its trading platform.

For IFCI's scheme-administration work, technology is both the product and the threat. IFCI built and runs the PLI portals, moved its systems to Oracle Cloud and added CRM and ESG modules. But portal-building and claims verification are low-moat services: other state institutions (SIDBI, IREDA) and private project-management consultancies compete for the same mandates, and a ministry can re-tender. The franchise is sticky only while IFCI performs and while ministries value continuity. For the refinance AIFIs, the eventual move to expected-credit-loss provisioning (not yet mandated for AIFIs) and the RBI's push for digital lending and account-aggregator data could change how SIDBI and NABARD price refinance; neither is an existential threat.

Bull vs bear: the merger, the NSE price and the 2030 wall

The bull case is a government approval that turns NSE shares into debt repayment and capital; the bear case is delay, a falling NSE price and a premium that has nothing left to support it.

For the sub-sector as a whole, the debate is settled in favour of the new model: the state is funding NaBFID, recapitalising SIDBI and consolidating the power financiers, and the old DFI balance sheets are being sold or merged. The live debate is about IFCI's equity, which sits on top of two variables it does not control. The bull and bear cases below set out each side's strongest arguments.

The bull case

  • The merger unlocks the stake. GoI approval would bring 10.38 crore NSE shares (₹17,337 crore at ₹1,670) onto IFCI's balance sheet, remove the regulatory deduction and could restore positive capital adequacy without a cheque.
  • Debt can be retired. Selling about a fifth of the NSE stake could repay the remaining ₹3,123 crore of principal, saving about ₹300 crore a year of interest and removing the FY31 wall (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Accretive arithmetic. Issuing new shares to SHCIL's minority at a price above look-through raises NSE value per IFCI share from about ₹36 to about ₹43 pre-tax (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • NSE optionality. Each ₹100 on NSE adds about ₹549 crore of pre-tax look-through; NSE trades below its ₹1,785 issue price.
  • A surviving franchise. PLI and semiconductor scheme administration, plus SHCIL's custody and e-stamping annuity, give the merged entity an operating business.

The bear case

  • The premium is already paid. At ₹15,945 crore IFCI trades 31–77% above an indicative gross SOTP of ₹9,000–12,200 crore, where holding companies normally trade at a discount.
  • Delay is the base case so far. Fifteen months at the government-approval step, with no Cabinet decision, scheme filing or tribunal step found.
  • Swap risk. Valuers could set terms that favour SHCIL's state-owned minority; dilution of ~59% could be larger (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • NSE could fall further. It already trades below its issue price; at ₹1,400 IFCI's look-through drops to about ₹8,266 crore pre-tax.
  • The 2030 wall without a merger. Own cash runs short by roughly ₹450–1,100 crore in FY31 (ILLUSTRATIVE); recoveries are concentrated in hard accounts; governance is weak.
