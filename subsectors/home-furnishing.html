00

The one-page view: a chemical spread with a brand on top

Two listed companies, two opposite ways of selling, one shared cost base that comes out of a refinery.

India spends roughly ₹14,500–16,000 crore a year on mattresses (calendar 2024, Redseer's estimate for Wakefit's prospectus), inside a home-and-furnishings market of ₹2.8–3.0 lakh crore. About seven rupees in ten of that mattress spend still goes to local makers, cotton-gadda stitchers and small shops. Two companies in the NSE 750 sell into this market: Sheela Foam, India's largest maker of polyurethane foam and the owner of Sleepwell and Kurlon, and Wakefit, the largest direct-to-consumer home brand, which listed on 15 December 2025. Together they reported ₹5,310 crore of revenue in FY26, up 12.7%, and were worth ₹10,403 crore at the 30 October 2026 close.

Five forces explain almost everything about the category. Each one gets a section, and the strongest four are deep dives.

  • The organised shift. Cotton mattresses are still about 60% of the market by value. Redseer's "organised" share (online plus large-format stores of 2,500 sq ft or more) rose from about 20% in CY19 to about 30% in CY24 and is projected at about 45% by CY30. Every share claim in this industry uses a different definition of "organised", and confusing them is the commonest analytical mistake (sections 03 and 08).
  • Chemistry sets the margin. Two crude-linked chemicals, toluene di-isocyanate (TDI) and polyether polyol, are 65–70% of Sheela's cost of goods. India imports most of them and charges anti-dumping duty on most import origins, a duty the mattress makers oppose. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 doubled polyol prices at the peak and cut Sheela's Q1 FY27 gross margin by 405 basis points (section 06, deep dive).
  • Two business models converging. Sheela sells through dealers: about 83% of its standalone sales go to dealers and distributors. Wakefit sells through its own website and 165 company-owned stores. Sheela is now opening company stores and selling online; Wakefit is selling through 2,250 multi-brand outlets. Both are heading for the same omnichannel middle (section 09, deep dive).
  • Kurlon and Sheela's margin rebuild. Sheela paid ₹1,940.78 crore for 94.67% of Kurlon in October 2023, saw its operating margin fall to 7% in FY25 and has since rebuilt it to 10.8% (core, FY26). It promises 11–12% in FY27 and 15% in FY28 (section 14, deep dive).
  • Wakefit's profit versus its IPO price. Wakefit's FY26 profit of ₹189 crore includes a ₹98 crore deferred-tax credit, and its pre-lease operating margin is 7.5%, not the 12.2% headline. The stock is 31% below its ₹195 issue price, yet still trades at about 44 times trailing earnings once the tax credit is removed (section 15, deep dive).

The two companies look alike from a distance and very different up close. Sheela is a converter of petrochemicals with brands on top: about half of its India revenue is mattresses, the rest is foam sold to other businesses, and it owns foam plants in Australia and Spain. Wakefit is a vertically integrated retailer that makes most of what it sells and keeps the margin a dealer would otherwise earn. That shows up in gross margin (55.8% at Wakefit against 43.7% at Sheela in FY26), but by the time rent, advertising and delivery are paid, the two land within a few points of each other at the operating line.

In plain terms

A mattress is mostly foam, and foam is mostly two chemicals made from crude oil. Whoever sells the mattress pays roughly the same price for those chemicals. What separates a good mattress business from a poor one is how fast it can pass a chemical price rise to the customer, how much it pays to reach the customer (a dealer's cut, or rent and advertising), and how many cotton-mattress buyers it can convert to foam each year.

The timing matters. Both companies last reported Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026). Sheela's Q2 FY27 results are due on 2 November 2026 with a call on 3 November; Wakefit has not announced a date, but listing rules require results by 14 November 2026. Q2 will be the first full quarter of the July 2026 re-spike in chemical prices, so the gross-margin line is the number to read first. Sheela has guided an FY27 operating margin of 11–12% and Wakefit a pre-lease margin of about 7.5%; both guides assume that the volatility fades by November.

01

How a mattress makes money: from toluene to the bedroom

Five steps separate a refinery from a bedroom, and the profit pools sit at the two ends of the chain.

A modern Indian mattress starts as two liquids. TDI (toluene di-isocyanate, in the 80:20 isomer grade used for flexible foam) is made from toluene, a refinery product, through a chlorine and phosgene chain. Polyether polyol is made from propylene oxide, which also comes from crude or naphtha. Mixed with water, catalysts and a silicone surfactant, the two react and expand into a continuous loaf of polyurethane foam, called slabstock, which is cut into blocks. Sheela, Joyce (its Australian unit) and Interplasp (its Spanish unit) use variable-pressure foaming, a process that controls the density of the foam more precisely and wastes less of it.

The foam block is then sliced into layers, glued to other layers (coir, springs, latex or memory foam where the design needs them), wrapped in quilted fabric called ticking and edge-taped. A dealer-sold mattress ships flat on a truck. A direct-to-consumer mattress is compressed, rolled and vacuum-packed into a box that a courier can carry, which is why the "bed-in-a-box" format made it possible to sell mattresses online at all.

Where is the value captured? Not in the foam itself. TDI and polyol are priced in Asian markets and every converter pays roughly the same. The larger prize sits with whoever owns the customer relationship. Gross margins show the pattern clearly.

In plain terms

Selling foam to a factory earns a thin spread over chemical cost. Selling a mattress through a dealer earns more, but the dealer keeps a fifth of the shelf price. Selling it from your own website or store earns the most, but you then pay the rent, the salespeople, the advertising and the delivery van yourself. The gross margin tells you how much of the chain a company owns; it does not tell you whether owning it pays.

Capital intensity differs too. Sheela carries fixed assets of ₹3,198 crore, of which about ₹1,612 crore is goodwill and brand value from the Kurlon purchase; strip that out and its operating capital employed is about ₹1,556 crore. Wakefit's asset base is mostly right-of-use assets for store leases and ₹149 crore of plant, and it runs on almost zero working capital because suppliers fund its inventory: net working capital was 2.2 days in FY26. These differences drive the definition traps in return ratios that section 11 unpacks.

02

A small bed in a big home: sizing the market

Mattresses are only about 5% of India's home spending, and per-head spend is a twenty-fifth of America's.

The only recent, detailed sizing of the category is Redseer's "Building India's Home Story" report, dated 19 November 2025 and reproduced in Wakefit's prospectus. Wakefit commissioned and paid for it, so it should be read as an informed but interested source. Its headline is that India's home and furnishings (H&F) market was worth ₹2.8–3.0 lakh crore in calendar 2024, growing at 11–13% a year to ₹5.2–5.9 lakh crore by 2030. (The report's own conclusion paragraph prints slightly different numbers, ₹2.9–3.2 lakh crore; the body figures are used here.) Redseer's figures are calendar-year and stated as ranges; company figures are fiscal-year point numbers. Any "share" calculated by dividing one by the other carries an error of 10–15% from the range alone.

Mattresses are small but they are the most branded, most standardised and most shippable product in the home. They are also where both listed companies started. Redseer puts the mattress market at ₹14,500–16,000 crore and 28–32 million units in CY24, after growing 7–9% a year since CY19, and projects 10–12% a year to ₹27,000–30,000 crore by CY30. Three cuts of the market matter for the listed companies. First, material: cotton is about 60% of mattress value and foam about 23%, with spring, coir, latex and hybrids making up the rest; by CY30 Redseer expects cotton to fall to about 49% and foam to rise to about 31%. Second, price: mattresses under ₹8,000 are about 72% of value and the ₹8,000–20,000 "masstige" band about 20%; premium (₹20,000–50,000) and luxury (above ₹50,000) are small but forecast to grow at 15–17% and 18–20% a year. Third, geography: the top eight metros are about 44% of value, but tier-2 and smaller towns are the fastest growing at 11–13% a year. Institutions (hotels, hostels, hospitals) buy about 18% of mattress value.

The long-run case for the category rests on how little India spends. Redseer puts per-capita home and furnishings spend at US$24–25, against about US$95 in China, US$475 in the UK and US$600 in the US. A mattress is replaced every eight to ten years in a developed market; with about 30 million units sold a year across more than 300 million Indian households, the effective replacement cycle here is longer than ten years (indicative arithmetic). Much of the gap is income, but some of it is habit: a cotton mattress that is re-stuffed rather than replaced, or a family that shares beds.

How big are the two listed companies against this market? Their combined FY26 mattress revenue was ₹2,411 crore (Sheela India ₹1,497 crore plus Wakefit ₹914 crore). Growing Redseer's CY24 midpoint of ₹15,250 crore by about 10% gives a CY25 market of roughly ₹16,000–17,500 crore, so the two together sell about 14–15% of India's mattresses by value. In plain terms

India buys about 30 million mattresses a year, and roughly one in eight is a Sleepwell or a Kurlon. Wakefit sells perhaps another million, at higher average prices. The other twenty-five million or so come from thousands of local foam makers, cotton stitchers and regional brands. That long tail is both the opportunity (it can be converted) and the competitive constraint (it sets the price ceiling for a mass-market mattress).

Furniture is the bigger and messier market. Redseer sizes it at ₹1.8–2.0 lakh crore, with beds and sofas about 55% of value, households about 65% of demand, and an organised share of only about 26% in CY24 (rising to about 30% by CY30). Organised furniture players include Godrej & Boyce's Interio, IKEA India, Wakefit, Royaloak, Durian and Stanley Lifestyles. Furnishings and décor (₹79,000–86,000 crore) include Welspun Living, IKEA and D'Décor. Wakefit is the only listed member with meaningful furniture exposure; for adjacent spend on finishes and décor see paints.

03

Cotton to foam: the organised shift and the arithmetic traps inside it

The industry's growth story is formalisation, but nobody agrees what "organised" means, so every share number needs its basis.

The single most important trend in Indian mattresses is the move from unbranded cotton and coir, made locally and sold by small shops, to branded foam and spring mattresses sold by national companies. Redseer measures it as a channel shift. In its definition, "organised" means sales through online channels (brand websites plus marketplaces) and through "branded, structured retailers and national chains" with stores of at least 2,500 sq ft. Everything else, including a Sleepwell sold across the counter of a 600 sq ft dealer shop, is "unorganised" in Redseer's split.

Applied to the CY24 market, each percentage point of share moving from the unorganised tail to organised channels is worth about ₹150 crore of annual revenue today (illustrative arithmetic). Why is the shift happening? Redseer lists rising middle-income households (144 million, or 46% of households, in CY19; 174 million, 51%, in CY24; 219 million, 58%, projected for CY30), nuclear families (about 30 million new nuclear households between CY19 and CY24), urbanisation from 37% towards 40%, and more women in paid work. Add GST, which pulled informal makers into the tax net, and the brands' own push into small towns. Redseer also notes that coir mattresses lost share after GST raised their effective tax.

The listed companies attack the unorganised pool from opposite ends. Sheela's U2O ("unorganised to organised") programme sells 5- and 6-inch foam mattresses through about 10,000 small dealers in more than 5,000 towns; an analyst on the Q1 FY27 call estimated the realisation at about ₹2,000–2,100 a mattress, a figure management did not confirm. Wakefit attacks from the top of the mass market, with opening-price roll-pack mattresses at about ₹5,000 for a single size, sold online and in its stores.

Two readings follow, and both should be kept in view. On the managements' own (wide) definitions, Sheela and Wakefit together hold about 30% of the organised market, which leaves the organised end itself fragmented: Duroflex, The Sleep Company, Peps, Springfit, Centuary and dozens of regional and marketplace labels share the rest. On Redseer's narrow channel definition, the organised pool was only about ₹4,400–4,800 crore in CY24 (30% of the market), and the two members' mattress revenue of ₹2,411 crore (FY26) would be roughly half of it. That second reading is illustrative and overstated, because much of Sheela's revenue flows through small dealer counters that Redseer counts as unorganised. The broad conclusion survives either way: the top of the market is crowded with a handful of brands, and the bottom is a long tail that is slowly being converted.

In plain terms

When a company says it has "30% share", ask "30% of what?" Sheela's 30% is of branded mattresses; its own chairman puts it nearer 20% of the organised market; Redseer would count much of its dealer business as unorganised altogether. None of these is wrong. They are answers to different questions, and mixing them produces nonsense.

Industry growth itself is modest. Sheela's chairman said on 5 August 2026 that the mattress industry's volume grew "closer to 5%" in Q1 FY27, while Sheela's own grew 6%. Most of the value growth in 2026 came from price increases forced by chemical costs: Sheela's mattress revenue rose 15% on 6% more units in Q1 FY27. The formalisation story is therefore a slow, multi-year one, and in any single year the listed companies' growth depends more on pricing, channel additions and competition than on the shift itself. The online slice is the fastest-growing part of it; for marketplace economics and quick commerce, see e-retail.

04

Why India buys a mattress: households, homes and the festive quarter

Demand is mostly replacement and new households, with a housing kicker that arrives years after the sale.

A mattress is a durable that nobody buys twice in a hurry. Demand comes from four sources, in roughly this order of size: households replacing an old mattress, new households being formed, families moving into new homes, and institutions (hotels, hostels, hospitals) refitting rooms. None of them is fast, which is why Redseer's long-run growth rate for mattresses (7–9% a year in CY19–24) is below that for furniture and furnishings. What changes the picture for the listed companies is not the size of the pool but the share of it that buys a branded foam mattress instead of a cotton one.

Replacement and new households

Replacement is the base load. A foam mattress is designed to last eight to ten years; a cotton one is often re-stuffed rather than replaced. With about 30 million mattresses sold a year against more than 300 million households, the average household replaces less often than once a decade (indicative arithmetic). New households add to this: Redseer counts about 30 million new nuclear families formed between CY19 and CY24, each of which needs at least one bed and one mattress. Rising incomes push both groups towards foam and towards higher price bands; Redseer expects the ₹8,000–20,000 masstige band to rise from about 20% to about 23% of value by CY30.

Housing: the lagged trigger

A new home is the strongest trigger for a full-home basket: bed, mattress, wardrobe, sofa, curtains and sheets bought together. That is why Wakefit, with its furniture range, leans on housing more than Sheela does. But the purchase happens at possession, typically two to four years after the flat is sold. The Indian housing boom of 2022–24 (Redseer counts residential sales rising from 0.23 million units in CY21 to 0.35 million in CY24; Anarock counts about 4.6 lakh units in the top seven cities in CY24) is therefore flowing into possessions and furnishing demand in 2025–27. Renters, hotels and institutions

More than 20% of urban homes are rented, according to Redseer. Renters and migrants buy cheaper roll-pack mattresses, or rent furniture outright; that is Furlenco's market, and its subscriber count rose 36% year on year in Q1 FY27. Hotels refurbish rooms every five to seven years, and Redseer cites a 17% rise in new branded hotel keys over CY19–24. Institutions buy about 18% of mattress value, mostly through tenders, where price and specification matter more than brand. Wakefit says hotels and co-living operators are only 2–3% of its revenue.

Premiumisation and the store effect

Premium mattresses sell in person. Wakefit says about half the mattresses sold in its stores are premium, against 15–20% across the company, and that average order value in its stores was 78.8% higher than on its website in H1 FY26. Mattresses priced above ₹25,000 sell mostly in stores. Sheela reports "some premiumisation" in average selling prices and has launched new premium ranges under both brands (Sleepwell Sense and Nexa Plus; Kurlon Spinekare and Duorest Latex, among others). The listed companies' push into company-owned stores is therefore partly a premiumisation strategy, not just a distribution one.

Seasonality: the festive quarter

The October–December quarter is the best of the year for both companies, because Diwali, weddings and year-end sales coincide; January–March is usually second. Foam is a petrochemical: TDI, polyol and the 2026 shock Deep dive

Two crude-linked chemicals are two-thirds of the cost of goods, India imports most of them, and 2026 showed what happens when supply breaks.

Every argument about margins in this sub-sector comes back to two chemicals. Sheela's group CFO said on 5 August 2026 that TDI and polyol together are 65–70% of cost of goods sold. Wakefit's management gave two figures on its Q1 FY27 call that use different bases: TDI and polyol are 60–65% of the raw-material cost of a mattress, or about 55% of its selling price. CRISIL puts raw materials as a whole at 60–65% of Sheela's manufacturing cost. However the share is cut, these two inputs dominate, and neither company makes them.

The chemistry in one paragraph

Flexible polyurethane foam is formed when an isocyanate (TDI) reacts with a polyol in the presence of water. The water reacts with part of the TDI to release carbon dioxide, which blows the mixture into foam; catalysts control the speed and a silicone surfactant stabilises the bubbles. The ratio of TDI to polyol, the amount of water and the additives set the density and firmness. As a rough rule, TDI is about a third of the weight of a typical flexible foam and polyol most of the rest (indicative). Polyol is the larger input by tonnage; TDI is the more volatile by price. Memory foam and high-resilience (HR) foams use modified polyols and sometimes MDI, a related isocyanate; spring and coir mattresses use less foam per unit, which is why foam-heavy products are hit hardest when chemicals spike.

In plain terms

How the two members absorbed it

The two companies came out of Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) with opposite gross-margin results, and the reasons are instructive. Sheela raised prices "commensurately", and its revenue numbers show it: foam revenue rose 26% on 4% more tonnes, and mattress revenue rose 15% on 6% more units. In May, management had expected "no material" margin effect beyond 50–100 basis points, because price changes take 10–15 days to implement. That proved too optimistic.

Sensitivity: what a 10% chemical rise does

A simple sensitivity shows why the two companies are not equally exposed. Sheela's FY26 cost of goods was about ₹2,150 crore, of which TDI and polyol at 65–70% is ₹1,400–1,500 crore. A 10% rise that it could not pass on at all would cost ₹140–150 crore, 3.7–3.9 percentage points of margin, and more than a third of FY26 EBITDA. Wakefit's mattress cost of goods is about ₹400 crore (44% of ₹914 crore), of which TDI and polyol at 60–65% is ₹240–260 crore; the same unrecovered 10% would cost about ₹25 crore, or 1.7 percentage points. These are illustrative upper bounds. In practice, institutional foam reprices quickly (CRISIL notes that costs are "readily passed on to institutional customers"), mattresses reprice in steps alongside competitors, and roughly half to two-thirds of a rise is usually recovered within a quarter (an indicative rule of thumb).

Two structural conclusions follow. First, a direct-to-consumer model with a high gross margin is less exposed per rupee of revenue to chemical spikes than a foam-plus-dealer model, because chemicals are a smaller slice of each rupee. Second, the unorganised sector is the most exposed of all: it has the lowest gross margin, no buying power and no inventory buffer. Sheela's chairman put it directly: volatility hurts the small maker that "produces foam only when it is opportune". Each chemical spike pushes some of those makers out and hands share to the brands, which is the formalisation argument in its sharpest form. Management expects the volatility to last "till October or even November" 2026 and for a couple of months after the war ends. Q2 FY27, reported from 2 November, will show whether the July re-spike repeated April's damage.

Duties the buyers fight, and a tax that stood still

Trade policy in this sub-sector protects the chemical supplier, not the mattress maker, and GST 2.0 gave the category nothing.

In most chemical sub-sectors, anti-dumping duties protect the listed companies. Here they do the opposite. India levies anti-dumping duty (ADD) on TDI from five of its main import origins, and on flexible slabstock polyol from Saudi Arabia and the UAE, at the request of domestic producers (GNFC for TDI). Those duties raise the landed cost of the mattress makers' main inputs, and Sheela Foam and Duroflex have appeared in past TDI reviews as interested parties opposing the duties, alongside Covestro India, Wanhua, Flexipol and Prime Comfort.

How much does the duty matter? Converted at ₹94.9 to the dollar, the TDI duties range from about ₹10 a kilo (BorsodChem) to about ₹42 a kilo (Korean producers other than Hanwha and BASF). GST: 28% at launch, 18% since, unchanged by GST 2.0

The consumer-facing tax has a short history. When GST launched on 1 July 2017, spring, foam and stuffed mattresses (HSN 9404) and most furniture (HSN 9403) were placed in the top 28% slab, while coir mattresses and cotton pillows, mattresses and quilts were at 18% (CBIC rate booklet, 3 July 2017). Mattresses and furniture moved to 18% in November 2017, and stayed there. GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, left mattresses and mainstream furniture at 18%, cut bamboo and cane furniture to 5%, and cut rates on other consumer durables. The category got no direct help. Redseer notes that coir mattresses became less affordable after GST, which helped foam take share.

Other rules that touch the P&L

  • Labour codes took effect in November 2025. Wakefit booked a ₹3.8 crore exceptional charge for gratuity and leave liabilities in FY26; Sheela booked ₹6.15 crore (consolidated; ₹4.86 crore standalone) of labour-code gratuity charges.

  • Legal Metrology and e-commerce rules require MRP and country-of-origin labelling on packaged goods, including roll-pack mattresses, and the 2023 dark-pattern guidelines govern online selling.
  • Extended producer responsibility covers plastic packaging; Sheela reports EPR for polythene, and Wakefit uses reusable covers and PU rollers.
  • Furniture QCOs: BIS standards exist for chairs, tables and storage, and a furniture QCO was notified with phased deadlines; its current enforcement status could not be verified. No mattress QCO was found.

The policy balance is therefore mildly unfavourable. The category pays duties on its inputs that protect a supplier, receives no GST relief on its outputs, and absorbs the same labour-law and compliance costs as every manufacturer. The offset is that every compliance requirement weighs more heavily on the unorganised maker than on a listed one, which again pushes share towards brands over time.

In plain terms

Selling mattresses in India is easy to start and hard to make money from. The companies that earn a steady margin are the ones that have been at it for decades (Sheela, Duroflex) or that have reached scale (Wakefit, only since FY26). How concentrated is the top?

There are two plausible but incompatible answers, and both should be shown with their basis. On Redseer's narrow channel definition, the organised mattress pool was about ₹4,400–4,800 crore in CY24. The three leaders' mattress revenue (Sheela India ₹1,357 crore in FY25, Wakefit ₹781 crore and Duroflex an estimated ₹900–1,000 crore) adds to about ₹3,000–3,150 crore, which would be 65–70% of that pool: a top-three oligopoly (illustrative). On the managements' wider definitions, Sheela claims about 20% and Wakefit about 10% of the organised market, which leaves 70% to everyone else: a fragmented field. The truth sits between, because much of Sheela's revenue passes through small dealer counters that Redseer counts as unorganised. The practical conclusion is that a handful of national brands dominate brand awareness and advertising, while dozens of regional and marketplace labels compete hard on price in every city.

Where the new competition comes from

Wakefit's management said in May 2026 that competitive intensity had risen over the previous six to nine months, and named the source: marketplace private labels and regional brands with revenues of ₹100–300 crore and lower cost structures. These brands sell mostly on Amazon and Flipkart, spend heavily on platform advertising and price aggressively. They are why Wakefit's marketplace revenue fell 15–20% year on year for two quarters before recovering 7.6% in Q1 FY27, and why Wakefit said it would accept a 1–2 point hit to EBITDA margin to keep growing at 20% or more. Sheela faces the same labels in e-commerce and the unorganised sector in towns.

No foreign mattress makers, and few imports

Global bedding companies such as Somnigroup (formerly Tempur Sealy), Serta Simmons and Emma have no meaningful Indian manufacturing. Mattresses are bulky, light and expensive to ship, so finished-mattress imports are small, and the market is defended by local brands. Furniture is different: India imports flat-pack and finished furniture from China and South-East Asia, and IKEA sources globally. Customs data on finished furniture and bedding (HS 9403 and 9404) could not be compiled for this report. Where China matters most is one step up the chain, in TDI and polyol (section 06).

Ownership tells the same story of a young, contested industry. Duroflex is controlled by its founding family with a private-equity investor selling down in the IPO. The Sleep Company and Sleepycat are venture-funded. Wakefit's pre-IPO register was dominated by Peak XV, Verlinvest, Investcorp, SAI Global India Fund I and Elevation Capital, several of which sold in the IPO. Sheela's promoters, the Gautam family, hold 65.39%. Only one large player, Sheela, has been profitable for decades, and it is the one that owns the foam.

Who wins the convergence?

The two routes are converging on a common omnichannel model: a website, a ring of company showrooms in the large cities for premium products and brand-building, and a wide partner network (dealers or MBOs) for reach in small towns. Somnigroup's purchase of Mattress Firm in the US in February 2025 is the global version of the same move (section 18). The question for investors is who builds the cheaper version. Sheela starts with reach, plants near every market and a balance sheet that needs no rent; its challenge is to make company stores and e-commerce pay without upsetting dealers who fear being bypassed. Wakefit starts with customer data, near-zero working capital and a higher gross margin; its challenge is that each new store adds a fixed rent that must be covered in a market growing only about 5% in volume. Other categories where direct brands attacked dealer-built incumbents offer useful parallels (see luggage), as do the store economics of other specialist chains (see speciality retail).

A practical tell is the advertising line. Wakefit's advertising and promotion rose from about 5.7% of revenue in FY26 to 7.6% in Q1 FY27, and it guides 7–8% for FY27, partly to counter newly funded competitors. Sheela spends 5.2% (the annual report's definition, including selling and promotion; the CFO's narrower "advertising" figure is about 4.5%). Whichever company lowers its blended cost of acquiring a customer, across stores, online and partners, will take share; the other will have to spend more to stand still.

Five decades, four shocks: how the industry got here

Coir brands, then foam, then the D2C insurgency, then consolidation; each phase ended with a raw-material or demand shock.

The Indian mattress market went through four phases, and each left a mark on today's structure. Until the 1970s the market was almost entirely cotton and coir. Kurl-on, founded in Manipal in 1962, and Duroflex, founded in Alappuzha in 1963, built regional brands on rubberised coir, mostly in the south. Sheela Foam began producing polyurethane foam at Sahibabad in Uttar Pradesh in 1971 and turned Sleepwell into the country's best-known foam mattress brand, becoming the largest slabstock foam maker in the process. Foam's advantages (consistent comfort, lighter weight, lower cost at scale) slowly pushed coir into a niche.

The second phase was internationalisation and listing. Sheela bought Joyce Foam in Australia in 2005 and listed in December 2016, when its promoters sold 14.32%. It bought Interplasp in Spain in October 2019. The third phase was the direct-to-consumer insurgency. Wakefit was founded in Bengaluru in 2016 and was the first organised player to offer a 100-night free trial; The Sleep Company (2019), Sleepycat and Duroflex's Sleepyhead followed. All used roll-pack mattresses sold online, and COVID-19 accelerated online home buying in 2020–21, the year Sheela's operating margin peaked at 15%.

The fourth phase began with a squeeze and ended in consolidation. TDI and polyol spiked after the pandemic, and Sheela's margin fell from 15% in FY21 to 11% in FY22; Wakefit lost ₹107 crore in FY22 and ₹146 crore in FY23. Sheela responded by buying scale: a ₹1,200 crore QIP at ₹1,078 a share in September 2023, 17.70% of Furlenco in August 2023, and 94.67% of Kurlon for ₹1,940.78 crore from 20 October 2023. FY25 was the integration trough (margin 7%, profit ₹90 crore). FY26 brought recovery for both: Sheela's core margin rose to 10.8%, Wakefit turned profitable and listed, and input prices fell. Then the West Asia war of February 2026 opened a fifth chapter whose outcome is not yet known.

In plain terms

Mattress makers earn a spread between what they pay for chemicals and what they charge for mattresses, and they reprice with a lag of about a quarter. When chemicals get more expensive, margins shrink for a few months; when chemicals get cheaper, margins widen. Every big swing in this industry's profits since 2020 has followed that rhythm, with one acquisition (Kurlon) and one IPO (Wakefit) layered on top.

The cycle has a clear signature. These are spread businesses with roughly a one-quarter pricing lag, so margins compress when inputs rise and expand when they fall. Inventory adds a twist: a company that holds more stock gains on the way up (it sells old, cheap stock at new, higher prices) and loses on the way down. Sheela's Australian and Spanish units, which carry longer inventory, gained in Q1 FY27 for exactly this reason, with EBITDA margins of 12.8% and 14.7%, while its Indian business lost. Demand shocks matter less than input shocks: even in the worst year, Sheela's revenue did not fall. The listed companies' combined operating margin swung from 5.8% in FY23 to 10.9% in FY26 on revenue growth that never exceeded 19% a year, which shows how much of the profit cycle is cost rather than volume.

Reading a mattress company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain a mattress company's results; half of them are defined differently by the two listed members.

A mattress company's results can look simple: revenue up, margin up or down. Underneath, three separate engines are running. A volume engine (how many mattresses and tonnes of foam were sold), a price engine (how much each one fetched, which mostly tracks chemical costs with a lag) and a channel engine (how much it cost to reach the customer, through a dealer's margin or through rent and advertising). This section takes each key performance indicator in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, what good looks like, how Sheela and Wakefit compare, and the trap hidden in its definition. Benchmarks are indicative ranges built from the two companies, their unlisted peers and global comparables, not industry standards.

The equation, and which terms management controls

Every mattress company's profit can be written as one chain. Revenue is units sold multiplied by realisation per unit. Gross profit is revenue minus materials, and materials are dominated by the kilos of foam in each product multiplied by the price of TDI and polyol. Operating profit (EBITDA) is gross profit minus the cost of reaching the customer, minus people and overheads. The flow below lays it out and marks which terms are in management's hands.

Management controls the price list, the channel and the cost base. It does not control the chemical price or market volume. The pricing lag between the two is what moves quarterly margins.

A worked example with round numbers shows how the chain behaves (illustrative). A company sells 10 lakh mattresses at an average realisation of ₹4,000, for revenue of ₹400 crore. Materials take 57%, or ₹228 crore, of which TDI and polyol are about two-thirds, ₹150 crore. Gross profit is ₹172 crore. Advertising and promotion at 5% (₹20 crore), freight at 5.5% (₹22 crore), employees at 12% (₹48 crore) and other overheads at 10% (₹40 crore) leave EBITDA of ₹42 crore, a 10.5% margin. Now let TDI and polyol rise 10%. Materials rise by ₹15 crore and, if prices are not raised, EBITDA falls to ₹27 crore, a 6.8% margin: a 10% chemical rise costs more than a third of operating profit. To hold EBITDA in rupees the company must raise its realisation by about 3.75% (₹15 crore over ₹400 crore). To hold the margin percentage it must raise prices a little more. That single calculation is the most important one in this sub-sector.

In plain terms

Because chemicals are such a large part of the cost, a small change in their price is a big change in profit. A 10% rise in chemical prices needs only a 4% price rise to neutralise, but if the company waits a quarter to raise prices, it loses a third of its profit for that quarter. Margins in this industry are made or lost in the timing.

KPI 1: mattress volume growth versus value growth

What it is. The year-on-year change in mattress units sold, set beside the change in mattress revenue. Formula. Volume growth = units this period ÷ units in the same period last year − 1; value growth uses revenue. The gap between the two is price and mix. Worked example. Sheela sold 859,000 mattresses in Q1 FY27 against 813,000 a year earlier (+6%) for ₹369 crore against ₹320 crore (+15%); the nine-point gap is price increases plus some premiumisation. Drivers. Volume rises with formalisation, distribution additions (new dealers, stores, MBOs), festive timing and competitors' supply problems; it falls when prices rise sharply (Sheela's CFO said double-digit volume growth would have been possible in Q1 FY27 without the price increases) and when marketplace labels discount. Benchmarks (indicative): good above 12% a year, average 6–12%, weak below 6%, against an industry growing about 5% in volume (Sheela's chairman, Q1 FY27). Comparison. Sheela's mattress volume rose 12% in FY26 and 6% in Q1 FY27; foam volume rose 18% and 4%. Wakefit does not disclose units at all; its management says volume growth "is not a KPI" for it, and that its 27.3% mattress revenue growth in Q1 FY27 was about two-thirds volume and one-third price. Definition trap. Sheela's units are India-only (standalone) and include very cheap U2O mattresses; a jump in U2O volume can lift unit growth while lowering average realisation, so volume growth alone can overstate the health of the brand.

KPI 2: realisation per mattress

What it is. Average revenue per mattress sold. Formula. Mattress revenue ÷ mattress units. Worked example. Sheela's FY26 mattress revenue of ₹1,497 crore over 3.692 million units gives about ₹4,055 per mattress; Q1 FY27's ₹369 crore over 859,000 units gives about ₹4,296, up 9% from ₹3,936 a year earlier (indicative arithmetic). Drivers. List-price changes (mostly chemical pass-through), product mix (premium ranges, spring and latex lift it; U2O and coir lower it), channel (an online sale at full retail price versus a dealer invoice) and the festive calendar (discounts lower it in Q3). Benchmarks. There is no single good number, because realisation reflects the target customer; the useful test is whether realisation is rising faster than inflation in a stable-cost year, which indicates premiumisation. Comparison. Sheela's realisation dipped in Q3 FY26 (₹3,927) on festive promotions and U2O mix and peaked in Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27 (₹4,300-plus) on price increases. Wakefit does not disclose units, but at an assumed average price of ₹8,000–12,000 its ₹914 crore of mattress revenue would be about 0.8–1.1 million mattresses (illustrative and flagged). Definition trap. Sheela's realisation is an ex-factory price to a dealer, net of GST and trade schemes; Wakefit's revenue is at retail price net of GST. A Sleepwell mattress with a ₹10,000 shelf price may show as about ₹6,500 of Sheela revenue and the same-priced Wakefit as about ₹8,500 (section 09). Never compare the two companies' realisations directly.

KPI 3: foam tonnage and capacity utilisation

What it is. Tonnes of foam sold to outside customers, and foam produced as a share of capacity. Formula. Utilisation = tonnes produced ÷ annual capacity. Worked example. Sheela sold 54,088 tonnes of foam in FY26 (technical 20,840; comfort 27,603; furniture cushioning 5,645), up 18%, and said it produced "over one lakh tonnes" in total, including foam used in its own mattresses. Against 1,39,000 tonnes of capacity that is roughly 72% utilisation (indicative, because the exact production figure is not disclosed). Drivers. Industrial demand (cars, footwear, furniture), the closure of weaker foamers in a chemical shock (Sheela's Q4 FY26 foam volume rose 34% as some small makers shut), and in-house mattress demand. Benchmarks (indicative): above 80% utilisation is good, 65–80% average, below 65% weak, for a slabstock line that runs well only when it runs continuously. Comparison. Only Sheela reports foam; Wakefit buys foam and converts it, so the metric does not apply. Definition trap. Foam tonnage includes three products with different prices and margins: technical foam (₹279 a kilo in FY26), comfort foam (₹204) and furniture cushioning (₹370), all indicative arithmetic. A mix shift towards comfort foam lowers average realisation without any change in price. Foam is also the faster pass-through business: when chemicals rise, foam revenue jumps first, flattering "growth".

In plain terms

Half of Sheela's India business is selling foam to other manufacturers, and that business behaves like a chemical company: its revenue goes up and down with chemical prices within weeks. When Sheela's foam revenue rose 26% in Q1 FY27 on 4% more tonnes, that was not a boom. It was the war showing up in the invoice.

KPI 4: gross margin and the chemical price

What it is. The share of revenue left after materials. Formula. (Revenue − materials consumed − purchases of traded goods ± inventory change) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Wakefit's Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹404.9 crore less cost of goods of ₹173.8 crore gives gross profit of ₹231.1 crore, a 57.1% margin. Sheela's consolidated gross margin was 40.6% in the same quarter. Drivers. TDI and polyol prices (the dominant factor), the lag before list prices change (10–15 days to implement, but competitors move in steps), inventory held (expensive stock sold into falling prices hurts), mix (mattresses carry higher margins than foam; at Wakefit, mattresses rank above furnishings, and furniture is lowest), and festive discounting. Benchmarks (indicative): for a dealer-model foam and mattress company, above 44% good, 40–44% average, below 40% weak; for a direct model, above 57% good, 52–57% average, below 52% weak. Comparison. Sheela's consolidated gross margin has ranged between 40.6% and 44.9% over eight quarters, dipping in Q3 FY25, Q3 FY26 and Q1 FY27; Wakefit's has ranged between 51.5% and about 57.7%, dipping in each festive quarter. Definition trap. Sheela reports gross margin on both consolidated and standalone bases, and they diverge sharply in a shock: in Q1 FY27 standalone (India) fell 688 basis points to 37.6% while consolidated fell 405 basis points, because Australia and Spain gained from cheaper inventory. Always ask which one is quoted.

KPI 5: advertising and promotion as a % of revenue

What it is. Spending on advertising, sales promotion and (in some definitions) dealer schemes, as a share of revenue. Formula. (Advertising + selling and promotion) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Sheela's FY26 annual report shows advertisement of ₹117.4 crore and selling and promotion of ₹82.1 crore, ₹199.5 crore in all, or 5.2% of ₹3,821 crore. Drivers. Competitive intensity (newly funded rivals raise everyone's spend; Wakefit's CEO calls it an arms race), the festive season (Q3 is the heaviest quarter), new launches, store openings and the mix between brand advertising and performance marketing on marketplaces. Benchmarks (indicative): 4–6% for an incumbent brand, 6–8% for a direct-to-consumer brand still building awareness; above 10% signals a customer-acquisition war. Comparison. Sheela 5.7% in FY25 and 5.2% in FY26. Sheela's CFO cites "around 4.5%" for FY26, a narrower advertising-only definition; the annual report's 5.2% includes selling and promotion. Wakefit's figure includes performance marketing on marketplaces, which a dealer brand books partly as trade discounts. KPI 6: employee cost as a % of revenue

What it is. Salaries, wages, benefits and share-based pay as a share of revenue. Formula. Employee benefit expense ÷ revenue. Worked example. Sheela: ₹495.1 crore ÷ ₹3,821 crore = 13.0% in FY26 (12.6% in FY25). Wakefit: ₹166.2 crore ÷ ₹1,489 crore = 11.2% (11.7% in Q1 FY27). Drivers. Factory automation, store staffing (each new store adds salespeople before it adds sales), overseas wages (Sheela's Australian and Spanish units), incentive provisions (Sheela's Q4 FY26 employee cost spiked as incentives were trued up) and stock options (Wakefit guides ₹10–12 crore of ESOP cost in FY27, against ₹0.6 crore in Q1, as senior hires join in H2). Benchmarks (indicative): below 11% good, 11–13% average, above 13% weak. Comparison. Wakefit is leaner today, but its ratio will rise with 80 stores a year and new senior hires; Sheela's ratio carries its overseas business and should fall as Kurlon's duplicated overheads go (operating units have already been cut from 21 to 12). Definition trap. Wakefit's company-defined "operating EBITDA" adds back ESOP cost, so a rising ESOP charge lowers reported EBITDA but not operating EBITDA. Wakefit also counts 2,178 permanent employees (March 2026); store staff hired through agencies may sit in other expenses.

KPI 7: EBITDA margin, reported versus pre-lease

What it is. Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, as a share of revenue. The trap is that there are several versions. Formula. Reported EBITDA (Ind AS) = revenue − materials − employees − other expenses, with lease rent excluded because Ind AS 116 moves it below the line into depreciation and interest. Pre-lease ("operating") EBITDA deducts the cash rent. Worked example. Wakefit's FY26 reported EBITDA was ₹182.0 crore (12.2%). Deduct rent of ₹80.4 crore and add back ESOP cost (₹3.3 crore), other non-operating items (₹6.4 crore) and IPO costs (₹1.0 crore), and the company's own "operating EBITDA" is ₹112.3 crore, 7.5%. In Q1 FY27 the two were ₹56.4 crore (13.9%) and ₹36.8 crore (9.1%). Drivers. Gross margin, A&P, store openings (new stores carry full rent before full sales), operating leverage on head-office costs (corporate overhead is 7–8% of revenue, according to Wakefit). Benchmarks (indicative, pre-lease): above 12% good, 8–12% average, below 8% weak. Comparison. Sheela's core EBITDA was 10.8% in FY26 and 10.6% in Q1 FY27; its rent is small because dealers pay for shops, so its reported and pre-lease margins are almost the same. Wakefit's pre-lease margin was 7.5% in FY26 and 9.1% in Q1 FY27, and it guides about 7.5% for FY27. Definition trap. Wakefit has three EBITDA figures (reported ₹182.0 crore; reported including other income ₹227.4 crore; operating ₹112.3 crore), Sheela has two (core ₹414 crore before a forex mark-to-market loss; reported ₹393 crore), and screener shows a fourth (₹186 crore and ₹395 crore). From Q1 FY27 Sheela closed its hedge, so core equals reported.

KPI 8: store count, revenue per store and payback

What it is. The number of company-owned stores, the sales each generates, and how long each takes to earn back its investment. Formula. Revenue per store = store revenue ÷ average stores open; payback = initial investment (fit-out capex + display inventory + deposits) ÷ annual store-level cash profit. Worked example. Wakefit's offline revenue in Q1 FY27 was about 47.3% of ₹404.9 crore, or ₹191.5 crore, but that includes MBOs. Dividing by about 151 average stores gives at most ₹1.27 crore a quarter per store, an upper bound because MBO sales are mixed in (illustrative). Drivers. Location, format (mega furniture stores versus mini mattress stores), age (new stores ramp up over 12–18 months), the share of premium products, and cannibalisation of the company's own website. Benchmarks (indicative): payback under 12 months good, 12–24 months average, over 24 months weak. Comparison. Wakefit had 165 COCO stores in June 2026 and reports payback of about 10–11 months (management), a 2.7–3× rise in a town's monthly sales after a store opens, and display inventory of ₹4–5 lakh a store. Sheela has about 42 COCO stores costing about ₹50 lakh each including working capital; it does not disclose their economics. Definition trap. Sheela's "brand outlets" (3,500–5,500 depending on the source) are dealer-owned shops whose rent Sheela does not pay; Wakefit's "stores" are company-run with rent on its own books. Wakefit's payback is management's figure, on a definition that is not published; it is quoted for the newer formats and may exclude central costs.

KPI 9: channel mix and repeat customers

What it is. The share of revenue sold through each route to market, and the share coming from repeat buyers. Formula. Channel revenue ÷ total revenue; repeat revenue = revenue from customers who bought before ÷ total. Worked example. In Q1 FY27 Wakefit's own channels (website plus stores) were 72.3% of revenue, about ₹293 crore of ₹404.9 crore, and grew 20.5%; external channels (marketplaces plus MBOs) were 27.7% and grew 7.6% after falling 15–20% in the two previous quarters. Repeat customers produced 36.7% of revenue (35.3% in FY26). Drivers. Store openings shift sales to own channels; marketplace sale events pull them back; cross-selling furniture and furnishings to past mattress buyers lifts repeat revenue (in FY26, 24.2% of furnishings buyers and 18.4% of furniture buyers were existing customers). Benchmarks (indicative): own-channel share above 65% good, 55–65% average, below 55% weak for a direct brand; repeat revenue above 35% good. Comparison. Sheela does not disclose a channel split beyond "83% of standalone sales to dealers and distributors" and growth rates for e-commerce (value +52% in FY26) and its website (+136%). Definition trap. Wakefit publishes two overlapping splits: own versus external (72.3% / 27.7%) and online versus offline (52.7% / 47.3%). The website is in "own" and "online"; marketplaces are in "external" and "online"; stores are in "own" and "offline"; MBOs are in "external" and "offline". Adding the two splits is meaningless.

In plain terms

For a direct brand, every sale through its own website or store keeps the margin a marketplace would have taken, and gives the brand the customer's phone number for next time. That is why Wakefit tracks repeat buyers so closely: a customer who comes back for a bedsheet or a wardrobe costs almost nothing to win.

KPI 10: inventory days and working capital

What it is. How many days of sales are tied up in stock, receivables and payables, and the net of the three. Formula. Inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods × 365 (screener uses revenue in some versions); net working capital days = inventory days + receivable days − payable days. Worked example. Wakefit in FY26: inventory 109 days, receivables about 1 day, payables 108 days, so net working capital is about 2 days (the company reports 2.2 days). Sheela: inventory 69 days, receivables 42 days, payables 80 days, so about 31 days. Drivers. Channel (dealers get credit; households pay up front), product range (furniture needs more stock than mattresses), raw-material buying (Sheela holds 15–30 days of chemicals in India and more overseas; Wakefit held 3–4 weeks in mid-2026, bought below spot), and supplier terms. Benchmarks (indicative): net working capital under 10 days is good for a direct model and under 35 days for a dealer model; 35–50 days average; above 60 days weak. Inventory under 60 days is good, 60–90 average, above 90 weak. Comparison. Wakefit's inventory is high, but suppliers fund it; Sheela's is lower, but dealer credit ties up six weeks of sales. Definition trap. Inventory days computed on revenue (screener's convention for some lines) and on cost of goods give very different answers for a 56%-gross-margin business; Wakefit's 109 days on cost of goods would be about 48 days on revenue. Also, inventory in a shock is a two-edged sword: more stock helps when prices are rising and hurts when they fall.

KPI 11: return on capital employed, reported and "cash"

What it is. Operating profit as a return on the capital tied up in the business. Formula. ROCE = EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), averaged over the year. Sheela also publishes a "cash ROCE" that excludes acquired goodwill and brands from capital employed. Worked example. Drivers. Margin, asset turns, acquisitions (goodwill dilutes ROCE for years), idle cash (lowers ROCE when held in capital employed) and depreciation policy. Benchmarks (indicative, reported): above 20% good, 12–20% average, below 12% weak. Comparison. Sheela's reported ROCE fell from 18% in FY22 to 4% in FY25 as the Kurlon purchase added goodwill; management targets 20–25% within about three years. Wakefit's screener ROCE is 10.9%, depressed by ₹958.6 crore of investable cash in the capital base and lifted by treasury income in the profit line, so it says little about the operating business. Definition trap. Cash ROCE, cash ROE and "cash PAT" (Sheela's ₹384 crore = profit + deferred tax + depreciation) are management constructs that flatter acquisitive companies. KPI 12: net debt, net cash and leases

What it is. Borrowings minus cash and liquid investments; for a store-led company, lease liabilities are a separate, debt-like obligation. Formula. Net debt = gross borrowings − cash − liquid investments; leverage = net debt ÷ EBITDA. Worked example. Sheela's net debt was ₹543 crore at March 2026, down ₹156 crore in the year, or 1.3 times EBITDA; in August 2026 its CFO put India debt at "a little above ₹300 crore" and overseas debt at about ₹350 crore. Wakefit had no borrowings, lease liabilities of ₹271.9 crore and "investable cash" of ₹958.6 crore, so net cash of about ₹959 crore excluding leases. Drivers. Free cash flow (Sheela guides ₹150–200 crore in FY27, all to debt), acquisitions, IPO proceeds and store leases. Benchmarks (indicative): net debt below 0.5× EBITDA good, 0.5–1.5× average, above 2× weak. Comparison. On this report's consistent basis (leases excluded on both sides), the two companies together hold net cash of about ₹416 crore. Definition trap. Screener's "borrowings" for Sheela (₹908 crore) include lease liabilities, and its "investments" (₹588 crore) include the strategic Furlenco stake, so a screener-derived net debt (about ₹320 crore) understates the true figure. For Wakefit, only ₹323 crore of its cash sits in lines labelled cash, bank balances and current investments; the rest is in fixed deposits booked as "other financial assets". Use the company's ₹958.6 crore.

In plain terms

Sheela borrowed to buy Kurlon and is paying it back from cash flow, about ₹150–200 crore a year. Wakefit raised money in its IPO and has not yet spent most of it, so it has nearly ₹1,000 crore in the bank. Combined, the listed sub-sector has more cash than debt. But Wakefit's rent contracts (₹272 crore of lease liabilities) are a form of debt too, and they grow with every store.

KPI 14: net working capital and cash conversion, the quality check

The last KPI is a cross-check on all the others: how much of operating profit turns into operating cash flow. Formula. Cash conversion = cash flow from operations ÷ EBITDA. Worked example. How to read a mattress company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Gross margin first, and both bases for Sheela. Compare with the same quarter last year and with the previous quarter. A fall of more than 200 basis points without a festive explanation means the chemical pass-through is lagging. For Sheela, check standalone (India) as well as consolidated; for Wakefit, check against its guide (Q2 FY27 about 100 basis points below Q1's 57.1%).
  2. Volume against value. For Sheela, read mattress units and foam tonnes against their revenue. Value far ahead of volume means price-led growth (chemicals); volume ahead of value means price cuts or mix down-trading (U2O). For Wakefit, listen for management's split of mattress growth into price and volume.
  3. The chemical price backdrop. Note whether TDI and polyol rose or fell during the quarter and in the month after it. A rise late in the quarter will hit the next one.
  4. Operating margin on the right basis. Sheela: core EBITDA (now equal to reported). Wakefit: pre-lease operating EBITDA, not the reported figure. Compare with guidance (Sheela 11–12% for FY27; Wakefit about 7.5%).
  5. A&P and employee cost. A jump in A&P above 8% (Wakefit) or 6% (Sheela) signals a competitive fight; a jump in employee cost may be ESOPs or incentive true-ups, so check the notes.
  6. Distribution additions. Wakefit: stores added and closed, cities, MBOs, own-channel share and repeat revenue. Sheela: COCO stores against the target of 50, U2O dealers, e-commerce growth.
  7. Below the line. Strip out exceptional items (Sheela booked gains in Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27), the Furlenco profit share (and whether it includes a tax credit) and, for Wakefit, deferred-tax movements and treasury income on IPO cash (other income was about 39% of Q1 FY27 profit before tax).
  8. Debt and cash. Sheela: net debt against the plan to retire India debt in about a year, and the Kurlon NCD repayment. Wakefit: investable cash and the use of IPO proceeds (marketing, rent, stores).
  9. Segments and subsidiaries. Sheela: Australia and Spain margins (inflated by inventory gains in Q1 FY27, expected to normalise) and Furlenco's revenue growth. Wakefit: furniture growth against the mid-to-late-teens guide.
  10. Guidance changes and red flags. Listen for changes to margin guidance, capex (Wakefit cut its FY27 range from ₹120–140 crore to ₹100–120 crore in August), store-closure counts, dealer stress, warranty claims and any change in how a KPI is defined. A KPI that quietly disappears from the presentation is itself a red flag.

Kurlon, deleveraging and the 15% promise Deep dive

Sheela bought its largest rival for ₹1,941 crore, took a two-year margin hit, and now promises a margin it has reached only once.

Sheela Foam's last three years are a single story: buy the number-two brand, absorb it, pay down the debt, and restore margins. Each step is now partly complete, and each has a number the market will test in the next two quarters.

The deal

Sheela signed a business-transfer agreement for Kurlon Enterprise on 17 July 2023 and took control on 20 October 2023, buying 94.67% (3,46,05,369 shares) for ₹1,940.78 crore: ₹1,869.78 crore in cash and a ₹71 crore holdback. It later bought another 2.76% for ₹59.25 crore, taking its holding to 97.43% by March 2024, and in FY26 the purchase consideration was remeasured up by ₹30 crore. All in, Sheela paid about ₹2,030 crore for 97.43% (indicative sum). To fund it, Sheela raised ₹1,200 crore through a QIP on 26 September 2023, issuing 1,11,31,725 shares at ₹1,078, and borrowed the rest; screener-basis borrowings (including leases) rose from ₹571 crore in FY23 to ₹1,493 crore in FY24. In the same quarter it bought 17.70% of Furlenco. On the balance sheet, the Kurlon purchase created goodwill of ₹406.80 crore and acquired intangibles of ₹1,413.10 crore, which is why consolidated goodwill and brands now total about ₹1,612 crore.

What did Sheela buy? Kurlon in its last full year as a separate company (FY25) had total income of ₹665.5 crore, a pre-tax loss of ₹40.7 crore and, after a tax credit, a net loss of ₹5.0 crore. The 15% promise

Management's targets are explicit: revenue growth above 15% and an EBITDA margin of 11–12% in FY27; about 15% growth and a 15% margin in FY28; and return on capital employed of 20–25% within about three years (against about 10% now). The global lens: vertical integration wins, store-heavy D2C struggles

America's bedding leader bought its biggest retailer; its best-known store-based brand shrank. India's two models face the same fork.

The global mattress industry offers two clean case studies for the Indian convergence. Somnigroup (formerly Tempur Sealy) is the US and global leader, a manufacturer of Tempur, Sealy and Stearns & Foster that bought Mattress Firm, the largest US mattress retailer, in February 2025. Its 2025 net sales were US$7,476.5 million, up 51.6% with the acquisition, with a 42.6% gross margin, adjusted EBITDA of US$1,306 million (17.5%) and net income of US$384.1 million; leverage was 3.2 times. Mattress Firm alone contributed US$3,505 million at a 33.4% gross margin. Somnigroup is the US version of a manufacturer moving into owned retail, the path Sheela is taking with company stores and Wakefit has taken from the start.

Sleep Number is the cautionary tale. It sells its own "smart beds" exclusively through about 600 company stores and online, with a 59.0% gross margin, higher than Wakefit's. In 2025 its sales fell 16% to US$1.4 billion, adjusted EBITDA was only US$78 million (5.5%), it lost US$132 million and it announced US$185 million of annualised cost cuts. A high gross margin and a fleet of owned stores produce a thin operating margin when store traffic falls, because rent and staff do not fall with it. The lesson for Wakefit is the store-economics break-even of section 11, played out at scale.

Technology and disruption: the box, the chemistry and the algorithm

The roll-pack mattress broke the dealer monopoly; the next disruptions are in materials, automation and the platforms that now sell a third of online demand.

The most important technology shift in this industry was not digital. It was compression: high-density foam that can be squeezed, rolled and vacuum-packed into a box and still recover its shape at the customer's home. That single change turned a mattress into a parcel, let it travel by courier and made online selling possible, which is how Wakefit, The Sleep Company and Sleepycat entered a market that dealers had controlled. Roll-pack is now table stakes; Sheela sells e-commerce-only roll-pack models under both its brands.

Sleep technology

Wakefit's "Zense" range includes a temperature-regulating mattress layer (Regul8) and a contactless sleep tracker (Track8); The Sleep Company markets its SmartGRID polymer lattice. These raise average selling prices and create talking points for stores. The global precedent is mixed: Sleep Number built its brand on "smart beds" and still saw sales fall 16% in 2025. Technology differentiates at the premium end; it has not protected margins anywhere.

Materials and chemistry

CRISIL lists substitutes for conventional polyurethane foam among the risks to Sheela: bio-based and soy polyols, polyolefin foams, natural latex and recycled polyol. None is yet large in India, but each could reduce dependence on TDI and polyol. Sheela has an R&D project on recycled polyol for footwear. In the opposite direction, Wakefit's management said in May 2026 that some unbranded foam makers add fillers such as limestone (chuna) to foam when chemical prices spike, which lowers quality and, over time, helps brands win customers.

Manufacturing automation

Sheela uses variable-pressure foaming to control density and reduce waste, and has moved to useful-life depreciation for foaming lines that last 40–50 years; one Greater Noida line has run for 22 years at more than 25,000 tonnes a year. Wakefit uses robotic arms, roller belts, CNC machines and an "Every Part Every Interval" production schedule, and allocates about 20% of its FY27 capex to automation. Kurlon's integration added new comfort-layer machines that should deliver the last ₹40 crore of synergies from Q3 FY27.

Channels and algorithms

Marketplaces and quick-commerce apps are now both channels and competitors: they run their own sale events, push their own labels and set the price of visibility. Wakefit's marketplace revenue fell 15–20% for two quarters, then grew 7.6% in Q1 FY27 as the platforms returned to growth mode. Sheela's own website grew 69% in Q1 FY27 against 19% on platforms. Sheela also ran AI-generated personalised advertisements for more than 2,000 dealers in Q1 FY27, a way of bringing digital marketing to a dealer network. For marketplace economics see e-retail.

In plain terms

Putting a mattress in a box let start-ups sell online and bypass the dealer. Today the bigger disruption risk comes from the shopping apps themselves, which decide whose mattress appears first and sell cheap labels of their own. Brands are responding by pushing customers to their own websites and stores, where they keep the margin and the customer's details.

Substitutes that have not worked

Mattress toppers (a thin foam layer placed on an old mattress) are the obvious cheap substitute, but Wakefit's CEO says they never gained traction because an opening-price single mattress costs only about ₹5,000. Furniture rental (Furlenco) is a different kind of substitute, replacing purchase with subscription for renters; it is growing fast, and Sheela owns part of it. The structural disruption to watch is therefore not a new product but a shift in who controls demand: from dealers to platforms, and from platforms to brands' own channels.

Bull versus bear: a formalising market against a volatile spread

The long-run case rests on conversion from cotton; the near-term case rests on chemistry and execution.

The case for the sub-sector and the case against it both rest on solid evidence. The bull case is structural and slow; the bear case is cyclical and immediate. Which one dominates over the next two years depends mostly on whether the 2026 chemical volatility fades and whether the store rollouts and the Kurlon integration deliver the margins management has promised.

The bull case

  • A long conversion runway. Cotton is still about 60% of mattress value and Redseer-organised only about 30%, projected at about 45% by CY30; each point of share is about ₹150 crore of revenue today (illustrative).
  • Chemical shocks speed up formalisation. Small foamers stop producing when TDI and polyol spike; Sheela gained foam customers in Q4 FY26 as some shut.
  • Margins have room to recover. Sheela's FY28 target of 15% needs about a two-point gross-margin recovery plus ₹40 crore of pending synergies; its FY21 peak shows the level is achievable.
  • Strong balance sheets. Combined net cash of ₹416 crore; Sheela's India debt is nearly gone, and Wakefit holds ₹958.6 crore.
  • Hidden value at Sheela. A Furlenco IPO or an international sale would crystallise assets not visible in the P&L.
  • De-rated valuations. Sheela at 16.4× EV to operating profit and Wakefit at 18.9× are well below their own recent levels.

The bear case

  • The spread can break again. TDI and polyol are 65–70% of Sheela's cost of goods; a 10% unrecovered rise costs it 3.7–3.9 points of margin (illustrative), and Q1 FY27 lost 405 basis points.
  • Slow volume. Industry volume grew about 5% in Q1 FY27; much of 2026's revenue growth was price, not demand.
  • Duties work against the members. The EU/Saudi TDI duty runs to 2031, and the China/Japan/Korea duty is under review for another five years.
  • Profit quality at Wakefit. Half of FY26 profit was a tax credit; treasury income is large; the pre-lease margin is 7.5% and guided flat; P/E excluding the credit is about 44×.
  • Competition from cheap labels. ₹100–300 crore marketplace and regional brands are raising advertising costs; Wakefit would accept a 1–2 point margin hit to keep growing.
  • Housing lag. Top-7-city sales fell 14% in CY25 and the repo was raised to 5.50% in October 2026, a headwind for furnishing demand around 2028–29.
In plain terms

The good news is that India will keep switching from cotton mattresses to branded foam ones for a decade or more. The bad news is that the profit on each foam mattress depends on chemical prices nobody in India controls, and that the market grows by only about 5% a year in units. Investors in this sub-sector are betting on the first while living with the second.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • A&P: advertising and promotion spending, as a % of revenue. Sheela's annual report figure includes selling and promotion.
  • ADD (anti-dumping duty): an extra customs duty on imports judged to be sold below fair value, imposed on DGTR's recommendation.
  • Appointed date: the date from which a merger takes accounting effect (1 Apr 2024 for Kurlon), which can be earlier than the effective date (31 Jan 2026).
  • Bed-in-a-box / roll-pack: a foam mattress compressed, rolled and vacuum-packed so a courier can deliver it.
  • Cash ROCE: Sheela's return on capital employed excluding acquired goodwill and brands from the denominator.
  • COCO store: company-owned, company-operated store; rent and staff sit on the company's own P&L.
  • Core EBITDA: Sheela's EBITDA before forex mark-to-market on hedges; equal to reported from Q1 FY27.
  • DGTR: Directorate General of Trade Remedies, which investigates dumping and recommends duties.
  • DTA (deferred-tax asset): future tax savings from past losses; recognising one creates a one-time accounting credit (Wakefit, ₹98 Cr in FY26).
  • Exclusive brand outlet: a dealer-owned shop selling only one company's brands; Sheela counts about 3,500–5,500 depending on definition.
  • Gross margin: revenue minus materials and traded goods, as a % of revenue.
  • HR foam / memory foam: high-resilience foam (springier, more durable) and viscoelastic foam that moulds to the body.
  • Ind AS 116: the lease accounting standard that moves rent out of operating costs into depreciation and interest, lifting reported EBITDA.
  • Jumbo store: Wakefit's planned very large showrooms in Bengaluru (mid-2027) to sell full-home furniture assortments.
  • Lock-in: a period after an IPO during which certain shareholders cannot sell.
  • Masstige: the ₹8,000–20,000 mattress price band between mass and premium.
  • MBO: multi-brand outlet, a third-party shop stocking several brands; Wakefit sells to about 2,250 on cash-and-carry terms.
  • MDI: methylene diphenyl di-isocyanate, an isocyanate used mainly for rigid and some high-resilience foams.
  • NWC days: inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days.
  • OFS: offer for sale, the part of an IPO in which existing shareholders sell shares (₹912 Cr of Wakefit's ₹1,289 Cr).
  • Operating (pre-lease) EBITDA: EBITDA with cash rent deducted and ESOP and one-offs added back; Wakefit's preferred measure.
  • Organised (Redseer): online sales plus large-format branded stores of at least 2,500 sq ft; a channel definition, not a brand one.
  • Polyol (polyether polyol): the larger chemical input to flexible foam by weight, made from propylene oxide.
  • QCO: Quality Control Order requiring BIS certification; the polyurethanes QCO was revoked on 12 Nov 2025.
  • QIP: qualified institutions placement, a fast share sale to institutions (Sheela, ₹1,200 Cr at ₹1,078, Sep-2023).
  • Realisation: revenue per unit sold (per mattress, or per kilo of foam).
  • Slabstock foam: foam produced as a continuous block on a moving line, then cut into sheets and shapes.
  • Sunset review: DGTR's review, near the end of a five-year duty, of whether to extend it.
  • TDI (toluene di-isocyanate): the isocyanate that reacts with polyol to make flexible foam; 80:20 is the isomer grade used for mattresses.
  • U2O: Sheela's "unorganised to organised" programme selling low-priced foam mattresses through about 10,000 small dealers.
  • VPF: variable-pressure foaming, a process that controls foam density and reduces waste.
