Solar cells, wind turbines, transformers and Kavach: a policy rent that keeps moving

A complete guide to India's listed "Other Electrical Equipment" companies, which are really four businesses sharing one exchange label: solar module and cell makers, wind-turbine builders, grid and industrial electricals, and a battery-and-railway-safety specialist. It explains how each one makes money, why solar margins depend on where a company sits relative to the government's moving list of protected products, why the market pays 99 times earnings for transformers and 20 times for solar, and what to watch as cell capacity floods in, the US closes its door and the tender pipeline thins.

00

The one-page view: four businesses wearing one label

The exchange calls them all "other electrical equipment". The market prices them as four different industries, and it is right to.

"Other Electrical Equipment" is a filing cabinet, not an industry. The thirteen companies in this NSE 750 bucket make solar cells and modules, wind turbines, power transformers and motors, nickel-cadmium batteries and the on-board computers that stop Indian trains colliding. They share a classification code and a customer base that ultimately runs back to the electricity system, but they do not share a demand driver, a cost structure, a cycle or a valuation. The only sensible way to read them is to sort them first. This report sorts them into four archetypes and builds everything else around that map.

  • Solar manufacturing (7 companies): Waaree Energies, Premier Energies, Emmvee Photovoltaic, Vikram Solar, Saatvik Green Energy, Websol Energy System and Fujiyama Power Systems (UTL Solar). Together they booked ₹52,391 crore of revenue in FY26, 51% of the group, at a 23.7% operating margin.
  • Wind (3): the turbine makers Suzlon Energy and Inox Wind, plus the operations-and-maintenance company Inox Green Energy Services. Revenue ₹21,410 crore, margin 18.4%.
  • Industrial electricals (2): ABB India and CG Power and Industrial Solutions, which make switchgear, transformers, motors, drives and automation. Revenue ₹25,621 crore, margin 14.4%.
  • Batteries and Kavach (1): HBL Engineering, a battery maker whose profits in FY26 came mostly from Kavach, Indian Railways' automatic train-protection system. Revenue ₹3,303 crore, margin 33.7%.

The first striking fact is the valuation split. The two industrial-electrical franchises are worth ₹2.83 lakh crore, 53% of the group's market value, on a quarter of its revenue and less than a fifth of its profit. They trade at about 99 times latest-year earnings. The seven solar makers earn more than half the group's profit and trade at about 20 times. The wind trio trades at about 19 times. In plain arithmetic, the market will pay roughly five times as much for a rupee of ABB or CG profit as for a rupee of Waaree or Suzlon profit, even though the solar and wind companies report higher returns on capital (Waaree 38%, Emmvee 45%, Suzlon 34%, against ABB 30% and CG 27%).

The reason is durability. The market treats the solar margin as a policy rent: a temporary premium created by government rules that restrict which modules and cells can be used, and which will fade as capacity catches up. It treats wind as a cyclical, working-capital-heavy business that has gone bust before. It treats ABB and CG as compounders riding a multi-year build-out of transmission lines, data centres and factories. Whether those judgements are right is the central question of this report.

The second striking fact is that the most interesting economics sit inside the solar archetype, and they are not uniform. In the June 2026 quarter (Q1 FY27), solar makers that own cell factories held their margins: Emmvee earned 35%, Websol 33.7%, Premier 29%. Makers that only assemble modules from bought-in cells collapsed: Vikram Solar fell to 8.1% and Saatvik Green to 6.6%, with Saatvik's revenue down 44%. The dividing line is a rule called ALMM List-II, which since 1 June 2026 requires most government-linked solar projects to use Indian-made cells. India has about 36 GW of cell capacity against roughly 229 GW of enlisted module capacity, so a compliant cell is scarce and earns a large premium. A temporary relief for non-domestic-content projects runs to 31 December 2026, which leaves module-only makers facing a cliff in January 2027.

In plain terms

India has built far more factories that assemble solar panels than it can use, but far fewer factories that make the solar cells inside those panels. The government now insists that many projects use Indian cells. So in 2026 the money is made by whoever owns a cell factory, and the panel assemblers without one are fighting a price war. That will last only until the cell factories now under construction come online, probably in 2027–28, and then the government's next rule, on wafers, will move the scarcity one step further up the chain.

The report is organised around five forces that explain almost everything in these companies' numbers:

  • Force 1, policy-made scarcity that moves up the chain. The Approved List of Models and Manufacturers (ALMM) protected modules from 2021, cells from June 2026 and will protect wafers from June 2028. Each new list creates a two-to-three-year window of supernormal margins at the newly protected step, followed by a capacity glut.
  • Force 2, module overcapacity against a thinning pipeline. 228.5 GW of modules are enlisted against about 45 GW a year of installations; quarterly renewable tendering fell from 15–18 GW in FY26 to 4.6 GW in July 2026, and 46.65 GW of awarded capacity still has no power-purchase agreement.
  • Force 3, China's price ceiling and the closing US door. Chinese TOPCon modules sell for about US$0.116 per watt free on board. The US has imposed final anti-dumping duty of 123.04% and countervailing duty of 126.09% on Indian cells, and adds a Section 232 tariff from 4 December 2026. The export valve that absorbed Indian overcapacity in 2023–25 is closing.
  • Force 4, the grid, data-centre and storage build-out. A ₹9.15 lakh crore transmission plan, a 40% jump in the FY27 transformation target and data-centre capacity heading from 2.2 GW to as much as 12 GW by 2030. This is what ABB and CG are priced for, and where solar majors are diversifying.
  • Force 5, wind's localisation-protected revival. A record 6.06 GW of installations in FY26 and domestic-content rules that favour Suzlon and Inox, offset by a thin wind tender pipeline and working capital that can swallow the profit: Inox Wind's cash conversion cycle is 443 days against Suzlon's 117.

Three of these forces get deep-dive sections: the cell bottleneck (section 08), China and the US (section 09) and wind (section 10). The KPI masterclass (section 13) explains every number a reader needs to judge these companies, and sections 14 to 17 compare them directly. A practical warning before going further: the companies define the same words differently. "EBITDA" includes other income at Inox Wind and Saatvik but not on screener; "capacity" can mean nameplate, ALMM-listed or actually operating; "order book" can include group-company memoranda of understanding. Where those differences change a conclusion, this report says so.

01

Four value chains, four places where the money sticks

In solar the money sits at whichever step the government last protected; in wind it sits in turbine IP and cash collection; in electricals it sits in engineering and delivery slots.

Each archetype sits on a different value chain, and in each chain profit concentrates at a different link. Understanding where it concentrates explains most of the margin differences in the peer table.

Solar: from sand to rooftop, and India makes only the last two steps

A solar module is built in five manufacturing steps. Polysilicon is refined from quartz; it is melted and grown into ingots, which are sliced into wafers; wafers are processed into cells, the part that actually converts light into electricity; and cells are soldered, laminated under glass and framed into modules. Inverters, mounting structures and cables are added by an engineering contractor, and the finished plant is owned by an independent power producer, a company or a household. India makes almost no polysilicon, about 2–5 GW of ingots and wafers, about 36.6 GW of cells and about 262 GW of modules (Mercom, June 2026). China holds more than 90% of the world's polysilicon and wafer capacity and over 80% of its cells and modules (ICRA).

Wind: a turbine is a ten-year relationship sold as a one-off product

A wind turbine generator (WTG) is assembled from blades, a tower, a gearbox, a generator, bearings, castings and power electronics. The OEM designs the turbine (Suzlon owns its technology; Inox historically licensed platforms), makes the blades and the nacelle, and often wraps the job as a turnkey contract: land, foundations, erection and the power line to the grid. The customer, an independent power producer or a company buying green power for its own factories, then signs a 20–25 year service contract. Ticket sizes in 2026 were about ₹7.6–8.0 crore per megawatt turnkey (Suzlon's Andhra Pradesh orders ₹7.9 crore per MW; Inox–NLC ₹8.0 crore; Inox–IOC ₹7.6 crore).

Industrial electricals: copper and steel in, engineered delivery slots out

ABB and CG buy copper, electrical steel (CRGO for transformers, CRNO for motors), aluminium, resins and power semiconductors and turn them into transformers, switchgear, ring-main units, motors, drives, traction equipment and automation. Their customers are utilities, factories, railways, buildings and, increasingly, data centres. Materials are 58–69% of sales, so gross margin depends on pricing power against metal costs, usually passed on with a lag of one or two quarters. The profit pool sits in engineering content, product breadth, an installed base that needs service, and the ability to deliver long-lead equipment: a 765 kV transformer takes 20–24 months to deliver.

Batteries, defence and rail electronics: qualification is the moat

HBL makes industrial batteries (lead-acid, nickel-cadmium, plant-type batteries for data centres), defence and aviation batteries, electronic fuzes, and Kavach equipment. Kavach has an on-board half, a computer, radio and driver display fitted to each locomotive, and a trackside half of RFID tags, station units and radio towers laid per route-kilometre. A vendor must be approved by RDSO, the railways' research arm, and equipment must work with other vendors' units. Once approved, a vendor sells high-value electronics with its own software into a market of a handful of suppliers, which is why HBL's electronics segment earned a 50% margin in FY26.

Laid side by side, the four chains explain the valuation split in section 00. Industrial electricals capture value through engineering and delivery capacity that competitors cannot replicate quickly. Solar captures value through a regulatory gate that any well-funded rival can eventually walk through. Wind captures value through technology and local supply chains but leaks it through working capital. HBL captures value through approvals, but in lumps that do not repeat on a schedule.

02

Size, growth and history: a boom that doubled in one year

India installed 44.6 GW of solar and 6.06 GW of wind in FY26, both records. The listed solar makers grew revenue about five-fold in three years.

The demand behind this sub-sector is India's renewable build-out, and in FY26 it went vertical. Solar additions rose 87% to 44.6 GW (ground-mounted 34.8 GW, rooftop 8.7 GW, off-grid about 1.1 GW). Calendar 2025, at 45.7 GW, made India the world's second-largest solar market, ahead of the US and behind only China's 382 GW. Wind additions rose about 46% to a record 6.06 GW, beating the 5.5 GW peak of FY17. The first quarter of FY27 added another 11.9 GW of solar and 1.35 GW of wind.

The industrial-electricals market is harder to size. IEEMA, the industry body, put domestic production at roughly ₹2.5–2.6 lakh crore in FY23; with growth running at 9–22% a year since, FY26 production is plausibly ₹3.3–3.6 lakh crore (an indicative estimate, not a published figure). Inside that, the grid lines are booming while motors are flat: in the October–December 2025 quarter, IEEMA's index showed power transformers up 26.6%, insulators up 29.6% and energy meters up 53.6% on smart-meter roll-outs, against rotating machines (motors) up just 0.8%.

Two caveats matter for anyone extrapolating these numbers. First, FY26 was flattered by a deadline: projects rushed to commission before the inter-state transmission charge waiver lapsed on 30 June 2025, which pulled demand into the first half of FY26. Calendar H1 2026 wind additions were actually down 16% year on year. Second, installations lag auction awards by 18 to 30 months, so FY26's record reflects awards made in FY23–FY25. What happened to awards since then is the subject of the next section, and it is less encouraging.

In plain terms

FY26 was the best year Indian renewables have ever had, and the listed makers grew five-fold in three years by riding it. But a power plant installed this year was usually auctioned two years ago. To know what these companies will sell in 2028, look at what the government is auctioning now, not at this year's installations.

03

Where demand comes from: a 500 GW target, a stalled pipeline and a grid that has to catch up

The national target needs about 54 GW a year of clean capacity. Tenders are running far below that, and the mix is moving away from plain solar.

Demand for this sub-sector comes from ten identifiable sources, and they are moving in different directions. The headline target is supportive. The tender pipeline that converts the target into orders is weakening. The grid and data-centre build is accelerating. Exports are being shut. A reader needs all ten in view to judge any one company.

The 500 GW target, and what it needs each year

India's non-fossil capacity stood at 297.37 GW at the end of June 2026. Reaching 500 GW by March 2030 needs roughly 200 GW in 3.75 years, about 54 GW a year (estimate). That is close to the 55.3 GW achieved in FY26, so the target does not require acceleration, only a repeat of the record year every year. Solar is likely to supply 40–45 GW of that annually and wind 6–10 GW (estimates). JMK Research expects 53–55 GW of solar plus wind in FY27.

The auction pipeline is now the binding constraint

Since FY18 roughly 427.6 GW of renewable capacity has been tendered. Tendering is not the same as demand, though. Of the 123.58 GW awarded by the four central renewable energy implementing agencies (SECI, NHPC, NTPC and SJVN) between April 2019 and July 2026, only 76.93 GW has a signed power purchase agreement (PPA) with a utility. The remaining 46.65 GW is awarded but unsigned: the developer has won the right to build, but no state distribution company has agreed to buy the power. Projects without a PPA rarely reach financial closure, so they rarely order modules or turbines.

The mix is shifting from plain solar to firm power

Distribution companies increasingly want power when the sun is not shining. Tenders have moved from plain solar to hybrid (solar plus wind), firm and dispatchable renewable energy (FDRE), round-the-clock and standalone battery storage. In Q4 FY26 storage was 8.9 GW of 17.2 GW tendered, and plain solar only 1.9 GW. Hybrid was about 60% of July 2026 tenders. This helps wind, which is needed for evening and night supply, and battery-storage makers (Waaree has a 5.15 GWh container line, Premier is building 12 GWh, Fujiyama is adding 2 GWh). It also means fewer module watts per rupee of tender.

Rooftop, farm pumps and the DCR pool

PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana, approved in February 2024 with a ₹75,021 crore outlay, subsidises rooftop systems for one crore homes. About 26 lakh systems (roughly 9.56 GW) were installed by March 2026, and an MNRE statement carried in the press put the figure at about 50 lakh households and 14.8 GW by July 2026 (unverified). Rooftop added 8.7 GW in FY26, up 69%. Because the subsidy requires DCR modules, every rooftop system consumes Indian cells; at about 3 kW per home, one crore homes is roughly 30 GW of DCR modules over the scheme's life (estimate). PM-KUSUM, the farm-pump scheme, is also DCR-only but slow: only 27% of its 34.8 GW target was achieved by December 2025.

Captive and open access

Companies buying green power for their own operations, through captive plants or open-access contracts, are now the backbone of the wind order book: commercial and industrial (C&I), captive and PSU customers make up 84% of Suzlon's 6.1 GW book. A graded waiver of inter-state transmission charges runs to June 2028 (secondary source), which keeps this channel open. From 1 January 2027, C&I solar projects must also use DCR modules, which shifts more demand towards cell owners.

The grid, data centres and railways

Industrial electricals ride a different wave. The National Electricity Plan for transmission envisages more than ₹9.15 lakh crore of investment over 2022–32. The FY27 target for new transformation capacity is 1,58,339 MVA, 40% above FY26's actual 1,13,013 MVA. ICRA expects ₹5–6 lakh crore of transmission capex over FY27–FY32 and notes that suppliers' order inflows more than doubled between FY22 and FY26. Data centres add a second leg.

Structure and concentration: fragmented modules, a club of cell makers, a duopoly in turbines

Each archetype has a different competitive shape, and in each the biggest rivals are unlisted, foreign-owned or sitting in another index bucket.

Concentration tells you who sets prices. In Indian solar modules nobody does, because hundreds of companies are enlisted. In cells, a club of about fourteen makers sets them, for now. In wind, two domestic OEMs and two Chinese ones split most of the market. In industrial electricals, a handful of multinationals dominate each product line. The thirteen listed members are a minority of each market, and some of the most important competitors are not listed at all.

How each layer works

The ALMM is a list of approved factories and models. It does not ban imports; it bans imported products from the projects that matter most: anything government-run, government-subsidised, net-metered or open-access. List-I, for modules, created a domestic module industry but no technology moat: anyone could build an assembly line and apply. List-II, for cells, extends the same logic one step up the chain, and because cell lines are expensive and slow to build, it created genuine scarcity. List-III will do the same for wafers from June 2028.

Customs duty sets the import-parity price for the non-protected part of the market. At roughly 40% on modules and 27.5% on cells, it lifts a Chinese module to about ₹14.3 per watt landed and a Chinese cell to about ₹5.6.

The DCR rules for rooftop, farm-pump and public-sector projects require both cells and modules to be Indian. Together with List-II, they define a "DCR pool" of demand that only cell owners can serve, which is why DCR modules sold at ₹25.4 per watt in January 2026 against ₹13.6 for non-DCR modules.

The PLI pays a per-watt incentive for sales from integrated, high-efficiency capacity. Waaree has a 6 GW wafer-cell-module award; Vikram 2.4 GW of cells and modules; Reliance, Indosol and First Solar have fully integrated polysilicon-to-module awards.

The 2026 transition, step by step

The order of events in 2026 explains the Q1 FY27 margin split. ALMM List-II became mandatory on 1 June 2026, after MNRE refused a deferral on 25 May. Projects bid before 1 September 2025 (about 45–50 GW, per ICRA) remain exempt. On 18 July 2026, an MNRE office memorandum gave non-DCR commercial and industrial and rooftop projects relief to 31 December 2026; Saatvik and Vikram call this a "postponement to 1 January". All C&I projects commissioned from 1 January 2027 must use DCR modules. For a module-only maker whose FY27 mix is mostly non-DCR, that is a cliff: the market it sells into shrinks to exempt utility projects, the remaining open-access projects and exports, just as exports to the US close.

Wind and electricals: lighter, but real, policy layers

Wind has its own ALMM, which replaced the older Revised List of Models and Manufacturers in July 2025. It requires approved domestic vendors for blades, towers, gearboxes, generators and bearings, plus in-India R&D and data servers, a cybersecurity motive. An April 2025 draft proposed at least 64% domestic content by cost. A February 2026 relaxation exempts main bearings to 31 July 2027 (secondary source). For industrial electricals, BIS quality control orders cover transformers, switchgear and cables; the ₹3.03 lakh crore Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme funds smart meters and distribution transformers; and the India Semiconductor Mission provides 50% fiscal support for chip assembly plants, including CG Semi's Sanand OSAT unit. For HBL, the decisive policy is that Kavach is mandated as India's national train-protection system and vendors must be approved by RDSO.

The cell bottleneck: how long do the DCR rents last? Deep dive

In Q1 FY27 the solar group split cleanly into cell owners and everyone else. The split will last until about 2027–28, and then the rent moves to wafers.

The most important finding in this report is simple to state. In the June 2026 quarter, the first in which ALMM List-II applied, solar makers that own cell capacity kept margins of 18–35%, and makers that do not fell to 7–8%. In FY27, owning a domestic cell factory decides the margin. This section explains why, puts numbers on the rent, and estimates when it fades.

Why the cell is where the money is

Start with the price gap. A DCR module, which must contain an Indian cell, sold for about ₹25.4 per watt in January 2026; a non-DCR module with an imported cell sold for about ₹13.6 (SMM, at ₹88 to the dollar). Waaree quotes DCR at US$0.20–0.25 per watt against US$0.13–0.14 for non-DCR; Emmvee's deck says DCR modules are priced about 60% above non-DCR. The physical difference between the two modules is a cell made in India rather than China. Now look at what it costs to make that cell. A wafer costs about ₹1.5 per watt; silver paste, chemicals, gases, power, labour and yield loss add roughly ₹3–4 (indicative). An Indian cell therefore costs about ₹5–6 per watt to make, against an imported cell landed at about ₹5.6 (US$0.05 × 1.275 duty × ₹88). The cell costs about the same as an import, but the module that contains it sells for ₹11 more. Strip out some softening and mix, and the rent is roughly ₹8–10 per watt. Premier's Q1 FY27 numbers are consistent with that: EBITDA of ₹759 crore (including other income) on 844 MW of cells is about ₹9 per cell-watt (estimate).

In plain terms

An Indian solar cell costs about the same to make as a Chinese one costs to import. But a panel containing an Indian cell sells for nearly twice as much, because the government's flagship projects can only use Indian cells. The difference, about ₹9 a watt, goes to whoever owns the cell factory. A panel maker that has to buy those cells from someone else hands most of that ₹9 over.

When does the rent fade?

Rents of this kind fade when capacity exceeds protected demand by a margin, usually 20–30%, because the marginal cell maker then cuts price to fill its line. On ICRA's projection that point arrives during 2027–28 (estimate). Two things could delay it. Cell projects have consistently run late: ICRA once expected about 50 GW by mid-2026 and India got about 36 GW, roughly a fifth behind plan. And nameplate is not output: new lines take three to six months to stabilise. Two things could accelerate it: the closure of the US market, which redirects export-oriented capacity inward, and the arrival of several large unlisted integrated makers (Reliance, Adani, Avaada, ReNew, Tata Power Solar).

The sensitivity is large. The dossier's worked example of an integrated 1 GW cell plus 1 GW module plant (detailed in section 13) earns about ₹9.5 per watt at a ₹22 per watt DCR realisation, a 43% margin and a payback of 1.2–1.5 years. If the DCR premium halves, so realisation falls to ₹17.5, EBITDA falls to about ₹5 per watt (29%) and payback stretches to about two years. If cells fall to import parity, so the module sells at about ₹14, EBITDA is about ₹1.5 per watt (11%) and payback is five years or more.

Then the rent moves to wafers

ALMM List-III makes enlisted wafers mandatory from 1 June 2028, once at least three independent makers with 15 GW of combined capacity are enlisted. India has about 2–5 GW today. If the policy logic repeats, the wafer step will earn the scarcity rent from 2028, and cell margins will converge towards import parity, perhaps 10–15%, for anyone who does not own wafers. This is why every member with ambitions has announced ingot-wafer capacity: Waaree 10 GW at Nagpur (FY28), Premier 10 GW at Naidupeta (first 5 GW December 2027), Emmvee 9 GW (FY29–30, ₹5,000–5,500 crore), Vikram 9 GW (FY29), Saatvik 6 GW (FY29) and Websol with Linton Crystal as technology partner.

In plain terms

The cell premium is a race against the calendar. It is large today because cell factories are scarce, and everyone is building them. By about 2028 there will probably be more cells than the protected market needs, and the premium will shrink, at which point the government's next rule, on wafers, starts a new race one step further back. The winners are the companies that are always first to the next protected step, and they need deep pockets to do it.

Implications company by company

  • Websol (44% FY26 margin, 100% DCR cells) and Premier (30%, cells 58% of its order book) have the most earnings at risk from cell-rent normalisation, and the most to gain from reaching wafers first.
  • Emmvee earns the highest integrated margin today but has the biggest cell gap (2.94 GW of cells against 10.3 GW of modules) until Unit 7's cell line lands in March 2027.
  • Waaree has the scale and the capex budget (₹31,497 crore) to be early to every step, including 10 GW of wafers, but its blended margin will track the cell-to-module ratio.
  • Vikram and Saatvik are in the price war until their cell lines run (Q4 FY27 and Q3 FY27 respectively), and face the January 2027 cliff before then.
  • Fujiyama uses its 1 GW of cells to sell subsidy-eligible rooftop kits; its margin depends more on its dealer network than on the cell premium.

China's price ceiling and America's closing door Deep dive

China sets the price of every input and the import-parity price of every unprotected module. The US, which bought 92% of India's solar exports, has just made Indian cells uneconomic.

Indian solar makers are price-takers squeezed between two governments. Beijing's industrial policy keeps global prices low; Washington's trade policy has closed the most profitable export market. Together they push surplus Indian capacity back into a domestic market that is already over-supplied, and they set the floor under the non-DCR module price.

China: overcapacity that the anti-involution campaign has not fixed

China has more than 1,100 GW of module capacity (ICRA, 2024) against well under 700 GW of global demand. Listed Chinese solar firms lost more than RMB 50 billion in 2025 at 40–50% utilisation (Caixin). In mid-2025 Beijing launched an "anti-involution" campaign against destructive price competition. Polysilicon futures rallied from RMB 30,400 a tonne on 28 June 2025 to RMB 61,985 on 18 December 2025. Then the competition regulator halted the RMB 3 billion buy-and-shut consolidation vehicle for polysilicon capacity in January 2026, and spot polysilicon was back at RMB 33.5 per kg by September 2026 (TrendForce).

In plain terms

China also matters as a supplier. Cells were 81% of India's solar imports by value in H1 2026 (Mercom), and imports of cells and modules rose 18% that half-year. Every Indian cell maker buys Chinese wafers (about RMB 1.00 per M10 n-type wafer in September 2026), and silver paste and equipment are largely imported. If Beijing ever succeeded in cutting capacity and lifting prices, Indian non-DCR economics would improve, but Indian cell makers' input costs would rise too.

By H1 2026 the US took 92% of India's solar exports.

Wind 2.0: a localisation moat against a pipeline gap Deep dive

Domestic-content rules favour Suzlon and Inox, and FY26 was a record. But wind tenders are thin, margins are slipping and cash collection decides who actually earns.

Indian wind is on its second life. The first ended in 2017, when auctions replaced feed-in tariffs and installations collapsed for six years; Suzlon spent the decade restructuring more than ₹11,000 crore of debt. The second life began with three years of recovery and a record 6.06 GW in FY26. It rests on two supports: corporate buyers who need round-the-clock green power, and local-content rules that keep import-heavy rivals at a disadvantage. Its weakness is that the auction pipeline feeding it is thin, and that turbine makers only make money if they collect cash on time.

The economics of a turbine order

Revenue is megawatts delivered times realisation per megawatt, plus EPC and service income. Turnkey orders in 2026 priced at about ₹7.6–8.0 crore per MW; Suzlon's blended FY26 realisation was about ₹6.8 crore per MW (₹16,732 crore over 2,456 MW, mixing supply-only, EPC and service), and its Q1 FY27 average selling price was ₹6.3 crore per MW against ₹5.6 a year earlier, as the EPC share of its book rose to 32%. At a 15–20% EBITDA margin, that is about ₹1.0–1.3 crore of EBITDA per MW. On Suzlon's 2.5 GW, ₹2,500–3,300 crore, consistent with FY26's ₹3,025 crore.

Margins are slipping

Suzlon's Q1 FY27 EBITDA margin fell to 15.6% from 19.2%. In plain terms

Rules that force wind-farm builders to use Indian-made parts protect Suzlon and Inox from cheaper Chinese turbines, and FY26 was the best year Indian wind has had. Grid, data centres and Kavach: the businesses that do not depend on a protected list

ABB and CG ride a transmission and data-centre build-out with real capacity constraints. HBL rides a railway-safety mandate in lumps. Neither depends on ALMM.

The industrial-electricals archetype is the most valuable part of this sub-sector, and the least policy-dependent. Its demand comes from the physical grid, from factories and from data centres; its constraint is engineering capacity, not a regulatory list. It also has a weakness that its 99 times multiple can obscure: margins have been falling while orders have been rising.

The transmission and equipment cycle

India added 1,13,013 MVA of transformation capacity in FY26, about 90% of the CEA target, and the FY27 target is 1,58,339 MVA, 40% higher. In April–July FY27, 35,670 MVA was added, a run-rate that has to accelerate to meet it. ICRA reports that renewable curtailment reached about 33% in May 2026 where transmission lags, and 50–60% under temporary access arrangements in solar hours in Rajasthan and Gujarat; only 12% of transmission projects commissioned by March 2026 were on time, with a median delay of more than ten months. The constraint on renewable growth is increasingly the grid, which is good news for the companies that equip it.

Three cycles in twenty years: why investors discount solar and wind margins

Indian solar has boomed and busted before, and Suzlon nearly died once. The current valuation gap is the market remembering.

The 20 times multiple on Indian solar makers is not an oversight. Investors have seen this movie. Indian solar manufacturing has been through two earlier cycles, each ending with Chinese overcapacity crushing the domestic industry, and Indian wind went through a boom, a bust and a near-death restructuring. Knowing those cycles explains why the market refuses to capitalise today's margins at the multiples it pays for transformers.

Solar cycle 1, 2008–2013: export boom and Chinese bust

Moser Baer Solar, Indosolar, Tata BP Solar and XL Energy built cell and module capacity mostly for European export. The Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission, launched in January 2010, imposed a domestic-content rule only on crystalline-silicon modules, so developers simply imported thin-film modules from the US. Chinese overcapacity then cut module prices by about 75% between 2008 and 2012. Indian cell lines ran at 10–20% utilisation. An anti-dumping recommendation in 2014 was never notified. Moser Baer was liquidated in 2018; Indosolar went through insolvency and was acquired by Waaree in 2022.

Solar cycle 2, 2018–2022: protection by duty

A safeguard duty from July 2018 (25%, stepping down to 15% and extended to July 2021), the ALMM order of January 2019, 40%/25% basic customs duty from April 2022 and the PLI scheme from 2021–23 built capacity, but mostly in module assembly using imported cells. Margins stayed thin: Waaree's operating margin was 3.7% in FY22 and Premier's about 4%.

Solar cycle 3, 2023–2026: the policy-rent super-cycle

Three things coincided. The US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (2022) and US duties on South-East Asian makers created demand for non-Chinese modules at high prices. ALMM List-I was reinstated in April 2024, alongside DCR-only rooftop demand under PM Surya Ghar. And installations doubled from 23.8 GW in FY25 to 44.6 GW in FY26. Margins expanded to 20–35%: Waaree from 3.7% to 22%, Premier from 4% to 30%, Emmvee from 19% to 34%. An IPO wave followed.

Reading the numbers: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain almost everything about these companies. Each is defined differently by someone, and each has a trap.

This section is a working manual. It starts with the equation that turns physical output into profit for each archetype, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked rupee example, what drives it, what good and weak look like, how the thirteen companies compare and where the definition can mislead. It ends with an illustrative unit P&L, a benchmark scorecard, the definition traps collected in one place and a ten-minute checklist for reading a quarterly result.

1. The equation: from watts and megawatts to rupees of profit

For a solar maker, revenue is simply watts sold multiplied by the realised price per watt (₹/Wp, rupees per watt-peak, the rated output of a module in standard test conditions). Operating profit (EBITDA) is watts sold multiplied by the gap between that price and the cost of everything inside the module: the cell (either bought in, at an import price plus duty, or made in-house), the rest of the bill of materials (glass, frame, encapsulant, backsheet, ribbon, junction box), conversion costs (power, labour, consumables) and overheads (freight, warranty provisions, selling and admin). For an integrated maker the equation has a second layer: the gap between what an imported cell would cost and what the captive cell actually costs, plus the DCR premium the module earns because the cell is Indian.

2. Capacity and utilisation: nameplate, ALMM-listed and actually running

Definition. Capacity is the annual output a line can produce; utilisation is actual production divided by capacity for the period. Formula: utilisation = production MW ÷ (nameplate MW × fraction of year). Worked example: Saatvik produced 408 MW in Q1 FY27 on 4.8 GW of operational module capacity, so annualised utilisation was 408 × 4 ÷ 4,800 ≈ 34% (estimate). Emmvee produced 970 MW of modules on 10.3 GW, which it reports as 45% effective utilisation because 5 GW was added during FY26.

Drivers: order flow, cell availability (a module line without cells is idle), new-line ramp-up and, since June 2026, whether the output is DCR-eligible. Good: above 75%. Average: 50–75%. Weak: below 50%. Industry-wide module utilisation in 2026 is probably 25–35% (indicative), so the good ones are the exceptions.

18. Definition traps, collected

  • EBITDA with or without other income. Screener's "operating profit" excludes other income. Inox Wind's company EBITDA (27%) includes it and excludes credit-loss provisions; screener shows 20.3%. Saatvik's company EBITDA (8.3% in Q1 FY27) includes it; screener shows 6.6%. Premier quotes ₹759 crore including and ₹714 crore excluding. Inox Green's company EBITDA is mostly other income.
  • Capacity: nameplate, ALMM-listed or operating. Lines can be enlisted before they run; newly commissioned lines run at 40–50% in their first year (Vikram's guidance for its cells).
  • Module watts versus cell watts. Per-watt metrics change by a factor of two depending on which denominator is used.
  • Order books. Firm versus memorandum (Inox Wind), fixed versus variable price (Saatvik), group-company orders, and self-originated orders (Suzlon's development arm).
  • Calendar versus fiscal year. ABB India reports January–December. Its "latest FY" is CY2025 and its latest quarter is Q2 CY26 (April–June 2026), which screener labels "Q2 FY27".
  • One-off gains. ABB's trailing PAT includes the robotics-sale gain; Suzlon's FY26 PAT includes a ₹742 crore deferred-tax credit (its underlying P/E is about 30 times, not 17); Websol's Q4 FY26 tax was near zero after an appellate order; Fujiyama's Q1 FY27 PAT is after a ₹143.6 crore fire provision.
  • Revenue lines. Websol's screener FY26 revenue (₹974 crore) is about ₹75 crore below the company's ₹1,049 crore; Inox Green's screener revenue is core O&M while "total income" includes other income; Fujiyama's consolidated history is sparse, so its FY25 comes from standalone and CRISIL.
  • Segment margins. PBIT (after depreciation) at CG and HBL, operational EBITA at ABB, contribution margin at Suzlon. They are not interchangeable.
  • Restated history. Screener has restated ABB's quarters for robotics as discontinued but not its CY2025 annual line; Inox Green's history is restated for its demerged evacuation business.
In plain terms

Before comparing any two numbers in this sector, check that they are measured the same way. "EBITDA", "capacity", "order book" and even "the latest year" mean different things at different companies here, and the differences are large enough to reverse a conclusion.

19. How to read an electrical-equipment company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Identify the archetype first. Solar, wind, industrial or HBL? Each needs a different set of numbers below; a 15% margin is excellent at ABB and alarming at Premier.
  2. Check the margin against the right baseline. Compare operating margin with the same quarter last year and the previous quarter, on screener's basis (excluding other income). A solar maker's drop of more than 300 basis points is a signal; ask whether it came from cells, metals or inventory.
  3. For solar, find volume and the cell ratio. MW sold, MW of cells produced, and the DCR share. Divide EBITDA by MW sold to get EBITDA per watt, and compare with the ₹1.5, ₹2–5 and ₹5+ bands.
  4. Read the realisation commentary. Did DCR and non-DCR prices move? Are escalation clauses limited to cells (Vikram) or broader? Are C&I orders fixed-price (Saatvik)?
  5. Check order inflow, not just the order book. Book-to-bill above 1.2 for industrials; for solar, compare GW added with GW executed, and ask why if executions lag.
  6. Look at working capital. Inventory and receivables against the March balance sheet. Rising inventory at a solar maker in a falling-price market is the early warning; rising receivables at a wind OEM mean cash is waiting on commissioning.
  7. For wind, separate deliveries from commissioning. And note the erected-but-not-commissioned stock. If the company stops disclosing megawatts, treat that as information.
  8. For industrials, read segment PBIT and material cost. Is Power Systems still above 20%? Did copper or the rupee push material above 60%? Is a one-off provision (railways) or a start-up drag (semiconductors) depressing the consolidated figure?
  9. Strip out the one-offs. Exceptional gains (ABB robotics), tax credits (Suzlon DTA, Websol), fire provisions (Fujiyama) and other-income swings (Inox Green). Recompute the P/E on underlying profit.
  10. Update the policy clock. Note ALMM List-II enforcement (the 31 December 2026 relief expiry), the US trade calendar (Section 232 from 4 December 2026), capex commissioning dates for new cell lines, and any change to guidance. Then decide whether the quarter changed the story or only the timing.
In plain terms

Ten minutes is enough if you ask the right questions in order: what kind of company is it, did margins move and why, did it sell more watts or megawatts, are new orders arriving faster than old ones are filled, is cash coming in, and what one-off items are distorting the headline? Everything else is detail.

How to read the table

Start with the two right-hand financial columns, P/E and Q1 FY27 operating margin, because together they show what the market is pricing. The two industrial names trade at about 96–106 times trailing earnings on margins in the low teens. Every solar maker trades below 25 times except Fujiyama, whose consumer brand earns it nearly 30. Within solar, the market has already marked down the module-only makers (Vikram at 14.4 times, with a share price 57% below its IPO price) but not dramatically: Saatvik still trades at 20.4 times trailing earnings that are falling fast, since its trailing PAT has already dropped to ₹246 crore from ₹357 crore.

Next, compare the FY26 margin column with the Q1 FY27 column. For the cell owners the two are similar (Premier 30.4% and 29.0%, Emmvee 34.3% and 35%, Websol 44.0% and 33.7%, the last fall being mix towards modules). For the module-only makers they diverge sharply (Vikram 19.1% to 8.1%, Saatvik 11.9% to 6.6%). For the wind OEMs they drift down (Suzlon 18.1% to 15.6%), and for HBL they fall from a one-order peak (33.7% to 23%). The FY26 column describes the boom; the Q1 FY27 column describes 2026's new regime.

Then check the balance-sheet columns. Borrowings are low almost everywhere, but the screener figures understate where the solar makers are heading: Premier's company-basis net debt was ₹1,642 crore at June 2026 and rising with capex; Saatvik's gross debt was about ₹1,250 crore and management expects peak net debt of ₹2,200–2,400 crore; Emmvee has ₹3,300 crore of project debt to draw for its Unit 7. The ROCE column needs the same caution: the highest figures (Websol 63.2%, HBL 59.3%) are rents and windfalls, while Inox Wind's 10.6% is a working-capital problem rather than a margin problem.

Finally, the promoter column flags supply. Three promoters sit above the 75% cap on promoter holding that Indian listing rules allow: Fujiyama at 86.63%, Emmvee at 80.03% and Saatvik at 75.99%. They must sell down to 75% within three years of listing, and Fujiyama's board was meeting on 15 October 2026 to approve an equity raise for exactly that purpose. Suzlon's promoter holds only 11.70% and Websol's 29.72%, which makes both widely held, retail-heavy registers. ABB India is 75% owned by its Swiss parent and pays it for technology, a related-party flow worth checking in the annual report.

In plain terms

The thirteen companies together sold about ₹1.03 lakh crore of goods last year, kept about ₹15,300 crore as profit and are worth about ₹5.32 lakh crore, roughly 35 years of last year's profit. But that average hides two worlds: two industrial companies worth about 99 years of profit and eleven others worth about 20.

Who is winning: growth, returns and what the market will pay for them

The winners on growth and returns are mostly solar; the winners on valuation are the two industrials. Three charts show why those are different races.

"Winning" means different things to an operator and to a shareholder. On operations, the leaders are the companies with scarce capacity, collected cash and durable orders. On valuation, the market rewards durability above everything else. The two maps below plot each company's FY26 growth and FY26 ROCE against its trailing P/E, using a log scale for P/E so the 9–106 times range fits on one chart.

The two maps say the same thing from two angles. The market does not reward this sub-sector's growth or its returns; it rewards confidence that earnings will still be there in five years. That confidence is highest for the grid and data-centre equipment makers, whose demand comes from a decade-long physical build-out and whose capacity cannot be replicated in a year. It is lowest for solar, where the margin is a policy artefact with a visible expiry. On that reading, the solar winners are not necessarily the companies with the highest current margins, but the ones best placed to stay ahead of the moving rent, which means scale, capital and an early position in wafers.

The same rupee of profit is worth five times as much at ABB or CG as at Waaree or Suzlon. The gap is a forecast about durability, and it can be tested.

A price-to-earnings ratio is a compressed forecast. At 99 times, the market is saying the industrial electricals will grow earnings fast for a long time with little risk. At 20 times, it is saying solar earnings are at or near a peak and will shrink or plateau. Unpacking those two forecasts is the most useful way to judge whether either is wrong.

What 99 times assumes for ABB and CG

For a company to justify about 100 times earnings, it needs a long run of high growth with high certainty. The case for ABB and CG is that India's grid, data-centre and industrial capex cycle runs for a decade (a ₹9.15 lakh crore transmission plan, a 40% higher FY27 transformation target, data centres from 2.2 GW to as much as 12 GW by 2030), that their capacity is hard to replicate (EHV transformer lead times of 20–24 months), and that order books are growing 45–50%. Global peers such as Siemens Energy, Hitachi Energy, Schneider, Eaton and GE Vernova re-rated on the same theme in 2024–26, and India is one of the scarcer growth markets for it. The risks are that margins have not followed orders (ABB's EBITA fell from about 19% to 13%; CG's consolidated margin is 12–13%), that motors are flat, and that CG's semiconductor venture absorbs profit for years. The multiple also embeds a scarcity premium: in this classification bucket, they are the only two such franchises.

What 20 times assumes for solar

The solar multiple implies that today's earnings are near a cyclical high. The arithmetic supports caution. If the DCR premium halves, the illustrative integrated plant's EBITDA falls from ₹9.5 to about ₹5 per watt; at import parity, to about ₹1.5. On that view, a 10 times EV/EBITDA multiple on Waaree or Emmvee could be fair for a business whose EBITDA may fall by a third to a half by FY29. The counter-argument is that the leaders are not standing still: each move up the chain (cells now, wafers from 2028) resets the rent, and scale players with balance sheets are best placed to capture each new one. The market also discounts the sheer volume of new listings and lock-in expiries: 18-month promoter lock-ins end around February 2027 (Vikram), March 2027 (Saatvik) and May 2027 (Emmvee, Fujiyama), and three promoters must sell down to 75%.

Wind and HBL in between

Suzlon's reported 17.4 times is flattered by a deferred-tax credit; its underlying multiple is closer to 30 times (estimate), which sits between solar and industrials and reflects a protected domestic market with a real service annuity, offset by a cyclical history and a thin tender pipeline. Inox Wind's 35 times on depressed earnings is a bet on working-capital release. HBL's 24.6 times on peak Kavach earnings is a bet that order wins keep coming; on normalised electronics margins its multiple would be meaningfully higher.

The global lens: Indian margins are an outlier, and policy is the reason

Chinese solar majors lost money in 2025 and Western wind OEMs earn mid-single-digit margins. Indian makers earn far more because their home market is fenced.

Set against the rest of the world, the Indian numbers look extraordinary, and the explanation is not cost. India is a price-taker on every solar input and a late entrant in turbines. Its makers earn more than their global peers because India's market is protected, and that is precisely why investors discount those earnings.

Solar: the world's second-largest market, a small share of manufacturing

The world installed 664 GW of solar in 2025, up 12%. China installed 382 GW, 57% of the total; India installed 45.7 GW and overtook the US as the second-largest market. India's share of global installations was therefore about 6.9%. For 2026, SolarPower Europe forecasts the first global decline in more than twenty years, to 612 GW (−8%), driven by a 24% drop in China, while the rest of Asia-Pacific grows 18%. A Chinese slowdown with Chinese capacity intact means more Chinese modules looking for buyers abroad, which keeps the global price anchor of roughly US$0.10–0.12 per watt firmly in place.

Wind and electrification

Global wind installations rose 40% to 165 GW in 2025, and China installed 120.5 GW of them. In grid equipment, the comparison runs the other way: Indian margins are similar to or below global peers, but the growth is faster, which is why ABB India and CG trade at multiples close to or above global leaders that re-rated on transformer shortages.

In plain terms

Chinese solar factories are losing money and Western turbine makers earn thin margins, yet Indian solar and wind makers earn 18–44%. That is not because Indian factories are cheaper; it is because Indian rules keep foreign competition out of most of the home market. Investors know that rules can change and that domestic rivals will keep building, which is why they pay less for those profits.

Technology and disruption: TOPCon today, something else by 2030

India is building TOPCon cell capacity at speed just as back-contact and tandem cells mature in China. Turbines are getting bigger; storage is changing what gets tendered.

Technology risk in this sub-sector is concentrated in one place: the solar cell. Indian makers are investing tens of thousands of crores in TOPCon lines, the current mainstream technology. If the next generation scales quickly in China, part of that capacity could be uncompetitive before it has paid back. Wind, storage and grid equipment carry slower-moving technology shifts.

The cell efficiency ladder

Mono-PERC cells, the workhorse of the late 2010s, reach about 23% efficiency. TOPCon (tunnel-oxide passivated contact) cells on n-type wafers reach about 24.5–25.5% and are now the default. Heterojunction (HJT) cells reach about 25–26%, back-contact (BC) cells 26% and above, and perovskite-silicon tandem cells have exceeded 33% in the laboratory. ALMM module capacity is about 80% TOPCon, 11% mixed mono-PERC/TOPCon, 4% PERC, 3% HJT and 2% thin-film (Mercom).

The risk is timing. India's 2025–27 TOPCon cell build-out (Premier's 7 GW, Waaree's 10 GW, Emmvee's 6 GW, Vikram's 9 GW and dozens of unlisted projects) could be partly obsolete by 2029–30 if BC or tandem cells scale in China. OPIS has reported the TOPCon efficiency premium narrowing as BC suppliers cut prices, an early sign. Some hedging is visible: Waaree's first 125 MW HJT order came from its Arizona plant. Websol faces the problem one generation earlier: its 1.2 GW is all PERC, and its TOPCon upgrade arrives in March 2027. Wafer formats are also moving (M10 to G12R to G12), and module power is moving to 620–650 W and above, with few suppliers able to deliver more than 640–650 W at scale. Lines that cannot keep up lose bill-of-materials competitiveness.

Wind: bigger rotors, new reliability risk

Onshore platforms are moving to 4–5 MW and above with 160–185 m rotors: Suzlon's S175 (5 MW, first unit May 2026) and S163 (6 MW, H1 2027), Adani's 5.2 MW, Envision's 5 MW, Sany's 4 MW with a 168 m rotor, Senvion India's 4.2M160 and Inox's 4.X MW. Bigger rotors raise plant load factors and cut land per MW, but bring new-platform reliability risk and blade-logistics limits; hybrid lattice towers of 160 m open up low-wind sites. Suzlon's order book is still 88% the proven S144.

Storage, grid and the next options

Battery storage is replacing pure solar in tenders: firm and dispatchable power cleared at ₹5.99 per kWh in an assured-peak tender, and standalone storage was the largest tendered category in late FY26. This is an opportunity for integrated players building battery lines (Waaree, Premier, Vikram, Fujiyama), but it makes demand less module-intensive. India's cumulative commissioned battery storage was only 9.6 GWh in August 2026, and Indian makers are at the container and pack stage, buying cells from China. In grid equipment, the shifts are silicon-carbide and gallium-nitride power electronics, digital substations, gas-insulated switchgear and data-centre power density and liquid cooling. CG's chip assembly plant and HBL's electric truck drivetrain (pilot sales from July 2027) and maritime propulsion joint venture with Cochin Shipyard are long-dated options; our auto components report covers the wider electric-vehicle supply chain.

In plain terms

Indian companies are spending heavily to build factories for today's best mainstream solar cell, TOPCon. Chinese makers are already scaling the next generation, which converts more sunlight into power. If that next generation gets cheap quickly, some of India's new cell factories could be out of date before they have paid for themselves, the same trap that caught India's first solar makers a decade ago.

Bull versus bear

The bull case rests on a decade of grid, storage and DCR demand; the bear case on rents that expire and a pipeline that is already thinning.

Both cases are coherent and both can be argued from the same data. The bull reads the 500 GW target, the grid plan and the ALMM ladder as a multi-year guarantee of demand for Indian-made equipment. The bear reads 228.5 GW of enlisted modules, a closing US door and a 46.65 GW backlog of unsigned PPAs as the setup for the third solar bust, while the industrial names already price in the good news.

The bull case

  • Demand is policy-anchored for a decade. About 54 GW a year of clean capacity is needed to reach 500 GW by 2030; FY26 delivered 55.3 GW; transmission capex of ₹5–6 lakh crore over FY27–32 and data centres heading to 5.6–12 GW add a second engine.
  • The rent ladder keeps resetting. List-II cells (2026) and List-III wafers (2028) give scale players a fresh protected step every two to three years; the leaders are already building wafers.
  • DCR demand broadens in January 2027. C&I projects must use DCR modules, widening the pool that only cell owners can serve.
  • Grid equipment is capacity-constrained. CG's backlog is up 45% and ABB's orders up 50%; transformer lead times of 20–24 months support pricing.
  • Wind's moat is real. ALMM-Wind favours Suzlon and Inox; Suzlon has net cash, a 6.1 GW book and a service arm earning about 40% EBITDA.
  • Valuations in solar already discount a lot. Waaree and Emmvee trade at about 10–11 times EV/EBITDA, Vikram at about five times and 1.6 times book.

The bear case

  • The cell rent has a visible end date. ICRA sees about 100 GW of cells by December 2027 against a DCR pool of 30–45 GW; the integrated plant's EBITDA could fall from about ₹9.5 to ₹1.5–5 per watt.
  • Module overcapacity is extreme. 228.5 GW enlisted against about 45 GW of demand, with US-oriented capacity now turning inward.
  • The pipeline is thinning. Tenders fell to 4.6 GW in July 2026; 46.65 GW of awards lack PPAs; S&P sees FY26 awards down 45%.
  • Margins are already slipping everywhere. Waaree 18.2% vs 22.5%, Suzlon 15.6% vs 19.2%, ABB EBITA 13% vs about 19%, HBL electronics 31% vs 50%.
  • Industrial multiples leave little cushion. At 96–106 times earnings, a slowdown in orders or stuck margins could compress multiples sharply.
  • Supply of shares and capital needs. Lock-in expiries in 2027, three promoters above 75%, Fujiyama's equity raise and ₹10,000+ crore capex plans at several solar makers.

Where does the balance lie? The strongest bull arguments apply to the industrial electricals, where demand is physical and capacity scarce, but their valuations already assume much of it. The strongest bear arguments apply to module-only solar makers, whose valuations have fallen but whose earnings may fall further. The most interesting debates sit in the middle: integrated solar makers trading at about 10 times EV/EBITDA, where the question is how fast the cell rent fades and whether the wafer step can replace it; and Suzlon, where the question is whether a domestic champion with net cash and a service annuity deserves a multiple closer to the grid names. A reader who believes cell capacity will arrive late, as it has so far (36 GW against a 50 GW forecast), will lean bullish on the integrated makers; one who believes the US closure and new listings will flood the market will lean bearish.

In plain terms

The optimist sees a decade of guaranteed demand for Indian-made electrical equipment, backed by national targets and protective rules. The pessimist sees too many panel factories, a protective premium about to expire, fewer new auctions and investors already paying top price for the transformer makers. Both are looking at the same facts; they disagree about how quickly the protection wears off.
