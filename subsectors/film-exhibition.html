A complete guide to the business of showing films in India: how a cinema turns a seat, a tub of popcorn and a two-minute advertisement into profit, why record box office has arrived on fewer tickets than in 2019, why a country of 1.45 billion people has barely 10,000 screens, how lease accounting hides the true economics, and what PVR INOX, the only listed pure-play exhibitor, tells us about where the industry is going.

00

The one-page view: a price-led boom on a flat screen base

Indian cinema has never taken more money, from fewer people, through roughly the same number of screens. Five forces explain almost everything that happens in this industry.

Indian cinemas took a record ₹13,395 crore at the box office in calendar 2025, 13% more than in 2024 and 22% more than in pre-Covid 2019. Yet Ormax Media estimates that only 83.2 crore tickets were sold, 19% fewer than the 103.0 crore of 2019. Every rupee of growth since the pandemic has come from price: the all-India average ticket rose from ₹106 in 2019 to ₹161 in 2025, and ₹161 itself was 20% above 2024. The industry is selling fewer, richer visits, and 2026 is running hotter still. Ormax counted ₹6,398 crore in the first half of 2026 (+10%), roughly ₹11,000 crore by the end of September, and expects a first ₹15,000 crore year. The July–September 2026 quarter was the first in Indian history to clear ₹4,000 crore on PVR INOX's in-period series, led by a record English-language quarter.

The physical base has barely moved. FICCI-EY counts 10,033 screens in 2025, up 1% on the year, against 9,527 in 2019. That is 6.8 screens per million people, against 109 in the US, 66 in the UK and 64 in China. About half of India's screens are single-screen halls, many family-owned and ageing, and 124 screens (mostly single screens) shut in 2025 even as 240 opened. Only about 3,150 of India's 19,500 PIN codes have a cinema at all. India is under-screened by any international measure, and yet the screen count is flat because new multiplexes have only just kept pace with closures.

Inside this market sits one dominant listed company. PVR INOX, formed when INOX Leisure merged into PVR in February 2023, runs 1,802 screens in 359 properties across 114 cities (4 November 2026), about 18% of India's screens and, by our calculation, 27–33% of India's gross box office in any given quarter. It is the only listed pure-play exhibitor in the NSE 750, so this report uses it as the lens through which to see the whole industry, set against unlisted Indian chains (Cinépolis India, Miraj), the shrinking universe of single screens and the global listed exhibitors (AMC, Cinemark, CJ CGV and IMAX). PVR INOX completed a turnaround in FY26: pre-Ind-AS EBITDA (the profit after paying rent, which is the economically meaningful figure) rose from ₹484.8 crore at 8.4% of income in FY25 to ₹968.0 crore at 14.4% in FY26, and then to a record 22.3% in Q2 FY27. Net debt, excluding leases, went from ₹1,430 crore at the merger to net cash of ₹141 crore at September 2026.

The five forces that define the sub-sector

  • 1. The slate is the demand curve, and the language mix decides who gets the money. Exhibitors do not make films. One weak calendar (2024: Hindi box office −13%, Hollywood still recovering from the 2023 strikes) produced a ₹281 crore reported loss at PVR INOX in FY25; a strong one (2025–26) produced record margins on almost the same estate. Hindi and English films skew to multiplexes; Telugu, Tamil and mass-market Hindi skew to single screens. When the industry grew 23% in Q2 FY27, PVR INOX's own box office grew 18% because the quarter's surprise hits played best in small towns.
  • 2. Fewer visits, more money per visit. Average ticket price (ATP) and food and beverage spend per head (SPH) are the industry's growth engines. At PVR INOX, ATP plus SPH reached ₹451 per visit in Q2 FY27, up 14% in a year. The constraint is affordability: the ₹100 GST threshold, state price caps and a mass audience that pays ₹110 at a single screen.
  • 3. A fixed-cost machine disguised by lease accounting. Rent, mall maintenance, staff and power cost PVR INOX about ₹3,529 crore a year, almost all of it fixed. That is why its rent-paid margin swings between 2% and 22% from quarter to quarter. Ind AS 116 moves rent below the EBITDA line and shows a reassuring 29–38% margin that hides this leverage.
  • 4. Under-screening, and a capital-light answer to it. After the collapse of the debt-funded Carnival roll-up and the Covid debt overhang, growth is moving to developer-funded "asset-light" and franchise screens, and to compact formats for small towns. PVR INOX expects about 85% of its FY27 openings to be capital-light, but it has also cut its FY27 opening guidance from about 150 screens to 90–100.
  • 5. Windows, OTT and the policy wedge. National chains still insist on an eight-week gap before Hindi films reach streaming, and in October 2026 they refused to play the Hindi version of Jailer 2 on a four-week window. Meanwhile GST (5% up to ₹100, 18% above), state entertainment taxes, Karnataka's stayed ₹200 cap, convenience-fee litigation and a competition-law probe each target a specific profit line.

In plain terms

An Indian cinema chain rents space in malls, pays the rent whether or not the films are good, and splits the ticket money roughly half-and-half with the distributor. It keeps nearly all of the money from popcorn and advertisements. In a good year that combination is very profitable; in a bad year it loses money fast. Today the industry is in a good year, and it is earning more from each visitor rather than from more visitors.

Where does that leave the industry in November 2026? On the upside: record box office, a revival of mid-budget films, Hollywood back to full supply, streaming platforms buying fewer films directly, a leader that is net cash and has started returning capital (a maiden ₹300 crore buyback in September 2026 and a policy of paying out about 30% of surplus cash), and a credit rating outlook raised to Positive on 13 November 2026. On the downside: footfalls that have not recovered, advertising that has stagnated, opening guidance that has been cut three times in nine months, regulators and courts looking at ticket and snack prices, and a share price that has risen 50% in a year to about 12.8 times EV to rent-paid EBITDA, roughly 1.6–2.0 times the multiples of US exhibitors. The rest of this report takes each of those points apart.

01

How a cinema earns: the ticket, the tub and the trailer

A cinema sells time-bound access to a seat, then sells everything else to the person in it. The ticket is shared with the film's owner; almost nothing else is.

Film exhibition is the last link in a chain that starts with a producer raising money to make a film. The producer recovers that money from several "windows": theatrical release in India and abroad, then streaming (OTT) rights, satellite television rights and music rights, which are increasingly priced on how the film did in cinemas. A distributor, often the producer itself or a studio such as Yash Raj, Dharma, Hombale, Mythri or the Indian arms of Disney, Sony, Warner and Universal, licenses the theatrical rights territory by territory across India's 14 film-trade "circuits". The exhibitor programmes shows, sets ticket prices within state rules, sells food and advertising, and pays the distributor a share of the net box office. Between them sit the digital cinema service providers (UFO Moviez and Qube Cinema), who supply projection systems and deliver encrypted digital copies, and the ticketing aggregators (BookMyShow and District), who sell tickets online for a convenience fee.

Six revenue streams, very different margins

PVR INOX's FY26 accounts show how the money arrives. Ticket sales, reported net of GST but before paying the distributor, were ₹3,530 crore, 52% of total income. Food and beverages (F&B) were ₹2,088 crore, 31%. Advertising on screen and in foyers brought ₹464 crore (7%), convenience fees on online bookings ₹241 crore (4%), other operating income ₹323 crore (5%, of which film distribution through PVR INOX Pictures was ₹222 crore) and treasury and other income ₹96 crore (1%). Total income on the pre-Ind-AS basis was ₹6,742.6 crore, up 16.4% on restated FY25. In H1 FY27 the mix moved further toward food: F&B was 32.5% of income and advertising 6.3%. Advertising grew only 1.3% in H1 FY27 while ticket sales grew 17%.

The streams differ sharply in what the exhibitor keeps. Out of every rupee of net ticket revenue, about 45 paise goes to the distributor as film hire cost (45.4% in FY26, 44.5% in Q2 FY27). Out of every rupee of F&B revenue, only about 22 paise goes on ingredients and packaging (cost of goods 22.5% in FY26, 21.5% in Q2 FY27), and none goes to the distributor. Advertising and convenience fees carry almost no direct cost. So although tickets are half of revenue, they are well under half of gross profit once the other streams are added.

Who keeps what from a ₹300 ticket and a ₹150 snack

Take one visitor who pays ₹300 for a ticket and spends ₹150 on food, close to PVR INOX's Q2 FY27 averages of ₹298 and ₹153. Because the ticket costs more than ₹100, it carries 18% GST, so ₹45.80 goes to the government and the cinema books ₹254.20 as "sale of movie tickets". The distributor takes about 45% of that, ₹114.40, leaving the exhibitor ₹139.80 of ticket gross profit. The ₹150 snack costs about ₹33 to make, leaving ₹117 of gross profit that is not shared with anyone. Add the visitor's share of advertising, convenience fees and other income, roughly ₹69 per admit in FY26, and the cinema has about ₹326 of gross profit from this one visit. It then has to pay its share of fixed costs, roughly ₹235 per admit in FY26, before it earns anything.

The example shows the single most important fact about cinema economics. For each visitor, the snack earns the exhibitor about 84% of what the ticket earns (₹117 against ₹140), on 59% of the revenue. A chain that raises spending on food by ₹10 per head adds almost ₹8 of gross profit; a chain that raises the ticket price by ₹10 adds only about ₹4.70, because GST and the distributor take the rest. That is why every multiplex chain obsesses over its "strike rate" (the share of visitors who buy food, about 85% at PVR INOX) and why the industry fights so hard for the right to ban outside food.

In plain terms

A cinema is half a film business and half a food business. The film business brings the customers in and keeps only about 47% of each ticket after tax and the distributor's cut. The food business keeps about 78% of each rupee. Neither works without the other: nobody buys ₹300 popcorn without a film, and the film alone does not pay the rent.

Is it true that "the snacks out-earn the movies"?

It is a popular line, and it needs care. On PVR INOX's FY26 figures, ticket gross profit was about ₹1,927 crore (₹3,530 crore × (1 − 45.4%)), while F&B gross profit was about ₹1,618 crore (₹2,088 crore × (1 − 22.5%)). Tickets still earn more. The claim only becomes true when advertising (₹464 crore) and convenience fees (₹241 crore) are added to food: the three non-ticket streams together produce about ₹2,323 crore of gross profit, comfortably more than tickets. The correct statement is that the money earned around the film now exceeds the money earned from the film, which is why chains price tickets to fill seats and then monetise the seat.

Two smaller streams deserve a word. Convenience fees, charged on every online booking, rose 14% in FY26 to ₹241 crore and 25% in Q2 FY27 as online penetration reached 72% of tickets. They are almost pure profit, which is why state governments have tried to ban them and why the Bombay High Court's July 2025 ruling striking down Maharashtra's ban mattered (see section 12). Advertising is more troubled. It sells on-screen slots before the film and at the interval, foyer displays and brand tie-ups, and it has been stuck at ₹450–465 crore a year at PVR INOX since FY24, still below the pre-Covid quarterly run-rate. India's entire in-cinema advertising market was only about ₹930 crore in 2025 (FICCI-EY), so PVR INOX already takes about half of it. 02

A record on fewer tickets: size, growth and the missing visitors

India's box office has grown at about 3% a year since 2019 in money terms, while the number of tickets sold has fallen by about 3.5% a year. Which footfall series you use changes the size of the gap, not its direction.

Ormax Media, the most granular source, puts India's gross box office (including GST) at ₹8,315 crore in 2015, rising steadily to ₹10,948 crore in 2019. Covid closures cut it to ₹2,056 crore in 2020 and ₹3,772 crore in 2021. Recovery came fast: ₹10,637 crore in 2022, led by South Indian pan-India hits such as KGF: Chapter 2 and RRR; a then-record ₹12,226 crore in 2023 (Jawan, Pathaan, Animal, Gadar 2); a dip to ₹11,833 crore in 2024 when Hindi films had a weak year; and the record ₹13,395 crore in 2025, carried by Dhurandhar (₹950 crore, an all-time Hindi record), Saiyaara and Kantara: Chapter 1. The compound growth rate was about 7.1% a year from 2015 to 2019 and about 3.4% from 2019 to 2025. FICCI-EY's independent estimates (₹130 billion on EY's own analysis, ₹133 billion on Comscore data) agree with Ormax within a few per cent.

Two footfall series that must never be chained

The headline everyone quotes, that India sold 1.46 billion tickets in 2019 and only about 860 million in 2024, comes from the MAI-EY report on film exhibition and uses Comscore's ticketing data for recent years. Ormax builds its own series by dividing each film's estimated gross by an estimated ticket price, and its 2019 figure is 103.0 crore, not 146 crore. The two series also disagree on direction in 2025: Ormax says footfalls fell 6% (88.3 crore to 83.2 crore), while Comscore, quoted in FICCI-EY 2026, says admissions rose 1.4% (857 million to 868 million). Neither is wrong. Comscore's panel is weaker for single screens that still sell paper tickets, and Ormax's is a model. What matters is never to compare a 2019 figure from one series with a 2025 figure from the other. On Ormax's consistent series, 2025 footfalls were 19% below 2019, a serious but not catastrophic decline. The "41% collapse" sometimes quoted mixes the two.

The price line that did all the work

The all-India average ticket price drifted from ₹92 in 2015 to ₹106 in 2019, a gentle 3.6% a year. Since the pandemic it has accelerated: ₹119 in 2022, ₹130 in 2023, ₹134 in 2024 and ₹161 in 2025, the last a 20% jump in one year. Part of the 2025 jump was a South Indian phenomenon. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana approved higher prices for premiere shows and opening weeks of tentpole Telugu films, and South Indian-language ATP rose 22% (₹101 to ₹123), while Hindi ATP rose 6% (₹203 to ₹215) and international ATP 9% (₹245 to ₹267). Over 2019–2025, ATP grew at 7.2% a year, comfortably above inflation, while footfalls fell 3.5% a year; the product of the two is the 3.4% box-office growth.

In plain terms

Indians are going to the cinema less often than before Covid but paying a lot more each time. Box office in rupees is at a record, but if ticket prices had stayed at 2019 levels, the 2025 box office would have been about a fifth smaller than 2019's. The industry has not won back its audience; it has charged the audience it kept more.

Film exhibition inside the wider film economy

Box office is the largest part of what FICCI-EY calls "filmed entertainment", a ₹205 billion (₹20,500 crore) segment in 2025, up 9%. Domestic theatrical revenue was ₹130 billion of that, overseas theatrical ₹25 billion, digital and OTT rights ₹29 billion (down 8%), broadcast rights ₹12 billion (down 10%) and in-cinema advertising ₹9.3 billion (up 3%). The trend inside these numbers matters as much as the total. From 2022 to 2025, domestic theatrical grew at about 7% a year and overseas theatrical at 16%, while OTT rights fell 4% a year and broadcast rights 5%. Streaming platforms, having overpaid for films in 2020–22, now price film rights on theatrical performance. Cinema is again the anchor of a film's economics, not a declining first window. FICCI-EY expects the segment to reach ₹224 billion in 2026 and ₹253 billion in 2028, about 7% a year.

03

The slate is the demand curve: the content cycle, 2015–2026 Deep dive

From the Bollywood slump of 2024 to a ₹4,000 crore quarter in 2026: what drives the box office, why its language mix decides which cinemas get paid, and how a single film can move a listed company's quarter.

Exhibitors are price-takers on their most important input. They cannot commission films, and the release calendar is set by producers balancing festival dates, cricket, rival releases and the readiness of visual effects. The result is a demand curve that moves violently with the slate. India released 1,972 films in theatres in 2025 (270 of them dubbed), up from 1,823 in 2024, but box office is concentrated in a few dozen titles: 37 films grossed more than ₹100 crore in 2025 against 22 in 2024 (Ormax). In the first half of 2026 the top 15 films took 58% of the box office, up from 49% a year earlier, and Dhurandhar: The Revenge alone accounted for about a fifth of India's January–June gross.

A language map of the box office

India does not have one film market but a dozen, divided by language and, to a large extent, by type of cinema. Ormax's language table for 2025 shows Hindi (including films dubbed into Hindi) at ₹5,504 crore, 41% of the total; Telugu ₹2,377 crore (18%); Tamil ₹1,805 crore (13%); international films in all language versions ₹1,403 crore (10%); Malayalam ₹1,164 crore (9%); Kannada ₹528 crore (4%); and Gujarati, Punjabi, Marathi, Bengali and others about 5% between them. The mix moves a lot from year to year. Hindi's share fell from 44% in 2023 to 40% in 2024 as Bollywood stalled, then recovered to 41% in 2025 and 44% in H1 2026. Malayalam almost doubled in 2024 (₹572 crore to ₹1,165 crore). Kannada rose 73% in 2025 on Kantara: Chapter 1. Gujarati nearly tripled to ₹242 crore on a single surprise hit, Laalo.

Why does this matter so much for an exhibitor? Because the languages do not play in the same cinemas. Hindi and English films over-index to multiplexes in metros and large cities, where PVR INOX and Cinépolis are concentrated. Telugu, Tamil and mass-market Hindi over-index to single screens and local chains in smaller towns, and to states where PVR INOX is thin. PVR INOX holds 59% of Delhi's screens and 24–33% of the screens in Maharashtra, Karnataka, Haryana and Punjab, but only about 4% in Andhra Pradesh, 5% in Kerala and 12% in Tamil Nadu (section 5). In plain terms

A Hollywood film shown in IMAX is a multiplex film: PVR INOX took 61% of The Odyssey's Indian gross. A Telugu family entertainer is a single-screen film: PVR INOX took 11% of one such hit. So the question for an exhibitor is not only "how big is the box office this quarter?" but "whose films are working?"

The Bollywood slump, and why it ended

The defining downturn of the post-Covid period was a Hindi-film problem. In calendar 2024 Hindi box office fell 13% to ₹4,679 crore, and almost a third of that (31%) came from South Indian films dubbed into Hindi, chiefly Pushpa 2 and Kalki 2898 AD. Original Hindi films took only ₹3,215 crore. Hollywood, meanwhile, was still feeling the 2023 writers' and actors' strikes, which delayed releases into 2024 and 2025; international box office fell to ₹941 crore. For PVR INOX, which over-indexes to both Hindi and English films, FY25 (April 2024 to March 2025) was the worst year since Covid: its Hindi box office fell 26% on 14% fewer titles and no superstar releases, its English box office fell 28%, admits fell 9.5% to 136.9 million, and its rent-paid EBITDA margin dropped to 8.4%.

The recovery came from three directions at once. First, original Hindi films came back. Hindi box office rose 18% in 2025 to a record ₹5,504 crore, and dubbed South films fell to just 7% of it; 93% of Hindi collections came from films made in Hindi. Second, the middle of the market revived. In PVR INOX's FY26, films grossing ₹100–200 crore rose from 12% to 20% of its box office, while its dependence on ₹500 crore-plus blockbusters fell from 30% to 19%. Third, surprise "sleeper" hits multiplied: Hanuman Ansh, made on a reported budget of ₹2–2.5 crore, grossed ₹351 crore in India; Mahavatar Narsimha took ₹301 crore in a quarter; and Mirzapur: The Movie (₹267 crore) became the first Hindi web series to cross over into a feature film. Hollywood then returned in force: international box office rose 49% in 2025 to ₹1,403 crore, its second-best year after 2019.

How a single film moves a listed company

The concentration of the box office makes a cinema chain's quarter unusually dependent on a handful of release dates. In Q4 FY26 (January–March 2026), Dhurandhar: The Revenge grossed ₹922 crore in India, of which ₹352 crore at PVR INOX; in Q3 FY26, the first Dhurandhar took ₹844 crore, ₹328 crore at PVR INOX. Each of these films was worth roughly a third of the company's gross box office in its quarter. Management's own "clash maths" from February 2026 shows how much capacity a big film can absorb: a large film opens at about ₹50–55 crore net a day nationally, and two big films released together can do ₹110–125 crore net a day, which suggests that the industry has spare capacity on most days and runs short only on the biggest weekends. That is why exhibitors want a steady release calendar more than any single blockbuster. A quarter without a big Hindi or English title, such as Q4 FY25 (when PVR INOX's rent-paid margin fell to 2.3%), can wipe out most of the year's operating profit.

In plain terms

The box office is a lumpy, hit-driven business that cinemas cannot control. What has changed since 2024 is that hits now come from more places at once: original Hindi films, Hollywood, regional cinema and small-budget surprises. A broader slate is better for exhibitors than a few giants, because it fills screens on more weekends of the year.

The forward slate is what the industry is now trading on. The October–December 2026 quarter has Ramayana: Part 1 (November), Shah Rukh Khan's King and Avengers: Doomsday (both December), alongside Jailer 2, whose Hindi version national chains refused to show over the window dispute. January–March 2027 has Sanjay Leela Bhansali's Love & War, Golmaal 5, Dune: Part Three, Jumanji: Open World and the pan-India Telugu releases of Chiranjeevi and Prabhas. Cinemark, in the US, speaks of a "strong 2027 film outlook". None of these titles is guaranteed to work, and the industry's experience is that slates disappoint about as often as they surprise. The structural point is that supply is plentiful again: streaming platforms are buying far fewer films directly (30 direct-to-OTT releases in 2025, against 60 in 2024), so more films are being made for theatres first.

04

Fewer visits, richer visits: the premiumisation engine

Multiplexes sell under a third of India's tickets but take half the money. The industry's growth model is to charge more per visit, on the ticket and at the food counter, without pricing out the audience it still has.

The single most useful new dataset on Indian cinema is Comscore's split of the market by type of cinema, published in FICCI-EY's 2026 report. In 2025 multiplexes sold 273 million tickets, 31% of India's 868 million admissions, but took 51% of gross box office. Single screens sold 595 million tickets, 69% of admissions, but took 49% of the money. The reason is price: the average multiplex ticket cost ₹251, up 17% from ₹215 in 2024, while the average single-screen ticket cost ₹110, up just 7% from ₹103. A multiplex ticket is now 2.3 times a single-screen ticket. The phrase "fewer, richer visits" is therefore really a description of money migrating to multiplexes, which raised prices far faster than single screens could.

The affordability floor: value pricing and unique visitors

The counter-current is affordability. A ₹300 ticket and a ₹150 snack is ₹450 per person, roughly a day's wage for a large part of India's workforce, and a family of four can easily spend ₹2,000 on an evening at a metro multiplex. Chains know that price rises alone cannot rebuild frequency, so they have built a ladder of cheaper entry points under the premium core. PVR INOX sells weekday morning shows at ₹99, which falls below the ₹100 threshold for the lower 5% GST rate; Friday first shows at ₹149; and front rows from ₹79. Its "Blockbuster Tuesday" discount and the industry's National Cinema Days and Cinema Lovers' Days (five such days drew 34 lakh admits at PVR INOX in FY25) are designed to recruit lapsed or new viewers. Management says the number of unique visitors grew in calendar 2025 against both 2024 and 2023, helped by Tuesday pricing, but it has not published the figure.

The multiplex chains run a two-speed price list. At the top, recliners and IMAX seats cost three times the average and are often fuller than ordinary screens. How far can price go? The evidence is mixed. On the bullish side, Ormax's 2025 numbers show ATP rising 20% while box office rose 13%, so demand was not very sensitive to price that year, and PVR INOX's Q2 FY27 shows ATP up 14% with admits still up 4%. On the bearish side, footfalls on Ormax's series are still 19% below 2019, the cheapest single screens are closing, and the Supreme Court's remarks in November 2025, made while hearing the Karnataka price-cap appeal ("₹100 for water, ₹700 for coffee… halls will be empty"), show that the politics of cinema pricing can change quickly. The fair reading is that premium formats, Hollywood and big Hindi films can still bear higher prices, while the mass audience that drives Telugu, Tamil and small-town Hindi box office is close to its limit. That is why the next leg of growth is supposed to come from new screens in under-served towns at lower prices (section 10), not from further price rises in the metros.

The screen gap: 6.8 per million and barely moving

India has one-sixteenth of America's screen density and a screen count that has risen by about 500 in six years. Openings in the north and west are offset by closures in the south and among single screens.

FICCI-EY, using screen data from UFO Moviez, counts 9,742 screens in 2023, 9,927 in 2024 and 10,033 in 2025. MAI-EY's earlier report put the 2019 figure at 9,527. On a population of about 1.45 billion that is 6.8 screens per million people, down from 7.6 per million in 2018 because the population has grown faster than the screen count. The comparison with other large markets is stark: the US has 109 screens per million, France 95, the UK 66 and China 64. India's screen density has barely moved since the multiplex era began. About 3,150 of India's roughly 19,500 PIN codes have a cinema hall; the other 16,000-plus have none. Management's figure of "about 10,500" screens is a rounder number that may include screens not on the digital-cinema networks; this report uses FICCI-EY's 10,033.

Why has supply not responded to such an obvious gap? Four reasons. First, multiplexes depend on organised retail real estate, and India's mall pipeline has been modest outside the largest cities (our speciality retail report covers the tenants that compete for the same mall space); a cinema needs a large, column-free box with high ceilings, parking and late-night access, which only some malls can provide. Second, the economics of a new screen are marginal at India's low ticket prices outside the metros: a screen costs ₹2–3 crore to fit out on the traditional model (INDICATIVE), and at a ₹110–150 ticket the payback can be long. Third, single screens are closing faster than they can be replaced. Many are old buildings on valuable land, in towns where the owner can earn more by redeveloping, and their operators lack the capital to convert to digital projection, air-conditioning and recliners. Fourth, the industry's balance sheets were broken by Covid. PVR INOX's net debt was ₹1,430 crore at the merger, and the third-largest chain of the late 2010s, Carnival Cinemas, collapsed under its debts.

Openings, closures and the single-screen exit

In 2025, 240 screens opened and 124 shut, mostly single screens, for a net gain of about 110 (FICCI-EY's own trend page says 106; the difference is within the report's rounding). The South lost screens: Andhra Pradesh was down 54, Karnataka 29 and Telangana 19, while the rest of India gained about 3%. FICCI-EY describes single screens in parts of the Hindi belt that now open "only for a few days a week or for major releases", and notes that manual ticketing at such halls creates data and tax-credibility problems. Single screens still make up about half of India's screens (management's estimate; no official split by screen count is published) and sell about 69% of tickets, but each year a few more go dark.

The leader's own screen count shows the same churn. PVR INOX had about 1,650 screens at the merger in February 2023, 1,718 at March 2024, 1,723 at March 2025, 1,798 at March 2026 and 1,797 at September 2026, rising to 1,802 after a Jaipur opening on 4 November 2026. The fixed-cost machine: where ₹100 of income goes

Almost two-thirds of a multiplex chain's costs do not change with attendance. That turns small changes in footfalls into large swings in profit, in both directions.

A cinema's costs fall into two groups. Variable costs move with each ticket and each snack: the distributor's share of ticket revenue (film hire cost, about 45% of net ticket sales) and the cost of food and packaging (about 22% of F&B sales). Fixed costs are paid whether the hall is full or empty: rent (mostly a minimum guarantee, or a revenue share if that is higher), common-area maintenance (CAM) charges to the mall, staff, electricity and water, and other overheads such as housekeeping, security, repairs, marketing and IT. On PVR INOX's FY26 figures, before lease accounting, the fixed group cost ₹3,528.9 crore, about 52% of total income, and the variable group a further 28–31%.

The merged company's cost discipline is one of the less appreciated parts of the turnaround. The risk is that this programme has run its course: minimum wages rose 35% in Haryana, about 24% in Uttar Pradesh and 15% in Punjab in Q1 FY27, power tariffs rose in Karnataka and Maharashtra, and the 2026 West Asia crisis lifted fuel and power costs.

Operating leverage, measured

Because two-thirds of costs are fixed, each extra visitor drops a large share of his spending straight to profit. On FY26 figures, one extra admit at average prices brings about ₹200–260 of contribution after film hire, food cost, variable rent and GST: roughly ₹130 of ticket gross profit, ₹114 of food gross profit and ₹16 of convenience fee, less any revenue-share rent. A 10% change in admits, about 15 million tickets, therefore moves rent-paid EBITDA by roughly ₹300–390 crore, or 30–40% of FY26's ₹968 crore. That is why FY25 to FY26 (+9.6% admits, +8% ATP) doubled EBITDA, and why a weak slate can erase it.

The two EBITDAs: Ind AS 116 and the true economics of a cinema chain Deep dive

Under lease accounting PVR INOX's FY26 EBITDA was ₹2,279 crore at a 33% margin. After paying rent it was ₹968 crore at 14%. Every valuation, leverage and return figure depends on choosing the right one, consistently.

Since April 2019 Indian companies have reported leases under Ind AS 116, which is identical in substance to IFRS 16. A lessee with a long, fixed-rent lease must recognise a "right-of-use" (ROU) asset and a matching lease liability equal to the present value of future fixed rent. In the P&L, the fixed rent disappears from operating costs and reappears below EBITDA as depreciation of the ROU asset and interest on the lease liability. Variable rent (the revenue-share component) and short-term rent stay in operating costs. For a cinema chain, which rents almost every property on long, fixed-minimum leases, the effect is enormous. EBITDA rises by roughly the amount of fixed rent, the balance sheet gains several thousand crore of "debt", and reported profit after tax actually falls in the early years of each lease because interest is front-loaded.

In plain terms

Lease accounting lets a cinema chain report its EBITDA as if it owned its cinemas, with the rent shown later as depreciation and interest. The rent is still paid in cash every month. The "pre-Ind-AS" number simply subtracts it again. For a business that rents almost every property, that is the number to use when asking how profitable it is and how much debt it can bear.

Why reported profit is lower, not higher

A common mistake is to assume that lease accounting flatters every profit line. It flatters EBITDA, but for a young lease book it depresses profit after tax. Under Ind AS 116 the interest on a lease liability is largest at the start of the lease, when the liability is largest, and ROU depreciation is charged evenly; together they exceed the cash rent in the early years and fall below it later. PVR INOX's book is relatively young because so many leases were signed or renewed in the 2016–2023 expansion and merger, so reported PAT has been lower than pre-Ind-AS PAT in every period: by ₹147 crore in FY24 (−₹32.7 crore against +₹114.3 crore), ₹129 crore in FY25, ₹54 crore in FY26 and ₹10 crore in Q2 FY27. The gap is closing as leases age. For screen-level economics the pre-Ind-AS P&L is the cleaner view, because it matches cost to the period in which the cash is spent.

The balance sheet shows the same split. At September 2026 PVR INOX had ₹500.9 crore of bank debt and ₹641.6 crore of cash and investments, so it was net cash by ₹140.7 crore excluding leases. It also had lease liabilities of ₹5,807.9 crore. Screener.in's "borrowings" figure of ₹6,309 crore adds the two together, which is why a screener-based net debt of about ₹5,667 crore appears in some databases. In plain terms

Three practical rules follow for anyone reading a cinema chain's results. First, always use the pre-Ind-AS EBITDA when comparing margins over time or across companies, and say so. Second, show net debt excluding leases and the lease liability separately, and pair the lease liability only with Ind AS EBITDA or with EBITDAR. Third, when a database shows a cinema chain's "free cash flow", check whether lease payments have been deducted: screener.in's FY26 free cash flow of ₹1,906 crore excludes them (lease payments sit in financing cash flows), whereas the company's own FY26 free cash flow after rent and interest was ₹790.1 crore, about 41% of the screener figure. Carnival: the cautionary tale of a debt-funded roll-up

The industry's structure is partly the product of one failure. Carnival Cinemas expanded by acquisition in the mid-2010s, buying Big Cinemas from Reliance Media Works in 2014 as well as HDIL's Broadway and the Stargaze chain, and claimed about 450 screens at its peak in 2018–19, which would have made it the third-largest operator. It financed the expansion with debt, struggled to integrate disparate properties, and then met the pandemic with no cushion. Through 2020–22 it fell behind on rent, closed most of its cinemas and entered insolvency proceedings. Its collapse left many landlords with empty cinema boxes, gave PVR and INOX the chance to take over sites on better terms, and taught developers and lenders that a cinema chain's fixed obligations can sink it in a single bad year. That lesson shaped the capital-light model the industry now favours (section 10).

In plain terms

One company, PVR INOX, sells about one in six cinema tickets in India but takes almost one in three box-office rupees, because it dominates the expensive multiplex end. Its only national rival is a quarter its size. Everyone else is regional, small or a single-screen hall, and the last chain that tried to buy its way to scale with borrowed money went bust.

The intermediaries: digital cinema and ticketing

Two sets of intermediaries shape the economics around the exhibitor. Digital cinema service providers replaced 35mm prints in 2008–10. UFO Moviez (listed, including Scrabble Digital) and Qube Cinema (unlisted) lease projection systems to single screens and smaller chains, deliver encrypted digital copies, and sell in-cinema advertising to single-screen audiences; UFO's screen database is the source FICCI-EY uses for its screen counts. Large chains own their projectors and charge distributors a virtual print fee (VPF) per screen, a Hollywood-era device for funding digital conversion that survives in India. PVR INOX booked VPF income of ₹14.5 crore in Q4 FY26 (about ₹58 crore a year at that run-rate), and the Competition Commission of India ordered an investigation into its VPF charges in September 2025 on a complaint by the Producers Guild (section 12). The CCI separately fined UFO, Scrabble and Qube ₹2.7 crore in April 2025 for anti-competitive equipment-leasing terms. A 2017 UFO–Qube merger was abandoned, and two joint ventures formed in 2023 were later unwound.

Ticketing aggregators, chiefly BookMyShow (Big Tree Entertainment) and Zomato's District, sell a large share of tickets online and charge a convenience fee, part of which they keep. PVR INOX sells increasingly through its own app and website, which lifts both its convenience-fee income and its customer data; online penetration across channels reached 72% of tickets in Q2 FY27. Aggregator dependence is a margin risk for smaller chains, which lack the scale to run their own apps. Distributors, finally, are concentrated by slate rather than permanently: Yash Raj, Dharma, Jio Studios, Disney, Sony, Warner, Hombale and Mythri each matter enormously in the quarters when they release big films. PVR INOX also distributes films itself through PVR INOX Pictures (₹222 crore of income in FY26 against ₹316 crore of distribution and print costs, a low-margin, volatile add-on).

Six ways to run a cinema

From a fully funded mall multiplex to a fee-only franchise, the same auditorium can sit on very different balance sheets. The choice of model decides how much capital a screen ties up and how much of its revenue the exhibitor reports.

Cinema operators in India use six broad business models. They differ in who pays for the fit-out (seating, projection, sound, air-conditioning, interiors), who books the revenue and the costs, and how the landlord is paid. The distinctions matter for reading the accounts, because a shift in model changes reported revenue per screen and admits per screen even if nothing changes for the cinemagoer.

Its early performance is "above what we were expecting". Section 10 tests what has to be true for the format to earn its cost of capital.

Two adjacent models complete the picture. Film distribution (PVR INOX Pictures and similar arms at other chains) buys Indian or foreign film rights and releases them to all cinemas, not only the parent's; it is volatile and thin-margin, and in H1 FY27 a weaker distribution slate cut PVR INOX's other operating income by about 20%. Alternate content, live sport, concerts and events shown in cinemas, is tiny but growing: in Q1 FY27 it was 1% of PVR INOX's box office (2.5 lakh admits at an ATP of ₹409), and the FIFA World Cup 2026 final drew 64,000 viewers after midnight at ₹380–400 a ticket. Revenue shares to rights holders for alternate content are 35–70% of the ticket, so it is a seat-filler rather than a profit engine. The JV with Devyani International, Devyani PVR INOX (49% owned), designs and runs food courts; it had three outlets open in mid-2026 and lost about ₹1 crore in FY26. Readers interested in quick-service food economics will find Devyani's core business in our restaurants report.

The capital-light pivot and SMART: growth without re-levering? Deep dive

PVR INOX wants to open 150–175 screens a year from FY28 and about 1,000 in five years, mostly on other people's capital. Its guidance for FY27 has been cut three times in nine months. Both facts matter.

The leader's growth plan rests on three claims: that the closures of loss-making screens are finished; that developers and franchisees will fund most new screens; and that a cheaper format can make small-town cinemas profitable at much lower ticket prices. Each claim has evidence behind it and each has a gap. This section sets out the numbers.

Openings, closures and the net count

Gross openings have never been PVR INOX's problem; net additions have. In FY25 it opened 77 screens but closed about 72, for a net gain of 5. In FY26 it opened 93 and closed 18, a net gain of 75. In H1 FY27 it opened 24 and exited 25, a net loss of one, because Q1 FY27 had zero openings (fit-outs were complete but awaiting regulatory licences) and most of the year's planned closures were taken early. Windows and OTT: the bargaining table that sets the industry's value

The eight-week window for Hindi films has survived the streaming boom, partly because streamers stopped overpaying and partly because the national chains enforce it. The South and dubbed versions are the weak flank.

A "theatrical window" is the period between a film's release in cinemas and its release on streaming or television. India has no law on windows; they are set by contract between producers, distributors and exhibitors. Before Covid, Hindi films typically reached streaming eight to twelve weeks after release. During the pandemic, with cinemas shut, many films went straight to streaming and windows for theatrical releases compressed to about four weeks. Since then, the national multiplex chains (PVR INOX and Cinépolis, joined by Miraj) have insisted on eight weeks, 56 days, for Hindi films, a norm India Ratings calls an industry "guideline". South Indian films commonly run four weeks before streaming (some deals now five or more), a practice the South's single screens and local chains accept.

The window matters because it decides how many people wait. The MAI-EY survey of 14,688 cinemagoers in mid-2025 found that 81% prefer watching films in theatres, but 81% also believe films reach streaming within four to eight weeks, 53% had skipped at least one cinema visit in the previous three months to wait for streaming, and 34% would wait if the window were short. The Global Cinema Federation estimates that short windows cost about 2.2 cinema visits per consumer per year worldwide. Every week of window is therefore worth real footfalls, especially for mid-budget films that are not "events".

The Jailer 2 test

The window's strength was tested in October 2026. The Hindi-dubbed version of the Tamil star vehicle Jailer 2, released on 15 October, came with a four-week streaming window. Pricing power vs affordability: GST slabs, price caps, fees and the snack bar Deep dive

Every profit line in a cinema has a regulator or a court looking at it: the ticket (GST and state caps), the food (GST and outside-food rules), the booking fee (state bans) and the projector fee (competition law). So far the courts have mostly sided with exhibitors.

Cinema pricing in India is set by the exhibitor but bounded by the state. The bounds come in five forms: the goods and services tax on tickets and food, local-body entertainment taxes that some states levy on top of GST, state price ceilings, rules on convenience fees and outside food, and competition law. Each targets a different line of the P&L, and together they explain why India's average ticket is ₹161 while its premium multiplex tickets cost three to five times that.

The ₹100 GST cliff

When GST replaced state entertainment taxes in July 2017, cinema tickets were taxed at 28% above ₹100 and 18% at or below. From January 2019 the rates were cut to 18% and 12%. Under "GST 2.0", CBIC Notification 15/2025-Central Tax (Rate) of 17 September 2025, effective 22 September 2025, the rate on tickets priced at ₹100 or less fell to 5% (2.5% central plus 2.5% state); tickets above ₹100 stayed at 18%. The industry body, the Multiplex Association of India, had asked for 5% on tickets up to ₹300; that request was not accepted. The result is a cliff at ₹100. A ₹100 ticket carries ₹5 of GST; a ₹101 ticket carries about ₹18. For a single screen whose tickets sit around ₹100–150, the cliff is a strong incentive to price at or below ₹100. For multiplexes, whose average ticket is about ₹250–300, it is a reason to run ₹99 morning shows and front-row offers, which is exactly what PVR INOX does.

Food is taxed differently. Under CBIC Circular 201/13/2023-GST of 1 August 2023, following the 50th GST Council meeting, food and beverages sold in a cinema separately from the ticket are treated as a restaurant service and taxed at 5% without input tax credit; if food is bundled with the ticket as a composite supply, the ticket rate (18%) applies. That is why ticket-plus-food "bundles" are priced and invoiced with care, and why PVR INOX's new F&B "Bundle" offer (50% extra food value when booked with the ticket) is structured as it is. The FY26 annual report shows a ₹53 crore legacy service-tax demand on F&B among its contingent liabilities, a reminder that cinema food taxation has a long litigation history.

Local entertainment taxes

GST allows local bodies to levy their own entertainment tax, and two southern states do. Tamil Nadu introduced a local-body entertainment tax (LBET) in 2017 that triggered a theatre strike; the rate was later set at 8% and, by a government order of about 31 May 2025, halved to 4% (some reports say for Tamil films only). Kerala scrapped its levy when GST arrived, re-introduced it at up to 10% in the 2019 budget, waived it in early 2021, and in January 2026 film bodies called off a strike after the government promised to address its removal. Most other states levy nothing on top of GST, although several (Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat and Bihar among them) have offered capital subsidies or tax holidays for new screens or single-screen revival. Legacy entertainment-tax exemptions from the first multiplex wave still oblige PVR INOX to keep certain cinemas open for set periods, which constrains closures.

Price caps: Tamil Nadu, the Telugu states and Karnataka

Tamil Nadu has long-standing ticket price ceilings by class of city and seat type, revised from time to time, which is a major reason Tamil-language ATP is the lowest in India. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana regulate prices too, but approve higher prices case by case for big releases; in 2025 those approvals for premiere shows and opening weeks of Telugu tentpoles lifted South Indian ATP by 22%. Karnataka is the live case. On 12 September 2025 the state notified a ₹200 cap on tickets (excluding taxes), exempting premium multi-screen halls of 75 seats or fewer. A single judge of the Karnataka High Court stayed it on 23 September 2025. A division bench kept the stay but imposed record-keeping and refund conditions; on 3–4 November 2025 the Supreme Court issued notice on the multiplex association's appeal and stayed the record-keeping direction. The merits were argued before the High Court on 7 July 2026 and judgment was reserved. As of 13 November 2026 no judgment had been found and no cap is in force; PVR INOX's CFO said in February 2026 that "there is currently no cap implemented in Karnataka". A 2017 Karnataka cap was withdrawn after a similar challenge.

Karnataka matters to PVR INOX more than to most exhibitors: it is the company's second-largest state, with 227–236 screens (about 13% of the circuit) and 27% of the state's screens. An ILLUSTRATIVE upper-bound estimate: if 80% of PVR INOX's Karnataka tickets (about 15.6 million of FY26's admits, assuming Karnataka's share of admits equals its share of screens) were forced down by ₹80 each, the loss of ticket gross profit after GST and film hire would be about ₹58 crore a year, roughly 6% of FY26 rent-paid EBITDA, before any increase in attendance from lower prices. The real figure is probably smaller, because South Indian ATP at PVR INOX is about 25% below the circuit average and premium screens are exempt.

In plain terms

Governments influence cinema prices in three ways: by taxing tickets above ₹100 at 18% instead of 5%, by adding local taxes in some southern states, and by capping ticket prices outright. Karnataka tried a ₹200 cap in 2025; the courts have frozen it while they decide. If it came into force, PVR INOX would lose a few per cent of its profit; if other states copied it, the damage would be larger.

Convenience fees, outside food and the projector fee

Convenience fees. On 10 July 2025 the Bombay High Court quashed Maharashtra government orders that had banned convenience fees on online cinema tickets, in petitions brought by PVR and BookMyShow, holding that the ban had no statutory backing and violated the right to carry on business. A separate line of Bombay High Court cases treats the convenience fee as part of "payment for admission" for entertainment-duty purposes, and PVR INOX's annual report shows a Maharashtra demand for entertainment tax on convenience fees falling to nil. No Supreme Court appeal outcome has been found. Convenience fees were ₹241 crore in FY26 and ₹84.5 crore in Q2 FY27 alone (up 25%), almost all of it profit, so the protection matters.

Outside food. In January 2023 the Supreme Court held that cinema owners may prohibit outside food and drink, provided they offer free, clean drinking water and allow food for infants. That ruling underpins the F&B engine. But the same court, while hearing the Karnataka cap appeal in November 2025, remarked on cinema prices ("₹100 for water, ₹700 for coffee… halls will be empty"), a reminder that F&B pricing is politically visible. A Bengaluru consumer commission awarded compensation against PVR INOX in February 2025 over about 25 minutes of pre-show advertising; the order was reported to be under appeal.

The virtual print fee. On 30 September 2025 the Competition Commission of India issued a prima facie order directing its Director General to investigate PVR INOX's virtual print fee charges, on a complaint by the Producers Guild alleging discrimination (about 70% of Hollywood releases were said to be exempt while Indian producers were billed). PVR INOX has offered commitments under Section 48B of the Competition Act; its CFO described the process in February 2026 as "ongoing… sub judice". VPF income is small (about ₹58 crore a year at the Q4 FY26 run-rate), but an adverse finding against a company with about a third of India's box office would set a precedent, and the statutory penalty ceiling is 10% of average turnover.

How much more can food spend rise?

The most important pricing question for the industry's profits is not the ticket but the snack bar. What drives food spend, and how far can it go? Management splits FY27's SPH growth roughly 60% price and 40% volume (Q2 FY27) or 70% price and 30% volume (Q1 FY27): selective price increases on top of promotions that raise the strike rate. In plain terms

Indian multiplex visitors spend about half as much on food as on their ticket; American visitors spend two-thirds to three-quarters. That gap is the industry's best hope for profit growth, because food is mostly profit. But it depends on visitors buying more often and more per visit, not just on higher prices, which already attract criticism from courts and consumers.

Three decades, six shocks: how the industry got here

The multiplex era began in 1997. Since then the industry has been reshaped by tax policy, digitisation, consolidation, a pandemic, a merger and a lease-accounting rule.

India's first multiplex, PVR Anupam in Saket, Delhi, opened in 1997 as a joint venture between Priya Exhibitors and Australia's Village Roadshow. Within a few years INOX opened its first multiplex in Pune, and several states granted entertainment-tax holidays to new multiplexes, which made the economics of large mall cinemas work and triggered the first expansion wave. PVR and INOX listed in 2006, alongside Adlabs (later Big Cinemas), Fame and Cinemax. Digital projection replaced 35mm prints from about 2008, slashing the cost of a print and allowing films to open on thousands of screens at once, which is the origin of the modern "wide release" and of the opening-weekend economics that now dominate.

The 2010s were a decade of consolidation. INOX bought Fame (2010) and Satyam (2014); PVR bought Cinemax (2012), DT Cinemas from DLF (2016) and SPI Cinemas in Chennai (2018, about ₹633 crore for 71.7%); Cinépolis bought Fun Cinemas (2014); Carnival bought Big Cinemas (2014) and others. GST arrived in July 2017. Ind AS 116 took effect from April 2019, just before the pandemic, transforming the reported accounts. Covid closed cinemas for most of 2020 and much of 2021, pushed films to streaming and destroyed Carnival. PVR and INOX announced their merger on 27 March 2022; the National Company Law Tribunal sanctioned it, the merger took effect on 6 February 2023, and the company was renamed PVR INOX Ltd on 20 April 2023.

Read as a cycle, the history has a recognisable shape. Each expansion wave was triggered by a policy or technology change (tax holidays, digitisation, GST), was financed partly with debt, and ended when a demand shock met the fixed obligations of leases and loans. The 2019–2024 cycle was the most severe: a pandemic, then a content drought, landing on a balance sheet loaded with merger debt and newly visible lease liabilities. The 2025–26 recovery differs from earlier ones in how it is being financed: through developer capital, cash flow and asset sales (Zea Maize brought ₹227 crore), not new debt.

In plain terms

Indian cinema has been through a boom-and-bust cycle roughly once a decade, and each bust hit companies that had borrowed to expand just before it. The industry is now in a recovery, and the leader is financing growth with other people's money and its own cash rather than debt. That does not abolish the cycle, but it makes the next downturn less dangerous.

Reading a cinema chain: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain almost everything about a cinema business. This section defines each one, shows how to calculate it, gives benchmarks, compares PVR INOX with the industry where data exist, and lists the traps.

Every cinema chain's profit can be written as one equation. Admits multiplied by the ticket price net of GST, less the distributor's share, gives ticket gross profit. Admits multiplied by food spend per head, at the food gross margin, gives F&B gross profit. Add advertising and convenience fees, subtract the fixed costs of running the cinemas, and what remains is rent-paid (pre-Ind-AS) EBITDA. The exhibitor controls some terms directly (ticket price within state rules, food price and menu, fixed costs, which screens to open and close), influences others (admits through pricing and promotions, film hire through scale), and controls none of the most important one: which films are released, and whether audiences like them.

Advertising (₹464 crore), convenience fees (₹241 crore) and other operating and treasury income (₹419 crore) add ₹1,124 crore. Gross profit is therefore about ₹4,679 crore. In plain terms

A cinema earns about ₹130 of profit on each ticket after tax and the film's owner, and about ₹110 on each customer's food. Add advertising and booking fees, and a chain the size of PVR INOX takes in roughly ₹4,700 crore a year of gross profit. Its cinemas cost about ₹3,850 crore a year to run regardless of what is showing. The difference, about ₹850–970 crore, is the profit, and it moves a lot when attendance moves a little.

1. Screens and properties

What it is. The number of auditoriums (screens) and the number of cinema buildings (properties or cinemas) in operation. Formula. A simple count, at period end; the company also reports a weighted average for the period. Worked example. 3. Occupancy

What it is. The share of available seats actually sold, across all shows. Formula. Admits ÷ (seats × shows). Worked example. 12. Capex per screen and payback

What it is. The capital the exhibitor spends to open a screen, and the years of EBITDA needed to recover it. Formula. Growth capex ÷ new screens; capex ÷ annual EBITDA of the new screens. Worked example. No exhibitor discloses capex per screen by model. PVR INOX's FY26 capex of ₹254.2 crore over 93 openings is about ₹2.7 crore per opening including renovation and maintenance; FY25's ₹333.5 crore over 77 openings is about ₹4.3 crore; the May 2026 plan of ₹225–250 crore of new-project capex for about 120 openings implied about ₹1.9–2.1 crore per opening blended across models. On the conventional model, ₹1.9–2.7 crore a screen is a reasonable ILLUSTRATIVE range; asset-light is roughly 20–60% of that, FOCO zero, and SMART 25–30% below a normal screen. What drives it. Format (luxury and IMAX cost far more), city, landlord contribution, imported equipment prices (projectors, IMAX systems and seats are largely imported, so a weak rupee raises costs). Benchmark. A payback of three years or less is good, three to five average, above five weak; management targets three to four years. The trap. Company-wide capex includes renovation and maintenance (₹80–100 crore of renovation alone in the FY27 plan), so dividing total capex by openings overstates the cost of a new screen.

Payback is where the KPIs meet. Using the anatomy below, a six-screen multiplex costing ₹15 crore that settles at 26% occupancy earns ₹5.45 crore of rent-paid EBITDA and pays back in under three years; one that settles at 20% takes more than a decade. The same capital, in the same mall, can therefore produce a 36% pre-tax return or a 9% one depending almost entirely on attendance, which is why site selection (catchment income, competing screens, mall footfall, parking, the language mix of the town) is the most important decision a chain makes, and why capital-light structures, which shrink the exhibitor's share of the ₹15 crore, are so attractive when the outcome is uncertain. New cinemas also take 12–18 months to mature, so the first year's numbers understate the eventual return.

13. Net debt excluding leases and lease-adjusted leverage

What it is. Bank borrowings less cash and investments; and, separately, debt including lease liabilities relative to EBITDA before rent. Formula. Net debt ex-leases ÷ rent-paid EBITDA; (net debt + lease liabilities) ÷ EBITDAR. Worked example. PVR INOX at September 2026: ₹500.9 crore of debt less ₹641.6 crore of cash = net cash of ₹140.7 crore, about −0.1 times TTM rent-paid EBITDA. Including ₹5,807.9 crore of lease liabilities, India Ratings computes lease-adjusted net leverage of 2.2 times (3.0 times FY26; 4.7 times FY25). What drives it. Free cash flow after rent and capex, buybacks and dividends, and the pace of new leases. Benchmark. Net debt ex-leases of 0.5 times rent-paid EBITDA or less is good (PVR INOX's own policy ceiling after distributions), 0.5–2 times average, above 3 times weak; lease-adjusted leverage below 3 times is investment-grade territory for the rating agencies. The trap. Screener-type databases include leases in "borrowings" (₹6,309 crore at September 2026). Pair each debt figure with the matching EBITDA.

14. ROCE: company vs screener

What it is. Operating profit as a share of capital employed. Formula. Company: pre-Ind-AS EBIT ÷ (gross debt + Ind-AS-adjusted net worth − merger goodwill). Screener: Ind AS operating profit ÷ capital employed including ROU assets and goodwill. Worked example. PVR INOX's TTM ROCE is 16.5% on the company's definition and 6.79% on screener's; FY26 was 10.2% and 7%. What drives it. Margin and capital intensity: capital-light openings raise it, merger goodwill (₹4,634 crore) suppresses the screener version permanently. Benchmark. 15% or more is good, 8–15% average, below 5% weak (company basis). Pre-Covid FY20 was 12.8%. The trap. Neither number is wrong; state which one you are using, and use the company's for screen-level returns and screener's for the return on the price paid for the merged entity.

The return KPIs can be broken into their parts. ROCE equals margin multiplied by capital turnover: rent-paid EBIT as a share of income, times income per rupee of capital employed. PVR INOX's improvement from −0.3% in FY25 to 16.5% TTM came from both: the margin recovered with the slate and the cost programme, while capital employed shrank as debt was repaid, depreciation outran capex and new screens moved to developers' balance sheets. A chain can lift ROCE by raising prices, filling seats or by asking someone else to pay for the screens. Only the first two create value for the whole system; the third shifts it to the developer, who earns a yield through rent. That is why the rent line deserves as much scrutiny as the ROCE headline.

In plain terms

The balance-sheet KPIs answer one question: how much fixed obligation sits ahead of shareholders? PVR INOX owes its banks almost nothing net, but it has signed leases worth about ₹8,300 crore of future rent. Lenders treat those leases as debt, which is why they rate the company highly but not at the top.

How to read a cinema chain's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the industry. Find the quarter's industry box office (PVR INOX's presentation gives it on its own series) and the language split. Ask whose films worked: Hindi and English favour multiplexes; South Indian and mass hits favour single screens. That sets your expectation for market share.
  2. Volume: admits and occupancy. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter. Admits growing faster than weighted screens means each cinema is busier. Occupancy below 22% is a warning; above 30% is a strong quarter.
  3. Price: ATP and SPH. Check both, and the combined spend per visit. Rising ATP with falling admits may mean prices are biting; rising SPH with a rising strike rate is the healthiest sign.
  4. Revenue lines. Tickets, F&B, advertising, convenience fees, other operating income. Is advertising keeping pace with tickets? (At PVR INOX it has not since FY24.) Is other income flattered by one-offs (₹7 crore in Q2 FY27)?
  5. Variable costs. Film hire % (is a low figure timing from holdovers?) and F&B cost % (is margin rising because of mix or price?).
  6. Fixed costs. Rent (separate fixed escalation from variable rent), CAM, staff and power. Fixed costs growing faster than revenue in a good quarter is a red flag; watch minimum-wage and power-tariff changes.
  7. The right EBITDA. Go straight to the pre-Ind-AS EBITDA and margin, and compute EBITDA per weighted screen. Ignore the Ind AS margin for performance analysis.
  8. Below EBITDA. Look for depreciation-policy changes, exceptional items (labour codes, impairments of closed screens), discontinued operations and restated comparatives. Use continuing PAT.
  9. Cash and balance sheet. Net debt or cash excluding leases, the lease liability, capex against guidance, free cash flow after rent, and any buyback or dividend under the capital-allocation policy.
  10. Screens and pipeline. Openings, closures, net additions, the capital-light mix, SMART openings and the signed pipeline against guidance. Red flags: closures that do not stop, guidance cut again, a rising contingent liability for landlord claims (₹96 crore in FY26), and capital-light screens that never appear in the admits.
In plain terms

Reading a cinema chain's results comes down to four questions. Were the films good for this kind of cinema? Did more people come, and did they spend more? Did costs stay flat while revenue moved? And is the chain growing without borrowing? Everything in the results pack answers one of those four.

A final word on what the KPIs cannot tell you. None of them forecasts the slate, which remains the dominant driver of any single quarter. The KPIs tell you how well a chain converts whatever slate it gets into cash: how much it charges, how much it sells around the ticket, how tightly it controls fixed costs and how efficiently it adds screens. Over a full cycle those conversion skills decide the winners, which is why the leader's numbers on F&B margin, cost per screen and capital-light growth deserve more attention than any one quarter's box office.

How to read the table

Read across the member row first. Its return on capital on the company's definition (10.2% for FY26, 16.5% trailing) is respectable; on screener's definition, which carries ₹4,634 crore of merger goodwill, it is 6.79%. The balance sheet is net cash excluding leases. The valuation is the striking column: 34 times trailing earnings and 12.8 times enterprise value to trailing rent-paid EBITDA, against about 6.3 times for AMC and 7.8 times for Cinemark on the same rent-deducted basis.

The reference rows explain that premium and its limits. On margin, PVR INOX's best quarter (22.3% in Q2 FY27) sits between AMC's 20.3% and Cinemark's 25.3% in their own strong third quarters, so India's leader is no longer a low-margin operator. On balance sheet, it is far stronger: AMC carries about ₹28,000 crore of net corporate debt, Cinemark about ₹12,500 crore, and Cineworld went through US Chapter 11 in 2022–23 and is now owned by its lenders. On volume, its 46.3 million quarterly admits are not far below Cinemark's 63.7 million despite a third of the screens. On price, its ₹298 ticket is a quarter of AMC's converted ₹1,208. The premium multiple therefore rests on growth (India's under-screening, rising ATP and SPH) and balance-sheet safety rather than on superior margins.

The Indian reference rows are mostly blank, and that is itself informative. Cinépolis India does not publish accounts in an accessible form, regional chains disclose little, and single screens are a collection of family businesses. Mukta Arts, the only other listed company with an exhibition arm, earns a 6% operating margin on ₹174 crore of sales, loses money and has negative book value: small-scale exhibition in India is a hard business. Every comparison across the reference rows mixes accounting standards (Ind AS, US GAAP, K-IFRS), periods (fiscal year, calendar quarter) and currencies, so the figures are for orientation, not precise ranking.

In plain terms

PVR INOX now earns margins similar to the big American cinema chains, with almost no bank debt where they carry a great deal. Investors pay about twice their multiple for it because India has room for many more screens and higher prices. Its Indian rivals are either private and opaque or small and loss-making.

It paid ₹4.7 crore of rent to a Bijli-family company. Managing Director pay was ₹25.5 crore and the Executive Director's ₹13.7 crore, together about 4% of rent-paid EBITDA. Ajay Bijli released part of a personal pledge on 2 November 2026, but about 2.2% of the company's capital (around 40% of his holding) remains pledged against personal borrowing. Contingent liabilities are about ₹247 crore (standalone), small relative to net worth but rising on landlord claims (₹96.4 crore, up from ₹72 crore) linked to exits from ageing malls. None of these is large; all are worth monitoring.

The third is the record against guidance. Debt reduction has consistently beaten targets, and the October 2026 capital-allocation policy (net debt no more than 0.5 times rent-paid EBITDA after distributions; about 30% of surplus cash flow returned) followed a buyback that an investor had requested only months earlier. Screen guidance has been less reliable: FY26 delivered 93 openings against 100–110 guided, and FY27 has been cut from about 150 to 90–100. The global lens: what US, Korean and licensor peers say about India

US chains show where an exhibitor's margins can go and how leverage can destroy equity; IMAX shows who else takes a cut of premium formats; Korea's CGV shows the technology licensor's angle.

The global exhibitors are useful less as competitors (none operates in India except Cinépolis through its subsidiary) than as reference points for what a mature cinema business earns. In Q3 2026, AMC, the world's largest exhibitor with 9,472 screens in 840 theatres, reported revenue of $1,711 million, adjusted EBITDA of $347.5 million (20.3%), 74.9 million admits, an average ticket of $12.73 and food spend of $7.87 per patron. Cinemark, with 5,618 screens (4,211 in the US and 1,407 in 13 Latin American countries), reported revenue of $1,059 million, adjusted EBITDA of $267.6 million (25.3%) and 63.7 million admits. Both define adjusted EBITDA after deducting facility lease expense (Cinemark's Q3 reconciliation does not add back $90.4 million of rent), so their margins compare with PVR INOX's pre-Ind-AS figure, not its Ind AS one.

Three lessons follow. First, scale and price: an Indian visitor brings in about ₹461 against roughly ₹1,600–2,200 in the US, so India's exhibitors earn comparable margins only because their costs per screen and per visitor are far lower. That leaves Indian margins more exposed to wage and power inflation, which take a larger share of a smaller ticket. Second, leverage: AMC survived the pandemic only through repeated equity issues (its share count rose from 612 million in May 2026 to 892.6 million by the third quarter) and still carries $3.75 billion of corporate borrowings; Cineworld, once the world's second-largest chain, filed for Chapter 11 in September 2022 and emerged in July 2023 owned by its lenders. Both are reminders of what happens when fixed leases meet debt and a demand shock. Third, valuation: on a consistent rent-deducted basis, US exhibitors trade at about 6–8 times EBITDA, PVR INOX at about 12.8. India's premium is a bet on growth and balance-sheet safety, and it is a large one.

Two technology licensors complete the global picture. IMAX operated 1,887 systems worldwide at September 2026, earned $174.6 million of revenue in Q3 2026 (+64%) at a 69.3% adjusted EBITDA margin, and recorded a record $712 million of quarterly IMAX box office, $538 million of it from The Odyssey. In India it shares revenue with exhibitors under joint-revenue arrangements: PVR INOX operates 27 IMAX screens (26 at July 2026 plus Jaipur) at an average ticket of ₹516, and The Odyssey became the first film to take more than ₹100 crore from Indian IMAX screens alone. Korea's CJ CGV (Q3 2026 revenue of about KRW 688 billion, operating profit about KRW 74 billion) invented and licenses the 4DX motion-seat and ScreenX side-wall formats; PVR INOX runs 37 4DX/MX4D screens, its highest-occupancy format, and four ScreenX screens, its weakest. Licensors take a share of the premium they create, which is why a premium format's ATP overstates what the exhibitor keeps.

In plain terms

American cinema chains charge four times as much per ticket and earn similar margins, but most are heavily indebted, and one of the biggest went bust in 2022. India's leader is far smaller per customer but much safer financially. Investors pay twice the American multiple for that safety and for India's room to grow.

Technology and disruption: screens, streams, pirates and data

Streaming is the obvious threat and has so far been contained. The quieter technology shifts, premium formats, data-driven pricing and digital advertising, decide how much each seat earns.

Streaming and windows remain the core structural risk (section 11). The risk has been contained for now by the eight-week Hindi window, falling OTT rights values and streamers' retreat from direct releases, but it persists in the South, on dubbed versions and for mid-budget films. Piracy is the second: MAI-EY found that 51% of media consumers access pirated content. The Cinematograph (Amendment) Act 2023 introduced jail terms of up to three years and fines of up to 5% of a film's production cost for camcording and piracy, but enforcement is patchy, and a pirated copy released during the window directly erodes footfalls.

Premium large formats are the industry's most effective technology response. IMAX laser, 4DX and MX4D motion seats, ScreenX, large-format P[XL] and BIGPIX screens, Dolby audio and LED cinema screens (PVR INOX runs two Samsung Onyx LED screens) give audiences something a living room cannot. At PVR INOX the premium formats run at 1.0–2.9 times the circuit's ticket price and, except ScreenX, at higher occupancy. The costs are capex, imported equipment priced in dollars (the rupee at about ₹94.9 makes every projector and IMAX system dearer) and revenue shares with licensors. The format mix is now a strategic choice for each new property, not an add-on.

Data, loyalty and dynamic pricing. With 72% of tickets sold online, chains now own a large customer database. PVR INOX uses AI-assisted dynamic pricing (discounts on Tuesdays, mornings and front rows; higher prices on weekends and opening days) and personalised food offers, and its own app reduces dependence on aggregators. Digital advertising is the most important unfinished project: management is building a "pay-per-eyeball" platform, about 8–10 months away as of October 2026, that would let advertisers buy audiences in real time, including for sleeper hits such as Hanuman Ansh, Saiyaara and Mahavatar Narsimha that draw crowds without pre-sold advertising. Web and app inventory, launched in June 2026, brings only ₹2–3 crore a year so far.

Equipment life and accounting. PVR INOX extended the assumed life of its projectors from 10 to 15 years from Q2 FY27, lowering depreciation by about ₹35–40 crore a year. That may be justified by laser projectors' longer lives, or it may defer a re-equipment cycle; it is a watch item. AI in production could lower the cost of visual effects and raise the supply of films, which would help exhibitors, while also flooding streaming platforms with cheap content, an effect whose net impact on cinema is unclear. Taken together, technology has so far strengthened the premium multiplex and weakened the single screen, which cannot afford the formats, the apps or the data systems.

In plain terms

Streaming did not kill cinema, but it raised the bar: people now go out for experiences they cannot get at home, such as IMAX, motion seats and luxury recliners. The chains that can afford those formats and the apps that sell them are pulling ahead; the old single screens cannot keep up.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian film exhibition

The sub-sector's prospects rest on whether a price-led recovery can turn into a volume-led one, and whether the leader can grow screens without diluting its returns.

The bull case

  • Under-screening: 6.8 screens per million against 64–109 in large markets, 16,000-plus PIN codes with no cinema, and a single-screen base that is ageing out.
  • Content supply is back: record box office, a revived middle of the market, sleeper hits, Hollywood at full strength and streamers buying films "theatrical first".
  • Pricing and food headroom: multiplex ATP rose 17% in 2025; food spend at 51% of the ticket against 65–78% in the US.
  • Operating leverage: each point of occupancy is worth about two points of margin; Q2 FY27 reached 22.3% at 30.4% occupancy.
  • A repaired, net-cash leader with a capital-return policy, an AA rating on Positive outlook and capital-light growth funded by developers.
  • Courts have protected the profit lines: convenience fees upheld, Karnataka's cap stayed, outside-food bans allowed.

The bear case

  • Footfalls have not recovered: 19% below 2019 on Ormax's series; growth has been price, and price has limits.
  • Slate dependence: one weak year (FY25) produced a ₹281 crore loss on the same estate; the next downturn will be no more forecastable.
  • Regulatory creep: price caps, Supreme Court remarks on food prices, a CCI probe, state taxes, and an 18% GST rate above ₹100.
  • Advertising stagnation at about half of a ₹930 crore market that grew 3% in 2025.
  • Execution: FY27 opening guidance cut from ~150 to 90–100; SMART economics unproven; closures may resume.
  • Valuation: about 12.8× EV to rent-paid EBITDA and 34× earnings, roughly twice US peers, and 16× on FY26 EBITDA.

Weighing the two, the bull case is stronger on the industry's long-term direction and the bear case stronger on near-term risk. India will almost certainly have more screens, higher prices and a larger organised share in ten years; the questions are how fast and at what return. The recovery to date has been a price recovery, and the next phase needs volume: more screens in under-served towns, more unique visitors and more visits per head. The leader's FY27 delivery on openings, its first SMART results and the trajectory of advertising will show whether that shift is happening. The policy wedge is a slow-moving risk that the courts have so far blunted, but a single adverse ruling on caps or fees, or a decision to copy Karnataka elsewhere, could change the economics of the South quickly.

In plain terms

The optimistic view is that India is finally building the cinemas it has always lacked, with good films and a strong leader. The cautious view is that the recent boom came from charging the same, shrinking audience more, and that hits, regulators and the price paid for the shares leave little cushion. Both can be true at once.

Policy and legal events are the second watch-list. The Karnataka High Court's judgment on the ₹200 cap has been reserved since July 2026; a ruling upholding the cap would hit the South's prices and invite copycats, while a ruling striking it down would end the matter unless appealed. Macro conditions matter at the margin. The RBI raised its repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which raises funding costs for the mall developers on whom capital-light growth depends.
