00

The one-page view: a commodity with two masters

Indian paper is priced by whatever an Indonesian or Chinese mill will accept at an Indian port, and costed by what an Indian farmer charges for trees planted half a decade ago.

India makes and uses about 25 million tonnes of paper and paperboard a year, roughly one-twentieth of the world's output, and the industry earns something like ₹80,000–1,00,000 crore of revenue. Most of that tonnage is brown packaging paper made from waste cardboard by hundreds of small mills. This report is about the other part: the integrated, virgin-fibre mills that grow or buy wood, cook it into pulp and turn it into white writing and printing paper (W&P) and multi-layer carton board. That part is roughly a fifth of the tonnage but a much larger share of the value, and it is where the listed companies sit.

Only one company in the NSE 750 falls in this sub-sector: JK Paper, India's largest maker of office copier paper (about 28% of the market by its own FY24 estimate), its second-largest maker of virgin packaging board and, since a string of acquisitions, its largest maker of corrugated boxes. A report about one company would be a primer, so this report does something different. It builds the industry from primary data (import statistics from IPMA and DGCI&S, the Directorate General of Trade Remedies' findings on Indonesian board, DGFT's minimum-price notifications, and the annual reports of JK and its peers) and then uses JK Paper as the lens. Five other mills appear throughout as reference companies: West Coast Paper (which owns 72.45% of Andhra Paper), Seshasayee Paper & Boards, Tamil Nadu Newsprint & Papers (TNPL) and ITC's paperboards division. They are outside the NSE 750 sub-sector list but they make the same products in the same states, so they show what is industry and what is JK.

The five forces that define this industry

  • Wood cost and the farm-forestry lag. Indian mills cannot cut natural forests. Their fibre comes from eucalyptus, subabul and casuarina grown by farmers on four-to-five-year rotations, so supply reacts to price only after half a decade. JK's delivered wood cost nearly doubled from ₹7,542 a tonne in FY23 to ₹14,460 in FY26. On our estimate that rise explains about two-thirds of JK's lost per-tonne margin (an indicative split). Trees planted in the boom years should be harvested in FY27–FY29.
  • Imports and the trade-remedy toolkit. Paper and board imports reached a record 2.05 million tonnes (about ₹15,000 crore) in FY25, nearly double FY21, as China and ASEAN exported surplus. Because ASEAN paper enters India duty-free, the landed import price is the domestic price ceiling. Since 22 August 2025 a minimum import price (MIP) of ₹67,220 a tonne (CIF) has applied to virgin multi-layer board, and it now runs to 31 March 2027. Board imports roughly halved in the second half of FY26. DGTR recommended anti-dumping duties in June 2026, but the Finance Ministry had not notified them by 23 October 2026.
  • The board wave and consolidation. Board is the growth grade, so every major mill invested in it, and that made board the most contested grade. The virgin-board industry is now a near-oligopoly. DGTR counts 16.9 lakh tonnes of domestic capacity running at about 100%, held by ITC, JK, TNPL, Emami, Century and West Coast. On 1 August 2026 ITC bought Century's Lalkuan mill, which leaves two large players at the core.
  • A split demand base. Writing and printing paper (about 7 mt) grows 2–3% a year on education and offices, helped by GST 2.0's nil rate on exercise-book paper. Packaging (about 65% of volume) grows about 8% on FMCG, pharma, e-commerce, quick-service food and the ban on single-use plastics.
  • The cycle. FY23 was a once-in-a-generation peak (operating margins of 26–35% across the majors). FY25–FY26 was the trough (4–13%). Q1 FY27 (Jun-26) shows the turn: a rupee near ₹94.9 to the dollar, dearer freight after the West Asia crisis, the MIP floor and easing wood lifted the reference set's operating profit 36% year on year.

In plain terms

A paper mill is a large, expensive machine that turns trees into sheets. In a good year it runs flat out, sells at whatever imports cost, and earns a quarter or more of its revenue as profit. In a bad year it still runs flat out, because shutting a mill is costly, but foreign mills with cheaper trees cut the price while Indian farmers charge more for the wood. In FY26 Indian mills sold more paper than ever and earned about a third of what they made three years earlier. The question for FY27–FY29 is whether policy, the rupee and the next harvest of farm-grown trees restore part of that spread.

01

How a tonne of paper earns: from clonal sapling to copier ream

An integrated mill is a chemical plant, a power station and a paper machine on one site. The money is made in the spread between the price of the finished sheet and the cost of the fibre.

"Paper" covers products as different as a newspaper and a pharmaceutical carton. The grades differ in fibre, machinery, customer and price, and an analyst who mixes them will misread every number. The value chain

An integrated wood-based mill works in five steps. A farmer within a few hundred kilometres plants clonal saplings, often supplied by the mill, and harvests them after four or five years. The mill buys the logs by the tonne at its gate, debarks and chips them, and cooks the chips in "white liquor" (caustic soda and sodium sulphide) in the kraft process. The pulp is washed and bleached with chlorine dioxide, peroxide and oxygen (ECF bleaching), then pumped to a paper or board machine, which forms, presses, dries, sizes, coats and calenders a continuous web up to several metres wide. Finished reels are cut into sheets or reams and sold through dealers, or sold as reels to printers and carton converters.

The spread between Step 3's price and Step 1's cost is where integrated mills make or lose money. Imports compete at Step 4, so they set the price; farmers sit at Step 1, so they set the largest cost.

Three features of this chain explain most of the industry's economics. The first is chemical recovery. The spent cooking liquor ("black liquor") is concentrated and burned in a recovery boiler. That recovers about 95% of the cooking chemicals and generates much of the mill's steam and electricity, so a modern integrated mill is a large renewable-energy producer and 70–80% self-sufficient in power. TNPL even turns its lime sludge and fly ash into cement, which is why the company appears in our cement report as an adjacent seller. The second is scale and continuity. A paper machine runs 24 hours a day for about 340 days a year, and stopping it is expensive, so mills keep producing in downturns and fight for volume through price and discounts. The third is fibre geography. Wood is bulky and cheap per tonne, so it cannot travel far, and each mill depends on a catchment of farms around it.

Where value is captured

Fibre cost control is the single biggest determinant of through-cycle returns. An integrated mill with contract wood nearby earns the spread between the paper price and its own fibre cost. A mill that buys pulp earns the spread between the paper price and the world pulp price multiplied by the rupee-dollar rate. Within the product range, board earns more per tonne than commodity W&P, and branded copier earns more than maplitho, because copier is sold through dealers under a brand and maplitho is sold to publishers and government tenders on price. Brand and distribution capture a premium in copier (JK Copier, Paperkraft, Reflection) and in notebooks, which is why ITC integrated from plantation to Classmate exercise book (see the diversified FMCG report for the rest of ITC). Converters capture value in packaging through service, design and proximity rather than fibre, which explains JK's purchases of corrugated-box and mono-carton makers (Section 16). And at every level, the importer caps the price. In a soft world market the domestic mill price converges on the landed import price (CIF plus duty, port and inland freight), and because ASEAN paper pays no basic customs duty under the ASEAN–India trade agreement, that ceiling can fall fast.

In plain terms

A paper company buys trees, turns them into pulp and turns the pulp into sheets. The trees are its biggest cost and the sheet price is set by foreign competitors, so its profit is the gap between two numbers it does not control. What it does control is how efficiently it turns trees into paper, how much of its own pulp and power it makes, which grades it sells, and whether its brand earns a premium. Those choices decide who survives the bad years.

02

Two industries sharing one tariff chapter

About 25 million tonnes a year, three-quarters of it recycled brown paper made by small mills. The virgin-fibre mills in this report make about a fifth of the tonnage and a much larger share of the value.

India's paper market was about 25 million tonnes (mt) in FY26, growing about 6% a year, according to JK Paper's FY26 annual report. Infomerics, citing the Central Pulp & Paper Research Institute (CPPRI), puts installed capacity at "~25 mt" and production in the latest census year at about 21.4 mt. Industry revenue was about ₹80,000 crore in FY24 and is projected at about ₹1,00,000 crore by FY28. The world makes about 435 mt of paper and board (2025), so India is roughly 5.7% of global volume, consistent with a rank among the five or six largest producers. (One rating-agency note ranks India "15th"; that figure does not square with the tonnage and we do not use it.)

The headline hides two industries. The recycled-fibre (RCF) industry makes kraft liner, fluting and duplex board from waste paper in hundreds of mills of 50–500 tonnes a day, clustered in Morbi and Vapi (Gujarat), Muzaffarnagar (UP), Punjab and Tamil Nadu. It needs little capital per tonne and earns thin, volatile margins. The virgin-fibre industry makes white paper and carton board from wood, bamboo and bagasse in about a dozen large integrated mills owned by ITC, JK Paper, West Coast and Andhra, TNPL, Seshasayee, Emami and a few others. It is capital-heavy, owns its pulp mills and power plants, and manages plantation programmes with tens of thousands of farmers.

How much paper Indians use

Per-capita consumption is the industry's favourite growth argument. India uses about 15–16 kg of paper per person a year, against roughly 45 kg in Asia, 58 kg worldwide, 75 kg in China, 129 kg in the EU and 320 kg in the United States. Each extra kilogram per head adds roughly 1.4–1.5 mt of demand at today's population (our arithmetic; Infomerics uses "about 1 mt"). The gap is real, but it closes slowly, and most of the closing happens in packaging, not in writing paper. China's path is the template: its consumption rose mainly with corrugated boxes and cartons, while its office-paper use peaked.

Growth history and the forecasts that were missed

Volume has grown about 5–6% a year for a decade, led by packaging at 7–8% while W&P grew 2–4% and newsprint shrank (domestic newsprint production fell from about 1.0–1.2 mt in FY12–FY19 to about 0.5 mt by FY23–FY24). Value grew faster in the boom years: Infomerics cites an 8.3% five-year value CAGR. Forecasts have run ahead of reality. In 2022 IPMA projected 30 mt of consumption by FY27 at 6–7% a year; on current estimates FY27 will be closer to 26–27 mt. The Paperex 2025 projection of capacity rising from 24 mt to 32 mt by 2030 implies 5–6% a year, which is plausible only if the recycled-packaging mills keep expanding. No integrated major has announced a large new writing-paper machine. Capital in the virgin segment is going into pulp, power, tissue and converting.

In plain terms

When people say "the paper industry" they usually mean the brown cardboard made from waste paper in small mills, which is most of the tonnage. The companies in this report make the white stuff: office paper, textbook paper and the stiff white board used for medicine and cosmetic cartons. That needs trees, chemicals and large machines, so there are only about a dozen serious makers. They are a small part of the tonnage but a large part of the money, and they compete less with India's small mills than with large mills in Indonesia and China.

03

Notebooks versus boxes: a demand base split in two

Writing paper grows with classrooms and offices at 2–3% a year. Packaging grows with consumption and e-commerce at about 8%. The mills have spent a decade moving from the first to the second.

Demand for virgin paper comes from two very different places. About 30% of Indian volume (roughly 7 mt, by JK's estimate) is writing and printing paper, used in education, offices, government and publishing. About 65% is paperboard and packaging, used to wrap, carry and display goods. The remainder is newsprint, tissue and specialty grades. The two halves respond to different drivers, follow different seasons and face different import threats, and a mill's mix between them largely decides its growth.

Writing and printing: education, offices and the slow drag of screens

Education is the anchor. India has about 250 million school students and roughly 43 million in higher education. Their textbooks are printed on maplitho bought through state-board and NCERT tenders, and their exercise books on creamwove and maplitho. TNPL sells about a quarter of its output to the Government of Tamil Nadu for textbooks, which gives it a policy anchor but also a price-taking customer. Demand is seasonal: the textbook and notebook season runs from January to May, so Q4 and Q1 are strong and Q3 is the weak quarter. JK's own quarterly margins show this, with Q3 FY25 and Q3 FY26 both at 10.3%.

GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, made uncoated paper for exercise books and the exercise books themselves nil-rated (from 12%), while other uncoated writing paper moved from 12% to 18%. Cheaper notebooks support volume, but mills reported confusion over billing paper at 0% to notebook makers, and JK said the inverted duty structure caused "lesser buying for two months" after the change. Seshasayee called the nil rate a source of "confusion" in its FY26 report.

Offices buy copier paper, the most branded and profitable W&P grade. Demand tracks white-collar employment, banks, insurers, courts and government departments. Digitisation (e-office, e-courts, UPI, e-invoicing, digital exams) is a structural drag, offset so far by rising formal employment. Globally, writing-paper demand is shrinking about 2% a year, and Western mills have been closing or converting machines to board. India is still growing, but the surplus from shrinking markets abroad is what floods into it. The publishing and commercial-print segment (coated paper for magazines and brochures) is flat-to-low growth, and more than 60% of Indian coated-paper demand was imported in FY25, according to JK.

Packaging: the growth engine

Packaging demand comes from consumption. FMCG (personal care, foods, beverages; see the packaged foods and personal care reports) buys folding cartons made from virgin board for display and from duplex for cheaper lines. Pharmaceuticals is the premium board market, because medicine cartons need clean, consistent, food- and drug-safe virgin board; Indian pharma uses tens of billions of cartons a year. Cigarettes are a large captive buyer for ITC. Liquor, quick-service restaurants and food service (cups, clamshells, bags) and e-commerce (corrugated boxes) are the faster-growing pockets.

Regulation tilts the field toward paper. The single-use plastic ban from 1 July 2022 covered 19 items, including straws, cutlery, plates and cups, under the Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules 2021, and extended producer responsibility for plastic packaging began the same year. Both push brands toward paper cups, straws and board-based packs. The plastics report covers the other side: rigid and flexible plastic packaging still wins on cost, barrier and weight for most liquids and snacks, and glass holds premium beverages and pharma vials. Board's gains come at the margin, but they compound.

The virgin-board market is the one that matters most for JK, and it has been the fastest-growing pool. DGTR's investigation found Indian demand for virgin multi-layer board rose 48% in three years (indexed 100 in FY22 to 148 in FY25). Domestic producers' sales rose from 9.0 to 11.7 lakh tonnes, while imports rose from 57,000 to 2.94 lakh tonnes. Demand was strong, but imports took a large share of the increase, and that is how a growing market produced falling prices.

GST 2.0 also reshaped the packaging chain. Cartons and boxes (HSN 4819) fell from 12% to 5%, while kraft paper and coated board (4804, 4810) rose from 12% to 18%. Box makers now pay 18% on their main input and charge 5% on output. They can claim refunds, but their working capital is tied up while they wait, and IPMA says about 90% of kraft used for boxes is affected. For the end customer, boxes became cheaper. For corrugators, including JK's own corrugation units, cash flow got tighter.

What moves demand in a quarter

  • The academic calendar: January–May textbook and notebook season lifts W&P in Q4 and Q1; Q3 is the seasonal low.
  • Government tender timing: state textbook tenders can shift tonnes between quarters.
  • FMCG volumes and the festive season: carton and box demand peaks before Diwali (Q2–Q3).
  • Dealer stocking: when dealers expect prices to fall (for example, when a container of cheap imports is due), they destock; when the rupee weakens or a trade remedy is announced, they restock. This swings mill volumes more than end-demand does.
In plain terms

Indian paper demand has two engines. Writing paper grows slowly because more children go to school but more adults read on screens. Packaging grows quickly because Indians buy more packaged food, medicine, cosmetics and online orders, and because plastic bans push some packs toward paper. Mills have been shifting to board for a decade. The catch is that everyone shifted at once, and foreign mills chased the same growth.

Where ₹100 goes: wood, chemicals, coal and a thin slice of profit

Fibre is a third of the cost of an integrated mill in a normal year and closer to half in FY26. The rest is chemicals, energy, freight and people, much of it priced in dollars or tied to crude.

Imported pulp and the rupee

Energy, coal and the West Asia shock

Paper is energy-intensive: roughly 1,000–1,400 kWh of electricity and 8–12 tonnes of steam per tonne of paper (industry norms, INDICATIVE). Integrated mills generate most of it themselves from black liquor in the recovery boiler and coal or biomass in captive power plants. Andhra Paper's 34 MW coal plant supplies 72% of its Rajahmundry power on linkage coal from Mahanadi Coalfields and Singareni. Seshasayee is about 80% captive, and about 70% of Erode's energy is "green". TNPL's Unit I runs 103.6 MW of captive capacity plus 35.5 MW of wind. JK says about 60% of its energy was renewable in FY26 and is building an 83 MW hybrid solar-wind captive plant for its Songadh unit, due in Q3 FY28.

The coal that remains is imported or rail-freighted, so energy costs follow global coal and freight. The West Asia crisis that began on 28 February 2026 closed the Strait of Hormuz for several weeks, sent Brent to about $118 a barrel before a 7 April ceasefire, and raised freight, LPG and chemical prices. JK says packaging raw-material costs rose 10–15% and were passed on with a lag. The same shock also raised the landed cost of imported paper, which is part of why realisations recovered in Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27. For an Indian mill, dearer freight cuts both ways, and in 2026 it helped more than it hurt.

Chemicals, recycled fibre and freight

Chemicals (caustic soda, sodium sulphate, sodium chlorate for chlorine dioxide, hydrogen peroxide, starch, fillers, coating pigments, sizing agents) are 10–15% of cost and follow the chlor-alkali and crude cycles. Paper mills are among the largest domestic buyers of caustic soda. Recycled fibre is the main input for the RCF mills: domestic waste paper sold at ₹10–18 a kg in mid-2026, plus imported OCC from the US, Europe and the Middle East. China's waste-paper import ban (2018–21) reshaped global OCC flows and at times made imported waste paper cheaper for India. Virgin mills use little recycled fibre in their main grades, although Andhra's Kadiam unit is moving into "green copier" with recycled content. Freight runs both ways: wood comes in by truck over 200–300 km and paper goes out to dealers nationwide. As JK's local catchment shrank, its average haul lengthened and its delivered wood cost rose.

Who makes India's paper: fragmented by tonnage, concentrated by grade

Some 550 mills operate, but in virgin board six companies make almost everything, and in copier five brands dominate the shelf. The marginal supplier is often foreign.

By tonnage, the Indian paper industry is fragmented. DPIIT counts 850–900 mills, of which about 526–550 operate. Most are small recycled-fibre mills making kraft and duplex. No single company makes more than about 6% of national tonnage, and the integrated majors together make roughly 15% (INDICATIVE). By grade, the picture changes completely. Virgin multi-layer board is a near-oligopoly. Branded copier is dominated by a handful of mills. Maplitho and textbook paper are contested by integrated mills and agro-based mills from Punjab and UP. Groups, owners and the companies that left

Indian paper is family- and state-controlled. JK Paper belongs to the JK Organisation (the Hari Shankar Singhania family); its Chairman and Managing Director is Harsh Pati Singhania, and the promoter group owns 52.94% after a March 2026 restructuring. ITC has no promoter; British American Tobacco is its largest shareholder. Emami Paper belongs to the Emami group.

Two exits tell the story of returns. International Paper, the world's largest paper company by revenue, bought 75% of Andhra Pradesh Paper Mills in 2011 and sold its 72.2% to West Coast in FY19 for ₹911 crore: a global leader that could not earn its cost of capital in Indian W&P. The Aditya Birla group sold Century Pulp & Paper's Lalkuan mill to ITC for ₹3,498 crore on 1 August 2026, turning Aditya Birla Real Estate into a property company. Ballarpur Industries (BILT), once India's largest paper maker, went through insolvency, and a 2023 resolution plan by Finquest left it a minor player. Hindustan Paper Corporation's Assam mills are closed. Unlisted mills of scale include Khanna Paper (Amritsar) and several Punjab and UP agro-based mills, and Security Paper Mill, Hoshangabad, makes currency paper for the government.

Imports: the twelfth "company"

The largest supplier that does not appear in any Indian ranking is the foreign mill. Imports of paper and paperboard (excluding newsprint) rose from 10.8 lakh tonnes in FY21 to a record 20.5 lakh tonnes in FY25, about 8% of Indian consumption (INDICATIVE). The import-parity arithmetic

To see why the MIP mattered more than the duty, work through the arithmetic of a tonne of Indonesian board arriving at Nhava Sheva. Under the ASEAN agreement it pays no basic customs duty. Before the MIP, the FY25 landed price DGTR recorded was ₹54,675 a tonne. With the MIP, it cannot clear customs below ₹67,220 CIF. Add port and clearing charges of roughly ₹1,500 and the tonne sits at about ₹68,700 on the quay. Add ₹2,500–3,500 of inland freight to a carton converter in Mumbai or Delhi and the delivered cost is roughly ₹71,500–72,000. An Indian mill selling ex-works can therefore price board at about ₹70,000–75,000 a tonne without losing the customer to imports. Without the MIP, at FY25 import prices, the same ceiling would sit near ₹58,000–60,000.

Did it work?

On volumes, yes. JK reports that board imports fell from about 30,000 tonnes a month in H1 FY26 to roughly half that in H2. On prices, partly. JK's operating EBITDA rose 16% year on year to about ₹290 crore. Board realisations started improving in Q4 FY26 (CRISIL), when the rupee's slide toward ₹95 and the West Asia freight shock also raised the cost of imported board. The limits of the floor

  • It covers five HS lines. Coated woodfree paper (4810.13/14/19) and uncoated copier (4802) are not covered, so importers can shift to other grades, and W&P mills got no direct relief.
  • It is temporary. The MIP has been extended four times, each time for six months or less. Its expiry on 31 March 2027 is now the industry's biggest policy date. Under Indian customs rules the government normally acts on DGTR findings within three months, a window that ended about 25 September 2026. If the duty has lapsed, the MIP is the only shield.
  • It invites circumvention. Exports through third countries were the reason DGTR set its highest rate on "others". Mis-declaration of HS codes is a standing risk that PIMS registration is meant to catch.
  • It does not fix cost. The floor stops imports undercutting Indian mills, but DGTR found Indian mills' cost of sales above the MIP. Margins recover only if wood costs fall too, which is the subject of the next deep dive.
  • The policy is under review. The ASEAN–India trade-in-goods agreement review, launched in February 2024 (13th Joint Committee in New Delhi, 6–10 July 2026), targets conclusion by end-2026. Rules of origin and paper tariff lines are on the industry's list.
In plain terms

In FY25 a tonne of Indonesian board arrived in India about ₹12,500 cheaper than Indian mills were charging, and Indian mills had to cut prices or lose customers. In August 2025 the government said no board could be imported for less than ₹67,220 a tonne. That did not make Indian mills rich, because it only restored the FY25 price, but it stopped the slide, and imports halved. An anti-dumping duty was also recommended, but it would have exempted the biggest Indonesian exporter and still had not been imposed by late October 2026. The minimum price runs to 31 March 2027, and what replaces it is the key policy question for the industry.

The forest on the farm: why wood doubled, and when it eases Deep dive

Indian mills grow their fibre on other people's land, on a five-year clock. That makes wood the slowest-moving and, in FY23–FY26, the most damaging cost in the industry.

Most of the world's large paper companies own or lease forests. Suzano in Brazil manages over a million hectares of eucalyptus; APP and APRIL in Indonesia run vast acacia concessions; Stora Enso and UPM own Nordic forests. Indian mills cannot do this. The Forest (Conservation) Act and the National Forest Policy of 1988 closed natural forests to industrial use, and private companies cannot hold large tracts of farmland. So from the early 1990s the integrated mills built a different model: farm forestry. The mill develops high-yield clonal saplings of eucalyptus, subabul and casuarina, sells or gives them to farmers within a few hundred kilometres, offers technical help and sometimes a buy-back price, and buys the wood at the mill gate when the trees are cut four or five years later.

JK Paper started in 1991 and says more than 85% of the wood for its Rayagada mill now comes from farm forestry. In FY26 it distributed 11.93 crore saplings covering 90,238 acres, and in Q1 FY27 another 2.61 crore saplings on 20,177 acres. ITC built the largest clonal programme around Bhadrachalam in Telangana. TNPL has developed plantations over 3,09,382 acres since 2004 and sources about half its wood from farmers, 30% from TAFCORN (the Tamil Nadu forest plantation corporation) and 20% from the open market. Andhra Paper's farm-forestry base covers about 3,15,107 hectares. The model works: it turned degraded farmland into fibre, gave farmers a cash crop that needs little water, and freed the industry from forest leases. But it has one structural flaw: supply responds to price with a five-year lag.

Why the price nearly doubled

Four forces pushed in the same direction. First, COVID-era under-planting: in 2020–21 harvesting was disrupted, mills cut purchases, and farmers planted less. JK's own diagnosis is that the wood shortage of FY24–FY26 traces to this gap. Second, the FY22–FY23 paper boom pushed mills to run above capacity and buy every log they could, drawing down standing timber before the replacement trees were ready. Third, competing buyers: the plywood and MDF industries buy the same eucalyptus and subabul logs, and biomass power plants buy wood as fuel. Fourth, distance: as the local catchment ran short, mills bought from farther away, and freight on bulky, low-value logs is a large part of delivered cost.

JK's data shows the fourth effect directly. The share of its wood coming from the mills' local catchment was 71% in FY18, rose to 100% in FY20 and FY21 when plantations were mature and demand was weak, then fell to 94% in FY22, 77% in FY23 and 70% in FY24. As local wood ran out, JK bought from farther away at almost double the price.

The cobweb: a five-year price cycle

Economists call this pattern a cobweb cycle. When wood is cheap, farmers plant less or switch to other crops. Five years later wood is scarce, prices rise, and farmers plant heavily. Five years after that the new trees are ready together, prices fall, and some farmers uproot their plantations. Mills try to damp the cycle with buy-back assurances, minimum support prices and sapling subsidies, and some state governments have set reference rates for pulpwood. But a mill cannot make a tree grow faster than four years, and it cannot oblige a farmer to plant.

In plain terms

JK Paper does not own forests. It pays farmers for trees, and those trees take about five years to grow. There is also a policy route. In plain terms

The best single number to watch for an Indian paper mill is not its paper price but its wood price. JK publishes it once a year, in a corner of the corporate-governance report. Delhi sets the floor: tariffs, GST, forests and carbon

More than any competitor, the government decides what an Indian paper mill earns: through the duty on imports, the minimum import price, the GST rate on a notebook, the rules on forest land and, from FY26, a carbon-intensity target.

Paper is unusually exposed to policy because its price is set at the border. Basic customs duty, free-trade agreements, minimum import prices and anti-dumping duties decide the landed cost of the imported tonne, and therefore the ceiling on Indian prices. GST decides how much of the price the buyer sees. Forest law decides where the fibre can come from. Environmental and carbon rules set the cost of compliance. The border: duty, preference and the zero-rated neighbour

India's most-favoured-nation duty on most paper is 10%, but the duty that matters is the one paid by the cheapest supplier. Under the ASEAN–India trade-in-goods agreement, in force from 2010, duty on most paper from Indonesia, Thailand and other ASEAN members fell to zero by about 2016. Under APTA, China receives a 30% margin of preference on some lines, an effective rate of about 7% where it applies. The result is that the two largest sources of imports, China and ASEAN, pay little or no duty. IPMA has asked for the MFN rate to rise to 25%, but that would not touch ASEAN cargo. Hence the industry's focus on the AITIGA review, on the MIP, and on anti-dumping cases.

GST 2.0: cheaper notebooks, an inverted duty for boxes

The GST rationalisation of 22 September 2025 (Notification 9/2025-CT(Rate)) moved most paper in opposite directions. For corrugated-box makers, including JK's own units, the inverted duty (18% on kraft in, 5% on boxes out) traps working capital until refunds arrive.

Carbon, water and the next set of rules

India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) sets greenhouse-gas emission-intensity targets for energy-intensive sectors. The GEI Target Rules 2025, effective 8 October 2025, cover 282 entities including 53 pulp and paper units, with two-year targets starting in FY26. A unit that beats its target earns tradable credits; one that misses must buy them. Integrated mills start with an advantage because black liquor and biomass are counted as renewable. JK reports Scope 1 emissions of 703,932 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent in FY26, about 1.02 tonnes per tonne of product, and expects its 83 MW hybrid renewable plant to cut purchased and coal-based power from FY28. TNPL's annual report refers to its GHG emission-intensity compliance, and Andhra's new 43,000 tpa precipitated calcium carbonate plant captures lime-kiln CO₂ (about 19,000 tonnes a year). Water and effluent norms are set by the CPCB charter for pulp and paper and enforced through state consents, and they decide how quickly capex gets approved.

Two rules are absent but possible. There is no extended producer responsibility for paper packaging yet; if one arrives it would raise costs for brand owners but could also favour paper over plastics if the rules for plastics stay tougher. And there is no quality-control order on imported paper, which IPMA has requested as a non-tariff barrier.

Outbound policy: US tariffs and the export valve

Trade policy also works in the other direction. Indian mills export a few hundred thousand tonnes of W&P and board a year to the Middle East, Africa, South Asia and the United States, and exports are the safety valve when domestic prices fall. Seshasayee's FY26 shows what happens when the valve closes: its exports to the US, its main export market, were suspended from May to September 2025 because of US tariffs, export volume fell 27.8% to 20,759 tonnes, and the diverted tonnes were sold into an already import-pressed domestic market. Its realisation fell about 6.7% even though volume rose 4.5%. The US tariff regime has kept changing: the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026, a 10% Section 122 tariff ran to 24 July 2026, and since then a 10% Section 301 tariff on top of MFN has applied, under legal challenge.

In plain terms

A paper mill's profit depends heavily on decisions made in Delhi and, sometimes, Washington. Delhi decides how much an imported tonne pays at the border (nothing, if it comes from ASEAN), whether board can be imported below ₹67,220 a tonne, what GST a notebook carries, and whether mills can grow trees on degraded forest land. From FY26 it also sets carbon targets. Most of these levers currently favour Indian mills, but several are temporary, and the minimum import price is the one that expires soonest.

Four cycles in thirty years: booms, gluts and a once-in-a-generation peak

Indian paper has a capital cycle (the FY11–FY14 glut), a demand shock (COVID), a price super-cycle (FY22–FY23) and an import down-leg (FY24–FY26). Each left a mark on today's balance sheets.

The industry's history explains its current caution about capacity. Under the licence regime, paper capacity was licensed and forest leases supplied bamboo and wood. Small agro-based and recycled mills multiplied after concessions for "non-conventional" raw materials. The large integrated mills that still dominate were founded between the 1940s and 1970s: Ballarpur (1945), West Coast (1955), Seshasayee (1960), JK's Rayagada fine-paper mill (1962), Andhra Pradesh Paper Mills (1964), and TNPL and ITC Bhadrachalam (both 1979). When forest access ended in the late 1980s and 1990s, JK (1991), ITC and later TNPL built farm-forestry programmes to replace it.

The FY08–FY10 boom was followed by a capacity wave in FY11–FY14, as ITC, JK (Rayagada's expansion), BILT, Century and TNPL all added machines. Prices fell, and the top ten listed paper companies' net debt reached 4.6 times EBITDA by FY14 (BOB Capital via industry reporting). JK lost ₹18 crore in FY15. The industry then spent FY16–FY20 deleveraging. Almost no new capacity was built, prices firmed and JK's operating margin rose to 27–28% in FY19–FY20. International Paper sold Andhra Pradesh Paper Mills to West Coast and JK bought Sirpur out of insolvency.

COVID (FY21) shut schools and offices and collapsed W&P demand. JK's margin fell to 20%, and West Coast, Andhra and TNPL made losses. Then came the FY22–FY23 super-cycle. Reopening demand, a global pulp spike (BHKP near $750–800 a tonne), Europe's energy crisis cutting W&P supply, and freight costs that made imports dear pushed Indian prices up about 30%. Operating margins reached 26–35% across the majors in FY23, and the top-ten net-debt ratio fell to about 0.3 times by FY24. The FY24–FY26 down-leg reversed everything: pulp fell, China's board glut arrived, Indonesian board surged, imports set a record and wood peaked. Industry EBITDA margin hit 6.9% in Q3 FY25, the lowest in two decades against a long-term average of 16.6% (BOB Capital, INDICATIVE). The policy response came in August 2025 with the MIP.

Three lessons from the cycles shape how the industry behaves today. First, capacity is the enemy. The FY11–FY14 glut, which left the industry at 4.6× net debt, is the reason no listed major has announced a large new writing-paper machine since. Capital now goes into pulp (JK's BCTMP), power (JK's hybrid plant, TNPL's boiler revamp), tissue (TNPL, Andhra) and converting (JK). ITC grew by buying Lalkuan rather than building. Second, peaks are short and set by outsiders. FY23 was made by a world pulp spike, a European energy crisis and high freight, none of them Indian. Third, policy arrives late. The MIP came almost two years after imports began to surge, and the anti-dumping duty, if it ever comes, a year after that.

In plain terms

Indian paper goes through a big cycle roughly every five to eight years. Mills earn a lot, build too much or attract too many imports, prices fall, debt rises, mills stop building, and prices recover. FY23 was the best year in living memory and FY26 one of the worst. The industry has learnt not to build new paper machines in a boom, which is why today's downturn hurt profits but did not break balance sheets the way the FY14 glut did.

Reading a paper mill: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain almost everything about an Indian paper company. Here is what each one means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, where JK and the reference mills stand, and how each one can mislead.

The paper equation

Every paper company's revenue and profit can be written as one equation. Revenue is the sum, across grades, of installed capacity multiplied by utilisation and by net realisation per tonne, plus any converting or other revenue. Operating profit (EBITDA) is the volume sold multiplied by the spread between realisation and the variable cost per tonne (wood, purchased pulp, chemicals, energy, freight, other conversion costs), minus fixed costs, plus the profit of any converting business. Some terms management controls: capacity (slowly, through capex), utilisation (through maintenance and debottlenecking), product mix, conversion efficiency, the share of its own pulp and power, and discounting. Others it does not: the import-parity price, the delivered price of wood, world pulp prices and the rupee.

5. Discount-to-gross ratio

What it is. Trade discounts, schemes and rebates as a share of gross sales. It is the hidden price lever. Formula. Discounts ÷ sales before discounts. Worked example. JK's FY26 gross sales were ₹7,462 crore and discounts ₹492.9 crore, which is 6.6%; in FY25 discounts were ₹402.1 crore, 5.8%. Drivers. Dealer competition, inventory clearance before price cuts, import pressure. Benchmark. Below 5% good, 5–7% average, above 7% weak (INDICATIVE; few companies disclose it). Comparison. Not disclosed by peers.

The definition trap. Companies that headline gross turnover (JK's press releases do) look 7% bigger than on the audited net line. A rising discount ratio can cut realisation while list prices stay flat, so a mill can report "stable prices" during a price war.

In plain terms

JK's ₹7,569 crore "turnover" and its ₹7,076 crore "revenue from operations" are the same sales before and after ₹493 crore of discounts to dealers. In FY26 the discounts grew faster than sales. That is what a price war looks like from inside a paper company: the list price stays the same and the cheque gets smaller.

7. Wood cost per tonne and wood per tonne of product

What it is. Two numbers: the delivered price of a tonne of wood, and the tonnes of wood needed per tonne of product. Their product is the wood bill per tonne. Formula. Value of wood consumed ÷ tonnes consumed; and tonnes of wood ÷ tonnes of product. Worked example. 9. Energy cost per tonne and captive power

12. Working-capital days

What it is. How long cash is tied up between buying wood and collecting from customers. Formula. Inventory days + receivable days − payable days (the cash conversion cycle); rating agencies also use gross current assets (GCA) days = gross current assets ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. JK FY26: receivables 28 days, inventory 113 days, payables 71 days, so a cycle of about 69 days; CRISIL puts GCA days at 125–130. Finished-goods inventory rose from 13 to 18 days, and the company's current ratio fell from 1.83 to 1.39. Drivers. Wood stocking (mills hold 30–50 days of wood; Andhra wants to go from 30 to 50), dealer credit, the GST inverted duty on boxes (refunds tie up cash) and export receivables. Benchmark. GCA below 110 days good, 110–140 average, above 140 weak; inventory below 80 days good, 80–110 average, above 110 weak. Comparison. Peers' figures were not compiled for this report (n/a). The definition trap. A rising inventory number can be strategic (buying wood ahead of a price rise) or a sign of unsold paper. JK's FY26 build was mainly finished goods, which is the second kind.

14. Landed import parity against the domestic price

What it is. The gap between what an imported tonne costs delivered to an Indian customer and what Indian mills charge. It tells you whether a mill has pricing room or is about to lose volume. Formula. CIF price × ₹/$ + basic customs duty + port and clearing + inland freight, compared with the domestic mill price on the same delivered basis. Worked example. FY25 Indonesian board landed at ₹54,675 a tonne against an Indian mill price of ₹67,244, a gap of about ₹12,500 against the Indian mill. Under the MIP, delivered parity is about ₹71,500–72,000 (Section 06, ILLUSTRATIVE). Drivers. World prices, the rupee (₹1 weaker on a $750 tonne adds ₹750), freight, duty and trade remedies. Benchmark. Domestic price at or below delivered parity is sustainable; more than ₹3,000–5,000 above parity invites imports (INDICATIVE). Comparison. Only DGTR publishes both sides. The definition trap. The MIP is a CIF price before duty, port and freight, not a domestic floor, and it covers only five HS lines.

In plain terms

To know whether an Indian mill can raise its price, compare it with what the same tonne would cost the customer if it came from Indonesia, including shipping, port charges and trucking. If the Indian price is lower, the mill has room. If it is higher, imports will take the order. The rupee matters a lot: every ₹1 fall against the dollar adds about ₹750 to the cost of a $750 imported tonne.

15. Sensitivities: realisation, wood, pulp and the rupee (ILLUSTRATIVE)

The KPIs above combine into a small set of sensitivities that explain most of the swing in a paper company's earnings. All of the figures below are ILLUSTRATIVE, built on JK Paper's FY26 base (8.19 lakh tonnes sold, operating EBITDA ₹924 crore, about 18.1 crore shares, tax at about 25%).

Realisation. A ₹1,000 a tonne change in net realisation on 8.19 lakh tonnes moves EBITDA by about ₹82 crore, roughly 9% of FY26 operating EBITDA. After tax that is about ₹61 crore, or about ₹3.4 a share. Because realisations moved by ₹15,000–20,000 a tonne between FY23 and FY26 at the pure W&P mills, price is the biggest swing factor for the industry.

Wood. A ₹1,000 a tonne change in delivered wood on JK's 10.18 lakh tonnes of consumption moves EBITDA by about ₹100 crore, about 11% of FY26 EBITDA. A return from ₹14,460 to about ₹12,000, roughly the FY24–FY25 midpoint, would add about ₹250 crore, more than a quarter of FY26 EBITDA, without any change in paper prices. That is the reason the wood cycle matters so much for FY28.

Imported pulp. A $50 a tonne change in world pulp, on roughly 1–1.5 lakh tonnes of purchased pulp a year at ₹94.9 to the dollar, moves EBITDA by about ₹47–71 crore. The BCTMP plant reduces this exposure from FY27, since hardwood BCTMP is now made in-house and only softwood is still imported.

The rupee. A ₹1 weaker rupee raises the import parity of a $750–850 tonne of board or W&P by roughly ₹750–850. If Indian mills could pass all of that through on 8.19 lakh tonnes, EBITDA would rise by about ₹60–70 crore, less a few crore of extra cost on imported pulp, coal and euro-denominated capex payables. In practice pass-through is partial and lagged, but for an Indian paper mill a weaker rupee is, on balance, good news: it makes every imported competitor dearer. The rupee's slide from about ₹85 to ₹94.9 over two years is worth several thousand rupees a tonne of import parity, and it is one of the three reasons realisations turned in Q4 FY26.

Putting them together. A modest recovery scenario (realisation +₹3,000, wood −₹1,500, other costs flat) would add roughly ₹246 crore plus ₹150 crore, about ₹400 crore, to JK's EBITDA, taking it from ₹924 crore toward ₹1,300 crore, close to IDBI Capital's FY27 estimate of ₹1,272 crore. A relapse (realisation −₹3,000, wood flat) would take EBITDA down to about ₹680 crore and net debt/EBITDA to about 2.7×. These are scenario arithmetic, not forecasts.

Definition traps, collected

  • Capacity versus output. Rated tpa, production and sales are three different numbers; above-100% utilisation is normal.
  • Gross versus net revenue. JK's "turnover" of ₹7,569 crore is before ₹493 crore of discounts; audited revenue from operations is ₹7,076 crore.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. JK's headline EBITDA of ₹984 crore includes ₹60 crore of other income; operating EBITDA is ₹924 crore (13.1%). Cash-rich peers look much better including other income.
  • Total versus owners' PAT. JK's FY26 PAT is ₹274 crore in total (₹273.5 crore audited) and ₹265.8 crore attributable to owners, after Quadragen's and Borkar's minorities. CRISIL's "₹364 crore" is profit before tax.
  • Paper versus everything. JK's consolidated revenue includes corrugated boxes, mono-cartons, animal nutrition and defence electronics; paper is about half of revenue (three-quarters in FY22).
  • Segment PBIT versus EBITDA. ITC reports its paper segment at PBIT (9.1%), gross of 21% inter-segment sales; derived EBITDA is about 13.5% (INDICATIVE).
  • Consolidation overlaps. West Coast consolidated includes Andhra Paper in full; never add the two.
  • One-off tax. TNPL's FY26 PAT of ₹248 crore includes a ~₹219 crore deferred-tax credit; its P/E of 3.5× is meaningless.
  • Capacity bases. Seshasayee's 2.55 lakh tpa includes Tirunelveli; Andhra's 2.98 lakh tpa (company) versus 2.56 (CARE).
  • List versus net price. Distributor lists sit 10–20% above mill net realisations.
  • Three net-debt numbers. Company, screener and agency definitions differ by up to ₹200 crore for JK.
  • Wood units. JK's wood tonnes are "as received" and its cost is delivered; farm-gate prices are lower.
  • The MIP is CIF. ₹67,220 is the import value before duty, port and freight, not the domestic price.
  • Restatements. JK's FY25 and Q1 FY26 were restated for the March 2026 scheme; FY24 PAT included a ₹174 crore deferred-tax reversal.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in paper analysis come from comparing numbers that look alike but are measured differently: gross and net sales, EBITDA with and without interest income, profit for all shareholders and for the owners, a whole company and its paper business. Before comparing two mills, check that each figure is on the same basis. This report uses audited revenue from operations, operating EBITDA and total reported PAT throughout, and says so wherever it departs.

How to read a paper company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its growth (one minute). Check whether the company reports gross turnover or net revenue and use net. Split growth into volume and price if volume is disclosed; if not, compare with the industry's import and price news. For JK, remember that converting businesses add revenue without paper tonnes.
  2. Operating EBITDA and margin, excluding other income (one minute). Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter: Q3 is seasonally weak and Q4/Q1 strong. A margin under 10% is trough territory; over 20% is a good year.
  3. EBITDA per tonne (one minute). Divide by tonnes if disclosed, or annualise and divide by the last annual volume. Compare with the company's own FY23 and FY26 levels (JK: ~₹25,900 and ₹11,280).
  4. Price commentary (one minute). Look for "realisation", "net sales realisation", "pricing" or "discounts" in the press release. JK's Q1 FY27 release credited "volume and mix", not price: that tells you the price recovery had not yet arrived.
  5. Wood and input costs (one minute). Look for "wood", "raw material" and "fibre" language. "Moderating" or "stabilising" is the signal the market is waiting for. Check raw-material cost as a share of revenue.
  6. Imports and policy (one minute). Check whether the MIP is still in force, whether the anti-dumping duty has been notified, and what the latest IPMA import data shows. Note the rupee level.
  7. Below the line (one minute). Interest (JK's fell from ₹67 crore to ₹40 crore in Q1 FY27), depreciation (rises with new plants), other income (treasury), exceptional items (Labour Codes, restatement losses) and tax (deferred-tax credits). Separate operating improvement from financial improvement.
  8. Minority interest (thirty seconds). For JK, total PAT and owners' PAT differ by Quadragen's 35% and Borkar's 12.6% minorities. Use one consistently.
  9. Balance sheet (one minute, half-yearly). Net debt, inventory days and capex. For JK, the test is net debt/EBITDA back below 2× (CRISIL's trigger).
  10. Red flags (one and a half minutes). Rising discounts while volumes rise (price war); finished-goods inventory building; margins falling while wood is easing (price problem, not cost); a large other-income contribution; repeated "challenging market" language; subsidiaries' losses (JKPL Packaging Products lost ₹12 crore and Borkar ₹6 crore in FY26); and any change in consolidation that breaks comparability.

For the next set of numbers, the calendar is Seshasayee on 24 October 2026, Andhra on 30 October, JK Paper and ITC on 2 November, and West Coast on 6 November; TNPL had not announced a date by 23 October. The Q2 FY27 (Sep-26) quarter is seasonally weaker than Q1 for W&P, so a stable margin would be a good result, and any sign of lower wood cost or smaller discounts would matter more than the headline profit.

In plain terms

When a paper company reports, look first at operating profit per tonne, then at what management says about prices and wood. Profit growth that comes from lower interest or more tonnes is welcome but limited. Profit growth that comes from better prices or cheaper wood is the real recovery. In mid-2026, JK's numbers showed the first kind; the second kind is what Q2 and Q3 FY27 need to show.

Why converting

The strategic logic has three parts. Growth: corrugated-box demand in India was about 7–8 million tonnes in FY25 and JK expects 11–12 million tonnes by FY30, against 2–3% growth in writing paper; mono-cartons grow 7–8% a year. Fragmentation: more than 10,000 box makers operate at 65–70% utilisation, so a well-capitalised buyer can consolidate and win national FMCG and e-commerce contracts that small converters cannot serve. Stability: converters earn a margin over paper rather than a spread over wood, so their profits move less with the paper cycle. JK says "normal" corrugation EBITDA is 12–14%, falling to about 10% in a bad year (May 2024 call), against paper margins that swung from 31% to 13%. The awkward parts

  • Animal nutrition and defence electronics are not packaging. Quadragen (feed additives) and Delopt are diversifications in their own right. Management describes Quadragen as the start of a "scalable speciality chemicals platform". That may create value, but it is a different business, with a future cash call for the remaining 35%.
  • The GST inverted duty. Since 22 September 2025, JK's corrugation units pay 18% GST on kraft paper and charge 5% on boxes, tying up working capital until refunds arrive. JK named this as a cause of "lesser buying for two months" after the change.
  • Input costs. The West Asia crisis lifted packaging raw-material costs 10–15% in 2026, passed on with a lag.
  • Integration risk. Four deals in about 20 months, two loss-making subsidiaries in FY26, and a new President (Paper), Gunjan Shah, from 12 October 2026.
In plain terms

JK Paper decided it did not want to be only a paper mill. It bought companies that turn paper into boxes and cartons, plus an animal-feed-additive maker, so now only half its sales are paper. Box-making earns a smaller but steadier margin. So far the deals have made JK's profits less volatile without yet making them higher, and they were paid for partly with debt at the bottom of the cycle. Whether they pay off depends on growing those businesses while the paper cycle recovers.

ITC buys Century: consolidation at a quarter of replacement cost

The largest paper deal in a decade doubled the price leader's lead over JK and showed what an Indian integrated mill is worth when it changes hands.

On 1 August 2026, ITC completed its purchase of Century Pulp & Paper's mill at Lalkuan, Uttarakhand, from Aditya Birla Real Estate for ₹3,498 crore, as a cash-free, debt-free slump sale. The business-transfer agreement had been signed on 31 March 2025 for "up to ₹3,500 crore"; the Competition Commission approved it on 16 December 2025, and the environment ministry approved the transfer of the leased forest land on 18 March 2026. The mill, commissioned in 1984, has about 4.8 lakh tpa of capacity, historically in copier and maplitho paper (the Paperkraft brand), tissue and board, using a mix of agro and wood fibre. ITC's stated rationale was that its own mills were "near saturation" and that Lalkuan sits near raw material and customers in north India. Aditya Birla Real Estate shows pulp and paper as a discontinued business and is now a property company.

What the price says about value

₹3,498 crore for 4.8 lakh tpa is about ₹73,000 per tonne of annual capacity. Building a greenfield integrated mill (pulp line, paper machine, recovery boiler, power) costs roughly ₹2–3 lakh per tpa, and a brownfield machine ₹1.0–1.5 lakh (INDICATIVE). An old but working mill therefore changed hands at a quarter to a third of replacement cost. The listed mills are valued in the same range by the market. Dividing each company's enterprise value (market cap plus screener net debt) by its capacity gives about ₹1.12 lakh per tpa for JK (₹1.09 lakh on company net debt), about ₹67,000 for West Coast, ₹42,000 for TNPL, ₹37,000 for Seshasayee and ₹27,500 for Andhra. JK's figure includes its converting businesses, so its pure paper value per tpa is lower.

What it changes for JK and the industry

Scale. ITC now has roughly 1.5 million tonnes of paper and board capacity, about twice JK's 7.61 lakh tpa. Before the deal, Century was one of the five DGTR applicants in virgin board; now two companies, ITC and JK, form the core of the domestic VMLP industry, with TNPL, Emami and West Coast smaller. Pricing. ITC is the board price leader, and its captive demand (21% of segment revenue goes to its own businesses) means it can run its mills full without chasing every external order. Fewer independent sellers should mean more disciplined pricing in board, which helps JK. Copier. Paperkraft, Century's copier brand, now has ITC's distribution behind it, which is a direct challenge to JK Copier's 28% share. Grade mix. ITC has not said how it will use Lalkuan; if it converts W&P capacity to board over time, board supply rises; if it keeps Lalkuan in copier and maplitho, W&P competition stiffens. Either way, the mill's integration will show first in ITC's Q2 FY27 numbers on 2 November 2026, which include about two months of Lalkuan.

The valuation of ITC's paper business inside the group is modest. ICICI Securities' August 2026 sum-of-the-parts valued paperboards at about ₹8 a share at roughly 10 times earnings, a small part of ITC's ₹341 total. The deal matters far more to the paper industry than to ITC's shareholders.

In plain terms

ITC, already India's biggest maker of white board, bought a large north Indian paper mill for about a quarter of what it would cost to build one. That makes ITC about twice JK's size and leaves two big companies at the centre of the board market, which should make price wars less likely. It also gives ITC a copier brand to push against JK's. The deal shows why nobody builds new paper mills in India: buying an old one is far cheaper.
