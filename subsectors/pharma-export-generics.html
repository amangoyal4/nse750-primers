00

The one-page view: India makes the pills, three buyers set the price

Seven listed companies earn ₹58,000 crore a year selling medicines that lose most of their value within two years of launch. This is how the machine keeps running, and why the market prices its parts so differently.

The seven companies in this report make finished generic medicines, mostly in India and increasingly in the US and Europe, and sell them abroad: to American pharmacies through three giant buying groups, to German sickness-fund tenders, to British wholesalers, to Australian pharmacies and to drugstores across Central America. In FY26 they earned ₹58,115 crore of revenue at a 20.4% EBITDA margin and ₹6,661 crore of profit. About ₹26,600 crore, roughly 46% of the total, came from the United States (part of that figure is our estimate, because three of the seven do not report US sales in rupees for the full year). The market valued the group at ₹2.06 lakh crore on 5 October 2026: about 31 times FY26 profit and 17 times EV/EBITDA.

The defining fact is a paradox. Indian companies supply about 47% of all generic prescriptions filled in the US (IQVIA Institute, 2022 data), and generics are 89% of US prescriptions. Yet the US spent only $106 billion on generic medicines in 2025, 11.6% of its prescription-drug bill (AAM 2026 Savings Report), and India's pharma exports to the US were roughly $9.5 billion in FY26, a figure that rests on a secondary source quoting Pharmexcil. India supplies close to half the pills and collects well under a tenth of what America spends on them. Wholesalers, pharmacies, pharmacy-benefit managers and other manufacturers keep the rest. US generics are cheap by design, and the Indian exporter sits at the point in the chain where the squeeze is hardest.

The second fact is the treadmill. A generic is profitable only while few companies sell it. With one generic maker on the market, the price sits about 39% below the brand's; with two, about 54% below; with six or more, more than 95% below (FDA, 2019). Every product starts losing price the day a competitor arrives, so a US generics book shrinks at its price-erosion rate, somewhere between 3% and 10% a year depending on the cycle, unless the company keeps launching. Earnings are a race between erosion on the old portfolio and the new launches that replace it, and the race never ends.

Three forces decide who wins the race. The buyers: Red Oak (Cardinal Health with CVS), WBAD (Walgreens with Cencora) and ClarusONE (McKesson with Walmart) have bought more than 90% of US generic volume since 2018 and re-bid every product whenever a new supplier appears. The regulator: one bad USFDA inspection freezes new approvals from a plant for one to three years. In 2025–26 that hit Granules' main finished-dose plant and Aurobindo's injectables arm Eugia, and left open questions at Caplin and Strides. The escape routes: the companies that earn the most, and that the market values highest, are those that have stepped furthest off the commodity treadmill. They make products few others can make (Rubicon's specialty generics, Granules' controlled substances, injectables), own a channel (Caplin's Central American distribution, Marksans' retailer shelves), or sell into markets with different rules (Aurobindo's European business, now above €1 billion a year).

FY26–27 is a hinge year because five things changed at once. First, gRevlimid ended. The volume-limited generic of the blood-cancer drug Revlimid, which earned Indian makers windfall profits from March 2022, went fully generic on 31 January 2026, and Aurobindo's US sales fell to $387 million in Q4 FY26, down 18% in constant currency. Second, price erosion is speeding up again as US drug shortages ease. Lupin, a diversified peer outside this group, guides to high-single to low-double-digit erosion in FY27. Third, tariffs. The April 2026 Section 232 proclamation put a 100% duty on patented drugs but explicitly exempted generics; the Commerce Department must report on generics by about April 2027. Fourth, the FDA. Eugia Unit-I received a warning letter on 14 August 2026, and Granules' Gagillapur plant has been under one since February 2025. Fifth, the rupee fell from about ₹87–88 per dollar in FY26 to about ₹92–95 in Q1 FY27, so rupee growth now overstates dollar growth by several percentage points.

In plain terms

These seven companies are the factories behind some of America's and Europe's cheapest medicines. They sell huge volumes at tiny prices to a handful of very powerful buyers, and every product they sell gets cheaper each year as rivals pile in. They make money by launching new products faster than old ones lose value, by keeping their plants clean enough that the US regulator keeps approving those launches, and, for the best of them, by finding products or channels where the usual price collapse does not happen.

01

Seven exporters, one rule: where the revenue comes from today

A company belongs here if most of its sales today come from finished generic medicines sold abroad. The rule is simple; one member sits right on the line, and we say so.

A generic formulation is a finished medicine (a tablet, capsule, injection, cream or nasal spray) that copies a drug whose patents have expired. It wins approval by showing that it delivers the same amount of the same active ingredient into the bloodstream at the same rate as the original, a test called bioequivalence, so it needs no new clinical trials. In the US it is filed as an Abbreviated New Drug Application (ANDA); in Europe, Britain and Australia as a marketing authorisation. It is sold under the chemical name or a retailer's label, and in most regulated markets the pharmacist can substitute it for the brand without asking the doctor. That last point drives everything else: the maker has no brand loyalty to protect, so price is the main weapon.

This report covers the seven companies in the NSE 750 whose revenue today comes mostly from finished generic medicines exported to regulated and emerging markets: the US, Europe, the UK, Australia and New Zealand, Canada, Latin America and Africa. That definition leaves out four neighbouring groups, each covered elsewhere or classified separately:

  • India branded formulations (see the sibling report on India branded formulations). In India the doctor chooses the brand, medical representatives sell it, and the company holds pricing power. The economics are close to the opposite of an export generic.
  • API and intermediate makers (see the sibling report on APIs and intermediates). They sell the active ingredient by the kilogram to formulators, a chemical business with a different cost curve and different customers.
  • CDMOs, which manufacture under contract for drug innovators and earn fees instead of selling their own products.
  • The diversified majors: Sun, Cipla, Dr Reddy's, Zydus, Lupin and Glenmark. All are large in the US, several larger than anyone here except Aurobindo, but each also owns a big India branded franchise that changes its margins, growth and valuation. They are classified separately so that their India businesses do not blur this picture.

The rule is where most revenue comes from today, not where a company hopes to be in five years or where it started. That keeps the classification checkable from published segment data. The evidence for each member is in the table below.

02

How a generic pill earns: the export value chain

Between a chemical plant in Telangana and a pharmacy counter in Ohio, about eight hands touch the pill. The Indian manufacturer controls two of them and is paid last.

A US generic begins with chemistry. A key starting material, often from China, is turned into an intermediate and then into the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API), which many Indian exporters make in-house. A finished-dose plant, usually in India and sometimes in the US, turns the API into tablets, capsules or injections. The product is then owned and sold by the company's US subsidiary, the legal holder of the ANDA and the "labeller" whose name is on the bottle: Aurobindo Pharma USA, Granules Pharmaceuticals Inc., Strides Pharma Inc., or Advagen for Rubicon. From there it passes through a buying-group contract, a wholesaler and a pharmacy before it reaches a patient whose co-payment, and the pharmacy's reimbursement, are set by an insurer or a pharmacy-benefit manager (PBM).

The manufacturer controls Steps 1–3. The price it receives is decided at Step 4, by a buyer that also owns Steps 5 and, through its pharmacy partner, part of Step 6.

Three features of the chain decide the manufacturer's economics. The first is automatic substitution. State laws let, and usually require, the pharmacist to dispense the cheapest product rated therapeutically equivalent (AB-rated) to the one prescribed. The doctor writes "atorvastatin" and the pharmacy's buyer picks whose atorvastatin goes on the shelf. The manufacturer has no relationship with the doctor or the patient worth paying for, which is why US generic makers spend almost nothing on marketing.

The second is chargebacks and gross-to-net. The manufacturer invoices the wholesaler at a list price called the wholesale acquisition cost (WAC). The wholesaler then sells to the pharmacy at the lower contract price the buying group has negotiated, and the manufacturer refunds the difference: the chargeback. On top come rebates, price protection when list prices fall, shelf-stock adjustments, returns, Medicaid rebates and failure-to-supply penalties. Together these deductions commonly take 50–70% of gross invoiced sales for a US generic (an industry-convention estimate, not a disclosed figure for any of the seven). The revenue Indian companies report is already net of all this, which is why a US subsidiary's gross invoicing can be two to two-and-a-half times its reported sales.

The third is the failure-to-supply clause. A buying-group contract is a promise to ship. If the manufacturer runs short, it pays the buyer the difference between its contract price and whatever the buyer had to pay someone else. That is why exporters keep two to three months of US safety stock and long API inventories, and why working capital in this business runs at 100–150 days of sales. It also explains why an FDA problem that slows a plant hurts even before any warning letter arrives.

Who keeps the dollar? Studies of the US distribution chain by the USC Schaeffer Center (Sood and colleagues, 2017, and follow-ups) suggest that on generics the manufacturer keeps about a third or less of each pharmacy dollar, with wholesalers, pharmacies and PBMs keeping the rest, because pharmacy and PBM spreads are wide when acquisition costs are low. Treat that share as an estimate: the source was not re-checked for this report. It is consistent with the headline paradox: Indian exports of about $9.5 billion against $106 billion of US generic spending.

Other markets, other rules

Outside the US the chain changes, and so does who sets the price. In much of Europe the payer runs a tender or sets a reference price; in Britain the market sets prices that the NHS then reimburses; in Central America the company can own the distribution itself. This matters because three of the seven earn a large share of revenue outside the US, and several of the group's escape routes run through these markets.

In plain terms

An Indian generic maker sells its pill at a contract price agreed with a giant buyer, then refunds the wholesaler for discounts it never sees. Its "revenue" is what is left after those refunds, often less than half of what it first invoiced. Making the pill is the cheap, easy part; the profit lies in selling something few others can, or in owning the route to the customer.

03

Size: four billion prescriptions, and India's thin slice of the dollars

The US market these companies serve is enormous in pills and modest in money, and it has barely grown in dollars for a decade. The seven grow by taking share, launching, and diversifying away from it.

The anchor market is the US. In 2025 Americans filled about 4 billion generic prescriptions, 89% of all prescriptions, for $106 billion, 11.6% of total prescription-drug spending. Branded medicines filled 485 million prescriptions for $804 billion. Generics and biosimilars saved the US system an estimated $496 billion in 2025 and $3.6 trillion over ten years (all AAM 2026 Savings Report). At ₹87 to the dollar, the US generic market is worth about ₹9.2 lakh crore a year at system prices, roughly three and a half times the entire Indian domestic pharmaceutical market.

That dollar figure has been roughly flat for a decade. Volumes grow 1–3% a year with an ageing, more chronically ill population, but price deflation on the existing base offsets most of it, and new loss-of-exclusivity launches only top it up. Meanwhile brand spending has soared on GLP-1 obesity drugs, biologics and cancer medicines, so generics' share of spending has fallen from around 18% in the mid-2010s (earlier AAM reports, from memory) to 11.6% in 2025. For an exporter this means there is no rising tide. Growth has to come from share gains, new products, exclusivities, shortages and other markets.

India's place in that market is outsized in volume. Indian companies supplied about 47% of US generic prescriptions in 2022 and about four in ten of all US prescriptions; in hypertension, mental health, cholesterol, nervous-system and anti-ulcer medicines they supply the majority. The IQVIA Institute estimates that Indian medicines saved the US $219 billion in 2022 and $1.3 trillion over 2013–22. If the 47% share held on 2025's volumes, Indian firms would be dispensing about 1.9 billion US prescriptions a year (our arithmetic).

The export side of the ledger

India's pharma exports hit a record $31.1 billion in FY26, up only 2.3%, after $30.47 billion (+9.4%) in FY25 (Pharmexcil). Finished formulations and biologicals were 79% of the total, about $24.5 billion. North America took 37.6% of FY25 exports. For FY26 the US figure is weaker: about $9.5 billion, down roughly 10% from about $10.5 billion, with the US share falling to around 30%. That number comes from a secondary source citing Pharmexcil and should be treated as an estimate until the official handbook confirms it. Plausible reasons include gRevlimid's price collapse, tariff-fear front-loading by US buyers in FY25 and renewed price erosion; Brazil, Europe and Africa made up the gap. Q1 FY27 total exports were about $8.1 billion (headline only; the regional split was not available). In August 2026 Pharmexcil cut its 2030 export target from $130 billion to $80–90 billion.

The rupee: why rupee growth overstates dollar growth

These companies sell in dollars, euros and pounds and pay most of their costs in rupees. The rupee averaged about ₹87–88 per dollar in FY26 (Aurobindo's FY26 US sales of ₹14,408 crore and $1,631 million imply ₹88.3) and about ₹92–95 in Q1 FY27 (Aurobindo's Q1 US sales imply ₹94.5; Strides' imply ₹92.4). Three buyers, ninety per cent: the US channel and its price-erosion machine Deep dive

Between 2013 and 2016 America's pharmacies and wholesalers merged their generic purchasing into three desks. Since then the buyer, not the maker, has set the price, and every Indian exporter's US margin has been decided in their tenders.

Until about 2013 a US generic manufacturer sold to dozens of customers: three big wholesalers, several national pharmacy chains, regional chains, mail-order houses and group purchasing organisations for independents. Each negotiated separately, and a manufacturer with a reliable supply record could hold price on a product for years. Then the customers combined. Between 2013 and 2016 the largest pharmacies paired up with the largest wholesalers to form joint sourcing ventures, and by 2018 three of them bought more than 90% of the generic drugs sold in America (Drug Channels' estimate).

How the price is actually set

Each group runs requests for proposals (RFPs) across hundreds of molecules, on an annual cycle and ad hoc whenever a new supplier offers a lower price. For each product the group awards volume to one or a few suppliers at a contract price, and the wholesaler partner then distributes to the pharmacy partner and beyond. When a new generic maker wins FDA approval, it approaches the groups with a bid below the incumbents'. The buyer can award it a "fair share", roughly one-nth of the market where n is the number of suppliers, and can use its bid to push every incumbent's price down at the next re-bid. The incumbent's choice is to match and keep volume, or refuse and lose 30–50% of that product's volume overnight (an industry estimate). There is no way round the groups: the three big wholesalers handle more than 90% of US drug distribution, and the large pharmacy chains are inside the groups.

The buyers' leverage showed in the numbers almost immediately. Oral-solid generic prices fell 10–15% a year through 2017 and 2018, a "massive and sustained period of generic price deflation" to which the consortia "contributed" (Drug Channels). They had help. The FDA cleared its approval backlog after the first Generic Drug User Fee Amendments (GDUFA, 2012), flooding the market with new competitors, and federal and state investigations into generic price-fixing from 2016 made any coordinated price rise unthinkable (Taro later paid $420 million in 2020; Teva entered a deferred prosecution agreement and paid $225 million in 2023). Indian exporters' return on capital fell from above 20% to the low teens, and the Nifty Pharma index fell roughly 40–50% from its April 2015 peak (an approximate figure).

In plain terms

Three purchasing desks buy nine in ten of America's generic pills. When a new supplier offers a lower price, the desk takes it and asks everyone else to match. That is why the price of an old generic almost never goes up in America, except when nobody else can supply it.

The counter-force: shortages and exits

The one thing that reliably restores pricing power is scarcity. When a supplier exits a product, or a plant is shut by the FDA, the remaining makers can raise prices or at least stop cutting them, and the buyer, facing empty shelves and failure-to-supply claims of its own, accepts. Scarcity peaked in early 2024. The US counted 323 active drug shortages in Q1 2024, an all-time high (ASHP and the University of Utah). Akorn went bankrupt in February 2023, Lannett went through Chapter 11 the same year, and Teva pruned its portfolio. US generic price erosion fell to about 1% in calendar 2023, the calmest year in a decade, and Indian exporters' US profits recovered just as the gRevlimid windfall was peaking.

What the companies do about it

No Indian exporter discloses how much of its US revenue comes from its top three customers. Industry convention is that a manufacturer's three or four largest customers account for 60–80% of its US sales (an estimate). The disclosure gap matters: the most important commercial relationship in the business is invisible in the published accounts of all seven companies. Reading their commentary, the defences fall into five groups.

  • Breadth. Aurobindo, with 737 final approvals, can offer a buyer hundreds of products and absorb a loss on any one. Its management says its "large product portfolio helps us to maintain… price stability".
  • Reliability. A supplier that never misses a shipment is worth a premium to a buyer exposed to failure-to-supply claims. This is a soft advantage that an FDA problem destroys.
  • Niche selection. Strides aims to rank top-three by volume in each product it sells; 37 such products make about 70% of its US revenue. Fewer competitors per product means fewer re-bids.
  • Hard-to-make products. Complex generics, injectables and controlled substances have fewer makers, so the buyer has fewer alternatives (section 9).
  • Different channels. Marksans sells store-brand OTC products to retailers who negotiate on their own account; Caplin sells through its own distributors in Central America. Neither escapes buyer power entirely, but neither sells into the three US desks for most of its revenue.

The buyer side is itself changing. Walgreens was taken private by Sycamore Partners in 2025, Rite Aid liquidated in 2025, and US policymakers continue to push PBM reform (delinking PBM pay from drug prices, more transparency). Any reshuffle of the buying groups' volumes, or a rule that changes how pharmacies are paid for generics, would alter the manufacturer's slice of the dollar. For now the structure is unchanged, and the base case for every US-facing member is mid-to-high single-digit price erosion on the existing book.

In plain terms

Shortages are the only time the generic maker gets the upper hand. From 2022 to 2024 there were many, and US prices barely fell. By 2025 the shortages had mostly cleared and the buyers went back to squeezing. A rising shortage count is good news for these companies' prices; a falling one is bad news.

05

Anatomy of a generic: from ANDA to zero Deep dive

A generic product earns most of its lifetime profit in the months when few others can sell it. Understanding that curve explains launches, lawsuits, the gRevlimid windfall and why a large book still needs constant replenishment.

Every product in an exporter's US book follows the same arc, from a decision taken five years before launch to a long, low-margin old age. The steps matter because each one is a lever, a cost or a risk that shows up in the company's numbers at a different time.

  • Selection (three to five years before launch). Pick a brand losing patent protection, estimate how many generics will arrive, check whether the API can be made in-house or bought, and decide whether to challenge the brand's patents.
  • Development (12–36 months). Formulation work, pilot and pivotal bioequivalence studies, and stability batches. Typical costs, by industry convention and not disclosed by any of the seven: about $0.5–1.5 million for a simple oral solid, $3–10 million or more for an injectable, nasal spray or eye drop, and $10–30 million or more for an inhaler or a product needing a clinical-endpoint study (ESTIMATES).
  • Filing and review. The FDA's goal is to act on a standard ANDA within about ten months, but one to three review cycles usually stretch approval to 18–36 months. First generics and shortage drugs can get priority. The company also pays user fees under GDUFA, an ANDA filing fee of roughly $0.3–0.4 million plus annual facility and program fees, with foreign finished-dose sites paying more (an estimate; the fee schedule changes each year).
  • Patent litigation. A filer that certifies the brand's patents are invalid or not infringed (a Paragraph IV certification) usually triggers a lawsuit and a 30-month stay on approval. Most cases settle with a licensed entry date. Launching "at risk" before the case ends exposes the company to damages that can be trebled.
  • Launch and exclusivity. The first Paragraph IV filer gets 180 days in which the FDA approves no other generic; only the brand's own authorised generic can compete. A separate 180-day exclusivity, Competitive Generic Therapy (CGT, since 2017), rewards the first approved generic of a drug with "inadequate" competition. The FDA granted 107 CGT approvals in its 2025 fiscal year.
  • Erosion, maturity and exit. Each new entrant triggers re-bids, the product settles into a long tail at 5–15% of the brand price with low single-digit annual erosion, and eventually it is pruned when the price no longer covers the cost of supply plus failure-to-supply risk. Pruning by many makers at once is a main cause of sterile-injectable shortages.

In plain terms

A generic medicine is like a market stall that is hugely profitable on the first day and barely worth opening a year later. The winners are the companies that are first through the gate, either because they won a patent fight, or because the product is so hard to make that few others can follow. Everyone else lives on thin margins from products that have already been through the price collapse.

gRevlimid: the windfall that defined 2022–26

The biggest example of the curve in recent years was gRevlimid, the generic of Bristol Myers Squibb's blood-cancer drug lenalidomide, which sold about $8–9 billion a year in the US before generics (BMS filings). The patent settlements allowed generic makers volume-limited entry from March 2022, with a capped share that rose each year, and unlimited entry only from 31 January 2026. Because volumes were capped, prices stayed high, and Indian participants are estimated to have earned $150–400 million a year each at the peak, at very high margins (an ESTIMATE). Aurobindo was one of them. When full generic entry arrived, its US sales fell to $387 million in Q4 FY26, 18% lower in constant currency, "mainly on account of high gRevlimid sales in Q4 FY25", and the product is now "insignificant". The diversified majors (Dr Reddy's, Cipla, Zydus) and Natco saw bigger hits. gRevlimid's end is the main reason FY26 US revenue was flat to down across Indian generics.

Portfolio math: a few products make most of the profit

Seen at company level, a US generics book is a portfolio in which a minority of products earns most of the money. The companies that disclose concentration confirm it. Strides sells 72 commercial products in the US; the 37 in which it holds a top-three position make about 70% of its US revenue. Rubicon's top five products are 39% of revenue and its top ten 55%, and its specialty products earn 36% of gross profit. Aurobindo has 737 final approvals and, by our estimate, 500-plus marketed products; its chief executive describes about $400 million a quarter as "the base", which works out to roughly $3 million a year per marketed product on average (an estimate). Caplin has more than 60 US approvals, of which 38–39 are launched. A common rule of thumb (an ESTIMATE) is that in a mature Indian oral-solid book the top 10% of products earn 40–60% of gross profit and the bottom 30–40% earn close to nothing, kept on the list because buyers want a full basket.

Demand beyond the script count: launches, shortages, shelves and tenders

US prescriptions grow slowly and generic dollars barely at all. The exporters' demand comes from six other places, and each member taps a different mix of them.

Underlying US demand is steady and slow. Prescription volumes grow about 1–3% a year with ageing and chronic disease, and generics already fill about 89–90% of prescriptions, so there is little substitution left to win. For an exporter the question is not how fast America's need for pills grows but how much of that need it can serve at a decent price. Six drivers answer it.

1. The loss-of-exclusivity calendar

Every brand that loses patent protection creates a new generic opportunity, worth most to the first one to three entrants. The 2025–2030 "patent cliff" is the largest since 2011–12, commonly put at $200–300 billion of brand sales losing exclusivity by 2030 (an ESTIMATE), but much of it is in biologics such as Keytruda and Stelara and in complex products. Oral-solid makers will see a smaller share of the wave than the headline suggests; companies with injectables, biosimilars and complex-generic capability will see more. FDA activity is a useful gauge of the flow: in its 2025 fiscal year the agency granted 689 full approvals and 250 tentative approvals, including 92 first generics and 125 complex generics.

Backward integration into the active ingredient is the group's single biggest cost lever. Aurobindo makes most of its own API and is now commissioning penicillin-G and 6-APA fermentation at Kakinada, a production-linked-incentive project of about ₹3,500 crore with capacity of about 15,000 tonnes a year of Pen-G and 3,600 tonnes of 6-APA. The project lost more than ₹200 crore at the EBITDA level in FY26 and turned EBITDA-positive in Q4; captive 6-APA cuts material cost but raises power and fuel bills. Granules is integrated from API to PFI to finished dose in paracetamol, metformin, ibuprofen, guaifenesin and methocarbamol, but imports its paracetamol precursor (PAP) largely from China. Marksans, Rubicon and Caplin buy most of their API, which makes them more exposed to Chinese price swings and less tied to their own chemistry.

The inputs nobody controls

Solvents, freight and packaging follow crude oil and geopolitics. Aurobindo's CFO said solvent prices had "gone by 2.5X" and freight rose sharply from March 2026. Strides' freight cost reached 6.2% of revenue in Q1 FY27 against 5.3% a year earlier, plus about ₹13 crore of geopolitical costs. Marksans' gross margin dipped to 54.4% in Q4 FY26 from 58.1% the quarter before on "rising input costs" before recovering to 59.1% in Q1 FY27. Labour is cheap but quality-assurance talent is scarce; the binding constraint on an Indian plant is the depth of its quality systems, not wages. Regulatory fees (GDUFA user fees for each ANDA and each plant) are small per product but add up across hundreds of filings.

Currency is the largest swing factor. Revenue is in dollars, euros and pounds; most costs are in rupees. Aurobindo estimates that each 1% fall in the rupee against the dollar adds about ₹100 crore to its EBITDA, close to 1.5% of its FY26 EBITDA. The 2026 fall from about ₹87–88 to ₹92–95 is therefore a material FY27 tailwind, partly offset by dearer imported inputs.

Seven escape routes from commodity generics Deep dive

Each member has given a different answer to the same problem: how to stop the price of everything you sell from falling every year. The market's valuation of each answer is the clearest guide to which ones it believes.

Commodity oral solids earn a manufacturing return and no more. Their margin depends on the cost of the active ingredient and on the pace of erosion, and both are largely outside the company's control. Every member of this group has spent the past decade trying to put a barrier between itself and that commodity middle. The barriers come in three kinds: complexity (products that are hard to make or to get approved), channel ownership (controlling the route to the customer), and geography (markets where the US buyer cartel does not reach). Channel ownership: controlling the route to the customer

The second route is to own the step where the price is set. Caplin registers its own products, runs its own warehouses and sells through its own distribution companies in about 11 Central and South American countries (Guatemala is about a third of its Latin American sales). It therefore keeps the distributor's margin, typically 15–25% in the region (an estimate), on top of the manufacturer's, which is how it earns 35–38% EBITDA on medicines that would earn about 20% sold through third parties. Marksans supplies retailers' own labels in the US, UK and Australia: the retailer, not a buying group, is the price-setter, and a retailer that has put Marksans' product on its shelf under its own name has a reason not to switch every year. Neither model escapes buyer power completely, as Caplin's slow government receivables and Marksans' 2025 UK erosion show, but both blunt it.

Geography: markets with different rules

The third route is to sell where the US buying desks do not reach. Aurobindo's European business, more than €1 billion in FY26 at about 20% EBITDA, grew 23% while its US business shrank. Strides earned about 46% of FY26 revenue outside the US, growing 21%, and is doubling its Africa business. Marksans earns about 48% outside North America. European tenders and reference prices are tough, but they move in steps rather than at every re-bid, and a supply-security push since 2023 has tilted some tenders towards reliable suppliers.

The two remaining answers: scale and patience

Aurobindo is the scale answer: so many products, plants and markets that no single price collapse matters, plus backward integration into penicillin chemistry and new bets on biosimilars and peptides. The cost of scale is that the company must launch 30–50 products a year just to grow, and must keep about 31 globally approved plants clean at the same time. Alembic is the patience answer: it built R&D and US-facing plants between 2015 and 2021, just as US prices collapsed, and has spent five years carrying the cost. The payback is now visible (US sales +49% in Q1 FY27 on seven launches, with about 15 more due in the rest of FY27), but its FY26 EBITDA margin of 15.3% and ROCE of 12.6% are still the group's lowest.

In plain terms

Investors are paying for one thing above all: the chance that a company's profits will not be eaten by price erosion. A company that sells hard-to-make products, or that owns its route to market, gets a much higher price for each rupee of profit than one that sells ordinary tablets to American buyers, even if today's margins look similar.

The escape routes are not free and none is permanent. Complexity costs R&D and longer development cycles, and the barrier erodes as rivals learn the technology: nasal sprays and ODTs had two or three makers a decade ago and have more now. Channel ownership brings country risk, currency risk and slow government payers. Geographic diversification brings its own tenders and price cuts. And every route still runs through FDA-inspected plants, which is the subject of the next section.

In plain terms

Each escape route buys time rather than permanent safety. A hard-to-make product becomes easier to make; a protected market opens up; a retailer finds a cheaper supplier. The companies that hold their valuations are the ones that keep finding the next barrier before the last one wears away.

The FDA as a growth gate: one inspection can freeze three years of launches Deep dive

Every US launch needs a clean plant behind it. In 2025–26 four of the seven members had an FDA observation, an adverse classification or a warning letter. Compliance is both the group's biggest cost line and its strongest moat.

The US Food and Drug Administration inspects every plant that makes medicines for the American market. A clean outcome is invisible; a bad one stops a company's growth. The mechanism is simple: a plant classified "official action indicated" (OAI) or under a warning letter receives no new product approvals until the FDA has re-inspected it and is satisfied, which typically takes 18–36 months. Existing products usually keep shipping. The damage is to the launches that were meant to replace eroding products, which is why a plant producing 2% of a company's revenue can hold back a large share of its future.

What an OAI really costs

The visible cost is remediation: third-party consultants, corrective and preventive action (CAPA) programmes, data-integrity audits, retraining and sometimes new equipment, estimated at ₹50–300 crore or more per site over 18–36 months. Case study: Granules, a warning letter that did not stop the margin

Granules' Gagillapur finished-dose plant near Hyderabad was inspected in August–September 2024, classified OAI and given a warning letter on 26 February 2025. Since then the company has continued to ship existing products from the site, moved selected products to its other Indian plant and its Virginia site, and completed what it calls an "essentially complete" remediation; the FDA has raised no concerns on the adequacy of its corrective actions. Gagillapur is the only one of its eight sites without a clean inspection report. About nine product approvals are waiting for the site to be cleared. The striking point is that Granules posted record results through the warning letter (Q1 FY27 PAT +60%, gross margin about 65%), because its growth came from controlled substances and other products made elsewhere. In plain terms

A US generic company cannot launch a new product unless the FDA is happy with the factory that makes it. When the FDA is unhappy, the factory keeps selling what it already sells, but nothing new is approved until it has been fixed and re-inspected, which usually takes two years or more. Since these companies need new products just to stand still, a frozen factory quietly turns growth into decline.

Compliance cuts both ways. The same rules that freeze an Indian plant also keep out thousands of smaller manufacturers, and a long record of clean inspections is part of what buyers pay for when they award volume. The 2025 shift to unannounced foreign inspections raises the frequency of adverse findings at Indian sites: four of the seven members have had a 483, an OAI or a warning letter in 2025–26. It also raises the value of a clean record. Investors should read a company's plant-by-plant FDA status as carefully as its income statement; it is the best leading indicator of launches two years out.

In plain terms

FDA trouble is the most common way an Indian exporter's growth story breaks, and the market usually reacts before the numbers do. The fix is slow and expensive, but a company that comes through it usually emerges with better plants, a cleaner record and a stronger case with American buyers, and the pending launches tend to arrive in a bunch once the site is cleared.

Tariffs, the US-plant hedge and the rulebook

America exempted generics from its 100% pharmaceutical tariff because it cannot do without them. The exemption is under review until about April 2027, and owning a US plant has become a strategic asset.

The rules that shape this sub-sector are mostly American. The Hatch-Waxman Act of 1984 created the ANDA, the Paragraph IV challenge and the 180-day first-filer exclusivity. The Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 added rules under which a first filer can forfeit its exclusivity. The Generic Drug User Fee Amendments (GDUFA I in 2012, II in 2017, III in 2022) funded faster FDA reviews; the backlog clearance after 2012 flooded the market with approvals and fed the 2015–19 erosion. The FDA Reauthorization Act of 2017 created CGT exclusivity. The DEA's annual quotas govern controlled substances. On the buy side, state substitution laws make generics interchangeable, and since 2015 generics have paid Medicaid inflation-penalty rebates if their prices rise faster than consumer prices. The price-fixing cases from 2016 onward hang over any attempt to raise prices together.

The exemption reflects dependence. America gets 47% of its generic prescriptions from Indian companies and saved an estimated $219 billion from Indian medicines in 2022. A tariff on generics would raise costs for pharmacies, insurers and Medicare almost immediately, and could worsen shortages in products where margins are already too thin for anyone to make them. That is the case for the exemption lasting. The case against is political: onshoring is a stated US goal, and a tariff is the simplest tool.

Rubicon bought a New Jersey plant out of bankruptcy for just $2.9 million; it passed an FDA inspection (VAI) in May 2026 and goes commercial in calendar 2027. Marksans already owns Time-Cap Labs in New York, Strides owns a plant at Chestnut Ridge, New York, and Granules has a site in Virginia. Washington's other onshoring tools, such as the FDA's PreCheck programme for new US plants and the Strategic API Reserve order of August 2025, point the same way.

Outside America the rules are national. In plain terms

The US has decided, for now, that it cannot afford to tax the cheap Indian medicines its pharmacies depend on. That decision will be reviewed by about April 2027. If a tariff does come, these companies would probably have to swallow most of it, because American buyers set the price. Those with factories inside the US, led by Aurobindo after buying Lannett, would be least exposed.

Forty years, four cycles: how the export model was built and broken

The Indian export generic industry has lived through a gold rush, a regulatory crackdown, a buyer revolt and a windfall. Each left a lesson that still shapes how investors price these companies.

India's export generic business is a product of American law. The Hatch-Waxman Act of 1984 made it possible to copy an off-patent drug with a short filing and no clinical trials, and India's 1970 Patents Act, which protected only manufacturing processes, had already produced a generation of chemists skilled at reverse-engineering molecules. Ranbaxy, Dr Reddy's and Lupin set up US subsidiaries in the 1990s; Aurobindo, founded in 1986, grew first as an API maker.

In plain terms

This industry has been through boom and bust several times. Reading an exporter: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number that matters in an export generics company, what it means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, how the seven compare, and the traps in how companies report it.

An export generics company can be read with about fifteen numbers. Some appear in every results release (US revenue, gross margin, EBITDA); others are scattered through investor presentations and conference calls (approvals, launches, plant status); a few are never disclosed at all (customer concentration). This section starts with the equation that links them, then takes each in turn. All worked examples are ILLUSTRATIVE and use round numbers.

The equation: erosion against launches, all times the rupee

Next year's US revenue starts from this year's book. Erosion takes a slice off it, volume and share gains add a little back, and pruning removes products no longer worth selling. New launches add revenue, and occasionally a limited-competition launch adds a lot. Finally the dollar total is translated into rupees at whatever exchange rate prevails. Profit then depends on the gross margin of that mix, minus the costs of the US front end, freight, R&D and the plants.

In plain terms

Every year an export generics company loses a slice of its US sales to falling prices. It wins the slice back only by launching new products, and it can launch only if the FDA is happy with its plants. The exchange rate then makes the result look better or worse in rupees. 1. US revenue, in dollars and in rupees

What it is: sales booked by the company's US (or North American) business, net of chargebacks and rebates. Formula: US revenue growth in dollars = this period's US sales in $ ÷ the same period last year − 1; growth in rupees adds the currency move. Worked example: $100 million of US sales at ₹87 is ₹870 crore; the same $100 million at ₹94 is ₹940 crore, 8% growth in rupees on zero growth in dollars. Drivers: launches against erosion, exclusivities, FDA status and acquisitions (Lannett adds about $300 million a year to Aurobindo from Q2 FY27). Benchmark: dollar growth above 10% is good, 3–10% average, below zero weak.

How the seven compare: Aurobindo is the giant, with $1,631 million in FY26 (₹14,408 crore, −2.7% in rupees) and $399 million in Q1 FY27, up 8.1% in rupees but down about 2% in dollars. Strides reported $284 million (+2%) and a flat $68 million in Q1. Rubicon's Q1 FY27 US sales were $55 million, +32% in dollars and +52% in rupees. Three companies do not give a full-year rupee US figure, so we estimate them: Granules about ₹3,900–4,000 crore (about $450–460 million, from its 72–79% North America share), Alembic about ₹2,000–2,100 crore, and Rubicon about $195–200 million. 3. Commercialisation rate: how many approvals become products

5. Base-portfolio price erosion

What it is: the year-on-year fall in the net price of products the company already sold a year ago. Formula: like-for-like price change = (this year's net price ÷ last year's net price − 1) on the same products, weighted by revenue. Worked example: on a ₹1,000 crore base, 4% erosion costs ₹40 crore a year and 10% costs ₹100 crore, the difference between growing and shrinking for most mid-sized companies. Drivers: the number of competitors per product, buying-group re-bids, shortages, competitor exits and FDA actions against rivals. Benchmark: 3–4% or less is good, 5–8% average, 10% or more weak.

How the seven compare: none publishes a number; all describe it in words. The market's reference point comes from outside the group: Lupin guides to high-single to low-double-digit erosion in FY27, up from "medium single digits". Strides cites "increased competition in certain products"; Marksans sees single-digit erosion on US prescription products and saw high-single-digit erosion on some UK products in FY26 before it stabilised. Aurobindo argues its breadth helps it "maintain… price stability". Definition trap: erosion is easy to hide inside "volume and mix", and a company with many recent launches can report rising US revenue while its base book shrinks quickly. Always compare US growth with the number of launches.

A useful cross-check is the launch arithmetic needed to stand still. At 7% erosion, a company with a ₹2,000 crore US book loses about ₹140 crore a year on price. If an ordinary launch earns ₹5 crore in its first year, it needs 28 such launches just to hold revenue flat, or two or three limited-competition launches worth ₹50–60 crore each. When a company guides to 10–15 launches against a book of that size, it is implicitly counting on a few of them being scarce products or on volume gains elsewhere. Dividing the year's lost rupees by the guided launch count gives the average first-year revenue each launch must deliver, a quick test of how realistic the guidance is.

In plain terms

Price erosion is the speed at which the floor moves backwards under these companies. Nobody reports it as a number, so you have to infer it from what managements say and from how much US sales grow given the launches they made. When managements start using words like "competitive intensity" or "pricing pressure in select products", erosion is speeding up.

6. Complex, injectable and specialty share

What it is: the share of US (or finished-dose) revenue from products with high barriers: complex generics, injectables, controlled substances, specialty and 505(b)(2) products. Formula: complex + injectable + specialty revenue ÷ US (or finished-dose) revenue. Worked example: if ₹400 crore of a ₹1,000 crore US book is complex and erodes at 3% a year while the other ₹600 crore erodes at 9%, blended erosion is 6.6%; raise the complex share to 60% and blended erosion falls to 5.4%. Drivers: R&D allocation, plant capability (sterile, containment, DEA licences) and acquisitions. Benchmark: above 40% is good, 15–40% average, below 15% weak.

How the seven compare: the measures are not comparable, but the direction is clear. Granules' complex generics reached 50% of finished doses in Q1 FY27. Rubicon's specialty products earn 36% of its gross profit, and almost its whole book is differentiated. Aurobindo's specialty and injectables arm (Eugia) earned $513 million in FY26, about 70% in the US, roughly 22% of US sales (our estimate). Caplin's US business is entirely sterile injectables and eye drops, but the US is only about 21% of its revenue. Strides earns about 5% of US sales from controlled substances and has five or six nasal-spray programmes. Alembic and Marksans do not disclose a share. Definition trap: each company defines "complex" to suit itself; Granules counts ADHD and controlled substances, Rubicon counts specialty by gross profit, not revenue.

7. Top-product concentration

What it is: the share of revenue from the largest product, the top five or the top ten; or, in Strides' version, the share from products where the company ranks top-three by volume. Formula: revenue of the top n products ÷ total (or US) revenue. Drivers: breadth of the book, exclusivities, and the timing of rivals' launches. Benchmark: top ten below 35% of revenue is good, 35–55% average, above 55% (or any single product above 20%) weak. How the seven compare: only two disclose (see the concentration chart in section 5). Aurobindo's concentration is low by construction, and gRevlimid is now "insignificant". The others do not disclose. Definition trap: high concentration in a few limited-competition products is good news until a competitor arrives; read it alongside the number of rivals expected in each.

8. R&D intensity and productivity

What it is: R&D spending as a share of revenue, and the revenue the company generates per rupee of R&D. Formula: R&D % = R&D expense ÷ revenue; R&D productivity = revenue ÷ R&D expense (Rubicon's own metric). Worked example: a ₹2,000 crore company spending ₹200 crore on R&D runs at 10% and a productivity of 10×; Rubicon ran at 5.9× in FY26 and 5.5× in Q1 FY27. Drivers: ambition and product complexity: bioequivalence studies for simple tablets are cheap; nasal sprays, injectables and clinical-endpoint studies are not. Benchmark: 8–12% is right for a complex or growth player, 5–7% for a scale player; below 3% would starve a US prescription pipeline, though Marksans' 3% is reasonable for an OTC business.

How the seven compare: Rubicon spends the most (10.4% in FY26, 10.9% in Q1 FY27; ₹500 crore guided over nine quarters). Alembic spends about 9% (₹186 crore in Q1 FY27), which is why its reported margin is about ten points below its pre-R&D margin. Aurobindo spends about 5% (₹1,590 crore in FY26), Granules about 5%, and Marksans 3% (₹89 crore). Strides and Caplin do not disclose an R&D percentage. Definition trap: some companies capitalise part of development spending, which flatters reported margins; and a high R&D ratio is only good if it produces approvals.

9. Gross margin (company-reported)

What it is: revenue minus the cost of materials, as a share of revenue. Formula: (revenue − cost of goods) ÷ revenue. Worked example: a company selling ₹1,000 crore with ₹400 crore of material costs has a 60% gross margin; moving 10% of sales into a product with a 75% gross margin, out of one at 45%, lifts the blend to 63%. Drivers: product mix (complex and specialty products carry higher margins), API integration, erosion, input costs (solvents, freight) and the rupee. Benchmark: above 65% is good, 55–65% average, below 55% weak.

How the seven compare: Rubicon leads at about 71% in FY26 (67.7% in Q1 FY27). Granules reached about 65% in Q1 FY27, a record, on its controlled-substance mix. 13. FDA compliance status

What it is: the inspection standing of every plant that supplies the US, and the share of revenue and pending approvals that sits at sites under adverse action. Formula: share of US revenue (and of pending ANDAs) from sites classified NAI or VAI. Worked example: if 40% of a company's 126 pending ANDAs sit at OAI sites, about 50 products cannot be approved until those sites are cleared, perhaps for two years; if 15% of them would have launched in each of those years, the company forgoes about 7–8 launches a year. Drivers: quality culture, data integrity, investment in systems and the FDA's inspection cadence. Benchmark: all US-facing sites NAI or VAI is good; one minor site OAI is average; a key site under a warning letter or import alert is weak.

How the seven compare: Rubicon (VAI at New Jersey; Pithampur 483 resolved), Marksans and Alembic have no live adverse actions cited. Granules has a warning letter at Gagillapur with about nine approvals waiting. Aurobindo has a warning letter at Eugia Unit-I and a reported OAI at Unit-III (weaker source). Definition trap: "% of revenue from the affected site" (Eugia Unit-I: about 2%) understates the damage; the right measure is the share of the pending pipeline behind the site.

In plain terms

A factory that makes 2% of today's sales can hold 40% of tomorrow's launches. When a company says an FDA problem affects "only a small part of revenue", ask how many of its pending approvals are waiting on that plant.

15. Customer concentration: the number nobody publishes

What it is: the share of US revenue from the largest customers, in practice the three buying groups and a few retailers. Formula: top-three customers' revenue ÷ US revenue. Worked example: a company with 70% of US sales through three buyers loses about a quarter of its US revenue if one of them moves its business elsewhere. Benchmark: below 50% would be good, 50–70% average, above 70% weak; industry convention puts most generic makers at 60–80% (an estimate). How the seven compare: none of the seven discloses it. Marksans' retailer customers are named in secondary sources, but not their shares. Annual-report notes on major customers were not checked for this report. Definition trap: the absence of the number is itself the trap; an investor cannot see the single most important commercial relationship in the business.

In practice, investors infer it. A company that sells mainly prescription generics in the US through its own subsidiary almost certainly has most of that revenue with the three buying groups and the three big wholesalers. A store-brand supplier like Marksans depends on a handful of large retailers. Caplin's Latin American revenue is spread across thousands of pharmacies and several governments, which makes it the least concentrated by customer but the most concentrated by country (Guatemala). Indian accounting standards (Ind AS 108) require disclosure, in the segment note of the annual report, of any single customer contributing 10% or more of total revenue; the notes were not checked for this report, and reading them is the most direct way to close the gap.

Definition traps, collected

  • Rupees versus dollars. FY27 rupee growth includes a currency gain of up to about ten points; use the company's dollar figure.
  • US versus North America. Granules and Marksans report North America, which includes Canada; Alembic's "US" is a business segment.

  • Gross margin. Company-reported figures (Aurobindo's "gross contribution", Strides' 59.7%) are not comparable with a database material-cost proxy, and the gap can exceed ten points.
  • EBITDA. Database EBITDA excludes other income and is struck after one-offs in operating costs; Alembic's reported margin understates its pre-R&D economics by about ten points; Caplin's "total income" (₹2,303 crore in FY26) includes other income, against revenue of ₹2,187 crore.
  • PAT. Strides' FY26 reported PAT of ₹575 crore compares with operational PAT of ₹518 crore, and its FY25 PAT included a demerger gain of about ₹3,300 crore, so year-on-year PAT growth is meaningless.
  • ROCE and net debt. Database and company bases differ on cash, leases and goodwill (Rubicon 28.4% vs ~36%; Strides net debt ₹1,254 crore vs ₹1,436 crore).

How to read an export generics company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. US revenue in dollars first. Compare with the same quarter last year and the previous quarter, and with the company's stated base (Aurobindo about $400 million a quarter before Lannett; Strides about $68 million). Flat dollars with rising rupees means no real growth.
  2. The rupee gap. Subtract dollar growth from rupee growth to see how much was currency; in Q1 FY27 it was 4–15 points depending on the company.
  3. Approvals and launches. How many came this quarter, how many are guided for the year, and which plant they come from. Fewer launches than guided is the first sign of trouble.
  4. Erosion language. Listen for "competitive intensity", "price erosion in select products" or "channel destocking". A shift from "mid single digit" to "high single digit" matters more than a revenue beat.
  5. Gross margin, quarter on quarter. A rise should be explained by mix (complex products, new launches); a fall by input costs (solvents, freight) or erosion. Unexplained moves are a red flag.
  6. EBITDA against guidance. Check R&D in rupees, not just as a percentage; a sudden R&D cut can flatter the margin while starving the pipeline.
  7. FDA news. Any Form 483, OAI, warning letter or establishment inspection report in the quarter, and the expected re-inspection date for affected plants.
  8. Non-US engines. Europe, emerging markets and ANZ growth, stripping out acquisitions (Marksans' QliniQ added about ₹44 crore in Q1 FY27; Lannett adds from Q2 FY27 at Aurobindo).
  9. Cash. Receivables and inventory against sales, operating cash flow against EBITDA (Strides' 47% in Q1 FY27 is a warning sign), and net debt.
  10. Specials. Exclusivity launches, product concentration, acquisitions, currency hedges and any change in tariff commentary.

Red flags to note immediately: US dollar sales falling for two quarters in a row; launch guidance withdrawn or "held back"; a new OAI or warning letter at a plant named in pending filings; gross margin down with no explanation; receivables growing much faster than sales; R&D falling as a share of revenue at a US prescription company; and any change in auditor commentary on gross-to-net accruals.

In plain terms

Start with US sales in dollars, check how much of the growth was just the currency, then look at launches and the FDA status of the plants behind them. Those three checks tell you more about an export generics company's next two years than its headline profit does.

The global lens: why Indian exporters trade at about double Teva's multiple

Indian exporters earn global-peer margins at far lower prices, carry less debt and grow faster. The market rewards all three, and adds a premium of its own.

The global generic industry is led by companies far larger than any Indian exporter. Teva had $17.3 billion of revenue in 2025, of which US generics were $3.66 billion (+2%). Technology and disruption: what could change the model

The threats to this sub-sector are mostly policy and competition rather than technology. The technology shifts that do matter (biosimilars, peptides, continuous manufacturing) cut both ways.

Export generics is not a business that a new technology can make obsolete; somebody will always need to make cheap copies of off-patent medicines. The risks are to who makes them, where, and at what price. Nine are worth tracking.

  • A US tariff on generics. The Commerce report due around April 2027 could recommend one. Because buyers hold the pricing power, even a 10–25% tariff would probably be absorbed largely by manufacturers (an estimate), and US plants become the hedge (section 11).
  • Faster price erosion. New shortages fell to a 2006 low in 2025 and the FDA keeps approving at a high rate (939 actions in its 2025 fiscal year). If shortages stay low, erosion of high-single to low-double digits could persist beyond FY27.
  • Tougher FDA inspections. Unannounced foreign inspections since May 2025 raise the frequency of adverse findings. Four of the seven members had a 483, OAI or warning letter in 2025–26.
  • Chinese finished-dose competition. Chinese plants are filing more ANDAs, especially in injectables, at lower prices, and already dominate key starting materials.
  • Changes on the buy side. Walgreens' take-private by Sycamore (2025) and Rite Aid's liquidation (2025) may reshuffle buying-group volumes. PBM reform could change how pharmacies are paid for generics and, indirectly, what they pay manufacturers.
  • Onshoring subsidies and "Buy American" procurement. Veterans Affairs and Defense purchasing, the Strategic API Reserve and FDA PreCheck all favour US manufacturing over time.
  • Biosimilar and peptide economics. Aurobindo's CuraTeQ notes ten or more players per biosimilar molecule in Europe and assumes price erosion of 75–85%. Management says that "even with a 75% price erosion, we expect to have around 65% gross margin" in the US, but each product costs $100 million or more to develop.
  • Continuous manufacturing and automation. AI-assisted process control and continuous production lower the cost of making oral solids anywhere, which over a long horizon could erode India's labour-cost edge (an estimate).
  • The currency. A rupee recovery would reverse the FY27 tailwind; each 1% is about ₹100 crore of Aurobindo EBITDA.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the export generics group

The bull case rests on cost, diversification and the US's dependence on India. The bear case rests on buyers, the FDA and a valuation that already assumes the escape routes work.

The bull case

  • Indispensable supplier: 47% of US generic prescriptions and about $219 billion of annual US savings (2022) explain why generics escaped the 100% Section 232 tariff.
  • Cost edge: Sandoz-like 20% EBITDA margins at far lower prices, from in-house API, Indian conversion costs and scale; net cash of about ₹1,900 crore across the group.
  • Escape routes working: Granules' complex share at 50%, Rubicon's 70% gross margin, Caplin's 35% EBITDA, Aurobindo's €1 billion Europe and Marksans' OTC growth all reduce dependence on commodity oral solids.
  • Launch waves: Alembic's ~22 FY27 launches, Granules' nine waiting approvals, Rubicon's 24 pending filings and Strides' nasal pipeline.
  • US-plant hedges: Lannett, Time-Cap, Chestnut Ridge, Virginia and New Jersey give the group an onshore footprint before any tariff decision.
  • The rupee: a weaker currency lifts FY27 earnings across the board.

The bear case

  • Erosion is back: shortages fell to a 2006 low for new cases in 2025, and the industry's reference guide is high-single to low-double-digit erosion for FY27.
  • FDA overhangs: warning letters at Granules and Eugia, a reported OAI at Eugia Unit-III, an unclassified 483 at Caplin and a pending outcome at Strides; possibly ~40% of Aurobindo's pending ANDAs stuck (unverified).
  • Tariff tail risk: the generics exemption is under review until about April 2027.
  • Flattered numbers: Q1 FY27 rupee growth includes up to ten points of currency; Aurobindo's US sales fell in dollars and Strides' were flat.
  • Valuation: 31× earnings and 17× EV/EBITDA, about double global peers; Rubicon at 92× requires years of high growth.
  • Hidden concentration: no member discloses its customer concentration, so the buying groups' leverage cannot be measured.

The two cases are less opposed than they look. Both accept that the US commodity business is a treadmill and that the group's future depends on how far each company escapes it. The bull case says the escapes are real and broadening: Granules' complex share rose 11 points in a year, Caplin's US label tripled, Europe is now a billion-euro business for Aurobindo, and the group's FY26 margin held at 20% through the end of gRevlimid. The bear case says the market has already paid for those escapes, while the risks that could interrupt them (FDA actions, a tariff, faster erosion) are rising rather than falling.
