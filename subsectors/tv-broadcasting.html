00

The one-page view: a shrinking screen, one giant and three very different bets

Indian television is losing homes and advertisers at the same time, while a single Reliance-Disney company sets the terms for everyone else.

Indian television earned ₹61,700 crore in calendar 2025, about 9% less than a year earlier and roughly a sixth less than in 2021. Both of its revenue streams fell together for the first time: advertising dropped 10.3% to ₹26,300 crore and subscription (what the industry calls "distribution") dropped 8.0% to ₹35,400 crore. About 11.5 million homes stopped paying for TV in a single year. Meanwhile the total Indian advertising market grew 13.5% to ₹1.5 lakh crore, and digital media took 63% of it. TV's share fell to 17.5%. This is not a cyclical dip in an otherwise healthy business. It is the moment the Indian TV industry started to look like American cable a decade ago.

Into this shrinking pie stepped a giant. On 14 November 2024, Reliance merged its Viacom18 entertainment business with Disney's Star India to create JioStar. In FY26 it earned ₹31,048 crore of operating revenue, about 2.1 times the combined revenue of the three listed broadcasters in this report. It holds the IPL and ICC cricket rights, the Star and Colors entertainment channels, the leading regional channels in Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam and Kannada, a 34% share of TV entertainment viewing and the JioHotstar streaming app with 530 million monthly users. It is unlisted, so it gets no row in our peer table, but it is the defining competitor for every company that does.

The three listed members are three different businesses that happen to share an industry code:

  • Sun TV Network is the regional cash machine: South India's dominant bouquet, a 50% operating margin, ₹7,727 crore of net cash and no borrowings. It also owns three cricket teams (Sunrisers Hyderabad in the IPL, Sunrisers Eastern Cape in South Africa and Sunrisers Leeds in England), which are now its fastest-growing business. It is 30% of the group's revenue but 70% of its market value.
  • Zee Entertainment is the national network in a margin crisis: ₹8,099 crore of revenue across Hindi and nine regional languages plus the ZEE5 app, but a reported EBITDA margin of just 4.3% in FY26 and 4.1% in Q1 FY27 (Jun-26). It trades at 0.56 times book value and faces a US$1.097 billion arbitration claim from JioStar, roughly 1.6 times its own market value.
  • Network18 is a news house with a slice of the giant: India's largest TV news network (CNBC-TV18, News18, CNN-News18) and a big digital news business (Moneycontrol, Firstpost), earning an operating margin of about 2%. Its reported profit comes almost entirely from its 7.51% look-through stake in JioStar and one-off gains.

Three things decide the next two years. First, how fast the pay-TV home disappears. Pay DTH (the satellite dish platforms) fell from 56.07 million active subscribers in June 2025 to 48.56 million in June 2026, and free TV (DD FreeDish, the government's free satellite service) is now larger than pay DTH at about 53 million homes. Subscription has been the industry's annuity, and the annuity is shrinking. Second, whether TV advertising stabilises. WPP Media forecasts TV ad spend falling another 6.8% in 2026, and the government removed the 12-minute-per-hour cap on ads in August 2026, which lets broadcasters sell more minutes into a market that wants fewer. Third, how JioStar uses its scale: cricket, the biggest Hindi and Southern entertainment channels, its own cable and fibre distribution through Reliance, and the largest streaming audience in the country.

The market has already sorted the three. Sun is worth ₹23,653 crore at 15 times earnings, of which roughly a third is cash. Zee is worth ₹6,605 crore, which is below its book value and only about three times its net cash after the warrant money arrived. Zee is 56% of the revenue but 19% of the market value; Sun is 30% of revenue and 70% of market value. Investors are paying only for margin and cash.

In plain terms

Television in India is still enormous (about 193 million TV homes and 745 million weekly viewers), but fewer of those homes pay for it each year and advertisers are moving their money to phones and connected TVs. The one company big enough to keep winning is JioStar. Of the listed players, Sun survives because Tamil and Telugu viewers have no real substitute for its channels, Zee is being squeezed between JioStar and free TV, and Network18 is a news business whose value sits mostly in a JioStar stake it does not control.

01

How a channel makes money: from a soap-opera set to a set-top box

Every broadcaster sells the same viewer twice: once to an advertiser and once, through a cable or DTH operator, to the viewer's own household.

A TV broadcaster is a content packager. It produces or buys programmes (daily serials, reality shows, films, cricket, news bulletins), schedules them into channels by language and genre, beams those channels by satellite to distribution platforms, and gets paid in two ways. Advertisers buy 10-second slots of airtime, priced off how many people of the right type are watching. Households pay a monthly fee for the channels they choose, collected by distribution platform operators (DPOs: DTH companies like Tata Play, cable operators, HITS and IPTV providers), who keep a share and pass the rest back to the broadcaster. Around those two core streams sit smaller ones: syndication of film and show rights to other platforms, theatrical film releases, music, streaming apps and, uniquely for Sun TV, cricket franchises.

The revenue lines, one by one

Advertising is the most visible stream and the most cyclical. Brands, mostly through media agencies, buy spots and sponsorships across general entertainment channels (GECs), films, news, kids and sports channels. FMCG companies (HUL, Reckitt, P&G, Nestlé, Dabur, Godrej Consumer, Marico, ITC) have long been the biggest buyers, followed by e-commerce, autos, telecom, financial services and, in election years, government and political parties. The price of a slot depends on the ratings the channel delivers in the advertiser's target group. Zee earned ₹3,224 crore from advertising in FY26 (40% of revenue) and Sun ₹1,292 crore on a consolidated basis (30%). Network18's ad revenue is not split out, but its rating agency CARE says advertising is "over 50%" of operating income.

Subscription is the annuity. Under TRAI's New Tariff Order (NTO), every pay channel carries a maximum retail price per month, and channels can be sold individually or in bouquets. The household pays the DPO; the DPO keeps a distribution fee of up to 20% of the channel's price plus its own network capacity fee, and remits the balance to the broadcaster. Sun's total subscription income was ₹1,980 crore in FY26 (46% of revenue); Zee's was ₹4,080 crore (50%), though Zee's figure also includes ZEE5's direct and telecom-bundled subscriptions and international feeds. Because subscription is paid per home, it rises with annual price increases and falls with every home that stops paying.

Syndication, films and music fill in the rest. Zee's "other sales and services" (about ₹795 crore in FY26) include film and show rights sold to other platforms, Zee Studios' theatrical releases and Zee Music. Sun's movie distribution and rights income jumped from ₹109 crore in FY25 to ₹405 crore in FY26 because Sun Pictures released Coolie, a Rajinikanth film, in August 2025. These lines are lumpy, which makes Sun's and Zee's quarters hard to compare except year-on-year.

Digital and streaming is where the industry hopes to replace what linear TV is losing. ZEE5 earned ₹1,489 crore in FY26 (up 53%); Sun NXT discloses nothing; Network18's digital properties (Moneycontrol, News18.com, Firstpost) earn from ads, the Moneycontrol Pro paywall (over one million paid subscribers) and lending leads. Sports franchises belong to Sun alone: cricket income of ₹652 crore in FY26, ₹630 crore in Q1 FY27 (Jun-26) alone.

Where the value is captured

Not every link in this chain earns the same. A broadcaster with a must-have regional bouquet captures the most: Sun owns the whole Tamil evening across entertainment, film, music, news and kids, and its content cost does not rise much when an extra home or an extra advertiser arrives. That is why its operating margin is 50%. A sports-rights holder captures the advertising premium of cricket but pays most of it straight back to the BCCI or ICC: JioStar's margin in the IPL quarter (Q1 FY27) was just 8.5%. News captures almost nothing: Network18 has the largest news audience in the country and earns about 2%. The distribution layer is squeezed hardest of all. DTH operators are losing subscribers at double-digit rates and most are barely profitable. And the talent (stars, directors, showrunners) captures rising fees as streaming platforms bid for the same content.

In plain terms

A TV channel is paid twice for every viewer: by the advertiser who wants that viewer's attention and by the viewer's household, through the cable or dish company. The best channels are the ones viewers would refuse to give up, because they can charge both sides. The worst position is a channel that viewers can easily replace with a free channel, YouTube or a streaming app, because then neither side pays much.

One more structural point shapes everything that follows. Content costs are largely fixed in the short run. A broadcaster commits to a year of daily episodes, a film library or a sports contract long before it knows what the ad market will do. When advertising falls 10%, costs barely move, so profit falls much further. When advertising rises, most of the extra rupee drops to profit. This operating leverage is why Zee's margin fell from 14.4% in FY25 to 4.3% in FY26 on a revenue decline of only 2.4%, and why the KPI masterclass spends so much time on the cost lines.

02

A pie that lost a tenth: the size and slide of Indian television

TV revenue has fallen four years running, and 2025 was the first year both advertising and subscription dropped hard together.

The FICCI-EY media and entertainment report, the industry's reference source, puts Indian linear TV revenue at ₹73,600 crore in 2021, ₹72,600 crore in 2022, ₹71,100 crore in 2023, ₹67,900 crore in 2024 and ₹61,700 crore in 2025, all in calendar years and gross of taxes. The decline accelerated from −1% to −2% to −4.5% to −9.1%. FICCI-EY expects ₹58,700 crore in 2026 and ₹53,500 crore by 2028, a further 5% a year of shrinkage. On those numbers the industry shrinks 16% from 2021 to 2025 and another 13% from 2025 to 2028.

The rest of media is growing fast

TV's decline is not part of a shrinking media economy. The whole Indian M&E industry grew 9% to ₹2,78,000 crore in 2025. Digital media crossed ₹1,00,000 crore (up 30.5%) and overtook TV as the largest segment. Advertising grew 13.5% to ₹1,50,000 crore, of which digital took ₹94,700 crore. Digital subscriptions rose 60% to ₹16,300 crore across 216 million paid video subscriptions in 143 million households. FICCI-EY counts "new media" at 47% of M&E revenue in 2025, heading to 53% by 2028. The money is not disappearing; it is moving.

There is one subtlety that changes the reading. FICCI-EY also counts advertising on connected TV (CTV: smart TVs streaming apps like JioHotstar or YouTube on the big screen). Linear plus CTV advertising was "stable" at ₹36,200 crore in 2025, with CTV growing 42% to ₹9,900 crore. FICCI-EY forecasts ₹37,700 crore for linear plus CTV in 2028. In other words, the advertising money is moving screens, not leaving the TV set. The households are still watching on the big screen in the living room; they are watching through an app instead of a set-top box. The winners are whoever sells CTV inventory at scale, which today means JioStar's JioHotstar and YouTube, not the linear sales teams of Zee or Sun.

In plain terms

Indians are not watching less television. The average TV home still watches 3 hours 44 minutes a day. What has changed is how the picture arrives (an app instead of a dish) and who gets paid for it (streaming platforms and YouTube instead of the old channel-plus-cable chain). For a listed broadcaster, the question is whether it can follow its viewers onto the new pipe and still get paid.

The members' own pie: flat revenue, falling profit

The two pure-play broadcasters, Sun and Zee, have held revenue roughly flat for four years: ₹11,771 crore in FY22, ₹11,860 crore in FY23, ₹12,919 crore in FY24, ₹12,314 crore in FY25 and ₹12,434 crore in FY26 (screener consolidated). Within that, Sun grew about 4.9% a year and Zee shrank slightly. Network18's reported revenue is not comparable across years because it consolidated Viacom18's entertainment business until 14 November 2024: ₹9,297 crore in FY24 became ₹2,121 crore of news and digital in FY26. Never compute a growth rate for Network18 across FY25 and FY26.

03

The vanishing pay-TV home: free TV now outnumbers pay DTH

Force 1. India lost 11.5 million paying TV homes in 2025, and the satellite dish platforms are shrinking at 13% a year.

For a decade, India's pay-TV story was one of growth. Cable digitisation between 2012 and 2017 forced every home onto a set-top box, made subscriber numbers visible and roughly doubled broadcasters' subscription revenue. TRAI's New Tariff Order of 2019 then gave broadcasters the right to set a price for every channel. Pay-TV homes peaked around 2018–19 at something above 120 million (an approximate industry figure) and have been falling since. In 2025 the fall turned into a rout: FICCI-EY says pay-TV homes dropped by 11.5 million to "just under 100 million", a number that still includes an estimated 8–10 million under-declared or pirated connections.

The cleanest data come from the four pay-DTH operators, which report active subscribers to TRAI every quarter. Pay DTH fell from 56.07 million in June 2025 to 52.78 million in September, 50.99 million in December, 49.09 million in March 2026 (as revised) and 48.56 million in June 2026. That is 7.5 million subscribers, or 13.4%, gone in one year. Tata Play remains the largest at 14.84 million (30.6%), followed by Airtel Digital TV (13.58 million), Sun Direct (10.93 million, owned by the Maran family that controls Sun TV, not by Sun TV Network itself) and Dish TV (9.21 million).

Where the homes went

The households did not stop watching television. They went three places. The first is free TV. DD FreeDish, the government broadcaster's free satellite platform, carried 484 channels by January 2026. FICCI-EY estimates free-TV homes at about 53 million in 2025, up 4.5 million in the year, and now "larger than active pay DTH". Free-TV homes have grown every year: 43 million in 2021, 45 million in 2022 and 2023, 49 million in 2024 and 53 million in 2025. These homes pay nothing to broadcasters, though broadcasters can still reach them with free-to-air channels and earn advertising. The second destination is broadband and streaming: connected TV sets reached about 68 million, with more than 40 million active every week. The third is IPTV, television delivered over fibre broadband, which is the one pay pipe that is growing: 6.21 million subscribers in June 2026, of which Airtel had 4.09 million and Hathway 1.28 million.

Why it matters for broadcasters: the price-versus-volume race

A broadcaster's subscription revenue equals the number of paying homes that carry its channels, times what it realises per home each month, times twelve. Broadcasters raise channel prices every year (Sun took a 7–8% tariff increase in FY26; Zee's next linear price increase comes with the next tariff cycle in February 2027). As long as price rises faster than homes fall, subscription revenue grows. Sun's domestic subscription grew 9.7% in FY26, which means price and mix more than covered any volume loss in the South. But in Q1 FY27 (Jun-26) Sun's domestic subscription grew only 3.3%, to ₹485.5 crore. If price rose 7–8%, then homes paying for Sun's channels fell by something like 4–5% (an indicative estimate; Sun does not disclose subscriber numbers). That is the first sign that the race is being lost even in the South.

In plain terms

Each year, broadcasters put up the price of their channels and hope that fewer homes cancel than the price rise makes up for. In FY26 Sun's price increase won comfortably. In the latest quarter, it barely won. If more Southern homes switch to free TV or streaming, a 7% price rise will no longer be enough, and subscription, which is almost half of Sun's revenue, will start to shrink.

The global base rate is sobering. American pay-TV lost roughly 6–8% of its subscribers a year from 2018 onward (an approximate figure from industry trackers). India's pay DTH is now falling faster than that, at 13%. FICCI-EY's own forecast, a 5% annual decline in linear TV revenue to 2028 with distribution falling only about 3% in 2026, may prove optimistic if DTH keeps its current pace. Two things could slow the bleeding. IPTV over fibre may recapture some homes and pay broadcasters through a new pipe. And regional markets with no free substitute for local-language pay channels may hold up better, which is the argument of Part 12. Neither changes the direction: the pay-TV home is shrinking, and every broadcaster's subscription line now depends on pricing power alone.

The ad money moved screens: TV's advertising recession

Force 2. India's ad market grew 13.5% in 2025 while TV advertising fell 10.3%, and 2026 is shaping up as another down year.

Television advertising in India fell to ₹26,300 crore in 2025, down 10.3%, in a year when the total ad market grew 13.5% and digital grew 26%. The one bright spot was sports channels, whose ad revenue rose 7%, which mostly helps JioStar.

The forecasts for 2026 are no better. Why the money left: four causes, one structural

The deleted rule: 12 minutes per hour is gone

For decades Indian channels were limited to 12 minutes of advertising per hour (10 minutes of commercials plus 2 of channel promotion) under the Cable Television Networks Rules, reinforced by TRAI regulations from 2012. Enforcement was patchy and TRAI's version was in court for years, but the cap shaped how analysts modelled the business: once prime time was full, revenue could grow only through rate. On 14 August 2026 the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting announced that the cap would go, and it notified the Cable Television Networks (Amendment) Rules, 2026 around 21–22 August, deleting the rule. TRAI repealed its own ad-duration regulations on 10 September 2026.

Removing a cap sounds like good news for sellers. In a falling market it is ambiguous. Broadcasters can now sell 13, 14 or 15 minutes an hour, but advertisers are buying fewer minutes, not more. More inventory chasing less demand usually means lower rates, and more ad clutter can annoy the pay subscribers whose fees matter more than ever. The likely winners are channels with excess demand at peak moments (cricket, big reality finales, election nights), where an extra minute sells at full price. 05

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: content is fixed, and that is the whole problem

Three archetypes, three cost structures: a regional bouquet that keeps half its revenue, a national network that keeps four rupees and a newsroom that keeps two.

Broadcasting costs fall into five buckets. Programming and content is the largest: the cost of producing daily episodes (talent, sets, crew), amortisation of film and show rights, sports-rights fees and, in bad years, write-downs of content inventory. Advertising, promotion and distribution (A&P) covers channel marketing, streaming-app customer acquisition and placement or carriage fees paid to DPOs. Employee costs are modest for entertainment and heavy for news, which needs reporters, anchors and production staff in many cities. Other costs include satellite transponders, uplink fees, technology and rent. Sun alone also carries cricket franchise costs (player salaries, team operations, franchise fees).

The key property of all of these, except some A&P, is that they are committed in advance and barely move when advertising moves. A broadcaster decides its prime-time schedule, film purchases and sports contracts a year or more ahead. That makes the cost structure the single best predictor of how a broadcaster's profits will react to an ad downturn.

The national network: Zee, FY25 versus FY26

Zee's cost lines are fully disclosed, and the two-year comparison is the clearest picture of operating de-leverage in Indian media. In FY25, out of every ₹100 of revenue, Zee spent about ₹54.5 on content, ₹13.8 on A&P, ₹11.2 on employees and ₹6.1 on other costs, leaving ₹14.4 of EBITDA. In FY26 content rose to ₹60.0 (including a ₹408.4 crore Q4 charge for a change in how movie rights are amortised plus impairments), A&P rose to ₹17.6 (ZEE5 marketing and new launches), employees fell to ₹10.4 and other costs rose to ₹7.7, leaving ₹4.3. Revenue fell only 2.4%; EBITDA fell 71%.

In plain terms

A broadcaster's costs are like a theatre's: the show, the cast and the building cost the same whether the seats are full or half empty. Sun's theatre is full and cheap to run, so half its takings are profit. Zee's costs went up while its takings fell, so its profit almost disappeared. The amortisation line deserves a separate word because it is where broadcasters differ most in accounting. Sun capitalises the cost of films it produces and the rights it buys, and amortises them through depreciation, so its D&A jumps in film years: ₹541 crore in FY25, ₹723 crore in FY26, with ₹404 crore in the Coolie quarter alone (Q2 FY26). Four ways the giant squeezes the listed players

(i) Advertisers. In the April–June quarter, the IPL absorbs a large share of national brand budgets. (ii) Distributors. A broadcaster's subscription income depends on how DPOs package and promote its channels. JioStar's must-have bouquets (cricket, Star Plus, Star Vijay) give it bargaining power with every DPO, and its parent owns several of them. (iii) Content costs. JioHotstar bids for Tamil, Telugu and Hindi film rights and for original series, which raises the price Sun and Zee pay for content. OTT: breakeven by restraint, profit only at JioStar's scale

Force 4. Streaming is where the viewers are going; for the listed broadcasters it is a stabiliser, not yet a replacement for linear profit.

Every Indian broadcaster runs a streaming app because every broadcaster can see its audience moving online. FICCI-EY counts 216 million paid video subscriptions across 143 million households in 2025, with digital subscription revenue up 60% to ₹16,300 crore. The economics, however, depend almost entirely on scale. A streaming app has high fixed costs (originals, sports, technology, marketing) and earns from subscriptions, telecom bundles and advertising. Below a certain audience size the fixed costs swamp the revenue; above it, each new user is nearly pure margin. In India only one platform is clearly above that line.

ZEE5: from a ₹548 crore loss to near-breakeven

ZEE5 is the only listed-company streaming platform in this report that discloses its numbers, and its turnaround is real. Revenue rose from ₹976 crore in FY25 to ₹1,488.8 crore in FY26 (+53%) and ₹457.1 crore in Q1 FY27 (Jun-26), up 57.6%. EBITDA improved from −₹548 crore in FY25 to −₹49 crore in FY26, with the first positive quarter (Q3 FY26, +₹56.4 crore) and another small profit in Q1 FY27 (+₹4.4 crore). The drivers were higher prices, telecom bundles, regional language packs and, in Q1 FY27, the FIFA World Cup: 83% of FIFA watch time was live on ZEE5, and direct-to-consumer subscriber additions more than doubled in the quarter. ZEE5 released 38 titles in Q1 FY27, including 15 originals.

Why streaming does not yet replace linear profit

The arithmetic at Zee makes the point. Network18's digital business shows another route: news and personal finance, where trust and habit allow a paywall. In plain terms

Price caps, a ratings blackout and a deleted rule: regulation in 2026

Force 5. TRAI decides how channels may be priced and bundled; the ministry decides who may measure them; both changed the rules in the past two years.

Indian broadcasting is regulated more tightly than almost any other media business. TRAI, the telecom regulator, sets the tariff and interconnection framework that governs how broadcasters price channels and how DPOs carry them. The Ministry of Information and Broadcasting licenses uplinking and downlinking, controls foreign ownership in news, sets the advertising-duration rules and, since 2026, licenses the ratings agency. Sports rights are shaped by a law that forces key cricket matches onto the free public broadcaster. And Zee, in particular, now operates under a SEBI order. Each of these touches the KPIs in Part 11.

The New Tariff Order: three versions in four years

NTO 1.0 came into force in February–March 2019. It required a maximum retail price for every pay channel, let households pick channels individually, allowed bouquets, and barred channels priced above ₹19 a month from bouquets. DPOs could charge a network capacity fee (NCF) capped at ₹130 plus GST for 100 standard-definition channels, and keep a distribution fee of up to 20% of the channel price. NTO 2.0 (January 2020) cut the bouquet price cap to ₹12 and raised the NCF allowance to 200 channels for ₹130; broadcasters challenged it and lost in the Bombay High Court in July 2021. NTO 3.0 (November 2022, effective February 2023) restored the ₹19 cap but limited bouquet discounts to 45% of the sum of individual prices. Broadcasters raised prices 10–15% in February 2023, which led cable operators to black out major broadcasters' feeds for a time.

The 8 July 2024 amendments (Tariff Fourth Amendment Order, Interconnection Sixth Amendment and Quality of Service Fourth Amendment Regulations) removed the NCF ceilings and put them under "forbearance", letting DPOs vary NCF by channel count, region and customer class. They also removed the HD/SD distinction in carriage fees and freed installation charges. Critically for Hindi broadcasters, they kept the rule that a channel offered free on DD FreeDish may not be declared a pay channel on addressable platforms, which TDSAT upheld. No NTO 4.0 has been notified as of October 2026, although FICCI-EY expects "greater flexibility on NCF and discounts".

The ratings blackout and the Television Ratings Policy 2026

BARC India, a joint venture of broadcasters, advertisers and agencies, has been the sole TV ratings agency since 2015. Its ratings are the currency for ad pricing. After the 2020 "TRP scam" allegations, the ministry rewrote the 2014 ratings guidelines into a new Television Ratings Policy, 2026: every agency needs fresh registration; at least a third of its board must be independent directors; the panel must expand to 80,000 metered homes (from roughly 55,000, an approximate figure); an establishment survey must be done every three years; and methodology, audit and data-protection rules tighten. The policy is under legal challenge.

In March 2026 the ministry paused news ratings for four weeks during West Asia war coverage, and extended the pause repeatedly. In July 2026 it directed BARC to stop publishing all ratings until it was registered under the new policy. BARC had applied in May. A report on 31 July said provisional registration would be granted, valid to a January 2027 review, but this was not confirmed as implemented. Around 21 September the broadcasters' federation (IBDF) demanded immediate restoration and set a November deadline. As of 22 October 2026, no resumption had been found. Any "network share" quoted after week 26 of 2026 does not exist.

Three decades in one chart: satellite boom, digitisation, consolidation, collapse

Indian TV margins peaked in FY18–19 and have compressed ever since; the cycle is structural decline punctuated by cricket, films and elections.

1991–2000: the satellite boom. Star TV arrived in 1991, Zee TV launched in 1992 as India's first private Hindi satellite channel, and Sun TV followed in 1993 in Tamil. Unregulated local cable operators wired cities almost overnight. 2000s: regional bouquets and analogue cable. Sun built clusters in all four Southern languages; Zee expanded into regional languages; Sun TV listed in April 2006 and TV18/Network18 in 2006–07. DTH arrived: Dish TV (2003), Tata Sky (2006), Sun Direct (2007) and Airtel (2008). 2008–2011: the IPL. The first IPL season in 2008 changed the economics of Indian sport and sports TV. Sun bought the Hyderabad franchise in December 2012, renaming it Sunrisers Hyderabad.

2012–2017: digitisation. Cable digitisation (DAS phases I–IV) made every home use a set-top box, which made subscriptions visible and payable; broadcasters' subscription revenue roughly doubled. Reliance took control of Network18 and TV18 in 2014. Sun NXT launched in June 2017, and Star won the IPL rights for 2018–22. 2019–2020: the tariff order and the Essel crisis. NTO 1.0 gave broadcasters channel-level pricing power, and pay-TV homes peaked. The Essel group's debt crisis forced Zee's promoters to sell or lose most of their stake as lenders invoked pledges, taking the family from about 41% to under 5%. Margins peaked here: Zee's operating margin was 32% in FY19 and Sun's 69%.

2021–2024: consolidation attempts. Invesco tried to remove Zee's CEO in 2021; Zee agreed to merge with Sony in December 2021, won NCLT approval in August 2023, and saw Sony terminate the deal on 22 January 2024 before both sides settled and withdrew all claims on 27 August 2024. The IPL 2023–27 rights sold for ₹48,390 crore in June 2022, and Viacom18 streamed the 2023 IPL free on JioCinema. In plain terms

Indian TV's golden age ran from digitisation to about 2019, when broadcasters could raise prices on a growing base of paying homes and advertisers had nowhere else to reach the whole country. Since then, every good year has been a smaller good year: a big film, an IPL season or an election lifts one quarter, and then the decline resumes. That pattern argues against buying broadcasters simply because their margins look depressed.

The cycle lesson is that this is not a mean-reverting industry. In a classic cycle, depressed margins attract no new capacity and eventually recover. Here, the competition for viewers' time comes from outside the industry (YouTube, Instagram, streaming) and the advertising base is migrating permanently. Event spikes such as the IPL quarter, a Rajinikanth release or a state election produce strong quarters, but they do not reset the trend. An investor should read every strong quarter by asking how much came from an event and how much from the underlying TV business, and the KPI masterclass that follows shows how to separate the two.

KPI masterclass: how to read a broadcaster's numbers Deep dive

Fourteen measures, the formula behind each, what good looks like, how the three members compare, and the traps that make the same word mean different things in different filings.

A broadcaster's results look simple: advertising, subscription, other income, costs, profit. In practice almost every line hides a definitional choice. Zee publishes four EBITDA figures for the same year. Network18's reported profit is mostly someone else's profit. Sun's quarters swing with cricket seasons and film releases. "Subscription" includes streaming at one company and excludes it at another. This masterclass starts with the equation that links the lines, then takes each native KPI in turn: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, good and weak benchmarks, how Sun, Zee and Network18 compare, and the trap to avoid.

The equation, and a worked example

Every broadcaster's profit and loss account follows one equation. Revenue is advertising plus subscription plus content and other income (plus cricket for Sun). Advertising equals the ad minutes sold across all channels, times the fill rate, times the effective rate per ten-second slot; that rate depends on the channel's ratings share and on the size of the total ad pool. Subscription equals the paying homes that carry the channels, times the monthly realisation per home after the DPO's share, times twelve, plus streaming subscribers times their monthly revenue. EBITDA is revenue minus content costs (largely fixed in the short run), minus A&P and distribution, minus employees and other costs. Profit adds treasury income and associates and subtracts depreciation and amortisation, interest and tax.

In plain terms

A broadcaster's costs are decided a year in advance and its revenue is decided by advertisers and households every month. So a small drop in revenue causes a big drop in profit, and a small recovery causes a big recovery. When reading results, the first question is always: did revenue move, and did costs move with it?

1. Advertising revenue and ad growth

What it is: money earned from selling airtime (and, increasingly, digital and CTV impressions) to advertisers, net of agency commissions and volume rebates. Formula: ad growth = this quarter's ad revenue ÷ the same quarter last year − 1. Always compare year-on-year, never sequentially, because Indian advertising is seasonal: the festive quarter (October–December) is strongest, and April–June is distorted by the IPL and, in some years, elections. Worked example: Zee's Q1 FY27 ad revenue of ₹671.4 crore against ₹758.5 crore a year earlier is a decline of 11.5%; on a ₹1,907 crore revenue base, that lost ₹87.1 crore went almost straight to EBITDA.

What drives it: the total TV ad pool (FMCG budgets, the economy, events), the channel's ratings share, the advertiser's shift to digital, and competition from JioStar's bundles. Benchmark: good is above +5% a year; average −3% to +5%; weak below −3%. In today's market, flat is an achievement. How the members compare: Sun −2.6% and Zee −11.5% in Q1 FY27; Network18 does not split advertising out, but reports its ad inventory up 10% against an industry up 3%. The trap: Sun's quarterly ad figures are standalone (they sum to ₹1,154 crore in FY26), while its annual report's consolidated figure is ₹1,292 crore; Zee's include international advertising (₹46 crore in Q1 FY27). Compare like with like.

2. Ad inventory and yield: minutes times rate

What it is: advertising revenue decomposed into volume (ad seconds sold) and price (realised rate per ten seconds). Formula: ad revenue = Σ channels (minutes available × fill rate × 6 slots per minute × rate per slot). Worked example (illustrative): a channel that sells 10 minutes in each of 24 hours has 87,600 minutes or 5.26 lakh ten-second slots a year; at a 57% fill and an average ₹8,000 a slot it earns about ₹240 crore. If the rate falls 10% and fill is unchanged, it loses ₹24 crore; to recover that by volume alone, it must sell 11% more slots.

What drives it: ratings (higher ratings, higher rate), the season, and since August 2026 the removal of the 12-minute cap, which turns volume into a lever. Benchmark: there is no published industry yield; the useful signal is the direction of volume and rate separately. FICCI-EY reports ad volumes −10% on national and −11% on regional channels in 2025, and TAM reportedly shows −7% in January–July 2026. Members: none discloses rate and volume. Network18 discloses inventory growth (+10% in Q1 FY27; non-government +2% vs an industry −10%+), which says it sold more seconds, likely at lower average rates. The trap: after the cap removal, "more inventory sold" no longer implies "more revenue". Ask what happened to rate.

In plain terms

Ad revenue is the number of ad slots sold multiplied by the price of each slot. For years the number of slots was capped at 12 minutes an hour, so growth had to come from price. The cap is gone, so channels can sell more slots, but if advertisers do not want more, the extra slots just push the price down.

3. Subscription revenue and realisation per home (ARPU)

What it is: fees from DPOs for linear channels (domestic and international feeds), plus, at Zee, ZEE5's consumer and telecom-bundle subscriptions. Formula: domestic linear subscription = paying homes carrying the channels × monthly realisation per home × 12; realisation = channel or bouquet MRP × (1 − the DPO's share of up to 20%), adjusted for bouquet discounts. Worked example (illustrative): Sun's domestic subscription of about ₹1,892 crore in FY26 is ₹158 crore a month. If Sun's channels reach 40–45 million paying homes (Sun does not disclose this), the realisation is about ₹35–40 per home per month. Industry-wide, FICCI-EY puts average pay-TV ARPU at ₹288 a month in 2025 (+2.4%), of which broadcasters receive only part.

What drives it: annual price revisions (Sun ~7–8% in FY26; Zee's next in February 2027), the number of homes (falling), mix (HD and premium packs) and, for Zee, streaming subscribers. Benchmark: domestic subscription growth above +6% is good, +2–6% average, below +2% weak. Members: Sun's total subscription rose 8.6% in FY26 (domestic +9.7%) but domestic only +3.3% in Q1 FY27; Zee's rose 3.9% in FY26 and 15.8% in Q1 FY27, "largely" from digital (ZEE5's FIFA-driven additions); Network18's subscription is small and not split. The trap: Zee's +15.8% and Sun's +3.3% are not comparable; one includes streaming and international, the other is domestic linear only. Sun's "total subscription" (₹1,980 crore) also differs from its "domestic subscription" (~₹1,892 crore).

4. Pay-TV homes and cord-cutting

What it is: the industry's paying base, measured quarterly by TRAI for DTH, HITS, IPTV and reporting cable operators, and annually by FICCI-EY for all pay and free homes. Formula: cord-cutting rate = change in active pay subscribers ÷ base a year earlier. Worked example: pay DTH 56.07 million (Jun-25) to 48.56 million (Jun-26) = −7.51 million = −13.4%. What drives it: price increases, the quality of free alternatives (DD FreeDish, YouTube), broadband and CTV adoption, and piracy. Benchmark: growth is good; 0 to −5% average; worse than −5% weak. India is now firmly weak. Members: none discloses its own subscriber count; Sun's subscription growth minus its price increase is the best indirect read (roughly −4% to −5% volume in Q1 FY27, indicative). 5. The ad-to-subscription mix

What it is: the balance between the cyclical stream (advertising) and the annuity (subscription). Formula: subscription ÷ advertising. Worked example: Sun FY26: ₹1,980 crore ÷ ₹1,292 crore = 1.53×. Zee FY26: ₹4,080 crore ÷ ₹3,224 crore = 1.27×; in Q1 FY27 it jumped to 1.69× as advertising fell and ZEE5 subscriptions surged. What drives it: pricing power over DPOs, the share of free-to-air channels, and the ad cycle. Benchmark: above 1.5× good (more resilient), 1.0–1.5× average, below 1.0× weak. Members: Sun 1.53×, Zee 1.27× (FY26), Network18 below 1.0× (advertising "over 50%" of income). The trap: a rising ratio is not always good news; Zee's Q1 FY27 jump came from advertising collapsing, not subscription strength alone. And as pay homes fall, a subscription-heavy mix carries its own risk.

7. Content cost and amortisation

What it is: the cost of programmes shown, including production, film and show rights amortised over their use, sports rights and write-downs. Formula: content cost ÷ revenue. Worked example: Zee's FY26 operational cost of ₹4,859 crore on ₹8,099 crore of revenue is 60.0%; excluding the ₹408 crore Q4 amortisation change and impairment it would be about 55%. 9. OTT subscribers, revenue and EBITDA

What it is: the streaming app's revenue (subscriptions, telecom bundles, advertising, syndication) and its operating profit or loss. Formula: OTT EBITDA margin = OTT EBITDA ÷ OTT revenue; also track paying subscribers × monthly ARPU where disclosed. What drives it: subscriber additions and price, content spend (originals, sports), marketing, and the cost split with the linear network. Benchmark: above +10% good, −10% to +10% average, below −10% weak. Members: ZEE5 average (−3.3% FY26, +1.0% Q1 FY27, from −56% in FY25); Sun NXT not disclosed; Network18's Moneycontrol Pro has over a million paid subscribers but no separate P&L. The trap: ZEE5's EBITDA excludes costs borne on the Zee network, and "breakeven" is a quarterly not an annual fact; ZEE5's subscriber and MAU numbers are no longer disclosed.

10. Sports and franchise income

What it is: income from owning teams (Sun) or from sports-channel advertising and subscription (Zee's new sports channels; JioStar). For franchises: a share of the league's central media-rights pool, sponsorship, ticketing and prize money, minus player costs and franchise fees. Formula: franchise contribution = cricket income − cricket costs. Worked example: Sun Q1 FY27: income ₹629.8 crore − costs ₹321.2 crore (standalone, Sunrisers Hyderabad and Eastern Cape) = ₹308.6 crore of contribution, against ₹216.9 crore a year earlier (₹473.0 − ₹256.1 crore). What drives it: the size of the IPL media-rights contract (₹48,390 crore for 2023–27), sponsorship deals, playoff runs and the number of teams owned. Benchmark: no industry norm; the useful measure is contribution margin (49% for Sun in Q1 FY27). Members: only Sun has this line: ₹641.7 crore in FY25, ₹652.4 crore in FY26 including Sunrisers Leeds' first ₹94.5 crore. The trap: almost all of the IPL income lands in the April–June quarter, so Sun's Q1 always looks spectacular; the primer's earlier "₹662.1 crore" FY26 figure is not in any filing.

11. Associate income and look-through value (Network18)

What it is: the share of profit from companies a group owns 20–50% of (or has significant influence over), booked as one line below operating profit. Benchmark: no norm; the test is whether associate income exceeds the operating business's own profit. 12. Working capital: receivables and content inventory

What it is: cash tied up in money owed by advertisers and DPOs (receivables) and in content not yet expensed (inventory of film and show rights). Formula: debtor days = receivables ÷ revenue × 365; inventory ratio = content inventory ÷ revenue. Worked example: Zee's content inventory of ₹6,512 crore at March 2026 is 80% of a year's revenue; including advances it was about ₹7,180 crore in Q1 FY27, of which movie rights were 62%. Its working-capital cycle was 322 days in FY26. What drives it: DPO payment discipline, agency terms, film-library purchases and sports-rights advances (Zee's inventory rose ₹174 crore in Q1 FY27 as FIFA cash went out). The trap: a large inventory is also a risk of future write-downs, as Q4 FY26 showed; and Sun's rising debtor days coincide with more subscription being collected through Maran-family-linked distributors (Part 16).

13. Cash conversion, free cash flow and net cash

What it is: how much of reported EBITDA turns into operating cash, how much is left after capital spending, and the resulting treasury. 15. A&P and distribution cost

What it is: spending on promoting channels and apps (on-air promos, outdoor, digital ads, influencer campaigns), acquiring streaming subscribers, and paying DPOs for carriage and placement on their channel guides. Formula: A&P ÷ revenue. Worked example: Zee's A&P was ₹1,425 crore in FY26, 17.6% of revenue, and ₹446.8 crore in Q1 FY27, 23.4% of revenue and up 62.4% year-on-year as it launched four "Unite8" sports channels and marketed FIFA on ZEE5. That ₹171.6 crore increase alone explains more than the entire fall in Zee's quarterly EBITDA (₹149.1 crore). What drives it: launches (new channels, sports, apps), competitive intensity in streaming, and carriage terms (the 2024 amendments removed the HD/SD distinction in carriage fees). Benchmark: below 12% good, 12–18% average, above 18% weak. Members: Zee 17.6% (FY26) and 23.4% (Q1 FY27); Network18's marketing and distribution was 26.1% of FY26 revenue; Sun does not split it out. The trap: A&P is the one large cost a broadcaster can cut quickly, so a sudden margin recovery that comes only from lower A&P may simply mean the company stopped investing, as ZEE5's path to breakeven partly shows.

The KPIs connect in a predictable chain. Pay-TV homes and price determine subscription; ratings and the ad pool determine advertising; the mix between the two determines how volatile revenue is; content cost and A&P determine how much of any revenue change reaches EBITDA; and working capital and capital allocation determine whether that EBITDA becomes cash for shareholders. Sun scores well at the top of the chain (mix, margin) and less well at the bottom (receivables, related-party collections). Zee scores badly in the middle (content cost, A&P) and its cash depends on the outcome of one arbitration. Network18 only makes sense through the associate line. A good analyst reads all fifteen measures together rather than any one in isolation.

Anatomy of one typical regional GEC channel (illustrative)

To make the KPIs concrete, here is an illustrative profit and loss account for one leading regional-language general entertainment channel, the kind of channel that anchors Sun's Tamil bouquet or Zee's Marathi or Bengali bouquets. The numbers are round and internally consistent; they are not any company's disclosure. The channel reaches 12 million paying homes and realises ₹15 per home per month after the DPO's share, so subscription is 12 million × ₹15 × 12 = ₹216 crore. Syndication, YouTube and digital add ₹24 crore. Revenue: ₹480 crore.

If advertising falls 10%, EBITDA falls ₹24 crore to ₹186 crore (−11.4%) and the margin to 40.8%. If homes fall 8% and the price rises 7%, subscription becomes ₹212.6 crore, another −₹3.4 crore.

  • Network18's perimeter changed. FY24 and FY25 include Viacom18's entertainment business (until 14 November 2024). FY26 is news and digital only.   • Zee has four EBITDAs. Reported ₹346.3 crore (4.3%), adjusted ₹754.7 crore (9.3%), screener ₹399 crore (4.9%) and a derived linear figure. Reported PAT was ₹271 crore; adjusted PAT figures in broker notes add back the Q4 write-downs.
  • ZEE5's breakeven is quarterly and partial. FY26 sum −₹49 crore; the EBITDA excludes costs borne on the Zee network.
  • Sun's quarters are seasonal. Q1 carries the IPL; Q2 FY26 carried Coolie (revenue +39%, D&A ₹404 crore). Compare year-on-year only, and strip cricket and film when judging the TV business.
  • Sun's ad numbers come in two scopes. Quarterly releases are standalone (FY26 sum ₹1,154 crore); the annual report's consolidated figure is ₹1,292 crore. Sun's standalone and consolidated revenue also differ (Q1 FY27 ₹1,423 crore vs ₹1,458 crore).
  • "Subscription" means different things. Sun's domestic subscription is linear pay-TV only; Zee's includes ZEE5 and international.
  • Sun capitalises content; Zee inventories it. Sun's film cost sits partly in D&A, below EBITDA; Zee's runs through operational cost, so margins are not like-for-like.
  • Viewership shares stopped in July 2026 and use different target groups. JioStar's 34% and Zee's 17.9% cannot be compared directly.
  • JioStar is not consolidated by any member; never add it to the members' totals.
  • Screener's cash and debt figures mislead. They under-count Sun's and Zee's treasuries, treat Sun's leases as borrowings, and count Network18's strategic stakes as investments.
  • Digital reach metrics differ. Network18's 350–360 million "monthly users" is a Comscore total digital population figure including social platforms, not unique website visitors.
In plain terms

Before comparing any two broadcasters, check three things: which EBITDA (reported or adjusted), which subscription (with or without streaming), and which period (an IPL or film quarter, or a normal one). Most misleading comparisons in this industry fail one of those three tests.

How to read a TV broadcaster's quarterly results in ten minutes

Results day for a broadcaster produces a flood of numbers, and the headline profit is often the least useful of them. The checklist below puts the lines in the order that tells you the most, fastest. It works for all three members, although Sun and Network18 publish only a results statement and a short release, while Zee adds a detailed deck and an earnings call.

  1. Advertising, year-on-year (minute 1). Pull ad revenue and its YoY change. Below −3% is weak. Check whether elections, the IPL or a festival timing shift flatter or depress it. Red flag: a decline larger than the industry's (−7% to −10% in 2025–26).
  2. Subscription, split if possible (minute 2). Domestic linear versus streaming versus international. For Sun, compare domestic growth with the ~7–8% price increase: anything lower implies homes are being lost. Red flag: domestic subscription growth below +2%.
  3. Strip the lumpy lines (minute 3). Remove cricket income and cost (Sun), film releases (Sun, Zee Studios) and sports events (Zee's FIFA) to see the underlying TV business.
  4. Content cost and A&P as % of revenue (minute 4). Rising content cost with falling revenue is the margin killer. Red flag: content above 55% at a GEC network, or a sudden "change in estimate".
  5. EBITDA and its basis (minute 5). Note reported and adjusted figures and the bridge between them. Red flag: an adjusted figure that is more than double the reported one.
  6. Streaming line (minute 6). ZEE5 revenue and EBITDA; check whether marketing was cut to reach breakeven. Red flag: ZEE5 losses widening while linear profit falls.
  7. Below EBITDA (minute 7). Other income (Sun's treasury), D&A (film amortisation), associates (Network18's JioStar share) and exceptional items. Red flag: profit growth that comes only from these lines.
  8. Cash and inventory (minute 8). Net cash movement, content inventory, receivables. Red flag: inventory rising faster than revenue, or debtor days climbing.
  9. Management commentary (minute 9). Ad outlook, the next price revision, sports and content commitments, any legal updates (Zee's arbitration, SEBI). Sun and Network18 hold no earnings calls, so read the release and any regulatory filings.
  10. Industry cross-check (minute 10). Compare with JioStar's quarter (via Reliance's results), TRAI's latest subscriber data and the ad forecasters. Red flag: a company claiming share gains with no ratings to prove them.
In plain terms

The fastest way to read a broadcaster's quarter is to ask four questions in order: did advertising fall more or less than the market, did subscription beat the price increase, how much of the profit came from cricket, films, treasury or associates rather than TV, and is the cash pile growing or shrinking? If those four answers are good, the quarter was good, whatever the headline profit says.

The South holds, the North bleeds: regional bouquets versus national networks Deep dive

Language is the best moat in Indian television. It is holding, but the first cracks are visible even in Tamil Nadu.

The single most useful fact for understanding the listed broadcasters is geographic. Sun earns almost all its money in the four Southern languages, where it leads in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh (its FY26 annual report's own description), with a smaller and growing presence in Bangla, Marathi and Hindi. Zee earns a large share of its money in Hindi, where it competes directly with JioStar's Star Plus and Colors and with free TV, although its regional channels in Marathi, Bengali, Odia, Kannada and Telugu are among its strongest assets. The two companies' FY26 results show what that difference is worth: Sun's operating margin was 50%, Zee's reported EBITDA margin 4.3%.

Why the South is stickier

Four reasons. Language-specific content with no national substitute: a Tamil viewer who wants a Tamil prime-time serial, a Tamil film premiere and Tamil news cannot get them from a Hindi network, and YouTube's long tail is less polished. Weaker free-TV presence: DD FreeDish carries fewer Southern pay channels, so the free alternative that is draining Hindi pay homes is less complete in the South. Distribution structure: state-linked cable networks (such as Arasu Cable in Tamil Nadu and AP Fibernet, whose IPTV service had 0.41 million subscribers) and the Maran family's own Sun Direct DTH platform (10.93 million active subscribers) give Southern bouquets dependable carriage. Owning the whole evening: Sun runs entertainment, film, music, kids and news channels in each language, so a household that wants any Tamil content tends to want the whole bouquet.

The North faces the opposite on every count. Hindi GEC viewers have JioStar's bundles, Sony's channels, free-to-air versions of pay channels on DD FreeDish (including JioStar's Star Utsav since April 2025) and the largest pool of free Hindi content on YouTube. TRAI's rule that a channel free on FreeDish cannot be pay on other platforms forces Hindi broadcasters to choose between rural reach and subscription. JioStar holds 44% of Hindi-speaking-market pay TV. FICCI-EY reports Hindi-market entertainment ad revenue down "up to 18%" in 2025, the steepest fall of any genre.

The cracks

The moat is not permanent, and the first evidence of erosion is already in the numbers. Sun's domestic subscription grew only 3.3% in Q1 FY27, down from 9–10% a quarter through FY26. With a price increase of around 7–8%, that implies the number of homes paying for Sun's channels fell by roughly 4–5% (an indicative estimate). JioStar's Star Vijay is a strong Tamil competitor, Star Maa leads in Telugu and Asianet in Malayalam, and all three can be bundled with cricket. Streaming platforms bid up Southern film rights, raising Sun's content cost and creating a direct-to-viewer alternative for the films that anchor Sun's movie channels. And a growing share of Sun's subscription is collected through Maran-family-linked distributors, which raises questions about how much of FY26's growth reflects end-consumer demand and how much reflects changes in collection arrangements (Part 16).

What we cannot see matters too. Sun does not disclose revenue by language, Tamil GEC share or the number of paying homes, and BARC ratings stopped in July 2026. The South's resilience is therefore inferred from Sun's consolidated subscription line, TRAI's national DTH data and Zee's regional commentary rather than measured directly. An investor must watch Sun's domestic subscription growth each quarter as the single best proxy.

Zee after Sony: margin collapse, a sports re-entry and a governance cliff Deep dive

Force 7, part one. Two years after its merger collapsed, Zee faces a US$1.1 billion claim, a SEBI order and a 4% promoter trying to buy back in at nearly twice the share price.

Zee agreed to merge with Sony's Indian business in December 2021. The deal would have created a network to rival Star, and the NCLT approved it in August 2023. On 22 January 2024 Sony terminated it, reportedly seeking a US$90 million break fee; on 27 August 2024 the two sides settled and withdrew all claims against each other. Zee was left as a standalone company with a promoter family holding 3.99% after the Essel group's debt crisis, a costly ICC cricket contract it could not pay for, and a strategy built around a merger that no longer existed. Everything since has been an attempt to rebuild.

The margin collapse

FY25 looked like recovery: a cost programme lifted Zee's EBITDA to ₹1,196 crore (14.4%) and ZEE5's losses narrowed. FY26 undid it. Advertising fell 10.2% to ₹3,224 crore, content costs rose to 60% of revenue (including the ₹408 crore Q4 charge for a change in how movie rights are amortised plus impairment), and A&P rose to 17.6%. Reported EBITDA fell to ₹346 crore (4.3%). Then in Q1 FY27 (Jun-26) Zee made a deliberate bet on sport: it took digital and broadcast rights to 39 FIFA events through 2034 (payments staggered over eight years, heavier for the 2030 World Cup), signed Bundesliga and Serie A, and launched four "Unite8" sports channels. A&P jumped 62% and the EBITDA margin fell to 4.1%. Management declined to give any margin guidance for FY27 on the 10 August 2026 call.

The Sunrisers Leeds acquisition is the largest capital decision Sun has made in years. It paid GBP 100.5 million (₹1,185 crore) for 100% of the franchise, effective 28 July 2025, and booked it as franchise rights, an intangible asset. That is why Sun's "other intangibles" rose from ₹693 crore to ₹1,708 crore in FY26 and why free cash flow was −₹409 crore despite ₹1,802 crore of operating cash flow. Management says the Leeds franchise "is expected to contribute positively to revenue and profitability", and its first quarter delivered ₹94.5 crore of revenue and ₹22.2 crore of profit before tax.

On 30 September 2026, after CNBC-TV18 and Moneycontrol reported a possible demerger of Sun's sports division, Sun told the exchanges that the report "is a rumour and the Company cannot comment on market rumours", and confirmed it had no pending price-sensitive information. That is a refusal to comment, not an explicit denial. The logic behind the rumour is easy to see: a portfolio of three franchises with fixed media-rights income would likely be valued very differently from a declining linear TV business. But no plan has been announced, and any valuation of the teams is speculative. The next real catalyst is the IPL 2028–32 media-rights auction, likely in 2027, which will set Sunrisers Hyderabad's central income for the next five years.

In plain terms

JioStar pays the BCCI billions for the right to show the IPL; Sun owns one of the teams that shares in that money. When the price of cricket rights goes up, JioStar's margin falls and Sun's income rises. That is why, in a year when Sun's TV advertising fell, its profits still grew: the cricket teams made up the difference.

The risks are specific. Franchise income is seasonal (Q1 for the IPL, Q4 for SA20, Q2 for The Hundred), so Sun's quarterly results are harder to read. Player costs can rise faster than income in auction years. A weaker IPL rights auction in 2027 would cut the central pool. And capital spent on franchises is capital not returned to shareholders; Sun cut its dividend from ₹15.00 to ₹12.50 a share in FY26, the same year it bought Leeds. For investors, the question is whether Sun is building a sports business worth more than its cost, or using TV cash to buy assets the promoter family values for reasons beyond return on capital.

A quarter of revenue from the sibling

Cash piles, dividends and related parties: governance as a valuation input

Force 7, part two. The cash piles are the valuation floor; who controls them, and where the revenue flows, decides how much that floor is worth.

For broadcasters in structural decline, capital allocation matters as much as operations. A business whose core revenue is shrinking should, in theory, return most of its cash to shareholders. Sun and Zee hold large treasuries; Network18 holds debt. How each uses its cash, and how transparently, explains part of why Sun trades at 15 times earnings while Zee trades below book.

Sun: ₹7,833 crore of treasury and no debt

Sun's FY26 annual report shows a treasury of ₹7,832.8 crore: current investments of ₹6,260.1 crore (mutual funds ₹5,521.2 crore, bonds ₹661.1 crore and others), non-current investments of ₹1,283.2 crore, cash of ₹243.0 crore and other bank balances of ₹46.5 crore. Sun has no borrowings; the ₹106 crore that screener shows as debt is lease liabilities. Net cash after leases is about ₹7,727 crore, a third of the market value. The treasury earned ₹493 crore of other income in FY26, down from ₹642 crore as the Leeds purchase and film spending drew it down.

The related-party finding: 56% of subscription via family-linked distributors

The most important governance fact about Sun, and one that our earlier primer missed, is in the FY26 annual report's related-party note. Sun booked about ₹1,111 crore of subscription income, 56% of its ₹1,980 crore total, from distribution companies linked to the Maran family, up from about ₹380 crore (21%) in FY25. The biggest changes were Sun Direct TV (the family's DTH platform), from ₹0.6 crore to ₹293.7 crore, and TVCH Distribution Services, from ₹13.4 crore to ₹289.0 crore. Sun also paid these entities ₹253.2 crore of "pay channel service charges" (₹92.0 crore in FY25), earned ₹149.4 crore of movie-distribution income from Sun Business Solutions (₹37.4 crore), and was owed ₹106.4 crore by Sun Direct at year-end (nil a year earlier). The report lists them as enterprises in which key management personnel or their relatives have control or significant influence, but does not explain the shift.

Why it matters. The most likely reading is that Sun moved the collection of DPO payments into group distribution or aggregator companies; nothing in the report suggests the underlying viewers changed. But three consequences follow. First, receivable risk: Sun's debtor days rose to 121, and a growing receivable from a family-owned DTH operator whose subscriber base is shrinking (Sun Direct had 10.93 million active subscribers in June 2026) is a credit exposure to a related party. Second, quality of revenue: when more than half of subscription income is booked through related parties that also receive service charges, outside shareholders need to trust that the pricing is at arm's length; FY26's +8.6% subscription growth coincided with the shift. Third, governance perception: Sun is 75% promoter-owned, and investors already apply a discount for the Maran family's political links and control. This disclosure will widen it unless Sun explains the arrangement.

In plain terms

More than half of the money households pay for Sun's channels now passes through companies controlled by the same family that controls Sun, up from a fifth a year earlier. That may be a sensible way to organise collections, but Sun has not said why it changed, Sun pays those companies fees for the service, and they owe Sun more money than before. Minority shareholders should want an explanation.

Zee: cash, a high payout and a restricted treasury

Zee's cash and treasury were ₹2,759.5 crore at March 2026 and ₹2,210.9 crore at June 2026 (mutual funds ₹1,180.0 crore, bank deposits ₹217.2 crore, subsidiaries' deposits ₹377.2 crore, bank and other ₹436.4 crore), the fall driven by upfront FIFA rights payments. Net of ₹254.6 crore of borrowings including leases, net cash was about ₹1,956 crore, or roughly ₹2,616 crore after the ₹659.8 crore of warrant money received in August. Zee paid a ₹2.00 dividend for FY26, a 70% payout, but SAT has barred it from using mutual-fund investments to fund the proposed dividend while the SEBI order stands. Zee's FY26 tax was near nil, helped by a ₹96.9 crore credit from writing off its Margo Networks investment.

Network18: debt, no dividend, Reliance's call

Network18 pays no dividend, has net debt of ₹2,948 crore excluding leases and generated −₹28 crore of operating cash flow in FY26. Its capital-allocation decisions (the TV18 merger, the Viacom18 restructuring, the News18 Marathi purchase from Lokmat for ₹25 crore in October 2025, followed by a ₹32 crore impairment in the standalone books) are made by a Reliance-controlled board, and the biggest question for minorities, whether and how the Studio18/JioStar stake is ever monetised, is entirely Reliance's to answer.
