Indian tea & coffee: a converter, a planter, a holdco

A complete guide to the two listed companies filed under "tea and coffee": CCL Products, which turns other people's green coffee into instant coffee for a fee per kilo and makes most of its profit in Vietnam, and Bombay Burmah, a South Indian tea grower that loses money on every kilo and owns half of Britannia. How each one earns, why the 2024–26 coffee price spike hit CCL's balance sheet but not its margin, why Indian tea estates struggle to cover their costs, and what a ₹6,462 crore lease-rent computation means for a ₹8,362 crore company.

00

The one-page view: one label, two very different machines

CCL earns a fee on every kilo of coffee it converts; BBTC grows tea at a loss and is valued as a slice of Britannia. Read them separately or not at all.

The National Stock Exchange's 750 largest companies include exactly two that are filed under "tea and coffee", and they have almost nothing in common beyond the label. CCL Products (India) is the world's largest pure-play maker of private-label instant coffee. It buys green coffee wherever it is cheapest, turns it into spray-dried, agglomerated or freeze-dried soluble coffee in India and Vietnam, and sells it in bulk to supermarket chains and coffee brands in about 90 countries. It earns a broadly fixed spread on each kilo, guided at ₹135–140 of EBITDA per kilogram. The Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation (BBTC) is a 163-year-old Wadia-group company that still owns one tea estate group in the Anamallai hills of Tamil Nadu. That estate lost money in FY26, as South Indian tea estates usually do. BBTC's share price, though, is driven almost entirely by something else: it controls 50.54% of Britannia Industries through a chain of overseas subsidiaries.

So this report has three subjects, not one. The first is instant-coffee conversion, a manufacturing business whose revenue follows the price of coffee beans but whose profit follows tonnes. The second is Indian tea growing, a farming business with fixed, legally mandated labour costs and a price set at auction, where the organised estates have been losing ground to small growers for two decades. The third is a holding-company discount: BBTC's market value of ₹8,362 crore is only about 15% of the ₹57,027 crore market value of its Britannia stake. Each subject gets its own deep-dive section, alongside a full treatment of the industry, the KPIs and the two companies.

Five forces define the sub-sector, and the rest of the report is built around them.

  • Two businesses under one label. CCL is a cost-plus converter: the green-coffee cost is passed through to the customer with a lag of a quarter or so, and CCL keeps a conversion spread that has stayed at roughly ₹135–140 a kilo through a tripling and partial halving of bean prices (indicative, because CCL does not disclose tonnes). BBTC's tea realised ₹172 a kilo in FY26 against an all-in cost of roughly ₹215 a kilo (indicative). One business converts; the other grows. Their aggregates mean little until they are separated.
  • The 2024–26 coffee super-spike and its reversal. ICE arabica peaked at 440.85 US cents a pound in February 2025 and stood at about 316 cents on 21 October 2026; robusta went from about $5,800 a tonne at its peak to about $3,400. The spike landed on CCL's working capital and debt, not its margin: inventory days went 185 → 208 → 131 (FY24 → FY26), gross debt peaked near ₹1,950 crore in December 2024, and net debt was ₹742 crore by September 2026.
  • Vietnam is CCL's profit engine, and the tariff map has just moved. Ngon Coffee, CCL's 36,000-tonne Vietnamese plant, earned about 81% of group profit in FY26. Since 24 July 2026 the US charges Indian goods 10% and Vietnamese and Brazilian goods 12.5%; the India–UK trade agreement has let Indian instant coffee into Britain duty-free since 15 July 2026; and an India–EU agreement, concluded in January 2026, is expected to apply from early 2027. Where CCL builds its next freeze-drying line is the signal to watch.
  • Indian tea's split year. India made a record 1,395.53 million kg of tea in 2025 and exported a record 282 million kg in FY26. Then the West Asia war cut January–August 2026 exports by 15.1%. Auction prices nonetheless rose year on year: North India averaged ₹224.40 a kilo in January–September 2026 (+9.5%) and South India ₹132.19 (+4.9%).
  • BBTC is a holding company wearing a plantation's clothes. Its own businesses (tea, auto-electric parts, dental equipment) lose money at the operating line. Its listed value is a fraction of its Britannia stake. And a Tamil Nadu Forest Department notice of 12 October 2026 has provisionally computed ₹6,462 crore of lease rent and other charges on the Singampatti estates it handed back, about 77% of BBTC's market value. The amount is not final and BBTC says it will contest it.

In plain terms

If you own CCL, you own a toll booth on the world's instant-coffee supply chain: the bean price is the customer's problem, and CCL collects a fee per kilo. 01

Bean to jar, bush to packet: how the money is made

Coffee value is captured by whoever converts or brands it; tea value is captured by whoever blends and brands it. Growers sit at the thin end of both chains.

Coffee and tea look like twin commodities. Both are tropical tree or shrub crops, both are harvested by hand in India, both are sold as a dried, processed product, and both end up in branded jars and packets. Their value chains, though, reward different steps. In coffee, the industrial step between the farm and the brand (roasting, extraction, drying) is large, capital-intensive and globally traded; a company can build a profitable business doing only that step, for other people's brands. In tea, the factory step is small and sits at the farm gate. The value is captured further down, by the blender and brand owner who buys auction tea and sells a consistent-tasting packet.

CCL sits at Step 3 and is paid a conversion spread. The green-coffee cost in Step 1 is passed through to the customer, with a lag, under cost-plus contracts.

CCL's process is chemical engineering more than agriculture. It receives green coffee in bags, blends origins to each customer's recipe, roasts and grinds the blend, then extracts the soluble solids with hot water under pressure in a battery of columns. The extract is concentrated and dried in one of three ways. Spray-drying atomises it into a hot-air tower, producing a fine powder for value jars, mixes and vending. Agglomeration steams that powder into granules for mid-price retail jars. Freeze-drying freezes the extract and sublimes the ice under vacuum, producing the crystals sold as premium "gold" ranges. Freeze-drying costs the most to build (CCL's next ~6,000-tonne line is expected to cost about ₹500 crore, roughly ₹8.3 lakh per tonne of annual capacity) and uses the most energy, but it earns the most per kilo. The product leaves in bulk cartons or as finished jars and pouches.

The customer is usually a supermarket chain with an own-label coffee range, a regional coffee brand without its own towers, or a global brand owner buying peak or specialty capacity. Nearly all CCL's contracts are struck on a cost-plus basis: the price per kilo equals the cost of the green coffee in the blend, plus a conversion fee, plus freight and packing where CCL delivers. Because CCL holds roughly 2.5–3 months of bean cover and reprices as contracts roll, a bean-price move reaches its selling price within a quarter or two. That is the mechanism that kept its spread intact through 2024–26.

The grower is paid the auction price. The brand owner pays that price and sells the packet at several times it. Most of the margin is earned at Step 4.

Tea manufacture is simpler and cheaper than coffee extraction, and it happens within hours of plucking, so it sits on or near the estate. An estate with its own factory processes its own leaf and often buys leaf from neighbouring small growers too; a bought-leaf factory owns no bushes and processes only purchased leaf. The made tea is graded and sent to an auction centre, or sold privately to a blender or exporter. Price is discovered lot by lot, every week, and depends on quality, region, season and the balance of supply and demand at that moment. The grower is a price-taker. Its costs, by contrast, are largely fixed: a resident workforce paid a notified daily wage plus housing, medical care and other statutory benefits, whether the bushes flush well or not.

Where is the value captured? In coffee, the two profitable positions are the brand (Nestlé India, which owns Nescafé, earns an EBITDA margin of about 24% across its business) and scale conversion for others (CCL earns about 16–18% of revenue, or ₹135–140 a kilo). Growers capture value only in price spikes; 2024–25 was a windfall for Karnataka's planters. In tea, the brand owners earn the margin. Tata Consumer's consolidated operating margin was 13.8% in FY26, on a business whose core is Indian tea, and branded tea typically earns ₹70–120 of EBITDA per kilo of packet tea (an illustrative range, not a disclosed figure). The organised estate, meanwhile, earns somewhere between a small profit and a loss per kilo, depending on the season and the region.

In plain terms

Coffee rewards the factory; tea rewards the brand. CCL makes money because instant coffee needs an expensive, specialised factory that many brand owners would rather not run themselves. BBTC loses money on tea because its factory step is simple, its labour bill is fixed by law, and the price it gets is set every week at an auction where it has no say.

Two further features matter for investors. The first is geography. Instant coffee is a globally traded manufactured good, so where it is made depends on bean access, energy and labour cost, and above all on tariffs into the customer's market. That is why CCL operates in India, Vietnam and Switzerland (the Swiss unit agglomerates and packs for Europe). Tea manufacture cannot move: it happens where the bush grows. The second is working capital. A converter must buy and hold months of green coffee before it is paid, so a price spike doubles the cash tied up in inventory even if margins are unchanged. A tea estate's working capital is small. Its burden is the fixed payroll and the welfare obligations that come with a resident workforce.

02

Counting the sub-sector without counting the biscuits

On the wrong basis, "Indian tea and coffee" is 80% Britannia. This report uses one basis for every aggregate, and shows why.

Any sub-sector total for tea and coffee runs into one problem: BBTC consolidates Britannia. In FY26 BBTC's consolidated revenue was ₹19,539 crore, of which the "Food – Bakery & Dairy" segment, which is Britannia, contributed ₹19,163 crore. Add that to CCL and the "tea and coffee" sub-sector becomes four-fifths biscuits. Britannia is also already counted in the library's Packaged Foods report, so any library-wide sum that used BBTC's consolidated numbers would count ₹19,152 crore of Britannia revenue twice.

03

Small members, big crops: size and growth

India grows about 18% of the world's tea and 3–4% of its coffee. The listed sub-sector is a ₹4,753 crore sliver of that, and growing only because of CCL.

The two listed members are small against the crops they touch. India produced 1,395.53 million kg of made tea in calendar 2025 (Tea Board final figure, +7.1%), about 18% of world output and second only to China. At the January–September 2026 all-India auction average of ₹202 a kilo, that crop is worth roughly ₹28,000 crore at the first point of sale (indicative arithmetic). India's coffee crop is smaller and mostly exported: the Coffee Board's post-blossom estimate for the 2025-26 crop year is a record 403,000 tonnes, of which about 284,900 tonnes is robusta, and about 70% of Indian coffee is shipped abroad.

Tea is the larger and older industry. North India (Assam and West Bengal above all) makes 83% of the crop, mostly CTC for the domestic market and orthodox for export; South India (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, a little Karnataka) makes 17%. Against those crops, the listed sub-sector is small and almost entirely CCL. Own-operations revenue grew at a 27.7% compound rate from FY24 to FY26, from ₹2,915 crore to ₹4,753 crore. 04

Who drinks what, and where: the demand drivers

CCL sells into supermarket own-label jars from Warsaw to Lagos; Indian tea is a domestic staple whose price is set at the margin by Iraq, the UAE and Russia.

The two crops face very different demand. Instant coffee is a global manufactured good whose growth comes from private label, from outsourcing by brand owners and from emerging markets that drink coffee mainly as instant. Indian tea is a staple drunk at home, so domestic volume barely moves, and the price swings come from the fifth of the crop that is exported.

Coffee: private label, outsourcing and the instant habit

Private label is CCL's core market. Supermarket own-label instant coffee is large in Europe (the UK, Germany, Poland, Spain), Russia and the CIS, and the Middle East. Private label tends to gain share when shoppers trade down, which is what happened during the 2025 price spike: European retail coffee prices rose sharply (roughly 20–40% on some estimates, an approximate range), and shoppers moved to cheaper own-label jars. CCL's volumes grew about 20% in Q1 FY27 and 10–15% in Q2 FY27, against a full-year guide of about 15%. Management says Q3 and Q4 order books are robust and that it has had "no big client loss".

Brand owners outsource. Global coffee companies such as Nestlé and JDE Peet's (which agreed in August 2025 to be bought by Keurig Dr Pepper) make most of their own soluble coffee, but they buy peak or specialty capacity from converters rather than build towers that would sit idle part of the year. A converter with plants in several countries can also supply the same customer from whichever origin gives the best tariff and freight, which is part of what CCL sells. Analysts put CCL's share of global B2B soluble capacity at about 9–10%; management's medium-term ambition is 14–15% before growth has to come from its own brands. New customers are coming from the Far East.

Emerging markets drink instant. Africa, the Middle East, South and South-East Asia and India's north and west consume coffee mainly as instant powder, often in single-serve sachets or 3-in-1 mixes. These markets grow faster than mature Europe and favour cheaper spray-dried formats.

India at home is small but improving. Indian coffee consumption was about 91,000 tonnes in 2023, growing about 2–3% a year in volume and faster in value. The south drinks filter coffee; the north and west drink instant. Three things help branded instant: quick commerce and modern trade, the café culture that introduces younger drinkers to coffee, and the cut in GST on instant coffee and coffee extracts from 18% to 5% from 22 September 2025. CCL's own brand, Continental, sells about ₹125–140 crore a quarter in India and is guided at about ₹550 crore for FY27, up from roughly ₹440 crore of branded sales in FY26. It is still loss-making after advertising and promotion and is expected to break even around FY28.

Price elasticity runs both ways. When bean prices rise, the shelf price rises and consumers trade down to private label, which helps CCL's volume. When bean prices fall, as in 2026, CCL's realisation per kilo falls under cost-plus pricing, so its revenue grows more slowly than its volume. In plain terms

CCL's customers are mostly supermarkets selling their own cheaper coffee. When coffee gets expensive, shoppers switch to those cheaper jars, so CCL sells more. When coffee gets cheaper, CCL's invoices get smaller but it keeps the same fee per kilo. Either way, what matters is how many kilos it ships.

Tea: a domestic staple with an export swing

Domestic demand is steady. About 84% of India's tea is drunk at home; consumption was about 1,197 million kg in 2023, about 840 grams per person, and grows only 1–2% a year in volume. Growth comes from packaged branded tea taking share from loose tea, especially in rural India, and from premium blends. That steadiness is why the brand owners are attractive businesses and why the growers are not: demand does not grow fast enough to absorb a big crop, so a good harvest depresses prices.

Exports set the price at the margin. About a fifth of the crop is exported. Orthodox tea goes mainly to Iraq, Iran, Russia and the CIS; CTC to Egypt, the UK, Pakistan and the UAE. West Asia takes about 46% of India's tea exports by one estimate. When that route closes, tea that would have gone abroad stays in India and weighs on auction prices; when it is open and buyers are stocking up, prices firm. Exports in 2025 were a record on the back of Iraq, Iran and China. In 2026 the war cut them sharply, but auction prices still rose year on year (section 08 explains why).

Competition abroad is fierce. Kenya exports roughly 550–600 million kg a year of cheaper CTC through the Mombasa auction and is the world's largest exporter. Sri Lanka sells "Ceylon" orthodox. Kenyan CTC also enters India: imports were 45.5 million kg in FY25, used for blending and re-export, and imported tea re-labelled as Indian is a long-running grievance. India's export price per kilo (about ₹307 in January–August 2026) is higher than Kenya's, which makes Indian tea a premium choice in its traditional markets but a weak competitor in price-sensitive ones.

Payments matter as much as prices. Trade with Iran runs through a rupee-vostro account mechanism, and shipments slow when Iran's rupee balances run low or when sanctions tighten. In 2026 the Indian Tea Association reported delayed shipments, disrupted payment cycles and some importers prepaying to secure supply. Iran bought only 3.76 million kg in January–August 2026, against 7.92 million kg a year earlier.

In plain terms

Indians drink roughly the same amount of tea every year, so the price a grower gets depends on how much of the crop can be sold abroad. When buyers in Iraq, the UAE or Iran stop buying, the tea stays in India and auction prices soften. Growers cannot cut their costs quickly in response, because their workers are permanent and their wages are set by notification.

05

Where ₹100 goes: three cost structures

For a converter the bean is a pass-through; for an estate the payroll is fixed; for a brand the advertising is the moat. Each cost structure produces a different kind of profit.

The quickest way to see the three business models is to follow ₹100 of revenue through each one. The instant-coffee converter spends about two-thirds of its revenue on green coffee and keeps a sixth as EBITDA. The tea estate spends most of its money on people and, at South Indian prices, more than it earns. The branded tea company spends less than half its revenue on tea and a large slice on packaging, advertising and distribution, and earns a steady low-to-mid-teens margin.

The coffee spike: who carried the risk Deep dive

Robusta nearly tripled and arabica more than doubled between 2023 and early 2025. CCL's fee per kilo barely moved; its borrowings, inventory and interest bill took the hit, and are now unwinding.

Between 2023 and early 2025 the world had its sharpest coffee price spike in half a century. A Brazilian frost in July 2021 had already tightened arabica supply; Vietnam's drought in 2023–24 then hit robusta, the bean used in most instant coffee, and Brazilian dryness in 2024 compounded it. London robusta rose from about $2,500 a tonne in 2023 to records above $5,000 in 2024 and about $5,800 in early 2025. ICE arabica hit an all-time high of 440.85 US cents a pound in February 2025, against roughly 170 cents in 2023. The US 50% tariff on Brazilian goods in August 2025 kept American prices high until coffee was exempted in November 2025. Since then supply has recovered: Brazil's 2026 crop is put at 67.6 million bags by its official agency Conab and 73.3 million by Rabobank, and a global surplus of 7–10 million bags was forecast for 2026/27.

What happened at CCL, line by line

Revenue followed the bean. CCL's consolidated revenue grew 17% in FY25 and 43.5% in FY26, to ₹4,457 crore, far faster than its tonnes. Operating profit grew more slowly (+25% and +32%), and the operating margin slipped from 18% to 16%, because a fixed fee per kilo becomes a smaller percentage of a larger invoice. Management's EBITDA per kilo, the number it actually manages to, stayed in a ₹130–140 band throughout (indicative, as tonnes are not disclosed).

The balance sheet took the strain. A converter must buy and hold green coffee for months before it is paid for the finished product. CCL keeps about 2.5–3 months of bean cover, plus work in progress and finished goods in transit to customers in 90 countries. When the bean price doubles, the rupee value of that inventory doubles too. CCL's inventory days, already high at 185 in FY24, rose to 208 in FY25; its cash conversion cycle reached 246 days. It funded the gap with working-capital loans. Gross debt peaked at about ₹1,950 crore in December 2024 (CFO, Q1 FY27 call), against borrowings of ₹655 crore in March 2022. Interest rose from ₹16 crore in FY22 to ₹113 crore in FY25 and ₹129 crore in FY26.

Mapping the spike across the chain shows that the price risk did not disappear; it moved to whoever could not pass it on. Growers in Vietnam, Brazil and Karnataka were paid record prices in 2024–25 and are now seeing them fall. Traders and exporters carried basis and margin-call risk on their hedges. Freight and insurance rose with the West Asia disruption; CCL's other expenses rose to about ₹190–200 crore a quarter from ₹140–150 crore in FY26, partly because some contracts moved from FOB to CIF terms (which grosses up both revenue and freight cost), partly because CCL spent more on advertising its Indian brand. The coffee spike showed what CCL's model protects and what it does not. It protects the fee per kilo against the bean price. It does not protect against higher freight, a stronger rupee, more advertising or slower volume. In 2026 those smaller lines started to matter, which is why the market, having paid about 41 times earnings in July, paid about 27.5 times in October.

Vietnam vs India: where instant coffee gets made Deep dive

CCL's Vietnamese plant earned about 81% of group profit in FY26. In 2026 the tariff map tilted back towards India for the first time, so where CCL builds its next freeze-drying line is the signal.

CCL describes itself as an Indian company, and its head office, its founding family and its oldest plants are in Andhra Pradesh. In profit terms, though, it is now mostly a Vietnamese soluble-coffee maker with an Indian parent. Its subsidiary Ngon Coffee Company, in Dak Lak province in Vietnam's Central Highlands, completed an expansion to 36,000 tonnes a year of capacity that became operational in FY26. The 2026 tariff map

For a converter that sells finished product into dozens of countries, trade policy decides which plant fills which order. Through 2025 the map favoured Vietnam: duty-free into the EU, and in August 2025 the United States put tariffs of up to 50% on Indian goods (a 25% reciprocal rate plus a 25% penalty linked to Russian oil purchases). Coffee and tea were exempted from the reciprocal tariffs in November 2025, and the US Supreme Court struck down the emergency (IEEPA) tariffs on 20 February 2026. A 10% Section 122 surcharge followed from 24 February to 24 July 2026, with raw agricultural coffee and tea exempt.

Then the map moved in India's favour. The US Trade Representative's Section 301 final action of 23 July 2026, effective 24 July, put India in a 10% tier and Vietnam, Brazil, Colombia and China in a 12.5% tier. Tea (HS 0902) is exempt; green coffee (HS 0901) is free at the most-favoured-nation rate and appears exempt; the instant-coffee line (HS 2101) is not confirmed either way, although one tariff database shows the additional 10% against HS 2101.11. Separately, the India–UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement came into force on 15 July 2026, giving duty-free UK access to about 99% of India's exports including instant coffee. (India kept tea and coffee out of its own tariff concessions; its import duty stays near 110%.) And the India–EU agreement, concluded on 27 January 2026, is expected to come into force in early 2027, with tea and coffee among the lines the EU frees on entry into force.

The next freeze-drying line: the decision that reveals the strategy

CCL's freeze-dried capacity (about 17,000 tonnes a year on one brokerage's estimate, indicative) is near full and expected to be fully used within 12–18 months. Spray-dried capacity has two to three years of headroom. Management's rule is to start new capacity when utilisation reaches 80–85% (group utilisation is about 70% today), and to have 30–50% of a new freeze-dried plant's output underwritten by customers before building it. The next step is likely to be about 6,000 tonnes of freeze-dried capacity ("it won't be 10,000 tons"), costing about ₹500 crore, with 9–12 months' lead time, on a brownfield site in either India or Vietnam. The decision is due by the end of Q4 FY27 or the start of Q1 FY28. As a stopgap, CCL may sign a short-term "strategic tie-up", either contract manufacturing or a joint venture.

Tea's split year: record exports, a war, and prices that rose anyway

2025 brought a record crop and record exports. 2026 brought the West Asia war and a 15% export slump, yet auction prices are above last year's. Both statements are true; the categories explain why.

Indian tea entered 2026 on a high. The Tea Board's final figure for calendar 2025 production was 1,395.53 million kg, up 7.1% after a drought- and pest-hit 2024, and exports set records in both calendar 2025 (280.40 million kg, ₹8,488 crore) and FY26 (282.11 million kg, ₹8,719 crore), driven by Iraq, Iran and China. Then the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026. The Strait of Hormuz closed, Brent crude peaked near $118 a barrel, and the Gulf shipping route that carries close to half of India's tea exports was disrupted. A ceasefire on 7 April eased the shipping crisis but not the trade: buyers delayed orders, payment cycles stretched, and some shipments waited more than 40 days.

The January–August 2026 numbers from the Tea Board, as compiled by the Indian Tea Association on 14 October 2026, show the damage. Exports were 155.33 million kg, down 15.1% from 183.00 million kg; value fell 12.6% to ₹4,775 crore; the export price rose 3.0% to ₹307.42 a kilo; and the dollar value fell 18.7% to $515 million, reflecting the weaker rupee. South Indian exports fell 21% (49.80 against 63.16 million kg), more than the North's 12%. By destination, the losses were concentrated in the Gulf and in Iran and China; Russia and Turkey bought more.

A South Indian estate pays a fixed, rising wage bill and sells at a floating auction price that averages about ₹132 a kilo. It takes a good crop and a good price at the same time to break even.

Converters, brands, growers: who holds the market

Instant coffee is concentrated among a handful of global makers; Indian tea growing is fragmented across thousands of estates and lakhs of small growers; branded tea and coffee in India are duopolies with long tails.

Concentration differs at every link. The global instant-coffee industry is dominated by Nestlé, which makes most of its own Nescafé, with a second tier of brand owners (JDE Peet's, now being bought by Keurig Dr Pepper) and a third tier of B2B and private-label specialists, of which CCL is the largest pure play. Indian tea growing is the opposite: the five largest estate groups produce well under 10% of India's tea (indicative estimate), and small growers produce more than half. Branded tea and branded instant coffee in India are each led by two large companies.

Five business models, five kinds of profit

The converter earns a fee, the grower earns a spread it does not control, the brand earns a premium, the holdco earns dividends, and the integrated company earns all of these and the risks that come with them.

Every company in Indian tea and coffee fits one of five business models, and each produces a different kind of profit, with a different relationship to the commodity price, a different capital need and a different valuation. Both members sit in the less glamorous models: CCL is a converter, and BBTC is a grower wrapped inside a holding company.

Boards, wages, residues and tariffs: the rules of the game

Tea is one of India's most regulated crops, from planting licences to plucking wages. Instant coffee is lightly regulated at home and heavily shaped by trade rules abroad.

Regulation bears on the two members in opposite ways. BBTC's tea business is hemmed in by domestic rules: a commodity board that licenses planting and manufacture, state-notified wages, statutory welfare duties, food-safety residue limits, and land leases that can produce claims decades later. CCL operates largely in export processing zones and under export schemes, so its regulatory exposure is mainly abroad: tariffs in the US, UK and EU, the EU's deforestation regulation, and contaminant limits in importing countries.

Commodity boards and the statute book

The Tea Board of India, set up under the Tea Act of 1953 and run by the Ministry of Commerce, licenses tea planting and manufacture, regulates auctions and exports, and funds development schemes. A draft Tea (Promotion and Development) Bill was published for consultation in 2025 to replace the 1953 Act; its status was not confirmed for this report. The Board's Tea Development and Promotion Scheme for 2021-22 to 2025-26 (roughly ₹970 crore, approximate) funded replanting, small-grower support and factory upgrades. It runs the pan-India e-auction system and in 2026 expanded the auction network to Dibrugarh and Jorhat, with Agartala due in October 2026 and Jalpaiguri to reopen. In 2024 it ordered North Indian factories to stop plucking early to curb poor-quality late-season tea, which contributed to that year's smaller crop.

The Coffee Board of India works under the Coffee Act of 1942. Until 1996 all Indian coffee had to be sold through a compulsory pool; marketing has been free since then. A Coffee (Promotion and Development) Bill has been pending since about 2022 (status not confirmed). The Board publishes crop estimates (post-blossom, post-monsoon and final) and daily export data, though in 2026 it had not released its post-monsoon or final 2025-26 estimates as of May. For CCL, the more relevant rules are export schemes: green coffee imported under Advance Authorisation or into special economic zones is duty-free if re-exported, and CCL's Kuvvakolli plant has export-unit status (approximate).

Labour: the estate's cost driver

The Plantations Labour Act of 1951 required estates to provide housing, medical care, crèches, schooling and other welfare to resident workers. Its provisions now sit within the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, one of the four labour codes that took effect on 21 November 2025. Wages are set by state notification or by tripartite agreements between employers, unions and the state. Assam's Brahmaputra Valley wage rose to ₹280 a day from 1 April 2026 (from ₹250); Barak Valley's to ₹258. In West Bengal, the latest confirmed revision was about 8% from June 2023; a 2024 report put the wage near ₹250 and noted that only about 35% of Darjeeling owners paid provident fund and gratuity on time. Tamil Nadu and Kerala pay roughly ₹475 and ₹546 a day respectively (single-source figures, indicative).

Food safety and residue limits

India's food regulator, FSSAI, sets maximum residue limits (MRLs) for pesticides in tea. Pesticides with no notified tea limit default to 0.01 mg per kg, a level that is hard to meet for chemicals growers commonly use, and from 2024 enforcement tightened: lots failing residue limits, or containing artificial colour, can be barred from auction. The industry has asked for limits for about 20 commonly used pesticides. A draft notification published around 15–16 October 2026 proposes the first tea-specific limits for two insecticides, acetamiprid (20 mg/kg) and imidacloprid (50 mg/kg), with 60 days for objections. Export markets have their own limits: the EU's for anthraquinone (0.02 mg/kg) and chlorate cause periodic rejections, and Iran and Russia test too. For coffee, the EU's ochratoxin A limit for soluble coffee is 10 micrograms per kg. The library's Agrochemicals report covers the pesticide side of this.

EUDR: coffee in, tea out

The EU Deforestation Regulation (Regulation 2023/1115, in force since 29 June 2023) requires companies placing coffee, cocoa, palm oil, soy, cattle, rubber or wood on the EU market to prove, with plot-level geolocation, that it was not grown on land deforested after 31 December 2020. Tea is not covered. Application was delayed twice: from 30 December 2024, then by Regulation 2025/2650 (December 2025) to 30 December 2026 for large and medium operators and 30 June 2027 for micro and small ones, with the due-diligence statement placed only on the operator that first puts the product on the EU market. The Commission's simplification review, published on 4 May 2026, left the core law and the dates unchanged. Soluble coffee (HS 2101.11) is a derived product in scope. CCL's EU private-label customers are the "operators", but they will need geolocation data from CCL for every origin it blends: India, Vietnam, Uganda, Brazil, Indonesia. The compliance cost is real, but it probably favours large, documented processors over small origin makers.

Two centuries of cycles: from teak and tea to Vietnam

The tea estate is a colonial institution in long retreat; the instant-coffee converter is a post-liberalisation export business. BBTC's history spans both.

Indian tea began as a colonial enterprise. The East India Company experimented with tea in Assam in the 1830s, the Assam Company was formed in 1839, and the first Kolkata tea auction was held in 1861. British managing agencies (Williamson Magor, James Finlay, Duncan, Macneill & Barry) controlled most estates until the foreign-exchange rules of the 1970s forced Indianisation; Tata and Finlay formed a joint venture that became Tata Tea in 1983. Coffee arrived earlier, with Baba Budan's seeds in Chikmagalur in the 17th century, and British planters developed estates in Coorg and the Nilgiris. The Coffee Act of 1942 pooled all Indian coffee for compulsory sale until 1996.

BBTC's history is the most colourful in the library. It was incorporated in 1863 by the Wallace brothers to extract teak from Burma; its dispute with King Thibaw over teak royalties in 1885 was the trigger for the Third Anglo-Burmese War. After Burma nationalised its forests, BBTC re-anchored itself on South Indian tea and coffee estates. The Wadia family took control (sources differ on the date), and in 1993 BBTC's group took joint control of Britannia with France's Danone, becoming sole controller in 2009 when Danone exited. Over the decades BBTC's own plantations shrank in relative importance until, today, they are a footnote to its Britannia stake.

CCL is a much younger company. It was incorporated in 1961, built its instant-coffee plant at Duggirala near Guntur in Andhra Pradesh in the mid-1990s and added India's first freeze-drying unit there in 2005. It went to Vietnam in 2013, building Ngon Coffee in Dak Lak, added a Swiss agglomeration and packing unit, a freeze-drying plant at Kuvvakolli and a spray-drying subsidiary at Tirupati (incorporated September 2022), and launched its Continental brand in Indian retail. By FY26 it had revenue of ₹4,457 crore, roughly $490 million at the year's average exchange rate of ₹90.13.

The cycles that shaped the industry

The tea crisis of 2002–07 set the template for estate economics. Exports collapsed when Iraq's oil-for-food programme ended and Russia shifted suppliers, auction prices fell below cost, many estates in the Dooars closed, and starvation deaths were reported among abandoned workers. Brand owners took the opposite path: Tata Tea bought Tetley in 2000 and became Tata Global Beverages and then Tata Consumer Products (February 2020); HUL sold its estates to McLeod Russel in 2005. McLeod's own debt crisis came in 2019. Unilever sold its global tea business to CVC in 2022, and Tata Coffee was merged into Tata Consumer from January 2024. The direction for two decades has been the same: brands up, estates out.

The coffee super-cycle of 2021–25 was the defining event for CCL: the Brazilian frost of July 2021, the Vietnamese drought of 2023–24, robusta records above $5,000 a tonne in 2024 and about $5,800 in early 2025, and the arabica all-time high in February 2025. CCL's inventory days rose to 208 and its gross debt to about ₹1,950 crore; the partial reversal in 2026 let it cut net debt to ₹742 crore by September.

Now apply FY27 guidance: volume +15% to about 63,000 tonnes at the same ₹135–140 a kilo gives EBITDA of about ₹850–880 crore, consistent with management's "similar" EBITDA growth of about 15% (₹852 crore). H1 FY27 company EBITDA was about ₹414 crore, so H2 needs about ₹438 crore, roughly 15% above H2 FY26 (about ₹381 crore). That is achievable but not easy after Q2's +9.5%, and Axis Securities cut its FY27–28 estimates by 8–22% after the quarter.

Now the estate. BBTC sold 37.5 lakh kg (3.75 million kg) of tea in FY26 and earned about ₹177 a kilo of segment revenue against about ₹215 a kilo of cost: 37.5 lakh kg × (₹177 − ₹215) = −₹14.3 crore, matching the reported segment loss of ₹14.26 crore. Note what is missing from the estate equation: no pass-through, no fee, no lever on price. The only short-run variables are the crop and the auction.

In plain terms

For CCL, ask two questions: how many tonnes did it sell, and how much did it make per tonne? Everything else (bean prices, revenue growth, margin percentages) is noise around those two. For a tea estate, ask three: how many kilos, at what price, and at what cost per kilo? The first two are mostly out of the company's hands.

1. Tonnes sold and capacity utilisation

What it is. Tonnes of soluble coffee sold in a period, and that figure as a percentage of installed annual capacity. Formula: utilisation = tonnes produced ÷ rated capacity (pro-rated for the period). Worked example: a group with 77,000 tonnes of annual capacity that sells 54,000 tonnes is running at 70%. Drivers: customer wins and losses, contract renewals, seasonality, and new capacity coming on stream (which lowers utilisation until it fills). Benchmarks: good 80–90% (where CCL starts its next expansion, at 80–85%); average 65–80%; weak below 65%.

How the members compare. CCL does not disclose tonnes ("we will not detail out the volume", Q1 FY27 call). It discloses volume growth (almost 20% in Q1 FY27; 10–15% in Q2 FY27; about 15% guided for FY27) and utilisation (65–70% in Q1, about 70% in Q2, with freeze-dried higher). Group capacity is about 77,000 tonnes on analysts' figures that management did not dispute (indicative). Two cross-checks put FY26 output at about 50,000–55,000 tonnes: dividing company EBITDA of ₹741 crore by ₹135–140 a kilo gives 53,000–55,000 tonnes; adding up plants from the environmental disclosures (India parent ~18,000 tonnes, Vietnam ~25,000–29,000, Tirupati ~3,500–4,000) gives 47,000–51,000. BBTC's tea equivalent is kilos sold (37.5 lakh kg in FY26), with no meaningful capacity measure; its "capacity" is hectares and the factory.

3. Green-coffee pass-through lag

What it is. The time between a change in the green-coffee price and the matching change in the converter's selling price. Formula: not reported; inferred from the quarterly margin pattern and from management's description of contract terms and bean cover. Worked example: if beans rise ₹50 a kilo of soluble-equivalent in a quarter and contracts reprice one quarter later, the converter absorbs the ₹50 on whatever volume is sold from new, expensive beans at old prices, then recovers it. On 14,000 tonnes a quarter, a full quarter's lag on ₹50 would cost up to ₹70 crore, which is why converters hold bean cover and reprice fast (illustrative). Drivers: contract length (customers moved to shorter contracts at high prices and are now returning to medium and long-term ones), bean cover (2.5–3 months at CCL), and hedging. Benchmarks: good one quarter or less; average one to two quarters; weak more than two.

How CCL compares. The quarterly operating margin shows the lag at work. In Q1 FY26, as beans stayed high, the margin dipped to 15%; it recovered to 18% in Q2 and Q3 FY26 as contracts repriced, then moved between 16% and 18% as prices fell. Definition trap: a falling margin percentage is not necessarily a lag effect; it can also mean rising bean prices (a larger denominator at an unchanged fee). Check rupee EBITDA per kilo, not the percentage.

4. Working capital: inventory days and the cash conversion cycle

What it is. How long cash is tied up between buying green coffee and collecting payment. Formula: inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods × 365 (screener uses sales); cash conversion cycle (CCC) = debtor days + inventory days − payable days. Worked example: at ₹4,457 crore of revenue, every 10 days of CCC ties up about ₹122 crore of cash (₹4,457 crore × 10 ÷ 365). Drivers: the bean price (the rupee value of the same tonnes of inventory), bean cover policy, seasonal buying (Vietnam's harvest runs November–January), freight times, and customer payment terms. Benchmarks (converter): inventory days good below 120, average 120–180, weak above 180; CCC good below 120, average 120–180, weak above 200.

5. Freeze-dried versus spray-dried mix

What it is. The share of capacity or volume in freeze-dried coffee, the premium format, versus spray-dried and agglomerated. Formula: freeze-dried tonnes ÷ total tonnes (or capacity). Worked example (illustrative): if freeze-dried earns ₹60 a kilo more EBITDA than spray-dried, moving 5 points of a 55,000-tonne mix to freeze-dried (2,750 tonnes) adds about ₹16.5 crore of EBITDA. Drivers: capacity (freeze-drying lines take ₹8 lakh or more per tonne and 9–12 months to build), customer demand for premium ranges, and competition from new freeze-drying capacity globally. Benchmarks: a mix above about 25% freeze-dried is rich for a B2B converter; below 15% is basic (indicative).

6. FX and hedging

What it is. The effect of the rupee–dollar rate on a business that sells in dollars and buys partly in dollars. Worked example: a $1.50 dollar spread is ₹135 at ₹90 and ₹142 at ₹94.9, a 5% lift with no change in the business (illustrative). Drivers: the exchange rate, the hedge ratio, and the natural hedge from importing green coffee in dollars. Benchmarks: a stable policy with modest hedging is good; large, volatile FX gains in reported EBITDA are a quality warning.

11. Bought-leaf share

What it is. The share of a factory's (or the country's) made tea that comes from leaf bought from small growers rather than from the company's own gardens. Formula: made tea from bought leaf ÷ total made tea. Worked example: nationally, small growers and bought-leaf factories made 790.52 of 1,395.53 million kg in 2025, a 56.6% share. Drivers: the spread of small growers (rapid in Assam, North Bengal and the Nilgiris), estates' willingness to buy leaf to fill their factories, and leaf quality. Benchmarks: for an estate, some bought leaf improves factory utilisation; for the country, a rising share means falling estate relevance. There is no "good" level, but the trend matters.

How the members compare. BBTC's production of 30.16 lakh kg includes bought leaf, but the split is not disclosed. Nationally, the small-grower share rose to 56.6% in 2025 and was 56.4% in January–August 2026. In Tamil Nadu it is about 68%. Definition trap: the Tea Board's "small growers" category is the bought-leaf sector (leaf sold to factories that own no gardens), and "estates" means big growers with their own factories; the categories describe how leaf is processed as much as who grows it.

12. Estate EBIT (segment result)

What it is. The plantation segment's profit after depreciation and before interest and tax, as reported in segment disclosures. Formula: segment revenue − segment costs (including depreciation and allocated overheads). Worked example: BBTC FY26: −₹14.26 crore on ₹66.33 crore of external revenue, a −21.5% margin; FY25 −₹23.93 crore. Drivers: everything in equation 2 (crop × (price − cost)), plus estate sales that reduce the cost base. Benchmarks: good above 10% of revenue; average 0–10%; weak below zero.

How the members compare. BBTC is weak on the annual figure (−21.5%) but improving, with Q1 FY27 at +₹0.18 crore. Among non-members, Rossell India earned an 11% operating margin in FY26 and the other four listed planters −4% to +5%. Definition trap: segment results exclude gains on selling estates, which go to exceptional items. BBTC's tea segment lost money in FY26 while the company booked ₹87.69 crore of gain from selling the Dunsandle estate in the same year; reading the standalone PAT as tea profit is the mistake to avoid.

13. Look-through value and the holding-company discount

What it is. The market value of a holding company's listed stakes per holdco share, and the gap between that and the holdco's share price. Formula: look-through value per share = (stake % × investee market value) ÷ holdco shares; gross discount = 1 − holdco market value ÷ look-through value. Worked example: 50.55% of Britannia's ₹1,12,813 crore market value = ₹57,027 crore = ₹8,174 per BBTC share (6.977 crore shares) against a price of ₹1,198 on 21 October 2026, a ratio of 0.147 and a gross discount of 85.3%. Drivers: the investee's share price, the holdco's float and liquidity, dividend pass-through, governance and control, tax on a notional exit, and holdco-level liabilities or claims. Benchmarks (indicative): good below 40%; average 40–65%; weak above 70%.

How BBTC compares. Weak and persistent: the ratio drifted from 0.156 in July 2026 to 0.147 in October. Section 18 works through the arithmetic. Definition trap: the stake is held through overseas subsidiaries, so a domestic capital-gains haircut does not straightforwardly apply. Any post-tax "NAV" is illustrative, and the gross (pre-tax) ratio is the cleaner measure. CCL has no holdco structure.

14. Return on capital employed (ROCE)

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a percentage of the capital (equity plus debt) used to earn it. Formula: EBIT ÷ (net worth + borrowings), on average or closing balances. Worked example: CCL FY26: EBIT about ₹589 crore (₹734 crore operating profit + ₹7 crore other income − ₹152 crore depreciation) on capital employed of roughly ₹3,700 crore gives about 16%, close to screener's 15.8% (indicative arithmetic). Drivers: margin, asset turnover (utilisation), working capital (bean price × inventory days) and the timing of new capacity. Benchmarks: good above 20%; average 12–20%; weak below 12%.

Third, a new plant starts with low returns (a full ₹600 crore net block and low utilisation) and earns more as it fills and depreciates, which is why CCL's ROCE dipped in FY24 and recovered by FY26.

19. Definition traps, collected

  • BBTC consolidated is Britannia. About 98% of consolidated revenue and more than all of operating profit is Britannia's; about half of consolidated PAT belongs to Britannia's minority shareholders. Use BBTC standalone for its operations and the look-through ratio for its value.
  • BBTC standalone PAT is not tea profit. FY26's ₹211 crore was ₹74.9 crore of pre-exceptional profit (which already included ₹122.8 crore of subsidiary dividends) plus ₹136.3 crore of one-off gains. The operating line lost ₹24 crore.
  • CCL revenue is a bean-price number; CCL profit is a tonnes number. Revenue grew 43.5% in FY26 and 7.5% in Q2 FY27; neither figure measures the business. Lead with tonnes and EBITDA per kilo.
  • CCL tonnes are inferred. "About 50,000–55,000 tonnes in FY26" is a derivation, as are EBITDA per kilo and the 77,000-tonne capacity. Treat any precise tonnage as an estimate.
  • Company EBITDA versus screener operating profit. CCL's company EBITDA includes other income: ₹741 crore against ₹734 crore in FY26; ₹217.3 crore against ₹212 crore in Q2 FY27.
  • Capacity means output, not input. CCL's capacity is soluble tonnes; green-bean throughput is 2.2–2.6 times larger. The Swiss unit packs and agglomerates; it is not a 3,000-tonne production plant.
  • "Tea price" has four meanings. Auction price by centre, all-India average, export unit value, retail packet price. Never compare a grower's price with a brand's.
  • North and South India are different industries. South India sells at about 59% of the North Indian price and pays higher wages. Assam headlines do not describe BBTC.
  • Coffee exports include re-exports. 69% of India's instant-coffee exports in January–August 2026 were made from imported beans. "Exports" in Coffee Board data mean exports including re-exports unless stated.
  • Production includes bought leaf; production is not sales. BBTC made 30.16 lakh kg (including bought leaf) and sold 37.5 lakh kg in FY26.
  • Kolkata CTC is not the national price. Its 17% fall in July–August 2026 coexisted with all-India prices 6% above 2025.
  • The holdco tax haircut is not a domestic LTCG calculation. BBTC's Britannia stake sits in overseas subsidiaries; any post-tax NAV is illustrative.

20. How to read a tea or coffee company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Identify the basis first (1 minute). Consolidated or standalone? For BBTC, open the standalone results and the segment table; ignore the consolidated headline, which is Britannia's. For CCL, note whether a figure is company "turnover/EBITDA" or reported revenue and operating profit.
  2. Converter: find volume growth (1 minute). CCL states it on the call, not in the results. Compare it with revenue growth: if revenue grew more slowly than volume, bean prices fell; if faster, they rose. Neither is good or bad in itself.
  3. Converter: estimate EBITDA per kilo (1 minute). EBITDA growth versus volume growth tells you whether the fee per kilo held. Q2 FY27's +9.5% EBITDA against 10–15% volume says the per-kilo figure slipped slightly; management says it is within ₹135–140.
  4. Strip out FX and other income (1 minute). FX gains fell from about ₹40 crore to about ₹11 crore between H1 FY26 and H1 FY27. A quarter flattered by FX is weaker than it looks.
  5. Check other expenses (1 minute). Freight, insurance and advertising are not passed through. CCL's rose to about ₹190–200 crore a quarter. A further step-up without matching volume is a red flag.
  6. Check debt, interest and working capital (1 minute). Net debt and quarterly interest show how much bean-price risk sits on the balance sheet. CCL: net debt ₹742 crore and interest ₹26 crore in Q2 FY27, both falling. A rise outside the November–January buying season is a warning.
  7. Grower: kilos, price, cost (1 minute). From the segment table, compute revenue per kilo and result per kilo where volumes are given. Compare the price with the regional auction average for the same months.
  8. Grower: separate exceptional items (1 minute). Estate sales, VRS costs and intra-group transfers sit in exceptional items. BBTC's Q1 FY27 standalone profit of ₹7.9 crore included a ₹14.9 crore gain; before it, the standalone business lost ₹7 crore.
  9. Holdco: update the look-through ratio (1 minute). Holdco market value ÷ (stake % × investee market value). A falling ratio means the discount is widening independently of the investee.
  10. Read the notes for contingencies (1 minute). For BBTC, look for any new provision, contingent liability or auditor emphasis on the Singampatti lease-rent computation (₹231.94 crore is the only figure in the FY26 accounts; the ₹6,462 crore provisional computation came later). For CCL, look for capacity decisions and tariff commentary.
In plain terms

For CCL, the quarterly result is a story about kilos, the fee per kilo and debt; for BBTC, it is a story about Britannia's share price, the standalone business's losses, estate sales and the Singampatti claim. If a headline number does not fit one of those stories, it probably belongs to a different basis.

Payout: BBTC paid ₹1.20 a share for FY24 and ₹17 for each of FY25 and FY26 (about ₹118.6 crore a year), almost exactly the ₹120–123 crore it received from subsidiaries in FY26; Britannia's dividends attributable to BBTC's stake are roughly ₹1,100 crore a year, or about ₹158 per BBTC share (indicative). Governance history: group exposures such as the ₹85 crore Go Airlines loans (impaired in FY23) and a SEBI settlement in January 2025 over disclosure of promoter holdings weigh on sentiment.

In plain terms

Buying BBTC is a way to own a piece of Britannia for about 15 rupees on the rupee. The catch is that the Britannia shares are held by BBTC's foreign subsidiaries, most of Britannia's dividends stay inside the group, the family controls the company, and minority shareholders have no way to force the value out. That is why the discount has persisted for years and why a cheap-looking P/E of 6.75 does not mean the shares are cheap in any useful sense.

Part 2: the Singampatti lease-rent claim

BBTC held the Singampatti group of estates (Manjolai, Kakachi, Oothu and Nalumukku, in Tirunelveli district of Tamil Nadu, inside what is now a tiger reserve) under a 99-year lease from 1929, which would have expired in 2028. In December 2023 it decided the estates were commercially unviable. Operations ceased on 15 June 2024 under a voluntary retirement scheme for 534 workers (cost ₹16.63 crore in FY25). More than 8,152 acres were handed back to the Tamil Nadu Forest Department in May 2025 and the last 221.44 acres on 11 July 2026, after machinery was removed and the factories demolished, about 8,370 acres in all.

The claim for back rent on that lease has escalated in steps. In February 2018 Tamil Nadu demanded ₹223.96 crore for 1958–2018, and in January 2019 a further ₹7.96 crore for 2019: ₹231.94 crore in total, which is the only figure in BBTC's FY26 accounts, as a contingent liability. On 18 August 2025 the Madras High Court set both demands aside and sent the matter back to the Deputy Director, Project Tiger, for re-quantification with due process. On 29 May 2026 a Supreme Court order in related civil appeals recorded, citing a Central Empowered Committee report, that about ₹4,655 crore of lease rent for 1958 to 31 August 2025 remained to be recovered. BBTC said no demand had ever been raised and applied for recall; on 19 August 2026 the Court recalled that observation because the amount had been neither noticed nor finally determined, and the stock rose 12.6% to ₹1,605 by 27 August.

Part 4: what BBTC's own numbers say

Strip out Britannia and the claim, and BBTC's own operations are small and loss-making but deleveraging. A Tamil Nadu government department has provisionally worked out that BBTC owes ₹6,462 crore for decades of rent on the forest land its old estates sat on. But it is larger than three-quarters of BBTC's stock-market value, so until it is resolved it will weigh on the share price far more than anything that happens in BBTC's tea gardens.

The global lens: India in the world's cup

India is the world's second-largest tea grower and a mid-sized coffee grower, but its listed companies are small next to the global brand owners. CCL is a global leader only in its niche.

Seen from abroad, Indian tea and coffee look different. In tea, India is a heavyweight: about 18% of world production in 2024, second to China (about 53%), ahead of Kenya (about 8%) and Sri Lanka (about 4%); the top four make about 83% of the world's tea. It is the third-largest exporter by volume, behind Kenya and China (some government releases call it second, using a different measure). In coffee, India is the world's seventh-largest producer, with about 3.5–4% of output, against Brazil's 35–40% and Vietnam's 16–17%. World production in 2025/26 was a record of roughly 175–179 million 60-kg bags (approximate). Instant coffee accounts for roughly a quarter to a third of world coffee consumption by green-bean equivalent (approximate), dominant in Russia and the CIS, the UK, much of Asia and India's north.

Three global patterns carry over to India. First, brand owners earn the margin everywhere. Nestlé, JDE Peet's and the Indian brand owners earn high-teens to mid-twenties margins; the global price spike of 2024–25 dented their margins temporarily but did not threaten their franchises. Second, estates trade below book everywhere. Sri Lanka's regional plantation companies trade below book value, Camellia plc trades at a deep discount to its assets, and Kenya's tea is grown mostly by smallholders organised into a co-operative factory system, not by estates. India's listed planters, at about 0.5 times sales, fit the pattern. Third, the soluble converters are a small, specialised club. CCL competes with ofi, Brazil's Cacique and Iguaçu, Germany's DEK and Vietnamese origin makers. It is mid-sized globally, but it is the largest pure-play private-label maker, and its 16–18% EBITDA margin and 16% ROCE sit between the commodity traders (low margin, high turnover) and the brand owners.

Global consolidation is still running. Keurig Dr Pepper's agreed purchase of JDE Peet's (August 2025) is expected to be followed by a separation of the combined coffee business; Unilever sold its non-Indian tea business to CVC in 2022; Tata Coffee was folded into Tata Consumer in 2024. Brand owners consolidating can be good for converters, because a larger brand owner that rationalises its own factories may outsource more. It can also be a risk, because a more concentrated buyer has more bargaining power over the conversion fee.

In plain terms

Around the world, the companies that make money from tea and coffee are the ones that own the brands, and the companies that grow the crops are valued cheaply. India follows the same rule. CCL is unusual because it has built a sizeable, profitable business in the middle of the chain, doing contract work for the brands and supermarkets.

Technology and disruption: freeze-dryers, cold brew, and a hotter climate

The main technology question for CCL is how much freeze-drying the world will build; for tea estates it is mechanisation. Climate and traceability rules sit over both.

Neither tea nor coffee is a fast-changing technology business, but several shifts matter for the two members over the next five years.

  • Freeze-drying is the premium technology, and the world is building more of it. It costs more to build and run but preserves aroma better and sells for more per kilo. CCL's freeze-dried capacity (about 17,000 tonnes a year, indicative) is near full and its next step (about 6,000 tonnes) is likely to be freeze-dried. The risk is that new freeze-drying capacity in Vietnam, Brazil and India compresses the premium. Management's view that EBITDA per kilo will not rise "from here" partly reflects this.
  • Liquid coffee, cold brew, ready-to-drink and capsules shift value towards extracts and concentrates and away from jar instant in mature markets. CCL is building a liquid and concentrate niche; it does not make capsules. Converters with extraction capacity can serve the liquid formats, so this is an opportunity as much as a threat.
  • Café and specialty growth in India (Tata Starbucks, Third Wave, Blue Tokai) expands roast-and-ground coffee rather than instant. But India's instant base is large, northern and western India drinks instant first, and the GST cut to 5% in September 2025 lowered the shelf price of branded instant.
  • Climate is the largest long-run risk to both crops: frost and drought in Brazil, drought in Vietnam, floods and heat in Assam (2026), monsoon variability in South India, and El Niño risk to Brazil's 2027 crop. For CCL, climate shocks mostly mean price and working-capital swings; for estates they mean crop losses against a fixed cost base.
  • Mechanised plucking (shears and harvesting machines) lowers labour per kilo but can hurt leaf quality. It is the main cost lever left to South Indian estates whose wages are 1.7–2 times Assam's.
  • Data and AI. The Tocklai Tea Research Institute's TocklAI advisory tool, approved in July 2026, draws on more than a century of research; e-auctions and digital grading pilots continue.
  • Traceability and residue testing. The EU Deforestation Regulation (from 30 December 2026) and tighter residue testing favour scaled, documented processors like CCL; small exporters may lose EU access. For tea, FSSAI's move to tea-specific residue limits may ease the zero-tolerance default for some pesticides.
  • "Beanless" coffee made from fermented or upcycled ingredients is tiny today, but in a high-price world it is a tail risk for the cheapest instant blends.

None of these is likely to change the basic economics described in this report within a few years. The most consequential for the members are the pace of global freeze-drying additions (for CCL's per-kilo fee), climate-driven price spikes (for CCL's working capital), and mechanisation (for any South Indian estate's cost per kilo). For BBTC's share price, technology is irrelevant next to Britannia and the Singampatti claim.

In plain terms

The technology that matters most to CCL is the freeze-dryer: the more of them the world builds, the less extra CCL can charge for freeze-dried coffee. The thing that matters most to tea estates is whether machines can do the plucking. And for both crops, a hotter, less predictable climate means bigger swings in prices and harvests.

Bull vs bear

For CCL, the bull case is tonnes plus a tariff map tilting towards India; the bear case is a flat fee per kilo meeting rising costs. For BBTC, both cases are mostly about Britannia and a lease-rent claim.

Because the two members are different businesses, the bull and bear cases below are organised around the forces that drive each one. The points are stated as arguments, not forecasts; the evidence for each is in the sections above.

The bull case

  • CCL's model survived a stress test. A near-tripling of robusta did not dent its fee per kilo; the cost was borne on the balance sheet, and that is now unwinding (net debt ₹742 Cr, down from a ~₹1,950 Cr gross peak).
  • PAT leverage from falling interest. Quarterly interest fell from ₹34 Cr to ₹26 Cr; H1 FY27 PAT rose 42% on EBITDA up about 15%.
  • The tariff map tilted towards India. US Section 301 charges India 10% against Vietnam's 12.5%; the UK takes Indian instant coffee duty-free since July 2026; the India–EU FTA is expected in early 2027.
  • Capacity runway. ~70% utilisation and two to three years of spray-dried headroom mean volume can grow ~15% a year without new spending; the next freeze-dried line is self-funded.
  • Private label gains share when shoppers trade down, and emerging-market instant demand grows faster than mature markets.
  • EUDR favours the documented. Traceability rules raise barriers for small origin makers.
  • BBTC: a ratio of 0.147 to its Britannia stake, a deleveraged parent, and tea that turned a small profit in Q1 FY27. Asset sales (Dunsandle at ₹120 Cr, 4.4× the tea segment's capital employed) show the land has value.

The bear case

  • The fee per kilo has stopped rising. Management says EBITDA per kilo will not change much "from here"; Q2 FY27 was the first quarter in nine where EBITDA growth trailed volume growth.
  • Costs not passed through are rising. Other expenses stepped up to ~₹190–200 Cr a quarter (freight, insurance, A&P); FX gains fell ~₹29 Cr year on year in H1.
  • The brand is a cost centre. Continental (~₹550 Cr guided for FY27) is loss-making until about FY28.
  • Profit concentration and tax. ~81% of CCL's PAT comes from one Vietnamese plant with no tax provision; any change in incentives or trade terms would show quickly.
  • Unresolved trade questions. The US treatment of instant coffee (HS 2101) is unconfirmed; the EU FTA has not been signed; Section 301 faces legal challenge.
  • A new bean spike would rebuild inventory, debt and interest; El Niño is a risk to Brazil's 2027 crop.
  • BBTC: a provisional ₹6,462 Cr lease-rent computation (~77% of market value); a structural ~85% holdco discount; ~11% dividend pass-through; tea that loses money in most years; and a Britannia share price that has fallen from ₹6,271 to ₹4,684.

Weighing the two cases for CCL comes down to whether volume can keep growing at about 15% a year while the fee per kilo holds. If it can, falling interest and a stable fee give PAT growth above EBITDA growth for another year or two, and the 27.5 times trailing multiple looks undemanding relative to that. If volume slows towards 10% and costs keep rising, EBITDA growth could fall to single digits, and the de-rating since July would have further to run. The capacity decision and the location of the next plant are the signals that will show which path management expects.

For BBTC, the operating businesses are too small to matter to the outcome. The share price will be set by Britannia's share price, by whether the holding-company discount widens or narrows, and by how the Singampatti computation is resolved. None of those is a tea or coffee variable.

In plain terms

The case for CCL is that it has proven its model through the worst coffee price spike in decades and now gets cheaper debt and better trade access. The case against is that its fee per kilo has stopped growing while its other costs rise. For BBTC, the arguments are about Britannia and the lease-rent claim, not about tea.
