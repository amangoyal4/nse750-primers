00

The one-page view: a managed market with the state at the table

Two strong private operators, one rescued by the government, a state-owned fourth, and two Tata enterprise businesses that share only the licence.

Indian telecom is the largest mobile market in the world outside China, and one of the most concentrated. In August 2026 TRAI counted 129.3 crore wireless connections. Reliance Jio held 39.3% of them, Bharti Airtel 38.0%, Vodafone Idea (Vi) 15.4% and state-owned BSNL 7.2%. Measured by the revenue that actually reaches operators, concentration is sharper still: in Q1 FY27 Jio took 44.6% of the industry's access revenue, the Bharti group (Airtel plus its listed subsidiary Bharti Hexacom) 40.0%, Vi 11.3% and BSNL 2.8%. Two private companies earn 84.6 rupees of every hundred that Indians pay for mobile and broadband access.

That structure is the product of a decade of violence. When Jio launched in September 2016 with free voice and data, industry ARPU (average revenue per user per month) roughly halved, a dozen operators exited or merged, and in October 2019 a Supreme Court ruling on adjusted gross revenue (AGR) handed the survivors a bill for unpaid licence fees and interest so large that Vodafone Idea came close to collapse. What emerged is a market where three private players behave rationally on price, a fourth is a policy tool, and the government is simultaneously licensor, regulator, largest creditor and, for Vi, the largest shareholder with 49%.

The five companies in this report sit in that market in very different ways. Bharti Airtel is the premium national operator and, with ₹2,10,973 crore of FY26 revenue and ₹11.81 lakh crore of market value, is 72.5% of the members' revenue and 82% of their market capitalisation. Its consolidated numbers include Airtel Africa, Indus Towers and Hexacom. Vodafone Idea is the distressed number three, kept alive by government conversions of dues into equity and a reassessment that cut its AGR bill from ₹87,695 crore to ₹64,046 crore. Bharti Hexacom is a 70%-owned, separately listed slice of Airtel covering Rajasthan and the six-state North-East circle. Tata Communications (TCOM) is a global enterprise and wholesale business with subsea cables and a loss-making digital-platforms arm. Tata Teleservices (Maharashtra) (TTML) is a small enterprise-connectivity business sitting under a balance sheet with negative net worth of ₹18,933 crore.

Five forces shape everything that follows, and the report is organised around them.

  • A three-private-player market where two hold 84.6% and the third's survival is state-backed. Pricing power depends on all three behaving rationally. A Vi collapse would leave a duopoly that regulators would watch closely; a recapitalised and aggressive Vi would undercut prices. The government's 49% stake makes the first unlikely and the second unaffordable.
  • ARPU without a tariff hike. The last industry-wide hike was on 3–4 July 2024. As of 24 October 2026 no further hike has been announced, yet ARPU keeps rising through mix: Airtel from ₹250 to ₹264 in a year, Vi from ₹177 to ₹195. The question is how long mix can substitute for price.
  • The capex cycle rolling off into free cash flow. The 5G build of FY23–FY25 is complete. Airtel's capex fell to 22.5% of revenue in FY26 and its free cash flow doubled in two years. The new money goes into fixed wireless access (FWA), fibre homes, data centres and dividends.
  • The government as creditor and shareholder. AGR dues, deferred spectrum instalments and the state's equity in Vi make the Department of Telecommunications (DoT) the sector's biggest lender. For Vi and TTML, investors are underwriting policy more than operations.
  • The enterprise archetype and the new fronts. TCOM and TTML earn their living from businesses, not consumers. Satellite broadband, data centres (Airtel's Nxtra), AI interconnect and Airtel Africa are where the next leg of growth, and the next capital claims, will come from.

Two numbers summarise where the sub-sector stands. The first is the gap between what the leading operators earn and what they charge. Airtel's India business ran at a 60.1% EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27 and Jio at 51.9% in FY26, margins that would be the envy of most telecom companies in the world. Yet Indian mobile ARPU, at ₹264 for Airtel, is about US$2.8 a month, among the lowest anywhere. The leaders are very profitable per rupee of revenue but still earn modest returns on the several lakh crore of spectrum and network capital they have sunk since 2016. Airtel's own stated view is that ₹300 is the ARPU level at which the industry earns a healthy return.

The second is the size of what is owed to the state. The members' borrowings on the screener basis, which counts lease liabilities and deferred government dues as debt, totalled ₹4,27,195 crore at March 2026. Of that, ₹2,13,397 crore sits with Vi and TTML and is mostly owed to DoT for spectrum and AGR. Vi's bank borrowing at June 2026 was just ₹211 crore, plus ₹3,278 crore of non-convertible debentures. For practical purposes the government is the lender, and every large change in Vi's equity story since 2021 has been a government decision.

In plain terms

Indian telecom used to be a crowded price war. It is now a market where two private companies make most of the money, a third is kept alive because the government owns half of it and is owed most of its debt, and prices have not gone up for more than two years. If prices rise again, the two leaders turn a large share of the extra revenue into free cash, and the weakest player gains most in percentage terms. If they do not, everyone keeps relying on customers trading up to bigger data packs and postpaid plans, which works but slowly.

01

How a telco earns: spectrum, sites and a 28-day recharge

A large fixed-cost network, a licence fee that scales with revenue, and a customer who pays in advance every month.

A mobile operator sells access to a network it has largely paid for in advance. It buys the right to use radio frequencies (spectrum) from the government at auction, builds or rents towers and base stations, connects them with fibre to a core network, and sells access to that network by the month. Almost all of its costs are fixed: the network costs roughly the same whether it carries 10 or 40 gigabytes per user. Almost all of its revenue is variable: subscribers multiplied by what each pays. That combination is why the business swings so violently between losses and very high margins depending on price.

The chain runs from the government to the customer. DoT auctions spectrum, which operators either pay for upfront or in 20 annual instalments after a two-year moratorium under the post-2021 rules. Network equipment comes from Ericsson, Nokia and Samsung; Huawei and ZTE have been effectively excluded since the 2021 "trusted source" rules. Passive infrastructure (the towers, shelters, batteries and power) is mostly rented from tower companies such as Indus Towers, which Airtel now consolidates, Summit Digitel (Jio's towers, in a Brookfield-sponsored trust) and DTL (formerly ATC India). Distribution runs through hundreds of thousands of retailers, who earn a few per cent of each recharge, and increasingly through UPI apps. Since January 2021 there has been no termination charge between operators for calls (bill-and-keep), so interconnect is a small cost.

What the operator sells has broadened. Revenue is collected in advance. A typical Indian prepaid customer recharges for 28 days at a time; the operator holds the cash before it delivers the service, and has almost no bad-debt risk on consumers. This is one reason telecom balance sheets can carry large deferred government dues: the working capital is negative and the cash comes in every month. The exceptions are enterprise and wholesale customers, who pay on credit, and the tower companies, which have learned how much a struggling tenant (Vi) can owe them.

Where is value captured? The scale owners of spectrum and network with pricing discipline capture most of it. Airtel India's 60.1% EBITDA margin and Jio's 51.9% compare with Vi's 43.1% on a sub-scale base that also pays heavy tower rentals. Tower companies earn steady, utility-like margins but carry the credit risk of their weakest tenant. The government captures 8% of AGR through the licence fee, a legacy spectrum usage charge on pre-2022 spectrum, the spectrum instalments themselves and GST on the consumer bill. Enterprise specialists such as TCOM earn much lower margins (21.3% in Q2 FY27) because they buy third-party last-mile circuits and wholesale voice. And the over-the-top apps (WhatsApp, YouTube, streaming) capture usage value without paying the network; the operators' push for a "fair share" network fee has not been adopted by TRAI or DoT.

In plain terms

Running a mobile network costs about the same each month no matter how many people use it, so once the network is built, each extra rupee of price goes almost straight to profit. The government takes 8% of revenue off the top and is paid for spectrum over twenty years. That is why prices, not volumes, decide whether an Indian operator makes money, and why the operator with the most customers on the same network has the lowest cost per customer.

Two features make Indian telecom different from most markets. First, the customer is overwhelmingly prepaid and price-sensitive, so price increases happen industry-wide or not at all, usually led by Jio and matched by Airtel and Vi within a day or two. Second, the cost of entry has been paid in spectrum, not just steel. The 2022 5G auction alone raised ₹1,50,173 crore, of which Jio committed ₹88,078 crore, Airtel ₹43,084 crore and Vi ₹18,799 crore. That spectrum sits on balance sheets as an intangible asset, is amortised over its 20-year term, and is financed largely by the government itself through deferred instalments. The economics of the business only make sense when that amortisation and interest are counted, which is why section 10 spends so long on the difference between EBITDA, EBITDA after leases and free cash flow.

02

Size: ₹3.35 lakh crore of AGR, growing on price, not people

Industry revenue grew 10.4% in FY26 with almost no growth in human subscribers. The money now comes from what each user pays and from homes.

TRAI's yearly report puts India's telecom industry gross revenue at ₹4,04,067 crore for FY26, up 8.59%, and adjusted gross revenue (the figure on which licence fees are levied) at ₹3,34,659 crore, up 10.44% from ₹3,03,025 crore in FY25. At ₹94.9 to the dollar, gross revenue is a little over US$42 billion. The quarterly run-rate kept rising into FY27: Q1 FY27 AGR was ₹88,796 crore, up 9.19% on a year earlier, and access AGR (the mobile, broadband and fixed-line services that make up 83.85% of the total) was ₹74,457 crore.

Usage, by contrast, is still compounding. Indian mobile users consumed 84,424 petabytes of data in Q1 FY27, 8.3% more than in the previous quarter, and the average data subscriber used 28.39 GB a month (26.70 GB in Q4 FY26). Operators who skew to heavier users report more: Jio 43.7 GB, Hexacom 36.2 GB and Airtel 34.4 GB, the last up 36% in a year. Vi reports 21.7 GB per 4G/5G subscriber, up 25.2%. Because prices did not move, revenue per gigabyte fell to ₹7.13 in the quarter from ₹7.51, down 5.07%; India remains among the cheapest data markets in the world. Voice minutes, by contrast, are falling slowly (933 minutes per user per month, from 959) as calling moves to apps.

How do the members' numbers relate to the industry's? Not neatly, and the mismatch is a useful lesson. TRAI's gross revenue includes pass-through charges, non-access services and BSNL; company revenue includes businesses TRAI does not measure at all, such as Airtel Africa (about a quarter of Airtel's consolidated revenue), Indus Towers' rentals from Jio and Vi, and TCOM's overseas business, of which only 35.1% of FY26 revenue came from India. A cleaner cross-check: annualising Q1 FY27, Airtel India (₹41,214 crore a quarter, about ₹1.65 lakh crore a year) plus Vi (about ₹46,800 crore a year) is roughly ₹2.1 lakh crore, against industry gross revenue of about ₹4.27 lakh crore annualised. Jio, with ₹1,46,885 crore of FY26 revenue, and BSNL make up most of the rest.

In plain terms

Almost every Indian adult already has a phone connection, so the industry no longer grows by signing up new people. It grows because each user pays a little more each year, by moving from a basic phone to a smartphone, from a small data pack to a bigger one, from prepaid to postpaid, and by adding a home broadband line. Revenue rose about 10% in FY26 that way, with no price increase. The extra connections TRAI counts are mostly machines, such as smart meters, which pay very little each.

The enterprise and global market sits largely outside TRAI's lens. TCOM's FY26 revenue of ₹24,803 crore splits into data services (connectivity plus digital platforms) of about ₹21,352 crore and a declining voice business of about ₹3,450 crore. Airtel Business grew 12.0% in Q1 FY27. The Indian enterprise-connectivity market is commonly put at ₹45,000–55,000 crore a year, an INDICATIVE range for which no primary source was found; Airtel Business, Jio, TCOM's India business, Vi Business, BSNL and TTML's TTBS share it. The growth there is in data-centre interconnect, SD-WAN, cloud messaging and IoT, rather than in plain leased lines, whose prices fall every year.

03

What still grows when everyone has a SIM

Price, mix, usage, homes and machines. Subscriber growth is over for humans; almost every other demand lever is still working.

Demand for telecom in India is no longer about penetration. It is about how much each connection is worth. Seven drivers matter, and only one of them, the tariff hike, is a single event. The rest are slow, compounding shifts in what customers buy: smartphones instead of feature phones, bigger data packs, postpaid plans, a home broadband line, and connections for devices rather than people.

1. Price. Industry-wide tariff increases came in December 2019 (around 40%, the first after Jio's 2016 launch), November–December 2021 (20–25%) and 3–4 July 2024 (Jio 12–25%, Airtel 11–21%, Vi 10–24%). Each was led by Jio or matched within a day or two, because a prepaid customer can switch SIMs in minutes. Since July 2024 there has been no headline hike, though operators have quietly raised effective prices by withdrawing cheap plans: Jio dropped its ₹249 one-gigabyte-a-day plan in August 2025, which the press read as a "stealth hike". The price lever is the subject of section 09.

2. Premiumisation. This is the driver that has carried ARPU for two years. At Airtel, 4G/5G data customers rose from 280.7 million (78.5% of the base) in Q1 FY26 to 301.8 million (80.5%) in Q1 FY27, and postpaid customers from 26.6 million to 30.0 million, with a record 1.0 million added in the latest quarter. At Vi, 4G/5G customers rose every quarter after Q2 FY25 even while its total base shrank, from 64.4% to 67.4% of the base in a year. The gap between Vi's 67% and Airtel's 80% is Vi's upgrade runway, if its network gives customers a reason to stay.

3. Data consumption. Usage per user grows 25–36% a year at the listed operators (Airtel +36% to 34.4 GB, Hexacom 36.2 GB, Vi +25.2% per 4G/5G user). Video is the bulk of traffic. Airtel admitted that cheap FWA acquisition brought high churn and weak cohorts; its Homes ARPU fell every quarter from ₹537 to ₹523, net adds slowed from 1.135 million in Q4 FY26 to 473,000, and it is now rationing FWA towards fibre.

In plain terms

Telecom demand in India now grows the way a premium brand grows: not by finding new customers, but by selling existing customers something better. Each year about 2–3% more of the base moves to smartphones and faster networks, a few more move to postpaid, and more homes add broadband. That alone has lifted revenue by about 9–10% a year. A price increase would add a step on top, but in 2026 inflation and the West Asia crisis have made operators wary of asking.

Where ₹100 goes: three very different cost structures

A scale operator keeps 60 rupees as EBITDA; a sub-scale one keeps 43 and loses 46 after interest; an enterprise carrier keeps 19. The difference is mostly fixed cost spread over revenue.

The cost structure of a telecom operator is dominated by four things: the government's share (licence fee and spectrum), the network (energy, maintenance, towers), the cost of selling and serving customers, and the capital charge for spectrum and equipment, which appears below EBITDA as depreciation, amortisation and interest. Because most of these are fixed, the same ₹100 of revenue produces very different profits depending on scale. In plain terms

A big operator and a small one pay roughly the same to run each tower, but the big one has many more paying customers per tower. So Airtel keeps 60 rupees of every hundred before capital costs, Vi keeps 43, and once both pay for their spectrum and network, Airtel is left with a profit of about 17 rupees and Vi with a loss of about 46. TCOM's business is different: it resells other carriers' lines to companies, so it keeps only about 19 rupees before capital costs, but it needs much less spectrum and capital.

Capacity and the capex cycle

India's 5G build was the fastest in the world: roughly 4.5–4.8 lakh 5G base stations in about two years after the October 2022 launch, covering nearly every district. That build created headroom, so current spending is on densification, fibre, FWA and, for Airtel, data centres. Airtel India added 1,579 towers and 14,540 mobile broadband base stations in Q1 FY27 and laid 45,171 km of fibre in twelve months; Hexacom added 399 towers in a year and has fibre in 121 cities. Vi is the exception: it is at the start of a ₹45,000 crore three-year build (FY27–FY29), with ₹9,000 crore of orders placed and Q1 FY27 capex of ₹1,930 crore slowed by West Asia-related supply constraints.

The point of the cost structure is operating leverage. Once the network is built, the only cost that rises automatically with revenue is the 8% licence fee and a little distribution commission. The state in every line: licensor, regulator, creditor, shareholder

DoT sells the spectrum, collects 8% of revenue, lends the purchase price back over twenty years, and for Vi now owns 49% of the equity. Every big equity event of the last five years has been a government decision.

No sector in India is as entangled with the government as telecom. The Department of Telecommunications is the licensor and spectrum seller; its apex body, the Digital Communications Commission, takes the big decisions. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) recommends spectrum pricing and policy, regulates interconnection and quality of service, and publishes the data used throughout this report; tariffs are under "forbearance", meaning operators set them freely but must file them. Disputes go to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT) and finally to the Supreme Court. And the government is paid at every step: licence fees, spectrum instalments, GST on bills, and for Vi, a share of any future equity value.

The legal framework

The Telecommunications Act, 2023 replaced the Telegraph Act of 1885. It moves operators from licences to "authorisations" (phased in through rules in 2024–26), provides for administrative allotment rather than auction of satellite spectrum, sets a right-of-way framework, renames the Universal Service Obligation Fund as the Digital Bharat Nidhi, mandates biometric customer verification, and keeps the government's powers of interception and suspension. The 15 September 2021 reform package, passed when Vi was close to failing, is the other foundation. It gave a four-year moratorium on AGR and spectrum dues with an option to convert the deferred interest into government equity; excluded non-telecom revenue from AGR for future periods; abolished spectrum usage charges for spectrum bought in later auctions; allowed 100% foreign ownership through the automatic route; extended spectrum tenure from 20 to 30 years with the right to surrender after 10; and rationalised bank-guarantee requirements.

Day-to-day regulation is less dramatic but constant. DoT's Sanchar Saathi and CEIR systems police subscriber verification and stolen handsets, and levy frequent small penalties: Airtel disclosed fines of a few lakh rupees each in September and October 2026. Net-neutrality rules from 2016–17 bar pricing data differently by content, and TRAI has not adopted the operators' proposal for an "OTT fair share" fee. TRAI required voice-and-SMS-only plans in December 2024–January 2025 so that users who do not need data are not forced to buy it.

AGR: the saga that shaped the industry

Adjusted gross revenue is the revenue on which licence fee (8%) and spectrum usage charge are levied. When the 1999 telecom policy moved operators from fixed licence fees to revenue sharing, DoT defined "gross revenue" to include non-telecom income such as interest, dividends, asset sales and rent. Operators disputed that from 2005. On 24 October 2019 the Supreme Court upheld DoT's definition, and the resulting demand, about ₹92,642 crore of licence fee and ₹55,054 crore of spectrum usage charge across operators including interest, penalty and interest on penalty, landed on an industry that had just been through Jio's price war. A September 2020 order allowed payment over ten years and barred recalculation.

Thirty years, six shocks

From fixed-fee licences to a dozen operators, a free-data price war, an AGR bill, a 5G auction and a state rescue. Each shock left fewer, stronger players.

Indian telecom's history is a sequence of policy shocks and price wars, each followed by consolidation. Understanding the sequence explains why the industry has three private operators, why its balance sheets are full of government dues, and why the market cares so much about whether the current calm lasts.

1994–2002: licences and the fixed-fee crisis. The 1994 National Telecom Policy auctioned mobile licences circle by circle. Bidders overpaid; fixed licence fees caused defaults; and the 1999 policy moved operators to revenue sharing, the root of the later AGR dispute. Airtel launched in Delhi in 1995, the same year Bharti Hexacom was incorporated. VSNL, the state international carrier, was privatised to Tata in 2002 and became Tata Communications.

2003–2010: hypergrowth. Incoming calls became free in 2003, unified licences arrived, and subscribers multiplied. Tata Teleservices launched CDMA and later GSM with Japan's DoCoMo (2008). Airtel bought Zain's African operations for about US$10.7 billion in 2010, the origin of Airtel Africa. The 2008 allocation of 2G licences at 2001 prices led the Supreme Court to cancel 122 licences in 2012.

2010–2016: twelve operators and expensive spectrum. The 2010 3G and broadband-wireless auction raised about ₹1.06 lakh crore. Reliance bought the only pan-India broadband-wireless winner, Infotel, and turned it into Jio. Tata's exit from DoCoMo led to a US$1.2 billion arbitration award in 2016–17. At its peak, more than a dozen operators competed in some circles.

2016–2019: Jio and the great consolidation. Jio launched in September 2016 with free voice and data until March 2017. Industry ARPU fell from roughly ₹170 to roughly ₹100. Telenor India went to Airtel (2017), Tata's consumer mobile business to Airtel (announced 2017, effective 2019), Aircel, Reliance Communications and Videocon exited, and Vodafone India merged with Idea in August 2018. Airtel Africa listed in London in 2019.

2019–2021: AGR and the near-death of Vi. The October 2019 AGR judgment forced huge provisions: Vi lost ₹73,878 crore in FY20 and Airtel ₹30,664 crore. The first post-Jio tariff hike (about 40%) came in December 2019. COVID lifted data traffic in 2020, Jio raised about ₹1.52 lakh crore from Meta, Google and private equity, and the September 2021 reform package and November–December 2021 hikes (20–25%) stabilised the industry.

2022–2026: 5G, harvest and rescue. The August 2022 auction raised ₹1,50,173 crore and 5G launched on 1 October 2022, with one of the fastest rollouts anywhere. The government took about 33% of Vi in February 2023. Vi raised ₹18,000 crore in a follow-on offer in April 2024, the same month Hexacom listed through TCIL's offer for sale at ₹570. July 2024 brought the third hike; Airtel took control of Indus in November 2024; the government reached 49% of Vi in April 2025; Vi launched 5G in March 2025. In 2026 Vi's AGR was cut to ₹64,046 crore, Airtel's revenue passed ₹2.1 lakh crore, and Jio came to the market. There has been no tariff hike since July 2024.

The pattern across the cycles is regular enough to be useful. Each tariff hike is followed by two or three quarters of SIM consolidation, then a step-up in ARPU and margins, then a capex or spectrum cycle (3G in 2010, 4G in 2015–16, 5G in 2022) that pushes leverage up again, which in turn creates the need for the next hike. What is new since 2021 is that the industry has three private operators that all need higher prices, no new entrant, and a government that has chosen to keep the weakest one alive rather than let the market shrink to two. That is the core of the bull case, and its fragility is the core of the bear case.

In plain terms

Every few years Indian telecom has had a crisis that killed off weaker companies: expensive licences, too many operators, Jio's free service, and then the AGR bill. Each time, the survivors became stronger and prices eventually went up. Today only three private companies are left and none can afford a new price war. The open question is whether that calm lasts long enough for prices to rise again, and whether the government's rescue of Vi keeps three players in the market or just delays a two-player outcome.

ARPU without a hike, and Jio as the new price anchor Deep dive

ARPU has risen every quarter for two years without a price increase. The next move looks less like a 2019-style hike and more like charging for data by the tier, while Jio's listing resets how the market values the whole industry.

ARPU, average revenue per user per month, is the master variable of Indian telecom. It is the only lever that moves revenue materially when subscriber growth is over, and because the network is a fixed cost, it converts into profit at a very high rate. This section covers how ARPU has moved, why it keeps rising without a tariff increase, why the next increase is late, what one would do to each operator, and why the Jio listing changes the frame for all of it.

The path so far

Company-reported mobile ARPU in Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) was ₹264 at Airtel, ₹259 at Hexacom, ₹215.6 at Jio and ₹195 at Vi. A year earlier the figures were ₹250, ₹246, ₹208.8 and ₹177. Vi's customer ARPU has now risen for 20 consecutive quarters, from ₹154 in Q1 FY25. TRAI's all-India wireless ARPU, which includes M2M connections and so is far lower, was ₹187.12 in Q1 FY27, up 3.11% on a year earlier. These definitions are not comparable with one another; section 10 explains why. The direction, however, is consistent: every operator's ARPU is rising, and none of it comes from a headline price increase.

Where does hike-free ARPU growth come from? Four sources. First, upgrades: a customer moving from a 2G feature phone to a 4G smartphone typically moves from the cheapest voice plan to a data plan worth several times as much. Vi's 4G/5G share rose three points in a year, Airtel's two. Second, postpaid: Airtel's postpaid base grew 12.9% in a year to 30.0 million, and its 5G network-slicing product "Fast Lane" gives postpaid users a priority lane. Third, plan pruning: withdrawing cheap plans and data-only packs (Jio's August 2025 removal of its ₹249 one-gigabyte plan is the best-known example) raises the effective entry price without a headline hike. Fourth, SIM clean-up: after the July 2024 hike, low-value secondary SIMs left, raising the average of those that stayed. Airtel's management expects 4–5% annual ARPU growth, "maybe slightly higher", for five to seven years from these sources alone.

In plain terms

Indian operators have been raising what customers pay without raising prices, by getting them to buy bigger, better plans and by quietly retiring the cheapest ones. That has added 5–10% a year to average revenue per user. Reading a telco: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number a telecom operator publishes, what it means, how it is calculated, what good looks like, and where the definitions trip people up.

Telecom companies publish more operating data than almost any other listed business: subscribers, ARPU, data usage, churn, network sites, several versions of EBITDA, several versions of debt. That is a gift and a trap. Nearly every headline KPI has two or three incompatible definitions, and the same word ("subscriber", "ARPU", "net debt") can mean very different things at Airtel, Vi, Jio and TRAI. This section takes each native KPI in turn. For each it explains what it is, the formula, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, a good/average/weak benchmark, how the members compare, and the definition trap.

The equation: from a SIM to free cash flow

A mobile operator's economics reduce to two lines. Revenue is subscribers multiplied by ARPU, multiplied by twelve months, plus homes, enterprise and other revenue. Free cash flow is EBITDA minus lease payments, capex, instalments to DoT, interest and tax. The operator controls some of these terms (network quality, which drives subscribers and churn; mix, which drives ARPU; capex; cost efficiency) and not others (the headline tariff, which in practice moves only when the whole industry moves; the 8% licence fee; spectrum prices; the AGR schedule).

Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE, round numbers). Take an operator with 100 million paying mobile customers at an ARPU of ₹250 a month. Revenue is 100 million × ₹250 × 12 = ₹30,000 crore a year. Licence fee and spectrum usage charge at about 8–9% take ₹2,400 crore, network operations about 16% (₹4,800 crore) and sales, staff, IT and overheads about 15% (₹4,500 crore), leaving EBITDA of ₹18,300 crore, a 61% margin. Lease costs for towers and sites of about ₹1,500 crore bring EBITDA after leases to ₹16,800 crore. Capex at 22% of revenue (₹6,600 crore), DoT instalments of ₹1,500 crore, interest of ₹1,800 crore and tax of ₹1,500 crore leave free cash flow of about ₹5,400 crore, 18% of revenue.

Now raise ARPU by ₹10, to ₹260. Revenue rises ₹1,200 crore. Only the 8% licence fee rises with it (₹96 crore), so EBITDA rises about ₹1,100 crore, and free cash flow, after tax at about 25%, by roughly ₹830 crore. A 4% increase in ARPU lifts free cash flow by about 15%. That operating leverage, 80–90% incremental margins on price, is the whole reason ARPU dominates every discussion of Indian telecom.

In plain terms

Count the paying customers, multiply by what each pays a month, and you have most of a mobile operator's revenue. Almost all costs are fixed, so when the monthly bill goes up by ten rupees, nearly all of it becomes profit. When reading any telecom company, the first question is what happened to customers and ARPU; the second is how much of the resulting cash went back into the network or to the government.

Subscribers: TRAI's count, the company's count and the active count

What it is. The number of connections an operator serves. Formula. Closing base at period end; net adds = closing − opening; average base = (opening + closing) ÷ 2, used in ARPU. Worked example. Vi's base was 192.8 million at March 2026 and 193.1 million at June 2026: net adds +0.3 million, the first positive quarter since the 2018 merger, and an average base of about 192.95 million for the quarter.

Churn

What it is. The percentage of customers who leave each month. Formula. Customers disconnected in the month ÷ average customers. Worked example. What moves it. Network quality, tariff hikes (secondary SIMs dropped), aggressive acquisition of low-quality customers (Airtel's FWA experience), and mobile number portability campaigns.

EBITDA versus EBITDAaL: the lease adjustment

What it is. EBITDA is operating profit before depreciation, amortisation, interest and tax. Under Ind AS 116, tower and site rentals are capitalised as lease liabilities; the rent disappears from operating costs and reappears as depreciation of a "right-of-use" asset and interest on the lease liability. That inflates EBITDA. Operators therefore also report EBITDAaL (EBITDA after leases), and Vi reports cash EBITDA on the pre-Ind AS 116 basis. Formula. EBITDAaL = EBITDA − right-of-use depreciation − lease interest (approximately, EBITDA − lease payments). Worked example. Airtel Q1 FY27: EBITDA ₹33,599 crore (57.4%) and EBITDAaL ₹29,840 crore (51.0%): ₹3,759 crore a quarter of lease cost. Vi Q1 FY27: EBITDA ₹5,034 crore (43.1%) and cash EBITDA ₹2,475 crore (21.2%).

The KPIs are links in one chain, and reading them in sequence avoids most errors. Network investment (capex, sites, spectrum) drives coverage and quality; quality drives churn and the 4G/5G and postpaid mix; mix drives ARPU; ARPU times the active base drives revenue; the fixed cost base turns revenue into a margin; the lease line turns EBITDA into EBITDAaL; capex and DoT instalments turn EBITDAaL into free cash flow; and free cash flow, set against net debt and the government's deferred claims, decides whether an operator can pay dividends or must raise capital. When one link breaks, the effect shows up two or three links later. Vi's under-investment between 2019 and 2024 showed up first as subscriber losses, then as a widening ARPU gap, and only then in the cash numbers. Airtel's FWA push showed up first as strong homes additions, then as falling homes ARPU and rising churn, and finally as management rationing FWA.   4. Subscribers differ by source. TRAI SIMs including M2M, company customers, active VLR. Vi: 199.6 million, 193.1 million, ~167 million.
  5. Indus Towers is inside Airtel. Do not add Indus on top of Airtel in any aggregate with the tower sub-sector.
  6. Airtel has three profit numbers. FY26 reported profit including minority interest ₹33,823 crore (screener); attributable about ₹26,700 crore (₹26,695 crore company); net income before exceptional items ₹26,904 crore. FY25 includes a ₹14,322.5 crore Indus consolidation gain.
  7. Vi's FY26 profit is an accounting gain. +₹34,552 crore reported includes +₹58,607 crore of exceptional items; the loss before exceptional items and tax was −₹24,059 crore. Never compute a P/E for Vi.
  8. Leases inflate EBITDA. Use EBITDAaL or cash EBITDA to compare operators with different tower ownership.

  12. TCOM and TTML are enterprise businesses. ARPU and subscriber KPIs do not apply; use constant-currency data growth, digital margin, EBITDA and interest cover.

In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading Indian telecom come from mixing bases: TRAI's numbers with company numbers, Airtel India with Airtel the group, profit including minority shareholders with profit belonging to Airtel's own shareholders, debt owed to banks with dues owed to the government. Before using any figure, ask which entity, which definition and which quarter.

How to read a telecom company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find the right entity and quarter (1 minute). Consolidated or India? Which quarter (Airtel, Vi and Hexacom are on Q1 FY27 until early-to-mid November; TCOM and TTML have reported Q2 FY27)? Is the prior-year comparison recast?
  2. Subscribers (1 minute). Net adds on the company basis, excluding M2M. Red flag: negative net adds with rising ARPU, which can mean low-value users leaving rather than mix improvement.
  3. ARPU (1 minute). Quarter-on-quarter and year-on-year change; check the number of days in the quarter. Good: +1–2% QoQ without a tariff move.
  4. Mix (1 minute). 4G/5G share and postpaid net adds. Red flag: mix stalling while ARPU still rises (plan pruning only lasts so long).
  5. Revenue by segment (1 minute). Mobile, homes, enterprise, DTH, Africa, passive. For TCOM: data, digital, voice; constant-currency growth.
  6. EBITDA and EBITDAaL margin (1 minute). Incremental margin on incremental revenue; for Vi, cash EBITDA. Red flag: EBITDA up but EBITDAaL flat (rising lease costs).
  7. Capex (1 minute). As % of revenue; compare with guidance (Vi's ₹45,000 crore over FY27–FY29). Red flag: capex falling while subscribers fall (under-investment).
  8. Cash and debt (1 minute). Net debt with and without leases, deferred payment liability, payments to DoT, leverage ratio. Red flag: net debt rising in a harvest year.
  9. Profit line (1 minute). Exceptional items, minority interest, attributable profit. Strip out one-offs (Vi's ₹1,611 crore net exceptional gain in Q1 FY27, Airtel's ₹353 crore Africa settlement).
  10. Notes and commentary (1 minute). Going-concern language, contingent liabilities (TCOM's ₹7,845 crore DoT demands), funding updates (Vi's bank consortium), tariff and capex guidance.

Vodafone Idea and the state: from AGR shock to 49% owner Deep dive

The government converted dues into equity twice, cut the old AGR bill by a quarter and pushed the payments a decade out. Vi's problem has moved from solvency to whether it can fund a ₹45,000 crore catch-up.

Vodafone Idea is the most important company in Indian telecom that almost nobody wants to own outright. Its survival keeps the market at three private players, which regulators prefer; its weakness is what lets Jio and Airtel raise prices without losing share to it; and its balance sheet is mostly a set of IOUs to the government, which is also its largest shareholder. Understanding Vi means understanding a negotiation between a company, its promoters (Vodafone Group and the Aditya Birla Group), its bankers and the state.

The arc: merger, AGR, rescue, reassessment

Vodafone India and Idea Cellular merged in August 2018 to survive Jio's price war, creating the largest operator by subscribers. Fourteen months later the AGR judgment added a bill that, with interest and penalties, was larger than the company's market value. Vi lost ₹73,878 crore in FY20 and kept losing ₹27,000–44,000 crore a year through FY25. The 2021 reform package gave it a four-year moratorium and the option to convert interest into equity, which the government exercised in February 2023 (about 33%). Vi raised ₹18,000 crore in an April 2024 follow-on offer, which diluted the government to about 24%. Then in April 2025 the government converted ₹36,950 crore of spectrum dues falling due in FY26–FY28 into 3,695 crore new shares at ₹10, taking it to 49%. In 2026 DoT reassessed the old AGR dues from ₹87,695 crore to ₹64,046 crore, repayable only from March 2032.

Funding the catch-up

Why the state keeps Vi alive, and what it means for the others

The government's logic is structural. A collapse of Vi would leave a duopoly in a market of 1.15 billion human subscribers, write off most of its dues to the state, leave Indus Towers (and therefore Airtel) with a large bad debt, and put about 193 million subscribers through a forced migration. Keeping Vi alive costs the state mostly time value: dues that are deferred rather than forgiven, and equity it can sell later if the business recovers. The trade-off is that a state-backed third operator is unlikely to start a price war, because the state as 49% owner and 8% licence-fee collector gains from higher prices, but it is also unlikely to be allowed to fail if it under-earns.

For Airtel and Jio this "managed three-player" equilibrium is close to ideal: a rival weak enough not to undercut, strong enough to keep regulators comfortable. The risk to them is a recapitalised Vi that competes harder for postpaid and enterprise customers, the most profitable segments. Management denied on its Q1 FY27 call that it was losing postpaid users to Airtel's Fast Lane, but did acknowledge that postpaid excluding M2M dipped slightly. A further risk is regulatory: the government refused to extend AGR relief to Airtel and the Tata companies in March 2026 because Vi's relief was distress-based, but it has now shown that the terms of telecom dues are negotiable.

In plain terms

The government owns half of Vi and is owed most of its debt, so it has every reason to keep Vi going and no reason to let it start a price war. That suits Jio and Airtel. For Vi's own shareholders, the question is no longer whether the company survives but whether it can raise enough money to rebuild its network and win back customers before its government dues start coming due in the 2030s. A tariff hike would help Vi more than anyone else in percentage terms.

Consolidation changed Airtel's exposure in three ways. Indus's rentals from Jio and Vi now appear as Airtel revenue and EBITDA. Vi's tower dues are now an Airtel receivable risk, which ties Airtel's interest to Vi's survival. And lease interest rose: ₹1,911 crore in Q1 FY27, up 20% in a year. In plain terms

Airtel is no longer just a mobile company. Who is winning: price, growth and the return on capital

Airtel and Hexacom combine premium ARPU, high margins and the best returns; Jio has the heaviest users at a lower price; Vi is catching up on mix from a weak base; the Tata companies run a different race.

"Winning" in Indian telecom means three things at once: charging more per customer than rivals without losing them, converting revenue into cash at a high margin, and earning a return on the very large capital the business requires. The three maps below plot the members, with Jio as a benchmark where data exist, on each of those tests, and the scorecard after them sums up.

The first map shows the commercial positioning. Airtel and Hexacom charge the most per user and their customers are heavy, though not the heaviest, users: a premium franchise built on network quality, postpaid and smartphone mix. Jio carries about 60% of India's wireless data traffic at an ARPU about ₹48 lower than Airtel's; its users are the heaviest in the market. That is the position from which a price increase does most good, and why Jio's listing matters for tariffs. Vi is in the bottom-left: lower usage per 4G/5G user and the lowest ARPU, the profile of an operator whose network has not kept pace.

The second map shows growth and margin together. Airtel consolidated sits furthest top-right, but part of its 18.4% growth is Africa; India alone grew 9.7%, close to Hexacom's 10.9%. Hexacom is the cleanest growth-and-margin story among the mobile members, helped by a low-fibre region where home broadband is growing fast. TTML has a high margin but its growth is recovering from an 11.3% fall in FY26. Vi grows at 6% with a margin that, after leases, is half what it looks. TCOM runs at about a fifth to a third of the mobile operators' margin and is the one whose margin is visibly rising.

The third map is about price. Hexacom, with the highest ROCE (21.9%), trades at about 15.7 times annualised EBITDA, a premium to its own parent that reflects its purity, dividend payout and small free float. Airtel, with an 18% return and the largest free cash flow in Indian telecom, trades at about 10 times on the company's own definition of EV, below Jio's IPO multiple on trailing numbers. TCOM sits close to Airtel on the multiple with a lower but improving return. Vi looks expensive only because its EV is dominated by government dues.

Valuation and the global lens: why India trades at a premium, and how big it is

On the companies' own definitions, Airtel trades at about 10 times EBITDA, below Jio's IPO and about a third above US peers. The premium rests on margins, growth and structure; the discount factors are dollar ARPU and the state.

Telecom valuations are usually compared on EV/EBITDA, because depreciation, spectrum amortisation and interest make earnings volatile and capital structures differ. In India the comparison needs one more adjustment: which EV. Screener-based multiples (section 14) add year-end gross borrowings, including leases and government dues, to market value and divide by FY26 operating profit. The companies' own definitions use net debt after cash and run-rate EBITDA, and give materially lower numbers for Airtel and Hexacom.

Why the premium? Indian leaders combine margins that are higher than those of any large developed-market operator (Airtel India 60.1%, Jio 51.9%, against about 36–37% in the US) with growth that is closer to an emerging market (mid-teens revenue growth for the members over four years, 9–10% for industry AGR without a price increase), a market structure that has stopped destroying value, and a capex cycle that has just turned into free cash flow. The counter-arguments are as concrete. Indian ARPU is about US$2.8 a month at Airtel and US$2.3 at Jio (at ₹94.9 to the dollar), against US$45–55 in the United States, so the "headroom" bulls see is set against incomes that bears see as a ceiling. Every major change in value in the past five years has come from a government decision. And a falling rupee, which hit a record low near ₹96.8 in 2026, cuts dollar returns for the foreign investors who hold a quarter of Airtel.

In plain terms

Investors pay about ten times a year's operating profit for Airtel and fourteen or fifteen times for Jio at its IPO price, against six to eight times for big American operators. They do so because Indian operators keep more of each rupee, are still growing, and no longer fight price wars. The risk is that Indian customers pay only about three dollars a month, so there is a limit to how far prices can rise, and that the government can change the rules at any time.

Technology and disruption: 5G, fixed wireless, satellites and AI

5G has not yet earned a premium, fixed wireless has hit its economics, satellite broadband is licensed but unlaunched, and AI is a new capex claim as much as a new revenue line.

The technology risks to Indian operators are less about a new entrant replacing them than about new spending they cannot avoid and new services they struggle to charge for. Eight are worth tracking.

1. The 5G monetisation gap. India built 5G faster than almost anywhere, but operators bundled unlimited 5G into 4G-priced packs. They have since restricted it to plans of two gigabytes a day or more, but there is still no explicit 5G price premium. Fixed wireless is the main 5G revenue use case so far (13.45 million 5G FWA connections in August 2026), and network slicing, such as Airtel's postpaid "Fast Lane", is the first attempt to sell 5G quality directly.

2. Fixed wireless economics. FWA lets operators reach homes where fibre is uneconomic, but Airtel's experience shows the trap: cheap entry pricing brought high churn and weak cohorts, memory and chipset inflation raised the cost of home receivers, and homes ARPU fell for five quarters. Operators are now rationing FWA towards fibre; Jio, with 14 million AirFiber users within 28.6 million fixed broadband, is the most exposed to the same economics.

3. Satellite broadband. Eutelsat OneWeb (2021), Jio–SES (2023–24) and Starlink (June 2025) hold satellite licences. Under the Telecom Act 2023, satellite spectrum is allotted administratively, not auctioned. TRAI recommended a charge of 4% of AGR plus ₹500 per urban subscriber a year in May 2025; the Digital Communications Commission approved most of it on 3 September 2026, with DoT reportedly preferring 5%. Cabinet approval and second-stage security clearance are pending, and no service has launched; Starlink's founder publicly criticised the delay on 11 October 2026. Starlink's Indian site briefly showed a placeholder price of ₹8,600 a month, and retail estimates are ₹2,500–3,000. At those prices, and with capacity limits per satellite beam, satcom is best understood as a rural, enterprise and backhaul complement rather than a mobile substitute (an INDICATIVE assessment). Airtel and Jio have both signed distribution deals with Starlink, and the Bharti group is the largest shareholder of Eutelsat after its merger with OneWeb, so the incumbents have hedged.

4. Apps and messaging. WhatsApp and other apps have already hollowed out voice and SMS revenue. Operators partly recapture it through enterprise messaging (one-time passwords, WhatsApp Business), which matters for TCOM's Kaleyra and Campaign Registry and for Airtel IQ; the DPDP Act rules shape that business.

5. AI and data centres. AI is both an opportunity and a capital claim. Airtel's Nxtra is targeting 1 GW from about 120–130 MW; TCOM won a multi-terabit contract to interconnect a global "neo-cloud" provider's GPU clusters in India and runs 80,000 km of terrestrial fibre, 30,000 km of metro fibre and connections to 118 data centres. Jio has AI partnerships with Google and Meta. The capex mix is moving from radio and spectrum to data centres, fibre and subsea cables.

6. Indigenous and open networks. BSNL's 4G stack, built by TCS, Tejas and C-DOT on about one lakh sites, is a cost-down lever and, if it becomes competitive, a state-backed price-taker. Open RAN could lower equipment costs over time; local manufacturing under the production-linked incentive scheme is covered in the telecom equipment report.

7. eSIM, spam and fraud. eSIMs lower switching costs, which raises churn risk; AI-based spam filters and DoT's verification regime add compliance cost. 8. Energy and sovereignty. Solar and lithium-ion batteries at sites cut the largest variable cost; trusted-source and data-localisation rules raise equipment costs but protect incumbents. Towers and fibre owners, including Indus, are covered in the other telecom report.

In plain terms

Nothing on the horizon is likely to replace India's mobile operators. Satellite internet is coming, but at several thousand rupees a month it will serve villages, ships and businesses, not replace a ₹300 phone plan, and Airtel and Jio have both signed up to sell it. The bigger technology question is cost: operators have to keep spending on fibre, home broadband and now data centres for AI, and they have not yet found a way to charge extra for 5G.

Bull versus bear

The case for the sub-sector rests on structure and cash; the case against on price, politics and new capex.

Both cases are about the same three things: whether the three-player structure stays rational, whether ARPU keeps rising with or without a hike, and whether the cash released by the end of the 5G build stays released.

The bull case

  • Structure. Jio and the Bharti group take 84.6% of access revenue, no entrant is coming, and the government's 49% stake in Vi makes a price war against its own investment unlikely.
  • ARPU without a hike. Mix alone lifted ARPU 5–10% in a year; Airtel guides to 4–5% a year for five to seven years from tiering and upgrades. A hike, when it comes, adds about 11% to Airtel's and 20–40% to Vi's EBITDA on a 15% increase (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • The cash turn. Airtel's free cash flow doubled to ₹76,683 crore in two years; leverage fell from 1.70× to 1.17×; dividends and deleveraging can continue.
  • Jio's listing. A listed price leader with the slowest ARPU growth and public shareholders has every reason to lead the next move, and its IPO multiple (14–15× trailing) sits above Airtel's (~10×).
  • Vi's repair. Going-concern doubts are gone, external debt is tiny, AGR is pushed to the 2030s, and net adds have turned positive.
  • New engines. Africa (+21% constant currency), homes, enterprise, data centres and TCOM's near-break-even digital arm.

The bear case

  • The hike that keeps not coming. 27 months without one; inflation, the West Asia crisis and politics may push it further out, and tiering may deliver less than a blanket increase.
  • Dollar ARPU. About US$2.8 at Airtel; incomes cap how far it can go, and every hike sheds SIMs.
  • New capex cycles. Data centres (Nxtra to 1 GW), fibre, Vi's ₹45,000 crore catch-up and any future spectrum round could keep group capex near US$4 billion a year at Airtel.
  • The state's hand. Every big value event since 2021 was a government decision; satcom pricing, licence fees and AGR terms remain political.
  • Vi's funding. The bank consortium has not closed; a recapitalised Vi could compete harder for postpaid and enterprise customers, while a failure would hit Indus and therefore Airtel.
  • Balance-sheet tails. TCOM's ₹7,845 crore of DoT demands, TTML's dependence on Tata Sons, Africa currency risk and Hexacom's TCIL overhang.

Weighing them, the bull case is stronger on structure and cash, which are observable today; the bear case is stronger on timing, which is not. The cash turn at Airtel is already in the reported numbers, Vi's solvency risk has fallen, and concentration is rising. What remains uncertain is when, and in what form, the next price move comes, and whether the capex freed by the end of 5G is quietly reabsorbed by data centres and fibre. Investors in Vi and TTML are underwriting policy; investors in Airtel and Hexacom are underwriting execution on mix and capital discipline; investors in TCOM are underwriting the digital break-even.

In plain terms

The good news is already visible: fewer competitors, rising revenue per customer and much more cash at Airtel. The uncertain part is when prices go up again and whether the money saved on 5G gets spent on data centres instead of returned to shareholders. For Vi and TTML, the decisive factor is what the government and the Tata group decide, more than anything the companies do themselves.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

  • AGR (adjusted gross revenue): revenue on which licence fee (8%) and spectrum usage charge are levied; access AGR is the mobile, broadband and fixed-line part.
  • ARPU: average revenue per user per month; TRAI's version includes M2M, company versions differ.
  • Cash EBITDA: Vi's EBITDA before Ind AS 116, i.e. after paying tower and site rentals.
  • Churn: share of customers leaving each month.
  • Circle (LSA): licensed service area; India has 22, grouped as Metro, A, B and C.
  • CPaaS: communications platform as a service, such as OTP and WhatsApp Business messaging.
  • DCC: Digital Communications Commission, DoT's apex decision body.
  • Deferred payment liability: spectrum or AGR dues payable to DoT in instalments; counted as debt.
  • DoT: Department of Telecommunications, licensor and spectrum seller.
  • EBITDAaL: EBITDA after leases (after right-of-use depreciation and lease interest).
  • FWA: fixed wireless access, home broadband delivered over 5G or a radio link.
  • GMPCS: the licence for satellite-based mobile and broadband services.
  • HHI: Herfindahl-Hirschman index, the sum of squared market shares; above 2,500 is highly concentrated.
  • Ind AS 116: the lease accounting standard that moves rent below EBITDA.
  • M2M / IoT: machine-to-machine connections such as smart meters and trackers.
  • MOU: minutes of use per user per month.
  • Net adds: closing subscribers minus opening subscribers.
  • RPS: redeemable preference shares (at TTML, held by its parent).
  • SUC: spectrum usage charge; abolished for spectrum bought from 2022.
  • TDSAT: the telecom appellate tribunal.
  • TRAI: Telecom Regulatory Authority of India; QPIR is its quarterly performance-indicator report.
  • VLR: visitor location register; the "active" subscriber count on the peak day of the month.
