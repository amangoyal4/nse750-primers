00

The one-page view: one listed company, three businesses, one chemistry

India's explosives industry is a coal-mining consumable, an ammunition supply chain and a bet on a single imported-and-rationed molecule, all at once.

Every tonne of coal India digs from an opencast mine starts with a blast. Before a shovel can reach the seam, the rock above it, the overburden, has to be broken, and the cheapest way to break rock is to drill a hole, fill it with a mixture of ammonium nitrate and fuel oil, and set it off with a detonator. Coal India alone uses roughly 0.9 million tonnes of explosives a year. Cement quarries, iron-ore mines, tunnels in the Himalaya and metro lines under Indian cities use more. The same chemistry, pushed further, gives the military its high explosives, its rocket propellants and its shells. That shared chemistry, called high-energy materials, is what holds this sub-sector together.

The listed universe is unusually thin. Only one company in the NSE 750 sits here: Solar Industries India (NSE SOLARINDS, BSE 532725), worth ₹1,64,873 crore on 23 October 2026. It is India's largest commercial-explosives maker, with about 24% of the domestic market on CRISIL's estimate. It is also an emerging-market explosives multinational with plants in 9–11 countries, and the country's leading private maker of defence energetics: explosives, propellants, rockets, ammunition and loitering munitions. Everything else in the industry is either unlisted (the state-owned Munitions India, Orica's Indian arm, dozens of licensed regional makers), listed in another sub-sector (Premier Explosives, now being bought by Apollo Micro Systems), tiny (Keltech Energies), or an input supplier (Deepak Fertilisers, which makes the ammonium nitrate). This report therefore treats Solar as a lens on the whole industry, not as its subject, and uses those other companies as clearly marked reference points.

Five forces define the industry in October 2026, and the report is built around them:

  • Overburden sets the base load. Explosives demand follows the volume of rock moved, not the coal mined. Coal India's overburden removal fell 2% in FY26 to 1,980 million cubic metres and was flat again in Q1 FY27, which is why Solar's Indian explosives revenue in FY26 (₹3,343 crore) was still below FY23's. Captive and commercial coal mines, up 10% to 210 million tonnes, are the growing customers.
  • Ammonium nitrate is now a geopolitical commodity. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 lifted Indian ammonium nitrate (AN) from ₹50,500 to ₹72,750 a tonne in one month. India banned AN exports on 18 March. Coal India's average explosive price was reset from ₹39,588 to ₹49,783 a tonne. Q1 FY27 explosives growth of 52% was mostly this price, not more tonnes.
  • Defence demand at home. India produced ₹1.78 lakh crore of defence equipment in FY26, and the FY27 capital budget rose 21.8% after Operation Sindoor in May 2025. Solar's defence revenue went from ₹396 crore in FY23 to ₹2,634 crore in FY26. The Ministry of Defence confirmed that Solar-made Nagastra loitering munitions were used in Sindoor.
  • The export turn. India's defence exports rose 63% to ₹38,424 crore in FY26 while Europe ran short of artillery shells. About two-thirds of Solar's ~₹18,000 crore defence order book is international, and roughly three-quarters of its FY26 defence revenue appears to have been exported (an estimate built from its annual report).
  • Overseas plants, M&A and the licence moat. Solar has bid ₹12,951 crore for Omnia Holdings of South Africa, which would bring its own ammonium nitrate plants. At home, licences issued by the explosives regulator are scarce enough that Solar is paying ₹115 crore for a licensed company with no revenue, and Apollo Micro Systems is assembling a second private group from IDL Explosives and Premier.

Three numbers sum up where the industry stands. First, domestic mining explosives are a slow, price-driven business: in rupees they shrank between FY23 and FY26 because the Russia–Ukraine AN spike of 2022 had inflated FY23, and because coal output went flat in FY26. Second, defence energetics is the growth engine, and for the one listed company it is mostly an export business. Third, the cost of the molecule is set outside any company's control: by the price of natural gas and ammonia, by war in the Gulf, and by a regulator that can ban exports or rewrite the transport rules overnight. The market prices Solar at 82.7 times trailing earnings, against about 7 times EBITDA for the Omnia business it is buying, because it believes the second fact matters more than the first and third. The rest of this report explains how each piece works and how to check whether that belief is holding.

In plain terms

This industry sells controlled explosions. Most of the tonnes go to coal mines, where the product is close to a commodity and the price moves with the cost of ammonium nitrate. The profits and the stock-market excitement come from a smaller, faster-growing slice: military explosives, rocket motors and ammunition, much of it sold abroad. One listed company does both, which is why its valuation looks nothing like that of a mining-supplies business.

01

From gas to a blasted bench: how the money is made

Two value chains share one chemistry. One ends in broken rock at a coal mine; the other ends in a qualified rocket on a launcher.

The commercial chain starts with natural gas, which is turned into ammonia. Ammonia is partly oxidised into nitric acid, and the two are combined into ammonium nitrate, a white salt that is also a fertiliser. For explosives, AN is made into low-density porous prills (technical ammonium nitrate, or TAN) or kept as a hot solution. The explosive maker mixes AN with a fuel and an emulsifier. The cheapest product, ANFO, is AN prills soaked in fuel oil. The dominant product at big mines is bulk emulsion: an AN-and-water solution dispersed in oil, which is water-resistant and pumpable. It is mixed on site by "site-mixed explosive" trucks that drive from a bulk plant, usually within 50–60 km of the mine, and pump it straight into the blast hole.

Two other product families matter more for profit than for tonnes. Packaged or cartridge explosives are factory-filled sticks used in underground mines, small quarries, tunnels and construction, and they sell for more per tonne. Initiating systems are the detonators, shock tube, detonating cord and boosters that set the main charge off in the right sequence. They are sold by the unit, not the tonne, and carry the highest margins in the commercial chain. Since July 2025 India has banned the manufacture and possession of electric detonators, so the market is moving to electronic detonators, which favours makers that design their own electronics. Solar's annual report implies initiating systems were about 11% of its FY26 revenue (an approximate figure from a product-mix chart).

The defence chain uses the same nitration and mixing skills, pushed to military specifications. High-energy materials such as RDX, HMX and TNT are made by nitrating organic compounds. They fill warheads, shells, bombs and mines. Composite propellants, made from ammonium perchlorate and a rubbery binder called HTPB, are cast into rocket motors for missiles such as Akash and BrahMos and for Pinaka rockets. The final steps, filling, integration and qualification, are long and trial-heavy. A product earns revenue only after the Army's quality agency or DRDO signs off, which is why defence revenue arrives in lumps and why a slipped trial can move a quarter's numbers by hundreds of crores.

The commercial chain has one awkward feature: the explosive maker does not control its main cost. AN is about 65% of Solar's raw-material bill and roughly a third of the selling price of a typical explosive. When ammonia doubles, AN follows, and the explosive maker must pass the increase on through price-variation clauses that recover only part of it at first. That is why revenue in this industry can jump 75% in a year (Solar in FY23) and fall 12% the next (FY24) while the business underneath barely changes. It is also why Solar wants Omnia, which owns AN plants. Section 05 follows the 2026 shock through each link.

The defence chain has the opposite feature: costs are specialised and largely in the maker's hands, but the timing of revenue is not. Raw materials such as ammonium perchlorate, HTPB and metal bodies matter, yet the margin is set by the contract and by the maker's yield, while revenue waits on trials, inspections and export licences.

In plain terms

There are two ways to earn money from the same chemistry. Selling explosives to mines is a volume business where the customer is powerful and the main ingredient's price swings wildly, so margins are thin unless you add detonators and services. Selling energetics to armies is a contract business where margins are fat but the money arrives only when each batch passes its tests. A company that does both can use the first to fund the second.

02

How big, how fast: a ₹10,000–14,000 crore market nobody measures

India publishes no official explosives tonnage series, so the market has to be rebuilt from Coal India's consumption, one company's revenue and a market-share estimate.

Start with what is measured. Coal India's subsidiaries consume about 0.9 million tonnes of explosives a year, a figure Coal India itself cited in April 2026 when it explained why it would absorb the explosives price rise rather than pass it on in coal prices. Coal India produced 768 million tonnes of the roughly 1,036 million tonnes of coal India mined in FY26, about 74%. Singareni Collieries and the captive and commercial mines produce the rest, and their opencast mines are similarly blasting-intensive. Coal is usually put at 65–75% of Indian explosives demand by volume, with limestone, iron ore, other metals and infrastructure making up the remainder. Scaling up, India uses an INDICATIVE 1.4–1.8 million tonnes of explosives a year.

The value can be cross-checked two ways. Solar's FY26 domestic explosives revenue was ₹3,343 crore across its three Indian baskets. If that is about 24% of the market, as CRISIL estimates, the market was about ₹13,900 crore in FY26 at pre-war AN prices. Building up instead, 1.4–1.8 million tonnes at ₹45,000–55,000 a tonne is ₹6,300–9,900 crore of explosive product, plus ₹2,500–4,000 crore of initiating systems and accessories, for ₹9,000–14,000 crore. The overlap gives an INDICATIVE ₹10,000–14,000 crore market in FY26. The April 2026 price reset alone would lift FY27's value by roughly a quarter before any volume growth, to perhaps ₹16,000–18,000 crore (INDICATIVE).

Growth is slower than the headlines suggest. Solar's annual report cites a third-party forecast of 9% a year for the Indian industrial-explosives market. Management's own long-run view is more conservative and more useful: mining explosives demand of 6–7% a year in volume, Solar growing volumes at 10–12% by taking share, and value growth of about 15% including mix and price. FY26 delivered roughly none of that: Indian coal production was about 1% lower, Coal India's overburden removal fell 2%, and Solar's domestic explosives revenue rose only 3%.

The global market is harder to pin down because published estimates use different definitions. One research house puts it at US$8 billion, another at US$14.4 billion. Adding up what the largest makers report gives a more defensible answer: Orica about US$5.3 billion of revenue, Dyno Nobel's explosives arm about US$2.1 billion, Enaex about US$2.2 billion, Maxam perhaps US$1.5–1.8 billion, AECI Mining about US$1 billion, Solar's explosives about US$0.8 billion and Omnia's mining arm about US$0.55 billion. Including China's large state-licensed industry and regional independents, the market is an INDICATIVE US$18–22 billion (about ₹1.7–2.1 lakh crore) including initiating systems and services, or US$8–10 billion for explosive product alone. India is about 6–8% of it.

In plain terms

India blasts somewhere around one and a half million tonnes of explosives a year, worth roughly ₹10,000–14,000 crore before the 2026 price jump. That is small next to the coal it unlocks, but it is a necessity: no blast, no coal. The number grows a little faster than mining volumes and swings a lot with the price of ammonium nitrate, so a year of 50% revenue growth can be mostly inflation.

03

Overburden, not coal: the base load nobody headlines

Explosives are consumed in proportion to rock moved. Coal India's rock-moving fell in FY26 and again in Q1 FY27, even as its coal dispatches rose.

A coal mine reports tonnes of coal, but an explosives maker sells into overburden removal, the cubic metres of soil and rock stripped away to reach the seam. Coal India's opencast mines move roughly 2.5–3 cubic metres of overburden for every tonne of coal, and most of that rock is blasted. The distinction matters because the two series can diverge. In FY26 Coal India produced 768.19 million tonnes, 1.6% less than FY25, and removed 1,980 million cubic metres of overburden, 2% less. Heavy monsoons hit both Coal India and Singareni; Solar's annual report calls FY26's coal year "muted". Only 12% of Coal India's overburden was removed by its own departmental crews; the rest was done by contractors, but the explosives are still bought by Coal India and its subsidiaries through rate contracts.

Why does management still talk of 6–7% demand growth? Because the long-run drivers have not changed. The Ministry of Coal projects coal demand of about 1.5 billion tonnes by FY30 (its upper projection is 1,665 million tonnes), and India's power demand is still rising. Cement capacity of about 680 million tonnes is heading for 850 million by FY30, and every tonne of cement needs blasted limestone. Infrastructure spending, including Himalayan rail and road tunnels and pumped-storage hydro projects, uses packaged explosives and precision initiation. After the West Asia shock, some industrial users switched from diesel and LPG to coal-based power, which supports demand. And captive mining, the faster-growing customer, is still ramping. Solar's management called FY26 "the bottom-out kind of situation for OB removal". That may prove right, but Q1 FY27's overburden numbers did not yet confirm it, so the claim needs checking month by month.

The long-run risk is the reverse: India's coal peak. As renewable power grows, coal production will eventually plateau, and with it the largest single source of explosives demand. Nobody in the industry expects that before the 2030s, and limestone, metals and infrastructure would cushion it, but it is the reason the industry's growth multiple should not look like a consumer company's.

In plain terms

Count the rock, not the coal. Explosives are used to break the rock above the coal seam, so the useful number is cubic metres of overburden removed, which Coal India reports every quarter. In FY26 and early FY27 that number fell slightly, even though Coal India dispatched more coal by selling from its stockpiles. Domestic explosives revenue rose in Q1 FY27 because prices went up, not because more rock was blasted.

In plain terms

The private and captive coal mines are where the volume growth is. They grew about 10% in FY26 while Coal India shrank, and they buy explosives on negotiated commercial terms rather than on a lowest-bidder tender. A supplier that wins those customers grows faster and earns more per tonne than one that depends on Coal India alone.

04

The molecule India rations: ammonium nitrate supply

One input is about a third of the selling price of an explosive. India makes most of it, imports the rest from Russia, and in March 2026 banned its export.

Ammonium nitrate is about 60% of a typical bulk explosive by weight and about 65% of Solar's raw-material bill. Raw materials were 49.7% of Solar's FY26 sales, so AN alone is roughly a third of the selling price of the explosives it makes (an INDICATIVE calculation, since the company does not split its material costs). For a pure bulk-explosives supplier with no initiating systems or services, the share is higher still. No other cost line comes close, which is why AN supply, AN price and AN logistics shape the whole industry.

India's explosive-grade AN comes from three places. The largest is Deepak Fertilisers (primer), through its Smartchem subsidiary: 587 thousand tonnes a year of technical AN capacity (544 at Taloja near Mumbai plus a 43 thousand tonne satellite unit), which ran at 92% utilisation in FY26. Deepak describes itself as India's only maker of high-density AN. The second is the state-owned Rashtriya Chemicals & Fertilizers, whose Trombay complex makes AN as a small product line. The third is imports. Trade-press figures put AN imports at about 356 thousand tonnes in FY23 and about 305 thousand tonnes in April–November of FY24, almost all from Russia. Against domestic capacity of about 1.1 million tonnes, that makes imports roughly 25–30% of supply (INDICATIVE), lower than the 40–60% often quoted. India's official trade database could not be queried to confirm it.

Supply concentration is a mixed blessing for explosive makers. A domestic supplier near the mines means shorter freight, no exposure to Russian shipping or sanctions, and less rupee risk. But it also means one price-setter. Deepak's own numbers show how much pricing power AN has when supply is tight: its group operating margin was 15% in FY26 and 26% in Q1 FY27, when it reported an "all-time high EBITDA" and its TAN revenue rose 36% to ₹904 crore on 12% lower volumes. Deepak is also building a downstream business, Deepak Mining Solutions, that sells explosives, initiating systems and blasting services. The supplier is becoming a competitor. The Fertilizers report covers the ammonia and nitrogen economics behind Deepak and RCF in more depth.

The second reason AN behaves like a policy variable is regulation. Under the Ammonium Nitrate Rules, 2012, any mixture with more than 45% AN is treated as an explosive: making, storing, transporting and selling it each require a licence from the Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation (PESO). The Beirut port explosion of August 2020, caused by badly stored AN, renewed the focus on storage and tracking. PESO's online Ammonium Nitrate Return System now requires advance intimation and a transport pass for every consignment (forms R-11(a) and R-11(b)) and barcoding of AN bags. When those portal procedures changed in 2026, Deepak's production and dispatches were disrupted: its Q1 FY27 TAN volume fell to 130 thousand tonnes, 12% lower than a year earlier and 21% below Q4 FY26, at the very moment the market wanted every tonne.

Price, not tonnes: the 2026 AN shock through the chain Deep dive

In five months ammonia went from $298 to $801 a tonne. Here is where each rupee of that increase landed: at the AN maker, the explosive maker, Coal India and the investor reading the results.

The 2026 shock is the best natural experiment this industry has had since 2022, and it explains most of what investors saw in Q1 FY27 results. It started upstream. Middle Eastern bulk ammonia, priced free on board, was $298 a tonne in June 2025. It rose through the second half of 2025 as gas markets tightened, reaching $528 in December. When the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026 and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted, ammonia jumped to $707 in April and $801 in May before easing to $777 in June. Even after the 7 April ceasefire, prices stayed far above pre-war levels.

Three lessons follow. First, a pure bulk supplier with no AN stock and a price formula that lags is underwater after a shock like this, which is why the small tender-driven suppliers suffered. Premier Explosives' operating margin fell to 5.7% in Q1 FY27 on revenue 28% lower, after a negative operating profit in Q4 FY26. Keltech held a 7.5% margin on 28% higher revenue, typical of its thin-margin model. Second, inventory bought before the spike is a temporary gift. Solar built inventory ahead of the war in Q4 FY26 (its inventory days rose from 95 to 130) and told investors to expect "inventory gain in the coming quarters". That gain cannot repeat. Third, product mix is the real defence. Packaged explosives, detonators and services carry less AN per rupee of revenue, and defence carries almost none.

How Solar's results absorbed it

Solar's Q1 FY27 (Apr–Jun 2026, reported 13 August) showed the shock in its top line. Domestic explosives revenue rose 52% to ₹1,361 crore and international explosives 65% to ₹1,364 crore; total revenue rose 70% to ₹3,668 crore. Why did the margin hold when the one-tonne example says it should have collapsed? Management has described the mechanics. Rise-and-fall clauses in its contracts pass on about 60% of an AN increase immediately, with more recovered after a lag of a quarter or so. In past episodes, when raw materials rose 50–70% (as in 2022), Solar's EBITDA margin fell by only 2–3 percentage points. A rough INDICATIVE elasticity shows the size of the gap that needed covering: if AN is about 32% of explosives sales, a 44% AN rise with 60% immediate pass-through leaves 0.32 × 0.44 × 0.40, or about 5.6% of explosives sales unrecovered at first. That was covered by pre-war inventory, lagged repricing, a higher share of defence and international revenue, and the operating leverage of a 70% larger top line.

In plain terms

When the main ingredient gets 44% dearer, the selling price follows only partly and only after a delay. A company that stocked up in advance, sells many products that use little ammonium nitrate, and has a big defence business can keep its margin through the shock. A small supplier that sells only bulk explosives to Coal India cannot. Q1 FY27 showed both outcomes.

What happens when AN falls

The shock also runs in reverse, and investors should be ready for it. If AN eases back towards pre-war levels, Coal India's price-variation formula will cut explosive prices, and domestic explosives revenue will deflate even if tonnes rise. The precedent is FY24, when AN fell after the 2022 spike: Solar's domestic explosives revenue fell 17% (₹3,667 to ₹3,052 crore) and group revenue 12%, while operating profit rose because selling prices lagged falling costs. A fall in AN is therefore usually good for margins and bad for reported growth. The FY27 guidance of about ₹14,000 crore already contains 18–20% price inflation in explosives; if AN normalises in the second half, that part of the guidance is at risk even as margins widen.

The global majors were less exposed. Orica said in May 2026 that it saw "no immediate material constraints" from the Middle East because its inputs are generally not shipped through Hormuz, and that it expected earnings to rise in every segment and region. AN-integrated majors own their supply; that, more than anything else, is what Solar is trying to buy in Omnia (Section 11).

In plain terms

Read this industry's revenue growth with suspicion. In a year when ammonium nitrate gets expensive, revenue jumps even if no extra rock is blasted; in a year when it gets cheaper, revenue shrinks even if volumes grow. The numbers to check are the share of growth from price versus volume, the raw-material cost as a share of sales, and the overburden data from Coal India.

The rules of procurement favour domestic makers. Under the Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP 2020), the "Buy (Indian-IDDM)" category, for indigenously designed, developed and manufactured equipment, sits at the top of the preference order. The ministry has issued five Positive Indigenisation Lists for the armed services and five for the defence public-sector companies, each embargoing imports of named items after a set date. A draft DAP 2026, released for comment on 10 February 2026, would cut the number of acquisition categories from five to four, raise the indigenous-content requirement for IDDM from 50% to 60%, and add long-term bulk-acquisition and low-cost capital-acquisition routes. A long-term bulk route would suit ammunition well, since it would let the services sign multi-year volume contracts. The DAP was still labelled a draft in a June 2026 government document, and no final version had been notified by 23 October 2026.

On 1 October 2021 the board's factories were split into seven state-owned companies, of which Munitions India took over ammunition and explosives. From grenades to Pinaka to Nagastra: building a private energetics house Deep dive

How a Nagpur explosives maker turned its licences and nitration chemistry into a ₹2,634 crore defence business with a ₹18,000 crore order book, two-thirds of it from abroad.

Solar's defence business is the most important single development in Indian energetics since the ordnance factories were corporatised, and it shows how a private company enters a market that was a state monopoly for decades. The logic was simple: the plants, licences, safety systems and nitration chemistry needed for commercial explosives are a large part of what is needed for military ones. The execution took fifteen years. Solar obtained the first private licence to make military explosives in 2010 and used its subsidiary Economic Explosives Ltd (EEL), incorporated in 1995, as its defence platform. Early products were explosives and propellants supplied to state integrators. Its multi-mode hand grenade, designed by DRDO, became the first private-sector grenade order in 2016–17. The Army took delivery of the first Nagastra-1 loitering munitions in 2024. On 17 February 2025 EEL was renamed Solar Defence & Aerospace Ltd (SDAL).

The export turn: India's share of a global ammunition shortage

Europe ran short of shells; India's defence exports rose 63% in a year. Solar's defence business is, in practice, an export business.

The global backdrop is the largest ammunition shortage since the Cold War. The war in Ukraine consumed artillery shells faster than Western factories could make them, and European and American stocks of ammunition and missiles fell to levels that governments now describe as a security risk. Solar's annual report refers to a "well-documented global shortage of medium and large calibre ammunition, especially 155mm", and its managing director told the 2026 AGM that ammunition and missile stocks in Europe and the US remain low. The clearest measure of the resulting boom is Germany's Rheinmetall. Its Weapon & Ammunition division earned €3.53 billion of sales in CY2025, up 27%, at an operating margin of about 29%, and in H1 2026 its sales rose another 33% to €1.76 billion. Rheinmetall's group backlog reached €80.5 billion by mid-2026, and its new Unterlüß plant is ramping towards 350,000 shells a year.

The export framing changes which risks matter. Each shipment of military goods needs an authorisation from the Department of Defence Production under the SCOMET list (Category 6, munitions) and an end-user certificate from the buying government; India has been a member of the Wassenaar Arrangement on export controls since 2017. Buyers are foreign governments or their contractors, so politics can stop a contract, and payment terms are less generous than at home: Munitions India's export customers pay 15% upfront, against 60% for the Army. Solar does not name its export customers or countries. The one publicly confirmed destination is Armenia, which received the first tranche of Guided Pinaka rockets in January 2026. Rupee weakness helps: at about ₹94.9 to the dollar in October 2026, near a record low, export contracts priced in dollars or euros translate into more rupees.

The global benchmark also shows the risk at the top of a cycle. Rheinmetall cut its 2026 sales guidance to €13.7–14.2 billion after a frigate cancellation, and its H1 2026 ammunition margin of 23.7% was below CY2025's 29% as it absorbed new plants. Ammunition booms end when stocks are rebuilt and new capacity arrives. Europe is building plants that will compete with Indian exporters later in the decade. Dyno Nobel, the US-Australian explosives major, has formed a joint venture called Nitradyn with REPKON USA to make defence energetics, a sign that commercial-explosives companies everywhere see the same opportunity.

In plain terms

Most of Solar's defence money comes from foreign governments, not the Indian Army. That is good while the world is short of shells and the rupee is weak. It also means the business depends on export licences, foreign politics and how long the shortage lasts, more than on India's defence budget.

In plain terms

The quickest test of whether an Indian defence exporter is gaining ground is to compare its growth with the national export total. In FY26 India's private defence exports grew 14%; Solar's implied defence exports roughly doubled. That gap is market share being won, at least for now.
