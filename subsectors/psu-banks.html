00

The one-page view: the repair is done, the discount is not

Twelve state-owned banks hold over half of India's deposits, earn as much as the entire private-bank sector, and are valued at half its price.

Ten years ago India's public sector banks (PSBs) were the problem at the centre of the economy. In FY18 they lost about ₹85,370 crore between them, their gross bad loans peaked at 14.58% of advances in March 2018, and the government had to put in ₹3.1 lakh crore of fresh capital over five years to keep them solvent. In FY26 the same banks, now merged into twelve, reported a combined net profit of ₹1.98 lakh crore, their fourth record year in a row, with gross non-performing assets (NPAs) down to 1.93% and net NPAs to 0.39%. Few banking systems anywhere have turned round this far, this fast, without a single depositor losing money.

This report covers the eleven PSBs that sit in the NSE 750: State Bank of India (SBI), Punjab National Bank (PNB), Bank of Baroda (BoB), Canara Bank, Union Bank of India, Bank of India (BoI), Indian Bank, Central Bank of India, Indian Overseas Bank (IOB), UCO Bank and Bank of Maharashtra. The twelfth PSB, Punjab & Sind Bank, is listed but too small for the index. Together the eleven held ₹154.8 lakh crore of deposits at March 2026, earned ₹1,96,888 crore of net profit in FY26 and were worth ₹17.3 lakh crore on 17 October 2026.

The single most important fact about the sub-sector is a comparison. The 19 listed private banks covered in our private-bank sub-sector report earned about ₹1.97 lakh crore in FY26, the same as the eleven PSBs. The PSBs did it with 1.6 times the deposits. Yet the market values the private banks at about ₹34.9 lakh crore, 2.13 times their book value and 17.7 times earnings, and the PSBs at ₹17.3 lakh crore, 1.25 times book and 8.8 times earnings. Same profit, half the price. Most of this report is an explanation of why, and of whether the gap is fair.

The discount has five sources, and they are the five forces that shape everything else in this report.

  • The great repair is complete, but the earnings base has reset upward with help. Bad-loan recognition (the RBI's 2015 Asset Quality Review), the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, ₹3.1 lakh crore of recapitalisation and three merger waves took the system from 27 PSBs and mass losses to 12 PSBs and record profits. Part of today's profit still comes from recovering loans that were written off years ago and from unusually low tax rates, which the market does not expect to last.
  • The government is the owner. GoI holds between 55.45% (SBI) and 92.44% (IOB) of the eleven, stakes worth about ₹11.1 lakh crore. That ownership gives the banks cheap, sticky deposits and an implicit guarantee, but it also brings directed lending, dividend demands, appointment cycles and, at three banks, a legal obligation to sell shares: the 1 August 2026 minimum-public-shareholding deadline passed with IOB, UCO and Central still above 75%.
  • The deposit franchise. In a system short of deposits, the banks with government flows and rural branches have the most stable funding. PSBs took 50.8% of the deposits added in FY26, against 38.6% for private banks, and grew loans faster for the first time in a decade.
  • The rate cycle and the MCLR book. Only about 45–55% of PSB loans are linked to the repo rate, against 86% at private banks, so PSB margins move more slowly in both directions. FY26 brought a margin squeeze (NII grew just 3.2%); Q1 FY27 brought the turn (NII up 12.9% year on year).
  • Valuation. At an assumed 13.5% cost of equity, the market is paying the ex-SBI group 1.0 times book, which implies a sustainable return on equity of about 13.5%, one to one and a half points below FY26's level. SBI trades at a premium because of its scale, its deposit franchise and listed subsidiaries worth about ₹1.87 lakh crore.

SBI dominates. It holds 38.6% of the group's deposits and earns 40.6% of its profit, yet it accounts for 51.2% of its market value. Put differently, the market values SBI at 1.57 times book and the other ten, as a group, at almost exactly 1.0 times. Any statement about "PSB valuations" is therefore really two statements: one about SBI, which trades like a large, well-run bank with a valuable financial-services conglomerate attached, and one about the other ten, which trade around book value whether their returns on equity are 9% or 23%.

In plain terms

A public sector bank is a normal bank with an unusual owner. It takes deposits, lends them out at a higher rate, and keeps the difference after costs and bad loans. Because the government owns most of it, savers trust it completely and give it cheap money; but the same owner also asks it to lend to farmers and small firms, takes a share of its profit as dividends, picks its chairman, and sometimes needs to sell its shares. Investors pay less for each rupee of profit because they are not sure how much of that profit will stay with them.

The near-term picture is better than the FY26 numbers suggest. Margins bottomed in the March 2026 quarter: SBI's domestic net interest margin rose from 2.93% to 3.00% in Q1 FY27 and PNB's domestic margin rose 10 basis points to 2.74% in Q2. Aggregate net interest income for the eleven rose 12.9% year on year in the June 2026 quarter after growing only 3.2% in the whole of FY26. Loan growth is running at 15–19% at the large banks, faster than private banks for the first time in years. Asset quality is at a multi-decade best: net NPAs of 0.13–0.51% across the eleven, provision coverage above 90% everywhere once written-off loans are counted.

The risks are known and dated. A new expected-credit-loss (ECL) provisioning regime starts on 1 April 2027 and could cost up to 120 basis points of core capital; Canara alone has guided a one-time hit of ₹12,000–15,000 crore. The 13th industry wage settlement is due from November 2027. Recoveries from written-off loans, a big part of "other income", shrink as the old bad-loan pool is worked through. The RBI raised the repo rate on 7 October 2026 for the first time since February 2023, which lifts loan yields quickly but hurts bond portfolios. And the government still has to sell about ₹16,900 crore of stock in three banks to meet the float rule, while it decides what to do with a sector it says it is not planning to merge further.

In plain terms

The crisis is over and these banks are now solidly profitable. The open questions are smaller and nearer: whether profits stay this high once a stricter accounting rule, a new wage deal and the end of windfall recoveries arrive in 2027, and whether the government will stop being a seller of the shares.

01

How a deposit becomes profit at a state bank

The same machine as any bank, run with a cheaper fuel, a heavier load and a different owner.

A public sector bank is a bank in which the Government of India holds a majority of the shares. Eleven of the twelve are "nationalised banks", taken over by the state in 1969 and 1980 under the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Acts, in which the President of India holds the government's shares. State Bank of India is the twelfth, created under its own statute, the SBI Act of 1955. IDBI Bank, often grouped with PSBs in conversation, has legally been a private bank since January 2019, when Life Insurance Corporation took control; it is being sold to Canada's Fairfax group and sits in the private-bank sub-sector.

The economic model is the one every commercial bank runs: borrow short-term money cheaply from depositors, lend it for longer at a higher rate, invest the rest in government bonds, earn fees on transactions, and hope that fewer borrowers default than the price of the loan assumed. What makes PSBs a distinct sub-sector is four differences in how they run that model.

  • A liability franchise built by the state. The eleven run about 84,000 branches (SBI alone more than 22,000), about half of them in rural and semi-urban India. They handle government salary accounts, tax collection, pension payments and scheme accounts (Jan Dhan, direct benefit transfers, MGNREGA wages). And depositors believe a state bank cannot fail. The result is a current and savings account (CASA) share of 29–49% of deposits and a cost of funds of about 4.4–5.5%, even though their posted deposit rates are close to private banks'.
  • An asset side tilted to corporates, infrastructure, agriculture and home loans. Like every bank, PSBs must direct 40% of adjusted net bank credit to the priority sector (agriculture, micro and small firms, weaker sections). They lend more to large corporates and government-linked infrastructure than most private banks, and a larger share of their loans is still priced off the older MCLR benchmark (section 05).
  • A sovereign owner. The Department of Financial Services (DFS) in the Finance Ministry sets capital policy, mergers and dividend expectations, and appoints chairmen and managing directors on the advice of the Financial Services Institutions Bureau. Wages are set industry-wide in negotiations between the Indian Banks' Association and unions. The owner is both a shield and a leash.
  • More leverage. PSBs run at about 13–14 times assets to equity, against about 8–10 times at the large private banks. A return on assets of about 1.1% therefore becomes a return on equity of about 15%.

In FY26 the eleven banks together earned ₹12.36 lakh crore of interest and paid ₹7.97 lakh crore to depositors and bondholders, leaving net interest income (NII) of ₹4.39 lakh crore. They added ₹1.93 lakh crore of other income, giving net revenue of ₹6.32 lakh crore. Roughly 70% of revenue is the spread; 30% is everything else. That second slice is unusually large and lumpy at PSBs because it includes not only fees and commissions but also treasury gains on bonds and, importantly, cash recovered from loans that were written off in the crisis years. Across all PSBs, total recoveries including written-off accounts were ₹86,971 crore in FY26.

The comparison is revealing. PSBs pay out about seven rupees more of every hundred as interest, because their loans earn lower yields and a larger share of their funding is term deposits rather than free current-account money. They spend about the same on operations. They then make up part of the gap lower down: provisions now take only about ₹4 of every ₹100, below private banks' ₹7, because PSB credit costs have fallen below private-bank levels, and tax takes less because several PSBs are still using up tax credits from their loss years. The PSB keeps ₹13.8 of every ₹100 as profit; the private bank keeps about ₹17.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 a state bank earns, about ₹56 goes straight back out as interest to the people whose money it borrowed, ₹22 pays for staff and branches, ₹4 covers loans that go bad, ₹4.5 goes to tax and ₹14 is profit. A private bank keeps ₹17. The state bank is not badly run; it lends at lower rates to safer, bigger or government-linked borrowers, and pays more for some of its deposits.

The other-income share is where the banks differ most from each other. Canara Bank earned 41.9% of FY26 net revenue outside the spread, helped by about ₹1,930 crore of one-off gains from selling stakes in its asset manager and life insurer at their October 2025 listings. Bank of Maharashtra earned only 20.6%, because almost all its profit comes from a fat lending margin. A bank whose profit relies heavily on other income is harder to forecast, because treasury gains reverse when bond yields rise (as they did in 2026) and written-off recoveries fade as the old pool shrinks.

Two features of PSB income statements need a warning up front. First, PSBs report both "global" and "domestic" margins and business figures. Their overseas branches, which are large at BoB, BoI, SBI and Canara, lend mainly in dollars to Indian corporates and trade-finance clients at thin spreads (BoB's international margin was 1.69% in Q1 FY27). Domestic margins are therefore 10–20 basis points higher than global ones, and comparing one bank's domestic figure with another's global figure is a common error. Second, the "operating profit" PSBs report is before provisions and tax, and it includes treasury gains. A rise in operating profit driven by a bond rally is not the same as a rise driven by the loan book.

The final step in the chain is distribution. PSBs typically pay out 20–22% of profit as dividends. Because the government owns 55–92% of each bank, most of that cash goes to the exchequer: in FY25 PSBs declared ₹34,990 crore of dividends, of which the government received ₹22,699 crore. The rest of the profit is retained, and since FY22 retained earnings plus market share issues have funded all of the banks' growth, without fresh government capital. That self-funding is the clearest sign that the repair described in section 03 is complete.

02

Size, share and a lost decade

PSBs ceded about twenty points of market share to private banks between 2014 and 2024. In FY26, for the first time in a decade, they grew faster.

The twelve PSBs ended FY26 with total business (deposits plus advances) of ₹283.3 lakh crore, up 12.8%. Deposits were ₹156.3 lakh crore (+10.6%) and gross advances ₹127 lakh crore (+15.7%). Operating profit was ₹3.21 lakh crore and net profit ₹1.98 lakh crore (+11.1%), according to the Finance Ministry's annual release of 12 May 2026. Gross NPAs were 1.93%, net NPAs 0.39%, the slippage ratio (new bad loans as a share of good ones) 0.7%, and the capital adequacy ratio 16.6%.

The eleven listed members account for almost all of that. Their combined standalone figures, summed from company filings, reconcile closely to the ministry's totals once Punjab & Sind Bank (roughly ₹1,000–1,200 crore of profit and ₹1.4 lakh crore of deposits, approximate) is added back.

SBI is the giant. Its ₹59.76 lakh crore of deposits at March 2026 (₹60.06 lakh crore by June) is 38.6% of the group and roughly 22–23% of all deposits in India's scheduled commercial banks (an estimate). The next four, PNB, BoB, Canara and Union, each hold ₹13–17 lakh crore; together with Indian Bank (₹8.3 lakh crore) they are the products of the 2019–20 mergers. The five smaller "standalone" banks, which were not merged, each hold ₹3.3–9.3 lakh crore.

The share story: from three-quarters to just over half

In March 2014 PSBs held 73.9% of the banking system's deposits and 73.2% of its credit. By March 2026, after adjusting the ministry's global figures for an estimated ₹10–12 lakh crore of deposits held at overseas branches, they held roughly 55% of domestic deposits and 52–54% of domestic credit. Both 2026 figures are estimates: the RBI's bank-group tables for March 2026 were not available to us. The private banks were the winners, from about 12% of credit in 2000 and about 21% in 2015 to well over a third today.

The share loss was not an accident of competition alone. Between 2015 and 2020 most PSBs could not grow: the RBI's Prompt Corrective Action framework restricted lending at eleven of them at the peak, capital was scarce, and management attention went into recognising and resolving bad loans. Private banks used those years to build retail franchises in home loans, cards and personal loans, which grew much faster than corporate credit. The PSBs that escaped restrictions, notably SBI, largely kept their share; the ones under PCA lost most.

FY26 marked a reversal. According to RBI data cited in the private-bank dossier, PSBs captured 50.8% of the deposits added to the system in FY26, against 38.6% for private banks. PSB gross advances grew 15.7% against 12.3% for private-bank credit, and the RBI's Report on Trend and Progress noted PSBs continuing to outpace private banks in 2025-26. The reasons are partly cyclical (several large private banks slowed loan growth to repair stretched credit-deposit ratios) and partly structural (PSBs finally had the capital and the clean books to grow).

In plain terms

For ten years the government banks shrank as a share of Indian banking because they were busy cleaning up bad loans and were not allowed, or not able, to lend much. Private banks took the growth. Now the clean-up is done, the government banks have capital again, and they grew faster than private banks in FY26. Whether that lasts depends on whether they can keep raising cheap deposits.

What drives demand for PSB credit? Nominal GDP growth of about 12–13% (the RBI projects 7.1% real growth and 5.2% inflation for FY27), with credit historically growing 1.1–1.3 times nominal GDP. Within that, the PSB growth engine is "RAM", retail, agriculture and MSME lending, now 57–63% of the large banks' domestic books. SBI's agriculture loans grew 25.4% and SME loans 22.3% in Q1 FY27; BoB's gold loans grew 84.9% as the 2025–26 gold-price surge made gold-backed lending attractive. Corporate and infrastructure credit is the second engine: SBI's domestic corporate book of ₹14.21 lakh crore grew 18.1%, helped by companies switching from bond markets to bank loans as bond yields rose in 2026. Government flows (DBT, scheme and state-government balances) and the overseas books are the third and fourth.

In plain terms

Most new PSB lending is now going to households, farmers and small businesses, and to gold loans in particular, rather than to the big infrastructure projects that caused the last crisis. That is a safer mix, but it also means PSBs are competing head-on with private banks and finance companies for the same customers.

03

The great repair, 2015–2026 Deep dive

How ₹2 lakh crore of losses became ₹7 lakh crore of profits: recognise, resolve, recapitalise, merge, re-mix, retain.

The defining fact of the PSB sub-sector is that it nearly failed and then recovered. Every number an investor looks at today, from the 0.39% net NPA to the 13.9 times leverage to the 1.0 times book multiple of the ex-SBI group, is a product of that decade. Understanding what was repaired, and what was only papered over, is the best guide to what can go wrong next.

How the hole was dug, 2008–2015

Between 2008 and 2013 Indian banks, and PSBs above all, lent heavily into infrastructure, power, steel, telecom and real estate. The loans were large, long and often made to promoters with thin equity, on the assumption that growth would continue at 8–9% a year and that the government would clear land, coal and environmental approvals. When growth slowed, coal-block allocations were cancelled by the Supreme Court in 2014, power purchase agreements went unsigned and commodity prices fell, many of those projects could not service their debt. Banks restructured the loans rather than call them bad. Under the forbearance rules of the time a restructured loan could stay "standard", so the stress did not show up in reported NPAs.

In 2015 the RBI, under Governor Raghuram Rajan, ended the pretence. Its Asset Quality Review, conducted between August and December 2015, required banks to classify a list of large stressed accounts as non-performing over the following quarters. The recognition was brutal. PSB gross NPAs climbed every quarter to a peak of 14.58% of advances in March 2018. Provisions overwhelmed operating profit. The aggregate PSB system reported losses for five consecutive years, FY16 to FY20, with the worst years FY18 (about −₹85,370 crore) and FY19 (about −₹66,636 crore). Cumulative losses over those five years were roughly ₹2.07 lakh crore.

Recognition without capital would have meant failures; capital without resolution would have been wasted; mergers without clean books would have spread the problem. The order mattered.

Recognition came first and hurt most, but without it nothing else could follow. Resolution changed the balance of power between lenders and defaulting promoters. Before the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of May 2016, a promoter in default could stay in control for years while debt recovery tribunals moved slowly; after it, a default could lead to the company being sold to a new owner and the promoter barred from bidding back. Large steel, power and cement accounts were resolved through the Code in 2018–2021, with substantial recoveries on the biggest steel cases and far smaller ones on the long tail of weaker accounts. The National Asset Reconstruction Company (NARCL), set up in 2021 with a government guarantee of ₹30,600 crore on its security receipts, took over a further pool of legacy loans.

Recapitalisation provided the capital to absorb the losses. The Indradhanush plan of August 2015 promised ₹70,000 crore over four years, which proved far too little. In October 2017 the government announced a ₹2.11 lakh crore programme, of which ₹1.35 lakh crore came as recapitalisation bonds: the government issued special non-tradable securities to the banks, which used the proceeds to subscribe to new shares, so no cash left the budget immediately. Over FY17 to FY21 the government infused ₹3,10,997 crore in total, according to Finance Ministry replies to Parliament (a recalled figure). IOB alone received about ₹24,000 crore or more.

In plain terms

Between 2015 and 2020 the government banks admitted to about ₹2 lakh crore of losses on bad loans made in the boom years. The government put in about ₹3 lakh crore of new capital, the weakest banks were merged into stronger ones, and a new bankruptcy law let banks take companies away from defaulting owners. Since 2021 the banks have earned about ₹7 lakh crore, and the government's shares are now worth over ₹11 lakh crore. The rescue paid for itself several times over.

What was not repaired

The repair fixed the balance sheets. It did not fix everything, and three legacies still shape earnings.

First, written-off loans still pay. A large share of the crisis-era bad loans were "technically written off": removed from the balance sheet after full provisioning but still pursued in court and through the IBC. When cash comes in on these accounts, it goes straight into other income with no cost attached. PNB alone is guiding for ₹12,000–13,000 crore of such recoveries in FY27 (₹2,805 crore in Q2), which on a pre-tax profit of perhaps ₹30,000 crore a year is a large share (an estimate). These recoveries are real cash, but they come from a pool that only shrinks.

Second, tax credits from the loss years flatter profits at some banks. Bank of Maharashtra paid an effective tax rate of only 11% in FY26 and IOB 17%, because of carried-forward losses and other deferred tax benefits. As banks move to the new corporate tax regime (25.17%) they must write down their deferred tax assets, as UCO did with a ₹1,237 crore charge in Q1 FY27 and Central with ₹632 crore in Q4 FY26. Section 17 measures the effect.

Third, provision coverage is so high that credit costs look unrepeatably low. With coverage ratios of 97–98.6% at PNB, Indian Bank and Maharashtra, almost every legacy bad loan is fully provided for, and any recovery writes back provisions. Credit costs of 0.25–0.47% of loans in Q1–Q2 FY27 are below private banks' and below what a through-the-cycle PSB book should cost. The question for FY27–FY28 is not solvency but whether a 15% return on equity survives lower write-backs, the ECL transition and a new wage settlement.

In plain terms

The banks are clean, but some of today's profit comes from the clean-up itself: cash trickling back on old loans already written off, lower tax because of past losses, and provisions being released. As those fade, the banks will need their everyday lending business to carry more of the load.

The deposit franchise: the moat that survived the crisis

Government flows, rural branches and an implicit guarantee give PSBs the steadiest funding in Indian banking. It is a wide moat in SBI's case, a narrower one elsewhere.

A bank's raw material is money. A bank that can raise money cheaply and reliably can lend at lower rates, survive a panic, and earn a margin on safer borrowers. The PSBs' greatest asset is not their loan book but their deposit base, and it is the main reason they survived a decade of losses without a run.

The base has four components. Current accounts, held by businesses and governments, pay no interest. Savings accounts pay about 2.5–3%. Term deposits pay market rates, recently 6–7% for one to three years. And bulk deposits (single term deposits of ₹3 crore or more), certificates of deposit and FCNR(B) money are the most expensive and the least loyal. The cheaper the mix, the lower the cost of funds. The CASA ratio, the share of current and savings accounts in total deposits, is the shorthand.

Ten years ago the conventional view was that private banks had better deposit franchises. Today most PSBs report CASA ratios equal to or above the large private banks'. Part of this is the PSBs' strength: government salary and pension accounts, scheme accounts and rural savers who do not shop for rates. Bank of Maharashtra (49%) and Central Bank (about 47%) lead because they are deposit-rich banks that, for years, lent relatively little; their branches in Maharashtra and the rural north collect savings accounts that stay put.

Canara Bank is the outlier at the bottom, with 29%. It is the fastest-growing large PSB, and it has funded that growth with term deposits, bulk deposits and, in 2026, about ₹55,000 crore of FCNR(B) deposits used to replace bulk money. Its target is 30–32%. Union Bank shows the opposite choice: in FY26 it deliberately let about ₹70,000 crore of expensive bulk deposits run off, so its deposits grew only 2.7%, but its CASA ratio rose from 32.5% to 35.1%. It chose margin over growth, and its loan growth is now constrained by deposit growth of 3.5%.

Is PSB money actually cheaper?

Less than the CASA ratios suggest. A simple cost-of-funds proxy, interest paid divided by average deposits plus borrowings, shows the PSBs bunched between 4.4% and 5.5% in FY26, with private-bank leaders in the same range. ICICI Bank's 4.48% beats every PSB except Bank of Maharashtra. The PSB edge is not a dramatically lower price per rupee but the scale and stability of the money, and it is concentrated in a handful of banks: Maharashtra, BoB, Central, UCO, SBI and Indian Bank.

Three forces will decide how much of the moat survives. The first is digital competition for young savers: a 25-year-old salaried customer in a metro is as likely to open an account with a digital-first private bank as with SBI's YONO, and that is where future CASA comes from. MCLR, EBLR and the margin trough Deep dive

PSB loans reprice more slowly than private banks' in both directions. That softened the FY26 squeeze, delayed the recovery, and shapes what the October 2026 hike will do.

Net interest margin is the single largest driver of a bank's profit, and it depends less on the level of interest rates than on how fast each side of the balance sheet reprices when rates change. PSBs and private banks differ sharply on the asset side, because of how their loans are priced.

Two benchmarks, two speeds

Indian banks price floating-rate loans off one of two internal or external benchmarks. The Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate (MCLR), introduced in April 2016, is calculated by each bank from its own marginal cost of funds. Loans linked to MCLR reset only every six or twelve months, and the MCLR itself moves slowly because it follows deposit costs. The External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR), mandatory for new floating-rate retail and MSME loans since 1 October 2019, is usually the RBI repo rate plus a spread, and it resets within about a quarter of a repo change.

At the end of March 2026, 67.6% of the banking system's floating-rate loans were EBLR-linked and 30.2% MCLR-linked, according to the RBI. But the split by bank group is very different. In December 2024, the most recent bank-group figure, only 44.6% of PSB loans were EBLR-linked, against 85.9% at private banks. PNB said in September 2026 that more than 56% of its advances were repo-linked, so the PSB share has risen since, but it remains well below private banks'.

In plain terms

When the RBI cut rates in 2025, the interest that PSBs earned on loans fell before the interest they paid on fixed deposits did, because fixed deposits only reprice when they mature. So their margins shrank through FY26. By mid-2026 enough deposits had rolled over at lower rates that margins began to recover, and net interest income jumped. PSBs felt the squeeze less than private banks because more of their loans reprice slowly.

What the October 2026 hike does

On 7 October 2026 the RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50%, its first increase since February 2023, describing it as calibrated tightening. The mechanics now run in reverse. Repo-linked loans, about half of PSB books, will reprice up within a quarter; MCLR loans will follow over six to twelve months; term-deposit costs will rise only as deposits mature and banks raise card rates to compete. That points to a modest margin tailwind for two or three quarters, smaller for PSBs than for private banks because of their lower EBLR share.

Three offsets matter. First, bond losses: the 10-year government bond yield traded around 7.0–7.14% in September 2026, its highest since May, and a further rise would cut treasury income. BoB's treasury income fell 59.9% in Q1 FY27 and Canara booked about ₹600 crore of mark-to-market losses in Q2. Under the RBI's investment norms in force since April 2024, losses on available-for-sale bonds go to a reserve in equity rather than the profit and loss account, which softens but does not remove the hit. Second, FCNR(B) costs: the money raised in the 2026 window carries hedging and swap costs. Third, deposit competition: with loans growing 15–19% and deposits 10–14%, banks may have to raise deposit rates faster than the textbook lag assumes.

The shield

Government ownership gives PSBs three things no private bank can buy. The first is depositor trust: savers treat a PSB deposit as a near-sovereign claim, so PSB deposits did not run even when banks reported losses of thousands of crores and were under lending restrictions. The second is access to government business: salary, pension, tax and scheme accounts, state-government balances and PSU banking relationships, which together form the cheapest part of the deposit base. The third is a recapitalisation backstop: when the losses came, the owner put in ₹3.1 lakh crore. A private bank in the same position would have been merged, rescued at a steep discount or, like Yes Bank in 2020, reconstructed with shareholders heavily diluted.

The leash

The same owner constrains the banks. Chairmen and managing directors are appointed by the government on the recommendation of the Financial Services Institutions Bureau, usually from within the PSB system, with tenures of three to five years and age caps (SBI's chairman retires at 63). There are no employee stock options. Wages are set industry-wide through the Indian Banks' Association, so a well-run bank cannot pay its staff more and a weak one cannot pay less. The government sets dividend expectations alongside its budget (it budgets dividends from the RBI, PSBs and financial institutions together; ₹2.56 lakh crore in FY26 budget estimates). It can steer lending, for example through credit-outreach campaigns, interest subventions or pressure to lend to a stressed sector, and through priority-sector targets that PSBs usually over-achieve. And it decides mergers.

The RBI regulates PSBs prudentially, but its powers over them are narrower than over private banks: Section 51 of the Banking Regulation Act exempts PSBs from several provisions, so the RBI cannot remove a PSB board or force a merger as it can at a private bank. In practice the two masters coordinate, but accountability is split.

In plain terms

The government is the friendly majority owner who guarantees that the bank will never be allowed to fail. It is also the owner who picks the boss, sets the pay, takes most of the dividend, sometimes asks for loans the bank would not otherwise make, and may sell its shares whenever the budget needs money. Minority investors get the safety but share the inconvenience, and they price that in.

The seller: minimum public shareholding

Under Rule 19A of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Rules, every listed company must have at least 25% of its shares held by the public. PSBs were given exemptions, most recently to 1 August 2026 under a government notification of mid-2024. In July 2025 IOB, UCO, Punjab & Sind and Central asked for a further one-year extension. When the deadline passed, three of the eleven were still non-compliant: IOB at 92.44%, UCO at 90.95% and Central at 81.19%, plus Punjab & Sind at about 93.85%. We found no official confirmation of a further extension after 1 August 2026; one secondary source refers to relief until September 2026. The status should be treated as deadline passed, extension pending or assumed, and is unverified.

The government has three routes to comply. An offer for sale (OFS): the government sells existing shares on the exchange; the bank receives no money. A qualified institutional placement (QIP) or follow-on public offer (FPO): the bank issues new shares to investors, receives the capital, and the government's percentage falls by dilution. Or a combination. The precedents are recent. In December 2025 the government sold 6% of Bank of Maharashtra (from 79.60% to 73.60%) and 2.17% of IOB (from 94.61% to 92.44%) through OFS. In May–June 2026 it sold about 8.1 percentage points of Central Bank (from 89.27% to 81.19%), with domestic institutions absorbing most of it (their holding rose from 4.82% to 10.63%). Bank of Maharashtra had already raised ₹3,500 crore by QIP in October 2024, and UCO about ₹2,000 crore by QIP (primer).

IDBI, privatisation and the merger question

On 14 October 2026 the Union Cabinet approved the sale of 60.72% of IDBI Bank (30.48% from the government, 30.24% from LIC) to Fairfax Financial's Indian vehicle for ₹52,878 crore, or ₹81 a share against a ₹75.99 benchmark. IDBI is not a PSB, but the deal matters for this sub-sector in two ways. It is the first true sale of control of a government-linked bank since the 1990s. Twelve banks, three tiers

One systemically important conglomerate, five merged universal banks, and six standalones that the 2020 mergers left alone.

After the 2017–2020 consolidation the PSB sector has a clear three-tier structure, and almost every pattern in the data, from capital ratios to valuation to the likelihood of further government action, lines up with it.

Tier one is SBI, a domestic systemically important bank (D-SIB) in the RBI's bucket that carries a 0.80% additional CET1 requirement. With about 22–23% of all Indian deposits, 22,900 branches and roughly 50 crore customers, it is less a bank than a piece of national infrastructure, with a listed life insurer, card issuer and asset manager attached. Tier two is the "large five": PNB, BoB, Canara and Union, each with ₹13–17 lakh crore of deposits, plus Indian Bank with ₹8.3 lakh crore. All five are products of the 2019–20 mergers, all have national footprints and large corporate books, and BoB and Canara have big overseas operations. Together they hold 45.6% of the eleven's deposits and earned 44.2% of their FY26 profit. Tier three is the "standalone six": BoI, Central, IOB, UCO, Maharashtra and the unlisted-in-the-index Punjab & Sind, with ₹1.4–9.3 lakh crore of deposits each. Four of them were under PCA, three still have government stakes above 75%, and they appear in every media list of merger candidates.

PSBs answer to two authorities. The RBI regulates them like any bank: capital, liquidity, asset classification, provisioning, lending benchmarks and conduct. The RBI's sanctioning powers over PSBs are narrower than over private banks, which is one reason the 2015–18 clean-up needed the government's active support.

ECL: the biggest rule change since 2015

Under the current "incurred loss" rules, a bank provides for a loan only once it is overdue past set thresholds. Under expected credit loss, which takes effect on 1 April 2027, a bank must provide from day one for losses it expects over the next twelve months on every loan, and over the whole life of loans whose credit risk has risen significantly. The change front-loads provisions. The RBI's final directions of 27 April 2026 let banks add the one-time transition impact back to CET1 gradually over four years, to 31 March 2031, so the effect on capital is phased.

Estimates of the hit vary. Crisil Ratings puts it at up to 120 basis points of CET1 net of tax (up to 170 basis points gross). Analysts' estimates for PSBs are 60–80 basis points of CET1 and ₹50,000–60,000 crore in aggregate. The one hard number so far is Canara's guidance on its Q2 FY27 call: a one-time provision of ₹12,000–15,000 crore including ₹2,000–3,000 crore of mark-to-market on held-to-maturity bonds, to be spread over one to two years. On Canara's estimated risk-weighted assets of about ₹8.8 lakh crore, that is roughly 135–170 basis points pre-tax and 100–130 basis points post-tax (an estimate), or 11–13% of its net worth. PSBs have built little of the "contingency" provision buffer that ICICI (₹13,100 crore) or Axis have, so their day-one impacts are likely to be proportionally larger.

Seven decades, eight shocks

From the Imperial Bank to record profits: nationalisation, liberalisation, a credit boom, a crisis, a rescue, three merger waves and a new rate cycle.

The PSB system is a product of policy more than of markets, and its history is a sequence of policy shocks. Eight of them explain the shape of the sector today.

1. Creation (1955). SBI was formed on 1 July 1955 from the Imperial Bank of India, itself the 1921 merger of the presidency banks (the Bank of Calcutta dates from 1806). Most of today's nationalised banks were founded as private or community banks between 1894 and 1943: PNB in 1894, Bank of India and Canara in 1906, Indian Bank in 1907, BoB in 1908, Central in 1911, Union in 1919, Maharashtra in 1935, IOB in 1937 and UCO in 1943. 2. Nationalisation (1969 and 1980). Fourteen banks were nationalised in 1969 and six more in 1980, to direct credit to agriculture and small industry and to spread branches into rural India. Branch networks and the priority-sector rule date from this era. 3. Liberalisation (1991–2007). The Narasimham Committees brought prudential norms (the 90-day NPA rule was phased in between 1993 and 2004), new private banks were licensed, and PSBs began listing: SBI in 1993–94, BoB in 1997, Canara, PNB and Union in 2002, Indian Bank in 2007.

4. The credit boom (2008–2013). PSBs lent heavily into infrastructure, power, steel and telecom. 5. Recognition and crisis (2015–2020). The Asset Quality Review, the IBC, PCA, the PNB fraud and five years of losses. 6. Rescue and consolidation (2017–2021). ₹3.1 lakh crore of capital and the merger of 27 PSBs into 12. 7. Recovery and self-funding (2021–2026). PCA exits, profits above ₹1 lakh crore from FY23, QIPs and subsidiary listings, OFS sell-downs. 8. The new cycle (2025–2027). 125 basis points of rate cuts, a margin squeeze, the turn, the October 2026 hike, the ECL transition and the IDBI sale.

The lesson of the history is that PSB earnings are driven by long policy-and-credit cycles more than by the quarterly ups and downs of interest rates. The 2008–13 boom took about five years to turn into losses; the 2015–20 recognition took about five years to turn into profits. On that timescale the sector is now about six years into an upswing, with credit growth accelerating, credit costs at historic lows, and loans growing faster than deposits. None of that implies an imminent downturn. But it is the point in the cycle at which lending standards tend to slip and at which investors should watch slippages most closely.

In plain terms

Government banks move in long waves. They lent too much in the late 2000s, spent the late 2010s cleaning up, and have been profitable and growing since 2021. Waves like this have lasted five or six years in each direction. Today's banks are much better capitalised and better supervised than in 2008, but fast loan growth after a long period of low losses is the moment to start paying close attention to new bad loans.

Reading a PSB: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Twenty numbers, what each one means, how to calculate it, what good looks like for a state bank, and where the definitions trip people up.

A bank's accounts do not look like an industrial company's. There is no "revenue" line in the usual sense, no gross margin and no inventory. The raw material is money, the product is a loan, and the main cost that matters, credit loss, is an estimate that management makes. Reading a PSB well means knowing which fifteen or twenty ratios carry the information and how each can mislead. This section takes them one by one, with formulas, worked rupee examples, benchmarks calibrated to the eleven listed PSBs, and how the eleven compare.

The example matches reality closely: the eleven PSBs' actual FY26 aggregate was a 1.09% RoA, 13.9 times leverage and 15.2% RoE. It also gives the sensitivities. A 10 basis point fall in NIM cuts profit by ₹688 crore (6.3%). A 50 basis point rise in credit cost cuts it by ₹2,580 crore (23.7%). Halving recoveries cuts it by ₹1,122 crore (10.3%). A 17% wage rise on ₹10,000 crore of staff cost cuts it by ₹1,272 crore (11.7%). And an ECL day-one hit of 100 basis points of CET1 on ₹5.5 lakh crore of risk-weighted assets would take ₹5,500 crore of capital, about 7% of equity, phased to FY31. Credit cost is by far the most powerful lever, which is why the market watches slippages more than margins.

For every ₹100 of assets, a typical PSB earns about ₹2.44 from lending spreads and ₹1.07 from everything else, spends ₹1.73 running the bank, ₹0.33 on bad loans and ₹0.36 on tax, and keeps ₹1.09. 1. Net interest margin (NIM): the price of the spread

What it is. NIM is net interest income divided by average interest-earning assets (loans, investments, interbank lending). It measures how much the bank earns on the money it lends and invests after paying for the money it borrows. Formula: NIM = (interest earned − interest expended) ÷ average interest-earning assets. Worked example: a bank with ₹9.2 lakh crore of average earning assets and NII of ₹26,680 crore has a NIM of 2.90%. What drives it: loan yields (mix of retail, MSME, corporate, government; EBLR versus MCLR pricing), the cost of deposits (CASA share, term-deposit rates, bulk money), the share of low-yield bonds and CRR, and, for PSBs, the share of thin-margin overseas lending.

Benchmarks for PSBs: good above 3.2%, average 2.7–3.2%, weak below 2.7%. Private banks run 3.5–4.5%. How the eleven compare: Bank of Maharashtra leads at 3.79% (Q1 FY27), followed by IOB (3.48% domestic), Indian Bank (3.41% domestic) and Central (about 3.4% in FY26). The large merged banks cluster at 2.55–2.80%, and Bank of India is lowest at 2.52% global because of its overseas book. Because not all banks disclose comparable margins, the chart below uses a uniform proxy, NII as a share of average total assets, which is 25–40 basis points lower than reported NIM.

The definition trap. PSBs quote global and domestic NIM, and some quote it on average total assets or on interest-earning assets, sometimes for the quarter and sometimes year-to-date. Domestic NIM is 10–20 basis points higher than global. One-off interest on income-tax refunds or recovered NPAs can add 5–10 basis points in a quarter. Always compare the same definition, and strip out tax-refund interest when the bank discloses it.

2. CASA ratio and cost of deposits: the price of the raw material

What it is. The CASA ratio is current plus savings deposits as a share of total deposits; cost of deposits is interest paid on deposits divided by average deposits. Formula: CASA = (CA + SA) ÷ total deposits; cost of deposits = interest on deposits ÷ average deposits. Worked example: ₹175 crore of deposits with ₹9.5 crore of current accounts at 0%, ₹57 crore of savings at 2.6% and ₹108.5 crore of term deposits at 6.5% has a CASA ratio of 38% and a cost of deposits of (₹1.48 crore + ₹7.05 crore) ÷ ₹175 crore = 4.88%. What drives it: branch footprint, government and salary relationships, savings-rate policy, term-deposit pricing and how fast the bank is growing (fast growth needs marginal term money).

Benchmarks: CASA good above 42%, average 35–42%, weak below 35%; cost of deposits good below 4.8%, average 4.8–5.2%, weak above 5.2%. How the eleven compare: Maharashtra (49%) and Central (about 47%) lead on CASA; Canara (29%) and Union (35%) trail (see the chart in section 04). On reported cost of deposits, BoB's domestic 4.86% (Q1 FY27) and Indian Bank's 4.97% (FY26) compare with Canara's 5.27% (Q2 FY27). The definition trap: "domestic CASA" excludes overseas deposits, which are almost all term money, so it is several points higher than global CASA. Daily-average CASA is lower than period-end CASA, because current accounts swell on the last day of a quarter. And CASA says nothing about the rate paid on savings: a bank paying 3.5% on savings to hold a 40% CASA ratio may have dearer money than one paying 2.5% with 35%.

In plain terms

The CASA ratio tells you how much of a bank's money comes from everyday accounts that pay little or no interest. The higher it is, the cheaper the bank's money. But look at the actual cost of deposits too: a bank can buy a high CASA ratio by paying generous savings rates.

3. Credit-deposit ratio: how fully the deposits are put to work

What it is. Advances divided by deposits. Formula: CD ratio = net (or gross) advances ÷ deposits. Worked example: ₹135 crore of loans on ₹175 crore of deposits is a CD ratio of 77%. After the 3% CRR and roughly 18% held in government bonds for the statutory liquidity ratio, a bank can lend at most about 79% of deposits without borrowing. What drives it: loan demand relative to deposit growth, and the bank's appetite to fund loans with borrowings. Benchmarks: good 75–82%, average 70–75% or 82–88%, weak below 70% (idle funding, low margin) or above 88% (dependence on wholesale money). The system ran at about 81% in March 2026. Canara was at 81.0% in Q2 FY27. Union's ratio is rising because its loans grew 12.5% while deposits grew 3.5% in Q1 FY27. The ex-PCA banks historically ran low CD ratios and are now raising them. The definition trap: global CD ratios include overseas loans funded by overseas borrowings, not deposits, and so overstate how stretched domestic funding is; some banks include certificates of deposit in deposits, others in borrowings.

4. Other income: fees, treasury and recoveries

What it is. Everything a bank earns outside the interest spread. For PSBs it has three very different parts: fees and commissions (processing, transaction, cross-selling insurance and mutual funds, trade finance, government business); treasury gains on bonds and foreign exchange; and recoveries from written-off accounts. Formula: other-income share = other income ÷ (NII + other income). Worked example: in the ₹10 lakh crore bank above, ₹10,000 crore of other income splits into ₹5,500 crore of fees, ₹1,500 crore of treasury and ₹3,000 crore of recoveries; only the first is reliably recurring. What drives it: product breadth and cross-sell for fees; bond yields for treasury (falling yields create gains); the size and age of the written-off pool for recoveries.

Benchmarks: recoveries from written-off accounts typically run at 0.1–0.3% of assets at PSBs; core fees at 0.5–0.7% of assets. How the eleven compare: other income is 30.5% of net revenue for the group, from 20.6% at Maharashtra to 41.9% at Canara (chart in section 01). PNB guides for ₹12,000–13,000 crore of written-off recoveries in FY27. The definition trap: screener and some summaries net one-offs into other income (BoB's Q1 FY27 other income shows as −₹2,210 crore because the ₹5,670 crore settlement was netted there), and banks reclassify recoveries between "other income" and "provision write-back". Ask how much of other income is recoveries, how much is treasury, and how much is a one-off sale of a stake.

5. Cost-to-income: the efficiency of the machine

What it is. Operating expenses divided by net revenue. Formula: cost-to-income = opex ÷ (NII + other income). Worked example: ₹17,500 crore of opex on ₹36,680 crore of net revenue is 47.7%. What drives it: staff costs (55–60% of PSB opex, set by industry-wide wage settlements and pension provisioning), branch count and productivity, technology spending, and the revenue denominator (a treasury gain improves the ratio without any change in efficiency). Benchmarks: good below 46%, average 46–52%, weak above 52%. How the eleven compare: reported figures are available only for three: Canara 47.80% (Q2 FY27), BoB 49.19% (Q1 FY27) and PNB 50.45% (Q2 FY27). The group as a whole ran at about 49% in FY26 (an estimate from the ministry's operating profit). For the others the figure cannot be derived from screener, whose "expenses" line includes provisions; n/a.

6. Slippage ratio: the leading indicator of credit trouble

What it is. New loans that turned bad during the period, as a share of loans that were standard at the start. Formula: annualised slippage ratio = fresh NPAs in the period × (4 for a quarter) ÷ opening standard advances. Worked example: a bank with ₹13 lakh crore of standard loans that sees ₹2,500 crore of new NPAs in a quarter has an annualised slippage ratio of about 0.77%. What drives it: underwriting quality, the credit cycle, sector concentrations (agriculture and small MSME loans slip more; top-rated corporate loans less), seasonality (agricultural slippages spike in the June and December quarters) and policy events such as loan waivers.

Benchmarks: good below 0.7%, average 0.7–1.0%, weak above 1.0%. How the eleven compare: SBI's 0.57% (Q1 FY27) and Canara's 0.58% (Q2) are best; PNB's 0.77% (Q2) and BoB's 0.91% (Q1, from 1.16% a year earlier) are higher. The PSB system ran at 0.7% in FY26. The others do not disclose comparably in our sources.

7. Credit cost: what bad loans actually cost

What it is. Loan-loss provisions charged to the profit and loss account, as a share of average advances. Formula: credit cost = loan-loss provisions (annualised) ÷ average advances. Worked example: ₹6.9 lakh crore of loans at 0.6% means ₹4,140 crore of provisions a year. What drives it: slippages, the provisioning rate on new NPAs, ageing provisions on old NPAs, write-backs when recovered accounts are closed, and from FY28 the ECL model. Benchmarks: good below 0.4%, average 0.4–0.8%, weak above 0.8%. A through-the-cycle figure for a PSB book is probably 0.7–1.0% (an indicative judgement).

In plain terms

Credit cost is the share of the loan book a bank sets aside each year for loans that go bad. At PSBs it is about a quarter of a per cent today, the lowest in decades, partly because old bad loans are fully covered and any recovery comes back as profit. A normal year might cost three or four times as much. That is the biggest single risk to today's profits.

8. Gross and net NPAs: the stock of bad loans

What they are. Gross NPAs (GNPA) are loans more than 90 days overdue, as a share of gross advances. Net NPAs (NNPA) deduct the specific provisions already held against them. Formulas: GNPA ratio = gross NPAs ÷ gross advances; NNPA ratio = (gross NPAs − specific provisions) ÷ net advances. Worked example: SBI's ₹74,272 crore of gross NPAs at June 2026 were 1.47% of gross advances; after provisions, net NPAs of ₹19,158 crore were 0.38% of net advances. What drives them: slippages in, recoveries, upgrades and write-offs out, and loan growth in the denominator. Fast loan growth lowers the ratios mechanically, which is why ratios should be read alongside absolute amounts.

Benchmarks: GNPA good below 1.8%, average 1.8–2.7%, weak above 2.7%; NNPA good below 0.3%, average 0.3–0.5%, weak above 0.5%. How the eleven compare: on GNPA, Canara and IOB (1.33%) are cleanest and Union (2.65%), Central (2.60%) and PNB (2.55%) carry the most. On NNPA the order is different: Maharashtra (0.13%) and Indian (0.14%) are lowest, and BoI (0.51%), BoB (0.50%) and Central (0.49%) highest.

9. Provision coverage ratio: how much is already paid for

What it is. The share of bad loans already covered by provisions. PSBs quote it two ways. Formulas: PCR excluding technical write-offs = specific provisions ÷ gross NPAs; PCR including technical write-offs (TWO, or AUCA, "advances under collection account") = (provisions + technically written-off amounts) ÷ (gross NPAs + technically written-off amounts). Worked example: SBI's June 2026 PCR was 74.20% excluding and 91.82% including AUCA. The gap means SBI holds a large pool of fully written-off loans still under recovery, which inflates the second measure. Benchmarks: including TWO, good above 95%, average 90–95%, weak below 90%; excluding TWO, good above 80%, average 70–80%, weak below 70%.

10. Return on assets: the purest measure of profitability

What it is. Net profit divided by average total assets, independent of leverage. Formula: RoA = PAT ÷ average total assets. Worked example: ₹10,876 crore of profit on ₹10 lakh crore of average assets is 1.09%. What drives it: every line of the profit equation above. Benchmarks: for PSBs good above 1.2%, average 0.95–1.2%, weak below 0.95%; private banks average about 1.6%. How the eleven compare: Maharashtra 1.76%, Indian 1.31%, Union 1.22%, IOB 1.20%, SBI 1.12%, Canara 1.08%, BoB 1.06%, BoI 0.95%, PNB 0.89%, Central 0.85%, UCO 0.73% (FY26, calculated; chart in section 08). PNB's quarterly RoA reached 1.05% in Q2 FY27. The definition trap: banks quote RoA on average assets for the year-to-date, annualised quarterly, or on period-end assets; a one-off (a tax write-back, a stake sale, a settlement) can move it 10–30 basis points. Maharashtra's 1.76% is flattered by an 11% tax rate; at a normal 25% its RoA would be about 1.5%.

12. CET1 and capital adequacy: the shock absorber

What it is. Common equity tier-1 capital (shareholders' equity less deductions such as intangibles and deferred tax assets) as a share of risk-weighted assets. Worked example: Canara's CET1 capital of ₹1.13 lakh crore at a CET1 ratio of 12.80% implies RWA of about ₹8.8 lakh crore. Minimums: CET1 8% including the 2.5% conservation buffer, CRAR 11.5%, plus 0.80% for SBI as a D-SIB. Benchmarks for PSBs: CET1 good above 15%, average 13–15%, weak below 13%.

13. RWA density: how risky the book is, in the regulator's eyes

What it is. Risk-weighted assets divided by gross advances (or total assets). Formula: RWA density = RWA ÷ gross advances. Worked example: Canara's ₹8.8 lakh crore of RWA against ₹13.75 lakh crore of advances is 64.2%. What drives it: loan mix (home loans and gold loans carry low risk weights, unrated corporates and unsecured retail high ones), external ratings of corporate borrowers, and the share of government bonds. Benchmarks: good below 60%, average 60–70%, weak above 70%. Only Canara disclosed it in our sources; the others are n/a. The definition trap: a falling RWA density boosts CET1 without any new capital, and regulators can raise risk weights by circular, as the RBI did on unsecured consumer credit and NBFC loans in November 2023.

14. Price-to-book and the justified multiple

What it is. Share price divided by book value per share; for banks, the main valuation yardstick, because book value is close to the economic capital the business runs on. Formula: P/B = price ÷ BVPS. A justified P/B for a bank in steady state is (RoE − g) ÷ (cost of equity − g), where g is the long-term growth rate. Worked example: with a 13.5% cost of equity and 9% growth, a bank that sustains a 15% RoE is worth (15 − 9) ÷ (13.5 − 9) = 1.33 times book; at 13% RoE, 0.89 times; at 18%, 2.0 times. Benchmarks for PSBs: above 1.3 times is a premium, 0.8–1.3 times average, below 0.8 times a deep discount. How the eleven compare: section 15. The definition trap: screener's book value includes revaluation reserves; companies' own book value often excludes them. BoB is 0.77 times on screener's ₹300 book value per share but 0.90 times on its own ₹256.2. This report uses screener throughout.

In plain terms

Price-to-book compares what the market pays for a bank with the shareholders' money already inside it. A bank that earns more on that money than investors demand should trade above 1; one that earns less should trade below. The formula is simple; the hard part is guessing which return the bank can keep earning for years.

Definition traps, collected

  • Global versus domestic. NIM, CASA, advances and deposits are all quoted both ways. Domestic NIM is 10–20 bp higher; domestic CASA several points higher. Never mix them across banks.
  • PCR with or without technical write-offs. SBI's 74.20% and 91.82% are the same balance sheet. Compare like with like.
  • Reported versus average-equity RoE. Banks exclude revaluation reserves and intangibles and report 2–4 points higher.
  • Screener's "other income" nets one-offs, and its "expenses" include provisions. Cost-to-income cannot be derived from it; BoB's Q1 FY27 other income appears negative because the settlement is netted there.
  • Gross versus net slippages. Some banks net upgrades and same-quarter recoveries.
  • "Provisions and contingencies" is not credit cost. It includes investment depreciation, standard-asset provisions, one-offs and, at some banks, tax.
  • Quarterly capital ratios exclude unaudited profit. Ratios jump in the March quarter.
  • Tax rate swings. Deferred-tax write-downs on switching tax regimes (UCO ₹1,237 Cr in Q1 FY27, Central ₹632 Cr in Q4 FY26, PNB in Q1 FY26) depress one quarter; carried-forward losses (Maharashtra 11%, IOB 17%) flatter several years.
  • Book value basis. Screener BVPS includes revaluation reserves; company BVPS often does not (BoB ₹300 versus ₹256.2).
  • Standalone versus consolidated. SBI's consolidated profit includes SBI Life, SBI Cards and SBI Funds; secondary sources disagree on its Q1 FY27 consolidated PAT (₹24,113 Cr or ₹24,579 Cr). BoI's reported consolidated Q1 PAT of ₹1,747 Cr below its standalone ₹3,068 Cr is unreconciled. This report uses standalone throughout.
  • Year-on-year bases. A quarter a year earlier can contain a one-off (PNB's DTA-hit ₹1,675 Cr in Q1 FY26 makes its Q1 FY27 growth look explosive); Central's Q1 FY27 profit growth is reported as +13.3% by one source and +3.2% by another.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading bank results come from comparing numbers that look the same but are measured differently: global against domestic, with write-offs against without, reported against calculated. Before comparing two banks on any ratio, check that both are using the same definition and the same date.

How to read a PSB's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Net profit, then the one-offs (minute 1). Headline PAT and its growth; then search the release for tax write-backs or DTA charges, stake-sale gains, settlements and large recoveries. Restate the profit without them. A red flag is profit growth that disappears once one item is removed.
  2. Net interest income and NIM (minute 2). NII growth year on year and NIM quarter on quarter, on the same basis (global or domestic). Is NII growth close to loan growth? If NII grows much slower than loans, margins are compressing.
  3. Loan and deposit growth (minute 3). Gross advances growth by segment (retail, agri, MSME, corporate, overseas) and deposit growth. Red flag: loans growing more than 5 points faster than deposits for several quarters, or a surge in overseas or gold lending.
  4. CASA and cost of deposits (minute 4). CASA ratio change in basis points and the cost of deposits. A falling CASA plus rising bulk deposits means the bank is paying up to grow.
  5. Other income split (minute 5). Fees, treasury and recoveries from written-off accounts separately. Red flag: core fees flat while total other income rises on treasury or recoveries.
  6. Operating expenses (minute 6). Staff cost growth and any wage or pension provisions; cost-to-income. Watch for the start of 13th-settlement provisioning.
  7. Slippages and SMA book (minute 7). Fresh slippages in rupees and as a ratio; the segments they came from (agriculture and MSME are the usual sources); SMA-1 and SMA-2. Red flag: slippages above 1% annualised or a rising SMA book.
  8. Provisions and credit cost (minute 8). Loan-loss provisions separately from other provisions; credit cost; any write-backs. Red flag: falling provisions while slippages rise.
  9. GNPA, NNPA and PCR (minute 9). Both ratios and both PCR definitions; write-offs in the quarter. Red flag: GNPA falling only because of write-offs.
  10. Capital and guidance (minute 10). CET1 and CRAR (with or without the quarter's profit), any ECL impact guidance, and the bank's full-year guidance on credit growth, NIM and credit cost compared with what it said last quarter.
In plain terms

Read a bank's quarter in this order: the profit and anything unusual in it; whether lending margins are rising or falling; whether loans are growing faster than deposits; whether new bad loans are appearing; and whether capital is comfortable. If the profit looks good only because of a tax credit or a recovery from an old loan, the quarter was weaker than the headline.

Then read across RoA and P/B together. The three banks priced well above book, Maharashtra (1.73 times), SBI (1.57) and Indian Bank (1.35), plus IOB (1.51), all earn RoAs of 1.12% or more. Same profit, half the price: what the market pays for Deep dive

PSBs earn as much as private banks and trade at half the multiple. Five reasons explain most of the gap; one of them is a judgement about the future the market could get wrong.

In FY26 the eleven listed PSBs earned ₹1.97 lakh crore and the 19 listed private banks earned ₹1.97 lakh crore. The PSBs are valued at ₹17.3 lakh crore, 1.25 times their ₹13.9 lakh crore of net worth and 8.8 times earnings. The private banks are valued at ₹34.9 lakh crore, 2.13 times their ₹16.4 lakh crore of net worth and 17.7 times earnings. This section explains the gap with numbers, then looks at how the market prices individual PSBs. The private-bank report examines the other side of the same comparison.

The PSB earns its return from a thin margin, low costs, low credit costs and high leverage; the private bank from a fat margin, high costs, higher credit costs and low leverage. The market pays about twice as much for the second because it regards a high RoA as more durable and less capital-hungry. A bank that earns 1.55% on assets can grow its loan book 14% a year from retained earnings and still pay a dividend; one that earns 1.09% at 13.3 times leverage needs to retain almost everything, or raise equity, to grow at the same pace without its capital ratio falling.

Five reasons for the discount

1. Lower RoA means more capital per unit of growth. The 11 PSBs' combined RoA was 1.09% in FY26, against about 1.6% for the private banks. Equal RoE from lower RoA requires higher leverage, and higher leverage means less room to absorb shocks such as the ECL transition. Investors pay for return on assets because it cannot be manufactured by thinning capital.

2. Growth durability. PSBs lost about twenty points of market share between 2014 and 2024. FY26 was the first year in a decade they grew faster than private banks. The market is not yet convinced that the shift is permanent rather than cyclical, especially when part of the 2026 growth was the FCNR(B) window and overseas lending.

3. Earnings quality. A meaningful part of PSB profit comes from sources that will fade: recoveries from written-off loans, write-backs on fully provided accounts, low tax rates from carried-forward losses, and one-off stake sales. Section 17 quantifies them. The market capitalises recurring earnings, and it treats some PSB profit as non-recurring.

4. Ownership. Government appointment of chairmen, short tenures, no stock options, directed lending, priority-sector over-achievement and the risk of policy-driven mergers all reduce what a minority shareholder will pay. At three banks, a known seller must place about ₹16,900 crore of stock.

5. Capital and payout. The government takes its share of a 20–22% payout and decides when equity is raised and at what price. ECL and a new wage settlement both arrive in FY28, and PSBs, with fewer contingency buffers than the large private banks, will feel the ECL transition proportionally more.

In plain terms

Government banks earn the same total profit as private banks, but they make it with thinner margins and less capital per rupee lent, and some of it comes from one-off sources. Investors also worry about the government's influence. So they pay about half as much for each rupee of government-bank profit. Some of that discount is deserved; some may be a habit left over from the crisis years.

How clean is the profit?

Tax rates from 11% to 37%, recoveries that pad other income, a ₹5,670 crore settlement, and two known costs, ECL and wages, arriving in FY28.

The record ₹1.98 lakh crore of FY26 profit is real, but it is not all equally repeatable. Before assuming that PSB earnings grow from here, it is worth separating five components that are likely to change in the next two years.

1. Tax rates that will normalise

The standard corporate tax rate for a bank that opts into the new regime under Section 115BAA is 25.17%. In FY26 the eleven paid effective rates ranging from 11% to 37%. Banks with large carried-forward losses or unabsorbed deductions paid less (Maharashtra 11%, IOB 17%, BoB 20%); banks that wrote down deferred tax assets on switching to the new regime paid more (PNB 37% after a Q1 FY26 charge, UCO 37%, Central 32% after a ₹632 crore Q4 charge). UCO took a further ₹1,237 crore charge in Q1 FY27.

2. Recoveries from written-off loans

PSBs recovered ₹86,971 crore in FY26 including written-off accounts. Recoveries on technically written-off loans go straight to other income, at almost 100% margin. PNB guides for ₹12,000–13,000 crore in FY27; BoB recovered ₹2,700 crore in Q1 FY27; SBI's gap between 74.20% and 91.82% coverage shows a large written-off pool still under pursuit. As the 2015–2020 vintage is resolved, these recoveries will fall, and no new pool of comparable size is being created, because slippages are low. A plausible path is a gradual decline over three to five years, which would take something like 5–10% off group profit (an indicative judgement, using the sensitivity in section 11: halving recoveries cuts a typical PSB's profit by about 10%).

3. One-offs, in both directions

FY26 and Q1 FY27 contained one-offs that flatter or depress comparisons. Canara booked about ₹1,930 crore of gains from listing its subsidiaries. BoB booked a ₹5,670 crore litigation settlement in Q1 FY27, cutting reported Q1 profit by 72%; excluding it, the group's Q1 FY27 profit rose about 23% rather than 13.4% (an estimate). Deferred-tax write-downs at PNB, Central and UCO depressed individual quarters. Treasury income swings with bond yields: falling yields in 2025 helped; the 2026 rise has hurt (BoB's treasury income −59.9% in Q1 FY27; Canara's ₹600 crore mark-to-market loss in Q2).

In plain terms

Some PSB profits come from things that will not repeat: unusually low tax bills, cash recovered on loans written off years ago, and gains from selling stakes. Others were held down by one-off charges that will not repeat either. Strip both out and underlying profit growth is steady but slower than the headlines suggest.

4. Two costs with dates on them

The ECL transition (from 1 April 2027). As section 09 explains, the switch to expected-credit-loss provisioning will bring a one-time hit, spread through CET1 over four years, plus a structurally higher provisioning run-rate on new lending. Canara's ₹12,000–15,000 crore guidance is the first hard number, about 11–13% of its net worth. Analysts' estimates for all PSBs are ₹50,000–60,000 crore, roughly a quarter of one year's profit, though much of it will bypass the profit and loss account through the transition rules.

The 13th wage settlement (due from 1 November 2027). The 12th Bipartite Settlement, signed on 8 March 2024, gave a 17% wage increase for November 2022 to October 2027, an annual cost to PSBs of about ₹8,284 crore. The next settlement is due from November 2027; banks typically start provisioning 12–18 months ahead, which means during FY27. A similar increase on about ₹10,000 crore of staff cost cuts the stylised PSB's profit by about 12%. Pension costs for pre-2010 joiners, who are on defined-benefit pensions, move with bond yields, and the 2021 family-pension revision is still being provided for. A shift to a five-day week, pending government approval, is a further cost and service risk.

Technology and disruption

PSBs run India's largest payment rails at no profit, and the digital-first competitor is after their youngest savers.

For most of the 2010s technology was where PSBs looked weakest: slower apps, frequent outages, branch-dependent processes. That gap has narrowed, led by SBI, but technology now shapes the PSB franchise in three distinct ways: as a cost lever, as a source of new lending, and as the main threat to the deposit moat.

Digital channels at scale. SBI's YONO app has about 10 crore registered users (primer). In Q1 FY27 more than 64% of SBI's new savings accounts were opened digitally through YONO, and about 98.8% of its transactions went through alternate channels (digital, ATM, business correspondents) rather than branches. A relaunched YONO 2.0 has been rolled out over 2025–26. Other PSBs are smaller and further behind but on the same path. The cost benefit shows up slowly, because staff numbers are governed by industry settlements and attrition rather than by the bank's own choices.

Payments without profit. PSBs are the largest remitter banks on UPI by account count, with SBI the biggest, because their customers are the most numerous. But UPI carries a zero merchant discount rate for most transactions, so the banks bear the processing cost and earn almost nothing. The payoff is indirect: transaction data, customer engagement and the float in savings accounts. The risk is that fintech apps own the customer interface while the PSB provides the plumbing.

Data-driven lending. Account Aggregator consent flows, GST returns and bank statements now allow digital MSME loans with little paperwork; SBI and BoB have built such journeys. PSBs also co-lend with NBFCs for priority-sector assets under the RBI's co-lending directions, revised in 2025, combining the NBFC's origination with the PSB's cheaper funding (see the NBFC report). Analytics in early-warning systems are credited with keeping overdue books low: BoB's SMA-1 and SMA-2 loans were 0.07% of advances in Q1 FY27.

Resilience and cyber risk. Core-banking outages and cyber incidents are a recurring PSB vulnerability, and the RBI's 2023 IT-governance directions tightened expectations on board oversight, vendor risk and recovery times. With more than 98% of transactions digital, an outage at SBI is a national event.

The disruption risk is to deposits, not loans. The system's savings-account share fell from 34.6% to 28.7% of deposits between March 2022 and March 2026 as households moved savings into mutual funds and equities. A young salaried customer who opens a first account with a digital-first private bank, and invests through an app, may never hold a meaningful PSB balance. The PSB defences are government flows, rural reach and trust, which are strongest among older and rural customers. Over a decade, that demographic tilt is the main threat to the cost advantage described in section 04.

In plain terms

SBI has moved almost all its transactions onto phones and machines, and most new accounts are opened in its app. But much of digital banking, especially UPI payments, earns the banks nothing, and young savers increasingly keep their money in mutual funds or with app-first banks. The PSBs' cheap deposits come disproportionately from older and rural customers.

Bull vs bear

The bull case is that the discount is a crisis-era habit; the bear case is that FY26 was close to peak profitability.

The bull case

  • The repair is complete and permanent: GNPA 1.93%, NNPA 0.39%, PCR above 90% at every bank, no PSB under PCA, four record years, and growth self-funded since FY21.
  • Margins have turned: NII up 12.9% in Q1 FY27 after 3.2% in FY26; the October 2026 hike lifts repo-linked yields within a quarter while deposit costs lag.
  • Share gains are back: PSBs took 50.8% of FY26 incremental deposits and grew loans faster than private banks, with RAM now 57–63% of books.
  • Valuation already assumes a fade: the ex-SBI group at 1.0× book implies a sustainable RoE of ~13.5%, below FY26's 14.6%; Canara, BoB and Union price in even more.
  • Strategic buyers pay more: IDBI at ~1.3× book, and foreign stakes in RBL and Yes, value Indian franchises above where most PSBs trade.
  • SBI's hidden assets are visible: listed subsidiaries worth ~₹1.87 lakh Cr, 21% of its market value.

The bear case

  • Credit costs cannot stay this low: 0.25–0.47% today against a through-the-cycle 0.7–1.0%; each 50 bp of normalisation cuts profit by about a quarter.
  • Earnings are flattered: recoveries from written-off loans, write-backs, low tax rates at the best-rated banks and one-off stake sales will fade.
  • Two dated costs: ECL from April 2027 (up to 120 bp of CET1; Canara ₹12,000–15,000 Cr) and the 13th wage settlement from November 2027.
  • Funding is getting dearer: loans growing 15–19% against deposits at 10–14%, CASA slipping, bulk and FCNR(B) money filling the gap.
  • Supply overhang: ~₹16,900 Cr of stock to place in IOB, UCO and Central, plus capital raises planned at BoB, IOB, Maharashtra and Indian Bank.
  • Ownership risk never goes away: directed lending, payout demands, appointment cycles and the possibility of policy-driven mergers.

The two cases disagree less about the facts than about durability. Both accept that the banks are clean, well capitalised and growing. The bull case argues that the structural changes since 2015, the IBC, a granular loan mix, better early-warning systems and a regulator that forces recognition, mean credit costs will settle well below the levels of the last cycle, and that the market's discount reflects memory rather than current risk. The bear case argues that six years into a credit upswing, with loans outgrowing deposits and credit costs at historic lows, the best of the earnings cycle is in the numbers, and that ECL and wages will absorb much of the margin recovery.
