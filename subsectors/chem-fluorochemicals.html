00

The one-page view: fluorine is a toll gate, not a product

Three Indian companies have learned to handle the most aggressive element in chemistry at scale. What each one sells after that decides its margin, its cycle and its multiple.

Every fluorinated product in the world, from the gas in a split air-conditioner to the coating on a non-stick pan to the active ingredient of a modern fungicide, starts as the same rock. Fluorspar, calcium fluoride, is mined, dried and reacted with sulphuric acid to make anhydrous hydrogen fluoride (AHF, usually just "HF"). HF is the master intermediate: every fluorine atom a chemical company sells passes through it. It is also acutely toxic, it dissolves glass, it needs special alloys and it is tightly permitted. The barrier to entry in this industry is not a patent or a brand. It is the ability to handle HF safely, at scale, for decades, and then to pass the slow customer audits that follow.

That is why India has only three scaled listed fluorine companies, and why all three are now integrated back into their own HF. SRF makes HF at Dahej and is adding 30,000 tonnes a year in Odisha. Gujarat Fluorochemicals (GFCL) goes one step further back and owns a fluorspar mine in Morocco. Navin Fluorine has about 60,000 tonnes of AHF capacity across Surat and Dahej, with the new Dahej plant running since the fourth quarter of FY26. Owning the toll gate gives each of them supply security. It does not, on its own, give them profit. Profit depends on what they make from the HF.

Climb the ladder from HF and the economics change at every rung. The first rung, refrigerant gases such as R-32 and R-125, is a regulatory rent: international treaties cap how much can be produced, so prices are set by quota scarcity and swing violently. China's quota freeze lifted China's R-32 price from about ₹250 a kilogram in 2023 to about ₹775 by April 2026 (illustrative conversion of a China spot quote). The next rungs, fluoropolymers such as PTFE, PFA and FKM, are a grade-mix business: commodity PTFE is a Chinese price war, but semiconductor- and battery-grade polymers are sticky and well paid. The top rungs, fluorinated intermediates for crop-protection and drug innovators, and contract development and manufacturing (CDMO) for pharma, are know-how rents protected by process chemistry, plant audits and multi-year contracts.

The three companies straddle the same ladder but stand on different rungs. Navin earns about half its revenue from specialty molecules and CDMO and reported a 32.6% EBITDA margin in FY26, the highest of the three. GFCL is mainly a fluoropolymer house, with about 63% of revenue from polymers (our estimate), a refrigerant arm that it is doubling, and a ₹6,000 crore bet on battery materials; it earned 24.0% on a consolidated basis. SRF's Chemicals Business, a refrigerant and agro-intermediate franchise, earned a 29.1% segment EBIT margin, but it sits inside a conglomerate that also makes packaging films and tyre cord.

The five forces that shape the sub-sector

  • Quota politics sets refrigerant profits. China froze HFC production in 2024. India's own baseline is being built from 2024–26 output, with a production freeze from 1 January 2028 and no new environmental clearances for HFC plants after 31 December 2027. Every Indian producer is therefore adding refrigerant capacity now, whatever the near-term price, and Navin's management itself expects Indian R-32 to stay oversupplied until about FY30–31.
  • Mix decides margin, and margin decides the multiple. Each step up the ladder adds stickiness. The three companies' margins and returns line up almost exactly with how much of their revenue sits on the upper rungs.
  • China is both the price-setter and the reason customers come to India. China holds roughly two-thirds of world HF capacity and more than half of PTFE and PVDF capacity (aggregator estimates, order of magnitude). Its overbuilds cap commodity prices; its geopolitics, US anti-dumping orders and Western retreat from PFAS chemistry push contracts towards India.
  • Integration is the moat and the capex bill. The three plan about ₹6,350 crore of capex in FY27 alone. Returns depend on filling new plants before the next downturn in prices.
  • Valuation pays for options. The market pays 33× trailing earnings for SRF, 54× for Navin and 80× for GFCL. The spread reflects HFO franchises, a CDMO pipeline and a battery platform that do not yet show up in profits.

The defining analytical problem in this sub-sector is SRF. Its Chemicals Business earned ₹7,779 crore of revenue in FY26 and ₹2,263 crore of segment EBIT, which makes it the largest fluorine operation in India by a wide margin. But only 49% of SRF's revenue comes from chemicals; the rest is BOPET and BOPP packaging film, aluminium foil, tyre cord and coated fabrics. Any number that treats SRF as a whole overstates the size of the fluorine business and understates its profitability. This report therefore shows every sub-sector aggregate twice: on a whole-company basis (₹24,097 crore of FY26 revenue) and on a fluorine basis that counts only SRF's Chemicals Business (₹16,089 crore). SRF does not disclose how its Chemicals revenue divides between fluorochemicals and specialty chemicals, so any split shown here is illustrative.

In plain terms

Making fluorine chemicals is hard and dangerous, so only three Indian companies do it at scale, and each now makes its own hydrofluoric acid. What they make from that acid decides how much they earn. Refrigerant gas prices are set by government quotas and swing wildly; polymers and custom molecules are steadier and better paid. Navin has the steadiest mix, GFCL has the biggest bet on the future, and SRF has the largest fluorine business hidden inside a company that also makes plastic film and tyre cord.

The table and flow make one point that matters more than any other in this sub-sector: the cost of the fluorine itself is a small part of the price of almost everything made from it. A kilogram of R-32 needs about 0.77 kg of HF, which in turn needs about 1.7 kg of acid-grade fluorspar. At roughly ₹44 a kilogram for the spar, the mineral cost behind a kilogram of R-32 is around ₹75, under a tenth of the April 2026 China price (illustrative arithmetic on stoichiometry). Refrigerant margins are therefore set by price, and price is set by quota. Fluoropolymer margins are set by grade mix. Specialty and CDMO margins are set by contracts. The input cost matters most at the two bottom rungs, which is why HF itself is a low-margin, strategic product rather than a profit centre.

Where value is captured

Rungs 0 to 2 capture cost advantage rather than margin. Owning a fluorspar mine, as GFCL does, protects against supply shocks such as the 2026 surge in sulphur and fluorspar prices that GFCL's analysts described on its Q1 FY27 call, but HF is not where the money is made. Rung 3, the HFC refrigerants, captures regulatory rent: because production is capped, the margin belongs to whoever holds quota or baseline history. That is why output in the 2024–26 baseline years matters more to Indian producers than the price they got for it. Rungs 4 to 6 capture know-how rent: grade qualification, process intellectual property, cGMP compliance and customer audits that take years. The mix of rungs in each company decides how stable its margin is. Navin's mix (about 52% specialty and CDMO in FY26, our estimate) gives the highest margin. GFCL's mix (about 63% fluoropolymers, estimate) gives mid-20s with high capital intensity. SRF Chemicals' refrigerant-plus-specialty mix earned a 29.1% EBIT margin.

The boundary: what belongs in this sub-sector

This sub-sector is defined as companies whose economic core is fluorine chemistry, from fluorspar through HF to refrigerants, fluoropolymers, fluorinated intermediates, fluorine CDMO and fluorinated battery materials. Three NSE 750 companies qualify. GFCL is a near pure-play: its Chemicals segment was about 99.7% of FY26 revenue. It includes caustic soda and chloromethanes ("bulk chemicals", about 13% of revenue) because they feed its own fluorine chain, and its EV Products segment counts because it is the frontier of fluorine battery materials. Navin is a pure-play reporting three segments: High Performance Products (HPP: refrigerants, HFOs, inorganic fluorides and AHF), Specialty Chemicals, and CDMO. SRF is the exception.

In plain terms

A kilogram of fluorspar costs about as much as a cup of tea; a kilogram of refrigerant gas made from it sells for roughly seventeen times as much, and a kilogram of a custom crop-protection molecule for many times more again. The rock is cheap. What is scarce is permission to make refrigerants, the know-how to make high-grade polymers, and the trust of the large drug and crop-protection companies. Each Indian company's profits depend on how much of each it owns.

Several companies outside the sub-sector touch fluorine and are worth naming to show the boundary. Anupam Rasayan owns Tanfac Industries, an HF and fluorides maker; Aarti Industries and PI Industries make fluorinated agro and pharma intermediates in pockets; Stallion India Fluorochemicals repackages and trades refrigerants. Global majors that sell into India include Chemours, Daikin, Honeywell's spun-off Solstice Advanced Materials, Arkema, Syensqo and Orbia's Koura, alongside the Chinese producers Dongyue, Juhua, Sanmei, Yonghe and Do-Fluoride. For the pharma end of the chain, our API and intermediates and CDMO/CMO reports cover the customers and competitors that Navin's and SRF's pharma lines sell to and compete with.

Who buys fluorine: cooling, crops, drugs, chips and cells

Six end-markets with six different cycles. The mix of end-markets each company sells into explains why their FY26 results looked so different.

Fluorine is not a single market. A kilogram of HF can end up in an air-conditioner in Lucknow, a PTFE gasket in a German chemical plant, a fungicide sprayed in Brazil, an inhaler in the United States, the lining of a semiconductor fab or the electrolyte of a lithium-ion cell. Each of these end-markets has its own growth rate, its own buyers and its own cycle, and the three Indian companies sell into them in very different proportions.

Cooling: the volume engine

Refrigerants are the largest use of fluorine by value worldwide, and cooling demand is rising on three fronts. In India, room-air-conditioner sales run in the mid-teens of millions of units a year (industry estimate, not fetched), and almost every new split unit uses R-32. That gives a structurally growing domestic base of 17,000–18,000 tonnes of R-32 a year. In the United States, the AIM Act's Technology Transitions rule requires new residential air-conditioners and heat pumps to use refrigerants with a global-warming potential below 700 from 1 January 2025. That retires R-410A in new equipment in favour of R-32 and R-454B, which is itself a blend of R-32 and the HFO R-1234yf. R-32 is therefore the global volume winner of the transition. The third front is data centres. GFCL and SRF both cite rising cooling demand from AI and data-centre build-outs, and Navin is Chemours' sole manufacturer of an Opteon two-phase immersion-cooling fluid; Chemours booked its first US$1 million of sales of that fluid in the quarter, with a 15-month scale-up planned.

The cooling market is also where policy bites hardest. Production of HFCs is capped in China, scheduled to be capped in India from 2028, and steadily cut in the United States and Europe. Demand that keeps growing against supply that is capped is the reason refrigerant prices roughly tripled in China between 2023 and 2026. Part 07 covers this in depth.

Pharma propellants: a small, sticky niche

SRF is one of the few global manufacturers of pharma-grade HFA-134a, the propellant in metered-dose inhalers. It is a specification product: inhaler makers qualify a supplier's gas for each drug and change suppliers reluctantly. The next transition is to lower-warming propellants such as HFA-152a and HFO-1234ze, which creates both a risk (the existing product's demand eventually shrinks) and an opportunity (new qualifications for whoever can make the new gases to pharma grade). SRF's planned Odisha HFO complex includes R-1234ze.

Fluoropolymers: chips, cells, membranes and pipes

Crop protection: the fluorinated molecule and the destock

A large share of new crop-protection active ingredients carry fluorine; the literature commonly cites around half of recent agro launches, against roughly a quarter to a third of new drugs. That cycle turned sharply after COVID: distributors and farmers over-ordered in 2021–22, then destocked through 2023–25, while Chinese generic makers cut prices to levels SRF's chairman has called unsustainable. In Q1 FY27 SRF reported "early signs of recovery" in agrochemical demand but said broad-based demand had yet to pick up and that generic pressure on innovators continued. Navin noted that Latin American pricing pressure persisted.

Pharma CDMO: the highest rung

Pharma innovators increasingly want a second source outside China for fluorinated advanced intermediates. Navin's CDMO portfolio spans oncology, respiratory, cardiovascular, neurology and animal health, with three or four late-stage molecules facing US FDA decisions in the next 8–12 months. CDMO demand does not follow a commodity cycle; it follows clinical success. A molecule that is approved can lift a supplier's revenue for a decade, and one that fails can leave a plant empty. Our CDMO/CMO report covers the wider Indian contract-manufacturing industry.

The raw materials are cheap relative to the price of the products, but they are controlled by China, exposed to shocks and hazardous to handle. The cost stack shows why margins vary so much between the three.

PVDF needs R-142b or vinylidene fluoride, both tied to Chinese quota and supply. LiPF₆ needs lithium carbonate and phosphorus pentachloride. And every step uses a lot of power and steam.

The cost of a kilogram of R-32

Refrigerant chemistry is simple enough to cost on the back of an envelope. The reaction is CH₂Cl₂ + 2 HF → CH₂F₂ + 2 HCl. Per kilogram of R-32 (molecular weight 52) a producer needs about 1.63 kg of MDC and 0.77 kg of HF, which takes about 1.7 kg of acid-grade fluorspar. At ₹44 a kilogram, the spar costs about ₹75; MDC at roughly ₹40 a kilogram adds about ₹65; sulphuric acid, power, catalyst, packing and freight perhaps ₹80. Variable cost is therefore about ₹220 a kilogram. Fixed costs for a 15,000-tonne line, around ₹40 crore a year, add about ₹30 a kilogram at 90% utilisation. Against a realisation of ₹450 a kilogram, that leaves about ₹200 of EBITDA per kilogram, a margin near 45%. Every one of these cost figures is an estimate; the point is the proportions, not the decimals.

India's listed fluorine industry is a club of three that overlaps only in refrigerants. The real competition is Chinese supply, and trade remedies decide where it can go.

At scale, Indian fluorine chemistry is a three-company oligopoly, and each company is strongest in a different niche. SRF has the broadest refrigerant portfolio, the largest agro custom-synthesis book and, from 2028, its own HFOs. Navin leads in specialty fluorination and CDMO, makes HFOs under contract for Honeywell, and is one of the world's largest makers of BF₃ and other inorganic fluorides. They meet head-on only in HFC refrigerants, where all three are adding capacity at once.

The Kigali land-grab: India's refrigerant race to 2028 Deep dive

India's HFC production rights after 2028 will be set by what producers make in 2024–26. That rule explains why every Indian producer is adding refrigerant capacity at once, and why prices may fall in India before they rise.

Refrigerant profits in this sub-sector are made by treaty. The Montreal Protocol of 1987 removed CFCs and is phasing out HCFCs such as R-22 for emissive uses. Its Kigali Amendment of 2016, in force from 2019 and ratified by India in 2021, phases down the HFCs that replaced them, measured in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent. Every major producing country now caps HFC output or is about to. Capped supply against growing cooling demand is why China's R-32 price roughly tripled between 2023 and April 2026. The question for Indian producers is not whether the cap exists but how big their share of it will be.

How India's baseline is built

The single most important rule for Indian producers is the baseline formula set out in the Ministry of Environment's office memorandum of 1 April 2026. Its baseline is the average of HFC production and consumption in 2024, 2025 and 2026, plus 65% of its 2009–10 HCFC-22 baseline, all measured in CO₂-equivalent. Production is frozen at that level from 1 January 2028 and then cut in four steps to 15% of baseline by 2047.

After 2028: who holds the rights, and the HFO hedge

From 1 January 2028, Indian HFC production will be frozen and then cut by 10% in 2032 and progressively to 15% of baseline by 2047. A producer that has built a large production history in 2024–27 will own what amounts to a grandfathered franchise: a share of a capped market, protected from new entrants by the end-2027 clearance cut-off. That is a real asset, and part of why Indian fluorine stocks trade at high multiples. Its value, though, depends on rules not yet written: the company-wise allocation method, the treatment of the HCFC-22 legacy component, and whether production rights can be traded or converted between gases.

The long-run hedge against the step-downs is to make gases that are not counted at all. HFOs sit outside HFC quotas. SRF's ₹2,300 crore Odisha complex, due around February 2028, will make 20,000 tonnes a year of HFO-1234yf, 1234ze and 1233zd with an in-house process the company describes as non-infringing, plus 30,000 tonnes of HF and derivatives. Our SRF primer describes the Odisha project as banking HFC quota; that is incorrect: it makes HFOs and HF, outside the quota, while the quota-related investment is the Dahej debottleneck. Navin has made HFO-1233zd for Honeywell since July 2022 and holds a 2016 licence to make R-1234yf for India. GFCL has no HFO programme. The risks to the hedge are patents (Honeywell and Chemours have historically litigated R-1234yf intellectual property) and the build-up of Chinese HFO capacity.

In plain terms

After 2028, the right to make refrigerant gas in India becomes a limited licence, and the companies that make the most now will probably hold the biggest licences. That licence will shrink over twenty years. The way out is to make the next generation of gases, HFOs, which are not capped at all. SRF is building its own HFO plant; Navin already makes one for Honeywell; GFCL does not make any.

Teflon meets PFAS: fluoropolymers between Chinese overcapacity and Western rules Deep dive

China overbuilt commodity PTFE and PVDF; Europe is deciding how far to restrict the whole PFAS family. India's largest polymer maker is growing by climbing the grade ladder and by filling gaps Western producers leave.

Fluoropolymers are the second-largest use of fluorine by value and the core of GFCL, which makes about two-thirds of its revenue from them. They are also the part of the sub-sector where the two great external forces, Chinese overcapacity and Western regulation of PFAS ("per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances", the "forever chemicals"), meet head-on. Understanding GFCL's last four years requires understanding both.

The collapse: what Chinese capacity did to prices

In 2021–22, battery demand for PVDF binder and a general shortage pushed fluoropolymer prices to records. Battery-grade PVDF peaked above RMB 400,000 a tonne in the first half of 2022 (one aggregator says 600,000) and then fell to about RMB 80,000 by 2024 as Chinese plants came on stream. Commodity PTFE followed a similar arc. China now holds roughly 55% of world PTFE capacity of about 320,000 tonnes (aggregator estimate), and its standard PTFE traded at RMB 52,000–55,000 a tonne in late July 2026, about ₹640–680 a kilogram at the working rate. A Chinese price service says capacity expansion has "largely stalled" with national capacity at 199,000–230,000 tonnes a year. US PTFE prices slipped 2.21% in early August 2026 and were stable in September.

In plain terms

China built so many PTFE and PVDF plants that the basic grades now sell for a fraction of their 2022 price. GFCL, India's biggest maker, lost two-thirds of its profit in a year. PFAS: the regulatory overhang

Fluoropolymers are PFAS by most regulatory definitions, and the rules being written in Europe and the United States are the largest long-term uncertainty for this rung. The European Union is working through a universal restriction on PFAS under its REACH chemicals law. On 3 March 2026 ECHA's Committee for Risk Assessment (RAC) adopted its opinion and rejected grouped derogations for fluoropolymers. The Committee for Socio-Economic Analysis (SEAC) produced a draft supporting targeted derogations; its consultation closed on 25 May 2026 and its final opinion is expected by the end of 2026. A Commission proposal is expected in 2027. Separately, the EU packaging regulation's ban on PFAS in food-contact packaging applies from 12 August 2026.

In the United States, 3M completed its exit from PFAS manufacturing, including its Dyneon fluoropolymers business, at the end of 2025, with product transition continuing. Chemours agreed a proposed consent decree with the US Environmental Protection Agency and West Virginia in June 2026: a US$22.5 million penalty plus US$90 million of mitigation over 15 years, pending court approval. EPA drinking-water limits and state-level PFAS bans add pressure (known, not re-fetched).

Threat and opportunity, both real

For Indian producers the PFAS process cuts both ways. The threat is market access: GFCL sells its fluoropolymers mainly to Europe and the United States. If the EU restriction is broad, some uses could be closed to imports, and customers may slow qualification of new suppliers while the rules are unclear. RAC's conditions would also require emissions-management plans at producing sites, including Indian ones that export to Europe. The opportunity is that Western supply is retreating. 3M's exit removed a large producer; Chemours is diversifying its supply chain, and SRF's strategic agreements with Chemours to make certain fluoropolymers and fluoroelastomers are explicitly intended to "strengthen its global supply chain footprint". Critical uses such as semiconductors, lithium-ion cells and hydrogen membranes have no ready substitutes today, which is where GFCL is steering its mix. Indian fluoropolymer growth is, in part, a story of absorbing Western PFAS retreat.

Molecules under contract: why Navin and SRF diverged in FY26 Deep dive

Both companies make fluorinated molecules for agro and pharma innovators. In FY26 one grew its specialty book 44% and the other called it subdued. The difference is molecule-specific contracts versus exposure to a sector-wide destock.

The upper rungs of the fluorine ladder sell chemistry rather than tonnes. A custom-synthesis supplier makes a specific fluorinated intermediate or active ingredient for a named innovator, often in a plant dedicated to that customer, under a contract that runs for years. A CDMO goes further, developing the manufacturing process for a drug candidate and supplying it from clinical trials through to commercial launch under pharmaceutical good-manufacturing practice (cGMP). These businesses earn the highest and steadiest margins in the sub-sector. They also explain the biggest divergence in FY26 results.

The agro destock and SRF's slump

After COVID, distributors and farmers over-ordered crop-protection products in 2021–22, then spent 2023–25 working down inventories. At the same time, Chinese generic producers cut prices to levels SRF's chairman described as unsustainable, and innovators facing generic competition squeezed their own suppliers. SRF's Specialty Chemicals business, the largest agro custom-synthesis book in India, absorbed both pressures. FY26 was a record year for SRF's fluorochemicals and a "subdued" one for specialty. In January 2026, SRF signalled it would miss its earlier guidance of 20% specialty growth, and the stock fell to a nine-month low. In Q1 FY27 the company said technology-led cost initiatives had helped it hold market share, but "pricing pressure continues" from Chinese players; "early signs of recovery are emerging in agrochemical demand. However, broad-based demand is yet to pick up," and generic pressure on innovator agrochemical players continues. On the brighter side, it said new pharma products were "demonstrating early market acceptance", and it is building a second pharma intermediates plant at Dahej (about ₹180 crore, per our primer).

Navin's decoupling

Navin's Specialty Chemicals segment grew about 44% in FY26 and 48% in Q1 FY27. The difference from SRF is not that Navin's customers escaped the agro cycle. It is that Navin's growth came from specific new molecules and dedicated capacity: five new molecules, a multi-purpose plant (MPP) debottleneck at Dahej for a "global innovator" that was commissioned on 29 September 2026 for ₹75 crore with a peak revenue potential of ₹140–160 crore and a purchase order for calendar 2026, and a dedicated agro-intermediate plant (about ₹125 crore) for another unnamed innovator. When growth comes from molecules launching rather than a market recovering, it can run against the cycle. Navin still noted that Latin American pricing pressure persisted.

Fluorine in the battery: GFCL's ₹6,000 crore ex-China bet Deep dive

One Indian company is trying to build a full fluorine battery-materials platform outside China. The targets are large, the revenue today is tiny, and the market already values the venture at roughly half of GFCL.

Lithium-ion cells need fluorine in several places: the electrolyte salt (LiPF₆), the formulated electrolyte, the PVDF binder that holds the cathode together and a range of fluorinated additives. China dominates all of them; it holds about 85% of world LiPF₆ capacity, with Do-Fluoride alone at about 65,000 tonnes a year (aggregator estimates). As the United States and Europe write rules that favour non-Chinese battery supply chains, a credible ex-China supplier of these materials has a market. GFCL is trying to become one, through its subsidiary GFCL EV Products, at its Jolva complex in Gujarat. Neither SRF nor Navin has a comparable programme.

  • Natural-graphite anode (NGAAM): a new addition in FY26.

Viability against China

GFCL's competitive case rests on three things. Supply-chain localisation: US rules on "foreign entities of concern" under the Inflation Reduction Act and the EU Battery Regulation favour non-Chinese salts and binders, which can earn a premium. Integration: GFCL makes its own HF, its own PVDF and its own fluorine chemistry, which few non-Chinese competitors can match. And qualification: once a cell or electrolyte maker has approved a material, switching is slow. Against it: Chinese producers have already shown they can cut LiPF₆ prices by 90% when they choose; ex-China cell factories have been repeatedly delayed; and battery chemistry is still moving. For investors, the battery venture is best treated as a separate asset with its own milestones: LiPF₆ volumes and customer count, PVDF binder and LFP commercial sales during FY27, quarterly revenue reaching ₹100 crore by Q4 FY27, the new funding round after Oman, and progress at the second Dahej-area site. Until revenue scales, every quarter of losses and capex will weigh on GFCL's consolidated returns, which is why its screener ROCE of 9.6% is the lowest of the three.

Cycle one (1967–2000s): from CFCs to the carbon-credit windfall

The founders built their businesses on refrigerants. The Montreal Protocol moved them from CFCs to HCFCs, chiefly R-22. In the mid-2000s, Indian R-22 makers earned a windfall from destroying HFC-23, a potent greenhouse gas produced as a by-product, under the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism. The credits briefly made by-product destruction more profitable than the refrigerant itself, then collapsed with carbon prices after 2012. The lesson, repeated in every later cycle, was that policy-made profits can vanish as fast as they appear.

Cycle two (2007–2021): up the value chain

Each company used the 2000s and 2010s to climb. GFL built Dahej fluoropolymers in 2007 and scaled exports of US and European grades. SRF entered specialty chemicals in 2004 and built a large custom-synthesis franchise at Dahej. Navin bought Manchester Organics in 2011 and signed its Honeywell HFO deals in 2016 and 2020. The Kigali Amendment of 2016 set the frame for the next decade's refrigerant economics.

Cycle three (FY22–FY25): boom and bust

The China+1 boom of FY22–FY23 lifted everything at once, and the FY24 bust hit everything at once. The bust was the most instructive period in the industry's history because it showed which revenues were cyclical and which were not. GFCL's commodity polymer exposure cost it two-thirds of its profit. Navin's return on capital halved. SRF's profit fell for two years as specialty and refrigerants weakened together.

Reading a fluorine company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Thirteen native numbers, what each means, how to compute it, what good looks like and where the definitions trip you up. Then a ten-minute routine for reading the quarterly results.

Fluorine companies are harder to read than most chemical companies because they combine businesses with opposite economics in one set of accounts: a refrigerant line whose margin is set by treaty, a polymer line whose margin is set by grade mix, a custom-synthesis line whose margin is set by contracts, and in GFCL's case a battery start-up that loses money. None of the three discloses the numbers an analyst would most like (realisation per kilogram, volumes, plant-level utilisation), so the craft lies in knowing which disclosed numbers are proxies for the undisclosed ones, and where each company defines them differently.

The fluorine profit equation

Every line of business in this sub-sector reduces to the same equation. Revenue is capacity, times utilisation, times realisation per kilogram. EBITDA is that revenue less variable costs (fluorspar or HF, chlorinated or organic feedstock, power) and fixed costs. Management controls capacity, the cost of HF (through integration), fixed costs and, over years, the mix of products. It does not control the realisation of commodity products, which is set by quotas and Chinese supply, nor the timing of customers' orders.

In plain terms

A fluorine company's profit is how much it can make, times how much of that it sells, times what it earns on each kilogram, less its fixed costs. It controls how much it builds and how efficiently it runs, but for commodity gases it does not control the selling price. The more of its business sells at negotiated, contracted prices, the less its profit swings.

Up with refrigerant prices, a richer mix of specialties and polymer grades, and operating leverage on new plants; down with Chinese price pressure, input-cost spikes that cannot be passed on, and start-up losses. Why it matters more than usual here. In most chemical businesses, utilisation drives margin through operating leverage: fixed costs spread over more tonnes. In fluorine it may also drive future rights. 4. HF self-sufficiency and fluorspar cost

What it is. The share of a company's HF needs it makes itself, and the cost and security of the fluorspar behind it. Formula. Drivers. Up with new AHF plants (Navin's Dahej plant from Q4 FY26; SRF's 30,000 tonnes in Odisha from about February 2028); down when downstream capacity grows faster than HF. Benchmarks. 6. Contracted revenue share

What it is. The share of revenue sold under multi-year, dedicated or take-or-pay-style arrangements rather than at spot. Formula. Contracted revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example. Navin: specialty (about ₹1,190 crore) plus CDMO (about ₹520 crore) is about ₹1,710 crore, 52% of FY26 revenue; adding the Honeywell HFO and Chemours arrangements inside HPP lifts the estimate to 55–60%. Drivers. Up with new molecules, dedicated plants, HFO contracts, and refrigerant offtake contracts (Navin aims to contract 35–45% of its new R-32 capacity for five years); down when commodity lines grow faster. Benchmarks. Good above 50%; average 25–50%; weak below 25%.

How they compare. All ESTIMATES (none disclosed): Navin 55–60%; SRF Chemicals 45–50% (specialty's share of the segment); GFCL below 10%, rising with battery supply agreements. The chart is in Part 09. The definition trap. "Contracted" does not mean "fixed price" or "guaranteed volume". In plain terms

A contract with a big crop-protection or drug company makes a supplier's sales more predictable and harder for a Chinese rival to take, but it does not force the customer to keep buying the same amount. The anatomy teaches three things. Definition traps, collected

  • SRF's EBITDA has two definitions. Company EBITDA (₹3,705 crore, 23.5%) excludes exchange-fluctuation gains and losses and includes other income; screener's operating profit (₹3,410 crore, 21.6%) does neither. Use the company figure in prose, screener's for like-for-like tables, and say which.
  • SRF's segment is "Chemicals", reported at EBIT. It mixes fluorochemicals and specialty chemicals, and the split is not disclosed. Any fluoro/specialty split, including ours, is an estimate.
  • GFCL's ₹1,291 crore is not consolidated EBITDA. Consolidated EBITDA was ₹1,201 crore (24.0%); the company's quarterly consolidated EBITDA matches screener's operating profit.
  • GFCL's Q4 FY26 PAT of ₹169 crore was the chemicals segment. Consolidated Q4 PAT was ₹112 crore before an exceptional labour-code item.
  • GFCL reports two segments, not one. Chemicals and EV Products (Battery Materials); the battery segment's losses sit inside consolidated numbers.
  • GFCL ROCE: company vs screener. 14.05% excludes two years of expansion capital and all CWIP; screener's 9.6% includes them.
  • GFCL net debt is gross of cash in our numbers. Borrowings ₹2,721 crore less investments ₹277 crore; cash was not read.
  • Navin is net cash, not net debt. Screener borrowings include leases; its investments are mostly liquid funds.
  • Navin's segment history is part-estimated. Q2 FY26 was not disclosed; FY26 segment totals (HPP ~₹1,600, Specialty ~₹1,190, CDMO ~₹520 crore) are back-solved, and our primer's split does not reconcile.
  • Capacity units differ. "R-32-equivalent", "HFC capacity across gases" and "tonnes of R-32" are not the same thing.
  • China refrigerant prices include 13% VAT, and spot is not contract.
  • "Largest" claims overlap. GFCL claims the largest refrigerant capacity in India at one site; SRF's disclosed HFC capacity is larger.
  • Investing cash flow is not capex. Navin's −₹1,235 crore FY26 investing flow mostly reflects parking placement proceeds in mutual funds.

How to read a fluorochemical company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Find the fluorine revenue first (one minute). SRF: Chemicals segment revenue and EBIT in the segment table. GFCL: Chemicals segment revenue and the three sub-vertical growth rates (fluoropolymers, fluorochemicals, bulk). Navin: HPP, Specialty and CDMO revenue. Compare each with the same quarter last year and the previous quarter.
  2. Check the margin on the right basis (one minute). SRF Chemicals EBIT margin (29.1% FY26 benchmark); GFCL chemicals-segment EBITDA (29% in Q1 FY27) and consolidated EBITDA; Navin consolidated EBITDA against its 32–33% guide. A margin above guidance is good news only if management does not attribute it to one-offs.
  3. Read the refrigerant sentence (one minute). Every presentation has a line on HFC "pricing environment" and volumes. Words such as "moderate", "competitive" or "domestic competition" are the earliest signal of the Indian oversupply Navin has warned about. Cross-check with the latest China R-32 price.
  4. Read the specialty sentence (one minute). For SRF, look for "broad-based recovery" in agro and any change in Chinese pricing language; for Navin, new molecules, MPP utilisation and customer concentration.
  5. Check the CDMO number (thirty seconds). Navin's CDMO revenue against the US$100 million FY27 target (about ₹880 crore, so roughly ₹233 crore a quarter for the rest of FY27); any FDA decisions on late-stage molecules.
  6. Check the battery line (thirty seconds). GFCL's battery-materials revenue (target ≥₹100 crore a quarter by Q4 FY27) and EBITDA loss (₹30 crore in Q1 FY27). A narrowing loss with rising revenue is the milestone.
  7. Find the commissioning dates (one minute). GFCL R-32 20 kt (Q2 FY27) and R-134a; Navin R-32 15 kt (Q3 FY27), the Chemours plant (end-Q2 FY27) and cGMP4 phase II (Q4 FY27); SRF's Chemours-linked polymer line (delayed from December 2026) and Odisha (about February 2028). Delays cost revenue; early commissioning brings depreciation forward.
  8. Look below EBITDA (one minute). Exchange gains or losses (SRF's ₹102 crore loss in Q1 FY27), depreciation stepping up as plants commission, interest, and minority interests at GFCL EV.
  9. Check capex and cash (one minute). Any change to capex guidance (SRF ~₹2,500 crore; GFCL ₹3,100 crore; Navin ~₹3,000 crore over FY27–30), funding news for GFCL EV after Oman, and half-year balance sheets (September quarter) for debt and working capital.
  10. Red flags (one minute). A margin rising while revenue falls (often one-offs); inventory or receivables rising faster than revenue (GFCL especially); "pricing pressure" spreading from specialty to refrigerants; a delayed plant with no new date; and any regulatory or safety incident involving HF.
In plain terms

When results come out, look first at the fluorine business's sales and profit margin, then read the one sentence about refrigerant prices and the one about crop-protection demand, then check whether the new plants are on schedule and whether spending plans have changed. Those few numbers and sentences tell you most of what moved, and the warning signs usually appear in the commentary before they show up in the figures.

Why 33× sits next to 80×: what the market is paying for

Indian fluorine stocks trade at several times the multiples of global peers, and at very different multiples from each other. Most of the gap can be explained; some of it is a bet.

On 13 October 2026 the three traded at 32.6×, 53.8× and 80.1× trailing earnings and 21–43× FY26 EBITDA. Global fluorochemical majors such as Chemours, Arkema and Orbia trade at roughly 6–9× EV/EBITDA (INDICATIVE, not fetched). Six things explain why India pays so much more, and why SRF is cheapest.

  • Scarcity. There are only three scaled listed fluorine companies in India, and domestic institutions have raised their holdings in all three since March 2025, chasing the China+1 theme.
  • Embedded franchise value. India's post-2028 HFC production rights will be a grandfathered asset in a capped market, and US duties keep Chinese R-32 out of the largest export market.
  • Contracted growth. Navin's CDMO, Honeywell and Chemours arrangements and SRF's custom synthesis give an earnings visibility that global commodity fluorine producers lack.
  • Optionality. HFOs (SRF Odisha, Navin–Honeywell), CDMO pipelines (Navin) and GFCL's battery platform are not yet in earnings but are in prices.
  • Cycle position. GFCL's FY26 earnings per share (₹52.6) are well under half its FY23 peak (₹121), so its trailing P/E overstates how expensive it is on through-cycle earnings. Navin's earnings are at a new high, so its P/E is the least flattering.
  • SRF's discount. Films and textiles are about 51% of revenue, group ROCE is mid-teens, specialty chemicals has been in a slump, and the market fears refrigerant earnings are near a peak.

China: competitor, customer flight trigger and price-setter

China is the reference point for every commodity price in this report. Its quota holders set world refrigerant prices; its PTFE and PVDF capacity sets polymer prices; its LiPF₆ makers set battery-salt prices. But China is also why Western customers come to India. The US anti-dumping regime excludes Chinese R-32 and HFC blends; the EU and US battery rules penalise Chinese materials; and Western innovators want a second source for fluorinated intermediates outside China. India's three are the most credible alternative at scale, because they are integrated back to HF and have decades of audited supply. The risk is that China's own quota cuts are later than its rivals hope (its first cut is in 2029) and that its producers compete aggressively in the unquota'd products (HFOs, specialty polymers, battery materials) where India is trying to grow.

The Western retreat

The most important global trend for India may be the retreat of Western producers from PFAS chemistry. 3M has left; Chemours faces litigation and settlements and is diversifying its supply chain through Indian partners; and EU rules may make new Western fluoropolymer capacity hard to permit. Each exit or constraint is market share that India's producers can bid for, provided they can satisfy the same customers' environmental and regulatory demands.

In plain terms

By world standards the Indian three are small, but they earn better margins than the big Western fluorine companies and they are growing much faster. Western companies are pulling back because of environmental lawsuits and rules, and Western customers want suppliers outside China. That combination is the main reason Indian fluorine stocks are so highly valued.

Technology and disruption: what could make fluorine less necessary

Natural refrigerants, PFAS-free substitutes, HFO patents and shifting battery chemistry each threaten a product line. None threatens the whole sub-sector, and some create new demand.

Fluorine chemistry is valuable because fluorine does things other elements cannot: it makes refrigerants efficient and non-flammable, polymers inert, molecules metabolically stable and battery electrolytes conductive. Each of those advantages has a challenger. The question for investors is not whether substitution happens but how fast, in which products, and which of the three companies is exposed.

Natural refrigerants

Propane (R-290), CO₂ and ammonia avoid the warming problem altogether, and the EU F-gas regulation actively pushes them. R-290 already dominates small refrigerators in many markets and is spreading to small air-conditioners in Europe and China. India's room-AC market is mostly R-32 today, and propane's flammability limits its use in larger split units, but R-290 penetration in India is a long-run risk to HFC volumes. HFO patents and Chinese HFOs

HFOs are the long-run hedge against HFC step-downs, but they carry their own risks. The leading molecules are patent-heavy, and Honeywell and Chemours have historically litigated their R-1234yf intellectual property. SRF says its Odisha plant will use an in-house process that does not infringe; that is a claim to watch through commissioning. Navin's position is the opposite: it makes HFO-1233zd under contract for the IP owner, which removes patent risk but caps its share of the value. Chinese HFO capacity is growing and will pressure prices as patents expire.

PFAS-free substitutes

Non-fluorinated alternatives are eroding some fluoropolymer uses, notably coatings, textile treatments, food packaging and firefighting foams. These are the uses most exposed to regulation and least important to GFCL's strategy. In critical applications, semiconductors, lithium-ion cells, hydrogen membranes and chemical-process equipment, no substitute currently matches fluoropolymers' performance, which is why GFCL is steering its mix there.

Battery chemistry

Battery chemistry is the most fluid of the four. Sodium-ion cells still use a fluorinated salt (NaPF₆), so a shift to sodium does not remove fluorine demand. LiFSI may partly replace LiPF₆ in premium cells. Dry-electrode processing would reduce PVDF binder demand and increase PTFE demand. Solid-state batteries would reduce liquid-electrolyte demand. GFCL's breadth across salts, electrolytes, binders, cathode and anode is a partial hedge, and its PTFE franchise would gain from a dry-electrode shift.

New demand from technology

Technology also creates demand. AI data centres need cooling; two-phase immersion fluids such as the one Navin makes for Chemours are an emerging market, though single-phase hydrocarbon fluids compete and PFAS rules could reach cooling fluids. Semiconductor fabs consume high-purity PFA and specialty chemicals. Electrolysers and fuel cells need fluorinated membranes. Low-GWP inhaler propellants (HFA-152a, HFO-1234ze) will need new pharma-grade capacity. Process safety remains the tail risk to every licence to operate: an HF release is the industry's worst-case event, and GFCL had a production incident at Dahej in FY25.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against Indian fluorine

Both cases are strong. The bull case rests on structural scarcity and contracts; the bear case on a visible capacity wave and valuations that already assume the options pay off.

The bull case

  • A grandfathered franchise. From 2028 India's HFC output is capped, new capacity cannot be cleared after 2027, and the three producers' 2024–27 output may become a durable share of a protected market.
  • China+1 is real in fluorine. US duties keep Chinese R-32 out; Western innovators want non-Chinese fluorinated intermediates; 3M has exited PFAS and Chemours is partnering with SRF and Navin.
  • Contracts and the grade ladder. Navin's CDMO (+82% in Q1 FY27), specialty molecules and HFO contracts, SRF's custom synthesis and GFCL's specialty polymers are sticky, high-margin and growing.
  • Integration. All three own HF; GFCL owns a fluorspar mine. Supply security is a selling point to Western customers.
  • Optionality not in earnings. SRF's Odisha HFOs and Chemours-linked polymers, Navin's CDMO pipeline and data-centre fluids, GFCL's ₹6,000 crore battery platform.
  • Record momentum. Q1 FY27 combined PAT up 67%; fluorine-basis revenue up about 29%.

The bear case

  • A self-inflicted refrigerant glut. GFCL and Navin alone are adding about 25,000 tonnes of R-32 capacity against 17–18 kt of Indian demand; Navin expects oversupply to about FY31 and SRF guides to softer Q2–Q3 pricing.
  • Specialty is not yet fixed. SRF's agro book is still under Chinese pricing pressure and "broad-based demand is yet to pick up".
  • PFAS uncertainty. The EU restriction's scope for fluoropolymers will not be clear until 2027; GFCL, the most polymer-heavy, sells mainly to Europe and the US.
  • Capex before returns. About ₹6,350 crore of FY27 capex; GFCL's free cash flow negative three years running and its battery arm losing ₹30 crore a quarter after the Oman setback.
  • Valuation. 54× and 80× trailing earnings at Navin and GFCL, and 21–43× EBITDA against 6–9× for global peers, leave the stocks vulnerable to any slip in HFO, CDMO or battery milestones.
  • Policy risk cuts both ways. India's quota allocation rules are unwritten; DGTR duties on Chinese R-32 are pending; any change can move prices sharply.

The two cases are less opposed than they look, because they operate on different timescales. The bear case is mostly about FY27–FY30: an Indian refrigerant price correction as new capacity arrives, a slow agro recovery, PFAS uncertainty and heavy capex. The bull case is mostly about FY28–FY35: a capped, protected refrigerant market after 2028, China's quota cuts from 2029, HFOs outside the quota, and contracted specialty, CDMO and battery revenue maturing. An investor's view depends largely on which period they think the market is pricing.

The cases also land differently on each company. The bear case bites hardest on SRF's refrigerant earnings in the near term (they carried FY26) and on GFCL's cash flow and valuation. The bull case is most visible at Navin, whose contracts and net cash give it the most room to wait out a refrigerant downturn, and at GFCL if the battery platform reaches its targets. SRF has the most balanced exposure: the deepest refrigerant franchise, a specialty book with recovery potential, the Odisha HFO hedge and the lowest valuation.

Three things would settle the argument. The first is Indian refrigerant pricing through 2027, visible in company commentary and in China spot prices; a sharp fall before 2028 would confirm the bear case's near-term leg. The second is the scope of the EU's PFAS restriction for fluoropolymers. The third is whether GFCL's battery revenue reaches ₹100 crore a quarter by Q4 FY27 and Navin's CDMO reaches US$100 million in FY27, the clearest tests of whether the options priced into the stocks are turning into earnings.

In plain terms

The optimistic view is that India's fluorine companies will own scarce, protected refrigerant licences after 2028 and growing contracted businesses with Western customers who want to avoid China. The pessimistic view is that they are all building refrigerant plants at once, which will push prices down for several years, while their share prices already assume their new businesses succeed. Both can be right: the first describes the next decade, the second the next three years.
