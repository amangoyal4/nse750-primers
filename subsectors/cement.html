Indian cement: two giants, a 70% ceiling and a fight over every tonne

A complete guide to the business of Indian cement: how a lump of limestone becomes a ₹350 bag and a few hundred rupees of profit per tonne, why an industry that sells almost half a billion tonnes a year still runs its kilns at only seven-tenths of capacity, how UltraTech and the Adani group bought their way to control of more than two-fifths of the country's capacity, what the 2026 fuel shock and the mining-levy reversal did to costs, and how the 15 cement companies in the NSE 750 compare on the numbers that decide who wins.

00

The one-page view: a ₹5,000 commodity with a hard ceiling on price

Cement is a local, heavy, cheap product made by a handful of ever-larger owners, who keep adding kilns faster than the country can fill them.

India made 491.4 million tonnes of cement in FY26, 8.6% more than the year before, and is the second-largest producer in the world after China. Each tonne leaves the factory gate for roughly ₹5,000–5,200 after GST and discounts, and a well-run plant keeps ₹900–1,200 of that as operating profit (EBITDA). Multiply those two numbers across the country and you have an industry with about ₹2.55 lakh crore of net revenue and a profit pool of roughly ₹45,000 crore (our estimate: about 491 million tonnes at the listed industry's ~₹925 a tonne). Five forces explain almost everything that happens to that profit pool.

First, consolidation. In September 2022 the Swiss group Holcim sold Ambuja Cements and ACC to the Adani family, and the remaining large owners reacted by buying almost every distressed or family-owned plant that came to market. UltraTech absorbed Kesoram's cement business (10.75 MTPA) and took control of India Cements (14.45 MTPA); Adani's Ambuja bought Sanghi (6.1), Penna (14) and Orient (8.5) and is now folding ACC into itself; Dalmia took Jaiprakash Associates' Central India plants (5.2). The result, by our estimate, is that UltraTech and the Adani group together own about 43% of India's capacity and the top five about 65%. That is a duopoly-plus: two pan-India giants, three or four strong regional players, and a long tail.

Second, the capacity race. The industry is building faster than demand grows. CRISIL expects 160–170 million tonnes of new grinding capacity over FY26–FY28 against incremental demand of only 30–40 million tonnes a year. Utilisation, the share of capacity that is actually used, has therefore been stuck at about 70% nationally, against a decadal average of 65%. Spare capacity is the single reason cement prices rarely rise for long: the average bag sold for ₹345 in FY26, up just 2%.

Third, fuel and freight. Power, fuel and freight together absorb about 45–55% of a cement company's costs. Fuel is imported petcoke and coal, priced in dollars; freight is mostly diesel trucks. When war in West Asia disrupted the Strait of Hormuz from early 2026, petcoke rose 51% in a year to ₹16,400 a tonne and diesel 9% in a month, and companies guided to ₹150–300 a tonne of extra cost within two quarters. Green power and shorter delivery distances are the only structural defences.

Fourth, demand that the state largely writes. Housing (rural and urban) takes about 60–65% of cement, infrastructure 25–30%, and commercial and industrial building the rest. Government spending drives both the infrastructure share and much of rural housing through PMAY. Union capital expenditure is budgeted at ₹12.2 lakh crore for FY27; cement output rose about 10% in April–August 2026.

Fifth, policy that has turned helpful. GST on cement fell from 28% to 18% on 22 September 2025, cutting the shop price of a bag by about ₹25–30. A 2024 Supreme Court ruling let states tax mineral-bearing land, and Tamil Nadu promptly charged ₹160 on every tonne of limestone; in August 2026 Parliament overrode that with Section 9D of the amended mining law. A carbon-intensity regime arrived for cement in 2025.

Put the five forces together and the shape of the industry becomes clear. Cement is a business in which the winners are decided by cost, not by product. Every company sells essentially the same grey powder, made to the same Bureau of Indian Standards specification. Price is set region by region by the balance between local capacity and local demand, and no single company can push it far above its neighbours for long. The winners are the companies that own cheap limestone close to large markets, burn the cheapest fuel per unit of heat, generate their own electricity from waste heat and solar, deliver the shortest distance, and have balance sheets strong enough to keep building through the inevitable bad years. Scale helps with all of these, which is why the two largest groups keep buying the rest.

The 15 listed companies covered in this report add up to ₹2.66 lakh crore of FY26 revenue, but that total counts three companies twice: India Cements sits inside UltraTech's consolidated accounts, and ACC and Orient sit inside Ambuja's. Strip those out and there are 12 real reporting groups with about ₹2.33 lakh crore of revenue, ₹42,306 crore of EBITDA and about 600 MTPA of capacity, roughly five-sixths of the country's total. On 13 October 2026 every one of the fifteen stocks traded at or near its 52-week low: the market is pricing a weak September quarter, with fuel at its peak and the monsoon at its worst, against a medium-term story of steady volume growth and slowly improving pricing discipline.

In plain terms

Indian cement is a game of musical chairs played with kilns. The music (demand) grows at a reliable 6–9% a year, but the players keep adding chairs (capacity) even faster, so there is always spare room and nobody can raise prices much. The way to win is to be the cheapest producer in your region, and the quickest way to become cheapest has been to buy rivals. Two players, UltraTech and Adani, have bought so many that they now set the tempo for everyone else.

The rest of the report works through each force in turn: how money is made along the chain from mine to bag (section 01), how big and how fast-growing the market is (02–03), why capacity always runs ahead of demand (04), where each ₹100 of revenue goes (05), who owns what and what they paid for it (06–07), why the South and East earn less per bag than the North and Central regions (08), how the 2026 fuel shock rippled through the cost line (09), the business models and the rulebook (10–11), the cycle (12), the full KPI masterclass (13), the peer table and the winners (14–16), and finally the global view, technology, the bull and bear cases and what to watch (17–20).

01

From limestone to a 50-kg bag: where a tonne earns its money

Cement is cheap to make in theory and expensive to move in practice; the profit hides in the mine lease, the fuel bill and the distance to the customer.

Cement is a hydraulic binder: a powder that sets hard when mixed with water. Making it is a three-step chemistry lesson. Limestone (calcium carbonate) is quarried, crushed and blended with small amounts of clay, laterite, bauxite or iron ore to get the chemistry right. The mixture is fed into a rotary kiln and heated to about 1,450°C, where it fuses into dark grey nodules called clinker. Clinker is then ground with about 4–5% gypsum to make Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC), or with fly ash from power stations (up to 35%, making Portland Pozzolana Cement, PPC) or slag from steel blast furnaces (up to 70%, making Portland Slag Cement, PSC). India is overwhelmingly a blended-cement market: Dalmia reports that 82% of its Q1 FY27 volume was blended, Ambuja 85% and Birla Corporation 88% in FY26.

The recipe matters because the kiln is where most of the cost and almost all of the carbon dioxide come from. Roughly 1.5–1.6 tonnes of limestone make one tonne of clinker, and a modern kiln burns about 700–750 kilocalories of heat per kilogram of clinker. Grinding then uses about 65–75 kilowatt-hours of electricity per tonne of cement (UltraTech reported 68.17 kWh in Q1 FY27). Every tonne of fly ash or slag that replaces clinker saves fuel, saves limestone and cuts emissions. The industry measures this with the clinker conversion (CC) ratio, the tonnes of cement made from each tonne of clinker: UltraTech is at 1.50×, Dalmia about 1.7× and Nuvoco 1.73×. A higher ratio means more blending, lower cost per tonne and a smaller carbon footprint.

Plant archetypes. An integrated unit puts the mine, the kiln and a grinding mill together at the limestone deposit. It takes three to four years to build and costs, on Ind-Ra's 2024 estimate, the rupee equivalent of US$110–120 per tonne of annual capacity, roughly ₹9,600–10,500 per tonne. A split grinding unit receives clinker by rail or road and grinds it near the market or near a source of fly ash or slag; it takes one to two years and costs perhaps a third as much per tonne (industry rule of thumb ₹3,000–4,500 against ₹8,000–10,000 for an integrated unit). CRISIL expects about two-thirds of the capacity added in FY26–FY28 to be split grinding units, and about 65% of it brownfield, built on existing sites. Bulk terminals and packing plants, often coastal or rail-fed, complete the network; Dalmia, for example, is building a 3 MTPA bulk terminal at Chennai for Q3 FY28.

Why the market is regional. Cement is heavy and cheap. At about ₹5,000 a tonne before GST, a 50-kg bag is worth roughly ₹250 at the factory gate, and trucking it 500 km can add ₹100 or more. The economic radius by road is therefore about 250–400 km, and companies report their average lead distance as a core metric: UltraTech 360 km, Dalmia 277 km, Ambuja 249 km in Q1 FY27, Shree a long 445 km. This is why India is not one cement market but five or six: South, East, North, Central, West and the North-East each have their own supply-demand balance, their own price and their own profitability (section 08).

Distribution and brand. About two-thirds of cement is sold through the trade channel: dealers and retailers supplying individual home builders (IHB), masons and small contractors, who buy by the bag and pay a premium for a trusted brand. The rest is non-trade: bulk sales to infrastructure contractors, real-estate developers, ready-mix concrete (RMC) plants and government projects, negotiated by tender at lower prices. UltraTech's mix is 66% trade, Dalmia's 66%, Ambuja's 78%, ACC's 81%, Star's 80%, JK Lakshmi's only 59%. Inside trade, companies push "premium" brands that sell for ₹20–55 a bag more than the base product; premium is 34% of Ambuja's trade volume, 44% of ACC's, 25% of Dalmia's and 67% of Prism's.

Downstream. The largest companies have pushed into the products made from cement. UltraTech runs 477 ready-mix concrete plants that sold 4.62 million cubic metres in Q1 FY27 for ₹2,235 crore of revenue at a 35% return on capital; ACC runs 119 RMX plants and Nuvoco 58. Construction chemicals, tile adhesives, wall putty and dry mixes are growing niches (Ramco's construction chemicals revenue rose 66% in FY26). Two companies have stepped further: UltraTech is launching cables and wires in Q3 FY27 with ₹1,800 crore of investment, and J.K. Cement sells decorative paints (about ₹380 crore of revenue in FY26). White cement and wall putty are a separate premium niche where J.K. Cement and UltraTech's Birla White dominate, with realisations of ₹13,000 a tonne or more.

In plain terms

Making cement is like baking with rock: dig limestone, roast it in a giant oven, then grind the result with ash or slag. The baking is costly, because the oven burns imported fuel, and the delivery is costlier still, because the product is heavy and worth little per kilo. So the companies that win are the ones that own a good quarry near lots of customers, run the most fuel-efficient ovens, mix in the most ash or slag, and drive the shortest distance to the buyer.

The profit pools follow directly. They sit with owners of low-cost limestone and fuel-efficient kilns in the limestone-surplus states (Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, Karnataka and Gujarat); with companies whose dense networks of grinding units sit close to the big consuming states (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, West Bengal); and with brands strong enough to command a trade premium. Companies that grind bought-in clinker without their own limestone, or that are sub-scale in a single crowded region, capture the least. Every KPI in section 13 is, in the end, a measure of one of these three advantages.

02

491 million tonnes and counting: size, growth and India's place in the world

Output has compounded at about 5.7% a year for a decade, faster since COVID, and India now makes one tonne in every eight produced on the planet.

India produced 491.4 million tonnes of cement in FY26 (April 2025 to March 2026), up 8.6% on the year. Two independent primary sources agree on the growth rate: ICRA's monthly tracker and the Office of the Economic Adviser's Index of Eight Core Industries, whose cement series also shows +8.6% for 2025-26. Back-calculating from the index gives roughly 452.5 million tonnes in FY25 and 425.7 in FY24. The momentum carried into FY27: April 2026 output was 42.8 million tonnes (+9.4%), July 42.3 (+13.1%), the first four months 172.6 million tonnes (+9.9%), and the official core-sector release put April–August growth at 10.3% (as reported by the press; we did not fetch the release itself).

Those numbers translate into two useful growth rates. Over the ten years from FY16 to FY26, output rose from 283 to 491 million tonnes, a compound rate of about 5.7% a year. Over the five years from FY21 to FY26 it compounded at about 10.5%, but that is flattered by the COVID-depressed starting point. CRISIL quotes a 9.5% volume compound rate over the three years to FY25. The sensible middle answer is that Indian cement demand grows at roughly 1.0–1.3 times real GDP, which in practice has meant 6–9% in normal years. Forecasters sit in that band for FY27: ICRA expects 6–7% (cut in July–August 2026 from 7–8%), HDFC Securities models about 8.5% a year over FY26–FY28, and Ambuja and ACC guide to only about 5% for the industry. The April–August outturn of about 10% suggests the cautious forecasts may prove too low.

The value of the market. At roughly ₹5,200 per tonne of net realisation (UltraTech's domestic grey realisation was ₹5,034–5,218 across Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27; BOBCAPS's universe was ₹5,182), 491 million tonnes is worth about ₹2.55 lakh crore at the factory gate. This is an estimate, not a reported figure: it multiplies an industry volume by a listed-company average price. The consumer pays more. ICRA's all-India average retail price was ₹345 per 50-kg bag in FY26 (+2%) and about ₹350 in the first four months of FY27 (−2% year on year), roughly ₹6,900 per tonne once GST, the dealer's margin and freight to the shop are included. Gross of those layers, end-buyers spend perhaps ₹3.1–3.2 lakh crore a year on cement (INDICATIVE).

Domestic, not global. India exports only a low single-digit percentage of its output, mostly to its neighbours (Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, the Maldives) and some Gulf and African markets; we could not find an official FY26 export tonnage. Imports are negligible. Unlike steel or chemicals, Chinese overcapacity does not reach Indian cement directly, because no one can profitably ship a ₹5,000 product thousands of kilometres. The channel through which global markets reach Indian cement is fuel: petcoke and coal prices, and the rupee-dollar rate at which they are bought.

India in the world. The US Geological Survey estimates world cement production at 3.8 billion tonnes in 2025. China makes 1.7 billion of that, about 45%, but its output has fallen from roughly 2.4 billion tonnes in 2020–21 as its property market contracted. India is second with an estimated 470 million tonnes on a calendar-year basis (slightly below ICRA's fiscal-year 491 because of period and coverage differences), about 12.4% of the world. Nobody else comes close: Vietnam is third at about 100 million tonnes, then Turkey, the United States, Iran, Brazil, Indonesia and Egypt in the 60–90 million range. India also has about 400 million tonnes of clinker capacity against China's 1.8 billion.

The consumption gap. India uses roughly 290 kg of cement per person a year on the Economic Survey 2025-26's figure (as reported by CemNet; we could not open the source), or about 336 kg if you divide FY26 production by 1.46 billion people. The world average is around 540 kg, and China peaked at 1,200–1,700 kg. Even Vietnam and Turkey make about a tonne per person. India has room to double its per-capita use before it reaches the world average, which is the arithmetic behind every long-term bull case. China is also the cautionary tale: its 30%-plus volume decline since 2020 shows what happens when a country overbuilds and the construction cycle reverses.

In plain terms

India already makes more cement than every country except China, yet each Indian uses only about half the world average and a fraction of what the Chinese used at their peak. That gap is why volumes keep growing at 6–9% a year. It is also why companies keep building: they are betting that India's per-person use will roughly double over the next decade or two.

History has not been a straight line. Output fell in FY17 (demonetisation), stalled in FY20 and collapsed 10.8% in FY21 during COVID, then rebounded 20.8% in FY22. Each of those shocks reset prices and margins, and section 12 maps them. The important pattern for an investor is that volume is the most reliable part of the cement equation: it almost always grows, and when it falls it recovers within a year. Price and cost are where the volatility sits.

03

Who pours the concrete: rural homes, highways and the state's chequebook

About nine-tenths of cement goes into housing and infrastructure, and the government directly or indirectly writes a large share of both cheques.

Cement has no export market to lean on and few industrial uses, so its demand is the sum of what Indians build. CRISIL's July 2025 breakdown puts rural housing at about one-third of domestic demand and infrastructure at about 30%, the second-largest segment. Urban housing takes roughly 25–30% and commercial and industrial building 8–12% (both our estimates, completing the picture). Broadly, housing takes 60–65%, infrastructure 25–30% and commercial and industrial 10–12%. About one-third of volume moves through non-trade channels (Motilal Oswal), which maps closely to the infrastructure and large-developer share.

Driver 2: housing. The 2024 decision to build 2 crore more rural houses under PMAY-Gramin and 1 crore urban houses under PMAY-Urban 2.0 is the single largest housing programme on cement's order book. It is running behind schedule: Dalmia's slide shows PMAY-G at a current run-rate of about 3.0 million units a year against the 6.8 million needed to hit target, and PMAY-U at 0.9 million against 4.3 million. That shortfall is catch-up potential rather than lost demand, provided the funds flow. Driver 3: the GST cut. On 22 September 2025 GST on cement fell from 28% to 18%. Shop prices fell by roughly ₹25–30 a bag, about 8%, as companies passed the cut through (Elara measured the all-India retail price falling ₹29 in a month to ₹340 a bag in September 2025, while the GST-adjusted price held at ₹369). For an individual home builder, cement became about 8% cheaper overnight. Most analysts expected only a marginal effect on volume, because cement is a small part of the total cost of a house, but it did improve affordability at the bottom of the market, and FY27 volumes have so far surprised on the upside.

Driver 4: commercial and industrial. Data centres and global capability centres are cited by UltraTech as strong demand pockets in the South, and solar parks in Kutch in the West. These are small but growing and tend to buy high-grade, non-trade cement and ready-mix concrete.

Regional demand is uneven. UltraTech's Q1 FY27 commentary gives a useful map. In plain terms

When a farmer in Uttar Pradesh adds a room, when a state builds a highway, and when the Centre pays for a rural house under PMAY, cement is sold. Most of that money comes, directly or indirectly, from government budgets. So the most useful forecast of cement demand is not GDP: it is whether the Centre and the states actually spend what they budget, and whether the money reaches contractors on time. The East and South were slow in Q1 FY27 for exactly that reason.

The elasticity that matters for investors is the link between demand and price, not demand and GDP. Strong demand helps only if it outruns new capacity in a given region. In FY27 so far, demand has grown at about 10% while ICRA expects 42–44 MTPA of new capacity (about 6% of the base). That is a slightly better balance than FY25, when realisations fell about 7% in a price war. It explains why companies managed small price increases in September and October 2026, and why the market's real worry is the North and Central regions in FY28, when a large wave of new capacity lands there (section 04).

Why the national number never reaches 85%. Four reasons. First, seasonality: the April–June and January–March quarters are strong, while the July–September monsoon quarter is typically 8–12% weaker than the one before (HDFC Securities expects about a 10% sequential decline in Q2 FY27). Why they build anyway. If spare capacity caps price, why does every company keep adding more? Because the economics of an individual company differ from those of the industry. A large, low-cost producer that adds capacity takes market share from higher-cost rivals, and its per-tonne fixed costs fall as volume rises. UltraTech's ₹178 per tonne EBITDA advantage over the rest of the listed industry in FY26 (₹1,103 against about ₹925) is partly a scale effect. Capacity is also a strategic asset: limestone leases, environmental clearances and land take years to secure, and a company that stops building risks losing its regional share permanently. Finally, the leaders can fund growth from operating cash flow, while weaker rivals cannot. The result is a race that the strong can afford and the weak cannot win, which is how the weak end up as acquisition targets (section 07).

In plain terms

Picture a town with ten bakeries that sell 70 loaves for every 100 they could bake. None of them can raise prices much, because a rival has spare ovens. Yet the two biggest bakeries keep adding ovens, because each new oven makes their bread a little cheaper to bake and steals customers from the smaller shops. Eventually some of the small shops sell out to the big two. That, compressed, is the story of Indian cement since 2022.

The capacity race has three practical consequences for investors. Realisation growth stays modest: ICRA expected FY27 realisations up only 3–5% before the fuel shock, and BOBCAPS's Q1 FY27 estimate was flat year on year. EBITDA per tonne stays below its decadal average of about ₹965 for the industry as a whole, even while the leaders earn more. And the timing of each region's new capacity becomes the most important input to any regional price forecast: the North and Central regions are tight today but will absorb a large share of the FY28 additions, while the East already has 10–11 MTPA a year landing in each of FY27 and FY28 (PL Capital).

In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 a cement company collects, roughly ₹22–24 pays for burning fuel and running the mills, another ₹21–24 pays the truckers and the railways, ₹16–19 buys limestone, ash and gypsum, and ₹13–15 goes on bags, spares and selling. About ₹16–20 is left as operating profit, and after depreciation, interest and tax the owner keeps ₹6–11. Because the fuel and freight lines are so large, a 10% move in either can swing profit by a quarter or more.

The leverage in that structure is the key to cement's volatility. Using the ILLUSTRATIVE industry composite, a 10% rise in power and fuel costs (2.4 rupees of every hundred) and a 5% rise in freight (1.2 rupees) together remove 3.6 rupees from an EBITDA of 16.5, a 22% fall in operating profit, unless prices rise to compensate. That is roughly what happened in the West Asia fuel shock of 2026 (section 09) and the Russia–Ukraine shock of 2022, and why per-tonne EBITDA, not revenue growth, is the number that moves cement shares.

Who owns the kilns: from a fragmented field to a duopoly-plus

Holcim's 2022 exit started a land-grab that has left two groups with about 43% of capacity and the top five with about 65%.

For most of its history Indian cement was a fragmented industry of regional family companies plus a few multinationals. Ind-Ra's numbers show how slowly that changed at first: the top five companies' share of capacity rose only about 100 basis points, to 53%, between FY11 and FY20. Then the pace quickened. It rose by more than 100 basis points again over FY20–FY24, Ind-Ra projected 56–57% for FY25 and more than 65% for FY26, and Dalmia's FY25 annual report counts the top four's share rising from about 48% in FY22 to about 58% in FY25. About 25 deals since 2016 have moved more than 200 MTPA of capacity, roughly 10 of them, for about 60 MTPA, in the 12–18 months to mid-2024 alone.

In plain terms

Four years ago Indian cement had two foreign owners and dozens of family firms. Today two Indian groups, the Birlas through UltraTech and the Adanis through Ambuja, control more than two-fifths of the country's kilns, and the next three players control another fifth. When you read about "fifteen listed cement companies", remember that three of them are really pieces of the two giants.

What concentration does, and does not, do. Higher concentration should, in principle, mean fewer price wars, because large players with overlapping regional footprints have more to lose from a fight. The industry has also attracted antitrust scrutiny: in June 2012 the Competition Commission fined eleven companies ₹6,307 crore for cartelisation and capacity withholding, re-imposed the penalty in August 2016 after a remand, and saw it upheld by the NCLAT in July 2018; the Supreme Court admitted the appeals in October 2018 on a 10% deposit and, as far as we can find, has not ruled. But concentration has not so far produced pricing power. FY25 saw a price war (realisations fell about 7%, per ICRA) precisely because the giants were competing for share, with Shree also pushing volume. The leaders now compete with each other rather than with weak independents. Whether pricing discipline improves once the integration phase ends is the most important open question in the sector, and the next phase of growth will be organic because few acquisition targets remain: Heidelberg India, the Jaypee remnants and a handful of small southern players.

Buying beats building: the economics of the consolidation wave Deep dive

Acquirers paid well below the cost of a new plant for limestone, clearances and market share, then set out to lift the acquired plants' margins towards their own.

The logic of the consolidation wave rests on one comparison. Ind-Ra estimated in 2024 that a new integrated cement plant costs US$110–120 per tonne of capacity (roughly ₹9,600–10,500 at today's exchange rate) and takes three to four years to build and fill, while integrated assets were changing hands at US$70–90 per tonne. Buying was therefore 25–40% cheaper than building, and delivered capacity, limestone reserves, environmental clearances, dealers and a brand on day one. For a company racing for regional share, that is decisive.

Where the return comes from. Buying cheaply is only half the trade. An acquired plant usually arrives earning far less per tonne than the buyer's own network. Under its previous owners it earned barely anything; on UltraTech's numbers its EBITDA per tonne climbed from ₹386 in Q2 FY26 to ₹400, ₹509 and ₹603 in Q1 FY27, against a target of ₹1,000 by FY28, helped by about ₹2,014 crore of modernisation capex (six-stage preheaters, cooler upgrades, waste-heat recovery, 2.8 MTPA of extra grinding). The levers are the same in every integration: move the acquired plant onto the parent's brand (India Cements' and Kesoram's brands were 100% migrated to UltraTech a quarter ahead of schedule), its fuel contracts, its logistics network and its green power, and cut overheads.

In plain terms

A cement acquirer is buying a run-down shop in a good location. It pays less than the cost of building a new one, puts its own sign over the door, sends its own suppliers and trucks, fixes the ovens, and within two or three years the shop earns nearly as much as its best branches. The location (limestone and customers) was the scarce thing all along; the ovens were the easy part.

The scarce asset is limestone plus share, not kilns. The deals reveal what really has value in cement. Kilns and mills can be ordered from equipment suppliers, including Chinese EPC firms such as Sinoma and CBMI that several Indian greenfields use to cut capex per tonne. What cannot be ordered is a 50-year limestone lease near a large market, an environmental clearance, land, rail sidings and a dealer network that already sells the brand. Since 2015, new limestone leases have been auctioned with a revenue-share premium on top of royalty, which makes a legacy lease with old terms even more valuable. That is why acquirers paid up for Penna (a southern platform for Adani) and India Cements (a southern platform for UltraTech) despite their poor earnings.

What could go wrong. Three things. First, integration can be slower than promised: Dalmia's 75 MTPA milestone has slipped from FY27 to FY28 to around end-FY28 or the first half of FY29, Adani's 155 MTPA target has moved from FY28 to about FY30, and India Cements' share price fell about 27% after its Q1 FY27 results as the market questioned the pace of its turnaround. Second, minority shareholders can lose out: Orient's public institutional shareholders voted only 64.19% in favour of the 0.33:1 merger ratio, a level of dissent that could surface at the NCLT. Third, acquisitions consume balance sheets: Ambuja's cash fell from more than ₹10,000 crore in FY24 to ₹844 crore by June 2026, and Dalmia's net debt rose from ₹1,428 crore to ₹4,431 crore in one quarter to fund the Jaiprakash assets.

In plain terms

The deals have mostly been sensible at the prices paid, but the payoff is back-loaded. For two or three years the acquirer carries an underperforming plant that dilutes its average margin. Investors should judge each deal on the quarterly progress of the acquired plant's EBITDA per tonne, not on the headline capacity it added.

With few large targets left (Heidelberg India's 12.6 MTPA, Jaiprakash's remaining plants at Bhilai and Nigrie that Dalmia is bidding for, a handful of small southern companies), the next phase of the race will be organic. That shifts the competitive edge from deal-making to project execution and capex per tonne, the metrics examined in section 13.

Why the East is cheap. Three reasons. The North-East: an incentive-made premium. Star Cement's FY26 EBITDA of ₹1,717 a tonne is the highest in the listed set, and it is partly a regional artefact. The North-East has high prices because of difficult logistics (the Siliguri corridor, rail route restrictions on the Northeast Frontier Railway, floods) and few producers, and new plants there receive state and central incentives linked to GST payments. In plain terms

A bag of cement is the same powder in Kolkata, Chennai and Delhi, but it sells for different prices because each region has a different number of empty kilns. The East has the most new plants and the most spare capacity, so it is cheapest. The South used to be just as bad, until the two giants bought up its weaker players and started leading prices up. The North is tight and profitable today, which is exactly why everyone is building there for 2028.

How regional exposure maps onto the companies. The listed companies sort neatly by region. North and Central specialists (Shree's Rajasthan core, J.K. In plain terms

A company in the North-East earning ₹1,500 a tonne and one in the East earning ₹800 may be equally well run; the difference is largely geography, incentives and local competition. The fairest comparison is between companies selling into the same region.

Regional pricing also explains why national price indices are a poor guide to any single company. ICRA's all-India average (₹345 a bag in FY26, ₹350 in the first four months of FY27) moved only 2%, but individual regions moved 10% or more in a quarter. An investor tracking a South-heavy company should watch the South price series and southern capacity additions; one tracking a North-heavy company should watch the North and the commissioning dates of UltraTech's, J.K.'s and Star's northern plants. The fuel and freight shock: how a war in West Asia reached a kiln in Rajasthan Deep dive

Petcoke up 51%, diesel up 9% in a month, packing bags up 47%: the 2026 shock took ₹150–300 a tonne off margins and sorted the strong from the exposed.

Cement kilns run on heat, and in India most of that heat comes from imported petroleum coke (petcoke) and coal. Petcoke import for fuel is generally banned in India because of pollution concerns, but cement (along with lime kilns, calcium carbide and gasification) is allowed to import it as a feedstock on an actual-user basis, with no quantity cap, under DGFT Notification 10/2023; about three-quarters of India's petcoke is used by cement. That makes the industry's single largest variable cost a dollar-priced, globally traded commodity.

From about February–March 2026, war in West Asia and disruption around the Strait of Hormuz pushed fuel prices sharply higher. ICRA reported domestic petcoke at ₹16,400 a tonne in May 2026, up 51% year on year (and 4% month on month), imported coal at US$111 a tonne (+10% month on month), and retail diesel at about ₹95 a litre (+9% month on month); Ambuja's own deck quotes diesel at ₹112 a litre against ₹81 a year earlier, a different (likely bulk or regional) basis. US petcoke delivered to India rose from about US$100–110 a tonne to US$150–160 by June 2026 on Dalmia's chart, before easing to US$130–135 (Arihant). The rupee weakened about 11% year on year (Ramco). And the price of polypropylene granules used for cement bags rose: bag prices went from about ₹9.5 to ₹14 each (Arihant).

Freight: the quieter half of the shock. Diesel is about 40–50% of road freight cost, and road carries about three-quarters of cement. In plain terms

A cement company's two biggest bills are for fuel to heat its kilns and diesel to move its trucks, and both rose sharply in 2026 because of a war thousands of kilometres away. The companies that suffered least had signed fuel contracts in advance, could burn coal or waste instead of petcoke, made their own electricity from kiln heat and solar panels, and had plants close to their customers. The ones that leaned on cheap imported petcoke and long truck journeys lost a quarter or more of their profit per tonne in a single quarter.

What happens next. HDFC Securities expected coal and petcoke prices to peak in Q2 FY27, and Shree said its fuel cost had "almost peaked" in Q1, with contracted petcoke and Omani gypsum supply normalising from Q2. If fuel normalises from Q3, the shock will have cost the industry roughly two quarters of margin, as in 2022.

In plain terms

The fuel shock did not change who the low-cost producers are in the long run, but it exposed which costs each company really controls. Green electricity, short delivery routes and fuel flexibility are permanent advantages that keep paying in every future spike. Expect every management team to talk about them more, and expect the market to reward the ones that can show the numbers quarter after quarter.

Six ways to run a cement company

Region, network shape, fuel strategy and product mix sort the fifteen companies into six archetypes with very different economics.

Every cement company sells the same product, but they make money in different ways. The differences come from five levers: the region they sell in, which sets price and utilisation; the lead distance and rail share of their network, which sets freight; their fuel flexibility and green-power share, which set power and fuel cost; their trade and premium mix, which sets realisation; and their balance sheet, which decides who can keep building through a downturn. In plain terms

Focus less on which company makes the best cement (they all make roughly the same thing) and more about which kind of machine each company has built. A national network with cheap power and short delivery routes earns the most per tonne. A strong regional player in a tight market can earn as much, for as long as rivals stay away. A grinder that buys in its clinker or slag spends little on plant but depends on its suppliers. And a sub-scale player in a crowded region earns the least, whatever it does.

The archetypes also predict behaviour. Scale leaders build ahead of demand and lead prices. Regional champions defend their home markets and expand cautiously into adjacent ones (Shree into the South and East, Star into Rajasthan). East blenders add grinding units cheaply and compete hard on price. South-heavy players have been the main acquisition targets. And mid-caps either invest heavily to stay relevant (JK Lakshmi's ₹5,000 crore plan, Birla's ₹4,800 crore) or, like Prism, stop adding capacity and wait. An investor should match each company's valuation to its archetype, not to the sector average.

The rulebook: a tax cut, a mining-levy U-turn, a cartel case and a carbon price

Prices are free, but the state sets the tax on every bag, the levy on every tonne of limestone and, from 2025, a ceiling on carbon per tonne.

Free prices, watched closely. Cement prices and distribution were controlled by the government from the 1950s until partial decontrol in 1982 and full decontrol on 1 March 1989; the industry was delicensed in 1991, setting off the capacity boom of the 1990s. Prices are now set by the market, region by region. Because a handful of players set those prices in each region, the industry has repeatedly drawn antitrust attention. In June 2012 the Competition Commission of India fined eleven companies (including UltraTech, ACC, Ambuja, Shree, India Cements, Ramco, J.K. Cement and Lafarge, now Nuvoco) ₹6,307 crore for cartelisation and withholding capacity, on a complaint by the Builders' Association of India. The appellate tribunal remanded the case in December 2015; the CCI re-imposed roughly ₹6,300–6,700 crore in August 2016; the NCLAT upheld it in July 2018; and the Supreme Court admitted the appeals in October 2018 on a 10% deposit. We found no judgment as of October 2026, so it remains a contingent liability for the incumbents. The CCI also clears every large merger, including UltraTech–India Cements, UltraTech–Kesoram and Ambuja–Penna and Orient.

GST: 28% to 18%. Cement sat in the top 28% GST slab from the tax's launch on 1 July 2017. The GST Council's meeting of 3–4 September 2025 cut it to 18% with effect from 22 September 2025. At the same time GST on coal rose from 5% to 18% (with full input credit) and the ₹400-a-tonne compensation cess on coal was removed, which JM Financial said would especially ease costs for eastern and central producers. The shop price of a bag fell by about ₹25–30 (7.5–8%) on pass-through. Elara measured the all-India retail price at ₹340 a bag in September 2025, down ₹29 in a month, while the GST-adjusted price held at ₹369. One unintended effect: incentives linked to the GST a plant pays (common in the North-East and other backward-area schemes) shrank with the rate, which cost Star Cement ₹39–40 crore year on year in Q1 FY27.

Five decades, six cycles: the timeline

Cement has swung between shortage and glut since decontrol; the current cycle is the first shaped as much by consolidation as by capacity.

Indian cement's history falls into six cycles, each defined by the interplay of capacity, demand, fuel and ownership. Control and shortage (1956–1982): prices and distribution were set by the state, investment was unattractive and the country ran short. Decontrol and boom (1982–1999): partial decontrol in 1982, full decontrol in 1989 and delicensing in 1991 unleashed a capacity boom. First consolidation (1999–2007): Grasim bought L&T's cement business and created UltraTech (2004); Holcim took Gujarat Ambuja and ACC (2005–06); Lafarge bought Raymond's and Tata Steel's cement plants. The capex super-cycle and the cartel case (FY08–FY13): about 150 million tonnes were added, utilisation fell from above 90% to about 70%, prices whipsawed, and the CCI issued its cartel order in 2012. Weak demand and the second consolidation (FY14–FY20): demand grew only 4–5% a year through the real-estate slowdown and demonetisation, and stressed plants were sold, many through insolvency: UltraTech bought Jaypee's 21.2 MTPA (2017) and Binani (2018), Nirma bought Lafarge India (2016) to create Nuvoco. COVID, fuel shocks and the land-grab (FY21–today): output fell 10.8% in FY21 and rebounded 20.8% in FY22; EBITDA per tonne peaked at about ₹1,250 in FY21 on low costs, troughed at about ₹850 in FY23 on the Russia–Ukraine fuel spike, and the Adani–Holcim deal of 2022 set off the current consolidation and capacity race.

In plain terms

Cement volumes almost always grow; it is profit per tonne that swings. When imported fuel gets expensive or the big players start a fight for market share, profit per tonne falls by a fifth or more within a year, even if India is building flat out. Reading a cement company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Cement is analysed per tonne. Learn a dozen numbers, and the traps hidden in each, and any results release becomes readable in minutes.

Every cement company's results come down to one identity: how many tonnes it sold, multiplied by the gap between what it got for each tonne and what each tonne cost to make and deliver. Everything else, from capacity and utilisation to fuel, freight, green power and premium mix, is a driver of one of those three terms. This section takes each native KPI in turn: what it means, how it is calculated, a worked example in rupees, what drives it, what good and bad look like, how the fifteen companies compare, and where the definition can mislead.

The equation has three important properties. First, it is multiplicative: a 10% gain in volume and a 10% gain in EBITDA per tonne compound into a 21% gain in EBITDA. Second, EBITDA per tonne is a small difference between two large numbers, so small percentage moves in realisation or cost produce large percentage moves in profit; on the illustrative plant, a 4% rise in price (₹200 a tonne, or ₹10 a bag) lifts EBITDA by 21%. Third, the denominator of return on capital is capacity, not volume: an idle tonne of capacity still carries its full capital cost, which is why utilisation matters so much for returns even when EBITDA per tonne looks healthy.

In plain terms

A cement company's profit is just tonnes sold times profit per tonne. Profit per tonne is the selling price minus the cost of making and delivering it. Because the price is about ₹5,000 and the profit only about ₹1,000, a small change in either the price or the cost makes a big difference to profit: a 4% price rise can lift profit by a fifth.

2. Capacity (MTPA)

Definition and formula. Capacity is the rated annual output of a company's cement grinding mills, in million tonnes per annum (MTPA). It is a stock figure, quoted at a date. Worked example: UltraTech's 205.5 MTPA global capacity at June 2026 is 200.1 MTPA of domestic grey cement plus 5.4 MTPA overseas; its FY26 consolidated volume was 154.3 million tonnes. Drivers: new integrated units, new grinding units, debottlenecking and acquisitions. Good, average, weak: more than 50 MTPA gives pan-regional reach; 15–50 MTPA is a strong regional player; below 10 MTPA is sub-scale. How they compare: UltraTech 205.5, Adani 109, Shree 69.3, Dalmia 54.7, J.K. 35.4 (32.3 grey), Nuvoco 27, Ramco about 26.4, JSW 24.1, Birla 21.4, JK Lakshmi 18.0, India Cements about 14.45, Star 9.7, Orient about 8.5, Prism 5.6 (section 06 charts them).

The definition trap. Grinding capacity is cheap to add and easy to overstate; the binding constraint is clinker. A company with a high ratio of grinding to clinker capacity either blends heavily (good) or must buy clinker from others (a vulnerability). JSW Cement has 24.1 MTPA of grinding but only 9.74 MTPA of clinker, because much of its output is GGBS slag that needs no clinker. Also check the date and perimeter: Ambuja's 109 MTPA is the whole Adani group including ACC and Orient; UltraTech's 200.1 MTPA includes India Cements; Prism's 5.6 MTPA excludes 1.37 MTPA of tolled grinding capacity it uses; and "effective" capacity can differ from nameplate (Adani excludes 1.6 MTPA of high-cost Jamul and Sindri capacity from its effective figure).

3. Capacity utilisation (%)

Definition and formula. Utilisation = cement volume sold (or produced) ÷ average grinding capacity over the period. For a quarter, annualise the volume (multiply by four) or divide by a quarter of capacity. Worked example: Adani sold 17.1 million tonnes in Q1 FY27 on 109 MTPA: 17.1 × 4 ÷ 109 ≈ 63%. In FY26 it sold 73.7 million tonnes on an average of about 103.5 MTPA: about 71%. Drivers: regional demand, seasonality, the ramp-up of new capacity (new plants start at 30–60%), maintenance shutdowns and pricing strategy (Ambuja deliberately exited "non-remunerative" markets in Q1 FY27). Good, average, weak: above 80% is good, 65–80% average, below 60% weak. The national average is about 70%.

How they compare. The spread is wide. Birla Corporation and Prism run effectively full; UltraTech, Nuvoco, JK Lakshmi and J.K. Cement run in the mid-70s to low-80s; Ramco, India Cements and Orient around 70%; Shree, Dalmia, JSW and Adani in the low 60s; Star in the mid-50s after commissioning its Cachar grinding unit in February 2026. Low utilisation is not always bad news: Shree and JSW are low because they have just added capacity, which will fill.

The definition trap. Companies use different numerators and denominators: some divide production by capacity, others sales (which can include traded cement and clinker); some use year-end capacity, others the time-weighted average. In plain terms

Utilisation tells you how many of a company's kilns and mills are actually busy. A plant running at 80% spreads its fixed costs over more tonnes and earns more per tonne than the same plant at 60%. 4. Volume growth (%)

Definition and formula. Volume growth = this period's tonnes sold ÷ the same period last year's − 1. Always compare year on year, because of seasonality. Worked example: J.K. Cement sold 5.96 million tonnes of grey cement in Q1 FY27 against about 5.05 a year earlier: 5.96 ÷ 5.05 − 1 ≈ 18%, against industry growth of about 7–9%. Drivers: regional demand, new capacity, market-share gains or losses, and pricing strategy. Good, average, weak: industry growth plus 3 percentage points or more is good (taking share); roughly matching the industry (7–9% in FY26–FY27) is average; below the industry is weak, unless it is deliberate price discipline.

How they compare. In Q1 FY27 India Cements (+18%, turnaround), J.K. Cement (+18% grey), Shree (+17%), JSW (+15%), UltraTech (+12–13%) and Ramco (+12%) all grew well ahead of the industry, while Adani (−7%), ACC (−6.5%), Orient (−21%) and Prism (−6%) shrank. The negative numbers at the Adani companies were a choice: management chose "value over volume", exiting distant, low-margin markets, which helped lift Ambuja's EBITDA per tonne by 27% quarter on quarter even as volume fell.

7. Power and fuel cost per tonne (and fuel cost per '000 kcal)

8. Freight per tonne and lead distance

Definition and formula. Freight (logistics) per tonne = freight and forwarding expense ÷ tonnes sold, covering primary freight from plant to depot or customer, secondary freight to the retailer, handling, and the movement of clinker to split grinding units. Lead distance is the volume-weighted average distance from plant to customer, in kilometres. Worked example: by road, freight costs roughly ₹2.5–3.5 per tonne-kilometre (an INDICATIVE industry range consistent with UltraTech's ₹1,149 a tonne over a 360 km lead, about ₹3.2); cutting the lead by 20 km saves about ₹60 a tonne. Drivers: diesel prices (about 40–50% of road freight cost), lead distance, rail share and rake availability, the density of grinding units and depots near markets, and the share of direct dispatch to customers. Good, average, weak: freight below ₹1,100 a tonne and lead below 280 km are good; ₹1,100–1,300 and 280–380 km average; above ₹1,300 and 400 km weak.

12. Green power share

Definition and formula. Green power share = (waste-heat recovery + solar + wind + other renewable electricity) ÷ total electricity consumed. Worked example: UltraTech's Q1 FY27 mix (22.2% WHRS at ₹0.71 a kWh plus 26.0% renewables at ₹4.52) gives 48% green on its power-mix chart, 47% on its headline figure; with grinding at about 68 kWh a tonne, every 10 percentage points switched from grid (₹7.87) to renewables (₹4.52) saves about ₹20–25 a tonne (68 × 10% × ₹3.3, ILLUSTRATIVE). 13. Mix: trade share, premium share and blended cement

Definitions. Trade share = volume sold through dealers to retail buyers ÷ total volume. Premium share = premium-brand volume ÷ trade volume. Blended share = (PPC + PSC + composite) ÷ total cement. Good, average, weak: trade above 70% good, 60–70% average, below 55% weak; premium above 25% of trade good, 15–25% average, below 10% weak; blended above 80% good.

16. Definition traps, collected

  • Perimeter. Ambuja consolidated includes ACC and Orient; UltraTech consolidated includes India Cements. Never add the subsidiaries to their parents, and remember that ACC's volume includes cement bought from Ambuja.
  • Ex-works selling. India Cements and Orient now sell mostly to their parents at the factory gate: realisation, freight per tonne and margin percentages are not comparable with earlier periods or with peers.
  • What counts as a tonne. Clinker sales (Shree, Star, Prism), GGBS (about 41% of JSW's volume), white cement (J.K.), traded cement (ACC) and RMC revenue (UltraTech, ACC, Nuvoco) all distort per-tonne figures.
  • EBITDA with or without other income. Nuvoco and UltraTech's "PBIDT" include other income; screener-style operating profit excludes it. JK Lakshmi's EBITDA per tonne is ₹758 without other income and ₹845 with it.
  • Incentives. State incentives booked as revenue flatter realisation and EBITDA: about ₹100 a tonne at J.K. in FY26; Star's North-East incentives; Dalmia's ₹45–75 crore a quarter.
  • Normalised versus reported profit. Tax write-backs doubled Ambuja's FY26 profit (₹5,637 crore reported, ₹2,647 crore normalised); land-sale gains inflated Ramco's (₹699 crore reported, about ₹250 crore adjusted, land gains of about ₹574 crore unverified); a CCPS charge turned JSW's into a ₹799 crore loss (charge of about ₹1,466 crore, unverified).
  • Consolidated EBITDA ÷ standalone volume. Overstates EBITDA per tonne when overseas or other subsidiaries earn without adding tonnes (Shree: ₹1,274 computed against ₹1,174 correct).
  • Net debt bases. Screener-style "borrowings minus investments" misses bank deposits and can include strategic or illiquid holdings; use company-reported net debt and say so. ACC's "₹13,500 crore cash" was really receivables and other assets.
  • Capacity dates and types. Grinding is not clinker; nameplate is not effective; "commissioned in trials" is not "producing"; UltraTech reached 200.1 MTPA domestic in April 2026, not at the FY26 year-end.
  • Capex per tonne. Grinding units are a third of integrated plant cost; "remaining capex" is not total cost; land and later phases are often excluded.
  • Green power. It cuts electricity cost, not kiln fuel cost; definitions vary between WHRS-plus-renewables and renewables only.
  • Period labels. Q4 figures are often quoted as full-year ones (UltraTech's ₹1,253 a tonne and Shree's ₹1,161 are Q4 FY26, not FY26).
In plain terms

Most of the mistakes people make with cement numbers come from comparing figures that look the same but are calculated differently: one company's "tonnes" include slag or clinker, another's "EBITDA" includes interest income or subsidies, a third sells at the factory gate to its parent. Read the footnotes, and when in doubt compare a company with its own past rather than with its neighbour.

17. How to read a cement company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Volume, year on year (1 minute). Compare with industry growth for the same quarter (about 9% in Q1 FY27). Ask whether the gap is share gain, new capacity, acquisition or deliberate exit.
  2. Realisation, quarter on quarter and year on year (1 minute). Check against the regional price trend for the company's main markets; strip out incentives and any change in freight terms.
  3. EBITDA per tonne (1 minute). The headline. Compare with last year's same quarter (seasonality) and with guidance. Above ₹1,100 is strong, below ₹700 weak.
  4. Cost per tonne by head (2 minutes). Power and fuel, freight, raw materials, other. Which moved, and is the move fuel price (external) or efficiency (internal)? Note fuel cost per thousand kcal and lead distance.
  5. Utilisation (1 minute). Rising or falling? If falling, is it because new capacity was commissioned?
  6. Green power and fuel mix (30 seconds). Rising green share and falling petcoke dependence are durable cost advantages.
  7. Mix (30 seconds). Trade share, premium share, blended share: are they moving in the company's favour?
  8. Net debt and capex (1 minute). Net debt to EBITDA, the capex spent in the quarter against the annual guide, and any change to the capacity timetable.
  9. One-offs (1 minute). Incentives, tax write-backs, land sales, exceptional items, other income: rebuild the normalised profit.
  10. Guidance (1 minute). Next quarter's cost guidance (for example UltraTech's +₹130–140 a tonne for Q2 FY27), price commentary, capacity dates and volume guidance. Then decide whether the quarter changed your view of the company's cost position, not just its quarterly profit.
In plain terms

If you only have a minute, look at three numbers: tonnes sold against the industry, profit per tonne against a year ago, and net debt against profit. Everything else in a cement results release explains why those three moved.

Who is winning: scale, shock-resistance and the balance sheet

Three maps separate the companies that can keep building and earning from those that are building on borrowed time.

Winning in cement means three things at once: earning a high EBITDA per tonne on a large base, holding that margin when costs spike, and being able to fund the next round of capacity without stretching the balance sheet. No company leads on all three, but the maps below show which ones come closest.

Map 1: scale and quality. UltraTech sits alone in the top-right quadrant with Shree just inside it, and Dalmia close to the line. Adani is the interesting case: large enough to be UltraTech's only national rival, but earning about ₹887 a tonne, below the decadal average, because Sanghi and Penna drag down its base business. If management delivers its plan to cut costs by ₹250 a tonne a year for two years, Adani would move up into the winners' quadrant. The mid-caps cluster tightly: J.K., Nuvoco, JSW, Ramco, Birla and JK Lakshmi all sit between 18 and 35 MTPA and ₹750–1,012 a tonne. That cluster is where the next round of consolidation is most likely to come from.

Map 2: who pays for the race. The capacity race is a balance-sheet contest. JSW Cement plans to grow capacity by about 80% and is already at 2.95 times EBITDA against a board ceiling of three; its pace is now set by its balance sheet. J.K. Cement (+55% to 50 MTPA by FY30) and JK Lakshmi (+67% to 30 MTPA) are mid-way, with leverage guided to rise to 2.5–2.75 times at JK Lakshmi. Ramco has the opposite problem: modest growth but high leverage, after its deleveraging stalled in Q1 FY27. Adani combines the largest absolute pipeline with a debt-free balance sheet, though its cash fell from more than ₹10,000 crore to ₹844 crore as it paid for Penna, Orient and capex. Shree is the outlier: net cash of about ₹8,400 crore on the screener basis and the slowest dated pipeline, with capex cut from ₹3,470 crore in FY25 to about ₹1,500 crore a year.

Map 3: the shock test. The third map tests a popular claim: that green power protects cement margins. It does, but only for the electricity bill. UltraTech, with 47% green power, best-in-class fuel buying and the shortest response time on pricing, held its margin. Prism held because of mix (premium share jumped to 67% of trade) and a fuel switch. In plain terms

The winner so far is clear: UltraTech is the biggest, earns among the most per tonne, held up best in the fuel shock and can afford its expansion. Adani is the only rival with similar reach and a clean balance sheet, but it still has to lift its profits from the plants it bought. Below them, most mid-sized companies are borrowing to keep up, and their success depends on whether prices in their home regions hold when all the new capacity arrives in 2028.

The global lens: the world's growth market, valued per tonne

India is the only large cement market still growing steadily, and investors pay more per tonne of Indian capacity than for global majors, even though each tonne earns less.

India against the world. World cement production was about 3.8 billion tonnes in 2025, on USGS estimates, and has been shrinking because China's output fell from roughly 2.4 billion tonnes in 2020–21 to 1.7 billion as its property market contracted. Global Cement's February 2026 outlook (as reported by CemNet) saw world demand returning to growth only in 2027. India, at about 470 million tonnes on a calendar basis and growing 6–9% a year, is the only large market with sustained growth, which makes it the swing market for equipment makers and fuel suppliers. It is also far from saturated: at roughly 290–340 kg per person it uses about half the world average and a quarter of China's level, while smaller emerging markets such as Vietnam and Turkey produce around a tonne per person (partly for export).

Low price, low margin, high turns. Indian cement is a high-volume, low-price business by global standards. Technology and disruption: greener kilns, less clinker, electric trucks

No product threatens cement; the technology race is about cost and carbon per tonne, and the leaders are pulling ahead.

Green power. The most valuable technology in Indian cement today is also the oldest: waste-heat recovery (WHRS), which captures hot exhaust from the kiln and preheater to raise steam for a turbine. At about ₹0.71 a kilowatt-hour at UltraTech, it is a tenth of the price of grid power (₹7.87). Captive solar and wind (about ₹4.52) are the second leg. CRISIL estimates that 10–15% of the industry's FY26–FY28 capex goes into green energy and efficiency. Targets are ambitious: UltraTech aims for 85% green power by FY30 (from 47%), Ambuja about 60% by FY28 with 1,498 MW of green capacity, J.K. 75% by FY30, Star 60% by FY28, JSW above 60% by Q3 FY27, and UltraTech's India Cements from 3% to 86% by the end of FY28.

Alternative fuels and raw materials. India burns very little alternative fuel in its kilns. The thermal substitution rate (TSR), the share of kiln heat from waste-derived fuels such as refuse-derived fuel, municipal solid waste, biomass and industrial waste, is about 5–7% in India (UltraTech 6.9% and Ambuja 7.1% in Q1 FY27, JK Lakshmi 9% in FY26) against 40–60% in Europe. Alternative raw materials are also rising: UltraTech used 49.8 million tonnes of them in FY26.

Less clinker. The cheapest tonne of clinker is the one not made. Blended cements (PPC with fly ash, PSC with slag) already dominate, and the Bureau of Indian Standards' IS 18189:2023 now allows composite cement and limestone calcined clay cement (LC3), which can cut the clinker factor further. Dalmia (82% blended, 1.7× conversion), Nuvoco (1.73×) and JSW (GGBS) lead. Lower clinker per tonne means lower fuel, lower limestone use and lower carbon: UltraTech's net Scope 1 intensity of 538 kg CO₂ per tonne compares with Dalmia's 475 and Nuvoco's 462, and the global industry average of about 580–600.

Logistics technology. Freight is the largest cost, so logistics innovation pays quickly. UltraTech has deployed 45 electric 55-tonne trucks within a fleet of more than 750 "green" vehicles, and Shree plans about 100 electric trucks in FY27. Rail sidings, coastal bulk shipping (Nuvoco's Sachana terminal, Dalmia's Chennai terminal), digitised dispatch planning and direct-to-site delivery all shorten leads and cut cost per tonne. Digital process control, including AI-based kiln optimisation and predictive maintenance, typically saves 1–2% of fuel. Carbon capture remains pre-commercial, with pilot agreements at Dalmia and UltraTech.

Disruption risks. There is no substitute for cement at scale in the medium term; mass timber and alternative binders are negligible in India. The real shifts are within the market. Ready-mix concrete and pre-cast construction move volume from the trade channel (bags sold to home builders at a premium) to non-trade (bulk sales at lower prices), though with better efficiency for the producer. Aluminium formwork and Mivan-style systems reduce wastage. And the carbon regime will gradually favour producers with low clinker factors and high green power over those reliant on pure OPC and grid electricity. None of these threatens volumes; all of them reward the low-cost, low-carbon producers the rest of this report has identified.

In plain terms

Nobody is about to replace cement, so "disruption" here means cheaper and cleaner ways to make the same thing. Generating electricity from kiln heat and solar panels, burning waste instead of imported petcoke, blending in more slag and ash, and using electric trucks all cut cost per tonne and carbon at the same time. The big companies can afford to invest in all of these first, which widens their lead.

Bull vs bear: discipline after the land-grab, or a glut in 2028

Both cases agree volumes will grow; they disagree about whether the new owners will hold prices when the next wave of capacity lands.

The bull case

  • Reliable volume growth: 6–9% a year on a per-capita base half the world average, FY27 running at about 10%, government capex of ₹12.2 lakh crore and PMAY housing behind schedule.
  • Consolidation discipline: two groups hold about 43% of capacity and the top five about 65%; the South's post-consolidation price premium shows leadership can work, and few targets remain to fight over.
  • Policy tailwinds: GST cut to 18%, state mineral-land levies abolished by Section 9D, petcoke imports kept open.
  • Structural cost-out: green power heading to 60–85%, shorter leads, more blending and alternative fuel; Ambuja targeting ₹4,000 a tonne, India Cements ₹1,000 a tonne of EBITDA.
  • Cheap at the low end: many mid-caps trade below replacement cost (₹3,970–6,500 a tonne against ₹9,600–10,500) and are natural targets; the fuel shock should fade from Q3 FY27.

The bear case

  • Capacity outruns demand: 160–170 MTPA added over FY26–FY28 against 90–120 million tonnes of new demand keeps utilisation near 70% and price growth minimal.
  • The North and Central glut: UltraTech adds 20.7 MTPA in the North by FY28, plus J.K., Star, JSW and JK Lakshmi; the most profitable regions face the biggest supply wave.
  • The giants still compete: FY25 showed concentration does not stop price wars when the leaders chase share.
  • Fuel and currency exposure: two shocks in four years (2022, 2026) each took ₹150–300 a tonne off margins; the rupee is weaker.
  • Leverage and execution: JSW at its 3× ceiling, Ramco and Nuvoco near 2.4–2.9×, JK Lakshmi guiding to 2.75×; slipping timelines at Dalmia and Adani; integration risk and minority-shareholder disputes.

Weighing the two. The two cases share a premise: volume is not the question. India will keep building, and cement demand will very likely grow at 6–9% a year through FY28. The disagreement is entirely about EBITDA per tonne, and specifically about the behaviour of the two giants once their integration phase ends. If UltraTech and Adani, having bought most of the weak independents, choose to lead prices and fill their capacity steadily, industry EBITDA per tonne can move back above its decadal average of about ₹965 and towards the ₹1,100–1,250 the leaders earn. If they keep competing for share as the FY28 capacity wave lands in the North and Central regions, the industry average could revisit the ₹800s of FY23 and FY25, as ICRA's unverified FY27 estimate of ₹820–870 already suggests for the fuel-hit year.

That ₹18,700 crore swing between a trough and a peak year, on unchanged volume, is the size of the bet investors are making when they buy or sell the sector. It explains why share prices react so sharply to quarterly EBITDA per tonne and to any sign of price discipline or price war. In plain terms

Everyone agrees India will need more cement every year. The argument is about price. Optimists think the two giants, now that they own so much of the market, will stop fighting and let prices drift up. Pessimists think the huge amount of new capacity due in 2027–28, especially in the North, will force everyone to cut prices to fill their plants. The difference between those two outcomes is roughly ₹18,000–19,000 crore a year of operating profit for the listed companies.
