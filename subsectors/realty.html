00

The one-page view: a flat housing market, a consolidating industry and a lumpy calendar

Homes sold in India’s big cities peaked in 2023. Listed developers have kept growing anyway, by taking share, selling dearer homes and building a rental business on the side.

India’s listed real estate developers sell a product that does not exist yet, collect most of the price while they build it, and are allowed to call it revenue only when they hand over the keys three to five years later. Almost every confusion about the sector, from its “expensive” price-to-earnings ratios to its “collapsing” quarterly bookings, comes from that gap between when the money moves and when the accounts admit it. This report is built to close that gap for the 19 companies the NSE classifies as Realty · Residential, Commercial Projects.

The 19 are not one business. Most are for-sale residential developers: DLF, Lodha, Prestige, Godrej Properties, Oberoi, Brigade, Sobha, Aditya Birla Real Estate (ABREL), Signatureglobal, Puravankara, Sunteck, Embassy Developments, Sri Lotus and TARC. Some are mainly landlords: Phoenix Mills owns malls, NESCO owns an IT park and the Bombay Exhibition Centre, and DLF and Prestige own large office books alongside their housing arms. A few are land-monetisation situations whose value depends on court orders and government files: Hemisphere Properties, Valor Estate and, until recently, Embassy Developments. Anant Raj is a Gurugram land bank turning itself into a data-centre company. Together they were worth ₹6,36,896 crore on 13 October 2026. DLF, Lodha and Phoenix alone are 54% of that, and the top six are 81%.

The market they sell into has gone flat on volume. ANAROCK, the consultancy most analysts use for the seven biggest cities (Mumbai Metropolitan Region, National Capital Region, Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad, Chennai and Kolkata), counted about 3,95,625 homes sold in calendar 2025, 14% fewer than in 2024 and 17% below the 2023 record of 4,76,530. Yet the value of those sales rose 6% to more than ₹6 lakh crore, because the average home sold was larger, more expensive and more often in the luxury bracket. The first nine months of 2026 were roughly flat on units. Meanwhile the number of unsold homes has climbed to about 6.31 lakh, equal to roughly 19 months of sales.

Listed developers have nonetheless compounded. The 28 listed developers tracked by the Press Trust of India booked about ₹1.95 lakh crore of sales in FY26, up 17%, and about 2.2 times what a smaller listed group booked in FY23. The 14 members of this sub-sector that report bookings sold ₹1,59,986 crore in FY26, also up 17%. That growth came from share, not from the market: the listed share of top-seven-city sales value has risen from roughly 10–15% before Covid to roughly 30% (an indicative estimate; Section 07 explains the arithmetic).

Then came a quarter that showed how much the listed numbers depend on the launch calendar. In April–June 2026 (Q1 FY27) the 14 reporting members booked ₹32,688 crore. DLF booked ₹657 crore against ₹11,425 crore a year earlier, when it sold out Privana North; Prestige booked ₹6,579 crore against ₹12,126 crore. Neither change says much about demand: both companies were waiting for approvals to launch. Q2 hints that the lull was timing: Oberoi’s Three Sixty North in Gurugram booked ₹8,109 crore in July, more than Oberoi’s entire FY26, and DLF sold all 172 homes at The Aureva for about ₹1,985 crore on 4 October.

The macro backdrop has turned less friendly. The Reserve Bank of India cut its policy repo rate by 125 basis points through 2025 to 5.25%, then on 7 October 2026 raised it by 25 basis points to 5.50%, its first increase since February 2023, citing oil near $95–100 a barrel and the West Asia conflict. Against that, the office market is at a record: CBRE counted 82.6 million sq ft of leasing in 2025 and 66.4 million sq ft in the first nine months of 2026, with global capability centres (GCCs) taking about 42%. The six listed REITs are worth more than ₹2.17 lakh crore.

Five forces shape everything in this report, and the section headings follow them. Formalisation and consolidation: the 2016–2020 regime change (RERA, GST, demonetisation, the NBFC crisis, a new revenue-accounting standard) raised the cost of being small and handed share to branded, listed developers. Premiumisation: value grew about 22% a year from 2022 to 2025 while units grew about 3%. Land as the profit pool: margin is decided when land is bought, and 2024–26 saw a land-buying race funded by large equity raises. The rate and affordability turn: cheap money in 2020–21 started the cycle; the October 2026 hike, slowing house-price growth and rising inventory define its late phase. The annuity pivot: offices, malls, hotels and data centres give some developers a second engine valued on rent rather than bookings, with REITs as the exit.

In plain terms

A developer is paid in advance and recognised in arrears. Buyers sign up and start paying years before the flat exists, so the cash and the bookings tell you what is happening now, while the profit and loss account tells you what was sold three to five years ago. Read the bookings, the cash collected and the debt first, and treat the reported earnings as a delayed echo.

The industry has been winning on price, not on volume. Fewer homes are being sold in India’s big cities than in 2023, but each one costs much more, and listed developers are taking a larger slice of a market that is otherwise standing still.

The report’s verdict, argued in detail in Sections 12 and 20, is that this is a mature, late-cycle plateau rather than a turning point into a bust. Inventory is rising and luxury demand in Gurugram is cooling, but listed balance sheets carry about a third of the leverage they did in the last cycle, RERA escrow stops a China-style chain reaction, and consolidation can keep listed bookings growing in a flat market. The risks that matter most are the price paid for land in 2024–26, a second rate hike, and how much of FY27’s guidance depends on approvals arriving on time.

01

How a developer makes money: sell first, build later, book the profit last

Construction is outsourced and earns thin margins. The developer’s money is made by buying land well, converting it to cash quickly and charging a brand premium.

Strip away the brochures and a for-sale residential developer does six things in sequence. It secures land, either by buying it outright or by signing a joint development agreement (JDA) with a landowner who is paid a share of the revenue or of the built-up area. It obtains approvals: building plans, environmental clearance, fire and aviation consents and, in Mumbai, premium floor space (FSI) and transferable development rights (TDR). It registers the project with the state RERA authority, without which it may not advertise or take a booking. It launches and sells apartments off-plan; these signed contracts are its “pre-sales” or “bookings”. It builds, collecting instalments from buyers as construction milestones are reached, with 70% of that money ring-fenced in a project escrow account. Finally, it obtains the occupancy certificate (OC) and hands over the keys, and only then books revenue and profit.

Cash peaks in Steps 3–4; accounting profit appears only at Step 5. Land (Step 1) decides most of the margin; approvals (Step 2) decide when it can be earned.

Each step has a different owner of the margin. The contractor who pours the concrete, whether a listed engineering firm such as those covered in our civil construction report or a developer’s in-house arm, earns roughly 8–12% EBITDA on its work, so construction is a cost to be managed rather than a source of advantage. The state takes a large and growing share through premiums, development charges and stamp duty. Brokers take 2–4% of the price on the deals they bring. Lenders earn 9–14% on construction finance from banks, NBFCs and credit funds (the funding side is covered in our NBFC report), although a developer whose buyers pay on schedule needs little of it, because customer advances are interest-free.

The developer’s own value-add comes from three places. First, land bought cheaper than the market: DLF’s Gurugram land was assembled decades ago and sits on its books at a fraction of today’s value, which is why its gross margins on recent launches run above 50%. Second, speed: the faster land becomes approved, launched and sold, the less equity is tied up and the higher the return on it. A developer that sells 60% of a tower on launch day has funded much of its construction with buyers’ money. Third, brand: industry estimates, widely repeated by brokers but not independently verified, put the premium that Godrej, Lodha, DLF, Prestige or Oberoi command over a local developer in the same neighbourhood at 10–25%. Since RERA, the brand also stands for something buyers can check: a record of delivering on time.

That is why the industry’s natural scorecard is about land, launches, bookings and cash, not about revenue and capacity, and why Section 14 is built around those measures. Revenue in any year is a function of how many projects happened to reach completion, and it can rise while the business shrinks or fall while it booms.

The annuity business works differently. A developer that builds an office tower or a mall and keeps it earns rent month after month, at EBITDA margins of 75–90% for offices and 60–75% for malls. The money is made through the gap between what the building yields on its cost (typically 10–14% a year) and the yield at which investors will buy it (7.5–8.5% for Grade-A offices). Build for ₹900 crore, earn ₹106 crore a year of net operating income, and the building is worth about ₹1,325 crore at an 8% capitalisation rate. That spread is what REITs crystallise, and it is why DLF, Prestige, Brigade, Phoenix and the Embassy group have kept building rental assets. Section 13 covers it in depth.

In plain terms

A home builder in India is mostly a land trader with a construction contract attached. If it buys land at the right price and sells the flats quickly, almost everything else (the contractor, the bank, the broker) is a cost that can be hired. If it overpays for land, no amount of good construction will rescue the margin.

02

Size and history: 3.96 lakh homes, ₹6 lakh crore, and a boom made of price

Since 2023 the upcycle has been about what each home costs, not how many are sold. That distinction matters for every forecast in this report.

Three numbers describe the market the 19 sell into. In calendar 2025 the top seven cities absorbed about 3.96 lakh homes worth more than ₹6 lakh crore at an average of about ₹9,260 per sq ft, according to ANAROCK. Knight Frank, which adds Ahmedabad to make eight cities, counted 3,48,207 homes for 2025, “broadly steady”, with homes priced above ₹1 crore making up half of them. The two consultancies count differently (ANAROCK includes more of the budget segment and the MMR periphery; Knight Frank uses registrations and its own surveys), so their totals should never be mixed in one series. This report uses ANAROCK for the long history because its series is the longest.

The shape of that chart is the shape of the last cycle. Sales peaked at 3.43 lakh in 2014, fell for three years as a glut of delayed projects in NCR and Mumbai met high interest rates, then took three regime shocks: demonetisation in November 2016, RERA in May 2017 and GST in July 2017. The IL&FS and DHFL defaults of 2018–19 cut off the NBFC money that smaller developers relied on for land and construction. Covid took 2020 sales to 1.38 lakh. Then record-low rates, a stamp-duty cut in Maharashtra and the work-from-home demand for bigger homes started the upcycle: 2.37 lakh in 2021, 3.65 lakh in 2022 (above the 2014 peak) and a record 4.77 lakh in 2023.

Since then volume has slipped. Units fell 4% in 2024 and 14% in 2025, and the first nine months of 2026 came to about 2.93 lakh homes (Q1 about 1,01,675; Q2 90,715, down 6% as the West Asia conflict and anxiety about IT jobs kept buyers waiting; Q3 1,00,220, up 3%). On that run-rate 2026 will land around 3.9–4.0 lakh units, flat on 2025 (an indicative estimate). But value tells a different story.

Between 2022 and 2025 the value of homes sold grew at roughly 22% a year while the number of homes grew at about 3%. The difference is price and mix. ANAROCK’s average price across the seven cities went from ₹6,150 per sq ft in 2022 to ₹9,260 in 2025, up 51%, and to about ₹9,700 in Q3 2026. (The 7% year-on-year rise in Q3 2026 is confirmed; the exact ₹9,714 figure appears only in press summaries.) Part of that is genuine price inflation in a rising market and part is mix: developers launched more large, expensive homes and fewer small ones, so the average rose even where like-for-like prices did not.

Two cautions apply to that price series. First, it measures what developers are asking on new launches, which skews towards premium neighbourhoods. The RBI’s house price index, which uses registered transactions across 18 cities, rose only 4.2% in the year to Q4 FY26 and 3.6% in Q1 FY27. For “how fast are Indian house prices rising”, the RBI series is the better answer; for “what are developers getting on new projects”, ANAROCK is. Second, the price momentum is fading: +21% in 2024, +8% in 2025, +7% year-on-year and only +1% quarter-on-quarter by Q3 2026.

Forward estimates are modest. ICRA expects the area sold in seven cities to grow 2–5% in FY27 and sales value 8–11%, with launches up 4–7%. Promotional aggregates such as “real estate is 7% of GDP” or “a US$1 trillion market by 2030” come from industry bodies and are not verified here; they are best ignored for analysis.

For the listed companies, the important point is that their growth has decoupled from the market’s. Listed bookings grew about 30% a year from FY23 to FY26 (indicative, and flattered by wider coverage) while market units went sideways. A listed developer in 2026 is therefore a bet on two things: share gain within a flat market, and its ability to keep launching dearer products that buyers accept. The first is structural and continuing; the second is cyclical and, as Section 12 shows, slowing.

In plain terms

Indian cities are not selling more homes than in 2023. They are selling bigger, more expensive ones. That has been great for developers’ revenue per project, but a market that grows only by raising the ticket size eventually runs into the limits of how many families can afford it.

03

Who buys, and why: wealth, GCC salaries and the EMI

Demand in this cycle came from wealthier households, white-collar hiring and cheap mortgages. Two of those three have weakened in 2026.

Housing demand in India’s big cities rests on three supports: what buyers earn, what the home costs, and what the mortgage costs. The 2021–24 boom began with all three lined up. Prices had been flat in nominal terms, and so falling in real terms, from 2014 to 2020. The RBI held its repo rate at 4.0% from May 2020 to May 2022, which took the best home-loan rates to roughly 6.7–7.0%. And incomes in the IT, start-up and financial sectors were rising fast. Affordability, measured as the monthly EMI against household income, was at a two-decade best.

Rates went up by 250 basis points from May 2022 to February 2023 and demand held, because buyers were still catching up on years of deferred purchases and prices had not yet run ahead. The 2025 cuts (125 basis points to 5.25%) should have been a tailwind but arrived as volumes were already falling, suggesting price rather than rates had become the constraint. The October 2026 hike to 5.50%, with the RBI shifting to what it called “calibrated tightening”, is the first rate headwind in three and a half years. Most home loans are now linked to an external benchmark such as the repo, so a hike passes through to EMIs within a quarter. The effect is largest on the ₹50 lakh–₹1.5 crore buyer who borrows most of the price, and smallest on the luxury buyer who pays largely from savings.

The rich are buying, the rest are not

The defining demand feature of this cycle is premiumisation. In Knight Frank’s eight cities, homes above ₹1 crore went from half of sales in 2025 to 55% in the first nine months of 2026, while homes below ₹50 lakh fell to 18%, down from about 37% in 2022. ANAROCK’s numbers tell the same story over a longer span: homes below ₹40 lakh fell from 38% of top-seven sales in 2019 to 18% in 2024, while homes above ₹1.5 crore rose from 7% to 26% (figures from search summaries of ANAROCK releases).

Several forces drove it. Equity-market wealth spread through demat accounts, IPO allotments and employee stock options. Covid made households want larger homes with a room to work from. Start-up and GCC salaries put a cohort of young professionals into the ₹1–2 crore bracket. A weak rupee made Indian property cheaper for non-resident Indians, whom developers say account for a meaningful slice of luxury sales in Mumbai, Bengaluru and Kerala (DLF said NRIs bought 25% of The Aureva). And developers chased the segment because land and approval costs are fixed per square foot, so a luxury unit earns far more absolute margin than a budget one on the same plot.

The other side of premiumisation is a shrinking addressable market. When affordable homes are only 6% of new launches (ANAROCK, Q2 2026), the industry is selling to the top slice of urban households. That slice is more sensitive to the stock market than to the EMI, which is why a sharp equity correction is a bigger threat to listed developers than a 25 basis point rate hike. It also explains why luxury cooled first where it ran hottest: Knight Frank counted NCR sales down 11% in the first nine months of 2026, with the ₹5–10 crore band down 39%.

Jobs, cities and households

White-collar employment is the other pillar. Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune and Chennai are IT cities, and global capability centres, the in-house technology and operations hubs of multinationals, are now the largest single source of new office jobs, taking about 42% of office leasing in the first nine months of 2026. The same sector is the current worry: ANAROCK explicitly cited IT layoffs and uncertainty about AI’s effect on jobs for the 2025 volume decline and the wait-and-watch mood of Q2 2026.

Underneath sit slower, structural forces. India’s urban population was about 36% of the total in 2021 and is expected to reach about 40% by 2030. The official urban housing shortage, estimated at 18.8 million units in 2012 and about 10 million in later estimates, is overwhelmingly in economically weaker and low-income households that listed developers do not serve. PMAY-Urban 2.0, approved in 2024 with ₹2.30 lakh crore of central assistance for one crore homes, supports that segment, not the listed developers’ core market. The shortage is real, but it is not the demand these companies are selling to.

Investors are the swing buyers. Plotted land and luxury launches in Gurugram, Goa, Alibaug and Ayodhya drew speculative money in 2023–25, and launch-day sell-outs were partly investor-driven. The July 2024 change to long-term capital gains tax on property (12.5% without indexation, with an option for older assets held by resident individuals) made property slightly less attractive as an investment. If investors retreat, the most exposed are the markets where they were most active, again Gurugram above all.

In plain terms

The buyers who powered this cycle were mostly affluent households spending equity-market gains and high tech salaries, not first-time buyers stretching for a mortgage. That makes demand less sensitive to interest rates than in the past, but more sensitive to the stock market and to hiring in IT and GCCs.

04

Five cities, five cycles

India does not have one housing market. Mumbai, Gurugram, Bengaluru, Pune and Hyderabad run on different buyers, different land rules and different inventory clocks.

Almost every national number in this report is an average of five quite different markets. The Mumbai Metropolitan Region (MMR) is the largest and most expensive; NCR is a speculative, plotted-and-luxury market centred on Gurugram; Bengaluru and Hyderabad are end-user IT markets; Pune is the volume mid-market. Each has its own cycle, and a developer’s results depend heavily on which of them it sells in. In 2025 MMR took about 32% of top-seven-city units, Pune 16.5%, Bengaluru 16%, NCR 14.5% and Hyderabad 11%; Chennai and Kolkata together under 10%.

Mumbai Metropolitan Region: expensive, approval-bound, redevelopment-led

MMR sold about 1,27,875 homes in 2025 at an average of roughly ₹17,000 per sq ft (₹17,230 in Q3 2025 per ANAROCK summaries), close to double the seven-city average. Land in the island city is scarce, so much new supply comes from redeveloping old housing societies, cessed buildings and slums under Mumbai’s development-control rules (the 33(7), 33(9) and 33(11) schemes), with the developer rehousing existing residents for free and selling the surplus. That makes MMR approval-heavy and slow, with high premiums paid to the state for extra FSI. It is home to Lodha, Oberoi, Sunteck, Sri Lotus, Valor and much of Godrej and ABREL. MMR units fell 18% in 2025, the steepest decline among the big cities, then rose 5% year on year in Q3 2026. It also holds the largest unsold stock, about 1.79 lakh homes at end-2025, though that has been stable.

NCR: plotted land, luxury towers and investor money

NCR, and within it Gurugram, was the star of 2023–25. Plotted colonies under Haryana’s DDJAY scheme, independent floors and luxury towers sold out on launch day; DLF’s Privana and The Dahlias, Signatureglobal’s projects and unlisted M3M led the market. ANAROCK recorded a 23% rise in NCR prices in 2025, to about ₹9,300 per sq ft, the fastest of any city. The cooling is now visible: Knight Frank’s NCR sales fell 11% in the first nine months of 2026, the ₹5–10 crore band fell 39%, and Gurugram holds 57% of NCR’s unsold stock. DLF, Signatureglobal, TARC, Anant Raj, Godrej and ABREL (46% of its FY26 bookings) are all exposed; Oberoi’s ₹8,109 crore Three Sixty North launch shows the deepest pockets are still buying.

Bengaluru: the end-user IT market with a supply problem

Bengaluru is the market where most buyers intend to live in what they buy, which historically made it the steadiest. Its share rose from 13% of units in 2023 to about 17% in Q3 2026. But supply has outrun sales: unsold homes rose 23% in 2025 to about 64,860 and then 34% year on year to about 79,180 by Q2 2026. Prestige, Brigade, Sobha, Puravankara and Embassy Developments are Bengaluru companies; Godrej booked ₹8,802 crore there in FY26. Approval delays in the city pushed ₹6,500–8,500 crore of Prestige’s planned bookings out of Q1 FY27.

Pune and Hyderabad: volume, and the most inventory

Pune is the volume mid-market: about 65,135 homes in 2025, down 20%, and down a further 6% in Q3 2026, with prices around ₹7,900 per sq ft. Godrej and Lodha are the large listed sellers. Hyderabad is the paradox. It has the highest months of inventory among the seven cities, about 28 months in Q3 2026 against a seven-city average of about 19, yet its sales rose 15% in Q3 2026 and its launches jumped 53% in Q2. Prestige booked 49% of its Q1 FY27 sales in Hyderabad, and Brigade, Godrej and Embassy are expanding there. Large unlisted local developers (My Home, Aparna, Rajapushpa) dominate the market.

In plain terms

Out of every ₹113 a buyer pays for a new flat, roughly ₹25 goes to government, about ₹22 to whoever owned the land, about ₹35 to the people who build it, and the developer keeps around ₹17. Land and approvals vary wildly from project to project; construction costs much the same for everyone. So the developer’s skill shows up in what it paid for the land and how fast it got permission to build.

Pre-sales are not revenue: the five-year lag in every developer’s accounts Deep dive

Under Ind AS 115, most Indian developers book revenue only at handover. Bookings, collections and cash are the live picture; the profit and loss account is a delayed echo of land bought years earlier.

Since 1 April 2018, Indian companies have recognised revenue under Ind AS 115, the local version of the global standard IFRS 15. A seller may recognise revenue “over time”, as it performs, only if one of a few tests is met. For an apartment the relevant test is whether the asset has no alternative use to the developer and the developer has an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date. Because Indian sale agreements and RERA allow buyers to cancel with limited forfeiture, most large developers (DLF, Godrej, Lodha, Prestige, Oberoi, Brigade, Sobha and Signatureglobal among them) concluded they do not have that enforceable right. They therefore recognise revenue at a point in time: when the project receives its occupancy certificate and the unit is handed over. In effect, Indian residential developers report on a completed-contract basis.

The switch was not cosmetic. Before 2018 many developers used the percentage-of-completion method (POCM), recognising revenue and profit as construction progressed. On transition they reversed revenue already recognised on unfinished projects into opening retained earnings, a large one-time hit to reported equity for several companies, and their reported revenue has lagged their sales ever since.

Why the lag distorts almost every standard ratio

  • Margins describe old land. FY26 revenue reflects homes mostly sold in FY21–FY23 on land bought in FY17–FY21. A developer that overpaid for land in 2025 will not show it in reported margins until about FY29–FY31.
  • Quarterly results swing for mechanical reasons. Godrej’s Q1 FY27 profit fell 42% because it delivered only 0.9 million sq ft against 7.4 million sq ft in Q4 FY26. Lodha’s Q1 FY27 profit doubled, helped by about ₹1,200 crore of land sales contributing roughly ₹600 crore of profit. Brigade’s Q1 EBITDA margin jumped from 28% to 36% because higher-margin projects happened to complete. Prestige’s quarterly EBITDA margin has swung between about 26% and 37%.
  • P/E and EV/EBITDA lose meaning. DLF trades at 38× trailing earnings and about 49× EBITDA, but most of its profit comes from its 66.7% share of the DCCDL rental JV, and its housing profits are timed by completions. Signatureglobal’s FY26 profit of ₹1,095 crore is almost entirely a one-off gain from selling half of a commercial subsidiary to RMZ.
  • The balance sheet grows before the P&L. In an upcycle, customer advances (contract liabilities) and work-in-progress inventory balloon. Rising inventory is normally a warning sign in other industries; for a developer selling well, it is the opposite.
  • “Operating cash flow” means different things. Godrej’s statutory cash flow from operations was −₹2,003 crore in FY26 because land and inventory purchases sit inside it, while its own “operating cash flow”, which excludes land and business-development spend, was +₹7,830 crore. Both are correct on their own definitions; Section 14 explains which to use for what.

Plotted developments are the exception that proves the rule. Plots need only roads, drains and utilities, so they can be delivered in two to three years and recognised faster; this is one reason plotted launches in NCR and around Bengaluru became so popular with developers in 2023–25. Commercial strata sales and land sales (as at Lodha) can also be recognised sooner.

The practical consequence for anyone reading these companies is a reordering of the financial statements. Read the operating update first (bookings, area sold, realisation, collections), then the cash flow and net debt, then the disclosures of unrecognised revenue, cost to complete and surplus cash from sold inventory, and only then the profit and loss account. DLF publishes the cleanest version of this bridge.

In plain terms

When a developer reports a big jump in profit, the first question is “which old projects were handed over this quarter?”, not “is business booming?”. Consolidation: how the listed share doubled in six years

Thousands of small developers still exist, but the regime change of 2016–2020 tilted the field towards brands with balance sheets. That shift can continue even if the market stays flat.

Indian housing remains one of the most fragmented industries in the economy. By June 2022 state RERA authorities had registered about 84,500 projects; a widely quoted but unverified tally at the launch of the Unified RERA Portal in September 2025 put the figure at about 1.51 lakh. Most developers are single-city, often single-project companies. Yet the share of the market held by large listed developers has risen faster than in almost any other Indian industry over the past six years.

The arithmetic is approximate but the direction is not in doubt. The 28 listed developers booked about ₹1.95 lakh crore in FY26, against top-seven-city sales of more than ₹6 lakh crore in CY2025, which puts the listed share at an upper bound of 30–33%. Because listed bookings also include tier-2 cities, plotted land and commercial sales outside ANAROCK’s frame, the like-for-like share is perhaps 25–30%. Around FY20, listed bookings of roughly ₹30,000–35,000 crore against a market of about ₹2.6 lakh crore implied 10–15%. On any reasonable reading, the listed share has roughly doubled.

Why the big got bigger

Four changes between 2016 and 2020 raised the cost of being small. RERA forced developers to put 70% of buyers’ money into project escrow and draw it only against certified progress, ending the practice of funding the next land purchase with the last project’s advances; that requires real equity, which small developers lacked. Demonetisation and GST pushed cash-funded land deals and unrecorded sales into the open. The NBFC crisis after IL&FS (September 2018) and DHFL (June 2019) cut off the structured land and construction finance on which many mid-sized developers depended. And Ind AS 115 made reported profits lumpier, which mattered less to developers with strong cash flows. Buyers, burned by the Jaypee, Amrapali and Unitech delays, began paying a premium for developers who would finish on time.

A fifth force has kept consolidation going since: landowners prefer large developers. A landowner offered a revenue-share joint development agreement wants a partner who will get approvals, sell fast and survive a downturn, so the JDA flow goes disproportionately to Godrej, Lodha, Prestige and their peers. That is why consolidation is not only a cyclical story: it can continue when the market is flat, as FY26 showed, when listed bookings grew 17% while top-seven-city units fell 14% in CY2025.

The listing wave and the equity that funded it

The listed universe has also widened. Macrotech (now Lodha) listed in April 2021, Signatureglobal in September 2023, Sri Lotus in August 2025; outside this group, Keystone Realtors (Rustomjee), Suraj Estate, Arkade and Kalpataru also came to market. Equity matters because banks are not allowed to lend to developers for land purchase under RBI rules on commercial real estate. Equity raised in a strong market became land that will be launched in a weaker one.

Large unlisted developers still matter in every city, and they are often the price-setters: M3M, Smartworld, Elan and Whiteland in Gurugram; Hiranandani, Runwal, Piramal Realty and Adani Realty in Mumbai; Tata Housing and Shapoorji Pallonji nationally; Sattva, Salarpuria and Total Environment in Bengaluru; My Home, Aparna and Rajapushpa in Hyderabad; Casagrand in Chennai. Foreign capital prefers platforms and joint ventures (Brookfield, Blackstone, GIC, CPP Investments, ADIA, Mitsui Fudosan, Mitsubishi Estate) to owning for-sale developers outright.

In plain terms

Rules introduced between 2016 and 2020 made it expensive to be a small developer: buyers’ money must sit in escrow, land must be bought with equity, and lenders who used to fund small builders went bust. Big, listed names with cash and a delivery record picked up the business. That shift does not need a booming market to continue, which is why listed developers grew 17% in a year when the big-city market sold 14% fewer homes.

Nine business models under one label

A Gurugram land bank, a Mumbai redevelopment boutique, a mall owner and a government land company share an NSE classification and almost nothing else. Their margins, returns and risks differ by design.

Return on equity in for-sale housing can be broken into three parts: the margin earned on each project, the speed at which capital turns over (how quickly land becomes cash), and the leverage used. Every developer chooses a point on that triangle, usually without saying so. A legacy-land developer earns very high margins on land bought decades ago but turns its capital slowly, because approvals and launches pace how fast the land can be used. A business-development “machine” earns lower margins on land bought at market prices but can compound if it turns that land into cash within three to five years and keeps replenishing it. An annuity owner earns steady rent at 60–90% EBITDA margins, trading growth for predictability, and is valued on rent and capitalisation rates rather than bookings.

The practical lesson is to match the valuation tool to the model. For-sale developers are best valued on the net present value of their land and projects (NAV) or on multiples of bookings and embedded profit. Annuity businesses are valued on net operating income capitalised at a market yield, or on EV/EBITDA. Land-monetisation companies are valued per acre, discounted for title, encroachment and time. A sum-of-the-parts approach is usually necessary for the hybrids: DLF (housing plus its DCCDL stake), Prestige (housing, offices, malls, hotels), Brigade, Oberoi and Anant Raj (housing plus a data-centre business it plans to demerge).

In plain terms

Two developers can both report a 30% margin and be completely different businesses: one may own its land outright and need years to use it, the other may share revenue with a landowner and turn its money every three years. To compare them, ask how much capital each ties up and how fast it comes back, not just what margin each earns.

Margin is made the day the land is bought Deep dive

Land is the largest and most variable cost in a project. The 2024–26 land-buying race will decide the margins of FY29–FY31, and joint development has changed what a land bank is worth.

Construction costs roughly the same per square foot for every developer in a city; marketing and overheads are a few rupees in a hundred. Land is different. It can be 15% of a project’s revenue or 45%, and the difference is decided by when and how it was acquired. A developer that bought Gurugram land in the 1990s, or Mumbai mill land in the 2000s, or Bengaluru plots at the 2019 trough, earns margins in 2026 that no newcomer can match. That is why the industry calls its land-acquisition teams “business development” (BD), and why BD announcements move share prices as much as bookings do.

The 2024–26 land race

The upcycle triggered the largest land-buying spree in a decade. ANAROCK tracked 133 land deals covering about 2,514 acres in 2024, then about 2,898 acres in the first half of 2025 alone, worth about ₹30,885 crore with roughly ₹1.47 lakh crore of revenue potential, and more than 3,772 acres across at least 126 deals for 2025 as a whole (these figures come from search summaries of ANAROCK releases and are approximate). Listed developers were the main buyers, funded by the QIPs and rights issues of 2024–25.

Redevelopment: Mumbai’s version of the land bank

In Mumbai the dominant land route is society redevelopment. A developer agrees with an old housing society to demolish its building and rebuild it, giving existing members larger new flats free of cost, paying their rent during construction and often a cash corpus. In return it uses the extra floor space allowed under the redevelopment rules (after paying premiums to the state) to build and sell additional homes. The model is land-light but cash-hungry early: rent and rehousing costs are paid long before the sale homes are sold. Sri Lotus’s statutory operating cash flow was −₹326 crore in FY26 despite a 37% operating margin for exactly this reason. ABREL (a ₹2,600 crore Vashi redevelopment), Puravankara (Malabar Hill and Chembur), Sunteck and Lodha all use the model.

Where the risk now sits

Margin is decided at land purchase, so the risk of the current cycle has shifted from demand to the price paid for land. Land bought in 2025, at record volumes and after a 58% rise in launch prices, will be launched in FY27–FY28 and recognised in the profit and loss account around FY29–FY31. If prices flatten, as the RBI index and slowing consultant data suggest, embedded margins on that land will be thinner than on the land being recognised today. The visible near-term cost is debt: Godrej’s net debt went from ₹3,269 crore (March 2025) to ₹7,637 crore (June 2026), Puravankara’s rose by ₹515 crore in one quarter on ₹574 crore of land payments, Sunteck swung from ₹125 crore net cash to ₹295 crore net debt after ₹813 crore of BD spend, and Signatureglobal’s net debt rose from ₹200 crore to ₹450 crore after Farrukhnagar.

In plain terms

A developer’s future profit margin is mostly fixed on the day it signs for the land. Buy cheaply and even an average project makes money; overpay and a well-built, well-sold project can still disappoint. Indian developers bought a record amount of land in 2025, after prices had already risen sharply. Whether those purchases were smart will only show up in profits around 2029–31.

Joint development lets a developer use someone else’s land and pay the owner from sales. It earns less profit per project but ties up far less money, so it can do many more projects. That is how a company can top the bookings table without owning the largest land bank.

The rulebook: RERA’s escrow, Ind AS 115, GST and stamp duty

Regulation turned Indian housing from a cash business with few protections into a formal, escrowed one. It also handed a structural advantage to developers with balance sheets.

No Indian industry was reshaped by regulation as quickly as residential real estate between 2016 and 2019. Five sets of rules matter for analysing the listed developers today: RERA, which governs how a project is sold and funded; Ind AS 115, which governs when revenue is recognised (Section 06); the GST regime for housing; state stamp duties and land rules; and the banking rules on what lenders may finance. A sixth, the SEBI REIT regulations, governs the main exit for rental assets.

RERA: the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016

RERA was passed in March 2016 and came fully into force on 1 May 2017. Every project above 500 square metres or eight apartments must be registered with the state’s real estate regulator before it is advertised or a single booking is taken. Homes must be sold on carpet area, the usable floor area inside the walls, ending the old practice of quoting “super built-up” area inflated by a loading of 30–45% for lobbies and walls. A developer may not take more than 10% of the price before a registered agreement for sale. It carries a five-year defect liability after handover, must refund with interest if it delays, and must post quarterly progress updates on the regulator’s website. Penalties run up to 10% of project cost.

The most important clause is the escrow rule in Section 4(2)(l)(D): 70% of the money collected from buyers must be deposited in a separate bank account for that project and used only for its land and construction costs, with withdrawals in proportion to completion certified by an engineer, an architect and a chartered accountant. Before RERA, a developer could use advances from one project to buy land for the next, which is how so many projects stalled when sales slowed. After RERA, each project must largely fund itself, and the developer needs its own equity for land. That single rule raised working-capital needs across the industry and is the main reason consolidation accelerated. It is also why DLF’s ₹15,200 crore of net cash comes with a caveat: about ₹11,305 crore of it sits in RERA escrow accounts and cannot be freely redeployed.

Implementation is uneven. Housing is a state subject, some states diluted the model rules, and enforcing refund orders against defaulting developers through revenue recovery has been slow. A Unified RERA Portal was launched in September 2025 to bring state data together.

GST and income tax

Since 1 April 2019, under-construction homes outside the affordable category carry 5% GST with no input tax credit for the developer, and affordable homes (carpet area up to 60 square metres in metros or 90 elsewhere, and price up to ₹45 lakh) carry 1%. One third of the price is deemed to be land and excluded, so the effective rate on the construction element is 7.5%. Completed homes sold after the occupancy certificate carry no GST, and commercial under-construction property carries 12% with input credit. Because developers cannot reclaim GST on their inputs, a cut in GST on cement (to 18% from 28% in September 2025) lowers their costs only by the tax saved, about 1% of construction cost.

For buyers, the main tax supports are the ₹2 lakh deduction for home-loan interest on a self-occupied home (old tax regime) and the principal repayment allowance. For investors, the July 2024 Budget changed long-term capital gains on property to 12.5% without indexation, with resident individuals allowed to choose the old 20%-with-indexation rule for property bought before 23 July 2024. The reinvestment exemption under Sections 54 and 54F has been capped at ₹10 crore since FY24, which matters at the luxury end. A 1% TDS applies to purchases of ₹50 lakh and above.

Stamp duty, land and approvals

Stamp duty is set by each state and is the single largest transaction cost a buyer sees. Maharashtra’s temporary cut to 2% (September–December 2020) and 3% (January–March 2021) produced the Mumbai sales surge that began the upcycle. Land rules are equally local: Maharashtra’s DCPR 2034 and UDCPR 2020 govern Mumbai redevelopment and premium FSI; Haryana’s DDJAY scheme and affordable-housing policy created Gurugram’s plotted and affordable segments that Signatureglobal built its business on; Karnataka’s khata regularisation and Telangana’s TS-bPASS approval system shape Bengaluru and Hyderabad. The 2013 land acquisition law (RFCTLARR), which requires compensation of two to four times market value, made assembling large parcels by compulsory purchase far more expensive.

Finance, insolvency and stressed projects

Banks may lend for construction and against completed, rented assets (lease rental discounting, or LRD) but not for land purchase, and commercial real estate carries higher risk weights. That leaves land to be funded by equity, NBFCs, alternative investment funds or customers. The government’s SWAMIH fund (₹25,000 crore, November 2019) provided last-mile money for stalled projects and has completed more than 50,000 homes; SWAMIH-2 (₹15,000 crore) was announced in the 2025 Budget.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code gave homebuyers real power. The Supreme Court’s Pioneer Urban judgment (August 2019) upheld homebuyers’ status as financial creditors; a 2020 amendment required at least 100 buyers or 10% of a project to file; and the Supertech case (2022) established a project-by-project approach to insolvency. The code still reaches listed developers: in December 2025 the NCLT in Delhi admitted Canara Bank’s petition against Embassy Developments over an alleged Indiabulls-era guarantee, before the appellate tribunal set it aside on 4 May 2026.

Litigation and environmental challenges are a live operating risk. In July 2026 the Punjab & Haryana High Court restrained fresh allotments at Oberoi’s Three Sixty North pending a licensing dispute (Oberoi says existing sales and construction are unaffected; the status after 25 September is unverified). Brigade’s Morgan Heights project in Chennai lost its environmental clearance over a marshland dispute and buyers were refunded. DLF has capped its Goa residential plans at “a couple of thousand crores” because of public-interest litigation.

In plain terms

RERA’s core rule is simple: most of the money you pay a developer for your flat has to be spent on your flat. That stopped the old habit of using one project’s customers to fund the next project’s land, which is what left thousands of buyers stranded in the 2010s. The price of that protection is that developers now need far more of their own money, which smaller builders often do not have.

Four decades, three busts: the cycle in dates

Indian housing has run on ten-to-twelve-year cycles. Each bust was caused by leverage and each recovery by a change in rules or rates.

The listed developers’ history explains their present behaviour. The 2005–08 boom created most of today’s listed names; the 2008 crash and the 2010–13 stagflation destroyed the over-leveraged ones; the 2014–20 winter, together with RERA and the NBFC crisis, removed thousands of small competitors; and the post-Covid upcycle rewarded those left standing. Developers that survived two busts now run with low leverage almost by instinct, which is one reason this cycle’s late phase looks different from 2013.

Late cycle, not end of cycle: reading the 2026 plateau Deep dive

Volumes peaked in 2023, prices are slowing, inventory is rising and the RBI has started hiking. The data point to a plateau, with launch timing doing most of the damage to quarterly numbers.

Every cycle has a moment when the headline numbers stop agreeing with each other, and Indian housing reached it in 2026. Units sold in the top seven cities are flat; value is still rising in single digits; listed developers grew 17% in FY26 and then reported a 21% fall in Q1 FY27; unsold inventory is at its highest in four years; and the RBI has just raised rates. This section sorts the signals into what is structural, what is cyclical and what is merely the launch calendar.

Supply has run ahead of sales

The clearest late-cycle signal is supply. Knight Frank reports that launches have exceeded sales for 16 consecutive quarters across its eight cities. ANAROCK’s data show the same turn: launches were below sales every year from 2022 to 2024 as developers sold down inventory, then crossed above sales in 2025 and widened the gap in 2026. In Q3 2026 developers launched about 1,14,300 homes (up 18% year on year) against sales of about 1,00,200, and unsold stock rose to about 6.31 lakh, up 12% from a year earlier.

Rates and the macro turn

The 7 October hike matters less for its size than for its direction. After 125 basis points of cuts in 2025, the RBI raised the repo to 5.50%, projected FY27 inflation at 5.2%, assumed crude at about $95 a barrel for the second half and called its stance “calibrated tightening”. Home-loan rates will rise through external-benchmark resets. A second hike at the December meeting would hit the sub-₹1.5 crore buyer most; luxury buyers are more exposed to equity markets than to EMIs. The West Asia conflict also affects NRI demand through remittances and sentiment, and Lodha blamed “the Iran-war geopolitical shock” for March 2026 deferrals that left it ₹470 crore short of its FY26 guidance.

The verdict

Taken together, the signals describe a mature plateau. Three things distinguish it from the 2013–14 top that preceded six lean years. Leverage is low: the 19 members’ company-reported net debt sums to ₹27,007 crore against a combined market value of ₹6.37 lakh crore, and most large caps run below 0.5 times equity. The overhang is in the right places: unsold stock is concentrated in luxury and in IT-city suburbs, not in stalled, debt-funded NCR projects. RERA escrow contains contagion: a weak project cannot drain cash from a healthy one. The downside scenario is a combination of further rate hikes, IT layoffs and an equity correction, producing luxury cancellations and price cuts in NCR and Hyderabad. The upside is rate stability, continued GCC hiring and strong launches that let the top developers take more share. Section 21 sets out the thresholds that would tell the two apart.

In plain terms

The big drop in developers’ bookings in April–June 2026 was mostly about timing: DLF and Prestige were waiting for permission to launch new projects. The genuinely worrying signs are slower: more homes are being built than sold, prices are rising more slowly, and interest rates have started to go up. Those point to a market that has stopped growing, not one that is collapsing, largely because developers today carry far less debt than in the last downturn.

The second engine: offices, malls, REITs and the cap-rate spread Deep dive

While housing volumes stalled, India’s office market set records and REITs became a large, liquid exit. For DLF, Phoenix, Prestige, Brigade, Oberoi and NESCO, rent is a second business valued on different rules.

India is the global outlier in offices. While office markets in the United States and Europe were still digesting post-pandemic vacancy, India leased more office space in 2025 than in any year on record: 82.6 million sq ft according to CBRE, about 89 million sq ft gross according to Cushman & Wakefield. The driver is global capability centres: multinationals building their own technology, analytics and operations hubs in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai and NCR took about 42% of leasing in 2026, roughly 28 million sq ft. CBRE says flexible-workspace operators were the single largest demand driver in Q3 2026, themselves serving many of the same GCC and technology tenants.

How the spread creates value

The economics of a rental asset rest on one spread. A developer that builds a Grade-A office on land it owns spends perhaps ₹9,000 per sq ft all in. Leased at ₹110 per sq ft per month at 92% occupancy, a 1 million sq ft building earns about ₹121 crore of gross rent a year; after maintenance and other costs, net operating income (NOI) is about ₹106 crore, a yield on cost of 11.8%. Investors, whether REITs, pension funds or sovereign funds, will buy that income at a capitalisation rate of roughly 7.5–8.5% for Grade-A offices, so at 8% the building is worth about ₹1,325 crore. The developer has created about ₹425 crore of value, 47% of its cost, before selling a single unit.

The data-centre turn

The newest annuity is powered land for data centres. India’s data-centre capacity is commonly put at 1.3–1.5 GW in 2025 with projections of 3–5 GW by 2030 (industry estimates, not verified here). The risks to the second engine are real but different from housing risks: vacancy if GCC hiring slows or AI changes how many people offices need; oversupply (51 million sq ft of new office supply in nine months, up 18%); SEZ rules that limit domestic tenants; consumption slowdowns for malls; and cap-rate expansion as rates rise. The rewards are equally different: predictable cash, low-cost debt, and a valuation the market sets on NOI rather than on a lumpy bookings line.

In plain terms

A developer that keeps an office tower instead of selling it earns rent every month, and investors will pay about 12 to 13 times that yearly income to own it. If the building cost less than that to build, the developer has made a profit the moment the tenants move in. India’s REITs, now worth more than ₹2 lakh crore, are where many of those buildings end up, which turns a slow-burning rental asset into cash the developer can reinvest.

Twelve measures explain almost everything about a developer. Each has a formula, a worked example, a benchmark and at least one trap that catches people who read only the headline.

Most of the standard financial ratios mislead in this industry, for the reasons set out in Section 06. 4. Collections and collection efficiency

Definition. Cash actually received from buyers in the period. Collection efficiency is the share of amounts billed (demanded under the payment plan) that buyers paid on time. Formulas: collections ÷ pre-sales (a rough conversion ratio); collection efficiency = collections ÷ (opening receivables + billing due in the period). Worked example: DLF collected ₹13,517 crore in FY26 on ₹20,143 crore of bookings, a ratio of 67%, while its collections rose 15% even as bookings fell 5%, because instalments on the huge FY24–FY25 launches were coming due.

Drivers. The payment plan (construction-linked plans spread payments over three to four years; some luxury launches front-load), construction progress (bills are raised against milestones), the growth rate of bookings (fast growth lowers the ratio mechanically) and buyer quality. Benchmarks: good is collections of 75–90% of pre-sales averaged over a cycle and collection efficiency above 95%; average is 60–75% and 85–95%; weak is below 60% and 85%. Few developers disclose collection efficiency itself.

5. Launches, GDV and sell-through

Definition. Launches are new projects or phases opened for sale after RERA registration, measured in area or in gross development value (GDV: saleable area × expected price). Sell-through, or absorption, is the share of launched inventory sold, often reported for the launch quarter. Formula: launch-year bookings ÷ launched GDV; pipeline cover = approved, launch-ready GDV ÷ annual bookings. Worked example: ABREL had booked ₹24,836 crore of the inventory it had launched by June 2026, 78% of the total; Sobha’s Crescent in Gurugram (₹2,200 crore) was about 60% sold in its launch quarter; Signatureglobal sold more than 300 of about 400 released units at its Tonino Lamborghini-branded project; Embassy sold ₹933 crore at Origins Riverine in two weeks.

Drivers. Approvals (the binding constraint in 2026), the size and pricing of each launch, and brand. Benchmarks: at market level, a launch-to-sales ratio of 0.9–1.1 is balanced; for a company, launch GDV of 1.2–1.5 times its bookings target gives room for normal sell-through, and a launch-ready pipeline of at least twice annual bookings is good (one to two times average, below one weak). Launch-day sell-outs of 50–70% indicate pricing power; sustenance sales of at least 40–50% of bookings indicate a business that does not live launch to launch.

Definition. The margin the developer expects to earn on what it has sold, before that margin reaches the profit and loss account. 8. Operating cash flow and surplus cash

Definition. Developers’ own measure of operating cash flow is usually collections minus construction spending, overheads, interest and taxes, before land and business-development payments. 9. Net debt to equity, and net debt to collections

15. Definition traps, collected

  • Gross versus own-share bookings. JDA partners’ and landowners’ shares are included by some companies and excluded by others (Sobha ₹8,136 crore total vs ₹6,706 crore own; Godrej reports total booking value).
  • Development management counted as sales. Embassy’s FY27 guidance includes ₹2,000 crore of DM bookings, and its Q2 FY27 figure includes a single ₹711 crore Juhu home it manages for a fee.
  • Carpet versus saleable area. Mumbai developers quote carpet; others saleable (about 1.3–1.45 times carpet). Realisations are not comparable without converting.
  • Collections that are not customer cash. Rent, land sales, stake-sale proceeds (Signatureglobal’s RMZ ₹1,237 crore) and TARC’s “business cash flow” are sometimes folded in.
  • Three kinds of operating cash flow. Statutory (includes land), company (excludes land), broker variants. Godrej FY26: −₹2,003 crore versus +₹7,830 crore.
  • Operating profit that excludes the business. Screener’s operating profit excludes other income, so it is negative for Godrej (JV income in other income) and Signatureglobal, and understates DLF (DCCDL profits below the line).
  • Profit including minorities and one-offs. Screener PAT includes minority interest (Phoenix owners’ profit ₹1,208 crore vs ₹1,557 crore; Prestige ~₹1,195 crore vs ₹1,305 crore) and one-offs (Signatureglobal’s ~₹1,293 crore RMZ gain; Lodha’s land sales).
  • Net debt that hides debt or traps cash. Payable-when-able NCDs (Puravankara), shareholder loans (Embassy), corporate guarantees (Prestige), escrowed cash (DLF), and screener’s missing fixed deposits (Brigade, Sobha, Sri Lotus).
  • EBITDA definitions. Company EBITDA includes other income at Brigade, Sobha and Embassy and excludes it at Sri Lotus; Lodha and Signatureglobal add back finance cost included in cost of sales (“adjusted EBITDA”).
  • GDV at whose price and whose share. Pipelines can include unapproved phases, overseas projects (Sunteck’s Dubai) and the landowner’s share.
  • Restated history. Puravankara now reports FY25 bookings of ₹4,783 crore, against ~₹5,006 crore originally, without explaining the change. Growth rates depend on which base is used.
  • Guidance labels. TARC’s ₹1,600–1,800 crore FY27 figure is cash-flow guidance, sometimes reported in the press as revenue.

16. How to read a developer’s quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Start with the operating update, not the P&L. Bookings value, area or units, and realisation per sq ft, against the same quarter last year and against full-year guidance. Note what was launched; a big launch or a missing one explains most quarterly swings.
  2. Split bookings into launch and sustenance. If more than half came from one new project, the growth rate tells you about the calendar, not the business.
  3. Check collections. Compare with the previous quarter and with bookings of a year or two earlier. Falling collections while bookings rise needs an explanation.
  4. Read the cash-flow slide. Operating cash surplus before land, then land and BD spend, then capex. Did the business fund its own land buying this quarter?
  5. Reconcile net debt. Opening net debt, plus land and capex, minus operating surplus, should equal closing net debt. Check the definition (escrow cash, shareholder loans, payable-when-able instruments).
  6. Look at BD additions and the launch pipeline. GDV added this quarter, and how much of the year’s launch plan has approvals. This is the leading indicator for the next four quarters.
  7. Only now read the P&L, through deliveries. How much area was handed over? A profit jump or collapse that matches deliveries is mechanical. Strip out land sales, stake-sale gains and other one-offs.
  8. Check the embedded-margin and backlog disclosures. Unrecognised revenue, cost to complete and surplus cash: are they rising with bookings, and is the margin on recent sales holding?
  9. For annuity businesses, check occupancy and rent. Leased area, occupancy, rental growth, exit-rental guidance and any delays to completions.
  10. Finish with the guidance and the call. Is full-year guidance reiterated, raised or quietly re-phased? Listen for approvals, price cuts dressed up as payment-plan offers, and cancellations.
In plain terms

If you have ten minutes with a developer’s quarterly results, spend eight on bookings, collections, cash flow and debt, and two on the profit and loss account. The profit is real, but it describes the past; the other numbers tell you whether the business is healthy today.

How to read it

Start with the last three columns, not the first three. Bookings and their growth are the live measure of a for-sale developer; net debt shows how that growth is being funded. The 14 members that report bookings sold ₹1,59,986 crore in FY26, up 17%, and ₹32,688 crore in Q1 FY27, with the decline concentrated at DLF and Prestige for the launch-timing reasons explained in Section 12. Five members (Phoenix, NESCO, Anant Raj, Valor and Hemisphere) do not report comparable bookings because they are landlords or land-monetisation stories, so their rows should be read on revenue, operating profit and net debt.

Treat revenue and profit as lagging indicators. Combined revenue rose 22% to ₹79,733 crore, but that mostly reflects homes sold around FY21–FY23 now being handed over: Prestige added ₹5,336 crore and Puravankara ₹1,726 crore of revenue as earlier bookings completed. ABREL’s revenue fell 67% because FY25 had large handovers and FY26 few; TARC’s rose almost tenfold because its first Delhi project began recognising. None of these changes says much about the current state of demand.

Do not read the operating-profit column as EBITDA. Screener’s operating profit excludes other income. That makes Godrej (−₹453 crore) and Signatureglobal (−₹47 crore) look loss-making at the operating level, when Godrej’s joint-venture income and Signatureglobal’s finance-cost accounting sit below that line. It also understates DLF, whose rental profits come through its DCCDL joint venture. The combined 24% operating margin is a floor for the group, not a description of it.

Adjust the profit column before using a P/E. Combined PAT of ₹17,591 crore includes minority interests (material at Phoenix and Prestige) and Signatureglobal’s one-off RMZ gain of about ₹1,293 crore; excluding that gain, profit is about ₹16,300 crore and the combined P/E rises from about 36 to about 39 times. Three members lost money in FY26 (ABREL, Embassy and Hemisphere) and two earned negligible profits (TARC ₹19 crore, Valor ₹27 crore), so P/E is not meaningful for them, and Signatureglobal’s is distorted by its one-off gain.

Read net debt with its date and definition. Gross borrowings of ₹83,427 crore overstate the group’s leverage: company-reported net debt sums to ₹27,007 crore, dominated by Prestige (₹11,900 crore), Godrej (₹7,637 crore), Lodha (₹4,930 crore), Embassy (₹3,732 crore), Phoenix (₹3,658 crore) and ABREL (₹3,438 crore, about nil after the ITC proceeds), and offset by DLF’s ₹15,200 crore of net cash, most of it in RERA escrow. Annuity owners’ debt (Phoenix, Brigade) is largely secured on rented buildings and is lower-risk than land debt.

Who is winning, and why

The winners of this cycle combined three things: a brand landowners trust, a balance sheet that could buy land without strain, and a launch machine that converts approvals into bookings on schedule.

“Winning” in Indian real estate has a specific meaning in 2026. Volume growth is no longer available from the market, so the winners are taking share; price growth is slowing, so they need a pipeline of launches rather than price rises; and land is expensive, so they need the balance sheet or the landowner relationships to keep buying without overreaching. Four positioning maps and a scorecard show who has the combination.

The first map tests whether growth has been earned or borrowed. The encouraging finding is how many members sit in the lower right quadrant, growing with modest leverage: Lodha, Godrej, Sobha, Sunteck and, at the extreme, Sri Lotus. Prestige is the largest company that has bought its growth with debt, at 0.69 times equity on its own measure and the highest borrowing cost among the large caps; it is also the company with the most to gain if its delayed launches arrive in the second half. Puravankara is more stretched again, at 1.57 times equity and an 11.1% cost of debt, with ₹1,410 crore of quasi-equity NCDs outside reported debt. TARC, top left, has the worst combination: bookings down 63% and debt at about 1.7 times equity, with a planned ₹900 crore repayment in FY27 that depends on hitting its cash-flow guidance.

The second map explains why fast growth is not always good news in cash terms. Embassy Developments and Sri Lotus more than doubled their bookings but collected only about a third of them in the year; their cash will come, but it depends on construction keeping pace with the paperwork. Sobha is the standout on this map, combining 30% growth with 87% conversion. Signatureglobal and ABREL sit in the weakest quadrant, with flat or falling bookings and slow conversion, which is why Signatureglobal trades at its 52-week low despite a clean balance sheet and why ABREL’s medium-term ₹15,000 crore bookings target needs its Worli and Navi Mumbai launches to land.

The third map is a warning against using standard return metrics. There is almost no relationship between ROE and price-to-book across the group, for reasons this report has already explained: ROE in FY26 reflects land bought years ago and the timing of handovers, and book value carries that land at historical cost. The market is instead pricing land quality, annuity predictability and confidence in management. Phoenix commands 6.2 times book for a mall business with visible rental growth; Sri Lotus 5.9 times for a land-light model with 21% ROCE; Embassy only 0.8 times because of its losses, legacy legal issues, a 64.9% promoter pledge and slow collections.

The fourth map is the most useful valuation lens for the for-sale developers, and its message is that the market pays for land quality and margins, not for growth. Godrej, the largest booker, trades at only 1.4 times a year’s bookings, reflecting its JV-heavy model (it keeps less of each rupee booked), rising net debt and a share price down about 32% from its 52-week high even as promoters bought ₹2,674 crore of stock. DLF and Oberoi trade at 8–12 times bookings because their land is cheap, their margins are high and both have rental books. Puravankara, at 0.7 times, is valued as the most levered of the group; Sunteck, at its 52-week low, at 1.3 times.
