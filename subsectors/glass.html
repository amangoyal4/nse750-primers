Indian glass: two furnaces, one duty wall

A complete guide to India's two listed glass makers in the NSE 750: Asahi India Glass, which melts float glass and turns most of it into car windshields, and Borosil Renewables, which rolls the low-iron glass that covers solar modules. Both run furnaces that never switch off, both sell a product priced against Chinese imports, and both earn their margins inside a wall of trade remedies. This report explains how that wall works, what the furnace does to their profits, and what to watch as solar-glass capacity triples and the float price floor comes up for renewal.

00

The one-page view: two furnaces, two borders, one rented moat

Two companies that share a technology rather than a market, and earn their margins where trade policy lets them.

The NSE 750 files these two companies under "Construction Materials — Glass - Consumer". The label misleads. Neither company makes tumblers, jars or tableware; that business belongs to other listed names, including Borosil Ltd, a separate company of the same Kheruka group that should not be confused with Borosil Renewables. Asahi India Glass (AIS) makes automotive safety glass (windshields, side and back windows, sunroof glass) and architectural float glass for buildings. Borosil Renewables makes textured, tempered, low-iron solar glass, the sheet that sits on the front (and, on bifacial modules, the back) of a solar panel. One sells to Maruti Suzuki and window fabricators; the other sells to Waaree-type module assemblers. They share a furnace, not a customer.

That shared furnace is the reason to study them together. Both melt sand, soda ash and dolomite at roughly 1,550–1,600°C in a tank that runs 24 hours a day for a decade or more. Both measure capacity in tonnes per day (tpd) of molten glass. Both pay for natural gas, power and soda ash, and both feel every move in those prices. Both sell a product whose price is set, at the margin, by Chinese and South-East Asian imports. And both have spent 2025 and 2026 inside an unusually dense web of Indian trade remedies: a reference-price anti-dumping duty on solar glass from China and Vietnam, a countervailing duty on Malaysian solar glass, and, from 18 August 2026, a minimum import price of ₹34,000 a tonne on clear float glass.

On the screener consolidated basis used throughout this report, the two companies together reported FY26 revenue of ₹6,538 crore (up 7.7%), operating profit of ₹1,350 crore (a 20.6% margin) and reported net profit of ₹472 crore. AIS is the larger by far: 76% of the revenue and 80% of the ₹29,070 crore combined market value. The profit line is distorted by Borosil's German write-offs; before exceptional items the combined FY26 profit was roughly ₹695 crore (an indicative figure). The latest quarter, Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), was much stronger: combined revenue of ₹1,819 crore (+15.4%), operating profit of ₹452 crore at a 24.8% margin against 16.2% a year earlier, and net profit of ₹236 crore against a loss of ₹148 crore. Operating profit grew five times as fast as revenue. That is what full furnaces do.

Five forces explain almost everything that happens to these two companies, and the report is built around them.

  • Trade defence sets the price. Solar glass and clear float glass are both priced at import parity. Borosil's realisation rose from ₹113.4 per mm per sq m in FY25 to ₹146.7 in FY26 after the anti-dumping duty, and its standalone EBITDA margin doubled from 16% to 32% on the same furnaces. AIS's float business now has an origin-neutral price floor that lasts one year. The moat is partly rented, and the lease has dates.
  • The furnace cycle sets the cost. Capacity arrives in 300–1,000 tpd blocks, takes 18–24 months to build, runs for 6–7 years (solar) or 12–15 years (float) before a 90-day cold repair, and cannot be idled. Utilisation drives operating leverage in both directions: AIS's float segment EBIT margin went from 9.5% to 21.5% in a year as its new Soniyana plant filled.
  • The solar build-out outruns domestic glass. India installed about 62 GW (DC) of solar in FY26, which needs roughly 11,000 tpd of solar glass. Domestic capacity was about 2,600 tpd, of which Borosil had 1,000. Borosil itself estimates domestic capacity will reach about 7,700 tpd by March 2027, much of it captive at module makers.
  • Cars and glass content drive AIS. Domestic passenger-vehicle sales hit a record 46.4 lakh in FY26 and rose 30.7% in Q2 FY27 after GST 2.0. AIS claims about 80% of passenger-car glass, and glass per car is rising with SUVs, sunroofs and camera-ready windshields.
  • Energy and soda ash are the swing costs. Power and fuel took 16–19% of AIS's standalone revenue and 22–25% of Borosil's. India's LNG tender price reached $26.4 per MMBtu in September 2026, while soda ash sat near decade lows, with a safeguard-duty decision due around 16 November 2026.

The stock market is not paying the two the same way for these earnings. AIS, at ₹914 on 22 October 2026, trades at about 52 times trailing earnings and is priced as a franchise: a near-monopoly supplier to Indian car makers that has just finished a heavy investment cycle. Borosil, at ₹396, is 45% below its 52-week high of ₹721 and about 35% below where it traded on the day of its Q1 FY27 results; at 14–16 times earnings the market is pricing its record margins as temporary. The stated worries are that domestic solar-glass capacity is about to triple, that the fuel surcharge in its price is unwinding, that Chinese solar glass sells below cash cost, and that imports have simply moved to Malaysia and Vietnam. No single disclosed trigger explains the fall.

In plain terms

Both companies own very large, very hot tanks of molten glass that cannot be switched off. When the tanks are full and the selling price is protected from cheap imports, they print money; when either condition fails, they lose it quickly. In 2026 both conditions held. The investment question is how long the protection lasts, how much new capacity arrives to compete for the same customers, and what energy costs do in between.

01

Sand to windshield, sand to module: how glass makes money

The same melt becomes three very different products. The value is captured downstream of the furnace, or at the border.

All flat glass starts the same way. A batch of silica sand (about 70% of the mix by weight), soda ash (about 20%, and the most expensive raw material), dolomite or limestone and a little sodium sulphate is fed into a refractory-lined furnace along with cullet, which is crushed recycled glass. At AIS, cullet is 25–30% of input; every 10% of cullet saves roughly 2.5–3% of the energy needed to melt the batch. The furnace is fired by natural gas, fuel oil or propane, often with electric boosting, and holds a lake of molten glass at around 1,550–1,600°C. What happens next depends on the product.

Float glass is made by pouring that melt onto a bath of molten tin. The glass floats, spreads into a perfectly flat ribbon, cools in a long annealing oven (the lehr) and is cut into "jumbo" sheets, typically 2–19 mm thick. Clear float is a commodity sold to processors, window fabricators, mirror makers and, in AIS's case, its own automotive plants. Tinted, reflective and coated float (including low-emissivity glass for energy-efficient buildings) earns more.

Solar glass is rolled, not floated. The melt, made from low-iron sand so that it transmits as much sunlight as possible (Borosil claims 91.8% transmission), passes between patterned rollers that press a texture into the surface. It is then cut, tempered (heated and quenched so it is four to five times stronger), given an anti-reflective coating and packed for module makers. Typical thickness is 2.0–3.2 mm. Borosil sells it by area and thickness, quoting its price in rupees per mm of thickness per square metre.

Automotive glass is float glass processed further. A windshield is two plies of float bonded with a polyvinyl butyral (PVB) interlayer, which holds the pieces together when it breaks; the PVB is imported mostly from Eastman, Kuraray and Sekisui and priced in dollars. Side and back windows are usually single-ply tempered glass. Around these sit printing (the black ceramic band), bending, encapsulation in polyurethane, and assembly of modular windows and sunroof glass sets. AIS has laminated capacity of 10 million-plus pieces and tempered capacity of 7.5–7.6 million car sets (FY26 annual report), and it delivers just in time to the car makers' assembly lines.

The ladder says something unusual. In most manufacturing chains, the further you go downstream, the higher the margin, because processing and customer intimacy are where differentiation lives. In Indian glass in 2026, the highest margin sat at the very top of the chain, on a commodity product: Borosil's solar glass earned 35% EBITDA, more than AIS earned processing windshields for car makers. That margin is not the reward for processing skill. It is the gap between a protected Indian price and a Chinese price that is below cash cost, and it is why trade defence gets its own deep dive in section 04.

In plain terms

AIS sells a component that is designed into a car for its whole model life, so once it wins a model it keeps it for five to seven years. Borosil sells a commodity that any module maker can buy from China tomorrow if the price is right. That single difference explains why AIS's margins are steadier and lower and why Borosil's swing from losses to 35% depending on what the government does at the border.

Architectural glass follows a third path. Jumbo float sheets go to thousands of small processors and fabricators, many of them unorganised, who toughen, laminate, double-glaze and cut them for windows, facades, shower cubicles and furniture. Value sits in coated and value-added glass and in distribution reach: AIS reports 1,424-plus stockists and 151 dealers. Since April 2022, BIS certification has been compulsory for glass sold in India, which raised the bar for low-quality imports. The company's own numbers put its share of the domestic architectural market at about 12% and of value-added glass at about 26% (FY26 annual report); CARE Ratings prints 13% and 65% respectively, a conflict the report flags rather than resolves.

02

How big the melt is: three markets, one small country share

India melts about 3% of the world's flat glass and about 3% of China's solar glass. Both numbers are rising fast.

Measuring the Indian glass market is harder than it should be, because no industry body publishes a reliable series. The best available figures are industry estimates quoted in the press, the rating agencies' rationales on individual producers, and trade data. What follows uses those sources and labels every estimate.

Flat (float) glass

Industry estimates put Indian flat-glass demand at about 2.75 million tonnes in 2026, against a "potential" of 3.8 million tonnes (industry estimates quoted by Outlook Business and Angel One, August 2026). That is roughly 7,500 tonnes a day of consumption. At a commodity price of ₹30,000–40,000 a tonne, the float-level market is worth about ₹8,000–11,000 crore a year (indicative). Once the glass is toughened, laminated, coated or turned into car parts, the downstream flat-glass market is perhaps ₹25,000–30,000 crore (indicative; no primary source exists). Per person, India uses about 2 kg of flat glass a year against 10–12 kg in China and a world average of 6–7 kg (indicative industry rules of thumb). Historic volume growth has been 6–8% a year, but capacity arrives in lumps: domestic float capacity grew 43% in FY18–FY19, which crashed prices, and another ~30% (about 3,200 tpd) in FY24–FY25, according to ICRA.

Imports fill the gap between that lumpy domestic capacity and demand. Under the two HS codes that the new minimum import price covers, India imported 44.25 million sq m of clear float worth ₹1,757 crore in FY26 (TradeStat), roughly 0.44–0.55 million tonnes at 10–12.5 kg per sq m (an indicative conversion). That is about 15–20% of consumption. Malaysia and Vietnam supplied about 85% of it.

Automotive glass

Auto glass demand is driven by vehicle production, and production of passenger vehicles in India is roughly domestic sales plus exports. SIAM reported record domestic passenger-vehicle wholesales of 46,43,439 units in FY26 (+7.9%), with exports of 9.05 lakh (+17.5%), so FY26 production was about 5.5 million (an estimate; SIAM does not publish a passenger-vehicle production split). Growth was weak in the first half of FY26 (−1.4%) and strong in the second (+16.7%) after GST 2.0 cut the tax on small cars from 28% plus cess to 18% from 22 September 2025. In H1 FY27, domestic sales were 26,37,067 (about +28%), and Q2 FY27 alone was 13,60,687 (+30.7%), with utility vehicles at 68% of the mix.

A standard car uses about 4–6 sq m of glass, roughly 18–25 kg: a windshield of 1.3–1.6 sq m, a backlite of about 1 sq m, four to six side windows and quarter glass. A panoramic sunroof adds another 1–1.5 sq m of laminated, tinted, high-value glass (all indicative industry rules of thumb). Dividing AIS's auto SBU revenue by the cars it supplies gives roughly ₹6,000–7,000 of AIS glass per Indian passenger vehicle, with premium SUVs carrying two to three times a basic hatchback (indicative). The Indian auto-glass market, including aftermarket and commercial vehicles, is about ₹4,500–5,000 crore (indicative).

Solar glass

India's installed solar capacity reached 150.3 GW at 31 March 2026 and 157 GW by May 2026 (CEA, via Borosil's presentation), after record FY26 additions of about 44.6 GW AC, or 62 GW DC. The government target is 280 GW by 2030. Module manufacturing capacity listed under ALMM List-I has grown from about 11 GW five years ago to roughly 193 GW (1 May 2026) and 203 GW (July 2026, per Borosil's call). Cell capacity is much smaller, about 30 GW, rising to about 75 GW by 2027.

Borosil's rule of thumb is that 62 GW of DC installations needs about 11,000 tpd of solar glass, about 177 tpd per GW once glass-glass bifacial modules (which use two sheets) are counted. That is about 4 million tonnes of melt a year, and at Indian prices of roughly ₹55,000–65,000 per packed tonne it is a ₹16,000–20,000 crore market (indicative). Domestic capacity was about 2,600 tpd. Most of the rest came from abroad: India imported 590.9 million sq m of glass under HS 70071900, the code where solar glass is classified, worth ₹13,149 crore in FY26 (TradeStat). Not all of that code is solar glass, but most of it is.

India's place in the world

World flat-glass output is about 80–90 million tonnes a year, more than half of it in China (an approximate industry figure); India's 2.75 million tonnes is about 3%. In solar glass the asymmetry is starker. China alone had 78,685 tonnes a day of solar-glass melting capacity in operation at the end of June 2026 (SMM), and Borosil's own annual report puts China's share of global solar-glass capacity above 96%. India's 2,600 tpd is about 3% of China's operating capacity, and Borosil's 1,000 tpd is about one seventy-ninth of it. Xinyi Solar, the largest Chinese producer, alone runs 21,400 tpd.

In plain terms

India is a small glass country with big glass needs. It consumes about a third of the world's average glass per person, imports most of its solar glass and a sixth of its float, and sits next to a neighbour that makes about half the world's flat glass and runs thirty times India's solar-glass capacity. Whatever India builds, China's surplus sets the outside price.

03

Where ₹100 goes: a cost structure built around the flame

Energy is the cost that moves, soda ash the one that compounds, and depreciation the one that arrives in lumps.

A glass furnace's cost structure is simple to describe and hard to manage. Raw materials and energy are the two big variable costs, and a large block of fixed costs (labour, refractories, maintenance, depreciation) continues whether the tank is full or not. The two members show the pattern from opposite ends: Borosil is a pure furnace, and AIS is a furnace bolted to a processing business.

Borosil: a pure furnace

Borosil's standalone FY26 accounts (India only, ₹1,534.83 crore of revenue) show the clean shape of a solar-glass maker. Of every ₹100 of revenue, about ₹22.4 went on materials (sand, soda ash, dolomite and packing), ₹21.8 on power and fuel, ₹6.0 on employees and ₹17.7 on other expenses such as stores, refractories, freight, repairs and coating chemicals, with ₹1.6 from a reduction in inventory. That left EBITDA before other income of about ₹30.5, or ₹468 crore. Depreciation took ₹5.7 and finance cost ₹0.9. Management describes the business as having a gross margin of about 78%, meaning raw materials are only 22–24% of sales and do not move much. In Q1 FY27, power and fuel rose to 25.2% of revenue (₹102.1 crore on ₹405.7 crore) as the 2026 energy shock hit, and Borosil passed it on through a temporary surcharge.

AIS: a furnace attached to a factory

AIS's standalone cost lines (₹4,676 crore of FY26 revenue) tell a different and more interesting story, because they show the Soniyana float plant replacing purchased glass. In FY25, raw materials were 37.1% of revenue and power and fuel 15.0%. In FY26, raw materials fell to 29.4% and power and fuel rose to 16.3%. By Q1 FY27 the shift was stark: raw materials 25.9% (from 38.1% a year earlier) and power and fuel 19.1% (from 16.9%). Raw-material spending fell from ₹435.7 crore to ₹341.9 crore even as revenue rose 15%. The reason is that AIS now melts its own glass at Soniyana instead of buying float, so it buys sand and soda ash and burns gas instead of paying another glassmaker's price. Employee cost was 8.7% of FY26 revenue and other expenses 26.4%, leaving about 19% as EBITDA.

The duty wall: who really sets the price of a square metre Deep dive

Solar glass and clear float are both priced at import parity. Since 2024 the government, not the market, has decided what parity is.

Every Indian glass price in this report is, at the margin, a Chinese price plus whatever India adds at the border. Chinese solar glass sold at about RMB 8 per sq m for 2 mm coated glass at the end of June 2026, below its makers' cash cost (SMM estimated producer losses of about RMB 2 per sq m). Chinese float glass sold for RMB 1,100 a tonne in late September 2026 (China's National Bureau of Statistics), roughly ₹14,400 a tonne at ₹13.1 per renminbi. Indian producers cannot match those numbers from a standing start: they pay more for gas, often more for sand and soda ash, and they lack China's scale. What they have instead is a set of trade remedies. This section takes them one at a time, then looks at the evidence that the wall is leaking.

Solar glass: a minimum landed price, not a fixed duty

The anti-dumping duty on textured tempered glass from China and Vietnam is often described in the press as a flat duty of $664 on every tonne. That is wrong, and the difference matters. The final measure, Ministry of Finance Notification 11/2025-Customs (ADD) of 8 May 2025, issued after the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) published final findings on 10 February 2025, is a reference-price duty. For each exporter it sets a reference amount in US dollars per tonne. If the landed value of a shipment is below that amount, the importer pays the difference as duty; if it is above, no duty is due. The reference amounts are $664 a tonne for most Chinese exporters (Shaanxi Topray, Flat/Anhui Flat, Anhui CSG, Wujiang CSG and all others), $658 for Dongguan CSG and the Xinyi group, $659 for the Kibing group, $570 for Flat Glass's Vietnamese unit and $664 for other Vietnamese exporters. The duty runs for five years from 4 December 2024, when the provisional duty began, to 3 December 2029. Borosil was the sole petitioner.

The product scope is precise: textured tempered glass, coated or not, up to 4.2 mm thick, with at least 90.5% transmission and at least one side longer than 1,500 mm. That is solar glass, not ordinary toughened glass. It was not India's first attempt. An earlier anti-dumping duty on Chinese and Malaysian solar glass was discontinued in August 2022, and Borosil's own annual report says it then "battled for survival": standalone operating margins fell from 38% in FY22 to 10% in FY24, and consolidated losses followed. The provisional duty from December 2024 reversed that within a year.

In plain terms

The government did not put a tax of $664 on every tonne of imported solar glass. It said: no Chinese or Vietnamese solar glass may land in India for less than about $664 a tonne; if it does, the importer pays the gap. In effect the government set a minimum price, and Indian makers now sell at roughly that price.

Converting the floor into Borosil's price unit

Borosil quotes its realisation in rupees per mm of thickness per square metre. Because glass weighs about 2.5 tonnes per cubic metre, one millimetre of thickness over one square metre weighs 2.5 kg. The conversion is therefore: ₹ per mm per sq m = (US$ per tonne × ₹ per US$ ÷ 1,000) × 2.5. At the $664 reference amount and ₹85 to the dollar (December 2024) the floor is about ₹141 per mm. At ₹88, roughly the FY26 average, it is about ₹146 per mm. At ₹92, roughly the Q1 FY27 average, it is about ₹153. At ₹94.9 (19 October 2026) it is ₹157.5.

Now compare Borosil's actual realisations: ₹146.7 per mm in FY26 and about ₹150.8 in Q1 FY27 once the ₹9.5 fuel surcharge is stripped out. Borosil's ex-factory price has tracked the reference price, converted at the prevailing exchange rate, almost exactly. The conversion ignores basic customs duty and freight, so it is an approximation (and labelled as such below), but the fit is too close to be a coincidence.

Three consequences follow, and each matters for valuing Borosil.

  • The rupee is a hidden price lever. Every ₹1 of rupee depreciation lifts the floor by about ₹1.66 per mm, a little over 1% of the price. The rupee's slide from about ₹92 to about ₹95 offsets roughly a third of the ₹9.5 surcharge that is now being unwound.
  • The floor is also a ceiling. Chinese solar glass at RMB 8–10 per sq m works out to only about ₹50–65 per mm. Any Indian price meaningfully above the floor-plus-freight would invite duty-paying imports, so however tight domestic supply is, Indian prices cannot rise much above the reference price. Management says as much: its reference price is import parity, and it sees "no challenge on pricing" because demand is about four times domestic supply.
  • Any origin outside the duty can undercut it. Indonesia and Thailand carry no Indian measure on solar glass. Malaysia carries only a 9.71% countervailing duty. Glass from those countries does not face the reference price at all.

Malaysia: a countervailing duty, extended

A countervailing duty (CVD) offsets subsidies rather than dumping. India's CVD of 9.71% on Malaysian textured tempered glass was extended for another five years by a Ministry of Finance customs notification of 2 June 2026, after a sunset review. Our own primer described it as new in 2026; it is an extension of an existing measure. A 9.71% ad valorem duty on glass invoiced at ₹218 per sq m adds about ₹21, a small fraction of the gap between Chinese prices and the Chinese reference price. That is why Malaysia matters so much in the import data.

The evidence: the duty moved the source, not the volume

India's solar installations nearly doubled, so demand explains much of the rise. But the mix by origin changed sharply.

Float glass: an anti-dumping duty that lapsed and a price floor that replaced it

Float glass has its own history. India imposed an anti-dumping duty of $218 a tonne on Chinese float in 2015; it was last seen extended to December 2020 and its later status is unverified, so it is best treated as lapsed (immaterial now: China supplied only about ₹53 crore of the two key float codes in FY26). The measure that mattered was the duty on clear float from Malaysia, $273 a tonne, imposed by Notification 37/2020-Customs (ADD) of 11 November 2020. Malaysia, home to several Chinese-owned "transplant" float plants, was by DGTR's count 85% of India's clear-float import volume (about 361 kt) and about 18% of the market. The duty was extended by Notification 22/2025-Customs (ADD) of 10 July 2025, but only to 10 February 2026. DGTR's sunset review recommended continuing it at a higher $370 a tonne, contingent on the Ministry of Finance accepting the recommendation. No notification continuing it has been found. The probable reading, not a certain one, is that the Malaysian float ADD lapsed on 10 February 2026.

What the government did instead was broader. On 18 August 2026, the Directorate General of Foreign Trade issued Notification 29/2026-27, moving clear float glass of 4–12 mm under HS 70051090 and 70052990 to the "Restricted" list unless its CIF value is at least ₹34,000 a tonne, for one year. Imports under advance authorisation or by EOU and SEZ units are exempt if not sold into the domestic market. Unlike an anti-dumping duty, a minimum import price (MIP) is origin-neutral: it applies whether the glass comes from Malaysia, Vietnam, Indonesia or anywhere else.

The pattern across both products is the same. Indian glass margins are high when the border is closed and collapse when it opens, and the border has opened twice in a decade: solar in August 2022 and Malaysian float in February 2026. The current protection is real but dated. The float MIP is a one-year instrument; the solar duty has three years left and is already being routed around. Investors in either company are, in part, investors in the continuity of Indian trade policy.

In plain terms

India's glassmakers rent their pricing power from the trade ministry, and the leases are short. The float floor lasts until August 2027. The solar floor lasts until December 2029 but does not cover Malaysia properly or Indonesia at all. A company that earns 35% margins inside such a wall should be valued on what it would earn if part of the wall came down.

Only one of the five has customers that cannot easily switch. The rest live and die by the import price.

Glass businesses differ less in what they melt than in where along the chain they sell. The float furnace upstream is what makes A's economics different from a pure processor's: when imports are cheap, owning the furnace is a cost; when imports are expensive or scarce, it is a moat.

Archetypes B and C sell commodities priced at import parity, so the trade remedy is the moat. B's margin is the spread between import parity and the cost of soda ash and gas, with new domestic lines periodically flooding the market (FY18–FY19 and FY24–FY25). C has the highest margins in the industry today (Borosil's 32–35% EBITDA) and the most policy-dependent ones. C also has a structural quirk: its customers can integrate backwards. An integrated module maker that builds its own solar-glass furnace becomes a competitor for Borosil's merchant volume.

Archetypes D and E need little capital and are where most of India's glass employment sits. AIS has said it has "remained small and slow" in consumer glass and merged four subsidiaries into AIS Consumer Glass Solutions from 1 July 2025 to change that; the chairman says he is "extremely optimistic about exponential growth". The aftermarket is the defensive part of AIS's auto book, roughly a quarter of auto revenue on the primer's estimate, and earns more than OEM sales.

The furnace cycle: Soniyana's J-curve and a German furnace that went cold Deep dive

A glass tank cannot be switched off, so every new furnace drags on profits before it lifts them, and a furnace behind the wrong border never lifts them at all.

The defining physical fact of glassmaking is that the furnace runs continuously. Once fired, a tank holding hundreds of tonnes of molten glass stays at around 1,550°C, 24 hours a day, for its whole campaign: 12–15 years for a float furnace and 6–7 years for a solar-glass furnace, according to industry practice and Borosil's management. Letting it cool damages the refractory lining, so producers keep melting even when prices fall below cost. That is why gluts in glass are so violent (China in 2025–26 is the current example) and why the order in which new capacity arrives matters so much.

Why utilisation is everything

Because most costs other than raw materials and part of the energy bill are fixed, profit is highly sensitive to how much of the furnace's capacity is sold. A furnace running at 95% spreads its labour, refractory wear, maintenance and depreciation over far more tonnes than one at 75%, and the energy used just to keep the tank hot is the same either way. This is why both members' operating profit rose 77% in Q1 FY27 on revenue up 15%. It is also why a new furnace hurts first: depreciation and interest start the day it is fired, while customers take quarters to win.

AIS: the Soniyana J-curve, step by step

FY26 was, in the chairman's words, a transition "from building capacity to absorbing it". The rules of thumb, and their traps

Borosil's own rule is that 1,000 tpd of melt supports about 6.5 GW of modules a year, and 1,600 tpd about 10.5 GW, or roughly 154 tpd per GW. The industry rule it quotes for total Indian demand is 62 GW ≈ 11,000 tpd, or about 177 tpd per GW. The difference is the module mix: glass-glass bifacial modules use a second sheet on the back instead of a polymer backsheet, which roughly doubles the glass per watt, and the industry average includes more of them. Pushing the other way, glass is getting thinner: 2.0 mm is now standard for many sheets, Borosil already makes 2 mm fully tempered glass, and 1.6–1.8 mm is coming. Thinner glass needs fewer tonnes per gigawatt but the same square metres, which matters because Borosil is paid per mm per sq m. A move from 2.0 mm to 1.6 mm cuts tonnage per gigawatt by a fifth and, at an unchanged price per mm, revenue per square metre by a fifth too.

Two further traps: tonnes melted are not tonnes sold (Borosil's pull-to-pack yield is about 70% on our arithmetic: 1,000 tpd for 91 days is 91,000 tonnes melted, while 12.5 million sq m at about 5 kg is about 63,000 tonnes packed), and demand comes from domestically assembled modules, not from installations. A solar farm built with imported Chinese modules uses no Indian glass at all.

Behind it all sits China. China also withdrew export VAT rebates on PV products from 1 April 2026, which supported global module prices. Energy and soda ash: the 2026 gas shock in a furnace that never sleeps

Energy is a fifth of revenue and moved violently in 2026. Soda ash is a fifth of raw materials and sits at decade lows, pending a safeguard decision.

A float furnace burns about 6–8 gigajoules of energy for every tonne of glass it melts (an industry rule of thumb), and it burns it continuously. Most Indian furnaces run on natural gas (pipeline gas or regasified LNG), fuel oil or propane, often switchable between them, with electric boosting. Energy is therefore the cost that moves most and fastest, and 2026 delivered the largest move in years.

The 2026 shock

The West Asia crisis that began on 28 February 2026 hit gas first. Qatar, which supplies 40–45% of India's LNG, declared force majeure in early March. Asian spot LNG, about $10 per MMBtu before the crisis, jumped to $24–25 and was still $17–18 in May. GAIL and IOC cut industrial gas supplies by 10–40% in March and April, prioritising city gas. India's LNG tender awards ran at $16.8–18.8 per MMBtu between March and June and reached $26.4 per MMBtu in September 2026, according to Wood Mackenzie data reported by Business Today on 13 October 2026; Wood Mackenzie expects South Asia's 2026 LNG demand to fall by about 6 million tonnes. The glassware cluster at Firozabad reported production down more than 30% during the gas cuts.

Soda ash: the tailwind with a policy risk attached

Soda ash is the most expensive raw material in glass: about 0.18–0.22 tonnes per tonne of glass. Its price is at or near decade lows. Chinese light soda ash was about RMB 1,156 a tonne at the end of June 2026, dense-grade exports from Qingdao about $185 a tonne FOB, and the Indian price about ₹30–31 per kg in Q1 FY27, against a peak of about ₹53 in May 2022 (from our commodity chemicals dossier). Each ₹5,000 per tonne move in soda ash changes the cost of a tonne of glass by about ₹1,000, or 2–3% of float realisation (an indicative rule of thumb). Xinyi Solar credits cheaper soda ash, sand and gas for part of its 2025 margin; the same input deflation helped AIS's float margin.

The risk is a domestic policy decision. India already has a minimum import price on soda ash of ₹20,108 a tonne (since 2023), and the DGTR began a safeguard investigation on soda ash imports (HS 2836.20) on 16 March 2026, on an application by the Alkali Manufacturers' Association, with an estimated final determination around 16 November 2026. A safeguard duty would raise glassmakers' costs, which is why AIS, Borosil, Gold Plus and container-glass maker AGI Greenpac are natural opponents of it, while soda-ash makers such as Tata Chemicals and GHCL want it. What hurts one set of listed companies helps the other; our commodity chemicals report covers the soda-ash side.

The solar manufacturing lists

The ALMM is the policy that makes Borosil's market exist. Without it, India's solar developers would buy the cheapest modules in the world, most of them made in China with Chinese glass already attached, and Indian solar-glass demand would be a fraction of today's. With it, every project that touches government money, net metering or the PM Surya Ghar rooftop scheme must use modules (and, since June 2026, cells) from an approved domestic list. The policy has worked on capacity: module capacity has grown from about 11 GW to about 203 GW in five years. It has also overshot. Module capacity is now more than three times annual installations, and the cell requirement will push out assemblers without cell lines. For Borosil, ALMM-II is double-edged: it locks in domestic demand but thins the customer list.

GST 2.0 and the car market

The tax cut on small cars from 28% plus cess to 18% from 22 September 2025, and a flat 40% on larger cars without cess, is the single biggest reason PV sales grew 16.7% in H2 FY26 and about 28% in H1 FY27. For AIS that is pure volume. The same reform cut GST on solar modules and cells from 12% to 5%, but solar glass, classified with other toughened glass, does not appear in the 5% list and so seems to remain at 18%. If so, module makers pay more GST on their glass input than they charge on their output, an inverted duty structure that ties up their working capital (we could not verify the CBIC notification text, so treat this as unconfirmed).

Quality and safety rules

The BIS quality control order of April 2022 requires certification for glass sold in India, which favours established producers with certified plants and slows the entry of unbranded imports. Automotive safety glass has its own standards (AIS-037 and IS 2553), and the spread of driver-assistance features under Bharat NCAP-style safety ratings pushes car makers toward laminated side windows and windshields with optical-grade zones for cameras, both higher-value products in which AIS and Saint-Gobain Sekurit lead. These rules rarely make headlines but they steadily raise content per car.

Abroad

Two foreign regimes matter. In the US, the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026, a temporary 10% Section 122 tariff ran until 24 July 2026, and a 10% Section 301 tariff on Indian goods has applied since then (12.5% for China and Vietnam), under legal challenge. US anti-dumping and countervailing cases in 2025 targeted modules from India and South-East Asia, which limits the export outlet for Indian module makers and therefore their glass purchases. In Europe, the lapse of duties on Chinese solar glass in 2020 is what killed GMB; the India–EU free-trade agreement concluded on 27 January 2026 is a future export opportunity, and the EU's carbon border adjustment will eventually reward low-carbon furnaces such as Borosil's 93%-renewable-powered plant and AIS's green-hydrogen-assisted Soniyana.

In plain terms

Borosil's customers exist because the government requires domestic modules on most subsidised solar projects, and AIS's volume surge exists because the government cut car taxes in September 2025. Both companies are, in different ways, riding policy: one on industrial policy that is about to force a shake-out among its customers, the other on a tax cut whose effect on growth rates fades from October 2026.

Four decades of glass cycles: from a Maruti joint venture to a solar duty wall

Every boom in Indian glass has been followed by a capacity wave or an open border. The current boom has both on the horizon.

The history of Indian glass is short enough to tell in five episodes, and each one left a mark on the two companies in this report.

1984–2003: auto glass is born inside the Maruti supply chain

AIS was formed in 1984 as a joint venture of the Labroo family, Asahi Glass of Japan (now AGC) and Maruti Udyog, and began production in 1987 to glaze the Maruti 800. Float glass was then dominated by a handful of producers: Saint-Gobain started float production at Sriperumbudur near Chennai around 2000, Gujarat Guardian (Guardian Industries of the US) arrived in the 1990s, and AGC's Float Glass India at Taloja was merged into AIS between 2001 and 2003, making AIS India's only integrated float-and-auto producer. Those three founding shareholders still shape AIS: AGC owns 21.18%, Maruti 10.59% and Sanjay Labroo 11.47%.

2008–2016: China's float arrives, and India's first duties

As China's float capacity exploded, India imposed an anti-dumping duty of $218 a tonne on Chinese float in 2015. Gold Plus entered with its first line (470 tpd) in January 2009. Borosil, then Borosil Glass Works, started India's first solar-glass furnace (180 tpd) at Bharuch in 2010.

FY18–FY19: the float glut

Domestic float capacity rose 43% in two years as Gold Plus added a 700 tpd line, AIS built Taloja's new float (about 550 tpd) and HNG Float came on stream, and prices crashed. AIS's operating margin stayed at about 16–18% only because auto glass carried the float business. In 2020 Borosil demerged its consumer glassware into Borosil Ltd and the remaining company was renamed Borosil Renewables.

FY21–FY23: the boom, and buying at the top

Post-COVID car and housing demand, the anti-dumping duty on Malaysian float (November 2020) and the BIS order (April 2022) produced a float boom: AIS's consolidated operating margin peaked at 24% in FY22 and Gold Plus earned 32.6%. Solar glass was even tighter: Borosil earned 38–39% standalone operating margins in FY21 and FY22. In October 2022, at the top of the cycle, Borosil bought 86% of Interfloat and GMB in Germany. In August 2022, India's earlier anti-dumping duty on Chinese and Malaysian solar glass was discontinued.

FY23–FY25: China's solar overbuild and the European collapse

With the border open, Chinese solar glass flooded India and Europe. Borosil's standalone margin fell to 23% in FY23 and 10% in FY24, and consolidated losses were ₹50 crore in FY24 and ₹87 crore in FY25 as GMB lost money every month. AIS built through the period: borrowings rose from ₹1,238 crore (FY22) to ₹2,696 crore (FY25) as it built Soniyana and refurbished Roorkee. Gold Plus added two 800 tpd lines in Karnataka, part of the ~30% (3,200 tpd) industry float capacity increase in FY24–FY25.

Dec-2024 to 2026: the policy reset, the energy shock, the car boom

The provisional solar-glass duty from 4 December 2024 and the final reference-price duty of May 2025 took Borosil's standalone EBITDA from ₹181 crore to ₹492 crore. GMB went insolvent in July 2025. AIS commissioned Soniyana, raised ₹1,000 crore through a QIP and turned the J-curve. In 2026 the West Asia crisis pushed LNG to $26.4 per MMBtu, the Malaysian float duty most likely lapsed, the MIP replaced it, ALMM-II took effect and car sales surged after GST 2.0.

In FY26, roughly 5.4 million passenger vehicles at an 80% share and about ₹5,500 of OEM glass each give about ₹2,400 crore; trucks, tractors, rail and white goods add about ₹200 crore, and the aftermarket about ₹800 crore, for an auto SBU of about ₹3,370 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE, built to match the reported ₹3,369 crore). Margin then depends on the gap between those resets and input costs: float (now largely its own), PVB interlayer bought in dollars, and energy for bending and tempering. A weaker rupee raises the PVB bill; a fuller Soniyana lowers the float bill. The auto KPIs below (segment margin, share, content per vehicle) sit alongside the furnace KPIs because AIS needs both sets to be read correctly.

1. Capacity (tpd) and pull rate

What it is. The rated melting capacity of a furnace, in tonnes of molten glass per day, also called pull. Formula: annual melt = tpd × 365 × utilisation. Worked example: AIS's 2,150 tpd at 85% utilisation melts about 667,000 tonnes a year; Borosil's 1,000 tpd at full utilisation melts 365,000. What moves it: new furnaces (in 300–1,000 tpd blocks), rebuilds during cold repairs (which usually add 10–20%), and electric boosting (AIS added 100 tpd from 3 MW of boosting in FY26). Benchmark: a float line under 550–600 tpd is sub-scale in India; modern lines are 800–1,000 tpd. Solar furnaces are smaller (Borosil's run from 210 to 550 tpd; its new ones are 300 tpd each). How the members compare: AIS has 2,150 tpd of float across three plants; Borosil has 1,000 tpd of solar glass across three furnaces, rising to 1,600 by March 2027. The trap: tpd is melt, not sales. A 1,000 tpd solar furnace does not sell 1,000 tonnes a day; after yield losses it packs about 700. Never multiply tpd × 365 × price.

2. Utilisation

What it is. The share of rated capacity actually melted. Formula: actual pull ÷ rated pull. Worked example: a 1,000 tpd furnace that melts 330,000 tonnes in a year runs at 90%. What moves it: demand, hot repairs, gas availability (Indian gas cuts of 10–40% in March–April 2026), and the deliberate choice to run a furnace lighter to protect price, which few producers make because the tank cannot be idled. Benchmark (indicative): good above 90%, average 80–90%, weak below 80%. How the members compare: Borosil ran at full capacity in Q1 FY27 despite the fuel disruption, with net production of 12.5 million sq m (+10% YoY); a four-day tile-patching stop on SG-1 cost about ₹3 crore of output. 3. Pull-to-pack yield

What it is. The share of molten glass that ends up as packed, saleable product, after edge trims, cutting losses, breakage in tempering and quality rejects (much of which returns to the furnace as cullet). Formula: tonnes packed ÷ tonnes melted. Worked example: Borosil melted about 91,000 tonnes in Q1 FY27 (1,000 tpd × 91 days) and produced 12.5 million sq m; at about 5.05 kg per sq m that is about 63,000 tonnes, a yield of about 69%. What moves it: thickness (thinner glass breaks more in tempering), product complexity, and furnace age. Benchmark (indicative): float above 85% good, 80–85% average, below 80% weak; rolled and tempered solar glass runs lower, around 70%. How the members compare: Borosil about 69–70% (our arithmetic); AIS not disclosed. The trap: because recycled cullet goes back into the batch, low yield costs less than it seems in materials but more in energy, since the cullet is melted twice.

7. Energy cost per tonne and power and fuel as a share of revenue

11. Net debt to EBITDA

13. Import penetration

What it is. The share of domestic consumption supplied by imports, the clearest single measure of how exposed domestic prices are to foreign supply. Formula: imports ÷ (domestic output + imports − exports). Worked example: in solar glass, FY26 imports of about 591 million sq m under HS 70071900 against domestic output of about 110–130 million sq m imply imports were about 80% of supply by area (INDICATIVE, since the code includes some non-solar glass). In clear float, 44.25 million sq m (about 0.44–0.55 million tonnes) against consumption of about 2.75 million tonnes is about 16–20%. What moves it: trade remedies, the domestic capacity cycle, the rupee. Benchmark: there is no good or bad level; what matters is direction and source. How the members compare: Borosil's market is 75–80% imported, so its price is import parity by definition; AIS's merchant float market is about a sixth imported, enough for imports to set the marginal price. The trap: falling import penetration is not always good news for an incumbent. If it falls because domestic capacity has tripled, the price may stop being import parity and start being set by domestic competition, which is lower.

14. Working-capital days

What it is. How many days of sales are tied up in inventory and receivables, net of payables. Formula: (inventory + receivables − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: ₹400 crore of net working capital on ₹1,600 crore of annual revenue is about 91 days. Benchmark (indicative): below 60 days good for a glass producer, 60–90 average, above 90 weak. How the members compare: screener shows AIS at 33 days in FY26 (from −1 a year earlier) and an inventory-days figure of 302 that is a definitional artefact of how screener treats cost of goods. What it is. Operating profit after depreciation (EBIT) as a share of the capital tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ average capital employed (equity + debt). 18. Definition traps, collected

  • tpd is melt, not sales. A solar furnace packs about 70% of what it melts; a float line about 80–85%. Never multiply tpd × 365 × price.
  • Tonnes are not square metres. 1 mm of glass over 1 sq m weighs 2.5 kg. Borosil is paid per mm per sq m, so thinner glass means fewer tonnes but not lower revenue per mm.
  • Borosil's realisation includes a surcharge. Q1 FY27's ₹160.3 per mm includes about ₹9.5 of temporary fuel surcharge; the underlying price was about ₹150.8.
  • Borosil standalone is not consolidated. FY26 consolidated revenue was ₹1,555.8 crore and reported profit ₹127.4 crore; standalone was ₹1,534.8 crore and ₹20.7 crore; standalone profit before the ₹359.78 crore exceptional was about ₹380.5 crore. Our primer's ₹1,608 crore is standalone trailing revenue to June 2026, and its ₹381 crore is standalone pre-exceptional profit.
  • Exceptional items hide in screener's other income. Borosil's FY26 "other income" on screener is −₹188 crore (consolidated) and −₹338 crore (standalone): the German write-offs.
  • Borosil's quarterly loss is often mislabelled. The −₹203.5 crore consolidated loss was Q1 FY26 (June 2025), not Q4 FY25 (−₹30 crore). Q3 FY26 consolidated profit was ₹100 crore (₹78 crore standalone).
  • Q4 FY26 is not a run-rate. Borosil's ₹169 crore profit includes about ₹75 crore of tax written back and ₹32 crore of sales of goods dispatched in earlier quarters.
  • AIS's segments are not "auto and architectural". They are Automotive and Float; float includes ₹516 crore (FY26) of sales to AIS's own auto plants. The SBU split (auto ₹3,369 crore, architectural ₹1,262 crore) is a different basis.
  • AIS's annual report misstates profit growth. Its "PAT up 15.96%" refers to standalone total comprehensive income of ₹331.45 crore, which actually fell 13.3%; standalone profit fell 15.2% to ₹329.94 crore. Do not quote it.
  • AIS's PV share depends on the source. ~80% (chairman), ~79% (another AR page and CARE), 72% (older interviews). It is a company claim.
  • Debt has two figures. AIS screener borrowings ₹2,198 crore; company debt ₹2,058.67 crore.
  • The solar duty is a floor, not a fixed duty. Describe it as "a minimum landed price of $570–664 a tonne", never as a flat per-tonne duty.
  • Borosil is not India's only solar-glass maker, and Borosil Renewables is not Borosil Ltd (glassware) or Borosil Scientific.
In plain terms

Half the mistakes made about these two companies come from mixing bases: standalone with consolidated, before and after exceptional items, with and without a surcharge, segment with SBU. Pick one basis per comparison and write it next to the number.

19. How to read a glass company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue and its split (1 minute). For AIS, take consolidated revenue and the auto and float segment revenues; note inter-segment sales. For Borosil, split revenue growth into volume (sq m produced and sold) and price (₹ per mm). Red flag: Borosil revenue up on price alone while volume falls.
  2. Realisation, stripped (1 minute). Borosil's ₹ per mm with and without the fuel surcharge; compare the ex-surcharge figure with the duty floor at the quarter's rupee rate (about ₹1.66 per mm per ₹1 of the rupee). Red flag: an ex-surcharge price falling below the floor, which signals domestic oversupply or circumvention.
  3. Margin on both definitions (1 minute). Screener operating profit and company EBITDA; check what sits in other income. Red flag: a margin flattered by other income or one-off dispatches.
  4. Energy (1 minute). Power and fuel as a share of revenue against the previous quarter and the LNG price. Red flag: energy share rising while the surcharge falls.
  5. Raw materials (30 seconds). Raw materials as a share of revenue; for AIS, read it together with energy because of Soniyana. Red flag: a rising share with flat soda-ash prices.
  6. Segment margins (1 minute). AIS auto and float EBIT margins and capital employed; float should benefit from the MIP from Q2 FY27. Red flag: auto margin falling while PV volumes rise, which signals OEM price pressure.
  7. Depreciation and interest (1 minute). Compare with a year earlier: a jump means a new furnace has started. Red flag: interest rising without a matching capacity addition.
  8. Tax and exceptional items (1 minute). Look for write-backs (Borosil Q4 FY26) and exceptional items (Borosil's German charges, AIS's labour-code cost). Red flag: profit above pre-tax profit.
  9. Capacity and calendar (1 minute). Commissioning dates (SG-4/SG-5 January–March 2027; AIS F4), cold-repair timing (SG-1/SG-2 in 2027) and capex spent. Red flag: a slipped commissioning date or an unannounced cold repair.
  10. The border (1 minute). Any change in duties, MIP, sunset reviews or new anti-circumvention cases, and the quarter's import data. Red flag: imports from an uncovered origin (Malaysia, Indonesia) rising faster than total imports.
In plain terms

For Borosil, the three numbers that matter each quarter are price per mm without the surcharge, square metres sold, and power and fuel as a share of revenue. For AIS, they are the float segment margin, the auto segment margin and the interest line. Everything else is detail.

How to read the table

Start with the margin gap. Borosil earned a 28.3% operating margin in FY26 on screener's basis against AIS's 18.3%, and 31.3% against 23.0% in Q1 FY27. That ten-point gap is the difference between a pure furnace selling at a protected import-parity price and an integrated business that also processes, assembles and distributes. It is also why Borosil is 24% of combined revenue but 33% of operating profit.

Then look at the two profit columns. Reported FY26 profit of ₹472 crore understates the members' earning power because Borosil wrote off its German subsidiaries. On a pre-exceptional basis the total is about ₹695 crore, and the trailing twelve months to June 2026 show ₹857 crore. The choice of denominator moves the combined P/E from 61.6× (FY26 reported) to about 41.8× (pre-exceptional) and 33.9× (trailing). For AIS alone, the trailing figure (52.9×) is the more representative because FY26 carried the full Soniyana depreciation and interest before the plant filled.

The valuation gap is the striking column. AIS trades at 27.6 times FY26 operating profit on enterprise value; Borosil at 12.1 times, despite a higher margin, a net-cash balance sheet and more than double the return on capital. The market is paying AIS for durability (design-in contracts, an 80% share, a duty that matters mainly for its merchant float) and discounting Borosil for dependence on a single trade remedy, the capacity wave in solar glass and the surcharge that is unwinding. Section 18 takes this further.

Leverage runs in opposite directions. AIS carries ₹1,875 crore of net debt on screener's basis, about 2.1 times operating profit, and is about to start another furnace cycle. Borosil holds ₹522 crore of net cash (more, after the August 2026 warrant money) and has said its ₹950 crore expansion is fully funded with no equity raise foreseen.

Native KPIs are not comparable across the two. AIS's 2,150 tpd is float, most of which becomes car glass; Borosil's 1,000 tpd is solar glass, which sells for roughly twice as much per tonne. That is why the capacity column is not summed and why per-tonne comparisons in section 13 are indicative.

In plain terms

Borosil makes the higher margin, has no debt and earns twice the return on capital, yet the market values each rupee of its operating profit at less than half of AIS's. Investors are betting that AIS's profits will last and that Borosil's will not. The rest of this report is, in effect, an examination of that bet.

Fuyao is the benchmark for what an auto-glass franchise can become. Fuyao has no Indian plant; its entry would be the most serious competitive threat AIS could face, and none has been announced.

Xinyi Solar shows what solar glass earns without a border. The world's largest solar-glass maker took RMB 2.32 billion of impairments in 2025, its solar-glass gross margin fell to 3.3% in H1 2026, and its profit fell 95%. It idled 1,800 tpd and runs 21,400 tpd, twenty-one times Borosil, and its response to trade barriers is to build abroad: two Indonesian lines of 2,400 tpd in 2026, with 2,300 more planned. Borosil's 35% EBITDA margin on the same product is the measure of India's duty. AGC and Saint-Gobain are the patient incumbents. AGC, with an operating margin of about 6%, owns 21.18% of AIS and provides its auto technology; its royalty from AIS fell from ₹51.9 crore to ₹8.4 crore in FY26, unexplained. The world's best car-glass company, Fuyao, earns a fifth of its revenue as profit; AIS earns a fifteenth to a tenth. The world's biggest solar-glass company, Xinyi, is barely profitable; Borosil earns a third of revenue as EBITDA on the same product because India blocks the cheapest imports. AIS has room to catch up with Fuyao; Borosil has room to fall toward Xinyi.

AIS: paying for durability

At 23.9 times trailing EBITDA, AIS is priced as a franchise rather than a glassmaker. A rough cross-check uses the growth CARE reflects from management: 8–10% revenue growth a year and operating margins around 20%. Two years at 9% takes revenue from ₹4,982 crore to about ₹5,900 crore and EBITDA at 20% to about ₹1,180 crore; on today's enterprise value of about ₹25,100 crore, that is about 21 times FY28 EBITDA (ILLUSTRATIVE). Investors are paying a multiple that assumes the auto franchise and content growth persist for many years, that the float business keeps its MIP-era margins after August 2027, and that the next float plant earns more than the last one did in its first year. The trailing P/E of 52 times is three times Fuyao's (roughly 15–20 times, an approximate figure), for a company a twelfth its size with lower margins. The premium reflects India's car-market growth and AIS's near-monopoly share; it leaves the stock exposed if the post-GST volume surge fades into the base effect from October 2026 or if float imports return.

Borosil: pricing in a lower price

Borosil's stock fell from ₹612 on its results day (16 July 2026) to about ₹543–518 in August, ₹449.6 on 18 September and ₹396 on 22 October, 45% below its 52-week high, even as it reported record margins. No single disclosed event explains it. The candidates are: domestic capacity tripling by March 2027; the surcharge unwinding; FY28 cold-repair downtime; management's warning of a customer shake-out; its refusal to comment on press reports of a strategic investment; TradeStat data showing Malaysian and Vietnamese imports up four- to eight-fold; Xinyi's Indonesian plant; Emerge Renewables lighting a 300 tpd furnace in July; and about ₹279 crore of warrant conversions at ₹530 due by 13 August as the stock fell through that level. The customs order of October 2026 is immaterial.

Reverse-engineering the price makes the assumption explicit. At 1,600 tpd, Borosil would sell about 80 million sq m a year. Today it earns about ₹114 of EBITDA per sq m on a price of about ₹324, so its cash cost is about ₹210 per sq m. If the market values the expanded company at about 10 times EBITDA, the implied EBITDA is about ₹530 crore, or about ₹66 per sq m on 80 million sq m, which requires a price of about ₹276 per sq m, roughly ₹137 per mm (ILLUSTRATIVE). That is about 9% below the ex-surcharge ₹150.8 and about 13% below the duty floor at today's rupee rate. In other words, the price assumes either that domestic oversupply or circumvention pushes Indian solar glass below import parity, or that the duty does not survive in its current form.

In plain terms

AIS's price assumes its car-glass dominance lasts for many years and its float margins survive the end of the import floor. Borosil's price assumes its selling price falls by about a tenth once its new furnaces arrive, which would happen if new Indian capacity floods the market or if imports keep finding ways round the duty. If neither happens, Borosil is cheap; if AIS's float or car volumes disappoint, AIS is not.

Technology and disruption: thinner glass, smarter windshields, cleaner furnaces

Most technology change in glass adds value per square metre. The disruptive changes are in who owns the furnace.

Glassmaking is an old technology, but the products are changing quickly, and in both of the members' markets the change mostly raises value per unit of glass.

In solar glass

  • Thinner glass. 2.0 mm is now common and 1.6–1.8 mm is coming. Thinner glass means fewer tonnes and less soda ash per gigawatt; on a per-mm price it also means less revenue per square metre. Borosil already makes 2 mm fully tempered glass, which it describes as a world first.
  • Glass-glass bifacial modules. Replacing the polymer backsheet with a second sheet of glass roughly doubles glass per watt, the main reason India's demand is about 177 tpd per GW against Borosil's 154. This is the biggest structural tailwind for solar-glass volumes.
  • Next-generation cells. Perovskite and tandem cells, expected later in the decade, still need front glass, possibly with transparent conductive coatings that are worth more.
  • Specialty products. Borosil's matt-matt, anti-glare and greenhouse glass and its patented antimony-free process are small but differentiated.

In automotive glass

  • Driver-assistance and head-up displays. Windshields with optical-grade camera zones and HUD-compatible wedge interlayers are worth several times a plain windshield.
  • Acoustic and solar-control laminates. Laminated side windows for quieter cabins, infrared-reflective glass for electric-vehicle range.
  • Roofs. Panoramic, switchable (PDLC) and illuminated sunroofs, all mentioned in AIS's annual report; electric vehicles favour large glass roofs. Polycarbonate competes in some roof applications.

In the furnace

Decarbonisation is reshaping furnaces: oxy-fuel firing, electric boosting (AIS added 100 tpd of pull with 3 MW of boosting), hybrid electric melters and hydrogen blending. AIS's Soniyana uses green hydrogen from an on-site plant under a 20-year contract; Borosil meets 93% of its power from renewables. These are cost hedges against LNG shocks as much as climate measures, and the EU's carbon border adjustment will eventually price embedded carbon in exported glass and modules.

The real disruptions

The changes that could hurt the members are structural rather than technical. For Borosil, it is captive glass: integrated module makers building their own furnaces shrink the merchant market even as total demand rises, and their surplus can depress prices. For AIS, it is Chinese-owned float in South-East Asia exporting at about ₹25,000 a tonne whenever the border opens, and the possibility, so far only that, of Fuyao building in India.

In plain terms

Technology is mostly on the members' side: bifacial modules need twice the glass and modern cars need cleverer windshields. The threats come from ownership, not invention: module makers making their own glass, and Chinese companies making glass next door.

Bull vs bear: the duty, the furnace and the car

The strongest case on each side, for the sub-sector and for each company.

The bull case

  • A structural glass shortage. India imports about three-quarters of its solar glass and a sixth of its float; even after tripling, domestic solar capacity covers only about 70% of FY26 demand, so prices stay at protected import parity.
  • Protection with years to run. The solar duty runs to December 2029; the Malaysian CVD was extended to 2031; the float MIP is origin-neutral and stronger than the Malaysia-only duty it replaced.
  • Operating leverage on full furnaces. Both members' operating profit rose 77% in Q1 FY27 on 15% revenue growth; Borosil adds 60% capacity, fully funded, from April 2027.
  • Content per car. SUVs at 68% of PV sales, sunroofs and camera-ready windshields keep AIS's auto revenue growing faster than volumes, and its ~80% share is protected by model-life contracts.
  • Integration and cleaner energy. Soniyana cut AIS's raw-material bill from 38% to 26% of revenue; Borosil's power is 93% renewable; soda ash is near decade lows.
  • Balance sheets repaired. AIS's debt-to-EBITDA fell to 2.15×; Borosil holds net cash.

The bear case

  • The wall leaks. Malaysian solar-glass imports rose 390% and Vietnamese 688% in FY26; Xinyi is building 2,400 tpd in Indonesia, outside any Indian duty.
  • Capacity triples. Domestic solar-glass capacity rises from 2,600 to ~7,700 tpd by March 2027, mostly captive; Borosil's share halves and captive surplus can undercut merchant prices.
  • Temporary gains. Borosil's ₹9.5 surcharge (~17% of Q1 FY27 EBITDA) is unwinding; AIS's royalty saving (₹43.5 Cr) is unexplained; Q4 FY26 profits included tax write-backs.
  • Dated protection. The float MIP expires around August 2027; the Malaysian float duty already lapsed; the solar duty faces a sunset review before 2029.
  • Cycle risks. A post-GST base effect from October 2026 and a rate hike; module-maker shake-out under ALMM-II; LNG at $26.4; a possible soda-ash safeguard; AIS's next furnace J-curve; Borosil's 2027 cold repairs.
  • Concentration and governance. Maruti is 32% of AIS's auto sales and a shareholder; Borosil pays a new brand royalty and sells to a company linked to an independent director's relative; AIS has ₹362 Cr of disputed customs claims; AIS trades at 52× earnings.

The two cases differ mainly on one question: whether Indian glass prices stay at protected import parity. The bull case assumes the border holds and the shortage persists, so both companies keep earning margins far above global peers. The bear case assumes the border keeps leaking through third countries and the capacity wave in solar glass breaks the link between domestic prices and the duty floor. For AIS the question matters mainly for its merchant float business; its auto franchise is robust under either case, which is why its multiple is higher. For Borosil it is the whole investment case.

What would settle the argument? For Borosil, the ex-surcharge price per mm in the first two quarters after SG-4 and SG-5 start (Q1 and Q2 FY28) and any new trade case against Malaysia or Indonesia. For AIS, the float segment margin after the MIP is renewed or allowed to lapse in August 2027, and whether auto segment growth stays ahead of PV volumes once the GST surge is in the base.

In plain terms

If India keeps cheap foreign glass out and the new Indian furnaces do not flood the market, both companies earn well and Borosil looks cheap. If imports keep arriving through Malaysia and Indonesia and the new furnaces dump spare glass, Borosil's margins fall toward the world's and AIS's float business loses its edge, while its car-glass franchise carries on.
