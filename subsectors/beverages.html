00

The one-page view: a weather business in a three-way price war

India drinks a fifth of what China drinks and a sixth of what Americans drink. Getting from here to there runs through hot summers, a ₹10 bottle and the plants of one very efficient bottler.

India's packaged soft-drink industry is small for a country of 1.45 billion people, it grows fast in hot years and barely at all in wet ones, and since 2023 it has been fighting the most aggressive price war in its history. Three organised systems now compete for every fridge in every kirana store: PepsiCo's, which is about 90% bottled by Varun Beverages (VBL); Coca-Cola's, now split between its own bottler HCCB and a ring of family-owned franchise bottlers; and Reliance's revived Campa, which sold more than ₹4,700 crore (gross) in FY26 and about ₹2,900 crore in the April–June 2026 quarter alone. Below them is a long tail of regional colas, water brands and juice makers.

This report covers the whole industry, not only the listed company. That means carbonated soft drinks (colas, lemon and orange flavours, energy drinks), packaged drinking water, fruit drinks and juices, hydration drinks and ambient flavoured milk, plus the African and South Asian bottling markets that now make up roughly a third of VBL. VBL is the only company in the NSE 750 that is primarily a soft-drink business, so it is the lens: its disclosures are the best public data on how Indian beverages behave, and its numbers anchor most of the charts. But the questions are industry questions. How much of a ₹20 bottle reaches the bottler? Who is paying for Campa? Why is Coca-Cola selling Indian territory to local families? Why does a 142%-of-normal pre-monsoon rainfall translate into a 7% fall in volume?

Five forces decide almost everything, in roughly this order of how much they move the numbers.

  • The weather and the season. Summer months carry more than half of annual volume. In 2025 IMD recorded pre-monsoon rain at 142% of the long-period average, and VBL's India volume grew just 2.2% for the year after falling 7.1% in the April–June quarter. In 2026 April was washed out but March and May–July grew more than 20%, and the monsoon ended at 87% of normal, one of the driest in 25 years.
  • The Campa price war, and who is actually paying for it. Reliance re-entered at ₹10 with richer margins for shopkeepers. Coca-Cola says it lost value share in India in Q2 2026; PepsiCo says its share was "stable or better"; VBL's India EBITDA margin rose in both Q1 and Q2 CY26. So far the war is costing Coca-Cola and the regional brands more than it costs VBL, which refuses to fight at ₹10 (under 2% of its volume) and answers with a 400 ml bottle for ₹20.
  • Coca-Cola's restructuring. Coke has handed Rajasthan, Bihar, the North-East, parts of West Bengal, Jharkhand and North Gujarat to local bottlers and sold 40% of HCCB's parent to the Jubilant Bhartia group for ₹11,724 crore (completed 22 July 2025). That price values the Indian Coke bottler at roughly 2.3 times sales. VBL trades at about 5.5 times trailing sales.
  • The shift from building plants to filling them. VBL lifted India capacity about 45% between the 2022 and 2024 seasons and added four more greenfield plants for the 2025 season. India volume grew only 28% over 2022–25. Management now guides India capex below ₹500–600 crore for CY26 and says it could absorb 50% more volume without a new plant. Free cash flow, which turned positive in CY25 (₹797 crore), should be the story of CY26–27 if the plants fill.
  • Africa as the second engine, with currency attached. International markets carried 30.8% of VBL's CY25 volume and grew 23%. South Africa (BevCo from 2024, Twizza from March 2026) is now the largest, but it earns about ₹35 of operating profit per case against about ₹45 in India, carries the group's debt, and is about to face a much bigger Coca-Cola bottler after Coca-Cola HBC completes its purchase of 75% of Coca-Cola Beverages Africa.

Around those five sit the second-order forces: input costs (PET resin up about 20% and closures up about 30% after the West Asia war began on 28 February 2026), tax (colas and energy drinks, including zero-sugar ones, sit in the 40% GST slab while water, milk drinks and juice drinks were cut to 5% from 22 September 2025), the FSSAI order to drop the word "energy" from caffeinated drinks, and the mix shift to low- and no-sugar recipes (about 73% of VBL volume in H1 CY26, up from 53% in CY24).

The listed universe is a single company, and that shapes how to use this report. There is no "sector total" to compute, no combined row in the peer table and no median multiple. Instead VBL is compared with reference companies that are either unlisted (HCCB, Reliance's RCPL, Bisleri, Parle Agro), listed in other sub-sectors (Tata Consumer, whose NourishCo arm sells Himalayan and Tata Copper+ water and Gluco+), or listed abroad (PepsiCo, The Coca-Cola Company, Monster, and the big Coca-Cola bottlers in Mexico, Europe and Africa). Those reference rows are never summed with VBL.

In plain terms

A soft-drink bottler is a factory-and-trucks business that rents a famous brand. It buys syrup from the brand owner, adds water, sugar or sweetener, gas and a plastic bottle, and then has to get the bottle cold into four million small shops before the shopper gets thirsty. In India the shopper buys mostly in four hot months, pays mostly ₹10 or ₹20, and pays 40% of the price as tax. The bottler that wins is the one that fills its plants in those four months and keeps its fridges in the most shops. Varun Beverages has been the best in India at both, which is why it is valued far above bottlers anywhere else in the world, and why a rainy summer or a determined new rival makes its share price move so much.

The market has priced those risks hard in 2026. VBL's share price fell from about ₹556 at its 52-week high to ₹393 on 23 October 2026, a market value of ₹1,32,702 crore and 39.2 times trailing earnings, even though volume grew almost 20% in the April–June quarter and profit grew 15%. Foreign investors cut their holding from 26.6% in December 2023 to 17.5% by September 2026. The worries are Campa and the ₹10 tier, the input-cost wave from the West Asia war, the FSSAI "energy" order against Sting, the lower-margin African acquisitions, a pattern of related-party dealings with the promoter group, and the resignation of the chief operating officer on 24 September 2026. The next test is the board meeting on 30 October 2026, which will report the July–September quarter (Q3 CY26) and a third interim dividend, after one of the driest monsoons since 2001.

01

How a bottle earns: syrup, water, plastic and a fridge

The brand owner creates demand, the bottler delivers it, the shopkeeper chills it and the state takes the biggest single slice.

Every branded soft drink in India passes through five hands. The brand owner (PepsiCo India, Coca-Cola India, or Reliance for Campa) owns the trademark and the recipe, makes the concentrate, and pays for national advertising. The bottler buys concentrate and every other input, runs the filling lines, owns the trucks and the visi-coolers, and sells to distributors. The distributor carries stock and credit to the retailer. The retailer, usually a kirana or paan shop, decides which brand goes in the front of the fridge. And the government collects GST at 40% on a cola or an energy drink.

The money concentrates at two points: the brand owner's concentrate margin (asset-light, high return) and the bottler's plant utilisation in the peak season (asset-heavy, high volume). The war since 2023 is fought at Step 4, over how much of each bottle the shopkeeper keeps.

The split of jobs between brand owner and bottler is what makes the industry unusual. PepsiCo describes its relationship with VBL as "demand creation" versus "demand delivery". PepsiCo spends on television, cricket sponsorships and new flavours; VBL spends on trade schemes, merchandising, coolers and every physical asset. The bottler's licence is exclusive in its territory (for VBL, 26 states and 6 union territories, which is all of India except Andhra Pradesh, Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh), so the bottler never competes with another bottler of the same brand. It competes with the other systems.

In plain terms

Of the ₹20 you pay for a cold cola, about ₹5.70 goes to the government, about ₹3.30 to the shop and the distributor, and about ₹11 reaches the bottler. The bottler then pays roughly ₹3 to PepsiCo or Coca-Cola for the syrup and about ₹3.60 for the bottle, cap, label and sugar, spends about ₹2.50 on its people, trucks and power, and keeps around ₹2 before depreciation and tax. The fight between brands is mostly a fight over that ₹3.30 for the shop.

Where ₹100 of a bottler's revenue goes

At the level of the whole company the split looks different, because VBL's revenue is net of GST and excise and its mix includes everything from 200 ml returnable glass to 2.25-litre family packs. In CY25 VBL spent ₹44.8 of every ₹100 of net revenue on materials (concentrate, sugar, PET, closures, labels and traded goods), ₹10.1 on employees and ₹21.8 on everything else (freight and distribution, power, trade and below-the-line spend, repairs, rent), leaving EBITDA of ₹23.3. Depreciation took ₹5.6, interest ₹0.8, tax ₹4.4, and other income (mostly interest on the India cash pile) added ₹1.6, leaving ₹14.1 of profit after tax.

Who earns the best return

Across the chain, the brand owner earns the highest return on capital because concentrate needs almost no assets. The Coca-Cola Company's operating margin was 33.4% in 2025 and Monster's 29.9%; Coca-Cola India Pvt Ltd, the Indian concentrate company, earned about ₹615 crore of profit on ₹5,043 crore of revenue in FY25. Bottlers earn lower margins on much bigger asset bases. What makes India unusual is that its best bottler earns more than most brand owners' bottlers anywhere: VBL's India business made a 25.9% operating margin in CY25, against 12–16% for the big global Coca-Cola bottlers and about 13% for HCCB.

02

Two calendars, one 8-ounce case: the traps before the numbers

VBL's year ends in December, everyone else's in March. Its volume unit is an American eight-ounce serving. Get these two wrong and every comparison in this industry breaks.

Before reading a single number, a reader needs two conventions. The first is the calendar. VBL's financial year is the calendar year, approved under section 2(41) of the Companies Act. Its filings call 2026 "Financial Year 2026", but this report uses CY labels to avoid confusion: CY25 is January–December 2025, Q1 CY26 is January–March 2026, Q2 CY26 is April–June 2026 (reported on 28 July 2026, the latest quarter), and Q3 CY26 is July–September 2026, due on 30 October 2026. Every Indian peer, from HCCB and Reliance to Tata Consumer and Bisleri, reports on a March year, so FY26 is April 2025 to March 2026 and Q1 FY27 is April–June 2026.

The practical rule: VBL's Q2 is everyone else's Q1. When Reliance reported Campa's ₹2,900 crore for Q1 FY27, the comparable VBL number is Q2 CY26 (India beverage revenue ₹5,877 crore). When HCCB reports FY26, it covers April 2025 to March 2026, which straddles VBL's weak CY25 summer and its strong Q1 CY26. Never compare VBL's CY25 with a peer's FY25 without saying the periods are a quarter apart. Global peers add another wrinkle: PepsiCo runs a 52/53-week year (its Q3 2026 was the 12 weeks to 5 September 2026), while Coca-Cola, Monster, Coca-Cola FEMSA and Coca-Cola HBC use calendar years.

The unit case: 5.678 litres counted as 24 American servings

The second convention is the volume unit. VBL, HCCB and every Coca-Cola and PepsiCo bottler in the world report volume in unit cases. A unit case is 5.678 litres of finished drink, defined as 24 servings of about 237 ml, which is 8 US fluid ounces (192 ounces in all). VBL's presentations footnote every volume slide with this definition, and management repeated on the Q2 CY26 call that "we always announce our volumes based on 8-ounce." Because the whole Coke and Pepsi world uses it, VBL's 1,213 million cases in CY25 can be set beside Coca-Cola HBC's 2,997 million and Coca-Cola Europacific's 3,958 million.

The unit case measures liquid, not transactions, and that has three consequences. A litre of Aquafina water counts the same as a litre of Sting, even though Sting sells for several times more per litre. When VBL moved its ₹20 cola from 250 ml to 400 ml in 2025–26 in response to Campa, each ₹20 sale started counting as 0.07 of a case instead of 0.044, so reported volume rose about 60% per transaction even if the number of bottles sold stayed flat. And realisation per case (revenue ÷ cases) falls when packs are upsized at the same price, which is exactly what happened: India realisation was −4% in Q4 CY25, −1.5% in Q1 CY26 and −0.6% in Q2 CY26. Management's view is that "if our realisation has not come down per 8-ounce case … that is immaterial".

In plain terms

When VBL says it sold 466.7 million cases in April–June 2026, it means it sold about 2.65 billion litres of drinks, measured as if all of it were poured into small American 8-ounce glasses and packed 24 to a box. It does not mean 466.7 million boxes left the factory. A bigger bottle at the same price raises the "cases" number without anyone buying more often, so always check what happened to revenue per case at the same time.

Two bases for "realisation per case"

VBL publishes realisation per case on two different bases, and mixing them is the most common error in coverage of the company. The annual and fourth-quarter figures in its press text divide total net revenue by cases: CY25 ₹178.8 is ₹21,685 crore ÷ 1,213.1 million cases, and Q4 CY25 ₹177.3 is ₹4,204 crore ÷ 237.1 million. The quarterly table headed "Realization per case – Beverages" in the Q1 and Q2 presentations divides beverage revenue only: Q2 CY26 ₹175.3 is ₹8,181.9 crore ÷ 466.7 million cases. On the total basis Q2 CY26 would be about ₹181. CY25 on the beverage-only basis would be about ₹170–171. So ₹178.8 and ₹175.3 cannot be compared; this report labels which one it uses each time.

Net revenue, gross revenue and "sales"

A third trap is the revenue line itself. VBL's "revenue from operations" is net of GST but gross of excise duty; it then deducts excise (₹540 crore in CY25 and ₹199 crore in Q2 CY26, mostly African excise and sugar levies) to reach "net revenue". Screener's "sales" equal net revenue (₹21,685 crore in CY25). The annual report's geographic split uses gross revenue (₹22,226 crore: India ₹14,449 crore, outside India ₹7,776 crore). Reliance's Campa figure of "₹4,700+ crore" is gross sales, very likely before trade schemes and possibly including GST, so it needs a haircut of perhaps 25–35% before it can sit next to VBL's net revenue. That haircut is an estimate, and the gap is large enough to change conclusions.

Consolidated, standalone, India, international

Finally, VBL reports consolidated (group) and standalone (parent company) numbers. Because the parent company is essentially the India bottling business, standalone ≈ India, with small distortions from Indian non-beverage sales (preforms, scrap, co-manufactured Kurkure Puffcorn) and some head-office costs. International is approximately consolidated minus standalone. In CY25 that gives India revenue ₹14,557 crore with operating profit ₹3,765 crore (25.9%) and international about ₹7,128 crore with about ₹1,305 crore (18.3%). This report uses that split throughout but labels it indicative, because the company does not publish a regional profit split.

In plain terms

Three quick checks before believing any beverage number: which months does it cover (calendar year or March year), is it per 8-ounce case or per bottle, and is revenue net of tax and discounts or gross. Most bad comparisons in this industry fail one of the three.

03

Size and the litres gap: a big country that drinks little

Estimates of the market run from ₹65,000 crore at the factory gate to ₹1.9 lakh crore at retail. Every one of them agrees that Indians drink a fraction of what the rest of the world does.

There is no official measure of India's soft-drink market and the private estimates disagree by a factor of nearly two, mostly because they draw the boundary differently. Redseer put India's ready-to-drink non-alcoholic market at about $20 billion in 2025 (roughly ₹1.9 lakh crore at ₹94.9 to the dollar) and expects it to double to about $40 billion by 2030, an implied growth rate near 15% a year. IMARC and Kenresearch put "non-alcoholic beverages" at $34–36 billion with growth near 8%, but their definition reaches into categories such as loose tea and bulk water. Nexdigm sits at $22.5 billion. These are secondary estimates, quoted here as indicative.

04

Heat, rain and the season: the force nobody controls

A dry April can be worth more than a year of marketing. A wet one can erase a year of growth. The industry's first driver is the sky.

No other Indian consumer category depends as much on the weather. Industry estimates put more than half of annual soft-drink volume in the summer months. For VBL, the April–June quarter alone carried 32.1% of CY25 volume and about 39% of its operating profit, and January–June together carried about 58% of volume. A hot, dry March–June fills the plants and the fridges; unseasonal rain empties both. Management has said that May used to be about 15% of the whole year's business, but in 2025 and 2026 it was not, because of rain.

The 2025 season is the cleanest natural experiment the industry has had. IMD's statement on the climate of India in 2025 records pre-monsoon (March–May) rainfall at 142% of the long-period average, annual rainfall at 110% and only about 35 heat-wave deaths in a year that ranked as the eighth-warmest, far cooler than the record 2024. VBL's India volume in April–June 2025 fell 7.1% and its India volume for the year grew 2.2%, from 821 to 839 million cases. The company's annual report blamed "unprecedented rainfall throughout the year". HCCB's rating agency said the same of HCCB's FY26: it was "impacted by unseasonal weather … heavy rains and extended monsoon".

The shape of the year

Seasonality is extreme enough that a single quarter tells you almost nothing about the year. In CY25, VBL's January–March quarter carried 25.8% of the year's cases, April–June 32.1%, July–September 22.6% and October–December 19.5%. Profit is even more concentrated, because fixed costs (depreciation, salaried staff, cooler maintenance, plant overheads) are spread across twice as many cases in the peak as in the trough. Operating profit in April–June 2025 was ₹1,998 crore; in October–December 2025 it was ₹637 crore. That is why VBL's operating margin swings from about 28% in Q2 to about 15% in Q4 every year, and why year-on-year comparisons are the only meaningful quarterly comparisons.

At the ₹50 per case that a peak-season case earns, losing all of it would cost ₹700–900 crore of EBITDA, or 14–18% of CY25's ₹5,049 crore. In plain terms

A soft-drink plant earns most of its year's profit in about sixteen weeks. If it rains through May, the bottles that were not sold are mostly gone for good, because nobody drinks two colas in October to make up for one they skipped in May. That is why analysts look at rainfall and temperature data almost as closely as at company results, and why one bad season can make a well-run company look as if it has stopped growing.

The season is moving

Two longer-run weather changes matter. First, the peak is spreading: March is hotter earlier and the monsoon is less reliable, so the industry's dependence on May is falling, as management observed. Second, volatility is rising, with more unseasonal rain and more extreme heat waves, which makes any single year harder to forecast and puts a premium on flexible capacity and inventory. VBL's response has been to keep spare capacity (see section 10), to stock raw materials early, and to push categories that sell all year, such as dairy drinks and water. Looking ahead, a "very strong" El Niño was forecast for October–January; such winters have often been followed by hot Indian summers, which would favour the 2027 season, though that link is a tendency, not a rule.

An analyst therefore models soft-drink volume as two parts: a structural trend (per-capita growth, new outlets, new coolers, price-pack architecture) and a weather index. Over 2021–24 the trend for VBL India was in the low-to-mid teens; the weather index pushed individual years from +2% to +44%. Reading 2025 as the new trend, as the share price arguably did, confuses the two. Reading 2026's 14% as proof of a return to trend also goes too far, because part of it is a rebound from a depressed base.

The ₹10 war: Reliance's Campa and who is paying for it Deep dive

Campa did not win the shopper with a cheaper bottle. It won the shopkeeper with a fatter margin. Three years in, the bill is landing mostly on Coca-Cola and the regional brands.

Campa Cola was India's national cola in the protected years after Coca-Cola left in 1977, and it faded after the multinationals returned. Reliance bought the brand from Pure Drinks in August 2022 and relaunched it in March 2023 at ₹10 for 200 ml, with retailer margins well above what the incumbents offered. Three years later Campa is the most important new fact in Indian soft drinks. Reliance says Campa made more than ₹4,700 crore of gross sales in FY26, calls it India's fourth-largest carbonated soft-drink brand, and reported about ₹2,900 crore of beverage sales in the April–June 2026 quarter alone, 2.4 times a year earlier and more than half of the whole of FY26. Reliance Consumer Products (RCPL), the FMCG company that owns Campa and was demerged from Reliance Retail Ventures, now describes itself as India's number-three non-alcoholic ready-to-drink player.

The weapon is the trade margin, not the shelf price

The insight that explains the war is that the shopper pays ₹10 or ₹20 either way. Indian soft drinks sell at round price points, and every brand sits at the same ones. What differs is how much of that ₹10 or ₹20 the retailer keeps, and that decides which brand goes in the front of the fridge, which gets recommended, and which is stocked at all. On a ₹10 pack, after GST of ₹2.86, an incumbent might leave the retailer and distributor about ₹1.4; Campa is believed to leave about ₹2.0. Those per-pack margins are not disclosed by anyone and are illustrative estimates, but the direction is not in dispute: Tata Consumer's management said in 2024 that rivals "adapted their pricing on the trade very, very quickly. We did not", and its ready-to-drink revenue fell 11% in a quarter as a result.

How the incumbents answered

Coca-Cola fought on price first. In Campa's early states in 2023 it cut its 200 ml bottle from ₹15 to ₹10, waived crate deposits, and cut prices on large take-home bottles in some states. VBL took a different line. It treats ₹10 as "a non-profitable category for us"; the ₹10 tier is "less than 2% of our total volume" and exists only "to make sure our distributors remain with us". Instead it moved its ₹20 pack from 250 ml to 400 ml, giving the shopper 60% more drink for the same money, and pushed cooler placement and outlet additions: about 4 million outlets, close to half a million more being added in CY26, and "a million plus" visi-coolers. The chairman's argument is that "when 2 people are sharing a Rs. 20 pack it effectively works out to Rs. 10 per person."

Campa itself has begun to take price. On the Q2 CY26 call an analyst noted that Campa had cut its ₹10 pack from 200 ml to 150 ml while keeping 500 ml at ₹20. That is an implicit price rise of a third per millilitre on the ₹10 pack, and it is the first sign that the entry price was not sustainable at 200 ml. An illustrative calculation shows why: at ₹10 the brand-and-bottler side receives roughly ₹5.1 after GST and Campa's richer trade terms. At 200 ml that is about ₹146 per unit case; at 150 ml it is about ₹194 per case, well above VBL India's ₹169.6. The ₹10 tier only pays at 150 ml.

Who is paying: the evidence

The single most useful fact about the war came from Coca-Cola itself. In its Q2 2026 release, The Coca-Cola Company said its global unit case volume grew 5%, "led by India, China, the United States and Brazil", but that it "lost value share in total NARTD beverages, as gains in Japan and China were more than offset by a loss in India". Asia Pacific price/mix was −9%, which it attributed to "unfavorable mix and affordability initiatives". In the same period PepsiCo said its India share was "stable or better" for March–May 2026. Both claims are consistent only if a third party is taking value share, and Reliance's 2.4-times beverage growth says who. The regional B-brands are the other loser: VBL's chairman described them as being "cannibalised", and Bisleri now faces RCPL claiming to be the third-largest branded water player.

VBL's own numbers point the same way. Its India realisation per case did fall (−4% in Q4 CY25, −1.5% in Q1 CY26, −0.6% in Q2 CY26), but mostly because of the deliberate 400 ml upsizing and new price-point launches, and the decline narrowed each quarter as dairy, premium packs and reduced discounts offset it. Its India EBITDA margin rose 112 basis points in Q1 CY26 and 38 basis points in Q2 CY26. India volume grew 14.4% in both quarters. Tata Consumer's ready-to-drink business, which lost ground to Campa in 2024, grew 41% in Q1 FY27 on premiumisation and launches.

In plain terms

Reliance bought shelf space by paying shopkeepers more per bottle. Coca-Cola matched Campa on price and has been losing value share in India. VBL did not cut price; it gave more drink for the same ₹20, put more fridges in more shops, and its Indian profit margin went up. So far the people paying for Campa are Coca-Cola, the small regional brands and, through lower cola prices per millilitre, the industry's future pricing power, not VBL's current margins.

Is Campa growing the market or taking share?

The open question is whether Campa mostly recruits new drinkers (rural consumers, first-time buyers at ₹10, people switching from regional brands) or mostly takes share from the majors. The evidence so far favours the first. VBL's chairman estimated industry growth at "about 20% or so" in the first half of 2026, from VBL at about 15%, Campa at "50% or doubling" and Coca-Cola "also growing". He was clear he has no hard data. PepsiCo's stable-share claim and VBL's 14% India volume growth are hard to square with Campa taking large chunks of PepsiCo's business. On the other side, analysts on the call asked whether Campa's growth reflected VBL's territories having more rain, and management could not separate growth from Campa's "same territory or expanded territory".

Three things would change the picture. If Campa extends its trade-margin war to the ₹20 and 600 ml tiers where VBL earns its money, VBL's realisation would have to fall further. If Reliance, which built greenfield beverage lines in 12 states and calls one of them "one of the largest beverage manufacturing facilities in Asia", needs to fill that capacity, it may push harder on price. And Campa's cans (launched in India around July 2026, helped by its Australian Goodness Group acquisition) and Campa Energy push it into higher-value segments. On the other hand, Campa's shrink of the ₹10 pack shows Reliance is also managing economics, not chasing volume at any cost.

What the war does to the industry's economics

For the industry as a whole the war has three lasting effects. Price per millilitre has fallen: a 250 ml serving that cost ₹20 in 2023 now costs ₹10–12.5 in the mass tier. Trade margins have risen and will be hard to take back, because retailers now know what a challenger will pay. And investment in coolers and outlets has accelerated at all three systems, which raises the fixed-cost base and makes the weather even more important. The winner of a long war is likely to be the system with the lowest cost per case delivered into a cold fridge. On that measure VBL, with its scale, backward integration and new large plants, starts in the strongest position, and HCCB, at about half its margin, in a weaker one.

The key numbers to track: Campa's quarterly sales in Reliance's results (Q2 FY27, July–September 2026, was not yet out on 23 October 2026), VBL's India realisation per case (watch whether the decline turns positive), Coca-Cola's India commentary in its Q3 2026 results on 27 October 2026, and any move by Campa into the ₹20 and above tiers with richer trade terms. In plain terms

Imports, China and the input supply chain

Soft drinks are not a China story. Imports of finished drinks are small and concentrate is made in India. Imports matter mainly for inputs: some PET chips and their raw materials (PTA and MEG), aluminium can sheet, high-intensity sweeteners (sucralose, acesulfame-K and stevia, where China is the main exporter), and compressors for visi-coolers, which is why VBL set up the White Peak Refrigeration joint venture to localise cooler making. A weak rupee, at about ₹95 to the dollar in October 2026 and a record low near ₹96.8, raises the landed cost of these inputs, as the Confederation of Indian Alcoholic Beverage Companies pointed out for the overlapping packaging inputs of the liquor trade (see the alcohol report).

The structural story of 2023–26 has three strands. Coca-Cola is moving from owning its bottlers to franchising them, with VBL as the template (section 07). Reliance is using a retail-scale balance sheet and distribution to enter at ₹10 (section 05). And the tail is being squeezed between them. The likely end-state is a market where three systems hold most of organised volume, the B-brands shrink to niches, and the competition shifts from price points to cooler density, distribution reach and product range.

Why refranchising matters for VBL

Refranchising changes the competition VBL faces in three ways. First, family-owned franchise bottlers are hungrier locally than a corporate-owned bottler. They know their territory, make faster decisions on trade terms and coolers, and own their capital, which is exactly the formula that made VBL. In Rajasthan, Bihar, the North-East and Gujarat, VBL now competes with Kandhari, SLMG and Moon rather than with HCCB. Second, HCCB itself, freed of its loss-making territories and with a strategic Indian partner, may become a sharper competitor in the West and South. Third, there is talk of an HCCB listing: Coca-Cola has reportedly hired bankers and press reports mention a "$10 billion" valuation, with timing variously given as 2026 and 2027. No draft prospectus has been found, and the Jubilant price undercuts the $10 billion figure. A listed HCCB would give investors a second pure-play Indian bottler and a direct valuation comparison with VBL.

The read-across from Coca-Cola's own disclosures is consistent. Its Q2 2026 release said unit case volume was led by India and that its Bottling Investments segment grew volume 5%, "largely due to growth in India", which suggests HCCB is still consolidated as a 60% subsidiary and is growing. But it also said Coca-Cola lost value share in India, so the volume is being bought with price. The next update comes with Coca-Cola's Q3 2026 results on 27 October 2026, and HCCB's FY26 accounts, which CRISIL says were hit by unseasonal weather, are due from the Registrar of Companies in October–November.

The deeper lesson is about where value sits in a franchise system. A global brand owner earns its return on concentrate and brand. A bottler earns its return on utilisation, cost and route-to-market density. When a brand owner runs its own bottling in a hard, fragmented, seasonal market, it tends to earn the worse half of both businesses. PepsiCo's choice to make VBL its Indian bottler, and Coca-Cola's decision a decade later to follow, say that both brand owners now believe the bottling returns are best earned by a focused local operator. That belief, written into a contract to 2049, is the foundation of VBL's position, and the subject of section 08.

One bottler, one brand owner, 2049: inside the franchise model Deep dive

VBL does not own Pepsi, Sting or Aquafina. It owns the plants, the trucks, the fridges and a contract, now lengthened twice in four years, that gives it the right to bottle them.

The franchise bottler is the least understood business model in Indian consumer stocks. VBL's annual report calls PepsiCo the risk at the top of its register, and the relationship explains both its strengths and its limits. VBL calls itself PepsiCo's "second largest franchisee … in the world (outside US)" and the largest in India, a description CRISIL repeats in its April 2026 rationale. It handles more than 90% of PepsiCo India's beverage volume.

The limits of the model

The franchise model has costs as well as strengths. VBL cannot set national advertising or decide which products PepsiCo launches; if PepsiCo misjudges a category, VBL's plants carry the cost. It cannot control the concentrate price, which is usually set as a percentage of the bottler's net price or as a fixed amount per case. And the newly won freedom to sell non-PepsiCo products creates a potential conflict: CALPIS, Carlsberg beer and VBL's own brands use the same distributors and fridges that PepsiCo's brands depend on. Management has been careful so far ("our starting point is Calpis right now"; on alcohol, "too early" and "we are not looking at Bira"), and PepsiCo approved the change.

There is also a governance dimension that the market discounts. VBL is controlled by the Jaipuria family's RJ Corp (promoters hold 59.43%), which also controls Devyani International, the KFC and Pizza Hut franchisee and a QSR fountain customer of VBL. Related-party flows are small but rising: the 2025 annual report shows sales to Devyani group entities of ₹48.6 crore (5.5 times the prior year), ₹46.3 crore of CSR routed through the director-linked RJ Foundation, and closures bought from the director-linked Alisha Torrent Closures. In July 2026 VBL agreed to buy the business of Devyani Food Industries (Kenya), a promoter-group company, for US$32 million (about ₹305 crore) at a valuer's net-asset value. Related-party purchases are 3.2% of total purchases and individually immaterial; it is the pattern that investors watch.

In plain terms

The franchise model gives VBL a famous brand without the cost of building one, and the right to it for 23 more years. In exchange VBL takes the factory risk, the weather risk and the price-war risk, pays an undisclosed amount for the syrup, and depends on PepsiCo's choices about products and advertising. Its new freedom to sell other companies' drinks is useful but has to be used without upsetting the partner that supplies most of its sales.

The comparison with other Indian franchise models is instructive. A quick-service restaurant franchisee such as Devyani International also rents a global brand, but its contract terms and royalties are disclosed. A bottler's are not, because the concentrate is a purchased input rather than a royalty. That opacity is why VBL's gross margin is the best available proxy for its terms with PepsiCo: if gross margin holds near 55% as input costs move, the concentrate price is behaving.

Why the margin held

First, inventory: VBL stocked key raw materials early, before the season, and values inventory at weighted-average cost rather than first-in-first-out, so price spikes enter the profit and loss gradually. Inventory on the balance sheet rose from ₹2,952 crore in December 2025 to ₹3,990 crore in June 2026. Sugar, sweeteners and the next input risk

Sugar was the input that defined bottler margins a decade ago. India's sugar supply depends on the monsoon, cane prices set by government, export policy and diversion of cane to ethanol. ISMA's first estimate for the 2025-26 season was 343.5 lakh tonnes gross and 309.5 lakh tonnes net of about 34 lakh tonnes of ethanol diversion; later reports of a cut to about 306 lakh tonnes appear to be on the net basis. Exports were banned from May 2026, and ex-mill prices in Uttar Pradesh were about ₹5,400 a quintal in August (secondary data; no official series was fetched). For VBL, sugar is now a second-order cost because the low- and no-sugar recipes replace much of it with high-intensity sweeteners. That shifts the risk to sweeteners, mostly imported from China, and to regulation of them (section 20).

The recycled-PET tailwind

One cost trend runs the other way. Reliance, India's largest PET maker, reported that domestic PET demand fell 3.7% in Q1 FY27 on "cautious downstream procurement and higher r-PET substitution". VBL used about 22,000 tonnes of recycled PET in 2025 and has a joint venture with Indorama for recycled resin; its target is 50% recycled content by 2030 (the risk table in the same annual report says 30%, an internal inconsistency). Under the Plastic Waste Management Rules brand owners must use rising shares of recycled content in rigid plastic packaging. Recycled resin is not always cheaper than virgin, but it diversifies supply away from crude. The plastics and glass reports cover the packaging industries on the other side of these contracts.

The bottom line on costs: VBL's model of early stocking, in-house packaging and de-sugaring has made its gross margin far steadier than its volume. The test is Q3 CY26, when the remaining higher-cost inventory flows through the P&L. Management's claim is that "we can comfortably maintain our margins even in a year which is the worst year for the geopolitical reasons", and that when the war's effects fade "our margins cannot get worse. It will only get better."

What filling the plants should do to cash and returns

The arithmetic of the next two years is favourable if volume cooperates. One cash metric has moved the wrong way. Screener's working-capital days went from −34 in CY21 to −24, −9, +16 and +31 in CY25. The 40% slab and the label police: tax and regulation

GST 2.0 left colas and energy drinks, sugared or not, at 40% and cut water, milk drinks and juice drinks to 5%. The tax wedge now does more to shape the product range than any health campaign.

Soft drinks carry one of the heaviest indirect-tax burdens of any Indian consumer product. Under the original GST of July 2017, aerated drinks paid 28% GST plus a 12% compensation cess, 40% in all. The 56th GST Council meeting on 3 September 2025 approved a restructuring, notified as Notification 9/2025-Integrated Tax (Rate) on 17 September and effective from 22 September 2025, that abolished the cess and created a 40% "demerit" slab alongside the main 5% and 18% slabs. For colas and energy drinks the effective rate did not change. For several adjacent categories it changed a great deal.

Why zero sugar does not mean low tax

The most important detail is in the wording. HS 2202 10 covers waters "containing added sugar or other sweetening matter or flavoured", so a zero-sugar cola sweetened with sucralose is taxed exactly like a full-sugar one. India's slab is not a sugar tax; it is a tax on sweetened or flavoured fizzy drinks. The industry had lobbied for a lower slab for low- and no-sugar drinks, and did not get it. VBL's shift to about 73% low- and no-sugar volume therefore brings it no tax relief. Its value is lower sugar cost and insurance against future health regulation. Meanwhile water, milk drinks and juice drinks fell to 5%, a wedge of 35 percentage points. That wedge, more than health, is the strongest commercial reason for VBL's push into value-added dairy (growing above 40%), CALPIS and juice drinks. Classification matters at the margin: a lemon drink such as Nimbooz is taxed according to its carbonation and juice content, and the "Nimbooz Masala Soda" carbonated variant is in the 40% slab.

In plain terms

Since September 2025, a bottle of flavoured milk or mango drink pays 5% tax and a cola pays 40%, even if the cola has no sugar at all. At the same ₹20 price, the milk drink leaves about ₹4.75 more for the company and the shopkeeper. That is why every big beverage company is suddenly keen on dairy and juice drinks.

VBL said the GST change had a "very minimal" effect on its business; it had to raise some prices because of West Asia costs and then restored the old prices. The annual report's risk register is more pointed: it lists "regulatory" risk including products "targeted for discriminatory tax". The global example is Mexico, which raised its excise tax on sugary drinks for 2026, as Coca-Cola FEMSA noted on its Q4 2025 call. India has no state-level sugar-sweetened beverage tax.

The "energy" label order

The second big regulatory event of 2026 hit energy drinks. On about 1 July 2026 FSSAI told makers of high-caffeine beverages, naming Red Bull, Sting, Adrenaline Rush, Campa Gold Boost, Hell Energy and Monster, to drop the word "energy" from labels, marketing and advertising within 90 days, because "energy drink" is not a standardised category under food law; it suggested "caffeinated beverage" and also flagged claims such as "vitalises body and mind". Caffeine has been capped at 300 mg per litre, with mandatory disclosure, since 2016, and an April 2024 FSSAI communication had allowed "energy drinks" for products licensed as caffeinated beverages. Red Bull challenged the order in the Delhi High Court and was denied interim relief on 28 September 2026 (press reports). PepsiCo is removing "energy" from Sting. VBL described a "temporary effect" in June and July, with volume shifting to other CSDs, and said that with the new labels "the volumes are coming back". The primary FSSAI order could not be retrieved for this report, so its exact wording rests on press coverage and VBL's call.

Plastic, water and labour

Three other rule-sets shape costs. Under the Plastic Waste Management Rules and extended producer responsibility, brand owners and bottlers must collect and recycle PET and use rising shares of recycled content in rigid packaging; FSSAI has permitted food-grade recycled PET. VBL says it recycled 100% of the 2,08,080 tonnes of PET it placed on the market in 2025. Groundwater permissions and local water stress have shaped plant siting since the Plachimada (Kerala) dispute closed a Coca-Cola plant in 2004; VBL reports a water-use ratio of 1.50 litres per litre of drink in 2025 with a 2030 target of 1.40. Packaged drinking water needs BIS certification and, since October 2024, is classed as a "high-risk food" requiring annual third-party audits. The Labour Codes notified on 21 November 2025 had no material incremental impact, according to VBL.

On 18 June 2026 it signed an alliance with Asahi to make and sell CALPIS, a Japanese fermented-milk drink (Original and Mango variants planned); management said "our starting point is Calpis right now" and that it has made no decision on other Asahi products or on ready-to-drink tea and coffee. Its competitors are formidable: Amul Kool from GCMMF, Cavin's from CavinKare, Heritage, Hatsun, Nestlé, and now Reliance's Joyland, launched in Tamil Nadu. In South Africa the failed Crickley Dairy deal leaves VBL without a local dairy manufacturer.

In plain terms

VBL is still mostly a cola company, but it is reformulating colas to use less sugar and growing faster in flavoured milk, lemon drinks and juices. Those drinks pay 5% GST instead of 40%, sell for more per litre, and are less exposed to the Campa price war. They are still small, about a sixteenth of volume, so they will take years to change the overall numbers.

Energy: the growth engine that hit a label

Mass-priced energy drinks were the fastest-growing CSD segment of 2022–25, led by PepsiCo's Sting, launched at ₹20 for 250 ml against ₹100–125 for Red Bull and Monster. VBL does not disclose Sting's share of its volume (a figure of about 15% circulates but is an estimate). The FSSAI order and the can shortage in Q1–Q2 CY26 interrupted the run: VBL called the label effect "temporary" and said Sting Classic, launched in cans and from April in PET, and Adrenaline Rush at ₹60 were doing well. The competitive set is wider than it was: Coca-Cola's Charged, Campa Energy, Hell, and imports of canned energy and functional drinks (HS 2202 10 imports rose five-fold in FY26). Energy is also where Indian GST hits hardest (40%) and where health regulation could tighten most.

Beer: the option nobody is pricing

The SPV-clause removal and VBL's amended Memorandum open a door into alcoholic beverages. VBL signed an exclusive Carlsberg agreement to test-market beer in some African subsidiaries in CY25. The promoter group hired a former Diageo executive "for the group … not alcohol only", and management said on the Q2 CY26 call that any move is "too early" and that it is "not looking at Bira". Indian beer is a tightly regulated, state-by-state business (see the alcohol report for how excise and state pricing work); a bottler's distribution and cold chain are relevant, but the licences, excise and pricing are not. For now this is optionality with governance questions attached, not part of the numbers.

Two numbers summarise the mix story for an analyst: the low/no-sugar share (near saturation at about 73%, so no longer a source of cost improvement) and the non-carbonated share of volume (5.9% in CY25, 6.4% in Q2 CY26). If the second reaches 10% within a few years at two to three times the realisation per case, it would lift India realisation by several rupees a case without any price increase. That is the most plausible route back to positive India realisation growth while the ₹10 war continues.

Fifty years, seven shocks: how the industry got here

From the 1977 exit of Coca-Cola to the 2026 war year, India's soft-drink history is a story of protected brands, returning multinationals, bottler consolidation and, now, a domestic challenger with a retail empire behind it.

India's soft-drink cycle is not a price cycle in the way steel or cement have one. It is a weather and competitive-intensity cycle layered on top of steady per-capita growth. Good years (2022, 2024) see India volume grow 25–40% at the leading bottler; bad years (2025) see 2%. Margins are steadier: VBL's EBITDA margin moved only between 18.6% and 23.5% over 2020–25. The big structural shifts came from policy (FERA in 1977, liberalisation in 1991, GST in 2017 and 2025) and from ownership changes (Coca-Cola's return, PepsiCo's refranchising to VBL, Reliance's purchase of Campa, Coca-Cola's refranchising).

The super-cycle of 2021–24 and what ended it

The period from 2021 to 2024 was the best in the industry's history for the leading bottler. In plain terms

India's soft-drink industry has been reshaped every decade or so by a policy change or an ownership change. The current shake-up is the biggest since Coca-Cola came back in 1993: Reliance has revived Campa, Coca-Cola is selling its bottling to local families, and VBL has locked in PepsiCo until 2049. Through it all, the weather has decided the good and bad years.

Two lessons from the history matter for investors. First, shocks to sentiment (the 2003 pesticide reports, the 2025 rain, the 2026 Campa scare) have tended to be shorter-lived than shocks to structure (Coca-Cola's exit and return, PepsiCo's refranchising). The present moment contains both: a weather and sentiment shock in 2025, and a structural shift in competition with Reliance's entry and Coca-Cola's refranchising. Second, the winners of each structural shift were the operators who owned distribution and capacity when the shift came: Parle in the 1980s, the bottlers Coca-Cola bought in the 1990s, VBL in the 2010s. The question for the 2020s is whether Reliance's retail reach becomes the equivalent asset.

Reading a bottler: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fifteen numbers explain a soft-drink bottler. Most of them are hidden inside one equation: cases times rupees per case, minus what each case costs, minus the cost of plants that sit half-empty for eight months a year.

Every bottler in the world, from VBL in India to Coca-Cola FEMSA in Mexico, runs on the same equation. Revenue is the number of unit cases sold multiplied by the net rupees realised per case, plus a little non-beverage revenue (snacks, preforms, scrap). EBITDA is that revenue minus the variable cost of each case (materials, freight, trade spend) minus the fixed cost of the system (plants, salaried staff, coolers, overheads). Profit after tax then depends on how much capital the system ties up (depreciation and interest) and the tax rate. The masterclass below takes each term in turn.

Why it matters now. VBL's spare capacity is both a cost and an option. The cost is visible: depreciation rose 28% in CY25 and 33.6% in Q2 CY26, faster than revenue, so profit after tax lags EBITDA until volume catches up. Against ₹178.8 of revenue per case, each rupee invested has so far added about ₹0.7 of annual revenue. Drivers. Greenfield versus brownfield, acquisitions, cooler intensity, utilisation. Benchmark. Definition trap. VBL quotes capex as "net capitalised" (assets put into use), so CY25's ~₹4,500 crore includes ~₹1,650 crore spent in CY24; cash capex in the cash-flow statement was ₹2,725 crore. Market infrastructure (coolers, crates, vehicles, about ₹400 crore in H1 CY26) is recurring capex that grows with outlets, not a one-off.

11. Return on capital employed

What it is. Operating profit as a share of the capital tied up in the business. Formula. EBIT ÷ (equity + debt), screener basis. Worked example. CY25 EBIT of roughly ₹3,830 crore (₹5,049 crore EBITDA − ₹1,216 crore depreciation) against capital employed of about ₹19,000–21,000 crore gives about 19–20%. Drivers. Utilisation, margin, capex timing, cash held (the 2024 QIP). Benchmark. Above 25% good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak; global bottlers typically earn 10–15% on invested capital. VBL: 29% (CY23) → 20% (CY25); 19.7% current. Definition trap. Cash raised but not yet spent depresses ROCE without saying anything about operations; HCCB and the franchise bottlers do not publish comparable figures.

The QIP arithmetic. The ₹7,500 crore raised in November 2024 explains much of the ROCE fall. Added to capital employed of roughly ₹12,000–13,000 crore before the issue, it would cut ROCE by about a third on unchanged profit, even though it was used to repay debt that had cost interest. The India standalone ROCE of 19% in CY25 also carries the four new greenfields, which earned only part of a season. If India EBIT grows in line with volume over two normal summers and capex stays near guidance, standalone ROCE should move back into the mid-20s (an indicative projection). The group figure will lag because South Africa carries the acquired goodwill (₹823 crore at June 2026, up from ₹354 crore after Twizza).

12. Low- and no-sugar share of volume

What it is. The share of cases sold as reduced- or no-sugar recipes. Formula. Low/no-sugar cases ÷ total cases. Worked example. H1 CY26: about 73% of 830 million cases, about 606 million. Drivers. Reformulation of regular SKUs, Zero launches, regulation, sugar prices. Benchmark. Above 60% good, 40–60% average, below 40% weak (indicative). VBL group 73%; India about 45% at 9M CY25 before the latest conversion. Definition trap. The 73% is consolidated, not India. And low sugar does not lower GST in India (section 12); its value is cost and regulatory insurance. Near saturation, it is no longer a lever for further margin gains.

The share still matters as a risk indicator. If India ever adopts a sugar-based tax, as several countries have, a high share would become a tax advantage; if regulators instead turn on high-intensity sweeteners, the same share becomes a liability. Watch it alongside the regulatory calendar rather than the cost line.

Drivers. Cooler capex, electrification, distributor economics, trade terms. Benchmark. Outlet growth above 10% a year is strong. 14. Working capital: inventory, receivables and deposits

What it is. Cash tied up in stock and customer credit, net of supplier credit and distributor deposits. Formula. (Inventory + receivables − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example. Inventory rose from ₹2,952 crore (December 2025) to ₹3,990 crore (June 2026) as raw materials were stocked against the war; receivables rose from ₹1,249 crore to ₹1,648 crore. Screener's working-capital days went from −34 (CY21) to +31 (CY25). Drivers. Pre-season stocking, Africa's six-month inventory, modern trade and quick commerce credit. Benchmark. Negative is good for a bottler, 0–30 days average, above 30 weak. Definition trap. June balances are seasonally high (stock for the peak, receivables from peak sales); compare June with June and December with December.

15. Concentrate cost and the brand owner's share

What it is. What the bottler pays the brand owner for syrup, and so how the system's profit is split. Formula. Concentrate purchases ÷ net revenue or per case. Worked example. Not possible from public data. An illustrative range is ₹13–16 per ₹100 of VBL revenue, or about ₹3 on a ₹20 bottle. Drivers. The contract (usually a percentage of the bottler's price or a fixed amount per case), category mix, and periodic renegotiation. Benchmark. None public. VBL: not disclosed. PepsiCo is not a related party, so the annual report is silent; TradeStat shows concentrate is made in India (imports of ₹37 crore), so customs data offer no proxy either. The best indirect evidence is Coca-Cola India Pvt Ltd's FY25 accounts: ₹5,043 crore of revenue and ₹615 crore of profit for the Coke concentrate business, against a Coke-system bottler revenue base of perhaps ₹20,000–22,000 crore (indicative). If PepsiCo's terms are similar, concentrate would be roughly a quarter of bottler revenue, toward the top of the materials range used above. Definition trap. Do not infer the concentrate price from gross margin changes alone: sugar, PET and mix move gross margin far more in any one year.

In plain terms

A modern bottling plant costs about ₹425 crore. Run at the level VBL's Indian plants seem to run at, it earns roughly a quarter of its cost each year before tax and pays for itself in about three years. If a rainy summer or a price war takes a quarter of its volume away, its return halves. If a hot summer fills it, the return jumps by half. Few businesses are this sensitive to a single variable that nobody controls.

What the model teaches. Three lessons carry over to the real company. First, the fixed-cost share is large: in the model, about ₹94 crore of the ₹377 crore of cash costs at 60% utilisation does not move with volume, so a 25% volume shortfall cuts EBITDA by 43%. That is the mechanism behind VBL's Q4 margin of 15%. Second, the return on a new plant is excellent only if it fills; at 45% it barely beats the cost of capital. Building ahead of demand, as VBL did in 2022–25, is a bet on future summers. Third, realisation and materials move together more than they appear to: a 400 ml upsizing cuts realisation per case but also cuts packaging cost per litre, so EBITDA per case can hold even as revenue per case falls. When reading VBL's results, the model suggests the question to ask of every quarter is not "did margin rise?" but "how full were the plants?"

Definition traps, collected

  • Calendar vs March year. VBL Q2 CY26 (Apr–Jun) = peers' Q1 FY27. Never compare VBL CY25 with a peer's FY25 without saying so.
  • Unit cases are litres in 8-oz equivalents. Upsizing inflates "volume" without more transactions; water and Sting count the same per litre.
  • Two realisation bases. Total-revenue basis (CY25 ₹178.8) and beverage-only basis (Q2 CY26 ₹175.3) differ by ₹6–8 a case.
  • Net, gross and "sales". VBL net revenue is after GST and excise; the annual report's geographic split is gross; Campa's ₹4,700 crore is gross sales.
  • Standalone ≈ India, not equal. Indian non-beverage sales and head-office costs sit in standalone, so regional per-case profits are indicative.
  • Acquisitions inflate growth. BevCo in CY24, Twizza from 18 March 2026, Kenya pending; quote organic and India growth alongside.
  • Net debt excludes leases. Company ₹373 crore vs ~₹914 crore including leases; "net-debt-free" applies to India only.
  • Capex capitalised vs cash. CY25 ~₹4,500 crore capitalised vs ₹2,725 crore cash.
  • EBITDA bases. Company ₹5,049 crore (23.3%); screener OP ₹5,070 crore; CRISIL 24.5% adjusted.
  • Low/no-sugar 73% is group, not India, and earns no GST relief.
  • Market shares are claims. No audited series exists; every share in this report is an estimate or a company claim.
  • Never annualise a quarter. Q2 carries ~32% of volume and ~39% of operating profit.
In plain terms

Most of the mistakes people make about Indian soft drinks come from mixing up two calendars, two ways of counting revenue per case, and group numbers with Indian numbers. If you check the months, the basis and the geography before you compare, you avoid nearly all of them.

How to read a bottler's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the calendar and the weather first. Which months does the quarter cover, and what did IMD say about rain and heat in those months? A Q2 (Apr–Jun) after a wet pre-monsoon will be weak whatever management does.
  2. Pull total volume and its growth, then split India and international. For international, subtract acquisitions (Twizza 11.8 million cases in Q2 CY26) to get organic growth. Red flag: international growth that is all acquisition.
  3. Read the India realisation per case and its basis. Is it the beverage-only table or total revenue ÷ cases? Is the change due to upsizing, mix or discounting? Red flag: realisation falling while EBITDA per case also falls.
  4. Check gross margin against last year's same quarter. Ask what happened to PET, sugar and freight in the prior two quarters, given average-cost inventory. Red flag: a drop of more than 100 basis points with no commodity explanation (possible concentrate or mix issue).
  5. Read EBITDA margin for the group and the change for India. VBL discloses the India margin change (+38 basis points in Q2 CY26). Red flag: India margin falling while volume grows (price war reaching VBL).
  6. Compute EBITDA per case, group and regional. Use standalone versus consolidated for a rough India–international split. Red flag: international per-case profit falling after a deal.
  7. Look below EBITDA. Depreciation (+33.6% in Q2 CY26) and finance cost (+55.8%) are rising faster than revenue as plants and Twizza come in. Red flag: PAT growth well below EBITDA growth for several quarters.
  8. Scan the balance sheet. Net debt (company basis and including leases), inventory and receivables versus the same month last year, capex and capital work in progress. Red flag: inventory and receivables growing much faster than revenue.
  9. Check mix and category disclosures. Low/no-sugar share, CSD/NCB/water split, dairy and energy commentary, snacks. Red flag: NCB share stalling while management talks up dairy.
  10. Read the competitor and regulatory context. Same-period Campa sales from Reliance, Coca-Cola's India commentary, any FSSAI or GST news, and related-party items in the notes. Red flag: new related-party deals or management exits without explanation.

Done in that order, the ten steps separate what the weather did, what the competition did and what the company did, which is the whole point of reading a bottler's quarter. Lessons from other markets

Mexico, the world's heaviest soft-drink market (its per-capita consumption is often cited above 150 litres a year, an indicative secondary figure), shows two things. First, that consumption can rise very far in a hot, middle-income country with ubiquitous small shops, which is the long-run bull case for India. Second, that governments eventually tax it: Mexico has taxed sugary drinks since 2014 and raised the excise again for 2026. Europe shows the end-state of a mature market: volumes growing 2–3%, value growth from price and premium packs, and bottlers valued at 15–20 times earnings. South Africa, where VBL now operates, shows the middle path: a sugar levy since 2018, consolidated bottling, and a market about to be dominated by two large bottlers, VBL's BevCo-Twizza and Coca-Cola HBC's CCBA.

In plain terms

Abroad, soft drinks are a slow, steady business: volumes rise a few per cent a year and bottlers are valued at about 15 to 20 times profit. India is valued very differently because people here drink so little today and the best bottler is unusually efficient. The question for investors is how long India can keep growing much faster than the rest of the world before it starts to look like Mexico or Europe.

Technology and disruption: sweeteners, plastic, apps and a retailer's brand

The threats to the bottler model are not new machines. They are new rules on what goes in the bottle and what the bottle is made of, new channels that weaken the bottler's grip on price, and a challenger that owns the shelf.

Soft-drink manufacturing is mature technology: high-speed blow-moulding and filling lines, water treatment, carbonation, and the backward-integrated packaging that VBL has made a competitive edge. Disruption comes from elsewhere. Nine forces could change the industry's economics over the next decade, in roughly descending order of importance for VBL.

  • Retailer-owned brands. Reliance's Campa is the most important disruption: a challenger with its own retail network, a large balance sheet and a willingness to run at low margin to buy shelf space. If it extends to the ₹20 tier, the industry's pricing changes.
  • Sweetener regulation. With about 73% of volume low- or no-sugar, VBL's risk has moved from sugar to sweeteners. The WHO's 2023 guidance against non-sugar sweeteners for weight control and IARC's 2023 classification of aspartame as "possibly carcinogenic" show where regulators could go. If India labels or restricts sweeteners, the reformulation becomes a liability.
  • Front-of-pack and marketing rules. FSSAI's draft star-rating for foods high in fat, sugar and salt (2022) is not final; warning labels or limits on marketing to children would weigh on CSD and energy.
  • Packaging mandates. Recycled-content targets under the plastic rules, possible deposit-return schemes and single-use plastic bans raise costs; cans and glass cost more per litre than PET. VBL used about 22,000 tonnes of recycled PET in 2025, about 11% of its 2,08,080 tonnes of PET bottles, against a 50% target for 2030.
  • Quick commerce and modern trade. Ready-to-drink sales on quick-commerce apps grew more than 100% (Redseer), and quick-commerce grocery may grow from about $4 billion to $25 billion by 2030. These channels favour chilled single-serve and premium SKUs, but they pay more slowly than kirana distributors (VBL's debtor days rose from 8 to 21 over CY23–25) and give the platform, not the bottler, control of price display.
  • Energy-drink regulation. The 2026 FSSAI order is the first step; a formal "caffeinated beverage" standard could restrict claims, packs or sales to minors.
  • Climate volatility. More unseasonal rain and shifting peaks (May no longer about 15% of the year), plus water stress at plants.
  • Logistics and cold chain. Positives: VBL has 2,000-plus electric vehicles in trade, low-global-warming R290 coolers and a cooler-making joint venture. Renewable power was 21% of its energy in CY25 against a 30% target for 2030, and it is taking 26% of Jager Renewables for captive solar.
  • Franchise conflict. The removal of the SPV clause lets VBL sell non-PepsiCo products (CALPIS, Carlsberg in Africa, own brands, Kenya), which could in principle create conflicts with PepsiCo over shelf and cooler space.

The danger for soft-drink companies is less a new invention than new rules: on sweeteners, warning labels, plastic and energy drinks. Bull versus bear: the case for and against Indian soft drinks

The bull case is the litres gap, an efficient bottler with a 23-year licence, and plants that are already built. The bear case is the weather, Reliance, and a valuation far above what anyone has paid for an Indian bottler in private.

The bull case

  • Structural runway: 15–20 litres per person against 70–80 in China and 100–120 in the US; management expects India to grow "double digits for the next 5–10 years at least".
  • A locked-in franchise: the PepsiCo India licence runs to 30 April 2049 and now allows non-PepsiCo products.
  • The best operator in the market: India operating margin about 26% against about 13% at HCCB; gross margin held at 55% through the 2026 cost shock.
  • The plants are built: India capacity up ~45% (CY22→CY24) plus four greenfields; CY26 India capex guided below ₹500–600 crore; FCF turned positive (₹797 crore) and should rise.
  • The war is costing others: Coca-Cola lost India value share in Q2 2026 while VBL's India margin rose in Q1 and Q2 CY26.
  • Mix tailwinds: dairy (+40%), hydration and juice drinks in the 5% GST slab; ~73% low/no-sugar.
  • A dry monsoon: 87% of normal in 2026, a setup for a strong Q3 CY26.

The bear case

  • Weather hostage: CY25 India volume +2.2% on 142%-of-normal pre-monsoon rain; one lost peak month costs an estimated 7–9% of annual EBITDA.
  • Reliance: Campa's beverages grew 2.4× in Q1 FY27; India realisation per case has fallen for three quarters; a move to ₹20 trade terms would hit VBL directly.
  • The valuation gap: 39× earnings and 5.5× sales against ~2.3× sales for HCCB in the Jubilant deal and 15–20× earnings for global bottlers.
  • Africa: about ₹10 less profit per case than India, volatile currencies, sugar taxes, a lapsed dairy deal and a stronger Coke bottler arriving with CCH–CCBA.
  • Costs in Q3: "the balance" of West Asia–era input costs lands in Jul–Sep; fuel is unhedgeable.
  • Regulation: 40% GST even on zero-sugar; the FSSAI energy order on Sting; possible sweetener or labelling rules.
  • Governance signals: related-party deals with the promoter group (Devyani Kenya, US$32 mn), COO exit in September 2026, alcobev optionality.
