Indian gaming: a ban at home, a market abroad

A complete guide to the business of games in India after the August 2025 prohibition of real-money gaming: how a free-to-play game turns installs into revenue, why the world's largest download market earns less than one per cent of global games revenue, what the 28% GST ruling and the Online Gaming Act did to a ₹27,000 crore industry, and how Nazara Technologies, the only listed pure-play, is turning itself into a global casual-games operator that earns 87% of its revenue outside India.

00

The one-page view: one law split the industry in two

India's largest gaming revenue pool was abolished in a week. What survives is smaller, global and run on a different arithmetic.

Until August 2025 the Indian games business was, in money terms, a real-money gaming business. Fantasy sports, rummy, poker and ludo-for-stakes earned an estimated ₹27,438 crore in 2024, or 85.7% of the industry's ₹31,938 crore of revenue by the WinZO–IEIC count. Dream11's parent made ₹1,295 crore of profit in FY24; Gameskraft made ₹947 crore. Then Parliament passed the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 (PROGA) in two days, the President signed it on 22 August, and every large operator switched off paid play within days, months before the Act formally came into force on 1 May 2026. Dream Sports' chief executive said the company lost "95% of revenues and 100% of profits".

The ban did not arrive out of nowhere. From 1 October 2023 the GST Council had already moved real-money games from 18% tax on the platform's commission to 28% on the full value of every deposit, roughly an eightfold increase in tax per rupee staked. Tax officers then issued retrospective notices for earlier years; by January 2025 there were 91 show-cause notices worth more than ₹1.44 lakh crore. On 27 May 2026 the Supreme Court, in DGGI v Gameskraft Technologies, upheld the 28% levy and its retrospective reach. The industry that the Act abolished is now being asked to pay tax bills that are larger than its remaining cash.

What remains is a different industry. Lumikai, the gaming venture fund, sizes the Indian "video games" market excluding real-money gaming at about US$1.5 billion in calendar 2025 (roughly ₹14,000 crore, indicative conversion), growing 17% a year. It has 555 million players, a quarter of whom pay something, and it is dominated by global franchises: all ten of India's top-grossing games are foreign intellectual property, led by Krafton's BGMI and Garena's Free Fire Max. India installs more games than any other country, about 8.45 billion downloads in FY25, yet earns about 0.74% of the US$201.6 billion the world spent on games in 2025.

That gap explains the listed universe. Nazara Technologies (NSE: NAZARA, BSE: 543280) is India's only listed gaming pure-play, and it earned 87.3% of its FY26 revenue outside India: 41.9% in the US and 22.4% in the UK. Its products are a Love Island narrative game, the Kiddopia pre-school subscription app, Animal Jam, the PC hit Human: Fall Flat, and from Q2 FY27 the Barcelona-based casual publisher Bluetile, bought for a fixed US$303 million (about ₹2,900 crore, indicative) in cash. Its one big bet on Indian real-money gaming, a 46.07% stake in PokerBaazi's parent Moonshine that cost ₹1,058.5 crore, has been written off in full.

Five forces define the sub-sector, and this report is built around them. First, prohibition of the biggest revenue pool, with the constitutional challenge (Head Digital Works v Union of India, T.C.(C) 133/2025) still pending before a bench led by the Chief Justice and no hearing date fixed. Second, tax as the precursor shock and the retrospective bill that outlives the business. Third, a small surviving market that is global-IP-dominated and in-app-purchase-led, where an Indian player is worth about US$2.7 a year against about US$56 globally. Fourth, M&A-led platform building: Nazara has deployed roughly ₹5,500–6,000 crore of capital in five years, and the economics of its new core business are user-acquisition arbitrage, with Bluetile spending 85% of its revenue on paid installs. Fifth, accounting complexity and currency: Nazara's FY26 profit contains a ₹1,098 crore revaluation gain, a ₹915 crore impairment and ₹278 crore of associates' losses, and its earnings move with the dollar and the pound far more than with Indian demand.

In plain terms

Indian gaming used to make most of its money by taking a cut of what players bet against each other. That model is now illegal. The part that is left sells game items, subscriptions and advertising, mostly in foreign-made games. The only listed company, Nazara, escaped the ban largely because it already earned almost all its money abroad, but it still lost the ₹1,058.5 crore it had put into a poker company.

For an investor, the practical conclusions are three. First, "Indian gaming" is no longer a single investable theme: the domestic market is growing at about 17% a year from a small base, but most of the money flows to foreign publishers, and no Indian-owned studio of scale reports audited India numbers. Second, the listed exposure is really an exposure to US and UK consumer spending on casual and kids' games, to paid-install economics, and to the rupee. Third, Nazara trades at about 62 times its FY26 company EBITDA on a trailing basis and about 35 times on an ILLUSTRATIVE pro-forma that adds Bluetile, while global casual and mid-core peers such as Playtika, Embracer and Krafton trade at 4–5 times. The premium is for scarcity and for a turnaround that the first Bluetile quarter, reported on 26 October 2026, will begin to test.

01

How a game makes money: one value chain, seven business models

The same phone screen hosts businesses with margins from 3% to 40% and with opposite relationships to the law.

A modern game is usually free to download. The developer or publisher earns money in three ways: it sells things inside the game (in-app purchases, or IAP, such as currency, passes and cosmetic items), it sells a subscription, or it shows advertisements, especially "rewarded" video ads that a player chooses to watch in exchange for an in-game reward. A minority of games still sell as a one-time purchase on PC or console. Real-money games worked differently: the operator ran a contest in which players staked money against each other and kept a commission, usually 10–20% of the entry fees, called the rake or platform fee. That difference, between selling entertainment and taking a cut of wagers, is exactly the line PROGA drew.

Value pools at Step 3 (platform fees) and Step 4 (ad networks) are the safest; the studio keeps what is left after both. Pre-ban, money-game platforms bypassed Step 3 (mostly sideloaded apps) and owned the wallet, which is why they were so profitable.

Who captures value along that chain is the first thing to understand. Apple and Google take 15–30% of every in-app rupee and bear no hit risk. The ad networks that sell installs (Meta, Google, AppLovin, Unity) take the largest single slice of a casual game's revenue: Bluetile spent 85% of its Q1 FY27 (Jun-26) revenue on user acquisition. A studio that owns a hit franchise earns 25–40% EBITDA margins (Nazara's Curve Games made 38.9% in FY26; Tencent and NetEase earn about 37% across their whole businesses); a middling studio earns 10–20%; an advertising reseller earns 5–6%. Before the ban the money-game platforms earned the most of all in India, because they needed no expensive IP, paid no store fee and captured the cash float of players' wallets.

In plain terms

A games company earns well when it owns something players love and do not need to be re-bought every month, such as a hit PC game or a beloved kids' world. It earns thinly when it must keep buying its customers through advertising (casual games), when it mostly passes other people's advertising money through its books (ad-tech), or when it rents stadiums and pays talent for one-off events (esports).

Two features of the money flow matter for everything that follows. First, the payer and the player are often different people: the advertiser pays for a rewarded video in a free game, and a parent pays for Kiddopia. Second, almost all costs are incurred before revenue arrives. A studio pays developers for 12–18 months before launch, and a publisher pays for an install today against revenue that trickles in over the following year. That is why the native KPIs of this sub-sector (retention, return on ad spend, lifetime value against acquisition cost) are about time and cohorts rather than about volume and price. The adjacent reports on software products and e-retail face the same "pay now, earn later" shape of customer acquisition; games simply run it at a faster, more volatile speed.

02

Three numbers for one market

Before the ban, about ₹30,000 crore; after it, about ₹13,000–14,000 crore and growing 17%. Never compare one with the other.

The most common error in writing about Indian gaming is to compare a pre-ban number with a post-ban number and conclude that the market shrank by 60%. It did not, or at least not in that sense. The pre-ban figures include real-money gaming revenue, which the law abolished; the post-ban figures exclude it, and on a like-for-like basis the surviving market grew. Three different measurement traditions exist, and each is correct about something different.

Including real-money gaming (pre-ban). The WinZO–IEIC report of March 2025 put 2024 industry revenue at ₹31,938 crore, of which real-money gaming was ₹27,438 crore, or 85.7%. FICCI–EY, the media-and-entertainment industry's standard reference, put online gaming at ₹23,200 crore for 2024, 77% of it "transaction-based", and PIB repeated that figure in its April 2026 backgrounder on the Rules. Lumikai's older fiscal-year series put FY24 at US$3.8 billion. The difference between ₹23,200 crore and ₹31,938 crore is mainly how each source measures money-game revenue: gross gaming revenue (the rake before promotional bonuses) or net revenue after them.

Excluding real-money gaming (post-ban). Lumikai's calendar-2025 report (March 2026) put the Indian "video games" market at US$1.5 billion, up 17%, after removing a money-gaming category it sized at US$2.3 billion. It forecasts US$3.2 billion by 2030, with in-app purchases growing 19% a year and advertising 13%. FICCI–EY's 2026 report says video games fell 17% in 2025 "following the ban on money gaming", while in-app purchases rose 15% and non-money video games rose 14%; it puts video games plus esports at ₹6,300 crore in 2025, rising to ₹9,200 crore by 2028. A mobile-only series cited in Nazara's FY26 annual report (from mixi's "India's State of Play") puts non-money mobile IAP plus ads at US$1.1 billion in 2025, US$1.5 billion in 2026 and US$2.4 billion by 2029.

The global yardstick has also moved. In plain terms

Before August 2025 Indian gaming was worth about ₹30,000 crore a year, but five-sixths of that was money games. Take those out and the rest was worth about ₹12,000 crore in 2024 and about ₹14,000 crore in 2025 by the widest measure. That remaining business is growing quickly, but most of what Indians spend on games goes to foreign companies.

Two further cautions apply to every market figure in this report. Lumikai switched its reports from a fiscal-year (April–March) basis to a calendar-year basis in 2026, so its FY24 US$3.8 billion and its CY25 US$1.5 billion are not consecutive points on the same series. And none of these numbers is a government statistic: they are compiled by investors and industry bodies from app-store data, company filings and surveys. Directionally they agree. Before the ban, money games were three-quarters to five-sixths of the industry. After it, the surviving market is in the low tens of thousands of crore and growing in the mid-teens. Precision beyond that is false.

03

Who plays, who pays: half a billion gamers, US$2.7 each

India has the players and the installs. It does not yet have the spending, and the ban removed the one model that had cracked monetisation.

India's gaming audience is enormous. Lumikai counted 555 million gamers in calendar 2025 on its ex-RMG basis, down 9% from 609 million a year earlier because money-game players who left did not all migrate to other games. FICCI–EY counts 515–525 million, with more than 100 million playing every day. About a quarter pay for something in a year, which implies roughly 139 million payers (indicative). Two-thirds of gamers live outside the metros. The audience is young, mobile-only, Android-heavy and price-sensitive, and it came online on the cheapest mobile data in the world after Reliance Jio's launch in September 2016.

The spending is thin. An Indian gamer generates about US$2.7 of revenue a year (US$1.5 billion divided by 555 million), against about US$56 for the average player globally (US$201.6 billion divided by about 3.58 billion players): a ratio of roughly 1 to 21. Paying players spend little each: Lumikai puts the average revenue per paying user at about US$15 a year in mid-core games (shooters, battle royale, strategy) and about US$3 in casual games. Advertising earns little too: the blended effective price per thousand ad impressions (eCPM) in India is about US$1.1, and US$2.43 for rewarded video, a fraction of US prices.

Five demand drivers shape the surviving market. Smartphones and data: some 650–700 million smartphone users (an estimate from industry sources, not a fetched figure) and very cheap data. Cricket: it drove fantasy sports before the ban and now drives free-to-play cricket games such as Nazara's World Cricket Championship and Nautilus' Real Cricket, while Dream11 relaunched as a free-to-play product. In plain terms

Most of the people who stopped playing for money did not start playing ordinary games instead. They went to music, short dramas, social media and, by one estimate, illegal offshore betting. The ban removed a revenue pool without transferring it to the legal games industry.

The picture that results is of an audience that is wide but shallow. Indian players install, sample and abandon: Lumikai's founder cites day-1 retention around 30% and day-7 retention below 10% for typical casual games in India. They pay readily for status items in competitive games, rarely for anything else. Advertisers pay little to reach them. That is the context for the most important strategic choice in the sub-sector, examined in section 7: whether an Indian studio should fight for this audience at US$2.7 a head, or build products for American, British and European players who spend twenty times as much.

04

One law, ₹27,000 crore: anatomy of the money-game ban Deep dive

PROGA did not regulate real-money gaming. It abolished it, skill and chance alike, and moved the industry's biggest question to the Supreme Court.

The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 is short, blunt and unusual. Most countries that worry about online gambling license it, tax it and police the unlicensed fringe. India chose prohibition. The Act bans every "online money game", defined by whether a user pays a fee or deposits money in the expectation of winning money or other stakes, regardless of whether the outcome depends on skill, chance or both. That last clause ended a debate that had run through Indian courts for decades, in which rummy and fantasy sports had been defended as "games of skill" and therefore not gambling. Under PROGA the skill question is irrelevant: if money goes in with the hope of more money coming out, the game is banned.

The Act was introduced and passed in the Lok Sabha by voice vote on 20 August 2025, with no debate while the opposition protested over the Bihar voter-roll revision, passed by the Rajya Sabha on 21 August, and received Presidential assent on 22 August as Act No. 32 of 2025. The industry did not wait for commencement. Within days Dream11, MPL, Zupee, Games24x7, PokerBaazi and the rest stopped accepting paid entries, because banks and payment companies were already cutting them off and the reputational and criminal risk of continuing was too high. The formal machinery came eight months later: draft Rules in October 2025, the final Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Rules, 2026 notified on 22 April 2026, and commencement notification S.O. 1994(E) bringing the Act and the Rules into force on 1 May 2026, together with notifications establishing the Online Gaming Authority of India (OGAI) and empowering investigating officers.

What the Act prohibits, and what it promotes

The prohibitions are layered so that the business cannot route around them. Offering or facilitating an online money game is banned. So is advertising or promoting one, which ended the money-game sponsorship of cricket and television overnight. Banks, payment aggregators and payment systems are barred from processing transactions for such games. The government can block access under the IT Act, 2000, and the Act explicitly covers services "operated from outside the territory of India", which is meant to catch offshore betting sites that target Indian users. The key offences are cognisable and non-bailable. Independent and non-executive directors are protected if they can show due diligence, a detail that matters for listed companies with investments in the sector.

In plain terms

The law asks one question of every game: does money go in with the hope of more money coming out? If yes, it is banned, whether winning takes skill or luck. The government's case, and the arithmetic of the trade-off

The government's justification was public harm. The IT minister told the Rajya Sabha that, "according to an estimate, 45 crore people are negatively affected by online money games and faced a loss of more than ₹20,000 crores"; between 2022 and June 2025, 1,524 betting and gambling websites and apps had been blocked. The PIB backgrounder links money games to addiction, financial distress, money laundering and terror financing. The figures are government estimates, not audited statistics, and the industry disputed them, but they framed the debate as one about household harm rather than about an industry's right to exist.

The fiscal side of the trade-off is easier to measure. In the first six months of the 28% GST regime (October 2023 to March 2024) online-gaming GST collections were ₹6,909 crore, against ₹1,349 crore in the previous six months. Official expectations for FY25 ranged from ₹7,500 crore to ₹14,000 crore. Banning the activity therefore gave up something in the range of ₹7,000–14,000 crore a year of GST (INDICATIVE range; no full-year actual was published), plus income tax on winnings and corporate tax. On the government's own numbers, it judged that avoiding more than ₹20,000 crore a year of player losses was worth that revenue.

The constitutional challenge: the sector's largest binary

The challenge to the Act turns on two arguments. The first is legislative competence: "betting and gambling" is Entry 34 of the State List in the Constitution, so the petitioners argue that Parliament could not ban skill games that States had permitted (and that courts had held are not gambling), while the Union relies on its powers over inter-state commerce, communications and the internet. The second is Article 19(1)(g), the right to practise any profession or carry on any business, which can be restricted only by reasonable restrictions; a blanket ban on lawful skill games, the petitioners say, is disproportionate. The High Court petitions were transferred to the Supreme Court on 8 September 2025. On 5 August 2026 a bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant and Justice Joymalya Bagchi agreed to a final hearing, directed the parties (with the Solicitor General for the Union) to complete pleadings and declined an interim stay. The three-judge bench includes Justice V.M. Pancholi. No hearing date had been fixed by 23 October 2026.

Even a win for the petitioners would not restore the old industry quickly. The GST question has been settled against the operators (section 5), so a revived money-game business would pay 28% on deposits, 40% if the GST 2.0 slab for such services is applied, plus the retrospective demands. Brands have been dismantled, staff dispersed, and players, in Lumikai's indicative estimate, partly lost to offshore sites. For Nazara the asymmetry is clean: its Moonshine stake is already carried at zero, so a strike-down could only add value, and an upheld Act costs it nothing further.

The grey zone that remains

The four OGAI tests leave edges. A free game in which players can win vouchers, cash prizes in sponsored tournaments, or rewards that can be withdrawn might fail the "monetisation of in-game assets outside the game" test. This matters to Nazara directly: BestPlay, part of the Bluetile acquisition, is a rewards app in which, in the company's own description, "the user has an option to withdraw the rewards". Its users are believed to be mostly overseas, where US state rules on sweepstakes are also tightening, so the Indian exposure is probably small, but it is a regulatory sensitivity that did not exist in Nazara's old portfolio. For the rest of the industry, the absence of deemed clearance means that every new social game with prize mechanics carries legal risk until the OGAI rules.

In plain terms

The industry's fate now rests with a Supreme Court bench that has agreed to hear the case but has not set a date. Even if the law falls, the old business would come back smaller and more heavily taxed. Investors should treat a revival as a distant possibility rather than a base case.

05

The tax that came first: 28% on every rupee staked

Two years before the ban, a change in the GST base cut money-game economics roughly in half, and a 2026 judgment made the arrears collectable.

Until September 2023 most skill-gaming platforms paid 18% GST on their gross gaming revenue, the commission they kept. On a ₹100 contest that returned ₹85 to winners, the platform's ₹15 rake attracted ₹2.70 of tax. The 50th GST Council meeting on 11 July 2023 decided that online gaming, casinos and horse racing would be taxed at 28% on the "full face value" of bets; a clarification followed on 2 August, the law was amended, and the new regime began on 1 October 2023. Tax on the same ₹100 deposit, if the operator absorbed it, rose to about ₹21.90 (28/128 of a tax-inclusive ₹100). That is about eight times the old tax, and more than the platform's entire rake.

The retrospective notices, and the judgment that revived them

The more damaging part was backwards-looking. Tax officers argued that the 2023 amendments merely clarified what the law had always meant, so 28% on face value applied to earlier years too. Show-cause notices followed. A Rajya Sabha written reply by the Minister of State for Finance in March 2025 counted 158 cases with ₹1,53,167 crore of tax detected and 91 show-cause notices for more than ₹1.44 lakh crore against online gaming companies for July 2017 to January 2025. Gameskraft's notice alone, issued on 23 September 2022, was reported at about ₹21,000 crore; Dream Sports' exposure has been reported above ₹28,000 crore (press reports, not company disclosures). The Karnataka High Court quashed the Gameskraft notice in May 2023, and the Supreme Court later stayed notices worth more than ₹1 lakh crore to about 50 firms pending its ruling.

That ruling came on 27 May 2026 in Directorate General of GST Intelligence v Gameskraft Technologies (SLP(C) 19366–19369/2023), decided by Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan. The Court held that the 28% levy was valid; that games involving staking on uncertain outcomes, including rummy and fantasy sports, are "betting and gambling" for GST purposes, the test being staking rather than skill; that operators supply actionable claims and are not mere intermediaries; that tax applies to the full face value; and that the 2023 amendments were clarificatory and therefore retrospective. It set aside the Karnataka High Court judgment and restored the 2022 notices. Assessees can still contest facts and amounts before the adjudicating authority, so the demands do not crystallise automatically, but the legal argument is over.

In plain terms

The tax change of 2023 made each bet roughly eight times more expensive to tax, and the Supreme Court has now said it applied to the past as well. Companies that no longer earn money from betting are being asked for tax bills worth more than a lakh crore rupees in total. Many cannot pay in full, which is why the next fight will be over settlement terms rather than the law.

Three side effects are worth noting. First, listed casino operator Delta Corp, which shut its online poker arm Adda52 after the ban, swung to a trailing loss of ₹289 crore in the twelve months to June 2026 against an FY26 profit of ₹85 crore; that is consistent with GST provisioning after the judgment, although the cause has not been confirmed from its filings. Second, income tax rules had already tightened: since 1 April 2023 net winnings have been taxed at 30% with tax deducted at source. Third, GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, placed money gaming in the new 40% slab for "sin" goods and services, moot after the ban but relevant if the Act were struck down. Non-money online games remain at 18%. The lesson for the surviving industry is that Indian tax policy treats anything resembling a wager as a sin good, and that the line between a reward and a winning will be policed.

After the ban: who won, who lost

India's most profitable consumer-internet companies lost almost all their revenue in a week. The money did not flow to the legal games industry.

The real-money operators were not marginal businesses. Dream Sports earned more profit in FY24 (₹1,295 crore) than all but a handful of listed Indian media companies; Gameskraft earned ₹947 crore that year on revenue of ₹3,475 crore. Gaming as a whole attracted about ₹25,896 crore (roughly US$3 billion) of foreign direct investment, about 85% of it into pay-to-play models, from investors including Tencent, Tiger Global, Peak XV, Kalaari, WestBridge and Malabar. The WinZO–IEIC report counted about 1,900 gaming companies employing about 1.3 lakh professionals before the ban. The Act turned most of that capital into impaired investments, and much of that employment into lay-offs.

The losers

Dream Sports stopped paid contests in August 2025, its chief executive said it had lost "95% of revenues and 100% of profits", and in December 2025 it reorganised into eight independent units, with Dream11 relaunched as a free-to-play product and FanCode (sports streaming) and DreamSetGo (sports travel) separated. It faces GST demands reported above ₹28,000 crore. Gameskraft, which had earned a 24.9% EBITDA margin in FY25, stopped paid play, cut staff, was searched by the Enforcement Directorate in November 2025 and is the named respondent in the judgment that revived its ₹21,000 crore notice. WinZO had about ₹505 crore frozen by the ED, its founders were arrested in late November 2025, and investigators allege about ₹490 crore was diverted to a US shell company. Games24x7, maker of RummyCircle and My11Circle, was reported to be cutting about 70% of its staff. MPL, Zupee, Probo and Head Digital Works (A23) paused and cut; Hike shut entirely in September 2025; and Flutter, the world's largest online betting group, closed Junglee Games, a business it had expected to earn about US$200 million of revenue in 2025.

The losses spread beyond the operators. Money-game companies were among the biggest advertisers in Indian media: Dream11 alone spent ₹3,913 crore on advertising and promotion in FY25, 58% of its expenses, and was the Indian cricket team's jersey sponsor. Broadcasters, digital publishers, ad-tech firms and sports bodies lost a category that had been growing fast. Listed casino operator Delta Corp closed its online arm. Nazara lost its ₹1,058.5 crore PokerBaazi investment and saw NODWIN's esports business lose sponsors; FICCI–EY estimates esports revenue fell about 7.5% in 2025.

In plain terms

The legal games business gained a little, mostly through cheaper advertising and some more players for foreign shooters. For investors in listed Indian equities, the ban is a lesson in regulatory concentration. A business with 85% of its industry's revenue, double-digit growth and high returns on capital disappeared because its product sat on the wrong side of a line drawn by Parliament in two days. The surviving segments are on the right side of that line today, but the OGAI's game-by-game tests, the treatment of rewards and prizes, and the 40% GST slab for anything resembling a wager mean that regulatory risk did not leave with the money games. It simply moved to the edges of the products that remain.

The monetisation gap: the world's biggest download market, under 1% of the revenue Deep dive

India installs a sixth of the world's games and pays for less than a hundredth of them. Every Indian studio of scale has had to choose how to escape that arithmetic.

Sensor Tower counted 8.45 billion game installs in India in FY25 (April 2024 to March 2025), down 8.7% from the year before but still the highest of any country and more than twice Indonesia's 3.34 billion. On a global base of roughly 50 billion game downloads a year (an industry estimate), India accounts for about one in six. Its share of global games revenue is 0.74% on Lumikai's ex-RMG basis. No other large market shows a gap that wide between engagement and money. Within India's own app economy the pattern is the same: Lumikai finds games take 36% of the revenue of the top-25 apps but only 8% of downloads, against 44% and 16% globally. Indians play a great deal, and pay very little.

Why Indian players pay so little

Income is the obvious reason but not the only one. Indian gamers are overwhelmingly on Android phones that cost under ₹15,000, where spending per user is lower everywhere in the world than on iPhones. Two-thirds live outside the metros. Most came online after 2016 on data that cost a few rupees a gigabyte and have learnt to expect digital entertainment to be free or close to it, which is also why video streaming and music in India are ad-supported or priced at a fraction of US levels. Card-based payment was rare until UPI, and app-store billing in rupees took years to become seamless. The games that do monetise are competitive: in mid-core shooters and battle-royale titles, status items and battle passes earn about US$15 per payer a year, against US$3 in casual games, and FICCI–EY says shooters and battle royale take more than 40% of Indian in-app spending.

Advertising cannot close the gap either. The blended eCPM of US$1.1 in India means a casual game that shows a player ten ads a day earns about one US cent per player per day from them; a rewarded video at US$2.43 per thousand earns a quarter of a cent each. In the United States the same impressions are worth several times as much. And because all ten of India's top-grossing games are global franchises (BGMI, Free Fire Max, Call of Duty Mobile, Clash games and others), much of the money that does flow goes abroad.

Four escape routes, and who takes each

Route one: sell to the world. Build or buy games whose players are in the US, UK and Europe, and run them with Indian or global teams. This is Nazara's route: its Fusebox narrative games (Love Island, Big Brother, The Traitors in development) earn 75%+ of revenue in the US and UK; Kiddopia is a US iOS subscription business; Animal Jam earns about 90% in the US; Curve's PC and console catalogue earns 75%+ in the US and Japan; and Bluetile, the casual publisher it is buying, earns more than half its revenue in the US. Gameberry Labs (Ludo Star) and Moonfrog have followed versions of the same path.

Route two: win the Indian mid-core player. Build a competitive game that Indians will spend on, as Krafton has done with BGMI and as SuperGaming is attempting with Indus. This is the route that captures the 19% annual growth Lumikai forecasts for Indian in-app purchases, but it is capital-intensive, hit-driven and dominated so far by global franchises with deeper pockets.

Route three: sell services to the world's publishers. Art, animation, quality assurance, porting and co-development for global studios, from Keywords' Lakshya Digital to Ubisoft Pune, Rockstar India, EA Hyderabad and Krafton's India studio. EY calls this "A Studio Called India". It is a talent-arbitrage business with the economics of IT services rather than of games, and none of it is listed as a gaming pure-play.

Route four: own the audience rather than the game. Esports leagues, creator networks, conventions and sports media earn sponsorship and media rights around games owned by others. NODWIN (Comic Con India, BGMI events, StarLadder) is the leader; its economics are discussed in section 18.

The currency consequence

An Indian-listed company with 87% foreign revenue is, for reporting purposes, a dollar and sterling business. The rupee traded around ₹94.9 to the dollar on 19 October 2026, near a record low of about ₹96.8, against roughly ₹83–84 a year earlier. Translated into rupees, the same dollar of Kiddopia subscription revenue was worth about 13–15% more in FY27 than in FY26 (INDICATIVE). That flatters reported growth and margins, and readers should separate it from underlying growth when the company reports. It also cuts the other way: the US$214 million balance Nazara owes Bluetile's sellers by 1 April 2027 costs more rupees every time the currency weakens. On Nazara's own 12 October filing, US$77 million was equivalent to ₹742.72 crore, or ₹96.46 to the dollar.

In plain terms

Indians download more games than anyone but spend about US$2.7 a year each on them, against about US$56 for the average player worldwide. So Indian gaming companies that want to make real money sell to Americans and Britons. Nazara is the clearest example: almost 90% of its revenue is earned abroad, which also means its rupee profits rise and fall with the dollar and the pound.

The strategic implication is counter-intuitive. The Indian games market is growing faster than the world's, but for an investor looking for listed exposure, Indian demand barely matters: the domestic market's 17% growth is almost irrelevant to Nazara's P&L, and the ban mattered to Nazara only through its PokerBaazi stake and the sponsorship losses at NODWIN. What matters is whether an Indian-listed, increasingly globally managed company can acquire and run Western casual and kids' games better than Western competitors, using Indian development costs, Indian capital-market currency and a willingness to pay for paid installs. That is a narrower, more testable thesis than "the Indian gaming boom", and it is the one the rest of this report evaluates.

Industry structure: foreign IP at the top, one listed pure-play, unlisted giants in retreat

The money in Indian gaming flows to Korean, Chinese and American publishers; the Indian champions were unlisted and mostly in money games.

Before the ban the industry's structure was easy to describe: a handful of venture-funded money-game platforms took most of the revenue, and two of them, Dream Sports and Gameskraft, took about ₹10,650 crore between them in FY25, roughly 40% of the ₹27,438 crore money-game pool on WinZO–IEIC's 2024 count (INDICATIVE: the bases differ, calendar 2024 for the pool and company FY25 net revenue for the operators). In plain terms

Foreign companies own the games Indians pay for. The Indian companies that made the most money were unlisted betting-style platforms, and they are now shut. That leaves one listed Indian pure-play, Nazara, which mostly sells games abroad, plus a few listed companies that touch gaming at the edges.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: the cost of buying players

Development is a sunk cost; stores and ad networks take their share of every rupee; and in casual games paid installs consume most of the rest.

A games company has no factories and few physical assets, so its cost structure is about people, stores and advertising. The largest variable cost in most surviving models is user acquisition (UA): the money spent on performance advertising to get a game installed. The second is the platform fee: Apple and Google take 15–30% of in-app purchases and subscriptions (Apple charges 15% on subscriptions after the first year), and Steam and the console stores take about 30% of premium game sales. The third is content: licence fees for TV formats and IP, revenue shares to external developers, and the salaries of the studio. Servers and AI tools are a small cost. Taxes are mostly not in the P&L: non-money online games pay 18% GST, collected from players.

User acquisition: the swing cost

UA is the one cost management can change month to month, and that makes it both a lever and a temptation. A casual publisher like Bluetile spends 85% of revenue on installs because, by its own data, each cohort of players returns more than its acquisition cost within its payback window; if that holds, spending more today buys more profit tomorrow. If it stops holding (because install prices rise, or new players are less engaged), the publisher can cut UA and report a jump in margin while its future revenue quietly shrinks. Kiddopia shows the sensitivity on a smaller scale: its cost per new subscriber fell from US$70.1 to US$62.6 over FY26 and Q1 FY27, and its UA rose 27% year on year in Q1 FY27 while its EBITDA fell from ₹8 crore to ₹7 crore. Section 13 shows how to judge whether UA is creating value.

Platform fees and the escape from them

Nazara's "commission" line, ₹164 crore or 9.0% of FY26 revenue, includes store and payment fees and agency commissions; for an IAP-heavy game the store fee alone can approach a quarter of gross revenue. Regulatory pressure is slowly loosening that toll: India's Competition Commission fined Google ₹1,337.76 crore and ₹936.44 crore in October 2022 over Android and Play billing practices and pushed user-choice billing, and in the US and Europe court rulings and the EU Digital Markets Act are opening web shops and alternative payment links. Each percentage point of fees a publisher moves to its own web store drops straight to EBITDA, which is why web shops are a priority for every casual publisher of scale.

In plain terms

A games company spends most of its money before it earns any: on developers to build the game and on advertising to find players. Apple and Google then take up to a third of what players spend. In casual games, buying players can eat 85 rupees of every 100 earned, which is fine only if those players keep spending for long enough.

One more cost is invisible in EBITDA but central to the economics: amortisation of acquired intangibles. When Nazara buys a studio, much of the price is recorded as intangible assets (IP, customer relationships, technology) and goodwill. The intangibles are amortised over their useful lives, which is why Nazara's depreciation and amortisation rose from ₹118 crore in FY25 to ₹231 crore in FY26 as Curve and Fusebox were consolidated for full years. Bluetile, at about ₹2,900 crore, will add a much larger amount. EBITDA comparisons across acquisitive and organic studios therefore flatter the acquirers; profit before tax is a more demanding test, and section 11 shows why even that needs adjusting.
