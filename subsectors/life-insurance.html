00

The one-page view: a promise business whose price is now being set by its regulator

Six listed insurers, two very different industries, and one question for 2027: how much of the value of a policy goes to the company that writes it and how much to the bank that sells it.

A life insurer sells a promise that runs for ten, twenty or forty years, collects the money up front, and books the profit slowly. That one fact explains almost everything odd about the sector: why its accounting profit understates its earnings, why investors value it on "embedded value" rather than on book value or earnings, why the person who sells the policy can capture a quarter of the first year's premium, and why a regulator can move the value of the whole industry by changing the rules on surrenders, tax or commissions.

India has 26 life insurers. In FY26 they wrote ₹4.60 lakh crore of new-business premium, 15.7% more than a year earlier. Six of them are listed in the NSE 750 and together wrote 83.3% of that new business: the state-owned Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC), and five private insurers — SBI Life, HDFC Life, ICICI Prudential Life, Axis Max Life (held through Max Financial Services) and Canara HSBC Life. Between them they manage roughly ₹74.6 lakh crore of policyholder and shareholder money and were worth ₹9.0 lakh crore on the stock market on 19 October 2026.

The first thing to understand is that these are two industries sharing one regulator. LIC is a 70-year-old state institution with about 14.85 lakh agents, a sovereign guarantee on its policies and a book dominated by traditional "participating" savings plans and employer group funds. It has 56.7% of all new-business premium, but only 36.6% of the individual first-year premium that private insurers compete for, because much of its share comes from large single-premium group fund mandates. The private five are bank-promoted manufacturers that sell market-linked savings plans (ULIPs), guaranteed-return savings plans, term cover and annuities, mostly through the branches of their parent bank. They have won the urban retail customer over the past two decades. LIC has kept the rural agency customer, the employer group and the par policy.

The second thing to understand is that value comes from product mix, not volume. A rupee of ULIP sales creates perhaps 5–15 paise of value for shareholders; a rupee of guaranteed savings 30–40 paise; a rupee of retail term cover 60 paise or more (indicative bands). An insurer that grows premium by selling more ULIPs in a bull market can see its value per rupee of sales fall. The best single number in the sector is therefore not premium but value of new business (VNB) — the present value of future shareholder profits on the policies sold in a year — and its ratio to sales, the VNB margin. The six wrote roughly ₹30,750 crore of VNB in FY26 (indicative: Canara HSBC's full-year figure is estimated), at a blended margin of about 23%.

The third thing is that the sector has just been through a regulatory reset and is entering a second one. Between October 2024 and February 2026, new surrender-value rules made early exits cheaper for customers and removed the "lapse profit" insurers used to earn; the GST exemption on individual policies from 22 September 2025 cut customer prices but took away the input-tax credit insurers used to claim on commissions and costs; and the Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha Act, effective 5 February 2026, allowed 100% foreign ownership and gave IRDAI an express power to cap commissions. Each of the first two cost private insurers somewhere between 100 and 300 basis points of VNB margin. On 23 September 2026 IRDAI used its new power: a consultation paper titled Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution proposes replacing the single overall expense ceiling with specific commission caps by product and channel (critics count more than 30 of them) and phasing the overall expense limit down over five years. Comments close on 25 October 2026.

The fourth thing is the valuation ladder, which has flipped since our primers were written in mid-2026. On 19 October 2026, SBI Life traded at 1.94× its June 2026 embedded value, Axis Max Life (on a look-through basis via Max Financial) at about 1.80×, HDFC Life at 1.78×, Canara HSBC at 1.58×, ICICI Prudential at 1.20× and LIC at 0.63×. SBI Life, which the primers described as the cheapest of the group, is now the most expensive, because it has the lowest costs, the highest return on embedded value and the least exposure to proposed commission caps. LIC's 0.63× means the market values it at less than its existing book of policies: it assigns a negative value to every policy LIC will sell in future, a verdict on state ownership and share supply rather than on LIC's recent operating improvement.

In plain terms

Life insurers are paid today for promises they keep for decades, so the better measure of how well they are doing is not this year's profit but the value of the business they have written. On that measure the private insurers are good businesses earning 15–20% a year on their embedded value, and LIC is a much bigger business that the market refuses to value fully because the government owns 90% of it and must sell more. The live question for 2027 is whether IRDAI's proposed caps on what banks and agents are paid hand the savings to insurers, to customers, or back to the banks in some other form.

01

How a life insurer earns: sell a promise today, earn on it for twenty years

The money is made in four places: the price loading on each premium, the spread on invested money, the mortality margin, and the fee on market-linked funds. The cost is front-loaded.

Strip away the jargon and a life insurer does three jobs. It manufactures a contract: it designs a product, prices it with assumptions about mortality, lapses, costs and interest rates, and underwrites the customer. It distributes the contract, almost never directly: a bank, an agent, a broker or a website finds the customer, and is paid a commission that is heavily concentrated in the first year. And it invests the money it collects — largely in government bonds, state development loans and high-grade corporate bonds for guaranteed products, and in equity and bond funds chosen by the customer for unit-linked products — until it has to pay out a death claim, a maturity, a surrender or an annuity.

The insurer earns from four sources, and which one dominates depends on the product. On a ULIP, the customer bears the investment risk and the insurer earns a fund-management charge (capped at 1.35% a year), mortality charges and policy administration fees — a thin, volume-driven margin. On a participating (par) policy, the surplus from investments and expenses is shared with policyholders as bonuses; shareholders can take at most one-tenth of the surplus distributed. On a non-participating (non-par) savings policy, the insurer guarantees a maturity or income and keeps the whole spread between what it earns on the bonds it buys and what it has promised. On protection (term cover), the insurer earns the gap between the premium and expected claims, after reinsurance; it is the richest product per rupee but a small share of sales. Annuities earn a spread on a lump sum while taking longevity risk.

The cost side has a peculiar shape. In the year a policy is sold, the insurer pays the distributor a large first-year commission, spends on underwriting, medicals, marketing and branch support, and must set aside a reserve that often exceeds the premium net of those costs. The result is an accounting loss on every new policy in year one, called new-business strain. The profits arrive in later years as renewal premiums come in, renewal commissions fall to 2–5%, and the insurer earns its spread or charges on the accumulated fund. A fast-growing insurer therefore reports lower accounting profit than a slow-growing one with the same underlying economics, which is why the sector's PAT is a poor guide to value.

Two more participants matter. Reinsurers — Gen Re, Swiss Re, Munich Re, RGA, Hannover Re and India's GIC Re — take most of the mortality risk on retail term policies and therefore effectively set the floor on term prices; they raised rates after the COVID-19 claims of 2021–22. The 2025 amendment cut the net owned funds required of a foreign reinsurer's Indian branch from ₹5,000 crore to ₹1,000 crore, which should widen capacity. And the shareholder–policyholder split decides who owns the profit. In par business, shareholders get at most a tenth of surplus; in non-par and protection, they get all of it. That is why every private insurer has spent a decade shifting towards non-par and protection, and why LIC's margin started rising once its 2021 restructuring split its single fund into par and non-par funds.

In plain terms

Every new policy loses money in its first year and makes money over the following ten to forty. The questions that decide whether an insurer creates value are how much it pays to win the customer, how long the customer keeps paying, and which product the customer bought. Premium growth on its own says very little.

One more structural feature shapes the economics: the money mostly belongs to policyholders. Of the six members' ₹74.6 lakh crore of assets under management, the shareholders' own funds are a small fraction; the rest backs liabilities to policyholders. Shareholders' profit comes from the margins on that money, not from owning it. That is why the industry's natural valuation is the present value of those margins — embedded value — and why a 1% move in bond yields can matter more to an insurer offering guarantees than a 10% move in premium.

02

₹4.6 lakh crore of new premium, and still only 2.7% of GDP

The market is large in rupees and small relative to the economy: life penetration has fallen three years running even as new business compounds at low double digits.

The size of the life-insurance market depends on which "premium" you count, and the four definitions differ by a factor of two. New-business premium (NBP) counts all first-year and single premiums at full value; this is the IRDAI measure behind market shares, and it was ₹4,59,713 crore in FY26, up 15.7% from ₹3,97,337 crore. LIC wrote ₹2,60,464 crore of it (+14.9%) and the 25 private insurers ₹1,99,249 crore (+16.7%). Total premium adds renewals: LIC alone collected ₹5,35,984 crore in FY26, and the industry total was roughly ₹9.6–9.8 lakh crore (INDICATIVE estimate: LIC plus about ₹4.3 lakh crore for the private sector). Weighted received premium (first-year plus 10% of single premium, individual business only) is what private insurers use for their own market share. Annualised premium equivalent (APE) is the companies' sales measure, close to WRP but including group business.

Measured against the economy, the market is small and not growing. IRDAI's annual report for 2024-25 put life-insurance penetration (premium as a share of GDP) at 2.7% in FY25, down from 2.8% in FY24 and the third straight fall. Total insurance penetration (life plus general) was 3.7%, flat, against a world average of 7.3%. Life insurance density — premium per person — was about US$72 in FY25. The gap with the rest of the world is the industry's favourite statistic, and it is real: the mortality protection gap in India was estimated by Swiss Re at more than US$16 trillion in its 2019–2020 study. But the falling ratio also says that household saving has been moving to mutual funds, equities and deposits faster than into insurance.

Assets under management tell the same story of LIC's weight. LIC managed ₹57.3 lakh crore (₹57 trillion) at March 2026 and ₹59.4 lakh crore at June 2026; the five listed private insurers together managed about ₹15.2 lakh crore at their latest dates, and the whole life industry roughly ₹76–78 lakh crore (INDICATIVE). LIC is India's largest single investor in equities, with a book of about ₹17 lakh crore, and in August 2026 the RBI allowed it to raise its HDFC Bank stake to 9.99%. Life insurance is entirely domestic — a licensed product cannot be sold across the border, though ICICI Prudential markets NRI policies through GIFT City — and India accounts for roughly 2–2.5% of world life premium (approximate).

In plain terms

India's life insurers write about ₹4.6 lakh crore of new policies a year, and that number grows faster than the economy in rupees. Relative to GDP, though, the industry has been shrinking slightly, because Indian households are putting more of their savings into mutual funds and markets. The growth case rests on protection and retirement products that the country plainly needs, not on the savings products that make up most of today's sales.

03

Why Indians buy, and why they stop: tax, rates, markets and a GST cut

Demand for life insurance is less a steady habit than a series of switches. Each one flips the product mix, and the mix decides the margin.

Life insurance competes for household savings with bank deposits, small-savings schemes, mutual funds and direct equity. It funds roughly 15–20% of household gross financial savings (approximate, RBI data), a share that has been flat to falling. Six drivers decide how much of the next rupee it wins and in which product.

1. Tax: a fading subsidy

For decades the January–March "tax season" drove traditional savings sales, because premiums up to ₹1.5 lakh were deductible under section 80C of the old income-tax regime and maturity proceeds were tax-free under section 10(10D). That subsidy is eroding from three directions. The new tax regime, which has no 80C deduction, became the default from FY24, and Budget 2025 made income up to ₹12 lakh effectively tax-free under it; a large majority of filers now use the new regime (roughly 70%+ by commonly quoted estimates, INDICATIVE). Budget 2021 removed the 10(10D) exemption for ULIPs with annual premium above ₹2.5 lakh, and Budget 2023 did the same for non-ULIP policies with aggregate annual premium above ₹5 lakh from 1 April 2023. The second change produced a rush of high-ticket non-par sales in Q4 FY23 and a hole in FY24 that hit HDFC Life and Max Life hardest.

2. Interest rates and equity markets: the product-cycle switch

The most powerful short-term driver is the market. When equities are rising, customers buy ULIPs; ULIPs made up more than 80% of private sales in FY08 and surged again in FY24 and the first half of FY25. When deposit rates fall, guaranteed non-par plans look attractive because they lock in a return. When markets wobble, customers stop buying ULIPs: SBI Life's ULIP APE grew only 7.5% in Q1 FY27 after an equity slowdown in early 2026, and its ULIP share fell from 59% of FY26 APE to 45.6%. Rates also change the insurer's side of the trade. A higher, steeper yield curve lets an insurer offer the same guarantee at a bigger spread; Axis Max Life said about 70% of its Q1 FY27 margin gain came from a favourable yield curve. The RBI's 25 basis point hike of the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 is therefore relevant to non-par and annuity pricing, though a hike can also tempt savers back to deposits.

3. Protection: under-penetrated, and price-sensitive

Term insurance is the product the country most obviously lacks, and it responds to price. From 22 September 2025 individual life premiums, including term, became exempt from GST, which had been 18% on term premiums and on ULIP charges and 4.5% (first year) and 2.25% (later years) on traditional plans. Retail term became roughly 15% cheaper overnight. Retail protection sales then jumped across the private sector: HDFC Life +43% in FY26 and +33% in H1 FY27, Axis Max Life +53% in FY26 and +44% in Q1 FY27, ICICI Prudential +32% in FY26 (and total protection +45.7% in Q1 FY27), and Canara HSBC +126% in 9M FY26 and +44% in H1 FY27, albeit from a small base. Protection is still a small share of APE — 7–18% for most members — but it carries the richest margin, so its growth matters more than its size.

4. Credit growth and credit life

Group credit-life cover, which pays off a borrower's loan if they die, rides on bank and NBFC loan disbursals. It is a large and growing pool for bank-promoted insurers — Axis Max Life's group credit life grew 57% in Q1 FY27 — and one of the most commission-heavy. That makes it the pool most directly in the line of fire from IRDAI's September 2026 proposals, which as reported would cap credit-linked term commission at 2% for single premium and 2.5% (first year) for multi-year policies and would stop lenders from compulsorily bundling cover with loans. Section 08 covers this in detail.

5. Demographics and retirement

An ageing, longer-lived middle class needs income in retirement, and annuities are the one product that converts a lump sum into guaranteed lifelong income. Demand is rising quickly: HDFC Life's annuity share of individual APE jumped from 4% to 12% between H1 FY26 and H1 FY27, and Axis Max Life's annuity sales grew 113% in FY26 and 116% in Q1 FY27. NPS annuitisation is a further pool, though the September 2026 proposals as reported would pay no commission on NPS annuities.

6. Reach of distribution

Finally, demand is partly a function of who knocks on the door. Banks sell an estimated ~45% of private-sector premium (a secondary-source figure; IRDAI's own split was not available), agents sell most of LIC's, and the long-promised Bima Sugam marketplace — meant to let customers compare and buy policies online at low cost — had an information site live from September 2025 but was not yet transacting in October 2026. About 65% of SBI Life's and Axis Max Life's business comes from tier-2 and tier-3 towns, which is where the next round of growth must come from.

In plain terms

People buy life insurance for tax breaks, for a guaranteed return, for market growth, for protection or for a pension, and each of those reasons waxes and wanes. The tax reason is fading, the market reason is fickle, and the protection and pension reasons are growing. Insurers that sell more of the last two earn far more per rupee, which is why the GST cut on term cover has mattered more to value than its effect on headline premium.

The net effect for FY27 is an industry growing at roughly 10% in individual premium, with a mix that is shifting away from ULIPs and towards non-par, protection and annuity. That supports margins while volume growth is muted — but it also increases the industry's exposure to interest rates and to the regulator's view of how guaranteed products are sold.

Mix is destiny: the same premium can be worth 5 paise or 80

Two insurers with identical sales can create value that differs by a factor of two. The product mix explains most of the gap between members, and most of each member's margin swings.

Section 01 set out the five product families and their very different margins. This section shows what the six members actually sell, and why the mix moves. The arithmetic is simple and unforgiving. Suppose an insurer sells ₹100 of APE, of which 50 is ULIP at a 10% margin, 20 non-par at 35%, 10 protection at 70%, 10 par at 18% and 10 annuity at 28%. The mix moves for four reasons, and investors should ask which one is at work in any quarter. Markets move ULIP demand, as Section 03 showed. Rates move both the demand for guaranteed products and the margin the insurer can earn on them: a steeper curve makes non-par and annuities more profitable, a flatter one squeezes them. Regulation and tax move individual products: the 2023 tax on high-ticket non-ULIP policies hollowed out HNI non-par for a year; the October 2024 surrender-value rules made traditional savings plans less profitable; the 2025 GST exemption boosted term. And management choice moves the rest: HDFC Life's push into annuities (4% → 12% of individual APE in a year) and ICICI Prudential's rotation into protection after FY22 were strategic decisions, not market accidents.

Every mix shift carries a hidden trade. Moving from ULIPs to non-par raises margin but adds interest-rate risk: the insurer has promised a return for 15–40 years and must reinvest maturing bonds at whatever rate prevails. Indian insurers hedge most of this with forward rate agreements and bond forwards with banks, which lock in future reinvestment yields, but hedges cost money and depend on bank balance sheets. Moving to annuities adds longevity risk — the chance customers live longer than priced. Moving to protection adds mortality and reinsurance risk, and makes the insurer dependent on reinsurer pricing. A high-margin mix is a riskier mix, which is why the market discounts non-par-heavy books when rates are volatile.

In plain terms

When an insurer's margin rises, the first question is whether it sold more of the profitable products or simply had a lucky quarter with interest rates. Mix-driven gains tend to last if the insurer has built the distribution for them; rate-driven gains can reverse. And a richer mix is usually a riskier one, because the insurer is promising more.

05

Where ₹100 of first-year premium goes

The supply side of life insurance is not factories but capital, distribution capacity, reinsurance and investment yield. The cost stack is front-loaded, and the distributor takes the first and largest slice.

A life insurer's inputs are unusual. It needs capital, because the regulator requires it to hold available solvency margin of at least 150% of the required level. It needs distribution capacity — bank branches, agents, brokers or digital reach — and pays for it mostly through commission. It needs reinsurance capacity to lay off mortality risk on term business. And it needs investment yield: LIC earned 8.92% on its policyholder funds in FY26, excluding unrealised gains, and 8.28% in Q1 FY27. None of these is scarce in the way beds or steel capacity are; all of them have a price, and the regulator sets the rules on most of them.

Reinsurance and investment yield complete the supply picture. Most retail term risk is reinsured, so reinsurers' mortality tables and rates — raised after COVID-19 — set the floor on term pricing; the 2025 Act's cut in foreign reinsurers' minimum net owned funds should widen that market. On the investment side, guaranteed products are backed largely by government securities and state development loans (roughly 50–70% of non-linked funds) and high-grade corporate bonds, so the bond market's level and shape feed straight into pricing.

In plain terms

A life insurer pays a large fee to whoever brings in the customer, spends more to set up the policy, and must lock away a big reserve in year one. It is the cheapest insurers — the ones whose bank or agent network costs least per policy — that can afford to offer good value and still make money. That is why a ratio as dull as "expenses as a percentage of premium" is about to become the most watched number in the sector.

Distribution sets cost, and cost — together with product mix — sets margin and return.

Who pays the distributor? The 2026 commission-cap and expense reset Deep dive

IRDAI's September 2026 consultation paper is the single biggest swing factor for private-insurer value over FY28–FY30. It is still a proposal, but it has already moved the stocks.

On 23 September 2026 the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India released a consultation paper titled Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution, with comments invited until 25 October 2026. Its premise is that distribution has become too expensive for customers: in the paper's data as reported, distributor remuneration has grown four to five times faster than the premium it is paid on, and life insurers pay more on policies that are surrendered than on policies that run to maturity. Its proposal is to replace today's single overall expense ceiling, inside which each insurer's board sets its own commission policy, with specific commission caps by product and channel — critics count more than 30 separate caps — and to phase the overall expense-of-management (EoM) limit down over five years. It also proposes tighter rules against volume-linked incentives paid to bank staff. The proposals are prospective: they would apply to new business, not to commissions already paid.

How we got here

The paper reverses a liberalisation. Until April 2023, IRDAI set commission caps product by product. It then scrapped those caps in favour of a board-approved commission policy within an overall EoM limit, consolidated in the IRDAI (Expenses of Management, including Commission, of Insurers) Regulations 2024. Commissions — now including "rewards" — rose sharply, and some were reclassified out of operating expenses. In November 2024, reports that the regulator might cap the share of business banks could source (50% was mentioned) knocked HDFC Life, SBI Life and Max Financial down by up to 7% in a session. The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha Act, effective 5 February 2026, then gave IRDAI an express statutory power to cap commissions. The September 2026 paper is the first use of that power.

Several features soften or sharpen the caps. A renewal escalator would add 0.5 percentage points every three years from year six (to a maximum of 7%), rewarding distributors whose customers keep paying. Towns with fewer than 10 lakh people would get 10% more than the first-year cap, and towns under 50,000 and rural areas 20% more. Clawback of commission on early lapses, suitability analysis, tagging of every policy to its seller and public disclosure of mis-selling incidence complete the conduct package. Policy terms would have to be at least the higher of ten years or the PPT, with premium-paying terms under five years allowed only for non-par non-linked products.

The expense side is the second leg. As reported, life insurers' EoM limit would fall to 15% of premium within two years and 12.5% within five, starting FY2027-28; insurers already below those levels would have to reach 10% within five years, and the carve-outs granted in 2023 would be absorbed. A breach would bring limits on new products and dividends and, in extreme cases, on new business through the offending channel. On bundling, banks and NBFCs could not compulsorily sell insurance with loans: the customer would have to see the loan price with and without insurance, could choose another insurer, and would pay the premium separately rather than having it financed from the loan. Reports of the paper also describe open architecture — any licensed IDE, including a bank, could elect to sell for any number of insurers, ending the three-insurer limit for banks that opt in — but this element has not been independently verified for this report and should be read as reported.

Who is exposed: the commission line and the counter

The commission caps cut differently. A captive bank that today receives about 27% on a long-term savings policy would be capped at 20%; the insurer that pays it saves seven points of first-year premium — if it keeps the saving. Credit life is hit hardest in percentage terms: group credit-life payouts have been estimated at around 45% by one broker (Citi, as reported), against a proposed 2–2.5%. SBI Life's group protection jump in Q1 FY27, Axis Max Life's 57% growth in group credit life and Canara HSBC's PSU-bank loan book are the most exposed pools among the members. Non-bank corporate agents (NBFCs, microfinance lenders, fintechs) averaging 61% would face the steepest fall, which is why new credit-life-focused insurers and aggregator platforms are most at risk.

The deeper question is the bank counter. If reported open architecture becomes final and a large bank opts in, rivals could sell through its branches at capped commission. That would erode the captive moat that underpins SBI Life's and HDFC Life's distribution — although banks have long had the right to three partners and the largest ones have chosen to concentrate on their own subsidiary, because they own the value as well as the commission. A bank that owns 50–55% of its insurer gains more from the insurer's value than from the extra commission it could earn by selling rivals' products.

What the market did

The market's first reaction, as reported, was to treat the paper as negative for the most exposed insurers. In plain terms

The proposal moves money away from distributors. Whether that money ends up with insurers' shareholders, with customers, or quietly back with the banks is the central uncertainty for the sector over the next three years. Insurers with low costs and a parent bank that owns most of them are safest; insurers with high costs and many paid partners are most exposed.

Four rule changes, up to 300 basis points: the 2023–26 reset scorecard Deep dive

Before the distribution paper, the industry had already absorbed a tax shock, a surrender-value rewrite, a GST change and a new Act. Each one took margin; the market re-rated the sector for all of them.

The distribution paper is the second leg of a reset that started in 2023. Between April 2023 and February 2026, four changes in tax and regulation rewired the economics of selling a life policy in India. Each was defensible on its own — protecting customers who surrender early, cutting the price of term cover, closing a tax loophole for the wealthy, opening the sector to foreign capital — but together they took one to three points off the VNB margins of the private insurers, cut their return on embedded value and took their valuations from roughly 2.0–2.5 times embedded value to 1.2–1.9 times.

Change 1: high-value policy tax and commission freedom (2021–2023)

Budget 2021 removed the maturity tax exemption for ULIPs with annual premium above ₹2.5 lakh, and Budget 2023 extended this to non-ULIP policies with aggregate premium above ₹5 lakh from 1 April 2023. The effect was a pre-deadline rush of high-ticket non-par sales in Q4 FY23 and a hole in FY24, felt most by insurers with affluent bank customers (HDFC Life, Max Life). At the same time, IRDAI's April 2023 switch from product caps to an overall EoM limit let commissions rise, which supported volume but raised the cost base the 2026 paper now targets.

Change 2: surrender values (effective 1 October 2024)

Under the IRDAI (Insurance Products) Regulations 2024 and its master circular, a special surrender value became payable after one full year's premium on non-linked savings policies, calculated on a stipulated discounting basis. Previously, a customer typically had to pay two to three years' premium before acquiring any surrender value, and the factors applied were harsh. The old rules gave insurers a "lapse profit": customers who stopped paying early forfeited most of what they had paid, and that forfeit subsidised the pricing of the policies that persisted. The new rules removed most of that subsidy. Insurers responded by deferring or clawing back commissions and repricing products, but VNB margins on traditional savings fell.

Change 3: GST exemption without input-tax credit (22 September 2025)

From 22 September 2025, individual life policies — term, ULIPs and endowments — and their reinsurance became exempt from GST. Customers saved the 18% GST on term premiums and ULIP charges and the 4.5%/2.25% on traditional plans. But an exempt supply does not allow input-tax credit, so insurers could no longer offset the GST they pay on commissions, rent, technology and other costs. The quantified hits were material: SBI Life said the change cost it about 110–120 basis points of margin, a drag that persisted into Q2 FY27; HDFC Life's FY26 margin of 24.2% compares with 25.5% excluding both the GST and surrender-value effects; Bajaj Life (unlisted) reported about 290 basis points; and Axis Max Life said it had absorbed about 80% of the impact by Q4 FY26 through repricing and distributor renegotiation.

In plain terms

Over three years the government and regulator made life insurance cheaper and fairer for customers — tax-free term cover, fairer payouts for people who quit early, and soon, perhaps, lower commissions. Each change took a slice of the insurers' profit per policy. The insurers have offset some of it by selling more profitable products, but investors now pay far less for each rupee of their value than they did in 2024.

Two lessons carry forward. First, regulation in Indian life insurance moves in one direction — towards the customer — and the industry's margin is therefore partly a regulatory variable, not purely a commercial one. Second, the insurers that have weathered the reset best are the ones with the lowest costs (SBI Life, LIC) or the most room to shift mix (ICICI Prudential, LIC). Those are the same qualities that matter most for the next leg.

LIC: the giant the market values below its back book Deep dive

LIC's margins have more than doubled since its IPO and its business is converging with the private insurers'. Its share price says the market gives no value at all to anything it sells from here.

The Life Insurance Corporation of India is the largest financial institution most Indians deal with directly. It has almost 200 million policyholders (the company's own figure; one primer's "250 million-plus" overstates it), about 14.85 lakh agents, ₹57.3 lakh crore of assets under management at March 2026 (₹57 trillion, not the "₹570 trillion" in one earlier write-up) and ₹59.4 lakh crore at June 2026. It sold 1.84 crore individual policies in FY26 and declared ₹59,726 crore of bonuses to policyholders. It is also, on 19 October 2026, valued by the stock market at ₹4,96,892 crore — 0.63 times its Indian embedded value of ₹7,89,185 crore.

The convergence: par to non-par, and a margin that has doubled

LIC's problem for most of its history was that it sold products whose profits belonged mostly to policyholders. Its par policies share surplus with policyholders, and before the 2021 amendments that prepared it for listing, shareholders' share of the surplus was just 5%. The amendments split LIC's single fund into separate par and non-par funds and moved the shareholders' share of par surplus towards 10%, while giving shareholders 100% of non-par surplus. That rewiring made the product mix matter to LIC's owners for the first time. Since then LIC has pushed non-par products hard: individual non-par APE grew 43.8% in FY26, and its share of individual APE rose from 27.7% to 35.1% (32.5% in Q1 FY27, against 30.3% a year earlier).

For the banks themselves, these stakes are among their most valuable subsidiaries — see the Private Banks and PSU Banks reports for how they are valued in sum-of-the-parts.

Bancassurance also carries a conduct risk. In plain terms

Banks are the reason private life insurers grew so fast: they already have the customer, the trust and the branch. They also own most of the insurers, so they profit from both the sale and the insurer's value. If the regulator cuts what banks can earn per sale and lets them sell anyone's products, a bank that owns most of its insurer will probably stay loyal; a bank that owns little may shop around. That makes the size of each bank's stake a key number for investors.

Reading a life insurer: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers, the equation that links them, two worked examples and a ten-minute routine for the results day. Insurers are the one part of finance where the income statement is the least useful page.

A life insurer's results presentation looks like a bank's or an asset manager's, but the numbers mean different things. Premium is not revenue in the usual sense, profit is not earnings power, and book value is not what the company is worth. The industry instead measures itself with a set of actuarial and sales metrics — APE, VNB, VNB margin, embedded value, return on embedded value, persistency, solvency — that together describe how much value the company created this year and how reliably. This section explains each, shows how they connect, and gives the benchmarks to judge them.

The equation, and which terms management controls

Everything in life-insurance analysis flows from one chain. Sales (APE) multiplied by the value per rupee of sales (VNB margin) gives the value of new business. Add the expected return on the business already on the books (the "unwind") and the gains or losses from experience being better or worse than assumed (operating variances), and you have embedded-value operating profit. Divide by opening embedded value and you have the operating return on embedded value, the sector's equivalent of return on equity.

That chain — sales, margin, VNB, EV, RoEV, multiple — is the whole analytical framework in one paragraph.

In plain terms

Ask three questions of any life insurer: how much did it sell (APE), how valuable was each rupee it sold (VNB margin), and how much did its total value grow from its operations (operating RoEV)? Everything else is detail on one of those three, or noise from markets.

1. APE: the sales line

What it is. Annualised premium equivalent is the industry's standard measure of new sales. Formula: APE = 100% of annualised first-year regular premium + 10% of single premium. The 10% weighting stops one-off lump sums, such as a ₹100 crore group fund mandate, from swamping the sales number. Worked example: an insurer sells regular-premium policies with ₹800 crore of annualised first-year premium and single-premium policies worth ₹2,000 crore. APE = ₹800 crore + 10% × ₹2,000 crore = ₹1,000 crore. Drivers: distribution reach (branches, agents, partners), product cycle (ULIPs in bull markets), tax-season timing (Q4 is the biggest quarter), and group deals. Benchmarks: APE growth of 15% or more is good, 8–15% average, under 8% weak. The members: in FY26 Axis Max Life (about +20%, derived), LIC (+17.8%) and SBI Life (+13.3%) led; ICICI Prudential (+2.2%) lagged badly. In the latest period, ICICI recovered to +15.1% (H1 FY27), HDFC Life grew 11% (H1 FY27) and LIC 8.2% (Q1 FY27); SBI Life's +35.3% in Q1 FY27 was inflated by a one-time group term deal, and its guidance for individual APE in FY27 is +14–15%.

2. NBP, WRP and total premium: four premiums, four answers

What they are. Gross or total premium counts everything received: new and renewal. New-business premium (NBP) counts first-year and single premium at full value, and is the basis for IRDAI market shares. Weighted received premium (WRP) is first-year premium plus 10% of single premium, individual business only, and is what private insurers use for their "private market share". APE, above, is the companies' sales measure. Worked example: an insurer receives ₹600 crore of first-year regular premium, ₹2,000 crore of single premium (₹1,500 crore of it group) and ₹3,000 crore of renewals. Total premium = ₹5,600 crore. NBP = ₹2,600 crore. Individual WRP = ₹600 crore + 10% × ₹500 crore = ₹650 crore (assuming all regular premium is individual). APE = ₹600 crore + ₹200 crore = ₹800 crore. Four numbers, an almost nine-fold range. Why it matters: LIC's 56.7% share is an NBP number inflated by single-premium group funds; on individual first-year premium it is 36.6%. HDFC Life reports a 15.2% private-market share and an 11.0% overall share in FY26 on individual WRP, while IRDAI's NBP basis gives it 7.97%. The definition trap: never compare market shares across bases, and note that even IRDAI and the company can differ on the same basis — HDFC Life's FY26 NBP is ₹36,646 crore in IRDAI data and ₹36,096 crore in its own disclosure.

In plain terms

"Premium" can mean four different things, and the difference between them can be several-fold. Use APE to judge sales growth, total premium to judge scale, and NBP only when comparing market shares — and always check which one a headline uses.

3. VNB and VNB margin: value per rupee of sales

What it is. The value of new business is the present value, at a risk discount rate, of all future after-tax shareholder profits from policies sold in the year, minus the cost of holding the required capital. The VNB margin is VNB divided by APE. Formula: VNB margin = VNB ÷ APE; VNB = APE × margin. Worked example: an insurer writes ₹16,641 crore of APE and ₹4,034 crore of VNB — HDFC Life's FY26 figures. Margin = 4,034 ÷ 16,641 = 24.2%. Drivers: product mix (the biggest single driver), commission and cost ratio, persistency assumptions, the yield curve for non-par and annuities, tax and GST, and the cost of capital. Benchmarks: 26% or higher is good for an Indian private insurer, 20–26% average, below 20% weak; VNB growth of 18% or more is good, 10–18% average, under 10% weak.

The policy contributes ₹1,00,000 to APE in the year of sale and ₹9,00,000 of renewal premium if it persists. In year one the shareholder is out of pocket by about ₹20,000: the bank's ₹27,000 commission, roughly ₹15,000 of acquisition cost and the reserve set-up exceed the margin on the first premium. In years two to ten, premium loading and the investment spread (the insurer earns about 7.5% on government bonds and credits about 6%) less renewal commission and maintenance yield about ₹6,500 a year. In years eleven to fifteen, the spread on the ₹14–19 lakh reserve yields about ₹5,000 a year. Nominal lifetime profit is ₹63,500.

VNB is about ₹26,000, a 26% margin on ₹1 lakh of APE. Pure non-par products often earn 30% or more; the bank's 27% commission is what holds this one down.

4. Embedded value: what the in-force book is worth

What it is. Indian embedded value (IEV) estimates what the business already written is worth to shareholders today. Formula: EV = adjusted net worth (shareholders' capital and free surplus, marked to market) + value of in-force business (the present value of future shareholder profits on existing policies, net of the cost of capital and the time value of guarantees). It excludes any value for future sales. Worked example: SBI Life's IEV at March 2026 was ₹80,800 crore (company ₹80,790 crore), against an accounting net worth of about ₹19,086 crore; the remaining ₹61,700 crore or so is the value of in-force and the mark-to-market of adjusted net worth. Drivers: VNB added each year, the unwind, experience variances, market moves (large for LIC, smaller for ULIP-heavy books where policyholders bear market risk), capital flows and dividends. The members: LIC ₹7,89,185 crore (March 2026); SBI Life ₹85,290 crore (June 2026); HDFC Life ₹66,461 crore (September 2026); ICICI Prudential ₹52,989 crore (March 2026); Axis Max Life ₹30,415 crore (June 2026, 100%); Canara HSBC ₹7,622 crore (September 2026).

6. Persistency: do customers keep paying?

What it is. Persistency is the share of policies (or premium) sold in a cohort that is still being paid after a set period. Formula: 13th-month persistency = premium (or policies) from a cohort still in force at month 13 ÷ premium (or policies) originally sold; the same at months 25, 37, 49 and 61. Worked example: an insurer sold ₹1,000 crore of regular premium in a year; ₹877 crore of that premium is still being paid thirteen months later — 87.7% 13th-month persistency, SBI Life's Q1 FY27 figure. Drivers: quality of sale (mis-selling kills persistency), product type (ULIPs lapse with markets, par with household stress), channel (bank-sold policies to depositors persist better than some agency books), the customer's income and the surrender rules (the October 2024 norms make early exit less costly). Benchmarks: 13th month 87% or more is good, 82–87% average, below 82% weak; 61st month 63% or more is good, 57–63% average, below 57% weak. LIC's 75% at month 13 is a structural outlier given its agency and par mix.

7. Solvency ratio: capital, neither too little nor too much

What it is. The solvency ratio compares the capital an insurer has available with the capital the regulator requires. Formula: solvency ratio = available solvency margin ÷ required solvency margin; the minimum is 150%. Worked example: if the regulator requires ₹5,000 crore and the insurer holds ₹9,000 crore of eligible capital, its ratio is 180% — HDFC Life's September 2026 level. Drivers: profit retained, new-business strain (fast growth consumes capital), dividends, sub-debt raised or repaid, equity raised, market moves and the product mix (guaranteed products need more capital). Benchmarks: 180–220% is usually efficient; 160–180% or 220–250% is average (the first tight, the second idle); below 160% signals a capital raise and above 250% wasted capital. The members ranged from 180% (HDFC Life, Canara HSBC) to 242% (LIC) — see the chart in Section 05.

The definition trap: a high solvency ratio is not a virtue in itself. ICICI Prudential's 225% is a reason its operating RoEV (11.9%) is the lowest of the private five: surplus capital earns little. Conversely, HDFC Life's fall to 177% in FY26 forced a ₹1,000 crore preferential issue to HDFC Bank. Watch the risk-based capital regime under development, which will change the required margin.

8. Expense ratio and EoM: the cost of selling and serving

What it is. The total expense ratio measures commission plus operating expenses as a share of premium; the regulatory EoM test is similar. Formula: expense ratio = (commission + operating expenses) ÷ total premium. Worked example: an insurer collects ₹79,387 crore of premium and spends ₹16,830 crore on commission and opex: a 21.2% ratio, close to HDFC Life's FY26 level. Drivers: distribution mix (captive bank cheapest, alliances and brokers dearest), scale (renewals dilute fixed costs), growth (fast growth raises the ratio because first-year costs are high), product mix (single premium has low commission), GST input-credit loss since September 2025, and one-offs such as the labour code. Benchmarks: 12.5% or lower is good (the proposed five-year target), 12.5–16% average, above 16% (the FY26 industry level) weak. The members: LIC 10.6% and SBI Life 12.0% (Q1 FY27); Axis Max Life 16% opex alone; Canara HSBC 20.5% and HDFC Life 22.3% (H1 FY27) — chart in Section 05.

The definition trap: companies define the ratio differently. Axis Max Life reports policyholder opex as a share of gross written premium, excluding commission; SBI Life reports a total cost ratio; LIC reports an expense ratio including commission. A fast-growing insurer will always look more expensive than a mature one on a total-premium basis, which is why the regulator's glide path is contentious for growing mid-sized players.

In plain terms

Customers who stop paying, capital that sits idle and costs that run too high are the three silent destroyers of value in life insurance. Persistency, solvency and the expense ratio are how you catch each of them early.

9. Product mix and protection share

What it is. The split of APE across ULIP, par, non-par, protection, annuity and group. Formula: product share = product APE ÷ total APE. Worked example: an insurer with ₹1,000 crore of APE selling ₹180 crore of term and group protection has an 18% protection share — ICICI Prudential's FY26 figure. Drivers and benchmarks: as in Section 04. Protection share of 15% or more is good, 8–15% average, under 8% weak. The definition trap: "protection" may include group term and credit life (low margin, lumpy) or only retail term (high margin). SBI Life's 26.4% in Q1 FY27 includes a large group term deal; its FY26 protection share was about 9%. Also, some insurers state mix on individual APE (HDFC Life) and others on total APE (SBI Life, ICICI Prudential).

10. Channel mix and bank concentration

What it is. The share of APE from each channel, and especially from the parent bank. Formula: banca share = APE sold through banks ÷ total APE. Worked example: HDFC Life sold 58% of H1 FY27 individual APE through banks; of ₹7,080 crore of individual APE, about ₹4,100 crore came through bank counters. Drivers: ownership (a bank parent), partnership wins, agency investment, digital reach. Benchmarks: below 35% from one bank is diversified, 35–55% average, above 55% a single-counter risk. The members: chart in Section 11 (SBI Life ~60%, HDFC Life 58%, ICICI 30%, LIC 7.5%). The definition trap: "banca" may mean the parent alone or all banks; "counter share" (Axis Max Life's 65–70% of Axis Bank's life sales) measures the bank's loyalty, not the insurer's dependence.

11. Claim settlement ratio

What it is. The share of death claims paid. Formula: claims paid ÷ claims received (by number), individual business. Worked example: 9,980 claims paid out of 10,000 received is 99.8% — HDFC Life's reported figure (SBI Life: 99.4%). Benchmarks: 99% or more good, 97–99% average, below 97% weak. Why it matters: it is the customer's measure of trust and a regulatory focus. The definition trap: ratios by number and by amount differ, and early-duration claims (within three years) are where disputes concentrate; a high overall ratio can hide a weak early-claims record.

12. PAT: why accounting profit understates value

What it is. Statutory profit after tax. Formula: PAT ≈ investment income on shareholders' funds + surplus transferred from policyholders' funds − new-business strain − tax. Worked example: in FY26 SBI Life earned PAT of ₹2,470 crore, wrote VNB of ₹6,672 crore and generated EV operating profit of ₹13,872 crore. Its accounting profit was 37% of the value it wrote and 18% of its EV operating profit. Drivers: growth (more strain), mix (non-par strain is heavier), surplus-transfer policy, investment gains on shareholders' funds, and for LIC the allocation of par surplus.

13. P/EV and the implied VNB multiple

What they are. Price-to-embedded value compares the market value with the value of the in-force book; the implied VNB multiple shows what the market pays for future new business. Formulas: P/EV = market cap ÷ EV; implied VNB multiple = (market cap − EV) ÷ VNB. Worked example: HDFC Life's market cap of ₹1,18,439 crore against EV of ₹66,461 crore gives 1.78×; the excess of ₹51,978 crore divided by FY26 VNB of ₹4,034 crore gives an implied multiple of 12.9×. Drivers: RoEV, VNB growth, risk (mix, regulation, ownership) and the market's discount rate. Benchmarks: 1.5–2.5× EV is normal for franchises earning 15–20% RoEV; below 1× signals doubt about the EV or governance. Implied VNB multiples of 15–25× imply a growth premium, 8–15× average, below 8× scepticism.

Definition traps, collected

  • Screener "sales", "operating profit", "OPM" and "ROCE" are meaningless for insurers. "Sales" adds premium to investment income, including mark-to-market gains on ULIP funds, so it swings with the Sensex: ICICI Prudential's screener "sales" were ₹25,401 crore in Q1 FY26 and ₹3,185 crore in Q4 FY26. Never quote an insurer EBITDA margin.
  • Four different "premiums". Total premium, NBP, WRP and APE can differ several-fold; market shares on different bases cannot be compared.
  • LIC's market share needs its basis. 56.7% of NBP, 36.6% of individual first-year premium (38.9% in Q1 FY27), 70% of group.
  • VNB margins are not on identical bases. LIC reports "net" margin on total APE including group; private insurers report post-tax on total APE; GST changed effective bases in 2025.
  • EV is not book value; P/EV is not P/B. Book value is a quarter to a third of EV for private insurers.
  • EV dates differ. Ratios built on March, June and September EVs are not like-for-like.
  • PAT understates value, and LIC's PAT is an allocation. P/Es of 8× (LIC) and 64× (SBI Life) are not comparable.
  • Max Financial is a holding company. Its PAT, P/E and P/B describe the holdco; its value is about 80% of Axis Max Life less any holdco discount.
  • Persistency bases differ. Premium vs policy count; regular vs all premium; cohort effects at 61 months.
  • "RoEV" can be total or operating. Only operating RoEV strips out markets.
  • Expense ratios differ by construction. Opex-only (Axis Max) vs total including commission (SBI, HDFC, LIC).
  • Group business is lumpy. One group term or fund deal can swing quarterly APE and protection share (SBI Life Q1 FY27).
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in life insurance has a twin that means something different: four premiums, two persistencies, two RoEVs, several margin bases. The discipline is to check the basis before comparing, compare a company with itself before comparing it with peers, and never use generic screens built for manufacturers.

How to read a life insurer's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. APE growth, individual and group separately (minute 1). Is individual APE growing at least in line with the private industry (about 10% in H1 FY27)? Strip out group deals. Red flag: headline growth driven by one group mandate.
  2. VNB and VNB margin (minute 2). Is VNB growing faster than APE? Red flag: margin up only because of the yield curve, or down without a mix explanation.
  3. Product mix (minute 3). ULIP share up or down? Protection and annuity growth? Red flag: a margin "beat" alongside a rising ULIP share, which usually means an assumption change.
  4. Channel mix (minute 4). Parent-bank share, agency growth, partnership wins. Red flag: rising single-bank dependence ahead of the IRDAI rules.
  5. Embedded value and operating RoEV (minute 5). EV growth, operating RoEV and the split between operating and market variances. Red flag: RoEV falling for a third period.
  6. Persistency, 13th and 61st month (minute 6). Red flag: 13th-month persistency down two points or more — a sign of aggressive selling.
  7. Expense ratio (minute 7). Against the proposed 15% and 12.5% steps. Red flag: rising while growth slows.
  8. Solvency and capital actions (minute 8). Ratio, sub-debt, equity raises, dividends. Red flag: approaching 160% while growing fast.
  9. Management commentary on regulation and rates (minute 9). GST drag, commission renegotiation, the IRDAI paper, hedging. Red flag: vague answers on how the cap saving will be shared.
  10. Valuation check (minute 10). Recompute P/EV on the new EV and the implied VNB multiple. Ignore P/E. Red flag: a multiple that implies VNB growth the quarter does not support.

Put together, the routine turns a forty-slide results deck into three judgements. The first is about quality of growth: did individual APE grow in the products and channels that create value, or did a group deal or a ULIP surge flatter the headline? The second is about quality of margin: did the VNB margin move because the insurer sold a richer mix and controlled costs, or because the yield curve, a tax change or an assumption reset moved it? The third is about quality of the balance sheet: is persistency holding, is capital adequate without being idle, and is the expense ratio heading towards where the regulator wants it?

Apply the routine to the latest periods and the members sort themselves. SBI Life scores well on margin, return and cost but its Q1 FY27 growth needs the group deal stripped out. HDFC Life's H1 FY27 shows recovering VNB growth (+12%) and a stable margin, but persistency has slipped and its expense ratio is the furthest from the proposed ceiling. ICICI Prudential has the best margin trajectory of the large insurers but the lowest return on embedded value because of its surplus capital. Axis Max Life's Q1 FY27 margin depended heavily on the yield curve. Canara HSBC's margin is rising fast from a low base while its 61-month persistency falls. LIC's margin gains are real and mix-driven, but its EV is hostage to the equity market. None of those conclusions can be reached from the income statement alone.

Two centuries, six shocks: how the industry got its shape

Every feature of today's market — LIC's size, the bank-promoted private insurers, the obsession with mix — traces back to a policy decision. The cycles are regulatory as much as economic.

India's first life insurer, the Oriental Life Insurance Company, was founded in Calcutta in 1818, and by the 1950s some 245 Indian and foreign insurers and provident societies were operating under the Insurance Act of 1938. The industry's modern history is best read as six shocks, each of which reset who sells what to whom.

Shock 1: nationalisation (1956). The LIC Act merged the private insurers into a single state corporation with a sovereign guarantee on its policies. For 44 years LIC was the only life insurer, and it built the agency army, the par-product habit and the rural reach that still define it. Shock 2: liberalisation (1999–2001). The IRDA Act opened the sector with a 26% foreign-investment cap. HDFC Standard Life, ICICI Prudential and Max New York Life started in 2000; SBI Life (SBI 74%, BNP Paribas Cardif 26%) and Bajaj Allianz in 2001; Canara HSBC OBC in 2008. The template was a bank or financial group plus a foreign insurer — the bank-JV land-grab.

Shock 3: the ULIP boom and bust (2008–2010). By FY08, ULIPs were more than 80% of private-sector sales, sold on equity returns with high front-loaded charges. After a turf war between SEBI and IRDA in April 2010 over who regulated ULIPs, IRDAI capped ULIP charges from September 2010 and lengthened the lock-in to five years. Private new business fell for several years and LIC regained share. The episode taught the private insurers that a volume model built on one product and generous commissions was fragile, and pushed them towards protection and non-par.

Shock 4: listing and margin re-engineering (2014–2022). The foreign-investment cap rose to 49% in 2015 and 74% in 2021. ICICI Prudential became the first listed life insurer in September 2016 (about ₹6,000 crore), followed by SBI Life (₹700 a share, about ₹8,400 crore) and HDFC Life in late 2017. A proposed HDFC Life–Max merger was blocked in 2016–17 because a listed non-insurer could not merge into an insurer — a problem only fixed by the 2025 Act. Insurers shifted mix towards protection and non-par; COVID-19 claims in FY22 and the reinsurers' repricing of term followed; Axis entered Max Life in 2021; HDFC Life bought Exide Life for ₹6,687 crore (merged October 2022); and LIC listed in May 2022 at ₹949 a share, India's largest IPO at the time.

Shock 5: the 2023 tax change and commission freedom. The tax on high-value non-ULIP policies from April 2023 produced a Q4 FY23 rush and an FY24 hole; IRDAI's April 2023 switch to an overall expense limit freed commissions; and the merger of HDFC Ltd into HDFC Bank in July 2023 made HDFC Bank the promoter of HDFC Life. Shock 6: the regulatory reset (2024–2026). Surrender-value norms (October 2024), the bancassurance-cap scare (November 2024), the GST exemption (September 2025), Canara HSBC's IPO (October 2025), the Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha Act (in force February 2026), Allianz's exit from Bajaj (January 2026), LIC's first share sale since its IPO (August 2026) and IRDAI's distribution paper (September 2026).

How to read the table

Start with the combined rows. The six members collected ₹8.2 lakh crore of premium in FY26 and wrote about ₹30,750 crore of VNB (INDICATIVE, because two inputs are estimated) on about ₹1.32 lakh crore of APE, a blended margin of 23.3%. They are worth ₹9.0 lakh crore against ₹10.3 lakh crore of embedded value, a combined P/EV of 0.87×. That aggregate is misleading on its own: LIC is 55% of the market value but 76% of the embedded value, so its 0.63× pulls the total below one. The private five trade at about 1.7× EV and about 24× FY26 VNB (m-cap ÷ VNB). Never quote a "sector P/EV" without saying whether LIC is in it.

Then read across each row in three blocks. The first block (premium, APE, VNB, margin) is this year's business: how much was sold and how valuable it was. The second (EV, operating RoEV, PAT, AUM) is the stock of value and how fast it grows. The third (m-cap, P/EV, implied multiple, P/E) is what the market pays. A strong insurer combines a margin above 25%, an operating RoEV above 18% and VNB growth above APE growth; SBI Life meets all three on FY26 numbers.

Note the contrasts. LIC's APE (₹66,961 crore) is larger than the four biggest private insurers' combined, and its VNB growth (+41.6%) was the fastest, yet it trades at 0.63× EV and a negative implied multiple. Why 0.63× to 1.94× embedded value: the valuation ladder, flipped

Insurers are valued on embedded value plus a multiple of new-business value. The spread across the six is explained mostly by return on EV, and the rest by ownership, structure and exposure to the new rules.

The market values a life insurer as the sum of two things: the value of the business it has already written (embedded value) and the value of the business it will write in future, usually expressed as a multiple of next year's VNB. This "appraisal value" framework turns into two observable ratios: price to EV, and the implied multiple of VNB the market is paying once EV is subtracted. For a franchise earning 15–20% on EV, 1.5–2.5× EV has been the normal range in India; anything below 1× says the market doubts the EV or the people running the company.

Technology and disruption: a marketplace that keeps slipping, and rails that already work

The biggest technology threat to the current model is a cheap public marketplace for policies. It has missed four deadlines. Meanwhile, quieter digital rails are cutting servicing costs.

The disruptive question in Indian life insurance is not artificial intelligence or new entrants; it is who owns the customer at the moment of sale. Today that is the bank branch, the agent or the online aggregator, and each is paid well for it. Technology could shift that ownership to a cheap public utility, to the insurer itself, or to a platform with its own pricing power.

Bima Sugam and the market infrastructure

Bima Sugam is IRDAI's planned insurance marketplace, one leg of the "Bima Trinity" (with Bima Vistaar, a simple bundled rural product, and Bima Vahak, a women-led field force) under the regulator's "Insurance for All by 2047" mission. The idea is a single platform where customers can compare, buy and service policies from all insurers at low cost. An information website went live on 17 September 2025. IRDAI's chair said in June 2026 that the first transactional products — motor, health and term — were targeted for the end of September 2026, but a live launch had not been confirmed by mid-October, and the September 2026 distribution paper describes the platform as operational in four to six months. Earlier targets of January 2023, June 2024, August 2024 and April 2025 were all missed.

If Bima Sugam works, it would commoditise the sale of simple products like term cover and shift pricing power from distributors towards manufacturers — good for low-cost insurers with strong brands, bad for intermediaries. If it keeps slipping, the bank counter remains the gatekeeper.

Aggregators and digital distribution

Online aggregators dominate the sale of term insurance on the internet, and their economics depend on commission. The proposed caps — 25% first year for multi-year pure term through an IDE, and all-inclusive of rewards and incentives — hit aggregators directly, which is why listed aggregator stocks reportedly fell much more than insurers on 24 September 2026. Insurers' own direct channels (HDFC Life 9–10% of individual APE; ICICI Prudential 14% of APE; Axis Max Life's online proprietary sales +27% in Q1 FY27) are the beneficiaries of any shift away from paid intermediaries.

Digital rails, data and accounting

Less visible but already working are the digital rails: e-KYC, UPI mandates for premium collection, account-aggregator data for underwriting and electronic insurance accounts. They lower servicing costs and make renewal collection easier, which supports persistency. On the risk side, mortality and longevity data increasingly sit with reinsurers, whose pricing sets the floor on term rates. Two further structural changes could matter. If composite licences — allowing one company to write both life and non-life — were enabled (their inclusion in the 2025 Act is unverified), general and health insurers could sell term cover. And Ind AS 117, the Indian version of IFRS 17, would change how insurers report profit, spreading it through a contractual service margin and narrowing the gap between accounting PAT and value metrics; LIC says it is preparing within the allowed regulatory time frames.

In plain terms

The technology that could most change life insurance in India is a cheap government-backed marketplace where anyone can compare and buy policies. It has been promised for four years and keeps slipping. If it arrives together with commission caps, the people who sell policies lose bargaining power and the insurers with the lowest costs and best brands gain it.

Bull vs bear: the case for the sector and the case against

Both cases are stronger in October 2026 than they were a year earlier: the private insurers are cheaper and more profitable per rupee of sales, and the regulator is more interventionist than at any time since 2010.

The bull case

  • Structural under-penetration: life premium is 2.7% of GDP against a world insurance average of 7.3%, with a mortality protection gap estimated in trillions of dollars and an ageing population that needs annuities.
  • Mix is improving: protection grew 33–53% after the GST exemption and annuities are compounding fast; every point of mix shift away from ULIPs adds margin.
  • The cap could be a gift: a 20% cap against a 27% bank average is a saving to manufacturers; if insurers keep most of it, margins rise three to six points (ILLUSTRATIVE).
  • Valuations have reset: the private five trade at about 1.7× EV and 24× VNB, down from 2.0–2.5× EV, with all five at or near 52-week lows.
  • LIC's convergence: net margin up from 17.6% to 22.9% in five quarters, the lowest costs in the sector, and a valuation (0.63× EV) that already assumes the worst.
  • Structure fixes: the 2025 Act opens the MFSL–Axis Max merger; 100% FDI could bring capital and partners.

The bear case

  • Regulation as a margin variable: four rule changes since 2023 each took 100–300 bps; the distribution paper is a fifth, and banks may recapture any saving through open architecture.
  • Penetration is falling: three straight years of decline as households move savings to mutual funds and equities; the tax reason to buy is fading under the new regime.
  • Return on EV is drifting down: HDFC Life 16.7% → 14.9%, ICICI ~13% → 11.9%, SBI 20.2% → 19.7%.
  • Cost cliff: HDFC Life, Canara HSBC and Axis Max Life must cut five to seven points of expenses by about FY29–FY30 on a strict reading of the EoM glide path.
  • Supply and structure: about 15% more of LIC to sell by 2032; MFSL's holdco discount and an unfiled merger scheme (see Other financial services on holdco discounts).
  • Rate and market risk: richer non-par and annuity mixes add interest-rate and longevity risk; ULIP demand tracks equity markets.

The bull case rests on two things that are measurable: whether insurers keep a meaningful share of the commission saving, and whether protection and annuity growth continue to lift the mix. If both happen, the 2026 de-rating will look like an overreaction to a proposal, and the lowest-cost insurers — which already command the highest multiples — will compound EV at 18–20% a year from a lower starting valuation. LIC adds a separate, larger option: a state insurer that has already closed most of its margin gap with the private sector, priced below the value of its existing book.

The bear case rests on a pattern. In every cycle since 2010, the regulator has moved value from insurers and distributors towards customers, and the industry has had to rebuild margin through mix. The distribution paper is the most direct intervention yet in the sector's cost structure; open architecture, if confirmed, would challenge the captive moat on which the largest private insurers are built; and the steady fall in penetration says that life insurance is losing the competition for household savings outside protection and pensions. On this view, the private insurers deserve lower multiples than they enjoyed in 2021–24, and LIC's discount is a permanent feature of state ownership rather than an opportunity.

The weight of evidence in October 2026 supports a split verdict. For the lowest-cost manufacturers with captive or equity-aligned distribution, the proposals are more likely to help than hurt, and the de-rating has priced in a good deal of the risk. For the higher-cost insurers, the next three years are a cost-cutting exercise whose success is not yet visible in the numbers. For LIC, the operating case is strong and the ownership case unchanged.

In plain terms

The optimists say India badly needs more life insurance, the products are getting better, and the regulator's plan may end up handing insurers part of what banks are paid today. The pessimists say the regulator keeps taking slices of insurers' profit, households prefer mutual funds, and costs at several insurers are too high. Both are partly right, and the answer differs company by company.

Glossary

The terms used in this report, in plain English.

Glossary of life-insurance terms
TermMeaning
APEAnnualised premium equivalent: 100% of annualised first-year regular premium plus 10% of single premium. The industry's sales measure.
NBPNew-business premium: first-year plus single premium at full value. IRDAI's basis for market share.
WRPWeighted received premium: first-year premium plus 10% of single premium, individual business only. Used for private-market share.
VNBValue of new business: present value of future after-tax shareholder profit on policies sold in the period, less the cost of required capital.
VNB margin (NBM)VNB ÷ APE. Value per rupee of sales.
EV / IEV(Indian) embedded value: adjusted net worth plus the value of in-force business. Excludes future sales.
VIFValue of in-force: present value of future shareholder profit on existing policies, net of cost of capital and the time value of guarantees.
EVOPEmbedded-value operating profit: unwind + VNB + operating variances.
Operating RoEVEVOP ÷ opening EV. The sector's return-on-equity equivalent.
UnwindThe expected return on opening EV at the risk discount rate.
Risk discount rate (RDR)The rate used to discount future shareholder profits in EV and VNB, typically ~8–10% in India.
Appraisal valueEV plus a multiple of future VNB; the basis for P/EV-based valuation.
P/EVMarket capitalisation ÷ embedded value.
Implied VNB multiple(Market cap − EV) ÷ VNB: what the market pays for future new business.
New-business strainThe first-year accounting loss on a new policy because commission, costs and reserves exceed the first premium's margin.
ULIPUnit-linked insurance plan: market-linked savings with life cover; the policyholder bears investment risk.
Par / participatingPolicies that share surplus with policyholders as bonuses; shareholders take at most one-tenth of distributed surplus.
Non-parNon-participating policies with guaranteed benefits; shareholders keep all the surplus and bear the interest-rate risk.
Protection / termPure risk cover with no maturity value; includes group term and credit life.
AnnuityA product that turns a lump sum into a guaranteed income, often for life.
PersistencyShare of a sales cohort's premium (or policies) still in force after 13, 25, 37, 49 or 61 months.
Special surrender valueThe minimum payout on early exit under the October 2024 rules, payable after one full year's premium on non-linked savings.
Solvency ratioAvailable solvency margin ÷ required solvency margin; regulatory minimum 150%.
EoMExpenses of management: commission plus operating expenses, limited by IRDAI as a share of premium.
Bancassurance / corporate agentSale of insurance through banks, which act as corporate agents for up to three life insurers under current rules.
IDEInsurance Distribution Entity: the single intermediary licence proposed in IRDAI's September 2026 paper (as reported).
Open architectureA distributor selling for any number of insurers; reported as an option for IDEs in the 2026 paper.
OFS / MPSOffer for sale of shares by an existing holder; minimum public shareholding required by SEBI.
Holdco discountThe discount at which a holding company trades to the value of its stakes.
Ind AS 117The Indian version of IFRS 17, the insurance-contracts accounting standard; adoption pending for Indian insurers.
Bima SugamIRDAI's planned online insurance marketplace; information site live since September 2025.
