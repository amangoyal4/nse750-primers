00

The one-page view: six companies, two businesses, one accounting trap

Five high-street franchisees that are barely profitable after rent, and one airport operator that earns more than all five put together.

India's listed quick-service restaurant (QSR) universe is small, and it is two different businesses wearing the same label. Five of the six companies are franchisees of American brands: Jubilant FoodWorks runs Domino's and Popeyes; Devyani International and Sapphire Foods run KFC and Pizza Hut for Yum! Brands; Westlife Foodworld runs McDonald's in the West and South; Restaurant Brands Asia (RBA) runs Burger King. The sixth, Travel Food Services (TFS), runs food courts and lounges inside airports, where the customer cannot walk across the road to a cheaper option.

Six forces define the sub-sector in late 2026, and the report is built around them.

  • The same-store-sales drought has broken, except in pizza. After three years of flat or falling like-for-like sales, Sapphire's KFC India grew same-store sales 14% in Q2 FY27, Westlife 10.1% (its first double-digit quarter in more than three years, after −1.1% for FY26) and Burger King India 12.6% in Q1. Domino's India managed 4.1%. Pizza Hut loses money at store level at both Yum franchisees.
  • Lease accounting flatters everyone. Under Ind AS 116 the five chains earned a 16.5% EBITDA margin in FY26; with rent treated as an operating cost, as it was before 2019, the figure is about 9.3%. On that basis RBA trades at about 84 times EBITDA and Westlife at about 44 times.
  • The delivery apps are growing faster than the chains. Eternal's food delivery order value grew 21.5% in Q2 FY27 and Swiggy's 17.7%, with Swiggy's 10-minute and ₹99 formats now more than a quarter of its food volumes. Domino's owns its customer through its app; the others pay 7–8% of sales in commissions.
  • The franchise contract sets the ceiling. Royalties actually expensed run from about 3.1% of sales at Jubilant to about 6.5–6.8% at the Yum franchisees, plus 5% or so for advertising. A Domino's store pays back in roughly two and a half years; KFC and McDonald's take four to five; Pizza Hut, at today's sales, never does (all indicative).
  • Consolidation is rewiring the map. Sapphire is merging into Devyani at 177 shares per 100, Inspira Global has taken control of RBA, and Jubilant is exiting Dunkin'.
  • The airport outlier is wobbling. TFS earns a 39% EBITDA margin and has no debt, but domestic air traffic fell 4.8% in July and 6.3% in August 2026, and its chief financial officer resigned on 7 October.

Why so little profit after so much revenue? Because a restaurant chain's cost base is heavy and fixed. Rent, crew and depreciation absorb most of the gross margin, and the interest charge on lease liabilities, which Ind AS 116 creates, front-loads costs into a store's early years. Across the six, interest and depreciation together took 15.9% of revenue in FY26 (₹4,021 crore). That is why an 18% headline EBITDA margin turns into a 2.6% net margin. When same-store sales rise, most of each extra rupee falls through to profit; when they stall, profit disappears. Westlife showed this in reverse between FY23 and FY25, when revenue rose 9% and profit after tax fell 89%, from ₹112 crore to ₹12 crore.

In plain terms

Indian fast food is a scale business that has not yet reached scale. Each chain pays a brand owner, a landlord and increasingly a delivery app before it pays itself. When stores are busy, the arithmetic works; when they are not, the fixed costs eat everything. The first half of FY27 is the first period in three years in which stores got busier, which is why sales are rising sharply while reported profit is still close to zero.

Three cautions apply throughout. First, every company defines its key numbers slightly differently: Jubilant says "like-for-like", the others say "same-store sales growth"; Devyani calls its pre-lease-accounting EBITDA "operating EBITDA", Sapphire calls the same idea "adjusted EBITDA", and Westlife's "operating EBITDA" is the post-lease figure. Second, "India QSR" is less Indian than it looks: about ₹5,500 crore, or 22%, of the six companies' revenue comes from Turkey, Thailand, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Nigeria, Nepal, Bangladesh, Malaysia and Hong Kong. Third, results arrive at different times: Sapphire and Westlife reported Q2 FY27 on 22 and 23 October 2026, RBA reports on 29 October, and Jubilant, Devyani and TFS are still at Q1 FY27, so every figure here carries its period.

01

Renting a brand, selling a meal: how the money is made

The listed companies own the stores, the leases and the risk. The brand belongs to someone in Louisville, Chicago, Ann Arbor or Miami.

A quick-service restaurant sells a limited, standardised menu at speed, for eating in, taking away or delivery. The listed Indian chains are not the owners of the brands they sell. Each is a master or development franchisee: it holds the right to open and run a brand's restaurants in a territory, and in return it pays the brand owner a royalty on every rupee of sales, contributes to an advertising fund, and follows the owner's recipes, store designs and supply rules. The franchisee funds the store, signs the lease, hires the crew, builds the kitchens and commissaries, runs the app, and keeps whatever is left.

The franchisee carries almost all the capital and almost all the risk. The brand owner and, increasingly, the delivery app are paid on sales whether or not the store makes a profit.

The order of payment matters more than the size of each claim. The royalty is charged on sales, so it is owed in a loss-making year. The aggregator commission is charged on the order value, so it rises with delivery volume regardless of the store's margin. The landlord's rent is mostly fixed, with a revenue-share kicker in malls and airports. The franchisee is paid last, out of what is left after food costs, crew, rent, power, royalty, advertising and commissions. That ordering is the single most important fact about the sub-sector: the claims of everyone else are senior to the shareholder's.

Where each link earns

Each link earns a very different return. The franchisors are asset-light: McDonald's Corp and Yum! Brands run margins in the region of 35–55% because they collect fees and, in McDonald's case, rent, without paying for most of the stores. Domino's Pizza Inc. earns an operating margin of about 20.4% (Q3 2026) on a system that is 99% franchised. The Indian franchisees earn about 9% of sales before Ind AS 116 at company level. The aggregators make thin margins on a large flow: Eternal's food delivery made adjusted EBITDA of 5.5% of order value in Q2 FY27. TFS, the airport operator, earns about 30% before lease accounting, because airport passengers pay ₹300 or more for a coffee that costs ₹120 on the street, and because banks pay for the lounge visits their card customers make.

In plain terms

A listed Indian QSR company is the part of the chain that builds the shop and takes the risk. The brand owner, the landlord and the delivery app are all paid on sales, before the shareholder sees anything. The operator wins only by squeezing more sales out of each rupee of store investment than its costs rise.

The one exception to the pattern is TFS. It also licenses brands (153 of them, its own and others'), but its landlord is an airport operator that sets the rules of the terminal and, through joint ventures with Adani and GMR, shares in TFS's own profit. Its customers are captive; its risks are passenger traffic and the next tender. It is analysed separately in Section 12.

Who wins within the franchisee group? The one with the cheapest store relative to its sales, its own delivery channel and the lightest royalty. On all three Domino's leads: a 600–700 sq ft delivery-first store costs about ₹1.5–1.6 crore to open, Jubilant delivers 76% of Domino's India revenue mostly through its own app and fleet, and its royalty is about 3.1% of sales. KFC, McDonald's and Burger King need bigger dining rooms, rely more on aggregators and pay higher royalties. That is the economic shape every later section fills in.

02

Size and the listed slice: a big market, a small listed share

India's food-services market is worth several lakh crore rupees; the six listed companies sell about ₹19,800 crore of it at home.

There is no single authoritative figure for the size of Indian quick-service food. The best ₹ series is the National Restaurant Association of India's India Food Services Report 2024, which put total food services at ₹5,69,487 crore in FY24, growing at 8.1% a year to ₹7,76,511 crore by FY28. The organised segment (chains and branded independents) was ₹2,49,649 crore, or 43.9%, growing faster at 13.2% a year to about 52.9% of the market by FY28. Within organised, QSR is roughly 27%, which implies an organised QSR market of about ₹67,000 crore in FY24 heading towards ₹1.3 lakh crore by FY28. That last step is our arithmetic on NRAI's shares and should be read as an estimate.

In plain terms

Indians spent somewhere between ₹6 lakh crore and ₹8.5 lakh crore eating food prepared by others in FY26, depending on whose study you use. Most of it was in dhabas, sweet shops, street stalls and independent restaurants. Branded quick-service food is perhaps a tenth of the total, and the listed companies sell perhaps a quarter of that.

The listed six: growth that is less organic than it looks

The six members' combined revenue rose from ₹15,821 crore in FY23 to ₹25,346 crore in FY26, a compound growth rate of about 17% a year. That flatters the underlying business. Jubilant's revenue jumped 43% in FY25 because it took control of DP Eurasia, its Turkish Domino's franchisee, and consolidated it for the first full year. Devyani's FY25 jump came from buying KFC's franchise in Thailand. TFS's FY26 revenue fell 2.4% because its Adani joint venture, Semolina Kitchens, stopped being consolidated in October 2024; system-wide sales, including joint ventures, rose 25%.

03

Who eats out, and why it stalled for three years

Demand is structural in the long run and cyclical in the short run, and FY24–FY26 was the cycle's worst stretch since the chains listed.

The long-run case for Indian QSR is simple and widely accepted. India's median age is about 28. The core customer, urban, aged 15–35 and often in a two-income household, is growing faster than the population. Indians still spend only about 10–12% of urban food budgets eating out, against 40–50% in the United States. Branded fast-food penetration is very low: India has roughly six branded QSR outlets per million people against more than 25 in China (approximate). Sapphire estimates KFC's annual customer base in its territories at 40–45 million and wants 100 million. Jubilant told analysts in August 2026 that only 6–7 million Indians eat pizza in a given month.

The short run is a different story. Same-store sales are the product of traffic (how many orders) and ticket (how much each order is worth), and both were squeezed from FY24 to FY26. The post-Covid reopening boom of FY22–FY23, when same-store sales grew 20% or more, ended. Food inflation stayed high (CPI food was around 7.5% in FY24), loan EMIs stayed high with the repo rate at 6.5% until February 2025, urban discretionary spending slowed, and the chains held prices flat or cut them with ₹99 value meals, which protected traffic at the cost of ticket.

In plain terms

A burger chain's sales from its existing restaurants grow only if more people come in or each person spends more. For three years neither happened: customers were stretched, and the chains cut prices to keep them coming. The recovery of the past two quarters has come mostly from more customers, not bigger bills.

Traffic, ticket and the value war

The H1 FY27 recovery is traffic-led. RBA described its 12.6% India same-store growth as "primarily traffic"; Westlife reported double-digit guest-count growth in all three months of Q2 FY27; Sapphire's ₹99 Chicken Krisper meal and ₹149 Thalaiva meal pulled in first-time buyers, 60–70% of whom came back. Jubilant shows the other side: in Q1 FY27 Domino's India orders grew 6.5% while like-for-like sales grew only 2.5%, which means the average ticket fell. The aggregators' ₹99 and ₹49 minimum-order formats forced Domino's to cut its own minimum order value to ₹99, a move its management called "a lagging action" that "hurts the economics". The steepest falls were in solo dine-in and takeaway occasions under ₹250.

Macro conditions in FY27 pull both ways. The GST rate cuts on many goods from 22 September 2025 and the Budget 2025 income-tax cut (no tax on income up to ₹12 lakh) freed household budgets. Against that, the West Asia war from 28 February 2026 raised fuel, freight and food costs, and the Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which is a headwind for discretionary spending in the second half of FY27.

The channel shift

Delivery is structural. NRAI-Redseer estimates that online ordering rose from 4% of food services in FY21 to 11% in FY26. The aggregators' food businesses are growing at around 18–22% a year, well ahead of the chains' same-store sales. But the chains are now deliberately pushing customers back into the restaurant, because a dine-in order carries no commission. Westlife's on-premise share is 59%; Devyani is targeting 59–60% dine-in salience at KFC (57% in Q1 FY27); Sapphire's KFC delivery share fell to 43% from 45%. Domino's is the exception, with delivery at 76.1% of Domino's India revenue, but most of that comes through its own app and riders.

Two further demand drivers matter for particular companies. Smaller towns are where the store count is going: Domino's is in more than 400 cities, Devyani's KFC in more than 280, Westlife in 80. Tier-2 and tier-3 stores pay lower rent but also take less per day, so payback is similar only if capex is scaled down, which is why Domino's and Devyani's BBK Express are shrinking their formats. Air travel drives TFS alone: its demand is passengers multiplied by spend per passenger, plus lounge visits paid by card issuers. Domestic passengers fell 4.8% in July 2026 and 6.3% in August, and banks have been tying lounge access to card spending.

Why now

Four things came together. The base was easy: FY26 was flat or negative for most brands, so even modest improvement shows as double digits. Value menus started working: ₹99 meals at KFC, McSaver, Burger King's 2-for-₹79 and Tasty Meal @₹99 pulled in traffic, and Sapphire's chief financial officer said plainly that the company does not mind giving up a few basis points of gross margin to do it. Marketing went up: Sapphire is adding 50–100 basis points of sales to KFC marketing and 100–150 to Pizza Hut in FY27. And wallets loosened after the September 2025 GST cuts and the income-tax cut. Jubilant's dine-in programme, which upgraded about 400 dine-in-heavy Domino's stores and added offers such as My Meal @₹119, helped its own modest recovery.

In plain terms

The chains bought back their customers with cheaper meals and louder advertising, against a year in which almost nothing had grown. It worked for chicken and burgers. Whether it lasts depends on whether the customers keep coming when the comparison base is no longer easy and the cost of each meal keeps rising.

Operating leverage, now running forward

Restaurant costs are about 60–65% fixed in the short run: rent, crew rosters, power for the kitchen and depreciation do not fall when traffic does. Why pizza is not recovering

Pizza is losing share of stomach on three fronts. The aggregators' ₹99 formats, Swiggy's Bolt and 99-Store and the quick-commerce cafés compete directly with the low-ticket pizza order that Domino's built its delivery business on. The franchisors are quietly subsidising growth

The expensed royalty is lower than the contract rate because brand owners pay incentives for opening stores, and the companies net those against the royalty line. Westlife's annual report says so explicitly: development incentives for meeting the store-opening targets in its master franchise agreement "have been netted off against the royalty expenses". The result is a royalty of 4.8% of sales in FY26, but 5.6% in Q1 and Q2 FY27 and only 2.4% in Q3 FY26, the quarter in which an incentive credit landed. Secondary reports say the contract rate is fixed at about 5% until FY29 and rises 50 basis points in FY30; that figure is from a single secondary source. Sapphire and Devyani net Yum's waivers and incentives the same way, and in the merger documents Yum has agreed to grant "certain waivers" to support Pizza Hut.

The implication is a risk most investors miss. If a chain slows its openings, it loses the incentives and its effective royalty rises back towards the contract rate. Westlife's restated target of "580+ restaurants by March 2028" (it was "580–630 by December 2027" in the Q1 deck) is therefore not only a growth signal; missing development targets would raise its royalty cost.

In plain terms

The brand owners give the Indian operators a discount for opening stores quickly. That discount shows up as a lower royalty bill. If the operator stops opening stores, the discount stops too, so a slowdown in openings hurts margins twice: less growth and a higher fee.

Popeyes: a new brand that works

The counter-example to Pizza Hut is Popeyes at Jubilant. In plain terms

Each company's value is mostly a weighted average of the brands it is stuck with.

For the Yum franchisees, the royalty-plus-advertising burden of about 11.5–11.8% of sales is the structural ceiling on margins, roughly double Domino's 6.5% at standalone level. At the top, each category has one or two franchise systems; below them sit thousands of independents, cloud kitchens and regional chains.

McDonald's is split between Westlife in the West and South and McDonald's own company in the North and East, which it took back from Vikram Bakshi's Connaught Plaza Restaurants in a 2019 settlement. Burger King belongs to RBA nationally.

Domino's own-app moat

Domino's India delivers 76.1% of its revenue, and most of that through its own app, website and 36,000-plus riders. The app had 19.5 million monthly active users and 5.8 million monthly transacting users in Q1 FY27; delivery revenue grew 12.1%, and NielsenIQ data cited by Jubilant show Domino's gained 80 basis points of delivery share among the top 25 QSR brands. Running its own fleet costs perhaps 10–12% of an order (estimate), against the 18–25% an aggregator charges, and it keeps the customer data. That is the single largest structural advantage any listed chain has.

But the moat has been breached at the low end. The aggregators' ₹99 and ₹49 minimum-order formats forced Domino's to cut its own minimum order value to ₹99, at a lower ticket and higher cost per order. Jubilant called it a lagging move that hurts the economics. The orders it lost most were solo meals under ₹250.

In plain terms

Domino's spent two decades building its own delivery network so that it would never have to pay Zomato or Swiggy. It mostly doesn't. But when the apps started offering ₹99 meals from cheaper kitchens, Domino's had to match the price, and a ₹99 pizza delivered by its own rider earns very little.

Ten-minute food: the platforms become competitors

The newer threat is that the aggregators are building their own food brands. Blinkit Bistro, launched in Gurugram around the turn of 2025, delivers meals, snacks and drinks in 10–15 minutes from in-house kitchens next to Blinkit's dark stores, with no restaurant partners. Eternal reports it inside "Others" with Nugget: ₹132 crore of adjusted revenue and an adjusted EBITDA loss of ₹91 crore in Q2 FY27. Zepto Café runs a standalone app with a target of 120 cities. Swiggy Bolt delivers food from partner restaurants in 10 minutes, and Swiggy Snacc is a standalone café app. Swiggy says Bolt and 99-Store together are now more than 25% of its food volumes, and it has launched a separate affordability app, Toing, in more than 50 cities.

These formats target exactly the ₹99–199 snack and value tier the chains use to build traffic. They also blur the line between platform and competitor: the platform sets the search ranking, the fees and the minimum order value, and now sells its own product next to the chains'. The National Restaurant Association of India has a long-running competition complaint against Zomato and Swiggy; the Competition Commission ordered an investigation in 2022, and the investigator's 2024 report reportedly found practices such as price parity and exclusivity to be anti-competitive. No final order was found. NRAI has also threatened to take the 10-minute apps to the CCI as private labels.

The tax change that would make delivery cheaper

One pending change cuts the other way. In October 2026 the 57th GST Council recommended bringing delivery services provided through e-commerce operators under section 9(5) of the GST law at 5% without input tax credit, against 18% generally charged before. This is a recommendation; it had not been notified as of 23 October 2026. If notified, it would lower the customer's delivery fee and make aggregator delivery cheaper relative to dine-in, the opposite of what the chains want. Restaurant food itself stays at 5% without credit.

The Ind AS 116 trap: profitable on paper, thin in cash Deep dive

Since 2019 lease accounting has moved rent out of EBITDA. For QSR chains, whose largest fixed cost is rent, it nearly doubles the headline margin.

Indian Accounting Standard 116, effective from FY20, requires a company to put its leases on the balance sheet. A store lease becomes a "right-of-use" asset and a lease liability. Rent no longer appears as an operating expense; instead the company books depreciation on the right-of-use asset and interest on the lease liability, both below EBITDA. EBITDA therefore rises by roughly the fixed rent. Profit after tax is lower in a lease's early years, because the interest is front-loaded, and higher in its later years. Cash is unchanged: the rent is still paid.

For most industries this is a footnote. For a restaurant chain whose largest fixed cost is rent, it changes the answer to the most basic question, whether the business is profitable. Screener's "operating profit" and every company's "reported EBITDA" are post-Ind AS 116. Every company also discloses a pre-Ind AS figure, under different names: Jubilant's "adjusted EBITDA", Devyani's "operating EBITDA", Sapphire's "adjusted EBITDA", RBA's company EBITDA. Westlife's "operating EBITDA" is the post-Ind AS figure, and its pre-Ind AS number comes from a quarterly reconciliation.

The accounting choice matters most when investors compare multiples. Thirteen numbers explain almost everything about a restaurant chain. Each is defined a little differently by each company, and each has a trap.

Worked example (round numbers, illustrative). A chain with 1,000 stores averaging ₹1 lakh a day takes 1,000 × ₹1,00,000 × 365 = ₹3,650 crore of revenue. In plain terms

Count the shops, multiply by how much each sells in a day, and you have revenue. Take off food, the brand owner's cut, the delivery app's cut, wages, rent and power, and what is left is the shop's profit. Every KPI below is one of those terms.

1. Store count and net additions

What it is. The number of restaurants open at period-end, and the change over the period: openings minus closures. Formula: closing stores = opening stores + openings − closures; net additions as a percentage of the opening base is the store growth rate. Worked example: a chain with 1,000 stores opens 120 and closes 20, so it adds 100 net, or 10%. If the new stores take ₹70,000 a day in their first year, against ₹1 lakh for mature ones, a full year of them adds 100 × ₹70,000 × 365 = ₹255.5 crore, about 7% of the base revenue of ₹3,650 crore. Store growth is the most controllable source of revenue growth, which is why chains keep opening stores even when same-store sales are flat.

What drives it: the chain's balance sheet and capex budget, the franchisor's development targets (and the incentives attached to them), site availability and, above all, whether new stores pay back. Benchmark: good is net additions of 10% or more of the base a year with new stores reaching mature sales within two years; average is 5–10%; weak is under 5% or net closures. 2. Same-store sales growth (SSSG) or like-for-like (LFL)

What it is. The year-on-year change in sales at stores that were open in both periods. It strips out new stores to show whether the existing estate is getting busier. Formula: SSSG = (sales this period at eligible stores ÷ the same stores' sales a year earlier) − 1, and approximately SSSG = (1 + traffic growth) × (1 + ticket growth) − 1. Worked example: if a chain's eligible stores serve 8% more orders at a 2% higher average bill, SSSG = 1.08 × 1.02 − 1 = 10.2%. If orders rise 6.5% but the average bill falls 3.8%, as at Domino's India in Q1 FY27 (orders +6.5%, LFL +2.5%), SSSG is only about 2.5%.

What drives it: traffic (marketing, value offers, the macro cycle, competition), ticket (price rises, mix, order size), calendar effects (festival timing such as Navratri, the number of weekends, IPL matches) and channel shifts. Benchmark: good is 8% or more; average 3–7%; weak 2% or less. A chain needs roughly 3–5% just to keep store margins flat against wage and rent inflation; Sapphire says its restaurant margin is neutral at 3–5% and expands only above that.

3. Average daily sales per store (ADS)

What it is. Average sales per store per day, usually for mature stores. It is the single best measure of a brand's pulling power in a given format. Formula: ADS = period revenue ÷ average stores ÷ days in the period. Worked example: Sapphire's KFC India ADS of ₹1,13,000 in Q2 FY27 means each store takes ₹1,13,000 × 365 = about ₹4.1 crore a year. Pizza Hut's ₹44,000 means ₹1.6 crore. With similar rents and crews, the KFC store has more than two and a half times the sales to cover them.

What drives it: same-store growth, store size and location, the share of new (immature) stores in the base and the format mix (drive-thrus and malls sell more; small-town and kiosk formats less). Benchmark: good is ₹1.2 lakh or more for burger and chicken formats and ₹90,000 or more for delivery pizza; average ₹80,000–1.2 lakh; weak below ₹60,000.

4. Delivery share and aggregator commission

What it is. The share of sales delivered to the customer, and the commission paid to Zomato or Swiggy on the part that comes through them. Formula: commission as % of total sales = delivery share × share of delivery via aggregators × commission rate. Worked example: a KFC-type chain with 45% delivery, 85% of it via aggregators at a 20% commission, pays 0.45 × 0.85 × 0.20 = 7.65% of total sales, close to what Devyani (7.0%) and Sapphire (7.2%) actually expensed in FY26. What drives it: the customer's habits, the chain's own-app investment, the platforms' negotiating power and promotions, and the deliberate push back to dine-in. Benchmark: good is either a delivery business that runs mostly on the brand's own channel (Domino's) or an aggregator share kept below 35–40% of sales; average is 35–45% via aggregators; weak is above 50% through aggregators.

5. Gross margin

What it is. The share of sales left after food and packaging. Formula: gross margin = (revenue − cost of food and packaging) ÷ revenue. Worked example: a ₹100 KFC meal whose chicken, buns, oil, sauces and box cost ₹32 earns a 68% gross margin. A ₹100 pizza whose dough, cheese and toppings cost ₹25 earns 75%.

What drives it: commodity prices (chicken feed, milk and cheese, oil), menu mix (beverages and sides are high-margin), pricing, value menus (which deliberately trade gross margin for traffic), waste and supply-chain efficiency. Benchmark: good is 72% or more; average 66–72%; weak below 65%. But a high gross margin is not the goal in itself: Pizza Hut's 76.3% at Sapphire goes with a loss at store level.

8. Ind AS 116 vs pre-Ind AS EBITDA: the gap

9. Capex per store and payback

What it is. What a new store costs to build and fit out, and how long its cash profit takes to repay that cost. Formula: payback (years) = capex ÷ annual store EBITDA (pre-Ind AS, pre-tax); sales-to-investment ratio = annual store sales ÷ capex. Worked example: a ₹1.5 crore Domino's store earning ₹63 lakh a year pays back in 1.5 ÷ 0.63 = 2.4 years; its sales of ₹3 crore are twice its capex. What drives it: format size, construction and equipment costs, landlord fit-out contributions, ADS and store margin, and the ramp-up curve (a new store may take 12–24 months to reach mature sales). Benchmark: good is payback within 3 years and sales/capex of 2× or more; average 3–5 years and 1.4–2×; weak beyond 5 years or below 1.2×.

10. Rent and occupancy cost

What it is. Fixed rent, revenue-share rent and common-area charges as a share of sales. Definition traps, collected

  • Pre- vs post-Ind AS 116. Screener's operating profit and every company's "reported EBITDA" are post-Ind AS. Devyani's "operating EBITDA", Sapphire's and Jubilant's "adjusted EBITDA" and RBA's company EBITDA are pre-Ind AS. Westlife's "operating EBITDA" is post-Ind AS. Use post-Ind AS with leases in enterprise value; use pre-Ind AS for store economics.
  • SSSG ≠ revenue growth ≠ system sales. Revenue growth = same-store growth on the mature base + new stores − closures ± currency. Jubilant's +11.9% Q2 FY27 revenue came with about 11% store growth and 4.1% like-for-like.
  • System sales vs consolidated revenue at TFS. System-wide sales (₹3,214 crore in FY26, +25%) include joint ventures; consolidated revenue (₹1,648 crore, −2.4%) does not.
  • Hyperinflation in Jubilant's group numbers. Turkey has been accounted for under Ind AS 29 since June 2022; a "monetary gain or loss" line moves EBITDA and PAT, and Eurasia's like-for-like is reported post-restatement.
  • "India QSR" is not all India. Devyani 33.1% international, Jubilant about 27%, RBA 19.5%, Sapphire about 16% (Sri Lanka is Pizza Hut and Taco Bell, not KFC); only Westlife is 100% Indian.
  • Store counts on four scopes. Group vs brand vs country; TFS outlets and lounges are not restaurants; Dunkin' is discontinued and excluded from Jubilant's count.
  • Royalty is net of incentives. The expensed royalty can swing three points between quarters (Westlife 2.4% in Q3 FY26, 5.6% in Q1–Q2 FY27).
  • Screener borrowings include leases; screener investments include JV stakes. TFS has no bank debt; Sapphire is net cash ex-leases; Devyani's ~₹800–870 crore of net debt is ex-leases.
  • RBA's balance sheet predates its recapitalisation. The March 2026 accounts exclude the July 2026 equity and warrant money.
  • P/E is mostly meaningless. Four of six have losses or near-zero profit because lease interest is front-loaded. Jubilant's ₹444 crore includes ₹58 crore from discontinued operations; TFS's Q1 FY27 profit includes a ₹13.1 crore one-off.
  • Different names for the same margin. Westlife's "ROM", Sapphire's "restaurant EBITDA", Devyani's "brand contribution" and RBA's "restaurant EBITDA" are close cousins, not identical measures.

How to read a QSR company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Same-store sales growth by brand (minute 1). Find the like-for-like number for each brand and split it into traffic and ticket if disclosed. Below 3% means margins will fall; above 8% means operating leverage. Red flag: growth driven only by price, with transactions falling.
  2. Check the calendar (minute 2). Did Navratri, Diwali, Ramzan or the IPL move between quarters? Did the number of weekends change? Sapphire's 14% was "close to double digit" once Navratri is adjusted.
  3. Store count and net additions (minute 3). Compare with guidance and with last year's pace. Red flag: closures rising, or openings slowing (which also cuts franchisor incentives).
  4. ADS or AUV (minute 4). Is the average store getting busier, or is new-store dilution hiding a decline? Compare ADS change with SSSG.
  5. Gross margin (minute 5). Change in basis points, and why: commodities, value menus or price. A fall is acceptable if traffic rose more.
  6. Pre-Ind AS restaurant and company EBITDA (minute 6). Use the company's own reconciliation, never the screener figure, for this. Red flag: revenue up double digits and pre-Ind AS margin flat or down.
  7. Cost lines that move (minute 7). Energy, wages, royalty and marketing as % of sales. Has the royalty jumped because incentives lapsed? Has marketing been raised to buy traffic?
  8. Channel mix (minute 8). Delivery share, own-app share and aggregator commission. Red flag: rising aggregator dependence with falling dine-in.
  9. Below EBITDA (minute 9). Interest and depreciation growth versus revenue; one-offs (property gains, GST write-backs, impairments, merger costs, labour-code charges, Ind AS 29 monetary gains); cash PAT as a cross-check.
  10. Balance sheet and guidance (minute 10). Net debt ex-leases, capex run-rate against guidance, and any change in the language of targets (Westlife's "580–630 by December 2027" becoming "580+ by March 2028" was in a footnote-sized line).
In plain terms

Start with how busy the existing shops were, then how many shops were added, then how much of each rupee survived before rent. If those three point the same way, the quarter was what it looked like. If they diverge, the headline numbers are hiding something.

Sapphire is merging into Devyani, Inspira Global has bought control of Burger King India's operator, and Jubilant is pruning brands while it digests Turkey.

Inspira takes Burger King India's operator

RBA's change of control was agreed in January 2026 and completed in July. The new owner is funding a fix, not an exit, in Indonesia. On 16 September 2026 RBA's board approved investing up to IDR 100 billion (about ₹55–60 crore) in PT Sari Burger Indonesia, through redeemable cumulative non-convertible preference shares in tranches, a debt-like instrument rather than common equity. Jubilant: Turkey in, Dunkin' out

Jubilant built control of DP Eurasia, the London-listed Domino's master franchisee for Turkey, Azerbaijan and Georgia, in steps: from 48.84% to 51.50% through market purchases in November–December 2023, then a final cash offer of 110 pence a share (up to €85.1 million) for the remaining 45.33% in January 2024. DP Eurasia was then delisted and is wholly owned. It is now a large business: Q1 FY27 revenue ₹665.1 crore (+28.2%), EBITDA ₹139.5 crore, PAT ₹45.9 crore, with 798 Domino's stores at September 2026 and 194 COFFY coffee shops at March 2026. Its numbers are restated for Turkish hyperinflation under Ind AS 29, so its like-for-like (−1.3% in Q1, −2.1% in Q2 FY27) and margins are not comparable with India's. Management guides a 7–9% PAT margin and has upstreamed about ₹52 crore of dividends.

At the same time Jubilant is narrowing its brand portfolio. It decided not to renew its Dunkin' development rights, with the agreement expected to end in December 2026; Dunkin' is reported as discontinued (15 stores in June 2026), and the disposal gain put ₹58 crore into FY26 profit. It is not investing further in Hong's Kitchen, a decision management summed up as "no coffee play". The capital is going to Domino's India (1,000 new stores across brands in India over FY26–FY28, on a supply chain sized for about 5,000) and Popeyes.

How the toll gate works

An airport passenger has limited time and no alternative, so prices are high: a cappuccino at ₹300–350 against ₹120 on the street. Lounges are paid for mainly by banks and card networks, which buy visits on behalf of their premium cardholders. In exchange, the airport operator, which under India's privatised airport model earns non-aeronautical revenue to support its aeronautical charges, demands a share. TFS's contracts are a minimum guarantee and/or a revenue share, whichever is higher; management says it "tends to always go above the minimum guarantee". In FY26 it expensed ₹258 crore of these variable concession fees and made ₹153.8 crore of fixed lease payments, about 25% of consolidated revenue in all (our arithmetic). Because the revenue-share fees stay inside EBITDA, TFS's pre-Ind AS EBITDA is still about ₹495 crore, or 30% (indicative).

The second feature is partnership with the landlord. At the Adani group's airports (Mumbai, Navi Mumbai, Ahmedabad, Jaipur, Lucknow, Guwahati and Thiruvananthapuram) TFS operates through Semolina Kitchens, in which it holds 24.99%, Adani Airport Holdings 50.02% and AJ Holding 24.99%. At GMR's airports (Hyderabad, Goa-Mopa and now Bhogapuram near Visakhapatnam, opened 17 August 2026) it holds 30% of GMR Hospitality. These are equity-accounted, so their sales are outside TFS's revenue: Semolina's FY26 revenue was ₹923.9 crore, up 47.1%, and its deconsolidation from October 2024 is why TFS's own revenue fell 2.4% in FY26 while system-wide sales rose 25% to ₹3,214 crore.

Vikas Kapoor, TFS's chief financial officer and whole-time director, resigned on 7 October 2026, citing "personal career aspirations"; his last working day and successor were to be announced "in due course". The second is supply. The promoter group, SSP Group (about 50%) and the Kapur family, holds 86.17% after the July 2025 IPO, which was entirely an offer for sale at ₹1,100 a share. Under the minimum public shareholding rule (Rule 19A of the Securities Contracts Regulation Rules), it must bring that down to 75% within three years of listing, by about July 2028. A March 2026 amendment gave longer windows to very large issuers (above ₹50,000 crore), which TFS is not, so the three-year deadline remains the working assumption. That implies about 11% of the company, roughly ₹1,700 crore at today's price, has to come to market through offers for sale or new issues. The stock, at ₹1,158, is 21% below its ₹1,471 high.

The case for TFS is that its moats (landlord partnerships, captive pricing, card-funded lounges and new airports at Navi Mumbai and Noida) remain intact, and traffic shocks have historically been temporary. The case against is that its multiple, about 22 times EV/EBITDA and 32 times earnings, assumes traffic recovers quickly while costs at new airports are front-loaded. For the hospitality side of India's travel economy, see the hotels report.

Rules of the kitchen: tax, food safety, competition and airports

Regulation rarely moves QSR profits suddenly, but three rules shape the economics every day: GST without credit, the aggregator tax regime and airport concession policy.

The most important rule is the 5% GST rate without input tax credit on restaurant services, in place since 15 November 2017, when the rate was cut from 12–18% with credit. It applies to standalone restaurants and to those in hotels with room tariffs up to ₹7,500. It looks like a low tax, but it means chains cannot recover the GST they pay on rent, equipment, packaging and services, much of it at 18%. We estimate the stranded tax at about 1.5–2.5% of sales (estimate). NRAI has long asked for an optional 12% rate with input credit. GST 2.0, effective 22 September 2025, rationalised rates on goods to 5% and 18% (plus a 40% rate for demerit goods) and cut butter, cheese and ghee to 5%, a small saving on inputs, but left restaurant services at 5% without credit.

Since 1 January 2022 the aggregators have collected and paid the 5% GST on restaurant food ordered through them, under section 9(5) of the GST law. The October 2026 GST Council recommendation, not yet notified, would bring delivery services through e-commerce operators under the same section at 5% without credit, against 18% generally charged before. If notified, the customer's delivery fee falls, which helps the platforms and works against the chains' push to dine-in. Lounge access billed to banks is typically charged at 18% (approximate).

Competition law and the platforms

The NRAI's complaint against Zomato and Swiggy alleges that the platforms enforce price parity, push exclusivity and use restaurant data to favour their own offerings. The Competition Commission ordered a Director General investigation in 2022, and the investigator's 2024 report reportedly found anti-competitive practices; we found no final order. The 10-minute food apps have widened the dispute, since the platforms now sell their own food alongside the restaurants they list. For the chains a ruling against price parity or self-preferencing would strengthen their bargaining position; a ruling that clears the platforms would leave the commission economics as they are.

Airports as regulated landlords

India's airport regulator, AERA, sets aeronautical charges (landing, parking, passenger fees). Food, retail and lounges are non-aeronautical revenue, part of which, under the hybrid-till model, offsets aeronautical charges. Airport operators therefore have every incentive to maximise concession income, which is why TFS pays about a quarter of its revenue to them and why the privatisation of six Airports Authority of India airports to the Adani group in 2019–2021 led to the Semolina Kitchens joint venture. Tenders are periodic; re-tendering is a standing risk for TFS.

In plain terms

A restaurant pays 5% GST on its food but cannot claim back the 18% it pays on its rent and equipment, so it carries a hidden tax of a couple of per cent of sales. The government is now proposing to cut the tax on app delivery fees, which would make ordering in cheaper just as the chains are trying to get customers to eat in.

Two accounting rules act almost like regulations. Ind AS 116 (Section 9) changed how every chain reports profit from FY20. Ind AS 29, hyperinflation accounting, has applied to Jubilant's Turkish business since June 2022 and adds a monetary gain-or-loss line that can move group profit by tens of crores a quarter. Neither changes cash, but both change what investors see.

Thirty years in six phases: from the first Domino's to the merger

Pioneers, reshuffles, a pandemic, a listing wave, a three-year drought and now consolidation.

1995–2010: the pioneers. KFC opened in Bengaluru in 1995; Pizza Hut followed in 1996; Domino's opened its first store in New Delhi in January 1996 under the Bhartia group; McDonald's entered in October 1996 through two joint ventures, Hardcastle Restaurants (the Jatia family) in the West and South and Connaught Plaza Restaurants (Vikram Bakshi) in the North and East. Domino's won with delivery, value pricing and a strong vegetarian menu, and Jubilant FoodWorks' February 2010 IPO made it the Indian consumer growth stock of the next decade.

2010–2019: expansion and franchise reshuffles. Westlife took full ownership of the McDonald's West and South business. Yum consolidated its Indian franchisees, and Devyani and Sapphire, the latter assembled from 2015 by Samara and other investors, emerged as the two big KFC and Pizza Hut operators. Burger King entered in November 2014 through Everstone. Swiggy was founded and Zomato began delivering. GST arrived in July 2017 at 18% for restaurants and was cut to 5% without credit in November. The McDonald's–Bakshi dispute ended in 2019 with McDonald's taking back the North and East, and Ind AS 116 took effect from April 2019.

2020–2022: Covid and the listing wave. Lockdowns closed dining rooms; delivery-led Domino's gained share while Westlife and Pizza Hut suffered. Burger King India listed in December 2020, Zomato in July 2021, Devyani in August 2021 and Sapphire in November 2021. Reopening brought same-store growth of 20% or more in FY22–FY23 (Burger King India's FY23 was 23.1%). Burger King India bought the Indonesian Burger King and Popeyes franchises and renamed itself Restaurant Brands Asia; Jubilant signed Popeyes for India and took its first stake in DP Eurasia.

FY24–FY26: the drought. Normalisation after reopening, an urban slowdown and food inflation hit together. Value wars followed (Domino's ₹49 and ₹99 pizzas, McSaver, KFC buckets, the BK Stunner), the aggregators kept growing at about 20% a year, and like-for-like sales went to zero. Westlife's profit fell from ₹112 crore to ₹12 crore; Devyani and Sapphire slipped into losses; RBA stayed loss-making. The chains responded with smaller formats, portfolio pruning and acquisitions.

FY26–FY27: consolidation and inflection. TFS listed in July 2025. The Devyani–Sapphire merger and Inspira's purchase of RBA were announced in January 2026. The West Asia war from February raised energy and packaging costs and cut air travel. In the June and September 2026 quarters same-store growth turned double-digit for KFC, McDonald's and Burger King.

How to read the table

Start with the two margin columns. The post-Ind AS EBITDA margin ranks the chains in a way that looks respectable (11.6–20%); the pre-Ind AS margin shows how thin they are (2.7–12.9%). The ranking barely changes, Jubilant first and RBA last, but the absolute level does: at pre-Ind AS margins of 7–8%, Devyani, Sapphire and Westlife have little cushion against a two-point cost shock.

Then the PAT column. Of the five chains, only Jubilant and Westlife reported a profit for FY26, and Westlife's ₹32 crore included a property gain. The combined ₹197 crore for the five is less than half of Jubilant's ₹444 crore on its own. TFS's ₹452 crore is larger than all five combined.

Then the two debt columns. Including leases, every chain except TFS carries meaningful debt; excluding them, Sapphire and TFS have net cash and Devyani has about ₹869 crore of net bank debt from its Thailand and Sky Gate purchases. Jubilant's figure includes DP Eurasia's Turkish-lira bank debt. RBA's is overstated until its September balance sheet, released with its Q2 results on 29 October, shows the Inspira money.

Then valuation, two ways. On EV/EBITDA with leases in EV, the group trades at 19.9 times, Sapphire is cheapest (16.2 times) and Westlife dearest (31.1 times). On market cap divided by pre-Ind AS EBITDA, the spread is much wider: Jubilant about 22 times, RBA about 84. The second measure is the better guide to how much recovery is priced in. The five chains on 318 times earnings and the group on 120 times are not useful numbers except as evidence that the market is valuing the stores, not the current profits.

Finally, the native KPIs. The last three columns show what drives the next year: same-store growth (Sapphire's KFC, RBA and Westlife strong; Domino's, Devyani's KFC and TFS weak), store count and ADS. A chain with a high multiple and weak same-store growth (TFS on the latest quarter) carries more risk than one with a high multiple and strong momentum (Westlife), and the market's ranking on the day of Westlife's Q2 print, when the stock rose 6.1%, reflects exactly that.

In plain terms

Together the six companies sell about ₹25,000 crore of food a year, make about ₹650 crore of profit, mostly at the airport business and at Domino's, and are valued at about ₹78,000 crore. On the profit figure closest to cash, investors are paying roughly 29 times for the group, and far more for the chains still in turnaround.

The global lens: Indian operators priced like global franchisors

The listed Indian chains build and run stores, as Yum China does. They trade at multiples closer to the asset-light brand owners'.

The right global comparison for an Indian QSR franchisee is not McDonald's Corp or Yum! Brands. Those companies are mostly franchisors: McDonald's is about 95% franchised and earns rent as well as royalties, Yum about 98%, Domino's Pizza Inc. about 99%. They carry little capital and earn margins of roughly 35–55% (approximate). The Indian listed companies are operators, who own the store risk. Their true peers are Yum China (company-operated KFC and Pizza Hut, 18% franchised), Jollibee in the Philippines and Domino's Pizza Enterprises in Australia, a master franchisee like Jubilant.

Technology and disruption: apps, kiosks, dark kitchens and drugs

Technology is both the chains' best defence (own apps, kiosks, electric fleets) and the platforms' best weapon (10-minute food).

Own apps and digital ordering are the chains' main defence against commissions. Domino's India's app had 19.5 million monthly active users in Q1 FY27, and Jubilant's fleet of more than 36,000 riders is 67% electric. Westlife's digital channels, including self-ordering kiosks (it has "experience of the future" kiosks in every restaurant), the McDonald's app (4.2 million monthly users in Q2 FY27) and McDelivery, account for more than 75% of its sales. Owning the order lets a chain run its own promotions, keep customer data and avoid the 18–25% commission.

Quick-commerce food is the main disruptive threat. Blinkit Bistro, Zepto Café and Swiggy's Bolt and Snacc deliver snacks, drinks and meals in 10–15 minutes, often from kitchens next to dark stores. They target the low-ticket, impulse occasions (coffee, a snack, a solo meal under ₹250) that chains use to build traffic. Eternal's "Others" line, which includes Bistro, lost ₹91 crore at adjusted EBITDA level in Q2 FY27, so the platforms are subsidising the push.

Cloud kitchens and digital-first brands such as Rebel Foods and Curefoods run many brands from shared kitchens with no dining room, competing on the aggregators at lower capex. Automation (kitchen display systems, kiosks, AI-based demand forecasting and prep planning) is reducing crew hours per order; Westlife's CFO targets 100–150 basis points of cost savings a year. Energy technology is now a cost lever: RBA is buying into a captive solar company, and electric delivery fleets cut fuel costs.

The long-run wild card is health. Front-of-pack and high-fat-sugar-salt labelling rules have been under discussion at FSSAI since 2022, and in the United States the spread of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs is being studied for its effect on calorie-dense food demand. Generic semaglutide was reported to have arrived in India in 2026 after patent expiry; adoption is small, and no effect on QSR demand is visible yet.

In plain terms

The chains are using apps and kiosks to keep orders for themselves; the delivery companies are using dark kitchens and 10-minute delivery to take the cheapest orders away. The chain that owns the most customer relationships directly, Domino's, has the strongest defence.

Bull vs bear: the case for the sub-sector, and against it

Both cases agree on the facts; they disagree on whether the H1 FY27 recovery lasts and whether the chains can keep more of each rupee.

The bull case

  • The drought has broken. Double-digit same-store growth at KFC (Sapphire 14%), McDonald's (10.1%) and Burger King (12.6%), led by traffic, after three flat years.
  • Operating leverage works forward. Westlife's Q2 operating profit rose 39% on 16% revenue growth; the five chains' EBITDA grew faster than revenue in Q1 for the first time since FY23.
  • Runway. About 1,419 KFCs in India against 13,789 in China; organised QSR growing at about 13% a year; online food services rising to 18% of the market by FY31 (NRAI-Redseer).
  • Consolidation unlocks value. Up to ₹210–225 Cr of merger synergies (about half of Devyani's pre-Ind AS EBITDA), Yum concessions on Pizza Hut, and recapitalised owners at RBA.
  • Franchisors are helping. Development incentives, waivers and Westlife's royalty reportedly fixed at about 5% to FY29.
  • Domino's moat and TFS's toll gate are durable franchises, at about 22 and 31 times pre-Ind AS EBITDA respectively.

The bear case

  • Thin real margins. The five chains' pre-Ind AS EBITDA is about 9.3%, and their FY26 PAT was ₹197 Cr on ₹23,698 Cr of revenue; four of five earn below their cost of capital.
  • Costs are rising. LPG still about two-thirds above pre-war levels, electricity at 6–7.5% of sales, minimum wages and labour codes; inflation expected to peak in Q3 FY27.
  • The platforms are winning the growth. Eternal food +21.5%, Swiggy +17.7%; Bolt and 99-Store over a quarter of Swiggy's food volumes; the pending 5% GST on delivery could make ordering in cheaper.
  • Pizza is broken in part. Domino's like-for-like only 4.1%; Pizza Hut loses money at both franchisees.
  • Valuations price the recovery. RBA about 84 times and Westlife about 44 times pre-Ind AS EBITDA; the group at 19.9 times EV/EBITDA against Yum China's 10–14.
  • Event risk. CCI timing on the merger, TFS's falling traffic, CFO exit and promoter sell-down, the RBI's October hike, and comparisons that get harder from Q3 FY27.

The bull case rests on one variable: whether same-store growth stays at 6–8% or more after Q3 FY27, when the comparison base stops being easy. If it does, the fixed-cost base turns most of the extra sales into profit and today's multiples fall quickly on forward numbers; Westlife, RBA and Sapphire move most. If growth drops back to 2–4%, the value meals and marketing that bought the traffic become a permanent cost, and the chains are left with higher energy and wage bills on the same store margins. The bear case is strongest for the companies with the thinnest pre-Ind AS margins and the highest multiples.

Two positions sit between the cases. Jubilant combines the best economics with the weakest momentum and the lowest pre-Ind AS multiple; it needs like-for-like growth back at 5–7% rather than a cost miracle. TFS has the best margin and balance sheet, but its next two quarters depend on air traffic recovering. For investors comparing the sector with steadier staples compounding, the diversified FMCG report is the natural contrast: lower growth, far higher margins, no leases.

In plain terms

The optimists say the customers are back and every extra customer is almost pure profit. The pessimists say the customers came back because meals got cheaper, costs are still rising, the delivery apps are taking the growth, and the share prices already assume it will all work. Both are describing the same quarter.
