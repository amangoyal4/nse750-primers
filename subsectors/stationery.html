00

The one-page view: a branded maker in an unbranded bazaar

Stationery is a ₹50,000-crore market of ₹5 and ₹10 items. It is growing slowly, it is mostly unbranded, and its one listed full-basket maker has grown at 36% a year by taking share from everyone else.

Every Indian schoolchild's bag holds the products of this industry: a pencil, an eraser and a sharpener, a geometry box, a packet of crayons, a set of ruled notebooks, two or three ball pens. Every office holds more of them: files, staplers, highlighters, registers. Very little of it is high technology, most of it sells at a fixed round-number price of ₹5, ₹10 or ₹20, and roughly two of every three rupees spent on it go to products that carry no recognisable brand at all. Yet this unglamorous trade has produced one of the most richly valued consumer stocks on the Indian market.

The report answers three questions. First, how does anyone make money selling a ₹5 pencil, and where in the chain from poplar log to school bag does the profit sit? Second, why has a single company, DOMS Industries, been able to grow revenue from ₹684 crore in FY22 to ₹2,326 crore in FY26, a 36% compound rate, in a market that grew only about 3% a year between FY17 and FY23? Third, what changed in 2025–26, when a tax reform made most school stationery tax-free and a war in West Asia pushed the cost of the plastics in a pen or a sharpener up by a fifth in a single quarter?

DOMS is the only company in the NSE 750 classified in this sub-sector, so the report is built around the whole industry and uses DOMS as the lens. The pen makers Flair and Linc, the art-materials maker Kokuyo Camlin, the notebook and publishing house Navneet and the writing-instruments arm of Cello World are listed but sit in other sub-sectors; they appear here as clearly marked reference peers. The two largest stationery businesses after DOMS are not listed at all: ITC's Classmate notebooks, the industry's biggest single business, and Hindustan Pencils, the maker of Nataraj and Apsara. Global brand owners such as F.I.L.A. of Italy, which still holds 19.01% of DOMS, complete the picture.

Six forces decide who earns what in Indian stationery:

  • Formalisation from a mostly unbranded base. Branded products were 29% of the market in FY17 and 36% in FY23, and Technopak projects 43% by FY28. Outside pens the branded share is only about 14%. A branded maker can grow at twice the market for a decade without any help from demand. Whether GST 2.0 speeds this up or slows it down is contested, and the report sets out both views.
  • The pencil wedge and the duopoly. Pencils are only about ₹1,650 crore of sales (FY23), but they are the first thing a child buys and the product that wins the shop counter. DOMS held about 29% of the pencil market by value in FY23 on Technopak's count and claims "upwards of 35%" today (both single-source and INDICATIVE); Hindustan Pencils' revenue was flat in FY25 and its profit fell about 78%.
  • Backward integration against input-cost shocks. Making your own leads, slats, erasers, tins and now pen tips lowers cost against importers and small makers, but it does not hedge commodity prices. DOMS's gross margin fell 4.7 points year on year in Q2 FY27; every listed peer lost 1–4 points.
  • Capacity-led growth and its absorption. DOMS is building a 50-plus-acre campus costing ₹850–1,000 crore, has bought the Reynolds pen brand, and has gone from net cash of ₹72 crore in March 2025 to net debt of about ₹159 crore in September 2026.
  • Falling school enrolment against rising spend per child. Total enrolment has been flat near 24.7 crore for three years, but primary enrolment, the pencil-buying cohort, is still shrinking by about 2.4% a year. Growth has to come from more rupees per child.
  • The FILA relationship. FILA is DOMS's technology partner, a ₹175.6-crore customer and, since 2024, a steady seller of its shares: 30.58% after the IPO, 19.01% today.

The tension running through the rest of the report is visible in that second chart. The franchise is intact: DOMS grew revenue 17.6% in the September 2026 quarter despite floods at its Gujarat warehouses. The economics are not: its EBITDA margin fell from 17.5% to 12.4% in a year, its profit after tax fell 37% to ₹38.3 crore, and the cash it generates no longer covers what it is spending on new plants. At ₹11,486 crore of market value and about 53 times trailing earnings on 3 November 2026, investors are still paying for the franchise. The sections that follow explain what would have to be true for that price to hold, and what the industry's own data say about each condition.

In plain terms

Selling pencils and notebooks is a volume game played at fixed price points. A brand that can make almost everything itself, cheaply and in one place, and get it into a hundred thousand shops, keeps the manufacturing profit that importers and small workshops give away. That model has let DOMS grow three times faster than the market. Its weakness showed in 2026: when the cost of plastic jumped, a company that sells ₹5 pencils cannot easily charge ₹5.50, so the shock lands on its margin first and on prices only slowly.

01

How stationery makes money: from poplar log to school bag

The trade keeps a third of the shelf price. The maker's profit depends on what it makes itself and on how many items it can put on the same shelf.

A stationery product passes through five hands before it reaches a child. Raw materials (farm-grown poplar wood, natural graphite and clay, polymer granules, paper, pigments and wax) are bought by a manufacturer that turns them into finished goods. The finished goods go to a super-stockist, usually one per state or large region, which breaks bulk for a network of distributors. Each distributor serves a few hundred shops: the neighbourhood stationery store, the general store near a school, the photocopy shop that also sells pens. The shop sells to the household. In the case of a school order or a state free-kit scheme, an institutional buyer replaces the last two steps and pays a lower price.

Technopak's trade survey for the DOMS IPO puts the economics of that chain in round numbers. The distributor buys at 30–40% below the printed maximum retail price (MRP) and keeps about 10% of its buying price for itself. The retailer keeps 25–30% of the MRP. Institutional buyers such as schools get 40–45% off MRP. Put together, a maker realises only about 60–70% of the MRP before any GST, and the trade keeps the rest. That share has not changed much in a decade, because general trade still sells about 70% of all stationery and modern retail only about 12%.

The trade keeps roughly ₹3.3–4 of every ₹10 of shelf price. A maker's edge comes from Step 2 (cost) and from how much of the shelf in Step 5 it fills with its own products.

Value is captured in two places. The first is the brand and distribution layer. A parent buying a ₹10 pencil pack does not compare prices; the price is printed and every competing pack costs about the same. What the parent and the shopkeeper choose between is the name, the look of the pack and whether the pencil breaks when sharpened. A brand that a shopkeeper trusts to sell through gets the front of the counter, and the front of the counter sells the eraser, the sharpener and the scale beside it. The second place is the manufacturing cost of the commodity core. Because price points are fixed by habit at ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20, the only way to earn more on a mass pencil is to make it for less. Technopak's report says it plainly: in volume categories, raw-material increases are mostly absorbed by manufacturers.

Three features of the economics recur through the report. Price points are sticky. In a decade, mass pencil MRPs have moved only from ₹5 to ₹6 or ₹7 at most, in DOMS's own description (Q1 FY27 call, August 2026). A price rise usually means a jump to the next round number, a smaller pack or more pieces per pack, and it takes several quarters to spread through the trade. Breadth per shop matters more than the number of shops. DOMS's stated policy is to win "maximum shelf space" in a store before adding the next one, because the cost of a sales visit is the same whether the shop buys one category or nine. Exports are a second customer. India is a net exporter of pencils, pens and notebooks, and a large part of DOMS's export is contract manufacturing for F.I.L.A.'s global brands, chiefly Dixon Ticonderoga in the United States.

In plain terms

Of every ₹10 a parent pays for a pencil pack, about ₹3.4 stays with the shop and the distributor, and the maker gets the rest. The maker cannot raise the ₹10 easily, so it earns more only by making the pencil more cheaply, by selling more items into the same shop, or by selling to a foreign brand that pays in dollars. Each of the large Indian makers has picked a different mix of those three levers.

02

Size, growth and the branded shift: a slow market with a fast-growing top

The industry grew about 3% a year from FY17 to FY23. The branded share rose 7 points over the same period. That gap is where the listed makers' growth came from.

The only bottom-up, IPO-diligenced size of the Indian market is Technopak's, written for the DOMS prospectus in November 2023. It puts domestic sales of stationery and art materials at ₹38,500 crore in FY23, excluding exports, and projects ₹71,600 crore by FY28, a compound rate of about 13% a year. Paper stationery (notebooks and paper) is the largest family at ₹16,200 crore, followed by writing instruments at ₹13,350 crore, office supplies at ₹4,700 crore, a group of "others" (technical instruments such as geometry boxes, erasers, sharpeners, adhesives, calculators) at ₹3,130 crore, and art and craft at ₹1,120 crore.

The projection should be read against the history in the same report. The market was ₹31,400 crore in FY17, fell to ₹28,000 crore by FY22 after schools closed in the pandemic, and rebounded 35% to ₹38,500 crore in FY23. From FY17 to FY23 that is a compound growth rate of only about 3.5% a year. The 13% forecast is therefore an acceleration written at the top of a post-COVID rebound, for an IPO document. No newer primary market study has been published. If Technopak's 13% had held, the FY26 market would be about ₹55,500 crore; on a more conservative 8–10% path it would be ₹48,500–51,200 crore. The evidence from listed companies (DOMS +21.6% in FY26, Flair +15.7%, Kokuyo Camlin +5.6%, Linc flat, Navneet's stationery flat to slightly up) is consistent with a market growing 8–11% while branded leaders take share. This report therefore uses ₹48–55k crore for FY26 as an indicative range, not a measured number.

Two other market sizes circulate, and neither should be mixed with Technopak's. The annual reports of DOMS, Flair and Linc for FY26 all quote Fortune Business Insights' figure of an Indian stationery market of "US$18.08 billion by 2026", about ₹1.6 lakh crore, roughly three to four times the Technopak base. The branded shift

The more useful number in Technopak's report is the branded share. Branded products were 29% of the market in FY17, 35% in FY22 and 36% in FY23, and are projected at 43% by FY28. In writing instruments the brands already dominate: 68% in FY17, 78% in FY23, 87% projected for FY28. Subtract writing instruments and the picture changes. Of the ₹25,150 crore of FY23 sales outside pens, pencils and markers, only about ₹3,440 crore, or about 14%, was branded. Notebooks, geometry boxes, erasers, crayons, files and registers are still mostly sold under no name, or under a printer's or a shop's name.

Formalisation in pens is largely done. In notebooks, maths boxes, erasers and crayons it has barely started, which is why DOMS's category expansion matters more than its pen share.

Branded share rising by a little over a point a year sounds slow, but its effect on the branded makers is large. In a market growing at 9%, a branded segment whose share rises from 36% to 43% in five years grows at roughly 13% a year, and the leaders inside it, if they also gain share from other brands, can grow at 18–20%. That arithmetic, not a boom in demand, explains most of the gap between DOMS's 36% compound growth since FY22 and the market's single digits. Part of DOMS's early growth also came from post-COVID recovery and from adding categories it had not sold before.

India's place in the world puts the long-run argument in one line. Technopak estimates the global stationery and art-materials market at about US$192 billion in 2022, growing about 2.8% a year, with India at roughly 2.4% of it. Per head, Indians spent about US$3 a year on stationery in 2022, against about US$38 in China, US$24 globally and well over US$100 in the United States, Germany and Japan. India uses about three pencils per person per year against about six in the United States. The gap says that per-capita spending can rise for decades. It does not say how fast, and the history from FY17 to FY23 is a warning that it can be slow.

In plain terms

Nobody knows exactly how big the Indian stationery market is today; the best guess is ₹48,000–55,000 crore. What is known is that about two-thirds of it is still unbranded, and that outside pens the unbranded share is closer to six in seven. A brand that is simply taking business from nameless notebooks and loose erasers can grow quickly even if India's children are not buying more stationery overall.

03

Who buys: the enrolment puzzle and the rupees per child

School stationery is 55% of demand. The number of schoolchildren has stopped growing and the youngest cohort is shrinking, so the growth has to come from each child spending more.

Technopak's split of FY23 demand is simple: about 55% of stationery is bought for school, about 30% for offices and about 15% by artists, professionals and households. Roughly 80% is bought by consumers and 20% by institutions (companies, schools, colleges and state free-kit schemes such as Telangana's education kits and Andhra Pradesh's student kits). Within consumer purchases, buyers up to age 16 account for about 45%, ages 16–25 for 13% and over-25s for 42%. Of student demand, about 77% comes from school-going children up to class 10 and 23% from college students.

The headline total hides the cohort that matters most to pencil makers. Primary enrolment (classes I–V) was 12.20 crore in 2020-21 and 10.19 crore in 2025-26, a fall of 16.5% in five years. Part of that is the method change; on the comparable series from 2022-23 the fall is 9.3% in three years, and in 2025-26 alone primary enrolment fell 2.36%. Secondary and higher-secondary enrolment rose. The cause is demographic: India's total fertility rate fell below replacement (1.91 in NFHS-5), and a 2022 NCERT study had already projected declines at every level. Children use pencils until about class V and then switch to pens, so the pencil wedge is losing roughly 2–3% of its users a year.

Inside the shrinking cohort, the mix is moving toward the buyer that branded makers want. There is no survey that measures this directly, so the point is an inference, but it is consistent with branded makers growing while the cohort shrinks.

Wood, graphite and polymer: the cost of a ₹5 pencil

Materials are more than half of revenue and about 70% of them move with crude oil. Labour is the next-largest cost. Integration lowers both, but cannot stop a commodity spike.

A stationery maker buys a surprisingly wide basket of inputs. Wood-cased pencils use slats of poplar, a fast-growing tree planted on farms across Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir; premium pencils use jelutong or cedar. The pencil core is natural graphite mixed with clay and fired; polymer pencils add a plastic binder. Pens, sharpeners, scales, boxes and polymer pencils are moulded from polypropylene, polystyrene, ABS and polycarbonate. Erasers are PVC or rubber compounds. Crayons are paraffin wax and pigment; poster and water colours use pigments, gums and PVA. Notebooks are mostly paper, which is 60–70% of a notebook's cost. Pens need tips (brass, nickel-silver or steel housings around tungsten-carbide balls) and ink. All of it is packed in tin, board and printed labels.

Capacity in stationery is quoted in pieces. DOMS's prospectus listed FY23 installed capacity of 1,512 million wooden pencils a year, 396 million polymer pencils, 1,058 million crayons, 650 million erasers, 420 million sharpeners and 250 million ball pens. In plain terms

A pencil maker's costs are mostly things it buys: plastic, wood, graphite, paint and packaging. Seven of every ten rupees of those purchases move with the price of oil, so when oil and plastics jumped in March 2026, costs jumped too. Making more of the parts in-house makes the pencil cheaper than a rival's, but it does not protect the maker from paying more for the plastic itself.

Imports, China and the duty wall

Customs data put the "China threat" in proportion. In calendar 2025, India imported US$21.9 million of pencils, crayons and pastels (HS 9609), 74% from China; US$88.7 million of pens and markers (HS 9608), 57% from China; and US$13.8 million of notebooks (HS 4820), 80% from China. Together that is about US$124 million, roughly ₹1,080 crore, or about 2% of the domestic market. India exported far more of each: US$56.3 million of pencils, US$220.5 million of pens and US$194.3 million of notebooks. The basic customs duty is 10% (most-favoured-nation rate) on pencils, ball pens and exercise books, which with nil GST gives an effective duty of about 12% on Chinese pencils and notebooks; pens, at 18% GST, carry about 32%. Under free-trade agreements, pencils and notebooks from ASEAN, Japan and Korea enter duty-free. No anti-dumping duty applies to finished stationery, and there is no mandatory Bureau of Indian Standards quality-control order on pens, pencils, erasers, crayons or notebooks.

Six business models, one shop counter

Stationery makers differ by how much they make themselves, the price points they sell at and how many categories they put in each shop. Those three choices explain most of the gap in margins and returns.

The companies that sell into an Indian stationery shop fall into six recognisable models. They compete for the same counter, but they earn money in different ways and fail for different reasons. The integrated full-basket brand (DOMS) makes eight or nine categories in its own plants, from pencils and erasers to crayons, maths boxes, notebooks and now pens, and sells them all through one distribution network, with contract exports on the side. The writing-instrument specialist (Flair, Linc, Luxor, Cello's pen arm, Rorito, Elkos) makes mostly pens, at ₹5–15 for the mass market with a premium or licensed range on top, and exports a fifth or more of its output. The pencil duopolist (Hindustan Pencils) built a vast volume business on pencils, erasers, sharpeners and scales under two brands. The paper-stationery house (ITC Classmate, Navneet, Sundaram, DOMS's Pioneer subsidiary) prints and binds notebooks, often alongside publishing. The art-materials specialist (Kokuyo Camlin) leads in crayons, colours and fine art. Finally there are contract exporters and importer-branders who make or buy goods for someone else's label.

Three variables separate these models. The first is how much of the commodity core the maker owns. DOMS extrudes its own leads, makes its own slats, erasers, crayons, sketch-pen caps, ink reservoirs and tins, and is now installing tip machines. That lowers unit cost against an assembler that buys parts, but it also puts more labour and fixed cost inside the company, so its gross margin looks lower than a pen specialist's while its EBITDA margin has been similar or higher. Pens carry more brand value per unit than pencils, so specialists like Flair and Cello earn gross margins near 50%; pencils and notebooks are closer to commodities. Pencils and the duopoly: how a ₹1,650-crore category decides a ₹50,000-crore market Deep dive

Pencils are 12% of writing-instrument sales and about 4% of the whole market. They are also the first item every child buys, the anchor of the shop counter and the place where share is now visibly moving.

Technopak sized India's pencil market at about ₹1,650 crore in FY23, roughly 400 crore pencils a year. Wood-cased pencils were 92% of value, polymer pencils 7% and mechanical pencils 1%. In plain terms

Pencils are cheap and small, but whoever owns the pencil shelf in a shop tends to sell the eraser, sharpener and crayons beside it. Two companies own most of that shelf. Nil is not the same as zero cost

A nil-rated supply is an exempt supply. Under section 17(2) of the CGST Act, a maker cannot claim input-tax credit on the polymers, paper, packaging, freight and services used to make exempt goods. Before the reform, a pencil maker paid GST on its inputs and set it off against the 12% it collected on its pencils. After the reform, the GST paid on inputs for nil-rated products becomes a cost. The headline tax cut therefore had two effects that pull in opposite directions: it removed 12% from the consumer's price, and it added the blocked input credit to the maker's cost.

DOMS described how it handled this on the Q2 FY26 call. It "reworked all our cost sheets" and set new MRPs that built in the lost credit. MRPs fell only slightly: a pencil pack that sold for ₹60 became ₹58, against about ₹53.60 if the full 12% had been passed through. At the primary level, DOMS's selling prices to the trade rose about 3–4%. The CFO's conclusion: "I don't see a significant impact of this on the margins." The cost was a transition: the trade destocked old-MRP goods in September and October 2025, which cut Q2 FY26 sales by about 3–4% and required credit notes to the channel, with restocking spread into the March–June back-to-school season.

Does GST 2.0 speed up or slow down formalisation?

This is the most contested question about the reform, and there is no data either way yet. Management's view is that the cut "creates a level playing field between organized and unorganized players" and will shift demand toward branded products, because a branded pencil pack is now cheaper relative to household budgets, the paperwork advantage of staying small has shrunk, and branded makers can absorb the input-credit cost more easily through scale and repricing.

The opposing hypothesis runs the other way. Before September 2025, an unregistered pencil or notebook maker that did not charge 12% GST had a built-in price advantage of up to 12% over a compliant brand. That advantage was one of the forces pushing customers toward cheap, unbranded goods, but it also capped how cheap they could be relative to a brand that could reclaim input tax. With nil GST for everyone, the small maker loses nothing by being unregistered, while the branded maker loses its input credits. On this view, the reform narrows the cost gap in favour of the unorganised tail in exactly the categories (pencils, erasers, sharpeners, notebooks) where the brands are trying to formalise the market. The fact that MRPs fell only about 3% supports neither view decisively.

View 1: a level playing field (management)

  • Shelf prices of branded pencils, erasers and notebooks fell, making the brand more affordable.
  • The tax-evasion edge of unregistered makers (not charging 12%) disappeared.
  • Brands absorbed the blocked credit through MRP resets and a 3–4% primary price rise, with no significant margin damage.
  • Kits, the most branded format, keep full input credit at 18%.

View 2: a cost edge for the small maker (hypothesis)

  • Nil GST removes input credits only for compliant makers who used them; the unregistered maker had none to lose.
  • The brand's blocked credit (~6% of materials, ILLUSTRATIVE) is a new cost the tail does not bear.
  • Consumers saw only ~₹2 off a ₹60 pack, a weak demand stimulus.
  • Imports of nil-rated pencils and notebooks now face only the 10% basic customs duty and no IGST.

The test will come in the data over FY27–28: whether branded makers' volumes in the nil-rated categories (DOMS's Scholastic Stationery, Paper Stationery; Hindustan Pencils' revenue) accelerate after the transition, or whether the unorganised tail holds its ground. For pens, the reform changed nothing, and the 18% rate keeps the formalisation benefit for compliant makers. For DOMS's hygiene arm, the drop from 12% to 5% on diapers is straightforwardly helpful: input credits remain available at 5%, and diapers became cheaper.

In plain terms

The government took GST off pencils, erasers, sharpeners and notebooks, but the makers could no longer claim back the tax they paid on plastic, paper and packaging. So they cut the shelf price only a little (₹60 to ₹58 on one pencil pack) and kept the rest to cover the tax they now cannot reclaim. Parents saved about 3%, the makers say they lost nothing, and nobody yet knows whether small unbranded makers gained or lost ground.

Why DOMS lost the most: a deliberate choice

The gap between DOMS and its peers is not an accident. Asked on the Q1 FY27 call why it did not price harder, management said it wanted "to ensure that the breathing space given to the small unorganized players is reduced". It was the same playbook it had used in COVID and in the 2022 polymer spike that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine: hold prices as long as possible, keep volume growing, and let smaller rivals, who cannot absorb the cost, lose share. Peers "did a similar sort of a step" after DOMS moved, management added. Flair took selective price increases and trimmed trade schemes; Linc passed on about 50% of its raw-material increase; Navneet faced only a ~2% rise in paper. DOMS chose volume: revenue grew 18.4% in H1 FY27, at the top of the listed group.

The 2022 precedent explains why management believes the strategy works. In FY22 DOMS's EBITDA margin was 10.2%; in FY23, the year of the Russia-Ukraine input spike, it rose to 15.4%, and to 17.7% in FY24, as volume growth (+77% in FY23, partly a post-COVID rebound) spread fixed costs and DOMS took share. Gross margin barely moved in FY23 (36.8% to 37.0%) and then jumped to 41.9% in FY24 as input prices eased while DOMS's price increases stuck. The 2026 version differs in one important way: DOMS is now adding large fixed costs at the same time, so the operating leverage that rescued FY23 is being used up by the new campus and Reynolds before they produce.

Price points make the recovery slow. In plain terms

In 2026 the plastic and paint in a pencil box suddenly cost a fifth more. Seven decades, four shocks: how the industry got here

Demand shocks are rare in stationery; margin shocks recur. The industry's history is a series of input-cost spikes that the biggest brands used to take share.

Indian stationery has two cycles. The first is the school calendar, an annual rhythm that loads sales into the April–June quarter and the months before it. The second is the input-cost cycle of crude oil, polymers, wood and paper, which arrives irregularly and hits margins hard because price points cannot move quickly. In two decades there has been only one true demand shock, the school closures of 2020–21, when the market shrank and DOMS made a small loss. Margin shocks have come more often: the 2022 polymer surge after Russia invaded Ukraine, the 2025 combination of a US tariff and the GST transition, and the 2026 West Asia war. Reading a stationery maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Sixteen numbers explain almost everything about a stationery company. Each one has a formula, a benchmark and at least one way to be misread.

Stationery companies disclose less than most consumer companies. They do not report volumes in a standard unit, never disclose capacity utilisation, and present their category and channel mixes on a gross-sales basis that differs from reported revenue. The KPIs below are the ones that can be built from what DOMS and the reference peers actually publish, with a note on where each is missing. Where only DOMS discloses a metric, the comparison is with DOMS's own history and with whatever partial peer data exist.

The equation, and which terms management controls

A branded stationery maker's revenue is the sum, across categories and channels, of how many shops it reaches, how many of its products each shop stocks, how many units each product sells and the price the maker realises per unit, plus exports and any adjacent businesses. Profit is that revenue times the gross margin, less employee and other costs. Management controls reach, range and much of the cost base; it does not control the input prices that set the gross margin, the school calendar or the price points the trade and the customer expect.

In plain terms

A stationery maker earns more by reaching more shops, putting more of its products in each shop and selling them at a price that covers its costs. It can control the first two. It cannot control what plastic and wood cost, and it can change its prices only slowly because shoppers expect ₹5 and ₹10 price tags. Most of what goes wrong shows up first in the gross margin.

KPI 1 — Volume: pencils a day and the volume-price split

What it is. The number of units made or sold. Stationery makers rarely report sales volumes; the nearest proxies are capacity (DOMS: wooden pencils 5.53 million a day in February 2026, 5.8 million in May, about 8 million planned) and management's split of revenue growth into price and volume. Formula. Volume growth ≈ revenue growth − price increase − mix effect. Worked example. DOMS's H1 FY27 revenue grew 18.4%; management took price increases of about 4–5% in the March and June quarters; so volume and mix contributed roughly 13–14 points (INDICATIVE). What moves it. Outlets, shelf share, new categories, capacity, exports, the school season, enrolment. Benchmarks. Volume growth of 12% or more is strong in a market whose volume grows low-to-mid single digits; 5–12% average; below 5% weak. Peers. Flair guided high-single-digit pen growth for FY27; Linc's Q1 FY27 operating income rose 1.4%; no peer discloses a clean volume figure. Definition trap. "Capacity" is quoted per day by management and per year in the prospectus (1,512 million wooden pencils in FY23); converting needs an assumption of about 300 working days, and 25–30% of DOMS's wood-pencil output is exported.

KPI 2 — Category mix (on gross product sales)

What it is. The share of revenue left after the cost of materials and bought-in goods. Formula. DOMS: 100 − (materials consumed + purchases of stock-in-trade + change in inventories) ÷ revenue. KPI 6 — Capacity and utilisation (never disclosed)

What it is. How much a plant can make, and what share of that it actually makes. Formula. Utilisation = output ÷ installed capacity. Worked example. If DOMS made 1.6 billion wooden pencils a year on 5.8 million a day of capacity over 300 days (1.74 billion), utilisation would be 92%; this is a hypothetical, because DOMS does not disclose output or utilisation. What moves it. Demand relative to new lines; the 90-day start-up, six-to-seven-month climb to "decent" utilisation and 18–24 months to full that management describes for new buildings. Benchmarks. Above 80% suggests a need for new capacity; 60–80% is normal; below 60% means fixed costs are under-absorbed. Peers. Flair says its steel-bottle lines run at about 65%; no stationery maker discloses pen, pencil or art utilisation. Definition trap. The prospectus listed 14 separate capacities (crayons, erasers, sharpeners, scales and more), and management now quotes only wooden pencils; a "doubling" of built-up area (2.0 to about 4.0 million sq ft) is not a doubling of capacity in every product.

In the absence of a utilisation figure, three proxies help. The first is the asset turn (KPI 7): if revenue per rupee of gross block rises, existing plants are being worked harder. The second is management's language about specific lines: in February 2026 it said wooden-pencil capacity of about 5.5 million a day was a constraint, and in FY26 it moved spare black-lead capacity to colour pencils when US exports fell, which tells you those lines were full enough to matter. The third is the order of the new campus's phases (pencils first, then pens, erasers and sharpeners, then art), which reveals where management believes it is shortest of capacity. None of these is a substitute for a disclosed number, and the gap should be raised with the company.

KPI 7 — Asset turn

What it is. Revenue earned per rupee of gross fixed assets, management's preferred efficiency target. Formula. Revenue ÷ gross block. Worked example. Utilisation, price increases (which raise revenue on the same assets) and the timing of new capacity, which adds assets before revenue. Benchmarks. 3× or more good, 2–3× average, below 2× weak. Peers. Linc reported fixed-asset turnover of 3.72× (Q1 FY27 call); Flair says its capex-to-revenue ratio is about 1:3. Definition trap. What it is. The size of the network that puts products on shelves. Formula. Counts of directly served outlets, distributors and super-stockists; revenue per outlet = channel sales ÷ outlets. KPI 13 — P/E and EV/EBITDA

What it is. What the market pays per rupee of profit (P/E) and per rupee of operating profit including debt (EV/EBITDA). Formula. P/E = market cap ÷ trailing PAT; EV/EBITDA = (market cap + net debt) ÷ EBITDA. Worked example. DOMS on 3 November 2026: market cap ₹11,486 crore at ₹1,893 a share. Screener shows a P/E of 52.8, which equals market cap ÷ about ₹217 crore, the trailing PAT before the flood exceptional; on reported trailing PAT of ₹203 crore it is about 56.6×, and on FY26 EPS of ₹37.93 it is 49.9×. For Indian consumer staples, 15–25× EV/EBITDA is typical; above 30× prices sustained high growth. Peers (FY26 EV/EBITDA). Cello 15.1×, Camlin 13.4×, Flair 10.8×, Linc 9.0×, Navneet 7.5×; F.I.L.A. ~6.1–6.3× (INDICATIVE). Definition trap. Screener's Navneet P/E of 23.2 excludes one-off gains; on reported trailing PAT of ₹353 crore it is 8.2×. Kokuyo Camlin's consolidated page is stale, so its standalone P/E (34.2) is used.

A multiple is easier to judge once it is turned into an expectation. An ILLUSTRATIVE exercise: if DOMS meets its 18–20% revenue guidance in FY27 (about ₹2,770 crore at 19%) and grows 18% in FY28 (about ₹3,270 crore), and if the EBITDA margin returns to management's "16-odd percent" in FY28, FY28 EBITDA would be about ₹520 crore. Definition traps, collected

  • Gross product sales versus revenue. DOMS's category, channel, brand and region mixes are on GPS (₹2,420.6 crore in FY26), before trade discounts and schemes; revenue was ₹2,326.4 crore. A mix percentage applied to revenue gives the wrong rupee figure.

  • EBITDA with or without other income. DOMS and screener exclude it; Cello's presentations include it; CRISIL adds back some items. Always check the line.
  • Net debt with or without leases and promoter loans. DOMS ~₹159 crore ex-leases (Sep-26) against screener's ₹260 crore including leases.
  • FILA sales standalone versus consolidated. ₹133.0 crore versus ₹175.6 crore in FY26; the consolidated figure matches the 7.3% channel share.
  • Four bases for "market share". 29% of all pencils (Technopak FY23), "35%+" (management), 12% of branded stationery (Technopak exhibit; 7–9% on its own arithmetic), and Hindustan Pencils' 15% of all writing instruments (Euromonitor).

  • Screener's P/E. DOMS's 52.8 uses trailing PAT before the flood exceptional; Navneet's 23.2 excludes one-off gains (8.2× on reported PAT).
  • Exports booked as domestic. Sales to merchant exporters appear in DOMS's West-region domestic sales.
  • Seasonality. Navneet and, to a lesser extent, other makers earn most of their year in the April–June quarter; compare quarters year on year, never sequentially.
  • UDISE+ method break. Enrolment from 2022-23 is counted student by student and is not comparable with earlier years.

How to read a stationery company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth, year on year. Compare with guidance (DOMS FY27: 18–20%) and with the same quarter last year, never the previous quarter, because of the school season. Red flag: growth below guidance with no one-off (floods, GST transition) to explain it.
  2. Gross margin. The first read on input costs and pricing. Compare year on year and with the pen specialists. Red flag: a fall of more than 2 points with no price increase announced.
  3. Price versus raw-material commentary. How much prices rose against how much inputs rose. The gap tells you how long the margin squeeze will last.
  4. Employee cost as a % of revenue. Rising staff costs before new plants produce are normal; rising staff costs with no capacity explanation are not.
  5. EBITDA margin and the segment split. Separate stationery from hygiene. Red flag: stationery segment margin below 14% for more than two quarters.
  6. Category and channel mix (on GPS). Is growth coming from the core (Scholastic Stationery) or from new categories? Is FILA's export share stable?
  7. Exceptional items. Strip them out (the Q2 FY27 flood: ₹10.39 crore net) to see underlying PAT, and check whether insurance recoveries are booked later.
  8. Balance sheet: borrowings, CWIP and inventory. At half-year, check net debt (Sep-26 ~₹159 crore), capital-work-in-progress (₹300.9 crore) and inventory (₹406.5 crore). Red flag: inventory rising faster than sales outside the pre-season build.
  9. Cash flow at the half and full year. Operating cash flow against capex; negative FCF is expected in a build phase, but should narrow as plants fill.
  10. Guidance and capacity timeline. Revenue guidance, any margin target (FY28 ~16%), commissioning dates for new buildings, and the Reynolds ramp. Red flag: a delay in commissioning combined with a cut in revenue guidance.
In plain terms

When a stationery company reports, look first at whether sales grew as promised, then at the gross margin to see what raw materials did, then at whether staff costs are rising because of new factories. After that, check debt, unfinished construction and cash flow. Ten minutes on those numbers tells you more than an hour on the press release.

Most disagreements between analysts about stationery companies turn out to be two people using different versions of the same number.

Who is winning: growth, returns and the shock quarter

On growth and returns DOMS leads every listed comparator. On resilience in 2026 it ranks last. Three maps and a scorecard show both.

The first map plots FY26 revenue growth against FY26 EBITDA margin. A winner in a branded consumer category should sit in the top-right: growing faster than the market while earning a good margin. DOMS and Flair are the only two companies there. Cello earns the highest margin of the group from its houseware franchise but grew under 9%. Navneet and Linc sit on the left, with flat or falling sales, and Kokuyo Camlin is the weakest on both axes.

The second map adds the market's verdict. It plots return on capital employed against EV/EBITDA. The market pays for return on capital, but not in a straight line: DOMS's 24% ROCE commands about 29 times EBITDA, while Linc's 19% commands 9 times. The difference is growth. Linc's revenue was flat in FY26; DOMS's grew 22%, and the market is paying for that growth to continue at a high return on a capital base that is about to double.

The third map looks only at the latest reported quarter: revenue growth against the change in EBITDA margin. It answers the 2026 question directly: who held margin, and at what cost to growth? The pattern is a trade-off. Linc held its margin best (−0.9 points) but grew 1.4%. Kokuyo Camlin and Cello lost 1.7–1.8 points with flat sales. Flair grew 6% and lost 3.9 points. Technology and disruption: screens, tips, quick commerce

The digital classroom is a slower threat to pencils than the falling birth rate. The faster changes are in how pens are made and where stationery is bought.

The obvious disruption, screens replacing paper, has so far been slow in Indian schools. Technopak's post-COVID assessment was that digital adoption in schools remained limited and that stationery use stayed prevalent; India's board examinations are still written by hand, and foundational-literacy programmes such as NIPUN Bharat rely on writing practice. Digitalisation bites harder in offices, where it already weighs on files, registers and office paper, about 30% of the market. Over a decade, digital classrooms under the National Education Policy are a gradual erosion risk for notebooks more than for pencils. The faster-moving demand risk is demographic: primary enrolment falling 2–3% a year (Section 03).

Manufacturing technology is where the competitive change is happening now. The pen tip is the most precise component in a ball pen, made on Swiss machines to tolerances of microns, and it has historically been imported or bought from a few Indian makers such as CRI Ltd (₹234 crore of FY25 revenue) and Newell's Indian unit. DOMS's first in-house tip plant, targeting 30–40% of its own needs over 2026–27, and Flair's existing in-house tip capability, move value back inside the brand. In pencils, polymer pencils (7% of pencil value), triangular and grip pencils and mechanical pencils (1%, launched by DOMS in Q2 FY26 and nil-rated under GST) are premium substitutes for the standard hexagonal wood pencil; they lift revenue per child even as the cohort shrinks. Injection-moulding automation and in-house slat-making yields lower unit costs for whoever has the scale to invest.

Channels are changing faster than products. E-commerce and quick commerce were about 5% of stationery sales in FY23 and Technopak projects 9% by FY28. DOMS's modern-trade and e-commerce share rose from 3.1% of gross product sales in FY22 to 7.3% in FY26, led in FY27 by diapers; Linc's e-commerce subsidiary grew 32% in Q1 FY27; Navneet credits e-commerce and quick commerce for its domestic paper-stationery growth. Quick-commerce apps favour brands with fast-moving, well-packed SKUs and kits, and they compress the long distribution chain that gives the trade a third of the shelf price. Over time that could shift margin from distributors to brands and platforms. It also makes price comparison easier, which may weaken the sticky-price-point habit that both protects and constrains the industry.

Two other pressures are worth noting. Sustainability rules: India's extended-producer-responsibility rules for plastic packaging add cost and paperwork, and export customers demand non-toxic certification (EN 71, ASTM D-4236). Poplar is a farm-grown wood, which gives Indian pencils a better sourcing story than tropical-wood pencils. Input regulation: India's quality-control orders on polymers (PP, PE, PVC, ABS) were rescinded in November 2025, easing resin imports for moulders; the economics of polymer inputs are covered in the Plastics report. Finally, cross-border e-commerce in cheap fancy stationery is restricted in India, so the main import competition still comes through wholesale channels rather than apps.

In plain terms

Tablets are not about to replace pencils in Indian classrooms; fewer children are a bigger threat. The real changes are in factories, where the big brands now make their own pen tips, and in shopping, where quick-commerce apps are starting to sell stationery that used to come only from the corner shop.

Bull vs bear: share-gain machine or capacity bet in a shrinking cohort

Both cases agree on the facts. They disagree on whether the 2026 margin loss bought lasting share, and on whether the new capacity can be filled at a good price.

The case for Indian stationery, and for its one listed member, rests on formalisation, integration and a proven playbook. The case against rests on demographics, a margin model that has just been shown to be exposed, and a capital cycle that is consuming cash at a valuation that leaves little cushion. The two columns below set out each side as its proponents would put it.

The bull case

  • A long formalisation runway. Only about 36% of the market is branded, and only about 14% outside pens. Branded leaders can grow at twice the market for years without demand help.
  • The duopoly is tilting. Hindustan Pencils' revenue was flat and its profit fell 78% in FY25 while DOMS grew 24%; DOMS claims 35%+ of pencils and targets ~45%.
  • The 2022 precedent. After the last input spike, DOMS's EBITDA margin rose from 10.2% to 17.7% in two years as share gains and price increases stuck.
  • Integration and breadth. Nine categories, in-house leads, slats, erasers, packaging and now tips; 6,300+ distributors; the highest ROCE in the listed group (24%).
  • New engines. Reynolds (target ~10% of revenue by FY29), pens (+59% in FY26), hobby and craft (+194%), kits, and the Seven bag venture with FILA.
  • Spend per child is rising. DOMS's domestic sales per enrolled student more than doubled from FY23 to FY26 (ILLUSTRATIVE), and private-school enrolment keeps gaining share.

The bear case

  • The pencil cohort is shrinking. Primary enrolment fell 16.5% in five years and 2.4% in 2025-26 alone; total enrolment is flat.
  • Margins are exposed. About 70% of the input basket follows crude; gross margin fell 4.7 points in a quarter and price points move only a rupee at a time.
  • The core is not growing. Scholastic Stationery rose only 3.2% in FY26; growth now depends on new capacity and new categories.
  • Capital intensity. ₹850–1,000 Cr of capex, negative free cash flow three periods running, net debt ~₹159 Cr; new buildings dilute margins for 18–24 months.
  • Overhang. FILA's 19.01% stake is ~43% of its own market value, which makes more block sales likely; the June block at ₹2,200 is above today's price.
  • Valuation. ~29× FY26 EBITDA and ~53× trailing earnings against 9–15× for listed peers; GST 2.0's effect on the unorganised tail is unproven.

Where do the two sides really disagree? Not on the market's size, its branded share or DOMS's competitive position, which both accept. The disagreement is about two conditional statements. The bull case says that the share DOMS bought in 2026 with lower margins will stay bought, and that input costs will settle enough for prices to catch up by FY28. The bear case says that the share may prove temporary if rivals also price aggressively to fill their own new capacity (Flair's lines, Cello's pen push, Hamilton's entry), and that a structurally shrinking primary cohort makes 40% more pencil capacity harder to fill than in 2022. The next four quarters of gross margin and Scholastic Stationery growth will settle much of it.

In plain terms

The optimist says DOMS has done this before: take a hit when costs jump, win customers from weaker rivals, and come out more profitable. The pessimist says this time is different, because there are fewer young children each year, a big new factory to fill and a partner likely to keep selling shares. Both agree DOMS is the best company in the business; they disagree about how much that is worth today.
