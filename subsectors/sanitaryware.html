00

The one-page view: a bathroom is a brand sold through a discount

A small, concentrated, cash-rich industry whose margins in 2026 are set by brass prices, gas access and how hard dealers can be pushed on discounts.

India spends roughly ₹20,000–25,000 crore a year, at manufacturer prices, on the ceramic fixtures and metal fittings that make up a bathroom. About a third of that is vitreous-china sanitaryware (water closets, basins, urinals, cisterns) worth some ₹7,000–8,000 crore, and about two-thirds is faucets, showers and other bath fittings worth some ₹13,000–14,000 crore. Those figures come from listed companies rather than an audited census, so they are indicative, but they replace the much larger numbers that circulate in market-research summaries and that our own Cera primer repeated. Organised brands hold roughly 60% of the market by value, according to Cera's FY26 annual report; the rest belongs to hundreds of small potteries in Thangadh and Morbi in Gujarat and thousands of brass and plastic tap workshops in Jamnagar, Rajkot and the Delhi region.

The industry looks like a manufacturing business and behaves like a consumer brand. A toilet is heavy, fragile and cheap to make; what earns money is the name on it, the dealer who stocks it, the plumber who recommends it and the after-sales technician who fixes it. That is why Cera Sanitaryware, the only listed pure play in the NSE 750, could buy about 59% of what it sold in FY26 from outside suppliers and still earn a 13% operating margin and a near-20% return on capital. It is also why the most important price in the industry is not the list price on the catalogue but the discount the dealer receives off it. Hindware's FY26 annual report shows ₹1,902 crore of discounts and rebates against ₹4,348 crore of contract value, a 43.7% gap between list and net.

Four forces define the sub-sector in late 2026, and this report is built around them. First, make versus buy is a gas-spread trade. Cera fires its kilns at Kadi in north Gujarat on contracted GAIL gas that cost ₹48.43 per cubic metre in Q1 FY27, against ₹82–90 per standard cubic metre for piped gas at Morbi. When Morbi gas was cheap in FY25–FY26, Cera made fewer toilets at Kadi and bought more from Morbi; after the West Asia gas shock of March 2026 shut much of Thangadh and Morbi, it started bringing the best-selling models back in-house. Second, brass has replaced gas as the swing cost. Faucets are now Cera's larger in-house business, brass is about 60% of faucet cost, and the price Cera paid rose from ₹665 per kg in December 2025 to about ₹900 per kg in July 2026. Gross margin fell from a typical 51% to 46% in Q1 FY27. Third, discounts and channel mix are the real price variable. Cera's FY26 margin decline was mostly higher trade discounts, its project (builder) share climbed from about 30% to 43% of sales, and from Q1 FY27 it reports revenue net of turnover discounts. Fourth, premiumisation and the brand ladder decide who captures the formalisation of the market, and in faucets one private company, Jaquar, holds roughly half the market by its own claim.

In plain terms

The Indian bathroom business is a brand-and-dealer game played over a cheap, heavy product. The winners own a name that plumbers and architects trust and a dealer network that reaches small towns; manufacturing is something they do selectively, when it is cheaper than buying. In 2026 three things went against everyone at once: brass got 35% dearer, gas for the unbranded potteries nearly doubled, and dealers had been trained by two weak years to expect big discounts. The question for FY27 is how much of that the brands can push through to the buyer.

Three timing facts frame every number in this report. Cera's Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) results are due at a board meeting on 4 November 2026, with an earnings call on 5 November; they are not out as of this writing, and management warned in August that Q2 would still carry "some impact" from old-price project orders. Hindware Home Innovation, the closest listed comparison, has not yet announced its Q2 board date, and its NCLT-sanctioned split into two listed companies, one holding bathware and pipes, is waiting for a record date. Kajaria reported on 24 October, with Kerovit bathware up 22% to ₹124 crore, and Somany reports on 4 November. The "What to watch" section at the end lists these dates first.

01

How a bathroom becomes revenue: clay, brass and a dealer's shelf

Two very different factories, one shared route to market, and a value chain in which the kiln earns less than the brand.

The industry sells two families of product that share a showroom but almost nothing else. Sanitaryware is vitreous china: a body of ball clay, china clay (kaolin), feldspar and quartz, mixed into a liquid slip, cast in gypsum or resin moulds, dried, sprayed with glaze and fired once in a tunnel or shuttle kiln at roughly 1,200–1,250°C for 12–20 hours. The fired piece is dense, non-porous and very hard, which is why a toilet lasts decades. The key manufacturing number is yield, the share of cast pieces that come out of the kiln as first quality; Cera disclosed a global yield of 83% in FY26 (82% in FY25), in the band expected for a good organised plant. Complex one-piece, rimless and wall-hung models have lower yields and need better moulds and kiln control, which is where branded players earn their premium over the potteries.

Faucets and bath fittings are metalwork. Brass ingot or rod, an alloy of about 57–63% copper and 35–40% zinc, is hot-forged or die-cast, machined on CNC lines, polished (robotically at Cera), electroplated with nickel and chrome or coated by physical vapour deposition (PVD) for colours, and assembled with a ceramic disc cartridge and aerator before pressure testing. Brass is about 60% of a Cera faucet's cost; gas use is negligible. At the low end, PTMT and ABS plastic taps with a chrome look sell for ₹150–800 and are pushed by pipe makers through plumbers. Around both families sit cisterns, concealed in-wall frames, seat covers, flush mechanisms, showers, wellness products (bathtubs, shower enclosures, steam units) and, for some brands, traded tiles.

Both families reach the buyer through the same channel. A brand ships from a central warehouse (Cera's has been centralised at Kadi since FY25) to distributors and dealers, who sell to retailers and sub-dealers, who sell to the household or its contractor. The person who actually chooses the product is often not the person paying: a plumber decides most mass-market tap purchases, an architect or interior designer decides premium bathrooms, and a developer's procurement team decides what goes into a 500-flat tower. That is why loyalty programmes for plumbers and masons matter (Cera reported 73,000+ registered plumbers, 23,000 of them added in FY26, and 11,000+ masons) and why the dealer's margin, delivered as discounts and schemes, is the largest commercial cost in the business. A second, parallel channel sells directly to builders, hotels, hospitals and government projects at negotiated prices that are fixed for 6–12 months.

Where is value captured? Not in the kiln. Cera's FY26 accounts show that only about 41% of its revenue came from products it made itself, yet it earned a 13.1% operating margin, better than Hindware's bathware arm (10.3% before other income), which makes about 70% of its sanitaryware and half its faucets. Value sits in four places: the brand's ability to hold a retail premium over unbranded goods; dealer reach and credit discipline; breadth of catalogue and design, which carries a cost in inventory (Cera's finished-goods stock was ₹303 crore at March 2026); and a cost advantage on the half that is made in-house, which comes from gas access, yields and brass buying. The unorganised potteries capture little: they sell on price to traders and to branded players' outsourcing desks, and their margins are hostage to fuel prices.

In plain terms

A bathroom company runs two factories that have nothing in common, a ceramics plant that lives on gas and a metal shop that lives on brass, and sells both through the same dealer. It makes the hard products itself and buys the easy ones. Its real assets are the brand, the catalogue and the network, which is why it can earn good returns while owning relatively little machinery.

One consequence of this structure deserves emphasis because it shapes every later section: the same company can be "integrated" and "asset-light" at once, and the balance shifts with relative costs. Cera's management describes a long-run make-or-buy ratio of about 50:50, "slightly skewed more towards outsourcing" in sanitaryware, and says cisterns' plastic parts, seat covers, flush mechanisms and polymer chrome-plated taps "will always be outsourced". Within that frame, the share of WCs and basins made at Kadi moved from 51% of manufacturing revenue in FY25 to 48% in FY26 as Morbi supply became cheap, and is now moving back. A reader who treats capacity utilisation or "in-house share" as a fixed attribute of the company will misread both the margin and the capex plan.

02

A ₹20–25k crore market, not a ₹50k crore one

The headline numbers in circulation are incompatible; two listed-company statements agree, and they say the market is smaller and more concentrated than most investors assume.

Market sizing in Indian bathware is unusually poor. Market-research summaries put Indian "sanitary ware" anywhere from about US$3.3 billion (ceramics only, 2025) to an implausible US$68 billion, and Indian "bath fittings" at US$11.5 billion, a figure that must include pipes, valves and accessories at retail prices. Our own first-pass research built a bottom-up estimate of ₹40,000–55,000 crore including the unorganised sector. Two listed companies give a different and mutually consistent answer. Hindware's bathware chief executive told analysts on 20 May 2026 that the sanitaryware market is about ₹7,000–8,000 crore and the faucet market about ₹13,000–14,000 crore. Somany's FY26 annual report, citing third-party data, gives US$948.5 million for sanitaryware in FY25 (roughly ₹8,000 crore) and US$1.89 billion for faucets in FY26 (roughly ₹16,500 crore). The two statements agree within about 10% on sanitaryware and bracket faucets at ₹13,000–16,500 crore.

Why did the bottom-up overshoot? Mostly because the largest company numbers are not like-for-like. Jaquar's group revenue includes lighting (about ₹700 crore), water heaters, wellness and exports; Kohler and Roca India figures are dated press estimates; and adding an assumed 40–55% unorganised share on top of an inflated organised base compounds the error. The smaller figure also fits the companies' own position statements. Hindware says "a large part of the industry is concentrated in the top five or six" national players, and Cera's annual report says organised players hold "nearly 60%". With a ₹20,000–25,000 crore market and 60% organised, the branded segment is worth roughly ₹12,000–15,000 crore, and Jaquar alone, if most of its India revenue is fittings, is about a third of all bathware value.

In plain terms

The Indian bathroom market is about the size of one large listed consumer company's sales, not a giant hidden sector. On the companies' own numbers it is roughly ₹21,000 crore at factory prices, two-thirds taps and one-third toilets and basins, with brands holding about 60%. That makes the main players' shares larger than the research reports imply: Cera is roughly 12–14% of sanitaryware and about 6% of faucets.

Implied shares follow from the arithmetic and are indicative. Cera's FY26 sanitaryware revenue of roughly ₹984 crore (48% of ₹2,050 crore) is about 12–14% of a ₹7,000–8,000 crore ceramics market and perhaps 20–23% of the organised part; its roughly ₹840 crore of faucets is about 6% of a ₹13,000–14,000 crore faucet market. Hindware's ₹1,520 crore of bathware is about 7% of the total. Jaquar's claim to "nearly 60%" of bath fittings is arithmetically possible only if most of its roughly ₹7,500 crore of Indian revenue is fittings; our cross-check of ₹7,000-plus crore of fittings-led revenue against a ₹13,000–14,000 crore faucet market gives about 50–55%, so "roughly half" is the defensible phrasing.

Growth has been steady rather than spectacular. Cera's revenue rose from ₹822 crore in FY15 to ₹2,050 crore in FY26, but the path was lumpy: a stall in FY20–FY21 (₹1,344 crore in FY19 to ₹1,202 crore in FY21) during the post-demonetisation property slump and the pandemic, a post-COVID boom to ₹1,803 crore in FY23, then two years of near-flat sales (+3.8% and +2.4%) as retail demand slowed from Q3 FY24. 03

Who buys the toilet: completions, renovation and a rural base

Fittings go in at the end of construction, so demand follows housing completions with a lag, sits on a renovation floor, and was broadened at the bottom by a government sanitation drive that mostly bought unbranded product.

Bathware demand comes from four places, and none of the shares below is disclosed by anyone; they are our indicative estimates built from company commentary. New residential construction is the largest, perhaps 40–50% of value. A WC and its taps are installed at the finishing stage, typically 24–36 months after a project is launched, so this demand tracks completions rather than sales or launches. Replacement and renovation is perhaps 35–45%: Indian bathrooms are typically redone every 10–15 years, and the urban housing stock built after 2000 is in its first big renovation cycle. This demand is retail-led and premium-skewed, because a renovator chooses the better product. Rural, tier-2/3 and government-linked demand is perhaps 10–20%, and commercial and institutional buildings (hotels, hospitals, offices, airports, malls, metro stations) perhaps 10–15%.

Two caveats temper the housing story. The West Asia crisis that began on 28 February 2026 put some 2026 deliveries at risk through labour shortages and higher cement, steel and energy costs, so "scheduled" completions may slip into 2027. And the developer channel is the least profitable one for a bathware brand. Big developers consolidated after RERA and prefer branded specifications, which is why Cera's project share rose from about 30% "a couple of years back" to 39% in FY26 and 43% in Q1 FY27, but project orders are priced for 6–12 months, carry lower margins, and come with longer receivables. Cera's receivable days on CareEdge's basis rose from 35 in FY23 to 48 in FY26 as project share climbed.

In plain terms

Toilets and taps are bought when a building is finished, not when it is sold, so the record number of flats being handed over in 2026 is good news for bathware even though flat sales are soft. But builders are tough customers: they lock in prices for a year, pay slowly and buy in bulk. A brand that grows by selling to builders grows its revenue faster than its profit.

The renovation floor is what makes sanitaryware a late-cycle housing derivative rather than a pure cyclical. Cera's revenue fell only twice in twelve years. But the renovation channel has its own cycle, driven by household confidence and discretionary spending, and it was the part that broke in FY24–FY25. Cera described demand as "sluggish" from Q3 FY24; its retail share fell from about 65% of sales in FY25 to about 61% in FY26 as dealers destocked and the company fought for share with discounts. Retail recovered from Q3 FY26, and by Q1 FY27 management called growth "predominantly volume-driven" and broad-based. Whether that recovery holds through the price increases of March–May 2026 is the central demand question for the Q2 and Q3 FY27 results.

The bottom of the market was reshaped by government programmes. The Swachh Bharat Mission, launched on 2 October 2014, built more than 10 crore household toilets and rural India was declared open-defecation-free on 2 October 2019; its second phase ran to FY26. PMAY-Urban 2.0 (one crore urban homes approved in September 2024) and PMAY-Gramin (two crore more rural homes) continue the push, and Cera's annual report cites a PMAY allocation of ₹78,126 crore in FY26. These programmes overwhelmingly used squatting pans and basic WCs from unorganised potteries, so they expanded the category more than they helped the brands. Their lasting effect is behavioural: they normalised the WC in households that will, a decade later, upgrade to a branded one. Cera's Polipluz value brand and Hindware's value lines are explicitly aimed at that customer.

Two structural demand trends run across all four segments. The single largest cost line was purchases of stock-in-trade, ₹759 crore or 37% of revenue, up 9.3% on FY25: toilets and basins bought from Morbi suppliers, tiles, wellness products, seat covers, mechanisms and plastic fittings. The archetypes explain why the 2026 shock sorted the industry so cleanly. For an unbranded Thangadh pottery, fuel is 15–25% of cost in a normal year; when propane went from about ₹41 to about ₹70 per kg in March 2026, a basic WC that sold to a trader for about ₹1,400 went from a small profit to roughly break-even, and around 90% of Thangadh's roughly 250 units stopped firing. For a branded asset-light house, energy is ₹3–4 of every ₹100, so a doubling of gas is a margin point or so; the real damage came through the bought-in line, through supply gaps from Morbi, and through brass in faucets. For an integrated maker such as Hindware, energy is a larger ₹5–7 because it fires more of its own product on city-gas or LNG, but it was also less exposed to Morbi supply failures, which is why Hindware's bathware margin rose to about 12% in Q1 FY27 while Cera's fell to 10.1%.

In plain terms

A branded bathroom company spends more on toilets it buys from other factories than on running its own, and more on getting products to and through dealers than on gas. So a jump in the gas price hurts it mainly by making the bought-in toilets dearer and harder to get, not by raising its own fuel bill. For the small unbranded potteries, by contrast, fuel is a fifth of the cost, and a doubling shuts them down.

Employee cost is the other line that moves in steps rather than gradually. Cera's workers' union settles wages every four years at increases of 15–20%; the settlement concluded in Q1 FY27 added ₹6.3 crore of back-dated cost for September 2025 to March 2026 and about ₹1 crore a month going forward, an FY27 hit of ₹18.3 crore. Management guides FY27 employee cost to about ₹300 crore against ₹259 crore in FY26. Separately, the new Labour Codes, effective 21 November 2025, forced a one-time increase in gratuity and leave liabilities: Cera booked about ₹18 crore in Q3 FY26, wrote back ₹10 crore in Q4, and shows a net exceptional charge of ₹7.81 crore for FY26. Readers of screener.in should know that it folds exceptional items into "other income", which is why Cera's December 2025 quarter shows negative other income of ₹7 crore.

Freight is the cost that ties the industry to geography. Sanitaryware is heavy, bulky and breakable, and at 6% of revenue freight is a reason regional plants matter and why imports from China are concentrated in high-value, low-weight products. A Kadi-made WC sold in Kerala carries a freight cost that a Tamil Nadu-made one from a rival does not; this is part of why Parryware has historically been strong in the south and Hindware in the north. Cera's revenue is evenly spread (north about 34%, south about 33%, west about 22%, central and east about 10%), served from a single manufacturing base and a centralised warehouse at Kadi, which makes its freight bill structurally higher than a multi-plant rival's and is one argument for the greenfield plant discussed in section 17.

The last line, the one that does not appear separately anywhere, is the dealer's discount. Turnover discounts (volume-linked schemes) sat inside Cera's expenses until FY26 and are netted from revenue from Q1 FY27, which reduced reported revenue by about 2.5–3% with no change in profit. Larger trade discounts are already netted from revenue and are invisible in the expense lines. Hindware's annual report shows the full gap: list or contract value of ₹4,348 crore, discounts and rebates of ₹1,902 crore, recognised revenue of ₹2,510 crore. Section 07 explains why that 43.7% gap is the most important number in Indian bathware pricing.

Kadi vs Morbi: the gas-spread make-or-buy machine Deep dive

The most gas-advantaged maker in India buys 60% of its sanitaryware from the most gas-exposed cluster, and moves that ratio when the spread between the two changes.

Cera's Kadi plant in Mehsana district, north Gujarat, was set up around 1980 as India's first natural-gas-fired sanitaryware plant, on gas from nearby isolated fields. That accident of location is still the company's main manufacturing advantage. In Q1 FY27 Cera bought about 69% of its gas from GAIL at long-term contracted, "relatively subsidised" rates and about 31% from Sabarmati Gas at market prices; CareEdge notes that it has historically met 75–80% of its in-house gas need from such isolated-field sources. Its weighted gas cost was ₹48.43 per cubic metre in Q1 FY27, up from ₹33.17 a year earlier and ₹35.55 in Q4 FY26. The ceramic cluster at Morbi, in Saurashtra, buys piped gas from Gujarat Energy Ltd (GEL, formed in May 2026 from the merger of Gujarat Gas, GSPC and GSPL) on a formula linked to imported fuel; from 2 September 2026 GEL charged ₹90 per SCM for tile makers and ₹82 for sanitaryware and long-term users, excluding 6% VAT. The Morbi prices come from press reports of GEL's notice and are indicative. Thangadh, the older sanitaryware cluster in Surendranagar district, runs largely on propane.

Here is the paradox that this deep dive is about. A company with gas at half its neighbours' price might be expected to make everything itself. Cera does the opposite: on its own disclosure, 60% of its sanitaryware and 46% of its faucets were outsourced in Q4 FY26, sanitaryware outsourcing is "mostly from Morbi, the multiple suppliers", and about 59% of FY26 revenue came from outsourced manufacturing, with tiles and wellness products entirely bought in. Our earlier Cera primer described its sanitaryware as "mostly in-house"; that is wrong. The FY26 annual report's new manufacturing disclosure shows only ₹398 crore of in-house sanitaryware revenue against total sanitaryware sales of roughly ₹984 crore, about 40%.

The reason is that the gas spread was not always wide, and gas is only part of the cost of a toilet. Before the West Asia war, Morbi's piped gas cost about ₹48 per SCM, and Morbi's potteries had a lower cost for labour, moulds and overheads, specialised in simple high-volume models, and competed fiercely for brand contracts. For a basic two-piece WC or pan, buying from Morbi was cheaper than making it at Kadi, where the kilns and casting lines were better used on complex one-piece and wall-hung models. Cera ran its make-or-buy as an arbitrage, and the FY26 numbers show it leaning further into buying: in-house sanitaryware output fell 11% to 18.73 lakh pieces (from 21.0 lakh) and utilisation fell from 86% to 81%, while sanitaryware revenue grew 5.4%. Cera's response was to reverse the arbitrage. What could break the machine? Three things. First, the GAIL contract: the cheap gas comes from nearby isolated fields at contracted rates, and any renewal at market-linked prices would erase most of the advantage; Cera has not disclosed the contract's tenor, which is a gap investors should press on. Second, the cost of idle capacity: one kiln shut for about two months cost ₹3.7 crore of under-absorption (0.75 percentage points of Q1 FY27 margin), so the flexibility is not free. Third, Morbi itself: if gas normalises, the potteries' lost export markets (US sanitaryware exports fell 23% in FY26) and spare capacity will push them back into the Indian mass market at low prices, which helps Cera's buying desk but hurts its value brand, Polipluz.

In plain terms

Cera has cheap gas but uses it only for about two-fifths of the toilets it sells; the rest it buys from Morbi, because in normal times Morbi is cheaper for simple models. When Morbi's gas price nearly doubled in 2026, that calculation flipped and Cera started making more at home. Its kiln utilisation, and so part of its margin, now depends on the gap between its gas price and Morbi's, not only on how many toilets India buys.

For investors, the practical implication is that Cera's sanitaryware utilisation figure should be read as a decision variable, not a demand signal. A fall from 86% to 81% in FY26 did not mean demand was weak (sanitaryware revenue rose); a rise back to about 80% in June 2026 does not by itself mean demand is strong. The right question at each results call is what share of sanitaryware was bought in, at what Morbi price, and whether the greenfield plant (₹150 crore for phase 1, land already bought for ₹27 crore, an 18-month build) is being revived because demand needs it or because the spread makes it pay. On the current timetable, a decision at the end of FY27 means first output around H2 FY29 at the earliest.

Brass at ₹900: faucets, the margin squeeze and the Jaquar question Deep dive

Faucets are the bigger and faster-growing profit pool, and now Cera's larger in-house business; brass is 60% of their cost and rose 35% in seven months.

Faucets are where Indian bathware's money is moving. They are about two-thirds of the market by value, they grow faster than ceramics, they need less capital per rupee of revenue, they use no gas, and they carry a higher value per bathroom (a premium bathroom may have one WC and six to twelve fitting pieces). At Cera, faucet revenue grew 13.7% in FY26 against 5.4% for sanitaryware, and 25% in Q1 FY27 against 14%. More striking, FY26 was the first year in which Cera's in-house faucet revenue (₹438 crore) exceeded its in-house sanitaryware revenue (₹398 crore): the plant made 46.56 lakh faucet pieces at 97% utilisation, launched 271 new faucet products against 35 in sanitaryware, and is the only line getting capacity investment. The "sanitaryware" in the company's name increasingly describes its history more than its manufacturing.

The risk that comes with faucets is brass, and 2026 showed how quickly it bites. Brass is roughly 57–63% copper and 35–40% zinc; its price follows the London Metal Exchange copper price, the rupee and the domestic scrap market. Copper set successive records through 2026 (about $14,533 a tonne in January, an all-time high of $14,850 on 17 August, about $14,675 in late October), US Section 232 tariffs of 50% on copper and copper-intensive goods diverted scrap and semi-finished flows, and the rupee weakened to about ₹95 to the dollar. Cera's procurement price, disclosed month by month on its Q1 FY27 call, went from ₹665 per kg in December 2025 to about ₹900 in July 2026. Hindware reported brass up about 40% year on year in Q1 FY27. The copper sub-sector report covers the metal itself.

Discounts, projects and the real price of a toilet Deep dive

List prices make headlines; the discount off list, the channel the sale goes through and when the price was fixed decide what a company actually earns.

The most useful single number about Indian bathware pricing is buried in a note to Hindware Home Innovation's FY26 annual report. Against a contract (broadly, list) price of goods sold of ₹4,348 crore, the company deducted discounts and rebates of ₹1,902 crore, 43.7% of contract value, before arriving at recognised revenue; in FY25 the ratio was 45.0% (₹2,030 crore on ₹4,516 crore). The figure covers Hindware's pipes and appliances as well as bathware, but the order of magnitude is what matters: in this industry, roughly 40–45% separates the price printed in the catalogue from what the manufacturer books. A dealer who stocks a ₹10,000 list-price mixer pays perhaps ₹5,600 for it, before volume schemes, cash discounts and year-end rebates.

Two consequences follow. First, price increases and discount control are levers of similar size. With net realisation at about 56% of list, a two-point change in the discount rate moves net realisation by about 3.5% (2 ÷ 56), as much as a meaningful list-price increase, and it does so silently, without a press release or a dealer circular. Second, list-price announcements are poor evidence of pricing power. A brand that raises list prices 12% while quietly widening schemes by three points has raised its real price by about 6%; one that holds list prices and tightens discounts by two points has raised its real price by 3.5%. Investors who read only the price-increase circulars, as most market commentary does, miss the variable that actually moved Cera's margin in FY26.

Cera's management was explicit about that. FY26's margin decline was attributed mainly to "higher trade discounts", which were "offered to counter the subdued demand" of FY24–FY25 and "kept on increasing"; in Q4 FY26 volume grew 12% and mix added 3–4 points, but price was minus 3 points because of discounts. Control started in Q1 FY27 and, in management's words, is "a gradual journey": discounts are sticky because dealers who have received them treat them as part of their margin, and a brand that withdraws them unilaterally risks losing shelf space to a rival. The 1–2 point gap between brass inflation and the faucet price increase is meant to be closed by "discount controls" rather than another list increase. That makes discount discipline the single most important qualitative point to listen for on the Q2 FY27 call.

In plain terms

The price on the box is not the price the factory gets. Dealers typically receive 40–45% off list in various forms, so a brand can raise its real price either by raising list prices or by cutting discounts, and the second is just as powerful and much less visible. Cera's profit fell in FY26 mostly because it gave dealers bigger discounts to keep sales moving during a slump; getting those back is slow, because dealers see them as their own money.

The accounting changed in Q1 FY27, which complicates every comparison. From that quarter Cera nets turnover discounts (volume-linked dealer incentive schemes) from revenue instead of booking them as an expense. On management's numbers this cut reported revenue by 2.5% in Q1 FY27 and by 3% in the restated Q1 FY26 comparative, which fell from about ₹420 crore to ₹407 crore; Q4 FY26 was restated from ₹644 crore to ₹624 crore. Profit is unchanged, so the margin rises mechanically: in management's example, 14% EBITDA on ₹100 of revenue becomes 14 ÷ 97, or about 14.4%. FY27's guidance of 13.5–14% on the new basis is therefore equivalent to roughly 13.9–14.4% on the old one. Screener.in's quarterly row mixes the two bases (Jun-25 and Mar-26 restated, Sep-25 and Dec-25 original), so its four FY26 quarters sum to ₹2,018 crore, not the ₹2,050 crore annual figure. On the new basis FY26 revenue would be roughly ₹1,990–2,000 crore (indicative; the company has not restated the full year).

Put the three variables together and the "real price of a toilet" has four layers: the list price (set by the brand, raised 10–16% in 2026), the discount off list (40–45%, sticky and moving by a few points a year), the channel mix (retail versus project, with project lagging and earning less), and the timing (dealers buying ahead of increases, projects locked for months). A brand can win on one layer and lose on another in the same quarter. Q4 FY26 at Cera is the textbook case: volume +12%, mix +3–4 points, price −3 points, revenue +11%, margin 15.2% helped by the restated base. Q1 FY27 is the mirror image: price +2–4 points, mix +2–3, but margin down because of timing and channel. The quarterly checklist in section 13 asks for all four layers.

One more piece of evidence supports the view that discounts, not list prices, are the competitive weapon: Hindware's discount rate fell from 45.0% to 43.7% in FY26 while its bathware revenue grew 10%, faster than Cera's sanitaryware, and its bathware margin before other income rose from 8.8% to 10.3%. Hindware, coming out of a weak period, was tightening; Cera, defending volume, was loosening. If both now tighten, FY27's price increases will reach revenue more fully than the FY26 pattern suggests. If one loosens to buy share, the other will have to follow. The paints report describes a similar dealer-scheme war in a sector with a much larger listed universe; the mechanism is the same.

The field: a private giant, five foreign groups, a listed break-up and 250 potteries

Five or six national brands share the organised market; only one is a listed pure play, the closest listed rival is splitting itself in two, and the leader in faucets publishes no profit.

The Indian bathware field divides into four groups. The private Indian leader is Jaquar, founded in Delhi around 1960 and owned by the Mehra family: ₹7,548 crore of group revenue in FY25 (a ₹8,703 crore target for FY26; Business Today also cites "over ₹8,500 crore"), 52.9 million bath fittings, 4.8 million sanitaryware pieces and 28.4 million lighting units a year, eight plants (seven in India, one in South Korea) plus three under construction, exports of about 5% of revenue, and no stated IPO plans. Its FY26 revenue is a company target, not a reported figure, and profit is not disclosed. Foreign groups own most of the rest of the premium and mid-premium market: Roca (Spain) owns Parryware, Roca and Laufen in India; Kohler (US, private) has its main Indian base at Jhagadia in Gujarat and says India is one of its top three markets; LIXIL (Japan) owns Grohe and American Standard; Masco owns Hansgrohe; Duravit, TOTO, Villeroy & Boch and Geberit serve the luxury and systems niches.

The listed Indian players are Cera, Hindware, and two tile makers with bathware lines, Kajaria (Kerovit) and Somany. Astral, the pipe maker, entered faucets and sanitaryware in 2018–20 and is named by CareEdge as a competitor to Cera in both, but does not report bathware separately. The unorganised sector is the roughly 250 sanitaryware units of Thangadh, an unknown number (perhaps 100–150) of sanitaryware units in Morbi, and thousands of brass and PTMT workshops in Jamnagar, Rajkot, Delhi-NCR, Jagadhri and Ludhiana, plus traders of Chinese imports. Hindware's bathware head describes "five or six major players" at national level, with "a large part of the industry concentrated in the top five or six".

From asset-light brand house to single-kiln pottery, the archetypes differ less in what they sell than in what they make, what they buy, and where their cost risk sits.

In plain terms

Owning fewer factories gives a bathroom brand higher returns in normal times, because it ties up less money; owning more factories protects it when suppliers fail, as they did in 2026. The archetypes also explain how each company is likely to respond to the next two years. Cera, model A, is internalising at the margin, adding faucet capacity cheaply, and keeping its greenfield option open, consistent with a company that wants to stay asset-light but not supply-hostage. Four brands, one dealer: premiumisation and the ladder

Cera is building a luxury brand and a value brand at the same time, through the same dealer network, on a publicity budget that fell in FY26 and is set to rise 72% in FY27.

Formalisation is the secular engine of this industry, and the brand ladder is how a company captures it. The idea is simple: one network, several price points, so that the same dealer can sell a ₹1,200 pan to a rural contractor and a ₹60,000 rimless WC to an architect, and the brand captures the customer at every level as incomes rise. Jaquar runs Artize (luxury), Jaquar (premium) and Essco (value); Hindware runs Queo (super-premium, targeted at ₹180–200 crore of revenue in five years), the Hindware Italian Collection and Hindware, plus value lines. Cera's ladder has four tiers, not the three our primer described: Senator (luxury), CERA Luxe (premium), CERA (core mass-premium) and Polipluz (value, aimed at rural and semi-urban buyers in a segment "currently dominated by unorganised players offering substandard products").

Cera is trying to be the brand a buyer meets at every price, from a cheap village toilet to a luxury bathroom. Two risks sit inside the ladder strategy. The first is people: Ramesh Baliga, the chief business officer who came from Jaquar and ran both Senator and Polipluz, resigned "for personal reasons" and left on 30 September 2026, with an existing national head taking over. Two loss-making brands losing their architect within a year of launch is a real execution risk, whatever the company says about "dedicated operating teams". Imports, exports and the rulebook: a net exporter with an open door

India sells twice as much ceramic sanitaryware abroad as it buys, its exporters were hit by US and Gulf duties, and at home there is still no quality-control order and no change in GST.

Trade in ceramic sanitaryware (customs heading HS 6910) is small relative to the domestic market but larger than earlier estimates suggested, and it runs in both directions. The Ministry of Commerce's TradeStat database, queried for this report, shows imports of ₹638 crore in FY24, ₹615 crore in FY25 and ₹715 crore in FY26 (+16.3%), of which China supplied ₹528 crore, or 74%, in FY26. Exports were ₹1,336 crore, ₹1,389 crore and ₹1,571 crore in the same years, so India ran a sanitaryware trade surplus of about ₹855 crore in FY26. A widely quoted export figure of ₹3,375 crore for 2023 probably includes tiles or other ceramics and should not be used.

The quality-control order is the policy most often misdescribed. A draft "Ceramic Tiles, Sanitary Wares and Laboratory Wares (Quality Control) Order, 2023", issued by DPIIT under the BIS Act, would make the ISI mark compulsory for washdown WCs, squatting pans, wash basins, urinals, one-piece and wall-hung WCs, bidets and vitreous-china cisterns (the IS 2556 series and IS 774:2021). Its notification number and commencement date are blank, consultancy listings show only a "draft order" dated 15 February 2023 with no implementation date, and the BIS list of products under compulsory certification, checked for this report, does not include IS 2556 or IS 774. As of November 2026, sanitaryware is not under a QCO, and there is no QCO for faucets at all. If notified, a QCO would act as a non-tariff barrier against sub-standard Chinese imports and a compliance cost for small potteries, each of which would need a BIS licence and in-house testing. It would favour the brands: Cera already holds BIS licences under IS 2556 for Parts 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 15.

In plain terms

India exports about twice as many ceramic toilets and basins as it imports, mainly from small Gujarat potteries, and those exporters lost ground in the US and Saudi Arabia because of new duties. Imports, three-quarters from China, are about a tenth of the home market and rising, and nothing currently stops low-quality ones: the quality rule that would require an ISI mark is still only a draft. Tax rates have not changed. For the big brands, the most important trade effect is indirect: potteries that cannot export sell harder at home.

Energy and environmental rules have done more to shape the industry than tax. The National Green Tribunal's 2019 order moved Morbi off coal gasifiers and onto piped gas and propane, which made the cluster cleaner and permanently more exposed to imported-fuel prices; the March 2026 emergency allocation and GEL's force majeure showed what that exposure means. Cera's own environmental position is stronger than most: 10.3 MW of captive wind and solar, about 55–60% of its energy needs, zero-liquid-discharge plants and effluent treatment at both facilities. Government demand programmes cited in Cera's annual report (Swachh Bharat ₹7,192 crore, AMRUT 2.0 with 583 projects worth ₹66,117 crore, PMAY ₹78,126 crore and 122 lakh houses sanctioned) support volume at the value end, where the organised players compete with the potteries.

Seven decades, five shocks: how the industry got here

A late-cycle housing business with a renovation floor, whose margins have been set by discounts and brass rather than by gas, and whose corporate map was redrawn twice in a decade.

Organised Indian sanitaryware began in the 1950s and 1960s with two companies. Parryware started under EID Parry of the Murugappa group, and Hindustan Sanitaryware & Industries (Hindware) was set up in 1960 by the Somany family with Twyfords of the UK; Jaquar started as a small Delhi bath-fittings business around the same time. Cera's founder, Vikram Somany, from the same ceramic family, set up the Kadi plant around 1980 on natural gas (the company says it "founded the business in 1980"; our primer cites a 1978 memorandum of understanding). The listed company's corporate shell is younger: incorporated in 1998 as Madhusudan Oils & Fats, acquired and renamed Cera Sanitaryware in a 2001 scheme. For three decades the market was an Indian oligopoly of a few brands above a large unorganised base.

The 2000s brought the foreign brands and the first premiumisation wave. The post-COVID boom of FY21–FY23 was the industry's best period: a housing upcycle, a renovation wave and pricing discipline took Cera's revenue from ₹1,202 crore to ₹1,803 crore and its profit from ₹101 crore to ₹210 crore, with a 16% margin and 26% return on capital. Index inclusion and retail enthusiasm took its shareholder count from about 35,000 to a peak of 1.79 lakh in September 2024. Then retail slowed from Q3 FY24. Projects kept growing, but dealers destocked and competition moved to discounts. Cera's revenue grew only 3.8% and 2.4% in FY24 and FY25, and its margin slid. Hindware fared worse: its consolidated revenue fell from ₹2,872 crore in FY23 to ₹2,510 crore in FY26 and it turned loss-making on pipes and appliances.

FY26 and FY27 brought three shocks in quick succession. Brass rose by about a third between December 2025 and July 2026. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 shut much of Morbi and Thangadh for weeks and forced Cera to idle a kiln. And the cost of labour stepped up through the four-yearly wage settlement and the new Labour Codes. Against that, the industry raised prices by the most in years, and demand, helped by record housing completions, held up: Cera's revenue grew 19.5% in Q1 FY27. At the same time the corporate map was redrawn again: Hindware's three-way scheme was sanctioned in October 2026, Kajaria bought out its private-equity partner in Kerovit, Jaquar announced ₹800–1,000 crore of capex and a ₹12,000 crore revenue goal, and Kohler committed ₹800 crore to India.

In plain terms

This industry's sales fall rarely and only a little, because people renovate bathrooms even when they are not buying new homes. Its margins move more, and over the past decade they have been pushed down by weak demand that led to discounting, and by metal prices, rather than by the cost of gas. The current dip, to about 10% in Q1 FY27, is the deepest in the record, but much of it is the brass spike and one-off costs.

The cycle lesson for investors is that sanitaryware is a late-cycle housing derivative with a renovation floor. Revenue follows completions and household confidence with a lag; margins follow discounts and brass; and the balance sheet, at least at Cera, never comes under pressure because the business generates cash and carries almost no debt. The defining events are rarely about gas: in twelve years of Cera's history, the margin troughs came from the property slump (FY19–FY21), the retail slowdown and discounting (FY24–FY26) and brass (FY27). The 2026 gas shock mattered mostly because it hit the potteries and the brands' bought-in supply, which is why it shows up in the make-or-buy story rather than in Cera's own fuel bill.

Reading a bathware company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Sixteen numbers, how each is defined, what drives it, what good looks like, how the listed companies compare and where the definitions trip people up.

Analysing a bathware company means reconciling three kinds of number: what the factory does (pieces, utilisation, yield, gas and brass per unit), what the channel does (list prices, discounts, project versus retail mix, dealer counts) and what the accounts show (revenue, gross and operating margins, working capital, cash and returns). Companies disclose these unevenly. Cera is the most transparent listed player on manufacturing and channel data; Hindware discloses bathware segment margins and capacity; Kajaria and Somany give a few bathware lines; Jaquar and the MNCs disclose almost nothing. Where a peer figure is not disclosed, this section says "n/a" rather than estimating it. All Cera figures are standalone; Q1 FY27 means April–June 2026, and Q2 FY27 results are not yet out.

Operating profit is revenue times gross margin, less the fixed and semi-fixed costs of employees, advertising, freight and overheads. In plain terms

A bathware company's sales are the number of pieces it sells times what the dealer actually pays, after a large discount. Its profit is what is left after the cost of the product (brass, clay, gas or the price paid to a supplier) and a large, mostly fixed bill for staff, advertising and delivery. Because that bill is fixed in the short run, a five-point swing in the product margin moves the operating margin by about the same five points.

8. Brass cost per kilogram

What it is: the price a faucet maker pays for brass rod or ingot, the alloy that makes up most of a tap's metal. Formula: procurement cost ÷ kilograms bought; Cera discloses it monthly on its calls. Worked example: brass is about 60% of a Cera faucet's cost; on roughly ₹840 crore of faucet revenue at about 50% cost of goods, the brass bill is about ₹250 crore a year, so each ₹10 per kg (about 1.3% at ₹750) moves it by roughly ₹3–4 crore, and each 10% move by about ₹25 crore, or 1.2 points of Cera's margin, before any price response (indicative). Trap: brass is a faucet cost, not a company cost, so it should be scaled by faucet revenue (about 40% of Cera's), and the impact on gross margin percentage persists even when rupee gross profit is protected (section 06).

In plain terms

For every 10% rise in the price of brass, Cera's yearly profit falls by about ₹25 crore unless it raises tap prices. It usually does raise them, but a quarter or two later, and in the meantime the margin dips. Watching the brass price, and how fast management responds, tells you more about the next two quarters than watching the gas price.

9. A&P and selling costs as a share of revenue

What it is: spending on advertising and publicity (A&P), and more broadly on all selling costs including dealer promotions and freight. Formula: publicity ÷ revenue; (publicity + sales promotion + freight) ÷ revenue. Worked example: Cera FY26 publicity ₹49.4 crore = 2.4% of revenue; adding sales promotion ₹122.0 crore and freight ₹122.3 crore gives ₹293.7 crore, 14.3% of revenue, larger than operating profit. The FY27 publicity budget of ₹85 crore would be about 3.5% of guided revenue. What drives it: brand launches (Senator, Polipluz), competitive intensity, celebrity campaigns and sponsorships; promotion rises when demand is weak. Benchmark: publicity of 3–5% of revenue is brand-building; 2–3% maintenance; below 2% under-investing. Peers: n/a in comparable form. Trap: "A&P" in management commentary can mean publicity only or publicity plus promotion; our primer's description of "heavier brand-building spend" in FY26 was wrong, because publicity fell 9% that year; and from Q1 FY27 the turnover-discount part of promotion leaves the expense line and reduces revenue instead.

10. Trade discounts and the list-to-net gap

What it is: the gap between list (contract) price and recognised revenue, made up of trade discounts, schemes and rebates. Formula: discounts and rebates ÷ gross contract value. Worked example: Hindware FY26: ₹1,902 crore ÷ ₹4,348 crore = 43.7% (FY25 45.0%). With net at about 56% of list, a two-point change in the discount rate moves net realisation by about 3.5%. Cera's turnover-discount slice is about 2.5–3% of revenue (the size of its Q1 FY27 restatement). What drives it: demand (discounts widen in slumps and are sticky in recoveries), competitive behaviour, channel mix and the timing of list-price increases. Benchmark: a falling discount rate with stable volume is good; rising discounts with flat volume is weak. Peers: only Hindware discloses the full gap; Cera discloses the turnover-discount slice and commentary. Trap: discounts are invisible in most accounts because they are netted from revenue, so a "price" line in a volume-price-mix bridge can be negative (Cera Q4 FY26: −3 points) even in a year of list-price increases.

11. Project versus retail mix (and tier mix)

What it is: the share of sales going directly or through project dealers to builders and institutions, versus through the retail dealer chain; and the split by town tier. Formula: project sales ÷ total sales. Worked example: Cera's project share of 43% in Q1 FY27 on ₹486 crore is about ₹209 crore; if project orders are priced 6–12 months ahead, a 12% list-price rise reaches that slice a quarter or two late, and management put the Q1 cost of foreclosing old orders at ₹4 crore (0.8 points). What drives it: housing completions, developer consolidation, retail weakness and the company's appetite for volume. Benchmark: 25–35% good (diversified without margin drag), 35–45% average, above 45% raises margin and receivable risk. Peers: Hindware institutional about 25%; others n/a. Tier mix: Cera tier-1 38%, tier-2 24%, tier-3 38% (Q1 FY27); Hindware tier-2/3 about 70%. Trap: project share in a single quarter can be inflated by one-offs (Q1 FY27's foreclosures) and by retail dealers pre-buying before increases; read it over four quarters.

In plain terms

Selling to builders is good for volume and bad for margin: builders negotiate hard, lock in prices for up to a year and pay later. A company whose builder share rises from 30% to 43% of sales should be expected to earn a lower margin on the same revenue, at least until prices reset. Management says the mix will settle at about 60% shops and 40% builders.

12. Dealer and retail network, and Style Centres

What it is: the number of distributors, dealers, retailers and branded outlets, and the loyalty-programme reach among plumbers and masons. Formula: counts, and revenue per dealer (revenue ÷ dealers). Worked example: Cera's ₹2,050 crore over 7,000+ distributors and dealers is roughly ₹29 lakh per stockist a year, or about ₹7 lakh per retailer across 29,000+ retailers (indicative). What drives it: geographic expansion (Cera's focus markets include the North East, West Bengal, Bihar, Odisha and Madhya Pradesh), new brands (Polipluz needs its own 200 distributors and 2,000 dealers), and format investment (1,613 Style Centres plus 1,400 planned). Benchmark: growth in branded outlets and loyalty members with stable revenue per dealer is good; adding dealers while revenue per dealer falls suggests a network being stretched. Peers: Hindware 500+ distributors, 35,000+ retailers, 500+ brand stores (CareEdge cites about 650); Somany 533 showrooms and 3,061 active dealers. Trap: "dealers", "distributors", "retailers", "touch-points" and "stores" are different tiers and companies mix them; our primer's "20,000 touch-points" is not a company number, and Senator store counts vary across Cera's own documents.

In plain terms

The returns on that money, mostly gains on bond funds, supply about a sixth of its pre-tax profit. 15. Return on capital: company vs screener vs ex-cash

Definition traps, collected

The traps in this industry are numerous enough to be worth listing in one place. Each has caught at least one published source, including our own primer.

  • Revenue basis. From Q1 FY27 Cera nets turnover discounts from revenue (−2.5% to −3%); FY26's ₹2,050 crore is on the old basis, about ₹1,990–2,000 crore on the new one (indicative), and the restatement lifts margins by about 0.4 points mechanically.
  • Standalone vs consolidated. Cera sold its two 51% LLPs on 29 September 2025 and now reports standalone only; screener's consolidated page stops at FY25 with stale ratios. Use the standalone page.
  • Screener's mixed quarters. Cera's FY26 quarters on screener sum to ₹2,018 crore, not ₹2,050 crore, because two were restated and two were not.
  • Other income includes exceptionals. Screener folds the Labour Code provision into other income, which is why the December 2025 quarter shows −₹7 crore.
  • Three EBITDA bases. Ex-OI 13.1%, incl.-OI 15.3% (on total income), CareEdge core 12.55% for FY26; FY25 was flattered by ₹34 crore of write-backs.
  • Hindware's bathware margin. 10.9% includes other income; 10.3% excludes it and is the figure to compare with Cera's 13.1%.
  • "Bathware" vs "sanitaryware". Hindware, Kerovit and Somany report bathware (ceramics plus faucets); Cera's "sanitaryware" is ceramics only. Jaquar's group revenue includes lighting and exports.
  • Pieces vs tonnes. Cera's sanitaryware capacity is 33,750 tonnes, about 23 lakh pieces implied; its faucet capacity is 48 lakh pieces nameplate, 52 lakh run-rate. Never add faucet and WC pieces.
  • Utilisation is in-house only. With about 60% of sanitaryware bought in, low utilisation can reflect cheap outsourcing, not weak demand.
  • Net cash vs net worth. ₹806 crore net cash, ₹1,472 crore net worth; the primer confused them.
  • ROCE. Company 17.8%, screener 19.2%, ex-cash about 30%: all valid, on different bases.
  • Network counts. 7,000+ distributors and dealers and 29,000+ retailers are different tiers; Senator outlet counts differ across Cera's own documents.
  • Jaquar's FY26 revenue. ₹8,703 crore is a target; ₹7,548 crore (FY25) is the reported figure.
  • Market size. ₹20,000–25,000 crore on company statements; the ₹40,000–55,000 crore and US$68 billion figures in circulation are not usable.

How to read a bathware company's quarterly results in ten minutes

The checklist below is ordered by how much each number tells you per minute spent. It is written for Cera, whose Q2 FY27 results arrive on 4 November 2026, but works for Hindware's bathware segment and, with less data, for Kerovit and Somany.

  1. Revenue growth and its basis (1 minute). Check whether the comparison is on the net-of-discount basis for both periods. Cera guides 18–20% for FY27; Q1 was 19.5%. Below 15% in Q2 would suggest price increases are costing volume.
  2. Volume, price and mix by segment (1 minute). Management gives this on the call. Q1 FY27: sanitaryware volume +10, faucets +18. Volume below mid-single digits with price above 8 points is a warning sign of demand destruction.
  3. Gross margin (1 minute). Q1 FY27 was 46%; management expects ~51% from Q3. Q2 should show movement toward 48–49% if the price increases have reached retail and projects are rolling over. Flat at 46% is a red flag.
  4. EBITDA margin ex-other income (1 minute). Compare with 10.1% (Q1 FY27) and 13.1%-ish a year earlier on a like-for-like basis. FY27 guidance of 13.5–14% needs H2 margins near 15–16%; Q2 should be at least 12–13%.
  5. The one-off list (1 minute). Note every item management excludes (wage settlement, idle kilns, project foreclosures). A one-off that recurs two quarters running is not a one-off.
  6. Brass and gas (1 minute). Brass ₹/kg against the ₹950–1,000 threshold for another price increase; gas ₹/m³ against Q1's ₹48.43 and the GAIL/market split.
  7. Discounts and channel (1 minute). Any comment on trade-discount control; project share against 43% (Q1) and the 40% "stable" target; whether projects have moved to new prices.
  8. Utilisation and make-or-buy (1 minute). Sanitaryware utilisation (about 80% since June), faucet utilisation (above 95% means bought-in faucets), and any comment on internalising Morbi SKUs or the greenfield plant.
  9. Working capital and cash (1 minute). Net working-capital days against 50 (Q1) and cash against ₹943 crore (June). A jump in receivables with a rising project share is the combination to watch.
  10. Other income and PAT (1 minute). Separate treasury MTM from operating profit; check for exceptional items inside screener's "other income". Then, and only then, look at reported PAT.
In plain terms

When a bathware company reports, start with how much of its sales growth came from selling more pieces rather than higher prices, then look at the product margin and whether the one-off excuses are shrinking. Check the brass price and whether builders are now paying the new prices. Leave the profit-after-tax figure for last, because income from the company's cash can make it look better or worse than the business really did.

A final note on what the KPIs cannot tell you. None of the companies discloses market share, piece volumes sold (as opposed to produced), discount rates by channel, or brand-level profitability on a regular basis, and the largest private competitors disclose almost nothing. The KPIs above are therefore partial instruments: good at showing direction and at flagging stress, poor at measuring competitive position. That is why the positioning maps in section 15 combine several of them, and why the qualitative signals (dealer commentary on discounts, Jaquar's pricing behaviour, the success or failure of Senator and Polipluz) matter as much as any single number.

The global lens: margins live in fittings and systems, not ceramics

Cera earns a Roca-like margin at a fraction of Roca's size; the global profit leaders sell in-wall systems and taps, and the ceramic-heavy Japanese majors earn less than Cera.

The world's bathware groups offer two lessons for India: where profit sits in the product mix, and how valuable a dominant position in installer-driven systems can be. Geberit of Switzerland, the global leader in concealed cisterns, flush plates and piping systems, sold CHF 3,163 million in 2025 (+2.5%, +4.8% in local currency) and earned EBITDA of CHF 931 million, a 29.4% margin, despite a one-off cost for closing a German ceramics plant; net income was CHF 598 million and free cash flow CHF 659 million. Masco's Plumbing segment (Delta, Hansgrohe) sold US$4,992 million (+3%) at an adjusted operating margin of 18.1% and an EBITDA margin of about 20%; Masco's group revenue was US$7,562 million. Roca, the private Spanish group that owns Parryware, sold €1.96 billion in 2025 at an EBITDA margin of 13.9%. The TOTO, LIXIL and Roca figures come from search-level sources and are indicative.

The mix lesson is clear. Geberit's 29% margin rests on concealed systems with installer lock-in: once a plumber is trained on its frames and the frame is in the wall, the flush plate and the replacement parts follow for decades. Masco's plumbing margins rest on faucet brands with strong retail and plumber pull in North America and Europe. Both are fittings businesses. The ceramic-heavy Japanese groups, which make WCs in high-cost plants for mature markets, earn single-digit margins. Cera, with about half its revenue in ceramics and much of the rest in mid-market faucets, earns a margin close to Roca's, a ceramics-and-fittings group of similar mix, at roughly a tenth of Roca's size. For India the implication is that the profit pool will keep moving toward faucets and, eventually, toward in-wall systems, the one segment where no Indian company yet has a franchise (Indian brands buy their concealed-cistern mechanisms).

The valuation lesson is less clear because we could not fetch global multiples without paid data. On the figures in hand, Cera trades at about 19 times EBITDA and 28 times earnings, which is high for a company with Roca-like margins, and closer to what investors typically pay for a premium systems franchise. The market is paying for India's formalisation runway (organised brands at about 60% of a market still growing in real terms), for Cera's net cash and for scarcity: it is the only listed pure play. Foreign parents treat India the same way, as a growth market worth investing in: Kohler's ₹800 crore plan, Roca's ₹150 crore, Grohe's local presence and Sloan's strategic stake all point to more competition in premium over FY27–FY30.

In plain terms

Around the world, the bathroom companies that earn the most are the ones that sell taps and the plumbing hidden in the wall, not the ones that make toilets. Cera's margin is about what Roca earns, a company ten times its size with a similar mix. Investors pay more for Cera than its margin alone would justify because India's bathroom market is still formalising and because there is no other listed way to own it.

Technology and disruption: robots, sensors, plastic and the wall

The technologies that matter shift value toward organised makers in ceramics and toward electronics and systems suppliers in fittings.

Pressure casting and robotics are the organised players' main manufacturing moat against the potteries. High-pressure casting with resin moulds is faster than gypsum casting, needs less skilled labour and gives fewer defects; robotic glazing and CNC master moulds raise yields on complex one-piece, rimless and wall-hung models. Cera cites robotic glazing, CNC master moulds and 3D printers for design in sanitaryware, and robotic surface grinding, CNC machining and what it calls India's first multi-colour PVD finishing in faucets. These are what let a brand produce the premium SKUs that carry its margin, and they are hard for a single-kiln pottery to finance. Kiln efficiency and decarbonisation are the next step: European makers have trialled electric and hydrogen-ready kilns, and Cera's own position (55–60% of energy from captive renewables, falling gas intensity until FY24) is ahead of Indian peers, though intensity rose again in FY25–FY26 as utilisation fell.

Smart and sensor bathrooms are the disruption with the most uncertain winner. Washlets (electronic bidet seats), sensor faucets, thermostatic showers and touchless flush systems are electronics-heavy products, often sourced from Chinese or Japanese suppliers; TOTO and Kohler's smart toilets at around US$18,500 are sold to wealthy Indian buyers. As these grow, value could shift from whoever fires the ceramic to whoever controls the electronics and software. Indian brands currently assemble or rebrand most smart products rather than designing their electronics, which makes this an area where imports (already 74% Chinese in ceramic sanitaryware) could grow.

Plastic fittings are the disruption at the bottom. PTMT and ABS taps with a chrome look sell for ₹150–800 and are pushed by pipe makers (Hindware's Truflo, Astral, Supreme, Prince) through the same plumbers who install their pipes. As brass gets dearer, plastic gains share in the mass market; it uses no copper and its economics follow polymer prices. Concealed systems and wall-hung WCs, the Geberit model, move value into the wall: the in-wall frame and cistern, and the installer training that goes with them. Indian brands buy these mechanisms rather than make them. E-commerce and direct-to-consumer sales (online marketplaces and brand webstores) add price transparency, particularly in faucets, where a buyer can compare a branded mixer with a Chinese-made lookalike in seconds. Cera's annual report shows that 99.96% of its revenue is still billed to dealers; direct-to-consumer sales were ₹0.89 crore in FY26.

In plain terms

Better machines and robots help the big brands make fancy toilets that small potteries cannot. But the next wave, electronic toilets and sensor taps, depends on electronics that Indian brands mostly buy from China or Japan, and cheap plastic taps are eating into the bottom of the market. The most profitable piece of a modern bathroom, the plumbing hidden in the wall, is one no Indian company makes yet.

Two adjacent technology shifts are worth noting. First, digital dealer and loyalty systems: Cera's dealer-management system now extends to its retailer programme, tracking ₹1,680 crore of secondary sales, and its plumber programme issued 4.4 crore loyalty "stars" in FY26 with 70% redeemed. Data on secondary sales lets a brand target discounts more precisely, which bears directly on the discount problem of section 07. Second, water-efficiency technology (dual-flush, low-flow aerators, waterless urinals) is moving from a premium feature to a specification requirement in green buildings and water-stressed cities, which favours brands that test and certify their products. Neither shift is dramatic, but both reinforce the organised players' advantage over unbranded supply.

Bull vs bear: formalisation and cash vs brass, discounts and better-funded rivals

The case for the sub-sector rests on a formalising market and a cash-rich listed lens; the case against rests on input costs, sticky discounts and competition from private and foreign groups with deeper pockets.

The bull and bear cases below are written for the sub-sector, with Cera as the listed vehicle. Both are plausible, and the evidence that will separate them over the next two or three quarters is specific: gross margin returning toward 51%, discounts tightening, projects repricing, and faucet growth holding near 18% against Jaquar's expansion.

The bull case

  • Formalisation runway: organised brands hold about 60% of a ₹20,000–25,000 crore market, and GST, quality rules, tier-3 retail expansion and the 2026 gas shock all push share toward them.
  • Demand at the right stage: top-seven-city completions at a decade high (5.4 lakh scheduled for 2026), a renovation cycle in the post-2000 urban stock, and rising launches supporting fit-outs into 2027–28.
  • A real cost edge: Cera's gas at about half of Morbi's, contracted from nearby fields, plus captive renewables for 55–60% of energy.
  • The trough is partly accounting and one-offs: on CareEdge's core basis FY26's margin fell only 1.15 points; Q1 FY27's 10.1% carries about 4.35 points of one-offs, and guidance of 13.5–14% is a genuine step-up.
  • Fortress balance sheet: about ₹806 crore of net cash, AA ratings from CareEdge and CRISIL, free cash flow of ₹192 crore, a 47% payout and capacity to fund a greenfield plant from cash.
  • Valuation already de-rated: 33% off the 52-week high; about 15–16 times FY27 EBITDA if guidance is met.

The bear case

  • Brass near the next threshold: copper near record highs; above ₹950–1,000 per kg Cera must raise prices again, with another lag.
  • Discounts are sticky: FY26's margin fall came mostly from trade discounts, and management calls control "a gradual journey"; Hindware's 43.7% list-to-net gap shows how large the lever is.
  • Channel drift: project share at 39–43%, lower-margin, pre-priced and slower-paying; management's "stable" 60/40 still means FY23's 16% margin will not return on mix.
  • Better-funded rivals: Jaquar (₹800–1,000 crore capex, ₹12,000 crore target), Kohler (₹800 crore), Roca and Grohe in premium; Hindware's split may create a cheaper, cleaner listed rival.
  • Supply dependence: about 59% of revenue is bought in, sanitaryware "mostly from Morbi", whose gas price and export markets are both unsettled.
  • Execution on brands: Senator and Polipluz are small and loss-making, and the executive who ran both left on 30 September 2026; the A&P step-up to ₹85 crore must earn its return.

Weighing the two, the bull case is stronger on the medium-term structure (formalisation, demand, balance sheet) and the bear case stronger on the next two or three quarters (brass, discounts, project lag). That is a familiar shape for a consumer-durables franchise in a cost shock, and it means the evidence will arrive in sequence: first gross margin (Q2 and Q3 FY27), then discount discipline and project repricing (H2 FY27), then whether Cera's faucet growth and brand ladder hold against Jaquar and the MNCs (FY28). An investor's view on the sub-sector should change with that evidence rather than with list-price circulars or housing headlines.

In plain terms

The optimistic view is that India's bathrooms keep moving to branded products, housing completions are at record levels, Cera has cheap gas and lots of cash, and its profit dip is mostly temporary. The pessimistic view is that brass may rise again, dealers will not give back their discounts quickly, more sales are going to builders at thinner margins, and much richer rivals are investing heavily. The next two quarters will show which matters more in the short run.
