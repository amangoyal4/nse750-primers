00

The one-page view: a brand business that sits on an oil price

India's lubricant market grows slowly in litres and quickly in rupees. In 2026 the oil price, the electric scooter and a change of owner all arrived at once.

A lubricant is mostly refined oil: 80–90% base oil from a refinery, plus a 10–20% package of additives that stops an engine from wearing, sludging or overheating. India is one of the few big markets where demand is still growing. Kline, the industry's standard reference, put Indian finished-lubricant demand at 2.6 million tonnes in 2023 and expects 3.6 million tonnes by 2033: 3–4% a year in volume and 6–8% a year in value. That makes India roughly a tenth of world demand and the third-largest market after the United States and China. At a blended trade value of about ₹170–220 a litre, the market is worth roughly ₹55,000–70,000 crore a year (indicative; third-party estimates disagree widely).

The industry has two very different kinds of seller. The state refiners, Indian Oil (Servo), HPCL (HP Lubes) and BPCL (MAK), make base oil, own petrol pumps and sell the most tonnes: Indian Oil alone reported 905 thousand tonnes of lubricant sales in FY26. The brand houses (Castrol, Gulf, Shell, Valvoline Cummins, Veedol, Mobil, Total) buy base oil, blend it, and win the mechanic's recommendation in the bazaar. The refiners win tonnes; the brands win rupees per litre. Castrol India sells perhaps a quarter of Indian Oil's tonnage but earned ₹950 crore of profit on ₹5,722 crore of revenue in CY25, with a 60% return on capital employed. Few Indian consumer businesses do better.

Only one lubricant company is in the NSE 750: Castrol India (CASTROLIND · 500870). This report covers the whole industry and uses Castrol as its lens, with Gulf Oil Lubricants, Veedol (formerly Tide Water Oil), Savita Oil, Valvoline Cummins and the state refiners as reference points. Five forces define the sub-sector, and each gets its own section:

  • Base oil is a pass-through with a lag, and the lag is the profit and loss account. India imported 3,178 thousand tonnes of base oil in FY26. The West Asia war from 28 February 2026 pushed the import cost from $864 to $1,116 a tonne in the first half of FY27, peaking near $1,490 in July. Castrol's EBITDA margin went 21% (Q1 CY26) → 26% (Q2) → 15% (Q3). Section 05 is the deep dive.
  • Volume is mature; value grows on premiumisation. Synthetic oils, thinner grades and longer drain intervals mean fewer litres per vehicle at a higher price per litre. Winners grow realisation per litre and market share, not the market.
  • Brand and distribution, not chemistry, earn the returns. Castrol reaches about 1.67 lakh outlets and works with some 7 lakh mechanics. Its EBITDA of roughly ₹56 a litre is about 1.8 times Gulf's ~₹31 (both illustrative estimates). The channel is shifting toward OEM workshops and business-to-business supply, where Gulf is growing volume at 16–17%.
  • Electric two-wheelers erode the largest engine-oil pool first. They were 11.6% of two-wheeler retail sales in September 2026, up from 8.2% a year earlier. The 243 million two-wheelers on the road delay the impact by a decade, but the direction is now visible. Section 18 runs the numbers.
  • Ownership is changing. BP agreed in December 2025 to sell 65% of global Castrol to the infrastructure investor Stonepeak. That triggered a mandatory open offer for 26% of Castrol India at ₹194.04 a share, rising at 10% a year until the offer is formally launched. The stock closed at ₹188 on 30 October 2026. The quarterly pattern is the whole industry in miniature. In April–June 2026 Castrol sold lubricant made from base oil bought before the war, at prices it had already raised twice, and earned a record ₹348 crore. In July–September the expensive base oil reached the blending plants: the raw-material bill rose more than 60% year on year, revenue rose 27%, EBITDA fell 19% to ₹262 crore and profit fell 23% to ₹176 crore. Taken together, the first nine months of CY26 still produced revenue of ₹5,151 crore (+20%), EBITDA of ₹1,086 crore (+11%) and profit of ₹766 crore (+9%). In plain terms

Lubricant companies buy a commodity (base oil) and sell a brand (the name on the bottle a mechanic trusts). When oil gets dearer, they raise prices a few months late and their margins shrink; when oil gets cheaper, they cut prices slowly and their margins swell. Over a full cycle the brand wins, which is why Castrol has earned 21–30% operating margins every year for a decade. The questions in late 2026 are how long the current squeeze lasts, how many two-wheeler engines will still need oil in 2035, and what a private-equity-led owner will do with a subsidiary that pays out nine-tenths of its profit.

Three further facts frame everything that follows. First, the industry is concentrated at the top in value but fragmented in tonnes: four private brands (Castrol, Gulf, Valvoline Cummins, Veedol) sell roughly a fifth of the national tonnage between them (indicative), the state refiners a much larger share that includes base oil, and hundreds of regional blenders the rest. Second, disclosure is poor: Castrol, the market leader, does not publish litres, and analysts on its October 2026 call complained that this makes fair value uncertain by "15%, 16%". Gulf publishes litres every quarter, which is why per-litre arguments in this report can be tested for Gulf and only estimated for Castrol. Third, the listed universe is small: the four listed lubricant-led companies (Castrol and the three reference peers) are worth about ₹30,500 crore together, and Castrol is 61% of that.

01

From crude to the mechanic: how a litre of lubricant earns

The refinery makes the base oil, the additive houses make the chemistry, and the money is made in the last two steps: the brand and the bazaar.

A lubricant's job is to keep moving metal surfaces apart, carry heat and dirt away, and stop corrosion. Base oil does the first job; additives do most of the rest. The chain that produces a bottle of engine oil has five steps, and the profit is not where the cost is.

Steps 1–3 are roughly half of the selling price and earn commodity-like returns. Steps 4–5 are where a brand house earns its 20%+ margin.

Step 1: base oil, the commodity

Base oil is a heavy fraction of crude oil (vacuum gas oil) that refineries upgrade into a stable, clean lubricant stock. It comes in grades. Group I is solvent-refined, cheaper and older; it still makes up a large share of Indian mineral oils. Group II is hydrocracked, low in sulphur and the workhorse for today's 15W-40 and 20W-40 grades. Group III is severely hydrocracked with a high viscosity index, used for the thin synthetic grades (0W-20, 5W-30) that modern petrol engines need. Group IV (PAO) and Group V (esters) are true synthetics. Castrol says it uses "predominantly" Group II and III, and its fully synthetic brands (Magnatec, Power1) need Group III plus PAO.

India makes mostly Group I and II at state refineries (HPCL runs a 428 thousand-tonne-a-year lube refinery in Mumbai; Indian Oil launched its first Group III grade in FY26) and imports the rest. In FY26 India imported 3,178 thousand tonnes of base oil worth $2.71 billion (PPAC). Much of the Group III supply comes from Korea and from Gulf refineries in Bahrain, the UAE and Qatar, which sit behind the Strait of Hormuz. That geography is why the 2026 war hit lubricants harder than most chemicals. For the crude and refining side of this chain, see the refining report and the oil exploration report.

Step 2: additives, the chemistry

Detergents, dispersants, anti-wear compounds (ZDDP), viscosity-index improvers and pour-point depressants are bought as ready packages from four global additive houses. They are priced in dollars and largely imported, so a weaker rupee raises costs even when base oil is flat; the rupee fell from about ₹86 to ₹94.9 to the dollar between late 2025 and October 2026. For Castrol the global group sets formulations and approvals; Castrol India's products are "approved globally" and blended locally.

Step 3: blending and packing, the cheap part

Blending is simple and cheap: a 50–100 million-litre plant costs perhaps ₹50–150 crore (indicative). Castrol produced 230+ million litres in CY25 across Patalganga (100+ million), Silvassa (85+ million) and Paharpur (45+ million), with 13 million litres a year of extra capacity added at Patalganga by debottlenecking. Its capex runs at ₹100–150 crore a year. Gulf blends 168 million litres a year with 140,000 kilolitres of its own capacity, running the rest through contract "satellite" blenders. Packaging (HDPE bottles, cartons) is a real cost line, and polymer prices rose with oil in 2026.

Steps 4 and 5: the brand and the bazaar, where the money is

Most lubricant is chosen not by the vehicle owner but by the mechanic who changes the oil. A two-wheeler owner in a small town asks for "good oil", and the mechanic picks the brand he trusts, the brand his customers recognise from cricket advertising, or the brand whose loyalty app pays him most. Castrol reaches about 1.67 lakh outlets (46,000 of them rural), 37,000 independent bike workshops, 16,000 multi-brand car workshops and roughly 870 Castrol Auto Service outlets, and counts 1.8 lakh "verified" mechanics on its FastScan loyalty platform (+24% year on year). That network, and the trust behind it, is the moat. A competitor can copy the oil; it cannot quickly copy the mechanic relationships.

In plain terms

Anyone with a tank and a mixer can blend oil, and hundreds of small Indian blenders do. What cannot be bought quickly is the trust of a mechanic in a Tier-3 town, approvals from Maruti or Tata, and a brand name a customer asks for by name. That is why Castrol earns about twice the margin of its nearest private rival from the same base oil.

Two features of this chain matter for the rest of the report. First, the brand house's working capital is tiny or negative: Castrol pays its suppliers in about 98 days, holds 68 days of stock and collects from distributors in 30, so suppliers finance its inventory. That is why it needs almost no capital to grow and can pay out nearly all of its profit. Second, the industry passes cost through with a lag: price lists change in steps, a few times a year, while base oil changes every week. The lag creates the margin swings that dominate quarterly results, and it is the subject of section 05.

02

2.6 million tonnes and two growth rates

Indian lubricant demand grows 3–4% a year in tonnes and 6–8% in rupees. The gap between the two is the industry's profit engine.

There is no official census of the Indian lubricant market, so its size must be pieced together from three sources that measure different things. Used carefully, they agree on the shape of the market even where they disagree on the level.

Kline: the level

Kline & Company's global lubricants study, quoted by Gulf Oil Lubricants in its investor presentations and now in its audited FY26 annual report, puts Indian finished-lubricant demand at 2.6 million tonnes in 2023, rising to 3.6 million tonnes in 2033. Volume grows 3–4% a year and value 6–8% a year. Within that, industrial lubricants grow 3–4% and automotive 2–3%. Grown at 3.5% a year, 2023's 2.6 million tonnes becomes about 2.85–2.9 million tonnes in 2026, or roughly 3.2–3.3 billion litres at a density of about 0.88 kg per litre (indicative). Castrol's managing director uses the same frame: market growth "in a regular year … 3% to 4%".

PPAC: the direction, and the 2026 shock

The Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell (PPAC) publishes monthly apparent consumption of "lubricants and greases". This is a wider and noisier series than Kline's: it includes base oils used in process oils, white oils, transformer oils and rubber-process oils, and it moves with refinery reclassifications. It was 4,961 thousand tonnes in FY26, nearly twice Kline's finished-lubricant number. Its year-to-year path is jumpy (4,540 in FY22, 3,737 in FY23, 4,087 in FY24, 4,581 in FY25), so it should be used for direction, not level. Its most useful recent signal is the 2026 shock: in the first half of FY27 (April–September 2026) consumption was 2,200 thousand tonnes, down 7.6%, with June down 28%.

Company data: the shares

The third source is what the companies themselves disclose, which is uneven. Indian Oil reported lubricant sales of 905 thousand tonnes in FY26 (~16% growth); HPCL about 686 thousand tonnes; BPCL's MAK 471.8 thousand tonnes in FY25. These state numbers almost certainly include base oil sold to other blenders, bulk industrial and process oils and exports, so their sum (about 2.06 million tonnes on mixed years) cannot be compared with Kline's 2.9 million tonnes of finished lubricant. Gulf disclosed 168,000 kilolitres (168 million litres) in FY26, up 11%. Castrol discloses only growth rates (+8% in CY25), but its annual report gives plant production of 230+ million litres, which with traded goods implies sales of roughly 235–245 million litres (indicative). Section 07 turns these into indicative shares.

Value: why rupees outgrow litres

Value grows about twice as fast as volume for three reasons. Premiumisation: buyers move from mineral to semi-synthetic to fully synthetic oils, and from 20W-40 to 10W-30 to 0W-20, each step costing more per litre. Mix: SUVs, BS-VI engines and turbo-petrols need oils that cost two to three times a basic mineral grade. Price pass-through: base-oil inflation is passed on, so revenue rises in rupees even when litres do not. Against that, longer drain intervals (a modern car may go 10,000–15,000 km between changes instead of 5,000) and electric vehicles subtract litres.

In plain terms

India is not running out of engines. It is running out of reasons to change the oil often. Every year there are about 4% more vehicles on the road, but each one needs a little less oil, and a growing number need none. The industry's answer is to sell fewer, better, dearer litres. A company that only grows with the market in litres is standing still.

The company track records fit this frame. Castrol's revenue grew at 8.1% a year from CY21 to CY25 (₹4,192 crore to ₹5,722 crore) while its volume grew perhaps 4–5% a year (indicative); it claims "eight consecutive quarters of volume growth ahead of the market" to the end of CY25. Gulf grew revenue at about 16.6% a year from FY22 to FY26 on volume growth of about 9% a year, and has compounded volume at 9% a year since 2009: a 17-year story of share gains. Veedol grew 9.0% a year in rupees, but its standalone Indian business grew only 1.3% in FY26 on "flat domestic volumes". Savita, which is mainly a transformer- and white-oil processor, grew 10.4% a year with volatile margins.

The market value in rupees is the least certain number in this report. Third-party estimates on paid-report landing pages range from about US$4.8 billion to US$9.7 billion and contradict each other. Building it bottom-up, 3.2 billion litres at a blended ex-factory value of ₹170–220 a litre across state and private, retail and bulk, gives ₹55,000–70,000 crore (indicative). A cross-check: the four listed lubricant-led companies plus Valvoline Cummins sell about ₹14,700 crore of lubricant-led domestic revenue, roughly a fifth to a quarter of that estimate, which is plausible given the weight of the state refiners and unlisted multinationals.

03

The 328-million-vehicle parc: what really drives demand

Lubricant demand follows the vehicles already on the road and the freight they carry, not this month's new-car sales.

A lubricant company sells into the stock of vehicles, not the flow. Castrol's investor presentation of August 2026 puts India's vehicle parc at about 328 million vehicles in March 2026: 243 million two-wheelers, 50 million four-wheelers and 12 million commercial vehicles, with three-wheelers, tractors and others making up the balance. The parc grew about 4% in FY25 (CRISIL, cited in Castrol's annual report). A record 2.97 crore vehicles were retailed in FY26, but each year's sales add only a small slice to the parc, and old vehicles keep consuming oil for 12–15 years.

Litres per vehicle: why one truck equals forty scooters

The parc count is dominated by two-wheelers, but the oil count is not. A scooter or motorcycle holds about a litre of oil and is drained two or three times a year: roughly 2–3 litres a year. A car takes 3.5–4.5 litres per change: about 4–6 litres a year. A heavy truck has a 15–30 litre sump and is drained three to five times a year, plus gear and axle oils and grease: 60–120 litres a year. A tractor uses 15–25 litres. On these indicative ranges, one heavy commercial vehicle uses as much oil as 30–50 two-wheelers. That is why diesel freight, the truck cycle and the tractor season matter as much to a lubricant company as headline two-wheeler sales, and why the electric-vehicle story is less simple than it looks (section 18).

Freight and diesel

Commercial-vehicle oil tracks freight, and freight tracks diesel consumption. India used 94,708 thousand tonnes of diesel in FY26 (+3.6%); in the first half of FY27 consumption was almost flat (+0.5%) as the war-driven price shock and four pump-price increases in May 2026 slowed activity, before October 2026 rebounded 11.7% (PPAC). Castrol's April–June quarter is its seasonal peak, because harvest movement lifts truck and tractor oil demand. Commercial-vehicle sales fell in FY25 as government capital spending slowed; the commercial vehicles report covers that cycle.

Rural motorisation

Rural India buys about 55% of the country's two-wheelers (FADA, cited by Castrol) and 2.24 million used cars were sold in rural markets in 2025. Ownership is still low: about 5 cars and 60 two-wheelers per 100 rural households, against 23 cars and 64 two-wheelers in cities (PRICE research cited by Castrol). That gap is why every lubricant company is chasing rural distribution. Castrol's rural outlets rose from about 40,000 in CY25 to 46,000 by September 2026, served by 1,100+ rural distributors and about 1,000 "Rural Service Express" points. Gulf counts 2,100+ rural stockists. The GST 2.0 rate cuts of September 2025 (small cars and two-wheelers up to 350cc, and tractors from 12% to 5%) added to the parc; see the two-wheelers, passenger vehicles and tractors reports.

Industry and agriculture

Industrial lubricants, by Mordor's count more than half of national volume, follow steel, cement, automotive and component plants, machinery and mining. Castrol's industrial business, about 15% of its total according to its CFO, has grown at double digits for 18–24 months, adding about 400 net new customers in the first nine months of CY26 and 700 new customers in Q3 alone. Tractor sales grew 18.7% in FY25 and India dispatched a record 11.6 lakh tractors in FY26; tractor oils are a battleground between Servo, Castrol, Gulf and the OEMs' own "genuine" oils.

In plain terms

To forecast lubricant demand, ignore the monthly car-sales headline and watch three slower numbers: how many vehicles are on the road, how much diesel the country burns, and how busy its factories are. The first grows about 4% a year, the second 3–4%, the third with industrial output. Together they give the 3–4% volume growth the industry quotes.

One more demand driver is still small but worth noting: new fluids. Data-centre cooling fluids, battery thermal-management fluids and AdBlue (the urea solution BS-VI diesel trucks need) are growing fast from tiny bases. Castrol had two thermal-management fluids certified by NVIDIA around September 2026 but says they "wouldn't form a substantial part of our revenue and profit today". Gulf is the second-largest AdBlue supplier in India, selling 151,000 kilolitres in FY26 (+8%) at a "mid-single-digit margin". These are options, not drivers, for the next three years.

In plain terms

Castrol and Gulf buy similar base oil at similar prices. The 2026 base-oil shock: who held price, who held volume Deep dive

The West Asia war doubled the price of some base oils within four months. Castrol defended rupees per litre, Gulf took litres, the processors booked windfalls and the state refiners had the oil.

On 28 February 2026 war in West Asia disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Crude, freight and LPG prices jumped; India cut fuel excise on 26 March and then raised pump prices four times in May. For lubricant makers the shock arrived through base oil, and specifically the Group III grades refined in the Gulf. This section follows the shock from the import data to four companies' results, because it is the clearest recent test of the industry's pricing power.

The input: base oil at $1,490 a tonne

PPAC's monthly import data show the price of imported base oil rising from $914 a tonne in April 2026 to $1,154 in May, $1,423 in June and $1,487 in July, before falling back to about $949 in August and September. The first-half average was $1,116 a tonne, 29% above the $864 of a year earlier. Import tonnage fell 18% at the same time (1,305 against 1,600 thousand tonnes), because cargoes were scarce, buyers ran down stocks and domestic Group I/II base oil from the state refineries filled part of the gap. Gulf's CFO said in August that "Group III base oils have become very, very scarce" and that some high-end grades were still rising even after crude eased. One price tracker cited Indian base-oil cargoes at $1,650 a tonne in April against $1,360 in March (indicative: single source, grade not stated).

The per-litre argument

Management's defence was that the percentage margin is the wrong measure in an inflationary quarter. In prepared remarks the CFO said "margin per liter is a true measure. And on that basis, we have fully absorbed cost inflation. Margins are steady to slightly higher." The logic is sound in principle. If base oil doubles and a company raises its price by exactly the rupees its cost rose, it earns the same rupees per litre, but the percentage margin falls because revenue is inflated by the pass-through. A shareholder is paid in rupees, not percentages.

The arithmetic, however, is hard to square with the reported numbers. Castrol does not disclose litres. If Q3 volume was flat year on year, gross profit per litre fell 6.6% (from roughly ₹113 to ₹106, illustrative). For gross profit per litre to be "steady", Q3 volume must have fallen by about 7%. For EBITDA per litre to be steady, volume must have fallen 19%, which nobody suggests. When an analyst asked whether flat per-litre margins with EBITDA down 20% meant volumes fell sharply, the CFO replied: "I wouldn't comment on that … that's an internal frame … what we always target is percentage margin as percentage of sales." Earlier on the same call the managing director said that between Q2 and Q3 "the volume growth is looking healthy" and that nine-month volume growth was "ahead of the market growth rate" of 3–4%.

Price versus percentage: the pass-through arithmetic

The cleanest way to see the trap is a worked example. In CY25 Castrol's materials cost about ₹121 a litre and its realisation was about ₹237, a gross margin of ₹116 a litre (49%). Suppose materials rise 50% to about ₹182. To keep the rupee gross margin at ₹116, the price must rise by the ₹61 of extra cost, to ₹298: a 25% increase, and the percentage gross margin falls to 39%. To keep the percentage margin at 49%, the price must rise 50%, to ₹356, which no consumer brand can push through in one quarter without losing customers. Castrol's 27% revenue growth in Q3 is consistent with roughly the first path. Whether it lost litres doing so is the question it declines to answer.

In plain terms

Lubricant margins in percent always fall when oil spikes and rise when oil falls, even if the company does nothing wrong. The useful questions are whether rupee profit per litre held, and whether the company kept its customers while it repriced. In this shock Gulf answered both with numbers; Castrol answered the first with words and the second not at all.

What comes next

Three forces decide Q4 CY26 and beyond. Base oil eased to about $949 a tonne in August–September, which helps Q4 costs with a one- to three-month lag. But Brent was back at about $114–115 a barrel on 26 October, according to Castrol's MD, so the relief may be short. Rebates: Castrol books an annual rebate on its global base-oil contracts every Q4; management called it "business as usual" and said its size will be known when the quarter closes. Pricing: further price action will be taken "as and when we decide", alongside reformulation, local sourcing and a wider supplier pool. History (section 11) says branded margins recover within one to three quarters of a spike and then overshoot when base oil falls. The cycle has repeated in 2008–09, 2021–22 and now 2026, and each time the brand leader came out with its margin intact over the full cycle.

The premium ladder

Engine oils climb a ladder. At the bottom is mineral oil (Group I/II base, thick grades such as 20W-40 and 20W-50), still about 55% of Indian lubricant volume according to IMARC (indicative, single source). Next is semi-synthetic (a blend with some Group III), then fully synthetic (Group III, PAO or esters, thin grades such as 5W-30 and 0W-20). Each rung lasts longer, protects better and costs more. BS-VI engines, turbo-petrol cars, SUVs and newer motorcycles increasingly specify the upper rungs. Castrol launched full-synthetic versions of Activ (two-wheeler) and GTX (car) in Q2 CY26 to pull the mass market up a step. Gulf, citing Kline, says Indian consumers are "fast adopting lighter viscosity engine oils & synthetics" and that the middle class will grow from 31% of the population today to about 60% by 2047.

The drain-interval paradox

Premiumisation shrinks the very volume it monetises. A car that needed a 3.5-litre change every 5,000 km on mineral oil may go 10,000–15,000 km on a synthetic: half or a third of the litres, at perhaps twice the price per litre. Castrol's annual report notes the same in industry: manufacturers focus on "total cost of ownership … leading to wider oil drain intervals with premium oils". For a brand leader that captures the upgrade, the trade is good (more rupees and more margin per customer). For a volume player selling mineral oil, it is a slow leak. This is a large part of why Kline's automotive volume grows only 2–3%.

In plain terms

A customer who switches to a better oil changes it less often. The lubricant company sells fewer litres but makes more money on each. Over ten years the litres barely grow, but the rupees do, and the companies that own the premium end of the shelf take the growth.

A worked example: one rider over a year

An illustrative two-wheeler rider covers 9,000 km a year and changes a litre of oil every 3,000–4,000 km: about 2.5 changes a year. At a ₹420 shelf price that is about ₹1,050 a year of oil at retail. Castrol's share of that, at its ₹237 average realisation, is about ₹590, and its EBITDA about ₹140. If the rider moves to a semi-synthetic that lasts 5,000 km at ₹560 a litre, changes fall to 1.8 a year and spend rises to about ₹1,010: litres down 28%, rupees almost unchanged, and the brand's margin per litre higher. If the rider switches to an electric scooter, the oil spend falls to perhaps ₹100–200 of e-fluids and grease over a whole decade. The first move is premiumisation; the second is section 18.

Who wins the value race

The companies that grow realisation per litre faster than cost per litre win. Castrol's revenue grew 8.1% a year from CY21 to CY25 on volume growth of about 4–5% (indicative), so roughly 3–4 points a year came from price and mix. Gulf's revenue grew about 16.6% a year on 9% volume growth, but part of that is AdBlue and batteries. Over the same years Castrol's EBITDA margin was stable at 23–25%, which says it captured the premium step without having to buy it with discounts. The state refiners are moving up the ladder too: Indian Oil launched its first domestic Group III base oil in FY26 and markets synthetic Servo grades, and BPCL's MAK sells premium car oils. The ladder is crowded at the top, which is why Castrol's 2026 full-synthetic launches into the mass two-wheeler market matter.

The same base oil earns 5% in one business model and 24% in another. The model, not the molecule, sets the return.

Indian lubricant companies fall into five archetypes. Each has a different source of advantage, a different weakness and a different margin. Knowing which archetype a company belongs to tells you most of what to expect from its numbers before you open the annual report.

Archetype 1: the multinational brand

Castrol is the purest example. It owns no refinery and no pumps. It owns a brand that mechanics and consumers ask for, approvals from 90+ OEM customers, and the most extensive bazaar network in the country. Its EBITDA margin has been between 21% and 30% every calendar year since 2014, and its return on capital employed between 55% and 67% over the last five years. It pays a royalty to its parent (₹132 crore, 2.3% of sales) and gets in return global formulations, approvals and the brand. The weakness is concentration: its biggest pool is two-wheeler oil, the pool most exposed to electric scooters, and its fate is tied to decisions taken in London and now by Stonepeak.

Archetype 2: the challenger

Gulf, Veedol and Valvoline Cummins earn 10–13% margins and 24–27% returns. They compete on price ladder, service and channel rather than pure brand pull. Gulf is the most successful: 17 years of roughly 9% volume growth, an "OEM franchise workshop" business growing at high double digits, factory fill for 50+ OEMs, and B2B industrial, infrastructure and mining contracts. Its management guides to an EBITDA band of 12–14%, aiming for 14–16% through premiumisation, and to volume growth of "2 to 3x the market". Veedol is a cautious version: it owns the Veedol trademark outright (bought from BP in 2011), sells ENEOS products through a joint venture, and chose "margin protection … over aggressive volume expansion" in FY26, deferring capacity expansion on "flat domestic volumes". Valvoline Cummins, an unlisted 50:50 joint venture, grew revenue 28% to ₹3,011 crore in FY26 on the strength of Cummins engines in trucks and gensets.

Archetype 3: the state refiner

Indian Oil, HPCL and BPCL make base oil, run tens of thousands of petrol pumps and win government and institutional tenders. They sell the most tonnes and, in a shortage, have the most secure supply. They do not report lubricant profits, and their retail brand premium is lower than the multinationals'. Their lube arms are increasingly run as businesses: Indian Oil says Servo gained 5.3% market share in FY26, and HPCL has talked of demerging HP Lubes.

Archetype 4: the specialty processor

Savita turns base oil into transformer oils, white oils, liquid paraffins and some automotive and industrial lubricants. Its products are bought on specification by power-equipment makers, cosmetics companies and tyre makers (see the tyres report), not chosen by mechanics. Its normal EBITDA is about ₹4.6 per litre (₹4,555 per kilolitre or tonne in FY26), against roughly ₹31 at Gulf and ₹56 at Castrol. Because it holds base-oil stock, its profits jump when base oil rises (Q1 FY27 EBITDA margin 25%) and fall when it drops. It is a base-oil-to-specialty conversion business, not a branded-lubricant peer.

Archetype 5: the OEM's own label

Every large carmaker and two-wheeler maker sells "genuine oil" through its authorised workshops. The oil is blended by a lubricant company, sometimes co-branded, sometimes under the OEM's label. The more servicing moves to authorised workshops, which is the case for newer and more complex vehicles, the more the default fill is the OEM's choice. Blenders respond by becoming the OEM's supplier: Gulf's "OEM FWS" business, Castrol's 90+ OEM customers, and Savita's new multi-year contract to supply genuine lubricants to a global tractor maker are all bets on this channel. The cost is margin: the OEM keeps much of the premium, and Gulf now pays "royalty to OEMs" for co-branding.

Why archetype 1 earns twice archetype 2

Four things combine. Price: Castrol's lubricant litre sells for roughly 15–20% more than Gulf's on a like-for-like basis (illustrative). Mix: Castrol's business is weighted to consumer two-wheeler and car oils, the highest-margin litres; its own presentation suggests roughly 61% consumer, 26% OEM and fleet and 13% industrial (read visually from an unlabelled chart, so indicative), and the CFO puts industrial at "about 15%". Scale in advertising: ₹479 crore of advertising and promotion is 8.4% of Castrol's sales; a challenger needs a similar absolute budget to be heard and so spends a larger share of a smaller revenue. Working capital: Castrol's payables (98 days) fund its stock (68 days) and receivables (30 days), so its capital employed is mostly plant and a little cash; Gulf runs a 37-day cash conversion cycle and funds imports with foreign-currency buyer's credit.

In plain terms

A lubricant brand leader is closer to a soap or biscuit company than to an oil refiner. It sells a cheap, frequently bought product through millions of small shops, wins on habit and trust, and needs little capital. The challengers are run like good industrial distributors. The refiners are run like refiners. The processors are run like chemical converters. Their returns line up accordingly.

Archetypes also differ in how they handle a cost shock. The brand leader raises prices and accepts a temporary drop in percentage margin. The challenger leans on contracts with cost-flex clauses and its lower price point to win share. The refiner has supply when others do not. The processor gains on its inventory on the way up and loses on the way down. The 2026 results in section 05 matched these predictions almost exactly.

The rulebook: BS-VI, used-oil EPR, duties and the takeover code

No regulator sets lubricant prices. Emission norms, used-oil recycling targets, customs duty and SEBI's takeover rules shape the economics instead.

Lubricants are lightly regulated compared with fuels or medicines. There is no price control, no licensing of brands and no quality-control order specific to engine oil that this report could confirm. The rules that matter work indirectly, through the engines the oil goes into, the waste it becomes, the duty on its raw material and, in 2026, the takeover code that governs Castrol's change of owner.

BS-VI and E20: better oil, fewer changes

India jumped from BS-IV to BS-VI emission norms on 1 April 2020 and added real-driving emission tests and on-board diagnostics in April 2023. Modern after-treatment devices (particulate filters, catalytic converters, SCR systems) are poisoned by the ash and phosphorus in older oils, so BS-VI engines need low-ash oils of newer specifications. These cost more and last longer. The net effect is the volume-versus-value pattern of section 06: more rupees per litre, fewer litres per vehicle. BS-VI diesel trucks also need AdBlue, a urea solution that has become a side business for Gulf. Nationwide E20 petrol, with 20% ethanol, increases fuel dilution and corrosion risk in the sump; Castrol markets oils for "flex fuel and hybrid vehicles".

Extended producer responsibility: a new cost and a new market

From 1 April 2024, under the Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Second Amendment Rules 2023, every producer of base oil or lubricating oil, every brand owner that blends third-party base oil, and every importer of base or lube oil must ensure that a rising share of the used oil generated is collected and re-refined. The target starts at 5% for 2024-25 (on 2022-23 sales) and rises each year to 40% for 2029-30. Compliance is shown through EPR certificates issued by the Central Pollution Control Board to registered re-refiners; certificates are tradeable and valid for two years, and shortfalls attract environmental compensation. Intermediate-year targets were not confirmed in a primary source.

Customs duty and GST

Base oil carries a basic customs duty of 5% plus small surcharges, about 5.8% of landed value before the 18% IGST, which is credited back. No anti-dumping duty applies. Because the state refiners make base oil domestically, any change in this duty moves the cost gap between them and the private blenders who import. GST on finished lubricants is 18%. The GST 2.0 rationalisation of 22 September 2025 cut rates on small cars, two-wheelers up to 350cc and tractors, which supports parc growth rather than lubricant pricing.

EV policy

The PM E-DRIVE scheme, which replaced FAME-II, pays an incentive of ₹2,500 per kWh, capped at ₹5,000 per electric two-wheeler (and at 15% of ex-factory price). The two-wheeler incentive lapsed on 31 July 2026 and was restored and extended in August 2026 to 31 March 2028, with the two-wheeler allocation raised from ₹1,772 crore to ₹2,767 crore (up to 45.8 lakh vehicles) within a total scheme outlay of ₹11,900 crore. Section 18 covers what this means for oil demand.

The takeover code and related-party rules

Because BP's sale of global Castrol changes the ultimate control of Castrol India, SEBI's takeover regulations require an open offer to Castrol India's public shareholders (an "indirect acquisition"). The offer price rises at 10% a year from the date of the global agreement to the date of the detailed public statement, which is due within five working days of completion. If the offer takes the promoter group above 75%, it must sell down to restore 25% public holding. SEBI's related-party rules mean any material change to Castrol India's royalty or brand licence would need approval from shareholders other than the promoter. Section 16 works through both.

In plain terms

The government does not tell lubricant companies what to charge. It tells carmakers how clean their engines must be (which makes oil better and dearer), tells oil sellers to pay for recycling used oil (a new cost that grows each year to 2030), and taxes base-oil imports lightly. For Castrol shareholders in 2026 the most important rulebook is SEBI's takeover code.

A century of brands and three base-oil squeezes

Every base-oil spike has compressed branded margins for one to three quarters and every fall has expanded them. The 2026 squeeze is the third large one since 2008.

The Indian lubricant industry's history has two threads. One is structural: the arrival of multinational brands after liberalisation, the build-out of the bazaar network, and the slow rise of challengers. The other is cyclical: the base-oil price, which has driven every large swing in branded margins.

The margin record

Castrol's EBITDA margin is the industry's best long-run record of pricing power. It was 21% in CY14, rose to 27% and 30% as oil collapsed in CY15–16, held 27–30% through CY17–20, fell to 23% in CY22 as base oil spiked after the Russia–Ukraine war, and recovered to 24% in CY23–25. The trailing-twelve-month figure to September 2026 is 22%. Across twelve years the margin has never been below 21% on an annual basis. Quarterly margins are more volatile: 28% in Q4 CY24, 15% in Q3 CY26.

The cycle pattern

The pattern repeats: base oil spikes, branded margins compress for one to three quarters, price increases restore rupee margins, and when base oil falls the price increases are not fully reversed, so margins overshoot. Branded players therefore earn above-normal margins on the way down (CY15–16, CY20, CY23). The 2026 spike is the third large episode since 2008, after 2008–09 and 2021–22. What differs this time is that the peak margin compression (Q3 CY26 at 15%) is the deepest in the record, and that it has coincided with a challenger growing volume at 16–17% and an ownership change.

The structural thread

Over the same decades the structure changed slowly. The state refiners kept their tonnage through pumps and tenders. Castrol built the most profitable franchise in the bazaar and became BP's highest-margin listed lubricant vehicle. Gulf, listed in 2014, compounded volume at about 9% a year and became the second-largest private brand. Veedol, after buying its brand from BP, stayed mid-sized and turned to overseas growth. Valvoline Cummins rode the diesel-engine franchise of its Cummins parent. BP sold down Castrol India in 2016 and in 2025 sold control of Castrol itself. Each of these moves is visible in the 2026 numbers.

In plain terms

Every few years the oil price jumps, lubricant brands look like they are in trouble, and then they come out the other side with their margins restored and sometimes higher. That happened in 2008–09 and again in 2021–22. The open question is whether 2026 is a fourth instance of the same story, or the year the story changed because a challenger grew while Castrol defended price.

Reading a lubricant company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Litres, rupees per litre and the spread between base oil and the shelf price explain almost everything. The trouble is that the market leader does not publish litres.

A lubricant company's results can be rebuilt from a handful of numbers. This section explains each one: what it is, how to calculate it, a worked example in rupees, what moves it, what good and bad look like, how Castrol and the reference peers compare, and the trap in how it is defined. It starts with the equation that ties them together.

The equation: litres times rupees per litre

Every lubricant company's revenue is the number of litres it sells multiplied by the average price it receives per litre, net of GST and trade rebates. Its EBITDA is its litres multiplied by the profit it keeps per litre after materials, advertising, the trade, freight, staff, royalty and overheads. Management controls price (with a lag), mix, advertising, network and overheads. It does not control the base-oil price, the rupee, the size of the vehicle parc or how fast electric scooters arrive.

In plain terms

Lubricant profit is "how many litres" times "how much you keep on each litre". The second number moves every time oil moves and the company has not yet changed its price list. The first number moves slowly, unless a rival is taking customers. Read every result by asking which of the two changed.

KPI 1: volume, and volume growth versus the market

What it is. The litres (or tonnes) of finished lubricant sold in the period. Formula. Reported directly, or derived as revenue ÷ realisation per litre. Worked example. Gulf sold 168,000 kilolitres in FY26 (+11%) against a market growing 3–4%, so it grew at roughly three times the market. What drives it. The parc, freight and industrial activity (the market), plus share gains or losses from distribution, pricing and OEM wins; minus drain-interval extension and EVs. Benchmarks. Good is more than twice market growth (above 7–8%); average one to two times (3.5–7%); weak below the market.

How the companies compare. Castrol does not publish litres. Its annual report gives CY25 plant production of 230+ million litres and volume growth of 8%; its presentations gave ">8%" for Q1 CY26 and ">5%" for Q2 CY26; it gave no number for Q3. Its guidance is volume growth of "at least 1.5x to 2x the market". Gulf publishes litres: 168 million in FY26, 48 million in Q1 FY27 (+17%), and +16% in Q2 FY27. Veedol reported "flat domestic volumes" in FY26. Savita sold 513,110 kilolitres or tonnes (+17%), mostly process oils.

The trap. Production is not sales (Castrol's "230+ million litres" is what its plants made; traded products add perhaps 4–5%). Tonnes are not litres (one tonne is about 1.13–1.15 kilolitres). State refiners' "lube sales" include base oil. And "volume growth ahead of the market" is a claim without a number until the litres are shown.

KPI 2: realisation per litre

What it is. Net revenue per litre sold, after GST, rebates and discounts. Formula. Revenue from operations ÷ litres sold. Worked example. Castrol CY25: ₹5,721.5 crore ÷ ~241 million litres ≈ ₹237 a litre (illustrative). What drives it. List-price changes, mix (synthetic versus mineral, car versus truck oil, retail versus bulk), pack size (a 1-litre bottle earns more per litre than a 210-litre barrel), and rebates. Benchmarks. For a branded B2C-led business, above ₹220 is good, ₹170–220 average, below ₹170 weak (indicative); a processor such as Savita sits near ₹85 because it sells bulk specialty oils.

How the companies compare. Castrol ~₹237 (CY25, illustrative); Gulf ~₹241 "all-in" per lubricant litre but ~₹195–210 for lubricants alone once AdBlue and batteries are removed; Savita ~₹85 per litre or kilogram. Revenue per litre rises when costs rise, so a higher realisation can mean pricing power or just pass-through. Compare it with materials per litre before calling it a win. And realisation is net of rebates: Castrol's CY25 revenue is after ₹679 crore of rebates, so a change in rebate policy moves "price" without moving the list.

The base-oil price and the rupee (with a one- to three-month inventory lag), the timing of price increases, mix, the Q4 rebate on global base-oil contracts, and inventory gains or losses. Benchmarks. KPI 4: EBITDA margin

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest, tax and other income, as a share of revenue. Formula. (Revenue − COGS − employees − other expenses) ÷ revenue. Worked example. Exceptional items and other income. Castrol's CY25 profit before tax is after a ₹22.5 crore labour-code charge; screener nets that into other income. Gulf includes AdBlue, batteries and EV chargers in its single "lubricants" segment. Compare like with like, and use several quarters.

KPI 7: royalty and brand fee

What it is. The payment to the owner of the brand and technology. Formula. Royalty ÷ revenue, and royalty ÷ profit before tax. Worked example. Castrol paid Castrol Ltd (UK) ₹132.49 crore in CY25: 2.3% of sales and about 10.2% of pre-exceptional profit before tax, about ₹5.5 a litre (illustrative). What drives it. The licence terms. A formula of 3.5% of turnover capped at 10% of profit before tax has been reported by secondary sources; it fits every year checked (CY19, CY20, CY24, CY25 all near 10% of PBT), but this report could not verify it in a primary document, so treat it as "reported". If true, the profit cap binds and the royalty behaves like a profit share. Benchmarks. Below 2% of sales good, 2–3.5% average, above 3.5% heavy.

How the companies compare. Castrol 2.3% of sales (~₹5.5/L); Gulf ₹43.80 crore to GOIL International, 1.1% of standalone sales (~₹2.6/L), not profit-capped; Savita nil (owns its brands); Veedol owns the Veedol brand outright but pays a ₹310.93 crore franchise fee (about 20% of standalone revenue) to its 50:50 ENEOS joint venture for ENEOS-branded products. That is a JV arrangement, partly recovered through Veedol's share of JV profit, not a royalty in the usual sense. The trap. A low royalty rate on sales can cost more than a capped one in a good year, and less in a bad one. And a fee routed through a joint venture does not appear as "royalty" at all.

KPI 12: mix, and the EV-resilient share

What it is. The share of volume or revenue from industrial, commercial-vehicle, tractor and OEM channels, as opposed to consumer two-wheeler and car oil. Formula. KPI 14: inventory days and the price-lag clock

What it is. How many days of cost of sales a company holds as raw material, work in progress and finished stock. In lubricants it sets how long a base-oil price change takes to reach the profit and loss account. Formula. Inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365. Worked example. Castrol's CY25 inventory days were about 68 on screener's basis. If base oil jumps on 1 June, stock bought at the old price keeps reaching the plants for about two months, so the gross margin holds (or widens, if prices were raised early) until about August, and then drops. That is what happened between Q2 CY26 (50.8% gross margin) and Q3 CY26 (36.5%). Castrol also hedges some base-oil and additive purchases one to three months forward, which stretches the lag.

What drives it. Import lead times (a Group III cargo from Korea or the Gulf takes weeks), the number of grades and pack sizes, the depot network (Castrol runs 14 depots and distribution centres; Veedol 32), and deliberate stock-building ahead of expected price increases. Benchmarks. For a branded blender, 45–70 days is normal; above 90 days the company is carrying either a price bet or slow-moving stock. A processor such as Savita, which holds base oil as a trading position, runs more and books gains or losses on it. Gulf says it keeps 30–45 days of base-oil cover and "we don't play on inventories".

The trap. A company with long inventory days looks brilliant in the first quarter of a price spike (it sells old, cheap stock at new prices) and poor in the second and third. Castrol's own CFO called part of Q2's margin a "one-time" benefit from lower-cost inventory. Reading Q2 CY26 at 26% as the new normal, or Q3 at 15% as the new normal, would both be wrong; the six-month average of about 21% is closer to the truth.

KPI 15: valuation on the right basis

What it is. The price the market puts on a lubricant franchise. Formulas. P/E = market value ÷ profit; EV/EBITDA = (market value + borrowings − cash) ÷ EBITDA; dividend yield = dividend per share ÷ share price. Worked example. Castrol on 30 October 2026: market value ₹18,596 crore; less net cash of about ₹1,078 crore gives an enterprise value of about ₹17,518 crore; divided by CY25 EBITDA of ₹1,348 crore that is 13.0×, or 12.1× trailing-twelve-month EBITDA of ₹1,453 crore. The P/E is 18.1× and the dividend yield 4.65%. At the open-offer price of ₹194.04 the enterprise value would be about 13.4× CY25 EBITDA.

How the companies compare. Gulf trades at 12.1× earnings and about 8.9× FY26 EBITDA on net cash; Veedol at 10.6× and about 10.0×; Savita at 10.8× and 16.7× FY26 EBITDA (its FY26 EBITDA was depressed and its Q1 FY27 inflated by inventory gains). Global Castrol changed hands at about 8.6× EBITDA. What drives it. The quality of the franchise (ROCE, margin stability), growth, the payout, and in Castrol's case the open-offer floor and the ownership change. Benchmarks. For a lubricant leader with 60% ROCE and a 90% payout, the market has paid 12–20× EBITDA over the last decade; challengers 8–12×.

The trap. Use the right cash figure (Castrol's investments are not cash; Gulf's cash is not on screener), the right year (Castrol's CY25 versus peers' FY26) and normalised margins (not Q2 CY26's 26% or Q3's 15%). For Castrol, also remember that the open offer is a time-limited floor for at most 26% of the shares, not for the stock indefinitely.

In plain terms

Lubricant stocks look cheap or dear depending on which year, which cash figure and which margin you plug in. Castrol at 13× EBITDA and Gulf at 9× are both "right" on net cash; Gulf at 11.6× is what you get if you forget its ₹1,300 crore of cash. A buyer paid 8.6× for the whole global Castrol business, which tells you what a sophisticated investor thought the brand was worth without India's growth.

Strip out the cash pile and a Castrol-type lubricant business earns several times its operating capital every year. That is why such businesses pay out nearly everything, why a buyer like Stonepeak finds them attractive, and why the only real threats are to the litres (EVs, a cheaper rival) and to the rupees per litre (base oil and the brand premium).

Definition traps, collected

  • Calendar year versus fiscal year. Castrol's "FY 2026" is January–December 2026; its "3Q" is July–September. Gulf, Veedol, Savita, the state refiners and PPAC use April–March.   • PPAC "lubricants and greases" is not the finished-lubricant market. It includes process, white and transformer oils and is nearly twice Kline's figure.
  • State refiners' lube volumes include base oil. Their tonnages cannot be divided by the finished market.
  • Production is not sales. Castrol's 230+ million litres was production; sales are an estimate of 235–245 million.
  • Percentage margin versus per-litre margin. In an inflation quarter, the percentage falls even if rupees per litre hold.
  • Revenue is net of rebates. Castrol's ₹679 crore of CY25 rebates never appear as a cost.
  • Investments are not cash. Castrol's ₹443 crore Ki Mobility stake is strategic. Real cash was ₹1,141 crore in December 2025.
  • Exceptional items and specials. CY25 profit is after a ₹22.5 crore labour-code charge; CY24's 149% payout included a ₹4.50 special dividend.

  • Royalty versus JV fee. Veedol's ₹311 crore ENEOS franchise fee is a joint-venture arrangement, not a brand royalty.

How to read a lubricant company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Check the calendar. Is this a calendar-year (Castrol) or April–March (everyone else) quarter? Line it up with the same calendar quarter for peers.
  2. Revenue growth, then volume growth. Find the volume number (Gulf gives it; Castrol gives words). Revenue growth minus volume growth ≈ price and mix. If volume is missing, note it as a red flag in a cost-shock quarter.
  3. Rebuild gross margin. Revenue minus materials consumed, purchases of traded goods and inventory change. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter (April–June is seasonally strongest).
  4. Look at the input price. Check PPAC's monthly base-oil import unit value for the three months before the quarter, and the rupee. A gross-margin fall that matches a base-oil spike is a lag; one that does not is competition.
  5. Compute per-litre figures where possible. Gross profit and EBITDA per litre, against last year. Rupees per litre held = pricing power intact.
  6. Read the cost lines below gross profit. Advertising, staff and other expenses. A sudden fall in "other expenses" can flatter EBITDA by cutting promotion.
  7. Strip out the one-offs. Exceptional items, inventory gains (Savita) or losses, rebate timing (Castrol's Q4), forex losses on import credit (Gulf).
  8. Check the balance sheet if published. Inventory and borrowings rising together means costlier stock being financed.
  9. Read the call for three things. Price actions taken and planned; what management says about market share; any change in guidance (Castrol's 21–24% frame, Gulf's 12–14% band).
  10. Red flags. Volume undisclosed while margins fall; revenue up much more than volume with gross profit down; rising receivables at distributors; payout cut; any change to royalty or related-party terms.
In plain terms

The ten-minute version: how many litres, how many rupees kept per litre, and why either changed. If a company tells you the second but not the first, ask why. If base oil moved, give the price list a quarter or two to catch up before judging. If the litres are falling while a rival's are rising, that is the real news.

Asset-light blending, supplier-funded stock and a brand that needs advertising rather than factories leave a branded lubricant company with little to do with its cash except return it.

Why so little capital is needed

Three features make the model cash-generative. Blending is cheap: Castrol's three plants run at 70–80% utilisation and need ₹100–150 crore a year of maintenance and growth capex, about 2% of revenue. Suppliers fund the stock: payables of about 98 days cover 68 days of inventory and 30 days of receivables, so working capital is around zero; growth does not absorb cash. The brand is expensed, not capitalised: the ₹479 crore of advertising and promotion runs through the profit and loss account each year, so there is no large intangible asset to build. Return on operating capital is therefore several hundred percent (section 12), and the reported 60% ROCE is held down mainly by the cash and the ₹443 crore Ki Mobility stake.

What the cash is not

Screener-style balance sheets show Castrol's "investments" of ₹377–443 crore, which are not cash. They are compulsorily convertible preference shares and equity in Ki Mobility Solutions, the restructured TVS aftermarket and servicing business, carried at fair value through other comprehensive income. The stake was marked down in CY25 (₹487.5 crore in December 2024 to ₹442.8 crore in December 2025). The real liquidity is ₹1,141 crore of cash and bank balances, against only lease liabilities. There is no debt.

The comparison

Gulf is cash-generative too but needs more capital to grow: its FY26 operating cash flow was ₹350 crore on EBITDA of ₹514 crore, free cash flow ₹299 crore, payout 73%, and it funds imports with foreign-currency buyer's credit while holding about ₹1,300 crore of cash. Veedol paid out 103% of FY26 profit. Savita retains most of its profit (19% payout) because its specialty business needs working capital and is more cyclical. The ranking of payout ratios matches the ranking of business quality almost exactly.

In plain terms

Castrol is a company that turns about ₹1,000 crore of cash a year into dividends. It needs very little of its own money to sell more oil, because the oil companies that supply it wait longer to be paid than Castrol's own customers do. For shareholders that means a dividend yield of around 5%. For a new owner it means a reliable stream of cash, which is exactly what an infrastructure-style investor buys.

The risks to the cash machine are the same as the risks to the business. A prolonged margin squeeze reduces the cash to distribute; the Q3 CY26 profit of ₹176 crore was 23% below a year earlier. A volume loss to rivals or EVs shrinks the base. A decision to invest heavily in new categories (thermal fluids, EV services, Ki Mobility) could lower the payout. And a change in the royalty, which would need minority approval, would move cash from all shareholders to one.

Melting ice cube or long tail? EVs and the engine-oil pool Deep dive

Electric two-wheelers were 11.6% of sales in September 2026. They are about 2% of the two-wheelers on the road. The lubricant question is how fast the second number catches up with the first.

An electric scooter has no engine oil. It needs a little gear oil or grease in the hub and a coolant or thermal fluid in some designs, perhaps 5–10% of the lubricant an engine scooter uses over its life (indicative). For an industry whose largest single pool is two-wheeler engine oil, electrification is the structural question. This section quantifies it segment by segment.

What it means for Castrol

Castrol does not disclose its two-wheeler share of volume. If two-wheeler oil is about 35% of its litres (an illustrative assumption consistent with "2W is the biggest volume pool"), and it holds share within that pool, the base case removes about 18% × 35% ≈ 6% of Castrol's volume by 2035, roughly 15 million litres at today's scale, or about 0.7 point a year of growth. In plain terms

One in nine new scooters and motorcycles sold in India is now electric, but only about one in forty on the road is. Trucks, tractors and factories, which use most of the oil, carry on. The company most exposed is the one with the biggest share of scooter and motorcycle oil, which is Castrol.

Two caveats cut in opposite directions. First, the 2026 jump in electric sales was helped by the fuel-price shock and by PM E-DRIVE money; if fuel prices ease and the subsidy ends in 2028, penetration could plateau, as it did in FY25–FY26. Second, longer drain intervals and synthetic oils are already reducing litres per engine two-wheeler, independent of EVs; the two effects together could make the two-wheeler pool peak earlier than the EV numbers alone suggest. Watch FADA's monthly penetration figures, and above all whether electric share holds above 10% after the subsidy window.

The global lens: what the world pays for a lubricant brand

India is one of the few lubricant markets still growing in litres, and its brand leader earns margins at the top of the global range.

Globally the finished-lubricant market is mature: volume is flat to declining in North America, Europe and Japan as engines become more efficient, drains lengthen and EVs spread. India, with about 10% of world demand and 21% of Asia-Pacific's (Mordor, cited by Castrol), is one of the few large markets where volume still grows 3–4% a year. That makes it strategically important to every global lubricant group, and explains why the 2025–26 wave of ownership changes touched India directly.

Who owns the global brands

The global industry has split into three kinds of owner. Oil majors (Shell, ExxonMobil, TotalEnergies, Chevron) run lubricants as a segment of a larger downstream business. Independent specialists such as Fuchs (Germany, industrial-led) and private Motul run lubricants as their core. And increasingly financial and national-oil owners: Saudi Aramco bought Valvoline's global products business in 2023 for about $2.65 billion (Valvoline Inc kept the US quick-lube retail business), and in December 2025 BP agreed to sell 65% of Castrol to Stonepeak. Both deals touched India: Valvoline Cummins is a 50:50 joint venture whose Valvoline side is now Aramco-owned, and Castrol India's controller is changing.

The valuation benchmark

Stonepeak's ~US$10.1 billion enterprise value is about 8.6 times global Castrol's last-twelve-month EBITDA, implying EBITDA of about $1.17 billion. Castrol India's CY25 EBITDA of ₹1,348 crore is about 12–13% of that (illustrative, depending on the exchange rate used). Castrol India trades at about 13 times EBITDA; the open offer at ₹194.04 implies about 13.4 times. India is valued richer than the global group because it grows and because its 60% ROCE and 24% margin sit at the top of the global range, where finished-lubricant businesses typically earn 12–20% EBITDA margins (indicative).

Technology and disruption beyond the electric scooter

Longer drains, OEM-genuine oils, digital loyalty, recycled base oil and data-centre fluids each shift value in the chain.

Electrification gets the headlines, but several quieter technology shifts are already changing lubricant economics. Some subtract litres, some move margin between players, and a few open new markets.

Longer drain intervals

Synthetic oils and BS-VI engines have stretched car oil-change intervals from about 5,000 km to 10,000–15,000 km, and industrial customers increasingly buy on total cost of ownership, which favours premium oils with longer service lives. The effect is fewer litres at higher prices: good for the brand that sells the upgrade, bad for the volume of mineral oil. It is the single largest reason automotive volume grows only 2–3% a year.

OEM-genuine oil and the service bay

As vehicles become more complex, more servicing happens at authorised workshops, where the OEM's genuine oil is the default. This moves margin from the blender to the OEM and turns blenders into contract suppliers. Gulf has leaned into it (OEM franchise-workshop business growing at high double digits, factory fill for 50+ OEMs, royalty paid to OEMs for co-branding); Castrol supplies 90+ OEMs and invests in its own service formats.

Digital loyalty and anti-counterfeiting

Mechanic-loyalty apps (Castrol's FastScan processes more than 1.5 lakh transactions a day; Gulf runs similar programmes) give brands data on who sells what, reduce leakage of spurious oil and let them target rewards. They strengthen the incumbent with the largest network. Counterfeit oil remains a risk named in Castrol's annual report.

Fuels and powertrains

E20 petrol, CNG, flex-fuel engines, hybrids and hydrogen combustion engines all still need engine oil, often of new specifications. Castrol markets oils for flex-fuel and hybrid vehicles and is experimenting with hydrogen-engine products. CNG is growing in cars and light commercial vehicles (see the gas distribution report). These shifts sustain lubricant demand and favour formulators with OEM approvals.

Re-refining

The used-oil EPR rules (5% re-refining obligation in 2024-25 rising to 40% by 2029-30) will create a growing supply of re-refined base oil. That could commoditise part of the base-oil market and reduce import dependence over time. Castrol has launched an engine oil made with re-refined base oil with an OEM and signed a used-oil collection agreement with Tata Motors' commercial-vehicle business.

New fluids: data centres and batteries

Immersion and direct-to-chip cooling fluids for data centres, battery thermal-management fluids and e-axle fluids are small but fast-growing markets. Castrol had two thermal fluids certified by NVIDIA around September 2026 and is running pilots; management says they are not yet material. Savita is developing ester-based fluids for data-centre immersion cooling, battery storage and EVs. Material revenue is unlikely before 2028 (indicative).

In plain terms

Technology is making each engine need less oil, moving the oil-change decision from the bazaar mechanic to the carmaker's workshop, and opening small new markets for specialised fluids. The brands that win are the ones with OEM approvals, the best mechanic networks and the chemistry for the new fluids. For a while that list still starts with Castrol.

Bull versus bear: a temporary squeeze or a structural turn?

The bull sees a cash-rich brand leader in its third base-oil squeeze since 2008, with an offer floor under the stock. The bear sees falling litres, a faster rival and an owner with debt to service.

The sub-sector's outlook turns on four questions: whether branded margins recover after the 2026 shock as they did after 2008 and 2022; whether the volume pause at the leader is temporary; how quickly EVs erode the two-wheeler pool; and what the ownership changes do to capital allocation.

The bull case

  • The cycle turns: base oil fell from ~$1,487 a tonne in July to ~$949 in August–September; Castrol's Q4 rebate and price increases already taken should restore margins toward the 21–24% frame, and history says branded margins overshoot on the way down.
  • A growing market: 3–4% volume and 6–8% value growth to 2033, a 328-million-vehicle parc growing 4% a year, rural motorisation and double-digit industrial growth.
  • Unmatched franchise: 1.67 lakh outlets, 1.8 lakh verified mechanics, first place in urban retail share, ~₹56 a litre of EBITDA and 60% ROCE.
  • Cash and payout: ₹1,141 crore of cash, no debt, ~₹1,000 crore of free cash flow and a 4.65% dividend yield; a PE-led parent wants the dividends to continue.
  • An offer floor: ₹194.04 rising at 10% a year (~₹214 at end-December 2026) for up to 26% of shares, against ₹188.
  • EV impact is slow: electric two-wheelers are ~2.3% of the parc; even a fast transition removes only ~0.5 point a year of market growth to 2035.

The bear case

  • The squeeze is not over: Brent was back at ~$114–115 on 26 October 2026; management says margins stay below 21–24% "in the short to medium term".
  • Share loss: Gulf grew volume 16–17% for two quarters while Castrol stopped disclosing litres; its Q3 gross profit fell 6.6% despite "healthy" volume claims.
  • EVs hit the richest pool first: e2W penetration doubled in a year to 11.6%; the two-wheeler oil pool may peak around 2030–2033.
  • Owner risk: a new PE-led parent could press for a higher brand fee or shared-services charges; three MDs and three CFOs in about 15 months adds execution risk.
  • Conditional offer: the open offer depends on clearances in ~24 jurisdictions; if the deal fails, there is no offer, and acceptance above 26% is pro-rated.

The ownership change is mostly protective in the near term (the offer, and minority votes on related-party changes) and uncertain in the long term. A reasonable reading is that the 2026 shock tests Castrol's share more than its margins, and that the Q4 CY26 results and any return to volume disclosure will tell which case is winning.

In plain terms

If oil prices settle and Castrol shows its litres are still growing, 2026 will look like a normal squeeze in a great business. If oil stays high and Gulf keeps growing four times faster than the market, 2026 will look like the year the leader started to lose ground. The open offer protects part of the downside for a few months; it does not answer either question.
