00

The one-page view: four chains, two engines, one customer

Every member sells "value" to the same household. Underneath, two very different machines are at work, and the market has just repriced both.

This sub-sector holds four listed companies: Avenue Supermarts (DMart), Vishal Mega Mart (VMM), V2 Retail and V-Mart Retail. Together they booked ₹88,583 crore of revenue in FY26, up 17.8%, earned ₹8,093 crore of operating profit and ₹4,095 crore of reported net profit, and ran 2,402 stores at the end of September 2026. On 23 October 2026 the market valued them at ₹2.59 lakh crore, about 63 times FY26 profit.

Those totals hide the most important fact about the group: it is really two businesses. DMart is a grocery-led, owner-occupied hypermarket. Foods and household consumables are 77% of its sales; it owns most of the land and buildings it trades from; it pays suppliers in about a week; and it makes money by turning stock 12–13 times a year on a 14–15% gross margin. DMart alone is 77.7% of the group's revenue, 72.5% of its profit and 79.5% of its market value. Any "sector average" is, for practical purposes, a DMart number.

The other three are leased, apparel-led value chains. Vishal Mega Mart is a "one-roof" store selling clothes (44% of H1 FY27 sales), general merchandise (28%) and groceries (27%), three-quarters of it under its own brands. V2 Retail and V-Mart are small-town fashion department stores: family clothing, footwear and a little home and grocery, in towns that often have no mall. These chains earn a 28–35% gross margin because they keep the brand owner's share, but they pay 5–7% of revenue in rent and carry three to six months of stock. In FY26 they grew revenue 24.6% to ₹19,762 crore and profit 50% to ₹1,125 crore.

Five forces define the sub-sector in late 2026, and the rest of this report is organised around them.

  • The march into small-town India. Redseer, cited in VMM's FY26 annual report, puts organised retail at ₹14.9 lakh crore in calendar 2023, heading to ₹36–38 lakh crore by 2028, with organised share rising from roughly 19% to roughly 30% (derived, indicative). Tier II and III towns are where that growth is fastest, and where all four chains are opening stores.
  • Store count is the engine; the store model sets the returns. Group stores rose 34% between March 2025 and September 2026. Whether a chain owns (DMart) or rents (the rest) decides its margins, its capital needs and how fast it can expand.
  • Quick commerce is now DMart-sized. Blinkit's net order value in Q2 FY27 was ₹20,940 crore, more than DMart's ₹19,644 crore of quarterly revenue. DMart's own online arm, DMart Ready, has shrunk from 19 cities to 11.
  • Policy and the calendar. GST 2.0 (from 22 September 2025) cut tax on almost every product these chains sell. Then Navratri moved from 22 September in 2025 to 11 October in 2026, pushing festival shopping out of Q2 FY27 and into Q3. Every Q2 and Q3 FY27 number has to be read through that shift.
  • The balance sheet and the shareholder register are turning. DMart has started to borrow (₹2,629 crore of standalone borrowings at September 2026, from ₹965 crore in March). V2 needed a ₹400 crore QIP to fund its stock. And at VMM, private-equity owners have sold down from 76% to 40% in under two years.

Why the stocks fell anyway. In October 2026 the market cut multiples hard. DMart closed at ₹3,158 on 23 October, about 17% below its 1 October level of ₹3,813 (a press-reported figure) and far below its 52-week high of ₹4,644; its market value fell to ₹2,05,980 crore, at 66 times trailing earnings. In plain terms

DMart is a giant, low-margin grocery machine that owns its buildings and wins by being cheaper and faster at turning stock than anyone else. The other three rent their shops and sell mostly their own clothes and household goods at much fatter margins, which lets them open stores quickly in small towns. All four are growing sales at double-digit rates. What has changed in 2026 is that DMart now faces a new rival for the grocery basket (ten-minute delivery apps), DMart has started borrowing to fund its stores, and investors have become much less willing to pay 60–70 times profit for any of them.

01

How a value retailer earns: a spread, turned fast

Retail profit is the gap between buying and selling price, multiplied by how many times a year the shelf is emptied and refilled.

A retailer adds value in two places. The first is buying: getting goods cheaper than a rival, through scale, payment terms or by designing and sourcing its own products. The second is turning: selling that stock quickly from a store whose rent, staff and electricity cost as little per rupee of sales as possible. Everything else, from store format to location strategy to the choice between owning and renting, is a way of improving one of these two.

The value chain differs by what is on the shelf. In groceries and FMCG (77% of DMart's sales), the brand owner keeps most of the margin. Hindustan Unilever earns a gross margin of roughly 50% on a bar of soap; the retailer selling it earns a thin slice. DMart's whole-store gross margin has sat in a 14–15% band for seven or eight years. DMart improves its share of the pie by paying suppliers in about seven days (its days-payable figure is 6.8–7.2) against a trade norm of 30–60 days. In return it gets cash discounts and better terms, which it passes to shoppers as lower prices. That is the "everyday low cost, everyday low price" model.

In private-label apparel and general merchandise (most of VMM, V-Mart and V2), the retailer is the brand. It designs in-house, places orders with contract manufacturers and job workers (largely domestic: Tiruppur, Ludhiana, Delhi NCR, Kolkata and Surat for fabrics), moves the goods through a central distribution centre and sells them under names the customer often does not realise are store brands. The retailer keeps the margin that a brand owner would otherwise earn, which is why VMM's gross margin (28.0–28.3%) is about double DMart's, and V-Mart's (34.5–35.3%) is higher still.

Two routes to the same margin

The striking result is that both models end up in the same place once rent is counted. DMart earns roughly ₹14.5 of gross margin on every ₹100 of sales, spends about ₹4 on staff, under ₹0.5 on rent and about ₹2.5 on power, logistics and other costs, and is left with an EBITDA of about ₹7.5 after rent. VMM earns about ₹28 of gross margin, but spends ₹6 on staff, ₹5 on rent and ₹8 on everything else, ending at about ₹9 in H1 FY27. V-Mart earns ₹34.5 but spends more on staff (₹10) and rent (₹7), and ends near ₹7.6 in Q1 FY27. On a rent-paid basis all four sit in a 7.5–9% EBITDA band; the leased chains need double the gross margin to get there.

Turns: the second half of the return

Margin alone does not make a return; the money tied up to earn it matters as much. DMart turned its inventory 12.8 times in FY26 (about 33 days of stock), and because it pays suppliers in a week, most of that stock is funded by its own equity. Its fixed-asset turnover, including leased stores' right-of-use assets, was 3.2 times. A 7.5–8% margin turned that fast produced a return on capital employed (ROCE) of 17.1% in FY26. The apparel chains hold much more stock relative to sales: VMM about 79 days of cost of goods at March 2026, V-Mart 86 days of sales on its own measure, V2 a net working-capital cycle of 74 days in Q1 FY27 after a year in which inventory doubled. Fashion stock has to be bought before the season and sold before it goes out of style, so the leased chains need higher margins to earn similar returns. Their ROCE (VMM 15.2%, V2 19.3%, V-Mart 13.2% in FY26, screener basis, which includes lease assets in capital) is in the same range as DMart's.

In plain terms

A grocery hypermarket earns a little on each item but sells an enormous amount from each square foot and turns its shelves every month. A value-fashion store earns much more on each shirt but sells less per square foot, pays rent and holds stock for a season. After rent, both keep about seven to nine rupees from every hundred rupees of sales. The difference shows up in how much capital each needs, how fast each can grow, and how exposed each is to the weather, the festival calendar and fashion risk.

The platform layer. A fifth participant has entered the chain: quick-commerce platforms such as Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart and Zepto. They monetise convenience through delivery and platform fees, advertising and, increasingly, their own inventory margin. Blinkit earned gross profit equal to 27.5% of its order value in Q2 FY27 and an adjusted EBITDA of 0.9%. It competes for exactly the FMCG basket that drives DMart's footfall, which is why section 07 treats it as a defining force rather than a side note. Their model is covered in depth in our e-retail report.

02

The march into small-town India

Organised retail is about a fifth of a ₹75–81 lakh crore market and growing at twice the pace of the whole. The growth is in towns most investors have never visited.

India's retail market is one of the largest in the world and still mostly unorganised: the kirana grocer, the local cloth merchant, the weekly haat. Redseer's March 2025 estimates, quoted in Vishal Mega Mart's FY26 annual report, give the cleanest recent sizing. Total Indian retail reaches ₹116–124 lakh crore by calendar 2028, growing at about 9% a year from 2023. Working backwards, the 2023 market was roughly ₹75–81 lakh crore (derived, indicative). Organised retail was ₹14.9 lakh crore in 2023 and is projected at ₹36–38 lakh crore by 2028, about 20% a year. That implies an organised share of roughly 19% in 2023 rising to roughly 30% by 2028. Both shares are our derivation from Redseer's ranges, not figures Redseer publishes, so treat them as indicative.

A second Redseer figure is often misquoted. The "₹104–112 lakh crore by 2028" that appeared in some IPO-note summaries is Redseer's "aspirational retail" addressable market (₹68–72 lakh crore in 2023), not organised retail and not the whole market. Using it as organised retail overstates the organised opportunity roughly threefold.

What gets bought

Redseer's category split explains why the leased chains chase general merchandise and apparel even though groceries are the larger pool. Staples and FMCG were ₹28 lakh crore in 2023 and are projected at ₹42 lakh crore by 2028 (9% a year). Apparel grows from ₹6 lakh crore to ₹10 lakh crore (10%), and general merchandise, from kitchenware to plastics to small appliances, from ₹3 lakh crore to ₹6 lakh crore (14%). Grocery is the traffic driver: people shop for it weekly or monthly. Apparel and general merchandise are where the margin is. VMM's one-roof format is designed to capture both: groceries bring the customer in, own-brand clothes and homeware make the profit.

Why Tier II–IV towns

Redseer expects organised retail in Tier II and III cities to grow about 32% a year through 2028, faster than in the metros, and notes that even Tier I is still 50–55% unorganised. India's middle-income segment is projected at 246 million households by 2028, 21 million more than today. The listed chains' footprints show where they are betting. VMM had 443 of its 844 stores in Tier III towns (populations under 2 lakh, 2011 census) at September 2026, against 204 in Tier I and 197 in Tier II; it trades in 582 cities across 28 states and 2 union territories. V-Mart had 376 of 591 stores in Tier III and IV towns at June 2026, with Uttar Pradesh (183 stores) and Bihar (82) its biggest states, and its Tier 4 stores grew sales per square foot 15% in Q1 FY27, the fastest of any tier. V2 says it serves "Tier 2 and Tier 3 consumption markets" in more than 300 cities across 25 states and a union territory. DMart is the exception: it remains concentrated in western and southern India (Maharashtra 132 stores, Gujarat 77) and in larger towns, and entered five new states only in FY26.

Competition is moving to the same places. Trent says more than 80% of the new Zudio stores it opened in Q1 FY27 were in Tier II and III towns, and Reliance Retail runs 20,169 stores across formats. A small-town customer who had a choice between a local merchant and nothing five years ago may now have V-Mart, V2, Zudio and Reliance Trends within a kilometre of each other. That is the arms race section 17 examines.

In plain terms

Roughly four out of five rupees spent in Indian shops still go to unorganised sellers. Industry forecasts expect organised chains to take about three of every ten rupees by 2028, with the fastest gains in smaller towns. That runway is the core of every bull case here. The catch is that every large retailer has read the same forecast, so the small towns are filling up with chains at the same time.

From FY22 to FY26 the group's revenue grew from ₹38,860 crore to ₹88,583 crore, a compound rate of 22.9% a year. The demand drivers to track. Four macro variables move these companies' same-store sales. Income tax: the February 2025 Budget made income up to ₹12 lakh a year tax-free under the new regime, which helps the salaried urban customer of DMart and VMM more than V2's or V-Mart's rural and semi-urban customer. Prices: food inflation lifts DMart's average bill but squeezes the discretionary wallet of the value-fashion shopper; September 2026 CPI food inflation was 7.74%, with rural food at 8.06% (snippet-sourced, indicative). The monsoon: 2026 rainfall was 87% of the long-period average, with the South Peninsula at 76% and the North-East at 74% (snippet-sourced), which points to weaker kharif farm income in the regions served by V-Mart's Unlimited stores (south) and by VMM's and V-Mart's eastern networks. And interest rates: the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which raises EMIs for the urban middle class. Section 22 sets thresholds for each.

03

The store is the engine: space, ramp and the J-curve

New stores now supply most of the growth. How fast they mature decides whether that growth is profitable.

A retailer's revenue can grow in only two ways: sell more from existing stores (same-store growth) or open new ones. In FY26 the group got most of its growth from new space. Group stores rose from 1,797 at March 2025 to 2,197 at March 2026 (+22%) and to 2,402 at September 2026, a 34% increase in 18 months. Same-store growth over the same period ran at 5–11%: DMart's like-for-like growth was 8.1% for FY26, VMM's same-store growth about 11% on an adjusted basis, V2's about 8.6%. Space added roughly twice as much growth as productivity.

The J-curve

A new store does not trade at the level of a mature one. Shoppers take time to change habits, the assortment needs tuning to local taste, and in fashion the store needs a full season to learn what sells. V2's management gives the clearest cohort data in the sector: stores older than two years sell about ₹1,070–1,100 per square foot per month; the new-store cohort about ₹730–740, roughly 34% less; the best stores ₹1,600–1,700; and about 10–12 laggards under ₹600. V-Mart says its stores take three to four years to mature. When a chain doubles its base, the blended productivity number falls even if every old store is doing well, because the average now includes many young stores.

In plain terms

When a chain opens many stores at once, its average store looks worse for a year or two because the new ones are still finding customers. That is normal. The warning sign is different: old stores whose sales stop growing, or new stores that never reach the level of the old ones. Analysts therefore watch same-store growth and cohort productivity, not just the headline store count.

Revenue per store, and why the arithmetic misleads

Dividing FY26 revenue by the March 2026 store count gives DMart about ₹138 crore per store, VMM ₹16.2 crore, V2 ₹9.4 crore and V-Mart ₹6.6 crore. These figures understate fast growers, because a store opened in February contributed only a few weeks of sales to the year. A better approach divides revenue by the average of opening and closing stores: DMart's ₹66,968 crore of standalone revenue over about 480 average stores gives ₹139.5 crore, and VMM's ₹12,906 crore over about 745 gives ₹17.3 crore. Even so, the gap is enormous: one DMart sells as much as eight VMMs, fifteen V2s or twenty V-Marts. That difference in throughput is why DMart can run on a 14–15% gross margin and the others cannot.

Where ₹100 of revenue goes: goods, people, rent and power

Merchandise is two-thirds to six-sevenths of every rupee. The rest is a short list of costs, and in 2026 the fastest-rising one is labour.

A value retailer's cost structure is simple and almost entirely variable with space and sales. The single largest line is the cost of the goods sold: about ₹85.5 of every ₹100 at DMart, ₹71.7 at VMM, about ₹70 at V2 and ₹65.5 at V-Mart. What is left after that, the gross margin, pays for everything else: people, rent, power, logistics, marketing and head office. The order of the companies on this line is the order of their own-brand share: the more of the shelf a chain designs and sources itself, the less it pays for its goods.

Merchandise: two very different input markets

For groceries and FMCG, the input price is the brand owner's list price less trade terms, and the retailer's lever is scale and speed of payment. When the government cut GST on soaps, shampoos, packaged foods, ghee and butter to 5% from 22 September 2025, brand owners cut their maximum retail prices. That lowers nominal revenue per unit for a grocer, even if volumes rise, which is one reason DMart's like-for-like growth was soft in some FY26 quarters. Food prices matter too: DMart blamed weak Q3 FY26 growth partly on staples deflation.

For apparel, the input is cotton, polyester and other fibres, plus conversion. This is where 2026 hurt. The West Asia crisis from 28 February 2026 drove up crude oil, freight and LPG prices; India had four pump-price increases in May. V-Mart's management said about 80% of its purchases are crude-linked (polyester and synthetics) and that raw-material inflation was running near 10%. Its response is a mix of vendor efficiency, changes to yarn blends and partial pass-through, accepting a 0.5–0.75 percentage-point gross-margin sacrifice, with average selling price increases capped at 3–5% because, as management recalled, FY23 price rises drove customers away. V2 said garment input costs were up 4–5% and that it would raise prices 4–5% in Q3 FY27. VMM's chief executive cited "renewed inflationary pressures across key raw material categories" in the Q2 FY27 release.

Rent: from almost nothing to 7% of sales

Rent is the biggest structural difference between the models. DMart owns most of its stores but now leases 68 of about 500, including 15 of its 85 FY26 openings. Its standalone cash outflow on leases was ₹252 crore in FY26 plus ₹13 crore of short-term rent, 0.40% of revenue. VMM paid ₹689 crore (5.3% of revenue) including short-term rent, across 911 leased sites. V2 paid about ₹178 crore (5.8%) on 347 store leases. V-Mart paid ₹278 crore (7.3%). These are cash figures from the companies' FY26 lease notes, the right basis for comparison because Ind AS 116 hides rent below EBITDA in the income statement.

People: the new pressure point

Labour is the cost line that moved most in 2026. In plain terms

Out of every ₹100 a shopper spends, a grocery chain passes about ₹85 to the companies that made the goods; a private-label fashion chain passes about ₹66–72. The difference pays for rent, more staff per sale and more stock. In 2026, wages rose because of new labour rules and higher state minimum wages, and polyester-heavy clothing got dearer because of the oil shock. The chains can raise prices only a little without losing their price-sensitive customers, so they need same-store sales to keep growing to protect their margins.

Capital per store. The supply side also includes the capital a store consumes. DMart's FY26 capex was "upwards of ₹4,000 crore" for 85 stores plus a land bank, roughly ₹45–50 crore per store including land (indicative). A leased VMM store needs perhaps ₹3–5 crore of fit-out (indicative). V2 says capex per store has risen to ₹1.2–1.22 crore, but each store also needs about two months of stock before it opens; with 50–55 openings a quarter, ₹100–150 crore of pre-opening inventory sits on the balance sheet at any time. For a fashion chain, inventory is the larger part of the capital bill. The KPI masterclass works through each model's payback.

Reliance, DMart and the long tail

One conglomerate, one founder-run grocer and a handful of chains dominate organised value retail. Beyond them, the market is still fragmented and mostly unlisted.

The largest organised retailer in India is not in this sub-sector. Reliance Retail, an unlisted subsidiary of Reliance Industries, ran 20,169 stores on 78.4 million sq ft at June 2026 and booked gross revenue of ₹90,408 crore in Q1 FY27 alone (up 12%), with EBITDA of ₹6,309 crore (7.9%). Three ways to run a value store

Owned grocery hypermarket, leased one-roof store, leased small-town fashion store. Same customer, different economics, different risks.

The four listed members fall into three business-model archetypes, and a fourth model, quick commerce, competes with all of them. Each archetype makes a different trade-off between margin, turns, capital and speed of expansion.

A. The owned, grocery-led EDLC/EDLP hypermarket (DMart)

DMart buys land, builds large stores (about 41,000 sq ft on average), clusters them so that distribution is cheap, pays suppliers in a week and sells a wide grocery and household range at prices it keeps low every day. Its gross margin is the lowest in the group (14–15%), its rent almost nil, its EBITDA after rent about 7.5%, and its inventory about 33–36 days. Its returns come from throughput: ₹33,422 of revenue per sq ft in FY26, more than three times any other member. Capital per store is high (land plus building, indicatively ₹45–50 crore), so expansion is slow and capex-heavy; free cash flow was negative in FY25 (−₹954 crore) and FY26 (−₹646 crore). The upside is that the land stays on the balance sheet, and the store cannot be evicted or re-rented at a higher price.

B. The leased one-roof, private-label-led store (VMM)

VMM rents mid-sized stores (about 16,700 sq ft) in Tier II and III towns and fills them with clothing (100% own brands), general merchandise (about 75% own brands) and groceries (about 60% own brands by volume). It earns a 28% gross margin, pays about 5% of sales in rent, and makes an operating EBITDA (after rent and before employee stock-option costs) of 10.2% in FY26. Its inventory is about 79–80 days, partly offset by supplier credit and some consignment stock, so its net working capital is modest. Capex is light (fit-out only) and it generated ₹1,299 crore of free cash flow in FY26. It can open 80–100 large stores a year without raising money.

C. The leased small-town value-fashion store (V-Mart, V2)

V-Mart and V2 run smaller stores (8,600 and 10,800 sq ft on average) in Tier II–IV towns, selling mostly family apparel. Gross margins are 29–35%, rent 6–7% of sales, rent-paid EBITDA 6–9%. Fashion stock is the main capital need, and profit is highly seasonal: V-Mart made a net loss in Q2 FY26 and earned 71% of its FY26 profit in Q3. Capex per store is low (V2 says ₹1.2 crore), so these chains can add 100–200 stores a year, but every store must be stocked before it opens, and markdowns on unsold stock are the main risk.

In plain terms

There is no single "best" model. Owning stores gives DMart the lowest costs and permanent sites, but it is slow and expensive to expand. Renting lets VMM, V2 and V-Mart open stores quickly and cheaply, but they pay rent forever and carry more fashion risk. Over a full cycle, all four have ended up earning similar returns on the money they use, between 13% and 19% in FY26. What separates them is how reliably they earn it and how fast they can grow.

The fourth model. Quick commerce uses small "dark stores" of 2,500–4,000 sq ft that serve deliveries only, within 10–30 minutes. Blinkit earns gross profit of about 27.5% of order value (it now holds much of its own inventory) and made adjusted EBITDA of 0.9% of order value in Q2 FY27; Instamart lost money. These businesses compete for the high-frequency top-up basket and the impulse buy, and their capital is cheaper and faster to deploy than a hypermarket's. How much of DMart's basket they can take is the subject of the next section.

Ten minutes vs ten rupees: quick commerce and the DMart model Deep dive

Blinkit now processes more order value in a quarter than DMart sells. DMart's answer has been to defend price and retreat online. The evidence on who is winning is mixed.

DMart's proposition is simple: drive to a large store once or twice a month and buy the household's groceries for less than anywhere else. Management says that on an average bill of about ₹1,600, the saving is "significantly more than 10%". Quick commerce offers the opposite trade: pay a little more, or accept a narrower range, and get the goods in ten minutes without leaving home. The question for DMart investors is how much of the grocery basket shoppers will move from "cheapest" to "fastest".

The scale of the challenge

The numbers have grown faster than almost anyone expected. Blinkit's net order value (NOV) was ₹11,679 crore in Q2 FY26 and ₹20,940 crore in Q2 FY27, up 79%. DMart's consolidated revenue in the same quarter was ₹19,644 crore. For the first time, Blinkit's quarterly order value exceeded DMart's quarterly revenue. Blinkit ran 2,751 dark stores at September 2026 (from 1,816 a year earlier) and guides to more than 3,500 by March 2027 and about 5,000 by March 2028. It served 37 million monthly transacting customers in Q2 FY27, up from 20.8 million a year earlier. Swiggy Instamart's gross order value was ₹9,230 crore from 1,240 dark stores. Zepto reported FY26 operating revenue of ₹22,624 crore and a loss of ₹5,905 crore from 1,139 dark stores at March 2026 (press-sourced, indicative).

In plain terms

Ten-minute delivery apps now handle more grocery orders by value each quarter than DMart sells in its stores. DMart's customers have not left: more of them are visiting than a year ago, but each visit is a little smaller. DMart has decided it cannot win the speed race and is concentrating on being cheapest for the big monthly shop, while shrinking its own online grocery service to 11 large cities. Its management now openly expects slower same-store growth for the next few years.

Why the value chains are less exposed

VMM, V-Mart and V2 face less direct pressure, for three reasons. Their baskets are mostly own-brand apparel and general merchandise, which quick-commerce platforms do not stock. Their towns are smaller: quick commerce is spreading beyond the metros (Blinkit says small-city baskets are already 85–90% of metro size), but it is still concentrated in larger cities. And they are using their stores as delivery hubs themselves. VMM delivers from 781 stores in 532 cities and has 1.52 crore registered app users (up 41%); hyperlocal is 2–9% of store revenue, its average order is about ₹800, and 20% of its quick-commerce customers had never shopped in a Vishal store. V2 soft-launched V2Kart in Delhi NCR and Lucknow in September 2026, fulfilled from stores. Using an existing store as a dark store costs almost no extra capital.

What would settle the argument

The bear case for DMart would be proven by like-for-like growth below 6% for three or more consecutive quarters, together with gross-margin cuts to defend price, a falling share of general merchandise, or bill growth turning negative. The bull case would be supported by like-for-like growth holding at 8–10%, quick-commerce platforms continuing to raise fees and cut discounts as they chase profit (Instamart still lost ₹895 crore at the adjusted EBITDA level in Q2 FY27, with a contribution margin of −1.0% of order value; it has not broken even), and DMart Ready narrowing its loss in its 11 cities. As of Q2 FY27 the evidence leans toward erosion at the margin, not collapse: DMart is growing revenue at 17.8%, but its multiple has fallen as investors price slower like-for-like growth for longer.

In plain terms

The test for DMart is not whether quick commerce grows (it will) but whether DMart's same-store sales stay above about 6% and its margins hold. So far they have, just. If same-store growth stays at 5–6% for several quarters and DMart has to cut prices to keep shoppers, the market's fears will be confirmed. For the other three chains, quick commerce is more an opportunity than a threat, because they can deliver from their own stores.

Owner vs tenant: real estate, Ind AS 116 and the balance-sheet turn Deep dive

DMart's owned stores were its moat and its "fortress" balance sheet. In 2026 it started to lease more and to borrow. Meanwhile one tenant needed fresh equity to fund its stock.

The deepest structural fork in this sub-sector is whether a retailer owns its stores or rents them. DMart has historically bought land and built its own stores; the other three rent. The choice runs through every line of the accounts: margins, depreciation, interest, capital employed, cash flow and, since 2019, the way profit is reported.

What owning buys

An owner pays no rent, so its store-level costs are lower and it cannot be evicted, re-priced at lease renewal or squeezed by a landlord when a store succeeds. The land usually appreciates, so a mature owned store carries a hidden asset. And an owner can design the building around its own operations: DMart's stores are large, plain and built for fast replenishment. The cost is capital. DMart's fixed assets rose from ₹16,206 crore (FY25) to ₹20,090 crore (FY26) and ₹21,588 crore at September 2026, plus ₹1,624 crore of work in progress. FY26 capex was "upwards of ₹4,000 crore" and outstanding capital commitments were ₹5,239 crore. Land-to-store takes two to three years, so owners cannot respond quickly to opportunities.

DMart's balance sheet has changed character

For most of its listed life DMart funded growth from its own cash flow and the money it raised at its 2017 IPO and 2020 QIP. That changed in FY25–FY26. Record openings pushed free cash flow negative (−₹954 crore in FY25, −₹646 crore in FY26), and the company began to borrow. Its standalone borrowings (excluding leases) were about ₹965 crore at March 2026, mostly commercial paper, and ₹2,629 crore at September 2026. Management told analysts in July that borrowing could reach about ₹2,000 crore by the end of FY27, all for capex; it passed that within six months. A ₹1,000 crore NCD programme has been approved to lock in longer tenor. At the same time DMart has become "more open" to long leases where clean land is not available: 68 of its roughly 500 stores are now leased, and standalone lease liabilities doubled from ₹693 crore (FY25) to ₹1,302 crore (March 2026) and ₹1,451 crore (September 2026).

How Ind AS 116 hides rent

Since FY20 Indian companies have reported leases under Ind AS 116. A long lease is recorded as a "right-of-use" asset and a matching lease liability. The rent is no longer an operating expense; instead the company books depreciation on the asset and interest on the liability, both below EBITDA. For a tenant, reported EBITDA therefore excludes rent entirely, and screener's "borrowings" include the lease liability. For an owner, EBITDA is almost unchanged, because there is little rent to move. The result: VMM reported an FY26 EBITDA margin of 14.9% and V2 15.4% (screener), against DMart's 7.5%. On those numbers the tenants look twice as profitable. To compare like with like, subtract the rent the tenant actually paid in cash.

Owner vs tenant: a worked comparison

A simple illustration shows why both models can work. Take a DMart-sized store of 41,000 sq ft selling about ₹140 crore a year. Owned, it might cost ₹45–50 crore (land ₹20–25 crore, building and fit-out about ₹25 crore) plus about ₹11 crore of inventory, and earn store EBITDA of about ₹11 crore: a pre-tax cash return of roughly 19–21% and a payback of about 4.5–5 years, with the land kept. Leased, the same store would pay perhaps ₹6–8 crore of rent (4.5–5.5% of sales), earn ₹3–5 crore, and need only about ₹15 crore of capital: a similar or higher return on capital, but a far thinner margin and no asset at the end. All figures here are ILLUSTRATIVE. Owning wins when land is cheap relative to rent and the company has capital to spare; leasing wins when speed matters more. DMart's 2026 shift toward leases and debt says that, at its current pace, capital has become the constraint.

What a chain sells, and whose name is on it, decides its gross margin more than any other choice.

The four members sell very different baskets. Why mix matters beyond margin

Mix also decides seasonality and sensitivity. Groceries are bought every week whatever the weather or festival calendar: DMart's quarterly profit is almost flat through the year (22–29% of the annual total in each quarter of FY26). Apparel is bought around festivals, weddings and winter, so the apparel chains earn most of their profit in Q3 and can lose money in Q2. Apparel is discretionary, so food inflation squeezes it; groceries are not, and food inflation actually raises DMart's average bill. And apparel is exposed to fashion risk: a design that does not sell must be marked down, which is why the shrinkage and aged-stock provisions in section 04 matter.

The DMart mix has been stable. Its general merchandise and apparel share edged up from 23.34% to 23.62% between H1 FY26 and H1 FY27, and management guides to a 22–23% "range" without plans to push it back to historical levels. Higher-margin general merchandise helps DMart's gross margin, but a shopper must walk through the store to buy it, which is one reason quick commerce's capture of the top-up trip worries investors.

In plain terms

A chain that sells its own brand of shirt keeps the profit a clothing company would otherwise make. Vishal sells its own brands for three-quarters of everything it sells, which is how it earns twice DMart's margin while still selling groceries. DMart sells mostly famous brands of soap, oil and biscuits, which earn little but bring shoppers back every week. The trade-off is that clothes are bought mainly at festival time, so the fashion chains' profits are lumpy, while DMart's are steady.

Where the apparel comes from. Private-label apparel links this sub-sector to India's garment makers. The chains buy from domestic contract manufacturers and job workers in Tiruppur, Ludhiana, Delhi NCR and Kolkata, and source fabric from Surat. V-Mart works with 600-plus vendors and handles 190 million-plus pieces a year through two distribution centres (an owned six-lakh-sq-ft facility at Palwal and Bengaluru). Rising synthetic-fibre costs in 2026 flowed straight into these chains' purchase costs. Our garments report covers the manufacturers, and the personal care and packaged foods reports cover the brand owners whose margins DMart's buying power presses on.

Rules of the game: GST 2.0, tax, labour and ownership

Value retailers sell almost entirely below ₹2,500 a piece. In September 2025 that price band got the biggest tax cut in a decade.

Policy has rarely helped value retail as directly as it did in 2025. Two changes stand out, plus several slower-moving rules that shape costs and ownership.

GST 2.0 (from 22 September 2025)

The GST Council's 56th meeting collapsed most goods into two main rates, 5% and 18%, with 40% for sin and luxury goods. For apparel and made-ups, the 5% threshold was raised from ₹1,000 a piece to ₹2,500: clothing priced between ₹1,000 and ₹2,500 moved from 12% to 5%, while clothing above ₹2,500 moved from 12% to 18%. Footwear up to ₹2,500 a pair is at 5%. Synthetic fibre and yarn went to 5%. In FMCG, soap bars, shampoo, hair oil, toothpaste and toothbrushes went from 18% or 12% to 5%; most packaged foods (namkeen, sauces, pasta, noodles, chocolate, coffee, butter, ghee) to 5%; and UHT milk, paneer and Indian breads to nil. Televisions and air-conditioners went from 28% to 18%.

Income tax (Budget 2025-26)

The February 2025 Budget raised the rebate so that income up to ₹12 lakh a year pays no tax under the new regime (₹12.75 lakh for salaried taxpayers after the standard deduction), forgoing roughly ₹1 lakh crore of revenue. Combined with GST 2.0, it gave the second half of FY26 a consumption tailwind that helped all four members open stores at record pace.

Labour rules and minimum wages

The new wage code took effect from December 2025, and several states raised minimum wages in 2026 (Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka, the last under a court stay). For a sector that employs tens of thousands of store staff at entry-level wages, these rules raise costs structurally. DMart's employee cost rose about 33% in FY26; VMM's employee cost per square foot rose 13% in Q1 FY27. The chains can offset some of this with productivity (automation, RFID stock counts) but not all.

Foreign investment, listing rules and accounting

Foreign direct investment is allowed up to 100% in single-brand retail and 51% in multi-brand retail, with conditions; foreign-owned e-commerce firms can only run marketplaces. VMM has capped foreign ownership at 49.99% so that it remains "Indian-owned and controlled", which limits how much of its private-equity owner's remaining stake can be sold to foreign investors (section 16). SEBI requires at least 25% public shareholding: DMart's promoters at 74.48% comply, so there is no live forced-sale issue. Ind AS 116, in force since FY20, changes how leases are reported (section 08). Food safety (FSSAI) and legal metrology govern labelling; Blinkit faced warehouse raids by state food regulators in 2026. Trader associations have complained to the Competition Commission about deep discounting by quick-commerce platforms; the status of those complaints is unverified.

In plain terms

In September 2025 the government cut sales tax on clothes priced between ₹1,000 and ₹2,500 from 12% to 5%, and on most soaps, shampoos and packaged foods to 5%. Almost everything these four chains sell falls in those bands, so they gained. A few months earlier, the income-tax cut put more money in salaried households' pockets. Offsetting this, new labour rules and higher minimum wages are pushing up store staff costs, and a rule that keeps Vishal "Indian-owned" limits how many of its shares foreign investors can buy.

What could change next. Three policy risks are worth tracking. A future GST Council could revisit the ₹2,500 threshold or rationalise rates again; any rise in the 5% slab would hit these chains directly. State-level minimum-wage increases will continue, and the Karnataka stay could be lifted. And regulators may act on quick-commerce discounting or dark-store food safety, which would narrow the price gap DMart has to defend. Each is covered in the watch-list in section 22.

Navratri started 19 days later in 2026 than in 2025. That single date moved hundreds of crores of apparel sales from one quarter to the next and will distort the comparisons for six months.

Twenty-five years, five shocks: how Indian value retail got here

Debt-fuelled booms, a spectacular collapse, a pandemic and a quick-commerce explosion. The survivors share one trait: patience with the balance sheet.

Indian organised value retail is younger than most listed sectors. Its history is a sequence of booms that ended badly for those who borrowed to grow, and a few companies that compounded quietly by growing within their means. Five shocks shaped today's structure.

Shock 1: the 2005–08 boom and bust

In the mid-2000s a wave of chains (Pantaloon's Big Bazaar under the Future Group, Subhiksha, Vishal Retail, Spencer's, More and Reliance Fresh) expanded rapidly, much of it on borrowed money. When the 2008 crisis hit, the weakest fell. Subhiksha shut in 2009. Vishal Retail, which had listed in 2007, collapsed under its debts; its brand and business were sold in 2011 to TPG and Shriram, and the listed shell became V2 Retail. The lesson that stuck with the survivors, and with investors, was that a retailer's leases and loans can sink it faster than weak sales.

Shock 2: DMart's patient model wins

While others chased scale, R.K. Damani's Avenue Supermarts (incorporated in 2000, first store in Powai in 2002) grew slowly, bought its own land, paid suppliers fast and avoided debt. It had about 29 stores by 2010. Its March 2017 IPO at ₹299 a share was subscribed about 105 times, and it compounded revenue at about 22–23% a year for the following decade. DMart became the template for the "owner" model and the most richly valued retailer in the country.

Shock 3: the Future Group collapse (2020–22)

Future Group, once the largest value retailer, became the subject of a legal fight between Amazon and Reliance over its sale. Big Bazaar's roughly 1,500 stores went dark, and in February 2022 Reliance took over many of the leases. A large share of organised value-retail space was handed to Reliance and the survivors almost overnight, which helped both DMart and the private-equity-owned Vishal Mega Mart take share in the years that followed.

Shock 4: COVID (FY21)

The pandemic hit apparel far harder than grocery. DMart's revenue dipped 3% in FY21 (₹24,870 crore to ₹24,143 crore) because food stores stayed open; V-Mart's fell 35% (₹1,662 crore to ₹1,075 crore). V-Mart then bought Unlimited from Arvind Fashions (74 stores, about ₹150 crore, FY22) and the distressed online marketplace LimeRoad (about ₹31 crore upfront, FY23). Integration pain, cotton inflation and weak demand produced a net loss of ₹97 crore in FY24, before a sharp recovery to ₹124 crore profit in FY26.

The history of Indian value retail is a warning about growing on borrowed money. Reading a value retailer: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number an analyst uses for these four companies: what it means, how to calculate it, what good looks like, and where the companies' definitions differ enough to mislead.

Retail analysis looks easy because the business is easy to understand. The difficulty is in the definitions. The four members report the "same" metrics on different bases: same-store growth over 15 months or 24 months, revenue per square foot per month or per quarter or per year, private label as a share of everything or only of apparel, EBITDA before or after rent. This section works through each native KPI in turn, with a worked example, benchmarks and the trap to avoid, then builds an illustrative store P&L and ends with a ten-minute checklist for reading quarterly results.

The revenue and profit equation

A value retailer's revenue is the space it trades from multiplied by how hard each square foot works. Its profit is that revenue multiplied by the gross margin, less the cost of running the stores. Every KPI in this section is a term in that equation, and management controls some terms far more than others.

A worked example in round numbers (ILLUSTRATIVE). A chain trades from an average of 10 million sq ft at ₹10,000 per sq ft per year: revenue is ₹10,000 crore. Add same-store growth of 6% on the old space (₹600 crore of extra revenue with little extra cost), and margin recovers. This is why same-store growth and new-store productivity together decide whether expansion is profitable.

In plain terms

A store chain's sales are its floor space times how much each square foot sells. Its profit is what is left after paying for the goods, the staff, the rent and the electricity. Opening new stores adds floor space quickly but new stores sell less at first. Selling more from old stores adds profit cheaply because most costs are fixed. Every number analysts track is a piece of that sum.

1. Stores and retail area

What it is. The number of operating stores at period end, and the total selling area in million square feet. Formula. Closing stores = opening stores + openings − closures; average area = (opening area + closing area) ÷ 2. Worked example. VMM had 795 stores and 13.45 million sq ft at March 2026 and 844 stores and 14.1 million sq ft at September 2026, so average area for H1 FY27 was about 13.8 million sq ft. Drivers. Site availability, capital, management bandwidth and, for owners, land acquisition timelines of two to three years. Benchmark. Net store growth above 20% of the base a year is fast, 10–20% average, under 10% slow. In FY26, V2 grew its base by about 72%, DMart by 20%, V-Mart by 16% and VMM by 14%. The trap. Store counts are quoted gross, net, at March or at September, and formats differ fourfold in size. Always compare area, not stores, and always ask for closures: VMM's "105 new stores" in FY26 were 99 net.

4. Bills, footfall, conversion and average bill value

What it is. The components of same-store growth. Footfall is the number of people entering; conversion is the share who buy; bills (or "memos") are transactions; average bill value (ABV, also "average transaction size") is revenue per bill; average selling price (ASP) is revenue per item. Healthy growth comes from more bills, not only higher prices: bills growing at least as fast as same-store sales is a good sign. Rising footfall with falling conversion, as at V-Mart, suggests more comparison shopping and more competition nearby; V-Mart's management said customers "visit, compare nearby options and come back". VMM's "18.1 crore loyalty customers" is a registration count accumulated over years, not unique active households (India has about 30 crore households); about 95% of VMM revenue comes from loyalty members, which shows reach, not frequency. And footfall counters are inaccurate: V-Mart admitted "some past counting errors".

What it is. The share of revenue left after the cost of the goods sold. Formula. (Revenue − cost of goods) ÷ revenue. Worked example. VMM's Q2 FY27 revenue of ₹3,398.5 crore and gross profit of ₹950.1 crore give a gross margin of 28.0% (28.3% a year earlier). V2's FY26 gross profit of ₹927.1 crore on ₹3,067.1 crore is 30.2%. Drivers. Category mix (grocery low, apparel high), own-brand share, buying terms, markdowns and clearance, shrinkage, input costs (cotton, polyester) and pricing decisions. V-Mart's Q1 FY27 gross margin fell 80 basis points to 34.5% on mix and a higher aged-inventory provision; VMM's Q1 rose to 28.7% on lower promotional spend. Benchmark. Grocery: above 15% good, 12–15% average, under 12% weak (DMart about 14.5%). Value fashion: above 32% good, 27–32% average, under 27% weak (V-Mart 34.5%, V2 30.2%, VMM 28.3%). Management guidance: DMart "broadly 14–15%" for years to come; V2 29–30%; V-Mart prepared to give up 0.5–0.75 points to hold prices.

The trap. Gross margin includes different things. V-Mart's includes LimeRoad's marketplace commission income, which has no cost of goods. Consignment stock (common at VMM) is recorded differently from owned stock. Some chains put freight-in or shrinkage above gross margin, others below. And a stable gross margin percentage can hide a falling rupee margin per item if prices fall; V-Mart's management says rupee gross margin matters more than percentage. Compare trends within a company more confidently than levels across companies.

6. EBITDA margin, before and after Ind AS 116

What it is. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, as a share of revenue. Formula (reported). Revenue − cost of goods − staff − other expenses (excluding long-lease rent). Formula (rent-paid, "pre-Ind AS"). Reported EBITDA − lease cash payments. Worked example. V-Mart, Q1 FY27: revenue ₹1,088.8 crore − cost of goods ₹713.6 crore = gross profit ₹375.2 crore; less staff ₹111.2 crore and other costs including rent ₹180.7 crore gives rent-paid EBITDA of ₹83.2 crore (7.6%); add back rent of ₹77.4 crore and you get the reported EBITDA of ₹160.6 crore (14.8%). 7. Inventory days and working capital

What it is. How long stock sits before it is sold, and how much of it suppliers fund. Formulas. Inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods × 365 (or ÷ revenue × 365 for "days of sales"); payable days = trade payables ÷ cost of goods × 365; net working capital days = inventory days + receivable days − payable days. Worked example. VMM's inventory of ₹2,017 crore at March 2026 against FY26 cost of goods of about ₹9,250 crore gives about 79 days. DMart's days of inventory rose from 28.8 (FY23) to 33.2 (FY26) and 36.1 (H1 FY27), while it pays suppliers in about 7 days. V2's inventory doubled to ₹1,142 crore in FY26, against payables of ₹463 crore.

8. Private-label share

What it is. The share of revenue from the retailer's own brands. Formula. Own-brand revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example. VMM's own brands were 73.9% of H1 FY27 revenue of ₹7,125.5 crore, about ₹5,266 crore. Drivers. Design capability, sourcing scale, customer trust and category: clothing is easy to own-brand (VMM's is 100%), branded FMCG much harder (VMM's is about 60% by volume). Benchmark. Above 60% good for a value chain, 30–60% average, under 30% weak. Each 10 points of own-brand share is worth roughly 1–2 points of gross margin, depending on category (indicative). The trap. VMM's 73.9% is a share of total revenue; V-Mart's 66%-plus is a share of apparel only; V2 discloses no figure; DMart excludes garments, general merchandise and its own packed staples from its definition. Never compare VMM's figure with V-Mart's. How the companies compare. VMM is the clear leader on disclosed own-brand share, and its gross margin (28%) is roughly double DMart's despite selling groceries. V-Mart's higher gross margin (34.5%) reflects an apparel-heavy mix rather than a higher own-brand share. V2 relies on its in-house design team and job workers, but buys some product from outside suppliers. DMart, by choice, remains a seller of national brands with a small, rule-bound private-label range in FMCG.

9. Capex per store and payback

What it is. The capital needed to open a store (fit-out, plus land and building for an owner, plus opening inventory) and the years of store EBITDA needed to recover it. Formula. Payback (years) = (capex + net working capital) ÷ annual store EBITDA. Worked example. V2 guides to capex of ₹1.2–1.22 crore per store and a payback of 2.5–3 years. A mature V2 store selling ₹12 crore a year at 9% rent-paid EBITDA earns about ₹1.1 crore; with ₹1.2 crore of capex and perhaps ₹2–3 crore of net inventory, the payback on total capital is closer to three years at maturity and longer in the ramp year (ILLUSTRATIVE).

10. Store openings, closures and cannibalisation

What it is. The pace and quality of expansion. Formula. Closure rate = closures ÷ opening store base; cannibalisation shows up as a gap between total and same-store growth in clustered markets. Worked example. VMM closed 6 stores in FY26 on an opening base of 696 (0.9%); V-Mart guides to 8–10 closures in FY27 on 577 (about 1.5%). DMart's management says older Mumbai and Bengaluru stores are near capacity and new nearby stores take some of their sales. Drivers. Site selection, lease renewals, local competition and the deliberate choice to cluster stores to cut logistics costs. Benchmark. A closure rate under 1% a year is good, 1–2% average, above 2% a warning. The trap. Chains rarely disclose which stores close or why, and "temporarily closed" stores (for reconstruction or renovation) drop out of the same-store base, which can flatter it.

In plain terms

Opening shops is easy; opening good shops is hard. Watch how many stores each chain closes, how fast new stores start selling as much as old ones, and whether new stores near old ones simply steal their customers. A chain that opens a hundred stores and closes ten is learning expensive lessons.

11. Return on capital employed (ROCE)

What it is. Operating profit (EBIT) as a share of the capital used to earn it. Formula. EBIT ÷ (equity + debt, including lease liabilities on a post-Ind AS basis, less cash). Worked example. DMart's company-reported ROCE was 17.1% in FY26, down from 20.1% in FY23. Screener's FY26 figures: V2 19.3%, DMart 17.2%, VMM 15.2%, V-Mart 13.2%. Drivers. Margin × asset turnover. DMart's has fallen as owned real estate, idle QIP cash and DMart Ready's losses diluted it; VMM's is held down by ₹4,284 crore of acquisition goodwill (37% of its assets); V2's has risen as its store base scaled. Benchmark. Above 18% good, 12–18% average, under 12% weak. The trap. Post-Ind AS ROCE includes lease assets in capital, which is right for comparing owners and tenants; but goodwill (VMM), land at historical cost (DMart) and cash balances all distort the figure. A rising ROCE driven by a lease re-estimate (V2's FY26 exceptional cut its right-of-use assets by ₹484 crore) is not the same as a rising ROCE driven by better stores.

12. Shrinkage, markdowns and full-price sell-through

What it is. Shrinkage is stock lost to theft, damage, expiry and recording errors. Markdowns are price cuts to clear slow-moving stock. Full-price sell-through is the share of sales made at the original price. Formulas. Shrinkage rate = shrinkage written off ÷ revenue; full-price sell-through = sales at full price ÷ total sales. Worked example. DMart recorded ₹275.24 crore of shrinkage and damage in FY26 on standalone revenue of ₹66,968 crore, a rate of 0.41%, and carried a ₹70.08 crore allowance for shrinkage and slow-moving stock. V-Mart's provision for aged stock and shrinkage was 0.7% of sales in Q1 FY27 against 1.3% a year earlier, and its year-end inventory of ₹987.53 crore was stated after a ₹72.64 crore provision. V2's old-stock provision rose to ₹48.18 crore from ₹19.70 crore, 2.4 times higher on an inventory twice as large; its full-price sales share slipped to 90% in Q1 FY27 from 92%. Drivers. Store controls, RFID and counting frequency (VMM is rolling out RFID so that a full clothing count takes four to five hours and can be done weekly), assortment accuracy, weather and festival timing. Benchmark. Shrinkage plus aged-stock provision under 0.8% of revenue is good, 0.8–1.5% average, above 1.5% weak; full-price sell-through above 90% good, 80–90% average, under 80% weak. The trap. Provisions are estimates: V-Mart's and DMart's apply an assumed shrinkage rate to sales since the last physical count, so a change in assumptions moves gross margin. V2 adjusted a prior-year barcode mismatch (items re-barcoded or scrapped in bulk) against a ₹22.15 crore provision built in earlier years. Falling provisions flatter margins; rising ones may be prudence or a sign of ageing stock. Read the auditors' key audit matters, which cover inventory at all four companies.

In plain terms

Every shop loses some stock to theft, breakage and mistakes, and fashion shops must cut prices on clothes that do not sell. These losses are small as a share of sales, under one rupee in a hundred at DMart and V-Mart, but because profit margins are only seven to ten rupees in a hundred, a small rise in losses takes a big bite out of profit. A fall in full-price sales is often the first sign that a season went badly.

15. Definition traps, collected

  • Same-store growth: DMart ≥24 months; VMM ≥15 months at the start of the quarter; V-Mart undisclosed; V2 calendar and festival-normalised. Festival-adjusted figures are management constructs.
  • Revenue per square foot: per quarter (DMart deck), per month (V-Mart, V2), per year (DMart annual report, V-Mart); VMM does not publish. DMart's FY26 figure is ₹33,422, not the ~₹35,000 an annualised Q2 suggests.
  • EBITDA: reported (post-Ind AS) excludes long-lease rent; "operating" (VMM) also excludes stock-option costs; "pre-Ind AS" (V2, V-Mart) deducts rent. Compare on a rent-paid basis.
  • Borrowings: screener "borrowings" for the tenants are mostly lease liabilities (VMM's ₹1,988 crore at March 2026 is almost entirely leases; it has no bank debt).
  • DMart standalone vs consolidated: the press-release headline revenue (₹19,206 crore in Q2 FY27, +18.4%) is standalone; consolidated is ₹19,644 crore (+17.8%). Consolidated minus standalone revenue is not DMart Ready's revenue, because DMart Ready buys from DMart stores and intercompany sales are eliminated. DMart Ready's FY26 revenue was ₹4,093.61 crore.
  • Store counts: gross vs net (VMM FY26 105 gross, 99 net); March vs September; V-Mart had 577 stores at March 2026, not "about 600".
  • Private label: VMM share of total revenue; V-Mart share of apparel; DMart excludes garments and general merchandise; V2 undisclosed.
  • Revenue: V-Mart's ₹3,804 crore is total income; revenue from operations is ₹3,789 crore. DMart's FY22 revenue was ₹30,353 crore standalone and ₹30,976 crore consolidated.
  • One-offs: DMart's FY23 profit includes a ₹138.8 crore one-off tax benefit (and ₹36 crore in FY25); V2's FY26 profit includes a ₹27.7 crore pre-tax lease-reassessment gain.
  • Per-share history: V-Mart issued a 3:1 bonus (record date 23 June 2025) and V2 split its shares ₹10 → ₹1 (record date 26 March 2026); earlier per-share figures must be adjusted.
  • Loyalty "customers": VMM's 18.1 crore is cumulative registrations, not active households.
In plain terms

The four companies use the same words for different things. "Same-store growth" covers different sets of stores; "sales per square foot" is quoted per month, per quarter or per year; "EBITDA" may or may not include rent; "private label" may mean all products or only clothes. Before comparing any two numbers, check that they measure the same thing.

16. How to read a value retailer's quarterly results in ten minutes

A practical checklist, in the order that gives the most information fastest.

  1. Check the basis first (one minute). Standalone or consolidated? Reported or rent-paid EBITDA? Which quarter, and does it contain festival days this year or last? For DMart, use consolidated for profit and standalone for store metrics.
  2. Revenue growth split into space and same-store (one minute). Compare revenue growth with growth in average retail area and with same-store growth. If revenue growth is much higher than same-store growth, new stores are carrying the chain; check that they are ramping.
  3. Same-store growth, reported before adjusted (one minute). Note the definition. Compare with the prior quarter and the same quarter last year, and against the 5% line. Ask what drove it: bills or footfall versus average bill.
  4. Gross margin (one minute). Change in basis points year on year. A fall in a fashion chain usually means markdowns or input inflation; at DMart, a price investment or mix. Read management's explanation, then check inventory.
  5. Rent-paid EBITDA margin (two minutes). Reported EBITDA minus lease payments (find them in the cash-flow statement or the company's pre-Ind AS disclosure). Has operating leverage worked? Look at staff cost per square foot for wage pressure.
  6. Inventory and working capital (one minute). Inventory growth against revenue growth; days of inventory against the same month last year. Inventory growing much faster than sales is the most reliable early warning of markdowns to come.
  7. Stores: openings, closures, area (one minute). Gross and net openings against guidance; any rise in closures.
  8. Below EBITDA (one minute). Interest and depreciation growth (DMart's are now rising faster than revenue); any exceptional items, lease re-estimates or one-off tax effects; the subsidiary drag at DMart.
  9. Cash flow and debt (half a minute). Operating cash flow after lease payments, capex, and ex-lease net debt or cash.
  10. Guidance and tone (half a minute). Store-opening guidance, gross-margin guidance and any comment on festive demand, input costs or competition.

Red flags. Same-store growth under 3–4% for two or more clean quarters; inventory growing more than ten points faster than revenue; gross margin down more than 100 basis points without a clear one-off; closures rising; reported EBITDA rising while rent-paid EBITDA falls; repeated exceptional gains; senior merchandising or operations exits before a peak season (V-Mart lost its COO and President-Merchandising between July and October 2026); and auditor emphasis-of-matter paragraphs (V2's auditor flagged a ₹12.88 crore advertising advance outstanding since 2019).

In plain terms

When a value retailer reports, look at five things in order: whether sales from old stores grew by more than about 5%; whether the gross margin held; what the profit margin was after paying rent; whether stock piled up faster than sales; and whether the company opened the stores it promised. If all five are fine, the quarter was fine, whatever the headline profit growth says. If stock is piling up and margins are slipping, trouble is coming even if sales look strong.

Why the overhang matters

Samayat still holds 40.02%. At ₹87.4, that stake is worth roughly ₹16,400 crore (ILLUSTRATIVE: 40.02% of the ₹40,922 crore market value), larger than either previous block (about ₹10,220 crore and ₹7,636 crore). The 150-day lock-in that followed the February 2026 sale ran out in late July 2026, so another block is possible at any time. Markets typically price that supply in advance: a block usually clears at a 5–10% discount to the prevailing price, and investors who expect one hold back from buying. Partners Group is reported to hold about 27% directly through the vehicle (single-source, indicative), the rest sitting with Kedaara.

Two features complicate the next sale. First, VMM has capped foreign ownership at 49.99% to remain an "Indian-owned and controlled" company, which matters for its ability to operate certain retail and e-commerce structures. Foreign holding is about 20% now, so there is room for roughly 30 points more, but the cap means the remaining 40% must again find mostly domestic buyers if foreign investors are lukewarm. Domestic mutual funds, already at 36.45%, have finite appetite for one stock. Second, the price: Samayat sold at ₹115–117 and the stock is now at ₹87. Sellers may wait for a recovery, prolonging the overhang, or accept a lower price and reset the market's reference point.

In plain terms

The private-equity funds that own 40% of Vishal Mega Mart want to sell, and everyone knows it. They sold big chunks at about ₹115–117 a share to Indian mutual funds and others. The shares now trade near ₹87. Until the funds finish selling, many investors will wait rather than buy, which holds the price down even though the business is growing well and has no debt.

Governance markers to watch

Private-equity control brings strong professional management but also specific checks. VMM's founder, CEO and MD Gunender Kapur earned ₹30.26 crore in FY26 (₹35.08 crore in FY25). The FY26 annual report shows ₹72.10 crore of transportation and ₹31.17 crore of warehousing charges paid to a party in the "entity exercising significant influence" column; the text layer places Samayat on those rows, which would be unusual for a holding LLP and may be a layout artefact (unverified). Goodwill of ₹4,284 crore from the 2018 buy-out is 37% of total assets and is tested for impairment using comparable-company multiples; the auditor (Walker Chandiok) treats it as a key audit matter. A sustained de-rating of value-retail multiples feeds directly into that test, though with the stock still at 46 times earnings there is ample headroom. On the operating side, merchandising leadership changed in 2026 (a ten-year merchandising head left and was replaced), and a small-format pilot (16 stores in UP and Haryana) and the "Belong & Co." urban apparel pilot are new bets to monitor.

What would end the overhang. Three outcomes are possible: one or two more blocks to domestic and foreign institutions over the next year, which would leave VMM a widely held company with no controlling shareholder; a strategic sale of the remaining stake to a single buyer, which would trigger an open offer; or a slow dribble of sales through the market. The first is the most common route for PE exits in India. Each block is a short-term negative for the price and a long-term positive for liquidity and index weights.

V2: the sprint

V2 more than doubled its store base in 18 months, from 189 stores at March 2025 to 427 at September 2026, and guides to 170–200 openings in FY27 (106 in H1), revenue growth of "at least 50%", same-store growth of 8–10% and a gross margin of 29–30%. Its management says it has MOUs for the next 100 stores and finalises 15–20 sites a month; its long-term ambition is at least 2,500 stores. Its stores are mostly standalone buildings of 10,000–12,000 sq ft rather than mall units, on leases of nine to eleven years with 12–15% escalation every three years. Mature stores sell ₹1,070–1,100 per sq ft per month, up from about ₹650 three years ago; returning customers within the first year rose from about 40% to about 55%.

The cost is visible on the balance sheet. Inventory doubled to ₹1,142 crore in FY26, operating cash flow turned negative (−₹103 crore), and V2 raised ₹400 crore through a QIP in November 2025, diluting the promoter from 54.22% to 51.43%. Its FY26 profit includes a ₹27.7 crore lease-reassessment gain. Smaller warning signs have appeared: full-price sell-through slipped to 90% from 92% in Q1 FY27; the old-stock provision rose to ₹48 crore; a prior-year barcode mismatch was adjusted against an older provision; the auditor's emphasis of matter covers a ₹12.88 crore advertising advance to Bennett, Coleman outstanding since April 2019; and an analyst noted weak Google ratings (about 3.6 against 4.1–4.2 at peers, mostly staff-behaviour complaints). None is serious alone; together they suggest controls are catching up with a business growing at twice its previous size every 18 months. Its chairman and MD and a whole-time director, both from the promoter family, draw no remuneration.

V-Mart: the consolidation

V-Mart grew more slowly, from 497 stores to 613, and spent FY25–FY26 repairing the damage of its FY24 loss. Profit rose from ₹46 crore to ₹124 crore in FY26, bank borrowings fell to ₹1.2 crore by June 2026, inventory days of sales fell from 93 to 86 and the aged-stock and shrinkage provision from 1.3% to 0.7% of sales. LimeRoad's quarterly EBITDA loss narrowed from ₹4.6 crore to ₹2.8 crore. Its southern chain Unlimited is the growth engine: Q1 FY27 revenue ₹184 crore (+33%), same-store growth 11–13% and segment EBITDA of 18.6%, against 14.4% for the core V-Mart brand. Management wants to accelerate Unlimited, guides to 90-plus openings and 8–10 closures in FY27, and caps price increases at 3–5% despite about 10% raw-material inflation.

Its weak spots are thin net margins (3.3% in FY26), extreme seasonality (a Q2 FY26 loss), conversion that fell from the mid-40s to 38–39% as footfall rose 39%, and people risk: its COO left in mid-2026 and its President-Merchandising resigned effective 9 October 2026, just before the festive quarter. Executive pay also rose sharply in the turnaround year: the MD's pay was 267 times the median employee's (+64%), and independent directors' fees rose 130–180%, while profit rose from ₹46 crore to ₹124 crore.

The global lens and the price of growth

Indian value retailers earn higher net margins than Walmart or Costco and trade at multiples two to four times those of global value chains. The premium is a bet on the runway.

Each Indian model has a global analogue. DMart's owned, high-turn, low-margin hypermarket resembles Costco's membership warehouses and Walmart's everyday-low-price supercentres. VMM's own-brand-heavy one-roof store borrows from Aldi and Lidl, the European hard discounters that sell around 90% private label. V-Mart and V2 resemble Europe's Pepco or Primark in value fashion, and in their small-town focus, America's Dollar General. The comparison is useful for two reasons: it shows what mature margins look like, and it shows how much more the Indian market is paying for growth.

What the multiple implies

A simple illustration shows what a 60-plus multiple asks. A company on 63 times earnings has an earnings yield of about 1.6%. If its profits compound at 18% a year, they roughly double in a little over four years and quadruple in about eight and a half; if the multiple then falls to a still-generous 35 times, the share price rises by about 2.2 times over those eight years (ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic: 4 × 35 ÷ 63). Profit growth of 18% requires revenue growth in the high teens with stable margins: possible for the value-fashion chains with their store pipelines, harder for DMart if like-for-like growth "hovers" near 8% and store growth slows to 15%. That is the arithmetic behind October's de-rating. The market is not doubting that these companies will grow; it is lowering what it will pay for growth that may be slower and more capital-hungry than it assumed in mid-2026.

In plain terms

Indian value retailers actually make more profit per rupee of sales than Walmart or Costco. Investors pay much more for each rupee of their profit because they expect sales to keep growing at 15–25% a year for many years as shoppers move from local stores to chains. If growth slows, the high price is hard to justify, which is why these shares fall sharply when growth disappoints, even slightly.

Lessons from abroad. Three global lessons are relevant. Walmart's response to Amazon, investing in store-fulfilled delivery and pickup rather than abandoning its stores, is the path VMM and V2 are taking with hyperlocal delivery from stores, and arguably the path DMart has chosen not to take beyond 11 cities. The rise of Aldi and Lidl in the UK showed that a hard-discount, private-label model can take share from established grocers for decades, which supports VMM's model. The general lesson from fast small-box roll-outs abroad is a caution for V2's sprint: store growth that outruns controls eventually shows up in shrinkage and margins.

Technology and disruption

Quick commerce is the headline threat. The quieter change is that the value chains are turning their own stores into delivery hubs and their stock rooms into data.

Six technology shifts matter for this sub-sector, ranked by their likely impact on the four members.

  • Quick commerce (high impact on DMart, low on the rest). Covered in section 07. It attacks the top-up FMCG basket and impulse general merchandise, DMart's high-frequency traffic. Blinkit's order value per store per day rose from ₹7.71 lakh to ₹9.02 lakh in a year; it plans about 750 net new stores in H2 FY27.
  • Store-fulfilled hyperlocal delivery (an opportunity for the value chains). VMM delivers from 781 stores in 532 cities, with 1.52 crore registered users (+41%), an average order of about ₹800 and hyperlocal at 2–9% of store revenue (target 5%). V2Kart launched in September 2026 in Delhi NCR and Lucknow. Reliance's JioMart fulfils from more than 3,100 stores. Using existing stores as dark stores costs almost no extra capital and reaches customers quick-commerce platforms do not yet serve.
  • Online value fashion (contained). Meesho, Flipkart's Shopsy and Ajio sell cheap apparel online. V-Mart's own LimeRoad marketplace had net merchandise value of ₹22.2 crore in Q1 FY27 (+18%) and a ₹2.8 crore EBITDA loss, narrowing; V-Mart's inventory supplies 35–54% of LimeRoad orders. Small-town shoppers still prefer to touch and try clothes, and returns make online apparel expensive.
  • Inventory technology. VMM is rolling out RFID across its stores over a little more than a year, which will cut a full clothing stock count to four to five hours and allow weekly counts. V-Mart's Palwal distribution centre uses automated conveyors and scanners. Better counts reduce shrinkage and markdowns, the line items that separate good and bad fashion retailers.
  • Loyalty data. VMM's loyalty programme covers about 95% of revenue and 18.1 crore registrations, enabling personalised offers. DMart does not run a comparable programme, relying on everyday low prices.
  • Payments and digital credit. UPI has made cashless payment universal in small towns, cutting cash-handling costs and giving retailers transaction data. Buy-now-pay-later and consumer credit are less relevant at these price points.

The dark-store economics, compared

The quick-commerce model's strengths are speed, convenience and space productivity (Instamart's order value per square foot is about twice DMart's revenue per square foot). Its weaknesses are cost and price: deliveries cost money, platforms subsidise customers to gain share, and as they push toward profit they raise fees (Instamart's revenue per order rose from ₹97 to ₹108). Blinkit earned adjusted EBITDA of just 0.9% of order value in Q2 FY27; Instamart lost ₹895 crore at the same level; Zepto lost ₹5,905 crore in FY26 (press-reported). DMart's bet is that the bulk monthly basket remains a price-led trip, and that quick commerce's costs will cap how far it can undercut a hypermarket. Blinkit's bet is that ten-minute delivery becomes the default for most urban grocery shopping. Both can be partly right: quick commerce can take most of the top-up trip while DMart keeps most of the planned monthly shop.

In plain terms

Delivery apps are very good at bringing small orders quickly, and their warehouses sell more per square foot than any supermarket. But each delivery costs money, and most of these apps still lose money or barely break even. The value chains are copying the idea cheaply by delivering from their own stores. DMart is betting that families will still drive to it for the big monthly shop because it is cheaper.

Where technology could surprise. Two developments would change the picture. If quick-commerce platforms extend into apparel and home goods at scale in small towns, the value chains would face a new competitor for the impulse purchase. And if DMart revived DMart Ready with a profitable model, perhaps using its stores as fulfilment hubs, it could reclaim some of the top-up basket. Neither is visible yet. The e-retail report covers the platforms' economics, and the restaurants report covers Eternal's and Swiggy's food-delivery businesses, which share the same delivery fleets.

Bull vs bear

The long-run case is about runway. The near-term case is about whether same-store growth and margins hold while costs rise and the competition arrives.

The bull and bear cases below apply to the sub-sector as a whole; each company's version differs in weight, as the profiles show. Both are argued at full strength.

The bull case

  • The runway: organised retail is roughly a fifth of a ₹75–81 lakh crore market, projected by Redseer to grow about 20% a year to ₹36–38 lakh crore by 2028, fastest in Tier II and III towns (about 32% a year).
  • Proven models: all four members earn 13–19% ROCE and 6–10% rent-paid EBITDA; VMM and V2 open stores with 2.5–3-year paybacks.
  • Policy tailwinds: GST 2.0 cut tax on almost everything they sell, and the income-tax cut raised disposable income.
  • Balance sheets: the group had ex-lease net cash of ₹705 crore at March 2026; VMM and V-Mart owe the banks almost nothing.
  • Own brands: VMM's 74% own-brand share and the fashion chains' private labels protect margins and limit quick commerce's reach.
  • Cheaper entry: after October's de-rating, VMM trades below its private-equity block prices and V2 at 37 times earnings with 60%-plus growth.

The bear case

  • Valuation: 63 times FY26 earnings for the group and 66 times for DMart still require high-teens profit growth for years.
  • Quick commerce: Blinkit's quarterly order value now exceeds DMart's revenue; DMart guides to same-store growth "hovering" near current levels and has retreated online to 11 cities.
  • Costs: wage codes and minimum-wage increases, about 10% apparel raw-material inflation and rent escalations, with price increases capped at 3–5%.
  • Crowded small towns: Zudio, Reliance, V-Mart, V2 and VMM are all opening in the same districts; V-Mart's conversion has already fallen.
  • Capital and controls: DMart is borrowing, V2's inventory doubled and it needed a QIP; V2 and V-Mart show small control and people flags.
  • Macro: food inflation of 7.74%, a monsoon at 87% of normal and an RBI rate hike squeeze the discretionary wallet; a ₹16,000 crore-plus VMM stake still overhangs.

Weighing them. The bull case is structural and slow; the bear case is cyclical and fast. Over five years, the runway argument is hard to dismiss: these are profitable, growing businesses in a market that is still four-fifths unorganised. Over the next two to four quarters, the bear case's specific risks (festival-season sell-through, input inflation, DMart's like-for-like growth and VMM's overhang) are the ones that will move share prices. The October de-rating has removed some of the valuation risk, but only some: at 63 times earnings the group is still priced for sustained growth.
