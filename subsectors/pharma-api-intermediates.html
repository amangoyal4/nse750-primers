00

The one-page view: a spread business living on China's price list

Nine companies that sell the medicine before it becomes a medicine. Their profit is the gap between what a kilogram fetches and what its inputs cost, and China sets both ends of that gap.

Every tablet, capsule and injection contains a small amount of chemical that actually does the work. That chemical is the active pharmaceutical ingredient, or API, and it is made in reactors and fermenters, dried into a powder, tested against a pharmacopoeia standard and sold by the kilogram to the companies that press it into pills. India's API industry was worth about $13.5 billion in calendar 2024, roughly ₹1.13 lakh crore and about a quarter of the Indian pharmaceutical industry (ICRA). India is the world's third-largest API producer by volume, behind only the United States and China. Nine companies in the NSE 750 earn most of their revenue from this business, and this report is about them.

The nine are Laurus Labs, Viyash Scientific, Aarti Drugs, Alivus Life Sciences, Cohance Lifesciences, Aarti Pharmalabs, Shilpa Medicare, Concord Biotech and Supriya Lifescience. Together they reported ₹22,856 crore of revenue in FY26, up 7.6%, at a blended EBITDA margin of 23.8%, and ₹2,910 crore of profit. On 5 October 2026 they were worth ₹2.04 lakh crore, about 70 times that profit. One company distorts every total. Laurus Labs is 30% of the group's revenue, 52% of its market value and almost all of its growth: without Laurus, FY26 revenue grew only 2.3% and the group traded at about 49 times profit rather than 70. The report shows combined figures both ways wherever it matters.

The business has a simple shape and a complicated politics. An API maker buys a starting chemical, runs it through several reactions, purifies the result and sells it. Its gross profit is the volume it sells multiplied by the spread between the price per kilogram and the cost of the inputs per kilogram. Its operating profit depends on how full its reactors are, because power, steam, effluent treatment and quality staff cost much the same whether a plant runs at 60% or 90%. The politics comes from the fact that China is the dominant source of the starting chemicals, the dominant competitor in finished APIs, and the reason Western customers want an Indian supplier in the first place. China supplied 73.7% of India's imports of APIs, key starting materials and intermediates in FY25, a share that rose after the government launched a ₹6,940 crore incentive scheme in 2020 to reduce it.

Five forces define the sub-sector, and the report is built around them. First, the China triangle: dependence on Chinese inputs, Chinese price-setting and the China+1 demand that both threatens and feeds Indian makers. Second, the spread and the cycle: a commodity API maker's profit swings by multiples on small moves in price, which is why the 2020–21 boom, the 2022–25 bust and the 2026 recovery look so violent in the numbers. Third, the paper moat: revenue sticks because a customer's regulatory filing names the API maker and its plant, and the same moat turns into a trap when an inspector finds problems. Fourth, the climb up the ladder, from commodity molecules to regulated-market generics, niche and high-potency chemistry, fermentation, peptides and contract manufacturing for innovators. Fifth, the physical gates: environmental clearance, water, power and the concentration of a company's output in one or two sites.

Where does the cycle stand? FY26 was a trough for most of the nine. Concord (−12%), Cohance (−13%) and Aarti Pharmalabs (−14%) all shrank, and bulk-drug exports from India grew only about 4.6% in dollars. The first quarter of FY27, April to June 2026, was the turn: combined revenue rose about 20%, Shilpa grew 45%, Aarti Pharmalabs 39%, Supriya 31%, Laurus 29% and Concord 26%, and India's bulk-drug exports rose 13.8%, their first double-digit quarter in three years. Cohance was the outlier, with revenue down 23% and a net loss of ₹45 crore. On 29 September 2026 the Directorate General of Trade Remedies opened anti-dumping investigations into Chinese penicillin-G, 6-APA, amoxycillin and montelukast. That is the industry asking for tariff protection for the plants the incentive scheme paid for.

In plain terms

These companies make the chemical inside the pill and sell it by weight to the companies that make the pill. Most of their raw materials come from China, most of their price competition comes from China, and much of their new business exists because Western drug companies want a supplier that is not in China. A company that sells an ordinary chemical anyone can make earns a thin margin and trades at about twenty times its profit. A company that makes something hard to copy, or makes it under contract for the inventor of a new drug, earns a fat margin and can trade at fifty to a hundred times. The whole sub-sector is a study of which of those two a company is becoming.

The valuation gap inside the group is extreme and it is not random. Price-to-earnings multiples on 5 October 2026 fanned out almost exactly by distance from commodity chemistry: Aarti Drugs, which sells metformin and old antibiotics at a 12% margin, traded at about 20 times trailing earnings; Alivus, a broad regulated-market generic API library at 31–34% margins, at 27 times; Concord, a fermentation specialist, at 51 times; Shilpa, an oncology API maker funding a move into biologics, at 71 times; Laurus, an antiretroviral API giant turning itself into a contract manufacturer, at 97 times; and Cohance, a niche API and antibody-drug-conjugate contract maker in a deep trough, at 159 times trailing profit. The report explains what each of those multiples is paying for and what would have to go right to justify it.

The rest of the report runs in a deliberate order. Sections 1 to 4 explain the product, the market, the customers and the cost structure. Sections 5 to 11 take the forces one by one: China and the incentive scheme, industry structure, business models, regulatory moats, the permit gates and the history of the cycle. Section 12 is the KPI masterclass, which teaches how to read an API maker's numbers from the profit equation outward. Sections 13 to 17 compare the nine companies on a common basis, map who is winning, profile each company and set the valuations against global peers. The report closes with technology risks, the bull and bear cases, a watch-list with thresholds and a glossary.

01

The drug before the pill: what an API is, and who counts as an API maker

Starting material, intermediate, API, formulation: four links in one chain, priced and regulated very differently. The classification rule for this report is where most revenue comes from today.

A medicine has two parts. The active pharmaceutical ingredient is the molecule that treats the disease: metformin for diabetes, tacrolimus to stop a transplanted kidney being rejected, lenalidomide for a blood cancer. Everything else in the tablet, the fillers, binders, coatings and colours, is an excipient that carries the API and controls how it dissolves. The finished tablet, capsule or injection is the formulation, also called the finished dosage form. API makers sell the first part; formulators sell the second. A 500 mg metformin tablet contains half a gram of API, and an Indian API maker may sell that half-gram for about 14 paise (illustrative, at roughly ₹280 per kilogram). The formulator sells the tablet for several times that, because it also carries the brand or the generic approval, the packaging and the distribution.

The API itself sits near the end of a long chemical chain. Basic chemicals such as methanol, toluene and acetic anhydride are turned into a key starting material, or KSM: the first molecule in a drug's regulated synthesis. From the KSM onward, good manufacturing practice rules and the regulatory filing apply, which is why the KSM is a regulatory term as much as a chemical one. The KSM is then converted, step by step, into intermediates. A simple API may need two or three steps; a complex one may need ten or more. The intermediates one or two steps from the finished molecule are called advanced intermediates. The final steps, followed by purification, crystallisation, drying and milling, produce the API, which must meet a pharmacopoeia standard (Indian, US or European) before it can be sold.

Who belongs in this sub-sector? The rule used across this library is simple: a company sits where most of its revenue comes from today, not where it hopes to be in five years. That separates API makers from three neighbours. Branded formulators sell finished medicines under brand names that doctors prescribe, mostly in India; they are covered in the branded formulations report. Export generic formulators sell finished tablets and injections into the US and Europe under their own approvals. Contract development and manufacturing organisations, or CDMOs, make molecules under contract for innovator drug companies. All three buy APIs, and several of the large ones make their own (captive) APIs, but their revenue comes from something else.

The rule produces four judgement calls, and they are made openly here. Laurus Labs is in this report, not in a CDMO report, because generic APIs and generic formulations, led by antiretrovirals, were still about 70% of FY26 revenue (₹4,733 crore) against about 30% for CDMO (₹2,080 crore). Management targets CDMO at half of revenue by FY30; if it gets there, Laurus will move. Cohance Lifesciences is here because its niche-API business ("API+", about 48% or ₹1,088 crore) was larger than its pharma CDMO arm (about 39% or ₹889 crore), with specialty chemicals making up the rest. Viyash Scientific is the closest call: in the first quarter of FY27 its formulations line (₹555 crore) was larger than its API line (₹383 crore). It stays here on FY26 and on its stated strategy of building an integrated API franchise, but it is flagged for review at the next reclassification. Aarti Pharmalabs earns its largest line, about 57%, from xanthines, mainly caffeine, much of which goes to beverage makers. It stays here because xanthines are API-type molecules, made in API plants to pharmacopoeia grades, partly sold to pharma customers, and because its remaining revenue is API, intermediates and contract manufacturing.

Two things stand out in the mix chart. Only three companies, Alivus, Supriya and Concord, are close to pure API businesses. Everyone else is a hybrid, and the hybrids are hybrids in different directions: Laurus and Cohance towards innovator contract work, Shilpa and Viyash towards their own formulations, Aarti Pharmalabs towards caffeine and contract work, Aarti Drugs slowly towards formulations and specialty chemicals. That matters for reading the peer table. A blended margin or return figure for a hybrid tells you less than it seems, because it mixes a spread business with a service business or a branded one. Where companies disclose segment detail, this report uses it.

Generic and custom APIs are also very different deals, even when they come out of the same reactor. A generic API is sold at a market price to many formulators, renegotiated each quarter or year and benchmarked against the Chinese quote. Its stickiness comes from the customer's regulatory filing, which names the API source; switching needs a filing change and new stability data. A custom API is made for one innovator under a multi-year contract, often cost-plus or volume-tiered, and is named in the innovator's new-drug application, so switching is close to impossible for the life of the molecule. The custom deal is steadier on price but lumpy on volume, because it rises and falls with one drug's sales and its customer's inventory decisions. Cohance's FY26, when two large contract molecules destocked, is the cautionary example.

In plain terms

The KSM is the first ingredient the regulator cares about, the intermediates are the half-built molecule, the API is the finished drug powder, and the formulation is the pill. Companies in this report mostly sell powder. Some of them also sell pills or make powder to order for the inventors of new drugs, and several are trying hard to do more of that, because powder made to order earns more and is harder to take away. Where a company sits is decided by what it sells most of today. Viyash and Aarti Pharmalabs are the two closest calls, and both are flagged.

02

Size, trade and a flat decade in value

A ₹1.13 lakh crore industry that is roughly balanced in trade, nine times smaller than China's export machine, and whose dollar exports barely grew for three years.

India's API industry was about $13.5 billion in calendar 2024, roughly ₹1.13 lakh crore at that year's average exchange rate, and about 25% of the Indian pharmaceutical industry (ICRA, December 2025). It grew at about 7% a year in revenue between 2017 and 2024, but calendar 2024 itself was flat: volumes rose and prices fell by about as much. ICRA expected 6–8% growth in 2025, tied to formulation demand and contract manufacturing. About 65% of Indian API output is consumed in India and about 35% is exported. Those two shares check out against trade data: 35% of $13.5 billion is about $4.7 billion, close to Pharmexcil's bulk-drug export figures of $4.76 billion for FY24 and $4.87 billion for FY25.

The import side completes the picture. India imported ₹39,200 crore of APIs and bulk drugs in FY25 (ICRA, on a CMIE basis), about 35% of its total API needs. Add roughly $8.8 billion of Indian-made API consumed at home to roughly $4.6 billion of imports and India's total API use comes to about $13.4 billion, of which imports are about 35%. On a narrower Directorate General of Commercial Intelligence and Statistics (DGCI&S) basis covering APIs, KSMs and intermediates, imports were about $4.35 billion in FY25, and China supplied ₹27,032 crore of them, 73.7%. So India is roughly balanced in APIs by value, a large net importer from China, and a net exporter to regulated markets. That is the structural fact the rest of this report keeps coming back to.

Set against China, India is small. China exported $42.87 billion of APIs in 2025, about nine times India's bulk-drug exports, according to customs data compiled by its chamber of commerce for medicines (CCCMHPIE). In plain terms

India makes a lot of API, uses about two-thirds of it at home and sells the rest abroad. It also buys about a third of what it needs from overseas, three-quarters of that from China. In money terms India exports about as much API as it imports, but it depends on China for the cheapest and most basic ingredients. For three years the industry sold more kilograms at lower prices, so its export earnings in dollars barely moved. The first quarter of FY27 is the first sign that prices have stopped falling.

What does this mean for the nine companies? First, the domestic market is the larger outlet, but much of it is captive: integrated formulators such as Sun, Aurobindo, Lupin and Cipla make a lot of their own API. The open, or merchant, domestic API market is not published; this report estimates it indicatively at $4–5 billion, about half of domestic consumption. Second, the companies with the highest margins and valuations in this group, Alivus, Concord, Supriya and Laurus's contract business, are export- and regulated-market-led, because that is where qualification is hardest and prices hold best. Third, India's export figures have been flattered in volume and hurt in price, which is exactly the spread squeeze that shows up in the FY23–FY26 margins of the commodity end of the group.

03

Who buys a kilogram, and why demand never disappears

Every pill needs API, so volume demand rises with prescriptions. What moves an API maker's revenue is who the customer is, how many suppliers it has qualified and when it decides to hold or run down stock.

Demand for APIs is derived demand. Nobody buys metformin powder for its own sake; formulators buy it because patients take metformin tablets. That makes volume demand unusually stable. India supplies about 20% of the world's generic medicines by volume and Indian companies supply about 47% of US generic prescriptions (IQVIA Institute), and global generic volumes grow perhaps 3–5% a year (indicative). The swings in an API maker's revenue come instead from price, from inventory, and from winning or losing a place on a customer's list of qualified suppliers. The drivers below explain why the nine companies' fortunes diverge so much from one year to the next.

  • Generic formulation volume. Chronic therapies such as cardiovascular, diabetes, central nervous system and oncology compound steadily; acute anti-infectives swing with disease seasons. Alivus's portfolio is built around the chronic end; Aarti Drugs's around anti-infectives and metformin.
  • Patent expiries. When a branded drug loses exclusivity, generic makers race to launch, and each one needs a filed API source. Early filers of a drug master file win the first-wave supply contracts. The US calendar from 2025 to 2032 includes large small-molecule drugs and, from 2031–32, the GLP-1 peptides.
  • China+1 de-risking. Western buyers are qualifying non-Chinese second sources. It is real but slow, because re-qualification takes perhaps 12–24 months (indicative). The US BIOSECURE Act, signed on 18 December 2025, adds pressure on contract manufacturing.
  • Innovator outsourcing. India holds only 2–3% of a $140–145 billion global contract research, development and manufacturing market, against about 70% for the US and Europe and about 20% for China (ICRA). Indian revenue in this market grew about 15% a year over 2019–24.
  • Donor-funded tenders. Antiretroviral, tuberculosis and malaria medicines bought through the Global Fund and PEPFAR built Laurus. US aid cuts in 2025 are a risk that is not yet quantified.
  • Inventory cycles. In 2020–21 customers built safety stock; in 2022–24 they ran it down. Destocking hits API makers harder than formulators, because they sit one step further up the chain.
  • Non-pharma customers. Caffeine goes to beverage makers (Aarti Pharmalabs's largest line); Cohance and Aarti Drugs sell specialty chemicals to agrochemical and electronics customers.

Two new sources of demand matter more than their tonnage suggests. The first is peptides, led by the GLP-1 drugs semaglutide and tirzepatide used for diabetes and obesity. India launched generic semaglutide on 20–21 March 2026, and Canada, Brazil and China open in 2026–27. A semaglutide patient on a 2.4 mg weekly dose uses about 125 mg of API a year, so ten million patient-years need only about 1.25 tonnes. The same ten million patients on 1 gram of metformin a day would need about 3,650 tonnes. Peptide APIs therefore sell for orders of magnitude more per kilogram, and the competition is about synthesis skill and capacity rather than reactor volume. China's peptide hormone exports rose 81.7% in 2025, which shows how fast this contest is moving.

The second is innovator contract work, which is a different kind of demand altogether. It depends on how many of a client's drugs move from trials into commercial supply, on biotech funding (which fell 53% year on year in the first seven months of 2025, according to ICRA) and on the client's inventory policy. Laurus's contract revenue grew 36% in FY26 and 67% in the first quarter of FY27; Cohance's pharma contract revenue fell 38.7% in the same quarter on shipment phasing of a few large molecules. Both are real demand. They show that the risk in contract work is concentration, not the absence of a market.

In plain terms

People keep taking their pills, so the world never stops needing API. What changes is the price per kilogram, how much stock customers decide to keep, and whether a buyer has put a particular supplier's name in its regulatory filing. That is why an API maker's revenue can fall 13% in a year when global medicine consumption rose. The newest demand, for peptide drugs such as semaglutide, is tiny in weight and large in value, so it rewards skill and specialist capacity rather than big reactors.

Why do the material ratios differ so much? Three reasons. The first is the molecule. Metformin, paracetamol and old antibiotics sell for a few hundred rupees a kilogram, and their starting materials are themselves traded commodities, so the API maker adds relatively little value per kilogram. A niche immunosuppressant may sell for tens of thousands of rupees a kilogram and its fermentation media cost very little. The second is the number of steps. Each step a company performs in-house rather than buying an intermediate lowers material cost and raises conversion cost; backward integration shifts rupees from the first line to the third and fourth. The third is mix: companies that also sell formulations, contract services or biologics have lower blended material ratios because those businesses carry more value per rupee of input.

Working capital is the hidden cost. ICRA puts the industry's net working capital at 29–31% of operating income since FY23, and it was rebuilt in FY24 when Red Sea shipping disruptions made buyers and makers hold more stock. An API maker keeps two to four months of starting materials as safety stock, holds work-in-progress in long multi-step syntheses, and stores finished batches while the customer runs its own quality tests before release. Export receivables are typically 60–120 days (indicative). The consequence is that every extra ₹100 of revenue ties up about ₹30 of cash before it is collected, which is why growth in this sub-sector consumes cash even when margins are good.

The China triangle: supplier, price-setter and reason to exist Deep dive

China sells Indian API makers their inputs, sets the price ceiling on their outputs and is the reason Western buyers want them. Which of the three roles dominates decides each company's margin.

No other sub-sector in this library depends on a single foreign country in so many ways at once. For an Indian API maker, China is first the supplier: it makes most of the world's fermentation starting materials (penicillin-G, 6-APA, 7-ACA, clavulanic acid), most vitamins and a large share of chemical starting materials and intermediates. It is second the price-setter: Chinese export quotes act as the ceiling on what an Indian commodity API can fetch, because any customer can buy from China instead. And it is third the reason to exist: Western drug companies, spurred by COVID shortages, by US legislation and by their own risk committees, want a qualified supplier outside China, and India is the obvious place to find one. Every company in this group is a bet on which of those three roles matters most for its molecules.

Price-setter: the 2025 price war

The second role became brutal after 2022. As the COVID safety-stock build unwound, Chinese makers with spare capacity cut prices across commodity APIs. Paracetamol, which had risen from roughly ₹250–350 a kilogram before the pandemic to ₹700–900 at the 2020 peak, was about 53% below that peak by August 2024 (Business Standard, indicative). Then, from February 2025, Chinese exporters cut prices on the 41 molecules targeted by India's incentive scheme by 40–50%; industry insiders quoted by The Ken said Chinese prices were "in some cases" below the Indian cost of production. Industry participants read the timing as an attempt to undercut the scheme-funded plants that had started production in November 2024. Whatever the motive, the effect was to make several of those new plants uneconomic within months of commissioning.

Why can China do this? Its cost advantages are structural: integrated chemical parks where the starting material, the intermediate and the API are made within a few kilometres of each other; cheaper coal-fired power and steam; state-supported land and utilities; and scale that dwarfs any single Indian plant. In commodity fermentation (penicillin-G, 6-APA, 7-ACA), industry commentary puts Chinese costs about 15–30% below Indian ones (indicative). China also controls the timing: when its plants have spare capacity, the export price falls, and Indian makers who bought starting materials at the old price see their spread squeezed from both sides. When China's own environmental enforcement tightens, as it did in 2016–18, or its factories shut, as in early 2020, prices spike and Indian makers enjoy a windfall they did not plan for.

In plain terms

Imagine buying flour from the baker down the road, making bread with it, and then trying to sell your bread to customers who could also buy from that same baker. When the baker wants to, it can raise the price of flour or lower the price of bread, and you lose either way. That is the position of an Indian maker of a commodity API. The ways out are to make something the Chinese baker does not make well, to make your own flour, or to sell to customers who are willing to pay more precisely because you are not the Chinese baker.

Reason to exist: China+1 is real but slow

The third role is the one Indian companies market. Western buyers learned in 2020 that a supply chain running through Hubei could stop overnight, and the 2018 valsartan recall, traced to nitrosamine contamination at Zhejiang Huahai, had already made regulators and buyers wary of some Chinese sources. The US BIOSECURE Act, signed into law on 18 December 2025, restricts US federal links with designated "biotechnology companies of concern", which pushes innovators to move contract work away from certain Chinese groups. The effect shows up most clearly in contract manufacturing enquiries rather than in generic API prices. Qualifying a new API source for an existing generic takes perhaps 12–24 months and a regulatory filing, so China+1 shifts market share slowly, one product and one customer at a time.

The regulatory filing data show China fighting back on this front too. India holds about 48% of all active US drug master files for APIs, against China's 18% (2024, US Pharmacopeia analysis). But on new filings China overtook India in 2024, with about 45% of submissions against India's 43%, down from India's 62% in 2021; China reached 49.5% of submissions in the third quarter of 2024 (PharmaCompass). New filings today are tomorrow's qualified supply, so this is the slow-moving threat to India's paper moat, covered further in Section 9. China is also moving up the value chain into peptides: its peptide hormone exports rose 81.7% in 2025 and its peptide API imports 47.4%, a sign of a fast-growing domestic peptide industry.

Which side of the triangle each company stands on

Put the three roles together and the group sorts itself. Aarti Drugs is the most exposed to the first two roles and the least protected by the third: it buys Chinese starting materials, sells molecules whose price China sets, and its regulated-market upgrade is gradual. Viyash and the legacy API lines at Laurus sit nearby. Aarti Pharmalabs is unusual: in caffeine, China is its main competitor, but being one of the few non-Chinese caffeine makers at scale is itself a selling point to beverage companies. Alivus and Supriya use China for inputs but sell into regulated markets on supply security, so the price-setter role bites less. Concord, Cohance's niche API and contract lines, Shilpa's oncology APIs and Laurus's contract business are the least priced off China and collect most of the China+1 benefit. The market's valuation ladder follows the same order, which is the clearest evidence that investors think the same way.

There is one more twist. China's 2025 trade data show that its penicillin exports fell 23.8% while India's incentive-funded penicillin-G plant ramped up. That is the first measurable import substitution the scheme has produced. It is also why the industry has now asked for anti-dumping duties: the substitution is fragile at current Chinese prices. The next section examines what the scheme cost, what it delivered and what trade defence can add.

In plain terms

India still files most of the paperwork that lets an API be sold in America, but China now files more new paperwork each year. China supplies almost three-quarters of the basic ingredients India imports, and that share went up, not down, after the government started paying companies to make those ingredients at home. Companies that make something China does not make well, or that are hired by Western drug inventors who want to avoid China, are the winners. Companies that make the same commodities China makes are at its mercy.

PLI and trade defence: ₹5,211 crore in, ₹3,792 crore of sales out

The 2020 incentive scheme built plants for a handful of choke-point molecules. It has not reduced India's dependence on China, and its newest plants now need tariff protection to survive Chinese pricing.

In July 2020, with Chinese supply still disrupted, the government approved a production-linked incentive (PLI) scheme for bulk drugs with an outlay of ₹6,940 crore, covering 41 products in four target segments: two fermentation-based (where China's grip was tightest) and two chemical. Companies committing to minimum capacities would receive an incentive on incremental sales for six years. A separate ₹3,000 crore scheme funded three bulk-drug parks, in Bharuch (Gujarat), Una (Himachal Pradesh) and Kakinada (Andhra Pradesh), with shared effluent treatment and pre-cleared environmental permits. A third scheme, the ₹15,000 crore PLI for pharmaceuticals, rewarded high-value formulations, biopharma and complex generics; it matters here because some of the nine are in it.

Six years on, the bulk-drug scheme's scorecard (Department of Pharmaceuticals data reported by Business Standard, 25 September 2026) reads as follows. Forty-eight projects were approved with committed investment of ₹4,330 crore. Actual investment to June 2026 was ₹5,210.74 crore, 120% of the commitment. The verdict is mixed, and it should be stated plainly. An incentive on sales is little help when the sales are loss-making.

In plain terms

The government paid companies to build factories for the handful of ingredients India could not make at all, and they got built. But China responded by cutting its prices on those exact ingredients by up to half, so the new factories struggle to sell at a profit. India now makes some penicillin again, which matters in a crisis, but it still buys almost three-quarters of its imported ingredients from China. The industry has gone back to the government asking for import duties to protect the factories the subsidy paid for.

Trade defence: the September 2026 probes

On 29 September 2026 the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) opened four pharmaceutical anti-dumping investigations, with a period of investigation from April 2025 to March 2026. The applicants are telling: three of the four are the owners of scheme-funded or large domestic plants. The amoxycillin case is the most detailed in the public notice: Apitoria (Aurobindo's API arm) holds more than 35% of Indian production, Apitoria and Centrient together more than 60%, and DGTR found prima facie evidence of dumping and injury. Earlier recommendations on APIs have sometimes been left un-notified because they collide with formulators' interest in cheap inputs.

In plain terms

Anti-dumping duties are taxes on imports that a government judges to be sold below a fair price. India has started investigating four Chinese drug ingredients. If duties follow, the Indian factories making those ingredients will be able to charge more, and the companies that buy them, including medicine makers, will pay more. None of the nine companies in this report is directly involved, but a decision either way will tell the industry whether India is prepared to protect the factories its subsidy built.

Thousands of plants, a handful of suppliers per molecule

The industry is fragmented by plant and concentrated by molecule. The real competitive unit is not the company but the product: how many qualified suppliers a given API has worldwide.

India has thousands of API and intermediate units, clustered where state industrial estates, solvent supply and effluent infrastructure were built: Hyderabad's belt (Patancheru, Bollaram, Jeedimetla, Pashamylaram) in Telangana; Visakhapatnam, Kakinada and Srikakulam in Andhra Pradesh; Ankleshwar, Dahej, Vapi and Bharuch in Gujarat; and Tarapur, Lote, Kurkumbh and Mohol in Maharashtra. Pharmexcil counted 748 USFDA-registered sites (bulk drugs and formulations together) and 348 EU-GMP-registered sites in 2024. Most units are small and serve the domestic market; the revised Schedule M manufacturing standard, whose deadline for smaller firms was 31 December 2025, is pushing the weakest of them out.

The nine listed companies in this report are not the whole listed universe. Divi's Laboratories, the global benchmark in generic API and custom synthesis with FY26 revenue of ₹10,560 crore and a 32.6% EBITDA margin, is classified elsewhere in this library because its custom-synthesis business is so large, but it competes directly with several of the nine. So do Granules (paracetamol, metformin, ibuprofen), IOL Chemicals (ibuprofen), Neuland, Solara, Hikal, Dishman Carbogen, Ipca's API arm and Alembic's API business (about 16% of its sales). Large integrated formulators make captive API too: Aurobindo's Apitoria and Lyfius, Sun, Lupin and Cipla. Why do the economics differ so much? The first reason is who sets the price. Paper moats: DMFs, CEPs and the inspector at the gate Deep dive

An API is only saleable to a regulated customer once a dossier describing it is on file and the plant that makes it has passed inspection. That paperwork is the industry's real moat, and a bad inspection turns it into a trap.

Two kilograms of the same API, chemically identical, can be worth very different amounts. One is made in a plant whose process is described in a drug master file that a US generic company has referenced in its own approval, and which the US Food and Drug Administration has inspected without serious findings. The other is not. The first can be sold to that customer for years; the second cannot be sold to it at all. This is why API makers spend heavily on regulatory filings and on quality systems, and why a single inspection outcome can move a company's share price more than a quarter's earnings.

How the filings work

In the United States, an API maker files a Type II drug master file (DMF) with the FDA: a confidential dossier describing how and where the API is made, its impurity profile and its stability. A DMF is not "approved" on its own. It must pass a completeness assessment and pay a one-time fee under the generic drug user fee programme (roughly $90,000–100,000 in recent fee schedules, from memory), and it is then reviewed when a customer's abbreviated new drug application (ANDA) references it. In Europe the equivalent is a Certificate of Suitability (CEP) from the European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines (EDQM), which certifies that the API meets the European Pharmacopoeia monograph and can be referenced across EU and other CEP-accepting markets; EDQM inspects sites too. Japan, Korea, China and Brazil have their own master files, and WHO prequalification (APIMF) matters for donor-funded tenders, which is how Laurus built its antiretroviral business.

Why does a filing create stickiness? Because the customer's own approval names the API source. A US generic formulator that wants to switch from one API supplier to another must file a supplement with the FDA, often a prior-approval supplement, and produce new stability data on batches made with the new API. That takes months, costs money, and carries the risk that the regulator asks questions. For a molecule where the API is a small part of the tablet's cost, the formulator will rarely bother to save a few percent. For a commodity molecule where API is most of the cost and a Chinese supplier offers 20% less, it might. The moat is therefore deepest where the API's price is a small share of the customer's product value: niche molecules, oncology, injectables, and innovator drugs made under contract.

Counting filings is a weak measure of the moat. Alivus has more than 611 DMFs and CEPs across markets; Aarti Drugs has 60 US DMFs and 44 CEPs on 61 commercial APIs; Supriya about 15 US DMFs and 10 CEPs, from a ratings rationale and a filings database. The other six do not disclose a comparable count. What matters more is the quality of the filings: whether the company filed early in a molecule's generic life (first-wave filers win the launch contracts), whether the molecules are complex (fewer rivals), and how many active customers reference each filing. None of the nine discloses referencing counts.

The inspection ladder

The filing is half the moat; the plant is the other half. The FDA inspects foreign API sites, increasingly without advance notice since its May 2025 policy shift. At the end of an inspection, investigators may issue a Form 483 listing observations. The agency then classifies the inspection: NAI (no action indicated), VAI (voluntary action indicated, the company fixes things itself) or OAI (official action indicated). An OAI can lead to a warning letter, and the most serious cases to an import alert (66-40), which stops shipments from the site into the United States. A site under an OAI or a warning letter typically gets no new approvals referencing it until the matter is resolved, often for 18–36 months. The European, British and Japanese regulators run parallel regimes.

In plain terms

Selling a drug chemical to a careful buyer is like being on an approved-vendor list that the buyer has registered with the government. Getting on the list takes years and paperwork, and once you are on it, the buyer has to do more paperwork to take you off, so customers tend to stay. But the government inspector can visit your factory at any time. A bad inspection can freeze new business at that site for two or three years, and the worst outcome stops all shipments to America. That is why a clean inspection report is often worth more to an API maker than a new reactor.

Nitrosamines and the rising cost of compliance

Compliance costs keep rising, and that favours scale. The July 2018 recall of valsartan, after a carcinogenic nitrosamine impurity (NDMA) was traced to a process change at Zhejiang Huahai in China, led regulators worldwide to require every API process to be risk-assessed for nitrosamines. That meant new testing, sometimes process changes and refiling, and more analytical capability in every plant. India's revised Schedule M, notified on 28 December 2023 and aligned with WHO and PIC/S good manufacturing practice, applies to API units too; smaller firms had until 31 December 2025 to comply. Each tightening raises the fixed cost of staying qualified, which squeezes small makers and pushes customers towards larger, better-documented suppliers. That is good for the listed nine relative to the unorganised tail, as long as their own plants keep passing.

The moat is also contestable. China's share of new US DMF filings overtook India's in 2024, as shown in Section 5. A new Chinese filing does not immediately take business from an Indian supplier, but it gives customers a second qualified option for the next generic launch. Over five to ten years, that erodes the scarcity of qualified supply on which regulated and niche makers' margins rest. The defence is to keep filing early and in harder molecules, and to keep plants clean: the companies with the cleanest recent inspection records (Alivus, Concord, Supriya, Laurus) are also the ones with the highest margins or the most aggressive capacity plans.

In plain terms

The rules keep getting stricter, so staying on the approved list costs more every year. Big, well-run companies can absorb that; small ones cannot, which helps the listed companies. But China is now putting more new chemicals on American approved lists than India is, so the advantage Indian companies have built over thirty years is slowly being contested.

Permits, water and single sites: the physical gates

An API block needs an environmental clearance, an effluent system, water, power and a stable plant. These are slow to obtain and fast to lose, and several of the nine concentrate their output in one or two places.

API manufacturing is chemistry at industrial scale, and it produces solvent vapours, acidic and saline effluent and hazardous solid waste. That makes it one of the most tightly permitted activities in Indian industry. Under the Environmental Impact Assessment Notification of 2006, synthetic organic chemicals including bulk drugs and intermediates are a Category A activity (item 5(f)): a new project needs central environmental clearance from the Ministry of Environment, usually after a public hearing. In March and April 2020 the ministry temporarily moved API projects into a faster category to speed up COVID-era capacity (from memory; the exact notification was not re-verified). Getting a clearance for a new site typically takes 12–24 months (indicative). Then come state consents to establish and to operate, construction, equipment qualification, validation batches and the regulatory filings and inspections described in the previous section.

Plant incidents are the third gate. On 14 February 2026 a fire at Alivus's Dahej site was confined to one of seven production blocks; production resumed around 1 March. In plain terms

Building a new drug-chemical factory in India needs government environmental approval that can take two years, plus a costly system to clean every drop of waste water. So companies prefer to expand on sites they already have. That makes them efficient but concentrated: if the water runs out, the power price jumps or a block catches fire at the one site that matters, a quarter's profits can disappear. Supriya lost a third of its usual margin in one quarter because its only factory ran short of water.

For an investor these gates have two implications. First, they are a real barrier to new competition: an entrant cannot simply build a plant next year if prices rise, which is part of why niche makers hold their margins. Second, they are a risk that does not show up in financial ratios until it happens. A useful habit is to ask, for each company, what share of output comes from its largest site, whether that site has its own water source and captive power, and how much headroom its environmental clearance leaves for expansion. Only some of the nine answer those questions publicly.

Five decades, four price shocks

From process patents to PLI: India built an API industry on reverse engineering, lost its fermentation base to China, rediscovered its dependence in 2020, rode a boom and then a long bust. FY27 is the first recovery year.

India's API industry was created by a law. The Patents Act of 1970 allowed only process patents on drugs, so Indian chemists could legally make any molecule by a different route. State-owned IDPL and Hindustan Antibiotics seeded fermentation and synthesis skills, and companies such as Ranbaxy, Cipla and Dr Reddy's (founded in 1984 as a bulk-drug maker) grew on API exports. The US Hatch-Waxman Act of 1984 created the generic approval pathway, which needed cheap, filed APIs, and Indian makers became the generic world's supplier. Aarti Drugs (1984), Shilpa (1987, as an oncology-API specialist), Divi's (1990), the API arm of Glenmark that became Alivus, Suven (now Cohance) and later Laurus (2005) all date from this era.

Between 2000 and 2015 China took over the low end. Its integrated chemical parks, cheap coal-fired power, state support and looser effluent enforcement made it the low-cost source of fermentation starting materials (penicillin-G, 6-APA, 7-ACA) and many chemical starting materials. India's own penicillin plants closed or were mothballed, and import dependence climbed to about 68% from China by FY19. Two shocks rehearsed what was to come: the 2008 heparin contamination crisis, traced to a Chinese source, put API supply chains on the US agenda; and China's 2016–18 environmental crackdown shut plants and spiked API and starting-material prices. The 2018 valsartan nitrosamine recall began the impurity era.

Whether this is a durable upswing or a restocking bounce is the central question for the next four quarters, and the leading indicators in Section 20 are designed to answer it.

In plain terms

India's drug-chemical makers had a bonanza in 2020–21 when Chinese supply stopped and customers panicked, then three lean years as customers ran down the stock they had hoarded and China cut prices to win back business. FY26 was the low point for most of the nine. The first three months of FY27 were the best quarter in years, with sales up about a fifth. The question now is whether customers are genuinely buying more or just refilling empty shelves.

Reading an API maker: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Every number that matters in an API business follows from one equation: kilograms sold, times the spread between price and input cost, minus the cost of running the plant. This section teaches each term, how to compute it, what good looks like and where the nine stand.

An API maker's results can look bewildering: revenue up 20% and profit down, or revenue flat and profit up a third. The confusion disappears once the business is written as an equation. Revenue is the sum, across every molecule the company sells, of kilograms sold times the realised price per kilogram. Kilograms sold cannot exceed the plant's capacity, which is reactor volume times the number of batches a year times the yield per batch, times how full the plant actually runs. Gross profit is kilograms times the spread: the realised price minus the cost of the starting materials, intermediates and solvents per kilogram. EBITDA is gross profit minus conversion cost (power, steam, unrecovered solvent, labour, effluent treatment, quality control) and overheads. Return on capital is EBITDA less depreciation, divided by the plant plus the working capital it ties up.

Management controls volume (by winning qualifications and filling plant), conversion cost (yield, solvent recovery, utilisation) and mix. It does not control realisation or input cost in a commodity molecule; it partly controls realisation in niche, fermentation and contract molecules, where it is one of few qualified suppliers.

Three features of this equation drive everything that follows. First, the spread is levered: in a commodity API, input cost is often 60% or more of the price, so a 10% fall in price with unchanged input cost can remove most of the gross profit. Second, utilisation is levered: most conversion cost is fixed per batch slot, so running a plant at 85% instead of 65% lowers cost per kilogram sharply. Third, mix compounds: each step up the ladder from commodity to regulated generic to niche to contract raises the price per kilogram far faster than it raises input cost, which is why companies chase mix even at the expense of volume.

In plain terms

A metformin maker sells each kilogram for about ₹280 and pays about ₹175 for the ingredients, leaving ₹105. Running the plant costs another ₹67, so it keeps about ₹38. If the selling price drops by just ₹28, a tenth, and the ingredient price stays put, the profit per kilogram falls from ₹38 to ₹10. That is why a small price war wipes out most of a commodity API maker's profit, and why a fuller factory is worth so much: the same running costs spread over more kilograms.

Spread per kilogram

What it is. The difference between the realised price of an API and the cost of the materials that go into a kilogram of it. Formula: spread = realisation per kg − (starting materials + intermediates + reagents + net solvent cost) per kg. Worked example: ₹280 − ₹175 = ₹105 a kilogram in the metformin line above. What drives it: Chinese export prices for the API (the ceiling) and for its starting materials (the floor); the number of chemical steps done in-house; yield (kilograms of API per kilogram of starting material); solvent recovery, which at 85–95% (indicative) turns a large cost into a modest one. Benchmarks: what matters is the direction, not the level: a stable or widening spread is good, a compressing one (as in FY23–FY25) is weak.

How the nine compare. No company discloses spread by molecule. The best public proxies are gross margin, where it is company-reported, and the screener material-cost ratio, which is a cruder raw-material-only measure. Aarti Drugs's material cost of about 62% of sales is the clearest sign of a thin-spread business; Supriya's and Shilpa's 30–31% the clearest sign of wide ones. Definition trap: a falling material-cost ratio does not always mean a wider spread. If the company sells more formulations or contract services, the blended ratio falls even if every API spread is unchanged.

Capacity utilisation

What it is. Output as a share of what the installed plant could produce. Formula: utilisation = kilograms produced ÷ (reactor KL × batches a year × kg per KL-batch × yield). In practice, companies speak of "batch slots" or "block utilisation". Worked example: 10,920 tonnes ÷ 16,800 tonnes = 65% for the metformin line; at Aarti Drugs's FY26 anti-diabetic revenue of about ₹297 crore and an assumed ₹280 per kilogram, that is about 10,600 tonnes, about 63% of metformin capacity (calculated, on an estimated price). What drives it: order wins and losses, customer destocking, new capacity coming on before demand, plant shutdowns for maintenance, fires, water or power shortages, and regulatory holds. Benchmarks: above 80% good; 65–80% average; below 60% weak (indicative). Industry utilisation after the 2021 capacity build is estimated at 60–75% (indicative; no official series exists).

How the nine compare. None of the nine reports a utilisation figure for the whole company. Cohance names "better plant utilisation" as a lever for its second-half FY27 recovery; Supriya's first quarter showed what 15–20 lost days do to a single-site maker. Definition trap: utilisation is meaningless without the denominator. A company that adds a block and counts it in capacity before it is validated will report falling utilisation even as output rises; one that counts only "commercial" blocks may report high utilisation on a shrinking base.

Revenue per kilolitre of reactor

What it is. A native measure of how valuable each unit of installed reactor volume is. Formula: revenue ÷ installed reactor (or fermenter) volume in kilolitres. Worked example: Alivus's FY26 revenue of ₹2,552 crore ÷ about 1,424 KL = about ₹1.8 crore per KL. Laurus's ₹6,813 crore ÷ about 7,900 KL = about ₹0.86 crore per KL, but that figure includes formulations and contract revenue that do not all run through API reactors. Concord's ₹1,055 crore ÷ 1,250 cubic metres of fermentation = about ₹0.84 crore per cubic metre, on a 2022 capacity figure that understates today's base. What drives it: the value per kilogram of the molecules, batch frequency (shorter cycle times mean more batches), yield, and utilisation. Benchmarks: above ₹1.5 crore per KL good; ₹0.8–1.5 crore average; below ₹0.6 crore weak (indicative).

What it is. Revenue generated per rupee of fixed assets, and the share of revenue reinvested in new plant. Formula: asset turnover = revenue ÷ gross block; capex intensity = capex ÷ revenue. Working capital: inventory and receivable days

What it is. The cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, net of what the company owes suppliers. Formula: inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365; receivable days = receivables ÷ revenue × 365; net working capital % = (inventory + receivables − payables) ÷ revenue. Worked example: at the industry's 30%, a company growing revenue by ₹500 crore needs about ₹150 crore more working capital. What drives it: starting-material safety stock (2–4 months), long multi-step syntheses, customer release testing, export credit terms. Benchmarks: inventory below 90 days good, 90–150 average, above 150 weak; receivables below 70 days good, 70–110 average, above 120 weak; net working capital below 25% of revenue good, 25–32% average (ICRA's industry figure is 29–31%), above 35% weak. How the nine compare: not captured in the fact packs (n/a). Definition trap: factoring or bill discounting can make receivable days look short while moving the cost into interest.

Definition traps, collected

  • Gross margin. A screener "100 minus material cost" figure is not a gross margin and overstates it. Use company-reported gross margin or label the proxy as material cost.
  • EBITDA. Company EBITDA may exclude share-based pay (Viyash), investment-phase costs (Concord adjusted), forex or other operating income (Alivus 33.6% vs 31.3%). State the basis.
  • Net debt. Calculated net debt from screener subtracts all investments, some of which may not be liquid; company figures differ (Shilpa ~₹620 Cr vs ₹557 Cr calculated; Aarti Pharmalabs ~₹680 Cr vs ₹550 Cr; Viyash ~₹166 Cr vs ₹220 Cr). This report prefers company figures.
  • "Regulated" share. Some companies count regulated-grade material sold in India; others count only exports to the US, Europe and Japan.
  • API share. Segments are drawn differently: Laurus reports API and generic formulations together; Aarti Pharmalabs separates xanthines; Viyash's formulations include animal-health products.
  • CDMO share. Aarti Pharmalabs's contract business is ~7% of the Q1 mix on one definition and ₹276 crore (~15% of FY26 revenue) on the company's CDMO definition; quote the rupee figure.
  • Reactor volume. KL of chemistry, cubic metres of fermentation and high-containment suites are not interchangeable; capacity figures may include unqualified blocks.
  • Utilisation. The denominator changes when new blocks are counted; falling utilisation can mean rising output.
  • ROCE. Build-phase capital depresses it; "adjusted" ROCE that excludes unproductive capital flatters it.
  • Growth rates. Merged companies restate prior years (Viyash FY25 restated; FY24 legacy SeQuent not comparable; Cohance's FY25 profit base differs by source).
  • Tax. Low tax rates flatter profit growth (Aarti Drugs ~8% in FY26; Shilpa's Q1 FY27 tax reversal).
  • Basis. Alivus and Supriya are standalone, the rest consolidated.
In plain terms

Almost every headline number in this industry has a catch: margins calculated by websites that leave out costs, debt figures that count investments the company cannot easily sell, "regulated" sales that are partly domestic, and growth rates distorted by mergers. None of these catches is fraud; they are just different definitions. The habit that protects you is to ask, for every number, "on whose definition, and compared with what?"

How to read an API maker's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Revenue growth and its split (1 minute). Pull revenue by segment: API, formulations, CDMO, other. Is growth coming from the higher-value line? Red flag: total growth driven only by the lowest-margin segment.

  2. Gross margin, company-reported (1 minute). Compare with the same quarter last year and the previous quarter. This is the best real-time read of the spread. Red flag: gross margin down while revenue is up, which means price or mix is slipping.

  3. EBITDA margin and its basis (1 minute). Check whether the company's EBITDA adds back ESOP, forex or "investment-phase" cost. Red flag: a growing gap between reported and adjusted EBITDA.

  4. Volume versus price commentary (1 minute). Management usually says whether growth came from volume or realisation. Volume-led growth with stable prices is healthier than price-led growth in a restocking quarter.

  5. Utilisation and capacity (1 minute). Note any new block commissioned, its validation status and stated utilisation. Red flag: depreciation stepping up with no matching revenue.

  6. Customer and molecule concentration (1 minute). Any mention of destocking, "shipment phasing" or a single large order. In contract work, ask which molecules drove the quarter.

  7. Regulatory events (1 minute). Form 483s, EIRs, warning letters, import alerts, EU or WHO inspections since last quarter. Red flag: data-integrity observations, or a repeat observation at the same site.

  8. Plant and utility events (1 minute). Fires, water or power shortages, effluent notices. Single-site companies deserve extra attention here.

  9. Working capital and debt (1 minute). Inventory and receivables against revenue; net debt movement. Red flag: net debt rising faster than capex explains, or inventory building ahead of soft demand.

  10. Guidance and the China read (1 minute). What does management say about Chinese pricing for its key molecules and starting materials, and has it kept or changed annual guidance? Supriya keeping its ₹1,000 crore, 33–35% guidance after a 25% margin quarter is an example of what to weigh.

In plain terms

When the quarterly numbers come out, look first at which business line grew, then at whether the gap between selling prices and ingredient costs widened or narrowed, then at whether any factory had an inspection, an accident or a utility problem. Those three checks explain most surprises in this industry. Profit growth that comes from a tax refund, a currency gain or an "adjusted" margin should be set aside until the underlying business confirms it.

Biology and new chemistry: fermentation, peptides, ADC payloads and contract work Deep dive

The way out of the commodity spread is to make something that is hard to copy. Four routes are open to Indian API makers, each with its own economics, its own capital needs and its own Chinese competitor.

Every company in this group is trying to move away from commodity chemistry. The reason is the margin ladder in Section 8: each step away from a molecule anyone can make roughly doubles the margin available, and each step makes the customer less likely to switch suppliers. Four routes stand out. Fermentation uses biology rather than chemistry and rewards proprietary microbial strains. Peptides, led by the GLP-1 drugs, need specialised synthesis and purification. High-potency molecules, including the cytotoxic payloads attached to antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), need containment facilities few plants have. And contract development and manufacturing for innovators sells the whole capability under a long-term agreement. This section takes each in turn.

Fermentation: a biology moat

A fermentation API is made by growing a microbe, a bacterium or fungus, in large stirred tanks, feeding it sugars and nutrients until it produces the target molecule, then extracting and purifying that molecule. Concord Biotech is the group's specialist, with about 1,250 cubic metres of fermentation capacity at Dholka (450) and Limbasi (800) at the time of its 2022 offer document, 41 production blocks and 387 reactors; post-listing expansions are not captured in that figure. It makes immunosuppressants used to prevent organ rejection and treat autoimmune disease, tacrolimus, sirolimus, everolimus, cyclosporine and mycophenolate, plus the antibiotic mupirocin and oncology molecules such as romidepsin. It claimed more than a 20% volume share across its identified fermentation APIs in 2022 and an estimated 6–7% of the global immunosuppressant API market.

The economics differ from chemistry in two ways. First, the key asset is the strain: its titre, the grams of product per litre of broth, determines how many kilograms each fermenter yields per cycle. A strain that produces 20% more product cuts the media and fermenter time per kilogram by about a sixth, and that saving drops almost directly into gross margin. Strains are developed over years and are proprietary, which is why fermentation niches have few global suppliers. Second, the inputs are biological (sugars, nitrogen sources, trace elements) rather than Chinese chemical intermediates, so fermentation makers are less exposed to the starting-material side of the China triangle. Concord's company-reported gross margin of 78.9% in the first quarter of FY27 is the clearest sign of that structure.

In plain terms

Some drug chemicals are grown rather than built: a microbe in a big tank makes the molecule, and the company harvests it. The secret is the microbe. A better strain makes more product from the same tank, and good strains take years to develop, so few companies in the world can make these medicines well. Concord is India's listed specialist, and it keeps almost 80 paise of every rupee of sales after paying for ingredients.

Peptides and GLP-1: grams, not tonnes

Peptides are short chains of amino acids. The most valuable of them today are the GLP-1 agonists, semaglutide and tirzepatide, used for diabetes and obesity. They are made by solid-phase peptide synthesis (building the chain one amino acid at a time on a resin bead), by liquid-phase or hybrid methods, or by recombinant expression in microbes followed by chemical modification. Which route wins at scale is still being decided. What is clear is that the tonnage is tiny and the value per kilogram enormous: ten million patient-years of semaglutide need only about 1.25 tonnes of API, against thousands of tonnes for a commodity pill. That makes peptide capacity a matter of specialised synthesisers, purification columns and analytical capability rather than reactor kilolitres.

The opportunity is real and close. Semaglutide's core Indian patent expired on 20 March 2026 and generics launched the next day, at 70–90% below the innovator's price; Canada, Brazil and China open in 2026–27 and the US, Europe and Japan in 2031–32. Laurus is qualifying commercial-scale peptide facilities in calendar 2026 for innovator clients; Alivus has peptides in development. Neither discloses peptide revenue. China is moving faster: its peptide hormone exports rose 81.7% in 2025 and its peptide API imports 47.4%, which suggests both a large domestic industry and a hunger for inputs. GLP-1 peptide API prices are not published; this report does not estimate them.

High-potency molecules and ADC payloads

Antibody-drug conjugates link a monoclonal antibody to a highly toxic small molecule, the payload, through a chemical linker. The antibody finds the cancer cell; the payload kills it. Payloads are among the most potent molecules made in chemical plants, often active at microgram doses, so they need high-containment suites, isolators and specialised handling that most API plants lack. That scarcity, plus the small volumes and very high value per gram, is why payload and linker chemistry commands gross margins well above ordinary APIs (70–80% is commonly cited in the industry; indicative). Cohance has built a niche here: camptothecin and s-Trione payloads, a broad set of linkers, an oligonucleotide block, a collaboration with Ajinomoto on its AJICAP conjugation technology, and a cGMP bioconjugation suite at NJ Bio in Princeton (a $10 million expansion, able to make up to about 2 kg of ADC through Phase 2b), which ran five GMP bioconjugation batches in FY26.

Shilpa approaches the same field from oncology APIs, which it has made since 1987 and which are themselves high-potency molecules handled under containment. Its biologics campus at Dharwad is EU-GMP-approved and has added an ADC unit; it has a partnership with Orion of Finland on a nivolumab biosimilar, and expects its first ADC biosimilar to enter clinical trials in FY27. Both companies' ADC positions are small in revenue today. The market's valuation of them (Cohance at 159 times trailing earnings, Shilpa at 71 times) is in large part a valuation of these options.

In plain terms

An antibody-drug conjugate is a cancer medicine that works like a guided missile: an antibody carries a tiny amount of an extremely toxic chemical straight to the tumour. Making that toxic chemical safely needs sealed rooms and special equipment that few factories have, so the few that can do it charge a lot. Cohance and Shilpa are building this capability. It earns little today, but much of their share price depends on it.

Contract manufacturing: selling the capability

Contract development and manufacturing (CDMO) sells an innovator the whole package: process development, scale-up, regulatory documentation and long-term commercial supply of an API or intermediate for a patented drug. The customer names the supplier in its new-drug filing, so switching is close to impossible for the life of the molecule. Margins are typically 25–35% at EBITDA and revenue is stickier than generic APIs, but it is lumpy, tied to a few molecules and to the client's inventory decisions. India holds only 2–3% of a global market of about $140–145 billion; the US and Europe hold about 70% and China about 20%. The BIOSECURE Act and Western buyers' China+1 policies are pushing work towards India, and Indian contract revenue has grown about 15% a year.

Technology and disruption: what could change the spread

Flow chemistry, enzymes, better microbes and new peptide routes can each rewrite an API's cost curve. So can a more aggressive China, a more protectionist United States and a thirstier India.

API manufacturing looks traditional, with batch reactors, solvents and crystallisers, but its cost curve can shift quickly when a new process arrives. The most important technology and policy disruptions for the nine are set out below. Some favour scale and skill and therefore the stronger listed companies; others threaten precisely the moats described earlier in this report. None of them is likely to change the industry within a year. Several could change which companies earn the best spreads within five.

  • Continuous-flow manufacturing. Instead of filling a reactor, reacting and emptying it, flow chemistry pumps reagents through small, controlled channels. It cuts solvent use, footprint and failed batches, and the FDA encourages it (ICH Q13 guidance, 2022). Early adopters gain cost and quality advantages, and installed batch kilolitres become a less meaningful measure of capacity. That would weaken revenue-per-KL comparisons and favour companies that invest in process engineering.
  • Biocatalysis and green chemistry. Enzymes can replace several chemical steps with one, as in the well-known enzymatic route to sitagliptin. A successful enzymatic route can make a multi-step chemical process, and the starting materials it consumes, obsolete. It helps companies with biology skills and hurts those whose edge is cheap execution of a long chemical route.
  • Strain engineering and synthetic biology. Higher-titre microbes can collapse the cost of fermentation APIs. That is both the biggest opportunity and the biggest threat for Concord and for Laurus's fermentation ambitions: whoever engineers the best strain for a molecule can undercut everyone else.
  • Peptide manufacturing routes. Solid-phase, liquid-phase, hybrid and recombinant routes compete to make GLP-1 peptides at scale. The winning route will decide who captures the generic GLP-1 opportunity from 2026 onwards; China is scaling quickly.
  • Impurity and nitrosamine rules. Each tightening raises testing and documentation costs, favouring larger, better-equipped makers over the unorganised tail.
  • US onshoring. An August 2025 executive order on a Strategic API Reserve, FDA incentives for domestic manufacturing, and a Commerce Department review of whether to put Section 232 tariffs on generic drugs and their ingredients (due within a year of the April 2026 proclamation, which exempted generics) are a long-term risk to India's share of US API supply.
  • Chinese moves up the value chain. China cut prices on India's incentive-scheme molecules by 40–50% in 2025, now files more new US DMFs than India, and is building peptide capacity fast.
  • Environmental tightening in India. Zero-liquid-discharge mandates, moratoriums in critically polluted areas and water scarcity (Supriya's Lote, first quarter of FY27) raise the cost and difficulty of growth.
  • Customer consolidation and destocking. Fewer, larger buyers manage inventory aggressively; contract molecules can destock abruptly, as at Cohance in FY26.

Who is best placed? Companies with process-engineering and biology depth, Laurus (flow chemistry and fermentation investment), Concord (strains), Cohance (high-potency and conjugation chemistry) and Alivus (a broad, well-documented library), can use new technology to widen their moats. The tightening of environmental and impurity rules is, on balance, good for the listed nine against the unorganised tail.

In plain terms

New ways of making drug chemicals, using enzymes, better microbes or continuous pipes instead of big tanks, can make an old factory uncompetitive almost overnight for a particular medicine. Companies that invest in science and engineering can use these changes to pull ahead; companies that compete only on cheap execution of old recipes are the ones at risk. Meanwhile China is moving into newer, more valuable products, and America is thinking about making more of its own medicine ingredients.

Bull versus bear: the case for and against Indian API makers

The bull case is a structural shift of supply away from China at a cyclical low. The bear case is that China still sets the price, the incentive scheme failed, and the market has already paid for the climb.

The arguments on each side are strong, and they mostly concern the same facts read differently. A bull sees a cyclical trough in FY26, a broad recovery starting in the first quarter of FY27, the first anti-dumping action in favour of domestic plants, and a decade-long shift of Western supply chains away from China. A bear sees a group whose combined revenue grew 2.3% in FY26 once Laurus is removed, a scheme that raised dependence on China rather than lowering it, a price war China can restart at will, and valuations that already assume the best outcome for the companies the market likes most.

The bull case

  • The cycle has turned. Q1 FY27 combined revenue rose about 20%, bulk-drug exports 13.8%, and two of FY26's three decliners rebounded by 26–39%.
  • China+1 is structural. BIOSECURE, nitrosamine scrutiny and COVID memories push Western buyers to qualify Indian sources; India holds 48% of active US API DMFs.
  • The climb is working. Laurus's contract revenue grew 67% in Q1 FY27; Aarti Pharmalabs's contract book is guided up 40–50%; Concord has three new US approvals.
  • Quality at the top is high. Supriya, Concord and Alivus earn 31–36% EBITDA margins, above Europe's leading listed peer, with net cash and clean inspections.
  • Trade defence has arrived. The September 2026 DGTR probes signal that India may protect non-Chinese capacity, which lowers the risk of building it.
  • Capacity is being built at the bottom. Alivus nearly doubles reactor volume by FY28; new blocks reach maturity returns of ~25% if they fill.

The bear case

  • China still sets the price. It supplies 73.7% of India's API imports, cut PLI-molecule prices 40–50% in 2025 and can do so again.
  • PLI did not cut dependence. ₹5,211 crore invested, ₹3,792 crore of cumulative sales, and China's share of imports rose.
  • The paper moat is eroding. China filed more new US DMFs than India in 2024 (45% vs 43%).
  • Valuations assume success. Laurus at 97×, Shilpa at 71× and Cohance at 159× need 23–45% annual earnings growth for five years to reach 25×.
  • Regulatory and plant risk is live. Cohance and Shilpa have open warning letters; Supriya's single site lost 15–20 days to water; Alivus had a fire.
  • Leverage at the weak end. Aarti Drugs, Aarti Pharmalabs and Shilpa carry net debt of 1.4–1.8× EBITDA just as new plants need to fill.

Weighing the two, the evidence supports a split verdict rather than a sector call. The structural case for Indian API makers as a non-Chinese source is real, but it benefits the companies that are least priced off China: niche, fermentation, regulated-market and contract makers. The commodity end of the group remains a cyclical spread business whose fortunes depend on Chinese pricing behaviour and, possibly, on Indian trade defence. The valuation gap inside the group already reflects that split, and in the case of the most richly valued companies it reflects a good deal of future success. The most useful question for any one company is therefore not whether the sector is attractive, but whether that company's position on the China triangle and the margin ladder is improving faster than its share price assumes.

Three tests would settle the argument over the next year. If Q2 and Q3 FY27 confirm the first quarter's growth without another Chinese price cut, the bull's cyclical case is right. If DGTR recommends duties and the Finance Ministry notifies them, the trade-defence case is right, with the cost falling on formulators. And if Cohance recovers in the second half as guided while Laurus's contract growth holds, the market's willingness to pay for the climb will look justified; if either disappoints, the richest multiples are the most exposed.

In plain terms

The optimist says the bad years are over, Western customers are moving business from China to India, and the best Indian companies are more profitable than their European rivals. The pessimist says China still controls the price of the basic ingredients, the government's subsidy did not change that, and the most popular stocks already price in years of success. Both are partly right: the good news mostly helps the specialist companies, while the commodity makers remain at China's mercy.
