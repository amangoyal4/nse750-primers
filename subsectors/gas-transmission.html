00

The one-page view: a regulated toll, an unhedgeable spread and a regulator's pen

Five forces explain almost everything about how gas is moved and sold in India. Here they are before the detail.

India burns about 188 million standard cubic metres of natural gas a day (MMSCMD), roughly 69 billion cubic metres a year. That is less than 2% of the world's gas, and only about 6% of India's own primary energy, against an official goal of 15% by 2030. Half of it is imported as liquefied natural gas (LNG), arriving by ship at eight coastal terminals. Almost all of it then travels through high-pressure steel pipelines to fertiliser plants, city gas networks, power stations, refineries and factories. The business this report covers sits in the middle of that chain: transmission (owning and running the trunk pipelines) and marketing (buying gas from producers and LNG sellers and reselling it to customers).

One company dominates both. GAIL (India) Ltd operates about 18,717 km of gas pipelines, roughly two-thirds of the country's operating network, and sells close to half of the gas India consumes. It is the only listed member of this sub-sector, so the report uses it as a lens, but the subject is the industry: the pipes, the contracts, the regulator, the price rules and the customers. Other listed companies in the gas chain, from the LNG importer Petronet to the city gas distributors, appear as reference points, not as members.

Everything that follows hangs off five forces:

  • The 2026 West Asia war and the LNG/LPG shock. Fighting from 28 February 2026 closed the Strait of Hormuz, and QatarEnergy declared force majeure on 4 March. About 52% of India's LNG came from Qatar and the UAE, and about 94% of its imported cooking gas (LPG) came from the Gulf. Imports collapsed in March to May, yet PPAC data show LNG imports for April–September 2026 ended 4.5% higher than a year earlier: the system re-routed cargoes from the US, Africa and elsewhere rather than going without. Who held flexible, non-Gulf supply decided who profited.
  • The index basis in the marketing book. A gas marketer earns the gap between what it pays and what it charges, and in India those two prices are often set by different indices: American Henry Hub gas prices, Brent crude, a lagged Japanese crude average (JCC) and Asian spot LNG. GAIL's standalone marketing profit has ranged from −₹702 crore (FY21) to ₹7,446 crore (FY25) in six years. Management plans on about ₹4,500 crore for FY27, and earned ₹3,481 crore in Q1 FY27 alone.
  • Regulated returns and the tariff pen. The Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB) sets pipeline tariffs to give a 12% post-tax return on capital over a pipeline's life. It also decides the zones of the national "unified" tariff and when tariffs are reviewed. GAIL's grid tariff rose from ₹58.61 to ₹65.69 per mmBtu from 1 January 2026 under an interim order; the full review takes effect on 1 April 2028. Transmission is about two-thirds of GAIL's normal-year profit.
  • Volume against the 15% goal. Consumption fell 3.6% in FY26, domestic production is lower than in FY14, and about 72% of what flows through GAIL's pipes is imported. City gas is the one sector growing reliably (+9.7% in FY26). Pipelines built for the 15% target are running far below capacity: GAIL's integrated network averaged 67% in FY26 and its newest eastern lines under 1%.
  • Price administration and the petrochemical re-mix. The government caps the price of old-field domestic gas (the APM price, $7.00 per mmBtu in FY27, while the formula said $11.22 in October 2026), sets a ceiling for deepwater gas, and decides who gets cheap gas first. Separately, GAIL is spending heavily to turn gas and propane into plastics and polyester feedstock, a business that lost ₹1,408 crore in FY26.

The point of those two charts is that the same company can look like a utility in one year and a commodity trader in the next. In FY26, a weak year for trading and a disastrous one for plastics, the pipes produced 71% of segment profit. In the first war quarter the trading book produced more than half. A reader trying to value the industry has to separate the parts: a bond-like regulated asset whose return is set by PNGRB, a trading book whose profit depends on gaps between global price indices, and conversion businesses (plastics and LPG) that depend on product prices and on how much cheap domestic gas the government allocates to them.

The stock market has drawn its own conclusion. GAIL trades at about 1.2 times book value, 10.9 times trailing consolidated earnings and roughly 9 times FY26 EV/EBITDA, below the 10–12 times that North American pipeline companies command (indicative). The discount reflects the trading book's volatility, the petrochemical losses, state control and a board that has had no independent director since 28 March 2026. The rest of this report explains each piece, and what would have to happen for the discount to narrow or widen.

In plain terms

India's gas business is a toll road with a trading desk built on top. The toll road (pipelines) earns a fixed return set by a regulator, and it gets paid more per unit when fewer vehicles use it, because the regulator spreads the cost over expected traffic. The trading desk buys gas priced off one benchmark and sells it priced off another, so it can make a fortune or lose money depending on how oil, American gas and Asian spot prices move against each other. The 2026 war showed both sides at once: traffic on the road stayed flat while the desk had its best quarter ever.

01

How gas earns its keep: from the LNG ship to the burner tip

Six links in the chain, each paid in a different way. The money in transmission is a fee; the money in marketing is a spread.

A molecule of gas burned in a Delhi kitchen or a Gujarat fertiliser plant has passed through up to six businesses, each with its own way of getting paid. Understanding which link is paid by volume, which by capacity, which by a regulated return and which by a price gap is the first step to understanding why companies in the same chain have wildly different profit profiles.

Transmission (Step 3) and marketing (Step 4) are this sub-sector. Steps 2 and 5 are where the reference peers sit. GAIL itself has a foot in all five through its pipelines, its LNG terminal at Dabhol, its trading book, its plastics and LPG plants and its city gas arm.

The six earning models

Upstream producers earn the gas price times volume. For old nomination fields of ONGC and Oil India, that price is administered and capped; for deepwater and high-pressure fields it is free up to a ceiling. LNG terminals earn a regasification fee: the importer pays to have its LNG unloaded, stored, warmed back into gas and injected into the grid. Petronet's revenue looks large because it also resells LNG it buys under long-term contracts, but its profit behaves like a fee: operating profit has stayed between ₹4,850 crore and ₹5,550 crore for five years while its sales swung by 40%.

Pipelines earn a tariff per unit of energy carried, measured in rupees per million British thermal units (mmBtu). The tariff is not set by the market. PNGRB calculates it so that, over the pipeline's economic life, the owner recovers its capital cost, its operating cost and a 12% post-tax return on capital employed, spread over an assumed "normative" volume. That design makes the pipeline more like a regulated utility than a toll bridge: if traffic falls short, the next tariff review tends to raise the tariff.

Marketers buy gas and resell it. Most of their revenue is the cost of the gas itself, passed through to the customer, so revenue is a poor guide to profit. GAIL's natural-gas marketing segment had ₹1,24,244 crore of revenue in FY26, about 90% of the company's total, but only ₹3,078 crore of segment profit, a margin of 2.5%. The profit depends on the spread per unit and on the volume sold, and the spread depends on the indices in the purchase and sale contracts. City gas distributors earn the gap between priority-allocated domestic gas and the price at which CNG and piped natural gas (PNG) compete with petrol, diesel and LPG. Gas converters (fertiliser, plastics, LPG extraction) earn a product spread over the cost of gas.

Why margin percentages mislead here

In most industries a higher margin is better. In gas, margin percentages mostly tell you how much of the revenue is pass-through. A marketer that resells ₹860 worth of gas per mmBtu and keeps ₹20 shows a 2.3% margin; if gas prices double and it still keeps ₹20, the margin halves while profit is unchanged. That is why this report measures marketing in rupees per mmBtu and transmission in rupees of tariff and profit per mmBtu carried, and never values a gas company on revenue or margin percentage alone. The same logic explains why Petronet's revenue fell 53% in Q1 FY27 (no Qatari LNG to resell) while its operating profit rose 32%: the fee survived even though the pass-through vanished.

In plain terms

Most of the money flowing through the gas chain is the price of the gas itself, passed from hand to hand. The profit sits in small fees and spreads along the way: a regas fee at the terminal, a regulated tariff in the pipe, a margin on resale. A gas company with ₹1.4 lakh crore of revenue can earn the same profit as a pipeline company with a tenth of that revenue. Always ask how many rupees per unit of energy each link keeps.

02

A market that will not grow: twelve years at 2.7% a year

India's gas use has grown by a third since FY15 while its target implies growth of two and a half times. Domestic output is lower than in FY14.

PPAC, the petroleum ministry's statistics cell, publishes India's gas balance in its Ready Reckoner. The latest edition (FY2025-26, dated 22 July 2026) shows consumption of 68,753 million standard cubic metres in FY26, or about 188.4 MMSCMD, down 3.6%. GAIL's annual report puts it at 189.2 MMSCMD, down 3.1%. Two primary sources agree within 0.5%, which settles an earlier conflict with a pre-war rating-agency forecast of about 212 MMSCMD.

The long series tells a sobering story. Consumption rose from about 140 MMSCMD in FY15 to 188 MMSCMD in FY26, a compound rate of about 2.7% a year, during a decade in which India's economy roughly doubled. Growth came in two bursts: the cheap-LNG years after the 2014 oil crash (FY16–FY20) and the post-COVID rebound (FY24–FY25). It went backwards twice: in FY21 (COVID) and in FY23, when the Russia–Ukraine war sent Asian LNG to more than $19 per mmBtu on average and price-sensitive users switched to naphtha, fuel oil and coal.

Domestic gas: twelve years without growth

India's gross domestic production in FY26 was 34,776 MMSCM, about 95.3 MMSCMD. In FY14 it was 97.0 MMSCMD. Twelve years of exploration licensing rounds, price reforms and deepwater investment have produced no net growth. Output from ONGC's old nomination fields fell every year from 23,746 MMSCM in FY20 to 18,481 MMSCM in FY26. Fields operated under production-sharing contracts, led by Reliance and bp's KG-D6 block, rose from 4,321 MMSCM in FY21 to 14,143 MMSCM in FY25 before falling 6.7% in FY26. In April–September 2026 gross production fell another 4.4%, missing the ministry's target of 17,740 MMSCM by more than 5%.

How big is the business in rupees?

India's LNG bill was about $13.4 billion in FY26, or roughly ₹1.18 lakh crore at PPAC's average rupee of ₹88.31. Add domestic gas at administered and ceiling prices and the value of gas consumed in India is in the region of ₹2.1–2.3 lakh crore a year (indicative). GAIL's own marketing revenue, ₹1.24 lakh crore standalone and ₹1.45 lakh crore consolidated including overseas trading, is roughly half of it. The pipeline toll pool is far smaller: GAIL's natural-gas transmission revenue was ₹11,206 crore in FY26, and adding the East-West pipeline, the Gujarat grid and smaller lines gives an India transmission revenue pool of perhaps ₹17,000–19,000 crore (indicative). That is the economic core of this sub-sector: a ₹2 lakh crore commodity flow with a ₹18,000 crore regulated toll attached.

On a global scale India is small. Its roughly 69 billion cubic metres is about 1.7% of world consumption of 4,100–4,200 bcm (indicative). Its LNG imports, 26 million tonnes in FY26, are about 6% of world LNG trade of about 440 million tonnes in 2025. That smallness is both a weakness and a strength: India is a price-taker in global LNG, but it is also small enough that a few new long-term contracts or one cold winter elsewhere move its supply picture sharply.

The 15% goal has been official policy since 2016. On current trends it is out of reach: with gas at about 6% of a growing energy pie, reaching 15% by 2030 would need gas use to rise roughly two and a half times in four years. In plain terms

India's gas market has been stuck. It uses about a third more gas than ten years ago, its own fields produce slightly less than they did in FY14, and almost all growth has come from importing LNG when the world price was low enough. Gas's share of India's energy is shrinking. That is the backdrop for every pipeline built and every tariff set: the infrastructure is sized for a market twice as large as today's.

03

Who burns India's gas: one protected customer, five price-sensitive ones

Fertiliser and city gas take half the gas and are protected by policy. Power, refineries and industry come and go with the price.

GAIL's annual report breaks India's FY26 consumption of 189.2 MMSCMD into six sectors. Fertiliser plants took 53.8 MMSCMD (28%), city gas distribution 45.3 (24%), "others" (steel, glass, ceramics, sponge iron, small industry, own use) 42.5 (22%), power 21.8 (12%), refineries 15.1 (8%) and petrochemicals 10.7 (6%). Only two sectors grew: city gas (+9.7%) and petrochemicals (+16.3%). Power fell 10.6%, "others" 11.7%, refineries 6.2% and fertiliser 4.3%.

The protected customers: fertiliser and city gas

Fertiliser is the single biggest user because urea is made from natural gas, which accounts for roughly 70–80% of a urea plant's cost. The government buys urea at a controlled price and subsidises the difference, and it pools gas prices for urea plants so that every plant pays the same blended price (our fertilisers report covers the urea economics). That makes fertiliser demand price-insensitive: plants run whenever the subsidy system pays them to. Demand fell in FY26 mainly because of plant maintenance and import choices, not because gas became unaffordable. New capacity is the growth lever. GAIL is evaluating two urea plants of about 1.2–1.27 million tonnes a year each, at Saoner in Maharashtra and in Chhattisgarh, under a new urea investment policy published in July 2026; management says plants on the Mumbai–Nagpur–Jharsuguda corridor would be "a big boost" for that pipeline in three to four years.

City gas distribution (CGD) is the growth engine. India had 8,916 CNG stations and 1.69 crore domestic PNG connections on 1 April 2026, up 10.5% and 12.7% in a year (PPAC). CGD gets first claim on cheap domestic APM gas for CNG for vehicles and PNG for homes, which is why its demand keeps rising even when LNG is expensive. Its growth depends on new connections in the 300-plus geographical areas licensed by PNGRB, on the CNG car and three-wheeler fleet, and increasingly on industries switching from LPG and diesel to piped gas. Management expects CGD demand to grow from about 45–46 MMSCMD today to 80–85 MMSCMD by 2030.

The swing customers: power, refineries and industry

The other half of demand is elastic. Power stations, about 25 GW of which are gas-fired and mostly idle, run only when gas is cheap enough to compete with coal or when the grid needs peaking power on summer evenings (see the power generation report). Refineries use gas for hydrogen and fuel, and switch to naphtha or fuel oil when LNG is dear. Industry (glass, ceramics, steel, small factories) moves between gas, LPG, propane, fuel oil and coal on price. The FY23 episode is the clearest proof: when Japan's average LNG import price rose to $19.08 per mmBtu for the fiscal year, India's consumption fell to 160.8 MMSCMD on GAIL's basis; when it fell to $13.26 in FY24, consumption recovered to 188.5 MMSCMD. Almost all of that swing came from power and industry.

The allocation ladder

Cheap domestic gas is rationed by policy. CNG for transport and PNG for homes get APM gas first, then fertiliser (pooled), then LPG production, then everyone else, who must buy imported LNG or higher-priced domestic gas. Since October 2024 the government has cut APM allocations to city gas and replaced them with costlier new-well gas or LNG, which is why CGD margins have fallen (Indraprastha Gas's operating margin went from 17% in FY24 to 12% in FY26 and about 6% in Q1 FY27). During the 2026 crisis, the Natural Gas (Supply Regulation) Order of 9 March 2026, issued under the Essential Commodities Act, formalised the ladder: homes, CNG, LPG production and pipeline fuel at 100% of requirement; fertiliser at 70%; grid-connected industry at 80%; city gas industrial and commercial customers at 80%; petrochemicals and power curtailed; refineries at about 65% (as reported). GAIL was made the nodal agency for pooling the gas.

The ladder changed the demand mix during the war months. PPAC's monthly shares show city gas jumping from about 24% of consumption in January 2026 to 29–30% in March and April, while refineries and petrochemicals were cut to 6–7% and 3%. For pipelines the ladder matters less than for marketers, because a molecule moved to a CGD network pays the same tariff (or, since January 2026, the lower ₹54 zone rate) as one moved to a refinery. For GAIL's own plants it mattered a great deal: polymer production at Pata fell to 51,000 tonnes in Q1 FY27 as gas was diverted to priority users.

PNGRB's projection puts city gas at 30% of 2030 demand and 44% of 2040 demand. That is the most credible part of the forecast because city gas has grown every year and has policy priority. The least credible part is power, which needs gas to be cheap at exactly the times when renewables and batteries are getting cheaper. For a pipeline owner, the forecast has a specific meaning: city gas networks are spread across hundreds of districts and need trunk lines to reach them, so the national grid's eastern and central extensions only fill if city gas, new fertiliser plants and the LPG-to-gas switch in industry all deliver.

In plain terms

Half of India's gas goes to users the government protects: urea plants and city gas for homes and vehicles. They keep buying almost whatever the price. The other half goes to power stations, refineries and factories that switch to coal, naphtha or LPG the moment gas gets expensive. So when world LNG prices spike, India's gas demand does not disappear; it shrinks to its protected core, and the swing users come back when prices fall.

04

Where the molecules come from: a grid that mostly carries imports

Domestic gas is capped, flat and allocated by rule. LNG is priced off four different indices. The pipes carry both, but mostly LNG.

India's supply is split almost exactly in half between its own fields and imported LNG. The two halves could hardly be more different. Domestic gas is produced by a handful of companies, priced by government formula and rationed by priority. LNG is bought from a dozen countries under contracts written in dollars and linked to oil, American gas or Asian spot prices, then shipped, re-gasified and fed into the same pipes. For a pipeline owner the difference is invisible: an mmBtu is an mmBtu. For a marketer it is everything.

Four tiers of domestic gas

APM gas comes from ONGC's and Oil India's old nomination fields, which are most of domestic output. Since April 2023 its price has been 10% of the monthly Indian crude-oil basket, with a floor of $4 and a ceiling that rises by $0.25 a year: $6.50 per mmBtu in FY24 and FY25, $6.75 in FY26 and $7.00 in FY27. New-well gas from the same fields gets a 20% premium under the formula, although GAIL's finance director describes the new-well gas its LPG plants now receive as costing "$12 to $13", probably because it is bought by tender at market-linked prices. High-pressure, high-temperature and deepwater gas, such as Reliance–bp's KG-D6, has marketing freedom up to a ceiling set every six months: $8.90 for April–September 2026 and $9.89 for October 2026 to March 2027. The fourth tier is gas sold on the Indian Gas Exchange, a small but telling market whose GIXI index hit $28.4 per mmBtu in September 2026.

In plain terms

When people talk about India's "gas grid", they mostly mean a set of pipes carrying imported LNG inland from the west coast.

A pipeline that carries little gas would be a disaster in a free market. Under India's tariff rules it is a slower-returning asset rather than a stranded one, because PNGRB sets the tariff on assumed rather than actual volumes and recovers early shortfalls over the asset's life. Section 09 explains that mechanism and why it makes the 2028 tariff review so important for GAIL. The other implication is that India has plenty of pipeline capacity for its present consumption. More pipes will not raise demand by themselves; cheaper gas and new customers will.

Who sells the gas

Marketing is less concentrated than transmission but still dominated by GAIL, which describes its share as "~48% in India's natural gas consumption". Its 104.21 MMSCMD of FY26 marketing volume includes overseas sales, so the domestic share is a little lower. The other sellers are the oil companies (IOC, BPCL and HPCL, through capacity booked at their own and others' terminals), ONGC (whose APM gas GAIL markets), Reliance–bp (KG-D6 gas by e-auction), Shell, Torrent, Gujarat Energy (which absorbed the trading company GSPC in May 2026), Adani–Total and H-Energy. City gas companies increasingly buy their own LNG and domestic gas rather than through GAIL, which is why analysts have noted for years that GAIL's marketing volume lags its transmission volume.

Why GAIL is all five at once, and why that matters

GAIL owns archetype 1 directly (its pipelines) and archetype 3 directly (its marketing book). It owns archetype 2 through Konkan LNG and a 12.5% stake in Petronet; archetype 4 through GAIL Gas, its own six city gas areas and stakes of 22.5% in IGL and 32.5% in MGL; and archetype 5 through its petrochemical complex at Pata, its LPG and liquid hydrocarbon (LHC) plants, its 70% of BCPL and its new PTA plant at Mangalore. The combination has a logic: the pipes need gas to carry, the trading book supplies it, and the conversion plants use gas that would otherwise be hard to sell. But it also produces profits that are hard to forecast and hard to value as a single number. That is why most analysts use a sum-of-the-parts approach (section 17).

The combination also creates conflicts that a regulator has to police. A company that owns the pipe and sells the gas could favour its own sales over rival shippers. PNGRB's response for a decade was clause 5A of its affiliate code, which required "legal separation" of transmission and marketing, originally by 31 March 2017. GAIL challenged it in court and the deadline never bit. On 8 July 2026 PNGRB issued an amendment that omits regulation 5A entirely. Unbundling is off the table; instead an independent transport system operator will monitor the 25% of each pipeline's capacity that must be available to third parties as common carrier.

The Hormuz year: India's gas system under stress Deep dive

The 2026 West Asia war took out half of India's LNG supply overnight. Six months later imports were higher than a year before. How the system bent without breaking, and who paid.

On 28 February 2026 war broke out in West Asia and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz stopped. On 4 March QatarEnergy declared force majeure after attacks halted production at Ras Laffan, the world's largest LNG complex. Petronet LNG, which buys most of India's Qatari LNG, passed the force majeure on to its offtakers; GAIL's allocation from Petronet was cut to zero with effect from 4 March. A ceasefire followed on 7 April. Qatari force majeure was reportedly extended into September and October for some Asian buyers, including India.

Allocation: rationing by priority

With supply short, the government rationed. Homes, CNG, LPG production and pipeline fuel were protected at 100% of requirement; fertiliser got 70%; grid-connected industry and city gas industrial customers 80%; petrochemicals and power were curtailed; refineries got about 65% (as reported from the March 2026 order). PPAC notified pooled prices for diverted gas and GAIL, with PPAC, managed the pool. The effect was to protect households and transport at the cost of industry. The quarter split the gas chain cleanly. GAIL was the biggest winner. What the year changed for good

Four things are likely to outlast the crisis. First, supply diversity is now worth paying for: GAIL's management says every new LNG deal carries west-coast optionality, and the government is studying strategic gas storage "very seriously". Second, the LPG shock strengthened the case for piped gas: management describes industrial customers that used LPG "all now coming up for tying up natural gas", and the ministry is pushing PNG connections for homes. In plain terms

When Qatar stopped shipping in March 2026, India lost half its LNG overnight. For three months it went short, rationed gas to homes and CNG first, and cut factories, refineries and plastics plants. The company holding American and lagged-oil-price contracts, GAIL, earned record profits; the city gas companies that had to buy expensive replacement gas, and the producers whose gas price was capped, paid for it.

Three indices, one contract book: how an LNG portfolio makes money Deep dive

GAIL buys gas priced off American gas, lagged Japanese crude and Brent, and sells it mostly on recent Brent. Its profit is the gap between indices, not the level of any one of them.

A gas marketer's profit is the difference between what it pays and what it charges, multiplied by volume. In a simple world both prices would move together and the marketer would earn a steady fee. GAIL's world is not simple. Its purchases are linked to at least four different benchmarks, its sales mostly to one, and the benchmarks can move in opposite directions. That turns the marketing book into a large, partly open position on the spread between oil and gas prices in different parts of the world. Understanding it is the key to understanding why GAIL's marketing profit has ranged from a loss of ₹702 crore to a profit of ₹7,446 crore in six years.

The American book: Sabine Pass and Cove Point

Between 2011 and 2013 GAIL signed two 20-year deals for US LNG. The first cargo arrived in March 2018, when US LNG looked expensive against oil-linked supply. It looked cheap in 2022 and again in 2026.

Together the US book supplies about 21 MMSCMD. What a marketer can and cannot control

GAIL controls how much of its book it leaves open, how it hedges, whether it buys spot cargoes, how much HH gas it diverts to Pata and where it routes cargoes. It does not control Brent, Henry Hub, the JCC lag built into contracts signed a decade ago, the timing of wars, or whether a counterparty defaults. Its stated plan to add 7–8 million tonnes a year of LNG by 2030, with west-coast optionality, will change the mix again. The new global supply wave from 2027 (Qatar North Field East, Rio Grande, Port Arthur, Nigeria LNG Train 7 and others, mostly US and Qatari) should push spot LNG lower and narrow the gap between American and Asian prices that has made the US book so valuable. A reader should treat ₹4,500 crore as management's planning number inside an observed band of roughly −₹700 crore to +₹7,400 crore, and expect large quarter-to-quarter swings rather than a smooth path back to normal.

In plain terms

GAIL buys some gas at American prices, some at an old, slow-moving Japanese oil average, and some at Brent, and sells most of it at recent Brent. When oil jumps and American gas does not, it earns a windfall on the gap. When oil falls fast, the slow-moving contract becomes a loss. Q1 FY27 was a ₹3,481 crore quarter because oil spiked while American gas stayed at $3; management expects most of that to fade, but the second oil spike in September means it may fade more slowly than they said in July.

One Nation, One Grid, One Tariff: the economics of a postage-stamp pipeline Deep dive

PNGRB sets a cost-plus tariff with a 12% post-tax return, pools it across the national grid, and charges most users one of two prices regardless of distance. Empty pipes raise the tariff rather than lowering returns.

Pipeline tariffs in India are not negotiated. They are calculated by PNGRB under its Natural Gas Pipeline Tariff Regulations, first issued in 2008 and amended many times. Three layers sit on top of each other: the tariff for each pipeline or integrated network, which determines what its owner is allowed to earn; the unified tariff that most shippers on the national grid actually pay; and the apportionment of that pool back to each owner. Understanding all three explains why GAIL's transmission profit grows in steps when PNGRB issues an order, and why the 2028 review matters more than next year's volumes.

Layer 1: the pipeline's own tariff

For each pipeline, PNGRB computes the revenue an efficient owner needs to recover its capital, operating costs and a 12% post-tax return on capital employed, discounted over the economic life, and divides it by a normative volume that ramps up over the early years toward about 75% of capacity. The result is a levelised tariff in rupees per mmBtu. If actual volumes fall short in the early years, the shortfall is recovered later through levelisation; tariffs are reviewed roughly every five years. PNGRB allows GAIL to pool most of its network into one integrated tariff, the INGPL tariff.

Two orders have shaped GAIL's recent earnings. The 22 March 2023 order set the INGPL tariff at ₹58.61 per mmBtu from 1 April 2023 (GAIL had reportedly sought about ₹68.55). Its effect on the accounts was dramatic: natural-gas transmission revenue rose 55% in FY24, from ₹6,661 crore to ₹10,308 crore, while volume rose only 12%. The order was explicitly interim: it dealt only with system-use gas (including a correction for calorific value) and capacity determination. The full true-up of capex, opex, transmission losses, working days and revenue sharing happens at the next review, effective 1 April 2028.

Who wins and who pays

The design has clear winners: city gas customers, who pay ₹54 everywhere; pipeline owners, whose return is protected; and the government's goal of extending gas to the east and north. The payers are bulk industrial and power users far from the coast, who now subsidise CNG and household gas, and, through the unified tariff pool, users of older, cheaper pipelines who help pay for new, empty ones. Two policy risks follow. A regulator under pressure to cut costs for fertiliser plants (whose gas costs flow into the urea subsidy) could resist large increases; and a gas market that does not grow leaves the new lines empty for longer, pushing more of the bill onto fewer users and encouraging them to switch away. The 12% post-tax return is generous by world standards, where regulated gas networks typically earn 6–8% (indicative); it is designed to compensate for India's volume risk.

In plain terms

India's pipeline owners are paid like utilities: the regulator adds up what they spent, adds a 12% after-tax return, and divides by the gas it expects them to carry. If the pipe is emptier than expected, the price per unit goes up at the next review rather than the owner losing money. Customers on the national grid pay one of two flat prices by distance, and homes and CNG pay the lower one everywhere. GAIL's next big tariff review is in April 2028, and it will include thousands of kilometres of new, nearly empty pipeline.

The price administrators: caps, ceilings and the allocation ladder

Government formulas decide what domestic gas costs and who gets it. Those choices move profit between producers, marketers, city gas firms and fertiliser plants.

India does not have a free market in domestic gas. The petroleum ministry, through PPAC, notifies the price of APM gas every month and the ceiling for deepwater gas every six months; it decides which sectors get first claim on cheap gas; and in emergencies it can direct supplies under the Essential Commodities Act. For this sub-sector those decisions matter in three ways: they set the cost of the gas GAIL markets to city gas and fertiliser customers, they set the feedstock cost of GAIL's LPG plants, and they shape how much imported LNG the country needs.

From the 2014 formula to the Parikh cap

Between November 2014 and March 2023, domestic gas was priced every six months by a formula linked to international gas hubs. The price was $5.05 per mmBtu in November 2014, fell to $1.79 in October 2020 (the COVID trough), and rose to $8.57 for October 2022 to March 2023 as the Russia–Ukraine war lifted global gas prices. That spike hurt city gas and fertiliser users, so a committee led by Kirit Parikh recommended, and the Cabinet approved on 6 April 2023, a new formula: APM gas at 10% of the Indian crude basket, monthly, with a floor of $4 and a cap of $6.50, the cap rising by $0.25 a year from FY26. New-well gas from nomination fields gets a 20% premium. The committee also recommended moving to market pricing by 2027; PPAC's documents show no notification to that effect, so it should be treated as not implemented.

Gas from high-pressure, high-temperature and deepwater fields has marketing and pricing freedom up to a ceiling reset every April and October. The other policy levers

Tax. Natural gas is still outside GST, so industrial users pay state VAT and central excise that cannot be offset against GST on their output. Bringing gas into GST is a long-standing industry demand; it would lower effective costs for factories and help the swing sectors. Compressed biogas. Fertiliser. The new urea investment policy published in July 2026 may revive gas-based urea capacity; an analyst on GAIL's call described it as offering 12–16% IRRs (the analyst's figure, not management's). Coal gasification has an ₹8,500 crore incentive scheme from 2024. Storage: after the war the government is studying strategic gas storage. Market design: unbundling was dropped on 8 July 2026 (section 06), and PNGRB's Action Plan 2027 keeps an independent transport system operator to police the 25% common-carrier capacity.

In plain terms

The government sets the price of most domestic gas with a formula linked to oil, then caps it at $7. When oil is high the cap gives homes, CNG users and urea plants cheap gas at the producers' expense; when oil is low the producers get less than the cap. It also decides who gets cheap gas first. Those rules shift hundreds of crores between ONGC, GAIL, the city gas companies and the fertiliser subsidy every year, without any change in how much gas is used.

Four decades of shocks: from the HBJ pipeline to the Hormuz quarter

The industry's history is a sequence of supply surprises (KG-D6, Gazprom, Qatar) and price shocks (2008, 2014, 2022, 2026), each followed by a policy reset.

India's gas industry was born as a pipeline project. Gas Authority of India Ltd was set up in 1984 to build the Hazira–Bijaipur–Jagdishpur (HBJ) pipeline carrying Bombay High gas to fertiliser plants in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. For twenty years the business was simple: domestic gas at administered prices, carried to state-allocated customers. Everything since has been about adding imports, adding regulation and coping with shocks.

The six defining shocks

KG-D6 (2009–2013). Reliance's deepwater find was supposed to make India self-sufficient in gas; a new pipeline was built for it and a generation of gas-fired power plants planned around it. Output peaked above 60 MMSCMD in 2010 and fell sharply as reservoirs disappointed. The East-West line it filled now runs at 40%, and about 25 GW of gas power plants sit largely idle. The lesson, repeated since, is that domestic supply forecasts are unreliable.

The 2014–16 oil crash. Oil-linked LNG became cheap, so India's imports rose and consumption grew; but GAIL's freshly signed US contracts, priced off Henry Hub plus fixed liquefaction costs, suddenly looked expensive against oil-linked supply. GAIL spent years swapping, reselling and renegotiating. COVID (2020–21) was a demand shock with the same price effect: GAIL's marketing book lost money in FY21 because its Brent-linked sales fell below HH-linked costs.

Russia–Ukraine and the Gazprom default (2022). European gas hit $70 per mmBtu in August 2022 and Japanese LNG import prices $23.73 in September. India's demand fell, and GAIL's contracted Gazprom volumes stopped arriving, so it lost volume at exactly the moment replacement cargoes were most expensive. Consolidated PAT fell 55% in FY23. The policy responses (the Parikh cap and the unified tariff, both April 2023) shaped the next three years.

The FY26 double squeeze. Cheap spot LNG and soft crude compressed marketing spreads, polymer prices fell under Chinese oversupply while GAIL's petrochemical gas cost rose by about ₹1,400 crore, and a ₹675 crore provision on NFCL's dues hit Q4. Consolidated PAT fell 39% to ₹7,582 crore. The 2026 war then reversed the marketing story within weeks.

Reading a gas company: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

The numbers that decide whether a pipeline and gas-marketing business is doing well, how to calculate each one, what good looks like and where the definitions trip people up.

A gas transmission and marketing company reports revenue, EBITDA and profit like any other, but those headline numbers hide more than they show. Revenue is mostly the pass-through cost of gas; EBITDA blends a 55%-margin pipeline with a 2.5%-margin trading book; and profit swings with indices the company does not control. The equation in round numbers

1. Transmission volume (MMSCMD) and utilisation

What it is. The average amount of gas carried through the company's pipelines each day, for all shippers including itself, in million standard cubic metres per day. Utilisation is volume divided by capacity. Formula. Utilisation = average flow (MMSCMD) ÷ design or authorised capacity (MMSCMD). Worked example. GAIL's integrated network carried an average of 109.41 MMSCMD in FY26 against capacity of 163.8 MMSCMD: 109.41 ÷ 163.8 = 66.8% (PPAC). GAIL's own headline "transmission volume" was 122.18 MMSCMD, a different measure that adds its other networks and counts gas crossing two GAIL networks twice.

What drives it. LNG affordability above all, since about 72% of GAIL's flow is imported; then city gas growth, fertiliser plant run-rates, power-sector peaks and new customer connections on new lines. Volume fell from 127.32 MMSCMD in FY25 to 122.18 in FY26 as power and industry cut back, and management's FY27 guidance is about 123 MMSCMD (cut from 133–134 "with the assumption that the geopolitical situation continues"). The 4 MMSCMD rise between Q4 FY26 (118.99) and Q1 FY27 (122.36) came from third-party shippers. Sensitivity. One MMSCMD for a year is about 14.5 million mmBtu; at ₹65.69 that is about ₹95 crore of revenue, most of which is profit because pipeline costs are largely fixed.

14. Anatomy of one 1,000 km trunk pipeline (ILLUSTRATIVE)

To make the tariff mechanics concrete, here is an illustrative new pipeline built under PNGRB's rules. It is 1,000 km long, costs ₹10,000 crore (₹10 crore a km including compressors) and has capacity of 30 MMSCMD. PNGRB assumes it will carry 22.5 MMSCMD (75% of capacity) on average over its life. The owner needs: depreciation of ₹250 crore a year (40-year life), operating costs (maintenance, system-use gas, staff) of ₹400 crore a year, and a 12% post-tax return on ₹10,000 crore, which is ₹1,200 crore after tax or ₹1,600 crore before tax at 25%. Its annual revenue requirement is therefore ₹250 + ₹400 + ₹1,600 = ₹2,250 crore. At 22.5 MMSCMD the pipe carries 22.5 × 14.48 = 325.9 million mmBtu a year, so the tariff is ₹2,250 crore ÷ 325.9 million = ₹69 per mmBtu. Definition traps, collected

  • MMSCMD, MMSCM, bcm, MTPA and mmBtu are different units. MMSCMD is a daily flow; MMSCM a quantity (PPAC's monthly data); bcm a yearly quantity (1 bcm a year ≈ 2.74 MMSCMD); 1 MTPA of LNG ≈ 3.6 MMSCMD (GAIL's conversion) or 1,325 MMSCM a year (PPAC). Prices and tariffs are per mmBtu; PPAC converts at 25.2 scm per mmBtu. Never add MMSCMD to bcm or compare a daily average with a monthly total without multiplying by about 30.
  • Transmission volume, marketing volume and national consumption overlap. GAIL's 122 MMSCMD carried, 104 MMSCMD sold and India's 188 MMSCMD consumed cannot be added or divided into each other.
  • Standalone and consolidated differ by more than subsidiaries. Consolidated adds the City Gas segment, overseas trading, BCPL and the equity-accounted share of Petronet, IGL, MGL and others. FY26 PAT: ₹6,968 crore standalone, ₹7,582 crore consolidated.
  • "Gross turnover", "revenue from operations" and screener "sales" differ slightly (Q1 FY27 standalone ₹38,912 crore, ₹38,982 crore and ₹38,953 crore). Do not mix them in one table.
  • Segment PBIT is not PBT or EBITDA. Management's marketing "PBT" (₹3,353 crore in Q1 FY27) is lower than segment PBIT (₹3,481 crore). Screener's operating profit excludes other income; company EBITDA includes it.
  • One-offs distort both FY25 and FY26. FY25 includes the ₹2,440 crore SMTS settlement; FY26 includes the NFCL provision (−₹675 crore), an entry-tax reversal (+₹291 crore) and depreciation write-backs from longer asset lives (+₹475 crore pipelines, +₹189 crore petrochemicals).
  • Three meanings of "tariff". The unified tariff zone rates (₹54, ₹102.86) are what shippers pay; GAIL's INGPL tariff (₹65.69) is what it is allowed to earn; other pipelines have their own.
  • Three network lengths. 18,717 km operational (PNGRB, June 2026, of which 5,642 km partial); about 14,200 km integrated network (GAIL deck); 13,658 km INGPL operating (PNGRB, March 2025).
  • APM "price" vs APM formula. PPAC publishes a calculated price ($11.22 in October 2026) and a price actually paid ($7.00 cap). Charting the formula as "the APM price" overstates producers' income.
  • GCV vs NCV. Indian domestic gas prices and LNG are quoted on gross calorific value; some power contracts use net (about 10% lower energy per unit).
  • Margin percentages. A marketing margin of 2.5% and one of 10% can mean the same rupees per mmBtu at different gas prices.
In plain terms

Most mistakes in reading gas companies come from units and bases. Check whether a number is a daily flow or a yearly total, standalone or consolidated, segment profit or pre-tax profit, the regulator's tariff or the price a customer pays, and whether a one-off is buried in it. Get those right and the numbers reconcile; get them wrong and a utility can look like a collapsing trader.

How to read a gas transmission and marketing company's quarterly results in ten minutes

  1. Transmission volume (MMSCMD) against guidance. GAIL guides about 123 MMSCMD for FY27. Below 120 in a quarter is a red flag for the toll; above 125 means third-party shippers or power demand are filling the pipes.
  2. NG transmission segment PBIT and margin. Expect about ₹1,700–1,900 crore a quarter at ₹65.69. A margin below 50% points to system-use gas or fuel costs; a jump above 60% usually means a one-off write-back.
  3. Marketing segment PBIT, and management's PBT figure. Divide by marketed mmBtu. Compare the running total with the ₹4,500 crore guidance. Ask which indices moved: Brent's three-month average against the JCC nine-month lag, and Asian gas against Henry Hub.
  4. Marketing volume and overseas share. Falling domestic volume with rising profit means a windfall, not a trend.
  5. Petrochemical production, sales and PBIT. Production below 200 thousand tonnes a quarter means gas is being diverted or the plant is down; check polymer price against the ₹1.3 lakh-a-tonne breakeven.
  6. LHC PBIT per tonne. Check LPG realisation and any change in APM or new-well gas allocation.
  7. One-offs. Read the notes to the results for provisions, write-backs, settlements and tariff orders; strip them out before comparing quarters.
  8. Consolidated additions. City Gas segment PBIT, GAIL Gas's profit, and the share of profit from Petronet, IGL and MGL.
  9. Capex, CWIP and borrowings. Track the PDH-PP and PTA project status, lease additions for LNG ships and the debt-to-equity ratio (0.32 at June 2026).
  10. Regulatory and governance notes. Any PNGRB order, unified tariff change, APM or ceiling notification, allocation change, and board composition (no independent director since 28 March 2026).

Red flags in a single quarter: transmission volume falling while India's consumption rises (share loss to other pipelines); a marketing loss without an identified provision (an open index position going wrong); petrochemical losses at full load (the spread, not the gas supply, is the problem); and a rising receivable or provision against a fertiliser or power customer (credit risk in the trading book).

Who is winning: returns, war exposure and full pipes

Three maps place GAIL against reference peers and against India's other pipelines. In each, GAIL (the member) is the green dot; reference companies are gold.

With one listed member, "who is winning" has two meanings: how GAIL compares with the listed gas-chain companies around it, and which pipelines in India's grid are earning their keep. The three maps answer both. None is a recommendation; each shows where the economics sit today.

That inversion is the clearest single picture of how the gas chain works under stress: the companies holding supply contracts and fees captured the price spike, the companies selling to retail customers at regulated or sticky prices absorbed it. The third map is the industry's physical scorecard. GAIL's integrated network is the one large, reasonably full system in India; GSPL Transmission's Gujarat grid is smaller and similarly used. Against other listed gas companies, GAIL earns lower returns and trades at a lower multiple; the companies that collect fees or run city franchises earn more on their money. In the war quarter GAIL and the LNG terminal won while the city gas companies lost. And among India's pipelines, GAIL's core network is the only big one that is reasonably full; most of the rest of the grid is waiting for gas that has not yet arrived.

The petchem bet and the capex bill

GAIL is shifting its spending from pipes to plastics and polyester feedstock just as its existing plastics plant posts record losses. Whether that works is the largest execution risk in the industry.

For two decades GAIL's capital went mostly into pipelines. That is changing. In the FY27 capital budget of about ₹11,500–11,600 crore, pipelines take ₹2,401 crore, petrochemicals ₹1,486 crore, renewables and net-zero projects ₹1,950 crore and "operational" items, including LNG ship charters, ₹3,530 crore. Behind those numbers sits a plan to take the group's petrochemical capacity, including subsidiaries and joint ventures, roughly 2.7 times, to about 2.9 million tonnes a year.

The projects

  • PDH-PP at Usar, Maharashtra (500 KTA). A propane dehydrogenation and polypropylene plant costing ₹11,256 crore. Its schedule has slipped from June 2027 to "may be completed by December '27". When commissioned, depreciation rises by about ₹312 crore a year and roughly ₹440 crore of annual interest on about ₹6,000 crore of borrowing stops being capitalised.
  • GAIL Mangalore Petrochemicals (1,250 KTA PTA). The former MCPI plant, bought through insolvency in 2021, was "in advanced stage of commissioning" in July 2026. PTA is a feedstock for polyester.
  • Pata feedstock change. A C2-C3 pipeline from Vijaipur to Pata (FY27) and an ethane pipeline from Dabhol aim to shift Pata from rich natural gas to imported ethane, cutting its exposure to gas allocation.
  • Pata polypropylene (60 KTA), commissioned on 4 February 2026, taking own capacity to 870 KTA.

The case for the bet is India's low plastics consumption. GAIL's deck puts per-capita polymer use at about 14–15 kg in India against 83 kg in China, 39–42 kg for the world and 93 kg in the United States. Demand growth is likely to stay high for a decade. The case against is the cycle. Chinese capacity additions drove the FY26 polymer glut (see the plastics and commodity chemicals reports), during which GAIL's petrochemical segment lost ₹1,408 crore on 785 thousand tonnes, and management's breakeven rule (gas at $13–14 per mmBtu and polymer at about ₹1.3 lakh a tonne) shows how thin the margin of safety is. Petrochemical segment assets were already ₹19,795 crore at March 2026, up from ₹16,995 crore a year earlier.

What the capex means for returns

The capex shift matters for the whole industry's economics because it changes what GAIL is. A pipeline rupee earns a regulated 12% post-tax return once commissioned, with tariff protection against low volume. A petrochemical rupee earns whatever the global polymer spread allows, which in FY26 was a large negative number. CWIP of ₹24,072 crore at March 2026, about 35% of consolidated fixed assets, is already depressing GAIL's return on capital; when the PDH-PP and PTA plants start, depreciation and interest will rise before their profits do. Management has built no profit from GMPL or PDH-PP into its FY28 thinking. Gross consolidated borrowings were ₹24,831 crore at March 2026 (standalone ₹17,414 crore; debt-to-equity 0.28, rising to 0.32 in June), and lease commitments for LNG carriers not yet delivered added ₹9,847 crore of future obligations.

There is also an energy-transition strand. GAIL runs a 10 MW electrolyser for green hydrogen at Vijaipur, is electrifying compressors, has a renewables programme of 1,063 MW under way (solar and battery storage in Uttar Pradesh, solar and wind in Maharashtra) and a target of 3.6 GW by 2035. These are small in profit terms but absorb a growing share of capex, and like petrochemicals they earn market rather than regulated returns.

In plain terms

GAIL is spending more of its money on plastics and polyester plants and less on pipelines. Pipelines earn a guaranteed 12% after tax once built; plastics plants earn whatever the global market pays, and in FY26 they lost ₹1,408 crore. India uses very little plastic per person, so demand should grow, but China has built so much capacity that prices may stay weak. The success or failure of this shift will decide whether GAIL's returns rise or fall over the next five years.

The global lens: fee-earning pipes abroad, an LNG supply wave on the way

North American pipeline companies earn fees and trade at higher multiples. GAIL's US supplier Cheniere is its mirror image. And a new wave of LNG export capacity is about to change the spreads Indian marketers live on.

The world's largest gas pipeline companies look very different from GAIL. Kinder Morgan operates about 70,000 miles of US gas pipelines and earned adjusted EBITDA of about $8.39 billion on revenue of $16.93 billion in 2025; Williams, owner of the Transco interstate system, earned about $7.7 billion on $11.95 billion; Enbridge and TC Energy run continental networks in Canada and the US. Their revenue is overwhelmingly fees for moving gas, often under long take-or-pay contracts, so their EBITDA margins are 50–65% and their market values (Kinder Morgan about $77 billion, Williams about $88 billion, Enbridge about $108 billion, TC Energy about $65 billion, all indicative) are many times GAIL's $11.4 billion even where revenues are similar.

Returns and multiples

Regulated gas transmission companies in Europe, such as Italy's Snam, typically earn about 6–8% post-tax on their regulated asset base and trade at about 1.1–1.3 times that base (indicative). North American midstream companies trade at roughly 10–12 times EV/EBITDA and Cheniere at about 9–11 times (indicative). India's 12% post-tax regulated return is generous by those standards; it is designed to compensate for the volume risk of pipelines that are often half-empty. GAIL's roughly 9 times EV/EBITDA and 3.4% dividend yield (against about 4–4.5% for Kinder Morgan, indicative) reflect a mix in which the regulated part is only about two-thirds of normal earnings.

GAIL and Cheniere: two sides of one contract

GAIL buys 3.5 million tonnes a year from Cheniere's Sabine Pass terminal on Henry Hub-linked terms. Cheniere's EBITDA (guided at $6.6–7.0 billion for 2025) comes mostly from fixed liquefaction fees: it is paid whether or not its buyers make money reselling the gas. GAIL wears the other side, the basis risk between Henry Hub and the Asian or Brent-linked prices at which it sells. When Asian prices are high, as in 2022 and 2026, GAIL captures the spread; when they are low, as in 2015–2020, it carries the loss. The contract is a clean illustration of how risk is distributed along a global gas chain: infrastructure owners take fees, portfolio holders take spreads.

Kitchens, factories and the EV: gas against LPG, electricity and biogas Deep dive

The LPG shock made piped gas look safer; electric vehicles and cheap renewables make it look less necessary. Which wins decides whether India's half-empty pipes fill.

The long-term volume case for India's gas grid rests on three substitutions: piped gas replacing LPG cylinders in homes and businesses, CNG replacing petrol and diesel in vehicles, and gas replacing coal, naphtha and fuel oil in industry. Each faces a competitor that is getting stronger: electric vehicles, rooftop and grid solar with batteries, and in the longer run green hydrogen. The 2026 war shifted the balance toward gas in kitchens and factories, and left the threat to gas in transport unchanged.

Gas against LPG: the war's lasting lesson

About 60% of India's LPG is imported and 90–94% of the imports come from the Middle East. When Hormuz closed, LPG supply was rationed and consumption fell 14.9% in April–September 2026, to 13,797 thousand tonnes from 16,218. Piped gas, carried from non-Gulf LNG and domestic fields, kept flowing to homes under the top rung of the allocation ladder. That has changed behaviour. GAIL's management says industrial customers that used LPG "are all now coming up for tying up natural gas", and the petroleum ministry is pushing domestic PNG hard. India had 1.69 crore domestic PNG connections on 1 April 2026, up 12.7% in a year, against an ambition of 12.5 crore by 2030, and 8,916 CNG stations against a target of about 18,000. In October 2026 Delhi households paid ₹50.59 per standard cubic metre for piped gas and drivers ₹86.98 a kg for CNG (IGL); in Mumbai, ₹53 and ₹89.

The constraint is not demand but cost and connections. Every new PNG home needs a city gas network, a meter and a pipe to the kitchen; every new industrial customer needs a connection and a supply contract. City gas companies have to fund that build while their margins are squeezed by APM allocation cuts. The unified tariff's ₹54 nationwide rate for CNG and domestic PNG helps, but the economics of extending networks to smaller towns depend on volumes that take years to arrive.

Gas against electricity: the EV and the solar panel

City gas is the fastest-growing gas sector, and a large part of it is CNG for cars, taxis, buses and three-wheelers. Electric two- and three-wheelers and e-buses erode CNG's growth in exactly the cities where the networks are densest. City gas is about 45 MMSCMD, a quarter of India's demand; a slowdown in CNG would hit the sector the PNGRB forecast relies on most. In power, gas is only 12% of demand and falling: India had about 274.68 GW of renewable capacity in March 2026, and batteries are taking over the evening peak that gas plants used to serve. Gas keeps a role as a balancing fuel in hot, dry summers (GAIL's eight spot cargoes in Q1 FY27 went largely to power plants during a deficient monsoon), but not as a growth engine. In the 2030s, green hydrogen could replace grey hydrogen made from gas in refineries and fertiliser plants, which together use about 70 MMSCMD (indicative); GAIL runs a 10 MW electrolyser at Vijaipur as a pilot.

Biogas: the molecule that uses the same pipes

Compressed biogas (CBG), made from farm waste, municipal waste and press mud, is chemically similar to natural gas and can be injected into city gas networks and pipelines. It is complementary to the grid rather than a competitor. Under the SATAT scheme, 194 plants were selling CBG on 1 October 2026, and 99 of them sold through GAIL. India's CBG sales were 96.5 thousand tonnes in FY26, of which GAIL bought 88.2 thousand tonnes (91%); in April–September 2026 alone they were 88.4 thousand tonnes (GAIL 85.5), already about 92% of the full FY26 volume. A CBG blending obligation for city gas rises to 5% by FY2028-29. CBG is small in rupees, but it is the fastest-growing gas molecule GAIL touches, and the obligation makes it mandatory demand.

Other technologies to watch

LNG trucking and small-scale LNG can reach customers beyond the pipeline network; PNGRB projects 4 MMSCMD by 2030 and 26 MMSCMD by 2040, and GAIL plans 29 LNG stations. Coal gasification, backed by an ₹8,500 crore incentive scheme, could add syngas supply for industry, though not pipeline-quality gas. Digital pipeline operations and electrified compressors (GAIL is installing a 3.6 MW electric drive at Vijaipur) reduce operating costs and system-use gas. Methane regulation, especially the EU's rules on imported gas, could raise costs on US LNG resold into Europe, a minor issue for GAIL's overseas trading.

Put together, the technology picture is mixed for volume: positive in homes, commerce and small industry, negative in transport and power, neutral-to-positive with biogas. For the pipeline toll the question is whether these shifts add enough MMSCMD to fill the eastern and central lines before the 2028 tariff review pushes their cost onto existing users. For the marketing book the question is who supplies the new customers: GAIL's own analysts' Q&A on the Q1 call noted that city gas companies increasingly source their own gas.

In plain terms

The war made piped gas look safer than imported LPG cylinders, and households and factories are switching. But electric scooters and buses are eating into CNG's growth, and solar power with batteries is replacing gas power plants. Biogas, made from waste, is small but growing fast, and GAIL buys almost all of it. Whether the new pipelines fill depends on homes and factories switching faster than cars and power plants switch away.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against India's gas highway

The arguments an optimist and a sceptic would make about the sub-sector and its one listed member, set side by side.

The bull and bear cases for Indian gas transmission and marketing turn on the same handful of variables: what PNGRB does in 2028, how wide the index spreads stay, whether volume finally grows, and whether the petrochemical bet pays. Both cases are internally consistent; the evidence for each is in the sections above.

The bull case

  • A regulated asset base with a scheduled catalyst. The 2023 order lifted transmission revenue 55% in a year; the April 2028 review must absorb about 5,600 km of new, nearly empty lines, which cost-plus arithmetic says should raise the tariff.
  • The shock absorber gets paid. GAIL's mix of US, JCC, Brent-linked and spot supply made it the system's balancing agent in 2026. The September Brent spike to $116.8 re-opened the JCC and cross-index spreads, so FY27 marketing may beat the ₹4,500 crore anchor.
  • LPG-to-gas switching. The Gulf LPG shock is pushing homes and industry onto piped gas; city gas demand is guided to 80–85 MMSCMD by 2030; CBG adds mandatory demand.
  • Cheaper LNG from 2027. The supply wave should bring back price-sensitive power and industry and fill the grid.
  • Overhangs removed. Unbundling dropped (8 July 2026); Konkan LNG fully owned; stakes worth about ₹13,150 crore; valuation near book.

The bear case

  • A market that does not grow. Consumption fell 3.6% in FY26, gas's share of energy is shrinking, domestic output is below FY14, and FY27 transmission is guided flat at about 123 MMSCMD.
  • A trading book that can lose. The JCC lag reverses when Brent falls; the 2027 supply wave narrows the Henry Hub advantage; marketing profit has ranged from −₹702 crore to ₹7,446 crore.
  • A petrochemical bet into a glut. FY26 loss ₹1,408 crore; PDH-PP (₹11,256 crore) slipping to about December 2027; Chinese oversupply; CWIP 35% of fixed assets.
  • Regulatory and policy risk. A 2028 review could lengthen lives or raise normative volumes rather than tariffs; users far from the coast already pay 28% more under the two-zone tariff.
  • Governance. State control of capex and dividends; no independent director since 28 March 2026; ₹3,768 crore naphtha excise dispute and ₹11,744 crore of non-fund guarantees off balance sheet.
