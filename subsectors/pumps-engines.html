00

The one-page view: three machines share a label, and a margin cycle

Nine companies, four business models, one dominant name, and a quarter in which sales rose 17% while operating profit fell 7%.

The NSE 750 files nine companies under "compressors, pumps and diesel engines". The label is administratively tidy and analytically misleading. It covers three different machines with three different customers. Engines and gensets (Cummins India, Kirloskar Oil Engines, Greaves Cotton) sell backup power to data centres, hospitals, offices and factories, plus engines for tractors, three-wheelers, trains and boats. Compressors (Elgi Equipments, Kirloskar Pneumatic) sell compressed air, the "fourth utility" of almost every factory, plus engineered gas-compression packages for city-gas stations and refineries. Pumps move water and process fluids, and this group itself splits in two: the engineered and industrial pump houses (KSB, Kirloskar Brothers) behave like capital-goods order-book businesses, while the solar-pump suppliers built on the government's PM-KUSUM scheme (Shakti Pumps, Oswal Pumps) behave like tender contractors whose customer is a state agency.

The common thread is a business model that matters more than the machine. All three are "sell once, service for fifteen to twenty years" products. A genset that goes into a data centre needs filters, oil, overhauls and after-treatment service for its whole life. A screw compressor in a textile mill needs airends rebuilt and controllers replaced. The companies with the largest installed base and a captive service network earn the best returns, convert the most profit to cash and get the highest valuations. The two KUSUM suppliers sit at the opposite pole: they sell a subsidised system once, at a price discovered by reverse auction, and wait months for a state treasury to pay. That single difference explains most of the spread between Oswal Pumps at about 8 times earnings and Cummins India at about 52 times.

Four forces define this sub-sector in late 2026, and the report is built around them.

  • Installed-base economics separate the compounders from the contractors. Cummins earns about 27% of revenue from its distribution (spares and service) business and Elgi 16–18% from aftermarket. They post 22–40% returns on capital and turn two-thirds or more of operating profit into cash. The KUSUM pair have almost no aftermarket, and Oswal's operating cash flow was negative in FY26.
  • Policy makes and unmakes demand. Emission rules (CPCB IV+ from July 2024) reset genset prices and pulled volumes forward. PM-KUSUM created a solar-pump boom and is now ending: version 1.0 closed to new sanctions on 31 December 2025, projects must be completed by 31 March 2027 with "no further extension", and version 2.0 (about ₹50,000 crore proposed) has not been launched. The Jal Jeevan Mission was extended to December 2028, but with smaller central releases and long contractor payment delays.
  • The AI data-centre build-out is the new genset driver. Cummins' domestic power-generation sales rose 35% in Q1 FY27 while non-data-centre demand grew only in single digits. The demand is real, concentrated and lumpy, and it favours the technology leader.
  • Who can pass on costs. With copper near record highs (about $14,300 a tonne in August 2026) and pig iron up 14%, the group's EBITDA margin fell 370 basis points in a single year-on-year quarter. Genset and compressor makers can reprice with a lag; engineered pump houses absorb inflation until their fixed-price orders roll over; KUSUM suppliers bid fixed prices and cannot recover anything.

A fifth theme runs underneath: parentage. Cummins India (51% owned by Cummins Inc.) and KSB (69.8% owned by KSB SE of Germany) borrow their parents' technology and export channels and trade at about 52 times earnings. They also pay for that technology. Cummins paid its parent a royalty of 2.5% of sales in FY26 against a "standardised" group rate of 4%, and management said the share would rise in FY27. If it reaches 4%, about ₹180 crore a year moves from Indian minority shareholders to the parent, roughly 150 basis points of margin (an illustrative calculation, shown in Part 10).

In plain terms

These nine companies make machines that factories, data centres and farms cannot run without. The best of them sell a machine once and then sell parts and service for twenty years, which makes them steady and cash-rich. The weakest sold solar pumps to a government scheme that is now ending, at prices set by bidding wars, and are still waiting to be paid. In mid-2026 every one of them faced higher copper and iron prices; the ones with pricing power raised prices, the ones selling to the government could not.

Three cautions apply to every number in this report. First, Cummins dominates the aggregates: it is 30% of combined revenue, 48% of profit and 57% of market value, so any group statistic is mostly a Cummins statistic. We show combined figures with and without it. Second, KSB's year ends in December, so its "FY26" is January–December 2025 and its June 2026 quarter is its second quarter of calendar 2026. Every combined figure that includes KSB mixes periods. Third, Kirloskar Oil Engines consolidates an NBFC (Arka Fincap), whose interest income sits in revenue and inflates the consolidated margin; for comparing engine businesses we use KOEL's standalone figures where they exist.

01

From casting to aftermarket: where the money is made

The value sits in the core machine's design and in twenty years of spares, not in assembling a package and bidding for a tender.

Every product in this sub-sector follows the same chain. Raw metal (pig iron, steel, copper, aluminium) becomes castings and windings. Those become a core machine: an engine block, a compressor "airend" (the pair of meshing screws that compress the air), or a pump's hydraulic end plus its motor. The core is packaged into something a customer can install: a genset (engine plus alternator, control panel and acoustic enclosure), a compressor skid, a pump-set, or a complete solar pumping system with panels, controller and mounting structure. It is sold through dealers, EPC contractors, state agencies or directly to large buyers such as hyperscale data-centre operators. And then, for fifteen to twenty years, the installed machine consumes spares and service.

A short vocabulary

  • Genset (DG set): a diesel or gas engine coupled to an alternator, with controls and an acoustic enclosure, rated in kVA. Low, medium and high horsepower (LHP, MHP, HHP) bands matter: HHP sets, roughly 750 kVA to above 3,000 kVA, are the data-centre and mining class.
  • CPCB IV+: the Central Pollution Control Board emission standard for gensets up to 800 kW, in force since 1 July 2024. It needs electronic engines and after-treatment, and it raised genset prices.
  • Distribution business / aftermarket: spares, filters, lubricants, overhauls, annual maintenance contracts and reconditioning sold into the installed base. Cummins calls this its Distribution Business Unit.
  • Screw, reciprocating, oil-free and centrifugal compressors: the main air-compressor technologies. Process-gas and CNG compressors are engineered packages for gas processing, city-gas stations, compressed biogas and hydrogen.
  • Engineered vs standard pumps: standard pumps are catalogue products for farms, homes and buildings; engineered pumps are designed for one plant's duty, such as boiler feed in a supercritical power station or reactor coolant in a nuclear plant.
  • PM-KUSUM Component B: the central scheme's subsidy for standalone off-grid solar pumps, the business of Shakti and Oswal. Component A funds small solar plants; Component C solarises grid-connected pumps.
  • DCR: Domestic Content Requirement. Subsidised solar systems must use Indian-made cells and modules, which is why both KUSUM suppliers are building cell lines.

Where value is captured

Three places earn above-average returns. The first is design and brand in the core machine, visible in gross margins: Cummins historically earned 36–37% gross margin on engines and gensets, and Elgi reported 50.2% in Q1 FY27 on compressors. The second is the installed-base aftermarket, the highest-margin and least cyclical stream, because a customer whose plant depends on a running machine buys the original maker's parts and service rather than risk a breakdown. The third is certification barriers: CPCB IV+ engine type-approval, nuclear and supercritical pump qualification (KSB, Kirloskar Brothers) and API-standard process-gas compressors (Kirloskar Pneumatic). Each takes years and testing to earn and keeps out cheap entrants.

Value is thinnest in subsidy-tender assembly. A KUSUM solar-pump system's largest cost is the bought-in Indian-made solar panel, its price is discovered by reverse auction among dozens of empanelled vendors, the customer is a state nodal agency, and the five years of operation and maintenance are bundled into the bid rather than sold as a separate annuity.

In plain terms

A genset maker earns twice from the same customer: once when it sells the machine and again, every year for two decades, when the machine needs parts and servicing. The second stream is smaller but steadier and more profitable, and it keeps flowing when new orders dry up. A company that sells a subsidised solar pump to a state agency earns once.

Two further structural points shape the chain. The packaging step is contested. Cummins sells engines to independent genset makers ("channel partners") who add alternators and enclosures; Kirloskar Oil Engines packages its own gensets. Each model has a trade-off: Cummins avoids bought-in content and inventory but shares the customer relationship with its partners; KOEL keeps the relationship but carries more low-margin content. The channel step decides credit risk. Dealers pay in weeks; EPC contractors and water-board projects pay in months; KUSUM state agencies pay after installation verification and central subsidy release, which in FY26 meant receivable days of 173 at Shakti and 199 at Oswal against 37–83 days at the engine makers.

02

Four archetypes under one label

Engines, compressors, engineered pumps and KUSUM pumps differ in customer, cycle, cash profile and valuation. Treat them as four sub-sectors.

Grouping the nine by business model rather than by product makes the numbers readable. Each archetype has its own revenue equation, its own customers and its own failure mode.

(a) Engines and gensets: volume × realisation + an installed-base annuity

Cummins India builds engines on 16- to 95-litre platforms licensed from Cummins Inc. and reports four lines: power generation (about 40% of FY26 sales), distribution (about 27%), industrial engines for construction, rail, mining, marine and compressors (about 16%) and exports (about 17%). It has almost no debt, a fixed-asset base that is small against ₹12,143 crore of sales, and a 39.5% return on capital employed. Kirloskar Oil Engines designs its own engines and packages its own gensets, and adds farm and industrial engines, a consumer pumps business and the NBFC Arka Fincap; its standalone engine business earned a 13.1% EBITDA margin on about ₹5,647 crore in FY26. Greaves Cotton is mainly small engines (three-wheeler diesel and CNG, small gensets, farm), a retail aftermarket business and Greaves Electric Mobility, which makes Ampere electric scooters and Eltra electric three-wheelers.

The economics favour scale. Engineering and test cells are fixed costs, so a genset maker with high volumes earns operating leverage, and the installed base compounds into aftermarket revenue. Emission rules periodically reset the industry: every new norm raises the cost of entry and pushes customers towards makers who can certify.

(b) Compressors: product plus aftermarket, or engineered packages plus order book

Elgi is close to a pure air-compressor company: about 91% compressors and the rest automotive service equipment, roughly half of revenue overseas, more than two million machines installed, and an estimated 22% share of the Indian air-compressor market behind Atlas Copco. Its model is high gross margin, high selling cost and a growing aftermarket. Kirloskar Pneumatic is order-book driven: about 92% compression systems (air, refrigeration, process gas and CNG, biogas and hydrogen packages) and about 8% transmission (naval gearboxes and railway gears). Its order book was ₹1,853 crore at June 2026 and management's sustainable margin band is 18–20%.

(c1) Engineered pump houses: order book × execution, with a nuclear niche

KSB, 69.8% owned by KSB SE, makes about 80% pumps and 18% valves and runs its own foundry. It is one of very few qualified Indian suppliers of reactor-coolant and supercritical boiler-feed pumps. Kirloskar Brothers is India's largest pump company by revenue (₹4,538 crore consolidated) with about 38% of revenue earned abroad, mainly through SPP Pumps in the UK and US. KBL has cut low-margin EPC work to about 3% of sales and had a consolidated order book of ₹4,062 crore at June 2026, up 21% year on year.

(c2) KUSUM suppliers: tender volume × realisation − panel cost, financed by receivables

Shakti Pumps, based in Pithampur, Madhya Pradesh, with capacity of about five lakh pumps a year, installed 86,086 solar pumps in FY26 and 27,678 in Q1 FY27. About 77% of its revenue comes from government programmes. Oswal Pumps of Karnal, Haryana, earned about 87% of revenue from PM-KUSUM in the nine months to December 2024, according to its IPO prospectus. Both are integrating backward into motors, controllers and solar modules, and now cells: Shakti a 2.2 GW cell-and-module plant, Oswal a 1.2 GW TOPCon cell plant costing ₹456 crore and due in April 2028.

The KUSUM pair were the fastest-growing archetype in FY26 (+20.7%) and had the second-highest margin (19.7%), yet they trade at 12.7 times earnings against 38.6–54.7 times for the other three. In plain terms

Read these nine as four separate industries. Engine makers sell to private businesses and earn a long tail of service income. Compressor makers do the same for factories, with more exports. Engineered pump makers build to order for power plants and water projects and get paid slowly. Solar-pump makers sell to state governments under a subsidy scheme and live or die by that scheme.

03

Size, growth and the Cummins weight

The markets are estimates that disagree with each other; company revenues are firmer ground, and one company tilts every total.

Industry market-size estimates for these machines are unreliable, and we present them as ranges. Research firms put India's diesel-genset market at roughly US$0.9–1.2 billion in 2024. That looks too low: Cummins India's domestic power-generation sales alone were about ₹4,800 crore in FY26 (around 40% of its revenue). Compressors are put at about US$2.3 billion (2025) by MarkNtel, roughly ₹22,000 crore at our working exchange rate, with air compressors about 79% of it and the top five players about 45%. Pumps are estimated at US$2.9–4.0 billion depending on the source and scope, roughly ₹28,000–38,000 crore, with agriculture and irrigation about a third.

Company revenues are the firmer ground

For the eight companies with three comparable years (Kirloskar Pneumatic's earlier history is not shown on the same basis), revenue grew from ₹25,437 crore in FY23 to ₹39,228 crore in FY26, a 15.5% compound rate; adding Kirloskar Pneumatic takes FY26 to ₹41,015 crore. Cummins and KOEL compounded at 15–16% on data centres and the CPCB IV+ price step-up. Elgi, Greaves and Kirloskar Brothers grew in single digits; KBL's FY26 was flat (+1.0%) by design as it ran down low-margin EPC contracts.

Domestic versus export

Exports matter more than the label suggests. Cummins exported ₹1,989 crore in FY26 (about 17% of revenue), mostly to its parent's network in more than 60 countries. Roughly half of Elgi's revenue is international. Kirloskar Brothers earns about 38% abroad, mainly through SPP. Shakti exported about ₹411 crore (around 15%). The others are mostly domestic. In plain terms

Nobody measures these markets well, so treat any single market-size figure with suspicion. The companies' own sales are better evidence: together the nine sold about ₹41,000 crore of machines and service in FY26, and nearly a third of that was Cummins. Whenever you read an average for "the sector", ask how much of it is really Cummins.

04

Where demand comes from: data halls, gas stations, canals and farms

Eleven end-markets feed these nine companies; in 2026 one of them (data centres) is booming, one (subsidised solar pumps) is ending and one (rural water) is slowing to the speed of state treasuries.

Demand for these machines is derived demand. Nobody wants a genset, a compressor or a pump for its own sake; they want uninterrupted power, compressed air on the shop floor, water in a field or a pipeline. That means the drivers are other industries' investment decisions and, for pumps, government budgets. The useful question for each company is which of those decisions it is exposed to and how cyclical they are.

1. Data centres: the new genset engine

India had about 1,700 MW of data-centre IT capacity at the end of 2025 after a record 440 MW was added during the year, a 160% jump in annual additions (CBRE). CBRE expects about 500 MW more in 2026 and more than 3 GW by the end of 2028; Savills forecasts 3,400 MW by 2030 and Colliers more than 4,500 MW in the top seven cities. Every megawatt of IT load needs standby power, and the rule of thumb is up to 1.5 MW of diesel backup per MW of IT load once redundancy is included. That is why Cummins' domestic power-generation sales rose 35% year on year to ₹1,424 crore in Q1 FY27 while management described non-data-centre power-generation demand as growing only in single digits. 2. The CPCB IV+ emission reset

From 1 July 2024 gensets up to 800 kW had to meet CPCB IV+, which requires electronically controlled engines and exhaust after-treatment. Buyers rushed to purchase cheaper CPCB II sets before the deadline, so the following year's volumes fell 15–20% on that inflated base, and prices later corrected by 10–12% as competition returned (Motilal Oswal, July 2025). The lasting effect is a higher value per unit and a richer service stream, because after-treatment systems need specialist maintenance. Part 7 covers the conflicting estimates of the price step-up.

3. Real estate, factories, warehouses and hospitals

The traditional genset market, backup for offices, malls, apartment towers, hospitals and factories, is mature and grows with construction activity. Quick-commerce dark stores and warehouses are a small new source. Cummins described this non-data-centre demand as single-digit growth in Q1 FY27, which is the base rate investors should assume when data-centre orders pause.

4. Infrastructure, rail, mining, marine and defence

Industrial engines go into excavators, compactors, locomotives, mining trucks, ships and defence vehicles. Cummins' domestic industrial sales were ₹458 crore in Q1 FY27 (+10%): construction ₹148 crore, rail ₹145 crore, compressor engines ₹52 crore and marine ₹50 crore. KOEL's industrial segment grew 19%. Kirloskar Pneumatic's transmission business sells naval gearboxes and railway gears, a small but defensible niche.

5. Industrial capex: compressed air as the fourth utility

Almost every factory uses compressed air to drive tools, move material and run automation, and electricity is 70–80% of a compressor's lifetime cost. Demand therefore tracks manufacturing output and new plant construction, and the replacement cycle is driven by energy savings from variable-speed and oil-free machines. Elgi's India revenue grew 28% in Q1 FY27, the clearest evidence of a healthy domestic capex cycle. Kirloskar Pneumatic said conflict in the Middle East slowed the conversion of its large package orders in the same quarter.

6. City gas, compressed biogas and hydrogen

India's city-gas distribution network is expected to roughly double its CNG station count from about 7,000 to about 15,000 by 2031 (analyst estimate). Each station needs compressors, and Kirloskar Pneumatic is the domestic leader in CNG compression packages; it also maintains more than 1,000 gas stations under O&M contracts, a service line growing 25–30% a year. Compressed biogas plants and early hydrogen projects add a smaller, newer stream.

7. Water: Jal Jeevan Mission, irrigation and urban sewage

The Jal Jeevan Mission was extended to December 2028 by the Cabinet on 10 March 2026 with a total outlay of ₹8.69 lakh crore, but the Expenditure Finance Committee recommended only ₹1.51 lakh crore of central release against ₹2.79 lakh crore sought. Contractor dues are large: Kerala's reached about ₹4,500 crore in early 2025 and Tamil Nadu sought ₹3,112 crore from the Centre in March 2026. Kirloskar Brothers and KSB sell pumps into these projects, and Oswal is pivoting towards them. Part 11 covers the working-capital consequences.

8. Power: supercritical thermal and nuclear

Kirloskar Brothers' power-sector orders grew 30% in Q1 FY27, "largely nuclear". KSB holds nuclear and supercritical qualifications. India's renewed thermal build and its nuclear expansion plans make this a multi-year niche where few suppliers are qualified, prices are better and orders are lumpy. See the power generation and heavy electrical reports for the plant-side view.

9. Oil and gas

KSB won an ONGC order for seven main injection pumps worth about ₹85 crore, deliverable by FY29, and a Dangote (Nigeria) order for 18 boiler-feed pump packages worth $12.4 million, about ₹118 crore at our working exchange rate. SPP, Kirloskar Brothers' UK subsidiary, won a £11.7 million vertical-pump order from Saipem. Kirloskar Pneumatic's process-gas compressors serve refineries and gas processing.

10. Agriculture: monsoon, power tariffs and solar subsidy

Farm pumps are the largest pump end-market by units, and about a third of pump revenue by value. Grid-connected electric pumps depend on rural power supply and tariffs; diesel pumps on diesel prices; solar pumps on subsidy. Under PM-KUSUM Component B, 9.03 lakh standalone solar pumps had been installed by September 2025 against 12.72 lakh sanctioned. Shakti's installations still rose 57.6% in Q1 FY27 as states rushed to complete KUSUM 1.0 work before the March 2027 deadline. Part 8 is the full story.

11. Exports

US and European industrial demand, Middle Eastern and African infrastructure, and the parents' global supply chains drive exports. In Q1 FY27 Elgi's North American revenue rose 37% and European revenue 21% (both helped by the weaker rupee), while Cummins called its export outlook "difficult" and KOEL's exports were weak. Who competes: MNC parents, Kirloskar cousins and a long unorganised tail

Gensets are concentrated, compressors moderately so, pumps fragmented; and the listed nine are only part of each market.

The three machines have three different competitive structures. Understanding who else sells into each market, and who owns the listed companies, matters as much as comparing the nine with each other.

Gensets: concentrated, certification-protected

The top five genset makers (Cummins India, Kirloskar Oil Engines, Caterpillar with its FG Wilson brand, Mahindra Powerol and Greaves) are said to hold 55–60% of organised revenue. That figure comes from the KOEL primer without a cited source, and we treat it as INDICATIVE. Cummins is said to dominate the high-horsepower and data-centre niche with about 80% share, also unsourced. Unlisted or part-of-larger competitors include Mahindra Powerol (a division of Mahindra & Mahindra), Caterpillar India, Jakson (private), Escorts Kubota, Ashok Leyland and Eicher; Cummins counts 10–12 competitors in power generation. CPCB IV+ consolidated the market towards engine makers able to certify electronic engines with after-treatment. Chinese gensets are marginal in India because emission type-approval, brand trust and service reach act as barriers.

Compressors: a global leader, a domestic challenger, a long tail

The top five compressor makers hold about 45% of the market (MarkNtel). Atlas Copco, the global leader, operates in India as an unlisted subsidiary. Elgi claims about 22% of the Indian air-compressor market as the number two. Ingersoll Rand India is listed but not in this sub-sector's NSE 750 group. Kaeser and Chicago Pneumatic compete in screw compressors, and Kirloskar Pneumatic leads in process-gas and CNG packages. Chinese screw compressors compete at the low end on price.

Pumps: fragmented, with an unorganised half

Unorganised makers supply "nearly half" of price-sensitive agricultural and domestic pumps (Indian Infrastructure, 2024). Organised players include Kirloskar Brothers, KSB, CRI (unlisted and a major KUSUM supplier), Crompton (listed, residential pumps), Grundfos, Wilo-Mather & Platt, Xylem, WPIL and Roto Pumps (both listed), Texmo/Taro and Lubi. KUSUM Component B is supplied by dozens of empanelled makers bidding against each other; Oswal claims about 38% of the scheme, counting direct and indirect supply, and Shakti is among the largest.

China matters differently for each machine. In gensets, emission certification and after-sales service keep Chinese sets marginal. The data-centre genset race and the CPCB IV+ reset Deep dive

AI data halls have become the swing factor in India's genset market; an emission rule has raised the price of every set below 800 kW. Both favour the incumbent with the technology.

For a decade the Indian genset market was a mature, single-digit-growth business tied to construction and the reliability of the power grid. Two things changed it between 2024 and 2026. An emission rule raised the price of every small and mid-sized set, and a wave of data-centre construction created demand for the largest sets at a scale India had not seen. Cummins India is the main beneficiary of both, Kirloskar Oil Engines is trying to follow it up the horsepower curve, and the question for investors is how much of Cummins' recent growth is durable.

A typical high-horsepower set is 2,500 kVA, roughly 2 MW at a 0.8 power factor. From there the arithmetic is simple.

Who wins the high-horsepower race

Cummins India has the QSK60, QSK78 and QSK95 platforms and its parent's global data-centre reference sites, which matter enormously to hyperscale buyers who standardise equipment across countries. It runs at 70–75% utilisation and says domestic capacity for its two largest engines is "not yet justified by demand", so it is supplying the very top end partly from imports or the parent's network. Kirloskar Oil Engines has the OptiPrime Dual Core 2,500 kVA set and one important credential: a 192 MW order from HyperNext for 96 sets (96 × 2 MW = 192 MW). Its Q1 FY27 commentary cited "strong data-centre order execution" without giving values. Caterpillar, unlisted in India, is the global data-centre incumbent, and MTU (Rolls-Royce Power Systems) and Mitsubishi compete at the very top of the range. Greaves is not in this market; its gensets are small.

The unsourced claim that Cummins holds about 80% of the high-horsepower niche cannot be verified, but the structure supports dominance: hyperscalers buy on global framework agreements, value reference sites and service coverage over price, and order in large lots that only a few suppliers can deliver on time. KOEL's challenge is to win enough of these orders to build its own reference base.

The CPCB IV+ reset

The second change was regulatory. Under rules notified in 2022, gensets up to 800 kW had to meet the CPCB IV+ emission standard from 1 July 2024, replacing CPCB II. The new standard needs electronically controlled engines with exhaust after-treatment, which raises the cost of the engine and the complexity of service.

What could go wrong

  • Lumpiness: data-centre orders come in large lots from a handful of buyers. A pause in hyperscaler capex, or a shift in where they build, shows up immediately in quarterly powergen sales, and the base business underneath grows only in single digits.
  • Technology substitution: battery storage can cover short outages and smooth ramps, and gas gensets or fuel cells could take share in a decarbonising market. The pollution rule caused a buying rush, then a slump, then settled at higher prices. The data-centre boom is real but comes in big, irregular orders from a few customers, and outside data centres the market is growing only slowly.

PM-KUSUM's end-game: the boom, the price war and the 2.0 cliff Deep dive

A subsidy scheme built two listed companies in four years. With version 1.0 closing and 2.0 not yet launched, their margins have halved and their receivables have grown.

Shakti Pumps' revenue rose from ₹968 crore in FY23 to ₹2,698 crore in FY26. Oswal Pumps' rose from ₹385 crore to ₹2,064 crore and it listed in June 2025 at ₹614 a share. Both rode one government scheme: PM-KUSUM Component B, which subsidises standalone solar pumps for farmers. In 2026 the scheme is ending in its current form. New sanctions under version 1.0 stopped on 31 December 2025, existing projects must be finished by 31 March 2027, and version 2.0 has been proposed but not launched. The suppliers' margins have collapsed under a bidding war, their receivables from state agencies have grown, and Oswal's shares trade about 60% below the IPO price.

How the scheme works

Under the standard Component B pattern, the central government (through MNRE) provides 30% of the benchmark system cost as central financial assistance, the state provides 30% and the farmer 40%, part of which can be bank-financed. In north-eastern and hill states the central share is 50%. States vary the pattern: Karnataka uses 30/50/20 and Maharashtra's state-run Magel Tyala programme reduces the farmer's share to about 5–10% (approximate). The Shakti primer's simplified split ("farmer about 10%, Centre and state about 60%, about 30% finance") describes one state-specific variant, not the national standard.

The state nodal agency runs the procurement. It tenders for systems by pump size, discovers the price by lowest-bid (L1) auctions, and empanels the vendors who accept that price. The vendor supplies the pump, motor, controller, DCR solar panels and structure, installs the system, and maintains it for five years. The agency pays after installation is verified, and the central share flows through the state. The supplier's customer, in credit terms, is a state agency waiting for its own budget and the Centre's release.

The price-war arithmetic

A small realisation cut does disproportionate damage to a business with high materials intensity and no pricing power after the bid. In plain terms

A solar pump sold under the scheme is mostly a solar panel the supplier buys from someone else, plus a pump and a motor. The price is set by whoever bids lowest. Receivables: profit that has not become cash

The customer is a state agency, and state agencies pay slowly. Four scenarios for the end-game

  1. Pull-forward to March 2027. States rush to complete sanctioned-but-uninstalled pumps before the deadline, so FY27 volumes stay high while realisations fall. This is what Q1 FY27 already shows.
  2. KUSUM 2.0 launches in the second half of FY27. New guidelines, then state allocations, tenders, empanelment and installation: a gap of two to four quarters before new-scheme revenue arrives, with a dip in between.
  3. 2.0 slips into FY28. FY28 volumes have no national scheme beneath them, leaving state programmes, exports and diversification to fill the gap.
  4. 2.0 arrives with a different shape. The proposal leans towards feeder-level solarisation, agri-PV (a dedicated 10 GW component) and batteries, which may favour solar developers and EPC firms over pump-system assemblers. Pump makers are responding by becoming solar manufacturers (DCR cells) and EPC players: Oswal has a 72 MW solar EPC order book and a 359 MW pipeline, plus an active order book of 22,025 pumps and about 12,500 more in the near-term pipeline.

The market is already pricing much of this. Oswal trades at 8.4 times earnings and Shakti at 24.6 times, against 31–52 times for the engineered names. The question is not whether KUSUM 1.0 ends (it does) but whether these companies' new bets (cell manufacturing, EPC, water projects, exports and, for Shakti, EV motors through Shakti EV Mobility) can replace a scheme that once provided most of their revenue, while their balance sheets carry large state receivables.

In plain terms

The solar-pump suppliers are in the last year of a government programme that made them. Orders are still coming in as states rush to finish, but at lower prices, and the money takes months to arrive. The successor scheme has been announced in principle but not launched, and it may favour a different kind of company. Their low share prices reflect that uncertainty.

The drivers

Copper was about $14,300 a tonne in August 2026, close to January's record and up about 50% in twelve months; the rupee's 8–10% fall added to the rupee cost. Every pump and compressor motor and every genset alternator carries copper. Pig iron and castings rose 14% year on year, according to Cummins. Wage resets: Cummins applied merit increases from 1 April 2026 plus a variable-pay true-up. Royalty: Cummins' payment to its parent is rising from 2.5% of sales (Part 10). Mix and one-offs: Elgi booked a ₹7.3 crore restructuring charge; Kirloskar Pneumatic began consolidating the loss-making SCIPL from May 2026; Kirloskar Brothers' SPP UK services contribution weakened; KOEL's exports were weak. And for the KUSUM pair, tender prices fell while their costs rose.

Cummins' own disclosure shows how the squeeze looks from the inside. Its gross margin, historically 36–37%, fell below that range and management said it did not expect a quick return. Profit before tax and exceptional items was ₹721 crore, 21.4% of sales, down 0.7% year on year and 12% quarter on quarter. Treasury income cushioned the bottom line, which is why reported PAT (₹609 crore on screener's consolidated basis; ₹543 crore in another source that appears to use a different basis) barely moved while operating margin fell 350 basis points.

In plain terms

In the April–June 2026 quarter these companies sold more than ever, but the copper and iron they buy got much more expensive, and most of them had not yet raised their own prices enough. The companies that sell to private customers lost some margin but still made about as much money as a year earlier. The two solar-pump makers, which cannot raise prices on a government tender, saw their profits roughly halve.

How much pricing would undo it?

A useful way to judge recoverability is to ask what price increase would restore the FY26 margin if costs stayed where they were in Q1 FY27. The formula is (target margin − current margin) ÷ (1 − target margin), because every rupee of price increase drops straight to EBITDA. For Cummins, restoring 21.4% from 18.0% needs about a 4.3% price increase on the whole book, roughly ₹117 crore of extra quarterly EBITDA on Q1 FY27 sales. That is plausible for a market leader that has already raised prices once and is "assessing more". For Oswal, restoring 24.9% from 15.6% needs about 12.4%, and the tender structure allows none of it.

Three recovery speeds

The analysis sorts the group into three recovery speeds. Private-market franchises (gensets and compressors) should recover most of the squeeze over two to three quarters through list-price increases: Cummins raised prices in July, Elgi expected pricing to land in Q2–Q3 FY27, and Greaves guided to improvement from Q2. Engineered-pump order books recover as new orders are priced at current costs, which takes longer and depends on whether contracts include escalation clauses; KSB's two latest quarters (8% and 12% margins) show the lag. Tender-driven KUSUM suppliers cannot pass costs through at all within an awarded tender; their only levers are cheaper inputs (their own cells and modules, from FY28) and better-priced future tenders.

The first real test is Cummins' Q2 FY27 result, due on 4 November 2026. If its operating margin moves back towards 20% on the July price increase, the squeeze was a lag. If it stays near 18% with copper still elevated and the royalty rising, the FY26 margin may have been a peak.

Seasonality: a warning about quarterly comparisons

Quarterly margins in this group are noisy for structural reasons. Kirloskar Pneumatic is the extreme case: in FY26 its fourth quarter delivered 40% of the year's revenue (₹712 crore of ₹1,787 crore) and 57% of its profit, at a 26% margin, against 12–15% in the other three quarters. Large package orders are invoiced on completion, and customers bunch completions into the financial year-end. Engineered-pump houses show similar, milder patterns, and KUSUM installations peak before scheme deadlines. Compare quarters year on year, never sequentially, and read a single quarter as a signal to be confirmed rather than a trend.

In plain terms

Generator and compressor makers can fix a cost squeeze by raising prices, and most have started to. A 4% price rise would restore Cummins' old margin. Pump makers working through older fixed-price orders need several quarters. Solar-pump makers would need price rises of 7–12% that their government customers will not pay. Watch Cummins' November result for the first sign of whether the squeeze is temporary.

Parentage and the royalty question

Foreign parents bring technology, export channels and a 52× multiple. They also take a royalty, decide where exports come from and, at Cummins, are raising their share.

Two of the nine are subsidiaries of global groups. Cummins Inc. owns 51% of Cummins India; KSB SE of Germany owns 69.8% of KSB. Both trade at about 52 times earnings, the joint-highest multiples in the group, and both pay their parents for the technology they use. For Cummins this payment is now moving, and it is the most under-discussed number in the sub-sector.

What the parent provides

The benefits are real. Cummins India builds engines on platforms designed and continuously upgraded by Cummins Inc., including the CPCB IV+ electronics and after-treatment that small competitors struggled to certify. It sells about ₹2,000 crore a year (₹1,989 crore in FY26) into the parent's global network, using India as a sourcing hub for high-horsepower engines. Hyperscale data-centre buyers deal with Cummins globally and value the parent's reference sites. KSB's nuclear and supercritical pump qualifications rest on KSB SE's designs, and recent export wins such as Dangote in Nigeria come through the group. KSB SE raised its stake from 66.69% to 69.8% in June 2024, a signal of commitment.

What the parent takes: the Cummins royalty

Cummins India pays Cummins Inc. a royalty for its technology. According to a summary of the Q1 FY27 earnings call, the royalty was 2.5% of sales in FY26 against a group "standardised rate" of 4%, and management said the share was expected to rise in FY27. On FY26 sales of ₹12,143 crore, 2.5% is about ₹304 crore. Older related-party filings show royalty of ₹25.0 crore in the second half of FY19 and ₹26.8 crore in the first half of FY20, about ₹52 crore a year then; on our estimate the share of sales has roughly tripled since. The Cummins primer does not mention the royalty at all.

Control over exports and capacity

The parent also decides which plant serves which market. Cummins India's exports were flat in Q1 FY27, with high-horsepower exports up 16% and low-horsepower exports down 20%; management called the outlook "difficult". Minority shareholders do not steer how much global demand is routed to India rather than to Cummins plants elsewhere, nor whether the very largest engines are built in Phaltan or imported. Cummins India gets a new managing director, Adegbile Adewunmi, from 1 November 2026, which is worth watching for any change in the parent's allocation of work to India.

Why the market still pays a premium

Despite the royalty and the control, both subsidiaries trade at multiples two to four times their parents' (indicative global multiples in Part 19). The reasons are scarcity (few listed Indian vehicles for data-centre power or engineered pumps), domestic growth, debt-free balance sheets with high payouts (Cummins declared ₹66 a share for FY26, and screener shows a payout ratio of about 70%), and index ownership. The counterweights are exactly the items above: royalty creep, parent control of export allocation, and the parent's freedom to serve India through other entities. KSB SE itself is indicatively valued at about 10–12 times earnings in Germany; the market pays roughly five times as much for its Indian subsidiary's earnings.

In plain terms

Cummins India pays its American parent a fee for using its engine designs. That fee was 2.5% of sales last year, and the company says it will rise towards the group's standard 4%. That would move about ₹180 crore a year from the Indian company's shareholders to the parent, without a shareholder vote, because the rules only require one above 5%. Investors paying 52 times earnings for Cummins India should know this number.

Water, the Jal Jeevan Mission and the engineered-pump order book

The engineered pump houses sell into water, power and process projects: good order books, slow customers, and cash that arrives long after the profit.

KSB and Kirloskar Brothers are order-book businesses. Their revenue in any year is mostly the execution of orders won in previous years, plus a stream of catalogue products (agricultural, building and retail pumps) sold through dealers. Their customers include state water boards and irrigation departments, EPC contractors building water and sewage schemes, thermal and nuclear power plants, refineries and oil producers, and building developers. That mix gives them better visibility than the genset makers but worse cash conversion, because government projects pay slowly.

The Jal Jeevan Mission: extended, but with less central money

The Jal Jeevan Mission, launched in August 2019 to bring a piped tap connection to every rural household, was extended by the Cabinet on 10 March 2026 to December 2028 with a total outlay of ₹8.69 lakh crore. The extension is good news for pumps and pipes. The funding detail is less so: the Expenditure Finance Committee recommended only ₹1.51 lakh crore of central release against ₹2.79 lakh crore sought, about 54% of the request. States must fund the rest or slow the work. Contractor dues are already large: Kerala's JJM dues reached about ₹4,500 crore in early 2025, and Tamil Nadu sought ₹3,112 crore from the Centre in March 2026. When contractors are not paid, they pay their suppliers late too.

The US tariff path

In August 2025 the United States imposed a 25% "reciprocal" tariff on most Indian goods plus a further 25% penalty linked to India's purchases of Russian oil, 50% in total. In February 2026 an interim India–US framework cut the reciprocal rate to 18%. The Engineering Export Promotion Council welcomed it but said sector detail was pending, and we could not confirm that the framework has been signed or finalised; investors should treat the 18% rate as provisional. Separate Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminium and some auto parts continue to burden steel-heavy engineering goods. In August 2026 the US Senate passed a Russia-sanctions bill that would allow tariffs of up to 100% on buyers of Russian energy; EEPC called it "concerning". Despite all this, Indian engineering exports to the US reached $19.6 billion in FY26, up 2.3%. Update (19 Oct 2026): the 18% rate did not last. The US Supreme Court struck down the emergency-powers (IEEPA) tariffs on 20 Feb 2026; a temporary 10% Section 122 surcharge applied until it lapsed on 24 Jul 2026, and since then Indian goods face a 10% Section 301 duty on top of normal (MFN) rates, versus 12.5% for China and Vietnam. That duty is under legal challenge.

Emission norms: the genset price-setter

Each emission reset raises the cost of a compliant engine, consolidates the market towards makers who can certify, pulls purchases forward into the months before the deadline and then leaves a volume air-pocket. CPCB II in 2014 and CPCB IV+ in 2024 followed that pattern. The next reset is not scheduled, but the direction of travel (cleaner engines, gas and dual-fuel kits, hydrogen-ready designs) favours the companies with the deepest engineering budgets, which in India means Cummins and, to a lesser extent, KOEL. Larger gensets above 800 kW, the data-centre class, sit outside CPCB IV+ and follow other norms.

Subsidies: the solar-pump volume-setter

KUSUM is covered in Part 8. Two regulatory details matter beyond the scheme's timing. First, the domestic-content rule now extends to cells: the ALMM list for cells applies from June 2026, so subsidised systems will increasingly need Indian-made cells as well as modules. That raises system costs in the short run and rewards integrated manufacturers, which is why Shakti and Oswal are building cell lines. Second, the NISE draft specification for solar-pump controllers (August 2026) calls for higher efficiency and battery or grid interaction, which hints at what KUSUM 2.0 systems may need to include, and which could favour suppliers with better electronics.

Water: the payment-setter

The Jal Jeevan Mission sets pump volumes for rural water schemes, but its more important effect on listed companies is on payment speed. When the central release falls short, states slow payments to contractors, and contractors slow payments to suppliers. KBL's and KSB's receivables and Oswal's pivot towards water projects all depend on how states fund the ₹1.28 lakh crore gap between what they asked for and what the Centre recommended.

Royalty rules: the parent's limit

SEBI's 2019 rule protects minorities only above 5% of consolidated turnover. Below that, a parent can raise its royalty through an ordinary related-party approval process in which minority shareholders do not vote separately. Cummins' move from 2.5% towards 4% is therefore a governance matter for disclosure and engagement rather than a vote.

Efficiency standards and trade

The Bureau of Energy Efficiency's labelling programme for pump-sets and motors pushes the market towards efficient designs and penalises the unorganised segment, which struggles to certify. Trade rules are covered in Part 12; the practical point is that the 18% US rate is provisional, Section 232 metals duties apply regardless, and a Russia-sanctions tariff remains a tail risk.

Booms, busts and resets: a century of Indian machine-making

Licensed technology, MNC control, a telecom-tower boom, two emission resets, a subsidy era and now an AI build-out: each left its mark on today's nine.

The companies in this group are among the oldest industrial firms in India. Greaves traces its origins to 1859 in Bombay; the Kirloskar group was founded in 1888 and incorporated Kirloskar Brothers in 1920. Their history explains today's structure: who owns the technology, who has the installed base, and why the newest entrants are the ones most exposed to a single scheme.

Seven phases

1. The licensed-technology era (1940s–1990s). The Kirloskar group built Kirloskar Oil Engines (1946) and Kirloskar Pneumatic (1958) alongside Kirloskar Brothers; Kirloskar Cummins was formed as a joint venture in 1962 and began engine production in 1964; KSB Pumps and Elgi were both founded in 1960. In a protected economy, technology came through licences and joint ventures, and installed bases were built over decades.

2. MNC control and opening (1997–2000s). Cummins Inc. took 51% of the joint venture in 1997 and renamed it Cummins India in 1998. Kirloskar Brothers bought SPP Pumps in the UK in 2003, its first big step abroad. KOEL's 2009 restructuring created the listed entity in its current form.

3. The telecom-tower genset boom and bust (2005–2014). Mobile-tower rollouts in a power-short country created a large market for small gensets. As grid reliability improved and tower companies switched to batteries and hybrid systems, that market faded. It is the clearest precedent for how a single, concentrated end-market can lift and then leave a genset maker. Data-centre investors should remember it, while noting that data centres need far more reliable backup than towers did.

4. Emission resets (2014 and 2024). CPCB II and then CPCB IV+ each produced a pre-buy, an air-pocket and a price step-up, and each consolidated the market towards engine makers who could certify.

5. The subsidy era for pumps (2019–2027). PM-KUSUM and the Jal Jeevan Mission both launched in 2019. The solar-pump suppliers boomed in FY24–FY25: Shakti's profit reached ₹408 crore and Oswal's margin 29% in FY25. FY26 and Q1 FY27 brought the price war and the receivables build-up.

6. The AI data-centre wave (2024 onward). India added a record 440 MW of data-centre capacity in 2025. Cummins' power-generation sales grew 24% in FY26 and its domestic powergen 35% in Q1 FY27.

7. The listing wave and its aftermath (2025–2026). Oswal Pumps listed in June 2025 at ₹614 a share, raising ₹1,387 crore, at the peak of the KUSUM cycle; it closed at ₹248 on 16 October 2026. Greaves withdrew the planned IPO of Greaves Electric Mobility in July 2026 and replaced it with a ₹530 crore rights issue.

Three lessons carry forward from this history. Reading the machine-makers: the KPI masterclass Deep dive

Fourteen numbers explain almost everything about these nine companies. What each one means, how to compute it, what good looks like, and where the definitions trip you up.

Every company in this group, whatever it makes, runs on the same underlying equation. It sells a number of machines at a price, earns a stream of service revenue from machines it sold in the past, pays for materials (mostly metal, copper and bought-in components) and for people and overheads, and then has to fund the working capital that sits between making a machine and being paid for it. The KPIs below are simply the terms of that equation, measured.

A worked example in round numbers

Take an ILLUSTRATIVE genset maker that sells 10,000 engines a year at an average of ₹10 lakh each, ₹1,000 crore of equipment revenue at a 34% gross margin, and serves an installed base of one lakh engines, each spending about ₹20,000 a year on parts and service, ₹200 crore of aftermarket revenue at a 50% gross margin. Revenue is ₹1,200 crore and gross profit ₹440 crore (₹340 crore plus ₹100 crore), a blended gross margin of 36.7%. Employees cost ₹100 crore and other overheads ₹80 crore, including a 2.5% royalty of ₹30 crore. EBITDA is ₹260 crore, a 21.7% margin.

Now stress it. A 4% price increase on equipment adds ₹40 crore straight to EBITDA, lifting it to ₹300 crore on revenue of ₹1,240 crore (24.2%). A 5% rise in materials costs (₹760 crore of materials) removes ₹38 crore, taking EBITDA to ₹222 crore (18.5%). Raising the royalty from 2.5% to 4% removes another ₹18 crore. The aftermarket provides ₹100 crore of the ₹440 crore gross profit (23%) from 17% of revenue, and it barely moves in a downturn. Those four sensitivities (price, materials, royalty, service mix) are the whole analytical game in this sub-sector.

In plain terms

A machine-maker's profit comes from how many machines it sells, at what price, plus what it earns servicing old machines, minus what it pays for metal and people. Price and service are mostly in management's hands. Metal prices, exchange rates and government auction prices are not. When you read a result, ask which of these moved.

1. Gross margin: where design value shows up

What it is: the share of revenue left after materials. Formula: (revenue − cost of materials consumed, adjusted for inventory change) ÷ revenue. Worked example: a compressor sold for ₹10 lakh with ₹5 lakh of materials has a 50% gross margin. What drives it: pricing power, product mix (oil-free, high-horsepower and aftermarket carry higher margins), the share of bought-in content (a genset packager that buys alternators and enclosures has a lower gross margin than an engine maker), and input prices with the pass-through lag. Benchmarks: engines above 36% is good, 30–36% average; compressors above 48% good, 40–48% average; engineered pumps above 40% good; KUSUM systems above 30% good, 22–30% average. How the nine compare: only Elgi reports it clearly (50.2% in Q1 FY27, down 196 bps year on year). Cummins discloses a historical 36–37% and says it is now below that range. Oswal disclosed a 548 bps quarter-on-quarter fall. The others do not disclose gross margin on a comparable basis.

2. EBITDA margin: one number, three definitions

What it is: operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, as a share of revenue. Formula: (revenue − materials − employees − other operating expenses) ÷ revenue. Worked example: ₹1,200 crore of revenue and ₹260 crore of EBITDA give 21.7%. What drives it: gross margin, operating leverage on fixed engineering and plant costs, selling intensity (compressor makers spend far more on sales and service people than engine makers), royalty, and one-offs such as restructuring. Benchmarks: above 18% good, 12–18% average, below 12% weak. How the nine compare: Oswal (24.9%) and Cummins (21.4%) led in FY26, but Oswal's was a peak-cycle number that had fallen to 15.6% by Q1 FY27. Greaves (7.0%) trails because its EV subsidiary loses money.

3. Realisation and the price-cost lag

What it is: the average price received per unit, and how quickly it follows input costs. Formula: segment revenue ÷ units shipped; the lag is the number of quarters between a cost increase and a matching price increase. Worked example: if copper adds ₹20,000 to the cost of a ₹10 lakh genset in April and the list price rises by ₹20,000 in July, one quarter of margin is lost on every unit shipped in between. What drives it: market position (leaders move first), contract structure (catalogue pricing vs fixed-price orders vs auctioned tenders), regulatory resets (CPCB IV+) and competition. Benchmarks: a one-quarter lag is good, two to three quarters average, no pass-through weak. How the nine compare: Cummins raised prices in July 2026 and is assessing more; Elgi expects pricing to land in Q2–Q3 FY27; Greaves guides to recovery from Q2; KSB and KBL depend on order turnover; Shakti and Oswal have no mechanism within a tender, and Oswal's realisation actually fell 9% in Maharashtra.

Definition trap: few companies disclose units, so realisation must usually be inferred, and a mix shift (more high-horsepower sets, more large pumps) can look like a price increase. Ask whether a higher average realisation came from list prices or from selling bigger machines.

In plain terms

When metal prices jump, every maker's costs rise the same day. What differs is how soon each can raise its own prices. Market leaders selling to private buyers can do it within a quarter. Makers bound by fixed-price contracts wait until old orders are delivered. Makers who bid in government auctions cannot do it at all until the next auction.

4. Aftermarket share: the annuity

What it is: revenue from spares, consumables, service and overhauls sold to the installed base, as a share of total revenue. Formula: aftermarket revenue ÷ total revenue. Worked example: one lakh installed engines spending ₹20,000 each a year produce ₹200 crore of aftermarket revenue; on ₹1,200 crore of total revenue that is a 17% share. What drives it: the size and age of the installed base, service-network reach, the complexity of the machine (CPCB IV+ after-treatment raised service intensity), annual maintenance contract penetration and the share of customers who use genuine parts. Benchmarks: above 25% good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak. How the nine compare: Cummins' distribution business is about 27% of sales and grew 22–26% in FY26 (14% in Q1 FY27). Elgi's aftermarket is 16–18% of group revenue (about 30% in India and 15–16% in the US) with a target of 20–22%. Kirloskar Pneumatic services more than 1,000 gas stations, a line growing 25–30%. The KUSUM pair have essentially none.

5. Order book and book-to-bill: visibility for the engineered businesses

What it is: the value of confirmed, unexecuted orders, and the ratio of new orders to revenue. Formula: order-book cover = order book ÷ trailing-twelve-month revenue; book-to-bill = order inflow ÷ revenue in the same period. Worked example: KBL's ₹4,062 crore book against ₹4,664 crore of trailing revenue is 0.87 years of cover; its Q1 intake of ₹1,395 crore against ₹1,105 crore of revenue is a book-to-bill of 1.26. What drives it: project awards in water, power, oil and gas; city-gas station build-outs; scheme tenders. Benchmarks: cover above 1.0 good, 0.6–1.0 average, below 0.6 weak; book-to-bill above 1.0 means the book is growing. How the nine compare: Kirloskar Pneumatic has the most cover (1.02 years), KBL 0.87, Shakti about 0.34 and Oswal about 0.26 on an ILLUSTRATIVE value of its 22,025-pump book. The genset and compressor product companies do not report order books because most of their sales are book-and-bill.

6. Export share: diversification or dependence

What it is: exports or international revenue as a share of total revenue. Formula: export revenue (including overseas subsidiaries' sales) ÷ consolidated revenue. Worked example: Cummins exported ₹1,989 crore on about ₹11,950 crore of company-basis revenue in FY26, about 17%. What drives it: the parent's sourcing decisions, global industrial demand, tariffs and the exchange rate. Benchmarks: 20–50% from a profitable, diversified base is good; 10–20% average; below 10%, or exports that lose money, weak. How the nine compare: Elgi about 50%, KBL about 38%, Cummins about 17%, Shakti about 15%, Oswal about 4%; KOEL, KSB, KPCL and Greaves do not disclose a comparable share. Profitability matters as much as share: KBL's overseas EBITDA margin was only 5.2% in Q1 FY27, and Elgi's Europe business runs near breakeven.

7. Receivable days: who is the customer, really?

What it is: how long customers take to pay. Formula: trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: ₹1,000 crore of revenue and ₹200 crore of receivables give 73 days. What drives it: the customer (dealers pay fastest, private EPC contractors slower, state agencies slowest), contract terms (retention money on projects), and the timing of shipments within the quarter. Benchmarks: below 60 days good, 60–110 average, above 120 weak. How the nine compare: Greaves (37), KOEL (45) and KBL (53) collect fastest; Cummins (83) is moderate because of large project and export receivables; Kirloskar Pneumatic (107) and KSB (118) carry project credit; Shakti (173) and Oswal (199) carry state-agency credit.

8. Inventory and working-capital days

What it is: how long stock sits before it is sold, and the net days of revenue tied up in operations. Formula: inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods × 365 (aggregators often use revenue); working-capital days = (receivables + inventory − payables) ÷ revenue × 365. Worked example: receivables of 70 days, inventory of 73 days and payables of 40 days give 103 working-capital days, about ₹282 crore on ₹1,000 crore of revenue. What drives it: manufacturing cycle length (engineered pumps and process compressors take months), spares stocking for the installed base, a captive foundry, global warehousing for exporters, and supplier credit. Benchmarks: working-capital days below 60 good, 60–100 average, above 100 weak. How the nine compare: KOEL (−2) and Greaves (19) run lean; Elgi (42), KBL (46) and Cummins (53) are good; Kirloskar Pneumatic (66) and Shakti (91) are average; KSB (104) and Oswal (187) are weak. Inventory days tell a different story: KSB (197), KBL (142) and Elgi (134) carry heavy stock, offset by supplier credit in Elgi's and KBL's case.

9. Cash conversion: CFO ÷ EBITDA

What it is: how much of operating profit turns into operating cash. Formula: cash flow from operations ÷ EBITDA. 10. Return on capital employed

What it is: operating profit earned on the debt and equity tied up in the business. Formula: EBIT ÷ (net worth + borrowings), usually averaged over the year. Worked example: ₹140 crore of EBIT on ₹532 crore of capital employed is 26.3%. What drives it: margin, asset turns (sales ÷ net fixed assets) and working capital; large idle cash balances reduce it. Benchmarks: above 25% good, 15–25% average, below 15% weak. How the nine compare: Cummins (39.5%) leads despite a large cash pile; Oswal (36.4%) and Kirloskar Pneumatic (29.6%) follow; KSB (24.7%), Shakti (23.6%), Elgi (22.1%) and KBL (20.4%) are average; KOEL (14.6%, diluted by Arka) and Greaves (10.1%, diluted by EV losses) are weak.

Three lessons from the anatomy. First, each point of aftermarket share is worth about 25 basis points of gross margin here (the 70% service margin minus the 45% equipment margin, times 1%), which is why Elgi's target of raising aftermarket from 16–18% to 20–22% matters. Second, at 103 working-capital days, every ₹100 crore of extra revenue ties up about ₹28 crore of cash, so fast growth depresses cash conversion even in a healthy business. Third, ROCE of about 26% is the reward for a business that turns its assets four times a year; a captive foundry (KSB) or a heavy inventory model lowers asset turns and therefore ROCE at the same margin.

The same plant can be stress-tested with the KPIs above. If copper and castings push materials up 5% (₹25 crore) and the plant raises prices only 2% on equipment (₹16 crore), EBITDA falls to ₹156 crore and the margin to 15.4%: a one-quarter lag costs about 110 basis points. If instead a state customer replaces private ones and receivable days rise from 70 to 170, working capital rises by about ₹274 crore, capital employed rises by about half and pre-tax ROCE falls from 26% to about 17%, before any change in the income statement. And if aftermarket share rose from 18% to 22%, gross profit would rise by about ₹10 crore with no change in revenue. Margin, cash and mix are three separate levers, and each one shows up in a different KPI.

In plain terms

A ₹1,000 crore compressor business keeps about ₹165 crore as operating profit and ₹112 crore after tax. Nearly a fifth of its sales, and a quarter of its gross profit, comes from servicing machines it sold years ago. Every time sales grow by ₹100 crore, about ₹28 crore of cash gets tied up in unpaid bills and stock.

Putting the KPIs together: three read-throughs

The KPIs are most useful in combination, because each one alone can be flattered. Three read-throughs from the current numbers show how.

Cummins India: high margin, high conversion, one moving cost. A 21.4% FY26 EBITDA margin, 39.5% ROCE and 67% cash conversion say this is a franchise. The aftermarket share (about 27%) explains why the margin is durable. But the royalty KPI (2.5% rising towards 4%) and the gross-margin KPI (below the historical 36–37% in Q1 FY27) both point the same way, and the data-centre share says a growing part of revenue depends on a few buyers. The combined reading is a high-quality business whose margin is under pressure from two directions that management does not fully control, and whose growth is more concentrated than before. That is consistent with a de-rating from about 65 to 52 times earnings without any deterioration in quality.

Kirloskar Pneumatic: lumpy, but the cash is real. A 20.1% FY26 margin with 107 receivable days might look stretched, but cash conversion of 65%, an order book covering a year of revenue, a growing service line at gas stations and ROCE near 30% say the profit is genuine. The trap is seasonality: Q4 delivered 57% of FY26 profit, so a weak first quarter is not evidence of decline, and the consolidated Q1 margin was further depressed by consolidating the loss-making SCIPL. The reading is a well-run engineered-package business that should be judged on annual, not quarterly, numbers.

Oswal Pumps: every income-statement KPI looked excellent. In FY26 it had the highest EBITDA margin (24.9%), the second-highest ROCE (36.4%) and the fastest growth in the group (44%). The balance-sheet and cash KPIs told the other story: receivable days of 199, working-capital days of 187 and negative operating cash flow. An export share of about 4% and an aftermarket of zero meant there was no second engine if the scheme slowed. Read together, the KPIs said the profit depended on one customer type, at prices that customer set, paid on its timetable. Q1 FY27's 9% price cut confirmed it. The 8.4 times P/E is the market's answer to the combination, not to any single number.

The general rule follows: in this sub-sector, read income-statement KPIs (margin, growth, ROCE) only alongside the cash KPIs (receivable days, working-capital days, CFO ÷ EBITDA) and the quality KPIs (aftermarket share, pass-through lag, export profitability). A company that scores well on the first set and badly on the other two is earning cyclical or subsidised profit, and will be valued accordingly.

In plain terms

Half the apparent differences in this group disappear, or appear, once you do.

How to read a compressor, pump or engine company's quarterly results in ten minutes

Ten minutes is enough if the numbers are pulled in the right order: first what was sold and at what price, then what it cost, then whether the cash came in, and only then what management says about the future. Most mistakes in reading this group come from stopping after the second step.

  1. Revenue growth, split by price and volume. Pull segment revenue and any unit data. Growth from list-price increases and mix is different from growth in units; growth from currency (Elgi's 7 points) is different again.
  2. Gross margin, year on year. If disclosed, this is the first read on commodities and pricing. A fall of 150–200 bps with copper up 50% is a lag; a fall that persists after price increases is a loss of pricing power.
  3. EBITDA margin on a consistent basis. Recompute it yourself excluding other income. Compare with the same quarter last year, not the previous quarter, because of seasonality (Kirloskar Pneumatic's Q4 can be 40% of the year).
  4. Management's price-hike commentary. When was the last increase, how much of the cost rise does it cover, and when is the next? "Not fully offsetting" is a warning for the next quarter.
  5. Aftermarket or distribution growth. It should grow steadily through cycles. A slowdown (Cummins' distribution from 22–26% to 14%) is worth an explanation.
  6. Order intake and book-to-bill for KBL, KPCL, KSB and the KUSUM pair. Below 1.0 for two quarters means revenue will slow; check the composition (nuclear, water, CGD, KUSUM).
  7. Receivables and their ageing. Compare quarter-end receivables with revenue, and read the ageing table if given (Shakti discloses it). A rise of ₹500 crore in a quarter is a red flag regardless of profit.
  8. Cash flow, at half-year and year-end. CFO against EBITDA. Below 45% for a private-market business, or negative for anyone, needs an explanation.
  9. Related-party items. For Cummins and KSB, royalty and technical fees as a share of sales; for KOEL, Arka's growth and credit costs; for Greaves, the EV segment loss and capital going into it.
  10. Policy and end-market signals. Data-centre share and its base (Cummins), KUSUM tender prices and scheme status (Shakti, Oswal), JJM payments (KBL, KSB), US tariff commentary (Elgi, Cummins exports). Red flags: margin down while revenue is up for two quarters; receivables rising faster than revenue; "exceptional" items every quarter; order book reported in a new unit.

Cash is the verdict: who is winning, and how the market scores it Deep dive

Cash conversion lines up with valuation more closely than growth, margin or return on capital does. Three maps and a scorecard show who is winning.

If one number predicts where a company in this group trades, it is not revenue growth or EBITDA margin. It is how much of its operating profit arrives as cash. The four companies that convert more than 60% of EBITDA into operating cash (Elgi, Cummins, Kirloskar Pneumatic, Kirloskar Brothers) trade at 31–52 times earnings. The two KUSUM suppliers, converting 29% and −15%, trade at 25 and 8 times. The exceptions, KSB and Greaves, are explained by parentage and by an EV option respectively.

Map 1: cash conversion against the multiple

Why should cash conversion matter more than reported margin here? Because the gap between the two measures the credit quality of the customer and the durability of the profit. A genset sold to a hyperscaler or through a dealer is paid for in weeks. A compressor sold to a private factory is paid for in two months. A pump delivered to a state water project or a solar pump installed for a state agency may be paid for in six months or more, and the amount ultimately paid can be disputed. Investors in this group have learned, through the KUSUM cycle, to value profit by when and whether it turns into cash.

Map 2: return on capital against the multiple

The second map shows why return on capital, the usual quality test, works poorly here on its own. Oswal's 36% ROCE and Kirloskar Pneumatic's 30% look similar, but one sits on negative operating cash flow and the other on 65% conversion. KOEL and Greaves look like poor businesses on consolidated ROCE, but each contains a distinct engine business with better returns, diluted by an NBFC (KOEL) or an EV start-up (Greaves). A sum-of-the-parts view is needed for both.

Map 3: growth bought with margin

The third map is the one to watch over the next two quarters. If the July price increases work, Cummins and KOEL should move up towards the zero line in Q2 and Q3 FY27 without losing growth. If the KUSUM pair's realisations stabilise, they move up too; if the scheme gap arrives, they move left. Elgi is the only company already in the top-right quadrant, which is why its valuation held through the de-rating.

In plain terms

The companies investors pay the most for are the ones whose profits turn into cash quickly. That matters more here than how fast a company is growing or how high its margin is, because the weakest companies in this group showed high margins and fast growth right up to the point when their government customers stopped paying on time and cut prices.

Each of the three machines has a global leader many times the size of anything listed in India. Why Indian subsidiaries trade at two to five times their parents

The premium has four sources. Scarcity: there are few listed Indian vehicles for data-centre power, engineered pumps or compressors, and domestic institutions that want exposure to India's capex cycle have limited choice. Growth: India's industrial and infrastructure investment, plus export sourcing, gives the subsidiaries faster growth than mature-market parents. Balance sheets and payouts: debt-free, cash-rich and with high dividend payouts. Index ownership: inclusion in major Indian indices brings steady passive demand.

The counterweights are the parentage costs covered in Part 10: royalty creep (Cummins from 2.5% towards 4%), the parent's control over export allocation and capacity, and the parent's freedom to serve India through other entities. The KSB gap is the starkest. The same group's earnings are valued at about 10–12 times in Germany and about 52 times in India, roughly a five-fold difference, despite the Indian company's weaker recent growth and cash conversion. That gap is partly a statement about the parent (a mature, low-growth European industrial) and partly a statement about the Indian market's appetite for scarce MNC capital-goods names.

What global peers teach about the Indian nine

Global leaders show where the economics can go. Atlas Copco and Ingersoll Rand earn 20%-plus operating margins on compressors, well above Elgi's 14.8% EBITDA, because they have dense service networks, a large aftermarket share and pricing power in mature markets. That is Elgi's target model, and why its overseas margin gap is the investment case. Xylem shows that water technology (smart metering, treatment, digital monitoring) can earn higher margins than pumps alone, a direction neither KSB nor KBL has moved far towards. Cummins Inc. and Caterpillar show that the data-centre power market is global and concentrated, with buyers who standardise suppliers across countries; this favours Cummins India's parentage and makes KOEL's challenge harder. KSB SE's modest margins are a reminder that engineered pumps are not inherently a high-margin business; KSB India's higher margin reflects Indian market conditions and its parent's choice to use India as a lower-cost base.

India as a sourcing hub

The most important global theme for the Indian nine is not competition but supply-chain allocation. Cummins India makes high-horsepower engines for its parent's global network; KSB supplies its group's projects in Africa and the Middle East; Elgi serves the US and Europe partly from Coimbatore; KBL owns manufacturing abroad. A weaker rupee and an 18% US tariff (if confirmed) against higher rates on China support more of this. But the decisions are made in Columbus, Frankenthal or by Indian managements competing for global orders, and minority shareholders in the Indian subsidiaries do not get a say in how much flows to India.

In plain terms

The Indian companies are small compared with the world leaders in each machine, but investors pay far more for each rupee of their profit than for each dollar of their parents'. That premium rests on India's faster growth and the scarcity of listed companies like them. It also means investors are paying a lot for businesses whose parents decide how much technology they get, what royalty they pay and how much export work comes their way.

Technology and disruption: electrons, batteries and smarter pumps

Electrification threatens small engines, batteries nibble at backup diesel, efficiency rules reshape pumps and compressors, and solar-pump makers are turning into cell makers.

Technology change affects the three machines very differently. Small engines face a direct substitution threat from electric vehicles. Large gensets face a slower, partial threat from batteries and gas. Compressors and pumps face efficiency-driven upgrades that mostly help the organised players. And the solar-pump makers are using technology (cells, controllers) to try to escape the economics of tender assembly.

1. Electrification and Greaves' EV pivot

Greaves Cotton's core business is diesel and CNG engines for three-wheelers. Every electric three-wheeler that replaces a diesel one removes an engine sale and, over time, an aftermarket stream. Greaves' answer has been to become an EV maker itself through Greaves Electric Mobility (GEML): Ampere electric scooters, with about 61,600 registrations in FY26 (up 51%), about 4.4% of the electric two-wheeler market and more than four lakh vehicles sold cumulatively, and Eltra electric three-wheelers. GEML's revenue was about ₹270 crore in Q1 FY27, up 97%. The EV segment lost ₹182 crore in FY26 (₹223 crore in FY25), and a Q1 FY27 segment loss of about ₹45.8 crore has been reported but not verified.

The financing tells its own story. Greaves planned to list GEML; in July 2026 it withdrew the IPO and replaced it with a ₹530 crore rights issue, of which Greaves subscribes about ₹331 crore, implying a stake of about 62% (our estimate). The parent's engine profits therefore keep funding the EV business. The new Ampere Nexus EX+ was launched at ₹1,29,999 in October 2026. The pivot is a hedge against the decline of the engine core, but it consumes the cash that core generates and depresses group margins and returns.

2. Batteries versus backup diesel

Battery energy storage can cover short outages and smooth the transition to backup power, and it is already standard as the first line (UPS) in data centres. For longer outages, data centres still rely on multi-hour diesel backup, which remains the cheapest way to hold large amounts of standby energy. The risk to genset makers is gradual: hybrid systems that combine batteries with gas gensets, or renewable diesel (HVO) that keeps diesel engines but changes the fuel, could shift specifications. For commercial buildings, where outages are short and grid reliability is improving, batteries are a more direct threat to small gensets, much as they displaced tower gensets a decade ago.

3. Fuel transition in engines

Cummins and KOEL are both developing gas gensets, dual-fuel kits and hydrogen-ready engines. Cummins' "Destination Zero" strategy includes hydrogen internal-combustion engines for Tata Motors trucks, and KOEL has alternative-fuel options in development. These transitions favour engine makers with deep engineering budgets and parent technology, in the same way CPCB IV+ did.

4. Compressors: energy efficiency and new gases

Electricity accounts for 70–80% of a compressor's lifetime cost, so the technology race is about efficiency: variable-speed drives that match output to demand, oil-free machines for food, pharma and electronics, and digital monitoring that predicts failures. This favours Elgi and the global leaders over low-cost Chinese machines, and it sells upgrades into the installed base. Compressed biogas and hydrogen compression are a new niche where Kirloskar Pneumatic is positioned.

5. Pumps: efficient motors, remote monitoring, smarter solar

Brushless DC and permanent-magnet motors, variable-frequency drives and remote monitoring systems are reshaping pumps. KUSUM already requires remote monitoring on subsidised systems, and the NISE draft specification of August 2026 points towards higher-efficiency controllers with battery and grid interaction. The BEE labelling programme pushes efficiency in grid-connected pumps. Each of these raises the technical bar for unorganised makers. The biggest technology bet in the group is the KUSUM pair's move into solar cells: Shakti's 2.2 GW cell-and-module plant and Oswal's 1.2 GW TOPCon cell plant. Owning the cell changes their capital intensity and makes them partly solar manufacturers, exposed to a different cycle (cell and module prices), which is the subject of the electrical equipment report's solar coverage.

6. China

Chinese competition is a price ceiling at the low end of compressors and pumps, marginal in gensets (because of emission certification and service), and blocked from subsidised solar by the domestic-content rule. Any relaxation of the DCR rule would cut KUSUM system costs and undermine the cell plants Shakti and Oswal are building.

In plain terms

Electric vehicles are a real threat to Greaves' small-engine business, so Greaves is building its own electric scooters and three-wheelers, which still lose money. Batteries are taking over the shortest backup jobs but not yet the long ones that data centres need. Compressors and pumps are getting more efficient, which helps the bigger, better-engineered makers. The two solar-pump makers are betting heavily on making their own solar cells.

Bull vs bear: the case for and against the sub-sector

The bull case rests on installed bases, data centres and pricing power; the bear case on a margin peak, royalty creep, a scheme cliff and multiples that still assume the best.

The sub-sector does not have one bull or bear case; it has one per archetype. The summary below sets out the strongest version of each side for the group as a whole, and the paragraphs that follow say where each argument applies.

The bull case

  • Installed-base annuities: Cummins (distribution about 27% of sales) and Elgi (aftermarket 16–18%, target 20–22%) earn steady, high-margin service revenue that cushions every downturn.
  • Data-centre demand: India added a record 440 MW in 2025 and is expected to exceed 3 GW by 2028; at up to 1.5 MW of backup per MW, that is years of high-horsepower genset demand.
  • Pricing power: genset and compressor makers raised prices within a quarter of the 2026 input shock; a 4% increase restores Cummins' FY26 margin (illustrative).
  • Balance sheets: Cummins, KSB, KBL and Kirloskar Pneumatic are net cash; most pay steady dividends.
  • Diversified demand: city gas, nuclear and thermal power, oil and gas, water and exports give the engineered businesses multiple growth paths.
  • De-rating done: Cummins' P/E has fallen from about 65 to 52, KBL's to 33 and Oswal's to 8.

The bear case

  • Margin peak: group EBITDA margin fell from 17.7% to 14.0% in Q1 FY27 with copper near a record; price increases lag and may not fully offset.
  • Royalty creep: Cummins' royalty rising from 2.5% towards 4% (about ₹180 crore, 150 bps, illustrative); KSB's rate undisclosed.
  • Scheme cliff: KUSUM 1.0 ends in March 2027 with 2.0 not launched; realisations already down 9% in Maharashtra.
  • Cash that does not arrive: Shakti's receivables at ₹1,799 crore, Oswal's operating cash flow negative, KSB converting 25% of EBITDA.
  • Data-centre concentration: a handful of buyers drive Cummins' growth; non-data-centre powergen grows in single digits.
  • Valuations: the group trades at 45 times earnings (37 without Cummins), with three companies above 50 times.

Where each argument applies

The bull case is strongest for the engine leaders and compressor makers. Cummins has the installed base, the data-centre position, pricing power and a net-cash balance sheet; Elgi and Kirloskar Pneumatic have resilient margins and strong cash conversion. For them the debate is about price, not quality: Cummins at 52 times needs the margin to recover towards 20–21% and the royalty step-up to be gradual.

The bear case is strongest for the KUSUM pair, where every bear point applies at once: no aftermarket, no pricing power, a scheme ending, receivables growing and capital being committed to cell plants just as revenue visibility falls. Their low multiples price much of this, so the question for investors is whether KUSUM 2.0 arrives in time and whether receivables are collected.

The engineered pump houses sit in between. KBL has an improving order book, a recovering overseas business and a multiple that has compressed to 33 times; KSB has the qualifications and the parent but weak cash and slowing growth at 52 times. KOEL and Greaves are both "two companies in one" whose valuation depends on how investors treat the second business (an NBFC and an EV maker).

In plain terms

The optimistic view is that the best of these companies earn steady money from servicing machines, are winning a boom in data-centre generators and can raise prices when costs rise. The pessimistic view is that margins have peaked, Cummins will pay its parent more, the solar-pump scheme is ending and share prices still assume the good times continue. Both views are right about different companies.
