Gallantt Ispat, the low-cost rebar maker (and the flour-mill question)

Why owning the furnace and the power plant makes it a low-cost rebar maker, why it is now buying its way into captive iron ore, and how a small-cap self-funds a ₹3,000 Cr programme without term loans.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Gallantt Ispat Limited is an integrated long-steel producer — it takes iron ore and coal at one end and ships out "Gallantt" TMT reinforcement bars at the other, running two self-contained plants at Sahjanwa near Gorakhpur (Uttar Pradesh) and Samakhiali in Kutch (Gujarat). It makes its own sponge iron, casts its own steel billets, rolls its own rebar, bakes its own iron-ore pellets, and — the part that lowers the cost line more than anything else — generates a large share of its own electricity. On the strength of that, it is a low-cost regional rebar maker feeding North and West India's construction boom.

Then there is the twist that needs re-checking. Gallantt has long been described — on its group website and in third-party profiles — as also running a wheat-flour milling business under the same brand (atta, maida, suji and bran). But the listed company's FY26 annual report says it is "predominantly engaged in single reporting segment of Iron and Steel" ("Steel & Power"); its product-wise turnover lists only sponge iron, billets and TMT/rounds, and flour appears only in a director's biography. The Q1 FY27 presentation shows no agro business either. Read the flour story below as group history, not as a current earnings line of the listed company.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Cost, not scale, is the edge. Gallantt is a mid-sized long-steel maker, not a Tata or JSW. What it competes on is landed cost per tonne of rebar — and that comes from being backward-integrated (sponge iron, billets, pellets in-house) and, above all, from captive power: waste-heat-recovery and thermal generation that lets it run the mills on cheap self-made electricity rather than costly grid power.
  • Profit is rising faster than sales. FY26 revenue grew a modest ~3% to ₹4,419 Cr, but net profit jumped 20.8% to ₹484 Cr — the gap is margin, not volume. When a steel company's profit climbs four times faster than its top line, the story is the cost line, and Gallantt's cost line is its captive power and integration.
  • A self-funded expansion is in build. The company now presents a ₹3,000 Cr programme — ~₹1,500 Cr to develop three iron-ore blocks it won as preferred bidder (Todpura, Rajasthan, ~85 MT; two Sonbhadra, UP blocks, ~51 MT; 7 MTPA planned, production from FY28), ~₹1,200 Cr of steelmaking that lifts total steel capacity from 9.93 to 12.29 lakh t (+23%) in H2 FY27, and ₹300 Cr for 85 MW of captive solar. ₹775 Cr was spent by Jun 2026, all from internal accruals with no term loans.
In plain terms

Gallantt's listed business is one production line that matters: it melts iron and rolls red-hot bars of rebar (the flour mill often attached to its name does not appear in the FY26 accounts). That steel line is where the profit sits, and it earns that profit by being cheaper per tonne than the regional rivals it sells rebar against — cheaper because Gallantt owns the whole chain from sponge iron to finished bar and powers it with its own electricity. The current chapter is a self-financed expansion — more steel capacity, captive solar and, from FY28, its own iron-ore mines — that aims to make the cheap steel line bigger and cheaper without a term loan.

01

Steel and atta under one roof

The single strangest fact about Gallantt — and why it exists at all.

Most companies that make TMT rebar make TMT rebar, full stop. Gallantt is unusual because the same "Gallantt" brand appears in two entirely different shops: on a bundle of reinforcement bars at a construction-materials dealer, and on a 10-kilo bag of atta on a kirana shelf in Patna or Guwahati. One brand, two aisles — the hardware trade and the grocery trade — sold by one listed entity. For a first-time reader that is worth pausing on — and worth checking: the FY26 annual report reports a single Iron & Steel segment, lists only sponge iron, billets and TMT in product-wise turnover, and mentions flour only in a director's biography. So the "two aisles" picture describes the group and its brand history more than the listed company's current accounts.

How did that happen? The Gallantt group grew up in eastern Uttar Pradesh, a wheat-growing, grain-trading belt, and the promoters were in agri-commodity and food-grain processing before and alongside building the steel plant. When the group put its assets into the listed vehicle, the flour-milling operation came with them. So the food business, where it exists, is a legacy of how a regional business-family group assembled itself, agri and steel side by side, in the same geography — not something the listed company's FY26 segment reporting shows.

02

Integrated long steel, in plain English

No engineering degree required — just what "integrated" and "long steel" actually buy you.

Steel begins as iron ore — rock rich in iron oxide. Turning that rock into a construction-grade reinforcement bar takes several hot, power-hungry steps. A steelmaker can do all of those steps itself, or buy in a half-finished product and only do the last bit. Doing more of the chain yourself is what "integrated" means, and in a commodity where nobody controls the selling price, doing more of the chain is the difference between a healthy margin and a wafer-thin one.

The words you need, and where Gallantt sits
TermWhat it is (and where Gallantt does it)
Iron ore & coalThe raw inputs. Ore supplies the iron; coal supplies the heat and the chemistry to strip out oxygen. Gallantt buys these in — securing cheaper, closer sourcing is a live cost battle.
PelletIron-ore fines rolled into marble-sized balls and baked hard, so a furnace can use them efficiently. Gallantt runs its own pellet plant (792 KT, expanding to 990 KT) — a cost-saver that feeds its sponge-iron units.
Sponge iron (DRI)Ore or pellet chemically stripped of oxygen without melting — "direct reduced iron". The metallic feed for the melt shop. Gallantt makes its own: FY26 DRI production was 914.8 KT (Gorakhpur 577.6 + Kutch 337.1).
MS billetA solid steel bar cast from melted sponge iron (plus scrap) — the semi-finished block that gets rolled. Gallantt casts its own (528 KT, expanding to 800 KT).
TMT rebarThe finished long product: thermo-mechanically treated reinforcement bar — the ribbed steel rod that goes inside concrete columns, beams and slabs. Sold branded as Gallantt TMT. Rolling capacity 528 KT, expanding to 805 KT.
Why integration is worth so much

Each step above adds cost and captures a slice of margin. A plain "re-roller" that only buys billets and rolls them into bar earns just the last, thinnest slice — and pays full market price for everything upstream. Gallantt owns the chain from pellet and sponge iron onward, so every rupee of margin a re-roller hands to a pellet-maker and a sponge-iron supplier stays inside Gallantt instead. Because the finished-rebar price is set by the market and nobody controls it, the only durable edge is a lower cost of the stuff going in — and each backward step is a piece of that lower cost.

"Long" steel, not "flat" steel — a useful distinction

Gallantt makes long products — rebar — not flat products like the sheet and coil that go into cars, white goods and packaging. The difference decides who its customers are and how it competes. Long steel is the workhorse of construction and infrastructure: the rebar inside every building, flyover, dam and metro pillar. It is a more regional, more fragmented market than flat steel — freight is a big share of delivered cost, so a rebar plant mostly serves the states around it, and brand-and-service can earn a small price premium with local builders. That keeps Gallantt out of head-to-head war with the flat-steel giants and anchored instead to the pace of building activity in North and West India, near its two plants.

One more feature makes Gallantt unusually self-reliant, and it deserves its own section below: power. Melting and rolling steel is electricity-intensive, and Gallantt runs its own captive generation — waste-heat recovery that captures the heat coming off its sponge-iron kilns, plus thermal units, and now a large solar build. Ore and coal it must buy; but a big chunk of its power, the other heavyweight cost line, it makes itself.

03

The value chain, walked

Follow one tonne of ore from a railhead to a bundle of ribbed rebar — and see where the cost is squeezed out.

Gallantt's plant runs mostly in one direction: ore and coal in, TMT rebar out, with a captive power plant humming alongside the whole way. The interesting part is not any single step but the fact that Gallantt does almost all of them itself. Where the money is actually made

  • The margin lives in the missing purchases. A re-roller buys billets at market and earns only the thin rolling spread. Gallantt makes the billet from its own sponge iron, which it makes from its own pellet — so the pellet margin, the sponge margin and the rolling margin all stack inside one company. Same finished bar, more of the chain retained.
  • Power is the multiplier. Every one of those hot steps runs on electricity. Buy that power from the grid and the integration edge leaks away in the power bill; make it yourself from waste heat and captive thermal, and the integration edge compounds. That is why the next section is about power, not steel.
  • Freight sets the map. Because rebar is heavy and low-value per tonne, the Gorakhpur plant naturally serves the east (UP, Bihar, Bengal, the North-East) and the Kutch plant the west (Gujarat and beyond). Two plants, two catchments — a deliberate regional footprint rather than one giant mill trying to ship nationwide.
The counter-intuitive bit: in a commodity where the selling price is the same for everyone in a region, a rebar maker cannot out-price its rivals — it can only out-cost them. So the entire competitive game for Gallantt is played on the left-hand side of the flow diagram (cheaper ore/coal, cheaper power, more in-house conversion), not the right. The rebar that leaves the gate is a commodity; the profit was decided several boxes upstream.
04

Captive power: the real margin lever

The single thing to understand about how Gallantt earns more per tonne than a plain re-roller.

In commodity long steel, the finished-bar price is out of any one producer's hands. So profitability is decided almost entirely by cost per tonne — and after the ore and coal you feed in, the biggest swing cost is electricity. Melting sponge iron in an induction or arc furnace and rolling billets both eat power. A rebar plant that buys all its power from the state grid pays the full industrial tariff on every one of those units. Gallantt largely doesn't.

Its edge is waste-heat recovery. When sponge iron is made, the kilns throw off enormous amounts of hot flue gas. A waste-heat-recovery boiler captures that otherwise-wasted heat and turns it into steam and then electricity — power that is, in effect, a free by-product of a step Gallantt was doing anyway. On top of that sit its thermal captive units, and now a large solar build. Together they mean a big share of the plant's electricity is made on-site, at a fraction of grid cost.

The device: profit up 21%, sales up 3%

Here is the cleanest way to see the lever at work. In FY26 Gallantt sold 766 KT of TMT — only about 3% more bar than the year before — into broadly the same market at broadly the same rebar prices, and its revenue rose about 3%, to ₹4,419 Cr. Yet net profit jumped 20.8%, to ₹484 Cr, and the EBITDA margin widened to roughly 16–17.6%. Almost none of that extra profit came from selling more steel. It came from making each tonne cheaper — better cost control, higher captive-power use, a fuller integrated chain. When you find a commodity producer whose profit grows seven times faster than its volume, you have found a company whose whole story is the cost line, and Gallantt's cost line is the power plant.

The solar build is the next chapter of the same idea. In a business earning a few hundred crore of profit, a saving of that size is not a footnote; it is a visible slice of next year's margin, locked in by concrete and panels rather than by hoping steel prices cooperate.

05

The wheat-flour business, taken seriously

A real second factory — what it does, why it's a hard business, and why a steel company keeps it.

It would be easy to wave away the flour operation as a quirk and move on. The group has been described as running an agro / food-grain processing business — buying wheat and milling it into atta (whole-wheat flour), maida (refined flour), suji (semolina) and bran under the Gallantt brand. But the listed company's FY26 annual report does not show it: it reports a single Iron & Steel segment, its product-wise turnover lists only sponge iron, billets and TMT/rounds, and the Q1 FY27 presentation has no agro line. What follows explains how such a business works and why a steel group might hold one; treat it as background, not as part of the listed company's FY26 earnings.

Why flour milling is a fundamentally different — and harder — business than steel

  • Margins are thin by nature. Wheat flour is a near-commodity staple sold against countless regional millers and packaged-atta brands. Value added per kilo is small, so the business runs on volume and turnover, not on fat unit margins. A few percent operating margin is normal; steel, when integrated, earns multiples of that.
  • Working capital is heavy. Millers buy wheat seasonally, often around the harvest, and hold grain inventory to run the mill through the year. That ties up cash and exposes the business to wheat-price swings and, at times, government stocking and export rules for grain.
  • It doesn't move with steel. People eat roti whether or not builders are pouring concrete. Flour demand is staple, steady and driven by food consumption; rebar demand is cyclical and driven by construction. The two rarely peak or trough together.
So why keep a low-margin mill inside a steel company?

Two reasons, and neither is glamorous. First, counter-cyclicality: when a steel down-cycle squeezes rebar spreads, the flour line keeps turning over cash and keeps the group's revenue base from collapsing to a single commodity. It is a shock-absorber, not a growth engine. Second, heritage and fit: the group came out of the eastern-UP grain belt, the milling assets were already theirs, and the food business shares distribution reach and a brand across the same states the steel is sold into. For a minority investor the plain read is that, if an agro leg sits anywhere in the group, it would be a stabiliser that dilutes margins — but on the FY26 filings the listed company is a pure-play steel-and-power business.

A related quirk to know: beyond steel, the FY26 annual report discloses a real-estate venture (hotels and a mall, with land of ~₹121 Cr) and a related-party real-estate arrangement, and its captive power is reported together with steel ("Steel & Power"). Third-party profiles still list segments as Steel · Agro · Power · Real Estate, but the company's own FY26 reporting is a single Iron & Steel segment.
06

The company and who controls it

A promoter-family group, a recent merger that reshaped the listed entity, and a conservatively-run balance sheet.

Gallantt Ispat is the flagship of the Gallantt group, a promoter-family business built by the Agrawal, Kandoi and Jalan families with roots in Kolkata and eastern Uttar Pradesh. Chairman Chandra Prakash Agrawal and family sit at the centre of both operations and capital allocation. This is a tightly held, founder-run company — the promoter group holds around 70% of the shares.

The merger that made today's Gallantt Ispat

The current listed entity is the product of a group consolidation that finished in 2022, and it is easy to get tangled in, so here it is plainly. There used to be two listed group companies with confusingly similar names — an older Gallantt Ispat Limited (the Gorakhpur steel business) and Gallantt Metal Limited (the Kutch business, incorporated 2005). Under an NCLT-approved scheme (final order 20 May 2022), the older Gallantt Ispat and several group entities were amalgamated into Gallantt Metal, and the surviving company was then renamed "Gallantt Ispat Limited". The net effect: the group's steel, agro and power assets were folded into a single listed vehicle, now trading as GALLANTT on the NSE and 532726 on the BSE. When you see references to "Gallantt Metal" or an older "Gallantt Ispat (merged)", they are earlier chapters of this same consolidation.

Ownership & balance sheet

  • Promoters (~70%). The Agrawal/Kandoi/Jalan family and group entities (Gallantt Industry, Gallantt Fincare and others) hold roughly 70%. In March 2026 the family re-plumbed the holding into a Gallantt Trust structure (~60% of the company), with SEBI exempting the move from triggering an open offer — a housekeeping reorganisation, not a stake sale.
  • Near net-cash. Against ~₹3,075 Cr of reserves, borrowings were ~₹548 Cr at FY26 (all working capital — no term loans) against ~₹503 Cr of cash and bank balances, so net debt was only ~₹45 Cr; management calls it net-cash. That is why a small-cap can bankroll its expansion from its own pocket rather than from lenders.
  • Returns. ROCE ~18% and ROE ~16% (FY26) — solid for a cyclical metals firm, and a direct read-through of the captive-power and integration cost advantage reaching capital returns.
What to watch as a minority holder: a ~70%-held, family-run group with multiple related entities means related-party dealings and intra-group asset movements are a permanent thing to keep an eye on — the 2022 merger and the 2026 trust reorganisation are both reminders that structure can be reshaped by the promoter. The counterweight is that the same tight control has, so far, gone hand-in-hand with a conservative, low-debt, self-funding approach to growth.
07

The expansion engine

A 23% step-up in steel, new iron-ore mines, paid for out of cash flow — and why that financing choice is the headline.

Gallantt's trailing revenue looks almost flat because the growth hasn't landed yet — it is in construction. The company now presents a ₹3,000 Cr programme over 2–3 years: ~₹1,500 Cr for its three new iron-ore blocks with beneficiation and pellet plants (7 MTPA of mining planned, production from FY28, a guided EBITDA uplift of ~₹2,000/t), ~₹1,200 Cr of steelmaking (the Gorakhpur brownfield below) that takes total steel capacity from 9.93 to 12.29 lakh t (+23%) in H2 FY27, and ₹300 Cr of solar. ₹775 Cr had been spent by Jun 2026, and the plan is to fund it from internal accruals — no term loans. For a small-cap steelmaker, that funding choice is as much the story as the tonnes.

Read the pipeline as one coherent bet

Notice that the expansion is not scattered — every line reinforces the same logic. Bigger billet and rolling capacity add volume; the bigger pellet plant and steady sponge units make sure that extra volume is fed by cheap, in-house inputs rather than expensive bought-in ones; and the extra captive power plus new solar make sure the bigger plant's electricity stays cheap. In other words, Gallantt is not just adding tonnes — it is adding tonnes while preserving the cost advantage that makes tonnes worth adding. Expanding a low-cost producer only pays if it stays low-cost at the larger size, and the shape of this capex is built to do exactly that.

The financing discipline is the reassuring part. A near-debt-free small-cap self-funding a ₹3,000 Cr programme — rather than raising equity or gearing up — is the kind of restraint that captive-power cash flow makes possible, and it caps the downside if a steel down-cycle arrives mid-build: there is no loan repayment clock forcing bad decisions. The execution risk is genuine (new lines to commission, ramp curves to climb, a first big solar build), but the financing risk is unusually low for a company of this size — though with ~₹2,200 Cr of the programme still to spend against FY26 operating cash flow of ~₹600 Cr, borrowings may have to rise around FY28.

08

Financials & valuation

Modest sales growth, margin-led profit, net-cash — and a multiple that is doing a lot of work.

Gallantt's FY26 income statement is a story of margin, not volume. Revenue edged up ~3% to ₹4,419 Cr, but profit did the heavy lifting: PAT rose 20.8% to ₹484 Cr, on an EBITDA of roughly ₹716 Cr — a margin near 16% on a conservative (Screener) basis, which the company's own release put higher, at about 17.6%, up from ~16% a year earlier. Either way, the direction is clear: the cost line, led by captive power and integration, improved faster than the top line grew.

The Q4 FY26 quarter fit the same pattern: revenue ~₹1,205 Cr (up ~12% YoY), PAT ~₹123 Cr (up ~6%), with an EBITDA margin in the 17% range and a ₹2 dividend recommended. Steady operational delivery, capex on track — an unremarkable-in-a-good-way quarter for a company mid-build.

The metric that settles the thesis

At ₹14,060 Cr market cap (13 Oct 2026, ₹588 a share) on ~₹484 Cr of FY26 profit, Gallantt trades near 32× trailing earnings and ~4.3× book — a clear premium to the ~21× that the average long-steel peer fetches. That is the whole tension: the market is pricing Gallantt not as a run-of-the-mill cyclical rebar maker but as a compounding one, on the expectation that the expansion lands and the cost edge holds. So the number to track is narrow and specific: EBITDA per tonne of steel (₹8,785 in FY26, ₹8,787 in Q1 FY27, down from ₹11,068 a year earlier), multiplied by the volume ramp toward 1.23 MT. If billet and rolling volumes climb through the new capacity and per-tonne EBITDA stays fat because captive power and the bigger pellet plant hold the cost line, then today's ~32× is buying a larger, still-high-margin business and looks reasonable in hindsight. If volumes ramp but per-tonne economics thin out — or a steel down-cycle hits mid-build — then a premium multiple sits on cyclical earnings, and that is the uncomfortable combination.

One honest caveat on the numbers: sources differ modestly on EBITDA (a ~₹716 Cr Screener figure versus a company-reported ~₹776 Cr / 17.6% margin), reflecting the treatment of other income (~₹59 Cr in FY26). The revenue, PAT, capacity and balance-sheet figures reconcile well across sources; the margin should be read as a range, not a decimal.

09

The cycle, China, imports, and the rivals

A cost-advantaged producer is still strapped to a violently cyclical industry.

No amount of captive power repeals the steel cycle. Gallantt sells rebar into a market whose price swings with construction demand, with the cost of scrap, ore and coal, and with what is happening in China and at India's ports. The cost edge changes how much Gallantt earns at any point on the cycle — it earns more per tonne than a re-roller at every price — but it does not flatten the cycle itself.

The forces that move Gallantt's year

China & imports. Chinese steel oversupply spilling into export markets, and cheap steel landing at Indian ports, periodically drag domestic long-steel prices down; India's safeguard duty (12% from Apr 2025, final from Dec 2025, tapering to 11.5% and 11% before it lapses in Apr 2028) covers flat steel; management noted in the Q1 FY27 call that India briefly became a net steel importer despite it, and that industry has sought anti-dumping action. Raw-material costs. Ore, coal and scrap prices set the input side — and because Gallantt still buys all its ore and coal today (its three iron-ore blocks only start producing around FY28), a spike in those can squeeze it more than a captive miner, as the Q1 FY27 pellet-plant shutdown showed. Construction & infra demand. Housing, roads, metros and the government capex cycle in North and West India set the base for rebar volumes. Power & fuel. The very lever that helps Gallantt also carries risk — fuel costs for its thermal captive units, and execution of the solar build, feed straight into the cost edge it depends on.

The demand backdrop, for now, leans favourable. India is the world's second-largest steel producer and its fastest-growing large consumer, with an infrastructure and housing push that pulls hard on long steel — which is precisely why so much mid-cap rebar capacity, Gallantt's included, is expanding at once. The mirror-image risk is obvious: if a global glut coincides with a domestic construction pause, everyone's new tonnes arrive together into a soft market. Gallantt's defence is structural — its cost sits below higher-cost re-rollers, so in a downturn it is among the later ones to bleed, and its near-net-cash balance sheet gives it staying power a stretched rival lacks. A low-cost producer does not dodge a bad market; it outlasts the higher-cost plants that close in one.

The dependency worth naming: because Gallantt owns its power but not its ore, it is more exposed than an ore-owning peer to a spike in iron-ore and coal prices. Its moat is real but partial — it removes the electricity swing from the cost equation and captures the conversion chain, yet it still buys the single largest raw input at market. That is the ceiling on how far the cost edge can carry it, and the reason raw-material sourcing (not power) is the live battle management keeps fighting.
10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cost-advantaged small-cap, mid-way through a self-funded expansion, wearing a growth multiple.

Gallantt is one of the more distinctive metals small-caps in India: a rebar maker whose profit outran its volume seven-to-one last year on a cost edge built from captive power and integration, throwing off enough cash to bankroll a ₹3,000 Cr expansion without term loans — and now buying its way into captive iron ore from FY28. The debate is not whether the cost edge is real; it is about the steel cycle, execution on the expansion, and how much of a premium a cyclical deserves.

The bull case

  • Cost edge that shows up in profit — captive power + full integration drove a 20.8% PAT rise on ~3% revenue growth; margin, not just volume, is the engine.
  • Self-funded expansion — a ₹3,000 Cr programme from internal accruals, near-net-cash sheet, steel capacity to 1.23 MT in H2 FY27; low financing risk so far.
  • Captive solar locks in savings — 85 MW being built (₹300 Cr), a concrete margin gain.
  • Regional TMT brand + two-plant footprint — Gorakhpur and Kutch serve east and west India near-source, riding a strong infra-and-housing demand cycle.
  • Captive ore from FY28 — three iron-ore blocks (~136 MT, 7 MTPA planned) with a guided ~₹2,000/t EBITDA uplift once mining starts.

The bear case

  • Premium multiple on a cyclical — ~32× trailing and ~4.3× book, well above the ~21× peer median; a lot is riding on the ramp landing cleanly.
  • Buys its ore & coal for now — 0% captive ore until its new blocks produce (~FY28), and mine development can slip; until then a raw-material spike squeezes it more than an ore-owning peer.
  • Steel cyclicality & imports — Chinese oversupply and cheap imports can compress domestic rebar spreads regardless of Gallantt's cost edge.
  • Execution risk — new lines and a first big solar build to commission and ramp on schedule.
  • Concentrated, related-party group — ~70% family control, a recent merger and a trust reorganisation; a real-estate venture and related-party arrangement add complexity.
The bottom line

Gallantt is a rebar maker that competes on cost, not scale, and the last year proved the cost lever works — profit climbed far faster than sales because captive power and integration cheapened every tonne. What it does not yet own is the thing that would fully justify a ~32× multiple: proof that the enlarged plant (9.93 → 12.29 lakh t) and, later, the new mines will keep per-tonne economics fat — and Q1 FY27, with PAT down 29% YoY, was a step the wrong way. The next concrete waypoint is legible and near: the commissioning and ramp of the expanded Gorakhpur lines (800 KT billet, 805 KT rolling, 990 KT pellet) and the switch-on of 85 MW of captive solar — the moment cheap steel becomes cheap steel at a much larger size. Track EBITDA per tonne through that ramp, and you are tracking the entire case. Then watch the iron-ore blocks: the move from 0% to meaningful captive ore around FY28 is the next step-change in the cost line.
