00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Inox Wind makes wind turbines — the tall towers with three long blades and a box on top (the "nacelle") that spin in the wind and generate electricity. It is India's leading listed pure-play wind-turbine manufacturer, and it does not just sell the machine: it offers a largely integrated, turnkey package — it manufactures the turbine, builds the wind farm (site development, roads, foundations, erection and commissioning), and then runs the farm for decades under long-term operations-and-maintenance (O&M) contracts. Its customers are power producers, utilities, state-owned firms and, increasingly, big corporates building their own captive wind supply.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The order book is huge; the delivery is the hard part. Inox Wind exited FY26 with a ~3.1 GW order book and reported ~4.4 GW by Jul 2026 — though that includes a 1.5 GW MOU with group company Inox Clean, of which only 500 MW is firm. Its lifelong challenge has never been finding demand; it has been converting that backlog into turbines that actually stand up and feed the grid. The company does not currently disclose delivered or commissioned megawatts, so that conversion has to be read from revenue, receivables and cash flow — and that conversion is the entire investment debate.
  • It has repaired — but not finished repairing — a wrecked balance sheet. The old Inox Wind was a byword for a debt-heavy, cash-strapped wind company that couldn't fund its own order book. After a group restructuring (merging the holding company in), a ₹1,249 Cr rights issue and internal cash generation, net worth rose to ~₹7,692 Cr. But the FY26 balance sheet still shows borrowings of about ₹1,551 Cr against cash and bank balances of ₹612 Cr — net debt of roughly ₹940 Cr (about ₹390 Cr even counting ₹550 Cr of investments) — and trade receivables jumped to ₹4,250 Cr, so FY26 operating cash flow was negative (−₹598 Cr).
  • It rides a genuine wind up-cycle. After nearly a decade in the doldrums, Indian wind is installing again — pulled by huge renewable targets, wind-solar hybrid and "firm & dispatchable" (FDRE) tenders, and a shift to far more economic 3.3 MW-class turbines. FY26 revenue rose ~23% to ₹4,569 Cr; management guides for ~75% revenue growth in FY27 at a 20–22% margin — yet Q1 FY27 income grew just 1%. The stock trades near 35× earnings on a ~₹12,055 Cr market cap (as of 13 Oct 2026), below the ₹120 rights price — the market has already marked down the execution ramp it once priced in.
In plain terms

Inox Wind is a heavy-engineering contractor for the wind business. Picture a firm that both builds the machine and installs it on a windy hilltop, then gets paid a small annual fee to keep it spinning for 20-plus years. For most of the last decade it had the orders but couldn't deliver — it ran out of cash, sites slipped, and turbines that were half-built earned nothing. The bet today is that the two things that always broke it — money and execution — have finally been fixed at the exact moment India's wind market woke up. The catch: an order book only becomes profit when a turbine is commissioned, and that last step is precisely where this company has stumbled before.

01

Wind power 101: how a turbine earns its keep

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A wind turbine is a machine that converts moving air into electricity. Wind pushes the blades, the blades spin a shaft, a generator turns that rotation into power, and cables carry it down the tower to the grid. Financially, a wind project is an up-front capital cost (buy and install the machine) followed by decades of near-free fuel (the wind). Inox Wind sits on the supply side of that equation: it sells the machines and builds the farms. To follow the business, you need five terms.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
WTG"Wind Turbine Generator" — the whole machine: tower + blades + nacelle. Inox Wind's core product. A modern one is a 3.3 MW machine standing well over 100 metres tall.
MW / GWMegawatts of rated capacity — the size of the machine or the order. 1,000 MW = 1 GW. Inox Wind's order book (~3.1 GW) and its yearly deliveries are all counted in MW. This is the industry's unit of everything.
PLF / capacity factorThe share of the year a turbine actually produces, since the wind isn't always blowing. Indian wind runs at roughly a 25–35% PLF — much lower than a coal plant, which is why turbine size and site quality matter so much.
EPC"Engineering, Procurement & Construction" — turning a patch of windy land into a working farm: roads, foundations, cranes, electrical evacuation, erection and commissioning. Inox offers this as part of its turnkey package.
O&M"Operations & Maintenance" — the long-term service contract to keep the fleet spinning (blade checks, gearbox servicing, uptime). A small, recurring, annuity-like fee per MW that compounds as the installed base grows.
The economics that decide everything: cost per MW

A wind project lives or dies on cost of electricity per unit, and that is driven by how much power a machine squeezes from the same wind. A bigger turbine — taller tower, longer blades — captures more wind and generates far more electricity for a broadly similar installation effort. That is why the whole industry is racing from the old 2 MW machines to 3+ MW ones: a 3.3 MW turbine produces materially more units per site than a 2 MW turbine, so the electricity it makes is cheaper, and a developer will pay up for it. For Inox Wind, moving its order book to the larger machine lifts both the revenue booked per turbine and, crucially, the margin.

Who buys wind turbines

  • IPPs (Independent Power Producers) — companies that build power plants and sell the electricity, e.g. through auctions. The traditional bulk buyer.
  • Utilities & PSUs — state electricity firms and public-sector giants (NTPC, SJVN, ONGC-type buyers) building renewable capacity to hit mandates.
  • C&I (Commercial & Industrial) buyers — factories, cement and metals firms, data centres and groups like Aditya Birla buying captive wind to cut power bills and green their operations. This is the fastest-growing new customer class, and a big part of Inox Wind's recent order wins.
02

What Inox Wind actually sells: one machine, three revenue lines

The integrated model — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Inox Wind's edge is that it does the whole job under one roof. A developer can hand it a windy site and get back a running wind farm, then keep paying it to maintain that farm for twenty years. That single relationship splits into three distinct revenue streams — and they have very different economics.

The three revenue lines, decoded

  • Turbine manufacturing (WTG) + EPC — the bulk of revenue. Inox makes the major components itself: blades (long fibreglass aerofoils), tubular steel towers, hubs, and the nacelle (the housing on top that holds the gearbox, generator and controls). In-house manufacturing of blades and towers is a real advantage — it controls quality, cost and the delivery schedule rather than waiting on suppliers.
  • Wind-farm EPC — the on-site construction. This is where a project can go slow: land, roads, cranes to lift a 100-metre-plus tower, and the electrical line to evacuate the power to the grid all have to line up. Do it well and you commission fast; hit a snag and a delivered turbine sits idle, earning nothing.
  • O&M service — the long tail. Once a turbine is running, Inox (through its listed subsidiary Inox Green Energy Services) is paid an annual fee to service it for the life of the asset. Small per MW, but it recurs for 20+ years and grows every time the group installs another machine.
Why "integrated" is worth explaining

A developer buying wind has two nightmares: a turbine that arrives late, and a farm that gets built but can't connect to the grid. By owning manufacturing and EPC and O&M, Inox Wind can — in theory — control the whole timeline and hand over a finished, warranted, serviced asset. That is attractive to a corporate buyer who just wants clean power, not a construction headache. The flip side is that Inox also owns every point of failure: if a blade factory slips, a crane is unavailable, or a transmission line isn't ready, the delay is Inox's problem, not a subcontractor's. Integration is a strength when it executes and a single-throat-to-choke liability when it doesn't.

03

The company, the group, and who controls it

A Jain-family / GFL-group company, freshly simplified.

Inox Wind belongs to the Inox / GFL group controlled by the Jain family — the same industrial house behind Gujarat Fluorochemicals (GFL) and other Inox-branded businesses. The wind arm was long tangled in a multi-layer holding structure that has now, deliberately, been simplified.

04

The bigger-turbine shift: why 3.3 MW changes the maths

The single biggest product driver — and the reason margins can rise.

For most of its life Inox Wind sold a 2 MW-class machine. Its future is built around the newer 3.3 MW platform (with a 4-plus MW machine already in the pipeline). This is not a small upgrade — it re-rates the economics of every project and every rupee of order book.

A 3.3 MW machine delivers ~65% more capacity than a 2 MW one, so a developer needs far fewer turbines, foundations, cranes and hectares to hit the same MW — lowering the cost of each unit of electricity and letting Inox book more value per machine.

The larger platform helps Inox on three fronts at once. Higher revenue per turbine — a 3.3 MW machine simply carries a bigger price tag than a 2 MW one. Better margins — more of the cost is fixed engineering that spreads over more megawatts, and the newer machine commands stronger pricing. And competitiveness — with fewer machines needed per project, the developer's total project cost falls, which is what wins auctions against solar and against rival turbine makers.

The factory that tells the story

Inox brought up a new nacelle-and-hub plant at Kalyangarh (Gujarat) to build the 3.3 MW machine — and it was designed from the start to also handle the coming 4-plus MW turbine. That is a tell: a wind manufacturer that keeps its factory one product-generation ahead is signalling it expects the up-cycle to run for years, not quarters. It also raises the stakes — capacity built for a bigger machine only pays off if the orders for that machine actually arrive and get commissioned.

A genuine surprise: Inox Wind's problem in its bad years was never that nobody wanted wind turbines. Even at its lowest, it carried a multi-gigawatt order pipeline. What killed the stock was that it couldn't fund and build what it had already sold — a company with a full order book quietly starving for working capital. That is why "this time" is framed around cash and execution, not demand.
05

The order book and the execution gap

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and it is unique to this company.

Every wind-turbine maker reports an order book in megawatts, and investors cheer when it grows. But for Inox Wind, the order book has almost never been the problem. The problem is the distance between three different megawatt numbers: MW ordered, MW delivered, and MW commissioned. Money is only truly made at the last one. Watch how far apart they sit — and note that Inox Wind does not currently publish all three (on its Q1 FY27 call management said it would not announce megawatt figures).

The jump from ~3.1 GW to ~4.4 GW is mostly a 1.5 GW MOU with group company Inox Clean, of which only 500 MW is a firm order (plus a 200 MW NLC letter of award). Strip out the non-firm 1,000 MW and the third-party book has grown far less than the headline suggests.

This is the whole company in one picture. A big order book is a promise. Delivered equipment is progress. But a turbine standing on a hill, un-commissioned, is a cost with no revenue behind it yet — steel and fibreglass earning zero until the grid connection is live and the machine is signed off. Inox's turbines have historically sat in exactly that limbo, held up by the last-mile items — evacuation lines, land, cranes, sign-offs — that have always been the industry's bottleneck. In the absence of megawatt disclosure, the best proxy is the balance sheet: trade receivables rose from ₹2,549 Cr (Mar 2025) to ₹4,250 Cr (Mar 2026) and FY26 operating cash flow was −₹598 Cr — revenue booked, cash not yet collected. The company is also shifting toward equipment-only supply (59% of the third-party book), which carries less site risk but less value per MW.

The device that only fits Inox Wind: MW ordered → MW delivered → MW commissioned

Most companies you can track with one operating number. Inox Wind needs three, and the gaps between them are the entire thesis. If ordered machines convert to commissioned megawatts in FY27, revenue and profit surge, which is why management can guide to ~75% revenue growth. If they don't convert — if grid connections slip again — revenue disappoints and receivables keep climbing. Q1 FY27 was a warning: total income grew just 1% to ₹872 Cr and PAT fell 34% to ₹64 Cr. With megawatt figures not disclosed, watch quarterly revenue, receivables and operating cash flow instead; the order book tells you the potential, but only cash collected tells you the company delivered.

06

The O&M annuity: the quiet, steadier half

The recurring business that grows every time a turbine is switched on.

Underneath the lumpy, project-driven turbine sales runs a steadier river of revenue: operations & maintenance. Every turbine Inox installs comes with a decades-long service contract, run through its listed subsidiary Inox Green Energy Services. It is small per machine, but it recurs year after year and — this is the key point — the installed base only ever grows.

Here is the mechanism that makes O&M valuable. A turbine sale is a one-time event; you book the revenue once and move on. But the O&M contract on that same turbine pays a fee every single year for 20-plus years. So each year of strong installations doesn't just add a one-off sale — it permanently thickens the annuity. Over time, a large fleet under management becomes a predictable, high-visibility earnings stream that doesn't swing with the order-book cycle the way turbine sales do.

Why the annuity is the calmer value

Turbine manufacturing is feast-or-famine: it booms in an up-cycle and can go quiet for years in a down-cycle, as Inox learned painfully. O&M does not behave that way — a turbine installed in a boom still needs servicing through the following bust. Inox Green has been expanding its portfolio across wind and solar (13.3 GWp at Jun 2026, and it paid ₹550 Cr in Oct 2026 for Wind World's ~4.5 GW O&M book, consolidated from Q3 FY27), turning the group into a broader renewable-asset services platform, not just a wind O&M shop. For a company whose manufacturing arm has burned investors on cyclicality before, this recurring base is the ballast — the part of the story that keeps paying while the order book waits to convert.

The nuance: the O&M annuity is genuinely attractive, but it is housed in a separate listco (INOXGREEN), so Inox Wind shareholders own it indirectly. When you value Inox Wind, be clear about how much of that annuity actually accrues to it versus to Inox Green's own shareholders — the group structure means the recurring jewel and the manufacturing engine sit in different pockets.
07

Why wind is back after a lost decade

The structural tailwind — and why it looks different from the 2015 boom.

Indian wind spent roughly 2016–2021 in the wilderness. A well-intentioned policy shift from fixed feed-in tariffs to competitive auctions crushed tariffs, froze projects, and pushed annual installations off a cliff — the down-cycle that broke a generation of wind companies, Inox included. What has changed since is not one thing but a stack of them.

Giant RE targetsIndia's push toward ~500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 needs far more wind, not just solar.Sets a multi-year floor under wind demand — a policy commitment that must be met with actual turbines.
Hybrid & FDRE tendersNew auctions demand firm, dispatchable round-the-clock renewable power — which needs wind (it blows at night and in monsoon, when solar is weak) blended with solar and storage.Wind is no longer competing head-on with cheaper solar; it is the complement that makes hybrid/FDRE bids work. The insight that separates this cycle from 2015

The last boom was a tariff boom — developers rushed to install before feed-in tariffs expired, a sugar high that policy could switch off overnight, and did. This cycle is a system-design boom: the grid physically needs wind to deliver round-the-clock renewable power, because solar alone goes dark every evening. That is a far more durable reason to keep buying turbines than a subsidy deadline — demand rooted in how the electricity system works, not in a tax break. It is the strongest argument that Inox's up-cycle has real legs rather than being another head-fake.

None of this makes wind immune to policy. Auctions still set tariffs, transmission still has to be built to carry the power, and a bad tender season can still stall installs. But the reason to install has shifted from a policy sweetener to a structural need — and that is the backdrop that lets Inox Wind guide to years of growth rather than one good year.

08

The balance-sheet cleanup

From debt-heavy and cash-starved to a much lighter — but not yet net-cash — balance sheet.

If the order book is the opportunity and execution is the test, the balance sheet is what decides whether Inox can even take the test. Wind manufacturing is brutally working-capital-hungry: you buy steel and components, build turbines, and ship them months before you get paid on commissioning. Do that without enough capital and you choke — which is precisely what happened to the old Inox Wind. Fixing it is the least glamorous but most important part of the turnaround.

Net worth jumped ~₹2,000 Cr year-on-year — a mix of the ₹1,249 Cr rights issue, the IWEL merger, and retained profits. Net debt of ~₹940 Cr is small against that equity (about 0.12×), but the company is not net-cash: receivables of ₹4,250 Cr are absorbing the capital that was raised.

Three moves did the work. The IWEL merger folded the levered holding company into the operating company, simplifying the structure and removing a layer of financial friction. The ₹1,249 Cr rights issue (at ₹120/share) brought in fresh promoter and shareholder equity earmarked for the ramp and deleveraging. And cash earnings — FY26 cash PAT of ~₹1,032 Cr (+28%) on the company's income-statement basis, or ₹863 Cr (+20%) on its highlights-table basis, both well above reported profit. The catch is that cash earnings are not cash collected: with receivables up ~₹1,700 Cr, FY26 operating cash flow was negative (−₹598 Cr).

Why a light balance sheet is the permission slip

A wind company scaling up deliveries carries an enormous amount of inventory and receivables in-flight at any moment. If it is also servicing heavy debt, every rupee of working capital is contested and any delay in a customer payment can stall the whole line — the exact trap that idled Inox's turbines before. Low debt changes the posture: the company can build ahead of payment, absorb a slow-paying customer, and push commissioning without a lender breathing down its neck. Inox Wind is much closer to that position than it was, but not all the way there — net debt is ~₹940 Cr and receivables of ₹4,250 Cr are where the new equity has gone. Collecting those receivables is the test of whether the repair is real.

09

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated turbine market with one big domestic twin and global majors.

Wind-turbine manufacturing is not a fragmented business — it is a game of a handful of scaled players, because building blades, towers and nacelles at quality needs deep engineering, capital and track record. In India, the listed conversation is really Inox Wind versus Suzlon, with global names competing on the margin.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, the domestic duopoly dynamic: with Suzlon and Inox Wind as the two scaled Indian pure-plays, a multi-year wind up-cycle can lift both — this is less a share-stealing knife-fight than a rising tide, at least while installations are growing. Second, the real competition for wind isn't only other turbine makers; it's solar. Wind has to keep justifying its place on a developer's cost sheet, and it does so precisely where solar is weakest — at night, in the monsoon, and in the firm/dispatchable tenders that need power around the clock. Inox's job is to keep the 3.3 MW machine cheap enough per unit that wind stays in the auction-winning mix.

The one number that settles the thesis: commissioned MW vs the order book

At ~35× trailing earnings and ~₹12,055 Cr of market value (as of 13 Oct 2026) — less than half the ~₹25,750 Cr of mid-2026 — the market has already marked down its confidence that Inox Wind will execute. The metric that proves or breaks the case is commissioned MW as a share of the order book — the conversion rate — but since the company does not currently disclose megawatts, read it through quarterly revenue, book-to-bill on firm third-party orders (excluding the non-firm part of the group MOU), and receivables and operating cash flow (is working capital under control?). If revenue climbs and receivables convert to cash, the FY27 guidance is credible and the multiple defensible. If they don't, the order book just sits there and the de-rating continues. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A repaired company in a real up-cycle — asked to prove it can deliver on schedule.

Inox Wind enters FY27 in better shape than for most of its listed life: lower debt, a ~4.4 GW order book (incl. a group MOU), a higher-value product, and a wind cycle with structural rather than subsidy-driven demand. The honest debate is no longer about the business existing — it is about whether a company with a history of missed timelines can convert a full order book at the pace its valuation demands.

The bull case

  • Structural wind up-cycle — RE targets plus hybrid/FDRE tenders make wind a system need, not a subsidy trade; multi-year demand visibility.
  • Full order book — ~4.4 GW (Jul 2026), plus a 200 MW NLC award; management guides ~75% FY27 revenue growth at 20–22% margins.
  • Repaired balance sheet — rights issue and IWEL merger lifted net worth to ~₹7,692 Cr; net debt ~₹940 Cr is modest against that equity.
  • Product & mix — the 3.3 MW platform lifts revenue and margin per machine, with a 4-plus MW machine already coming.
  • Recurring O&M annuity — a growing, cycle-resistant service base via Inox Green (adding Wind World's 4.5 GW from Q3 FY27) underpinning group value; the IRSL listing could unlock EPC value.

The bear case

  • Execution / conversion risk — the company's entire history is orders that slipped; commissioning still hostage to land, cranes, evacuation lines and sign-offs.
  • Still-full valuation — ~35× trailing earnings (as of 13 Oct 2026) on a profit base that fell 34% in Q1 FY27; any further commissioning miss can extend the de-rating.
  • Cyclicality & policy dependence — auctions, tariffs, transmission build-out and tender timing can stall installs, as they did for years.
  • Customer & working-capital risk — receivables reached ₹4,250 Cr (Mar 2026, from ₹2,549 Cr) and FY26 operating cash flow was −₹598 Cr; input costs and inventory swings pressure cash.
  • Group complexity & competition — O&M value sits in a separate listco; a third of the headline order book is a group-company MOU (only 0.5 GW firm); promoters hold a minority 44.18%; Suzlon and global OEMs compete, and solar caps how much wind gets built.
The bottom line

Inox Wind has spent a decade being a company that could sell wind turbines but couldn't reliably deliver them, weighed down by a broken balance sheet. Both of those failings have been partly fixed — far less debt, simplified structure, a full order book, a better machine — at exactly the moment India's wind market turned structurally, not cyclically, higher. What remains unproven is the one thing that always mattered: turning despatched steel into commissioned, grid-feeding, paid-for megawatts on time. With megawatt figures undisclosed, the next few quarters answer it in two checkable lines — quarterly revenue against the ~75% growth guidance, and whether the ₹4,250 Cr of receivables turns into cash. Watch those each quarter; it will tell you whether FY27's ~75% growth guidance was a plan or a promise.
