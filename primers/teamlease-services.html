TeamLease Services, the six-day float behind 3.4 lakh payslips

A plain-English walk through the Bengaluru company that is the legal employer of roughly 3.4 lakh people who work at other firms' counters, branches, warehouses and IT desks — why a ₹11,800-crore revenue line yields barely ₹158 crore of operating profit, how it gets paid within a week while carrying 3.4 lakh salaries on its books, why apprentices and government rulebooks matter more here than anywhere else in staffing, and why a business whose profits grew six-fold since its 2016 listing now trades for about what it was worth on IPO day.

00

Snapshot: one employer, four thousand clients

What TeamLease is, in the three facts that matter most.

TeamLease Services is, on paper, one of the largest private-sector employers in India — and almost none of its employees work in a TeamLease office. Its ~3.41 lakh associates and trainees (June 2026) sell loans in bank branches, stock shelves in supermarkets, pick orders in e-commerce warehouses, run telecom towers, train as apprentices on factory floors and write code inside the India offices of global companies. TeamLease hires them, puts them on its own payroll, deducts and deposits their provident fund (PF) and state health insurance (ESIC), issues their payslips, handles their exits — and bills more than 4,000 client employers for all of it. It was founded in Bengaluru in 2002 by Manish Sabharwal, Ashok Reddy and Mohit Gupta, listed in February 2016, and since February 2026 has been run by its first outside chief executive, Suparna Mitra, formerly head of Titan's watches and wearables division.

Three facts frame the rest of this primer:

  • The revenue is mostly other people's salaries. In FY26 TeamLease booked ₹11,791 Cr of operating revenue but only ₹158 Cr of EBITDA — a 1.34% margin. That is not a sign of a weak business. The invoice it raises on a client is mainly the associate's wage and the employer's statutory contributions, which TeamLease collects and pays straight out. The part it actually earns is a fee of a few hundred rupees per worker per month.
  • It gets paid almost as fast as it pays. Days sales outstanding (DSO) in the staffing business were just 6 days in FY26 and Q1 FY27. A company running close to a thousand crore of payroll a month without borrowing to do it ended FY26 with roughly ₹600 Cr of net free cash. It used ₹238 Cr of that to buy back 8.87% of its shares at ₹1,600 in July 2026, leaving about ₹350 Cr.
  • The market has stopped paying for the story. At about ₹1,087 on 8 October 2026, TeamLease is worth roughly ₹1,661 Cr — around 11× FY26 earnings and near the bottom of its 52-week range. The stock trades about a third below the price at which the company itself bought shares back three months ago, and only about 28% above its 2016 IPO price, even though EBITDA has grown about six-fold since then.

In plain terms

A bank wants 2,000 people to sell credit cards across 60 towns but does not want them on its own payroll. TeamLease finds them, employs them, pays them on the first of the month, files their PF and ESIC, and sends the bank one bill. The bank pays that bill within about a week. TeamLease keeps a few hundred rupees per person and passes the rest through. Do this for 3.4 lakh people, and you get a company with an enormous turnover, a tiny margin and very little need for capital. The open question for investors is not whether the model works. It plainly does. The question is whether it can grow again after two years in which headcount went sideways and the share price fell by about four-fifths from its 2021 peak.

01

Anatomy of a TeamLease invoice

Follow one associate's monthly bill and the 1% margin explains itself.

The quickest way to understand TeamLease is to take apart a single invoice. On average, across its general staffing and apprenticeship book, TeamLease billed roughly ₹26,700 per associate per month in FY26 (segment revenue of ₹10,880 Cr spread over an average of about 3.4 lakh heads). Almost all of that is money that belongs to someone else before it ever reaches TeamLease's bank account.

Seen this way, the headline margin is the wrong denominator. Against the fee it actually earns, TeamLease's EBITDA is roughly 40–45% of the general-staffing PAPM: of each ₹680, about ₹390 goes on its own people, offices and systems and about ₹290 survives. Against the gross invoice, the same ₹290 is just over 1%. Both are true descriptions of one rupee flow. Only the first tells you whether the business is efficient.

The fee itself is set in a few different ways. Most clients pay a fixed markup per head. A growing share of new clients sign up on a variable markup or outcome-linked structure, in which TeamLease's fee moves with the client's productivity or hiring outcomes: 55% of new general-staffing logos in Q3 FY26, more than 70% in Q4 and more than 65% in Q1 FY27. On top of the fee, TeamLease now charges a separate hiring fee on a rising share of placements — 49% of offer letters in Q3 FY26 against 36% a quarter earlier, according to management — which is why the PAPM edged up from ₹669 to ₹680 in a quarter when headcount fell.

The detail that most people miss: the tax on the bill is bigger than the fee

On a ₹26,700 invoice, GST at 18% comes to about ₹4,800 — roughly seven times the ₹680 TeamLease keeps. For a manufacturer or a retailer that sets the GST off against its own output tax, that is irrelevant. For an employer that cannot fully reclaim it, it is a large cost that disappears the moment the worker is moved onto the client's own payroll. Banks and NBFCs can generally take only half the input credit under the special option for financial institutions; exempt activities (some agriculture, some education and health) may get none. That one quirk of the tax code explains why the boundary between "outsourced" and "in-house" is never fully settled in sectors like BFSI — and why section 06 is about regulation rather than demand.

The other lever hidden in the invoice is where the worker lives. Management noted in early 2026 that more general-staffing hiring is coming from tier-2 and tier-3 towns, where average salaries run 30–40% below metro levels. A small-town associate produces a much smaller invoice, but the fee per head is similar. So a shift to smaller towns slows revenue growth while leaving profit per head intact — another reason to watch associates and PAPM rather than the top line.

02

Three engines, wildly unequal

Ninety-two percent of revenue comes from one segment; profit is spread far more evenly.

TeamLease reports three segments. General Staffing & Allied Services combines volume staffing with the Degree Apprenticeship programme. Specialised Staffing places IT, engineering, telecom and finance professionals, increasingly inside Global Capability Centres (GCCs). Other HR Services holds the education, compliance-software and payroll-technology businesses. Their sizes look nothing like their profit contributions.

The TeamLease-only arithmetic: 2% of the people, 28% of the profit

On TeamLease's FY26 numbers, about 7,500 specialists — around 2% of the headcount — produced more than a quarter of operating profit, and management said on its Q3 FY26 call that IT (including GCCs) is about 6% of revenue but nearly 30% of the bottom line. That means moving 1,000 people from general into specialised staffing would add as much EBITDA as adding about 20,000 general associates. It also explains why the company's mood swings with the IT cycle more than its 92%-general-staffing revenue mix suggests. When IT services companies cut contractor headcount by about half over two years, as TeamLease says happened to its IT-services book, the profit effect was much larger than the revenue effect.

The asymmetry cuts both ways. General staffing is the stable base and the source of scale: the compliance machine, the branch network across 28 states and the client relationships that make TeamLease hard to replace. Specialised staffing is the profit lever, but a cyclical one. Other HR Services is the optionality — small, seasonal and, as section 08 shows, still finding its shape.

03

The apprentice franchise — built on a rulebook

TeamLease's most distinctive business exists because of a law, and once nearly vanished because of a notification.

Roughly one in eight people on TeamLease's rolls is not an employee at all but an apprentice. Its Degree Apprenticeship (DA) business placed 43,200 trainees at client sites in June 2026. These trainees earn a stipend while working, often while studying for a vocational degree or diploma through TeamLease-linked institutions. No other listed Indian staffing company has built apprenticeship into a business of this size, and it is the clearest expression of the founders' long-running argument that India's jobs problem is really an employability problem.

How an apprenticeship differs from a temp job

  • It sits under a different law. Apprentices are engaged under the Apprentices Act and the government's National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme (NAPS) and National Apprenticeship Training Scheme (NATS), not under the labour laws that govern employees. Establishments of a certain size are expected to engage apprentices equal to 2.5–15% of their workforce.
  • The client gets a pipeline, not just a pair of hands. Employers use apprentices to build trained entry-level talent, meet their Apprentices Act obligations and test people before hiring them. GCCs have become a fast-growing source of demand, partly for compliance reasons.
  • TeamLease's role extends to the classroom. It runs the programme, handles stipends and compliance, and increasingly sells "integrated learning" — structured training linked to the on-the-job work. In June 2026, 32% of the DA base had adopted these learning solutions, up from 30% in March.

The December 2022 lesson

The business's previous form shows its weak spot. For years TeamLease ran NETAP — the National Employability Through Apprenticeship Program — as a public-private partnership of TeamLease Skills University, the CII and the National Skill Development Corporation, under the AICTE's National Employability Enhancement Mission (NEEM). On 23 December 2022, NEEM was discontinued. TeamLease had to release about 19,000 trainees immediately and move another ~26,000 to other arrangements. The DA business turned net positive on headcount again only in Q3 FY24, after being rebuilt on the NAPS/NATS framework.

The last year has shown the same sensitivity on a smaller scale. DA headcount fell from 51,600 in September 2025 to 46,000 in December, as two clients took trainees onto their own rolls for regulatory reasons — the large NBFC discussed in section 06, and an agricultural company whose GST exemption on certain worker profiles was rejected by tax officials. A further 5,500 planned exits in Q1 FY27 (trainees completing their terms) took the base to 43,200, although management said net additions excluding those exits were about 2,100 in the quarter.

What the apprentice business really is

A general-staffing associate exists because a client wants labour without the paperwork. An apprentice exists because the State has defined a category — a learner who works, earns a stipend and is not yet an employee — and encourages employers to use it. TeamLease built a business on operating that category at scale. That gives it a franchise few rivals can copy, because it needs a university, training content, compliance systems and employer relationships at the same time. It also ties the franchise to the wording of government schemes, which can change without notice. In 2022 a single notification removed close to half of the programme. That is the risk an investor accepts in exchange for the most distinctive business TeamLease owns.

04

Founders, funds and a hand-over

A founder-built company that is now owned mostly by Indian mutual funds and run by an outsider.

TeamLease grew out of a friendship and a previous company. Manish Sabharwal, Ashok Reddy and Mohit Gupta had earlier built India Life, a pension and HR-outsourcing business that Hewitt Associates acquired in 2002. That same year the three started TeamLease from four offices with 20 clients and 40 employees. Sabharwal became one of India's best-known voices on labour reform and formal employment, and TeamLease's annual labour reports and its push for apprenticeships made the company as much a policy advocate as a staffing firm.

Who owns it

  • Founders — about a third. The promoter group held 31.11% before the buyback (about 52.2 lakh shares) and roughly 33% after it, because the share count fell. The largest promoter vehicle is Singapore-based HR Offshoring Ventures Pte Ltd, with about 24% before the buyback. NED Consultants LLP and MKS Management Consultancy Services LLP act in concert with it, and Hansini Management Consultant is in the promoter group. A small number of promoter shares (about 1.6% of their holding) were encumbered in March 2026.
  • Domestic institutions — the largest block. DIIs held about 44% in July 2026, mostly mutual funds (around 39% in June 2026). Nippon India Multi Cap Fund held close to 9% in March 2026. ICICI Prudential Mutual Fund cut its stake from 5.3% to 3.2% in July 2026.
  • Foreign investors held about 8%, and retail and other shareholders about 14%.
Why the shareholder register matters: few Indian companies of this age have founders holding only a third and domestic mutual funds holding more than that. It means TeamLease has no single controlling family or parent to steer capital allocation, and big fund flows can move the price sharply in a thinly traded mid-cap. It also explains the tone of recent earnings calls, where fund managers pushed openly for buybacks because idle cash was holding down return on equity.

The hand-over

The February 2026 change is the biggest leadership shift in TeamLease's history. It is the first time both co-founders have stepped back from day-to-day executive control at the same time, with an outsider taking the top job. Ashok Reddy told analysts the aim was a "board-run company that outlives the promoters." He said the board chose Suparna Mitra for her background in consumer businesses, higher-margin products and building product portfolios, and for a fresh perspective. Her stated priorities are profitable growth, deeper client relationships, technology platforms and higher-margin adjacencies. The day-to-day operating team — CFO and COO Ramani Dathi, and segment heads for staffing, specialised staffing and apprenticeships — is largely unchanged.

Reading the transition

TeamLease's founders built a mission-driven company whose stated goal was to become India's largest private-sector employer. The new brief is subtly different: grow profit, not just headcount, and shift some revenue from business clients toward services sold directly to the associates themselves — earned-wage access, small loans, learning and benefits. That is a big change of emphasis for a firm that has always measured itself in people employed. Investors will judge it by whether the EBITDA margin moves, not by how many offices open.

05

Six days to cash — and the State's IOU

How a company carrying 3.4 lakh salaries ended up with surplus cash, and why part of it sat with the tax department.

A staffing company's hidden risk is timing. It must pay every associate on payday, deposit PF and ESIC on statutory deadlines and pay GST on invoices it has raised, often before the client settles. If a client pays in 60 days, the staffing firm is in effect lending it two months of payroll. TeamLease has made the opposite its signature: in FY26 the staffing business had a DSO of 6 days, against about 15 days for the group as a whole (the education and software businesses collect more slowly).

Management describes this simply as receivables discipline, and tracks a second measure it calls funding exposure — broadly, how much of the staffing payroll TeamLease has to fund from its own balance sheet before client money arrives. It was 14% through FY26 and 16% in Q1 FY27. The practical result is that TeamLease has never needed much debt to grow. Gross borrowings on Screener's classification were only about ₹116 Cr at March 2026, against reserves of about ₹1,027 Cr.

What six days is worth — a counterfactual only TeamLease's numbers allow

At 6 days, TeamLease's FY26 revenue of ₹11,791 Cr implies about ₹195 Cr owed by clients at any moment. Collect at Quess's ~24 days instead and the figure rises to roughly ₹775 Cr. The difference — about ₹580 Cr — is working capital TeamLease does not need. That is around 35% of its entire market value in October 2026, or almost four years of net profit. Staffing firms differ less in their margins than people assume. They differ hugely in how much cash they tie up to earn them, and on that measure TeamLease is unusually lean.

The surprise in the balance sheet: tax paid in advance

The other large item in TeamLease's cash story is not owed by clients but by the income-tax department. Clients withhold tax at source (TDS) on what they pay a staffing firm — commonly 2% for contract work — and that withholding is calculated on the gross bill, which is mostly salary. TeamLease's own profit before tax, however, is only about 1.3% of revenue, and its tax bill on that profit is reduced further by the Section 80JJAA deduction for new hiring. The result is that much more tax is collected upfront than TeamLease ultimately owes, and the excess sits as a receivable until assessments are completed and refunds arrive.

This explains the jumps in TeamLease's cash. Net free cash was about ₹430 Cr in December 2025 and about ₹600 Cr in March 2026, helped by those refunds; operating cash flow for FY26 was about ₹302 Cr, twice reported profit, for the same reason. After the ₹238 Cr buyback, net free cash was about ₹350 Cr in June 2026, helped by another ₹38 Cr refund. It also explains part of the "other income" line: refunds carry interest, which inflated PBT growth in FY26.

The tax rate is a risk as well as a gift: on the Q3 FY26 call, management said tax authorities had reopened completed assessments on whether outsourced associates qualify for the 80JJAA deduction, although no demand was outstanding. If the deduction were narrowed, TeamLease's effective tax rate could move toward the statutory ~25%, which on FY26 figures would cut net profit by roughly a fifth without any change in the business.
06

When the rulebook hires — and fires — your associates

Labour codes, RBI directives and GST rulings moved TeamLease's headcount more than demand did over the past year.

Every staffing company is exposed to regulation, but TeamLease's past year shows how directly. In a single quarter, Q3 FY26, it lost about 27,000 associates and apprentices at one large NBFC client — identified on the earnings call as Bajaj — because the RBI directed that lender to move certain roles onto its own payroll. Management said a similar directive had hit once before, in Q4 FY25. Over the same months, an agricultural client took apprentices in-house after GST officials ruled that their roles did not qualify for an exemption it had been claiming. Neither change had anything to do with whether those clients still needed the people.

The labour codes: a slow tailwind, not a switch

The labour codes are the reform India's organised staffing industry lobbied for over a decade. Their logic favours companies like TeamLease: with one wage definition, one national licence and standardised appointment letters, informal contractors who cut corners on PF and ESIC find it harder to compete on price. TeamLease shares rose about 11% on the day the market priced in the codes in November 2025. But management has been cautious about the near term. On the Q3 FY26 call it said clients were first working out the cost impact and restructuring salaries, and that it did not expect the codes to lift demand substantially in the immediate cycle. Industry data agreed: the Indian Staffing Federation reported that sequential growth in flexi staffing slowed to about 0.5% in October–December 2025 as companies paused hiring during implementation.

For TeamLease's own P&L, the important detail is the contract structure. Its client agreements say any new statutory cost on associates is passed through to the client, and the company books matching gratuity and leave liabilities and client receivables. The only hit it took was the ₹5.68 Cr provision for its own core staff, shown as an exceptional item in Q3 FY26.

The regulator as both supplier and competitor

For most companies, regulation is background noise. For TeamLease it is closer to the product. The labour codes make outsourcing to a compliant firm more attractive; the GST code adds 18% to every bill; the RBI can tell a bank which jobs it must employ directly; and the Apprentices Act decides whether a trainee category exists at all. In a single year, those rules both widened TeamLease's long-term market and removed about 7% of its headcount. Forecasting this company means forecasting rule changes, not just GDP growth or hiring cycles.

07

Specialists, GCCs and the AI question

The profit engine moved from IT services to global capability centres — just as AI changed what gets hired.

TeamLease's specialised-staffing arm was built mainly through acquisitions between 2015 and 2020 — ASAP, Nichepro, Keystone, and the IT-staffing businesses of Ecentric and I.M.S.I. By 2021 the company claimed to be India's largest IT staffing company by headcount. Then the customer base changed underneath it. Indian IT services firms, once the biggest buyers of contract engineers, cut back hard. TeamLease told analysts in February 2026 that its IT-services headcount had fallen by almost half over two years.

The replacement has been Global Capability Centres — the in-house India technology, finance and research hubs of multinational companies. GCCs now produce more than 67% of specialised-staffing revenue. TeamLease served 120+ GCC clients in June 2026, up from 100+ at December 2025, out of what management estimates are roughly 1,800–1,900 GCCs in India. It also offers them a build-operate-transfer (BOT) model, in which TeamLease builds and runs a team that the client later takes over — a structure management says earns better margins than plain staffing.

  • Growth is back. Specialised headcount rose 13% year-on-year to 7,630 in June 2026, and gross revenue rose 21%. TeamLease added 40 new specialised clients in Q1 FY27, 15 of them GCCs.
  • Margins dipped seasonally. Segment EBITDA margin was 7.4% for FY26 but 6.2% in Q1 FY27, which management attributed to the annual appraisal cycle and higher leave-taking by associates — seasonal rather than structural, in its telling.
  • The skills mix is shifting. Demand for Java and .NET developers, manual testers and people managers has dropped sharply; demand for AI, data, cloud and cybersecurity roles is rising. Volumes are lower but rate cards are higher. About 30% of hiring is now for non-tech specialist skills such as finance and engineering.
  • International is new and small. TeamLease entered Singapore and the UAE in 2025; its global business passed 100 heads in early 2026. The CFO told analysts that Quess's larger international specialised-staffing book is the main reason Quess reports higher margins.
Why AI matters more here than in general staffing

A retail associate or a field-sales executive is hard to automate away. A contract developer writing routine code is not. Generative AI tools reduce the need for exactly the conventional IT roles that once filled TeamLease's specialised bench — while creating demand for scarcer, better-paid specialists. Because each specialist earns TeamLease about twenty times the profit of a general associate, losing a thousand routine coders hurts more than losing ten thousand general associates. The GCC pivot has so far offset that, but it ties TeamLease's most profitable segment to the global corporate technology budget — a risk management itself flagged in its Q1 FY27 presentation.

HR services: a school calendar inside a staffing firm

Small in revenue, outsized in its effect on quarterly profit — and still being pruned.

Other HR Services is TeamLease's attempt to sell more than headcount. It earned ₹242 Cr of revenue and ₹10.5 Cr of EBITDA in FY26, both up more than 20%. Inside it are three quite different businesses, and one of them follows the academic year rather than the business cycle.

The quarterly pattern is the real story. EdTech revenue rose from about ₹30 Cr in Q1 FY26 to ₹59 Cr in Q4, and its EBITDA went from a loss of about ₹5 Cr to a profit of about ₹12 Cr. In fact, the Q4 quarter alone produced EdTech's entire full-year profit. The cycle then reset: in Q1 FY27, segment revenue fell 34% quarter-on-quarter to ₹50 Cr and the segment lost about ₹2 Cr. Alongside these sits a payroll-outsourcing (HCM) business built from the TSR Darashaw acquisition, which manages more than 3.5 lakh monthly employee records; management has described it as a roughly 15%-margin enterprise service. The 30% stake in Crystal HR (Wallet HR), a payroll-software platform for small firms that management said earned about 30% margins, is being exited as part of what the company calls portfolio rationalisation — an early sign that the new leadership is prepared to prune rather than only add.

How to read the small segment

Other HR Services is only about 2% of revenue, but it explains much of the quarterly noise in TeamLease's profits. Anyone comparing quarters should look at "business EBITDA" — the operating profit of the three segments before corporate costs — and compare each quarter with the same quarter a year earlier, never with the quarter before. On that basis, Q1 FY27 business EBITDA was up 18%. The strategic test is whether RegTech, now profitable, and the payroll services can become a steady, higher-margin layer that does not depend on university admission seasons.

The field: a formal market still finding its size

India's organised flexi workforce is about 1.9 million people — and TeamLease employs roughly one in seven of them.

India has hundreds of millions of workers, but only a small fraction are employed through organised, fully compliant staffing firms. The Indian Staffing Federation (ISF), the industry body TeamLease helped found, counted a formal flexi workforce of about 1.91 million among its members at the end of FY26, after adding about 1.18 lakh people (+7.9%) in the year. Growth slowed from FY25, when the industry added about 1.39 lakh. TeamLease's 2.87 lakh general-staffing associates at March 2026 were roughly 15% of that ISF member base; the company itself cites a market share of about 6% on a broader definition of the market.

The ISF's quarterly readings over FY26 tell the same story as TeamLease's own: 6.1% year-on-year growth in April–June 2025, the weakest first quarter since the pandemic; a pause to about 0.5% sequential growth in October–December as labour-code implementation began; and a modest 0.9% recovery in January–March 2026, driven by e-commerce, quick commerce and warehousing. IT staffing recovered through the year, as TeamLease's own specialised numbers did.

The rivalry is not mainly about price. A client choosing a staffing partner for 5,000 people cares about whether every PF and ESIC deposit will be made on time across 15 states, whether replacements arrive quickly when people quit, and what the partner's terms are on payment. Large clients do drive hard bargains — management has repeatedly said its biggest clients pay a lower PAPM — but TeamLease's choice to accept slower growth rather than slow-paying clients is the clearest strategic difference between it and some peers.

The valuation puzzle against Quess

On most operating measures TeamLease looks at least as disciplined as Quess: far faster collections, net cash, a similar general-staffing margin by the CFO's account. Yet in October 2026 the market valued Quess at roughly 19 times earnings and TeamLease at about 11. The gap reflects what investors have been rewarding — Quess's higher margin from its international and professional book, its ~21% return on equity against TeamLease's ~14% (held down by idle cash), and TeamLease's two years of flat-to-falling headcount. Each of those is something TeamLease can change. None of them is a structural disadvantage.

The surprise: a six-fold bigger business for the same price

In February 2016 investors paid about ₹1,450 Cr for TeamLease. In October 2026 they can buy a company with six times the EBITDA, around ₹350 Cr of net free cash and another ~₹145 Cr of tax owed back to it, for about ₹1,661 Cr. Shareholders who held from the IPO have earned about 28% in ten years, with no dividends along the way — the company has returned cash only through buybacks, at ₹3,050 in 2023 and ₹1,600 in 2026. The operating record was not the problem. The starting price was: TeamLease listed at a valuation that assumed growth would also come with rising margins, and margins went the other way. The decade's profit growth was almost entirely absorbed by a fall in the multiple from the mid-50s to about 10 times EBITDA.

Valuation today

  • Price and size: about ₹1,087 a share on 8 October 2026; roughly 1.53 Cr shares after the buyback; market capitalisation about ₹1,661 Cr. The 52-week range was roughly ₹1,063–1,870.
  • Earnings multiple: about 11× FY26 PAT (Screener shows ~10.9× trailing twelve months).
  • Enterprise value: after deducting about ₹350 Cr of net free cash, roughly ₹1,310 Cr — about 8× FY26 EBITDA. Counting the ~₹145 Cr TDS receivable as cash-like would take it closer to 7×.
  • Returns: ROE about 14% (Screener), held down by cash; the buyback should lift it mechanically.
The number that settles the thesis — the EBITDA margin, in basis points: on ₹11,800 Cr of revenue, every 0.10 percentage point of margin is about ₹12 Cr of EBITDA — roughly 7.5% of FY26's total. Getting back from 1.34% to the FY22 level of 2.2% would add about ₹100 Cr a year with no extra revenue. That is the case the new management is being judged on: not more associates for their own sake, but a richer mix (specialists, GCCs, BOT deals, RegTech and payroll services, associate-facing products) that lifts the margin a few basis points every year. Q1 FY27's 1.05% was seasonal; the full-year FY27 figure against FY26's 1.34% is the test.
11

Future, risks, and the bull/bear ledger

What happens next, what could go wrong, and how the two cases line up.

TeamLease enters the second half of FY27 with a new chief executive, a cleaner balance sheet after the buyback, a headcount base that has stopped shrinking, and a set of reforms that should favour organised staffing over time. What it does not yet have is a visible growth engine in general staffing, where BFSI demand is still soft and the effect of the labour codes on outsourcing is unproven.

What to watch

  • Q2 FY27 results (expected around late October 2026): whether general-staffing net additions continue after Q1's ~4,000, and whether specialised margins recover from 6.2%.
  • Q3 FY27 — the first clean comparison. December 2026 is the first quarter compared against the post-insourcing base of 3,35,165. Year-on-year headcount growth should become visible there if demand is real.
  • State labour-code rules. Each state's rules decide how quickly the single licence and uniform wage definition take effect — and how soon unorganised contractors lose their cost advantage.
  • Capital allocation after the buyback. About ₹350 Cr of net free cash, the ~₹145 Cr TDS receivable and proceeds from the Crystal HR exit leave room for acquisitions or further returns. Management has said it has looked at small acquisitions.
  • Suparna Mitra's first full plan: particularly the size of the direct-to-associate business (earned-wage access, loans, learning, rewards) and any further pruning of the HR-services portfolio.

Key risks

  • Regulatory insourcing. One RBI directive removed about 27,000 heads; BFSI is still about a fifth of general staffing. Similar directives, or GST rulings, at other large clients could repeat the hit.
  • Tax. The ~6% effective tax rate depends on the 80JJAA deduction, which tax officials have questioned. A separate ₹32.29 Cr CGST penalty is under challenge in the Karnataka High Court.
  • AI and the IT cycle. The most profitable segment depends on GCC and technology hiring budgets, which AI tools and global spending cuts can shrink quickly.
  • Apprenticeship policy. The NEEM withdrawal in 2022 showed how quickly a scheme change can erase trainees; the DA business still depends on the NAPS/NATS framework and stipend support.
  • Client concentration and pricing. Large clients pay lower fees and can move work in-house; variable-markup contracts share upside but also downside.
  • Execution during the hand-over. A first-time industry outsider is leading a 3.4-lakh-person operation whose edge depends on operating discipline.

Bull case

  • At ~8× EV/EBITDA with net cash and tax refunds due, the price implies little growth for a business that has grown EBITDA in each of the last three years.
  • Six-day collections and back-to-back pass-through of statutory costs make the model unusually resilient; the labour codes raise the bar for unorganised competitors.
  • Specialised staffing and GCC demand are growing double digits; each shift of people toward specialists lifts profit far more than revenue.
  • The NBFC insourcing is now in the base, so reported headcount growth should turn positive.
  • A new CEO focused on margin, a profitable RegTech business and portfolio pruning point toward a higher-margin mix.

Bear case

  • Headcount has gone sideways for two years; general staffing is growing in low-wage towns, which slows revenue, while BFSI stays weak.
  • The EBITDA margin is still far below FY22's 2.2%, and Q1 FY27 was only 1.05% — the recovery is slow and seasonal.
  • Profit growth in FY26 leaned on tax-refund interest and a very low tax rate that tax officials are questioning.
  • AI could keep shrinking demand for the routine IT roles that once made specialised staffing so profitable.
  • Government policy can remove whole categories of TeamLease's workforce — as NEEM and the RBI directive both showed.
The bottom line

TeamLease is an efficient machine for employing people on behalf of others — it collects within six days, passes statutory costs straight through and needs almost no capital. It has spent two years with flat headcount and falling margins in its best segment, while government rules reshaped its client base, and the market has marked it down to roughly the value it carried on IPO day. Whether that is too pessimistic will be visible soon and in specific numbers: the December 2026 quarter, when headcount is first compared against the post-insourcing base of 3,35,165, and the full-year FY27 EBITDA margin, which needs to rise above FY26's 1.34% under Suparna Mitra's first full year in charge. If both move up, the six-day float and the ₹350 Cr cash pile become the base for a re-rating. If they stall, TeamLease remains a well-run payroll processor priced for little growth.
